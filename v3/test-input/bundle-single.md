# Retelling 01 — Vira Toland, a Hessel Bottom patron's daughter, recorded at Ostrey Hollow, 1979

*Tape made at her kitchen table by a young man from the county who asked three questions in an hour and let her have the rest of it; transcribed the way she said it, with her own doublings back left in.*

I was born in nineteen twelve at the top end of Ostrey Hollow, in the house my father's father put up, and except for two years down at Ashlin when I was first married I have never lived anywhere else, and I would not count those two years anyhow. My father hauled milk to the station the same as everybody up here did. There were nine families of us hauling to that station and we were in and out of each other's kitchens the whole year round and I could name you every one of them, but you don't want names, you want the trouble, they always want the trouble.

The station stood at the very top of the Hollow where the road bends and then goes to nothing but the ridge. It was a low building, whitewashed, cold as a spring house inside even in August, and it smelled of milk and steam and hot iron, and you could hear the engine going before you came round the last turn. Ansel Keddie ran it. He went up there in ninety-five, the year before they opened it, to get it ready, and he tested that first summer too, and he was still there when I was a girl big enough to ride on the wagon with my father, so you can count that up and see how long a man can stand in one room.

He kept a book. I want to say about that book because everybody knew about it, it wasn't any secret thing. It was ruled, and the ruling was his own, done with a straightedge, and every line in it was the same height as every other line, and his figures were like print. He wrote in it standing up at a slope desk by the window where the light came in. If a man came in and asked him what his milk had done, he did not have to hunt. He turned to the day and he read it off. He tested a thousand cans a week and never a blot in that book, and I heard my father say more than once that if the rest of the world kept its accounts the way Ansel Keddie kept his there would be no lawyers in Ashlin. Which was my father's way of talking. There are still lawyers in Ashlin.

Now. Nineteen nineteen. That is the year everything went sour up here and I was seven years old and I remember it the way you remember a smell. A letter came from the Association, printed, over the manager's signature, and it said there was water in the cans coming down from Ostrey Hollow. That is what it said. Water in the cans. It went to nine families and my father was one of the nine and he read it at the table with his hat still on, which he never did, and my mother stood at the stove with her back to him and did not turn around the whole time he read it. He read it twice. Then he put it in the drawer of the desk in the front room and it was still in that drawer when he died and I believe my brother has it yet. Some of them kept theirs till they died. One woman I knew had hers pinned inside the lid of her trunk. You can tell people a thing like that is only business and they will still keep the paper forty years to prove what was said of them.

The manager that signed it was Selby Vose, down at Larrow Green. He was old Gideon Vose's nephew, and old Gideon was the one that started the whole Association back in the beginning, and I think the family always thought the valley belonged to them a little. And I will tell you another thing that everybody up here knew and nobody wrote down anywhere. Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins. Everybody knew that. So when the letter came it was not only the Association saying it, if you follow me, it was somebody's cousin saying it, and that is a different thing altogether in a place this size.

Orra was Ansel Keddie's wife. And Orra was Emmet Brant's sister. Emmet Brant was the biggest man on the Bottom, he had the most cows and the most hay ground and he hauled more milk to that station than anybody, and he was the one that letter cost the most. Now think about that a minute. The man that tested the milk had married the sister of the man that got the worst of it. Before nineteen nineteen those two families ate their Sunday dinners together, I have seen them at it, the two houses in one kitchen and the men out at the fence after. After nineteen nineteen they did not speak. Not one word, not at the store, not at a funeral. Orra would walk down to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane. Years and years those two lived within sight of each other's chimney smoke and never said good morning, and both of them long in the ground now, and for what.

Then the tests went up. All at once, one summer, the figures came up and stayed up, and nobody ever said why. Not the station, not the Association, not anybody. My father came in from hauling and said the milk was testing better and he did not know what he had done different, and he had not done anything different, none of them had. That was the queerest part of the whole business to me even as a child, that the thing corrected itself and no man stood up and gave a reason for it.

And after that there was the hearing down at Ashlin. That was the winter and spring after. They sat in an office down there, lawyers and the officers of the Association and our men going down in the wagons, and it went on and on. And at the end of it they put Ansel Keddie out. An old man, and out, after all those seasons in that cold room, and they paid the patrons thirty-five hundred dollars and called it settled. Thirty-five hundred dollars, split up nine ways over all those years — my father's share of it did not buy a hay rake.

He was taking the cream. They caught him at it and they put him out, and that is why the numbers went right the summer after. That is what it was. I am not going to dress it up for you. A man stands in a room by himself with the separator running and the door shut and nobody in the valley can add, and after a while he helps himself, and it is not that he is a wicked man, it is that nobody ever came up that road and looked. My father never would say it out loud. My father said Ansel Keddie was an honest man who had got into something, which is a nice way of saying it and I never saw the difference.

He died in the thirties and he was still under it when he died. That is the part that sits badly with me now that I am old. Whatever he did or did not do, he had all those years of this valley looking at him a certain way in church, and Orra had it too, and she outlived him a good while and had it the whole time.

There was a young woman came round here in nineteen fifty-eight from the college away east, asking after all this, and she went up and poked about the old station building, which was shut by then and had been for years, they kept fence wire and salt in it. She sat where you are sitting. Nice girl, careful, wrote everything down. She was Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed, so she was of the family both sides in a manner of speaking, and I remember thinking, well, here it comes round again after all this time and there is nobody left alive that it can do any good to. I told her about the letter in my father's drawer. I don't know what she made of it.

That is the whole of what I know. It rained the day of that letter, I remember the wet on my father's shoulders when he came in, and I have never in sixty years been able to think about the one without the other.

# Retelling 02 — Effie Loomis, secretary of the Association 1901–1940, "Notes toward a history of the Association," 1941

*Written out in numbered sections for the Association's own files, in the year after I laid down the minute book: from the minutes wherever the minutes will bear it, from my own recollection where they will not, and the two kept apart here as they ought to be kept.*

**1. Of the organizing of the Association.** In 1889 the farmers of the Larrow Valley met at Larrow Green and organized themselves into the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose was elected the first president at that meeting and he held the office until 1911, which is twenty-two years, and no other man has held it so long. I did not see any of this. I was fourteen years old in 1889 and I came into the office as secretary in 1901, so for the first twelve years of the Association I am only a reader of its papers like anybody else, and a poorer reader than some, because the papers of exactly those years are the ones we have lost. The 1889–1892 minute book was lost when the office was moved in 1938. I say this here at the beginning because it governs everything I can and cannot write in the sections following.

**2. Of the creamery at Larrow Green.** The main creamery was built at Larrow Green in 1891, on Larrow Creek, and it is the same building yet. Before it was built the members sold their milk where they could get a price for it and there was no common test and no common price, and every man's arithmetic was his own. The creamery ended that. Whatever is said of the Association afterward, it began as a plain remedy for a plain grievance, and I have never heard anybody in this valley say we should have been better without it.

**3. Of the by-laws, and of Article VII.** Gideon Vose drafted the by-laws himself. He was not a lawyer and they read as a farmer wrote them, which is to their credit. The article that governs this history is the seventh, and because it is short and because it has been misquoted at me across this desk for forty years I set it down entire:

> *Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*

Article VII was adopted at the annual meeting of 1892, the year after the creamery opened. What it did was this. Before it, a man was paid for what his cans weighed, and a hundred pounds of milk was a hundred pounds of milk whosever cow it came out of. After it, a man was paid for the fat that was found in his milk by a test made at the place where he delivered it, and the weight of his cans decided nothing by itself. The rule is a just rule and I have always said so: it pays a man for what he actually brings and not for the water that is naturally in it. But I would have the reader notice the last clause, which is the one nobody quotes. *The test of the station where he delivers shall govern.* Not the test of the creamery, not the average of the valley, not a test made over again anywhere else. The station's test, and no appeal from it. That clause is the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business and it was written in 1892 by a man who died in 1917 and could not be asked about it afterward.

**4. Of the officers in my time.** I was secretary from 1901 to 1940 and kept the minute book in my own hand throughout. Wilbur Teague came in as bookkeeper in 1905 and kept the ledgers. Hobart Sill was the buttermaker at Larrow Green and had the churn book and the cream receiving book. Selby Vose, son of the late president, was made manager in 1913 and served until 1938. I will say of the four of us that we were in that office together a very long time, that we were none of us idle, and that not one of us ever went up the valley and stood in the Ostrey Hollow station on a working morning to watch the work being done. I include myself first in that.

**5. Of the station at Ostrey Hollow and the men who served it.** The skimming station stood seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green, at the head of the Hollow, and it took in the milk of the farms up there, separated it, sent the cream down to us and gave the skim back to the patron. The best of that milk came off Hessel Bottom, the wet meadow below the hamlet. Seven farms had rights on Hessel Bottom and they cut hay and pastured on it, and the largest of the seven was Emmet Brant's, who was the station's largest patron in every year of which we have a record.

**6. Of the petition of 1925 and of the board of arbitration.** In January 1925 the patrons of Ostrey Hollow petitioned the Association. Their complaint was that their milk had never tested as their neighbors' milk tested and that they had been paid accordingly for a very long time, and they asked to be made whole. The board of arbitration sat at Ashlin under Alonzo Frick, attorney, of that place, from 17 March to 4 May 1925. Three explanations were argued before it. None of the three was accepted and I shall not rehearse them here; they are in the minutes and the minutes are in the safe. I recorded the award myself on the last day, and it reads:

> *The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account.*

I have written many things into that book with more pleasure. *Finds no cause established*, and then an award of thirty-eight hundred dollars: I took it down as it was given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once.

**7. Of the quantities, from the bookkeeper's ledgers.** For the twenty-five years over which the patrons laid their claim, the ledgers show that the Ostrey Hollow station received forty million pounds of milk, which is an average of one million six hundred thousand pounds in a year. Over the same period the price paid for butterfat averaged twenty-five cents a pound. These two figures are not mine and I do not vouch for the cows, only for the copying: they are Mr. Teague's, out of the annual sheets, and I have added the columns twice.

**8. Of what the record does not contain.** A secretary's authority is the record, and it is a duty of the office to say where the record stops. It stops in two places here. The first is the office move of 1938, in which the 1889–1892 minute book was lost. It was not thrown out; it was carried out with the rest and it did not arrive, and I have looked in every place a book can be in this building. What went with it went for good, and anybody who tells you what was resolved at the first four annual meetings is telling you a story he had from his father.

The second is the crate. The crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all. I took every word down and it is not there. I mean the crate of measures that the state's inspector condemned up at the station in June of 1923, which was set aside and new ones ordered in its place. Not a word of it was spoken in any of the three sessions, by the patrons, by the officers, or by the board, and the award's phrase about "the books and appliances of the station" is a form of words the board used and not a list of anything. Forty years of taking down what men say has taught me that the interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying.

# Retelling 03 — Ansel Keddie, tester at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station, his statement to the board and an unsent letter, spring 1925

*Two papers. The first is what he said, standing, before the board of arbitration at Ashlin on the seventeenth of March, 1925 — nine sentences and no more, taken down as he gave them. The second is a letter written at Ostrey Hollow later that spring; it was never sent, and was found among his papers.*

**I. Statement to the board of arbitration, Ashlin, 17 March 1925.**

> I have kept the test book of this station in my own hand since the station opened, and there is not a figure in it that I did not put there. I have set down the date, the patron, the pounds, the test and the fat, and where I have made an error I have drawn a line through it and put my letters against the line, and I have never written a figure over a figure. I have made the tests in the same order and at the same hour of every working day of every season. I have heard what the patrons say of me and I have heard what the officers say of me. Neither of them has come up that road on a working morning and stood at my elbow and watched the work done. I am asked to account for figures, and the figures are what I read and what I wrote and nothing besides. I am an old man and I have no other trade. I have tested as I was taught and as the state man saw me do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, and I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are. I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself.

**II. Letter, written at Ostrey Hollow in the spring of 1925. Not sent.**

I set this down because I could not say it in that room, where a man is allowed to answer and not allowed to explain, and because it seems to me that somebody ought to know how the work was done before everybody who did it is gone.

The day began at half past five. The wagons came up the Hollow in the dark most of the year, and I had the intake room open and the lamps lit and the scale swinging free before the first of them was at the door. Every can went on the scale and the weight was called and written before the milk went anywhere else. That scale was tested by the state's man on his rounds and it was true, and I have never in twenty-nine seasons had a patron dispute a weight with me and be right.

From each patron's milk I took a sample as it was weighed, and the samples went into his own jar, numbered to him and to nobody else, and the jar was made up over ten days before it was tested. On the testing morning the jar came out and was set in warm water until it was blood-warm right through, and then it was poured from vessel to vessel, and poured again, until the cream that had risen was carried down through the whole of it. A sample that is not warmed and mixed is worth nothing and a boy could tell you so.

Then the charge. The milk was drawn up in the measure to the mark on the stem, and the tip wiped, and the charge blown out into the bottle that carried the patron's number. Then the acid, poured down the side, and the bottle turned until the milk was dark. Four bottles in the hand tester, weighted against each other, the cover down, and the crank turned at the speed I was taught to turn it and no faster, and no man ever stood beside me and made me hurry it. Then the fat is up in the neck of the bottle in a clear column, and it is read off in per cent against the rulings, with a pair of dividers, and the figure is written down. That is the whole of it. It is not a hard trade. It is only a trade that must be done the same way every time, which is a harder thing than it sounds.

The glass I began on in ninety-six was borrowed off the Ashlin creamery, and we used it until our own outfit came up the valley in a crate the spring following.

The book I kept in five columns: the date, the patron, the pounds, the test, the fat. Where I made an error I crossed it and set my initials against the crossing, and I never wrote over a figure, because a figure written over another figure is a figure no man can swear to afterward. Twenty-nine seasons, from 1896 through 1924. I did not test a pound in the season of 1925.

The first leaf of the book reads:

> *Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie.*

In 1922 I made up a leaf at the back with the averages on it, because I had been asked for them so often. It reads:

> *Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80.*

Those nine weeks in 1911 are the strangest thing in my book and I have never had an account of them to give. Our measure broke on the eighth of May and I made shift with one borrowed, and from that morning the tests came up and stood at 3.80, and on the tenth of July our own was in use again and the tests were down again the same day. The two entries stand as I made them:

> *May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure.*

> *July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.*

That question mark is mine and I have let it stand for fourteen years. I could not answer it then and I cannot answer it now, and I will not put anything in its place that I do not know.

I will say one thing more about the figures and then let them alone. They did not come and go with the season. The gap was there in July as it was there in February, in the flush of the grass and in the dead of the winter on dry hay, and it was the same in a wet year as in a dry one. In 1924 the average of this station was 3.80, and I wrote that figure with the same pen and the same measure of care as all the others.

The complaints began in 1902, when it was first said down at Larrow Green that Ostrey Hollow milk did not test with the rest. In 1908 a committee came up and sampled the herds through the winter and reported that the wet hay of the Bottom thinned the cream, and that the remedy lay with the land. In 1919 a circular went out from the Association to nine families up here saying there was water in their cans. I carry no charge against any man who put his name to that paper. I only say that I stood in that room every morning of those years and I know what came into it, and that nine kitchens in this Hollow have had that letter in a drawer ever since.

My wife Orra is Emmet Brant's sister. Emmet brought more milk to my door than any other man on the Bottom, and every pound of it I weighed myself and every test of it I made myself and wrote in the book. He and I have not spoken since the circular. That is the plainest fact in this letter and it is the one I find I have the least to say about.

I was put out on the thirtieth of April. They found no cause established and they put me out on the last day of the month, and both of those things are true at once, and I have given up trying to hold them in my head together. I have tested as I was taught. I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are.

# Retelling 04 — Ivy Keddie, "What the Crate Held," a talk to the Ordell County Historical Society, Ashlin, 1959

*Read from typescript at the Society's meeting, with the crate itself on a table at the front and the borrowed books laid open beside it; the papers quoted here were read aloud from the originals.*

My name will have told most of you already. Ansel Keddie, who kept the test book at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station, was my grandfather. My father, Duncan Keddie, who taught school at Ashlin, was his son. I mention it once, because a granddaughter who reads her grandfather's books ought to say so, and then I intend to leave it alone, because nothing I am going to show you tonight depends in the smallest degree on my opinion of my grandfather. It depends on nine pieces of glass and a burette.

Five sets of records were needed and no two of them had ever been in one room before last year. The station test book has been in the Association's office since 1925 and came to these Rooms this year. The Brant weigh book, kept at Hessel Bottom from 1897 onward, was lent to me by Roy Brant, who is Emmet Brant's son and who has kept it up himself since his father died. The Alder Corners Cheese Company's test figures, from over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County, were copied out for me by Rosalie Cudd, who runs that factory and is Jerome Cudd's niece; the book itself has never left Vessey County and I have never seen it. Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks, kept by the state's dairy inspector for this county from 1908, have been in my own college's library at Farland since 1947 — eleven years in which nobody put a question to them. And the Association's own annual ledgers, which are here tonight.

Now the crate. In February of last year I went up the Hollow to the station building, which has been used for storage since it closed. There is a loft over what was the intake room, reached by a ladder, and under a tarpaulin at the back of it there was a wooden crate stenciled on the end AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET. It had been carried up there in June of 1923 and it was there still. Inside, packed in straw, were the station's condemned milk-test pipettes. Six dozen had been bought new for the station in 1897, on an invoice that came to seventy-seven dollars and ninety-three cents for the whole outfit — pipettes, bottles, acid measure, hand tester and freight together. Nine of them were unbroken in the crate.

On the ninth of March I measured those nine at Farland against a calibrated burette. This is my sheet, and I read it as I wrote it:

> *Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for.*

Everything else follows from that line, and I will go through it slowly, because it is arithmetic and not conjuring.

A butterfat test is made by drawing a measured charge of milk into a bottle whose neck is ruled to read percent for that charge and no other. Put in a short charge and you get a short reading, in the same proportion. These pipettes delivered 16.72 where they were blown for 17.6. Sixteen point seven two divided by seventeen point six is ninety-five hundredths, exactly. Every test made at that station with that glass read ninety-five percent of the truth.

Here is my grandfather's summary leaf, made up in 1922:

> *Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80.*

Three point six one divided by ninety-five hundredths is three point eight. The recorded average was 3.61; the true average was 3.80; the gap is nineteen hundredths of a point. And I want you to notice what kind of number nineteen hundredths is, because everyone who looked at this before treated it as a fixed quantity, and it is not one. Nineteen hundredths is five percent of three point eight. The station did not lose a set number of points. It lost a twentieth of whatever was there.

In the twenty-five full delivery years from 1898 to 1922 the station took in forty million pounds of milk, an average of one million six hundred thousand pounds a year. At the recorded 3.61 percent, the patrons were credited with one million four hundred forty-four thousand pounds of fat. At the true 3.80 percent, they had delivered one million five hundred twenty thousand pounds. The difference is seventy-six thousand pounds of butterfat. Butterfat over those years averaged twenty-five cents a pound, so the unpaid fat was worth nineteen thousand dollars. The board of arbitration in 1925 awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons thirty-eight hundred dollars. That is a fifth of it.

Two percentages have been quoted at me and both are right, so long as you say what they are percentages of. Seventy-six thousand pounds is five percent of the fat actually delivered — that is, of the million five hundred twenty thousand. It is five and a quarter percent of the fat credited, of the million four hundred forty-four thousand. The Association's ledger line uses the second, because a bookkeeper naturally reckons a surplus against the figure he has already entered. Neither figure is an error. But five percent is the one that is also the glass.

Because the error was a proportion, it fell hardest on the best milk. A herd whose milk was recorded at 4.56 was short 0.24 of a point. A herd recorded at 3.42 was short 0.18. The richer the milk, the more was taken from it, and no man had any way of knowing that his good cows cost him more than his poor ones.

For nine weeks in 1911 there was an experiment at that station, and nobody knew it was one. The station's own measure broke on the eighth of May and a substitute was used until the tenth of July. The measure they borrowed in 1911 came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon. Same patrons, same herds, same pasture, same season, same acid, same bottles, same man at the crank — one object changed — and the average went to 3.80 and came back to 3.61 the day the station's own measure returned. No explanation of this valley's twenty-six-year anomaly survives those nine weeks except the one I have given you.

I will take the kindest of the old explanations, because it was offered in good faith. It was said the wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream. The State Agricultural College tested that in 1911 and printed the answer in Bulletin No. 214:

> *Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture.*

Five hundredths in winter, nothing in summer — against a gap of nineteen hundredths standing flat in every month of every year, July and February alike. It is four times too small and it is the wrong shape. And over the ridge, the Alder Corners factory tested the milk of four Hessel Bottom men in their summer flush; where the two books overlap, fourteen seasons of them, that same milk off that same wet ground ran about nineteen hundredths higher at Alder Corners than at home.

Bulletin No. 471 was issued last October, and I will close with its last paragraph, which is Professor Vaught's wording and mine together:

> *No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain.*

The glass was condemned in 1923 and the cause was found in 1958: thirty-five years. My grandfather was dismissed in 1925: thirty-three years. He died in 1934 and had been dead twenty-four years when the sheet was read that clears him. I have no remedy to propose. I have only the crate, and it is on the table, and you may look into it on your way out.

# Retelling 05 — Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper (retired), a chapter of "The Books of the Association," 1948

*Chapter Nine of an unpublished memoir, written out at Ashlin in the winter of 1948, three years after I gave up the desk. I have set the figures down as they stood in the books, which is the only way I know to set anything down.*

I kept the books of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association from 1905 to 1945, forty years by the calendar and a good deal more than that by the wrist, and in the whole of it I never carried a column that would not prove. That is not said to be admired. In my experience a bookkeeper who wants admiring has generally got something behind him that he would rather you did not turn over. It is said because this chapter concerns a set of entries that everybody in this valley has had an opinion about and almost nobody has troubled to read, and I am the man who wrote them.

The office at Ashlin was one room over a harness shop, cold at the north end and never cold enough at the south, and the ledgers lived on a shelf that a taller man had built for himself before me. I worked at a sloped desk with a brass rail across the foot of it to keep the sheets from sliding, and I can still tell you the sound the station sheets made coming out of their envelope in March, which was a stiffer paper than the creamery used. Forty years of a thing will do that to a man. I remember the paper and the ruling and the smell of the ink pad better than I remember most of the faces that came up the stairs.

The Association in my time was three things on paper: a creamery at Larrow Green, an office at Ashlin, and a skimming station seven miles up the valley at Ostrey Hollow. Mr. Selby Vose was manager over the whole of it, Miss Effie Loomis was secretary and kept the minutes, and I sat at the ledger. The station was the smallest of the three and it made the most noise. Its business was simple enough to explain to a child. The patrons of that neighborhood hauled their milk up to the station instead of driving the seven miles down to Larrow Green with it. The milk was weighed in, a sample was taken from each man's can and tested for its butterfat, and the man was paid on his pounds and his test together. The station then separated the milk, sent the cream down the valley to us at Larrow Green, and gave the skim back to the farmer for his hogs and his calves.

That arrangement produced the one column in my ledger that a stranger always stops at, and I may as well explain it here in my own words before somebody else explains it in his. On one side of the station account I entered the butterfat credited to the patrons on the station's own test — that is, what the station's books said it had bought. On the other side I entered the butterfat that actually came down the valley in the station's cream and was weighed and tested into our vats at Larrow Green — that is, what we had in hand. The two were never the same. The second was always the larger. The difference between them was the station's account to the good, and I carried it to the general account of the Association where it belonged, since the Association had received it.

The station-gain line was my own device; I began it when I came in 1905. I have heard it said in the valley that the line was some old dodge of the founders. It was not. It was a piece of ordinary bookkeeping, invented at that desk by the man sitting at it, because a difference that will not be entered somewhere will come back and bite you at audit. I give the entry as it stands, and I give it complete, because a figure with the words taken off it is worth nothing:

> *Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received
> at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per
> cent, as usual. Carried to the general account.*

That is the entry for 1898, and there is one like it for every year after, in the same terms and in the same hand for as long as the hand was mine. The gain ran at five and a quarter percent of the fat that was actually in the cream. It ran there year after year, so evenly that the words "as usual" got into the entry and stayed there, and a man may as well be honest about what evenness does to a bookkeeper: it stops him looking. A figure that jumps about will keep you awake. A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture.

I will set down the two large numbers of that station, since they are asked for. Over the years the account ran, that station took in forty-four million pounds of milk. And the average test of all the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, taken over the whole period and not over some picked year that suits an argument, was 3.16. Down at Larrow Green we did better than that, and there was not a season in twenty-five years when the Ostrey Hollow men were not sore about it.

I remember only one break in the run of it. The station's own measure broke in the spring of 1911, and they made shift with a borrowed one for six weeks before their own came up from town. It went into my accounts as a small charge for replacement and nothing else, and I would not mention it at all except that in later years people came asking me about that year in particular, as though a bookkeeper kept the weather.

Now I will say the thing I said to the board of arbitration in 1925, and I will say it here in the same words, because I have not changed my mind and I am too old to begin. Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons. I said so to the board and I say it here. One man weighed the milk, one man drew the sample, one man made the test, one man wrote the figure that the pay slip was made from, and one man ran the separator. There was no second man in that building from the spring to the fall. I do not say what any particular person did on any particular day, because I was not there and I do not know. I say that a business which is arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken from it, and that no set of books ever made can prevent it. The remedy is two men or a lock, and the Association had neither.

The patrons petitioned in the winter, when a farmer has time to be angry, and the board sat at Ashlin under Mr. Alonzo Frick, who was as fair a chairman as ever a hearing had, and I was called and gave my evidence and answered what I was asked. The board found nothing established against anybody. It then awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800, without admission, and charged it to the station account, and I entered that too, and it balanced.

Two things more and I have done with the station. The first is that Mr. Vose knew what that account was doing. He signed the sheet every year. I laid the annual sheet before him each spring and he put his name at the foot of it, and a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him. What he made of it he never told me. The second is that I did not sit silent for forty years, whatever has since been said. I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone. I let it alone. I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience, and a bookkeeper who takes his employer's business up a second time after being told once has generally begun to look for another employer.

That is the whole of the station account as I kept it. The figures are in the ledgers at Ashlin, in ink, dated, and anybody who wishes to quarrel with this chapter is at liberty to go and read them, which is more than most of the quarrelers have ever done.

# Retelling 06 — Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector (retired), "Extracts from twenty-eight circuit books," with a covering note, 1936

*Copied out this spring at the request of the state dairy office, from the pocket books I carried on the Ordell circuit. I have copied what is in them and put in nothing that is not.*

**Covering note.** I took the Ordell circuit in nineteen hundred and seven and I have laid it down this year. It was fifty-odd plants in four counties — creameries, cheese factories, skimming stations, one or two little places that made butter three days a week and called themselves a creamery — and the state expected each of them seen at least once in the year and the doubtful ones oftener. I went by train where there was a train and by livery where there was not, and in the spring by neither, the roads up the side valleys being what they are in April. A man on this circuit learns the plants by their smells before he learns them by their names: the sour-sweet of a receiving platform in July, the lime and steam of a wash room, the flat cold nothing of a well-run cold room, which is the smell I liked best because it meant I would have no letter to write.

The work at a plant was always the same four things and I did them in the same order every time, so that I would not have to remember which of them I had done. First the scales, against the sealed weights I carried, both the intake scale and the cream scale, loaded light and loaded heavy. Second the lactometer on the day's milk, patron by patron where there was any question, for added water. Third the boiler, the water supply and the cold room — pressure, gauge, the cased well if there was one, and whether the room would hold what they put in it overnight. Fourth, and the one most men grudged me, I stood at the tester's elbow and watched him make a test through from the sample to the reading, and I did not let him hurry it.

I put down what I found. I did not put down what I thought about it, and I have not begun now. The state pays a field man to weigh and to look, and every time in my life I have seen a field man paid for an opinion instead, the opinion has outlived the weighing and done harm after the man was dead.

**1907.** First round of the new circuit. Ostrey Hollow station, up seven miles from Larrow Green, opened before my time. Scales true against my weights. Cold room fair. Their tester keeps his book in his own hand, and his hand is better than most. Watched him through two tests. He works slow and clean and reads his own figure without being coaxed to it.

**1909.** Ostrey Hollow, in the fall. Scales true. Boiler in order, safety tried. The station takes from the farms of the wet bottom below it and from three places on the hill. Milk sweet, cans clean, one man cautioned about a rusted seam.

**1911.** Ostrey Hollow, June, on my way over to the other side of the county. Scales true. Watched a test through. Nothing here to note.

**1912.** Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. Cream shipped down the valley the same day it is separated, which is more than some do. Creek out over the flat road and I went round by the ridge and lost the afternoon.

**1913.** Larrow Green creamery, two days, the churn room and the receiving books. Scales true. New manager at the Association this year and he came out with me to the platform, which the last one never did.

**1915.** Ostrey Hollow, and the plants down the county line after it. Scales true. Wash room floor holding water at the drain and I ordered it graded. A field man who lets a floor go will be shown a floor at every plant on his circuit inside of two years.

**1916.** Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. The tester complains that his patrons blame him for their figures. I told him that is the trade and he said he knew it.

**1917.** Cheese factory in the next county, then Larrow Green. Scales true at both. A bad year for help; the plants are short-handed and I found two testers doing the work of three men, which is where mistakes live.

**1919.** Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. The manager of the Association has sent a circular round to the patrons of this station about water in the cans. I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so.

**1920.** Ostrey Hollow, twice this year, spring and fall. Scales true both times. Lactometer, no added water. Cold room short of ice in the fall and I said so and it was seen to before I was out of the county.

**1921.** The state has begun issuing burettes to the field men this year, graduated, in a wooden case, for checking the delivery of measures and pipettes at the plants. Mine came in the spring with a printed sheet of directions. It is a slow business to run a plant's glass against it and most plants have never had it done.

**1922.** Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. Boiler in order. I did not run their glass against the burette this year, the day being short and the road home being what it was, and I have put that down because it is what happened.

**1923, June 12.** Ostrey Hollow. I had carried the burette two seasons before this check.

> *Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk
> pipettes against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot,
> the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not
> weigh what it has cost them.*

**1923, October.** Ostrey Hollow. New glass in use since July. Ran the new pipettes against the burette. They deliver their mark. Scales true.

**1924.** Ostrey Hollow. Scales true, no added water, tests improved.

**1925.** Ostrey Hollow, in the fall. Scales true, no added water, tests improved. New man at the tester's bench this year. He is quick, and quick is not the thing I look for.

**1926.** Word from the state office that the burette program is discontinued from this year. The instruments are to be kept by the men who have them but no more are to be issued and no returns are wanted. Mine is in its case in my grip and has been used twice since.

**1928.** Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. The station is quieter than it was. The loft over the intake room is full of odds and ends and I told them a loft over a milk room is a place for nothing but air, and was told in return that it had been full since before the war.

**1931.** Larrow Green creamery and Ostrey Hollow in the same week. Both in order. Bad ice year; every plant in the four counties short and complaining, and two of them buying from the railroad.

**1934.** Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. A separator bearing run hot and shut down two days, since repaired. The tester's book is kept up, though not in the hand it used to be kept in.

**1936, March.** Last round. Ostrey Hollow, Larrow Green, and the two factories over the county line. Scales true at all four. Nothing to note anywhere and nothing left to say about it.

**Closing note.** The books themselves — twenty-eight of them, one to the year, oilcloth covers, pocket size — go to the state dairy office with this copy, as the rules require of a man laying down a circuit. There is nothing in them but what a field man puts in a field book: what I weighed, what I tested, what I told the plant to mend, and whether it was mended when I came back. They are dull reading and they were meant to be. I have been asked once or twice, by men who ought to know better, to say in a general way how a certain plant stood in my estimation over the years, and I have always answered that I do not keep an estimation, I keep a book, and the book is open to anybody the state sends for it. I never had a theory about a plant and I do not recommend having one. A theory is a thing a man carries out to the platform with him and it gets in the way of the weights.

# Retelling 08 — Selby Vose, manager, annual address to the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, 1926

*The manager's address to the members at the annual meeting, printed in the annual report for the year.*

Members of the Association:

It is thirteen years this spring, since 1913, that you have had the management of this Association in my hands, and I have stood up before you thirteen times to give an account of it. I have never had a year I liked less to report on than the one behind us, and I have never had one I was gladder to have behind us. I mean to speak plainly about it, because plain speech is the only thing that has ever done this valley any good, and because a manager who talks around a difficulty leaves the difficulty exactly where he found it, with interest.

Take the ordinary business first. The season opened wet and stayed wet into June, and the hauling was heavy on the horses and heavier on the men. The creamery at Larrow Green ran through the flush without a day lost, which is more than the creameries below us can say. Our butter has held its place with the trade that buys it, our churn room is clean, our ice went into the house early and lasted, and I have no complaint of any man on the payroll. The skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley, ran its season out and its cream came down to us daily and sweet. That is the whole of the ordinary business and I would to heaven it were the whole of the address.

Now to the other matter.

You know that in January of last year, 1925, the patrons of the Ostrey Hollow station laid a petition before this Association. You know that a board of arbitration was constituted upon it, that it sat at Ashlin under Mr. Alonzo Frick, an able and impartial man, and that it heard everybody who wished to be heard, at length, and at some cost in time to men who could ill spare it in the spring. You know its finding. The board found no cause established. It found no cause established against the station, against the office, or against any officer or servant of this Association, and I ask you to sit with that sentence a moment, because it is the only sentence in this business that was arrived at by men under oath with the books in front of them.

Having found no cause established, that board nevertheless awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons thirty-eight hundred dollars, without admission, and charged it to the station account. I supported the payment then and I support it now. It was, I say to you frankly, a generous settlement of an unproved claim. This Association paid thirty-eight hundred dollars rather than let a body of its own members go on believing they had been used badly by their neighbors. There is not a business house in Ordell County that would have done it. We did it because we are not a business house; we are the farmers of this valley in company together, and a company that will not spend money to keep peace among its own members has not understood what it is for.

But I will not have peace bought a second time, and so I will say what I believe to be the cause of the whole affair, and say it in this room, where I have said everything else.

Water was going into cans at the head of that valley and it stopped when the Association began to look. The rise of 1924 is the measure of it. For a generation the milk brought to that station tested below the milk brought to Larrow Green, and every year we were told it was the wet hay, or the tester, or the office, or the weather. Then the Association's attention came to rest on the station, and there was a petition, and a hearing, and men were required to answer questions in front of their neighbors. And in the very next season the tests at that station rose by twenty-one hundredths and the average for 1924 stood at 3.90, which is as good a figure as any station in this county will show you. Nothing was done to those cows. Nothing was done to that bottom land. Nobody changed their feed and nobody changed their pasture. The tests came up when the looking began, and any man who wants a different explanation of that is welcome to stand where I am standing and give us one.

I am told, and I have been told it kindly and by men who mean well, that the state man's visit of that period explains the whole thing. It does not. He came through the county as he came through it every year or two, he tried our scales against his weights and found them true, and he condemned the station's testing apparatus on the twelfth of July, 1923, and told us to order new. We ordered new inside the month and had it in use before the flush was over. Apparatus wears. A station in daily use since 1896 will wear its fittings out twice over in that time, and it is the state man's business to say so before they fail him, and ours to replace them when he does. I decline to build a mystery on a piece of housekeeping that appears in our own order book with a date and a price against it.

I am aware that this is not a comfortable thing to say to a room with Ostrey Hollow men in it, and I say it without naming a name, because I do not know a name and I would not use it if I did. A little water in a can is not a felony in a man's own mind. It is a way of making a hard year come out even, and the man who does it tells himself he will stop when the price improves. I have no wish to see anybody prosecuted, and nobody has been or will be. I wish only to have it understood, once, in the hearing of the whole membership, why that station's figures were what they were and why they are what they are now.

It follows that I defend the circular I sent to the patrons of that station in 1919, which has been thrown at me a good deal in the last two years. I wrote it because it was my duty to write it. It warned the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, in temperate language, that added water would be looked for and would be dealt with. It named nobody. It accused nobody. It said that this Association pays for butterfat and cannot pay for creek water, and I would write every word of it again tomorrow. A manager who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing to the men who make them is not being kind to them; he is being idle at their expense.

Let me put one more thing before you and then I will sit down. My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law under which every patron in this Association is paid on the test of the station where he delivers, kept a herd of Jerseys at the home place, as some of you remember, and I kept them after him. In 1910 that herd tested 4.56. I do not bring that forward to boast of the cows; they are gone now and the ones I have are no better than yours. I bring it forward because it settles a thing that gets said about this county in the trade below us — that the Larrow Valley is poor milk country, that our land is sour and our cattle are indifferent and our figures are what a man must expect off ground like ours. That is not true. There is no better milk in the state than the milk this valley can produce when it comes to the platform as it left the cow, and the proof of it stood in my own barn.

That is what I want from every man in this room in the coming season. Bring it as it left the cow. Wash the cans, cool the milk, keep the dog out of the milk house, and bring it as it left the cow. Do that, and the Ostrey Hollow tests will stay where they went in 1924, and this business will be dead and buried where it belongs, and your manager can stand up here next spring and talk to you about butter prices, which is what he is paid for.

I thank you for thirteen years of your patience and I ask for one more.

# Retelling 09 — Roy Brant, farmer at Hessel Bottom, an account for the county farm bureau's local-history collection, 1955

*Set down in longhand at Hessel Bottom in the winter of 1955 by Roy Brant, sixty-seven years old and still milking, with a postscript he added three years afterward.*

The bureau asked me for something on the old days at the head of the valley. I would rather write about my father's book, because the old days are gone and the book is on the shelf behind my chair, and a man does better to talk about what he can put his hand on.

My father was Emmet Brant. He was born in 1854 and he died in 1929. From the first of January, 1898, until the week before he died, he wrote down every can of milk that went out of this barn, and I have kept it up since, in the same book until it was filled and then in another, in the same way, because he showed me how and because I never found a reason to stop.

**The Bottom.** Hessel Bottom is a hundred and sixty acres of wet meadow lying under the hill at Ostrey Hollow. Six farms had rights on it and cut hay off it and pastured it. Ours was the largest of the six — sixty-odd acres of the meadow, and thirty-eight cows in a good year, which for this valley was a herd. The water stands on the low end into May, and there is a soft place by the alders that has taken a horse to the hock in my time and my father's. The hay off it is coarse, and it cuts late, and a cow will eat it and do well enough, whatever has been said about that hay by men who never fed it. I have cut that meadow with a scythe and with a mower behind two horses, and I will say for it that it never failed to grow something.

**The book.** It is a ledger, a plain one, ruled by hand. Two columns for each day of delivery. In the first column my father entered the weight of every can as he took it off his own scale in his own barn before the wagon went down the road. In the second he entered the figure the station gave him back for that same milk. That is all it is. There is no comment in it anywhere but three or four lines in thirty years, and no opinion at all. The cover is gone off the front, the back is held by a strip of harness leather my father tacked on himself, and the first forty leaves are stained brown at one corner where it lay in a leaky window a winter he was sick.

The two columns agreed. That is the thing about the book that people do not take in when I tell them, so I will say it slowly. Day after day, month after month, for the whole run of years my father kept it and for the years I have kept it, his weight and the station's weight came out the same, barring a pound or two here and there, and those were days when a can got slopped on the hill. Two scales, his and the station's, kept by two men who by the end were not speaking to one another, and the scales never fell out at all. Whatever was wrong at that station — and something was wrong at that station for a very long time — it was not the weighing.

Here is the leaf for 1910, which is the one I am always asked for, in his hand:

> *Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42.*

Two hundred thousand pounds even. He was pleased about the evenness of it; he wrote "even" and underlined it, which for him was carrying on. The price of butterfat that year was twenty-four cents. Over the whole stretch of years the complaint covers, one year with another, it came to about twenty-five cents.

He wrote at the kitchen table after the second milking, in the lamp, with his hat still on, and my mother would not have the table cleared till he was done. It took him no time at all, and it is the only reason there is anything left to argue about now.

**The trouble.** Our milk at the Hollow ran nineteen hundredths of a point under what the same class of milk was making down at Larrow Green. Nineteen hundredths is nothing to look at on one pay slip. Set the slips in a row for a lifetime of farming and it is a farm.

The first men to say so out loud said it in 1902, and they were laughed at, and one of them was my father. What I would have anybody notice is this: it was nineteen hundredths in July and it was nineteen hundredths in February. It did not come and go with the season. It did not care whether the cows were on the meadow or on hay in the barn. Nobody would take account of that, then or since, though it was in front of them.

In 1908 the Association appointed a committee, with Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green at the head of it, and the committee came up and sampled the Bottom herds through the winter and reported that the wet hay of this bottom thins the cream, and that the remedy lay with the land and not with the station. My father read that report twice and put it in the drawer and said that the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not.

There were nine weeks in the summer of 1911 when the station's own measure was out of use, it having been broken. The state man left his own measure that June and that is what they used. Through those nine weeks our tests came up, all of us, every farm on the Bottom; and then the station's own came back into use and the figures went down again to where they had been. My father noticed the rise and he noticed the fall and he entered both, and he could make nothing of it, and I could make nothing of it either, and there it sat for near on fifty years.

In 1919 the manager sent a paper round to the Hollow patrons warning us against putting water in the milk. Nine families at the head of the valley got that paper and nine families read it for what it was. My father was one who never forgave it. He and Ansel Keddie were brothers-in-law, and from 1919 they did not speak — not from any quarrel over the water, but because my father held that the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not.

From 1915, four of the Bottom men hauled their milk over Redlow Ridge to the cheese factory in the summer flush and back to the Hollow the rest of the year. The factory over there had opened in 1914. They went on that way for sixteen seasons. It was for the price and the flush market and a shorter haul while the road was open, and it was talked about here as though they had gone over to the enemy, which shows you what a valley is. I did not go over. We had the biggest herd on the meadow, and the biggest herd is the one that cannot afford to be talked about, and I have thought since that this was a poor reason and cost us money.

**The family.** Orra Keddie was my father's sister. They were full brother and sister, same mother and same father, whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it. So Ansel Keddie, who kept the station's test book, was married into the family that had more to lose by that station than any other family on the meadow, and I have never known what to do with that fact except set it down.

Duncan Keddie, who taught school at Ashlin, was Ansel and Orra's boy, and he and I are first cousins. My daughter Hazel and his daughter Ivy are second cousins, and I mention it because both of them have had to have it explained to them by me more than once.

**The book, again.** I am sixty-seven and the book has outlived two of the three men who are in it. It goes to Hazel when I am done with it. That is all the arrangement I have made and all I intend to make.

*Postscript, 1958.* Duncan's girl Ivy came out to the place this year and asked whether she might borrow the book for a piece of work she is doing at the college at Farland. I let her have it. She is careful with paper. It comes back to me when she is through, and after me it is Hazel's.

# Retelling 10 — Rosalie Cudd, cheesemaker at Alder Corners, a letter to Ivy Keddie, 1958

*A long reply from the Alder Corners Cheese Company in Vessey County to a graduate student at Farland who had written to ask what four Ordell County farmers' milk tested on the far side of Redlow Ridge.*

Alder Corners, Vessey County

Dear Miss Keddie,

Your letter came Tuesday and I have read it twice, once standing up in the make room with my hands wet and once properly after supper, and I am glad you wrote. In thirty-odd years nobody from over the ridge has ever asked me a plain question about our books, and here is a young woman from the college who asks one in four lines and apologizes for troubling me. You are not troubling me. Sit down and I will tell you the whole of it, and you may take what you want out of it.

First, so you know what kind of place you are asking about. My father, Jerome Cudd, built this factory in nineteen thirteen, on the flat below the church, because there was spring water on the lot and a hard road out to the east. He made cheese here until he died in 1941, in March, between the setting and the cutting of a vat, which he would have arranged that way if he had been asked. I took the factory over that same spring and I have had it since — seventeen years now, and I have changed the boiler and the roof and nothing else. I was a girl of twelve when the first vat was set and I was drying hoops before I was tall enough to see into one, so when I say I know what the milk did here, I am not going on anybody's word.

Now, the ridge. You will have been up it. Four miles of switchback, cut for a lumber road and never much improved since. It shuts in November and it does not open before April, some years May, and there is no arguing with it — the drifts sit in the cut below the top and stay there after everything else is bare. So no man from your valley ever hauled to me in winter, whatever the price was. It was not possible.

From 1915, four men off Hessel Bottom hauled their milk over to me in the summer flush. They came from about the first of June to the middle of September, and the rest of the year they went down to your station at Ostrey Hollow, which was the only sensible thing to do. Their reasons were plain enough and none of them was mysterious: cheese paid better than butter in a flush month, I was buying hard in flush because a cheese factory can use everything you can carry it, and while the road was open my gate was a shorter pull for those four farms than the run down the valley. They came for sixteen seasons. The last of those summers was 1930.

You would have liked the look of it. They started before daylight to have the cans on my platform by seven, because milk that has been four miles down a switchback in a wagon on a July morning is not improving while it stands, and they came in with the horses steaming and the tarps wet with the cloud that sits on the top of the ridge till the sun is well up. One of them brought his boy, who used to sit on my scale platform and be weighed for a joke. That boy is a man in the state road department now and does not remember me.

You ask how my father tested. He tested himself, on his own bench, in the little room off the intake, with his own glassware that he bought new and washed in the evening and kept in a rack he had made, and he wrote his own book. He never compared a figure of his with anybody else's, and I want to be honest with you about the reason: it never entered his head. He had no business with what your station made of the same milk. A cheesemaker tests what comes in his door so he knows what he is paying for and what his vat will do. He is not keeping a check on another man's arithmetic seven miles off over a mountain, and if you had suggested it to my father he would have laughed at you.

I have the books here. Here is what he wrote at the end of the summer of 1919, and I copy it exactly, because you asked for his own words and not for mine:

> *The four Hessel Bottom men, summer of 1919: 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge.*

That last clause is my father all over. He had heard what was said in Ordell County about that meadow — that the wet hay of the bottom thinned the cream, and that the men who farmed it were bringing in poor stuff and had better drain their land before they complained. He thought it was nonsense, and he wrote so in his own book where nobody would ever read it, which is where he generally put his opinions. He used to say that the Ordell people had a theory about that bottom the way some families have a ghost: it explained things, and it cost nothing, and nobody was ever going to test it.

And I will tell you what I told a man from your county who came in here once in a temper about it. That milk was good milk. Season after season those four men came in at about three-eighty, and not one of them ever gave me an hour's trouble in the vat. I have set the figures out for you at the end of this letter, every summer of the sixteen, and you will see they sit between 3.78 and 3.82, year in, year out, wet years and dry ones, whether the meadow had been under water in May or hard as a road. They are the season averages of the four men taken together, copied straight off the leaves; I have not smoothed anything and I have not thrown out a season. If you want the men separately I can copy those too, but you will find they are within a few hundredths of each other and always were, which is what you would expect of four herds off one piece of ground.

Since you asked me particularly about water, I will answer it flatly. A cheesemaker knows watered milk inside a day. He does not need a test to know it, though the test will tell him too. The curd comes soft and it will not knit; you get a slack, weak body and your yield is down, and you can stand at the vat and see your money going out with the whey. It cannot be hidden from the man who makes the cheese, because the cheese tells him, and it tells him the next morning, not next year. Butter is more forgiving that way. Cheese is a witness. In sixteen summers my father never made one complaint of those four men, never docked one of them a cent, and never — not once that I heard, and I was in the room — said a word about their milk except that he wished he had more of it in July.

I am aware, of course, that there was trouble in your valley for years about the milk at the head of it, because trouble carries over a ridge better than anything else does. It came to me the way weather comes, in pieces and late, and always with somebody's name attached to it. I paid no attention. It was not my county, they were not my patrons in September, and I had a make to get out. If I sound short about Ordell it is only that I have listened to forty years of your valley explaining that meadow to itself, and I never once heard anybody go up there and look.

I do not know what your station made of the same cows, and I have not the first idea what your county decided about any of it. I never saw those figures and I would not know how to get them. All I can give you is our side of the ridge, honestly copied, and you are welcome to it, and welcome to print it if it is any use to you.

Come over in the summer and see the place. The road is open by then, mostly, and I will give you your dinner and show you my father's book, which is a nicer object than anything I have said about it here.

Yours very truly,
Rosalie Cudd

# Retelling 11 — Leland Vaught, professor of dairy husbandry, introduction and method to Bulletin No. 471, State Agricultural College at Farland, October 1958

*The opening and method sections of the bulletin, written for extension agents and county workers, closing with the bulletin's own concluding paragraph reproduced entire.*

## I. Why this bulletin was written

This station has been asked, at intervals for fifty years, why the butterfat tests at one skimming station in Ordell County ran below those of its own creamery seven miles down the same valley. The question has been answered at various times by reference to the land and by reference to the men. It is answered here by reference to a piece of glass.

The answer is not complicated, and its simplicity is itself part of the history. Nobody in that valley lacked the intelligence to find it. What they lacked was any occasion to suspect the one article in the plant that nobody in the trade is in the habit of suspecting.

The extension worker who reads no further should carry away this: **the milk pipette is the one instrument in the butterfat test which the plant cannot check against anything else, and it is the instrument on which every figure in the plant depends.**

## II. The instrument, and why the neck of the bottle can be marked in percent

The test as practiced in every creamery in this state consists of four operations. A measured charge of milk is drawn into a pipette and delivered into a test bottle. Sulfuric acid is added and dissolves everything but the fat. The bottle is spun in a hand tester, and the liberated fat rises into the graduated neck. The operator reads the fat off directly, in percent.

Notice the word *directly*. The operator weighs nothing. He reads the length of a column of fat in a narrow neck and calls it a percentage, and he is entitled to do so for one reason only: the charge of milk that went into that bottle is a fixed and known quantity. The maker rules the neck for that quantity and no other. Every mark on it means "this much fat, out of the standard charge."

The standard charge in this state, and on the glassware here examined, is **17.6 cubic centimeters**.

Consider then what follows if the pipette delivers less than its mark. Less milk goes into the bottle; less fat is liberated; a shorter column stands in the neck; and the neck, which knows nothing of the substitution, is read off in the ordinary way. The reading is not merely low. It is low *in the exact proportion by which the charge was short*.

Three consequences deserve emphasis, and I set them out separately because in fifty years of this history nobody appears to have held all three in mind at once.

**First, the error is proportional and not additive.** It does not remove a fixed number of points from every test. It removes a fixed *fraction*. Rich milk therefore loses more than poor milk, absolutely, and the ratio between any two tests made on the same short glass is undisturbed. A station using such glass will show a perfectly orderly, internally consistent set of figures. Its herds will rank correctly against one another. Only against the outside world is it wrong.

**Second, the error is precise.** A leaking pipe or a slipping scale gives a scattered error, and scatter attracts attention. A pipette that delivers 16.27 c.c. where it is blown 17.6 delivers 16.27 c.c. every time, for a quarter of a century, in January and in July, for a careful operator and a careless one. It has the appearance of excellent work.

**Third — and this is the reason the thing ran so long — the error is invisible from inside the plant.** Every other element of the test can be verified against a standard. The scales can be checked with test weights. The tester's speed can be counted. The acid can be checked for strength. The bottle's graduation can be checked with mercury. But the pipette is not checked against anything, because in the ordinary practice of the trade *the pipette is the standard*. It is what the plant measures with. An instrument that is itself the measure cannot be caught by measuring with it. It can be caught only by a burette brought in from outside, and burettes reached the state's field men very late in this history.

## III. The material examined

Of the lot bought for the Ostrey Hollow station in 1897 — **five dozen** pipettes were purchased — **nine** were recovered from the station's condemned crate, unbroken and legible, and these nine constitute the material of this bulletin. Their stems are blown **17.6 c.c.** They were delivered against a calibrated burette, five runs to each pipette, and the mean delivery of the nine is **16.27 c.c.**

The station's own glass went into use on **the first of May, 1898**, and remained in use until the state inspector condemned the lot in June of 1923. Before the earlier date, and after the later, the station's tests were made on other glass.

The method of measurement is within the reach of any county worker and I set it out so that it may be repeated. The pipette is filled to its mark with water at room temperature, the tip is wiped, and the contents are delivered in the ordinary working manner — the same interval of drainage, the same touch of the tip to the wall of the receiver — into a burette of certified graduation. Five deliveries are made from each pipette and the readings averaged. The whole of it occupies an afternoon and requires no apparatus a creamery does not own, save the burette. There is no reason why every station in this state should not be checked once in this way, and several reasons in this history why it should be.

## IV. The interval of 1911, which is worth more than the arithmetic

I would ask the reader to weigh what follows above anything computed in this bulletin.

For nine weeks in the summer of 1911 the station's own pipette was broken and a borrowed measure was used in its place. The patrons were the same patrons; the herds were the same herds; the pasture, the season, the acid, the bottles, the tester and the tester's habits were all unaltered. **One variable changed.** The station's average test rose from 3.61 to 3.80 and held there for the nine weeks, and returned to 3.61 on the day the station's own measure came back into service. This is recorded contemporaneously, in the operator's hand, by a man who did not know what he was recording and set a question mark against it.

I have spent my working life urging students to prefer an experiment to a calculation. Here is one that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven years. It disposes of every explanation that looks to the herds, to the ground or to the men, for the sufficient reason that none of those was altered in those nine weeks, and the figures were.

## V. Magnitude

The ratio of delivered to marked capacity is 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = **0.9244**. The recorded station average over the affected years is **3.61**; the corresponding true average is 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = **3.905**, a shortfall of **0.295** of a point. Applied to the 40,000,000 lb of milk received at the station in the twenty-five full delivery years, this yields **118,000 lb** of butterfat delivered and not credited.

It should be said that this figure is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the glassware.

The corresponding quantity appears on the Association's own side of the account. Its ledgers carried, annually, a credit for fat received at the creamery in excess of fat credited to patrons at the station, under the heading "station gain." The gain is five and a quarter percent of the butterfat actually delivered.

## VI. The question of feed

Because the herbage of the wet meadow at the head of that valley has been blamed, the College's own feeding trial of 1911 should be set down. Bulletin No. 214 reported:

> *Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture.*

Five hundredths in winter; nothing at all in summer. The discrepancy under examination is present in every month of the year and is some six times the largest effect the trial could produce under conditions harsher than any farmer would impose.

## VII. Conclusion of the bulletin

> *No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain.*

— L. VAUGHT

# Retelling 12 — Dell Rundle, schoolteacher, "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years," a jubilee pamphlet, 1939 (reprinted 1959)

*Printed at Larrow Green for the Association's fiftieth year, compiled from the recollections of old members and two winters of asking questions at the store; reissued in 1959 with one footnote and the author's closing note unaltered.*

**TO THE MEMBERS**

Fifty years is not long for a country, but it is a long time for a valley to keep one thing going, and that is what this pamphlet is about. I have taught the school at the Green a good many years now and I have listened to this history in pieces, at funerals and in the back of the store, for the whole of them. What follows is not a record. It is what the valley remembers, tidied, and where the valley is certain of a thing I have written it down as certain.

---

**I. THE MEN OF 1889**

In 1889 the farmers of this valley met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. They were tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking whatever the merchant chose to allow them for it, and they had worked out — the way farmers do, slowly and then all at once — that the way to be paid properly for a thing is to own the place that buys it.

They met in the schoolhouse, in cold weather, and the older men who were there remembered afterward chiefly that it went on very late and that somebody's horse got loose in the middle of it.

Gideon Vose was elected the first president. He was a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the law and thought better of it, and he wrote the by-laws himself, at his own kitchen table, in a hand that the Association's clerks were still complaining about thirty years later. His nephew Selby Vose, afterward manager of the Association, used to say that his uncle wrote by-laws the way other men build stone wall — slowly, and to last.

In 1891 the creamery was built at Larrow Green, on the creek below the mill, and the churn was set up in it, and the valley began to be paid in cash once a month, which had never happened to it before. There are men still living who can tell you what the first check did in their mother's kitchen, and they will tell you whether you ask or not.

**II. THE RULE THAT MADE THE TEST THE MASTER**

Of all Gideon Vose's by-laws, one has done more to this valley than the rest together, and I print it whole:

> *Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*

Article VII dates from 1891, the year the churn was set up. It was thought a hard rule at the time and there were men who left the Association over it. It is a just rule. A man who keeps good cows and feeds them ought not to be paid the same as the man who keeps poor ones, and the pound of milk is a liar where the pound of butterfat is not.

But mark the last clause, because the valley has had fifty years to learn what it means. *The test of the station where he delivers shall govern.* Not the test of the creamery. Not any test but the one made at his own door. Gideon Vose put that in for convenience and it was convenient for thirty years, and it is the sentence on which the whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned.

**III. THE STATION AT OSTREY HOLLOW**

The Hollow is seven miles up from the Green, and in the nineties the pull down was three hours in mud and could not be done at all in March. So the Association built a skimming station up there, and it opened in 1896: the milk was weighed and tested and separated on the spot, the cream came down to the Green by wagon, and the skim went home to the calves and the pigs the same morning. It was a great convenience and the Hollow was glad of it. The building is a plain one, boarded and battened, with the intake room at the road end and a loft over it that I believe has never been cleared out since it was put up; and behind it the creek, which in a wet April comes up to the sill and has twice come over it.

**IV. THE TROUBLE AT THE HEAD OF THE VALLEY**

I would rather not write this chapter and it cannot be left out.

From 1902 the men of the Hollow complained that their milk tested lower at their own station than the same class of milk tested at the Green. They were not believed. In 1908 the Association sent a committee up under Nyle Grigg, which sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through the winter and reported that the wet hay of that bottom thinned the cream, and that the remedy lay with the land. The Hollow did not accept it. In 1919 the manager circulated a warning to the Hollow patrons against adding water to their milk, and after that the two ends of this valley did not speak civilly to one another for six years. I have had the children of both ends in one schoolroom through those years and I can tell you that it came down to them entire, without a word of it being explained, the way a debt does.

It is the settled opinion of the valley that cream left that station by the back door, and the figures went right when the man who kept it did not.

I set that down as I have heard it set down a hundred times, by men who were there and men who were not, and I have never heard it contradicted at the store.

The state inspector condemned the station's glassware on the twelfth of July, 1923, for reasons of his own that had nothing to do with the quarrel, and new glass was put in. In January of 1925 the Hollow patrons petitioned the Association. The station took in, over the years complained of, forty-four million pounds of milk. The patrons' loss upon it was nineteen thousand six hundred dollars, and their petition set the figure out to the dollar. A board of arbitration sat at Ashlin that spring under Alonzo Frick, attorney, of that town. It heard everybody. It found nothing established, it made an award to the patrons without admitting anything, and it sent this valley home to live with itself, which the valley has done, after a fashion, ever since.

**V. OVER THE RIDGE**

I should mention, because it belongs to the history and because the young people no longer know it, that in 1914 the Alder Corners Cheese Company was opened over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County, by Jerome Cudd, and that in the summers a few of our Hessel Bottom men hauled over the mountain to him rather than down to their own station. That was resented here. It is Jerome Cudd's daughter, Rosalie Cudd, who makes the cheese at Alder Corners now.[^1]

[^1]: *Added to the 1959 reprint.* The Hollow matter has since been taken up again at the college at Farland by Miss Ivy Keddie, who is second cousin to Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom. Eleven pipettes out of the station's old condemned crate were found still to be in existence. I am too old to rewrite a pamphlet and I refer the reader to the college. — D.R.

**VI. FIFTY YEARS**

Set against the quarrel, put the rest: fifty Junes of cream coming down that road in the early cool, fifty Januaries of it going down over ice with the horses roughshod, a check every month in kitchens that had known none, and a creamery still standing that was built by men with nothing but each other and a piece of paper they had written themselves. Whatever this valley did wrong at the head of it, it did this right, and it did it for half a century, and the men of 1889 would think we had done well.

---

**NOTE ADDED WHILE IN PRESS**

While this pamphlet was being set up in type I was allowed to look at the Association's old ledgers in the office, on another errand altogether. The station account carries, every year, an entry of this form, in the bookkeeper's hand:

> *Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account.*

I must go back on Chapter IV. Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand. I leave it as written and correct it here.

— D. RUNDLE, Larrow Green

# Retelling 13 — Alonzo Frick, article for the yearbook of the Ordell County Bar Association, 1946

*Recollections of the Ostrey Hollow arbitration by the attorney who sat as chairman of the board, with the exhibit list reprinted from his own file.*

**RECOLLECTIONS OF THE ORDELL ARBITRATION**

Our editor has asked the older men of this bar for something out of their own practice, and at seventy-four I take him to mean me. I have tried titles and boundaries for forty years, and two homicides, and none of them has stayed with me as the Ostrey Hollow matter has. It was not litigation at all. It was an arbitration under the by-laws of a creamery association, with no appeal from it, no reported decision, and nothing whatever to cite it for; and I have thought about it oftener than about anything I ever argued in a courthouse.

**The complaint.** The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association pays a patron for the butter fat in his milk and not for the weight of it. The fat is found by test, and the test of the station where the man delivers is the test that governs him. I say nothing against the rule. But it makes the test the whole of the bargain, and it leaves a dissatisfied man with no remedy on earth except to quarrel with a figure.

At Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green, the Association kept a skimming station: milk weighed in, a sample drawn for each patron, the cream sent down the valley and the skim milk handed back. The patrons of that station had been paid, year upon year, on an average test of 3.16, and the milk of the rest of the valley tested better than theirs. Three and sixteen is not a scandalous figure standing by itself. It becomes one when the man over the hill is paid on a better figure for milk out of cows no different from yours; when the difference holds steady for a quarter of a century; and when nobody in the office can tell you the cause of it.

**The petition and the board.** In January of 1925 the patrons of the station petitioned the Association. The by-laws provided for arbitration, and the officers, whatever else may be said of them, did not resist it. A board of three was constituted and I was named its chairman, I have always supposed because I was the one of the three who kept no cow.

We sat three times. The first session was at the Association's office at Ashlin on the seventeenth of March, 1925. The second and the third I asked to have at Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's own weigh room, so that the board might see the plant it was to pass upon instead of hearing it described by men with an interest in the description. That was my doing and I am still content with it. Every physical appliance of that station was before us and we satisfied ourselves upon it. We saw the scales tried. We saw the sampling done and the test made, and I will say plainly that I did not understand the test then and do not understand it now; I am a lawyer and the thing is chemistry. But I could see that it was done the same way twice, and that the men who did it were not hiding their hands.

**The three explanations.** The board heard three explanations of the Ostrey Hollow figures, and it is worth setting down who put each of them, because the three did not sit easily together and nobody seemed to mind that they did not.

The first was water. This was the manager's, Selby Vose. He had circulated a warning to those patrons on the subject some years before and he stood by it before us: that the Hollow's milk came in thinned, that a little water in a can is the oldest trick in the trade, and that a station cannot test what is not there. He put it without heat and without proof.

The second was that the tester had been taking cream for himself and writing low tests to cover the loss. This was put by the bookkeeper, Wilbur Teague, in the way an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it — he was asked about the station's account and answered at length about how such an account would look if a man were helping himself, and then observed that he made no charge against anybody. There is no cross-examination that reaches a witness of that kind, and I did not attempt one.

The third was the hay. Somebody read the report of the 1908 committee into the record, and the bog meadow at Hessel Bottom was before us for the better part of an afternoon — the wet ground, the coarse hay off it, the cows that eat it all winter. It is an old opinion in that valley and it had the advantage of blaming nobody in the room.

**Keddie.** Ansel Keddie had tested at that station since it opened in 1896 and had kept the book in his own hand every season since. He made a statement to the board on the seventeenth of March. He said he had tested as he was taught and as the state man had seen him do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, that he could not tell us why the figures were what they were, and that he had never taken a pound of cream out of that station. I believed him then and I believe him now. His denial was accurate and it did him no good at all, and the reason it did him no good is that a man who cannot account for a figure is presumed by everybody in the room to be the figure's author.

I add one thing which is not in the minutes. In the corridor at Ashlin, Keddie told me privately that he had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the room. He was not a bitter man about it. He had lived among them thirty years and would not stand up and accuse his neighbors, and I record it here because it has been said since that he went to his grave accusing nobody, which is true, and that he suspected nobody, which is not.

**The dismissal and the award.** On the thirtieth of April, four days before we made our award, the Association dismissed him. There was no finding against him from us and there never was one; the board had not concluded and did not conclude that he had done anything whatever. He was simply let go while the board was still sitting, and I have never been able to think well of the Association for it.

On the fourth of May, 1925, the board found no cause established and awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons thirty-eight hundred dollars, without admission, to be charged to the station account. The patrons' own reckoning of what they had lost was sixty-seven thousand pounds of butter fat over the years complained of, and nothing was ever brought forward to disturb that figure. We were not able to say who owed it or why, and we said so. An award without a finding is an unsatisfactory thing and every member of that board knew it.

**The exhibits.** I reprint the list from my own file, exactly as it was made up:

> EXHIBITS BEFORE THE BOARD OF ARBITRATION, OSTREY HOLLOW MATTER, 1925
>
> 1. The test book of the Ostrey Hollow station, 4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924, in the hand of Ansel Keddie.
> 2. The receiving book of the said station.
> 3. The private weigh book of Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom, begun 1 January 1897.
> 4. The circuit notebook of the state dairy inspector for the year 1923.

Four books and nothing else. It is a short list for a matter of that size, and I have looked at it many times since.

**Reflection.** A board of arbitration can only decide between the accounts that are put to it. Three were put to us; each of them was urged by a man of standing; each of them would have been believed by half the valley; and not one of them was proved. I have wondered since whether the answer was in that room and simply was not in any of the three stories, and whether a lawyer who understood chemistry might have found it. I did not understand chemistry. I understood that an honest man was ruined by a figure nobody could explain, and that we paid his neighbors money without being able to tell them what they were being paid for.

# Retelling 14 — Hobart Sill, buttermaker (ret.), remarks recorded at the Larrow Green creamery on the day he left it, 1934

*Taken down as spoken by a man who made the Association's butter for forty-one years and had no patience with anybody who did not.*

Forty-one years I have stood at that churn. Eighteen ninety-three I came here, and there was no floor in this room but plank, and we cooled with creek ice out of the pond. You can write that down. Forty-one years and I never sent out a tub of butter I would not eat myself, which is more than the office can say about anything it ever sent out.

You want to know about the Hollow. Everybody wants to know about the Hollow. I will tell you what I know, which is what came off that wagon, because that is what I handled.

The cream wagon came down from Ostrey Hollow every day of the week but Sunday, seven miles of hill road, and in July it came down warm no matter how early they started. It was set off at the platform and it was weighed and it was sampled and it went into the cold room, and I took it from there. Now here is the thing about that cream, and I have said it to men in this room a hundred times and none of them ever thought it was worth anything. That cream ran heavy. Every year it ran heavy. There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there. The office would send down its sheet and the sheet would say so many pounds of fat bought at the station, and the fat that came into this room off that wagon was more than the sheet said. Always more. Never less.

And I weighed it myself, most years. The platform man weighed it and I weighed it over again when it came into the cold room, because I have never in my life taken another man's figure for what was going into my churn. Cans off, cans on the scale, cans into the ice, and the book ruled and signed before I put my coat on. You may go and look at the receiving book if they will let you at it, and you will find my hand on every page of it from ninety-three, and you will find the Hollow's cream coming in heavy on page after page after page, in my own writing, for as long as I have been in this room.

Well, that is cream for you. Cream is not milk and it will not stand still and be counted. It is a thick thing and it clings and it separates on you and any man who tells you he can put a figure on cream to the pound and hold it is a man who has never had his arm in a can up to the elbow. So it ran heavy. So does everybody's. I never made anything of it, and I will say plain, nobody ever asked me to.

Our own milk, here at Larrow Green, our own patrons, the men on this creek — that ran 3.78 taking one year with another right through those years. Three seventy-eight. I know it because I made the butter out of it and a buttermaker knows what his cream is worth before anybody hands him a paper about it. Ordinary milk, ordinary county, ordinary cows. There is nothing wonderful about 3.78 and there is nothing shameful about it either.

Now the Hollow. The Hollow milk was really a 3.85 and no better, and I will say that to any man in this valley. Wet ground up there, but good cows, and I never had a word to say against the cows. A 3.85 and no better, and if the figures they were paid on came out under that, then somebody up that hill road was helping the cans along, and I said so at the time and I say it now.

You will get it told to you a dozen ways. You have been in the office, I expect, and they have their ways of putting it. I put it the one way. You cannot tell me nine families all got honest in one summer. There was water in those cans and I said so at the time. Because I will tell you what happened — one summer, along in the twenties, those figures came up and they stayed up, and nothing on this earth changed but the season, and men do not suddenly start pouring better milk out of the same cows in the same barns. Something got stopped. Something got said to somebody. I never knew what and I never asked, because the day a buttermaker starts asking about other men's cans is the day he stops making butter.

They had been hollering up there a long while before that, mind. Away back. Nineteen two is the first I remember of it, a man in here shouting that his milk tested under his brother-in-law's down the creek and it was the same feed and the same water and what did we mean by it. And the manager put out a paper to them about it — that would be nineteen nineteen — a circular, warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons about water in the milk, plain as that. I thought well of him for it. It was the only plain thing that office ever did, and of course they hated him for it up the Hollow, and of course it changed nothing, because a paper never emptied a can.

That is the offices for you. Papers. The Association has a paper for everything and butter for nothing. I have had men come in here in a collar and tell me my churn was running slow when they could not have told you which end of a cow gets up first. I made butter that took premiums in three states out of this room, and not one man in that office ever came down here to see how it was done, and every one of them could tell you to the half cent what the fat was worth. Twenty-seven cents. That is what fat was through those years, take one year with another, twenty-seven cents the pound, and there is not a man in the office could tell you what makes it churn.

I will tell you one more thing and then you can go. When they opened that station up at Ostrey Hollow — that was ninety-six, in May, and there was a crowd went up for it — they had no outfit of their own. Not a piece. It was ordered and it was not come, and they were ready to start taking milk, and there was talk of holding off the whole season. And I said no, they can have mine. I went to my own bench in this room and I made up an outfit for them out of my own glass, my own, that I had used and knew, and it went up the valley on the wagon and they opened on it. Their own came the spring after and they sent mine back to me, and one piece of it was chipped and I never said a word about that either.

That first season they took in about nine hundred thousand pounds up there. May to the end of the year, that is, so it was not even a whole one. That is a good deal of milk for a hill station starting from nothing, and it was the biggest thing that ever happened to Ostrey Hollow, and I was glad enough to have had a hand in it.

Do I think about it? I am sixty-four years old and I have thought about butter. I never went up there but twice in my life. I took their cream off the platform and I made it into butter and the butter was good — as good as any, and better than most, and there was never a season it was not. Whatever the Hollow was doing with its cans, and I have told you what I think about that, the fat came down that hill and it came into my churn and it went out of this room as good butter with this creamery's name on the tub.

Now that is forty-one years and I would like to go home. Put in about the butter. Do not put in that I said anything against the cows up there, because I did not. The cows were never the trouble. Put in that Hobart Sill made the butter and it was good butter and he stood at the churn until the day they gave him a chair.

# Retelling 15 — Nyle Grigg, covering letter of 1919 to the reprinted report of the investigating committee of 1908

*A farmer of standing sends his eleven-year-old report back into circulation because the same complaint has been raised again, and adds a letter saying that the intervening science has proved him right.*

**COVERING LETTER**

Larrow Green, 1919.

To the officers and patrons of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association.

Gentlemen: I am asked to let the report of our committee be printed again, the Ostrey Hollow question having been reopened this season by men who were boys when we made it. I consent willingly and I have nothing in it to take back. I ask leave only to add a page, because a report is a dry thing and does not say why it was written, and because eleven years have brought us some light from a quarter that had none to give us in 1908.

You will remember the state of things. From about 1902 there had been grumbling out of Ostrey Hollow that the milk of that station tested under the milk of the rest of this valley. It was not one man and it was not one season. It went on and it grew, and it got into the talk at the annual meeting and into the talk at every crossroads store between here and Ashlin, and it took the shape such things always take, which is that somebody must be doing it on purpose. By the winter in which we were named, the board could not let it alone any longer without seeming to have an interest in letting it alone, and we three were named to look into it. I have never in my life been given a duty I liked less. I had known the men of that bottom since I was a young man and I have bought hay off two of them.

We went at it as plain farmers do. We sampled those herds through the whole of 1908 — every month of that year, in the barn and on the pasture, out of the night's milk and the morning's, and we did not let a man know which day we were coming. We weighed. We watched the test made and we watched it made again. And what we found, we put in the report, and the report is below and I will not repeat it here.

I will add, since a man who prints a thing twice ought to say where he stands in it, that I had no interest whatever in the answer. I am not a patron of that station. I haul to Larrow Green and always have, and whatever the Hollow was paid or not paid never put a cent into my pocket nor took one out of it. I was chosen for the work on that account and for no other, and I have been abused for the finding by men on both sides of it, which I take to be a fair sign that the finding was an honest one.

What I will say is this. When we wrote, we had nothing to go on but our own eyes and the general opinion of practical men, and I knew that a committee of farmers saying that wet hay thins the cream would be told that farmers will say anything about their own land. But the State Agricultural College at Farland has since published its Bulletin No. 214, which was a feeding trial upon bog hay carried out under the College's own eye, with weighed rations and no interest in the outcome. That trial found the bog hay to depress the butter fat test by two tenths in the winter feeding. Two tenths, gentlemen, out of a College that had never heard of Hessel Bottom and had no reason on earth to flatter us. There is our finding, arrived at by another road and by better men, three years after we set our names to it.

I am content to let the matter rest where the College and this committee have jointly put it. The remedy lies with the land and not with the station. Drain that bottom, sweeten it, keep the stock off it in the wet of the spring, and feed the coarse hay off it with a mixed ration and not by itself, and the Hollow will test with the rest of us. Go on as they are going and abuse the man who reads their figures for them, and they will be grumbling in twenty years' time as they were grumbling when we began, and no better off.

Respectfully, NYLE GRIGG, Larrow Green.

**THE REPORT AS PRINTED IN 1908**

> REPORT OF THE COMMITTEE APPOINTED TO INQUIRE INTO THE COMPLAINTS OF THE PATRONS OF THE OSTREY HOLLOW STATION.
>
> To the President and Directors of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association.
>
> Your committee, appointed in January last, respectfully reports as follows.
>
> The complaint before us is of some years' standing and is in substance this: that the milk delivered at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station is returned by the test at a figure below that of milk delivered elsewhere in this valley, and that the patrons of that station are in consequence paid less for the same labor and the same cows than their neighbors are paid. Since the meeting of 1893, when payment by test came in among us and the pound of butter fat took the place of the pound of milk as the measure of a man's month, a difference of this kind is no longer a matter of opinion. It is a matter of money, and it comes out of the same pocket every month, and your committee has approached it in that light and not as a quarrel.
>

Its herbage is sedge, rush, and the coarse sour grasses that come in on such land, and its hay is cut late and heavy and is fed as the main dependence of those farms from November to April. There is no better winter keep to be had there. We say this without reproach to any man on that bottom; the land is what it is, and they took it as their fathers left it to them.
>

We saw the herds milked, we saw the cans filled, and we saw them hauled to the station and weighed in at the intake, and we stood by while the test was made and saw nothing whatever in the doing of it to remark upon. We tried the station scales against a known weight and we tried the patrons' own scales against the station's. We inquired into the water supply at each barn and satisfied ourselves upon it.
>
> Your committee further observed the general condition of the stock upon that bottom, as men who have kept cattle all their lives are entitled to observe it. The herds are thin in the flank at the end of the winter feeding and slow to come to their flesh in the spring. Their coats are rough later in the season than the coats of hill herds. They do well enough in flow, for the wet land is generous of milk, but it is our opinion, formed on the ground and not out of a book, that what such land gives in quantity it withholds in quality, and that a cow cannot put into her pail what her keep has never put into her.
>
> We report that we found no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter, and we wish that finding to be printed in the same type as the rest.
>
> We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March. The milk is whole and the scales are honest. It is our opinion that the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station.
>
> NYLE GRIGG, chairman, and the committee.

That report was received and accepted, and the question was closed by it for eleven years — until this season, and the men who have reopened it have brought nothing new before us but their impatience.

# Retelling 16 — Dr. Roland Kepp, "The bog meadows and the milk," a paper read before the Ordell County Farm Club, 1926

*A veterinary surgeon of thirty years' practice brings the agricultural literature to bear on an old local grievance, and the discussion from the floor is appended as taken down by the club's secretary.*

**THE BOG MEADOWS AND THE MILK**

*By Roland Kepp, V.S., of Ashlin*

Mr. Chairman and gentlemen: it is my habit, when I am called to a herd that is doing badly, to ask first what the cattle are standing on. I have found in thirty years that the answer to nine cases in ten is under the animal's feet, and that the tenth is in the water she drinks. I want to put before you this evening a question that has been in this county longer than I have practiced in it, and that has been dealt with hitherto by opinion, by grumbling, and by the naming of names. It deserves better. It is a question of soil, of herbage, and of the mineral economy of the cow, and I mean to treat it as such.

**The land.** A wet bottom is not a rich meadow with too much water in it. It is a distinct kind of ground with a distinct kind of chemistry, and the water is the least part of it. Where drainage is wanting, the year's growth does not rot down as it does on a hill field; it lies sour, and the soil that results is an acid soil, poor in lime, and poor in the phosphates. On such ground the sweet grasses will not hold. They are crowded out within a few seasons by sedge and rush and the coarse tussock grasses, which want nothing better than sour wet land and will take it over completely if you let them. That herbage carries bulk and it carries water. What it does not carry, in any quantity worth the name, is lime or phosphorus, and it is cut late because such ground cannot be got onto early, and late cutting takes out what little was in it.

**What the cow does with such keep.** Now consider the animal. A cow in milk is a manufactory working on a narrow margin, and she is obliged to find the material of her milk somewhere. If her ration is short of the minerals, she does not simply give less; she robs her own frame to make good, and she goes on robbing it until she cannot. You will see the consequences in any wet-meadow herd in this county and I have seen them in all of them. The stock come out of the winter feeding thin over the loin and rough in the coat. They are slow to come to condition in the spring. They are ready subjects for every disorder of the digestion in the fall of the year, and I am oftener called to that class of trouble on wet land than on any other. They breed later. Their feet are worse.

They will milk, gentlemen. Wet land is generous of flow and no man on a bog meadow ever complained that his pail was light. But what is in the pail is another matter, and the fat is the first thing to suffer, because the fat is the dearest thing the cow makes and it is the first economy she practices when she is pressed.

**The measure of it.** I have gone about this county for thirty years and I have kept my eyes open. The wet bottoms of this county yield a milk poorer in fat by a fifth of a point and every practical man knows it. Set the hill herds of this county against the bottom herds and you will find that difference, farm for farm, wherever you look for it, and the difference does not depend on the breed, on the man, or on the barn. It depends on the ground.

**Hessel Bottom.** I come to the case which is in everybody's mind. Hessel Bottom, under Ostrey Hollow, is the completest example of wet meadow in this county. Six farms cut hay and pasture stock upon it. It lies along the creek; the springs come out at the foot of the slope and run in it the year round; a part of it is under water every spring and is not fit to be walked on until well into May. Its hay is sedge hay, and on those farms it is the winter dependence and very nearly the whole of it.

The patrons of the skimming station at Ostrey Hollow have complained for something like a quarter of a century that their milk tests below the milk of the rest of the valley. Their complaint is perfectly just and I have never doubted it. The figure of the gap, as it has been given to me and as I have satisfied myself, is twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21 — between the milk of that station and the milk of this valley at large. A committee of practical men went over the ground in 1908 and found the herds sound, the milk whole, and the scales honest, and laid the cause to the hay of that bottom. The matter has been before committees since and before boards, and it is not settled, and it will not be settled by men shouting at one another across a weigh room. It is settled already, in the literature, for anybody who will read.

**The evidence of the College.** The State Agricultural College at Farland conducted a feeding trial upon bog hay and published it as its Bulletin No. 214. I commend it to every man here. The College fed the bog hay under control and measured what it did, and it found the butter fat test depressed by two tenths in the winter feeding. That is the whole case. Two tenths from the College, laid alongside the fifth of a point I have found in the field and the twenty-one hundredths that station has been complaining of, and the three agree so nearly that I do not know what more a reasonable man requires.

**An objection.** It has been urged upon me, by a gentleman who I know will forgive my noticing it here, that in the summer of 1911 the testing at that station was broken off for six weeks, their own apparatus having failed them and a borrowed one being used, and that the figures stood higher during those six weeks than before or after. Six weeks in the flush of the season, gentlemen. Six weeks of June and July, when every herd in the county is at its best and the stock are off the wet ground and up on the aftermath. I am astonished that anybody should found an argument on six weeks of summer figures. I could produce you six weeks in any year that would prove anything you liked.

**The remedy.** It is not a mystery and it is not cheap. Drain the bottom; open the old ditches and cut new; lime the ground and lime it again; sow it down when it will carry a seeding; keep the stock off it in the wet of the spring, when a hoof does more harm in a day than a season will repair; and never feed that hay alone. Feed it with a mixed ration and a mineral supplement, as the College directs. Fat has been worth twenty-seven cents the pound through these years, and a fifth of a point taken off every hundredweight a farm ships, year in and year out, will pay for a great deal of tile.

**DISCUSSION**

*A member* asked whether the difference might not be in the cows rather than the land, the bottom farms being poorer men with poorer stock.

*Dr. Kepp* replied that he had considered it and rejected it; that some of the best-bred cattle in the county stand on wet land, and some very indifferent cattle on the hills test well; and that a man may improve his herd for twenty years and never get his fat up while his ground is sour.

*A member* asked whether the trouble might lie in the station rather than the bottom, as had been said in the valley for years.

*Dr. Kepp* replied that he had no interest in that quarrel and no competence in it; that he had never in his life read a test and would not know how; but that when a cause is found in the soil, in the herbage, in the condition of the stock, in the College's trial, and in every wet bottom in the county alike, it is not good sense to go looking for a second cause in a building.

*The chairman* thanked Dr. Kepp and observed that the paper would be printed for the members.

# Retelling 17 — Hazel Brant, farmer's daughter and last keeper of the weigh book, recorded at Ashlin, 1981

*Taped at her kitchen table over two afternoons; the interviewer's prompts are in brackets. She had no papers in front of her and did not want any.*

I'll tell you the trouble with me before you start the machine, and then it's your lookout. Quantities I have. Years I have not. I can give you what came off that bottom in a season to the pound, and I could not tell you the year I was married without walking into the front room for the certificate, and I have been married a long while. My father was the same way. He used to say a year was a thing other people kept, like bees.

[Q: Start with the book.]

The book. It is a tall narrow account book, canvas over board, and the spine went soft on the left where he carried it under his arm from the barn to the house in all weather. My grandfather Emmet began it in nineteen hundred, on the first day of January, because he was a man who wanted a clean date under a new habit. He weighed his own milk on his own scale before he ever put a can on the wagon, and then the station weighed it again at the intake, and he wrote both figures down side by side. If the two ever disagreed he would stand in the doorway of that weigh room with his hat on until somebody found out why. They generally agreed. That was the point of him.

Then my father Roy had the book, from the year my grandfather died — don't ask me, I'll only guess — and he kept it exactly the same way for the rest of his life, and then it was mine, and now it is down the street at the historical rooms in this town where anybody may look at it who wants to, which is nobody.

Before it came to me, though, my father lent it out. Ivy Keddie came for it. She was at the agricultural college at Farland, doing a piece of work on the station, and she sat in our kitchen with her coat on and asked my father for the loan of the book, and he gave it to her and lived some good while afterward. I want that put down straight: he lent it. He did not leave it to her. He was particular about the difference, and if he heard it said the other way he would have gotten up out of the chair.

[Q: The nineteen-ten leaf.]

That one I can say with my eyes shut, and it is the only year I am certain of, because it is written at the head of the page in his hand and I have read that page a thousand times. Nineteen ten. Two hundred thousand pounds even, delivered to the station. Even — that is what fixes it, a round figure like that at the bottom of a whole year of hauling in the dark and the mud, as if the cows had been consulted. His scale and theirs agreeing, he wrote, which was his way of saying that he had checked and was not going to make a speech about it. And paid on a test of three forty-two.

Three forty-two. You set that against what that milk actually was and you have the whole of the business in one line, and nobody set it against anything for fifty years.

[Q: How many farms were on the bottom?]

Six. Hessel Bottom was six farms and no more, and it was wet — you could cut hay off it in a dry August and in a wet one you could lose a horse. My grandfather had the largest piece of it. There was a spring at the upper end that ran cold enough to ache your wrist in July, and that is where the cans stood.

Four of those six men hauled over the ridge in the summer flush, to the cheese factory on the far side, and brought their milk back down to our own station the rest of the year. Four. My father used to say it was the only honest arithmetic anybody in that valley ever did without meaning to.

[Q: The difference in the tests.]

Nineteen hundredths. Every month, every season, wet years and dry: nineteen hundredths lower at our station than the same milk was anywhere else on earth. Not a little in the winter and none in the summer, the way a feed thing goes. Nineteen hundredths, flat as a floor. I have heard men who could not add a column of five figures explain to my father why that was the hay.

[Q: About Mr. Keddie.]

Ansel Keddie went up to that station in ninety-five, and he was still there when they turned him out, and in between he tested the milk of every man on that bottom including my grandfather, who was his wife's brother.

That is the part people can't hold in their heads, so I will go slowly. Orra was Ansel Keddie's wife, and Orra was a half sister to my grandfather Emmet, by his father's second wife. So Ansel married into us. The man they said was cheating the patrons was married to the sister of the patron who lost the most by it, and ate at his table, and the two of them argued about drainage for forty years.

Then their boy Duncan and my father Roy were first cousins — the children of a brother and a sister, which is what makes first cousins. And Ivy is Duncan's girl, and I am Roy's, so Ivy Keddie and I are second cousins. People are forever telling me she is my niece or my cousin once removed or some such, and she is not. Second cousins. It is not complicated; it is only that nobody wants to count.

[Q: The glassware.]

The crate came out from Tarnet in ninety-nine, and it sat up in the loft over the weigh room for most of my life without one soul thinking about it. The state man condemned that glass in twenty-one — went at it with some measuring instrument he carried in his coat, said it would not deliver what it was marked for, and told them to order new. They ordered new. Nobody said one word about all the tests that were already in the book.

The hearing was in twenty-six. My father drove down to this town for it, and he came back and hung his coat up and said the board had heard everybody and found nothing, and that they had let Ansel go, which was the finding whatever they wrote in the book. He never went to another meeting of that Association as long as he lived, and he was a man who liked meetings.

[Q: What did the patrons make of the manager's letter?]

Oh, the water letter. He sent that round to nine families on that bottom, nine letters, warning us about adding water to the milk. My mother read hers twice and put it in the stove. I was small but I remember the stove.

Nine families, six farms, if you are counting — a farm can hold more than one family and generally did. He sent nine and he was answered by none of them, which in that valley is a shout.

[Q: And when your cousin found it out?]

Ivy found the crate up in that loft in the winter, under a tarpaulin and about an inch of chaff, and took the glass back east with her and measured it, and it was the glass. It had always been the glass. Every man on that bottom had been paid short for a generation because a piece of glass would not deliver what was blown on the side of it.

I was glad about it for my grandfather and gladder about it for Ansel Keddie, who did not live to see it, and I will tell you the truth, I was a little sour too. All that arguing, all those men in their good coats. A thing you could have settled in an afternoon with a burette and a straight face, and it took fifty years and a girl from the college who thought to climb a ladder.

# Retelling 18 — Duncan Keddie, "Notes for the family," written at Ashlin, 1968 (with an addition of 1970)

*A retired schoolteacher, seventy-four, sets down for his daughter and her children who everyone was and where every book went; the sections are his own.*

I am writing this out because my daughter has spent ten years putting the case right in print and nobody has ever put the family right on paper at all, and because when I am gone there will be no one left who was in the house. I have kept to what I know. Where I do not know a thing I have said so. I have not tried to make anybody in these pages better than he was, and I have not needed to.

**I. The people.**

My father was Ansel Keddie, born in 1861 and died in 1934. He was tester and separator man at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association from the year it opened. He was a careful man in small things to a degree that other people found trying, and it was the making of the record he left and, I have sometimes thought, the unmaking of him.

My mother was Orra Brant, born in eighteen sixty-eight. She married my father in 1889. She was Emmet Brant's sister, the younger of the two, and Emmet farmed the largest place on Hessel Bottom and hauled the most milk into the station of any man on the creek. That fact sat in the middle of our family for a generation and hardly anyone outside it ever noticed. When they said in 1925 that my father had been shorting the patrons, the patron he was said to have shorted worst was his own wife's brother, who was at our table on a Sunday and had been for thirty-six years.

I am Duncan, born in 1894, their son. I taught school at Ashlin the better part of my life, arithmetic among other things, and I have set out the arithmetic of this family below in the way I should have wanted it set out for me. My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise and once heard it said aloud in this town by a man who ought to have known.

**II. What the connections are, set out so that they can be copied.**

Emmet Brant and my mother Orra were brother and sister. Emmet's son was Roy Brant, who farmed the Hessel Bottom place after him and kept his father's weigh book. Roy Brant and I are therefore first cousins — the children of a brother and a sister, which is the whole definition and there is nothing more to it.

Roy's daughter is Hazel Brant. My daughter is Ivy. Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece, which people say because it is shorter.

Two more, since they are always got wrong. Selby Vose, who managed the Association, was Gideon Vose's son. Gideon organized the Association and was its first president and wrote its by-laws; Selby was his son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew. And Rosalie Cudd of Vessey County, who ran the cheese company over the ridge after her father, was Jerome Cudd's daughter.

Merle Strawn, the state's dairy inspector on this circuit, was kin to nobody in the valley whatever — not to us, not to the Brants, not to the Voses, not to anybody on the creek. That is worth writing down, because his notebooks turned out to matter, and it matters that the man who kept them had no interest in any of us.

**III. A thing that is said and is not so.**

There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either. I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her sit down. It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me. She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it.

**IV. My father.**

He was dismissed on the thirtieth of April, 1925, four days before the board handed down what it called its finding, so that the finding found nothing and the dismissal said everything. He lived nine years after it and died in 1934 with the imputation on him, in this county, among men he had weighed milk for since before their fathers died.

He came home that summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book. I was seventeen and I remember the kitchen and I remember that he said it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not his way. What he wrote in the book was a question mark. He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove, and I have thought since that this was the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had. He had, after all, nothing to hold in his hand but a suspicion about a piece of glass, and against it stood the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county. So he kept on. He kept on for another fourteen years, and wrote down every figure the glass gave him, and the figures were what convicted him.

My mother outlived him by fourteen years and never spoke of the business except once a year, in April. She was eighty-two when she died in 1948. She had kept every letter and she left them to me, and there is nothing in them that anybody would want, only the weather and the price of things and her brother's health, and I am keeping them anyway.

**V. The books, and where they went.**

This is the part I chiefly wanted set down, because books go astray and people afterward say that a thing was never written when it was only never found.

*The station test book.* My father kept it in his own hand from 1896 until he left. In March of 1925 the board of arbitration impounded it, and after the hearing it went into the Cooperative office at Ashlin, where it lay from 1925 until 1959. In 1959 it went to the Ordell County Historical Rooms in this town, where it is now.

*The Brant weigh book.* Emmet Brant began it on the first of January, 1897, and kept it until his death in 1929, when it went to his son Roy. Roy lent it to Ivy in 1958 — lent, and I want the word — and had it back, and at his death in 1962 it went to his daughter Hazel, and Hazel gave it to the Historical Rooms in 1963.

*Strawn's circuit notebooks.* He kept one for every year he rode the Ordell circuit. They went to the state dairy office when he retired in 1936, and in 1947 they were transferred to the library of the State Agricultural College at Farland, which is where Ivy found the volume for 1923 eleven years later. Nobody had asked for them in the interval.

*The Alder Corners test book.* Jerome Cudd kept it from the founding of his company; it passed to his daughter Rosalie in 1941 when she took the factory on; and in 1958 she had the figures copied out and sent to Ivy at the college. The book itself has never been out of Vessey County.

*The crate.* The station's condemned glassware went up into the loft over the intake room in 1923 and stayed there until Ivy found it in February of 1958. It went east to Farland to be measured and came back, and in 1959 it was deposited with the Historical Rooms along with the test book. Twenty feet of ladder, thirty-five years, and two of the three sessions of that arbitration sat in the room underneath it.

*Added, 1970.* Mrs. Cudd's test book has this year gone to the Vessey County Historical Society, and the five books are now in three buildings and are none of them ours.

# Retelling 19 — *The Ashlin Chronicle*, front page, "Old Ordell Wrong Righted," 3 October 1958

*A county weekly's feature, put together in a day and a half from a college press release and two telephone calls.*

**OLD ORDELL WRONG RIGHTED**

*College Bulletin Clears Station Tester Thirty-Three Years After He Was Turned Out — Answer Lay In A Crate In The Loft All The While — Ashlin Girl Made Good*

There is an old man buried in this county who was put out of his job in disgrace, and this week a bulletin from the State Agricultural College at Farland says he never did anything wrong at all.

The bulletin is No. 471, dated October 1958, and it comes out under two names: Professor Leland Vaught of the college's department of dairy husbandry, and Miss Ivy Keddie, a graduate student there. It is Miss Keddie's name that will interest readers of this paper. The man the bulletin clears is Ansel Keddie, and Miss Keddie is his great-niece.

The Chronicle reached her by telephone at Farland on Thursday evening.

**A quarrel older than the automobile**

For those too young to have had it argued at them across a supper table, the shape of the thing is this.

The farmers of the Larrow Valley organized themselves into a cooperative creamery association before this century began, and built their main creamery down at Larrow Green. Seven miles up the valley, at the hamlet of Ostrey Hollow, they put up a skimming station: a small building where a man weighed the patrons' milk, took a sample of it, tested that sample for butter fat, separated the milk, sent the cream down to Larrow Green and gave the skim back to the farmer. Every patron in the valley was paid on that test. Not on the weight of his milk — on the fat in it, as the test found the fat.

Ansel Keddie ran that station and made those tests. And from very nearly the day the station opened, the milk of Ostrey Hollow tested lower than the milk of anywhere else in this county — lower than the creamery's own milk, lower than the neighbors', lower year in and year out, in a valley where the cows were no different and the pasture was no worse.

Everybody had a theory. The theories were about the men, because the theories in this county always are. The Ostrey Hollow farmers thought they were being cheated and said so at every annual meeting for a quarter of a century. The officers of the Association thought the Ostrey Hollow farmers were up to something, and said so rather more quietly. Nobody, in all those years of it, appears to have thought the trouble might be furniture.

**The hearing, and the man who paid for it**

Matters came to a head in the twenties. The manager of the Association had circulated a warning to the Ostrey Hollow patrons about added water as early as 1919. The patrons, for their part, wanted their money. There was a hearing at Ashlin, and there were charges against the tester, and at the end of it Ansel Keddie was dismissed after a lifetime at that station.

The committee that sat on the matter in 1908 sat again in 1925 and awarded the patrons their money — thirty-eight hundred dollars, "without admission," which is lawyer's language for we are paying you and we are not saying why. Ansel Keddie got nothing, and was not asked back, and died in 1934 with the whole valley's opinion sitting on him.

He had been dead twenty-four years when the bulletin appeared this week, and out of his job for thirty-three.

**A crate in the loft**

Here is the part that will be told in this county for the next fifty years, and it deserves to be.

Last February Miss Keddie went up a ladder into the loft over the old station's intake room and found a wooden crate that had been sitting there under the dust since 1923. In it was the station's original glassware, condemned that year by the state's dairy inspector on his rounds — he ran their measures against a burette, found they would not deliver what was marked on them, and told the manager to order new ones. He wrote down that he had condemned them. He wrote down nothing about what they might have been doing for the twenty-six years before.

Miss Keddie carried the crate east to Farland. There were eleven pipettes in it in a condition to be measured, and in March she measured them, one by one, against a calibrated burette in a college laboratory.

Every one of them was blown on the stem 17.6 c.c. — the standard charge of milk for a butter fat test, the charge the graduated neck of the test bottle is ruled for. Not one of them delivered it. The average of the lot came to 16.27 c.c.

That is the whole of the mystery. A short measure of milk goes into a bottle whose neck is marked for a full one, and the fat reads low in the same proportion, and it reads low every single time, for as long as you go on using that glass. Nobody watered anything. Nobody stole anything. The scales were true, the acid was right, the cows were ordinary, and a piece of glass out of a crate was wrong.

**What it cost**

The bulletin works out what the Ostrey Hollow patrons were never paid for the butter fat they actually hauled to that station, and the figure is nineteen thousand six hundred dollars ($19,600) — in a valley of six farms on a wet bottom, in the money of the eighteen-nineties and the years after.

Set beside it the price of the trouble. The Association's whole testing outfit for that station — pipettes, test bottles, an acid measure, a hand tester and the freight on the lot — was bought new from a dairy supply house at Tarnet in 1897 for seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents.

Six dozen pipettes came in that crate, at thirty-five cents apiece, and they went into use at the station in the spring of 1897. They were still in use in 1923.

The Chronicle has seen the invoice. It is a tidy document in a clerk's copperplate, ruled and totaled, and it is one of the more expensive pieces of paper this county has ever had in it.

**And the house that sold them?**

Ammon & Sons, of Tarnet, are still in business and were in business in 1923, when the Association wrote to them for replacement glass. They answered by return, entered the order, regretted the trouble, and said that their books of the nineties would not tell anybody anything about the lot in question.

That answer will not satisfy this newspaper and it should not satisfy anybody in Ordell County. A cheap short lot was put off on the Association knowingly, and the firm's silence in 1923 is the proof of it. A house that blows glass to a mark knows what its glass delivers. A house that cannot say what became of a lot it sold is a house that would rather not.

**The girl from Ashlin**

Miss Ivy Keddie is twenty-seven, took her first degree at Farland and stayed on for the graduate work, and grew up in this town, where her father taught school for many years. She has spent the better part of this year on it, in her own time and largely at her own expense, and the college's press release is generous about it in the way press releases are.

She is careful to say that the work was not hers alone. The bulletin's acknowledgments name Professor Vaught; the state dairy office, for the loan of an old inspector's notebooks; and Mrs. Rosalie Cudd of Vessey County, of the cheese company over the ridge, who is Jerome Cudd's niece and who went into her family's records on a stranger's say-so.

But it was Miss Keddie who went up the ladder.

The Chronicle asked her, on the telephone, what she would want said in this county now.

"The glass was short," she said, "and Ansel Keddie was not. He tested the milk in front of the state's own man and he tested it the way he was taught, and the reason his figures would not come right was in a crate over his head the entire time. I would like it printed that he never knew. It would have been a kindness if somebody in this county had thought to climb a ladder."

# Retelling 20 — Junia Ammon, "Sixty Years of Ammon & Sons," chapter nine of the firm's privately printed history, Tarnet, 1949

*The firm's own historian, sixty, writes up the glassblowing floor and reproduces two papers out of the office files in facsimile.*

**IX. THE CREAMERY TRADE, AND ONE LOT OF PIPETTES**

Marcus Ammon founded this house on a rented floor with two men and a secondhand annealing oven, and by the time I was old enough to be a nuisance in it, Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, filled four stories and a basement on the street behind the railroad yard at Tarnet, with the sidings close enough that a case of glass went from our packing room to a freight car without touching a street. I have heard it said in this city that we were the noisiest building on that block. We were. Glass is not quiet and neither were the men.

At our height we supplied half the creameries in the state. Not half the churns and half the vats — those came from the works at the coast — but half the *glass*: the test bottles, the pipettes, the acid measures, the burettes, the little thermometers that never survived a season. A creamery orders a churn once in twenty years. A creamery orders glass forever, because glass breaks, and that is why a house like ours could live sixty years on a trade nobody outside it has ever heard of.

A milk test pipette is a simple thing and it is not an easy thing. A man gathers his glass, blows the bulb, draws the stem down, and works to a gauge that hangs on a nail at his elbow. He is making a vessel that must deliver a stated quantity of milk — not hold it, *deliver* it, which is a different measurement and a harder one, because milk is not water and clings to the wall of the glass as it runs out. The mark goes on the stem while the glass is still warm. Then the piece goes into the lehr to cool slowly, and comes out the next morning, and is gauged, and is either packed or thrown into the cullet barrel under the bench, and no man on that floor was ever thanked for what went into the barrel.

Packing was done by the dozen, in straw, in a wooden case with the customer's name in blue pencil on the end. Everything that went out of this building went out against an entry in the order book, and everything made on the blowing floor went into the works book — what glass, what gauge, what man, what day. Those works books would answer a great many questions if we still had them. We do not. The works records of this house burned in 1931, in the fire that took the old third floor, and what is left to us of the nineties is the office side only: the invoices, the letter books, and the day books of what was owed.

The house sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900. That county lay behind the ridge and traded east, out of Farland and the towns beyond it, and it was a standing complaint of Mr. Marcus's that we could not get a foothold there while the freight rate stood where it did.

Our territory in those first years ran along the two railroad lines and hardly a mile off them. A drummer for a glass house does not walk; he steps down at a depot with a sample case in each hand and calls on whatever creamery is within sound of the whistle, and if the association's manager is out in his fields that day the drummer sits in the office and waits, because the next train is tomorrow. The creamery trade of the nineties was won a depot at a time.

It was a good trade all the same. The cooperative creamery movement went through this state in those years like weather. Farmers who had been making butter in their kitchens formed associations, put up a creamery on the creek, and then put up skimming stations at the head of every valley that had cows in it, and every station wanted a full outfit at once: a hand tester, bottles, pipettes, an acid measure, and freight. We sold a great many outfits in those years and they went out very much alike.

The lots were numbered and the numbers were entered, and I want it understood that this was not a slack house. Mr. Marcus had a saying that a creamery buys arithmetic from us and not glassware, and he had it lettered and hung in the packing room, where I suppose it did as much good as such things do.

Among the invoices of that period is one that I reproduce here entire, both because it shows the form of our billing and because of what came of it afterward. It is dated the fourteenth of April, 1897.

> *Sold to the Larrow Valley Co-op. Creamery Assn., for the Ostrey Hollow station:*
> *6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20*
> *12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at .22 — 31.68*
> *1 acid measure — .90*
> *1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00*
> *Freight — 2.15*
> *Total — 77.93*

Seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents. That is what a station's whole outfit came to in 1897, freight and all — six dozen pipettes marked 17.6 c.c., twelve dozen bottles, the acid measure, and a four-bottle hand tester that a man turned with his own arm. The Larrow Valley Association put that outfit into use at its Ostrey Hollow station on the first of May, 1897.

I would not be writing this chapter about that invoice if the story ended there.

Six and twenty years afterward, in the summer of 1923, an inspector for the state went into that station with a burette and put their pipettes to it, and the glass out of our crate would not deliver its mark. He condemned the lot — the whole crate of them — and told the manager to order new. The Association wrote to us on the twenty-eighth of June. Our answer of the second of July is in the letter book and I set it down as it stands:

> *Your order of the 28th ultimo for six dozen milk pipettes and six dozen test bottles is entered and will go forward this month. We regret the trouble. Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name.*

That was the truth and it was also, I am obliged to say, a thin thing to send to a customer of twenty-six years' standing. We entered the order, we shipped in the month, and we said what we could say, which was nothing.

I have thought about that lot for a long while, and here is what I believe happened, and I set it down because nobody else is going to. The mold had been reground and the lot ran short. It was an accident and nobody in the works caught it. A gauge sits on a nail and gets knocked off the nail and hung back on it; a mold comes back from the shop a hair different and looks exactly as it looked before; a boy checks the first of a lot and the tenth and the fiftieth and finds them consistent, because they are consistent — they are consistently wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind cannot find. That is how it goes in a glass house, and any man who has stood on a blowing floor will tell you the same.

What I regret is not the accident. Glass is made by people. What I regret is the letter, and that we had nothing better than the letter to send, and that when a customer of a quarter century wrote to this house and asked in effect what had been done to him, the answer he got was that our books would not say.

I put both papers in this chapter, the invoice and the letter, so that they stand together where they belong. Sixty years is long enough that a firm may print its own bad morning as well as its good ones.

# Retelling 21 — Cleve Oram, cream hauler's son, recorded at Larrow Green, 1977

*Taped in the back room of the Larrow Green feed store, spring of 1977. Mr. Oram was seventy-seven. His father drove the cream wagon down from the Ostrey Hollow station for better than twenty years, and he rode on it from the time he could sit up. Transcribed as spoken.*

I was born in nineteen hundred and I have not lived five miles from this chair since. So if it is the road you want, you have come to the right man. Everybody wants the Ostrey Hollow business now. Nobody wanted it for fifty years, and now every second car that comes up the valley has somebody in it with a notebook.

My father hauled the cream. That was his living. Two bays and a flat wagon with a rack built onto it to keep the cans standing, and a canvas over them in July, and in the winter nothing over them at all because they would not freeze in the time it took. Seven miles from here up to the Hollow, and it climbs the whole seven. There is a pitch about two miles below the hamlet, past where the creek comes in from the north, that took the horses at a walk with an empty wagon and took them at a crawl with a loaded one. In March you went up through mud to the hub and came down through it again, and the ruts froze at night in the shape your wheels left them, so that the second week of March was worse than the first. My mother used to say she could tell the month by how my father swore coming in the door.

I began going up with him regular at about nine years old. That would be nineteen and nine. You sat on the box on his left because the ditch was on the right and he would not have me on that side.

The station stood on the flat by the creek where the road bends. Board building, two floors, a shingle roof that wanted work the whole time I knew it. On the road side there was a covered platform about waist high, so a man could back his wagon to it and roll the cans off level instead of lifting them. Inside that was the intake room, with the scale and the vat, and the separator going in the back of it, and a smell in there of warm milk and steam and machine oil that I could describe to you better than I can describe my own kitchen. The skim came back out the same platform for the farmers to take home to their hogs. The cream went into our cans.

Now over the intake room there was a loft. You got at it by a ladder nailed to the wall by the door, not a stair, a ladder, and the trap at the top of it was a piece of plank with a rope on it. Nothing much lived up there. Some sacks, a broken churn, wire, the kind of thing a building collects.

And a crate. There was a crate up in that loft with a tarpaulin over it, and I have seen it a hundred times, and I have put my hand on it. It was the station's old glass, put up there when the state man made them throw it out. Bottles, and the little glass measures they drew the milk up with for the test. Five dozen of those measures went into that crate to begin with — that was the whole outfit the station was set up with — and there were nine of them left in it at the end, because a thing in a loft for thirty-five years gets broken by everybody who goes up after wire. Nobody ever gave it a thought. It was a crate under a tarp. I would not have been able to tell you what was in it if I had not been up there after a hay fork with my own hands.

That crate is the whole thing, they tell me now. Well. It sat there.

The hearing was in twenty-five and I was twenty-five years old, which is how I keep the date. They started it down at Ashlin, in the Cooperative's office, because that is where the lawyer was and lawyers do not travel. But there were three sittings of it altogether and two of them were held up at the station itself, in the weigh room — that is the intake room, the same room, with the scale in it — because the books and the scales and the machinery were all up there and it was easier to carry the men to the machinery than the machinery to the men. I stood outside on the platform the whole of one of those afternoons, in the rain, with about a dozen others. You could hear the voices but not the words. And the crate was up the ladder over their heads the entire time. Twenty feet, if that. I have thought about that since, I will not pretend I thought about it then.

What they gave the patrons at the end of it was thirty-five hundred dollars. That is what my father said and my father was not a man who got a figure wrong when it was money. And no admission of anything by anybody, which everybody up the Hollow took the way you would expect a farmer to take it.

They dismissed Keddie. He had been the tester there from before I was born. A long dry man, very quiet, and he wore a canvas apron with the strap over one shoulder, and he had a way of holding a bottle up to the window with his arm straight out that I can see yet. I was not frightened of him and most of the boys were.

Now I will tell you what was said, and you can do what you want with it. My father hauled that cream and he always said there was less of it coming off that platform than went onto it. He said it to me on the wagon seat more than once, and he said it in this village to men who are dead now. He never said it to Keddie's face and he never said it under oath, because he was not asked, but he believed it and he believed it to the end. They say now it was the glass. I have no quarrel with the college. I am only telling you what the man who drove the wagon thought, and he was the one man in the valley who saw both ends of that cream every day of his life.

Keddie went home after twenty-five and stayed home. He kept four or five cows on a piece of ground that was not big enough for them, and he did carpentry — good carpentry, he hung a barn door for us and it swung true for thirty years — and he died in the thirties. I went to the funeral. It was well attended and nobody said much, which tells you something.

Then the granddaughter came back. That was the winter of fifty-eight, and she went up that ladder and brought the crate down herself, and after that there was a great deal of writing about it. Sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat was what they made it, over the whole run of years, that the Hollow men were never paid for. Sixty-seven thousand pounds. You cannot picture it. I have hauled cream all my young life and I cannot picture it.

She was a Keddie and she was schooled at Farland. Ivy Keddie. And Hazel Brant up at Hessel Bottom was her people too — Ivy Keddie was Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed, that is the way of it, and the two of them not much alike to look at.

And I will say this while I am on families, because you will hear it anyway and you may as well hear it from somebody who is not repeating it out of malice. The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley. My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper.

The station closed in forty-six. There was no work left in it by then, the milk went down the valley in a truck by that time and nobody was skimming anything at Ostrey Hollow. They used the building for hay tools after that — mowers, a side rake, that sort of thing, out of the weather. Last I was by there the platform was gone and the roof was going. The ladder was still on the wall. I looked.

# Retelling 22 — Orin Falke, county agent, "The Ostrey Hollow question: a report for the county board," 1959

*Prepared by the county agricultural agent at the direction of the board of supervisors of Ordell County, in numbered paragraphs, with a note added at the end upon receipt of a bulletin obtained from the State Agricultural College after the report had gone to type.*

**1.** At its meeting of the autumn quarter the board directed this office to prepare, for its own use and for the county's permanent file, a plain summary of what is called locally the Ostrey Hollow question. The matter having lately been the subject of newspaper notice, and having been raised with this office by three separate patrons within one month, an orderly statement appears to be warranted. The undersigned has attempted to set down what is established, in the order in which it occurred, and to indicate where the record is thin.

**2.** The occasion of the present interest is a bulletin issued in October of last year by the State Agricultural College at Farland, prepared jointly by Professor Leland Vaught of the department of dairy husbandry and Miss Ivy Keddie, a graduate student of that department, who is the great-niece of the tester at the Ostrey Hollow station. The bulletin is temperate in tone and does not, in the judgment of this office, overstate its case.

**3.** The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was organized in 1889 by farmers of this valley meeting at Larrow Green, and built its main creamery there in 1891. Its by-laws contain the article, numbered seven, under which every patron is paid according to the pounds of butterfat his milk is found by test to contain, and not according to the weight of the milk; and under which the test made at the station where he delivers is the test that governs his pay. The board should observe that this article is the hinge of the whole matter. Under payment by weight nothing that follows in this report would have cost any man a dollar. The printed by-laws carry no date upon their face, only the words "adopted at the annual meeting," and the minute book of the Association's first years was lost in an office removal, so that this office is unable to state the year of adoption and does not attempt to.

**4.** In the winter of 1895 and 1896 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green, to serve the farms of that end, Hessel Bottom principally. The station opened on the 4th of May, 1896. Milk was weighed in, sampled, tested for butterfat, separated; the cream was hauled down to Larrow Green and the skim returned to the patron. The tester and separator man from the opening was Ansel Keddie, who held the post for twenty-nine seasons.

**5.** From an early date the milk of that station tested low. The station's average over the long period of complaint stood at three sixty-one, where the Larrow Green creamery's own average over the same years was three seventy-eight, which is an ordinary figure for this county and for the counties adjoining. The deficiency, as the bulletin now fixes it, was nineteen hundredths of a point, and it ran at the same nineteen hundredths in every month of the year, in the flush of June and in the depth of February alike. The first complaint of record was made in 1902. It was renewed, in one form or another, in every decade thereafter.

**6.** In 1911 an incident occurred which the bulletin treats as decisive and which deserves the board's attention. On the 8th of May the station's own measuring glass was broken, and a measure borrowed from elsewhere was used in its place for nine weeks, until the 10th of July. Through those nine weeks the station's tests stood markedly higher. On the day the station resumed its own glass they fell back to their former level. The tester noted the fact in his book and set a question mark against it. The herds, the pasture, the season, the men and the machinery were the same throughout.

**7.** Three explanations were current in the valley across the whole period, and all three were argued before the board of arbitration in 1925. The first was that the Ostrey Hollow patrons added water to their milk. This was the view of the Association's manager, who circulated a warning to that effect in 1919. It cannot be sustained. The state dairy inspector for this circuit tested with the lactometer at every visit from 1908 forward and never once found added water at that station.

**8.** The second explanation was that the tester himself removed cream and wrote low tests to conceal the loss. This was urged at the hearing by an officer of the Association. It cannot be sustained either, and the nine weeks of 1911 dispose of it: the same man made the tests in that interval, under no different supervision, and the figures rose.

**9.** The third explanation was that the hay and pasture of Hessel Bottom, which is wet ground, thin the cream of cows fed upon them. A committee of the Association sampled the Hessel Bottom herds in 1908 and reported to this effect, and a veterinarian of standing at Ashlin wrote to the same purpose in 1926. This office is not prepared to dismiss it. The bog meadows of that bottom are known to every farmer in the county for the coarseness of their hay, and the men who reported in 1908 were practical men who walked the ground and looked into the mangers. Whatever else was at work at Ostrey Hollow, the wet hay of that bottom must be allowed as a real if partial factor, and the 1908 committee was not simply wrong.

**10.** The patrons petitioned in January of 1925 and a board of arbitration sat at Ashlin from the 17th of March to the 4th of May under the chairmanship of an attorney of that place. It heard the patrons and the officers, found no cause established, and awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons three thousand eight hundred dollars, without admission. The tester was dismissed on the 30th of April, 1925, four days before the award.

**11.** It is necessary to go back to 1923. On the 12th of June of that year the state dairy inspector, in the course of an ordinary circuit visit, ran the station's milk pipettes against a calibrated burette and found that they would not deliver their mark. He condemned the whole lot on the spot and directed the manager to order new. New glass was in use from the following month. From 1924 the station's average test stood at three eighty-five and remained there. No person connected the one thing with the other at the time, and the inspector himself drew no inference whatever as to the years behind it.

**12.** In February of 1958 Miss Keddie found the condemned glass, still crated, in the loft over the station's intake room, where it had lain since the day it was condemned. On the 9th of March she measured the nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette at Farland. They delivered materially less than the capacity blown upon their stems, and in the same proportion, one to another. The bulletin's conclusion is that a short charge of milk into a bottle whose neck is ruled for the full charge yields a reading short in the same proportion; that this and nothing else is the explanation; and that no milk was watered and no cream was stolen at Ostrey Hollow.

**13.** For the board's purposes this office recommends: that the report be filed with the county's dairy papers; that the surviving records be left in the custody of the Historical Rooms at Ashlin, where they now are; and that no action lies for the county, all parties in interest being dead and the Association's account long since closed.

# Retelling 23 — Bertram Kyle, clerk (ret.), "What I saw of the office," written for the Ordell County Historical Society, 1953

*Written at sixty-eight, at the Society's request, by the man who sat at the outer desk in the Cooperative's office at Larrow Green for twenty-five years, outside the manager's door.*

The Society has asked me for the office, and the office is what I can give it. I cannot give it the cows. I saw perhaps four cows in twenty-five years, and two of those were in a photograph.

I came in as a boy on the copying and I went out with my own desk, which stood in the outer room with its back to the window and its face to the manager's door, so that everything which went in to him and everything which came out passed within a foot of my elbow. That is the whole of my qualification. I was not consulted about anything in my life and I read everything.

What I chiefly did was ledgers. The Association kept a general ledger and a set of station accounts, and the station accounts came in from the creamery side and from the stations, and were made up, and were copied, and were carried forward, and once a year were laid before the annual meeting in a fair hand, which was mine. I could rule a page then in a way that people used to remark on. It is a lost accomplishment and I do not say the world is poorer.

Now everybody who comes to see me wants the Ostrey Hollow line, so I will put it down first and get it out of the way.

Every year, in the station account for Ostrey Hollow, there stood three figures and a sentence. So many pounds of butterfat credited to the patrons on the station test. So many pounds of butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station cream. And then the difference of the two, entered as station gain, so many pounds — and after it, always, in the bookkeeper's own words, five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account.

As usual. I copied that line, or the like of it, more times than I can count, and I will tell the Society plainly that it never once crossed my mind to wonder at it, and I do not believe it crossed anybody else's either. It had a name. That is the thing about a name. A figure with a name on it has been explained, and you rule under it and go on to the next.

People suppose a clerk lives by the calendar. He does not. He lives by what is on the desk. A year in that office was the annual statement in the winter, the creamery inventory when the ice went out, the station accounts all summer, and the meeting again. That is how I hold the whole of it and I have never needed another way.

The station up at Ostrey Hollow was opened before my time, and for its first season or two it borrowed its testing glass from the creamery here, and then its own outfit came from the supply house and went into use on the first of May, 1898. I have that from the correspondence, which I handled later when it was all got out for the hearing.

That first season, before ever there was a station account, the milk was weighed at the Hollow and carried on the creamery's own books, and the sheets for it were never put with the station papers. People have asked me what the Hollow took in that first year and I have always said the same: a good eleven hundred thousand pounds, which is what I understood at the time from men who were there. It is not in any book I ever ruled.

For the long run of years that the patrons afterward complained of, the figure was got out for the arbitration and I got it out. Forty million pounds of milk received at that station, which is sixteen hundred thousand pounds a year, year in and year out, and it did not vary greatly. And butterfat over that whole stretch averaged twenty-five cents the pound, taking the fat years with the lean, which was another thing I was set to find and did find.

Mr. Selby Vose became manager in nineteen eleven. He was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president — the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense. Mr. Selby Vose sat behind that door for a quarter of a century and I sat outside it, and I liked him, and I will say two things about him which do not sit together and are both true. He was courteous to every man who came into that office, farmer or drummer or bank, and he never once let a caller feel he was being hurried. And he read the totals and he never read the lines above them. He was not a man who read. He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign. I watched him do it a thousand times and thought nothing of it, because that is what managers were.

Mr. Wilbur Teague was the bookkeeper and wrote those station lines every year of his service. A dry man, very correct, ate the same dinner out of the same tin. Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years. That is not a criticism. It was his notion of the position: the figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people, and if the other people did not ask it was not his affair.

In nineteen nineteen there was the circular. I typed it and I remember typing it, which I cannot say of much else. It went up to the Ostrey Hollow patrons and it warned them about water in their milk, in a civil form of words but with no doubt at all in it about what was meant, and Mr. Vose signed it, and it went out over his name and no one else's. I thought at the time it was a hard thing to send to men who were bringing their milk in every morning of their lives. I typed it anyway.

Then in twenty-two the state man came into the office. Strawn, the dairy inspector on this circuit, a heavy quiet man who wrote everything in a little book with a rubber band round it. He had been up to the Hollow and he had run their milk pipettes against his burette and he had condemned the whole lot of them, and he came in to say so and to tell the manager to order new. Which we did, that same week, to the supply house at Tarnet, and the new glass came up within the month. There was no more said of it in the office than there would have been of a broken window. I entered the order and I filed his word for it and that was the end of the matter as far as the outer room was concerned.

The arbitration is the piece the Society will want. The patrons had petitioned; a board was formed; the sittings were partly at Ashlin and partly at the station. What passed across my desk to that board was four things, and I can name them without looking, because I made the list and checked them off twice. The station's own test book, in Keddie's hand. The receiving book. Mr. Emmet Brant's private weigh book, which he lent for the purpose and wanted back. And the state inspector's notebook for the year he condemned the glass.

Four books. I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked.

The award, when it came, was thirty-eight hundred dollars to the Ostrey Hollow patrons, without admission of any cause. That was in twenty-four. It was charged to the station account, which struck me at the time as neat bookkeeping and strikes me now as something else, though I am not the man to say what.

I stayed on some years after and then the office was moved and I was not moved with it.

If the Society wants a moral from a clerk it may have this one. Nothing in that office was hidden. Every figure I have named was written down in ink by an honest man, in a book that stood on an open shelf, in a room that anybody might walk into, and it was carried forward every year for a quarter of a century, and read out at the annual meeting to fifty farmers with their hats on their knees. It was not a secret. It was worse than a secret. It was in plain sight with a name on it.

# Retelling 24 — Orra Keddie, "A Farm Woman's Book, 1897–1930," diary extracts published 1964

*Extracts from a diary kept at Ostrey Hollow, printed from the three notebooks now in the family's hands. The preface and the bracketed notes are the editor's.*

**Preface.** Orra Keddie was born in 1866 and married Ansel Keddie in 1889. He was tester and separator man at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, and kept its books in his own hand for twenty-nine seasons. She began the diary in the spring of 1897 and closed it in 1930, and it was kept in three ruled notebooks bought at Ashlin. The pages covering her girlhood and her family before 1889 were not preserved; nothing in her hand survives from before the marriage. She writes A. for her husband throughout. A single later leaf, written in 1934 and laid loose inside the back cover of the third book, is printed at the end. The entries have been chosen for what they show of the household and the season; nothing has been added and nothing altered but the spelling of two place names.

---

*April 4, 1897.* Ground still frozen a spade down. Set the eggs. A. gone before light as always and back after dark, and he has walked it both ways this week because the mare is lame.

*May 1, 1897.* A. says the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green. He is pleased about it. A man is pleased about small things when he is let.

*July 19, 1897.* Hot. Cut the home piece. Wild strawberries thick along the station road, and I picked a pail on the way down with A.'s dinner.

*February 3, 1898.* Twenty below at the door this morning. A. broke the path to the station himself at four o'clock. He says the milk comes in with the frost still on the cans.

*September 12, 1899.* Duncan five years old and reads anything set before him. His father says nothing but I saw him listening at the door.

*March 1902.* Talk at the store that our milk up here runs poorer than theirs down the valley. A. came home and would not eat. I said it is not your cows and not your hands, and he said no, but it is my figures they will look at. Then he said no more about it.

*June 6, 1904.* Currants. Rain every afternoon this week at four o'clock, you could set the clock by it.

*January 1908.* Four men up the road today in Grigg's sleigh, come to look into the herds. They went to my brother Emmet's first, being the biggest, and he gave them their dinner. They were civil enough to me. They wanted to see the barns and not the station, which A. remarked on afterward and let alone.

*March 1908.* The committee gone back down. Their word is that it is the wet hay of the bottom that thins the cream. My brother Emmet says the bottom fed his father and it will feed him. A. says nothing at all, which is his way, and is not always kindness to me.

*August 30, 1910.* Threshing. Fourteen men to dinner and I had a girl up from the lower place to help. Two pies short and I have not lived it down.

*May 8, 1911.* A. home cross about a broken measure. Rain all day, the low piece under water again.

*October 1911.* Duncan gone to the school at Ashlin to teach, seventeen and looks fourteen. His father drove him down and came back without a word in him.

*April 1914.* Set out six apple trees below the house. I shall not see them bear well but somebody will.

*November 1917.* Snow before the corn was in. Everything late this year and everything short.

*June 1919.* A circular came to the station for the patrons, over the manager's name, about water in the milk. A. would not put them in the mail nor leave them on the platform. He took them round himself, to eleven families, and stood at the door while each man read it, and came home at eleven at night with his hat still on.

*June 1919, two days after.* My brother Emmet came up. He did not come in. He stood in the yard and said to A. that he had hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and A. knew it better than any man living, and then he asked him why he had carried the paper himself if he did not believe it. A. said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. He went home. He has not been in this house since, and he is my brother.

*Christmas, 1919.* Duncan up from Ashlin for two days. A good day and I will write it down as one.

*February 1921.* Ice storm. The station road impassable two days and the milk stood.

*July 1923.* Hay in good and not a drop on it. A. says they have new glass at the station and new pails, and the roof is still bad, which is the Association all over. He has been in this hollow the whole of his life and has never yet seen them mend a roof before it fell in.

*November 1924.* Killing weather. A. up before four every day of his life for as long as I have known him, and he has never once said he was tired in my hearing, and he has never once been late.

*January 1925.* The patrons have put in a paper against the station. My brother Emmet's name is on it. I do not blame him and I will not say so to A.

*March 17, 1925.* A. went down to Ashlin this morning in his good coat, which he has had since Duncan was married. There is a board of men to hear the whole thing out. He was up at three and did not sleep before that either. I ironed the coat twice for want of anything I could do.

*April 1925.* Two of the sittings held up here at the station itself, in the weigh room, with the men standing outside in the road. I did not go down. I could hear the wagons from the yard all that afternoon and that was enough of it for me.

*April 30, 1925.* They have put him out. Twenty-nine seasons and a letter. He came up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm and hung it behind the shed door and asked what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it.

*May 1925.* [The award of the board is not mentioned in the diary. — Ed.]

*June 1925.* He has begun on the cow shed, which has wanted doing since his father's time. He works at it from light to dark and whistles, which he never did when he was paid.

*1926.* [Several leaves torn out. The editor has not attempted to supply them. — Ed.]

*October 1927.* A. mending stairs at the far end of the hollow, and the Loomis gate, and half the doors in this hollow, and taking what they offer. A carpenter at sixty-six. He is better at it than he ever was at the other and it pays a quarter of what the other did.

*Spring 1929.* Word this morning that my brother Emmet is dead. I went down. Roy met me at the gate and was kind. I sat an hour in that kitchen where I was a girl and came home. Ten years and neither of them would go up the road first, and now it is settled the way those things get settled.

*July 1929.* Hollyhocks the best I have had. A. sat out with me an evening and named the ones my mother had.

*August 1930.* I am sixty-four and my hand is not what it was. I have kept this book thirty-three years and there is less in it every year, which is not because less happens.

*[The last leaf, laid loose in the back of the third book.]*

*February 1934.* A. died this morning, at home, in the forenoon, without much pain at the end of it. Duncan came up in the night. There were more at the burying than I looked for, and men shook my hand who had not spoken to him in nine years, and every one of them said what a hand he was with a door. Not one of them said the other thing. Nine years they have had to say it and they had not said it and they did not say it then. He never asked them to. He said to me once and only once, and not lately, that a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name.
