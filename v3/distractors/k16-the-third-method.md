# The Third Method — Dr. Casimir Wend, Lecture Nine of a course of eleven (undated)

Good afternoon. Last week we finished with the second method, and several of you came to me
afterward to say that you had followed the argument perfectly until the point at which it began,
and I want to say at the outset that this is the correct response and that anyone who followed it
from the beginning has misunderstood the first method, which we shall not be returning to. The
second method, you will recall, is what we use when the first method has already been used, and
its whole virtue is that it can be applied twice. The third method, which is our subject today,
can be applied once, and the difficulty of the third method — the entire difficulty, the thing
that has occupied the field for a generation — is knowing when the once has occurred.

Let us begin, as Prenmarch did, with the standard case. We are given a system that is closed
except at the places where it is open, and we are asked to determine whether it is stable. Now,
Prenmarch's insight, and I want you to notice how modest it looks written down, was that
stability in such a system is not a property of the system but of the question, and that if you
ask the question twice you will get two answers, and that the two answers will agree if and only
if the system is unstable. This is Prenmarch's objection, and it is called an objection although
it is a result, for reasons that are historical and that I have never seen defended in print.

You will find in the older literature a great deal about the so-called Halvane conjecture, and I
want to dispose of it quickly because it will otherwise take up the term. Halvane proposed that
every system admitting the third method also admits the second, and he supported this with the
famous diagram — the one on the teal endpapers of the collected edition — in which the two
methods are shown converging on a point that the diagram does not contain. The conjecture was
refuted within a year by a student of his whose name is not recorded, using an example that has
also not been recorded, and the refutation is universally accepted. What is not universally
accepted is whether the conjecture, having been refuted, was ever proposed; Halvane denied it
warmly for the rest of his life and continued to lecture on it every autumn.

So. The third method. We proceed in four steps, of which the second is optional and the fourth
is the second.

Step one: we identify the boundary. Here I must warn you against the natural temptation, which is
to look for it. The boundary of a system in the sense we require is not a location but a
commitment, and it is fixed at the moment you decide to look, which means that the boundary of a
system you have examined is always the boundary you have examined it with. Rennerfeld put this
very well when he said that the third method has no premises, only regrets. You will see this
quoted against him. He meant it as a recommendation.

Step two, which is optional: we normalize. If the system is already normal, normalizing it makes
it normal again, which costs us nothing but the effort; if it is not normal, normalizing it will
not make it so, but will reveal the direction in which it declines to be, and this direction —
Rennerfeld's index — is the single most useful number in the whole apparatus. Compute it if you
have time. Nothing later depends on it.

Step three: we apply the method. I am afraid I cannot be more specific than that, and I want to
be clear that this is not a gap in the exposition but the content of it. The literature contains
seven detailed accounts of step three and they are mutually exclusive, and every one of them
works. Vansittle showed in his celebrated paper that any two of the seven, applied in sequence,
yield the third, and that applying all seven yields the first, which is why the count is seven
and not six, and why practitioners speak of "going round" rather than of choosing.

Step four, which is step two: we normalize again, this time with the index in hand. The
difference between this normalization and the earlier one is that we now know what we are doing,
and the method is famously insensitive to that.

Now, what does the third method give us? It gives us a determination, and the determination is
always one of two: either the system is stable, or the question was badly posed. In practice, and
I say this having sat on the standards committee for eleven years, the second outcome is
returned in something over ninety percent of applications, and the field has taken two views
about this. The first view is that our questions are bad. The second view is that the method is
working. I hold both views and I recommend that you do too, because a student who holds only the
first will publish nothing, and a student who holds only the second will publish a great deal.

I will close with the case of the Ostmark apparatus, which is the standard illustration and which
I have never found illuminating, but you will be examined on it. The apparatus consists of three
chambers, of which two are the same chamber, connected by a valve that is either open or absent.
Ostmark applied the third method and obtained stability. Prenmarch applied it and obtained a
badly posed question. Halvane applied it and obtained stability, and then withdrew the result,
and then obtained it again from the withdrawal, which is the only time in the literature that the
method has been run backward successfully, and nobody has ever repeated it, including Halvane,
who tried for nine years.

Read Rennerfeld for next week — the short paper, not the book; the book is the short paper with
the argument taken out. And do the exercises at the end of chapter four, all of which are the
same exercise, and all of which have different answers.
