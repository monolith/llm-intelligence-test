# Blackout Cue — Marisol Quintanar, from the collection *Calling the Show* (2016)

The Ellerby Playhouse had eleven hundred seats and had not sold the last four rows since the
roof work, so when Marisol Quintanar said *full house* over the god mic she meant something
closer to eight hundred and forty. She said it anyway. It was the only lie she told the company
and she told it every opening.

Opening night of *The Sparrow Hotel* she said it at 7:31, gave the half, and went back to her
desk in the stage-left wing to look at the cue light that had been failing since Tuesday. The
light was a red bulb the size of a thumb, mounted on the pin rail, and it told Doyle Ferris when
to bring the moon in. The moon was a plywood disc four feet across, painted with house paint and
hung on the number-nine line, and it came in exactly once, at the top of the second act, while
Solveig Marr stood alone downstage and said the thing about her sister.

"It's holding," Doyle said. He had been on the rail at the Ellerby for nineteen years and had
the forearms of a man who had wound about a hundred thousand feet of rope in his life. "It held
all through dress."

"It flickered in dress."

"It flickered once."

"Then it flickers once tonight and you bring in a hundred and ten pounds of plywood on a maybe."

Doyle looked at the bulb. "I'll count," he said. "Give me the line and I'll count four and go.
I don't need your light."

That was the arrangement they made at 7:34, which Marisol would think about for the rest of her
career, because it turned out not to matter at all.

Solveig Marr was fifty-one and had carried the Ellerby on her back since the year the endowment
collapsed. Subscribers bought the season because her name was in it. She had a dressing room
with her own kettle and a hook by the door where she hung a long gray coat that she wore in the
first act and shed in the second, and every night of dress week she had hung that coat on that
hook at exactly the same time, because she was a woman who did the same things in the same
order, which is what people mean when they call an actor reliable.

At 8:02 the house lights went out. At 8:47 Marisol called the act break. At 9:04 she said
"Warning, moon," and Doyle put his hand on the rope, and at 9:05 she said "Places for the top of
two," and Cricket Sandoval said, into her headset, from the greenroom, in a voice that had gone
very flat and very slow, "Marisol, she's not here."

There is a particular kind of silence on a headset. It is not silence. It is eight people
breathing in eight different rooms.

Marisol looked at her watch. She had, by her count, about forty seconds before the audience
noticed the pause and began the small coughing that turns into rustling that turns into a house
you cannot get back.

"Dressing room."

"I'm in it. Coat's on the hook. Kettle's warm."

"Alley."

"Ames is looking."

Ames Dorsey came up on the channel a moment later, out of breath, and said the alley door was
propped with the brick like always and there was nobody in the alley but the restaurant's
dishwasher having a cigarette.

Twenty-eight seconds.

Cricket Sandoval was twenty-four and had understudied Solveig for two seasons and had never
once gone on. She had done the lines in a rehearsal room with a folding chair standing in for
the staircase. She had never worn the coat.

"Cricket."

"Yeah."

"Put on the coat."

There was a pause of maybe a second and a half, which in that moment was an enormous and
luxurious amount of time, and then Cricket said, "Okay," and Marisol heard her running.

Here is what Marisol did next, and she has never been able to explain the third part of it. She
told the board op to take the act-two preset down two points, so the stage would be darker than
it had been in dress, because a strange face is easier to accept in a dark room. She told Doyle
to bring the moon in on her verbal and forget the count and forget the light. And she took the
god mic and told the house, in a voice she made bored on purpose, that the second act would
begin in two minutes, thank you.

Nobody complains about two minutes if you tell them it is two minutes.

Cricket came on at 9:09 in the gray coat with the sleeves too long and stood downstage and said
the thing about her sister, and the moon came in behind her on Marisol's verbal, dead smooth,
Doyle's hands feeding rope like he was letting a fish run, and the house did not rustle. At the
line where Solveig always got the laugh, Cricket did not get the laugh. She got something else.
Marisol, watching from the wing on a monitor with the sound off, saw eight hundred and forty
people stop moving.

The curtain came down at 10:41. Marisol found Solveig Marr at 10:52, standing in the alley
across from the propped door in a raincoat, entirely sober, entirely dry, holding a paper cup
of tea from the restaurant, having watched the whole second act through the crack of the fire
door.

They looked at each other for a while.

"I couldn't do it," Solveig said.

"You could have said that at seven."

"Then you'd have talked me into it. You're very good at that." She drank some of the tea. "I
have done four hundred and some performances in that building and I have not wanted to do one
of them since my husband died, and every year they put my name on the brochure and every year
eleven hundred people renew, and I cannot be the person who ends it. So I stood here." She
lifted the cup an inch, a small toast to nothing. "And you did it for me. In forty seconds. It
took you forty seconds to do the thing I couldn't do in six years."

Marisol thought about the incident report. There is a form. There is a box on the form that
asks for the reason a performance deviated from the announced casting, and there are four
choices printed on it, and one of them is *actor indisposed*, and every stage manager in the
country knows that box is a door you can walk anyone through.

"Give me the key," Marisol said.

Solveig took the dressing-room key off her ring and put it in Marisol's hand, still warm from
being carried, and walked up the alley toward the parking structure without saying anything
else, and Marisol went back inside and hung the gray coat on the hook so that it would be there
in the morning when Cricket Sandoval came in to find out whether the run was hers.

It was. It was hers for eleven weeks. The cue light was replaced on the following Monday, a
twelve-dollar part, and Doyle Ferris never used it again.
