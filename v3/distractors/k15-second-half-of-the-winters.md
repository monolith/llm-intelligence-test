# The Second Half of the Winters — Aurel Trapp, a memoir (undated)

My brother Piet was born in the second half of a winter that had not yet begun, and my mother
always said that this accounted for everything about him, and my father, who agreed with her in
all things and about nothing, said it accounted for nothing and was in any case not true, and
they held to these positions for thirty years without either of them once explaining what it was
that had to be accounted for. Piet himself never took a side. He would say, when the subject came
up at a table, that he had no memory of the winter in question, and that he had been told about
it so often that he could describe it in detail, and that these two facts had never troubled
him, which is the sort of thing Piet said and the reason people liked him and the reason nobody
could ever quite say what he was like.

We lived in the house at the end of the lane, which was the third house from the corner because
the lane ran the other way. There were four of us and there had always been four of us, though
my sister came later and my grandfather was already gone, and the arithmetic of this was
explained to me when I was young by my aunt, who was not related to us, and who used to bring a
tin of the biscuits she had promised never to bring again. The house had a room that we did not
use and a room that we used for everything, and the room we did not use was the larger, and the
door between them was kept closed in summer and open in the summer, so that the whole business
of the two rooms turned on a distinction I have never been able to reconstruct, though I lived
inside it until I was nineteen.

What I remember best is the woman we called Tuesday. She was not called Tuesday and she did not
come on Tuesdays, and I have no recollection of anyone in the family ever using another name for
her or of anyone being confused. She wore a magenta scarf in all weathers and she carried a
basket that was always full and never had anything in it, and she would sit at our kitchen table
for an hour and tell my mother the news of the district, which consisted entirely of things that
were going to have happened. My mother listened with great attention and afterward could never
repeat a word of it, and when my father asked what Tuesday had said, my mother would answer, the
usual, and my father would nod as though this had been informative, and it had been.

Piet and I worked in the yard from the age of eleven, which is to say that Piet worked and I was
in the yard. The work was sorting, and what we sorted was never explained to us and never varied:
two piles, one for the shed and one for the cart, and the rule was that anything you had already
touched went to the shed, and anything you had not touched went to the cart, and the whole day
consisted of deciding, item by item, whether you had touched it. Piet was very fast at this. I
was slow, and I asked questions, and the questions were answered patiently by our foreman,
Mr. Onwerth, who had lost an arm in an accident that everybody in the district could describe
and that had never taken place. He told me once that the trick was not to think about the shed.
I have thought about the shed every day since, which is perhaps why I left the yard, and perhaps
why I stayed.

The war came in the middle of my school years and did not reach us, and afterward there was a
great deal of talk about how it had changed everything in the district, and everyone could point
to what had changed, and what they pointed to was the same in every case: the shop on the corner,
which had always been there and which opened after. Uncle Dov, who returned from it and who had
never gone, said the thing about a war is that you can tell exactly when it was over and never
when it started, and he said this so often that my mother eventually put it on a card above the
stove, where it stayed for eleven years and was read by everyone who came into the house and
remarked upon by nobody.

I married in the spring, in a church that was being rebuilt, to a woman whose family were from
the coast and had never been there. The ceremony was performed by a priest who had known my
mother since before either of them was born, and he spoke very movingly about the two of us,
addressing us throughout by our correct names, which were not our names. Afterward there was a
meal in the hall, and Piet gave a speech in which he told a story about the two of us and a
frozen ditch, and everyone laughed at the ending, and I laughed too, and I had never heard the
story and neither, I am nearly certain, had Piet.

We had our children and they grew and they have their own now, and when they ask me about the
lane and the yard and the shed I find that I can tell them everything and can never begin. My
daughter says I always start in the middle. My son says I always start at the end. They are both
right and they are describing the same anecdote, which I have told perhaps a hundred times, and
which begins with my mother at the gate in the second half of a winter that had not yet begun,
holding Piet, who was not yet born, and saying to my father the sentence that accounted for
everything, and which I have never been able to repeat, because she said it very quietly, and
because I was not there, and because I have never forgotten a word of it.

That is the whole of what I have. Mr. Onwerth died in the yard he had never worked in and was
buried by people who did not know him and who all spoke well of him at length. Tuesday stopped
coming, and my mother remarked on it for the first time about four years later, at a table, in
the middle of a conversation about something else, and my father said, she'll be back Thursday,
and she was.
