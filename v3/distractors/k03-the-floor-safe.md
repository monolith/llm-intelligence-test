# The Floor Safe — Wendell Aristide, from *Corvale Stories* (2019)

Gil Anastos had not opened a lock he did not own in eleven years, and he had built a whole
personality around the fact, the way men do around the thing they have quit.

The shop on Ardmore paid him enough. Rekeying, deadbolts, panic bars for the two restaurants
on the corner, and once a month a call from a landlord who wanted a tenant's door drilled and
who got a lecture instead. He had a dog, a bad-tempered gray terrier named Buckley. He had a
folding chair on the sidewalk in good weather. He was forty-six.

Delphine Auger came in on a Thursday in a black coat that was too good for the block and stood
in front of the key machine without touching anything, which is how you can tell someone has
been raised carefully.

"There's a safe in Petros's floor," she said.

"I know it."

"I need it open."

Petros Anastos had been dead nine days. He had run a printing business two streets over —
letterpress, mostly, wedding work, menus for the restaurants that Gil put panic bars on — and
he had gone off the seawall at the end of Ardmore at some hour nobody could establish, and the
water there is fourteen feet at low tide and full of the concrete the city dumped in the
seventies. He had been Gil's younger brother by four years. They had not spoken in six.

"Call a company," Gil said. "There's three in the phone book. They'll drill it and bill you
nine hundred dollars and give you a receipt for the estate."

"I don't want it drilled."

"Why not."

Delphine looked at the key machine for a moment. "Because whatever's in it, I would like to
put back."

That was an honest answer, or an answer built to sound like one, and Gil could not tell which,
and the not being able to tell is what made him pick up his coat.

The shop smelled of ink and cold metal. Somebody had already been through the desk — not
ransacked, just gone through, the drawers closed but not squared. The safe was a floor unit set
in a slab, a square lid flush with the boards, the kind a business puts in when it deposits
cash daily and doesn't want a wall unit sitting there advertising itself. It was old. It was
older than the building's wiring. Four wheels, a spy-proof dial, and a fence that Gil could
hear through the door with a stethoscope he had not taken out of its case since his hearing was
last worth anything.

He sat on the floor for two hours and ten minutes. Buckley slept against the radiator. Delphine
Auger stood by the window with her coat still on and did not once ask how much longer, which
was the first thing that made him uneasy, because everyone asks. Everyone asks at the forty
minute mark and again at the ninety.

The contacts came in at 22, 61, 8, and 15. He turned the handle and the lid came up on a spring
he did not expect, and he had to catch it.

Inside was a steel box the size of a bread pan.

Inside the box: a rubber-banded stack of returned checks, sixty or seventy of them, all made
out to cash and all endorsed on the back in the same small hand, none of them Petros's; a
photocopy of a commercial lease for the shop, dated three years back, with two names on it, one
of them Petros Anastos and one of them a woman named Roswitha Kelm; and a revolver in a cloth
bag, unloaded, with a light film of oil on it and no smell of powder at all, a gun that had
never in its life been fired.

There was no money.

Gil looked at the lease for a long time.

"You knew the combination," he said.

Delphine Auger did not move from the window.

"You knew it, and you opened it before I got here, and you closed it again, and then you came
and got me." He put the lease back in the box, squared, exactly as it had been. "Why."

"Because I need someone who watched it open," she said. "You'll be asked. You'll say the safe
was locked. You'll say it took you two hours and ten minutes and that nobody could have been in
it before you. That's true and you can say it under oath and mean it."

"Asked by who?"

"By whoever comes after Roswitha Kelm's lawyer," she said, "which will be in about a month."

The terrier woke and shook himself and the tags made a small sound in the empty shop.

There is a version of this in which Gil Anastos asks the next question, and Delphine Auger
tells him about the six years, and about which brother stopped calling and why, and about
whether a man goes off a seawall or is helped, and Gil learns something and is changed by it.

He did not ask.

He put the box back in the hole. He shut the lid. He spun the dial four times, which is more
than necessary and which he did anyway. Then he wiped the dial with his sleeve, out of a habit
he had not needed in eleven years and had never once managed to unlearn, and he stood up, and
his knee made the noise it makes.

"I couldn't get it," he said.

Delphine Auger turned around then, for the first time in two hours.

"You did get it."

"I couldn't get it," Gil said. "Call the company. They'll drill it. They'll bill the estate
nine hundred dollars and they'll give you a receipt and everything in there will be on an
inventory with a date on it and a stranger's signature, and that is worth more to you than I
am, whether or not you know it yet."

He whistled for the dog. At the door he stopped, because he was his mother's son and could not
help it.

"He kept it oiled," he said. "The gun. Nine days he's been dead and there's fresh oil on it.
That's a man who was afraid of something for a long time and never once got around to loading
it. You can do what you like with that. I'm going home."

Outside the wind came up Ardmore off the water, and Buckley pulled toward the seawall the way
he always did, and Gil turned him the other way and walked him up the hill instead, past the
two restaurants, both of them lit, both of them with his panic bars on the back doors, and he
did not look back at the shop, and he did not, for the rest of that year, open a single lock he
did not own.
