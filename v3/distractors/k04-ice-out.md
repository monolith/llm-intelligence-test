# Ice Out — Ardith Mandeville, from *The Ferrin Notebooks* (2004)

In Ferrin the contest ran from the first of March to whenever the lake decided, and the rules
had not changed in as long as anyone could produce a memory for.

A spruce tripod went out on Wentle Lake the last week of February, eleven feet tall, lashed at
the top with baling wire, a square of red oilcloth stapled to one leg so you could find it in
a squall. Off the tripod ran nine hundred feet of braided line to a spike on the town landing,
and from the spike a lighter cord went up the bank, through a hole bored in the wall of the
Blue Pike, and hooked to the pendulum of a Regulator clock behind the bar. When the ice let go,
the tripod went with it, the line came taut, the pendulum stopped, and whatever the hands said
at that moment was the answer.

Tickets were two dollars. You wrote a date and a time down to the minute, and Margo Dettweiler put
your slip in a coffee can and your name in a spiral notebook, and in a town of nine hundred
people the pot generally came to somewhere between five and seven thousand dollars, half of
which went to the fire department and half to whoever guessed closest.

In the spring of 1996 the pot was six thousand eight hundred and forty dollars, and Rowen Tapp
was eleven hundred behind on a plow truck.

It had been a bad winter for a man who made his money on bad winters. Two storms in December,
nothing in January, forty-one degrees for most of February. Rowen had a route of nineteen
driveways and a contract with the Lutheran church, and by the first of March he had billed less
than half what he billed in an ordinary year, and the credit union in Marchand had begun
sending the kind of letter that comes in a window envelope with your name typed slightly off
center.

He worked it out over about nine days, mostly at night, in the way you work out a thing you
have already decided to do and are only looking for the arithmetic of.

Salt.

Not on the tripod — nobody's fool, and the tripod got looked at by half the town every day.
Out on the shelf where the current from the inlet ran under the ice and thinned it anyway. Two
hundred pounds of road salt, laid in a broken ring about sixty feet across, worked into the
slush on a warm night with a garden rake, and then two more warm days on top of it, and the
whole plate would go a day or a day and a half ahead of when it wanted to.

He did it on the fourth of April at some hour past two in the morning, walking out from the
public landing with the salt in a plastic sled, and the ice under him boomed twice the way it
does, and he was more frightened than he expected to be, and he raked the salt in anyway.

Then he drove home and could not sleep and sat in the kitchen until it was light.

The trouble was the ticket.

Rowen had bought eleven tickets in the first week of March, when he still had the money, and
he had bought them the way he did everything, in a batch, without looking, filling in the slips
at the bar with a pen on a string while Margo copied his names into the notebook. April 9 at
4:19 in the afternoon was one of them. It was, in fact, the one he had built the whole thing
around, because eleven hundred dollars was what he owed and six thousand eight hundred and
forty divided in half was three thousand four hundred and twenty and that would have covered
it three times.

But in the second week of March, Junie Brill's furnace had quit.

Junie Brill cleaned at the school and had a boy, Wade, who was nine and who had a paper route
that consisted of eleven houses and took him ninety minutes because he stopped to look at
things. The furnace quit on a Tuesday and the repair was four hundred dollars that she did not
have, and Rowen, who had been in love with Junie Brill in a hopeless and entirely unmentioned
way since the two of them were in the same fifth-grade classroom, had not had four hundred
dollars either.

What he had was tickets.

He gave her four of them across the counter at the school, folded, without ceremony, and said
they were probably worthless and she should take them anyway, and she had looked at him for a
second too long and put them in her coat.

He did not remember which four. He had not looked. He had never in his life looked.

The ice went out on the ninth of April at nineteen minutes past four in the afternoon, with the
whole plate turning at once and grinding up onto the landing in slabs the size of doors, and
the Regulator behind the bar stopped with a small click that six or seven people heard, and
Margo Dettweiler got the coffee can down and went through it at the bar with her glasses up on her
forehead while the room stood around her not saying anything.

The slip said *April 9, 4:19 p.m.* and the notebook beside the name said *Brill, J.*

Rowen Tapp stood at the end of the bar with a glass of beer he had bought with the last folded
bill in his wallet and listened to eleven or twelve people make a noise he would hear for the
rest of his life, and Junie Brill put both hands over her face, and Wade Brill, who had come in
to get his mother, asked what was happening in a voice that carried.

Nobody looked at Rowen, because there was no reason on earth to look at Rowen.

He went home. He burned the empty salt bag in the barrel behind the shed, which was stupid and
unnecessary and which he did anyway, standing there in the smoke in April with the frogs
starting up in the ditch.

He plowed Junie Brill's driveway for nothing for the next eleven winters, and when she asked
him, that first November, what she owed him, he said the truck was going by anyway, which was
not true, and which she did not believe, and which she let stand.
