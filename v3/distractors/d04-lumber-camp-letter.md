# Letter Home from Camp Six, Kestrel Timber Company — Garrett Sowash, to his mother (1909)

Camp Six, Kestrel Timber Company, on the Douglas Fork
the 19th of January, 1909

Dear Mother,

I have your letter of the 2nd, which came up with the tote team on Saturday along with the mail for the other forty-one men here, and I was glad to have it, being three weeks since your last. I will try to set down the news of the camp since I wrote in December, for you asked for particulars and Father always says a letter with no particulars in it is not worth the stamp.

We are forty-two men and two cooks at Camp Six this winter, up two from last year on account of the company's having taken the Halloway tract as well as the old ground along the Fork. The foreman is a Norwegian named Ole Halvorsen, called Big Ole by everybody though he is no bigger than most, and he runs a tight camp. Breakfast is at half past four by lamplight, in the woods by daylight, dinner brought out to the cutting by the cookee at noon, and we work till the light fails, which this time of year is not much past four. Supper is at six and the bunkhouse quiet by nine, Big Ole himself putting out the lamp.

The pay here is $26.00 the month and board for a common chopper, which is what I am rated, and $34.00 for a sawyer, which I hope to be rated by spring if my hand holds out. The teamsters get $30.00, on account of the horses being worth more care than a man, so the joke runs here, though nobody says it to a teamster's face. We are paid at the end of each month, less what we draw against it at the camp store, which they call the wanigan. I have drawn $3.40 since November for tobacco, two pairs of wool socks at 35 cents the pair, and a new axe handle at 20 cents, my own having split on the 8th of January when it came down wrong on a frozen knot. My wages after the wanigan account for December came to $22.60, which I am sending you $18.00 of by the same tote team that brings this, keeping $4.60 against need.

The cook is a Frenchman they call Skinny Purdue, though his real name is something I cannot spell, and he feeds us better than the name of the camp would lead you to expect. We had beans and salt pork most days, but on Christmas he put out roast goose, six of them, and a currant duff besides, and every man at the table said it was worth the whole fall's work just for that one dinner. He bakes 90 loaves of bread a week by his own count, and goes through four barrels of pork and near two hundred pounds of beans in a month for the crew.

We lost time the week of the 4th of January to a cold snap that ran the thermometer down to eleven below of a morning, and Big Ole kept the teams in rather than risk their lungs on the haul road, though the chopping crews went out anyway, it being felt that a man can keep himself warmer swinging an axe than a horse can hauling a load. We made it up the following week, cutting on Sunday the 17th, which the company does not usually ask of us, but the river drive depends on getting the winter's cut down to the rollway before the ice breaks, and Big Ole said any man who did not care to work Sunday could draw his time and walk to the settlement, twenty-two miles, in this weather. Every man stayed.

There was an accident on the 11th, a young fellow named Perley Rell, new this season from down toward the county seat, caught his foot under a log as it rolled off the skidway and broke two bones in it, the doctor from the settlement says, when he came up on the 13th, two days late on account of the roads. Rell is laid up in the bunkhouse and the company is paying him half wages, $13.00 the month, until he can walk, which the doctor thinks will be six weeks or better. The men took up a collection besides, $9.40 in all, which I put in fifteen cents of myself, being all I had to spare after the wanigan.

We have cut, by the scaler's tally through the 15th of January, some 2,600,000 feet, board measure, off the two tracts together, against a season's contract of 4,000,000 feet, which Big Ole says we are well ahead of schedule to make before the roads go soft, likely the last week of March in an ordinary year. The scaler, a company man who comes through every ten days or two weeks, is one Cyrus Deems, and there was some grumbling among the sawyers in December that his tally ran short of what the mill later credited, but Big Ole looked into it and says the discrepancy was in how the crooked logs were being scaled, not in any wrongdoing, and the matter is settled.

I will close now, Mother, as the lamp is going and paper is dear up here, being freighted in same as everything else. Tell Father the axe he gave me held up until the frozen knot beat it, which is no fault of the axe. Tell my sister I have not forgotten her birthday is the 2nd of February and will send something by the next tote team if the store here has anything fit to send, which is doubtful, the wanigan running mostly to tobacco, socks, and patent medicine for men's backs. We expect to break camp and start the drive down the Fork about the middle of April, and I should be home by the first week of May unless the company asks men to stay on for the log drive itself, which pays extra, $1.50 the day over camp wages, and which I am inclined to do if my hand is sound, the money being good and home being no less home for a few weeks' delay.

Your loving son,
Garrett

P.S. Big Ole says to tell any man in the settlement who wants a place next winter that the company is hiring for Camp Seven, to be opened on new ground further up the Fork, and will pay a dollar a day more than Camp Six on account of the longer haul to timber.
