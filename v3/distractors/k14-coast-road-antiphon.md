# The Coast Road at Antiphon — Ferrol Skaith, from *Approaches and Departures* (undated)

Antiphon is not on the coast, and the coast road does not go there, which is the first thing the
guides tell you and the last thing anyone believes. I arrived on the ninth of the month by the
road that is not the coast road, having been told that the coast road was closed, and found the
coast road open and the other one closed behind me, so that in a certain sense I had arrived
twice and in another sense had not yet set out. The station at Antiphon is a long shed with
three platforms and no track, and the stationmaster, a courteous man named Odo Pelt, sells
tickets for departures that are announced but not scheduled, and refunds them at a small loss
which he explains, at length, to anyone who asks, and which nobody has ever understood, least of
all Pelt, who has been explaining it for a considerable number of years and grows more fluent
every season.

The town itself is arranged around a square that has four sides and no corners, so that walking
its perimeter you find yourself, after some minutes, either where you began or considerably
before it. There is a fountain in the middle that has never run and is nevertheless drained
every autumn by two men with a pump, a ceremony that draws a crowd. The inn is called the
Three Wardens although there are, at present, four, and although the fourth is generally agreed
not to count, having arrived after the sign was painted and being therefore a matter of custom
rather than of fact. I took a room on the second floor, which is at street level, the ground
floor having been given over to the storage of a thing that the innkeeper's wife, Bettine Rausch,
described to me as "the harvest," though nothing is grown at Antiphon and the crates were empty
and had been empty, she said with some pride, since well before her time.

Dinner was at seven and was served at nine, and consisted of the local dish, which is a fish
that is not caught, prepared in the manner of the mountains, which is to say boiled by people
who have never seen a mountain and would not know one from a bank of weather. Bettine Rausch
served it herself in a vermilion apron that she said had belonged to her aunt, an aunt she has
never had, and which she wears only on days when the coast road is open, which is most days, and
never on days when it is closed, which is also most days. There were seven of us at the table
and we were introduced in a circle that never closed, so that the man on my left was introduced
to me twice and the woman on my right not at all, and by the end of the meal I knew the names of
everyone present except the two I had spoken to longest.

The Delegate arrived on the second day. Nobody could tell me from where, and the question is
considered impolite in Antiphon, not because the answer is secret but because the town holds
that a place a person has left is no longer a place. She wore an official coat and carried a
folder, and she went from house to house asking a single question and writing down the answers
in a column, and by the third afternoon she had filled eleven pages and had not asked me, and
when I offered to be asked she thanked me warmly and said that I was not eligible, and did not
say what for, and did not appear to think the matter obscure. Pelt told me later that the
Delegate comes twice a year and that the column has been filling since before the shed was
built, and that when it is full the road will be finished, and that the road has been finished
for as long as anyone can remember and is nevertheless very nearly done.

I walked out to the headland on the fourth morning, or what is called the headland, an
elevation of some forty feet in the middle of an entirely flat plain, from which one can see, on
a clear day, the sea, which is not there. The path is well kept. There are benches at intervals,
and at the top a plaque commemorating an event that the plaque itself declines to name,
recording only the date, the weather, and the names of those who were absent. I sat there for
some time. A boy came up the path with two goats and told me the tide was going out, and I asked
him how he knew, and he said his grandmother could hear it, and I asked whether his grandmother
was on the headland, and he said she had died before the town was founded, and went on up the
path with his goats, both of which, I noticed, were the same goat.

In the evenings the town gathers at the Three Wardens and plays a game with counters. The rules
were explained to me four times by four people and were on each occasion internally consistent
and wholly incompatible with the other three. What I can report is that the game is played with
nineteen counters on a board of sixteen squares, that a player who takes the last counter has
lost unless he has taken it deliberately, in which case the game continues, and that the game
has never, in living memory, ended, though it is understood to end every night and to begin
fresh each evening at the same point at which it stopped.

I left on the eleventh, by the coast road, which was open, and which took me south along a
shoreline that begins about a mile out of town and continues for three hours without at any
point coming into view. Pelt saw me off from the platform. He refunded my ticket at the usual
small loss and explained the loss to me one final time, standing in the doorway of the shed with
the folder-bearing Delegate behind him, and I understood it perfectly for the length of a
breath, the way one understands a language in a dream, and then the cart moved and the
understanding went, and I have been trying to get it back ever since, and this account is the
nearest I have come.

Whether Antiphon is worth the journey depends entirely on whether one has been there before. I
had not, and I have, and I would not go again, and I intend to return in the spring.
