# Answer sheet — Part 1

## Section A — Reconstruction (stories 1–4)

*Note on method used throughout Section A: where sources conflict I have preferred (1) a
contemporaneous quoted document over a memory, (2) arithmetic that closes over arithmetic that
does not, (3) a first-person witness over a repeater, and (4) independent agreement over numerous
agreement. Corrected values only are used below.*

---

### Story 1 — A creamery of their own, and the rule that made the test the master (1889–1938)

**Who.** The farmers of the Larrow Valley in Ordell County; Gideon Vose, their first president;
and the four officers who ran the Association through the whole of what followed — Effie Loomis,
secretary 1901–1940; Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945; Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow
Green from 1893; and Selby Vose, Gideon's **son**, manager 1913–1938. Bertram Kyle sat at the
outer desk copying ledgers for about twenty-five years to the 1938 office move.

**What happened.** In 1889 the farmers met at Larrow Green — in the schoolhouse, in cold weather,
and somebody's horse got loose — tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking whatever the
merchant allowed, and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose
was elected president and held it until 1911, twenty-two years. He wrote the by-laws himself at
his own kitchen table; he was not a lawyer and they read as a farmer wrote them. In 1891 the
creamery was built at Larrow Green on the creek, and the valley was paid cash once a month, which
had never happened to it before. Before that there was no common test and no common price and
every man's arithmetic was his own.

Article VII is the hinge of everything that follows: *every patron shall be paid for the milk he
delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and
not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall
govern.* The first half is a just rule. The second half — the clause nobody quotes — makes the
station's own figure final: not the creamery's test, not the valley average, not a re-test
anywhere. Under payment by weight, nothing in the seven stories that follow would have cost any
man a dollar.

**When.** Organized 1889; creamery 1891; Article VII adopted at an annual meeting whose year
**cannot be determined from the sources** — Rundle says 1891, Loomis 1892, Grigg 1893. The printed
by-laws carry no year, only "adopted at the annual meeting"; the 1889–1892 minute book was carried
out in the office move of 1938 and never arrived; Gideon Vose died in 1917 and could not be asked.
Loomis's confident 1892 is undercut by her own opening section, which says the papers of exactly
those years are the ones the Association lost.

**Documents.** By-laws pamphlet (Historical Rooms Item 1); Loomis's 1941 *Notes toward a history*;
Rundle's 1939 jubilee pamphlet; Falke's 1959 county report ¶3; Kyle's 1953 recollections; Selby
Vose's printed 1926 address, in which he names his father as the by-law's author.

### Story 2 — The station at the head of the valley, and the man at the bench (1895–1946)

**Who.** Ansel Keddie, born 1861, married to Orra Brant in 1889; tester and separator man at the
Ostrey Hollow skimming station. Around him: his wife Orra, keeping a diary from 1897; the patrons,
nine families hauling up the Hollow; Merle Strawn, the state's dairy inspector on the Ordell
circuit from 1907, kin to nobody in the valley; Cleve Oram's father, who drove the cream wagon
down.

**What happened.** The pull down to Larrow Green was seven miles, three hours in mud and
impossible in March, so the Association built a skimming station at the head of the valley. Keddie
went up in 1895 to get it ready and tested there for twenty-nine seasons, 1896 through 1924 — not
a pound in 1925. Milk was weighed in at a covered platform, a sample drawn from each man's can,
the milk separated, the cream sent down daily and the skim handed back the same morning for hogs
and calves. Over the intake room was a loft reached by a ladder nailed to the wall.

His method, set out in a letter he wrote in 1925 and never sent: day began at half past five, lamps
lit and scale swinging free before the first wagon; every can weighed and the weight written before
the milk went anywhere; each patron's sample into his own numbered jar, composited over ten days;
warmed to blood heat and poured from vessel to vessel; the charge drawn to the mark, tip wiped,
blown into the numbered bottle; acid down the side; four bottles in the hand tester at the taught
speed; the fat read off in per cent with dividers. Thirty-one tests a week. Five columns — date,
patron, pounds, test, fat — errors struck through and initialed, never a figure written over a
figure.

The station opened **4 May 1896** on glass borrowed from Larrow Green: Hobart Sill made up an
outfit from his own bench so the season need not be lost, and had it back the spring after with
one piece chipped. That first season the book records an average of **3.80**. From 1898 it records
**3.61** and stays there.

**The nine weeks of 1911.** The station's own measure broke 8 May; a borrowed one was used; the
average stood at 3.80 until the station's own came back on 10 July, when it fell the same day.
Keddie entered both facts and a question mark, and let the question mark stand fourteen years. He
told his family that summer it was something in the glass and would not write what he could not
prove.

**Documents.** The station test book, 4 May 1896 – 31 December 1924 (Item 3); Keddie's statement to
the board (Item 8) and his unsent letter; Strawn's circuit notebooks (scales true, no added water,
"works slow and clean," every visit); Orra Keddie's diary; Oram's description of the building;
Rundle ch. III. The station closed in 1946 and the building held hay tools; the loft was never
cleared.

### Story 3 — Six dozen pipettes from Tarnet (1897–1949)

**Who.** Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet — Marcus Ammon's house, four stories behind the
railroad yard, supplying half the creamery *glass* in the state; and Junia Ammon, the firm's own
historian, who wrote the affair up in 1949.

**What happened.** A milk test pipette must *deliver* a stated quantity of milk, not hold it — a
harder measurement, because milk clings to the wall as it runs out. The mark goes on the stem while
the glass is warm; the piece is annealed overnight and gauged in the morning.

On **14 April 1897** the firm billed the Larrow Valley Co-operative Creamery Association, for the
Ostrey Hollow station: 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20; 12 doz. test bottles,
8 per cent neck, at .22 — 31.68; 1 acid measure — .90; 1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00; freight —
2.15; **total 77.93**. The outfit went into use at the station on **1 May 1897**, and Orra Keddie
wrote in her diary that day that A. was pleased they were done borrowing of Larrow Green.

The lot was short. Measured in 1958 against a calibrated burette, the nine surviving pipettes
delivered a mean of **16.72 c.c.** where they were blown 17.6 — **0.88 c.c. short each, one part in
twenty** — and short consistently, one to another. Junia Ammon's account of how: the mold had been
reground and the lot ran short, and nobody in the works caught it, because a boy checks the first
of a lot and the tenth and the fiftieth and finds them consistent — they *are* consistent; they
are consistently wrong, which is the one fault such a check cannot find. The works books that
would have settled it burned in the fire of 1931; only the invoices, letter books and day books
survive.

In 1923, condemned, the Association ordered replacements on 28 June. The firm's answer of 2 July
entered the order, promised shipment that month, regretted the trouble, and said its books of the
nineties would tell nothing about the lot named. Junia Ammon prints that letter beside the invoice
and calls it a thin thing to send a customer of twenty-six years' standing.

**Quantities.** 72 pipettes; blown 17.6 c.c.; delivered 16.72; short 0.88 each; $77.93 for the
whole outfit — against the $19,000 of unpaid butterfat the outfit went on to produce.

**Documents.** The invoice (Item 2, reproduced in facsimile in Ammon ch. IX); the firm's letter of
2 July 1923 (Item 10); the crate and its nine pipettes (Item 5); Ivy Keddie's measurement sheet of
9 March 1958 (Item 14). The *Ashlin Chronicle* of 3 October 1958 charged the house with knowingly
putting off a cheap short lot, calling the 1923 silence the proof of it. That **cannot be
determined**: the works records burned in 1931, the surviving papers are the office side only, and
an inference from silence is not evidence. The same article misprints the invoice total as
$77.39 — as, against her own reproduced invoice, does Junia Ammon in her prose.

### Story 4 — The grievance, and the three wrong answers (1902–1930)

**Who.** The patrons of the Ostrey Hollow station, Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom foremost; Nyle
Grigg of Larrow Green, chairman of the 1908 committee and no patron of that station; Dr. Roland
Kepp, veterinary surgeon of Ashlin; the State Agricultural College at Farland; and, over Redlow
Ridge, Jerome Cudd of the Alder Corners Cheese Company in Vessey County.

**What happened.** From 1902 the Hollow men said aloud that their milk tested under the same class
of milk down the valley. The recorded gap was 0.17 of a point against Larrow Green's own recorded
3.78; measured against the truth it was **0.19**. What nobody would take account of — though Roy
Brant says it was in front of them the whole time — is that it was the same 0.19 in July as in
February, on grass and on hay, in wet years and dry. A feed effect comes and goes with the season.
This did not.

In January 1908 the Association named a committee under Grigg. Its members sampled the Hessel
Bottom herds, drew and sealed their own samples, tried the station scales against a known weight
and the patrons' scales against the station's, and reported: the milk is whole, the scales are
honest, the wet hay of that bottom thins the cream, and the remedy lies with the land and not with
the station. It also reported, in the same type, that it found no dishonesty of any kind in any
quarter. The report was accepted and closed the question for eleven years.

Two things undo it. First, the report itself says the sampling was of **January, February and
March 1908 only** — a winter-only sample, which cannot speak to a gap that stands in July; Grigg's
1919 covering letter claims they sampled every month of the year, and the report he reprints
beneath his own letter contradicts him. Second, the College's feeding trial, **Bulletin No. 214**
(1911), which Grigg in 1919 and Kepp in 1926 both cite as vindication, actually found that bog hay
fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the test "by not more than five hundredths of one per
cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Both men quote it as
*two tenths in the winter feeding*. The true finding is four times too small and the wrong shape.

Kepp's 1926 paper built the case out to soil chemistry — sour ground, no lime, no phosphates,
sedge hay, cows thin over the loin — and put the county's wet-bottom penalty at a fifth of a point.
Asked from the floor whether the trouble might be in the station, he said he had never read a test
in his life and would not know how.

**The control nobody in Ordell asked for.** From 1915, four of the six Hessel Bottom farms hauled
over Redlow Ridge in the summer flush to Jerome Cudd's factory — sixteen seasons, ending 1930 (the
ridge road shuts in November and does not open before April, so no man hauled there in winter).
Cudd's book gives the four men together at 3.78 to 3.82 every season, about **3.80**: the same wet
ground, the same cows, a fifth of a point better than their own station said.

**Documents.** Item 11, the 1908 report; Grigg's 1919 covering letter; Item 12, Bulletin No. 214;
Kepp's paper with the floor discussion; Rosalie Cudd's 1958 letter and her sixteen-season table;
Item 13, the Vessey County correspondence file.
