## Section A — Reconstruction (stories 1–4)

*Note on method used throughout Section A: where sources conflict I have preferred (1) a
contemporaneous quoted document over a memory, (2) arithmetic that closes over arithmetic that
does not, (3) a first-person witness over a repeater, and (4) independent agreement over numerous
agreement. Corrected values only are used below.*

---

### Story 1 — A creamery of their own, and the rule that made the test the master (1889–1938)

**Who.** The farmers of the Larrow Valley in Ordell County; Gideon Vose, their first president;
and the four officers who ran the Association through the whole of what followed — Effie Loomis,
secretary 1901–1940; Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945; Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow
Green from 1893; and Selby Vose, Gideon's **son**, manager 1913–1938. Bertram Kyle sat at the
outer desk copying ledgers for about twenty-five years to the 1938 office move.

**What happened.** In 1889 the farmers met at Larrow Green — in the schoolhouse, in cold weather,
and somebody's horse got loose — tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking whatever the
merchant allowed, and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose
was elected president and held it until 1911, twenty-two years. He wrote the by-laws himself at
his own kitchen table; he was not a lawyer and they read as a farmer wrote them. In 1891 the
creamery was built at Larrow Green on the creek, and the valley was paid cash once a month, which
had never happened to it before. Before that there was no common test and no common price and
every man's arithmetic was his own.

Article VII is the hinge of everything that follows: *every patron shall be paid for the milk he
delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and
not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall
govern.* The first half is a just rule. The second half — the clause nobody quotes — makes the
station's own figure final: not the creamery's test, not the valley average, not a re-test
anywhere. Under payment by weight, nothing in the seven stories that follow would have cost any
man a dollar.

**When.** Organized 1889; creamery 1891; Article VII adopted at an annual meeting whose year
**cannot be determined from the sources** — Rundle says 1891, Loomis 1892, Grigg 1893. The printed
by-laws carry no year, only "adopted at the annual meeting"; the 1889–1892 minute book was carried
out in the office move of 1938 and never arrived; Gideon Vose died in 1917 and could not be asked.
Loomis's confident 1892 is undercut by her own opening section, which says the papers of exactly
those years are the ones the Association lost.

**Documents.** By-laws pamphlet (Historical Rooms Item 1); Loomis's 1941 *Notes toward a history*;
Rundle's 1939 jubilee pamphlet; Falke's 1959 county report ¶3; Kyle's 1953 recollections; Selby
Vose's printed 1926 address, in which he names his father as the by-law's author.

### Story 2 — The station at the head of the valley, and the man at the bench (1895–1946)

**Who.** Ansel Keddie, born 1861, married to Orra Brant in 1889; tester and separator man at the
Ostrey Hollow skimming station. Around him: his wife Orra, keeping a diary from 1897; the patrons,
nine families hauling up the Hollow; Merle Strawn, the state's dairy inspector on the Ordell
circuit from 1907, kin to nobody in the valley; Cleve Oram's father, who drove the cream wagon
down.

**What happened.** The pull down to Larrow Green was seven miles, three hours in mud and
impossible in March, so the Association built a skimming station at the head of the valley. Keddie
went up in 1895 to get it ready and tested there for twenty-nine seasons, 1896 through 1924 — not
a pound in 1925. Milk was weighed in at a covered platform, a sample drawn from each man's can,
the milk separated, the cream sent down daily and the skim handed back the same morning for hogs
and calves. Over the intake room was a loft reached by a ladder nailed to the wall.

His method, set out in a letter he wrote in 1925 and never sent: day began at half past five, lamps
lit and scale swinging free before the first wagon; every can weighed and the weight written before
the milk went anywhere; each patron's sample into his own numbered jar, composited over ten days;
warmed to blood heat and poured from vessel to vessel; the charge drawn to the mark, tip wiped,
blown into the numbered bottle; acid down the side; four bottles in the hand tester at the taught
speed; the fat read off in per cent with dividers. Thirty-one tests a week. Five columns — date,
patron, pounds, test, fat — errors struck through and initialed, never a figure written over a
figure.

The station opened **4 May 1896** on glass borrowed from Larrow Green: Hobart Sill made up an
outfit from his own bench so the season need not be lost, and had it back the spring after with
one piece chipped. That first season the book records an average of **3.80**. From 1898 it records
**3.61** and stays there.

**The nine weeks of 1911.** The station's own measure broke 8 May; a borrowed one was used; the
average stood at 3.80 until the station's own came back on 10 July, when it fell the same day.
Keddie entered both facts and a question mark, and let the question mark stand fourteen years. He
told his family that summer it was something in the glass and would not write what he could not
prove.

**Documents.** The station test book, 4 May 1896 – 31 December 1924 (Item 3); Keddie's statement to
the board (Item 8) and his unsent letter; Strawn's circuit notebooks (scales true, no added water,
"works slow and clean," every visit); Orra Keddie's diary; Oram's description of the building;
Rundle ch. III. The station closed in 1946 and the building held hay tools; the loft was never
cleared.

### Story 3 — Six dozen pipettes from Tarnet (1897–1949)

**Who.** Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet — Marcus Ammon's house, four stories behind the
railroad yard, supplying half the creamery *glass* in the state; and Junia Ammon, the firm's own
historian, who wrote the affair up in 1949.

**What happened.** A milk test pipette must *deliver* a stated quantity of milk, not hold it — a
harder measurement, because milk clings to the wall as it runs out. The mark goes on the stem while
the glass is warm; the piece is annealed overnight and gauged in the morning.

On **14 April 1897** the firm billed the Larrow Valley Co-operative Creamery Association, for the
Ostrey Hollow station: 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20; 12 doz. test bottles,
8 per cent neck, at .22 — 31.68; 1 acid measure — .90; 1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00; freight —
2.15; **total 77.93**. The outfit went into use at the station on **1 May 1897**, and Orra Keddie
wrote in her diary that day that A. was pleased they were done borrowing of Larrow Green.

The lot was short. Measured in 1958 against a calibrated burette, the nine surviving pipettes
delivered a mean of **16.72 c.c.** where they were blown 17.6 — **0.88 c.c. short each, one part in
twenty** — and short consistently, one to another. Junia Ammon's account of how: the mold had been
reground and the lot ran short, and nobody in the works caught it, because a boy checks the first
of a lot and the tenth and the fiftieth and finds them consistent — they *are* consistent; they
are consistently wrong, which is the one fault such a check cannot find. The works books that
would have settled it burned in the fire of 1931; only the invoices, letter books and day books
survive.

In 1923, condemned, the Association ordered replacements on 28 June. The firm's answer of 2 July
entered the order, promised shipment that month, regretted the trouble, and said its books of the
nineties would tell nothing about the lot named. Junia Ammon prints that letter beside the invoice
and calls it a thin thing to send a customer of twenty-six years' standing.

**Quantities.** 72 pipettes; blown 17.6 c.c.; delivered 16.72; short 0.88 each; $77.93 for the
whole outfit — against the $19,000 of unpaid butterfat the outfit went on to produce.

**Documents.** The invoice (Item 2, reproduced in facsimile in Ammon ch. IX); the firm's letter of
2 July 1923 (Item 10); the crate and its nine pipettes (Item 5); Ivy Keddie's measurement sheet of
9 March 1958 (Item 14). The *Ashlin Chronicle* of 3 October 1958 charged the house with knowingly
putting off a cheap short lot, calling the 1923 silence the proof of it. That **cannot be
determined**: the works records burned in 1931, the surviving papers are the office side only, and
an inference from silence is not evidence. The same article misprints the invoice total as
$77.39 — as, against her own reproduced invoice, does Junia Ammon in her prose.

### Story 4 — The grievance, and the three wrong answers (1902–1930)

**Who.** The patrons of the Ostrey Hollow station, Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom foremost; Nyle
Grigg of Larrow Green, chairman of the 1908 committee and no patron of that station; Dr. Roland
Kepp, veterinary surgeon of Ashlin; the State Agricultural College at Farland; and, over Redlow
Ridge, Jerome Cudd of the Alder Corners Cheese Company in Vessey County.

**What happened.** From 1902 the Hollow men said aloud that their milk tested under the same class
of milk down the valley. The recorded gap was 0.17 of a point against Larrow Green's own recorded
3.78; measured against the truth it was **0.19**. What nobody would take account of — though Roy
Brant says it was in front of them the whole time — is that it was the same 0.19 in July as in
February, on grass and on hay, in wet years and dry. A feed effect comes and goes with the season.
This did not.

In January 1908 the Association named a committee under Grigg. Its members sampled the Hessel
Bottom herds, drew and sealed their own samples, tried the station scales against a known weight
and the patrons' scales against the station's, and reported: the milk is whole, the scales are
honest, the wet hay of that bottom thins the cream, and the remedy lies with the land and not with
the station. It also reported, in the same type, that it found no dishonesty of any kind in any
quarter. The report was accepted and closed the question for eleven years.

Two things undo it. First, the report itself says the sampling was of **January, February and
March 1908 only** — a winter-only sample, which cannot speak to a gap that stands in July; Grigg's
1919 covering letter claims they sampled every month of the year, and the report he reprints
beneath his own letter contradicts him. Second, the College's feeding trial, **Bulletin No. 214**
(1911), which Grigg in 1919 and Kepp in 1926 both cite as vindication, actually found that bog hay
fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the test "by not more than five hundredths of one per
cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Both men quote it as
*two tenths in the winter feeding*. The true finding is four times too small and the wrong shape.

Kepp's 1926 paper built the case out to soil chemistry — sour ground, no lime, no phosphates,
sedge hay, cows thin over the loin — and put the county's wet-bottom penalty at a fifth of a point.
Asked from the floor whether the trouble might be in the station, he said he had never read a test
in his life and would not know how.

**The control nobody in Ordell asked for.** From 1915, four of the six Hessel Bottom farms hauled
over Redlow Ridge in the summer flush to Jerome Cudd's factory — sixteen seasons, ending 1930 (the
ridge road shuts in November and does not open before April, so no man hauled there in winter).
Cudd's book gives the four men together at 3.78 to 3.82 every season, about **3.80**: the same wet
ground, the same cows, a fifth of a point better than their own station said.

**Documents.** Item 11, the 1908 report; Grigg's 1919 covering letter; Item 12, Bulletin No. 214;
Kepp's paper with the floor discussion; Rosalie Cudd's 1958 letter and her sixteen-season table;
Item 13, the Vessey County correspondence file.

### Story 5 — The station gain: the answer written in ink, every year, in plain sight (1897–1938)

**Who.** Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper; Hobart Sill, buttermaker, who weighed the cream at the
receiving end; Selby Vose, manager, who signed the annual sheet; Bertram Kyle, the clerk who
copied the line; Dell Rundle, the schoolteacher who read the ledgers by accident in 1939 and had
to retract a chapter.

**What happened.** Because the station bought milk on its own test and then shipped the fat down
the valley as cream, the Association's books recorded the same butterfat twice — once as bought at
the Hollow, once as received at Larrow Green. The two never agreed. The second was always larger.
The difference was entered as "station gain" and carried to the general account. The typical
entry, in the bookkeeper's hand:

> *Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received
> at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per
> cent, as usual. Carried to the general account.*

That is 1898, and there is one like it for every year after. 57,760 lb is 3.61 per cent of
1,600,000 lb of milk; 60,800 lb is 3.80 per cent of the same milk. The whole answer to the
valley's quarrel stands in three figures and a sentence, and it stood there for a quarter of a
century, was carried forward annually, and was read out at the annual meeting to fifty farmers
with their hats on their knees. As Kyle puts it, it was not a secret; it was worse than a secret —
it was in plain sight with a name on it. A figure with a name has been explained; you rule under
it and go on.

Three men could have stopped it and did not. **Teague** wrote the line and admitted what evenness
does to a bookkeeper: a figure that jumps keeps you awake, a figure that comes in at five and a
quarter every year is furniture. He put something to Vose in 1916, was told to let it alone, and
let it alone — "I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience." (His claim that the
line was his own device, begun when he came in 1905, is defeated by his own quotation of the 1898
entry and by the ledgers, whose station column runs from 1897.) **Sill** independently reweighed
every can of Hollow cream into the cold room for forty-one years and said in that room a hundred
times that it ran heavy, and nobody ever asked him to make anything of it; he put it down to
cream being cream. **Vose** signed the sheet every spring — but by Kyle's account he read totals
and never the lines above them: thumb on the bottom figure, and sign.

**Quantities.** 3,040 lb of gain a year; 5.25 per cent of the fat credited, which is 5.00 per cent
of the fat delivered; 76,000 lb accumulated over 1898–1922; $19,000 at the period's 25¢ a pound.

**Documents.** The annual ledgers, station account (Item 6); the Larrow Green receiving and churn
books (Item 16); Teague's 1948 memoir ch. IX; Kyle's 1953 recollections; Rundle's "Note added
while in press," in which, having read the ledgers, he retracts his own chapter: cream stolen at
the Hollow would have reached the Green light, and it reached the Green heavy.

### Story 6 — The circular of 1919, and the brother-in-law (1919–1934)

**Who.** Selby Vose, who wrote it; Bertram Kyle, who typed it; Ansel Keddie, who carried it;
Emmet Brant, born 1854, died 1929, the station's largest patron, who received it; Orra Keddie,
Ansel's wife and Emmet's sister, who stood between them; Merle Strawn, who had already told the
manager there was no water.

**What happened.** In June 1919 a printed circular went out over the manager's signature to the
patrons of the Ostrey Hollow station, warning them in civil words that added water would be looked
for and dealt with. It named nobody. It was read as naming everybody. Vose defended it to the end
of his career: a manager who watches his figures go one way for twenty years and says nothing is
being idle at his patrons' expense.

Two facts sit hard against it. Strawn had tested with the lactometer at that station on every
visit and had never once found added water, and says in his 1919 entry that he told the manager
so. And the letter reached families who kept it: one read it at the table with his hat still on
and put it in a drawer where it stayed until he died; one woman pinned hers inside the lid of her
trunk; one put hers in the stove. Forty years later they still had the paper to prove what had
been said of them.

The family cost was heavier than the money. Ansel Keddie would not post the circulars nor leave
them on the platform. He took them round himself, stood at each door while the man read, and came
home at eleven at night with his hat still on. Two days later Emmet Brant came up the yard, would
not come into the house, said he had hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and
that Ansel knew it better than any man living, and asked why he had carried the paper himself if
he did not believe it. Ansel said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it.
Emmet went home and never entered that house again.

They were brothers-in-law: Orra was Emmet's sister, and the man who kept the test book had married
into the family with more to lose by that station than any other on the meadow. They did not speak
for ten years, within sight of each other's chimney smoke, until Emmet died in the spring of 1929
and Orra walked down and sat an hour in the kitchen where she had been a girl. Roy Brant is
explicit that the quarrel was not over the water charge as such: his father held that the man who
kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not.

**Quantities.** Nine families of record (Orra's contemporaneous diary says eleven — see Section E);
six farms on Hessel Bottom, 160 acres of wet meadow, Emmet holding sixty-odd of them and milking
thirty-eight cows in a good year; ten years of silence; Ansel died 1934, Orra in 1948.

**Documents.** The circular itself (typed by Kyle, signed by Vose; no copy is transcribed in the
sources); Orra Keddie's diary for June 1919 and Spring 1929; Strawn's 1919 circuit entry; Roy
Brant's 1955 account; Vira Toland's recollection; Vose's 1926 address defending it; Duncan
Keddie's family notes.

### Story 7 — The glass condemned, and the man dismissed (1923–1926)

**Who.** Merle Strawn; Ansel Keddie; the Ostrey Hollow patrons; Alonzo Frick, attorney of Ashlin,
chairman of the board of arbitration; Wilbur Teague and Selby Vose, who each put a theory to it.

**What happened.** The state began issuing graduated burettes to its field men in 1921, for
checking the delivery of plant measures. Strawn carried his two seasons before he used it at the
Hollow — in 1922 the day was short and the road home was what it was, and he wrote that down
because it is what happened. On **12 June 1923** he ran the station's milk pipettes against the
burette, found they would not deliver their mark, condemned the whole crate on the spot and told
the manager to order new. The Association ordered on 28 June; the new glass was in use from July;
Strawn checked it in October and it delivered its mark. The condemned crate went up the ladder
into the loft. In 1924 the station's book gave the year at **3.80** — a rise of exactly the 0.19
that had been missing — and Strawn recorded it. Nobody connected the two things, and Strawn, who
kept a book and not an estimation, drew no inference about the twenty-six years behind him.

In January 1925 the patrons petitioned. A board of three sat under Frick from **17 March to 4 May
1925**: the first session at the Association's office at Ashlin, the second and third at Frick's
own request in the station's weigh room, so that the board might see the plant instead of hearing
it described. Three explanations were argued — water (Vose), cream taken by the tester (Teague,
put obliquely, in the way an accountant says a thing without saying it), and the wet hay (the 1908
report read into the record). Each was urged by a man of standing; each would have been believed
by half the valley; not one was proved. Four books came before the board and nothing else: the
station test book, the station receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book, and Strawn's
circuit notebook for 1923 — which recorded the condemnation. Kyle carried them in and could not
say that any man laid the second beside the fourth and looked. The condemned crate itself was
never mentioned in any of the three sessions, and it lay in the loft twenty feet above two of them.

Keddie made a nine-sentence statement on 17 March: he had kept every figure in his own hand,
tested as he was taught and as the state's man had watched him, thirty-one times a week for
twenty-nine seasons, could not tell them why the figures were what they were, and had never taken
a pound of cream out of that station.

On **30 April** the Association dismissed him — twenty-nine seasons and a letter. Four days later,
on **4 May 1925**, the board found no cause established and awarded the patrons **$3,800**, without
admission, charged to the station account. There was never any finding against him. Frick, who
believed him, could not think well of the Association for it: an honest man ruined by a figure
nobody could explain, and his neighbors paid money nobody could account for.

**Documents.** Strawn's 1923 notebook and its photostat (Item 9); Ammon's letter of 2 July (Item
10); the board's minute book (Item 7) with the award; Keddie's statement (Item 8); Frick's own
exhibit list; Loomis's §6 and §8; Kyle's list; Orra's diary for 17 March, April and 30 April 1925;
Vose's printed 1926 address, still defending the water theory.

### Story 8 — A ladder, a burette, and thirty-three years too late (1958–1972)

**Who.** Ivy Keddie, born 1931, Ansel's granddaughter and Duncan Keddie's daughter, twenty-seven,
a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland; Professor Leland Vaught; Rosalie
Cudd; Roy and Hazel Brant; Orin Falke, the county agent; Halbert Nym, the curator.

**What happened.** Five sets of records bore on the question and no two had ever been in one room:
the station test book (in the Association's office since 1925), the Brant weigh book (lent by Roy
Brant in 1958 — *lent*, and both he and Hazel insist on the word), the Alder Corners figures
(copied out by Rosalie Cudd, the book itself never leaving Vessey County), Strawn's circuit
notebooks (in the Farland library since 1947, unasked for in eleven years), and the Association's
own ledgers. Ivy Keddie assembled them.

In **February 1958** she went up the Hollow, climbed the ladder into the loft over the intake
room, and found under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff the crate stenciled AMMON & SONS DAIRY
SUPPLY TARNET, put there in June 1923. Inside, in straw, were the condemned pipettes; nine were
unbroken. On **9 March 1958** she delivered each of the nine five times against a calibrated
burette at Farland: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 — mean **16.72
c.c.** against a blown 17.6, short 0.88, ninety-five hundredths of the mark.

Everything follows as arithmetic. 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80. Forty million pounds of milk over the
twenty-five full delivery years 1898–1922: 1,444,000 lb of fat credited, 1,520,000 lb delivered,
**76,000 lb** never paid for, worth **$19,000** at 25¢ — of which the patrons had received $3,800,
a fifth. The error was proportional, not additive, so it fell hardest on the best milk and left the
herds correctly ranked against each other, which is why nothing inside the plant ever looked
wrong.

**Bulletin No. 471** was issued in October 1958 over Vaught's name and hers: *No milk was watered
at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there… the station's tests were low by one part in twenty
because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand
pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station
gain.* The glass was condemned in 1923 and the cause found in 1958: thirty-five years. Keddie was
dismissed in 1925: thirty-three. He had been dead twenty-four years; Orra ten.

The record then dispersed and was corrected. The *Ashlin Chronicle* ran it in a day and a half and
got the mean, the count of pipettes, the invoice total and Ivy's own kinship wrong. Falke's county
report for the supervisors called her the great-niece and conceded the hay as a partial factor,
then withdrew both in a note added at the end. Duncan Keddie wrote out for his family in 1968 who
everyone was and where every book went. By 1972 the test book, the weigh book and the crate were
at the Ordell County Historical Rooms; Strawn's notebooks at Farland; the Alder Corners book at
the Vessey County Historical Society — five books in three buildings, and none of them the
family's.

**Documents.** Item 14, the measurement sheet; Item 15, Bulletin No. 471; Item 5, the crate; Ivy's
1959 talk to the Historical Society; Vaught's introduction and method; the *Chronicle* of 3
October 1958; Falke's 1959 report and its added note; Duncan Keddie's 1968 notes; Nym's 1972
accession notes; Hazel Brant's 1981 tape.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.**
Derivation: Ansel Keddie (b. 1861) married Orra Brant in 1889; their son Duncan was born in 1894
(confirmed by Orra's diary — "Duncan five years old" in September 1899, and "seventeen" when he
went to teach at Ashlin in October 1911); Duncan's daughter Ivy was born in 1931. Ivy states it
herself in her 1959 talk ("Ansel Keddie… was my grandfather. My father, Duncan Keddie… was his
son"), and Duncan states it in his family notes, adding that he writes it plainly because he has
twice seen it printed otherwise. Two sources say great-niece: the *Ashlin Chronicle* of 3 October
1958, and paragraph 2 of Falke's county report. They are not independent — both descend from the
same October 1958 college publicity — and Falke retracts his in a note appended to his own report
("I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece;
the error on page two is mine"). First-person and household testimony, plus a narrator's own
published correction, outrank a weekly paper assembled in a day and a half.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins.**
Derivation: Orra Keddie and Emmet Brant were sister and brother; their children — Duncan Keddie
and Roy Brant — are therefore first cousins, the children of a brother and a sister; the children
of first cousins are second cousins, and Ivy is Duncan's daughter while Hazel is Roy's. Duncan,
Roy and Hazel all state it independently and all three explicitly reject the alternatives; the
1959 footnote to Rundle's pamphlet agrees. Against it, Vira Toland and Cleve Oram both say "first
cousin once removed" — village shorthand for "related somehow," and Oram concedes he could not
draw the line on paper. What the sources leave open about the intervening generations: (i) whether
Orra and Emmet were **full** or **half** siblings — Roy and Duncan say full, and Roy names and
rejects the stepmother story, while Hazel says Orra was a half sister by Emmet's father's second
wife; if Hazel is right they are half second cousins, and Hazel is a generation further off but
was told this in the family. (ii) Neither woman's mother is named anywhere in the sources — Duncan's
wife and Roy's wife are absent entirely — so a second, maternal connection is neither established
nor excluded. (iii) Nothing in Orra's own hand survives from before her 1889 marriage; the diary's
earlier pages were not preserved, so her birth family cannot be checked against a document.

**B3. Selby Vose was Gideon Vose's son.**
Selby says so himself in his 1926 address — "My father, who was your first president and who wrote
the by-law under which every patron in this Association is paid on the test of the station where
he delivers." Loomis calls him "son of the late president," Kyle says "the son, whatever is said
at Ashlin," and Duncan corrects the record specifically ("Selby was his son and not, as I have
heard it given, his nephew"). Vira Toland and Rundle say nephew; that is valley currency, and
first-person testimony settles it. It matters for four reasons. Gideon wrote Article VII, the
clause making the test of the station where a man delivers govern his pay with no appeal — so the
man who ran the Association through the entire quarrel was the son of the man who had made the
station's own figure final and unreviewable. Second, it gave the office an inherited certainty
that the rule, and therefore the figure, was not the thing to doubt. Third, it made the 1919 water
circular land in the Hollow as a dynastic accusation rather than an administrative notice — Vira
says plainly that in a place that size it was not only the Association speaking. Fourth, Selby
leaned on the descent in his own defence, invoking his father's by-law and his father's Jersey herd
in the same address in which he blamed the patrons.

**B4. No relationship between the Voses and the Keddies is established by the sources.**
Two narrators assert one: Vira Toland says Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins and
that "everybody knew that"; Cleve Oram says the Keddies and the Voses "were cousins of some sort,"
which his mother had from her mother, and adds that he could not draw the line on paper. These are
not two sources but one piece of valley talk twice retold — neither traces to a document, a name,
or a connecting generation. Against it stands Duncan Keddie, Orra's son, who denies it flatly,
says the confusion is with the Ashlin Voses (no relation to Gideon either), reports that two women
in Ashlin have told him the first-cousin version, and observes that his mother could not pass a
headstone without giving you the connection and never once put a Vose in it. Method: hearsay from
outside the household loses to testimony from inside it, especially where the alleged link is the
narrator's own mother. Strictly, a distant cousinship in a small valley cannot be disproved from
these papers; what can be said is that nothing supports one, and that the rumour did real work —
it made the 1919 circular feel to the Hollow like a betrayal by kin.

## Section C — Math

### C1.

**(a) Proportion.** The tests read **0.95 of the truth** — short by exactly **one part in twenty
(5 %)**.

Working. Nine pipettes survived in the condemned crate. Each stem was blown **17.6 c.c.**, the
standard charge in this state and the charge the graduated neck of the test bottle is ruled for.
Ivy Keddie delivered each five times against a calibrated burette (9 March 1958): 16.70, 16.71,
16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Sum = 150.48; 150.48 ÷ 9 = **16.72 c.c.**
16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95** exactly. Shortfall 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c.; 0.88 ÷ 17.6 = 0.05 = 1/20.
Because the neck is ruled for a 17.6 c.c. charge and knows nothing of the substitution, a charge
short by a fixed *fraction* gives a reading short by the same fraction: the error is proportional,
not additive.

(Vaught's Bulletin gives the mean as 16.27 c.c. and the ratio as 0.9244. That is a digit
transposition; see E1 item 1. His own concluding paragraph says "one part in twenty," which is
0.95, not 0.9244.)

**(b) True average and the shortfall in points.** Recorded station average **3.61**; true average
**3.80**; recorded short by **0.19 of a point**.

Working. 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80 exactly (check: 3.80 × 0.95 = 3.61). 3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19. Three
independent confirmations that 3.80 is the true figure: the borrowed measure in 1911 produced
3.80; the new glass produced 3.80 in 1924; and Alder Corners, over the ridge, tested the same
Hessel Bottom milk at about 3.80 for sixteen summers.

**(c) Butterfat not credited.** **76,000 lb** over the twenty-five full delivery years 1898–1922.

Working. Milk received over those years, from the Association's ledgers: 40,000,000 lb (an average
of 1,600,000 lb a year).
Credited at 3.61 %: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**.
Actually delivered at 3.80 %: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lb**.
Difference = 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = **76,000 lb**.
Cross-check from the other side of the books: the ledger's annual station gain of 3,040 lb ×
25 years = 76,000 lb. The two routes meet exactly.

**(d) Money, and the fraction received.** Worth **$19,000**; the patrons received **$3,800**,
which is **one fifth (20 %)**.

Working. 76,000 lb × $0.25 (the average price paid for butterfat over the period, from Teague's
annual sheets) = $19,000. Award $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20.

### C2. Emmet Brant, 1910 (200,000 lb of milk delivered, paid on a test of 3.42)

**(a) Credited:** 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of butterfat.

**(b) Owed and not credited:** true test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = **3.60**; true fat = 200,000 × 0.0360 =
7,200 lb; shortfall = 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb**. (Check by proportion: 6,840 × (1/0.95 − 1) =
6,840 × 0.052631… = 360.) In points, he was short 0.18 — less than the station's average 0.19
because his recorded test was below the station average; the error takes a twentieth of whatever
is there.

**(c) Value that year:** butterfat was 24¢ a pound in 1910 (Roy Brant, who gives that year's price
specifically). 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40**. At the period average of 25¢ it would be $90.00; I use
the year-specific price because the question asks what it was worth *that year*.

### C3. Over the same twenty-five full delivery years (1898–1922)

**(a) Credited to patrons:** 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**.
**(b) Reached Larrow Green in the station's cream:** **1,520,000 lb** (= 40,000,000 × 0.0380; and
independently = 1,444,000 credited + 76,000 accumulated station gain, per the ledgers).
**(c) The difference, 76,000 lb, expressed as a percentage — of what:**
- 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5.00 % of the fat actually delivered / received in the cream**. This is
  the figure that is also the glass (one part in twenty).
- 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.26 %, the ledger's "five and a quarter per cent," of the fat credited**.
  A bookkeeper naturally reckons a surplus against the figure he has already entered.

Both are right of their own denominator and of no other. Two narrators state the denominator
wrongly — Teague ("the gain ran at five and a quarter percent of the fat that was actually in the
cream") and Vaught ("the gain is five and a quarter percent of the butterfat actually delivered").
Five and a quarter is of the *credited* fat. The curator (Item 6) and Ivy Keddie both get it right.

### C4. The invoice

**(a) Total: $77.93.** Working: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93. (The *Chronicle*, and
Junia Ammon's own prose against her own reproduced invoice, say $77.39. Addition settles it.)

**(b)** It bought **six dozen = 72 pipettes** (6 doz. at $0.35 = $25.20, which is 72 × 0.35 — the
line item proves the count). Each was short **0.88 c.c.** (17.6 blown − 16.72 delivered). Vaught
says five dozen and Oram says five dozen; the invoice and its own arithmetic defeat both.

### C5.

**(a) How long the glass was in use vs. how many years the money runs over.**
- In use: **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 — 26 years, 1 month and 11 days** (about 26 years and six
  weeks).
- Money calculation: **25 full delivery years, 1898 to 1922**.

They differ for two reasons. First, 1897 and 1923 are part-years of use — the glass went in on 1
May 1897 and was condemned on 12 June 1923 — so neither is a full delivery year on that glass.
Second, the 40,000,000 lb intake figure was compiled from the Association's ledgers for exactly
the twenty-five complete years over which the patrons laid their claim; the station's own column
does not begin until 1897 and its 1896 season is not in it at all. The consequence worth stating:
because roughly fourteen months of short-glass testing (May–December 1897 and January–June 1923)
fall outside the reckoning, **76,000 lb and $19,000 are a floor, not a ceiling**.

**(b) Milk received in the station's first season: cannot be determined from the sources.**
The curator's note is explicit — the intake for 4 May to the end of 1896 "is not in the ledgers and
is nowhere else; the station column begins with 1897, and inquirers wanting a figure for 1896 must
be told there is none." Kyle explains why: that first season the milk was weighed at the Hollow
and carried on the creamery's own books, and those sheets were never put with the station papers.
Two hearsay estimates survive and disagree — Sill says "about nine hundred thousand pounds," Kyle
says "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds, which is what I understood at the time from men who
were there." Both are memories of talk, neither is a record, and they are 200,000 lb apart. The
true figure is unrecoverable.

## Section D — Logic

### D1. The nine weeks of 1911

**When and how long.** The station's own measure broke on **8 May 1911** and a borrowed one was
used until **10 July 1911** — 63 days, exactly **nine weeks**. (Teague and Kepp both say six weeks;
the two dated entries in the test book settle it, and Kepp's dismissal of "six weeks of June and
July" silently drops the whole of May.)

**The variable.** One object: the milk pipette. Everything else was held constant, and this is why
the interval is worth more than any calculation in the case — the same patrons, the same herds, the
same pasture in the same season, the same acid, the same bottles, the same hand tester, the same
man at the crank turning it at the same speed at the same hour of the same working day.

**The result.** The station average rose from 3.61 to 3.80, held there for the nine weeks, and
returned to 3.61 **on the day** the station's own measure came back into service. It was recorded
contemporaneously, in the operator's hand, by a man who did not know what he was recording and set
a question mark against it.

**What it establishes that a calculation cannot.** A calculation from the 1958 measurements is an
inference: it shows that glass of that delivery *would* produce a reading 5 % low. The 1911
interval is an experiment that actually ran, forty-seven years before anyone read it, and it
excludes the alternatives by construction rather than by argument. Nothing about the land, the
hay, the cows, the men, the sampling or the honesty of anybody changed in those nine weeks, and
the figures did — so no explanation resting on land, hay, cows or men can account for the gap.
It also fixes causal direction and timing: the figures track the instrument, and they track it
same-day.

**What it does not establish.** It does not identify *which* property of the glass was wrong, nor
by how much in cubic centimetres — that needed a burette in 1958. It does not quantify the money.
It does not prove the borrowed measure was itself accurate; it proves only that the two measures
differed (that the borrowed one was the true one is established elsewhere, by the agreement of
3.80 with Alder Corners and with the post-1923 figures). It does not prove that no milk was ever
watered and no cream ever taken by anybody at any time — only that neither explains the gap. And,
standing alone, nine summer weeks cannot by themselves refute a summer-only feed effect, which is
precisely the objection Kepp raised; that is closed by other evidence — the flatness of the gap in
every month of every year, and Bulletin No. 214's finding of nothing measurable on summer pasture.
Finally, the sources disagree on where the borrowed measure came from — Ivy says it came up from
Larrow Green with the cream wagon, Roy Brant says the state man left his own that June — and that
**cannot be settled**, though the Larrow Green account fits the dates (the break was 8 May, and a
June loan cannot cover May) and Strawn's own June 1911 entry records nothing of a loan.

### D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream

**Reason 1 — the cream arrived heavy, and theft makes cream arrive light.** The Association's own
station account recorded, every year, more butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station's
cream than the station's books said it had bought: 60,800 lb against 57,760 lb in 1898, and one
like it every year after. Cream removed at the Hollow would have reached the Green short. Rundle
reached this conclusion independently in 1939, reading the ledgers on another errand, and retracted
his own printed chapter over it.

**Reason 2 — the nine weeks of 1911.** Same man, same everything, one object changed; the figures
rose and fell with the glass, not with the tester. A thief does not stop for nine weeks and resume
on the day a measure comes back from repair.

**Reason 3 — an outside officer watched the work and weighed the plant.** Merle Strawn, the state's
dairy inspector, who was kin to nobody in the valley and had no interest in the answer, checked
both scales against sealed weights at every visit and found them true, ran the lactometer for
added water and never found any, and stood at the tester's elbow through a whole test, from sample
to reading, and did not let him hurry it.

**Reason 4 — the figures came right while he was still at the bench.** The glass was replaced in
July 1923; the station's book gives 1924 at 3.80; Keddie made those tests and was not dismissed
until 30 April 1925. The correction preceded the removal of the man by more than a year, so the
removal of the man cannot explain the correction. (Vira Toland's account contains both halves of
this and never notices that they contradict each other.)

**Reason 5 — the signature of the error is mechanical, not human.** It was a fixed *fraction*, not
a fixed amount: it took most from the richest milk, stood at the same 0.19 in July and February,
in flush and on dry hay, in wet years and dry, for twenty-six years, and left the station's herds
correctly ranked against one another. Theft scatters; this did not.

**Why these are independent.** They come from different custodians, institutions and decades, and
none derives from another: Reason 1 from the Association's own accounting *plus* Sill's separate
re-weighing of every can into the cold room at the receiving end; Reason 2 from the station's test
book and Emmet Brant's private farm weigh book, at the delivery end; Reason 3 from a state
officer's contemporaneous circuit books; Reason 4 from the test book and Strawn's notebook
together; Reason 5 from the arithmetic of the recorded series.

**Which hold without the 1958 measurement.** Reasons 1, 2, 3 and 4 all hold — every one of them
existed in 1925, and Reasons 1 and 2 were physically in the room. Only Reason 5's explanation
(*why* it was a fixed fraction) needs the glass; the pattern itself was visible from the books.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question the entries that refute his own accusation?** No. Teague wrote
that line himself for forty years and admitted what its evenness did to him: "A figure that jumps
about will keep you awake. A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture."
He raised something once, with Vose in 1916, was told to let it alone, and let it alone — "I was
the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience." In 1925 he put the theft theory to the board
without ever reconciling it with his own station-gain line, which showed the opposite; and he was
still writing the chapter in 1948 without noticing. He questioned the *arrangement* (one man alone
in a building, no second man and no lock) and never the entries.

### D3. Did the manager know the tests ran low before the glass was condemned?

**Yes — certainly from 1916, and on his own account for far longer; but there is no evidence he
understood what the ledger's other column meant.**

The evidence that he knew the tests ran low. (i) He signed the annual station sheet every spring:
Teague laid it before him and he put his name at the foot of it, "and a man who signs a sheet has
had the sheet in front of him." (ii) Teague put the matter to him in 1916 in terms and was told to
let it alone — so he was told, by his own bookkeeper, nine years before the arbitration and seven
before the condemnation. (iii) The 1919 circular is itself proof: a warning about added water is
only intelligible as a response to figures running low, and Vose defends it in exactly those terms
— "A manager who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing to the men who
make them is not being kind to them." (iv) In his 1926 address he says outright that "for a
generation the milk brought to that station tested below the milk brought to Larrow Green."

The distinction that must be kept. Knowing the tests ran low is not the same as reading the
station-gain line and seeing that the fat nevertheless arrived heavy — the two facts that, laid
side by side, give the answer. On that, the sources point the other way: Kyle, who sat outside his
door for a quarter of a century, says Vose read totals and never the lines above them — "take a
sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign." So: he knew the low tests, acted on
them by accusing his patrons, and had been told once by his bookkeeper to leave it — and there is
no evidence in any source that he ever grasped the significance of the line he was signing.

## Section F — Theory

### F1. What actually produced the shortfall

**The theory.** The pipettes bought for the Ostrey Hollow station from Ammon & Sons in April 1897,
and put into use on 1 May 1897, were blown to a 17.6 c.c. mark but delivered a mean of 16.72 c.c.
— 0.88 c.c. short, one part in twenty. In the Babcock-style test the operator weighs nothing: he
reads a column of fat in a bottle neck that is *ruled in percent for a 17.6 c.c. charge and no
other*. Put in a charge short by a fixed fraction and the reading is short by the same fraction.
Every test made at that station on that glass therefore read 95 % of the truth — 3.61 recorded
where 3.80 was delivered, 0.19 of a point, five per cent of whatever fat was there.

*Why that size.* Because it is the size of the glass. A reground mold ran the lot short and nobody
in the works caught it, since a batch check finds inconsistency and this lot was perfectly
consistent — consistently wrong. Uniform glass gives a uniform error: 0.88 c.c. in 17.6 is 1/20,
and 1/20 of 3.80 is 0.19.

*Why proportional, and why invisible.* The error removed a fraction, not a quantity. So it took
most from the richest milk (a herd recorded 4.56 was short 0.24; Emmet Brant, recorded 3.42, was
short 0.18), it stood at the same 0.19 in February and in July, in wet years and dry, and it left
every herd correctly ranked against every other. The station's books were internally flawless. And
the pipette is the one instrument in the plant that cannot be checked from inside it: scales go
against test weights, the acid can be titrated, the bottle's neck can be checked with mercury, the
crank can be counted — but the pipette *is* the measure. An instrument that is itself the standard
cannot be caught by measuring with it. It can only be caught by a burette brought in from outside,
and burettes did not reach the state's field men until 1921.

*Why it began when it did.* The station opened 4 May 1896 on glass borrowed from Larrow Green and
recorded 3.80 — the true figure — for that season. The Ammon outfit went into use on 1 May 1897,
and from 1898 the book reads 3.61 and never moves. The change of number tracks the change of
object, not any change on the farms.

*Why it ended when it did.* On 12 June 1923 the state inspector ran those pipettes against his
burette, found they would not deliver their mark, and condemned the lot. New glass was in use from
July. The 1924 average was 3.80. Again the number tracks the object.

*Why nothing had to change at either end.* Because nothing at either end was ever the cause. The
same cows, the same wet meadow, the same man, the same acid, the same bottles and the same method
produced 3.61 with one piece of glass in his hand and 3.80 with another — demonstrated twice, for
nine weeks in 1911 and permanently from July 1923. And the fat was never lost: it went down the
valley in the cream and was weighed into the creamery, where it was entered every year as "station
gain," 3,040 lb a year, 76,000 lb over twenty-five years, $19,000 — credited to the Association in
its own ledgers, in ink, on an open shelf.

**The three competing explanations, and what defeats each.**

1. **Water in the cans.** Advanced by **Selby Vose**, the manager: circulated as a warning in 1919,
urged before the board in 1925, and defended in his printed address of 1926; echoed by Hobart Sill
("you cannot tell me nine families all got honest in one summer"). Defeated by: Merle Strawn ran
the lactometer at that station on every visit from 1907 to 1936 and never once found added water,
and told the manager so in 1919 — before the circular went out; watered milk yields *less* fat,
yet the cream reached Larrow Green heavy every year; the shortfall was identical in flush and in
winter and in wet years and dry, which no human practice produces; the four Bottom men who hauled
over Redlow Ridge tested about 3.80 for sixteen summers, and, as Rosalie Cudd puts it, cheese is a
witness — watered milk gives a curd that will not knit and a short yield, visible the next
morning, and her father never docked one of them a cent; and the tests rose in 1924 while the same
men brought the same milk from the same barns.

2. **Cream taken by the tester.** Put obliquely before the board by **Wilbur Teague**, the
bookkeeper ("cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands"), and held as settled
opinion in the valley (Rundle's chapter IV, Vira Toland, Cleve Oram's father, who hauled the
wagon). Defeated by: the Association's own station account, which shows more fat arriving at
Larrow Green than the station's books said it bought — theft would make the cream arrive light,
and Rundle retracted his chapter the moment he read the ledgers; Sill's independent re-weighing of
every can into the cold room, heavy year after year; the nine weeks of 1911, same man, figures up
and then down with the glass; and the fact that the correction of 1924 preceded the dismissal of
April 1925 by more than a year.

3. **The wet hay of Hessel Bottom.** Advanced by the **1908 committee under Nyle Grigg**, endorsed
in Grigg's 1919 covering letter and elaborated by **Dr. Roland Kepp** in 1926. Defeated by:
Bulletin No. 214, which both men cited as their proof and both misquoted — it found bog hay fed as
the whole of the roughage depressed the test "by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in
winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture," against a gap of 0.19 standing
flat in every month: four times too small and the wrong shape; the Alder Corners figures, where
the same wet-ground milk ran about 3.80 for sixteen summers; the nine weeks of 1911, in which the
hay did not change and the figures did; the 1908 sampling itself, which covered only January to
March, so it could not have detected a summer gap it never looked for; and the ending of the gap
with a change of glassware rather than of land.

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?** **No.** The crate had lain in the loft
over the intake room since June 1923, and two of the board's three sittings were held in the
weigh room directly beneath it — twenty feet of ladder. Frick's own exhibit list contains four
books and no appliance whatever. Loomis, who took every word down, says the crate "was never
mentioned in the hearing at all… not a word of it was spoken in any of the three sessions, by the
patrons, by the officers, or by the board," and that the award's phrase "the books and appliances
of the station" is a form of words the board used and not a list of anything. What the board did
see tried was the plant *in use* — which by then was the new glass, installed in July 1923 and
delivering its mark. The bitter part is that the condemnation was in evidence: Exhibit 4 was
Strawn's circuit notebook for 1923, which records it. Kyle carried the four books in and out and
could not say that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked.

**What did the tester himself believe?** In public, nothing he could prove: he told the board he
had tested as he was taught and could not tell them why the figures were what they were, and what
he put in his book for 1911 was a question mark he let stand for fourteen years — "I will not put
anything in its place that I do not know." Privately, the sources give two different beliefs at
two different dates, and both come from well-placed witnesses. Duncan Keddie, seventeen at the
time, says his father came home in the summer of 1911 saying **it was something in the glass**,
said it twice, at supper and again by the door, and never dared write it. Frick says that in the
corridor at Ashlin in 1925 Keddie told him privately that **he had always thought the patrons were
at fault** and would not say so in a room full of his neighbours. The sources do not settle which
he held at the end, and they may both be true of a man who had a suspicion he could not test and a
second suspicion he would not voice. What can be said is that the correct answer occurred to him
in 1911, fourteen years before the hearing that ruined him, and that his refusal to write down
what he could not prove — the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley had — is the reason it
went unrecorded.

## Section G — Summary

### G1. (147 words)

A cooperative creamery paid its farmers on butterfat found by test, and its by-law made the test
of the station where a man delivered final, with no appeal. In 1897 its hill station at Ostrey
Hollow began using pipettes blown for 17.6 c.c. that delivered 16.72 — short by one part in
twenty. For twenty-six years every test there read 95 % of the truth: 3.61 instead of 3.80,
76,000 lb of butterfat, $19,000, unpaid. The farmers blamed the tester, the office blamed the
farmers, the experts blamed the meadow. The glass was condemned in 1923 by an inspector who drew
no inference; the tester was dismissed in 1925; a burette settled it in 1958, twenty-four years
after he died. It takes eight records because no one person held two of the relevant facts at
once — and the missing fat was in the Association's own ledgers all along, entered annually as
"station gain."
