### Story 1. The Rule That Made the Test the Master (1889–1892, with a hole in the record)

In 1889 the farmers of the Larrow Valley met at Larrow Green — in the schoolhouse, in cold
weather, and it ran late — and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association.
They were tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking what a merchant chose to allow them.
Gideon Vose was elected first president and held the office twenty-two years, to 1911; he died
in 1917. In 1891 the creamery was built at Larrow Green on the creek, and the valley began to be
paid in cash once a month, which had not happened to it before. Until then there was no common
test and no common price, and every man's arithmetic was his own.

In 1892, at the annual meeting the year after the creamery opened, the members adopted Article
VII, which Gideon Vose had drafted himself — he was not a lawyer and the by-laws read as a farmer
wrote them:

> *Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter
> fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the
> test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*

Two halves, and only one of them was ever quoted. The first is just, and nobody in the valley
ever argued otherwise: a man is paid for what he actually brings. The second — *the test of the
station where he delivers shall govern* — meant no appeal. Not the creamery's test, not the
valley's average, not a test made over again anywhere. Effie Loomis, secretary 1901–1940, calls
that clause the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business, written by a man who died in 1917 and
could not be asked about it afterward. Alonzo Frick, who chaired the 1925 arbitration, put it
professionally: the rule "makes the test the whole of the bargain, and it leaves a dissatisfied
man with no remedy on earth except to quarrel with a figure." Orin Falke told the county board in
1959 that under payment by weight nothing that followed would have cost any man a dollar.

**Date, and why it is contested.** Loomis dates the adoption to 1892 from the minutes. Dell
Rundle's jubilee pamphlet says 1891; Nyle Grigg's 1919 letter says "the 1893 meeting." Both are
recollection against a minute book, and the printed by-laws carry no year on their face — only
"adopted at the annual meeting" (Nym, Item 1) — which is why Falke declines to state a year at
all. I take 1892.

**The hole.** The 1889–1892 minute book was carried out in the office move of 1938 and never
arrived. Loomis looked in every place a book can be in that building and records the loss at the
head of her history, so that nobody would mistake a story had from a father for a resolution.

*Bearing documents:* Loomis, "Notes toward a history" (1941); Nym's accession notes, Item 1;
Rundle (1939/1959); Falke (1959) ¶3 and ¶9; Frick (1946); Selby Vose's 1926 address.

### Story 2. The Station at Ostrey Hollow and the Man Who Kept the Book (1895–1925)

Ostrey Hollow lies seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green, and the road climbs the whole
seven; in March it was mud to the hub and in the nineties the pull down took three hours and in
some months could not be done at all. So the Association built a skimming station at the head of
the Hollow, on the flat by the creek where the road bends: a board-and-batten building with a
covered platform at waist height, an intake room with the scale and the vat, a separator behind,
and a loft over the intake room reached by a ladder nailed to the wall. Milk was weighed in,
sampled patron by patron, tested for butterfat, and separated; the cream went down to Larrow
Green by wagon daily, and the skim went home the same morning for the hogs and calves.

Ansel Keddie (b. 1861) went up in 1895 to get the place ready and tested through that first
summer. The station opened on **4 May 1896** and he was its tester and separator man for
**twenty-nine seasons**, 1896 through 1924. He kept the test book in his own hand throughout, in
five columns — date, patron, pounds, test, fat — ruled by himself with a straightedge, every
line the same height, the figures like print, written standing at a slope desk by the window.
Where he made an error he struck it and set his initials against the crossing; he never wrote a
figure over a figure, "because a figure written over another figure is a figure no man can swear
to afterward."

His method, from the unsent letter of 1925: the day began at half past five with the lamps lit
and the scale swinging free. Every can weighed and the weight called and written before the milk
went anywhere. A sample from each patron's milk into his own numbered jar, made up over ten days;
on the testing morning the jar warmed in water to blood heat and poured from vessel to vessel
until the risen cream was carried down through the whole of it. Then the charge drawn up in the
measure to the mark on the stem, the tip wiped, the charge blown into the numbered bottle; the
acid down the side; four bottles in the hand tester, cover down, crank at the taught speed; the
fat read off in percent against the rulings with dividers, and written down. "It is not a hard
trade. It is only a trade that must be done the same way every time."

The first leaf reads: *Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of
Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie.* The borrowed outfit was Hobart Sill's own glass,
made up at his bench at Larrow Green when the station's order had not come and there was talk of
holding off the season; it went back to him the spring after, one piece chipped, and he never said
a word about that. On a summary leaf made up in 1922 Keddie set down the yearly averages: 1896,
3.80; 1897, part year; 1898, 3.61; "and 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since,
save the nine weeks of 1911."

He was dismissed on 30 April 1925. He kept four or five cows on ground too small for them, did
carpentry — good carpentry — and died in February 1934.

*Bearing documents:* Keddie's statement and unsent letter (1925); the station test book (Nym Item
3; Frick's exhibit 1); Sill's remarks (1934); Oram (1977); Orra Keddie's diary; Falke ¶4; Vira
Toland (1979).

### Story 3. The Short Lot: Ammon & Sons of Tarnet (1897, 1923, 1931, 1949)

Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet, founded by Marcus Ammon on a rented floor with two men and
a secondhand annealing oven, grew to four stories and a basement behind the railroad yard and at
its height supplied half the glass in the state — not the churns and vats, which came from the
coast, but the bottles, pipettes, acid measures, burettes and thermometers. A creamery orders a
churn once in twenty years; it orders glass forever, because glass breaks. The cooperative
movement went through the state "like weather," and every new skimming station wanted a full
outfit at once.

On **14 April 1897** the house billed the Larrow Valley Association for the Ostrey Hollow station:

> 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20
> 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at .22 — 31.68
> 1 acid measure — .90
> 1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00
> Freight — 2.15
> **Total — 77.93**

The outfit went into use at the station on **1 May 1897** — the date is in the Association's office
file, in the bulletin's conclusion, and in Orra Keddie's diary for that day: "A. says the station's
own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green."

Junia Ammon, the firm's historian, explains in 1949 what a pipette is and why it is hard. It must
*deliver* a stated quantity of milk, not hold it — a different and harder measurement, because
milk clings to the wall of the glass as it runs out. The man works to a gauge hanging on a nail at
his elbow; the mark goes on the stem while the glass is warm; the piece anneals overnight, is
gauged next morning, and is packed or thrown in the cullet barrel. Her considered reconstruction
of what happened to this lot, offered because nobody else would: **the mold had been reground and
the lot ran short.** A gauge is knocked off its nail and hung back; a mold comes back from the
shop a hair different and looks exactly as it looked before; a boy checks the first, the tenth and
the fiftieth and finds them consistent — "because they are consistent — they are consistently
wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind cannot find."

When the state condemned the lot in 1923 the Association wrote on 28 June; the firm answered on
2 July, entered the order, shipped that month, regretted the trouble, and said its books of the
nineties would not tell anybody anything about the lot named. Junia Ammon calls that answer true
and "a thin thing to send to a customer of twenty-six years' standing," and says what she regrets
is not the accident — glass is made by people — but the letter. The works books, which recorded
what glass, what gauge, what man, what day, burned in the fire that took the old third floor in
1931; only the office side survives.

*The Ashlin Chronicle's* 1958 charge that "a cheap short lot was put off on the Association
knowingly" and that the firm's 1923 silence "is the proof of it" is unsupported and its reasoning
is fallacious. No source establishes bad faith.

*Bearing documents:* Junia Ammon (1949); the invoice (Nym Item 2, reproduced again by Ammon); the
letter of 2 July 1923 (Nym Item 10); Orra Keddie's diary; the Chronicle (1958).

### Story 4. The Bog-Hay Answer, and the Two Things That Killed It (1902–1959)

Hessel Bottom is about a hundred and sixty acres of wet meadow lying under the hill below Ostrey
Hollow, on which **six** farms had rights and cut hay and pastured. Water stands on the low end
into May; there is a soft place by the alders that has taken a horse to the hock. The hay is
sedge and rush, cut late and heavy, and on those farms it was the winter dependence from November
to April. Emmet Brant's was the largest of the six — sixty-odd acres and thirty-eight cows in a
good year — and he was the station's largest patron in every year of record.

From about **1902** the Hollow men said out loud that their milk tested under the same class of
milk down the valley. They were laughed at. In January **1908** the Association named a committee
under Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green, a man who hauled to Larrow Green himself and had no interest in
the answer. It went up in a sleigh, visited each of the six farms, drew and sealed its own
samples, watched the milking and the hauling and the testing, tried the station's scales against a
known weight and the patrons' scales against the station's, and reported: no dishonesty of any
kind in any quarter, the milk whole and the scales honest — but the herds thin in the flank at the
end of winter and rough in the coat, and "it is our opinion that the wet hay of that bottom is
what thins the cream, and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station."

The theory held for fifty years because it blamed nobody in the room. Grigg reprinted it in 1919
with a covering letter; Dr. Roland Kepp, a veterinary surgeon of Ashlin, dressed it in soil
chemistry in 1926 — sour ground, no lime, no phosphorus, a cow robbing her own frame, "what such
land gives in quantity it withholds in quality."

Two things kill it.

**The College's own trial.** Bulletin No. 214 (1911) fed bog hay as the whole of the roughage
under control and found it "depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one
per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Four times too
small, and the wrong shape: the Ostrey Hollow gap stood flat at nineteen hundredths in July and in
February, in wet years and dry. Both Grigg and Kepp quote this trial as finding "two tenths in the
winter feeding," which it does not say. Falke sent for the bulletin in 1959, printed its summary
entire, and withdrew the concession he had made on page four: "Five hundredths in winter and
nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July."

**The factory over the ridge.** From 1915, four Hessel Bottom men hauled over Redlow Ridge in the
summer flush to Jerome Cudd's cheese factory at Alder Corners in Vessey County — sixteen seasons,
the last in 1930, June to mid-September only, the road being shut from November to April. Cudd
tested on his own bench with his own glass and never compared a figure with anybody's; it never
entered his head to. The same milk, off the same wet ground, ran **3.78 to 3.82** — about 3.80 —
season after season. His own note for 1919: "Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the
ridge."

*Bearing documents:* the 1908 report (Nym Item 11; Grigg 1919); Bulletin 214 (Nym Item 12, quoted
also by Ivy Keddie, Vaught and Falke); Kepp (1926); Rosalie Cudd's letter and table (1958); Roy
Brant (1955); Hazel Brant (1981); Falke (1959).

### Story 5. Nine Weeks in 1911: the Experiment Nobody Knew He Was Running

On the morning of **8 May 1911** the Ostrey Hollow station's own milk pipette broke. Ansel Keddie
made shift with a borrowed measure and wrote in his book: *"May 8. Pipette broke this morning.
Using a borrowed measure."* Orra Keddie's diary for the same day: *"A. home cross about a broken
measure. Rain all day, the low piece under water again."*

From that morning the station's tests came up and stood at **3.80** and held there. On **10 July
1911** the station's own measure came back into service and the tests fell the same day. The
second entry reads: *"July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they
were. ? — A.K."* The question mark is his, and he let it stand for fourteen years: "I could not
answer it then and I cannot answer it now, and I will not put anything in its place that I do not
know."

The interval was **nine weeks**. Wilbur Teague remembered six, and Dr. Kepp built an argument on
six; the two dated entries decide it.

It changed one object. The patrons were the same patrons; the herds, the pasture, the ground, the
season, the acid, the bottles, the hand tester, the scales, the intake routine, the man at the
crank and his habits were all unaltered. Roy Brant saw it from the other end and independently:
"Through those nine weeks our tests came up, all of us, every farm on the Bottom; and then the
station's own came back into use and the figures went down again to where they had been. My father
noticed the rise and he noticed the fall and he entered both, and he could make nothing of it."
Professor Vaught, in 1958: "Here is one that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could
have bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven years."

Two footnotes belong to it. First, Duncan Keddie — seventeen that summer, and in the kitchen —
says his father came home saying **it was something in the glass**, said it twice, at supper and
again by the door, which was not his way; and would not write it, having nothing in his hand but
a suspicion against the outfit he had been taught to trust. He kept on another fourteen years and
wrote down every figure the glass gave him, and those figures were what convicted him.

Second, nobody can say where the borrowed measure came from. Ivy Keddie says it came up from
Larrow Green with the cream wagon; Roy Brant says the state man left his own that June; Teague
says they borrowed until their own came up from town. Strawn's own June 1911 entry records only
"Scales true. Watched a test through. Nothing here to note." The source is undetermined, and
nothing in the logic depends on it.

*Bearing documents:* the test book's two 1911 entries (Keddie's letter, Ivy Keddie's talk, Falke
¶6, Vaught §IV); Orra Keddie's diary, 8 May 1911; Roy Brant (1955); Duncan Keddie (1968); Strawn's
1911 entry.

### Story 6. The Circular of 1919, and the Ten Years in Which Two Houses Did Not Speak

In June 1919 a printed circular went out over the manager's signature to the patrons of the Ostrey
Hollow station, warning them that added water would be looked for and dealt with. Bertram Kyle
typed it at the outer desk and remembers typing it, which he says of little else; he thought at
the time it was a hard thing to send to men bringing their milk in every morning of their lives,
and typed it anyway. Selby Vose signed it and defended it to the end: "This Association pays for
butterfat and cannot pay for creek water, and I would write every word of it again tomorrow."

It named nobody and accused nobody, and it did not matter. It went to **nine families** (Vira
Toland, Roy Brant, Hazel Brant, and Keddie himself all say nine; Orra Keddie's diary, the one
contemporaneous record, says eleven, and I cannot settle that). Vira Toland's father read it at
the table with his hat still on, which he never did, read it twice, and put it in the desk drawer
where it was when he died. One woman had hers pinned inside the lid of her trunk. Hazel's mother
read hers twice and put it in the stove. "You can tell people a thing like that is only business
and they will still keep the paper forty years to prove what was said of them."

Ansel Keddie would not put them in the mail nor leave them on the platform. He carried them round
himself, stood at each door while the man read it, and came home at eleven at night with his hat
still on.

Two days later Emmet Brant came up the road and would not come in. He stood in the yard and said
he had hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and Ansel knew it better than any
man living — and then asked him why he had carried the paper himself if he did not believe it.
Ansel said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet went home and never
entered that house again.

The two men were brothers-in-law: Orra Keddie was Emmet Brant's sister, and Emmet was the
station's largest patron and the man the circular cost most. Before 1919 the two households ate
Sunday dinner together; after it they did not speak at a store or at a funeral for ten years. Orra
walked down to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane. Roy Brant is precise about the
grievance: not the water, but that "the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front
of that circular and did not." When Emmet died in the spring of 1929 Orra went down; Roy met her
at the gate and was kind; she sat an hour in the kitchen where she had been a girl and came home.
"Ten years and neither of them would go up the road first, and now it is settled the way those
things get settled."

Merle Strawn had already answered the circular's premise in his field book that same year: "I have
tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and
I told him so."

*Bearing documents:* Orra Keddie's diary, June 1919 (two entries); Kyle (1953); Vose's 1926
address; Strawn's 1919 entry; Vira Toland (1979); Roy Brant (1955); Hazel Brant (1981); Keddie's
unsent letter.

### Story 7. Station Gain: the Answer Written in Ink, on an Open Shelf, Every Year for Forty Years

Wilbur Teague kept the Association's books from 1905 to 1945 in one room over a harness shop, cold
at the north end and never cold enough at the south. When he came in he invented a ledger line —
his own device, he insists, not any dodge of the founders — because a difference that will not be
entered somewhere comes back and bites you at audit. On one side of the station account he entered
the butterfat credited to the patrons on the station's own test; on the other, the butterfat that
actually came down the valley in the station's cream and was weighed and tested into the vats at
Larrow Green. The second was **always** the larger.

The entry for 1898, and one like it every year after in the same terms and the same hand:

> *Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at
> Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as
> usual. Carried to the general account.*

Those are the station's average year exactly: 1,600,000 lb of milk at the recorded 3.61 percent
gives 57,760 lb; at the true 3.80 percent it gives 60,800; the difference is 3,040. The line is
the whole crime, annually, in ink.

Teague is honest about why it was never followed: "The gain ran at five and a quarter percent year
after year, so evenly that the words 'as usual' got into the entry and stayed there... A figure
that jumps about will keep you awake. A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is
furniture." He put it to Selby Vose once, in **1916**, and was told to let it alone, and let it
alone: "I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience."

Bertram Kyle copied that line into the fair-hand annual statement more times than he can count and
says plainly that it never once crossed his mind to wonder at it. His explanation is the best
sentence anybody in these records writes about institutions: "It had a name. That is the thing
about a name. A figure with a name on it has been explained, and you rule under it and go on to
the next." And his moral: "It was not a secret. It was worse than a secret. It was in plain sight
with a name on it" — read out at the annual meeting to fifty farmers with their hats on their
knees.

Vose signed the sheet at the foot every spring; Kyle watched him take a sheet, turn it, put his
thumb on the bottom figure, and sign, a thousand times.

At the receiving end Hobart Sill weighed the same fact for forty-one years. "That cream ran heavy.
Every year it ran heavy. There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up
there... Always more. Never less." He weighed it himself into the cold room because he never took
another man's figure for what went into his churn — and he explained it away: cream is a thick
thing and clings and will not stand still to be counted. "I never made anything of it, and I will
say plain, nobody ever asked me to."

Dell Rundle is the one man who read the line and drew the conclusion. Having written a chapter
saying cream left the station by the back door, he saw the ledgers while his pamphlet was in type
and added a note: "Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the
Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand. I leave it as written and correct
it here."

*Bearing documents:* Teague (1948); Nym Item 6; Kyle (1953); Sill (1934); Rundle's note added
while in press (1939).

### Story 8. Condemned, Dismissed, Cleared (1923 · 1925 · 1958)

**1923.** The state began issuing graduated burettes in wooden cases to its field men in 1921, for
checking the delivery of measures and pipettes; it is slow work and most plants never had it done.
Merle Strawn, on the Ordell circuit since 1907, records that in 1922 he did not run the Ostrey
Hollow glass, "the day being short and the road home being what it was, and I have put that down
because it is what happened." On **12 June 1923** he did:

> *Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk pipettes
> against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole
> crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has
> cost them.*

The crate went up the ladder into the loft over the intake room that June. New glass was ordered on
28 June, shipped in July, in use from July, and verified by Strawn against the burette that
October. His 1924 entry: "tests improved. Their book gives the year at 3.80." No one connected the
one thing with the other, and Strawn — who kept a book and not an estimation — drew no inference
about the twenty-six years behind it.

**1925.** In January the Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned; Emmet Brant's name was on it. A board of
three sat under Alonzo Frick, attorney of Ashlin, chosen chairman (he supposed) because he was the
one of the three who kept no cow. It sat three times: **17 March** at the Association's office at
Ashlin, and twice more in the station's own weigh room at Frick's insistence, so the board might
see the plant instead of hearing it described. Men stood outside in the road; Cleve Oram stood on
the platform in the rain for one whole afternoon and could hear voices but not words.

Keddie made a nine-sentence statement on 17 March and ended it: "I have never taken a pound of
cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by
himself." Three explanations were argued — water (Vose), theft by the tester (Teague, insinuated
and then disclaimed), and the wet hay (the 1908 report read in). None was proved. Four books were
in evidence: the station test book, the station receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book,
and **the state inspector's circuit notebook for 1923** — the volume containing the condemnation.
Nobody laid the second beside the fourth.

On **30 April** the Association dismissed Keddie — twenty-nine seasons and a letter. He came up the
road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm, hung it behind the shed door, and
asked what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it. On **4 May** the board found no cause
established and awarded the patrons **$3,800**, without admission, charged to the station account.
Frick: "An award without a finding is an unsatisfactory thing and every member of that board knew
it."

**1958.** In February, Ivy Keddie — Ansel's granddaughter, a graduate student at the State
Agricultural College at Farland — went up the ladder into the loft, and under a tarpaulin and an
inch of chaff found the crate stenciled AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET, still holding the
condemned pipettes in straw. She took the nine unbroken ones east and on **9 March** measured them
against a calibrated burette, five runs each: mean **16.72 c.c.** against stems blown 17.6 —
**0.95 of the mark**. Bulletin No. 471 followed in October, over Vaught's name and hers. Ansel
Keddie had been out of his job thirty-three years and dead twenty-four; Orra had died in 1948.

*Bearing documents:* Strawn's circuit books (1923–24 entries; Nym Item 9); Frick (1946); Kyle
(1953); Loomis §6 and §8; Orra Keddie's diary, 1925; Oram (1977); Ivy Keddie's 1959 talk;
measurement sheet of 9 March 1958 (Nym Item 14); Bulletin 471 (Nym Item 15); Falke (1959).

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** Derivation: Duncan Keddie, born 1894, was
Ansel and Orra's son and taught school at Ashlin; his daughter is Ivy, born 1931. Duncan states it
flatly in his family notes — "which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly
because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise" — and Ivy herself opens her 1959 talk to
the Historical Society: "Ansel Keddie... was my grandfather. My father, Duncan Keddie, who taught
school at Ashlin, was his son." Cleve Oram calls her "the granddaughter." Against this stands the
*Ashlin Chronicle* of 3 October 1958, which calls her his great-niece, and Falke's county report,
which repeated it. Those two are not two sources: the Chronicle was assembled in a day and a half
from a college press release and two phone calls, and Falke took it from the press. Falke then
corrected himself in a note added to his own report: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss
Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." Method: a
first-hand family statement plus the person's own words, against a newspaper and its one
downstream repeater, with the repeater retracting.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins.** Derivation, which three narrators perform
independently: Orra (Ansel's wife) and Emmet Brant were sister and brother. Their children —
Duncan Keddie and Roy Brant — are therefore first cousins, being the children of a brother and a
sister; both men say so in those words. Hazel is Roy's daughter, Ivy is Duncan's; the children of
first cousins are second cousins. Hazel says it herself and is tart about it: "People are forever
telling me she is my niece or my cousin once removed or some such, and she is not. Second cousins.
It is not complicated; it is only that nobody wants to count." Rundle's 1959 footnote agrees.
Against: Vira Toland and Cleve Oram both say "first cousin once removed" — village shorthand,
both at second hand, and neither can trace the line. The derivation settles it without needing to
weigh testimony.

What the sources leave open about the intervening generation: **whether Orra and Emmet were full
or half siblings.** Roy Brant is emphatic that they were full — "same mother and same father,
whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it" — and Hazel, his own
daughter, tells exactly that story: "Orra was a half sister to my grandfather Emmet, by his
father's second wife." Duncan says only "Emmet Brant's sister, the younger of the two." Orra's
diary calls him "my brother" throughout and mentions her mother's hollyhocks, which settles
nothing, and the editor notes that nothing in her hand survives from before her 1889 marriage.
This cannot be determined. It does not disturb the cousin degree either way; it would only make
the relations half-first and half-second cousins.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose**, the Association's first president (1889–1911, d.
1917) and the man who drafted the by-laws, including Article VII. Four sources: Loomis's history
("Selby Vose, son of the late president"); Duncan Keddie ("Selby was his son and not, as I have
heard it given, his nephew"); Kyle, who sat outside his door for twenty-five years ("the son,
whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is
nonsense"); and Selby himself, from the platform in 1926 — "My father, who was your first
president and who wrote the by-law under which every patron in this Association is paid on the
test of the station where he delivers." Vira Toland and Rundle say nephew; that is valley talk
against the man's own words and two office insiders.

Why it matters, three ways. First, the manager who enforced Article VII against the Hollow for
twenty-five years was the son of the man who wrote its unappealable last clause — the clause that
left a patron no remedy but to quarrel with a figure. Second, it gave the Hollow the sense that
the valley belonged to the Voses a little, so that the 1919 circular landed not as an office
notice but as a family verdict. Third, it shapes his 1926 defense, which leans on his father's
by-law and his father's Jersey herd: he was defending the family's rule as much as his own
management, which is part of why he could not entertain the idea that the fault lay in the
apparatus the rule made sovereign.

**B4. No relationship between the Voses and the Keddies is established, and the best source
denies one outright.** Duncan Keddie, who is the family's careful genealogist and gets everything
else right, writes: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused
them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either. I have heard it from two
women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell
you first cousins if you let her sit down... My mother would have told me... and she never once
put a Vose in it."

Against him: Vira Toland — "Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins. Everybody knew
that" — and Cleve Oram, "The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small
valley. My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I
could not draw you the line on paper." Both are avowedly hearsay at two removes, and Oram concedes
in the same breath that he cannot trace it. Vira's other kinship claim (Ivy as Hazel's first
cousin once removed) is demonstrably wrong, which weakens her as a witness on kinship generally.
A negative cannot be proved absolutely, and I mark this as *not established* rather than
disproved. But the belief matters historically whether or not it was true: Vira states its whole
effect — "it was not only the Association saying it, it was somebody's cousin saying it, and that
is a different thing altogether in a place this size."
# Answer Sheet — Part 3

## Section C — Math

### C1.

**(a) The proportion.** The station's pipettes were blown and marked to deliver **17.6 c.c.**, the
standard charge in this state and the charge for which the graduated neck of the test bottle is
ruled. The nine survivors, measured by Ivy Keddie at Farland on 9 March 1958 against a calibrated
burette, five runs each — 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 — gave a
mean delivery of **16.72 c.c.**, short by 0.88 c.c.

    16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly

So every test made on that glass read **95 percent of the truth** — low by **one part in twenty**.
The mechanism: a short charge liberates proportionally less fat, the column in the neck is
proportionally shorter, and the neck, which knows nothing of the substitution, is read off in the
ordinary way. The error is a fixed *fraction*, not a fixed number of points.

*Resolving the conflict.* Vaught's Bulletin 471 body text and the *Ashlin Chronicle* both give
16.27 c.c. (ratio 0.9244). I reject that as a digit transposition, on four grounds: the nine
individual readings printed in the measurement sheet average to 16.72, not 16.27; 16.72/17.6 is
0.95 *exactly*, and 3.61/0.95 is 3.80 *exactly*, matching both the 1911 interval and the station's
actual 1924 average; the bulletin's **own concluding paragraph**, reproduced under Vaught's
signature, says "low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty";
and Vaught himself flags that his 16.27 arithmetic "is not readily reconciled with the station's
own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the glassware." The Chronicle is
downstream of the bulletin's press release and is not an independent witness.

**(b) True average and shortfall.**

    recorded 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80
    3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19

The true average test was **3.80 percent**; the recorded tests were short by **nineteen hundredths
of a point** (0.19). Confirmed independently three ways: the station's own 1896 average on
borrowed Larrow Green glass was 3.80; the nine weeks of 1911 on a borrowed measure stood at 3.80;
and the 1924 average on new glass was 3.80 (Strawn's field note, "Their book gives the year at
3.80").

Because the error is proportional, 0.19 is not a constant. It is 5 percent of whatever was there:
milk recorded at 4.56 was short 0.24, milk recorded at 3.42 was short 0.18. The richer the milk,
the more was taken.

**(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited.** Over the twenty-five full delivery years 1898–1922 the
station received **40,000,000 lb** of milk (an average of 1,600,000 lb a year).

    credited at 3.61%:  40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb
    delivered at 3.80%: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb
    difference:                                  76,000 lb

**76,000 pounds of butterfat.**

**(d) Money, and the fraction received.** Butterfat over those years averaged **25 cents a pound**.

    76,000 × $0.25 = $19,000
    award: $3,800
    $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20

Worth **$19,000**; the patrons received **$3,800**, which is **one fifth (20 percent)** of it.

### C2. The largest patron, 1910 (Emmet Brant)

From the leaf of his own weigh book: *"Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand
pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42."*

**(a) Credited:**

    200,000 × 0.0342 = 6,840 lb of butterfat

**(b) Owed and not credited:**

    true test  = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60
    true fat   = 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb
    shortfall  = 7,200 − 6,840 = 360 lb

(Cross-check: 6,840 ÷ 0.95 = 7,200. And 3.60 − 3.42 = 0.18, which is the per-point shortfall Ivy
Keddie computes for milk recorded at 3.42.) **360 pounds.**

**(c) Money.** Roy Brant gives the price of butterfat in that particular year as **24 cents**:

    360 × $0.24 = $86.40

At the period average of 25 cents it would be $90.00. I give **$86.40** as the answer for 1910
specifically, because the year-specific price is available from the son of the man who was paid it.

### C3. Credited vs. received, over the twenty-five full delivery years

**(a) Credited to patrons on the station's test:** 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**.

**(b) Actually reaching Larrow Green in the station's cream:** 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000
lb**. (This is not a reconstruction — it is what Hobart Sill weighed into the cold room and what
Teague entered on the other side of the station account.)

**(c) The difference, and its denominator.** The difference is **76,000 lb**, and the percentage
depends entirely on what you divide by:

    76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = 5.00 %   — of the butterfat actually delivered
    76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 5.26 %   — of the butterfat credited ("five and a quarter per cent")

Both are correct of their own denominator and neither is interchangeable with the other. The
Association's ledger line — *"Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual"* —
uses the second, because a bookkeeper naturally reckons a surplus against the figure he has
already entered. **Five percent is the one that is also the glass**: 0.05 short is exactly
16.72/17.6. Two narrators state the base wrongly — Teague ("the gain ran at five and a quarter
percent of the fat that was actually in the cream") and Vaught ("five and a quarter percent of the
butterfat actually delivered"). Arithmetic on their own quoted entry refutes both: 3,040 is 5.00%
of 60,800 and 5.26% of 57,760. Only Nym's curatorial note states it correctly.

### C5.

**(a) Two different spans, and why.**

- *In use:* **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923** — twenty-six years and about six weeks. This is the
  span in Bulletin 471's conclusion, and it is the span of the physical fault: the day the Ammon
  glass went into service to the day Strawn condemned it.
- *The money calculation:* **twenty-five full delivery years, 1898 through 1922.**

They differ because the first and last years on that glass were part-years and cannot be used. The
glass arrived on 1 May 1897, a third of the way through the delivery year, so 1897 is a part year
— Keddie's own summary leaf says exactly that ("1897, part year"). It was condemned on 12 June
1923 and replaced in July, so 1923 is a mixed year, part on short glass and part on sound. The
ledger's station column and the 40,000,000 lb figure therefore run over the twenty-five whole years
in between, which is also the period the patrons' 1925 petition covered. The two numbers are not in
conflict; they measure different things, and any calculation that applied 26 years of loss to 25
years of milk would be double-counting.

**(b) The first season's intake: cannot be determined from the sources.** The station opened 4 May
1896 and ran to the end of that year. Three claims are on offer and none is a record. Hobart Sill:
"about nine hundred thousand pounds." Bertram Kyle: "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds, which
is what I understood at the time from men who were there. It is not in any book I ever ruled." And
Halbert Nym, the curator, going to the ledgers themselves: "The station's intake for its first
season, 4 May to the end of 1896, is not in the ledgers and is nowhere else; the station column
begins with 1897, and inquirers wanting a figure for 1896 must be told there is none." Kyle
explains why — that season the milk was carried on the creamery's own books and the sheets were
never put with the station papers. Two recollections forty and fifty years after the fact, differing
by more than twenty percent, with the custodian of the records stating flatly that no figure exists,
is not enough to settle anything. It also does not matter to the money: 1896 was worked on borrowed
Larrow Green glass and tested a correct 3.80.

## Section D — Logic

### D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment

**When and how long.** 8 May to 10 July 1911 — **nine weeks**, dated at both ends by the tester's
own contemporaneous entries ("May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." /
"July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.") and
corroborated the same day from the house by Orra Keddie's diary. Teague and Kepp both say six
weeks; the dated entries decide, and the six-week version does real work for Kepp, because it lets
him call the interval "six weeks of June and July" and conceal that it began on 8 May.

**The variable:** one object — the milk pipette. The station's own broke and a substitute was used.

**Held constant:** the patrons, the herds, the ground, the pasture, the feed, the season, the
weather, the acid, the test bottles, the hand tester, the scales, the sampling routine, the
operator and his habits, and the supervision (none).

**The result:** the station average rose from 3.61 to 3.80, held there for nine weeks, and fell
back to 3.61 on the day the station's own measure returned. Every farm on the Bottom saw its own
tests rise and fall — Roy Brant reports it independently from the farm side, and his father entered
both movements in the weigh book.

**What it establishes that a calculation cannot.** The 1958 arithmetic shows that a set of
*surviving* pipettes is short and that the shortfall would account for the gap. That is inference
from a sample of nine, measured thirty-five years after they left service. The 1911 interval is an
intervention, not an inference: it shows that removing that object moved the figures and restoring
it moved them back, on the actual apparatus, in the actual plant, on the actual herds, in the
actual year — a controlled trial with a reversal, recorded contemporaneously by a man who did not
know what he was recording. It disposes of every land-, feed-, season-, herd- and man-based
explanation at a stroke, without needing to model any of them, because none of those things changed
in those nine weeks and the figures did. Vaught rates it above anything computed in his own
bulletin, and he is right to.

**What it does *not* establish.** (i) It does not fix the *size* of the error over the whole period:
nine weeks of one summer is not twenty-five years, and it does not prove the same glass behaved
identically in 1899 or 1920. (ii) It does not identify *which* measure was wrong. It shows a
difference between two pipettes; it is the 1958 burette work that shows the station's own was the
short one, and in principle the borrowed one could have been long. (iii) It does not establish where
the borrowed measure came from — Ivy Keddie says Larrow Green by the cream wagon, Roy Brant says the
state man left his own, Teague says "from town," and Strawn's own June 1911 entry mentions nothing;
that is undetermined and immaterial. (iv) It establishes nothing about intent by anybody. (v) It
does not by itself exclude a small additional contribution from some other factor; it only shows
that the glass was sufficient to produce the whole of the movement observed.

### D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream

**Reason 1 — the direction of the discrepancy (the creamery's receiving books and the Association's
ledgers).** Stolen cream leaves the station *light*. The Ostrey Hollow cream arrived at Larrow Green
*heavy* — every year, by about 3,040 lb of fat a year — more fat in the cans than the office's sheet
said had been bought up the hill. Hobart Sill weighed it in himself for forty-one years, because he
never took another man's figure for what went into his churn, and said so in that room a hundred
times to men who thought it worth nothing. Teague entered the same fact annually as "station gain."
Dell Rundle, who had written the theft chapter, saw the ledgers while his pamphlet was in type and
retracted in a note: "Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the
Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand."

**Reason 2 — the nine weeks of 1911 (the station's own test book).** The same man, at the same
bench, alone in the building as always, under no different supervision, produced tests nine weeks
higher and then lower again the day one piece of glass was swapped. A thief does not steal less
because his pipette broke, nor resume on the exact day it is replaced.

**Reason 3 — the control over the ridge (the Alder Corners test book).** From 1915 to 1930, four
Hessel Bottom men hauled to Jerome Cudd in Vessey County each summer flush. Their milk — the same
cows off the same wet ground — tested 3.78 to 3.82, about 3.80, on another man's bench with another
man's glass, in a county that never saw the Ordell figures and never compared them to anything.

**A fourth, on the shape of the error.** The shortfall was an exact 5 percent proportion, flat in
every month for twenty-five years, with the herds ranking correctly against each other throughout.
Cream taken by hand produces scatter. Perfect regularity is the signature of an instrument, not a
man.

**Why these are independent.** They come from different ends of the pipe, different custodians,
different decades, and no shared informant: Reason 1 is downstream, in the creamery's and office's
own books at Larrow Green and Ashlin; Reason 2 is at the station itself, in the tester's book;
Reason 3 is outside the Association entirely, in another county's private records. Nobody who
produced any one of them knew what the others contained — Rosalie Cudd says plainly, "I do not know
what your station made of the same cows, and I have not the first idea what your county decided
about any of it."

**Which survives without the 1958 measurement.** Reasons 1 and 3 both do, entirely. Reason 1 is
arithmetic on entries that existed from 1898 and were read aloud at every annual meeting; Reason 3
was available to anyone from 1915 onward who was willing to write a letter over a ridge. Reason 2
also survives in weakened form — it shows the tester was not the cause even if it cannot say what
was.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question the entries that refute his own accusation?** Effectively, no —
and this is the sharpest irony in the record. Teague *wrote* those entries; the station-gain line
was his own invention, made at his own desk in 1905, and he carried it for forty years and let the
words "as usual" into it. He explains why he stopped looking: "A figure that comes in at five and a
quarter every year is furniture." He raised the account with Selby Vose **once, in 1916**, in an
unspecified form, was told to let it alone, and let it alone — "I was the Association's bookkeeper
and not its conscience, and a bookkeeper who takes his employer's business up a second time after
being told once has generally begun to look for another employer." Nine years later he stood before
the board and insinuated that the tester was helping himself, in the manner Frick describes — "he
was asked about the station's account and answered at length about how such an account would look
if a man were helping himself, and then observed that he made no charge against anybody" — without
ever putting his own ledger, which showed the fat arriving in surplus, beside the accusation. He
never questioned it again, and in his 1948 memoir he still had not connected the two.

### D3. Did Selby Vose know, before the condemnation, that the station's tests ran low?

**Yes, unequivocally, as to the tests running low.** Four lines of evidence. (i) He signed the
annual sheet carrying the station-gain line every spring from 1913. Teague: "Mr. Vose knew what that
account was doing. He signed the sheet every year. I laid the annual sheet before him each spring
and he put his name at the foot of it, and a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of
him." (ii) Teague put the account to him directly in **1916** and was told to let it alone — so the
recurring discrepancy was raised with him in words, seven years before the glass was condemned.
(iii) His 1919 circular presupposes it; he defends it in 1926 on exactly that ground: "A manager who
sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing to the men who make them is not
being kind to them; he is being idle at their expense." (iv) In the same address he states the
pattern outright: "For a generation the milk brought to that station tested below the milk brought
to Larrow Green."

**But distinguish the two things, which is where the question bites.** Knowing that the *recorded
tests* ran low is not the same as knowing that *the station's cream arrived carrying more fat than
the station had bought* — the fact that refutes both his own theory and Teague's. On that second
point I would not go past this: the information was in front of him annually, in a line he signed,
and was put to him verbally in 1916; but Kyle, who sat a foot from his door for twenty-five years,
testifies that Vose "read the totals and he never read the lines above them... take a sheet, turn
it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign. I watched him do it a thousand times."

So my answer, with the inference marked as inference: he knew the tests were low; he knew there was
a recurring station gain, because his bookkeeper had told him so once; and there is no evidence he
ever held the two facts against each other. His conduct in 1919 and 1926 is that of a man who had
settled early on an explanation — water — and stopped inquiring, which is why he could dismiss the
condemnation of 1923 as "a piece of housekeeping." **Whether he ever consciously understood that his
own ledger contradicted him cannot be determined.** No source reports him saying anything at all
about the gain, and Teague is explicit: "What he made of it he never told me."

I group these as (i) contested values, (ii) two narrators carrying the same wrong value, (iii)
narrators contradicting themselves on the page, and (iv) things the sources cannot settle. Some
items appear in more than one group and I say so.

### (i) Contested values, resolved

   against Vaught's body text and the *Chronicle*. **16.72.** Method: internal arithmetic. The nine
   printed readings average to 16.72; 16.72/17.6 = 0.95 exactly; 3.61/0.95 = 3.80 exactly, matching
   1896, 1911 and 1924. *Also group (ii) and (iii) — see below.*

   *Chronicle* and Rundle's 1959 footnote. **Nine.** The sheet lists exactly nine readings. *Also

   Oram. **Six dozen (72).** 72 × $0.35 = $25.20 = the line total. A self-checking document beats
   two recollections.

4. **Invoice total: $77.93 vs $77.39** — the invoice against the *Chronicle* and Ammon's own prose.

    books. **Both true, and the resolution is the darkest fact in the record.** The condemned crate
    itself was not an exhibit and was never spoken of; the inspector's notebook recording the
    condemnation *was* an exhibit. Loomis is right about what was said; Frick and Kyle are right about
    what was in the room. Method: distinguishing "given in evidence" from "listed as an exhibit."
    Frick's own closing line — "I have wondered since whether the answer was in that room" — is
    written by a man who has since worked out what his exhibit 4 contained.

### (ii) Cases where two narrators carry the same wrong value — and what breaks the tie

The instruction to count independent sources rather than numerous ones does most of the work here.

- **16.27 c.c. (Vaught + the *Chronicle*).** Not two sources: the paper was written "in a day and a
  half from a college press release." One source, and it contradicts its own quoted conclusion.
  Broken by arithmetic on the nine printed readings.
- **Eleven pipettes (the *Chronicle* + Rundle's 1959 footnote).** Rundle says in the footnote itself
  that he is too old to rewrite and refers the reader to the college — he is repeating the news.
  One source. Broken by the measurement sheet listing nine readings.
- **$77.39 (the *Chronicle* + Junia Ammon).** These two genuinely *are* independent (1949 and 1958),
  which is why counting would fail here. Broken by addition of the invoice's own five line items.
- **Five dozen pipettes (Vaught + Oram).** Independent. Broken by the invoice: 72 × .35 = 25.20.
- **3.16 average (Teague + Frick).** Not independent: Frick heard it from Teague's evidence at the
  hearing. Broken by Teague's own quoted ledger entry, 57,760 = 1,600,000 × 3.61%.
- **44,000,000 lb (Teague + Rundle).** Not independent: Rundle wrote from valley talk in which
  Teague was the authority. Broken by the ledgers themselves, cited by Loomis, Nym and Kyle.
- **"Two tenths" from Bulletin 214 (Grigg + Kepp).** Not independent in the way that matters: Kepp
  wrote in 1926 in a valley where Grigg's 1919 reprint was the circulating version, and both had a
  stake in the hay theory. Broken by the bulletin's own words, which Falke sent for and printed.
- **12 July 1923 (Vose + Rundle).** Not independent: Vose's printed annual address was the valley's
  authoritative statement. Broken by Strawn's dated field entry in facsimile.
- **0.21 gap (Vose + Kepp).** Not independent: Kepp says the figure was "given to me," and the giver
  was the Association side. Broken by 3.80 − 3.61.
- **Six weeks in 1911 (Teague + Kepp).** Independent of each other, but both loose recollection.
  Broken by the two dated entries.
- **27¢ butterfat (Sill + Kepp).** Probably one piece of shop talk. Broken by the annual sheets,
  cited independently by Loomis, Kyle and Roy Brant.
- **$3,500 award (Vira + Oram).** Both Hollow memory, plausibly one rumor. Broken by the quoted
  award.
- **1 May 1898 for the glass going into use (Vaught + Kyle).** Independent, and both wrong. Broken by
  a contemporaneous diary entry of 1 May 1897 plus the invoice of 14 April 1897 and the bulletin's
  own conclusion.
- **"Five and a quarter percent of the fat delivered" (Teague + Vaught).** Independent. Broken by
  arithmetic: 3,040 is 5.00% of 60,800 and 5.26% of 57,760.
- **First cousin once removed for Ivy/Hazel (Vira + Oram).** Both village shorthand. Broken by
  derivation from the parent generation.
- **"Nephew" for Selby Vose (Vira + Rundle).** Broken by Vose's own words from the platform.

### (iii) Narrators who contradict themselves on the page

- **Leland Vaught (R11).** His body text gives 16.27 c.c., a ratio of 0.9244, a true average of
  3.905, a shortfall of 0.295 and 118,000 lb; his own concluding paragraph, reproduced entire under
  his signature, says "low by one part in twenty... seventy-six thousand pounds." He even flags the
  mismatch himself: "It should be said that this figure is not readily reconciled with the station's
  own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the glassware." He also dates the
  glass to 1 May 1898 while quoting a conclusion that says 1 May 1897, and says five dozen were
  bought while the invoice says six. The most consequential self-contradiction in the collection.
- **Junia Ammon (R20).** (a) Reproduces "Total — 77.93" and writes in the next line "Seventy-seven
  dollars and thirty-nine cents." (b) States that "the house sold nothing into Ordell County before
  1900" nine lines above her own reproduction of a 14 April 1897 invoice to the Larrow Valley
  Association for its Ostrey Hollow station, in Ordell County.
- **Nyle Grigg (R15).** His 1919 covering letter says "We sampled those herds through the whole of
  1908 — every month of that year"; the report reprinted immediately below it says "We have sampled
  the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March," which Nym's Item 11 note confirms
  is the whole of it. This is not a trivial slip: winter-only sampling is precisely the window in
  which bog hay could show any effect at all, so the committee never looked at the season that would
  have falsified it. Orra Keddie's diary corroborates the January sleigh and adds that the committee
  "wanted to see the barns and not the station."
- **Halbert Nym (R07).** Heads Item 3, the station test book, "kept by E. Brant," and then quotes
  under it a first leaf signed "A. Keddie"; heads Item 4, the farm weigh book, "kept by A. Keddie,"
  and then gives its provenance as Hessel Bottom, one household throughout, to Roy Brant, to Hazel
  Brant. The two headings are simply transposed. He also places the crate's discovery in the Ashlin
  office while every other source, including the finder's, puts it in the station loft.
- **Selby Vose (R08).** Claims the tests rose "by twenty-one hundredths" to an average of 3.90 —
  arithmetically impossible on his own premise, since 3.61 + 0.21 = 3.82. He also offers as proof
  that the valley's land is sound a Jersey herd at the *home place* testing 4.56 in 1910; that herd
  delivered to Larrow Green, not to the station, so its figure is evidence about the creamery's
  glass, not the Hollow's — it quietly demonstrates the very thing he is denying.
- **Ansel Keddie (R03).** Says in the unsent letter that the 1896 glass was "borrowed off the Ashlin
  creamery," and quotes, in the same letter, his own first leaf: "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green."
- **Merle Strawn (R06).** Says he took the circuit in 1907 and laid it down in 1936 — thirty years —
  and that "twenty-eight" books, "one to the year," go to the state office. Arithmetic slip; nothing
  turns on it, and the 1923 volume is what matters.
- **Wilbur Teague (R05).** States the gain "ran at five and a quarter percent of the fat that was
  actually in the cream" and then quotes the entry that makes 3,040 exactly 5.00% of the cream's
  60,800 lb. Also gives 44,000,000 lb and 3.16 in prose while quoting a ledger line that implies
  40,000,000 and 3.61.
- **Hobart Sill (R14).** Insists there was water in the Hollow's cans while simultaneously asserting
  that the Hollow's milk "was really a 3.85 and no better" — a figure *higher* than his own
  creamery's 3.78 — and that the cream came down heavy every year. Every fact he supplies refutes
  the theory he supplies it in support of.

### (iv) Not settled by the sources

- **Where the borrowed 1911 measure came from.** Ivy: Larrow Green, by the cream wagon. Roy Brant:
  the state man left his own. Teague: "from town." Strawn's own June 1911 entry says nothing about
  lending anything, and burettes did not reach field men until 1921 (though a pipette is not a
  burette, so this is suggestive only). Undetermined; immaterial to the logic.
- **How many families received the 1919 circular — nine or eleven.** Four sources say nine,
  including the man who carried the letters; Orra Keddie's diary, the only contemporaneous record,
  written by his wife the night he came home at eleven o'clock, says eleven. Possible reconciliation:
  nine may be the number of *patrons addressed* and eleven the number of *doors he stood at*, since
  Hazel notes that six farms held nine families and "a farm can hold more than one family." I cannot
  decide it and do not.
- **Whether Orra and Emmet were full or half siblings.** See B2.
- **The station's intake in its first season (1896).** ~900,000 (Sill) vs ~1,100,000 (Kyle) vs "there
  is none" (Nym, from the ledgers). Undetermined.
- **What Ansel Keddie ultimately believed the cause was.** See F1(ii).
- **Whether the Voses and Keddies were kin.** See B4 — denied by the one first-hand source, asserted
  by two hearsay ones; not established.
- **Whether Selby Vose ever grasped that his own ledger refuted him.** See D3.
- **Where the Association's office actually was in a given year.** Teague places it at Ashlin, "one
  room over a harness shop"; Kyle says he sat in "the Cooperative's office at Larrow Green" for
  twenty-five years; Frick's first session was at "the Association's office at Ashlin"; Loomis
  records an office move in 1938. There were plainly premises at both places at different dates and
  possibly at once, and the sources do not let me put a date on the arrangement. Low importance.
- **Whether Ammon & Sons acted in bad faith.** The *Chronicle* asserts it; nothing supports it, and
  the firm's own historian supplies an innocent mechanism. Note honestly that in 1923 the works
  books had not yet burned (the fire was 1931), so the firm's reply that "our books of the nineties
  are not such that we can tell you anything" is either about the office side only or is, as Junia
  Ammon herself calls it, "a thin thing to send." That is as far as the record goes; it does not
  establish concealment.

## Section F — Theory

### F1. What actually produced the shortfall

**The theory.** In April 1897 Ammon & Sons of Tarnet supplied the Ostrey Hollow station with six
dozen milk-test pipettes blown and marked to deliver 17.6 cubic centimetres — the standard charge
for which the graduated neck of a test bottle is ruled. The lot was short. The nine survivors,
measured in 1958, delivered a mean of 16.72 c.c., 0.88 short, and short uniformly, one to another.
Junia Ammon's reconstruction accounts for the manufacture: a mold reground a hair small, a gauge
knocked off its nail and hung back, a lot spot-checked for consistency and found consistent
*because it was consistently wrong* — the one fault such a check cannot catch.

**Why that particular size.** 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly. The operator weighs nothing; he reads the
length of a fat column against a scale ruled for the full charge and calls it a percentage, and he
is entitled to do so only because the charge is a fixed and known quantity. Put in 95 percent of the
charge and you liberate 95 percent of the fat, stand 95 percent of the column, and read 95 percent
of the truth. So the error was a fixed *fraction*, not a fixed number of points — which is why the
recorded 3.61 corresponds to a true 3.80, why the deficit was 0.19 at that average but 0.24 on milk
recorded at 4.56 and 0.18 on milk recorded at 3.42, and why the best milk was robbed hardest with
nobody able to see that his good cows cost him more than his poor ones.

**Why it began when it did.** The station opened on 4 May 1896 on glass borrowed from Larrow Green —
Hobart Sill's own outfit, made up at his bench because the station's order had not come — and its
1896 average was 3.80, which is correct. The Ammon glass went into service on 1 May 1897; 1897 is
recorded as a part year; and from 1898, the first full year on it, the average sat at 3.61 and
stayed within three hundredths of that for a quarter of a century.

**Why it ended when it did.** The state began issuing calibrated burettes to its field men in 1921.
On 12 June 1923 Merle Strawn ran the station's pipettes against his, found they would not deliver
their mark, condemned the whole crate on the spot and told the manager to order new. New glass was
ordered on 28 June, shipped in July, in use from July, and checked against the burette that October.
The station's average for 1924 was 3.80.

**Why nothing at the station or on the farms had to change for either.** Because nothing at the
station or on the farms was ever wrong. The cows, the ground, the feed, the scales, the acid, the
bottles, the centrifuge, the operator and his method were constant and sound throughout — Strawn
checked the scales against sealed weights and tested with the lactometer on every visit for thirty
years and never found a fault at that plant. The only thing that changed in 1897 and again in 1923
was one article of glassware, and the figures moved with it both times, immediately and completely.

**Why it survived twenty-six years.** This is Vaught's contribution and it is the real lesson. Every
other element of a butterfat test can be checked against something: scales against test weights,
bottle graduations against mercury, acid for strength, the crank by counting. The pipette cannot,
because in ordinary practice *the pipette is the plant's standard* — it is what the plant measures
with. An instrument that is itself the measure cannot be caught by measuring with it; it can only be
caught by a burette brought in from outside. The error was also *precise*, not scattered: a slipping
scale gives noise, and noise attracts attention, whereas a pipette that is 5 percent short is 5
percent short every time, in January and July, for a careful man and a careless one. It therefore
had the appearance of excellent work, and the station's herds ranked correctly against each other
throughout. Only against the outside world was it wrong.

### The three competing explanations, and what defeats each

**1. Water in the cans.** Advanced by **Selby Vose**, the manager: circulated as a warning to the
Hollow patrons in 1919, urged before the board in 1925, defended from the platform in 1926 ("Water
was going into cans at the head of that valley and it stopped when the Association began to look").
Defeated by: (a) Merle Strawn tested with a lactometer at that station on every visit for thirty
years and never once found added water — "as always here" — and told Vose so to his face in 1919;
(b) Rosalie Cudd's mechanism, that watered milk cannot be hidden from a cheesemaker because the curd
comes soft and will not knit and the yield drops the next morning, and in sixteen summers Jerome Cudd
never docked one of the four Hessel Bottom men a cent nor made one complaint of their milk; (c) the
direction of the ledger — watered milk makes cream arrive light, and this cream arrived heavy every
year; (d) the 1908 committee, which had every reason to find against the Hollow, expressly reported
"the milk is whole"; (e) Vose's own proof, the 1924 rise, is fully explained by the July 1923 glass
replacement, and no one was ever prosecuted, named, supervised or even cautioned in the interval,
so nothing happened that could have reformed nine independent households in one summer; and (f) a
fraud practised separately by nine families would scatter, not hold flat to a hundredth for
twenty-five years, in February as in July.

**2. The tester was taking cream and writing low tests to cover it.** Insinuated before the board by
**Wilbur Teague**, the bookkeeper, in the manner Frick describes — at length, hypothetically, and
then disclaimed. It was the valley's settled opinion (Rundle: "I have never heard it contradicted at
the store"), held by Vira Toland and by Cleve Oram's father, the hauler, who "always said there was
less of it coming off that platform than went onto it." Defeated by: (a) the ledgers and the
creamery receiving books, which show the opposite — 3,040 lb a year *more* fat arriving than the
station said it had bought, weighed in by Sill himself for forty-one years; Rundle, who wrote the
accusation, retracted it in a note added while his pamphlet was in type; (b) the nine weeks of 1911,
when the same man alone in the same building produced higher figures and then lower ones the day one
piece of glass was swapped; (c) the Alder Corners control, the same milk at about 3.80 on another
man's bench in another county; (d) the shape of the error, an exact and unvarying 5 percent, which
is the signature of an instrument and not of a hand; and (e) the board found no cause established,
and Frick, who watched the work done and questioned him, believed him.

**3. The wet hay of Hessel Bottom.** Advanced by the 1908 investigating committee under **Nyle
Grigg**, reasserted by Grigg in his 1919 covering letter, and given a scientific dress by **Dr.
Roland Kepp** in 1926. Defeated by: (a) Bulletin No. 214 (1911), the College's own controlled feeding
trial, which found bog hay as the whole of the roughage depressed the test "by not more than five
hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture" —
four times too small and the wrong shape; both Grigg and Kepp misquote it as "two tenths in the
winter feeding," and neither mentions the summer null; (b) the shape of the actual gap, flat at
nineteen hundredths in every month of every year, in wet years and dry, whereas a feed effect must
vary with the feeding — the point Roy Brant and Hazel Brant both make from the farm and which makes
Falke withdraw his concession ("Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry
nineteen hundredths in July"); (c) the Alder Corners figures, 3.78 to 3.82 for the same cows off the
same wet ground; (d) the 1911 interval, in which the hay did not change and the figures did; (e) the
committee's own sampling was confined to January, February and March 1908, the only window in which
the theory could show anything, and Grigg later claimed in print that it had run "every month of
that year"; and (f) Kepp's dismissal of 1911 as "six weeks of June and July, when the stock are off
the wet ground and up on the aftermath" mis-states the interval — it began on 8 May and the figures
fell back on 10 July, in the middle of the flush, with the herds still on aftermath. Kepp also
conceded under questioning that he had never read a test in his life.

### Subsidiary (i): did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?

**No.** It examined the *replacement* glassware and thought it was examining the station. Frick
records that every physical appliance of the station was before the board and that they watched a
test made from the sample to the reading — but that was the spring of 1925, and the glass on that
bench had been bought new in July 1923 and verified against a burette that October. Of course it
performed correctly.

The condemned crate was not an exhibit, was not produced, and was never mentioned. Effie Loomis, who
took down every word, is explicit: "The crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all... Not a word
of it was spoken in any of the three sessions, by the patrons, by the officers, or by the board, and
the award's phrase about 'the books and appliances of the station' is a form of words the board used
and not a list of anything." At that moment the crate was under a tarpaulin in the loft over the
intake room, and for two of the board's three sittings it was roughly twenty feet directly above the
board's heads — a point made independently by Duncan Keddie ("two of the three sessions of that
arbitration sat in the room underneath it") and by Cleve Oram, who stood outside on the platform in
the rain and had himself put his hand on that crate a hundred times.

What *was* before the board was the paper record of the condemnation. Exhibit 4 on Frick's own list
is "the circuit notebook of the state dairy inspector for the year 1923" — the volume containing the
12 June entry, "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of
them." Bertram Kyle made the list, checked it twice, and carried the books in and out, and his verdict
is the epitaph of the whole affair: "I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any
man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked." Frick, twenty-one years later: "I
have wondered since whether the answer was in that room and simply was not in any of the three
stories."

### Subsidiary (ii): what did the tester himself believe the cause was?

On the record, he claimed no explanation at all, and said so three times: to the board — "I cannot
tell you why the figures are what they are"; in the unsent letter, in the same words; and against
the 1911 entry, where he wrote a question mark and let it stand for fourteen years — "I could not
answer it then and I cannot answer it now, and I will not put anything in its place that I do not
know."

Privately, the sources give two accounts and they point different ways.

Duncan Keddie, seventeen that summer and in the kitchen, says his father came home in 1911 saying
**it was something in the glass**, said it twice, at supper and again by the door, "which was not his
way," and never dared write it, having nothing in his hand but a suspicion against the outfit he had
been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county standing against it.

Alonzo Frick says that in the corridor at Ashlin in 1925, Keddie told him privately that **he had
always thought the patrons were at fault** and would not say so in the room — that he had lived among
them thirty years and would not stand up and accuse his neighbors. Frick records it deliberately,
against the softer legend: "it has been said since that he went to his grave accusing nobody, which
is true, and that he suspected nobody, which is not."

These need not be inconsistent — fourteen years and a great deal separate them, and a man may suspect
his glass in 1911, find no way on earth to prove it, and by 1925, after the 1919 circular and a
petition with his own brother-in-law's name on it, have fallen back on the explanation the whole
valley kept offering him. But **which he actually held, and for how long, cannot be determined.**
Each report comes from a single interested witness, in different decades, neither corroborated, and
Keddie himself committed neither to paper. What the record does establish is the discipline: he
would not write down what he could not prove. That discipline preserved the book that eventually
cleared him, and it cost him the one chance anyone had to name the cause while it could still have
been acted on.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** (149 words)

From 1897 to 1923 the milk-test pipettes at the Larrow Valley Association's skimming station at
Ostrey Hollow delivered 16.72 cubic centimetres where they were marked 17.6. Every butterfat test
made there read exactly 95 percent of the truth. Because Article VII paid patrons on the test of
their own station, with no appeal, nine families were underpaid a twentieth of their fat for
twenty-five years — 76,000 pounds, about $19,000. The valley blamed the wet hay, then the farmers
for watering, then the tester for stealing; he was dismissed in 1925, given no finding, and died
under it in 1934. The disproof sat all along in the Association's own ledgers, which showed the
station's cream arriving heavy every year, and in nine weeks of 1911 when a borrowed measure lifted
the figures. It took eight records — farm, station, office, creamery, inspector, supplier, a factory
over the ridge, a college laboratory — because none could see outside itself.
