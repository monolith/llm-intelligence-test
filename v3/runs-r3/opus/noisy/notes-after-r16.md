## 0. THE CASE IN ONE PARAGRAPH (orienting summary)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association ran a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven
miles up the valley from its creamery at Larrow Green. For roughly 26 years the station's
butterfat tests ran about 0.19 of a point below what the same milk tested elsewhere. The valley
blamed, in turn: the wet hay of Hessel Bottom (1908 committee), the patrons watering their cans
(1919 circular from the manager), and the tester Ansel Keddie stealing cream (the popular
verdict, and the reason he was dismissed in 1925). In 1958 Ivy Keddie — the tester's
granddaughter — measured the station's condemned pipettes and found they delivered 16.72 c.c.
against a blown mark of 17.6 c.c., i.e. 95% — a proportional 5% short reading on every test ever
made with that glass. The shortfall over 1898–1922 was 76,000 lb of butterfat worth $19,000,
which sat all along in the Association's own ledgers as "station gain." The 1925 arbitration
award was $3,800 — about a fifth of it. Nobody was ever watering milk and nobody was ever
stealing cream.

**What r09–r16 add to that summary.** Three men held the decisive fact in their hands and talked
themselves out of it: Wilbur Teague (the station-gain line was "furniture"), Hobart Sill (the
Hollow's cream came in heavy every year on every page of his own receiving book — "well, that is
cream for you"), and Dell Rundle, who in **1939** looked at the ledger by accident, reasoned
correctly that stolen cream would arrive *light* and this arrived *heavy*, printed the correction
at the back of his own pamphlet — and changed nothing. Meanwhile the wet-hay theory was kept
alive by a **misquotation of Bulletin No. 214** ("two tenths" for the actual "five hundredths"),
introduced by Nyle Grigg in 1919 and repeated verbatim by Dr. Roland Kepp in 1926. And the 1925
board had the state inspector's 1923 notebook — the book containing the condemnation entry — in
front of it as **Exhibit 4**, and nobody read what it meant.

## 1. RETELLING 01 — Vira Toland, "What the Hollow Said"

**Source description.** Retelling 01. Vira Toland, a Hessel Bottom patron's daughter, recorded at
Ostrey Hollow, 1979. Header note: *"Tape made at her kitchen table by a young man from the county
who asked three questions in an hour and let her have the rest of it; transcribed the way she
said it, with her own doublings back left in."* Oral, spoken register, repetitions preserved.

**Her life.**
- Born 1912 at the top end of Ostrey Hollow, in the house her father's father built.
- Except for two years at Ashlin when first married, has never lived anywhere else — "and I would
  not count those two years anyhow."
- Her father hauled milk to the station like everyone up there.
- Nine families hauled to that station; "in and out of each other's kitchens the whole year
  round"; she could name every one but says the visitor wants "the trouble, they always want the
  trouble."
- She was seven years old in 1919.
- Has a brother, who she believes still has their father's copy of the 1919 letter.
- Speaking in 1979, "in sixty years" she has never thought of the letter without the rain.

**The station building.** At the very top of the Hollow where the road bends and then goes to
nothing but the ridge. Low building, whitewashed, "cold as a spring house inside even in August,"
smelled of milk and steam and hot iron; the engine audible before the last turn.

**Ansel Keddie (per Vira).**
- Ran the station. Went up there in **1895** ("ninety-five") to get it ready, the year before
  they opened it; tested that first summer too; still there when she was a girl big enough to
  ride the wagon. "So you can count that up and see how long a man can stand in one room."
- His book: ruled by his own hand with a straightedge, every line the same height, figures "like
  print." Wrote standing at a slope desk by the window for the light. If a man asked what his
  milk had done, "he did not have to hunt. He turned to the day and he read it off."
- "He tested a thousand cans a week and never a blot in that book."
- Her father's saying: "if the rest of the world kept its accounts the way Ansel Keddie kept his
  there would be no lawyers in Ashlin." Vira's dry coda: "There are still lawyers in Ashlin."

**1919 — the letter.**
- "That is the year everything went sour up here" — she remembers it "the way you remember a
  smell."
- A **printed** letter came from the Association, **over the manager's signature**, saying **there
  was water in the cans coming down from Ostrey Hollow**. "That is what it said. Water in the
  cans."
- It went to **nine families**; her father one of the nine.
- He read it at the table **with his hat still on, which he never did**. Her mother stood at the
  stove with her back to him and did not turn around the whole time. He read it **twice**.
- He put it in the drawer of the desk in the front room; it was still there when he died; she
  believes her brother has it yet.
- Others kept theirs to their deaths; **one woman had hers pinned inside the lid of her trunk**.
- "You can tell people a thing like that is only business and they will still keep the paper
  forty years to prove what was said of them."
- **It rained the day of that letter** — she remembers the wet on her father's shoulders.

**The families and the kinship map (per Vira).**
- The manager who signed it: **Selby Vose**, down at **Larrow Green**.
- Selby Vose was **old Gideon Vose's nephew** (conflicts with r02/r08, which have son; **r12 sides
  with Vira: "His nephew Selby Vose"** — see conflicts).
- Old Gideon started the whole Association "back in the beginning"; Vira thinks the family
  "always thought the valley belonged to them a little."
- **Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins.** "Everybody knew that." So the letter was
  "not only the Association saying it… it was somebody's cousin saying it, and that is a
  different thing altogether in a place this size."
- **Orra was Ansel Keddie's wife.** **Orra was Emmet Brant's sister.**
- **Emmet Brant** was the biggest man on the Bottom — most cows, most hay ground, hauled more
  milk to that station than anybody — "and he was the one that letter cost the most."
- "The man that tested the milk had married the sister of the man that got the worst of it."
- Before 1919 the two families ate Sunday dinners together — "the two houses in one kitchen and
  the men out at the fence after." After 1919 they did not speak. **Not one word, not at the
  store, not at a funeral.** Orra would walk down to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's
  lane. They lived within sight of each other's chimney smoke for years and never said good
  morning; "both of them long in the ground now, and for what."

**The tests going up.**
- "Then the tests went up. All at once, one summer, the figures came up and stayed up, and nobody
  ever said why." Not the station, not the Association, not anybody.
- Her father came in from hauling saying the milk was testing better and he did not know what he
  had done different — "and he had not done anything different, none of them had."
- "That was the queerest part of the whole business to me even as a child, that the thing
  corrected itself and no man stood up and gave a reason for it."

**The hearing at Ashlin (per Vira).**
- "The winter and spring after" the rise. They sat in an office down there — lawyers, officers of
  the Association, and "our men going down in the wagons," and it went on and on.
- "At the end of it they put Ansel Keddie out." An old man, out, after all those seasons in that
  cold room.
- **"They paid the patrons thirty-five hundred dollars and called it settled."** ($3,500 —
  conflicts with the documented $3,800 in r02, r04, r05, r07, r08, r13.)
- "Thirty-five hundred dollars, split up nine ways over all those years — my father's share of it
  did not buy a hay rake."

**Her verdict (the folk verdict).**
- "He was taking the cream. They caught him at it and they put him out, and that is why the
  numbers went right the summer after. That is what it was. I am not going to dress it up for
  you."
- Her theory of how: "A man stands in a room by himself with the separator running and the door
  shut and nobody in the valley can add, and after a while he helps himself, and it is not that
  he is a wicked man, it is that nobody ever came up that road and looked."
- Her father never would say it out loud; he said **"Ansel Keddie was an honest man who had got
  into something,"** which she calls "a nice way of saying it and I never saw the difference."
- Keddie "died in the thirties and he was still under it when he died."
- What sits badly with her now she is old: whatever he did or didn't do, he had all those years of
  the valley looking at him a certain way in church — "and Orra had it too, and she outlived him
  a good while and had it the whole time."

**The 1958 visitor.**
- "There was a young woman came round here in **nineteen fifty-eight** from **the college away
  east**, asking after all this."
- She went up and poked about the old station building, shut by then for years — "they kept fence
  wire and salt in it."
- "She sat where you are sitting. Nice girl, careful, wrote everything down."
- **"She was Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed, so she was of the family both sides in a
  manner of speaking."** (This is Ivy Keddie. **r09 and r12 both say Ivy and Hazel are SECOND
  COUSINS** — Vira's degree is wrong. "The college away east" also conflicts with Farland.)
- Vira remembers thinking, "well, here it comes round again after all this time and there is
  nobody left alive that it can do any good to."
- She told the young woman about the letter in her father's drawer. "I don't know what she made
  of it."

## 2. RETELLING 02 — Effie Loomis, "Notes toward a history of the Association," 1941

**Source description.** Retelling 02. Effie Loomis, secretary of the Association 1901–1940.
Written 1941, "in the year after I laid down the minute book," for the Association's own files,
in numbered sections. Method statement: *"from the minutes wherever the minutes will bear it,
from my own recollection where they will not, and the two kept apart here as they ought to be
kept."*

**§1. Organizing of the Association.**
- **1889**: farmers of the Larrow Valley met at **Larrow Green** and organized the **Larrow Valley
  Cooperative Creamery Association**.
- **Gideon Vose** elected first president at that meeting; held the office **until 1911** — 22
  years; no other man has held it so long.
- Effie saw none of this: she was **fourteen years old in 1889** (so born ~1875) and came into the
  office as secretary in **1901**. For the Association's first twelve years she is "only a reader
  of its papers like anybody else, and a poorer reader than some, because the papers of exactly
  those years are the ones we have lost."
- **The 1889–1892 minute book was lost when the office was moved in 1938.** She flags this at the
  start "because it governs everything I can and cannot write in the sections following."

**§2. The creamery at Larrow Green.**
- Main creamery **built at Larrow Green in 1891, on Larrow Creek**; "it is the same building yet."
  (r12 adds: "on the creek below the mill.")
- Before it, members sold milk where they could get a price, **no common test and no common
  price**, "and every man's arithmetic was his own." The creamery ended that.
- "Whatever is said of the Association afterward, it began as a plain remedy for a plain
  grievance, and I have never heard anybody in this valley say we should have been better without
  it."

**§3. The by-laws and Article VII.**
- **Gideon Vose drafted the by-laws himself.** Not a lawyer; "they read as a farmer wrote them,
  which is to their credit."
- Article VII quoted **entire** (because "it has been misquoted at me across this desk for forty
  years"):

  > *Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of
  > pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of
  > the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*

- **Article VII adopted at the annual meeting of 1892**, the year after the creamery opened.
  (**r12 says 1891; r15's Grigg says "payment by test came in with the 1893 meeting."** Three
  dates.)
- What it changed: before it, a man was paid for what his cans weighed — "a hundred pounds of
  milk was a hundred pounds of milk whosever cow it came out of." After it, paid for the fat
  found by a test made where he delivered; weight of cans decided nothing by itself.
- Effie: "The rule is a just rule and I have always said so: it pays a man for what he actually
  brings and not for the water that is naturally in it."
- **The clause nobody quotes**: *"The test of the station where he delivers shall govern."* Not
  the creamery's test, not the valley average, not a re-test anywhere else. **The station's test,
  and no appeal from it.**
- "That clause is the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business and it was written in 1892 by a
  man who died in **1917** and could not be asked about it afterward."

**§4. Officers in her time.**
- **Effie Loomis** — secretary 1901–1940, kept the minute book in her own hand throughout.
- **Wilbur Teague** — came in as **bookkeeper in 1905**, kept the ledgers.
- **Hobart Sill** — buttermaker at Larrow Green; had the churn book and the cream receiving book.
- **Selby Vose** — "son of the late president," made **manager in 1913**, served **until 1938**.
- Her indictment of all four: "we were in that office together a very long time… and **not one of
  us ever went up the valley and stood in the Ostrey Hollow station on a working morning to watch
  the work being done. I include myself first in that.**"

**§5. The station and Hessel Bottom.**
- The skimming station stood **seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green, at the head of the
  Hollow**.
- Took in the milk of the farms up there, separated it, **sent the cream down** and **gave the
  skim back to the patron**.
- "The best of that milk came off **Hessel Bottom**, the wet meadow below the hamlet."
- **Seven farms had rights on Hessel Bottom** (**r09, r15's own 1908 report, and r16 all say
  SIX**); they cut hay and pastured on it.
- **The largest of the seven was Emmet Brant's**, "who was the station's largest patron in every
  year of which we have a record."

**§6. The petition of 1925 and the board of arbitration.**
- **January 1925**: the patrons of Ostrey Hollow petitioned the Association.
- Complaint: their milk **had never tested as their neighbors' milk tested** and they had been
  paid accordingly **for a very long time**; they asked to be made whole.
- The **board of arbitration sat at Ashlin under Alonzo Frick, attorney, of that place, from 17
  March to 4 May 1925**. (**r13, Frick himself: only the FIRST session was at Ashlin; the second
  and third sat in the station's own weigh room at Ostrey Hollow.**)
- **Three explanations were argued before it. None of the three was accepted.** She won't rehearse
  them: "they are in the minutes and the minutes are in the safe." (**r13 names all three and who
  put each — see below.**)
- She recorded the award herself on the last day:

  > *The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and
  > appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow
  > patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged
  > to the station account.*

- Her comment: "I have written many things into that book with more pleasure. *Finds no cause
  established*, and then an award of thirty-eight hundred dollars: I took it down as it was given
  to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once."

**§7. Quantities from the bookkeeper's ledgers.**
- "For the **twenty-five years** over which the patrons laid their claim, the ledgers show that the
  Ostrey Hollow station received **forty million pounds of milk**, which is an average of **one
  million six hundred thousand pounds in a year**."
- "Over the same period the price paid for butterfat averaged **twenty-five cents a pound**."
- Provenance caveat: "These two figures are not mine and I do not vouch for the cows, only for the
  copying: they are **Mr. Teague's**, out of the annual sheets, and **I have added the columns
  twice**."

**§8. What the record does not contain — the two gaps.**
1. **The office move of 1938**, in which the **1889–1892 minute book** was lost. "It was not thrown
   out; it was carried out with the rest and it did not arrive, and I have looked in every place a
   book can be in this building. What went with it went for good, and anybody who tells you what
   was resolved at the first four annual meetings is telling you a story he had from his father."
2. **The crate.** "The crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all. I took every word down and
   it is not there." She means **the crate of measures that the state's inspector condemned up at
   the station in June of 1923**, set aside with new ones ordered in its place. **"Not a word of it
   was spoken in any of the three sessions, by the patrons, by the officers, or by the board"**,
   and the award's phrase about *"the books and appliances of the station"* is "a form of words the
   board used and not a list of anything."
   - **MAJOR NEW TENSION (r13):** Frick's own exhibit list has as **Exhibit 4 "The circuit notebook
     of the state dairy inspector for the year 1923"** — the very volume containing the 12 June
     condemnation entry. The *crate* may never have been named, but the *document recording its
     condemnation was in evidence before the board.*
- Her closing maxim: "Forty years of taking down what men say has taught me that the interesting
  part is generally what none of them thought worth saying."

## 3. RETELLING 03 — Ansel Keddie: statement to the board + unsent letter, spring 1925

**Source description.** Retelling 03. Two papers by **Ansel Keddie, tester at the Ostrey Hollow
skimming station**. (1) His statement before the board of arbitration at Ashlin, **17 March 1925**
— *"nine sentences and no more, taken down as he gave them."* (2) A letter written at Ostrey
Hollow later that spring, **never sent, found among his papers**.

### I. Statement to the board, 17 March 1925 (quoted entire)

> I have kept the test book of this station in my own hand since the station opened, and there is
> not a figure in it that I did not put there. I have set down the date, the patron, the pounds,
> the test and the fat, and where I have made an error I have drawn a line through it and put my
> letters against the line, and I have never written a figure over a figure. I have made the tests
> in the same order and at the same hour of every working day of every season. I have heard what
> the patrons say of me and I have heard what the officers say of me. Neither of them has come up
> that road on a working morning and stood at my elbow and watched the work done. I am asked to
> account for figures, and the figures are what I read and what I wrote and nothing besides. I am
> an old man and I have no other trade. I have tested as I was taught and as the state man saw me
> do it, **thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons**, and I cannot tell you why the figures
> are what they are. I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a
> man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself.

### II. The unsent letter, spring 1925

**Why he wrote it.** "I set this down because I could not say it in that room, **where a man is
allowed to answer and not allowed to explain**, and because it seems to me that somebody ought to
know how the work was done before everybody who did it is gone."

**The working day.**
- Began at **half past five**. Wagons came up the Hollow in the dark most of the year.
- He had the intake room open, the lamps lit, and **the scale swinging free** before the first
  wagon reached the door.
- **Every can went on the scale and the weight was called and written before the milk went
  anywhere else.**
- The scale was tested by the state's man on his rounds and it was true. "I have never in
  twenty-nine seasons had a patron dispute a weight with me and be right." (**r09 corroborates
  from the other side: Emmet Brant's book shows the two scales agreeing for thirty years.**)

**Sampling.**
- From each patron's milk he took a sample as it was weighed; samples went into **his own jar,
  numbered to him and to nobody else**.
- **The jar was made up over ten days before it was tested.**
- On the testing morning: jar out, set in **warm water until blood-warm right through**, then
  poured from vessel to vessel and poured again "until the cream that had risen was carried down
  through the whole of it." "A sample that is not warmed and mixed is worth nothing and a boy
  could tell you so."

**The test itself (the procedure, in detail).**
- "Then the charge. The milk was drawn up in the **measure to the mark on the stem**, and the tip
  wiped, and the charge blown out into the bottle that carried the patron's number."
- "Then the **acid**, poured down the side, and the bottle turned until the milk was dark."
- "**Four bottles in the hand tester**, weighted against each other, the cover down, and the crank
  turned at the speed I was taught to turn it and no faster, and **no man ever stood beside me and
  made me hurry it**."
- "Then the fat is up in the neck of the bottle in a clear column, and it is **read off in per cent
  against the rulings, with a pair of dividers**, and the figure is written down."
- "That is the whole of it. It is not a hard trade. It is only a trade that must be done the same
  way every time, which is a harder thing than it sounds."

**The glass.** "The glass I began on in **ninety-six** was **borrowed off the Ashlin creamery**, and
we used it until our own outfit came up the valley **in a crate the spring following**."
(**RESOLVED AGAINST HIM by r14: Hobart Sill, first person, says he made up the outfit out of his
own glass at his own bench at Larrow Green and sent it up the valley — matching the test book's own
first leaf, "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green."** Sill adds that it came back the spring after with
one piece chipped and he never said a word about it.)

**The book.**
- Kept in **five columns: the date, the patron, the pounds, the test, the fat.**
- "Where I made an error I crossed it and set my initials against the crossing, and **I never wrote
  over a figure**, because a figure written over another figure is a figure no man can swear to
  afterward."
- **"Twenty-nine seasons, from 1896 through 1924. I did not test a pound in the season of 1925."**
  (Frick's Exhibit 1 dates the book **4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924**.)
- First leaf of the book:

  > *Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until
  > our own comes. A. Keddie.*

- Summary leaf made up at the back **in 1922**, "because I had been asked for them so often":

  > *Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61
  > or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted
  > in their place, when it stood at 3.80.*

**The nine weeks of 1911 (the accidental experiment).**
- "Those nine weeks in 1911 are the strangest thing in my book and I have never had an account of
  them to give."
- **"Our measure broke on the eighth of May and I made shift with one borrowed, and from that
  morning the tests came up and stood at 3.80, and on the tenth of July our own was in use again
  and the tests were down again the same day."**
- The two entries as he made them:

  > *May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure.*

  > *July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.*

- "**That question mark is mine and I have let it stand for fourteen years.** I could not answer it
  then and I cannot answer it now, and I will not put anything in its place that I do not know."

**The shape of the gap (his key observation).**
- "They did not come and go with the season. **The gap was there in July as it was there in
  February, in the flush of the grass and in the dead of the winter on dry hay, and it was the
  same in a wet year as in a dry one.**" (**r09's Roy Brant makes the identical observation
  independently: "it was seventeen hundredths in July and it was seventeen hundredths in
  February."**)
- "**In 1924 the average of this station was 3.80**, and I wrote that figure with the same pen and
  the same measure of care as all the others." (Conflicts with Vose's 1926 address, which gives
  1924 as **3.90** and the rise as **twenty-one hundredths**; Strawn's 1924 entry agrees with
  Keddie: "Their book gives the year at 3.80.")

**The history of complaints (his chronology).**
- **1902** — "The complaints began in 1902, when it was first said down at Larrow Green that
  Ostrey Hollow milk did not test with the rest." (Corroborated by r09, r12, r14, r15.)
- **1908** — "a committee came up and sampled the herds through the winter and reported that **the
  wet hay of the Bottom thinned the cream, and that the remedy lay with the land**."
- **1919** — "a circular went out from the Association to **nine families** up here saying **there
  was water in their cans**." His response: "**I carry no charge against any man who put his name
  to that paper.** I only say that I stood in that room every morning of those years and I know
  what came into it, and that **nine kitchens in this Hollow have had that letter in a drawer ever
  since**."

**Family.**
- "**My wife Orra is Emmet Brant's sister.**" (**r09: full brother and sister, same mother and
  father — Roy Brant corrects "people who like a story with a stepmother in it."**)
- "Emmet brought more milk to my door than any other man on the Bottom, and **every pound of it I
  weighed myself and every test of it I made myself and wrote in the book**."
- "**He and I have not spoken since the circular.** That is the plainest fact in this letter and it
  is the one I find I have the least to say about." (**r09 gives Emmet's reason, which is not the
  obvious one: not the water charge itself, but that "the man who kept the test book ought to have
  stood up in front of that circular and did not."**)

**His dismissal.**
- "**I was put out on the thirtieth of April.**" (30 April 1925 — four days before the board's
  session ended on 4 May. Frick confirms and condemns it.)
- "**They found no cause established and they put me out on the last day of the month, and both of
  those things are true at once, and I have given up trying to hold them in my head together.**"
- Closing: "I have tested as I was taught. I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are."

**Private stance not in these papers (r13, Frick).** In the corridor at Ashlin, Keddie told Frick
privately **that he had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the
room** — he had lived among them thirty years and would not stand up and accuse his neighbors.
Frick: "it has been said since that he went to his grave accusing nobody, which is true, and that
he suspected nobody, which is not."

## 4. RETELLING 04 — Ivy Keddie, "What the Crate Held," talk to the Ordell County Historical Society, Ashlin, 1959

**Source description.** Retelling 04. Read from typescript at the Society's meeting, **with the
crate itself on a table at the front and the borrowed books laid open beside it**; the papers
quoted were read aloud from the originals.

**Who she is.**
- **Ansel Keddie was her grandfather.**
- **Her father was Duncan Keddie, who taught school at Ashlin**, Ansel's son.
- "I mention it once, because a granddaughter who reads her grandfather's books ought to say so,
  and then I intend to leave it alone, because **nothing I am going to show you tonight depends in
  the smallest degree on my opinion of my grandfather. It depends on nine pieces of glass and a
  burette.**"
- She is at **Farland** — the State Agricultural College. (r10 addresses her as "a graduate student
  at Farland.")

**The five sets of records — "no two of them had ever been in one room before last year."**
1. **The station test book** — in the Association's office since 1925; came to these Rooms this
   year (1959).
2. **The Brant weigh book**, kept at Hessel Bottom **from 1897 onward** — lent to her by **Roy
   Brant**, who is **Emmet Brant's son** and who has kept it up himself since his father died.
   (**r09 is Roy Brant's own account of that book and that loan.**)
3. **The Alder Corners Cheese Company's test figures**, from **over Redlow Ridge in Vessey
   County** — copied out for her by **Rosalie Cudd**, who runs that factory and is **Jerome Cudd's
   niece**; the book itself has never left Vessey County and Ivy has never seen it. (**r10 is that
   very copying letter, and Rosalie says plainly she is Jerome Cudd's DAUGHTER.**)
4. **Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks**, kept by the state's dairy inspector for this county **from
   1908** — in her own college's library at **Farland since 1947**, "eleven years in which nobody
   put a question to them." (r06 has Strawn taking the circuit in **1907**.)
5. **The Association's own annual ledgers**, "which are here tonight."

**The crate — how she found it.**
- **February of last year (February 1958)** she went up the Hollow to the station building, "which
  has been used for storage since it closed."
- "There is a **loft over what was the intake room, reached by a ladder**, and **under a tarpaulin
  at the back of it** there was a wooden crate stenciled on the end **AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY
  TARNET**."
- "**It had been carried up there in June of 1923 and it was there still.**"
- Inside, **packed in straw**, were the station's **condemned milk-test pipettes**.
- "**Six dozen had been bought new for the station in 1897, on an invoice that came to seventy-seven
  dollars and ninety-three cents for the whole outfit — pipettes, bottles, acid measure, hand
  tester and freight together.**" (**r11's Vaught says FIVE dozen.**)
- **Nine of them were unbroken in the crate.** (**r12's 1959 footnote says ELEVEN.**)
- (r07's Item 5 says the crate "was found in the Association office at Ashlin, in February 1958" —
  a direct conflict with Ivy's own account of finding it in the station loft.)

**The measurement, 9 March 1958.** Her sheet, read as written:

> *Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette,
> mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean
> 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of
> the bottle was ruled for.*

(Nine individual means: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Overall
mean 16.72. Short by 0.88 c.c. from 17.6.)

**The arithmetic, step by step.**
- "A butterfat test is made by drawing a measured charge of milk into a **bottle whose neck is
  ruled to read percent for that charge and no other**. **Put in a short charge and you get a short
  reading, in the same proportion.**"
- "These pipettes delivered 16.72 where they were blown for 17.6. **Sixteen point seven two divided
  by seventeen point six is ninety-five hundredths, exactly.** Every test made at that station with
  that glass read **ninety-five percent of the truth**."
- The 1922 summary leaf quoted again (identical text to r03 and r07).
- "**Three point six one divided by ninety-five hundredths is three point eight.** The recorded
  average was 3.61; the true average was 3.80; **the gap is nineteen hundredths of a point.**"
- Her key insight about the *kind* of number: "**everyone who looked at this before treated it as a
  fixed quantity, and it is not one. Nineteen hundredths is five percent of three point eight. The
  station did not lose a set number of points. It lost a twentieth of whatever was there.**"

**The big totals.**
- "In the **twenty-five full delivery years from 1898 to 1922** the station took in **forty million
  pounds of milk**, an average of **one million six hundred thousand pounds a year**."
- At the recorded **3.61%**, patrons were credited with **1,444,000 lb of fat**.
- At the true **3.80%**, they had delivered **1,520,000 lb**.
- "**The difference is seventy-six thousand pounds of butterfat.**"
- "Butterfat over those years averaged **twenty-five cents a pound**, so **the unpaid fat was worth
  nineteen thousand dollars**."
- "The board of arbitration in 1925 awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons thirty-eight hundred dollars.
  **That is a fifth of it.**"

**The two percentages reconciled.**
- "Two percentages have been quoted at me and both are right, so long as you say what they are
  percentages of."
- **76,000 lb is 5% of the fat actually delivered** (of the 1,520,000).
- **It is 5¼% of the fat credited** (of the 1,444,000).
- "**The Association's ledger line uses the second, because a bookkeeper naturally reckons a surplus
  against the figure he has already entered.** Neither figure is an error. **But five percent is
  the one that is also the glass.**"

**Why it fell hardest on the best milk.**
- "Because the error was a proportion, it fell hardest on the best milk."
- "**A herd whose milk was recorded at 4.56 was short 0.24 of a point. A herd recorded at 3.42 was
  short 0.18.**"
- "The richer the milk, the more was taken from it, and **no man had any way of knowing that his
  good cows cost him more than his poor ones**."
- (4.56 is Selby Vose's own Jersey herd figure for 1910 in r08; 3.42 is the test paid on in the
  1910 leaf of the Brant weigh book in r07 and r09.)

**The nine weeks of 1911 as a controlled experiment.**
- "For nine weeks in 1911 there was an experiment at that station, **and nobody knew it was one**."
- Measure broke **8 May**; substitute used until **10 July**.
- "**The measure they borrowed in 1911 came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon.**" (**r09
  says "the state man left his own measure that June"; r05 says "came up from town."** Three
  accounts of the substitute's origin.)
- "**Same patrons, same herds, same pasture, same season, same acid, same bottles, same man at the
  crank — one object changed — and the average went to 3.80 and came back to 3.61 the day the
  station's own measure returned.**"
- "**No explanation of this valley's twenty-six-year anomaly survives those nine weeks except the
  one I have given you.**" ("Twenty-six-year anomaly" — 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923.)

**Demolishing the wet-hay explanation.**
- "I will take the kindest of the old explanations, because it was offered in good faith. It was
  said the wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream."
- "**The State Agricultural College tested that in 1911 and printed the answer in Bulletin No.
  214:**"

  > *Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five
  > hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture.*

- "Five hundredths in winter, nothing in summer — against a gap of nineteen hundredths standing
  flat in every month of every year, July and February alike. **It is four times too small and it is
  the wrong shape.**" (r11's Vaught, working from his own larger gap figure, says "some six times.")
- **The Alder Corners cross-check**: "over the ridge, the Alder Corners factory tested the milk of
  **four Hessel Bottom men** in their summer flush; where the two books overlap, **fourteen seasons
  of them**, that same milk off that same wet ground ran **about nineteen hundredths higher at
  Alder Corners than at home**." (**r10, the source itself, gives SIXTEEN seasons, 1915–1930, and
  r09 independently says sixteen. r07's Item 13 says the file holds only the 1919 season.**)

**Bulletin No. 471.**
- "**Bulletin No. 471 was issued last October** [October 1958], and I will close with its last
  paragraph, which is **Professor Vaught's** wording and mine together:"

  > *No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May,
  > 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because
  > its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds
  > of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station
  > gain.*

**Her closing arithmetic of delay.**
- "**The glass was condemned in 1923 and the cause was found in 1958: thirty-five years.**"
- "**My grandfather was dismissed in 1925: thirty-three years.**"
- "**He died in 1934 and had been dead twenty-four years when the sheet was read that clears him.**"
- "I have no remedy to propose. I have only the crate, and it is on the table, and you may look into
  it on your way out."

## 5. RETELLING 05 — Wilbur Teague, "The Books of the Association," Chapter Nine, 1948

**Source description.** Retelling 05. Chapter Nine of an **unpublished memoir**, written at Ashlin
in the **winter of 1948**, "three years after I gave up the desk." Method: "I have set the figures
down as they stood in the books, which is the only way I know to set anything down."

**Who he is.**
- Kept the books of the Association **from 1905 to 1945** — "forty years by the calendar and a good
  deal more than that by the wrist."
- "In the whole of it I never carried a column that would not prove. That is not said to be
  admired. **In my experience a bookkeeper who wants admiring has generally got something behind
  him that he would rather you did not turn over.**"

**The office.** One room over a harness shop at Ashlin, "cold at the north end and never cold
enough at the south." Ledgers on a shelf a taller man had built for himself before him. He worked
at a sloped desk with a brass rail across the foot to keep sheets from sliding. "I can still tell
you the sound the station sheets made coming out of their envelope in March, which was a **stiffer
paper than the creamery used**." "I remember the paper and the ruling and the smell of the ink pad
better than I remember most of the faces that came up the stairs."

**The Association as three things on paper.** A creamery at Larrow Green; an office at Ashlin; a
skimming station seven miles up the valley at Ostrey Hollow. **Mr. Selby Vose manager over the
whole of it; Miss Effie Loomis secretary and minute-keeper; Teague at the ledger.** "**The station
was the smallest of the three and it made the most noise.**"

**How the station worked (his plain explanation).** Patrons of that neighborhood hauled milk up to
the station instead of driving seven miles down to Larrow Green. Milk weighed in; a sample taken
from each man's can and tested for butterfat; the man paid on **his pounds and his test together**.
The station then separated the milk, sent the cream down the valley to Larrow Green, and gave the
skim back to the farmer **for his hogs and his calves**.

**The station-gain column.**
- "That arrangement produced **the one column in my ledger that a stranger always stops at**, and I
  may as well explain it here in my own words before somebody else explains it in his."
- One side: **the butterfat credited to the patrons on the station's own test** — what the station's
  books said it had bought.
- Other side: **the butterfat that actually came down the valley in the station's cream and was
  weighed and tested into the vats at Larrow Green** — what they had in hand.
- "**The two were never the same. The second was always the larger.**" (**r14: Hobart Sill, who did
  the weighing at the receiving end, says exactly the same thing from the platform — "Always more.
  Never less."**)
- The difference was the station's account to the good; he carried it to the general account of the
  Association "since the Association had received it."
- **"The station-gain line was my own device; I began it when I came in 1905."** He explicitly
  rebuts the valley rumor: "I have heard it said in the valley that the line was some old dodge of
  the founders. **It was not.** It was a piece of ordinary bookkeeping, invented at that desk by the
  man sitting at it, because **a difference that will not be entered somewhere will come back and
  bite you at audit**."
- The entry, "complete, because a figure with the words taken off it is worth nothing":

  > *Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received
  > at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per
  > cent, as usual. Carried to the general account.*

- "**That is the entry for 1898**, and there is one like it for every year after, in the same terms
  and in the same hand for as long as the hand was mine."
- "The gain ran at **five and a quarter percent of the fat that was actually in the cream**." (Loose:
  per Ivy Keddie and the curator, 5¼% is of the fat **credited**, 5% of the fat delivered. His own
  numbers bear that out: 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26%; 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.00%. **Vaught in r11 makes the
  identical slip.**)
- **Why nobody noticed**: "It ran there year after year, so evenly that **the words 'as usual' got
  into the entry and stayed there**, and a man may as well be honest about what evenness does to a
  bookkeeper: **it stops him looking. A figure that jumps about will keep you awake. A figure that
  comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture.**"

**His two large numbers for the station.**
- "**Over the years the account ran, that station took in forty-four million pounds of milk.**"
  (44,000,000 — differs from the 40,000,000 lb figure of Effie Loomis, Ivy Keddie and the curator,
  which is explicitly scoped to 1898–1922. **r12's Rundle also says forty-four million, and scopes
  it "over the years complained of," which is the 25-year scope — so Rundle's use of it is wrong
  even if Teague's is not.**)
- "**And the average test of all the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, taken over the whole period and not
  over some picked year that suits an argument, was 3.16.**" (Conflicts with the station book's
  3.61. **r13's Frick independently gives 3.16 as well — see conflicts.**)
- "Down at Larrow Green we did better than that, and **there was not a season in twenty-five years
  when the Ostrey Hollow men were not sore about it**."

**The 1911 break, as it looked from the ledger.**
- "I remember only one break in the run of it. **The station's own measure broke in the spring of
  1911, and they made shift with a borrowed one for six weeks before their own came up from
  town.**" (**Six weeks** — conflicts with the nine weeks of 8 May to 10 July in Keddie's book.
  **Kepp in r16 also received it as six weeks, so the short version was in public circulation by
  1926.**)
- "It went into my accounts as **a small charge for replacement and nothing else**, and I would not
  mention it at all except that in later years people came asking me about that year in particular,
  **as though a bookkeeper kept the weather**."

**What he told the 1925 board (repeated verbatim in the memoir).**
- "**Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons.**"
- "**One man weighed the milk, one man drew the sample, one man made the test, one man wrote the
  figure that the pay slip was made from, and one man ran the separator. There was no second man in
  that building from the spring to the fall.**"
- "**I do not say what any particular person did on any particular day, because I was not there and
  I do not know.** I say that a business which is arranged so that a single man can take a little
  and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken from it,
  and that no set of books ever made can prevent it. **The remedy is two men or a lock, and the
  Association had neither.**"
- (**r13 is the chairman's view of exactly this testimony: Frick classes it as the second of the
  three explanations — the tester taking cream — "put by the bookkeeper, Wilbur Teague, in the way
  an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it… There is no cross-examination that
  reaches a witness of that kind, and I did not attempt one."**)

**The hearing.**
- "The patrons petitioned **in the winter, when a farmer has time to be angry**."
- The board sat at Ashlin under **Mr. Alonzo Frick, "who was as fair a chairman as ever a hearing
  had"**; Teague was called, gave evidence, answered what he was asked.
- "The board found nothing established against anybody. It then awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons
  **$3,800**, without admission, and charged it to the station account, **and I entered that too,
  and it balanced.**"

**Two things more.**
1. "**Mr. Vose knew what that account was doing. He signed the sheet every year.** I laid the annual
   sheet before him each spring and he put his name at the foot of it, and **a man who signs a sheet
   has had the sheet in front of him.** What he made of it he never told me."
2. "**I did not sit silent for forty years**, whatever has since been said. **I put it to Mr. Vose in
   1916 and was told to let it alone. I let it alone.** I was the Association's bookkeeper and not
   its conscience, and **a bookkeeper who takes his employer's business up a second time after being
   told once has generally begun to look for another employer.**"

**Closing.** "The figures are in the ledgers at Ashlin, in ink, dated, and anybody who wishes to
quarrel with this chapter is at liberty to go and read them, **which is more than most of the
quarrelers have ever done.**"

## 6. RETELLING 06 — Merle Strawn, "Extracts from twenty-eight circuit books," 1936

**Source description.** Retelling 06. **Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector (retired)**, extracts
with a covering note, **1936**. "Copied out this spring at the request of the state dairy office,
from the pocket books I carried on the Ordell circuit. **I have copied what is in them and put in
nothing that is not.**"

**Covering note.**
- "**I took the Ordell circuit in nineteen hundred and seven and I have laid it down this year**"
  (1907–1936). (Ivy Keddie in r04 dates his notebooks "from 1908.")
- The circuit: "**fifty-odd plants in four counties** — creameries, cheese factories, skimming
  stations, one or two little places that made butter three days a week and called themselves a
  creamery." The state expected each seen **at least once in the year** and the doubtful ones
  oftener.
- Travel: "by train where there was a train and by livery where there was not, and **in the spring
  by neither**, the roads up the side valleys being what they are in April."
- "A man on this circuit learns the plants by their smells before he learns them by their names: the
  sour-sweet of a receiving platform in July, the lime and steam of a wash room, **the flat cold
  nothing of a well-run cold room, which is the smell I liked best because it meant I would have no
  letter to write**."
- **His four-step routine, always in the same order** "so that I would not have to remember which of
  them I had done":
  1. **The scales**, against the sealed weights he carried — **both the intake scale and the cream
     scale, loaded light and loaded heavy**.
  2. **The lactometer** on the day's milk, patron by patron where there was any question, **for
     added water**.
  3. **The boiler, the water supply and the cold room** — pressure, gauge, the cased well if there
     was one, and whether the room would hold what they put in it overnight.
  4. "**and the one most men grudged me** — I stood at the tester's elbow and watched him make a
     test through from the sample to the reading, **and I did not let him hurry it**."
- His creed: "**I put down what I found. I did not put down what I thought about it, and I have not
  begun now.** The state pays a field man to weigh and to look, and every time in my life I have
  seen a field man paid for an opinion instead, **the opinion has outlived the weighing and done
  harm after the man was dead.**"

**The year-by-year extracts.**
- **1907.** First round of the new circuit. Ostrey Hollow station, up seven miles from Larrow
  Green, **opened before my time**. Scales true against my weights. Cold room fair. "**Their tester
  keeps his book in his own hand, and his hand is better than most. Watched him through two tests.
  He works slow and clean and reads his own figure without being coaxed to it.**"
- **1909.** Ostrey Hollow, in the fall. Scales true. Boiler in order, safety tried. "**The station
  takes from the farms of the wet bottom below it and from three places on the hill.**" Milk sweet,
  cans clean, **one man cautioned about a rusted seam**.
- **1911.** Ostrey Hollow, **June**, on his way over to the other side of the county. Scales true.
  Watched a test through. "**Nothing here to note.**" (June 1911 falls inside the nine weeks of the
  borrowed measure, 8 May – 10 July — he watched a test with the borrowed glass and found nothing
  amiss. **r09 claims Strawn is the man who supplied that borrowed measure — "the state man left
  his own measure that June."**)
- **1912.** Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. "**Cream shipped down the valley
  the same day it is separated, which is more than some do.**" Creek out over the flat road; went
  round by the ridge and lost the afternoon.
- **1913.** Larrow Green creamery, two days, the churn room and the receiving books. Scales true.
  "**New manager at the Association this year and he came out with me to the platform, which the
  last one never did.**" (= Selby Vose, made manager 1913.)
- **1915.** Ostrey Hollow, and the plants down the county line after it. Scales true. **Wash room
  floor holding water at the drain and I ordered it graded.** "A field man who lets a floor go will
  be shown a floor at every plant on his circuit inside of two years."
- **1916.** Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. "**The tester complains that his
  patrons blame him for their figures. I told him that is the trade and he said he knew it.**"
- **1917.** Cheese factory in the next county, then Larrow Green. Scales true at both. "**A bad year
  for help; the plants are short-handed and I found two testers doing the work of three men, which
  is where mistakes live.**"
- **1919.** Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. "**The manager of the Association
  has sent a circular round to the patrons of this station about water in the cans. I have tested
  for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told
  him so.**" (Direct contemporaneous rebuttal of the 1919 circular, delivered to the manager's face.)
- **1920.** Ostrey Hollow, **twice this year, spring and fall**. Scales true both times. Lactometer,
  no added water. **Cold room short of ice in the fall**, said so, "and it was seen to before I was
  out of the county."
- **1921.** "**The state has begun issuing burettes to the field men this year, graduated, in a
  wooden case, for checking the delivery of measures and pipettes at the plants. Mine came in the
  spring with a printed sheet of directions. It is a slow business to run a plant's glass against
  it and most plants have never had it done.**"
- **1922.** Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. Boiler in order. "**I did not run
  their glass against the burette this year, the day being short and the road home being what it
  was, and I have put that down because it is what happened.**" (The near-miss: the discovery could
  have come a year earlier.)
- **1923, June 12.** Ostrey Hollow. "**I had carried the burette two seasons before this check.**"
  The entry:

  > *Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk
  > pipettes against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot,
  > the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not
  > weigh what it has cost them.*

- **1923, October.** Ostrey Hollow. **New glass in use since July.** Ran the new pipettes against the
  burette. **They deliver their mark.** Scales true.
- **1924.** Ostrey Hollow. Scales true, no added water, **tests improved. "Their book gives the year
  at 3.80."**
- **1925.** Ostrey Hollow, in the fall. Scales true, no added water, tests improved. "**New man at
  the tester's bench this year. He is quick, and quick is not the thing I look for.**"
- **1926.** "Word from the state office that **the burette program is discontinued from this year**.
  The instruments are to be kept by the men who have them but no more are to be issued and no
  returns are wanted. **Mine is in its case in my grip and has been used twice since.**"
- **1928.** Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. "The station is quieter than it
  was. **The loft over the intake room is full of odds and ends and I told them a loft over a milk
  room is a place for nothing but air, and was told in return that it had been full since before the
  war.**" (The loft where the crate sat — the crate had been there five years by then. **r12's
  Rundle, writing in 1939, says the same loft "I believe has never been cleared out since it was
  put up."**)
- **1931.** Larrow Green creamery and Ostrey Hollow in the same week. Both in order. "**Bad ice year;
  every plant in the four counties short and complaining, and two of them buying from the
  railroad.**"
- **1934.** Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. **A separator bearing run hot and
  shut down two days, since repaired.** "**The tester's book is kept up, though not in the hand it
  used to be kept in.**"
- **1936, March.** Last round. Ostrey Hollow, Larrow Green, and the two factories over the county
  line. Scales true at all four. "Nothing to note anywhere and nothing left to say about it."

**Closing note.**
- "**The books themselves — twenty-eight of them, one to the year, oilcloth covers, pocket size — go
  to the state dairy office with this copy, as the rules require of a man laying down a circuit.**"
- "There is nothing in them but what a field man puts in a field book: **what I weighed, what I
  tested, what I told the plant to mend, and whether it was mended when I came back.** They are dull
  reading and they were meant to be."
- "I have been asked once or twice, by men who ought to know better, to say in a general way how a
  certain plant stood in my estimation over the years, and I have always answered that **I do not
  keep an estimation, I keep a book**, and the book is open to anybody the state sends for it. **I
  never had a theory about a plant and I do not recommend having one. A theory is a thing a man
  carries out to the platform with him and it gets in the way of the weights.**"

## 8. RETELLING 08 — Selby Vose, manager's annual address, 1926

**Source description.** Retelling 08. The manager's address to the members at the annual meeting,
**printed in the annual report for the year** (1926).

**Opening.**
- "**It is thirteen years this spring, since 1913, that you have had the management of this
  Association in my hands, and I have stood up before you thirteen times to give an account of
  it.**"
- "I have never had a year I liked less to report on than the one behind us, and I have never had
  one I was gladder to have behind us."
- "**a manager who talks around a difficulty leaves the difficulty exactly where he found it, with
  interest.**"

**The ordinary business of the year.**
- "The season opened wet and stayed wet into June, and the hauling was heavy on the horses and
  heavier on the men."
- The creamery at Larrow Green ran through the flush **without a day lost**, "which is more than the
  creameries below us can say."
- "Our butter has held its place with the trade that buys it, our churn room is clean, **our ice went
  into the house early and lasted**, and I have no complaint of any man on the payroll."
- "The skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley, ran its season out and its
  cream came down to us daily and sweet."
- "That is the whole of the ordinary business and **I would to heaven it were the whole of the
  address**."

**The arbitration, as he frames it.**
- "**in January of last year, 1925, the patrons of the Ostrey Hollow station laid a petition before
  this Association**."
- A board of arbitration was constituted upon it; **sat at Ashlin under Mr. Alonzo Frick, "an able
  and impartial man"**; heard everybody who wished to be heard, at length, "**and at some cost in
  time to men who could ill spare it in the spring**."
- "**The board found no cause established. It found no cause established against the station, against
  the office, or against any officer or servant of this Association, and I ask you to sit with that
  sentence a moment, because it is the only sentence in this business that was arrived at by men
  under oath with the books in front of them.**"
- "Having found no cause established, that board nevertheless awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons
  **thirty-eight hundred dollars, without admission, and charged it to the station account. I
  supported the payment then and I support it now.**"
- "It was, I say to you frankly, **a generous settlement of an unproved claim**. This Association paid
  thirty-eight hundred dollars rather than let a body of its own members go on believing they had
  been used badly by their neighbors. **There is not a business house in Ordell County that would
  have done it. We did it because we are not a business house; we are the farmers of this valley in
  company together, and a company that will not spend money to keep peace among its own members has
  not understood what it is for.**"
- "**But I will not have peace bought a second time**, and so I will say what I believe to be the
  cause of the whole affair, and say it in this room, where I have said everything else."

**His accusation — the watered-milk theory.**
- "**Water was going into cans at the head of that valley and it stopped when the Association began
  to look. The rise of 1924 is the measure of it.**"
- "For a generation the milk brought to that station tested below the milk brought to Larrow Green,
  and every year we were told it was the wet hay, or the tester, or the office, or the weather."
- "Then the Association's attention came to rest on the station, and there was a petition, and a
  hearing, and men were required to answer questions in front of their neighbors. **And in the very
  next season the tests at that station rose by twenty-one hundredths and the average for 1924 stood
  at 3.90, which is as good a figure as any station in this county will show you.**"
- "**Nothing was done to those cows. Nothing was done to that bottom land. Nobody changed their feed
  and nobody changed their pasture. The tests came up when the looking began, and any man who wants
  a different explanation of that is welcome to stand where I am standing and give us one.**"

**His dismissal of the apparatus explanation.**
- "**I am told, and I have been told it kindly and by men who mean well, that the state man's visit of
  that period explains the whole thing. It does not.**"
- "He came through the county as he came through it every year or two, he tried our scales against
  his weights and found them true, and **he condemned the station's testing apparatus on the twelfth
  of July, 1923**, and told us to order new." (**12 July** — Strawn's own notebook and the photostat
  say **12 June 1923**. "Every year or two" understates Strawn's near-annual visits. **r12's Rundle
  reproduces the same 12 July error in 1939, which suggests Vose's printed address is where the
  valley got it.**)
- "**We ordered new inside the month and had it in use before the flush was over.**"
- "**Apparatus wears. A station in daily use since 1896 will wear its fittings out twice over in that
  time, and it is the state man's business to say so before they fail him, and ours to replace them
  when he does. I decline to build a mystery on a piece of housekeeping that appears in our own
  order book with a date and a price against it.**"

**His theory of the watering man.**
- "I am aware that this is not a comfortable thing to say to a room with Ostrey Hollow men in it, and
  **I say it without naming a name, because I do not know a name and I would not use it if I did.**"
- "**A little water in a can is not a felony in a man's own mind. It is a way of making a hard year
  come out even, and the man who does it tells himself he will stop when the price improves.**"
- "**I have no wish to see anybody prosecuted, and nobody has been or will be.** I wish only to have it
  understood, once, in the hearing of the whole membership, why that station's figures were what they
  were and why they are what they are now."

**His defense of the 1919 circular.**
- "**It follows that I defend the circular I sent to the patrons of that station in 1919, which has
  been thrown at me a good deal in the last two years. I wrote it because it was my duty to write
  it.**"
- "**It warned the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, in temperate language, that added water would be looked
  for and would be dealt with. It named nobody. It accused nobody. It said that this Association pays
  for butterfat and cannot pay for creek water, and I would write every word of it again tomorrow.**"
- "**A manager who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing to the men who
  make them is not being kind to them; he is being idle at their expense.**"

**His father and the Jersey herd.**
- "**My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law under which every patron in this
  Association is paid on the test of the station where he delivers, kept a herd of Jerseys at the home
  place, as some of you remember, and I kept them after him.**" (Selby says he is Gideon Vose's
  **son** — conflicts with Vira Toland's "nephew" and with r12's "his nephew Selby Vose"; agrees
  with Effie Loomis's "son of the late president.")
- "**In 1910 that herd tested 4.56.**"
- "I do not bring that forward to boast of the cows; they are gone now and the ones I have are no
  better than yours. **I bring it forward because it settles a thing that gets said about this county
  in the trade below us — that the Larrow Valley is poor milk country, that our land is sour and our
  cattle are indifferent and our figures are what a man must expect off ground like ours. That is not
  true.**"
- "**There is no better milk in the state than the milk this valley can produce when it comes to the
  platform as it left the cow, and the proof of it stood in my own barn.**"
- (4.56 is precisely the figure Ivy Keddie uses in r04 as her example of a rich herd short 0.24 of a
  point. The Vose home-place herd was **not** an Ostrey Hollow station patron — its 4.56 was measured
  at/for Larrow Green — so his own best evidence is drawn from outside the short glass.)

**His closing charge to the members.**
- "**Bring it as it left the cow. Wash the cans, cool the milk, keep the dog out of the milk house, and
  bring it as it left the cow. Do that, and the Ostrey Hollow tests will stay where they went in 1924,
  and this business will be dead and buried where it belongs, and your manager can stand up here next
  spring and talk to you about butter prices, which is what he is paid for.**"
- "**I thank you for thirteen years of your patience and I ask for one more.**"

## 9. RETELLING 09 — Roy Brant, "My Father's Book," account for the county farm bureau's local-history collection, 1955 (postscript 1958)

**Source description.** Retelling 09. *"Set down in longhand at Hessel Bottom in the winter of 1955
by Roy Brant, sixty-seven years old and still milking, with a postscript he added three years
afterward."* (Sixty-seven in 1955 → born ~1888.)

**Why he wrote it.** "The bureau asked me for something on the old days at the head of the valley.
I would rather write about my father's book, because the old days are gone and the book is on the
shelf behind my chair, and a man does better to talk about what he can put his hand on."

**Emmet Brant.**
- **His father was Emmet Brant. Born 1854, died 1929.** (Consistent with r07: the weigh book passed
  to Roy Brant in 1929.)
- "**From the first of January, 1898, until the week before he died, he wrote down every can of milk
  that went out of this barn**, and I have kept it up since, in the same book until it was filled
  and then in another, in the same way, because he showed me how and because I never found a reason
  to stop." (**CONFLICT: r07 Item 4 and r13 Exhibit 3 both date the book from 1 January 1897.**)

**The Bottom.**
- "Hessel Bottom is **a hundred and sixty acres** of wet meadow lying under the hill at Ostrey
  Hollow."
- "**Six farms** had rights on it and cut hay off it and pastured it." (**Effie Loomis says seven;
  the 1908 report in r15 and Kepp in r16 both say six.**)
- "**Ours was the largest of the six — sixty-odd acres of the meadow, and thirty-eight cows in a
  good year, which for this valley was a herd.**"
- "The water stands on the low end into May, and there is **a soft place by the alders that has
  taken a horse to the hock** in my time and my father's."
- "The hay off it is coarse, and it cuts late, and a cow will eat it and do well enough, **whatever
  has been said about that hay by men who never fed it**. I have cut that meadow with a scythe and
  with a mower behind two horses, and I will say for it that it never failed to grow something."

**The book (physical description and method).**
- "It is a ledger, a plain one, **ruled by hand. Two columns for each day of delivery.**"
- **Column 1: the weight of every can as Emmet took it off his own scale in his own barn before the
  wagon went down the road. Column 2: the figure the station gave him back for that same milk.**
- "That is all it is. There is **no comment in it anywhere but three or four lines in thirty years,
  and no opinion at all.**"
- "**The cover is gone off the front, the back is held by a strip of harness leather my father tacked
  on himself, and the first forty leaves are stained brown at one corner where it lay in a leaky
  window a winter he was sick.**"

**THE CENTRAL FINDING OF THE BOOK — the scales agreed.**
- "**The two columns agreed.** That is the thing about the book that people do not take in when I
  tell them, so I will say it slowly. Day after day, month after month, for the whole run of years
  my father kept it and for the years I have kept it, **his weight and the station's weight came out
  the same, barring a pound or two here and there, and those were days when a can got slopped on the
  hill.**"
- "**Two scales, his and the station's, kept by two men who by the end were not speaking to one
  another, and the scales never fell out at all. Whatever was wrong at that station — and something
  was wrong at that station for a very long time — it was not the weighing.**"
- (This is the independent confirmation of Keddie's claim never to have lost a weight dispute, and it
  eliminates the weighing as a cause — which is why the pipette is left standing.)

**The 1910 leaf, in Emmet's hand (identical to r07 Item 4):**

> *Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and
> theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42.*

- "Two hundred thousand pounds even. **He was pleased about the evenness of it; he wrote 'even' and
  underlined it, which for him was carrying on.**"
- "**The price of butterfat that year was twenty-four cents.** Over the whole stretch of years the
  complaint covers, one year with another, **it came to about twenty-five cents.**" (Agrees with
  Effie Loomis/Teague's sheets and Ivy Keddie at 25c average; **conflicts with Sill's and Kepp's
  27 cents.**)
- "He wrote at the kitchen table after the second milking, in the lamp, **with his hat still on**,
  and my mother would not have the table cleared till he was done. **It took him no time at all, and
  it is the only reason there is anything left to argue about now.**" (Echo of Vira's father reading
  the 1919 letter "with his hat still on, which he never did" — for Emmet it was the ordinary habit.)

**The trouble, as Roy states it.**
- "**Our milk at the Hollow ran seventeen hundredths of a point under what the same class of milk
  was making down at Larrow Green.**" (0.17 — vs Ivy's 0.19; **exactly reconcilable with Sill's
  3.78 Larrow Green average against the station's recorded 3.61.**)
- "Seventeen hundredths is nothing to look at on one pay slip. **Set the slips in a row for a
  lifetime of farming and it is a farm.**"
- "**The first men to say so out loud said it in 1902, and they were laughed at, and one of them was
  my father.**"
- "What I would have anybody notice is this: **it was seventeen hundredths in July and it was
  seventeen hundredths in February. It did not come and go with the season. It did not care whether
  the cows were on the meadow or on hay in the barn. Nobody would take account of that, then or
  since, though it was in front of them.**"

**1908.**
- "In 1908 the Association appointed a committee, **with Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green at the head of
  it**, and the committee came up and sampled the Bottom herds through the winter and reported that
  the wet hay of this bottom thins the cream, and that the remedy lay with the land and not with the
  station."
- "**My father read that report twice and put it in the drawer and said that the land had been wet
  since the flood and the tests had not.**"

**1911.**
- "There were **nine weeks** in the summer of 1911 when the station's own measure was out of use, it
  having been broken. **The state man left his own measure that June and that is what they used.**"
  (**NEW AND CONFLICTING: attributes the borrowed measure to Strawn. Ivy Keddie says it came up from
  Larrow Green with the cream wagon; Teague says "from town." Note also that Keddie's book has the
  borrowed measure in use from the morning of 8 May, before June — so either Roy misremembers the
  source, or a second substitute arrived in June.**)
- "Through those nine weeks **our tests came up, all of us, every farm on the Bottom**; and then the
  station's own came back into use and the figures went down again to where they had been. **My
  father noticed the rise and he noticed the fall and he entered both**, and he could make nothing
  of it, and I could make nothing of it either, **and there it sat for near on fifty years.**"

**1919.**
- "In 1919 the manager sent a paper round to the Hollow patrons warning us against putting water in
  the milk. **Nine families** at the head of the valley got that paper and nine families read it for
  what it was."
- "**My father was one who never forgave it.** He and Ansel Keddie were brothers-in-law, and **from
  1919 they did not speak — not from any quarrel over the water, but because my father held that the
  man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not.**"
  (**KEY NEW FACT: the estrangement's actual cause. Not that Emmet believed the accusation, but that
  he thought Keddie owed the patrons a public defense and withheld it.** Set against r13: Keddie
  privately thought the patrons *were* at fault — so his silence was not simple timidity.)

**Over the ridge.**
- "**From 1915, four of the Bottom men hauled their milk over Redlow Ridge to the cheese factory in
  the summer flush and back to the Hollow the rest of the year. The factory over there had opened in
  1914. They went on that way for sixteen seasons.**" (**Sixteen seasons matches r10 exactly,
  1915–1930. The 1914 opening date conflicts with Rosalie Cudd's own "nineteen thirteen"; r12 also
  says 1914.**)
- "It was for the price and the flush market and a shorter haul while the road was open, and **it
  was talked about here as though they had gone over to the enemy, which shows you what a valley
  is.**"
- "**I did not go over. We had the biggest herd on the meadow, and the biggest herd is the one that
  cannot afford to be talked about, and I have thought since that this was a poor reason and cost us
  money.**"

**The family (Roy's own kinship map — the most authoritative in the set).**
- "**Orra Keddie was my father's sister. They were full brother and sister, same mother and same
  father, whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it.**"
- "So Ansel Keddie, who kept the station's test book, **was married into the family that had more to
  lose by that station than any other family on the meadow**, and I have never known what to do with
  that fact except set it down."
- "**Duncan Keddie, who taught school at Ashlin, was Ansel and Orra's boy, and he and I are first
  cousins. My daughter Hazel and his daughter Ivy are second cousins**, and I mention it because
  both of them have had to have it explained to them by me more than once." (**Settles it: Ivy and
  Hazel are SECOND COUSINS — agreeing with r12's 1959 footnote and correcting Vira's "first cousin
  once removed."**)

**The book's future.** "I am sixty-seven and the book has outlived two of the three men who are in
it. **It goes to Hazel when I am done with it.** That is all the arrangement I have made and all I
intend to make." (Matches r07's provenance: to Hazel Brant in 1962.)

**Postscript, 1958.** "**Duncan's girl Ivy came out to the place this year and asked whether she
might borrow the book for a piece of work she is doing at the college at Farland. I let her have it.
She is careful with paper. It comes back to me when she is through, and after me it is Hazel's.**"

## 10. RETELLING 10 — Rosalie Cudd, cheesemaker at Alder Corners, letter to Ivy Keddie, 1958

**Source description.** Retelling 10. *"A long reply from the Alder Corners Cheese Company in Vessey
County to a graduate student at Farland who had written to ask what four Ordell County farmers' milk
tested on the far side of Redlow Ridge."* Headed "Alder Corners, Vessey County." Salutation "Dear
Miss Keddie," signed "Yours very truly, Rosalie Cudd." **This is the primary source behind r04's
third record set and r07's Item 13.**

**Opening.** "Your letter came Tuesday and I have read it twice, **once standing up in the make room
with my hands wet and once properly after supper**, and I am glad you wrote. **In thirty-odd years
nobody from over the ridge has ever asked me a plain question about our books**, and here is a young
woman from the college who asks one in four lines and apologizes for troubling me. You are not
troubling me. Sit down and I will tell you the whole of it."

**The factory and her own history.**
- "**My father, Jerome Cudd, built this factory in nineteen thirteen, on the flat below the church,
  because there was spring water on the lot and a hard road out to the east.**" (**CONFLICT: r09 and
  r12 both say 1914.**)
- "**He made cheese here until he died in 1941, in March, between the setting and the cutting of a
  vat, which he would have arranged that way if he had been asked.**"
- "**I took the factory over that same spring and I have had it since — seventeen years now**, and I
  have changed the boiler and the roof and nothing else." (1941 + 17 = 1958.)
- "**I was a girl of twelve when the first vat was set** and I was drying hoops before I was tall
  enough to see into one, so when I say I know what the milk did here, I am not going on anybody's
  word." (Born ~1901.)
- **SHE IS JEROME CUDD'S DAUGHTER, first person.** This settles the r04-vs-r07 conflict: Ivy Keddie's
  "Jerome Cudd's niece" is wrong; the catalogue's "from its founder to his daughter in 1941" is
  right; r12 also says daughter.

**The ridge.**
- "**Four miles of switchback, cut for a lumber road and never much improved since. It shuts in
  November and it does not open before April, some years May**, and there is no arguing with it —
  the drifts sit in the cut below the top and stay there after everything else is bare."
- "**So no man from your valley ever hauled to me in winter, whatever the price was. It was not
  possible.**" (Which is why the Alder Corners comparison is summer-only — and therefore why it
  cannot be answered by "summer flush" objections of the Kepp sort: the *station's* own figures were
  3.61 in those same summers.)

**The four Hessel Bottom men.**
- "**From 1915, four men off Hessel Bottom hauled their milk over to me in the summer flush. They
  came from about the first of June to the middle of September, and the rest of the year they went
  down to your station at Ostrey Hollow, which was the only sensible thing to do.**"
- Reasons, "plain enough and none of them mysterious": **cheese paid better than butter in a flush
  month; she/her father was buying hard in flush because a cheese factory can use everything you can
  carry it; and while the road was open her gate was a shorter pull for those four farms.**
- "**They came for sixteen seasons. The last of those summers was 1930.**"
- The picture: "**They started before daylight to have the cans on my platform by seven**, because
  milk that has been four miles down a switchback in a wagon on a July morning is not improving
  while it stands, and they came in with the horses steaming and the tarps wet with the cloud that
  sits on the top of the ridge till the sun is well up. **One of them brought his boy, who used to
  sit on my scale platform and be weighed for a joke. That boy is a man in the state road department
  now and does not remember me.**"

**How her father tested.**
- "**He tested himself, on his own bench, in the little room off the intake, with his own glassware
  that he bought new and washed in the evening and kept in a rack he had made, and he wrote his own
  book.**"
- "**He never compared a figure of his with anybody else's, and I want to be honest with you about
  the reason: it never entered his head.** He had no business with what your station made of the
  same milk. A cheesemaker tests what comes in his door so he knows what he is paying for and what
  his vat will do. **He is not keeping a check on another man's arithmetic seven miles off over a
  mountain, and if you had suggested it to my father he would have laughed at you.**"
- (Note: Jerome Cudd bought his own glassware new and used it for his own purposes — an entirely
  independent instrument, which is what makes the comparison decisive.)

**Her father's own words, end of the summer of 1919, "copied exactly":**

> *The four Hessel Bottom men, summer of 1919: 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground,
> whatever they say over the ridge.*

- "**That last clause is my father all over.** He had heard what was said in Ordell County about that
  meadow — that the wet hay of the bottom thinned the cream, and that the men who farmed it were
  bringing in poor stuff and had better drain their land before they complained. **He thought it was
  nonsense, and he wrote so in his own book where nobody would ever read it, which is where he
  generally put his opinions.**"
- **His maxim:** "**the Ordell people had a theory about that bottom the way some families have a
  ghost: it explained things, and it cost nothing, and nobody was ever going to test it.**"

**Her testimony on the milk.**
- "**That milk was good milk. Season after season those four men came in at about three-eighty, and
  not one of them ever gave me an hour's trouble in the vat.**"
- "I have set the figures out for you at the end of this letter, every summer of the sixteen, and you
  will see they sit **between 3.78 and 3.82, year in, year out, wet years and dry ones, whether the
  meadow had been under water in May or hard as a road.** They are **the season averages of the four
  men taken together, copied straight off the leaves; I have not smoothed anything and I have not
  thrown out a season.**"
- "If you want the men separately I can copy those too, but you will find **they are within a few
  hundredths of each other and always were**, which is what you would expect of four herds off one
  piece of ground."

**On water — the cheesemaker's answer.**
- "**A cheesemaker knows watered milk inside a day.** He does not need a test to know it, though the
  test will tell him too. **The curd comes soft and it will not knit; you get a slack, weak body and
  your yield is down, and you can stand at the vat and see your money going out with the whey.**"
- "**It cannot be hidden from the man who makes the cheese, because the cheese tells him, and it
  tells him the next morning, not next year. Butter is more forgiving that way. Cheese is a
  witness.**"
- "**In sixteen summers my father never made one complaint of those four men, never docked one of
  them a cent, and never — not once that I heard, and I was in the room — said a word about their
  milk except that he wished he had more of it in July.**"
- (This is the single strongest independent refutation of the watered-milk theory, stronger even than
  Strawn's lactometer, because it is a physical process test rather than an instrument reading, run
  daily for sixteen summers by a man with a commercial interest in catching it.)

**On the Ordell quarrel.**
- "I am aware, of course, that there was trouble in your valley for years about the milk at the head
  of it, **because trouble carries over a ridge better than anything else does. It came to me the way
  weather comes, in pieces and late, and always with somebody's name attached to it. I paid no
  attention.** It was not my county, they were not my patrons in September, and I had a make to get
  out."
- "**If I sound short about Ordell it is only that I have listened to forty years of your valley
  explaining that meadow to itself, and I never once heard anybody go up there and look.**" (The
  fifth independent voice on that point — with Effie Loomis, Ansel Keddie, Vira Toland, and, from the
  other end, Hobart Sill complaining nobody ever came *down* to watch him make butter.)
- "**I do not know what your station made of the same cows, and I have not the first idea what your
  county decided about any of it. I never saw those figures and I would not know how to get them.
  All I can give you is our side of the ridge, honestly copied, and you are welcome to it, and
  welcome to print it if it is any use to you.**"

**Closing invitation.** "Come over in the summer and see the place. The road is open by then, mostly,
and I will give you your dinner and show you my father's book, **which is a nicer object than
anything I have said about it here.**"

## 11. RETELLING 11 — Leland Vaught, introduction and method to Bulletin No. 471, State Agricultural College at Farland, October 1958

**Source description.** Retelling 11. *"The opening and method sections of the bulletin, written for
extension agents and county workers, closing with the bulletin's own concluding paragraph reproduced
entire."* Signed "— L. VAUGHT." He is professor of dairy husbandry, and (per r04) the co-author with
Ivy Keddie of the bulletin's closing paragraph.

**§I. Why this bulletin was written.**
- "This station has been asked, at intervals for fifty years, why the butterfat tests at one skimming
  station in Ordell County ran below those of its own creamery seven miles down the same valley. **The
  question has been answered at various times by reference to the land and by reference to the men.
  It is answered here by reference to a piece of glass.**"
- "**The answer is not complicated, and its simplicity is itself part of the history. Nobody in that
  valley lacked the intelligence to find it. What they lacked was any occasion to suspect the one
  article in the plant that nobody in the trade is in the habit of suspecting.**"
- The takeaway for the extension worker who reads no further: "**the milk pipette is the one
  instrument in the butterfat test which the plant cannot check against anything else, and it is the
  instrument on which every figure in the plant depends.**"

**§II. The instrument, and why the neck of the bottle can be marked in percent.**
- **The four operations:** (1) a measured charge of milk is drawn into a pipette and delivered into a
  test bottle; (2) sulfuric acid is added and dissolves everything but the fat; (3) the bottle is spun
  in a hand tester and the liberated fat rises into the graduated neck; (4) the operator reads the fat
  off directly, in percent.
- "**Notice the word *directly*. The operator weighs nothing.** He reads the length of a column of fat
  in a narrow neck and calls it a percentage, and he is entitled to do so for one reason only: **the
  charge of milk that went into that bottle is a fixed and known quantity. The maker rules the neck
  for that quantity and no other. Every mark on it means 'this much fat, out of the standard
  charge.'**"
- "**The standard charge in this state, and on the glassware here examined, is 17.6 cubic
  centimeters.**"
- "Consider then what follows if the pipette delivers less than its mark. Less milk goes into the
  bottle; less fat is liberated; a shorter column stands in the neck; and the neck, which knows
  nothing of the substitution, is read off in the ordinary way. **The reading is not merely low. It is
  low *in the exact proportion by which the charge was short*.**"

**The three consequences** — "I set them out separately because **in fifty years of this history
nobody appears to have held all three in mind at once**."
1. "**First, the error is proportional and not additive.** It does not remove a fixed number of points
   from every test. It removes a fixed *fraction*. **Rich milk therefore loses more than poor milk,
   absolutely, and the ratio between any two tests made on the same short glass is undisturbed. A
   station using such glass will show a perfectly orderly, internally consistent set of figures. Its
   herds will rank correctly against one another. Only against the outside world is it wrong.**"
2. "**Second, the error is precise.** A leaking pipe or a slipping scale gives a scattered error, and
   scatter attracts attention. **A pipette that delivers 16.27 c.c. where it is blown 17.6 delivers
   16.27 c.c. every time, for a quarter of a century, in January and in July, for a careful operator
   and a careless one. It has the appearance of excellent work.**"
3. "**Third — and this is the reason the thing ran so long — the error is invisible from inside the
   plant.** Every other element of the test can be verified against a standard. **The scales can be
   checked with test weights. The tester's speed can be counted. The acid can be checked for strength.
   The bottle's graduation can be checked with mercury. But the pipette is not checked against
   anything, because in the ordinary practice of the trade *the pipette is the standard*. It is what
   the plant measures with. An instrument that is itself the measure cannot be caught by measuring
   with it. It can be caught only by a burette brought in from outside, and burettes reached the
   state's field men very late in this history.**"

**§III. The material examined — RIDDLED WITH ERRORS.**
- "Of the lot bought for the Ostrey Hollow station in 1897 — **five dozen** pipettes were purchased"
  (**the invoice, r07 Item 2, and Ivy Keddie both say SIX dozen / 72**).
- "**nine** were recovered from the station's condemned crate, unbroken and legible, and these nine
  constitute the material of this bulletin. Their stems are blown **17.6 c.c.**"
- "They were delivered against a calibrated burette, five runs to each pipette, and **the mean
  delivery of the nine is 16.27 c.c.**" (**Ivy Keddie's signed sheet of 9 March 1958 and the curator's
  Item 5 both give 16.72 — Vaught has transposed the digits, and everything in his §V descends from
  the transposition.**)
- "**The station's own glass went into use on the first of May, 1898**, and remained in use until the
  state inspector condemned the lot in June of 1923. Before the earlier date, and after the later, the
  station's tests were made on other glass." (**1 May 1897 per the office file, per Ivy, and per this
  same bulletin's own concluding paragraph, which Vaught reproduces four sections later.**)
- **The method, set out so it may be repeated:** "The pipette is filled to its mark with water at room
  temperature, the tip is wiped, and the contents are delivered in the ordinary working manner — **the
  same interval of drainage, the same touch of the tip to the wall of the receiver** — into a burette
  of certified graduation. Five deliveries are made from each pipette and the readings averaged. **The
  whole of it occupies an afternoon and requires no apparatus a creamery does not own, save the
  burette. There is no reason why every station in this state should not be checked once in this way,
  and several reasons in this history why it should be.**"

**§IV. The interval of 1911, "which is worth more than the arithmetic."**
- "**I would ask the reader to weigh what follows above anything computed in this bulletin.**"
- "For nine weeks in the summer of 1911 the station's own pipette was broken and a borrowed measure
  was used in its place. **The patrons were the same patrons; the herds were the same herds; the
  pasture, the season, the acid, the bottles, the tester and the tester's habits were all unaltered.
  One variable changed.** The station's average test rose from 3.61 to 3.80 and held there for the
  nine weeks, and returned to 3.61 on the day the station's own measure came back into service. **This
  is recorded contemporaneously, in the operator's hand, by a man who did not know what he was
  recording and set a question mark against it.**"
- "**I have spent my working life urging students to prefer an experiment to a calculation. Here is
  one that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and lay unread for
  forty-seven years.**" (1911 + 47 = 1958.)
- "**It disposes of every explanation that looks to the herds, to the ground or to the men, for the
  sufficient reason that none of those was altered in those nine weeks, and the figures were.**"
- (Note that §IV uses **3.61 → 3.80**, i.e. the 0.19 gap and the 0.95 ratio — which contradicts §V's
  own arithmetic in the same document.)

**§V. Magnitude — the section built on the transposed number.**
- "**The ratio of delivered to marked capacity is 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244.**"
- "**The recorded station average over the affected years is 3.61; the corresponding true average is
  3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.905, a shortfall of 0.295 of a point. Applied to the 40,000,000 lb of milk
  received at the station in the twenty-five full delivery years, this yields 118,000 lb of butterfat
  delivered and not credited.**"
- **His own flag, left unresolved:** "**It should be said that this figure is not readily reconciled
  with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the glassware.**"
  (He notices that his 3.905 does not match the 3.80 the station actually recorded on good glass in
  1924, and prints the objection without acting on it.)
- "The corresponding quantity appears on the Association's own side of the account. Its ledgers
  carried, annually, a credit for fat received at the creamery in excess of fat credited to patrons at
  the station, under the heading 'station gain.' **The gain is five and a quarter percent of the
  butterfat actually delivered.**" (**Same slip as Teague's: 5¼% is of the fat *credited*; 5% is of the
  fat delivered. Ivy Keddie and the curator both state it correctly.**)

**§VI. The question of feed.** Bulletin No. 214 quoted, identical text:

> *Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five
> hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture.*

- "**Five hundredths in winter; nothing at all in summer. The discrepancy under examination is present
  in every month of the year and is some six times the largest effect the trial could produce under
  conditions harsher than any farmer would impose.**" ("Six times" follows from his inflated 0.295;
  Ivy Keddie, on the correct 0.19, said "four times too small and… the wrong shape.")

**§VII. Conclusion of the bulletin** (reproduced entire, identical to r04 and r07 Item 15):

> *No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897,
> to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its
> pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of
> butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain.*

**THE DOCUMENT CONTRADICTS ITSELF ON ITS OWN LAST PAGE.** Vaught's §III and §V give 16.27 c.c., five
dozen pipettes, glass in use from 1 May 1898, a ratio of 0.9244, a true average of 3.905, a shortfall
of 0.295, and 118,000 lb. The concluding paragraph he prints verbatim on the same sheet gives **one
part in twenty** (= 0.95, i.e. 16.72/17.6), **1 May 1897**, and **76,000 lb**. Ivy Keddie's signed
measurement sheet — the primary record — says 16.72. The correct figures are hers; the bulletin's
prose introduction is where the corruption entered.

## 12. RETELLING 12 — Dell Rundle, schoolteacher, "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years," jubilee pamphlet, 1939 (reprinted 1959)

**Source description.** Retelling 12. *"Printed at Larrow Green for the Association's fiftieth year,
compiled from the recollections of old members and two winters of asking questions at the store;
reissued in 1959 with one footnote and the author's closing note unaltered."* Signed "D. RUNDLE,
Larrow Green."

**TO THE MEMBERS.**
- "Fifty years is not long for a country, but it is a long time for a valley to keep one thing going,
  and that is what this pamphlet is about."
- "I have taught the school at the Green a good many years now and I have listened to this history in
  pieces, **at funerals and in the back of the store**, for the whole of them."
- **His method statement, which is also his warning label:** "**What follows is not a record. It is
  what the valley remembers, tidied, and where the valley is certain of a thing I have written it
  down as certain.**"

**I. THE MEN OF 1889.**
- 1889: the farmers met at Larrow Green and organized the Association. "They were tired of hauling
  butter to Ashlin and taking whatever the merchant chose to allow them for it, and they had worked
  out — the way farmers do, slowly and then all at once — that **the way to be paid properly for a
  thing is to own the place that buys it**."
- "They met **in the schoolhouse, in cold weather**, and the older men who were there remembered
  afterward chiefly that **it went on very late and that somebody's horse got loose in the middle of
  it**."
- **Gideon Vose** elected first president. "**A heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of
  the law and thought better of it**, and he wrote the by-laws himself, at his own kitchen table, **in
  a hand that the Association's clerks were still complaining about thirty years later**."
- "**His nephew Selby Vose, afterward manager of the Association, used to say that his uncle wrote
  by-laws the way other men build stone wall — slowly, and to last.**" (**NEPHEW — siding with Vira
  Toland against Effie Loomis and against Selby's own "My father, who was your first president."
  Note the internal oddity: Rundle attributes the saying to Selby himself and has Selby calling
  Gideon "his uncle."**)
- 1891: "the creamery was built at Larrow Green, **on the creek below the mill**, and the churn was
  set up in it, and **the valley began to be paid in cash once a month, which had never happened to
  it before**. There are men still living who can tell you what the first check did in their mother's
  kitchen, and they will tell you whether you ask or not."

**II. THE RULE THAT MADE THE TEST THE MASTER.** Article VII printed whole (identical text to r02 and
r07).
- "**Article VII dates from 1891, the year the churn was set up.**" (**r02 says adopted at the annual
  meeting of 1892; r15's Grigg says payment by test "came in with the 1893 meeting." The printed
  by-laws carry no year, per r07 Item 1 — so nothing settles it.**)
- "**It was thought a hard rule at the time and there were men who left the Association over it.** It
  is a just rule. A man who keeps good cows and feeds them ought not to be paid the same as the man
  who keeps poor ones, and **the pound of milk is a liar where the pound of butterfat is not**."
- "But mark the last clause, because the valley has had fifty years to learn what it means. *The test
  of the station where he delivers shall govern.* Not the test of the creamery. Not any test but the
  one made at his own door. **Gideon Vose put that in for convenience and it was convenient for
  thirty years, and it is the sentence on which the whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward
  turned.**"

**III. THE STATION AT OSTREY HOLLOW.**
- "The Hollow is seven miles up from the Green, and **in the nineties the pull down was three hours in
  mud and could not be done at all in March**. So the Association built a skimming station up there,
  and **it opened in 1896**: the milk was weighed and tested and separated on the spot, the cream came
  down to the Green by wagon, and the skim went home to the calves and the pigs the same morning. **It
  was a great convenience and the Hollow was glad of it.**"
- "The building is a plain one, **boarded and battened, with the intake room at the road end and a
  loft over it that I believe has never been cleared out since it was put up**; and behind it the
  creek, which **in a wet April comes up to the sill and has twice come over it**." (Written 1939 —
  the crate had been in that loft sixteen years and had nineteen more to sit.)

**IV. THE TROUBLE AT THE HEAD OF THE VALLEY.**
- "**I would rather not write this chapter and it cannot be left out.**"
- From 1902 the Hollow men complained; **they were not believed**.
- 1908: "the Association sent a committee up **under Nyle Grigg**, which sampled the herds of Hessel
  Bottom **through the winter** and reported that the wet hay of that bottom thinned the cream, and
  that the remedy lay with the land. **The Hollow did not accept it.**"
- 1919: "the manager circulated a warning to the Hollow patrons against adding water to their milk,
  and **after that the two ends of this valley did not speak civilly to one another for six years**."
- His schoolmaster's observation: "**I have had the children of both ends in one schoolroom through
  those years and I can tell you that it came down to them entire, without a word of it being
  explained, the way a debt does.**"
- **THE FOLK VERDICT, printed as the valley's certainty:** "**It is the settled opinion of the valley
  that cream left that station by the back door, and the figures went right when the man who kept it
  did not.** I set that down as I have heard it set down a hundred times, by men who were there and
  men who were not, **and I have never heard it contradicted at the store.**"
- "**The state inspector condemned the station's glassware on the twelfth of July, 1923, for reasons
  of his own that had nothing to do with the quarrel, and new glass was put in.**" (**12 July — the
  same error as Vose's printed 1926 address, against Strawn's own 12 June. Note also Rundle's
  editorial aside "for reasons of his own that had nothing to do with the quarrel," which is exactly
  backwards: it had everything to do with it.**)
- "**In January of 1925 the Hollow patrons petitioned the Association. The station took in, over the
  years complained of, forty-four million pounds of milk.**" (44,000,000 — Teague's figure, but
  Teague scoped his to "over the years the account ran," not to the years complained of.)
- "**The patrons' loss upon it was nineteen thousand six hundred dollars, and their petition set the
  figure out to the dollar.**" (**$19,600 — vs Ivy Keddie's $19,000. And r13's Frick says the
  patrons' own reckoning was 67,000 lb of butterfat.**)
- "**A board of arbitration sat at Ashlin that spring under Alonzo Frick, attorney, of that town. It
  heard everybody. It found nothing established, it made an award to the patrons without admitting
  anything, and it sent this valley home to live with itself, which the valley has done, after a
  fashion, ever since.**"

**V. OVER THE RIDGE.**
- "**in 1914 the Alder Corners Cheese Company was opened over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County, by Jerome
  Cudd**, and… in the summers a few of our Hessel Bottom men hauled over the mountain to him rather
  than down to their own station. **That was resented here.**"
- "**It is Jerome Cudd's daughter, Rosalie Cudd, who makes the cheese at Alder Corners now.**"
  (Daughter — agreeing with r07 and r10 against r04's "niece.")

**FOOTNOTE ADDED TO THE 1959 REPRINT** (the only change to the text):

> *Added to the 1959 reprint.* The Hollow matter has since been taken up again at the college at
> Farland by Miss Ivy Keddie, **who is second cousin to Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom**. **Eleven
> pipettes** out of the station's old condemned crate were found still to be in existence. I am too
> old to rewrite a pamphlet and I refer the reader to the college. — D.R.

- (**"Second cousin" agrees with Roy Brant's own account in r09 and corrects Vira. "Eleven pipettes"
  conflicts with the nine of Ivy's sheet, the curator's Item 5, and Vaught's bulletin.**)

**VI. FIFTY YEARS.**
- "Set against the quarrel, put the rest: **fifty Junes of cream coming down that road in the early
  cool, fifty Januaries of it going down over ice with the horses roughshod, a check every month in
  kitchens that had known none, and a creamery still standing that was built by men with nothing but
  each other and a piece of paper they had written themselves. Whatever this valley did wrong at the
  head of it, it did this right, and it did it for half a century, and the men of 1889 would think we
  had done well.**"

**NOTE ADDED WHILE IN PRESS — THE MOST IMPORTANT PASSAGE IN THE WHOLE SET AFTER THE MEASUREMENT
SHEET.**
- "**While this pamphlet was being set up in type I was allowed to look at the Association's old
  ledgers in the office, on another errand altogether.**"
- He quotes the station-gain entry verbatim (identical to r05 and r07 Item 6): *Ostrey Hollow: butter
  fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station
  cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the
  general account.*
- "**I must go back on Chapter IV. Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green LIGHT. It
  reached the Green HEAVY, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand. I leave it as written and
  correct it here.**"
- **Significance.** In **1939**, nineteen years before Ivy Keddie's measurement, a schoolteacher who
  wandered into the ledger on an unrelated errand refuted the cream-theft verdict by pure logic in
  three sentences, printed the refutation in a pamphlet distributed to the whole membership — and it
  changed nothing. The pamphlet was even *reprinted in 1959*, the year of Ivy's talk, with the note
  "unaltered." Vira Toland was still telling the cream-stealing version in 1979.
- Note also what Rundle did **not** conclude: he showed that cream was not stolen, but he did not ask
  where the surplus came from, so the discovery stopped one step short of the glass.

## 13. RETELLING 13 — Alonzo Frick, "Recollections of the Ordell Arbitration," yearbook of the Ordell County Bar Association, 1946

**Source description.** Retelling 13. *"Recollections of the Ostrey Hollow arbitration by the attorney
who sat as chairman of the board, with the exhibit list reprinted from his own file."*

**Who he is and why he wrote.**
- "Our editor has asked the older men of this bar for something out of their own practice, and **at
  seventy-four** I take him to mean me." (Born ~1872; he was ~53 at the 1925 hearing.)
- "**I have tried titles and boundaries for forty years, and two homicides, and none of them has
  stayed with me as the Ostrey Hollow matter has.**"
- "**It was not litigation at all. It was an arbitration under the by-laws of a creamery association,
  with no appeal from it, no reported decision, and nothing whatever to cite it for; and I have
  thought about it oftener than about anything I ever argued in a courthouse.**"

**The complaint.**
- The Association pays for the butterfat and not the weight; the test of the station where the man
  delivers is the test that governs him. "**I say nothing against the rule. But it makes the test the
  whole of the bargain, and it leaves a dissatisfied man with no remedy on earth except to quarrel
  with a figure.**" (Frick reaches Effie Loomis's "hinge" conclusion independently.)
- The station described: milk weighed in, a sample drawn for each patron, cream sent down the valley,
  skim milk handed back.
- "**The patrons of that station had been paid, year upon year, on an average test of 3.16**, and the
  milk of the rest of the valley tested better than theirs." (**3.16 — matching Teague's memoir figure
  and conflicting with the station book's 3.61. Note that Teague testified before Frick, so the two
  may not be independent.**)
- "**Three and sixteen is not a scandalous figure standing by itself. It becomes one when the man over
  the hill is paid on a better figure for milk out of cows no different from yours; when the
  difference holds steady for a quarter of a century; and when nobody in the office can tell you the
  cause of it.**"

**The petition and the board.**
- **January 1925**: the patrons petitioned. "The by-laws provided for arbitration, and **the officers,
  whatever else may be said of them, did not resist it.**"
- "**A board of three was constituted and I was named its chairman, I have always supposed because I
  was the one of the three who kept no cow.**"
- "**We sat three times. The first session was at the Association's office at Ashlin on the seventeenth
  of March, 1925. The second and the third I asked to have at Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's
  own weigh room, so that the board might see the plant it was to pass upon instead of hearing it
  described by men with an interest in the description. That was my doing and I am still content with
  it.**" (**NEW AND SIGNIFICANT: the hearing was not wholly at Ashlin, as r02 and r08 imply. Two of
  three sessions were held in the station itself.**)
- "**Every physical appliance of that station was before us and we satisfied ourselves upon it. We saw
  the scales tried. We saw the sampling done and the test made**, and I will say plainly that **I did
  not understand the test then and do not understand it now; I am a lawyer and the thing is
  chemistry. But I could see that it was done the same way twice, and that the men who did it were not
  hiding their hands.**"
- **THE DEEPEST IRONY IN THE SET:** the board went to the station specifically to inspect the
  apparatus, watched a test made — **in 1925, on the new glass that had been in use since July 1923**.
  The instrument they were sent to examine had already been destroyed as evidence and replaced. What
  they watched was accurate, and proved nothing about 1897–1923.

**The three explanations, with who put each.** "it is worth setting down who put each of them, because
**the three did not sit easily together and nobody seemed to mind that they did not**."
1. **WATER — the manager's, Selby Vose.** "He had circulated a warning to those patrons on the subject
   some years before and he stood by it before us: that the Hollow's milk came in thinned, that a
   little water in a can is the oldest trick in the trade, and that a station cannot test what is not
   there. **He put it without heat and without proof.**"
2. **THE TESTER TAKING CREAM — the bookkeeper's, Wilbur Teague.** "**put by the bookkeeper, Wilbur
   Teague, in the way an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it — he was asked about
   the station's account and answered at length about how such an account would look if a man were
   helping himself, and then observed that he made no charge against anybody. There is no
   cross-examination that reaches a witness of that kind, and I did not attempt one.**"
3. **THE HAY.** "**Somebody read the report of the 1908 committee into the record**, and the bog meadow
   at Hessel Bottom was before us for the better part of an afternoon — the wet ground, the coarse hay
   off it, the cows that eat it all winter. **It is an old opinion in that valley and it had the
   advantage of blaming nobody in the room.**"
- (This confirms and names what Effie Loomis would only count: "Three explanations were argued before
  it. None of the three was accepted." The earlier notes' surmise was exactly right — hay, water,
  cream — and **the glass was not among them and was never named**.)

**Keddie before the board.**
- "Ansel Keddie had tested at that station since it opened in 1896 and had kept the book in his own
  hand every season since. He made a statement to the board on the seventeenth of March. He said he
  had tested as he was taught and as the state man had seen him do it, **thirty-one times a week for
  twenty-nine seasons**, that he could not tell us why the figures were what they were, and that he
  had never taken a pound of cream out of that station."
- "**I believed him then and I believe him now. His denial was accurate and it did him no good at all,
  and the reason it did him no good is that a man who cannot account for a figure is presumed by
  everybody in the room to be the figure's author.**"
- **NOT IN THE MINUTES — Keddie's private view:** "**In the corridor at Ashlin, Keddie told me
  privately that he had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the room. He
  was not a bitter man about it. He had lived among them thirty years and would not stand up and
  accuse his neighbors, and I record it here because it has been said since that he went to his grave
  accusing nobody, which is true, and that he suspected nobody, which is not.**"
- (Sets against r03's "I carry no charge against any man who put his name to that paper" — that was a
  charge withheld from the *signers of the circular*, i.e. the officers, and is not the same as
  believing the patrons innocent. It also cuts against Emmet Brant's grievance in r09: Keddie did not
  stand up for the patrons because he privately thought they were watering. Both men were wrong, and
  the estrangement was built on two mistaken beliefs.)

**The dismissal and the award.**
- "**On the thirtieth of April, four days before we made our award, the Association dismissed him.
  There was no finding against him from us and there never was one; the board had not concluded and
  did not conclude that he had done anything whatever. He was simply let go while the board was still
  sitting, and I have never been able to think well of the Association for it.**"
- "On the fourth of May, 1925, the board found no cause established and awarded the Ostrey Hollow
  patrons thirty-eight hundred dollars, without admission, to be charged to the station account."
- "**The patrons' own reckoning of what they had lost was sixty-seven thousand pounds of butter fat
  over the years complained of, and nothing was ever brought forward to disturb that figure.**"
  (**67,000 lb — against Ivy Keddie's 76,000 lb; possibly another digit transposition, possibly the
  patrons' own conservative estimate. r12 gives their money claim as $19,600 "set out to the
  dollar."**)
- "**We were not able to say who owed it or why, and we said so. An award without a finding is an
  unsatisfactory thing and every member of that board knew it.**"

**THE EXHIBITS — reprinted from his own file, exactly as made up:**

> EXHIBITS BEFORE THE BOARD OF ARBITRATION, OSTREY HOLLOW MATTER, 1925
>
> 1. The test book of the Ostrey Hollow station, 4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924, in the hand of Ansel Keddie.
> 2. The receiving book of the said station.
> 3. The private weigh book of Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom, begun 1 January 1897.
> 4. The circuit notebook of the state dairy inspector for the year 1923.

- "**Four books and nothing else. It is a short list for a matter of that size, and I have looked at it
  many times since.**"
- **WHAT THE EXHIBIT LIST PROVES:**
  - **Exhibit 4 is Strawn's 1923 notebook — the volume containing the entry of 12 June 1923: "Ran their
    milk pipettes against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot,
    the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new."** The answer was physically in the
    room, in writing, in evidence, and nobody read it for what it was. Effie Loomis's "the crate was
    never mentioned in the hearing at all" is true of the *crate*; it is not true of the *record of its

  - **Exhibit 1 credits the station test book to ANSEL KEDDIE** — independently confirming that r07's
    catalogue heading "Item 3. Station test book, kept by E. Brant" is a misattribution.
  - **Exhibit 3 credits the weigh book to EMMET BRANT** — independently confirming that r07's "Item 4.
    Farm weigh book, kept by A. Keddie" is the other half of the same swap.
  - **Exhibit 2, "the receiving book of the said station," is a fifth book** not otherwise described in
    any retelling; not to be confused with Hobart Sill's receiving book at Larrow Green (r14, r07 Item

  - Exhibit 3's date, 1 January 1897, conflicts with Roy Brant's own "first of January, 1898."

**Reflection.** "**A board of arbitration can only decide between the accounts that are put to it.
Three were put to us; each of them was urged by a man of standing; each of them would have been
believed by half the valley; and not one of them was proved. I have wondered since whether the answer
was in that room and simply was not in any of the three stories, and whether a lawyer who understood
chemistry might have found it. I did not understand chemistry. I understood that an honest man was
ruined by a figure nobody could explain, and that we paid his neighbors money without being able to
tell them what they were being paid for.**"

## 14. RETELLING 14 — Hobart Sill, buttermaker (ret.), remarks recorded at the Larrow Green creamery on the day he left it, 1934

**Source description.** Retelling 14. *"Taken down as spoken by a man who made the Association's
butter for forty-one years and had no patience with anybody who did not."* Oral, blunt, unedited.

**Who he is.**
- "**Forty-one years I have stood at that churn. Eighteen ninety-three I came here**, and there was no
  floor in this room but plank, and **we cooled with creek ice out of the pond.** You can write that
  down." (Buttermaker 1893–1934.)
- "**Forty-one years and I never sent out a tub of butter I would not eat myself, which is more than
  the office can say about anything it ever sent out.**"
- "**I am sixty-four years old and I have thought about butter.**" (Born ~1870; came to the creamery at
  ~23.) "**I never went up there but twice in my life.**"

**THE CREAM CAME DOWN HEAVY — his central and unwitting testimony.**
- "**The cream wagon came down from Ostrey Hollow every day of the week but Sunday, seven miles of hill
  road, and in July it came down warm no matter how early they started.** It was set off at the
  platform and it was weighed and it was sampled and it went into the cold room, and I took it from
  there."
- "**Now here is the thing about that cream, and I have said it to men in this room a hundred times and
  none of them ever thought it was worth anything. That cream ran heavy. Every year it ran heavy.
  There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there. The office would send
  down its sheet and the sheet would say so many pounds of fat bought at the station, and the fat that
  came into this room off that wagon was more than the sheet said. Always more. Never less.**"
- "**And I weighed it myself, most years. The platform man weighed it and I weighed it over again when
  it came into the cold room, because I have never in my life taken another man's figure for what was
  going into my churn.** Cans off, cans on the scale, cans into the ice, and the book ruled and signed
  before I put my coat on."
- "**You may go and look at the receiving book if they will let you at it, and you will find my hand on
  every page of it from ninety-three, and you will find the Hollow's cream coming in heavy on page
  after page after page, in my own writing, for as long as I have been in this room.**"
- **HOW HE EXPLAINED IT AWAY:** "**Well, that is cream for you. Cream is not milk and it will not stand
  still and be counted. It is a thick thing and it clings and it separates on you and any man who
  tells you he can put a figure on cream to the pound and hold it is a man who has never had his arm
  in a can up to the elbow. So it ran heavy. So does everybody's. I never made anything of it, and I
  will say plain, nobody ever asked me to.**"
- (**The third man to hold the decisive fact and dissolve it. Teague made the surplus into "furniture";
  Sill made it into "that's cream for you"; Rundle alone, in 1939, reasoned from it correctly — and by
  then Sill was five years gone from the churn.** Note that Sill's independent re-weighing makes the
  surplus a *measured* fact, not a bookkeeping artifact — the strongest possible corroboration of
  Teague's ledger line, and it was never put before the 1925 board.)

**Larrow Green's own average — a number nobody else supplies directly.**
- "**Our own milk, here at Larrow Green, our own patrons, the men on this creek — that ran 3.78 taking
  one year with another right through those years. Three seventy-eight.** I know it because I made the
  butter out of it and **a buttermaker knows what his cream is worth before anybody hands him a paper
  about it.** Ordinary milk, ordinary county, ordinary cows. **There is nothing wonderful about 3.78
  and there is nothing shameful about it either.**"
- (3.78 against the station's recorded 3.61 = **0.17**, which is exactly Roy Brant's figure and matches
  the curator's "a little under two tenths of a point better" for Item 16. It is also within 0.02 of
  the true 3.80 and of Alder Corners' 3.799 — three independent bodies of good glass all landing
  between 3.78 and 3.80.)

**His estimate of the Hollow's milk — which undercuts his own theory.**
- "**The Hollow milk was really a 3.85 and no better, and I will say that to any man in this valley.
  Wet ground up there, but good cows, and I never had a word to say against the cows. A 3.85 and no
  better, and if the figures they were paid on came out under that, then somebody up that hill road
  was helping the cans along, and I said so at the time and I say it now.**"
- (Note what he has just said: his own working estimate of the Hollow's true milk, **3.85**, is *above*
  Larrow Green's 3.78 and above the true 3.80 — i.e. the buttermaker who handled both believed the
  Hollow's milk was the *better* milk. He then attributes the shortfall to water anyway. He also
  contradicts the entire wet-hay theory in passing: "**Wet ground up there, but good cows… The cows
  were never the trouble.**")

**His water verdict.**
- "You will get it told to you a dozen ways. You have been in the office, I expect, and they have their
  ways of putting it. I put it the one way. **You cannot tell me nine families all got honest in one
  summer. There was water in those cans and I said so at the time.**"
- "**one summer, along in the twenties, those figures came up and they stayed up, and nothing on this
  earth changed but the season, and men do not suddenly start pouring better milk out of the same cows
  in the same barns. Something got stopped. Something got said to somebody. I never knew what and I
  never asked, because the day a buttermaker starts asking about other men's cans is the day he stops
  making butter.**" (The same causal inversion as Vose's — and the same blindness to the July 1923
  glass change.)

**His chronology.**
- "**Nineteen two is the first I remember of it, a man in here shouting that his milk tested under his
  brother-in-law's down the creek and it was the same feed and the same water and what did we mean by
  it.**" (1902, agreeing with Keddie, Roy Brant, Rundle and Grigg.)
- "the manager put out a paper to them about it — **that would be nineteen nineteen** — a circular,
  warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons about water in the milk, plain as that. **I thought well of him
  for it. It was the only plain thing that office ever did, and of course they hated him for it up the
  Hollow, and of course it changed nothing, because a paper never emptied a can.**"

**On the office.**
- "**That is the offices for you. Papers. The Association has a paper for everything and butter for
  nothing.** I have had men come in here in a collar and tell me my churn was running slow when they
  could not have told you which end of a cow gets up first. **I made butter that took premiums in three
  states out of this room, and not one man in that office ever came down here to see how it was done,
  and every one of them could tell you to the half cent what the fat was worth.**"
- (The "nobody ever came and looked" complaint, inverted: at the *other* end of the valley, and about
  the *office* rather than the station.)

**The price of fat.** "**Twenty-seven cents. That is what fat was through those years, take one year
with another, twenty-seven cents the pound, and there is not a man in the office could tell you what
makes it churn.**" (**27c — conflicts with the 25c of Effie Loomis/Teague's annual sheets, Ivy Keddie
and Roy Brant, and with Roy's 24c for 1910. Kepp in r16 also says 27c, so the higher figure was in
circulation.**)

**THE LOAN OF THE GLASS — first-person, and it settles conflict F.**
- "**When they opened that station up at Ostrey Hollow — that was ninety-six, in May, and there was a
  crowd went up for it — they had no outfit of their own. Not a piece. It was ordered and it was not
  come, and they were ready to start taking milk, and there was talk of holding off the whole season.
  And I said no, they can have mine. I went to my own bench in this room and I made up an outfit for
  them out of my own glass, my own, that I had used and knew, and it went up the valley on the wagon
  and they opened on it. Their own came the spring after and they sent mine back to me, and one piece
  of it was chipped and I never said a word about that either.**"
- **This confirms the test book's first leaf ("Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes") and
  the curator's office-file gloss ("Larrow Green having lent until then"), and refutes Ansel Keddie's
  own unsent-letter memory that the 1896 glass was "borrowed off the Ashlin creamery."** It also means
  the station's first season, at 3.80, was run on **known, working glass belonging to a man who used it
  daily at the creamery** — which is why 1896 reads true.

**THE FIRST SEASON'S INTAKE — the figure the curator says does not exist.**
- "**That first season they took in about nine hundred thousand pounds up there. May to the end of the
  year, that is, so it was not even a whole one. That is a good deal of milk for a hill station
  starting from nothing, and it was the biggest thing that ever happened to Ostrey Hollow, and I was
  glad enough to have had a hand in it.**"
- (r07's note and query: "**The station's intake for its first season, 4 May to the end of 1896, is not
  in the ledgers and is nowhere else… inquirers wanting a figure for 1896 must be told there is
  none.**" Sill has it — from memory, in 1934, thirty-eight years after the fact, and the curator in
  1972 evidently never saw these remarks.)

**His closing instructions to the man taking it down.**
- "Whatever the Hollow was doing with its cans, and I have told you what I think about that, **the fat
  came down that hill and it came into my churn and it went out of this room as good butter with this
  creamery's name on the tub.**"
- "**Now that is forty-one years and I would like to go home. Put in about the butter. Do not put in
  that I said anything against the cows up there, because I did not. The cows were never the trouble.
  Put in that Hobart Sill made the butter and it was good butter and he stood at the churn until the
  day they gave him a chair.**"

## 15. RETELLING 15 — Nyle Grigg, covering letter of 1919 to the reprinted report of the investigating committee of 1908

**Source description.** Retelling 15. *"A farmer of standing sends his eleven-year-old report back
into circulation because the same complaint has been raised again, and adds a letter saying that the
intervening science has proved him right."* Two documents: the 1919 covering letter, and the 1908
report reprinted below it in full — much fuller than the fair-copy paragraph the curator holds as
r07 Item 11.

**Timing.** The reprint is dated **1919, Larrow Green** — **the same year as the manager's water
circular.** So in 1919 the Hollow patrons received, in the same season, a printed accusation of
watering from the office and a reprinted committee report blaming their land. Both were wrong.

### I. THE COVERING LETTER

- Addressed "To the officers and patrons of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association."
- "**I am asked to let the report of our committee be printed again, the Ostrey Hollow question having
  been reopened this season by men who were boys when we made it.** I consent willingly and I have
  nothing in it to take back. I ask leave only to add a page, because **a report is a dry thing and
  does not say why it was written**, and because **eleven years have brought us some light from a
  quarter that had none to give us in 1908.**"
- **The history as he tells it:** "From about **1902** there had been grumbling out of Ostrey Hollow
  that the milk of that station tested under the milk of the rest of this valley. **It was not one man
  and it was not one season.** It went on and it grew, and it got into the talk at the annual meeting
  and into the talk at every crossroads store between here and Ashlin, and **it took the shape such
  things always take, which is that somebody must be doing it on purpose.**"
- "**By the winter in which we were named, the board could not let it alone any longer without seeming
  to have an interest in letting it alone, and we three were named to look into it. I have never in
  my life been given a duty I liked less.**" (A committee of **three**.)
- **THE FOURTH DATE FOR ARTICLE VII:** "**Payment by test came in with the 1893 meeting, as I have
  always understood it**, and from that year a difference of this kind stopped being a matter of
  opinion and became a matter of money." (r02: 1892. r12: 1891. Grigg: 1893, explicitly hedged with
  "as I have always understood it." The printed by-laws carry no date at all, per r07 Item 1.)
- "**I had known the men of that bottom since I was a young man and I have bought hay off two of
  them.**"
- **HIS METHOD CLAIM — CONTRADICTED BY HIS OWN REPRINTED REPORT:** "We went at it as plain farmers do.
  **We sampled those herds through the whole of 1908 — every month of that year, in the barn and on
  the pasture, out of the night's milk and the morning's, and we did not let a man know which day we
  were coming.** We weighed. We watched the test made and we watched it made again."
  - The report printed immediately below ends: "**We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through
    January, February and March.**" The curator's note on Item 11 says flatly: "**The sampling was of
    January, February and March 1908 only, as the report states.**"
  - **This matters enormously.** If they had truly sampled every month, in the barn *and on the
    pasture*, they would have found the gap unchanged in July on summer grass — which destroys the
    wet-hay theory outright, since the theory depends on the winter hay. Grigg's 1919 memory has
    inflated three winter months into a full year, and thereby converted a fatal weakness into an
    apparent strength.
- **His disclosure of interest:** "I had **no interest whatever in the answer. I am not a patron of
  that station. I haul to Larrow Green and always have**, and whatever the Hollow was paid or not paid
  never put a cent into my pocket nor took one out of it. **I was chosen for the work on that account
  and for no other, and I have been abused for the finding by men on both sides of it, which I take to
  be a fair sign that the finding was an honest one.**" (Honest, and wrong — his impartiality is real
  and irrelevant.)
- **HIS APPEAL TO SCIENCE — THE MISQUOTATION THAT PROPAGATED:** "**But the State Agricultural College
  at Farland has since published its Bulletin No. 214, which was a feeding trial upon bog hay carried
  out under the College's own eye, with weighed rations and no interest in the outcome. That trial
  found the bog hay to depress the butter fat test by TWO TENTHS in the winter feeding. Two tenths,
  gentlemen, out of a College that had never heard of Hessel Bottom and had no reason on earth to
  flatter us. There is our finding, arrived at by another road and by better men, three years after we
  set our names to it.**"
  - **Bulletin 214 actually says: "not more than FIVE HUNDREDTHS of one per cent in winter, and…
    nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."** Grigg inflates 0.05 to 0.20 — a fourfold
    error — **and omits the summer half entirely**, which is the half that kills the theory. 0.20 is
    also, by coincidence, about the size of the real (glass) gap, which is why the misquotation was so
    persuasive.
  - "Three years after we set our names to it" dates Bulletin 214 to **1911**, matching r04 and r07
    Item 12.
  - **The same misquotation reappears verbatim in Dr. Kepp's 1926 paper (r16) — "it found the butter
    fat test depressed by two tenths in the winter feeding" — so the error was still circulating as
    authority fifteen years after the bulletin was printed and seven years after Grigg introduced
    it.**
- **His prescription and his unintended prophecy:** "**Drain that bottom, sweeten it, keep the stock
  off it in the wet of the spring, and feed the coarse hay off it with a mixed ration and not by
  itself, and the Hollow will test with the rest of us. Go on as they are going and abuse the man who
  reads their figures for them, and they will be grumbling in twenty years' time as they were
  grumbling when we began, and no better off.**" (They were: in 1939, and in 1979. And the man who
  read their figures for them was abused, and lost his place for it, six years later.)
- Signed "Respectfully, NYLE GRIGG, Larrow Green."

### II. THE REPORT AS PRINTED IN 1908 (quoted in full)

> REPORT OF THE COMMITTEE APPOINTED TO INQUIRE INTO THE COMPLAINTS OF THE PATRONS OF THE OSTREY
> HOLLOW STATION.
>
> To the President and Directors of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association.
>
> Your committee, appointed in January last, respectfully reports as follows.
>
> The complaint before us is of some years' standing and is in substance this: that the milk
> delivered at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station is returned by the test at a figure below that of
> milk delivered elsewhere in this valley, and that the patrons of that station are in consequence
> paid less for the same labor and the same cows than their neighbors are paid. Since payment by test
> came in among us and the pound of butter fat took the place of the pound of milk as the measure of a
> man's month, a difference of this kind is no longer a matter of opinion. It is a matter of money,
> and it comes out of the same pocket every month, and your committee has approached it in that light
> and not as a quarrel.
>
> Hessel Bottom, where six farms cut hay and turn out stock, is a wet meadow lying along the creek
> under Ostrey Hollow. Its ground is springy the year round and is under water in part every spring.
> Its herbage is sedge, rush, and the coarse sour grasses that come in on such land, and its hay is
> cut late and heavy and is fed as the main dependence of those farms from November to April. There is
> no better winter keep to be had there. We say this without reproach to any man on that bottom; the
> land is what it is, and they took it as their fathers left it to them.
>
> Your committee visited each of the six farms, some of them three and four times. We drew our samples
> ourselves and we sealed them ourselves. We saw the herds milked, we saw the cans filled, and we saw
> them hauled to the station and weighed in at the intake, and we stood by while the test was made and
> saw nothing whatever in the doing of it to remark upon. We tried the station scales against a known
> weight and we tried the patrons' own scales against the station's. We inquired into the water supply
> at each barn and satisfied ourselves upon it.
>
> Your committee further observed the general condition of the stock upon that bottom, as men who have
> kept cattle all their lives are entitled to observe it. The herds are thin in the flank at the end of
> the winter feeding and slow to come to their flesh in the spring. Their coats are rough later in the
> season than the coats of hill herds. They do well enough in flow, for the wet land is generous of
> milk, but it is our opinion, formed on the ground and not out of a book, that what such land gives in
> quantity it withholds in quality, and that a cow cannot put into her pail what her keep has never put
> into her.
>
> We report that we found no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter, and we wish that finding to be
> printed in the same type as the rest.
>
> We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March. The milk is whole
> and the scales are honest. It is our opinion that the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the
> cream, and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station.
>
> NYLE GRIGG, chairman, and the committee.

**Points to note in the 1908 report itself.**
- "**six farms**" on Hessel Bottom — agreeing with Roy Brant (r09) and Kepp (r16), against Effie
  Loomis's seven (r02).
- **They checked the scales — both the station's and the patrons' — and nobody ever checked the
  glass.** This is the same blind spot Vaught later names: the pipette is the standard, so it is not
  among the things one checks.
- "**we stood by while the test was made and saw nothing whatever in the doing of it to remark upon**"
  — watching the *procedure* was, as always, useless: the procedure was correct. The same futile
  observation is made by Strawn (1907, 1911), by the 1925 board (r13), and is precisely what Ansel
  Keddie kept demanding somebody come and do.
- "**We report that we found no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter, and we wish that finding to be
  printed in the same type as the rest.**" — an explicit, emphatic exoneration in 1908, seventeen
  years before Keddie was dismissed for dishonesty.
- The stock-condition observations (thin in the flank, rough coats, slow to flesh) are real and
  genuinely caused by the bog ground — they are just not the cause of the test gap. Kepp builds his
  whole 1926 paper on the same true premise and the same false inference.

**Closing line of the retelling.** "**That report was received and accepted, and the question was
closed by it for eleven years — until this season, and the men who have reopened it have brought
nothing new before us but their impatience.**"

## 16. RETELLING 16 — Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S., "The bog meadows and the milk," paper read before the Ordell County Farm Club, 1926

**Source description.** Retelling 16. *"A veterinary surgeon of thirty years' practice brings the
agricultural literature to bear on an old local grievance, and the discussion from the floor is
appended as taken down by the club's secretary."* By **Roland Kepp, V.S., of Ashlin**. Thirty years'
practice → began ~1896, the year the station opened.

**Opening.**
- "**it is my habit, when I am called to a herd that is doing badly, to ask first what the cattle are
  standing on. I have found in thirty years that the answer to nine cases in ten is under the animal's
  feet, and that the tenth is in the water she drinks.**"
- "I want to put before you this evening a question that has been in this county longer than I have
  practiced in it, and **that has been dealt with hitherto by opinion, by grumbling, and by the naming
  of names. It deserves better. It is a question of soil, of herbage, and of the mineral economy of
  the cow, and I mean to treat it as such.**"
- (Note the shape of the error: he is right that the debate has been conducted by rumor, right to want
  evidence, right about the soil science — and wrong about the case, because the evidence he brings is
  misquoted and the one real experiment he waves away.)

**The land.**
- "**A wet bottom is not a rich meadow with too much water in it. It is a distinct kind of ground with
  a distinct kind of chemistry, and the water is the least part of it.**"
- Where drainage is wanting, the year's growth does not rot down as on a hill field; **it lies sour**,
  and the resulting soil is **acid, poor in lime, and poor in the phosphates**.
- "**On such ground the sweet grasses will not hold. They are crowded out within a few seasons by sedge
  and rush and the coarse tussock grasses, which want nothing better than sour wet land and will take
  it over completely if you let them. That herbage carries bulk and it carries water. What it does not
  carry, in any quantity worth the name, is lime or phosphorus, and it is cut late because such ground
  cannot be got onto early, and late cutting takes out what little was in it.**"

**What the cow does with such keep.**
- "**A cow in milk is a manufactory working on a narrow margin**, and she is obliged to find the
  material of her milk somewhere. **If her ration is short of the minerals, she does not simply give
  less; she robs her own frame to make good, and she goes on robbing it until she cannot.**"
- The consequences he says he has seen in every wet-meadow herd in the county: **thin over the loin and
  rough in the coat coming out of the winter feeding; slow to come to condition in the spring; ready
  subjects for every disorder of the digestion in the fall; they breed later; their feet are worse.**
- "**They will milk, gentlemen. Wet land is generous of flow and no man on a bog meadow ever complained
  that his pail was light. But what is in the pail is another matter, and the fat is the first thing to
  suffer, because the fat is the dearest thing the cow makes and it is the first economy she practices
  when she is pressed.**"

**The measure of it — his own field impression.**
- "**I have gone about this county for thirty years and I have kept my eyes open. The wet bottoms of
  this county yield a milk poorer in fat by a fifth of a point and every practical man knows it. Set
  the hill herds of this county against the bottom herds and you will find that difference, farm for
  farm, wherever you look for it, and the difference does not depend on the breed, on the man, or on
  the barn. It depends on the ground.**"
- (**0.20 — an unmeasured impression, and it happens to land within a hundredth of the real gap, which
  is what makes it feel confirmatory. Note also that his hill-vs-bottom comparison across the county
  would mostly be drawn from plants with sound glass, so any real effect he saw elsewhere is not the
  Ostrey Hollow effect.**)

**Hessel Bottom.**
- "**Hessel Bottom, under Ostrey Hollow, is the completest example of wet meadow in this county. Six
  farms cut hay and pasture stock upon it.** It lies along the creek; the springs come out at the foot
  of the slope and run in it the year round; **a part of it is under water every spring and is not fit
  to be walked on until well into May.** Its hay is sedge hay, and on those farms it is the winter
  dependence and very nearly the whole of it." (Six farms — with r09 and r15 against r02's seven.)
- "**The patrons of the skimming station at Ostrey Hollow have complained for something like a quarter
  of a century that their milk tests below the milk of the rest of the valley. Their complaint is
  perfectly just and I have never doubted it.**"
- "**The figure of the gap, as it has been given to me and as I have satisfied myself, is twenty-one
  hundredths of a point — 0.21 — between the milk of that station and the milk of this valley at
  large.**" (**0.21 is Selby Vose's 1926 figure for the *rise* of 1924, not for the gap. Kepp, speaking
  the same year, has taken a number out of the manager's address and repurposed it. "As it has been
  given to me and as I have satisfied myself" is the whole mechanism of this document in one clause.**)
- "A committee of practical men went over the ground in 1908 and found the herds sound, the milk whole,
  and the scales honest, and laid the cause to the hay of that bottom. **The matter has been before
  committees since and before boards, and it is not settled, and it will not be settled by men shouting
  at one another across a weigh room. It is settled already, in the literature, for anybody who will
  read.**"

**The evidence of the College — the propagated misquotation.**
- "**The State Agricultural College at Farland conducted a feeding trial upon bog hay and published it
  as its Bulletin No. 214. I commend it to every man here. The College fed the bog hay under control
  and measured what it did, and it found the butter fat test depressed by two tenths in the winter
  feeding. That is the whole case. Two tenths from the College, laid alongside the fifth of a point I
  have found in the field and the twenty-one hundredths that station has been complaining of, and the
  three agree so nearly that I do not know what more a reasonable man requires.**"
- **THE FALSE TRIANGULATION.** He presents three converging numbers — 0.20 (the bulletin), 0.20 (his
  own field sense), 0.21 (the station's gap) — and treats the convergence as proof. In fact:
  - the bulletin's actual finding is **0.05 in winter and nothing measurable in summer**, so his first
    number is Grigg's 1919 error repeated at fourfold magnification;
  - his second number is an unrecorded impression, not a measurement;
  - his third number is Vose's figure for something else entirely (the 1924 rise), and the real gap is
    0.19;
  - and all three, being roughly the size of the glass error, would "agree" with anything of that size,
    including the true cause.
  - **He also drops the bulletin's summer clause, exactly as Grigg did** — and the summer clause is
    what makes the hay theory untestable-shaped rather than merely small.

**The objection he waves away — and what it tells us.**
- "**It has been urged upon me, by a gentleman who I know will forgive my noticing it here, that in the
  summer of 1911 the testing at that station was broken off for six weeks, their own apparatus having
  failed them and a borrowed one being used, and that the figures stood higher during those six weeks
  than before or after. Six weeks in the flush of the season, gentlemen. Six weeks of June and July,
  when every herd in the county is at its best and the stock are off the wet ground and up on the
  aftermath. I am astonished that anybody should found an argument on six weeks of summer figures. I
  could produce you six weeks in any year that would prove anything you liked.**"
- **Three things this passage establishes:**
  1. **Somebody was publicly arguing the 1911 interval as evidence in 1926** — thirty-two years before
     Vaught called it the experiment that ran itself. An unnamed gentleman had the right argument and
     brought it to the county farm club, and an expert laughed it off from the platform.
  2. **The interval had reached him as SIX weeks**, the same wrong duration Teague gives in r05 — so
     the six-week version was the one in public circulation, and it is materially more vulnerable to
     Kepp's objection than the true nine weeks.
  3. **His rebuttal collapses on the true dates.** The real interval was **8 May to 10 July** — it
     begins in early May, before the flush, when the cows were still on the winter's bog hay; the
     station's own figures for the *surrounding* Junes and Julys of every other year stand at 3.61 on
     the same pasture; and **the tests fell back to 3.61 on 10 July, in the middle of the flush, on the
     day the station's own measure returned.** Every element of Kepp's seasonal explanation is refuted
     by the very entries he never read.

**The remedy.**
- "**Drain the bottom; open the old ditches and cut new; lime the ground and lime it again; sow it down
  when it will carry a seeding; keep the stock off it in the wet of the spring, when a hoof does more
  harm in a day than a season will repair; and never feed that hay alone. Feed it with a mixed ration
  and a mineral supplement, as the College directs.**"
- "**Fat has been worth twenty-seven cents the pound through these years, and a fifth of a point taken
  off every hundredweight a farm ships, year in and year out, will pay for a great deal of tile.**"
  (27c — agreeing with Hobart Sill, against the 25c of the ledgers. The advice would have cost the
  Bottom farmers a great deal of money to cure a defect that was not on their land.)

**DISCUSSION (appended, as taken down by the club's secretary).**
- *A member* asked **whether the difference might not be in the cows rather than the land**, the bottom
  farms being poorer men with poorer stock.
  - *Dr. Kepp* replied that he had considered it and rejected it; "**that some of the best-bred cattle
    in the county stand on wet land, and some very indifferent cattle on the hills test well; and that
    a man may improve his herd for twenty years and never get his fat up while his ground is sour.**"
- *A member* asked **whether the trouble might lie in the station rather than the bottom, as had been
  said in the valley for years.**
  - *Dr. Kepp* replied "**that he had no interest in that quarrel and no competence in it; that he had
    never in his life read a test and would not know how; but that when a cause is found in the soil,
    in the herbage, in the condition of the stock, in the College's trial, and in every wet bottom in
    the county alike, it is not good sense to go looking for a second cause in a building.**"
  - **THE MOMENT THE RIGHT QUESTION WAS ASKED AND DISMISSED.** An anonymous member of the Ordell County
    Farm Club, in 1926, put the correct hypothesis to the room — the trouble is in the station, not the
    bottom — and was answered by an expert who states in the same sentence that **he has never in his
    life read a test and would not know how.** The dismissal rests on an argument from the coherence of
    a story ("a cause is found in the soil… it is not good sense to go looking for a second cause"),
    which is exactly the reasoning Vaught later diagnoses: the station's figures were internally
    orderly, so the wrongness was invisible from inside the story as well as from inside the plant.
- *The chairman* thanked Dr. Kepp and observed that **the paper would be printed for the members.** (So
  the misquotation of Bulletin 214 got a third printing: Grigg 1908 report, Grigg 1919 letter, Kepp
  1926 paper.)

## 18. PEOPLE AND RELATIONSHIPS

**The Keddie family**
- **Ansel Keddie** — tester at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station. Went up 1895 to ready it; tested the
  first summer; kept the test book in one hand 4 May 1896 – 31 Dec 1924 (29 seasons); tested "thirty-one
  times a week for twenty-nine seasons"; put out 30 April 1925; died 1934, "still under it." Privately
  believed the patrons were at fault but would not say so in the room (r13).
- **Orra Keddie** — his wife. **Emmet Brant's full sister** (same mother and father — r09 rebuts the
  stepmother story). **First cousin of old Mrs. Vose** (r01). Would walk to Larrow Green rather than
  pass her brother's lane; outlived Ansel "a good while" and carried the stigma the whole time.
- **Duncan Keddie** — Ansel and Orra's son; **taught school at Ashlin**; Ivy's father; **first cousin of
  Roy Brant** (r09).
- **Ivy Keddie** — Ansel's granddaughter, Duncan's daughter; graduate student at the **State
  Agricultural College at Farland**; found and measured the pipettes (Feb–Mar 1958); wrote to Rosalie
  Cudd and borrowed the Brant weigh book from Roy Brant; co-wrote Bulletin No. 471's closing paragraph
  with **Professor Vaught**; gave the 1959 talk. **Second cousin of Hazel Brant** (r09, r12).

**The Brant family**
- **Emmet Brant** — born 1854, died 1929. Biggest man on Hessel Bottom: sixty-odd of its 160 acres,
  thirty-eight cows in a good year, largest of the six farms, the station's largest patron in every
  year of record; the man the 1919 letter "cost the most"; **Orra Keddie's brother**; never spoke to
  Ansel again after the circular — because he held that the tester should have publicly defended the
  patrons and did not. Kept the weigh book daily (from 1897 per the catalogue and the exhibit list;
  from 1898 per his son) until the week before he died.
- **Roy Brant** — Emmet's son, b. ~1888; farmer at Hessel Bottom, still milking at 67; kept the weigh
  book up after his father; had it from 1929; lent it to Ivy Keddie in 1958; wrote r09; **first cousin
  of Duncan Keddie**; father of Hazel.
- **Hazel Brant** — Roy's daughter; had the weigh book in 1962; **second cousin of Ivy Keddie**.

**The Vose family**
- **Gideon Vose** — first president of the Association, 1889–1911 (22 years); "heavy, quiet, obstinate…
  had once thought of the law and thought better of it"; drafted the by-laws himself at his kitchen
  table, including Article VII; kept Jerseys at the home place; died 1917.
- **Old Mrs. Vose** — first cousin of Orra Keddie.
- **Selby Vose** — manager 1913–1938; signed the 1919 circular and defended it in 1926; signed the
  annual station sheet every year; supported the $3,800 payment; put the watered-milk explanation to
  the 1925 board "without heat and without proof" (r13). **Son of Gideon per r02 and per his own 1926
  address; nephew of Gideon per r01 (Vira) and r12 (Rundle, who attributes to Selby himself a saying
  about "his uncle").**

**The Association's officers and staff**
- **Effie Loomis** — secretary 1901–1940; kept the minute book in her own hand; recorded the 1925 award;
  wrote the 1941 history; named the two silences (the lost minute book, the unmentioned crate).
- **Wilbur Teague** — bookkeeper 1905–1945; invented the station-gain line in 1905; raised it with Vose
  in 1916 and was told to let it alone; put the cream-taking explanation to the 1925 board; wrote the
  1948 memoir. Gives the station average as 3.16 and the intake as 44 million lb.
- **Hobart Sill** — buttermaker at Larrow Green 1893–1934 (41 years), b. ~1870; kept the churn book and
  the cream receiving book in his own hand from 1893; **lent his own glass so the station could open in
  May 1896**; re-weighed the Hollow's cream himself and recorded it heavy every year; puts Larrow Green
  at 3.78, the Hollow's true milk at 3.85, and fat at 27 cents; believed in the water theory to the
  end. Never went up to the station but twice in his life.

**The state and the college**
- **Merle Strawn** — state dairy inspector, Ordell circuit 1907–1936; 28 pocket notebooks, one per year,
  oilcloth covers; found no added water at Ostrey Hollow on every visit and told the manager so in
  1919; condemned the pipettes 12 June 1923; refused on principle to hold theories.
- **Professor Leland Vaught** — professor of dairy husbandry at Farland; wrote the introduction and
  method of Bulletin No. 471 (Oct 1958) and, with Ivy Keddie, its closing paragraph. His own prose
  carries the transposed 16.27 c.c. and the derived 0.9244 / 3.905 / 0.295 / 118,000 lb, plus "five
  dozen" pipettes and a 1 May 1898 start — all contradicted by the conclusion he prints on the same
  sheet.

**Others**
- **Alonzo Frick** — attorney of Ashlin, b. ~1872; chairman of the 1925 board of arbitration (a board of
  three; "I was the one of the three who kept no cow"); moved the second and third sessions to the
  station; believed Keddie; wrote the 1946 recollections and preserved the exhibit list. Called "as
  fair a chairman as ever a hearing had" (Teague) and "an able and impartial man" (Vose).

## 19. PLACES

- **Ostrey Hollow** — the hollow and hamlet at the head of the valley; the skimming station stood at the
  very top where the road bends and goes to nothing but the ridge; **seven miles up the valley from
  Larrow Green**; in the nineties the pull down was three hours in mud and impossible in March. The
  building: low, whitewashed, boarded and battened, cold as a spring house even in August, smelling of
  milk and steam and hot iron; intake room at the road end, **a loft over it reached by a ladder** that
  was never cleared out; the creek behind it comes up to the sill in a wet April and has twice come
  over. After closing, used for storage — fence wire and salt.
- **Hessel Bottom** — the wet meadow below the hamlet, along the creek; **160 acres**; springs run in it
  the year round; part under water every spring, not walkable till well into May; a soft place by the
  alders that has taken a horse to the hock; sedge, rush and coarse tussock grass; hay cut late, fed
  November to April as the winter dependence. **Six farms had rights on it** (r09, r15, r16) / seven
  (r02). Emmet Brant's the largest, sixty-odd acres and 38 cows.
- **Larrow Green** — the main creamery (built 1891 on Larrow Creek, "below the mill") and where the
  Association was organized in 1889 in the schoolhouse; Selby Vose "down at Larrow Green"; Hobart Sill's
  churn room; Nyle Grigg's and Dell Rundle's home place.
- **Ashlin** — the Association's office (one room over a harness shop); site of the first session of the
  1925 arbitration; Duncan Keddie taught school there; Dr. Kepp practiced there; the Ordell County
  Historical Rooms, the Historical Society and the county bar are there; the merchants the 1889 farmers
  were tired of hauling butter to.
- **Ordell County** — the county; Strawn's circuit was named for it; the Farm Club and the Bar
  Association are its.
- **Vessey County / Redlow Ridge / Alder Corners** — over the ridge. **Redlow Ridge: four miles of
  switchback cut for a lumber road, shut from November to April or May.** The Alder Corners Cheese
  Company stands on the flat below the church, chosen for spring water on the lot and a hard road east.
  The Vessey County Historical Society has held the test book since 1970.
- **Farland** — the State Agricultural College; its library holds Strawn's notebooks from 1947; Ivy
  Keddie measured the pipettes there; Bulletins 214 and 471 were issued from it.
- **Tarnet** — where Ammon & Sons Dairy Supply was.

## 21. THE NUMBERS, RECONCILED

**The glass.**
- Blown/stamped mark: **17.6 c.c.** (the standard charge in the state — r11).
- Measured mean delivery: **16.72 c.c.** (Ivy Keddie's sheet, r07 Item 5). **Vaught's bulletin prose
  says 16.27 — a digit transposition.**
- Short by: **0.88 c.c.**
- Ratio: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95 exactly** → every test read **95% of the truth**; "one part in twenty."
  (Vaught's transposed figure gives 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244.)
- Nine surviving pipettes, individual means: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74,
  16.71 (five runs each). Two chipped at the tip (r07). **Eleven survivors per r12's 1959 footnote.**
- Purchased 1897: **six dozen (72)** at $0.35 each = $25.20 (invoice, r04, r07). **Five dozen per
  r11.** Nine survived unbroken in the crate.
- Whole outfit invoice total: **$77.93**.

**The averages.**
- Recorded station average from 1898: **3.61** (±0.03 every year). **Teague (r05) and Frick (r13) both
  say 3.16.**
- 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80** — the true average.
- Gap: **0.19 of a point**, which is **5% of 3.80**. Proportional, not fixed.
- Other statements of the gap: **0.17** (Roy Brant, r09; and Sill's 3.78 minus 3.61); **0.20 / "a fifth
  of a point"** (Kepp's field impression, r16); **0.21** (Kepp's stated gap, borrowed from Vose's 1924
  rise); **0.295** (Vaught's §V, from the transposed 16.27); **"a little under two tenths"** (curator,
  r07 Item 16).
- 1896, first season, on Hobart Sill's borrowed Larrow Green glass: **3.80**.
- 1911, nine weeks 8 May – 10 July, on a borrowed measure: **3.80**.
- 1924, first full season on new glass: **3.80** (Keddie's book, Strawn) / **3.90** (Vose).
- Larrow Green creamery over the same years: **3.78** (Sill, r14); "a little under two tenths of a
  point better" than 3.61 (curator, r07 Item 16).
- Alder Corners, the same four Hessel Bottom herds, sixteen summers 1915–1930: range **3.78–3.82**,
  mean **≈3.799** (r10).
- Sill's gut estimate of the Hollow's true milk: **3.85**.
- Vose home-place Jersey herd, 1910: **4.56**.
- Brant farm, 1910, test paid on: **3.42**.
- **Four independent bodies of sound glass — the 1896 season, the 1911 interval, the 1924 season, and
  sixteen summers at Alder Corners — all put the same milk between 3.78 and 3.82. The only instrument
  that ever read 3.61 was the station's own.**

**The volumes and money.**
- 1898–1922 (25 years): **40,000,000 lb of milk**, averaging **1,600,000 lb/yr** (r02, r04, r07).
- **44,000,000 lb** per Teague, "over the years the account ran" (r05) and per Rundle, "over the years
  complained of" (r12 — a wrong scope for the figure).
- First season 4 May–31 Dec 1896: **~900,000 lb** (Sill, r14; the ledgers have nothing).
- Credited fat at 3.61%: **1,444,000 lb**. Delivered fat at 3.80%: **1,520,000 lb**.
- Difference: **76,000 lb of butterfat**. (**67,000 lb** was the patrons' own reckoning in 1925, r13.
  **118,000 lb** is Vaught's transposition-derived figure, r11.)
- Butterfat price over the period: **$0.25/lb average** (r02, r04, r09); **$0.24** in 1910 (r09);
  **$0.27** per Sill (r14) and Kepp (r16).
- Value of the unpaid fat: **$19,000** (r04, r07). **$19,600 "set out to the dollar" in the patrons'
  petition** (r12).
- The 1925 award: **$3,800** — "a fifth of it." **$3,500 in Vira Toland's memory** (r01).
- Specimen year 1898: 57,760 lb credited, 60,800 lb received, 3,040 lb gain.
  - 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.263% (the ledger's "five and a quarter per cent," reckoned on credited fat).
  - 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.000% (reckoned on fat delivered — "the one that is also the glass").
  - **Teague (r05) and Vaught (r11) both state this backwards; Ivy Keddie (r04) and the curator (r07)
    both state it correctly.**
- Proportional shortfall examples: a herd recorded at **4.56 was short 0.24**; a herd recorded at
  **3.42 was short 0.18**.
- Keddie tested "thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons"; Vira says he "tested a thousand cans
  a week."
- Hessel Bottom: **160 acres**, six (or seven) farms; Brant's share sixty-odd acres and 38 cows.
- Redlow Ridge: four miles of switchback; the four men hauled ≈1 June to mid-September for sixteen
  seasons.

## 22. CONFLICTS, ODDITIES AND UNRESOLVED POINTS (the heart of the exercise)

*A–EE were established in the first eight retellings; several are now resolved or complicated by
r09–r16. FF onward are new.*

**A. The award amount.** $3,800 in the minute book (r02), the ledger (r05), the catalogue (r07), the
manager's address (r08) and the chairman's recollection (r13). **$3,500 in Vira Toland's memory
(r01).** Documentary figure wins.

**B. The date of the condemnation.** **12 June 1923** in Strawn's own notebook and its photostat (r06,
r07 Item 9) and in Bulletin 471 (r04, r07, r11). **12 July 1923** in Selby Vose's 1926 address (r08)
**and, thirteen years later, in Rundle's pamphlet (r12)** — which suggests the printed annual report
is where the valley got the wrong month. Vose's error is convenient for him: it compresses the
interval between condemnation and the tests rising, and makes the replacement look prompter.

**C. The 1924 average.** **3.80** per Keddie's book (r03) and Strawn's 1924 entry (r06). **3.90,
"rose by twenty-one hundredths,"** per Vose (r08). Two independent sources agree against him; and
**Kepp in 1926 takes Vose's 0.21 and reuses it as the size of the gap (r16)**, so the error propagated
within the year.

**D. The length of the 1911 substitution.** **Nine weeks**, 8 May to 10 July, per the test book, both
Keddies, Roy Brant and Vaught (r03, r04, r09, r11). **Six weeks** per Teague (r05) **and per Kepp
(r16), who received it that way from an advocate** — so the shortened version was the one in public
circulation, and it is the version Kepp's "six weeks of summer figures" rebuttal depends on.

**E. Where the 1911 borrowed measure came from.** "**Came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon**"
(r04, Ivy). "**Before their own came up from town**" (r05, Teague). "**The state man left his own
measure that June**" (r09, Roy Brant). Three accounts. Roy's has a further difficulty: the book has
the borrowed measure in use from the morning of **8 May**, before Strawn's June visit — so either Roy
misremembers, or a second substitute arrived mid-interval.

**F. Where the 1896 glass was borrowed from. — RESOLVED.** The test book's first leaf says "**Glass
borrowed of Larrow Green**" (r03, r07); Keddie's unsent letter says "**borrowed off the Ashlin
creamery**" (r03); the office file supports Larrow Green (r07 Item 2). **r14 settles it in the first
person: Hobart Sill made up an outfit out of his own glass at his own bench at Larrow Green, sent it
up the valley on the wagon, and got it back the spring after with one piece chipped.** Keddie's memory
of his own first season was wrong.

**G. Where the crate was found.** **The loft over the intake room at the station, under a tarpaulin,
reached by a ladder** — Ivy Keddie's first-person account (r04), corroborated by Strawn's 1928 note
that the loft was full and had been "since before the war" **and by Rundle's 1939 note that the loft
"has never been cleared out since it was put up" (r12)**. **"Found in the Association office at
Ashlin"** — the curator's Item 5 (r07). The finder's own account, now doubly corroborated, outranks
the accession slip.

**H. The catalogue's swapped attributions (r07). — CONFIRMED BY r13.** Item 3 "Station test book, kept
by E. Brant" vs. Item 4 "Farm weigh book, kept by A. Keddie." **Frick's 1925 exhibit list, made up
twenty-one years before the catalogue, has it right both ways: Exhibit 1, the test book, "in the hand
of Ansel Keddie"; Exhibit 3, "the private weigh book of Emmet Brant."** The curator swapped the names.

**I. Item 3's column count.** The catalogue lists four columns (date, patron, pounds, test); Keddie
says five (date, patron, pounds, test, fat) — r03.

**J. Teague's 3.16 average. — NOW CORROBORATED, AND MORE PUZZLING.** Every documentary source has the
station's recorded average at **3.61**. **Teague (r05) and Frick (r13) both give 3.16.** Frick is
independent of the ledgers but *not* independent of Teague, who testified before his board — so the
likeliest reading is still a transposition that entered through the bookkeeper and lodged in the
chairman's memory. But two witnesses now carry it, and Frick builds a paragraph of reasoning on it
("Three and sixteen is not a scandalous figure standing by itself").

**K. Teague's 44 million vs. everyone's 40 million pounds. — WORSE NOW.** 40,000,000 is expressly for
**1898–1922**; Teague's 44,000,000 is "over the years the account ran," which is a wider window and
therefore reconcilable. **But Rundle (r12) attaches 44,000,000 to "the years complained of"** — the
25-year window — which is not reconcilable, and shows the figure migrating loose of its scope.

**L. "Five and a quarter percent of the fat that was actually in the cream."** By the ledger's own
numbers 5¼% is of the **credited** fat and 5% is of the fat delivered. Ivy Keddie (r04) and the curator
(r07) state the distinction correctly; **Teague (r05) and Vaught (r11) both state it backwards.**

**M. Selby Vose: son or nephew of Gideon? — NOW 2–2.** **Son** per r02 ("son of the late president")
and r08 ("My father, who was your first president"). **Nephew** per Vira Toland (r01) **and per Rundle
(r12), who attributes to Selby himself a remark about "his uncle."** Selby's own first-person claim in
a printed annual report should govern; Rundle's is store-and-funeral history, and the anecdote form
("used to say") is exactly where a relationship gets miscast.

**N. Strawn's circuit start year.** **1907** in his own covering note (r06). **"from 1908"** per Ivy
Keddie's description of his notebooks (r04). Minor.

**O. The Alder Corners evidence: one season, fourteen, or sixteen? — NOW THREE COUNTS.** **Sixteen
seasons, 1915–1930**, per Rosalie Cudd's own letter and table (r10) and per Roy Brant (r09).
**Fourteen** per Ivy Keddie's talk (r04). **One — "the season of 1919"** per the curator's Item 13
(r07). The source document is r10 and it gives sixteen; Ivy's "fourteen" is unexplained (she may have
used only the overlap she could pair with station figures); the curator's file evidently holds a
subset.

**P. Rosalie Cudd: niece or daughter? — RESOLVED. DAUGHTER.** r04 has her as "Jerome Cudd's niece";
r07 has the book passing "from its founder to his daughter in 1941"; **r10 is Rosalie herself: "My
father, Jerome Cudd, built this factory… He made cheese here until he died in 1941, in March… I took
the factory over that same spring."** r12 also says daughter. Ivy Keddie got it wrong in her own talk
about her own correspondent.

**Q. The identity of Vira's 1958 visitor. — CONFIRMED AS IVY KEDDIE, DEGREE CORRECTED.** Vira
describes her as "from the college away east" and as "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed." **Roy
Brant, who is Hazel's father and Ivy's first cousin once removed himself, states the relationship
exactly: "My daughter Hazel and his daughter Ivy are second cousins" (r09) — and Rundle's 1959
footnote independently says "second cousin to Hazel Brant" (r12).** Vira was still telling the
cream-stealing version in **1979, twenty years after Bulletin No. 471**. The finding never reached the
Hollow.

**R. The crate was never mentioned at the 1925 hearing — BUT THE CONDEMNATION WAS IN EVIDENCE.** Effie
Loomis is explicit (r02 §8): not a word about the crate, from anybody, in any of the three sessions.
**Frick's exhibit list (r13) shows that Exhibit 4 was "the circuit notebook of the state dairy
inspector for the year 1923"** — the volume containing "Ran their milk pipettes against the burette.
These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them." The document
that solved the case was one of only four books before the board, and no one read it for what it was.
This is now the single most consequential fact in the record, and it upgrades Effie's "silence" from
an omission to a failure of reading.

**S. The 1922 near-miss.** Strawn had the burette from spring 1921 and chose not to run the station's
glass in 1922. Had he run it, the cause would have been found a year earlier — before the hearing
framed it as theft.

**T. Strawn's June 1911 visit.** He watched a test through in June 1911 — inside the nine weeks when
the borrowed measure was giving 3.80 — and wrote "Nothing here to note." He watched the right
procedure with the right glass. **Roy Brant's claim that Strawn *supplied* that borrowed measure, if
true, would make the inspector the unwitting author of the experiment.**

**U. Strawn's repeated lactometer findings.** "No added water" at Ostrey Hollow on **every visit**
(1912, 1916, 1919, 1920, 1922, 1923 "as always here", 1924, 1925, 1928, 1931, 1934) — and he told the
manager so in 1919, the year of the circular. **Rosalie Cudd adds an independent and stronger refutation
from the other side of the ridge: sixteen summers of cheesemaking on the same four herds' milk with
never a soft curd, never a complaint, never a docked cent (r10) — "Cheese is a witness."** Vose sent
and later defended the circular anyway, and never mentions either.

**V. The two "true at once" sentences — now three.** Effie Loomis: "how both halves of a sentence
could be true at once" (r02). Ansel Keddie: "they found no cause established and they put me out on
the last day of the month, and both of those things are true at once" (r03). **Frick: "An award
without a finding is an unsatisfactory thing and every member of that board knew it" (r13)** — and he
adds that the dismissal happened **while the board was still sitting**, which he says he has never been
able to think well of the Association for.

**W. Vose's self-defeating evidence.** He cites his father's Jersey herd at **4.56 in 1910** to prove
the valley is good milk country. That herd was at the home place near Larrow Green, tested by the
creamery, not through the Ostrey Hollow glass — so it proves the opposite of what he intends. Ivy
Keddie later uses 4.56 as her example of the herd that lost the most (0.24 of a point).

**X. Vose's "rise came when the looking began."** The tests rose in **1924**, the first full season on
the **new glass** (in use from July 1923) — and the petition was **January 1925**, *after* the rise.
His chronology is backwards. **Hobart Sill (r14) makes exactly the same inversion — "one summer, along
in the twenties, those figures came up… Something got stopped. Something got said to somebody" — and
he, unlike Vose, had no stake in it.** The reasoning error was general, not merely self-serving.

**Y. The nine families / six or seven farms.** The 1919 circular went to **nine families** (r01, r03,
r09) and Vira divides the award "nine ways." **Six farms** had rights on Hessel Bottom per Roy Brant
(r09), the 1908 report (r15) and Kepp (r16); **seven** per Effie Loomis (r02). The station also took
milk "from three places on the hill" (r06, 1909). Six Bottom farms + three hill places = nine — which
fits the nine letter families exactly and suggests **six is right and Effie's seven is the error**.

**Z. Nobody ever went up and looked — now five voices.** Effie Loomis ("not one of us ever went up the
valley… I include myself first in that"), Ansel Keddie ("Neither of them has come up that road on a
working morning and stood at my elbow"), Vira Toland ("nobody ever came up that road and looked"),
**Rosalie Cudd ("I never once heard anybody go up there and look" — r10)**, and, inverted, **Hobart
Sill ("not one man in that office ever came down here to see how it was done" — r14)**. The men who
*did* stand at the tester's elbow — Strawn, the 1908 committee, the 1925 board — all found the
procedure faultless, because it was. Looking at the man was never going to work; only measuring the
glass was.

**AA. The Article VII trap.** "The test of the station where he delivers shall govern" — no appeal, no
re-test elsewhere. Effie Loomis names it "the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business"; **Rundle
reaches the same verdict independently ("it is the sentence on which the whole trouble at the head of
the valley afterward turned" — r12); and Frick reaches it a third time from the law's side ("it makes
the test the whole of the bargain, and it leaves a dissatisfied man with no remedy on earth except to
quarrel with a figure" — r13).** The man who wrote the clause died in 1917.

**BB. The gain hid in plain sight — and was seen three times.** The 76,000 lb the patrons lost is the
same 76,000 lb the Association booked as "station gain," in ink, signed off by the manager every
spring. **Teague saw it and made it furniture (r05). Sill weighed it himself, every year, on every
page of his own receiving book, and made it "that is cream for you" (r14). Rundle saw it once by
accident in 1939 and reasoned correctly — cream stolen would arrive light, this arrived heavy — and
printed the correction where nobody acted on it (r12).**

**CC. The 1896 figure that is not recoverable — except it is.** The curator says the station's first
season (4 May–end of 1896) "is not in the ledgers and is nowhere else" (r07). **Hobart Sill gives it
from memory in 1934: "about nine hundred thousand pounds" (r14).** The test book gives that season's
average as **3.80** — on Sill's own good glass — making 1896 one of four independent confirmations of
the true rate.

**DD. Three explanations argued in 1925 — NOW NAMED AND ATTRIBUTED.** Effie Loomis gave only the count
(r02 §8). **Frick names them and says who put each: water (Selby Vose, "without heat and without
proof"); the tester taking cream (Wilbur Teague, "in the way an accountant puts a thing he wants said
without saying it"); and the hay (the 1908 report read into the record by "somebody," and "it had the
advantage of blaming nobody in the room") — r13.** The glass was not among them and was never named,
though the notebook recording its condemnation was Exhibit 4.

**EE. The delay arithmetic (Ivy's closing).** Glass condemned 1923 → cause found 1958 = **35 years**.
Keddie dismissed 1925 → cleared 1958 = **33 years**. Died 1934 → **dead 24 years** when the sheet was
read. Vaught's version: the 1911 experiment "lay unread for **forty-seven years**." And Vira Toland,
in 1979, still believed he took the cream.

### New conflicts and findings from r09–r16

**FF. The date of Article VII — three answers, and the pamphlet can't settle it.** **1891** (Rundle,
r12, "the year the churn was set up"); **1892** (Effie Loomis from the minutes, r02, "the year after
the creamery opened"); **1893** ("payment by test came in with the 1893 meeting, as I have always
understood it" — Grigg, r15). **The printed by-laws "carry no year on their face, only the words
'adopted at the annual meeting'" (r07 Item 1), and the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in 1938 (r02) —
so the one document that could decide it is precisely the one that is gone.**

**GG. Six farms or seven on Hessel Bottom.** **Six** — Roy Brant, who farms the largest of them (r09);
the 1908 report, whose committee "visited each of the six farms" (r15); Kepp (r16). **Seven** — Effie
Loomis (r02). Three sources against one, one of them the man who lives there and one a contemporary
document. See also Y above: six Bottom farms plus three hill places = the nine letter families.

**HH. When the Brant weigh book begins.** **1 January 1897** — the curator's provenance (r07 Item 4)
and Frick's Exhibit 3, made up in 1925. **1 January 1898** — Roy Brant's own account of his father's
book (r09). Two documents against a son's memory; but Roy is describing a book he has had in his hands
daily for twenty-six years.

**II. When the Alder Corners factory was built.** **1913** — Rosalie Cudd, who was twelve when the
first vat was set (r10). **1914** — Roy Brant (r09) and Rundle (r12). The founder's daughter should
govern; note that the four men's hauling began in 1915 either way.

**JJ. The mean delivery of the pipettes: 16.72 or 16.27.** **16.72** — Ivy Keddie's signed measurement
sheet of 9 March 1958 with all nine individual means listed (r04), and the curator's Item 5 (r07).
**16.27** — Vaught's §III and §V (r11), from which he derives 0.9244, 3.905, 0.295 and 118,000 lb.
**The bulletin contradicts itself: its own concluding paragraph, printed four sections later on the
same sheet, says "low by one part in twenty" (= 0.95) and "seventy-six thousand pounds."** Vaught even
flags the inconsistency ("this figure is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80
in the year following") and prints it anyway.

**KK. Five dozen or six dozen pipettes purchased.** **Six dozen** — the 1897 invoice itself (r07 Item
2) and Ivy Keddie (r04). **Five dozen** — Vaught (r11).

**LL. Nine or eleven pipettes surviving.** **Nine** — Ivy's sheet, the curator's Item 5, Vaught's §III.
**Eleven** — Rundle's 1959 footnote (r12), written at second hand from "the college."

**MM. When the station's own glass went into use.** **1 May 1897** — the office file (r07 Item 2) and
Bulletin 471's conclusion. **1 May 1898** — Vaught's §III (r11). The 1897 date is also what makes the
"twenty-six-year anomaly" (1 May 1897 – 12 June 1923) come out right.

**NN. The size of the patrons' claim.** **76,000 lb / $19,000** — Ivy Keddie and the ledgers, computed
in 1958. **67,000 lb** — "the patrons' own reckoning… and nothing was ever brought forward to disturb
that figure" (Frick, r13). **$19,600 "set out to the dollar" in the petition** (Rundle, r12). **118,000
lb** — Vaught's erroneous derivation (r11). The 67/76 pair looks like another transposition, but it may
simply be that the patrons in 1925, working from pay slips, underestimated.

**OO. The price of butterfat.** **25 cents** average over the period — Effie Loomis from Teague's annual
sheets (r02), Ivy Keddie (r04), Roy Brant (r09). **24 cents in 1910** — Roy Brant (r09). **27 cents
"through these years"** — Hobart Sill (r14) and Kepp (r16). The 25-cent figure is the one out of the
books and the one the $19,000 rests on; 27 cents is what two men who were not bookkeepers remembered.

**PP. Bulletin 214 misquoted, twice, in print.** The bulletin says "**not more than five hundredths of
one per cent in winter, and… nothing that could be measured on summer pasture.**" **Grigg's 1919
covering letter (r15) reports it as "two tenths in the winter feeding" and drops the summer clause.
Kepp's 1926 paper (r16) repeats "two tenths in the winter feeding" and drops the summer clause again,
and the chairman announces the paper will be printed for the members.** A fourfold inflation, printed
three times, that made the wet-hay theory look scientifically confirmed for fifty years. It is also
worth noting that Grigg's inflated 0.20 and Kepp's field impression of 0.20 both land near the real
glass gap of 0.19 — which is why the false explanation fitted the facts so comfortably.

**QQ. Grigg's 1908 committee: three winter months or a whole year?** His 1919 letter: "**We sampled
those herds through the whole of 1908 — every month of that year, in the barn and on the pasture.**"
**His own report, reprinted directly beneath it: "We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through
January, February and March."** The curator (r07 Item 11) confirms the report's scope. **A document
contradicted by the document it is prefaced to** — and the inflation converts the committee's fatal
weakness (no summer sampling, so no test of the hay hypothesis) into an apparent strength.

**RR. The 1925 board did not sit only at Ashlin.** r02 and r08 both leave the impression of a hearing at
Ashlin. **Frick: three sessions, the first at Ashlin on 17 March, "the second and the third I asked to
have at Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's own weigh room" (r13).** Which raises a further
question about Effie Loomis's "I took every word down": she wrote the minute book, but the two
station sessions are not described by anybody else.

**SS. The board inspected the apparatus — the wrong apparatus.** Frick: "Every physical appliance of
that station was before us and we satisfied ourselves upon it. We saw the scales tried. We saw the
sampling done and the test made." **In 1925 the station had been on new, accurate glass since July
1923.** The board went to the station expressly to examine the plant and examined the replacement.

**TT. Keddie suspected the patrons.** r03 shows a man who refuses to accuse anyone and says "I carry no
charge against any man who put his name to that paper." **r13: in the corridor at Ashlin he told the
chairman privately "that he had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the
room."** Frick is careful: "he went to his grave accusing nobody, which is true, and… he suspected
nobody, which is not."

**UU. The estrangement rested on two mistaken beliefs.** Emmet Brant stopped speaking to his
brother-in-law **not because he believed the water charge but because he held that the tester should
have publicly defended the patrons and did not** (r09). Ansel Keddie did not defend them because he
privately believed some of them were watering (r13). Neither man was right, neither said so, and they
lived within sight of each other's chimney smoke for ten years without a word (r01).

**VV. Rundle solved half of it in 1939 and nothing happened.** The note added while his pamphlet was in
press disposes of the cream-theft verdict on the Association's own ledger evidence — nineteen years
before Bulletin 471, in a document printed for the whole membership, and **reprinted unaltered in
1959**. He stopped one step short (he never asked where the surplus came from), and the valley went on
believing the version he had just refuted for another forty years.

**WW. The 1911 interval was argued in public in 1926 and laughed out of the room.** Kepp: "It has been
urged upon me, by a gentleman who I know will forgive my noticing it here, that in the summer of 1911
the testing at that station was broken off for six weeks…" **Somebody had the right argument thirty-two
years before Vaught called it the experiment that ran itself.** Kepp's rebuttal — "six weeks of June
and July, when every herd in the county is at its best" — fails on the true dates: the interval began
**8 May**, before flush, and the tests **fell back to 3.61 on 10 July, in the middle of the flush**, the
day the station's own measure returned.

**XX. The right question was asked from the floor in 1926.** An unnamed member of the Ordell County
Farm Club asked "whether the trouble might lie in the station rather than the bottom, as had been said
in the valley for years." Kepp answered that **he had no competence in the quarrel, had never in his
life read a test and would not know how**, and that "when a cause is found in the soil, in the herbage,
in the condition of the stock, in the College's trial, and in every wet bottom in the county alike, it
is not good sense to go looking for a second cause in a building." The coherence of the wrong story
was itself the argument against looking.

**YY. Every check that was ever run was run on the wrong instrument.** The 1908 committee tried the
station's scales *and* the patrons' scales. Strawn tried the scales at every visit, ran the lactometer
for water at every visit, tested the boiler and the cold room, and stood at the tester's elbow. The
1925 board watched the scales tried and a test made. Emmet Brant's weigh book independently verified
the station's scale for thirty years. **Not one of them measured the pipette, because — as Vaught puts
it — "in the ordinary practice of the trade the pipette is the standard… An instrument that is itself
the measure cannot be caught by measuring with it."** The state issued burettes only in 1921 and
discontinued the program in 1926.

**ZZ. The corroborating evidence was always available and never assembled.** By 1925 the following all
existed: the Larrow Green receiving book showing the cream heavy every year in Sill's hand from 1893;
the station-gain column in the ledgers from 1898; the Brant weigh book proving the scales agreed; the
1911 entries with Keddie's question mark; Strawn's lactometer findings; Jerome Cudd's book showing the
same milk at 3.80 over the ridge; and Strawn's 1923 condemnation, which was physically in the hearing
room. **Ivy Keddie's achievement in 1958 was not discovery but assembly — "no two of them had ever been
in one room before last year."**

## 23. VOICE AND STANCE OF EACH WITNESS (useful for weighing them)

- **Vira Toland (r01)** — oral, warm, digressive, certain. Eyewitness to the household effects of the
  letter, not to any figure. Her numbers are memory. Delivers the folk verdict without hedging. Her
  value is social: what the accusation did to two families and nine kitchens, and the fact that the
  truth never reached the Hollow.
- **Effie Loomis (r02)** — scrupulous archivist; explicitly separates minutes from recollection;
  self-accusing about the office's failure to visit; names the two silences (the lost book, the
  unmentioned crate) — though Frick's exhibit list shows one of those silences was less complete than
  she thought.
- **Ansel Keddie (r03)** — the accused, precise and unyielding; refuses to speculate; his procedural
  detail is what later makes the pipette explanation testable; he preserved the 1911 anomaly and his
  own question mark rather than tidying it. Wrong about where his first glass came from; privately
  suspected his neighbors and never said so.
- **Ivy Keddie (r04)** — the investigator; declares her interest and then removes it from the argument;
  brings five record sets together for the first time; her key move is recognizing the error is a
  **proportion**. Minor slips of her own: "niece" for daughter, fourteen seasons for sixteen.
- **Wilbur Teague (r05)** — the bookkeeper; owns the station-gain line as his own invention; honest
  about the psychology of evenness; his 1925 testimony is the most damaging single contribution to the
  case against Keddie, delivered — as Frick saw clearly — as an insinuation no cross-examination could
  reach. Carries the 3.16, the 44 million, the six weeks, and the reversed percentage.
- **Merle Strawn (r06)** — the disciplined field man; refuses opinions on principle; his notebooks are
  the closest thing to instrument-grade evidence in the file, and his refusal to theorize is exactly
  why his 1923 finding sat unused. He found the fault the moment he was issued an instrument that could
  measure the glass, wrote it down, told the manager, and drew no conclusion.
- **Halbert Nym (r07)** — the curator; transcriptions exact, glosses modest — and yet his headings carry
  two swapped attributions (both refuted by Frick's 1925 list), a wrong find-place for the crate, a
  wrong column count, and an under-description of the Alder Corners file. The retelling's quiet
  demonstration that even a careful catalogue introduces error.
- **Selby Vose (r08)** — the manager defending his own decisions; plain-spoken and self-assured; wrong
  on the condemnation date (and his printed error propagated to Rundle), wrong on the 1924 average (and
  his 0.21 propagated to Kepp), wrong on the direction of causation, and the only witness with a
  personal interest in the watered-milk theory being true. Generous in one respect: he supported the
  $3,800 and wanted nobody prosecuted.
- **Roy Brant (r09)** — the farmer with the primary document; flat, unrhetorical, physical ("a man does
  better to talk about what he can put his hand on"); the most reliable kinship map in the set; supplies
  the two facts that eliminate the weighing and pin the gap's shape (the columns agreed; 0.17 in July
  and 0.17 in February); and the only source for why the two families stopped speaking. Disagrees with
  two documents about his own father's start date.
- **Rosalie Cudd (r10)** — the outside control. No stake, no knowledge of the quarrel's contents, no
  theory. Copies sixteen seasons without smoothing or dropping one, offers the individual herds if
  wanted, and supplies the best refutation of the water theory in the file because it is a process
  witness rather than an instrument reading. Also the only warm document in the set — "come over in the
  summer and see the place."
- **Leland Vaught (r11)** — the expert who explains the mechanism better than anyone and gets his own
  numbers wrong. §II's three consequences are the intellectual center of the whole case; §III and §V
  are corrupted by a transposed digit that he never reconciles with the conclusion printed on the same
  sheet, and which he half-notices and prints anyway. The bulletin that clears Keddie contains, in its
  own introduction, the error pattern that convicted him.
- **Dell Rundle (r12)** — the folk historian who labels his own method honestly ("what the valley
  remembers, tidied"), prints the valley's slander as certainty, and then — having wandered into the
  ledger on an unrelated errand — refutes it himself in five sentences and leaves both the slander and
  the refutation standing side by side, twenty years apart, unaltered in reprint.
- **Alonzo Frick (r13)** — the chairman, forty years a lawyer, writing at seventy-four about the one
  matter that never left him. The most self-aware document in the set: he says plainly that he did not
  understand the chemistry, that an award without a finding is unsatisfactory, that the board could only
  choose among the accounts put to it, and that he has wondered ever since "whether the answer was in
  that room and simply was not in any of the three stories." It was — as Exhibit 4.
- **Hobart Sill (r14)** — the craftsman; contemptuous of paper, proud of butter, and in possession of the
  single most decisive physical fact in the valley, which he weighed himself, recorded in his own hand
  for forty-one years, mentioned "a hundred times," and dismissed with a shrug. Also the man who lent
  the glass the station opened on, and the only source for the 1896 intake and the Larrow Green average.
  Believed in the water to the last, while insisting the cows were never the trouble.
- **Nyle Grigg (r15)** — the honest, disinterested, thoroughly wrong investigator. Chosen for having no
  stake and abused by both sides, which he takes as proof of honesty. His 1919 letter shows how a
  finding hardens: three winter months become a whole year, five hundredths become two tenths, a
  tentative opinion becomes "settled by better men," and the men who reopen it "have brought nothing new
  before us but their impatience."
- **Dr. Roland Kepp (r16)** — the expert in the wrong discipline. Sound soil chemistry, sound veterinary
  observation, a true account of what bog land does to a cow — deployed on a case where none of it was
  the cause. Triangulates three numbers that are all wrong or borrowed, dismisses the one real
  experiment on a misremembered duration, and answers the correct question from the floor by saying he
  has never read a test in his life and it would not be good sense to look for a second cause in a
  building.
