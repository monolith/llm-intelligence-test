# Answer sheet — Part 1 of 3

## Section A — Reconstruction (stories 1–4 of 8)

*My partition: I have cut the history at the eight points where a distinct set of people, a
distinct span of years and a distinct body of documents hang together. Stories 1–2 are how the
trap was built; 3–4 are the two explanations offered while it was working, one false and one
true-but-unread; 5 is what it cost socially; 6 is the accident that ended it; 7 is the verdict
that punished the wrong man; 8 is the fifty-year delay in reading what had always been written
down.*

---

### 1. The Rule That Made the Test the Master (1889–1917)

**Who.** Gideon Vose, first president of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association;
the farmers who organized it; Effie Loomis, secretary 1901–1940.

**What and when.** In 1889 the farmers of the Larrow Valley met in the schoolhouse at Larrow
Green — in cold weather, late, and somebody's horse got loose — and organized a cooperative
creamery association, being tired of hauling butter to the Ashlin merchants and taking what was
allowed them. Gideon Vose was elected first president and held the office until 1911, twenty-two
years. In 1891 they built the creamery at Larrow Green on the creek below the mill; it is the same
building yet, and the valley began to be paid in cash once a month, which had never happened to
it before. Gideon Vose, not a lawyer, drafted the by-laws himself at his kitchen table. Article
VII reads entire:

> *Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter
> fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the
> test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*

**Quantities.** Adopted in 1891, 1892 or 1893 — the three narrators who give a year give three
different years, the printed by-laws carry no date on their face, and the 1889–1892 minute book
was lost when the office was moved in 1938. **The year cannot be determined and I do not guess
it.**

**Why it belongs by itself.** The last clause is the whole trap. Not the creamery's test, not the
valley average, not a re-test anywhere: the station's test, and no appeal from it. Under payment
by weight, everything in stories 2–8 would have cost nobody a dollar. Three narrators reach this
independently — the secretary from the minutes, the schoolteacher from the store, the arbitration
chairman from the law: it leaves a dissatisfied man no remedy on earth except to quarrel with a
figure. Gideon Vose died in 1917 and could not be asked what he had meant. His son Selby was
manager 1913–1938 and enforced it.

**Documents.** Effie Loomis's 1941 history; the printed by-laws (catalogue Item 1); Rundle's 1939
pamphlet; Frick's 1946 recollections; Falke's 1959 county report; Kyle's 1953 memoir.

### 2. Borrowed Glass and Bought Glass (1895 – 1 May 1897)

**Who.** Ansel Keddie, tester; Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green; Ammon & Sons, Dairy
Supply, of Tarnet.

**What and when.** Ansel Keddie went up the Hollow in 1895 to make the station ready. It opened on
**4 May 1896** — a crowd went up for it — seven miles above Larrow Green, where the pull down was
three hours in mud and impossible in March. The outfit ordered for it had not come, and there was
talk of holding off the whole season, so Hobart Sill made up an outfit at his own bench out of his
own working glass and sent it up the valley on the wagon. The test book's first leaf records it:
*Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes.* That first season the station's average test
was **3.80**.

On **14 April 1897** Ammon & Sons invoiced the Association for the station's own outfit: six dozen
milk test pipettes blown 17.6 c.c. at $0.35 ($25.20), twelve dozen test bottles at $0.22 ($31.68),
one acid measure ($0.90), one four-bottle hand tester ($18.00), freight ($2.15) — **total
$77.93**. It went into use on **1 May 1897**; Orra Keddie's diary for that day: *A. says the
station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green. He is pleased about it.*
Sill's glass went back the spring after with one piece chipped, and he never said a word about it.

**Quantities.** From 1898 the station's average stood at **3.61**, and at 3.61 within three
hundredths every year afterward. The true rate never moved: 3.80 in 1896 on Sill's glass, 3.80 in
1924 on new glass.

**The cause, per the maker.** Junia Ammon's 1949 firm history: a mold reground, the lot run short,
nobody in the works catching it — *a boy checks the first of a lot and the tenth and the fiftieth
and finds them consistent, because they are consistent — they are consistently wrong, which is the
one fault a check of that kind cannot find.* Not fraud; the newspaper's 1958 charge of a knowingly
short lot is inference from silence.

**Documents.** The invoice (Item 2); the test book's first leaf and 1922 summary leaf; Sill's 1934
remarks; Orra Keddie's diary; Junia Ammon's chapter nine.

### 3. The Land on Trial (1902–1926, with a coda in 1959)

**Who.** Nyle Grigg, farmer of Larrow Green and chairman of the 1908 committee; Dr. Roland Kepp,
V.S., of Ashlin; the six farms of Hessel Bottom.

**What and when.** From **1902** the Hollow men said out loud that their milk tested under the rest
of the valley's; Emmet Brant was one of the first and they were laughed at. In January 1908 the
Association named a committee of three under Grigg, chosen because he was not a patron of that
station. They visited each of the **six** farms on Hessel Bottom — 160 acres of wet meadow, sedge
and rush, hay cut late and fed November to April — some of them three and four times, sampled the
herds **through January, February and March 1908 only**, tried the station's scales and the
patrons' scales against each other, and reported that the wet hay thins the cream and the remedy
lies with the land and not with the station. They also reported, emphatically, that they found *no
dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter* — seventeen years before a man was dismissed for it. Orra
Keddie watched them arrive in Grigg's sleigh and noticed what mattered: *They wanted to see the
barns and not the station.*

**The misquotation.** In 1911 the college published Bulletin No. 214: *Bog hay fed as the whole of
the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in
winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture.* Reprinting his report in 1919,
Grigg reported it as **two tenths in the winter feeding** and dropped the summer clause entirely.
He also remembered his three winter months as *the whole of 1908 — every month of that year, in
the barn and on the pasture*, on the page facing his own report saying otherwise. Kepp repeated
the same "two tenths," minus the same summer clause, to the Ordell County Farm Club in 1926, and
the paper was printed for the members.

**Why it is fatal.** 0.05 in winter and nothing in summer cannot carry 0.19 standing flat in July
and February alike: four times too small and the wrong shape. Kepp triangulated three numbers —
the misquoted 0.20, his own unmeasured field impression of 0.20, and 0.21 lifted from the
manager's address (where it described the 1924 *rise*, not the gap) — and called their agreement
proof.

**Coda.** In 1959 the county agent conceded the hay as "a real if partial factor," then sent to
Farland for Bulletin 214, read it, and withdrew the concession in a note appended to his own
report. He is the only man in seventy years who checked the citation.

**Documents.** The 1908 report in full; Grigg's 1919 covering letter; Bulletin 214 (Item 12); Kepp's
1926 paper and the discussion appended to it; Falke's 1959 report and its note.

### 4. Nine Weeks in 1911

**Who.** Ansel Keddie and his measure; Merle Strawn, who walked through the middle of it; Duncan
Keddie, seventeen that year.

**What and when.** On **8 May 1911** the station's own pipette broke. Keddie made shift with a
borrowed measure and entered it: *May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure.*
From that morning the tests came up and stood at **3.80**. On **10 July 1911** his own measure came
back into use and the tests fell the same day: *July 10. Our own measure in use again from today.
Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.* He let the question mark stand fourteen years.

**Why it is the center of the case.** Nine weeks in which the patrons, the herds, the pasture, the
season, the acid, the bottles, the hand tester, the tester and his habits were all unaltered, and
one object changed. Every explanation lodged in the ground, the cows, the feed or the men fails on
those nine weeks, because none of them was altered and the figures were. It is an experiment
nobody designed, recorded contemporaneously by a man who did not know what he was recording.

**Corroboration.** Roy Brant: through those nine weeks the tests came up on every farm on the
Bottom, and his father entered both the rise and the fall. Orra Keddie's diary, 8 May 1911: *A.
home cross about a broken measure.* Strawn watched a test through at the station in **June 1911**
— inside the interval, on the borrowed glass — and wrote "Nothing here to note."

**What Keddie made of it.** Duncan Keddie says his father came home that summer saying it was
something in the glass, said it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not his way —
and never dared put it in the book, having nothing in his hand but a suspicion against the whole
practice of the trade. So the question mark was not blankness; it was a man who would not write
what he could not prove. He kept on for fourteen more years and wrote down every figure the glass
gave him, and the figures were what convicted him.

**What it does not settle.** Where the borrowed measure came from: three incompatible accounts
(from Larrow Green with the cream wagon; "from town"; the state man left his own that June). The
last cannot be right as stated — the substitute was in use from 8 May, before Strawn's June visit.

**Documents.** The two 1911 entries in the test book; the 1922 summary leaf; the Brant weigh book;
Strawn's 1911 circuit entry; Orra Keddie's diary; Duncan Keddie's 1968 family notes; Vaught's §IV.

### 5. The Circular, and Two Houses in Sight of Each Other (1919–1929)

**Who.** Selby Vose, manager, who signed it; Bertram Kyle, the clerk who typed it; Ansel Keddie,
who carried it; Emmet Brant, who received it; Orra Keddie, sister to one and wife to the other.

**What and when.** In **June 1919** a printed circular went out over the manager's signature and no
one else's, warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons that added water would be looked for and would be
dealt with. It named nobody and accused nobody, and everyone who got it understood exactly what it
meant. Its text has never been quoted in any account; seven narrators describe it and not one
reproduces it.

**The delivery.** Ansel Keddie would not put them in the mail nor leave them on the platform. He
took them round himself, stood at the door while each man read it, and came home at eleven at
night with his hat still on. Orra recorded it two days later: Emmet Brant came up, would not come
in, stood in the yard and said he had hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened
and Ansel knew it better than any man living — and then asked him **why he had carried the paper
himself if he did not believe it**. Ansel said he carried it because he would not have another man
carry it. Emmet went home and was never in that house again. This is the true cause of the
estrangement; Roy Brant's later version — that the tester should have stood up in front of the
circular and did not — is a son's compression of what his grandfather actually asked.

**Quantities.** Nine families is the figure carried by the recipients (Vira Toland's father was one
of nine; Roy Brant; Hazel Brant: "nine letters"), and it reconciles with six farms on Hessel Bottom
plus the three places on the hill that Strawn recorded in 1909. Orra, whose husband carried them,
says **eleven**. I take nine as the number of patron families and note that the best-placed witness
of all stands alone against it; the discrepancy is unresolved.

**What was already known to be false.** Merle Strawn had run the lactometer for added water at that
station on every visit of his career and never found any, and told the manager so to his face in
1919. Over the ridge, Jerome Cudd made cheese for sixteen summers out of the milk of four of those
same farms and never once docked a man or complained of a curd — and watered milk cannot be hidden
from a cheesemaker, because the curd comes soft the next morning. Vose defended the circular in
print in 1926 and would have written every word of it again.

**The cost.** Ten years' silence between a brother and a sister's husband who lived within sight of
each other's chimney smoke. Orra went down when word came that Emmet was dead in the spring of
1929, sat an hour in the kitchen where she was a girl, and came home. Households kept the paper for
forty years to prove what had been said of them; one woman had hers pinned inside the lid of her
trunk; Hazel Brant's mother read hers twice and put it in the stove.

**Documents.** Orra Keddie's diary, June 1919; Kyle's memoir; Vose's 1926 address; Strawn's 1919
entry; Rosalie Cudd's 1958 letter; Vira Toland's and Hazel Brant's tapes; Roy Brant's account.

### 6. The Condemnation Nobody Read (1921–1923)

**Who.** Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector on the Ordell circuit 1907–1936; Selby Vose; Ammon &
Sons.

**What and when.** In **1921** the state began issuing graduated burettes in wooden cases to its
field men, for checking the delivery of measures and pipettes at the plants; Strawn's came in the
spring with a printed sheet of directions. It was slow work and most plants had never had it done.
At Ostrey Hollow in **1922** he did not run their glass — *the day being short and the road home
being what it was, and I have put that down because it is what happened.* Had he run it, the cause
would have been found a year before the hearing framed it as theft.

On **12 June 1923** he ran it:

> *Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk pipettes
> against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole
> crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has
> cost them.*

The crate went up the ladder into the loft over the intake room. The Association ordered
replacements from Tarnet on **28 June**; Ammon & Sons acknowledged on **2 July**, promised shipment
that month, and declined to say anything of the earlier lot. New glass was in use from July 1923.
In October Strawn ran the new pipettes against the burette: they delivered their mark. In **1924**
the station's average was **3.80** and stayed there. In 1926 the state discontinued the burette
program.

**Why nothing followed.** Strawn kept a book and refused on principle to keep an estimation — *a
theory is a thing a man carries out to the platform with him and it gets in the way of the
weights* — which is exactly why the man who found the fault drew no inference from it. In the
office it was treated as a broken window: the clerk entered the order, filed the inspector's word
for it, and that was the end of the matter. Vose, in his printed 1926 address, misdated it **12
July** and dismissed it as housekeeping — *apparatus wears* — and his printed error propagated
into Rundle's 1939 pamphlet and into the valley.

**The inversion.** Vose, and Sill after him, read the 1924 rise as proof that the looking had
frightened somebody honest. But the tests rose in the first full season on new glass, and the
patrons' petition came in January 1925, *after* the rise. The chronology is backwards, and it was
backwards for two men, only one of whom had an interest in it.

**Documents.** Strawn's circuit books for 1921, 1922, 1923 and 1924 and the photostat of the 1923
entry (Item 9); the Ammon letter of 2 July 1923 (Item 10); Vose's 1926 address; Kyle's memoir.

### 7. An Award Without a Finding (January–May 1925)

**Who.** Alonzo Frick, attorney of Ashlin, chairman; a board of three; Ansel Keddie; the petitioning
patrons.

**What and when.** In **January 1925** — in the winter, when a farmer has time to be angry — the
Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned, saying their milk had never tested as their neighbors' tested and
they had been paid accordingly for a very long time. Their own reckoning of the loss was **67,000
lb of butterfat**, and nothing was ever brought forward to disturb it. The by-laws provided for
arbitration and the officers did not resist it. Frick was named chairman, he supposed because he
was the one of the three who kept no cow.

**Three sittings, not one.** The first was at the Association's office at Ashlin on **17 March
1925**. Frick asked to have the second and third at the station itself, in the weigh room, so the
board might see the plant instead of hearing it described — a fact confirmed by four separate
witnesses: Frick, Duncan Keddie, Cleve Oram (who stood outside on the platform in the rain through
one of them), and Orra Keddie's diary.

**Three explanations, all defeated elsewhere.** Water, put by Selby Vose "without heat and without
proof." The tester taking cream, put by the bookkeeper Wilbur Teague in the way an accountant puts
a thing he wants said without saying it — *there is no cross-examination that reaches a witness of
that kind, and I did not attempt one.* The hay, from the 1908 report read into the record by
somebody, which had the advantage of blaming nobody in the room. **The glass was not among them and
was never named.**

**The four exhibits.** The station test book in Keddie's hand, 4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924; the
receiving book of the station; Emmet Brant's private weigh book, begun 1 January 1897, lent and
wanted back; and **the circuit notebook of the state dairy inspector for the year 1923** — the
volume containing the 12 June condemnation. The answer was in the room, in writing, in evidence.
Kyle carried the books in and out and could not say that any man laid one beside another and
looked. The crate itself was twenty feet overhead through two of the three sessions, and was never
mentioned by anyone in any session.

**The outcome.** Keddie gave nine sentences on 17 March and said he had never taken a pound of
cream out of that station. He was **dismissed on 30 April 1925**, four days before the board
reported, with the board still sitting and no finding against him from it. On **4 May** the board
found no cause established and awarded the patrons **$3,800**, without admission, charged to the
station account. Effie Loomis took it down and did not think it her place to ask how both halves of
the sentence could be true at once.

**Documents.** Frick's 1946 recollections with the exhibit list; Keddie's statement and unsent
letter; Effie Loomis's minute-book quotation; Kyle's memoir; Oram's tape; Orra Keddie's diary.

### 8. The Ladder (1939–1979)

**Who.** Dell Rundle, schoolteacher; Ivy Keddie, graduate student at Farland, born 1931; Rosalie
Cudd; Professor Leland Vaught.

**What and when.** In **1939**, while his jubilee pamphlet was in type, Rundle was allowed to look at
the Association's old ledgers on an unrelated errand and saw the station-gain entry. He wrote: *I
must go back on Chapter IV. Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green LIGHT. It
reached the Green HEAVY, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand.* He printed the
refutation, left the slander standing beside it, never asked where the surplus came from, and
reprinted the whole thing unaltered in 1959.

In **February 1958** Ivy Keddie went up the ladder into the loft over the intake room and found,
under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff, the crate stenciled AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET,
carried up in June 1923 and never moved. On **9 March 1958** she measured the **nine** surviving
pipettes against a calibrated burette, five runs each: mean **16.72 c.c.** against a blown mark of
17.6 — 95 hundredths of the mark. Her insight was about the *kind* of number: everyone before her
had treated the gap as a fixed quantity and it is not one. **The station did not lose a set number
of points. It lost a twentieth of whatever was there.**

She assembled five bodies of record that had never been in one room: the test book, the Brant weigh
book (lent by Roy Brant, and lent is the word his family insists on), sixteen seasons copied out for
her by Rosalie Cudd at Alder Corners, Strawn's notebooks, and the Association's ledgers. **Bulletin
No. 471** was issued in October 1958. Its conclusion — that no milk was watered and no cream was
stolen, that the tests were low by one part in twenty because the pipettes were short by one part in
twenty, and that the difference stands in the Association's own ledgers as station gain — is right.
Vaught's own introduction to it is corrupted by a transposed digit (16.27 for 16.72) that he never
reconciles with the conclusion printed four sections later on the same sheet.

**The delay.** Glass condemned 1923, cause found 1958: thirty-five years. Keddie dismissed 1925:
thirty-three. Dead twenty-four years when the sheet was read that cleared him.

**And it did not arrive.** The newspaper got the mechanism right and nearly every detail wrong. Cleve
Oram in 1977 still had his father's belief that cream left the platform light. Vira Toland in 1979,
twenty years after the bulletin, was still saying he took the cream and *I am not going to dress it
up for you.*

**Documents.** Rundle's 1939 note in press and 1959 footnote; Ivy's measurement sheet (Item 14) and
1959 talk; Rosalie Cudd's letter and table; Bulletin 471; the Chronicle of 3 October 1958; Falke's
1959 report; the Toland and Oram tapes.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie and Ansel Keddie: she was his granddaughter.**
Ansel and Orra's son was Duncan Keddie, who taught school at Ashlin; Ivy was Duncan's daughter,
born 1931. Three first-person sources inside the family say so: Ivy herself ("Ansel Keddie was her
grandfather… her father was Duncan Keddie"), Duncan in his 1968 notes ("My daughter is Ivy, born in
1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already
twice seen it printed otherwise"), and Roy Brant. Pointing elsewhere: the *Ashlin Chronicle* of 3
October 1958 calls her his great-niece, and the county agent's 1959 report repeats it. Those two are
not independent — the report post-dates the paper and reproduces its error — and the agent
retracted in a note appended to his own report ("Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the
great-niece; the error on page two is mine"). **Method: first-person family testimony plus a
self-correction, against a newspaper error and its echo.** Duncan's remark that he had "twice seen
it printed otherwise" is itself a contemporaneous record of the error being made.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant: second cousins — the children of first cousins.**
Orra Keddie and Emmet Brant were sister and brother. Orra's son Duncan and Emmet's son Roy are
therefore first cousins. Ivy is Duncan's daughter and Hazel is Roy's, which makes them second
cousins. All three people in the line say exactly this — Roy Brant ("My daughter Hazel and his
daughter Ivy are second cousins, and I mention it because both of them have had to have it explained
to them by me more than once"), Duncan Keddie, and Hazel Brant ("People are forever telling me she
is my niece or my cousin once removed or some such, and she is not") — and Rundle's 1959 footnote
agrees. Against them: Vira Toland and Cleve Oram both say "first cousin once removed," which is the
valley's shorthand and is precisely the error Hazel says she is forever being handed.
**What the sources leave open in the intervening generation:** whether Orra and Emmet were whole or
half siblings. Roy says full brother and sister, same mother and same father, and goes out of his
way to rebut "people who like a story with a stepmother in it"; his daughter Hazel says Orra was "a
half sister to my grandfather Emmet, by his father's second wife"; Duncan says only "brother and
sister." Nothing in Orra's own hand survives from before her 1889 marriage, so no document decides
it. Note that it does not change the answer — half-siblings still produce first cousins and then
second cousins — but the blood degree is genuinely open, and I lean to Roy's version because his
denial is specific and pointed and his daughter disclaims her own reliability on family history.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose, the Association's first president.**
Sources: Effie Loomis ("son of the late president"); Selby himself in a printed annual report ("My
father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law under which every patron in this
Association is paid on the test of the station where he delivers"); Duncan Keddie; and Bertram Kyle,
who sat outside his door for twenty-five years and calls the nephew story nonsense. Against: Vira
Toland and Dell Rundle say nephew — and Rundle's version has Selby himself referring to "his
uncle," which is exactly the anecdote form in which a relationship gets miscast. **Method: a
first-person claim in a printed document, plus two office witnesses, against store-and-funeral
history.**
**Why it matters.** Gideon wrote Article VII, including the clause that the test of the station
where a man delivers shall govern — no appeal, no re-test anywhere else. His son administered that
clause for twenty-five years, sent the 1919 water circular under its authority, and defended both in
1926. The rule that converted a faulty instrument into twenty-six years of underpayment, and left
the patrons no remedy but to quarrel with a figure, was written by the father and enforced by the
son; and the father died in 1917, before anybody thought to ask him what the clause was for. Three
narrators — the secretary, the schoolteacher and the arbitration chairman — arrive independently at
the judgment that this sentence is the hinge of the whole business.

**B4. The Vose and Keddie families: probably no relationship — but the sources cannot settle it.**
Vira Toland states it flatly and makes it the sting of the 1919 letter: Orra Keddie and old Mrs.
Vose were first cousins, "everybody knew that," so the accusation was "not only the Association
saying it… it was somebody's cousin saying it." Cleve Oram says the Keddies and the Voses "were
cousins of some sort," has it from his mother who had it from hers, and admits he could not draw the
line on paper. Against them stands Duncan Keddie, who denies it outright: he has heard it from two
women in Ashlin, people confuse them with the unrelated Ashlin Voses, and his mother — a woman who
could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection — never once put a Vose in it.
**Method and verdict.** Both affirmations are self-declared hearsay at second or third hand; the
denial comes from the son in the house and is specific about the mechanism of the mistake. That
tilts it, but a family negative is not proof, and the one record that could decide it does not
exist: Orra's diary survives only from 1897, nothing in her hand survives from before the marriage,
and no Vose appears in what does survive. **I therefore decline to call it settled**, and record it
as: no relationship established, the balance of evidence against one, and the valley's belief in it
sociologically real whether or not it was true.

## Section C — Math

### C1.

**(a) The tests read 95% of the truth — short by one part in twenty (5%).**
The nine surviving pipettes were blown and stamped on the stem at **17.6 c.c.**, the standard
charge in the state. Measured against a calibrated burette, five runs each, their individual means
were 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 — **mean 16.72 c.c.**, short by
0.88 c.c.

    16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly
    0.88 ÷ 17.6  = 0.05 exactly

The neck of a test bottle is ruled to read percent *for one charge and no other*. Put in 95% of the
charge, liberate 95% of the fat, get 95% of the column, and read it off in the ordinary way. The
error is therefore **proportional, not additive**.

**(b) True average 3.80; recorded tests short by 0.19 of a point.**

    3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80
    3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19

Cross-checked four independent ways, all on sound glass: the 1896 first season on Larrow Green's
borrowed glass, 3.80; the nine weeks of 1911 on a borrowed measure, 3.80; the 1924 season on the new
glass, 3.80; and sixteen summers of the same four Hessel Bottom herds at Alder Corners, mean 3.799.

**(c) 76,000 lb of butterfat.** Over the twenty-five full delivery years 1898–1922 the station
received 40,000,000 lb of milk (1,600,000 lb a year).

    Credited at 3.61%: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb
    Delivered at 3.80%: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb
    Difference:                                  76,000 lb

**(d) $19,000; the patrons received exactly one fifth of it.**

    76,000 lb × $0.25 = $19,000
    $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20

*Rejected inputs and why:* 44,000,000 lb (the bookkeeper's, scoped to a wider window and then
mis-scoped by Rundle to the 25 claim years); 3.16 for the station average (the bookkeeper's, echoed
by the chairman who heard him testify — not an independent second source, and 3.16 ÷ 0.95 = 3.33,
which matches nothing observed); 27 cents for fat (two non-bookkeepers from memory, against the
annual sheets); $19,600 (the patrons' own petition figure); 118,000 lb (derived from a transposed
16.27).

### C2. Emmet Brant, 1910 — 200,000 lb even delivered, paid on a test of 3.42.

**(a) Credited:** 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb of butterfat.**

**(b) Owed and not credited:**

    True test: 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60
    True fat:  200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb
    Shortfall: 7,200 − 6,840 = 360 lb

(Check by proportion: 6,840 × (1 ÷ 0.95 − 1) = 6,840 × 0.052632 = 360 lb.)

**(c) Worth $86.40** at the 24 cents a pound that butterfat made in 1910 (360 × $0.24). At the
25-cent average used for the period totals it would be $90.00; I use the year-specific price
because the question asks for that year, and flag that the two are different figures for different
purposes.

*Note on the shape of the loss:* because the error was a proportion, the richest milk lost most.
A herd recorded at 4.56 was short 0.24 of a point; one recorded at 3.42 was short 0.18. No man had
any way of knowing his good cows cost him more than his poor ones.

### C3.

**(a) Credited to patrons on the station's own test: 1,444,000 lb.**
**(b) Received at Larrow Green in the station's cream: 1,520,000 lb.**
**(c) The difference, 76,000 lb, is 5.26% of one thing and 5.00% of another, and the distinction is
the whole point:**

    76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 0.05263 → 5.26%, i.e. "five and a quarter per cent" — of the fat CREDITED
    76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = 0.05000 → 5.00% exactly           — of the fat actually DELIVERED

Confirmed on the specimen year 1898 in the ledger: 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26%; 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.00%.
The ledger's "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" is reckoned against the figure the bookkeeper
had already entered, which is natural for a bookkeeper and is the *wrong* denominator for
diagnosis. **Five percent is the one that is also the glass** — 0.05 is exactly 0.88 ÷ 17.6. Both
the bookkeeper and the bulletin's author state this backwards ("five and a quarter percent of the
fat actually in the cream"); Ivy Keddie and the curator state it correctly.

**(a) $77.93.**

### C5.

**(a) The glass was in use twenty-six years and about six weeks — 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923. The
money runs over twenty-five full delivery years, 1898–1922.**
They are different numbers because both end years are partial. The station's own outfit went into
service on 1 May 1897, a season already begun (the test book's summary leaf records 1897 simply as
"part year"), and it came out of service in the middle of the 1923 season. The ledger's station
column, from which the 40,000,000 lb and the price average were taken, gives complete years, so the
computation is scoped to the twenty-five whole years that lie inside the twenty-six-year span. The
bulletin's conclusion uses the full span for the *anomaly* and the 25-year window for the *money*,
and both are correct for what they describe.

**(b) The first season's intake cannot be determined from the sources.**
The station opened 4 May 1896 and ran to the end of that year, but that milk was carried on the
creamery's own books before there was a station account; the curator states flatly that the figure
"is not in the ledgers and is nowhere else." Two men supply it from memory decades later and they
do not agree: the buttermaker, in 1934, says **about 900,000 lb**; the office clerk, in 1953, says
**a good 1,100,000 lb**. They are independent of each other, which is what makes this
irreducible — two unaided recollections nearly forty and nearly sixty years after the fact, with no
document to arbitrate. **The honest answer is that it is not recoverable; the range is 0.9–1.1
million lb.** What *is* established about that season is its average test: 3.80, on borrowed
Larrow Green glass.

## Section D — Logic

### D1. The nine weeks of 1911.

**When and how long.** From the morning of **8 May 1911**, when the station's own pipette broke and
a borrowed measure was put in its place, to **10 July 1911**, when the station's own measure came
back into use — **nine weeks**.

**The variable:** the measuring pipette, and nothing else. **Held constant:** the same patrons, the
same herds, the same pasture and the same ground, the season, the acid, the test bottles, the
four-bottle hand tester, the same tester turning the crank at the same taught speed, the same book
and the same five columns, the same station, the same scales. **The result:** the recorded average
rose from 3.61 to 3.80 the morning the substitute went in, held there for nine weeks, and fell back
to 3.61 the day the station's own measure returned — recorded contemporaneously by a man who did
not know what he was recording and who set a question mark against it. Roy Brant confirms the rise
was on every farm on the Bottom, and that his father entered both the rise and the fall.

**What it establishes that a calculation cannot.** The arithmetic of 16.72 ÷ 17.6 shows only that a
short pipette *could* produce a 5% shortfall. The nine weeks show that swapping the instrument
*did* move the figures, by that amount, in both directions, with a clean before-and-after and a
restoration. It is an intervention, not a correlation, and it kills at a stroke every explanation
lodged in the herds, the ground, the feed, the weather or the men — because none of those was
altered and the figures were. It also clears the tester specifically: the same man made every test
in that interval, under no different supervision.

**What it does not establish.** (i) It does not *measure* anything — neither instrument was checked
against a burette in 1911, so the interval fixes no magnitude and cannot by itself say the error was
proportional rather than additive. (ii) It does not, on its own, say which of the two glasses was
the true one; that direction is fixed only by the 1958 burette measurement and by the outside
comparisons (Larrow Green 3.78, Alder Corners 3.80, the 1896 and 1924 seasons at 3.80). (iii) It
does not establish where the borrowed measure came from — three incompatible accounts survive, and
the one naming the state inspector cannot be right as stated, since the substitute was in use from
8 May and his visit was in June. (iv) It did not even survive intact into the record: the version in
public circulation was **six** weeks, and that shortened version is what let the 1926 lecturer
dismiss it as "six weeks of June and July, when every herd in the county is at its best." On the
true dates the rebuttal collapses — the interval began 8 May, before the flush, with the cows still
on bog hay, and the figures fell back on 10 July in the middle of the flush.

### D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream.

**Reason 1 — the direction of the surplus (the Association's own ledgers, and a scale).** Cream
stolen at the Hollow would have reached Larrow Green *light*. It reached Larrow Green *heavy*:
every year, 3,040 lb over on the specimen year 1898, "five and a quarter per cent, as usual," every
year for the life of the account. Dell Rundle reasoned this out in 1939 from the ledger and printed
it. Independently, the buttermaker re-weighed every can himself off the wagon — *the platform man
weighed it and I weighed it over again, because I have never in my life taken another man's figure
for what was going into my churn* — and recorded the Hollow's cream coming in heavy on page after
page of his own receiving book from 1893.

**Reason 2 — the nine weeks of 1911 (the test book).** The same man, in the same room, with the
same habits and no supervision at all, produced 3.80 for nine weeks and 3.61 on either side of
them. A thief does not become honest on 8 May and dishonest again on 10 July, on the day a piece of
glass is returned.

**Reason 3 — the milk over the ridge (Alder Corners).** From 1915, four Hessel Bottom men hauled to
a cheese factory in another county through sixteen summer flushes, on a stranger's own glass, in a
trade where watered milk announces itself in the vat by next morning. Their season averages sat
between 3.78 and 3.82 for sixteen years running. Keddie never touched those cans.

**Why these are independent.** They come from different instruments, different counties, different
trades and different interests: a bookkeeping series read by an outsider on an unrelated errand; a
buttermaker's own scale; a contemporaneous entry in the tester's own book; and a cheesemaker with a
commercial motive to catch anything wrong and no knowledge of the quarrel at all. No two of them
share a source, and none of them depends on the 1958 measurement.

**Which survives without the 1958 measurement.** All three, but the cleanest is Reason 1. It was
available in ink from 1898, was seen and shrugged at by the bookkeeper who made the entries and by
the buttermaker who weighed the cream, and was correctly reasoned out in print in 1939 — nineteen
years before anybody put a pipette to a burette. Reason 3 was available from 1915 and Reason 2 from
1911.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question those entries?** Barely, and never again. He invented the
station-gain line himself in 1905 and carried it every year to 1945. He says he put the station's
account to the manager **once, in 1916, was told to let it alone, and let it alone** — "a bookkeeper
who takes his employer's business up a second time after being told once has generally begun to look
for another employer." He explains the psychology exactly: because the figure came in at five and a
quarter every year, the words "as usual" got into the entry and stayed there, and *a figure that
comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture*. He never connected that furniture to the
accusation he then put to the 1925 board — that a station in one pair of hands would sooner or later
have a little taken from it. The clerk who copied the line for a quarter of a century says the
bookkeeper "never asked anybody anything in forty years," which conflicts with the bookkeeper's own
account of 1916; I take the bookkeeper's, as first-person testimony about his own act, and note the
clerk's as evidence of how it looked from the outer room.

### D3. Did the manager know the tests ran low before the glass was condemned?

**That the tests ran low: certainly, and he never denied it.** It is the express premise of
everything he did. He sent the 1919 circular precisely because, in his own printed words, *a manager
who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing to the men who make them is not
being kind to them; he is being idle at their expense.* In 1926 he told the membership that for a
generation the milk brought to that station tested below the milk brought to Larrow Green. He was
also told, twice and directly: by his bookkeeper in 1916, who raised the station account and was
told to let it alone; and by the state inspector in 1919, who informed him to his face that he had
tested for added water at that station on every visit and had never found any.

**That the ledger showed the fat arriving heavy — i.e. that the shortfall was on his own side of the
account: cannot be determined, and the balance is against it.** The bookkeeper's argument is
inferential: he laid the annual sheet before the manager every spring, the manager signed at the
foot, and "a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him." Against that stands the one
eyewitness to the physical act — the clerk who sat outside the manager's door for twenty-five years:
*He read the totals and he never read the lines above them. He was not a man who read. He would take
a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign. I watched him do it a thousand
times.* That is specific, repeated, first-hand observation against an inference drawn from a
signature, and I weight it accordingly.

**Conclusion.** He knew the station tested low and had known it for years; he had been warned twice
and acted on neither warning; he had every opportunity to know that the fat came down heavy and
almost certainly never took it in. What is not in doubt is that in 1926, three years after the
condemnation, he was still explaining the 1924 rise by the arrival of scrutiny rather than by the
arrival of new glass, and had the date of the condemnation itself a month wrong in print.

## Section E — Contradictions

*Format: claim A vs claim B — who — resolution and method. "UNSETTLED" where the sources do not
decide.*

### E1(i). Two or more narrators carrying the same wrong value

These matter because numbers look corroborated when they are only copied. In each case I count
**independent** sources, not narrators.

1. **The award: $3,500 vs $3,800.** $3,500 — Vira Toland (1979) and Cleve Oram (1977). $3,800 — the

## Section F — Theory

### F1. What actually produced the shortfall

**One instrument, wrong by a fixed proportion, for twenty-six years.**

On 1 May 1897 the Ostrey Hollow station stopped borrowing the Larrow Green buttermaker's own working
glass and put into service its own outfit from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet: six dozen milk-test pipettes
blown and stamped **17.6 c.c.**, the standard charge in the state. They delivered a mean of
**16.72 c.c.** — 95 hundredths of their mark. On the maker's own reconstruction a mold had been
reground and the whole lot ran short, and nobody caught it, because a lot that is uniformly wrong
passes every consistency check there is.

A butterfat test reads the fat column **directly** in percent off a bottle neck ruled for one charge
and no other. The operator weighs nothing. Put in a charge short by one part in twenty and you
liberate a column short by one part in twenty, and the neck — which knows nothing of the
substitution — is read off in the ordinary way. So:

- **Why an error at all:** the pipette is the one instrument in the plant that is *itself the
  standard*. Scales can be checked against test weights, acid against a titration, bottle necks
  against mercury, the tester's speed by counting. An instrument that is the measure cannot be caught
  by measuring with it — only by a burette brought in from outside, and burettes did not reach the
  state's field men until 1921.
- **Why that particular size:** 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly. The size of the error is the size of the
  manufacturing shortfall and nothing else. It was **proportional, not additive** — the station did
  not lose a set number of points, it lost a twentieth of whatever was there — which is why every
  herd still ranked correctly against every other herd at that station, why the figures looked
  orderly and internally consistent, and why the richest milk lost most (0.24 of a point off a herd
  recorded at 4.56; 0.18 off one recorded at 3.42).
- **Why it began when it did:** 1 May 1897, the day the short glass went into service. The station's
  first season, 1896, on borrowed good glass, averaged 3.80. From 1898 the average was 3.61 and
  stayed within three hundredths of it for a quarter of a century.
- **Why it ended when it did:** 12 June 1923, when the state inspector — carrying, for the first
  time in his career, a burette the state had begun issuing in 1921 — ran the pipettes against it,
  found they would not deliver their mark, condemned the lot and told the manager to order new. New
  glass was in use from July 1923; 1924 averaged 3.80 and stayed there.
- **Why nothing had to change at either end:** because nothing did. The cows, the ground, the hay,
  the men, the scales, the acid, the bottles, the hand tester, the tester's method and his book were
  identical before and after both boundaries. Only the glass changed — twice, in 1897 and in 1923,
  and for nine weeks in 1911 as well, and the figures moved all three times and at no other time.

**Consequences.** Over the twenty-five full delivery years 1898–1922 the patrons were credited with
1,444,000 lb of butterfat and had delivered 1,520,000 lb. The missing **76,000 lb**, worth
**$19,000** at 25 cents, was never lost: it arrived at Larrow Green in the cream, was weighed in,
and stood in the Association's own ledgers year by year as **"station gain, five and a quarter per
cent, as usual."** The patrons' loss and the Association's surplus were the same 76,000 lb, written
down in ink, on an open shelf, in a room anybody might walk into, and read out at the annual meeting
to fifty farmers with their hats on their knees. *It was not a secret. It was worse than a secret.
It was in plain sight with a name on it.*

### The three competing explanations, and what defeats each

**1. Watered milk.** Advanced by **Selby Vose**, the manager — in the printed circular of 1919, in
his evidence to the 1925 board ("without heat and without proof"), and in his 1926 annual address,
where he called the 1924 rise "the measure of it." Endorsed independently by **Hobart Sill**, the
buttermaker ("You cannot tell me nine families all got honest in one summer").
*Defeated by:* (a) the state inspector's lactometer, run for added water at that station on every
visit of a twenty-nine-year circuit, never once positive — and said to the manager's face in 1919;
(b) sixteen summers of cheesemaking over the ridge on the milk of four of those very farms, with
never a soft curd, never a complaint and never a docked cent — watered milk cannot be hidden from a
cheesemaker, because the curd tells him the next morning; (c) the nine weeks of 1911, in which
nobody's cans changed; (d) the 1908 committee's explicit finding of *no dishonesty of any kind, in
any quarter*; and (e) the chronology, which is backwards — the tests rose in 1924, the first full
season on new glass, and the petition came in January 1925, after the rise.

**2. The tester stealing cream.** Advanced by **Wilbur Teague**, the bookkeeper, to the 1925 board,
as an insinuation the chairman correctly identified as unanswerable ("cream is portable, and that
station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons… I do not say what any particular person
did"). It became the valley's settled verdict — printed as certainty in the 1939 pamphlet, held by
the cream hauler's family, and still being told in 1979.
*Defeated by:* (a) **the direction of the surplus** — stolen cream reaches the creamery light; this
reached it heavy, every year, by 5¼% of the credited fat, in the Association's own ledger and on the
buttermaker's own re-weighing; (b) the nine weeks of 1911, when the same man in the same room
produced 3.80; (c) the flatness of the gap — 0.19 in July and 0.19 in February, wet years and dry,
which is an instrument's signature and not a man's; (d) the shortfall falling proportionally hardest
on the richest milk, which no thief could arrange and none would; and (e) the Brant weigh book,
which shows the tester's scale and the patron's scale agreeing for thirty years, eliminating the
weighing and leaving the glass standing.

**3. The wet hay of Hessel Bottom.** Advanced by the **1908 investigating committee under Nyle
Grigg**, reprinted with a covering letter by Grigg in 1919, elaborated with real soil chemistry by
**Dr. Roland Kepp** in 1926, and briefly conceded by the **county agent** in 1959.
*Defeated by:* (a) the college's own feeding trial, Bulletin No. 214 — *not more than five
hundredths of one per cent in winter, and nothing that could be measured on summer pasture* —
against a gap of nineteen hundredths standing flat in every month: four times too small and the
wrong shape; (b) the same four Bottom herds testing 3.78–3.82 at Alder Corners off that same wet
ground, sixteen summers running; (c) the nine weeks of 1911, in which the hay did not change and the
figures did; and (d) the 1908 committee's own sampling, which was of January, February and March
only — so it never tested the hypothesis it announced. Grigg and Kepp both quoted the bulletin as
"two tenths" and both dropped the summer clause; the county agent alone sent for it, read it, and
withdrew.

### Subsidiary 1 — Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?

**No — and the failure is sharper than a simple omission.** The board did go to look at the plant:
at the chairman's own request, the second and third of its three sessions were held in the station's
weigh room, and it watched the scales tried and a test made from sampling through to reading. But
the station had been on new, accurate glass since July 1923. **The board went to the station
expressly to examine the apparatus and examined the replacement.** The condemned crate had been in
the loft over that very room since June 1923 and was twenty feet above the board's heads throughout;
the secretary, who took every word down, states flatly that the crate was never mentioned by
anybody, in any of the three sessions.

**But the record of its condemnation was in evidence.** The chairman's own exhibit list has four
items, and Exhibit 4 is *"the circuit notebook of the state dairy inspector for the year 1923"* — the
volume containing "Ran their milk pipettes against the burette. These measures will not deliver
their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them." The answer was physically in the hearing
room, in writing, as one of only four books before the board, and nobody read it for what it was.
The clerk who carried the four books in and out says he could not tell you that any man in that room
laid one beside another and looked. The chairman, twenty-one years later, wrote that he had wondered
ever since "whether the answer was in that room and simply was not in any of the three stories."
It was.

### Subsidiary 2 — What did the tester himself believe?

**Publicly: nothing he could prove.** "I have tested as I was taught and as the state man saw me do
it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, and I cannot tell you why the figures are what
they are." He would not write down what he could not swear to; the 1911 anomaly got a question mark
and he let it stand for fourteen years rather than put anything in its place that he did not know.

**Privately, two things that do not sit comfortably together.** His son, seventeen that year and in
the house, says he came home in the summer of 1911 saying **it was something in the glass** — said
it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not his way — and never dared put it in the
book, having nothing in his hand but a suspicion against the settled practice of every station in
the county. And the arbitration chairman records that in the corridor at Ashlin in 1925 Keddie told
him privately that **he had always thought the patrons were at fault** and would not say so in the
room, having lived among them thirty years.

**Best reading.** He suspected the instrument in 1911, could not prove it, kept on for fourteen more
years writing down every figure the glass gave him — and by 1925, with the glass long since replaced
and unmentioned, had fallen back on the only explanation the room was offering. His granddaughter's
wish, in 1958, that it be printed "that he never knew," is generous and not quite accurate: he
suspected and could not prove, which is the harder thing. His own summary of his position stands as
the epitaph of the whole affair — *a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name.*

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** (149 words)

For twenty-six years a cooperative creamery's hill station tested its farmers' milk with pipettes
marked 17.6 c.c. that delivered only 16.72 — five percent short. Every test therefore read
ninety-five percent of the truth, and because the by-law made the station's own test final, six
farms were underpaid 76,000 pounds of butterfat, worth $19,000. The missing fat was never missing:
it reached the creamery in the cream and sat in the ledgers as "station gain." The valley blamed
wet hay, then watered cans, then the tester, who was dismissed in 1925 and died under it in 1934. A
state inspector condemned the glass in 1923 without drawing the inference; his notebook was an
exhibit at the hearing and nobody read it. It took eight separate records — ledger, test book, farm
weigh book, inspector's circuit books, a cheesemaker's figures from another county, a diary, a
by-law and a burette — because no single one of them was wrong, and no single one was enough.
