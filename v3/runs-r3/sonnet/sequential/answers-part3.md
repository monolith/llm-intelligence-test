## Section C — Math

**C1.**
(a) The pipettes were blown to deliver 17.6 cc but actually delivered a mean of 16.72 cc (the figure on the signed measurement sheet and in Ivy Keddie's own talk — I reject Bulletin 471's internal Section-V figure of 16.27 cc as a transposition error, because the bulletin's *own* final conclusion paragraph, quoted identically elsewhere, gives "one part in twenty" and "76,000 lb," which only follows from 16.72, not 16.27). Ratio: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly. So every test read 95% of the truth — short by one part in twenty (5%).
(b) Recorded average: 3.61. True average = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. Shortfall = 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 of a point**.
(c) Over the 25 full delivery years (1898–1922), the station received 40,000,000 lb of milk (Effie Loomis, Ivy Keddie, and the catalog's curatorial note all agree on this figure; I reject Teague's and Rundle's "44,000,000," which is corroborated by only two sources that may share one origin and may cover a different span). Credited fat: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb. True fat: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb. Difference = **76,000 lb of butterfat not credited**.
(d) At the period average price of $0.25/lb: 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000**. The patrons actually received the 1925 arbitration award of $3,800. Fraction received: 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **1/5 (one-fifth)**.

**C2.** Largest patron (Emmet Brant), 1910 leaf: 200,000 lb delivered, paid on a test of 3.42.
(a) Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of butterfat.
(b) True test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60. True fat: 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb. Owed and not credited: 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb**.
(c) At 1910's specific price of $0.24/lb (given by Roy Brant for that exact year): 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40**. (Using the multi-year average of $0.25 instead gives $90 — I use the year-specific figure since the question asks about 1910 specifically.)

**C3.** Using the same 25-year, 40,000,000 lb window as C1:
(a) Credited to patrons: **1,444,000 lb**.
(b) Actually received at Larrow Green in the station's cream: **1,520,000 lb**.
(c) The difference, 76,000 lb, is **5% of the fat actually delivered** (76,000 ÷ 1,520,000) but **5.25% of the fat credited** (76,000 ÷ 1,444,000). Both are correct; they differ only in denominator. The bookkeeper's ledger entries use the 5.25% figure (a bookkeeper naturally reckons against the number already entered in his own books); the 5% figure is the one that matches the glassware's actual shortfall ratio and is therefore the physically meaningful one.

**C4.**
(a) $77.93 — the invoice total (quoted identically in the catalog and in Ammon & Sons' own firm history; I reject "$77.39," which appears in two places — the Chronicle, and, oddly, in Ammon's own prose paraphrase of her own quoted invoice — as a transposition of the digits).
(b) **Six dozen (72) pipettes** (per the invoice, "6 doz. milk test pipettes" — I reject Vaught's bulletin and Cleve Oram's "five dozen"). Each was blown to 17.6 cc and delivered a mean of 16.72 cc, short by **0.88 cc**.

**C5.**
(a) The faulty glassware was in service for about 26 years: from 1 May 1897 (per the invoice, catalog provenance, Ammon's own history, and — most tellingly — Orra Keddie's diary entry dated exactly that day) to 12 June 1923 (per Merle Strawn's own contemporaneous inspection notebook — the primary eyewitness source; I reject "12 July 1923," given by Vose and Rundle, as an error, since it appears only in two secondary, non-eyewitness accounts). The money calculation, however, runs over only 25 *full* delivery years (1898–1922). These numbers differ because the full glass-usage period includes two partial years at its edges — the stub of 1897 (glass installed partway through the year) and the stub of 1923 (glass condemned partway through the year, in June) — which cannot be cleanly costed against whole-year ledger totals, so the financial reckoning is confined to the years for which complete annual figures exist.
(b) Cannot be determined precisely. The catalog explicitly notes the station's first-season (4 May–31 December 1896) intake "is not in the ledgers and is nowhere else." Two personal, non-ledger estimates conflict: Hobart Sill recalls "about nine hundred thousand pounds"; Bertram Kyle recalls "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds." Neither is a record; they bracket an unknown true figure by about 20%.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The station's own measuring glass broke on 8 May 1911; a borrowed measure was used until 10 July 1911 — a span of **nine weeks** (well corroborated by Keddie's own dated book entries, Ivy Keddie's talk, and Vaught's bulletin; Dr. Kepp's claim of "six weeks" is an isolated error). The only variable that changed was the measuring instrument itself (sources disagree on where the substitute came from — Ivy Keddie says Larrow Green, Roy Brant says the state inspector's own measure — and this specific point cannot be settled from the sources). Held constant: the same patrons, herds, pasture, season's natural progress, acid, bottles, and tester. Result: the recorded average rose from 3.61 to 3.80 for the full nine weeks and fell back to 3.61 the day the station's own measure returned. This is significant because it is a real, contemporaneous, single-variable experiment — it rules out any theory resting on the herds, the land, the season, or the tester's honesty, since none of those changed, yet the number moved with the glass alone. It corroborates the 1958 finding independently of arithmetic on old pipettes. What it does *not* establish: the specific mechanism (that a short pipette charge produces a proportionally low reading) — that required the 1958 burette calibration; nor does it, strictly on its own, prove the tester never took cream at any other time, only that changing the glass alone explains the nine-week rise.

**D2.** Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream:
1. The ledger's own "station gain" line (Teague's bookkeeping, corroborated by the catalog and by Rundle's own retraction) shows *more* butterfat arriving at Larrow Green every year than was ever credited to patrons — the opposite of what theft would produce.
2. The 1911 natural experiment: with the same tester and no change in supervision, the recorded test rose when only the measuring glass changed — inconsistent with a constant rate of skimming.
3. Merle Strawn's independent, disinterested state inspections found the scales true and no added water at every visit — and, per Duncan Keddie, Strawn "was kin to nobody in the valley whatever," giving his testimony special independence.
These are independent because they come from entirely separate record-keeping systems and people — the creamery's bookkeeper, the station's own tester's book, and an outside state official — none derived from or dependent on the others. Reason 1 (the ledger) and reason 2 (the 1911 interval) both predate and do not depend on the 1958 glassware measurement, so either would still hold even if the crate had never been found. Did the bookkeeper ever question the ledger entries that refute his own accusation? No — Teague raised the general discrepancy with manager Vose once, in 1916, and was told to drop it; there is no indication in his own 1948 memoir, written 23 years after he insinuated theft at the hearing, that he ever connected his own "station gain" figures to disprove his own suspicion. Ironically, it was Dell Rundle, a schoolteacher with no stake in the office, who saw the same ledger line and immediately retracted his own theft-theory chapter.

**D3.** Yes, on the sources' evidence, Vose had actual notice well before 1923. Teague's own memoir states he personally raised the discrepancy with Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone — seven years before the glass was condemned. Kyle's memoir adds that Vose signed the annual station sheet every year, though Kyle also notes Vose "read the totals and never read the lines above them," which complicates whether he genuinely absorbed the anomaly's significance rather than merely having had the opportunity to. Vose's own 1926 address never admits a station-level error was ever suspected; he frames the entire history as deliberate watering that "stopped when the Association began to look." My reading: he was informed and had continuous access to the figures, and chose (per his own bookkeeper's account) not to pursue the question — but the sources do not establish that he understood the specific cause before 1958, only that he knew something in the numbers was persistently anomalous and declined to investigate it.

## Section F — Theory

**F1.** The Ostrey Hollow station's tests ran low for 26 years because its milk-test pipettes — a stock lot bought new in 1897 from Ammon & Sons — were manufactured with a bore that delivered less milk than the mark on their stem promised (16.72 cc actual against a 17.6 cc mark, a shortfall of one part in twenty), almost certainly the accidental result of a reground mold rather than any deliberate act. Because the test bottle's graduated neck is ruled to read percent for the *full* marked charge only, a short charge produces a reading low by exactly the same proportion, every time, regardless of season, herd, or the man at the crank — which explains why the gap was flat across every month of every year (a seasonal or land-based cause could never do that) and why it fell hardest, in absolute terms, on the richest milk. It began in spring 1897, when this new but flawed glass replaced the accurate glass borrowed from Larrow Green, and it ended in June 1923, when a state inspector — using a calibrated burette, a tool only issued to field inspectors from 1921 — finally checked the pipettes against an outside standard and condemned them; nothing at the station or on the farms needed to change, because nothing there was ever wrong.

Three competing explanations, and what defeats each:
1. **Water added by patrons** (Selby Vose). Defeated by the state inspector's own lactometer tests at every visit from 1908 onward, which never found added water.
2. **Cream stolen by the tester** (Wilbur Teague, insinuated; believed by Hobart Sill and Cleve Oram's father). Defeated by the ledger's own "station gain" figures, showing surplus fat arriving at the creamery every year (the opposite of theft), and by the 1911 natural experiment, in which the same tester's figures moved only when the glass changed.
3. **Wet hay thinning the milk** (the 1908 Grigg committee; Dr. Kepp, 1926). Defeated by Bulletin 214's actual feeding-trial result — a depression of only five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer, a small fraction of the constant, year-round 0.19-point gap — and by Alder Corners' fully independent tests of the same land's milk, which consistently ran near 3.80, not depressed.

Subsidiary questions: The 1925 board of arbitration never examined the condemned glassware — the crate, already set aside in the loft since 1923, was never mentioned in any of the three sessions; the award's phrase "books and appliances of the station" was, per the curator's own note, "a form of words… and not a list of anything." What the tester himself believed: the sources give two different private admissions. To his son Duncan, he said as early as 1911 that he thought it was "something in the glass," though he would not commit an unproven suspicion to his own book and wrote only a question mark. To Alonzo Frick, in the hearing corridor in 1925, he privately said he believed the patrons themselves were at fault. I cannot fully reconcile these two reported private beliefs from the sources as given.

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

For 26 years, a small creamery skimming station in Ostrey Hollow paid its farmers on milk tests that ran mysteriously low, while the rest of the valley's milk tested normally. Three explanations circulated — dirty water in the cans, a thieving tester, or poor wet-meadow hay — and each was believed by someone with a stake in believing it. A 1925 arbitration heard all three, proved none, and dismissed the tester anyway, days before awarding the patrons a fifth of what they were actually owed. The truth sat the whole time in a crate of condemned glass pipettes, forgotten in the station's own loft: bought new in 1897, they delivered five percent less milk than their mark promised, so every test read proportionally low — not because of cows, land, or dishonesty, but a manufacturing flaw nobody thought to check. It took a granddaughter with a burette, in 1958, to find it, because the flaw was invisible from every single one of the eight vantage points that had looked at everything except the glass itself.
