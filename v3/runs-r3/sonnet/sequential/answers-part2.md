# Answers — Part 2: Section A Stories 5–8, and Section B

---

## Story 5 — The 1925 Arbitration at Ashlin

**Who:** The board of arbitration (Alonzo Frick, chairman), the Ostrey Hollow patrons, and the Association's officers.
**What:** The patrons petitioned in January 1925. A three-man board sat under attorney Alonzo Frick, with sessions at Ashlin (17 March 1925) and two more at the station itself, in its own weigh room. Three explanations were argued — water (Vose), theft (Teague, by insinuation), and wet hay (the reprinted 1908 report) — and the board found none of them established. Ansel Keddie testified on 17 March that he had tested faithfully for 29 seasons and never taken a pound of cream; Frick believed him. On 30 April 1925, four days before the board's decision, the Association dismissed Keddie anyway — a dismissal with no finding behind it. On 4 May 1925 the board awarded the patrons $3,800, "without admission," charged to the station account. Frick himself called it "an unsatisfactory thing": an award with no finding of cause or fault. The award figure is well corroborated ($3,800: Effie Loomis's minutes, the minute book itself per the catalog, Vose's own address, Frick's recollection, Kyle's memoir) against two folk-memory accounts claiming $3,500 (Vira Toland, Cleve Oram's father) — the quoted primary record outranks memory here.
**Quantities:** Petition Jan. 1925; hearing 17 March–4 May 1925; dismissal 30 April 1925; award $3,800; patrons' own claimed loss at the time was 67,000 lb of butterfat (per Frick) — a different, cruder figure than the 76,000 lb later established scientifically in 1958 (Story 7).
**Documents:** r13 (Frick), r02 (Effie's minutes), r08 (Vose), r23 (Kyle), r01 and r21 (folk accounts, with the wrong award figure).

## Story 6 — The Ledger's Silent Evidence: The Station-Gain Line

**Who:** Bookkeeper Wilbur Teague, and later the clerk Bertram Kyle and pamphleteer Dell Rundle.
**What:** From 1905, Teague entered an annual line in the Association's ledgers: butterfat credited to patrons on the station's own test, against butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in the station's cream. The second figure was always larger — a "station gain," running steadily at about five and a quarter percent, so uniform that "as usual" entered the wording itself. Teague read this discrepancy as consistent with (not proof of) his suspicion that the tester was skimming cream and privately raised it with manager Vose in 1916, who told him to drop it; he never seems to have drawn the more obvious inference from his own numbers — that cream arriving heavy, not light, is the opposite of what theft would produce. Kyle, who carried the same ledger sheets across the office for twenty-five years, likewise "never once" wondered at it. It took Rundle, writing a 1939 jubilee pamphlet, to see the ledger entry by chance and immediately retract his own earlier chapter blaming theft, in a note added while the pamphlet was in press: "Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy… I cannot make my paragraph stand." This ledger evidence, entirely independent of the 1958 pipette discovery, had disproved the theft theory decades before anyone found the crate.
**Quantities:** Typical single-year entry: 57,760 lb credited, 60,800 lb received, 3,040 lb gain (5.25% of credited / 5% of received) — figures identical across three sources.
**Documents:** r05 (Teague), r23 (Kyle), r07 (Nym's catalog, Item 6), r12 (Rundle, including his self-correcting "note added while in press").

## Story 7 — The 1958 Discovery: The Crate, the Pipettes, and Bulletin 471

**Who:** Ivy Keddie (Ansel's granddaughter), Professor Leland Vaught.
**What:** In February 1958 Ivy Keddie, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, climbed to the loft over the old station's intake room and found the crate of pipettes condemned by the state inspector in 1923 and never examined since. Nine survived intact. On 9 March 1958 she measured them against a calibrated burette at Farland: blown to 17.6 cc, they delivered a mean of 16.72 cc — short by 0.88 cc, or 95% of the mark (one part in twenty short). Since a butterfat test reads a column of fat against a neck ruled for the *full* marked charge, a short charge produces a proportionally low reading, in every test, on every day, regardless of season or operator — explaining the flat, unchanging 26-year gap that no herd, land, or personnel theory ever could. Working with Professor Vaught, she published Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958), concluding: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there… the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty." Note: the bulletin's own Section V, using an apparently transposed figure of "16.27 cc," computes a different (and internally inconsistent) shortfall of 118,000 lb, which the bulletin itself admits "is not readily reconciled" with its own final conclusion of 76,000 lb — the conclusion, and the 16.72 cc measurement sheet, are the reliable figures. Ansel Keddie was formally cleared 24 years after his death and 33 years after his dismissal.
**Quantities:** 9 surviving pipettes measured 9 March 1958; mean delivery 16.72 cc against a 17.6 cc mark; bulletin published October 1958.
**Documents:** r04 (Ivy Keddie's talk), r11 (Vaught's bulletin), r07 (Nym's catalog), r19 (Chronicle, with several errors), r22 (Falke's report, which self-corrects two errors after checking the primary bulletin).

## Story 8 — Corroboration Over the Ridge, and the Manufacturer's Side

**Who:** Rosalie and Jerome Cudd of the Alder Corners Cheese Company; Junia Ammon of Ammon & Sons.
**What:** From 1915, four of the six Hessel Bottom farms hauled their summer-flush milk over Redlow Ridge to the Alder Corners Cheese Company in Vessey County rather than to Ostrey Hollow, for sixteen seasons (1915–1930), returning to Ostrey Hollow the rest of each year. Jerome Cudd tested this same milk on his own independent glassware and kept his own book; his figures for those four men, season after season, ran consistently between 3.78 and 3.82 — never anywhere near the Ostrey Hollow-recorded 3.61 — proving on entirely independent equipment, years before the crate was found, that the milk itself was never poor. His daughter Rosalie supplied these figures to Ivy Keddie by letter in 1958. Separately, Ammon & Sons — the Tarnet firm that sold the station its original 1897 outfit for $77.93 — later gave its own account of what likely happened: a reground mold, producing pipettes that were "consistently wrong," a defect no ordinary spot-check on the blowing floor could catch, since checking a short pipette against another short pipette shows nothing amiss. This account (Junia Ammon, 1949) undercuts the Chronicle's later, evidence-free 1958 accusation that the firm knowingly sold defective glass — though Ammon's own chapter contains a notable internal slip, claiming the firm "sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900" in the same breath as reproducing its own 1897 invoice into that very county.
**Quantities:** Four patrons, 16 seasons (1915–1930), Alder Corners average ≈3.80; invoice total $77.93 for six dozen pipettes, twelve dozen bottles, one acid measure, one hand tester, and freight.
**Documents:** r10 (Rosalie Cudd), r20 (Junia Ammon), r12 (Rundle, background), r18 (Duncan Keddie, provenance).

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**
Granddaughter. Ansel's son Duncan Keddie (b. 1894) is Ivy's father (b. 1931) — Duncan states this explicitly in his own family notes and adds that he has "already twice seen it printed otherwise." Two sources do print it otherwise, calling her "great-niece": *The Ashlin Chronicle* (1958) and county agent Orin Falke's report, in its original text. Falke's report is the one place the error is resolved within the sources themselves: in an appended note, after verifying with the family, Falke writes "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." I take this as settled — a father's direct statement plus a document's own explicit self-correction outrank a hurried newspaper's guess.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**
Second cousins. The chain: Orra Brant (Ansel Keddie's wife) and Emmet Brant were siblings; Orra's son Duncan and Emmet's son Roy are therefore first cousins; Duncan's daughter Ivy and Roy's daughter Hazel, as children of first cousins, are second cousins. Both Hazel herself ("Second cousins… it is not complicated") and Duncan Keddie independently give this exact relationship, explicitly ruling out "cousins once removed." Two outside narrators (Vira Toland, Cleve Oram) call them "first cousin once removed" instead — both wrong by the same margin, both outsiders repeating valley talk rather than family members. What the sources leave open: whether Orra and Emmet were full or half-siblings (Roy says full; his own daughter Hazel says half, "by his father's second wife"). This does not change the cousin arithmetic under ordinary usage, but the sources never resolve which sibling relationship is correct.

**B3. Selby Vose's father.**
Gideon Vose — the Association's founder, first president, and author of the by-laws, including Article VII. This is confirmed independently by Wilbur Teague ("son of the late president"), Bertram Kyle (explicit: "the son, whatever is said at Ashlin… nonsense"), and Duncan Keddie's family notes (explicit correction of the record). Two sources instead call Selby Gideon's "nephew" (Vira Toland; implied by Rundle's pamphlet). Why it matters: Selby, as the son of the man who wrote the very rule — "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern," with no appeal — that made the whole Ostrey Hollow dispute possible, had a personal and institutional stake in defending both his father's rule and the Association's conduct under it. That is plausibly why his 1926 address blames the patrons (water) rather than ever questioning the station's own equipment, and why he told his own bookkeeper in 1916 to drop the question.

**B4. Relationship between the Vose and Keddie families.**
None, on the weight of the evidence — though this cannot be proven with certainty, only shown to be unsupported. Vira Toland reports as settled fact that "Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins. Everybody knew that." Cleve Oram independently repeats a similar claim ("cousins of some sort… my mother had it from her mother"). But Duncan Keddie — Orra's own son — states he investigated this directly: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses… My mother would have told me… and she never once put a Vose in it." He attributes the rumor to confusion with a different, unrelated Vose family at Ashlin. I favor Duncan's account as the closest and most directly informed source, while noting that a family denial, however credible, is not the same as documentary proof of a negative.
