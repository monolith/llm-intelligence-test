# Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

*Written entirely from memory of the 24 retellings, in the order read, with no re-reading permitted. Eight-story partition is my own construction — no list of the "real" eight was given.*

---

## Story 1 — The Founding and Article VII

**Who:** Gideon Vose and the farmers of Larrow Valley.
**What:** In 1889 the valley's farmers met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, tired of taking whatever price a merchant at Ashlin allowed them for butter. Gideon Vose was elected first president (he held the post until 1911, per Effie Loomis) and wrote the by-laws himself in his own hand. The main creamery was built at Larrow Green on Larrow Creek in 1891. The critical clause is Article VII: patrons are paid on the pounds of butterfat their milk tests at, not on the weight of the milk, and — the hinge of everything that follows — "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern," with no appeal to any other test. Sources disagree on the adoption year: Effie Loomis's official secretary's notes (the most institutionally authoritative surviving account) say 1892, "the year after the creamery opened"; Dell Rundle's jubilee pamphlet says 1891, "the year the churn was set up"; Nyle Grigg's 1919 letter says 1893. I favor 1892 (Effie), though she herself admits the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in a 1938 office move, so even her figure may rest on secondary knowledge — this date cannot be called fully certain.
**Quantities:** One creamery (1891, Larrow Green); one governing by-law (Article VII); founding year 1889; disputed adoption year 1891/1892/1893.
**Documents:** r02 (Effie Loomis), r12 (Rundle pamphlet), r15 (Grigg letter), r22 (Falke report, which explicitly declines to fix the year, citing the lost minute book).

## Story 2 — The Ostrey Hollow Station and Ansel Keddie's Twenty-Nine Seasons

**Who:** Ansel Keddie, tester and separator man.
**What:** The Association built a skimming station seven miles up the valley at Ostrey Hollow over the winter of 1895–96; it opened 4 May 1896. Keddie went up in 1895 to ready it and tested there every season from opening until his 1925 dismissal — 29 seasons. He had no outfit of his own the first year; Hobart Sill, the Larrow Green buttermaker, personally lent his own glass to open the station, and it was used until the station's own outfit arrived. Orra Keddie's diary independently confirms this happened and dates the changeover to 1 May 1897 ("done borrowing of Larrow Green"), matching the station's own book's first leaf ("Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes") — this contradicts Keddie's own unsent letter, which claims in prose the borrowed glass came from "the Ashlin creamery," a self-contradiction within his own document (r03), resolved against him by three other sources (his own book's leaf, Sill, Orra's diary). Keddie's method (described in his unsent 1925 letter) was careful and unvarying: weigh, sample into a numbered jar over ten days, warm and mix, charge the pipette to its mark, acid, spin, read the column against rulings, record in five columns, cross out and initial errors, never overwrite a figure. He tested 31 times a week. He was dismissed 30 April 1925, four days before the arbitration award, and died in 1934, still under suspicion.
**Quantities:** Opened 4 May 1896; 29 seasons (1896–1924); station's own glass in use from 1 May 1897 (per invoice, catalog, Ammon's own history, and Orra's diary — contra Vaught's bulletin and Kyle's memoir, both of which say 1898).
**Documents:** r03 (Keddie's statement/letter), r06 (Strawn's circuit book), r14 (Sill), r24 (Orra's diary), r18 (Duncan Keddie), r01 (Vira Toland, background).

## Story 3 — The Long Complaint and Three Competing Theories (1902–1926)

**Who:** The Hessel Bottom patrons versus the Association's officers and outside observers.
**What:** From 1902 the patrons of Ostrey Hollow complained their milk tested lower than the rest of the valley's, a complaint that recurred every decade. Three theories were advanced, none correct:
(1) *Wet hay* — the Association's 1908 committee, headed by Nyle Grigg, sampled Hessel Bottom herds and blamed the bog meadow's coarse, mineral-poor hay. Grigg reprinted the report in 1919 claiming (wrongly, and contradicting the very report he was reprinting) that sampling ran "the whole of 1908, every month," when the report itself states sampling was confined to January–March. Veterinarian Dr. Roland Kepp revived this theory in 1926, citing the same misquoted "two tenths" figure from Bulletin 214 (the bulletin's real figure, quoted correctly elsewhere four times, is "five hundredths… in winter, and nothing in summer").
(2) *Water added by patrons* — manager Selby Vose's theory, expressed in a 1919 circular to nine families and defended in his 1926 annual address, where he claimed tests rose to 3.90 in 1924 "when the Association began to look." State inspector Merle Strawn's own circuit notebooks record testing for added water at every visit from 1908 on and never finding any.
(3) *Cream theft by the tester* — insinuated by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague at the 1925 hearing and believed outright by buttermaker Hobart Sill and cream-hauler Cleve Oram's father, on the grounds that a single unsupervised man ran the whole process. Defeated by the station-gain ledger (more fat arrived at Larrow Green than was ever credited to patrons — the opposite of what theft implies) and by the 1911 natural experiment (see Story 6/D1).
**Quantities:** Complaint gap variously given as 0.17 (Roy Brant), 0.19 (Ivy Keddie's calculated figure, best corroborated), and 0.21 (Vose, Kepp).
**Documents:** r15 (Grigg), r16 (Kepp), r08 (Vose), r05 (Teague), r14 (Sill), r21 (Oram), r09 (Roy Brant).

## Story 4 — The Hessel Bottom Families and the 1919 Rift

**Who:** The Keddie and Brant families.
**What:** Orra Brant married Ansel Keddie in 1889. Her brother Emmet Brant farmed the largest place on Hessel Bottom (about 60 of the meadow's 160 acres, up to 38 cows) and was the station's largest patron every recorded year. Ansel therefore tested milk for his own brother-in-law for three decades. When the manager's 1919 circular accusing patrons of watering their milk went out to nine families, Ansel personally carried the letters door to door rather than mail them (per Orra's diary) — and it was specifically this act, not the accusation itself, that triggered the final break: Emmet confronted Ansel in the yard asking why he had carried a paper he didn't believe, and the two men — brothers-in-law who had shared Sunday dinners for a generation — never spoke again, reconciling only in the sense that Emmet died in 1929 without a word exchanged. The genealogy afterward: Orra's son Duncan Keddie (b. 1894, schoolteacher) and Emmet's son Roy Brant (b. earlier) are first cousins; Duncan's daughter Ivy Keddie (b. 1931) and Roy's daughter Hazel Brant are second cousins. One family dispute is unresolved in the sources themselves: Roy Brant insists he and Emmet's sister Orra were full siblings, "same mother and same father," explicitly denying a "stepmother" story — but his own daughter Hazel later describes Orra as only a half-sister to Emmet, "by his father's second wife." I favor Roy's account (closer generation, explicit rebuttal of the exact claim later made) but the sources do not fully settle it.
**Quantities:** Six farms on Hessel Bottom, nine families/eleven per Orra's diary received the 1919 circular (nine is the better-attested figure — three independent sources).
**Documents:** r09 (Roy Brant), r17 (Hazel Brant), r18 (Duncan Keddie), r24 (Orra's diary), r01 (Vira Toland, with errors).
