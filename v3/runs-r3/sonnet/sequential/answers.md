# Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

*Written entirely from memory of the 24 retellings, in the order read, with no re-reading permitted. Eight-story partition is my own construction — no list of the "real" eight was given.*

---

## Story 1 — The Founding and Article VII

**Who:** Gideon Vose and the farmers of Larrow Valley.
**What:** In 1889 the valley's farmers met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, tired of taking whatever price a merchant at Ashlin allowed them for butter. Gideon Vose was elected first president (he held the post until 1911, per Effie Loomis) and wrote the by-laws himself in his own hand. The main creamery was built at Larrow Green on Larrow Creek in 1891. The critical clause is Article VII: patrons are paid on the pounds of butterfat their milk tests at, not on the weight of the milk, and — the hinge of everything that follows — "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern," with no appeal to any other test. Sources disagree on the adoption year: Effie Loomis's official secretary's notes (the most institutionally authoritative surviving account) say 1892, "the year after the creamery opened"; Dell Rundle's jubilee pamphlet says 1891, "the year the churn was set up"; Nyle Grigg's 1919 letter says 1893. I favor 1892 (Effie), though she herself admits the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in a 1938 office move, so even her figure may rest on secondary knowledge — this date cannot be called fully certain.
**Quantities:** One creamery (1891, Larrow Green); one governing by-law (Article VII); founding year 1889; disputed adoption year 1891/1892/1893.
**Documents:** r02 (Effie Loomis), r12 (Rundle pamphlet), r15 (Grigg letter), r22 (Falke report, which explicitly declines to fix the year, citing the lost minute book).

## Story 2 — The Ostrey Hollow Station and Ansel Keddie's Twenty-Nine Seasons

**Who:** Ansel Keddie, tester and separator man.
**What:** The Association built a skimming station seven miles up the valley at Ostrey Hollow over the winter of 1895–96; it opened 4 May 1896. Keddie went up in 1895 to ready it and tested there every season from opening until his 1925 dismissal — 29 seasons. He had no outfit of his own the first year; Hobart Sill, the Larrow Green buttermaker, personally lent his own glass to open the station, and it was used until the station's own outfit arrived. Orra Keddie's diary independently confirms this happened and dates the changeover to 1 May 1897 ("done borrowing of Larrow Green"), matching the station's own book's first leaf ("Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes") — this contradicts Keddie's own unsent letter, which claims in prose the borrowed glass came from "the Ashlin creamery," a self-contradiction within his own document (r03), resolved against him by three other sources (his own book's leaf, Sill, Orra's diary). Keddie's method (described in his unsent 1925 letter) was careful and unvarying: weigh, sample into a numbered jar over ten days, warm and mix, charge the pipette to its mark, acid, spin, read the column against rulings, record in five columns, cross out and initial errors, never overwrite a figure. He tested 31 times a week. He was dismissed 30 April 1925, four days before the arbitration award, and died in 1934, still under suspicion.
**Quantities:** Opened 4 May 1896; 29 seasons (1896–1924); station's own glass in use from 1 May 1897 (per invoice, catalog, Ammon's own history, and Orra's diary — contra Vaught's bulletin and Kyle's memoir, both of which say 1898).
**Documents:** r03 (Keddie's statement/letter), r06 (Strawn's circuit book), r14 (Sill), r24 (Orra's diary), r18 (Duncan Keddie), r01 (Vira Toland, background).

## Story 3 — The Long Complaint and Three Competing Theories (1902–1926)

**Who:** The Hessel Bottom patrons versus the Association's officers and outside observers.
**What:** From 1902 the patrons of Ostrey Hollow complained their milk tested lower than the rest of the valley's, a complaint that recurred every decade. Three theories were advanced, none correct:
(1) *Wet hay* — the Association's 1908 committee, headed by Nyle Grigg, sampled Hessel Bottom herds and blamed the bog meadow's coarse, mineral-poor hay. Grigg reprinted the report in 1919 claiming (wrongly, and contradicting the very report he was reprinting) that sampling ran "the whole of 1908, every month," when the report itself states sampling was confined to January–March. Veterinarian Dr. Roland Kepp revived this theory in 1926, citing the same misquoted "two tenths" figure from Bulletin 214 (the bulletin's real figure, quoted correctly elsewhere four times, is "five hundredths… in winter, and nothing in summer").
(2) *Water added by patrons* — manager Selby Vose's theory, expressed in a 1919 circular to nine families and defended in his 1926 annual address, where he claimed tests rose to 3.90 in 1924 "when the Association began to look." State inspector Merle Strawn's own circuit notebooks record testing for added water at every visit from 1908 on and never finding any.
(3) *Cream theft by the tester* — insinuated by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague at the 1925 hearing and believed outright by buttermaker Hobart Sill and cream-hauler Cleve Oram's father, on the grounds that a single unsupervised man ran the whole process. Defeated by the station-gain ledger (more fat arrived at Larrow Green than was ever credited to patrons — the opposite of what theft implies) and by the 1911 natural experiment (see Story 6/D1).
**Quantities:** Complaint gap variously given as 0.17 (Roy Brant), 0.19 (Ivy Keddie's calculated figure, best corroborated), and 0.21 (Vose, Kepp).
**Documents:** r15 (Grigg), r16 (Kepp), r08 (Vose), r05 (Teague), r14 (Sill), r21 (Oram), r09 (Roy Brant).

## Story 4 — The Hessel Bottom Families and the 1919 Rift

**Who:** The Keddie and Brant families.
**What:** Orra Brant married Ansel Keddie in 1889. Her brother Emmet Brant farmed the largest place on Hessel Bottom (about 60 of the meadow's 160 acres, up to 38 cows) and was the station's largest patron every recorded year. Ansel therefore tested milk for his own brother-in-law for three decades. When the manager's 1919 circular accusing patrons of watering their milk went out to nine families, Ansel personally carried the letters door to door rather than mail them (per Orra's diary) — and it was specifically this act, not the accusation itself, that triggered the final break: Emmet confronted Ansel in the yard asking why he had carried a paper he didn't believe, and the two men — brothers-in-law who had shared Sunday dinners for a generation — never spoke again, reconciling only in the sense that Emmet died in 1929 without a word exchanged. The genealogy afterward: Orra's son Duncan Keddie (b. 1894, schoolteacher) and Emmet's son Roy Brant (b. earlier) are first cousins; Duncan's daughter Ivy Keddie (b. 1931) and Roy's daughter Hazel Brant are second cousins. One family dispute is unresolved in the sources themselves: Roy Brant insists he and Emmet's sister Orra were full siblings, "same mother and same father," explicitly denying a "stepmother" story — but his own daughter Hazel later describes Orra as only a half-sister to Emmet, "by his father's second wife." I favor Roy's account (closer generation, explicit rebuttal of the exact claim later made) but the sources do not fully settle it.
**Quantities:** Six farms on Hessel Bottom, nine families/eleven per Orra's diary received the 1919 circular (nine is the better-attested figure — three independent sources).
**Documents:** r09 (Roy Brant), r17 (Hazel Brant), r18 (Duncan Keddie), r24 (Orra's diary), r01 (Vira Toland, with errors).

## Story 5 — The 1925 Arbitration at Ashlin

**Who:** The board of arbitration (Alonzo Frick, chairman), the Ostrey Hollow patrons, and the Association's officers.
**What:** The patrons petitioned in January 1925. A three-man board sat under attorney Alonzo Frick, with sessions at Ashlin (17 March 1925) and two more at the station itself, in its own weigh room. Three explanations were argued — water (Vose), theft (Teague, by insinuation), and wet hay (the reprinted 1908 report) — and the board found none of them established. Ansel Keddie testified on 17 March that he had tested faithfully for 29 seasons and never taken a pound of cream; Frick believed him. On 30 April 1925, four days before the board's decision, the Association dismissed Keddie anyway — a dismissal with no finding behind it. On 4 May 1925 the board awarded the patrons $3,800, "without admission," charged to the station account. Frick himself called it "an unsatisfactory thing": an award with no finding of cause or fault. The award figure is well corroborated ($3,800: Effie Loomis's minutes, the minute book itself per the catalog, Vose's own address, Frick's recollection, Kyle's memoir) against two folk-memory accounts claiming $3,500 (Vira Toland, Cleve Oram's father) — the quoted primary record outranks memory here.
**Quantities:** Petition Jan. 1925; hearing 17 March–4 May 1925; dismissal 30 April 1925; award $3,800; patrons' own claimed loss at the time was 67,000 lb of butterfat (per Frick) — a different, cruder figure than the 76,000 lb later established scientifically in 1958 (Story 7).
**Documents:** r13 (Frick), r02 (Effie's minutes), r08 (Vose), r23 (Kyle), r01 and r21 (folk accounts, with the wrong award figure).

## Story 6 — The Ledger's Silent Evidence: The Station-Gain Line

**Who:** Bookkeeper Wilbur Teague, and later the clerk Bertram Kyle and pamphleteer Dell Rundle.
**What:** From 1905, Teague entered an annual line in the Association's ledgers: butterfat credited to patrons on the station's own test, against butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in the station's cream. The second figure was always larger — a "station gain," running steadily at about five and a quarter percent, so uniform that "as usual" entered the wording itself. Teague read this discrepancy as consistent with (not proof of) his suspicion that the tester was skimming cream and privately raised it with manager Vose in 1916, who told him to drop it; he never seems to have drawn the more obvious inference from his own numbers — that cream arriving heavy, not light, is the opposite of what theft would produce. Kyle, who carried the same ledger sheets across the office for twenty-five years, likewise "never once" wondered at it. It took Rundle, writing a 1939 jubilee pamphlet, to see the ledger entry by chance and immediately retract his own earlier chapter blaming theft, in a note added while the pamphlet was in press: "Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy… I cannot make my paragraph stand." This ledger evidence, entirely independent of the 1958 pipette discovery, had disproved the theft theory decades before anyone found the crate.
**Quantities:** Typical single-year entry: 57,760 lb credited, 60,800 lb received, 3,040 lb gain (5.25% of credited / 5% of received) — figures identical across three sources.
**Documents:** r05 (Teague), r23 (Kyle), r07 (Nym's catalog, Item 6), r12 (Rundle, including his self-correcting "note added while in press").

## Story 7 — The 1958 Discovery: The Crate, the Pipettes, and Bulletin 471

**Who:** Ivy Keddie (Ansel's granddaughter), Professor Leland Vaught.
**What:** In February 1958 Ivy Keddie, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, climbed to the loft over the old station's intake room and found the crate of pipettes condemned by the state inspector in 1923 and never examined since. Nine survived intact. On 9 March 1958 she measured them against a calibrated burette at Farland: blown to 17.6 cc, they delivered a mean of 16.72 cc — short by 0.88 cc, or 95% of the mark (one part in twenty short). Since a butterfat test reads a column of fat against a neck ruled for the *full* marked charge, a short charge produces a proportionally low reading, in every test, on every day, regardless of season or operator — explaining the flat, unchanging 26-year gap that no herd, land, or personnel theory ever could. Working with Professor Vaught, she published Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958), concluding: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there… the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty." Note: the bulletin's own Section V, using an apparently transposed figure of "16.27 cc," computes a different (and internally inconsistent) shortfall of 118,000 lb, which the bulletin itself admits "is not readily reconciled" with its own final conclusion of 76,000 lb — the conclusion, and the 16.72 cc measurement sheet, are the reliable figures. Ansel Keddie was formally cleared 24 years after his death and 33 years after his dismissal.
**Quantities:** 9 surviving pipettes measured 9 March 1958; mean delivery 16.72 cc against a 17.6 cc mark; bulletin published October 1958.
**Documents:** r04 (Ivy Keddie's talk), r11 (Vaught's bulletin), r07 (Nym's catalog), r19 (Chronicle, with several errors), r22 (Falke's report, which self-corrects two errors after checking the primary bulletin).

## Story 8 — Corroboration Over the Ridge, and the Manufacturer's Side

**Who:** Rosalie and Jerome Cudd of the Alder Corners Cheese Company; Junia Ammon of Ammon & Sons.
**What:** From 1915, four of the six Hessel Bottom farms hauled their summer-flush milk over Redlow Ridge to the Alder Corners Cheese Company in Vessey County rather than to Ostrey Hollow, for sixteen seasons (1915–1930), returning to Ostrey Hollow the rest of each year. Jerome Cudd tested this same milk on his own independent glassware and kept his own book; his figures for those four men, season after season, ran consistently between 3.78 and 3.82 — never anywhere near the Ostrey Hollow-recorded 3.61 — proving on entirely independent equipment, years before the crate was found, that the milk itself was never poor. His daughter Rosalie supplied these figures to Ivy Keddie by letter in 1958. Separately, Ammon & Sons — the Tarnet firm that sold the station its original 1897 outfit for $77.93 — later gave its own account of what likely happened: a reground mold, producing pipettes that were "consistently wrong," a defect no ordinary spot-check on the blowing floor could catch, since checking a short pipette against another short pipette shows nothing amiss. This account (Junia Ammon, 1949) undercuts the Chronicle's later, evidence-free 1958 accusation that the firm knowingly sold defective glass — though Ammon's own chapter contains a notable internal slip, claiming the firm "sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900" in the same breath as reproducing its own 1897 invoice into that very county.
**Quantities:** Four patrons, 16 seasons (1915–1930), Alder Corners average ≈3.80; invoice total $77.93 for six dozen pipettes, twelve dozen bottles, one acid measure, one hand tester, and freight.
**Documents:** r10 (Rosalie Cudd), r20 (Junia Ammon), r12 (Rundle, background), r18 (Duncan Keddie, provenance).

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**
Granddaughter. Ansel's son Duncan Keddie (b. 1894) is Ivy's father (b. 1931) — Duncan states this explicitly in his own family notes and adds that he has "already twice seen it printed otherwise." Two sources do print it otherwise, calling her "great-niece": *The Ashlin Chronicle* (1958) and county agent Orin Falke's report, in its original text. Falke's report is the one place the error is resolved within the sources themselves: in an appended note, after verifying with the family, Falke writes "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." I take this as settled — a father's direct statement plus a document's own explicit self-correction outrank a hurried newspaper's guess.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**
Second cousins. The chain: Orra Brant (Ansel Keddie's wife) and Emmet Brant were siblings; Orra's son Duncan and Emmet's son Roy are therefore first cousins; Duncan's daughter Ivy and Roy's daughter Hazel, as children of first cousins, are second cousins. Both Hazel herself ("Second cousins… it is not complicated") and Duncan Keddie independently give this exact relationship, explicitly ruling out "cousins once removed." Two outside narrators (Vira Toland, Cleve Oram) call them "first cousin once removed" instead — both wrong by the same margin, both outsiders repeating valley talk rather than family members. What the sources leave open: whether Orra and Emmet were full or half-siblings (Roy says full; his own daughter Hazel says half, "by his father's second wife"). This does not change the cousin arithmetic under ordinary usage, but the sources never resolve which sibling relationship is correct.

**B3. Selby Vose's father.**
Gideon Vose — the Association's founder, first president, and author of the by-laws, including Article VII. This is confirmed independently by Wilbur Teague ("son of the late president"), Bertram Kyle (explicit: "the son, whatever is said at Ashlin… nonsense"), and Duncan Keddie's family notes (explicit correction of the record). Two sources instead call Selby Gideon's "nephew" (Vira Toland; implied by Rundle's pamphlet). Why it matters: Selby, as the son of the man who wrote the very rule — "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern," with no appeal — that made the whole Ostrey Hollow dispute possible, had a personal and institutional stake in defending both his father's rule and the Association's conduct under it. That is plausibly why his 1926 address blames the patrons (water) rather than ever questioning the station's own equipment, and why he told his own bookkeeper in 1916 to drop the question.

**B4. Relationship between the Vose and Keddie families.**
None, on the weight of the evidence — though this cannot be proven with certainty, only shown to be unsupported. Vira Toland reports as settled fact that "Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins. Everybody knew that." Cleve Oram independently repeats a similar claim ("cousins of some sort… my mother had it from her mother"). But Duncan Keddie — Orra's own son — states he investigated this directly: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses… My mother would have told me… and she never once put a Vose in it." He attributes the rumor to confusion with a different, unrelated Vose family at Ashlin. I favor Duncan's account as the closest and most directly informed source, while noting that a family denial, however credible, is not the same as documentary proof of a negative.

## Section C — Math

**C1.**
(a) The pipettes were blown to deliver 17.6 cc but actually delivered a mean of 16.72 cc (the figure on the signed measurement sheet and in Ivy Keddie's own talk — I reject Bulletin 471's internal Section-V figure of 16.27 cc as a transposition error, because the bulletin's *own* final conclusion paragraph, quoted identically elsewhere, gives "one part in twenty" and "76,000 lb," which only follows from 16.72, not 16.27). Ratio: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly. So every test read 95% of the truth — short by one part in twenty (5%).
(b) Recorded average: 3.61. True average = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. Shortfall = 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 of a point**.
(c) Over the 25 full delivery years (1898–1922), the station received 40,000,000 lb of milk (Effie Loomis, Ivy Keddie, and the catalog's curatorial note all agree on this figure; I reject Teague's and Rundle's "44,000,000," which is corroborated by only two sources that may share one origin and may cover a different span). Credited fat: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb. True fat: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb. Difference = **76,000 lb of butterfat not credited**.
(d) At the period average price of $0.25/lb: 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000**. The patrons actually received the 1925 arbitration award of $3,800. Fraction received: 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **1/5 (one-fifth)**.

**C2.** Largest patron (Emmet Brant), 1910 leaf: 200,000 lb delivered, paid on a test of 3.42.
(a) Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of butterfat.
(b) True test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60. True fat: 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb. Owed and not credited: 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb**.
(c) At 1910's specific price of $0.24/lb (given by Roy Brant for that exact year): 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40**. (Using the multi-year average of $0.25 instead gives $90 — I use the year-specific figure since the question asks about 1910 specifically.)

**C3.** Using the same 25-year, 40,000,000 lb window as C1:
(a) Credited to patrons: **1,444,000 lb**.
(b) Actually received at Larrow Green in the station's cream: **1,520,000 lb**.
(c) The difference, 76,000 lb, is **5% of the fat actually delivered** (76,000 ÷ 1,520,000) but **5.25% of the fat credited** (76,000 ÷ 1,444,000). Both are correct; they differ only in denominator. The bookkeeper's ledger entries use the 5.25% figure (a bookkeeper naturally reckons against the number already entered in his own books); the 5% figure is the one that matches the glassware's actual shortfall ratio and is therefore the physically meaningful one.

**C4.**
(a) $77.93 — the invoice total (quoted identically in the catalog and in Ammon & Sons' own firm history; I reject "$77.39," which appears in two places — the Chronicle, and, oddly, in Ammon's own prose paraphrase of her own quoted invoice — as a transposition of the digits).
(b) **Six dozen (72) pipettes** (per the invoice, "6 doz. milk test pipettes" — I reject Vaught's bulletin and Cleve Oram's "five dozen"). Each was blown to 17.6 cc and delivered a mean of 16.72 cc, short by **0.88 cc**.

**C5.**
(a) The faulty glassware was in service for about 26 years: from 1 May 1897 (per the invoice, catalog provenance, Ammon's own history, and — most tellingly — Orra Keddie's diary entry dated exactly that day) to 12 June 1923 (per Merle Strawn's own contemporaneous inspection notebook — the primary eyewitness source; I reject "12 July 1923," given by Vose and Rundle, as an error, since it appears only in two secondary, non-eyewitness accounts). The money calculation, however, runs over only 25 *full* delivery years (1898–1922). These numbers differ because the full glass-usage period includes two partial years at its edges — the stub of 1897 (glass installed partway through the year) and the stub of 1923 (glass condemned partway through the year, in June) — which cannot be cleanly costed against whole-year ledger totals, so the financial reckoning is confined to the years for which complete annual figures exist.
(b) Cannot be determined precisely. The catalog explicitly notes the station's first-season (4 May–31 December 1896) intake "is not in the ledgers and is nowhere else." Two personal, non-ledger estimates conflict: Hobart Sill recalls "about nine hundred thousand pounds"; Bertram Kyle recalls "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds." Neither is a record; they bracket an unknown true figure by about 20%.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The station's own measuring glass broke on 8 May 1911; a borrowed measure was used until 10 July 1911 — a span of **nine weeks** (well corroborated by Keddie's own dated book entries, Ivy Keddie's talk, and Vaught's bulletin; Dr. Kepp's claim of "six weeks" is an isolated error). The only variable that changed was the measuring instrument itself (sources disagree on where the substitute came from — Ivy Keddie says Larrow Green, Roy Brant says the state inspector's own measure — and this specific point cannot be settled from the sources). Held constant: the same patrons, herds, pasture, season's natural progress, acid, bottles, and tester. Result: the recorded average rose from 3.61 to 3.80 for the full nine weeks and fell back to 3.61 the day the station's own measure returned. This is significant because it is a real, contemporaneous, single-variable experiment — it rules out any theory resting on the herds, the land, the season, or the tester's honesty, since none of those changed, yet the number moved with the glass alone. It corroborates the 1958 finding independently of arithmetic on old pipettes. What it does *not* establish: the specific mechanism (that a short pipette charge produces a proportionally low reading) — that required the 1958 burette calibration; nor does it, strictly on its own, prove the tester never took cream at any other time, only that changing the glass alone explains the nine-week rise.

**D2.** Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream:
1. The ledger's own "station gain" line (Teague's bookkeeping, corroborated by the catalog and by Rundle's own retraction) shows *more* butterfat arriving at Larrow Green every year than was ever credited to patrons — the opposite of what theft would produce.
2. The 1911 natural experiment: with the same tester and no change in supervision, the recorded test rose when only the measuring glass changed — inconsistent with a constant rate of skimming.
3. Merle Strawn's independent, disinterested state inspections found the scales true and no added water at every visit — and, per Duncan Keddie, Strawn "was kin to nobody in the valley whatever," giving his testimony special independence.
These are independent because they come from entirely separate record-keeping systems and people — the creamery's bookkeeper, the station's own tester's book, and an outside state official — none derived from or dependent on the others. Reason 1 (the ledger) and reason 2 (the 1911 interval) both predate and do not depend on the 1958 glassware measurement, so either would still hold even if the crate had never been found. Did the bookkeeper ever question the ledger entries that refute his own accusation? No — Teague raised the general discrepancy with manager Vose once, in 1916, and was told to drop it; there is no indication in his own 1948 memoir, written 23 years after he insinuated theft at the hearing, that he ever connected his own "station gain" figures to disprove his own suspicion. Ironically, it was Dell Rundle, a schoolteacher with no stake in the office, who saw the same ledger line and immediately retracted his own theft-theory chapter.

**D3.** Yes, on the sources' evidence, Vose had actual notice well before 1923. Teague's own memoir states he personally raised the discrepancy with Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone — seven years before the glass was condemned. Kyle's memoir adds that Vose signed the annual station sheet every year, though Kyle also notes Vose "read the totals and never read the lines above them," which complicates whether he genuinely absorbed the anomaly's significance rather than merely having had the opportunity to. Vose's own 1926 address never admits a station-level error was ever suspected; he frames the entire history as deliberate watering that "stopped when the Association began to look." My reading: he was informed and had continuous access to the figures, and chose (per his own bookkeeper's account) not to pursue the question — but the sources do not establish that he understood the specific cause before 1958, only that he knew something in the numbers was persistently anomalous and declined to investigate it.

## Section E — Contradictions

*(Selected list; not exhaustive given the volume of material, but covering the major and best-evidenced cases.)*

**Resolvable, with quoted-document or majority/arithmetic resolution:**
- Selby Vose "nephew" (Vira, implied by Rundle) vs. "son" of Gideon Vose (Duncan Keddie, Kyle, Teague). → Son; three independent, more authoritative sources, one an explicit correction.
- Ivy Keddie "great-niece" (Chronicle, Falke's original text) vs. "granddaughter" (Duncan Keddie, Falke's own appended correction). → Granddaughter; resolved within the sources themselves.
- Ivy/Hazel "first cousin once removed" (Vira, Oram) vs. "second cousins" (Hazel herself, Duncan Keddie). → Second cousins; family members outrank outsiders, and it matches the stated generational chain.
- Arbitration award "$3,500" (Vira, Oram) vs. "$3,800" (Effie's minutes, catalog, Vose, Frick, Kyle). → $3,800; a quoted primary record outranks memory decades later.
- Glass condemnation "12 July 1923" (Vose, Rundle) vs. "12 June 1923" (Strawn's own notebook, catalog, Vaught's bulletin, Ivy Keddie). → June 12; the inspector's own contemporaneous record is primary.
- Pipette mean "16.27 cc" (Vaught's Section V, Chronicle) vs. "16.72 cc" (signed measurement sheet, Ivy Keddie's talk, and the bulletin's own conclusion arithmetic). → 16.72; internal consistency of the bulletin's own conclusion plus the primary signed sheet.
- Pipettes purchased "five dozen" (Vaught, Oram) vs. "six dozen" (the invoice, quoted twice verbatim). → Six dozen; primary document.
- Pipettes recovered "eleven" (Rundle's footnote, Chronicle) vs. "nine" (Ivy Keddie's talk, catalog Items 5 & 14, Vaught, Falke). → Nine; four sources including the primary measurement sheet.
- Recorded average "3.16" (Teague, Frick) vs. "3.61" (Keddie's own book, catalog, Ivy Keddie, Falke). → 3.61; the tester's own primary book, and Teague/Frick are not fully independent of each other (Frick heard Teague testify).
- Total milk "44,000,000 lb" (Teague, Rundle) vs. "40,000,000 lb" (Effie, Ivy Keddie, catalog). → 40,000,000 for the specific 25-year window; the two dissenting sources may be using a different span.
- 1924 average "3.90" (Vose) / "3.85" (Falke) vs. "3.80" (Keddie's own book, Strawn's inspection log). → 3.80; two independent contemporaneous 1924 sources agree exactly, one of them disinterested (Strawn).
- Bulletin 214's hay finding misquoted as "two tenths" (Grigg, Kepp) vs. the verbatim "five hundredths… winter, nothing… summer" (quoted identically five times, including by Falke's own self-correcting note). → Five hundredths; verbatim primary text, repeatedly confirmed.
- Invoice total narrated as "$77.39" (Ammon's own prose, Chronicle) vs. the quoted invoice line itself, "$77.93" (catalog, and Ammon's own quotation of it). → $77.93.
- 1911 anomaly "six weeks" (Kepp) vs. "nine weeks" (Keddie's dated entries, Ivy Keddie, Vaught, Falke). → Nine weeks; exact dates in the primary source.
- Station's own glass into use "1898" (Vaught's bulletin, Kyle) vs. "1897" (invoice, catalog, Ammon's history, Chronicle, and Orra Keddie's diary entry dated 1 May 1897). → 1897; a same-day diary entry is about as strong a primary source as this corpus contains.
- Selby Vose became manager "1911" (Kyle) vs. "1913" (Teague, and Vose's own stated tenure length). → 1913; corroborated by the man's own first-person statement.
- Families receiving the 1919 circular: "nine" (Vira, Roy Brant, Hazel Brant) vs. "eleven" (Orra Keddie's diary). → Nine is better attested by three independent sources, though Orra's diary is a strong primary source and the discrepancy may reflect households vs. families rather than a plain error.

## Section F — Theory

**F1.** The Ostrey Hollow station's tests ran low for 26 years because its milk-test pipettes — a stock lot bought new in 1897 from Ammon & Sons — were manufactured with a bore that delivered less milk than the mark on their stem promised (16.72 cc actual against a 17.6 cc mark, a shortfall of one part in twenty), almost certainly the accidental result of a reground mold rather than any deliberate act. Because the test bottle's graduated neck is ruled to read percent for the *full* marked charge only, a short charge produces a reading low by exactly the same proportion, every time, regardless of season, herd, or the man at the crank — which explains why the gap was flat across every month of every year (a seasonal or land-based cause could never do that) and why it fell hardest, in absolute terms, on the richest milk. It began in spring 1897, when this new but flawed glass replaced the accurate glass borrowed from Larrow Green, and it ended in June 1923, when a state inspector — using a calibrated burette, a tool only issued to field inspectors from 1921 — finally checked the pipettes against an outside standard and condemned them; nothing at the station or on the farms needed to change, because nothing there was ever wrong.

Three competing explanations, and what defeats each:
1. **Water added by patrons** (Selby Vose). Defeated by the state inspector's own lactometer tests at every visit from 1908 onward, which never found added water.
2. **Cream stolen by the tester** (Wilbur Teague, insinuated; believed by Hobart Sill and Cleve Oram's father). Defeated by the ledger's own "station gain" figures, showing surplus fat arriving at the creamery every year (the opposite of theft), and by the 1911 natural experiment, in which the same tester's figures moved only when the glass changed.
3. **Wet hay thinning the milk** (the 1908 Grigg committee; Dr. Kepp, 1926). Defeated by Bulletin 214's actual feeding-trial result — a depression of only five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer, a small fraction of the constant, year-round 0.19-point gap — and by Alder Corners' fully independent tests of the same land's milk, which consistently ran near 3.80, not depressed.

Subsidiary questions: The 1925 board of arbitration never examined the condemned glassware — the crate, already set aside in the loft since 1923, was never mentioned in any of the three sessions; the award's phrase "books and appliances of the station" was, per the curator's own note, "a form of words… and not a list of anything." What the tester himself believed: the sources give two different private admissions. To his son Duncan, he said as early as 1911 that he thought it was "something in the glass," though he would not commit an unproven suspicion to his own book and wrote only a question mark. To Alonzo Frick, in the hearing corridor in 1925, he privately said he believed the patrons themselves were at fault. I cannot fully reconcile these two reported private beliefs from the sources as given.

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

For 26 years, a small creamery skimming station in Ostrey Hollow paid its farmers on milk tests that ran mysteriously low, while the rest of the valley's milk tested normally. Three explanations circulated — dirty water in the cans, a thieving tester, or poor wet-meadow hay — and each was believed by someone with a stake in believing it. A 1925 arbitration heard all three, proved none, and dismissed the tester anyway, days before awarding the patrons a fifth of what they were actually owed. The truth sat the whole time in a crate of condemned glass pipettes, forgotten in the station's own loft: bought new in 1897, they delivered five percent less milk than their mark promised, so every test read proportionally low — not because of cows, land, or dishonesty, but a manufacturing flaw nobody thought to check. It took a granddaughter with a burette, in 1958, to find it, because the flaw was invisible from every single one of the eight vantage points that had looked at everything except the glass itself.
