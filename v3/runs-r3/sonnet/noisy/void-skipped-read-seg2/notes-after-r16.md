# Retention notes — retellings r01 through r16 (Ostrey Hollow / Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association dossier)

All sixteen retellings concern the same underlying historical matter: a decades-long discrepancy in butterfat testing at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station (Hessel Bottom, up the Larrow Valley), operated by the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (creamery at Larrow Green, office at Ashlin). Sources span oral history, institutional memoir, personal testimony, bookkeeping memoir, curatorial catalog, inspector's field notes, manager's public address, a farmer's ledger memoir, a cheesemaker's letter, a scientific bulletin, a jubilee pamphlet, a lawyer's recollections, a buttermaker's retirement remarks, a committee report with covering letter, and a veterinarian's paper with floor discussion — spanning 1854 (Emmet Brant's birth) to 2019 (curatorial/compilation dates aside) in narrated content, with the core events 1889–1972. The retellings do NOT agree with each other on many material points, including within single documents; conflicts are flagged explicitly below.

---

## R01 — "What the Hollow Said": Vira Toland (b. 1912), Hessel Bottom patron's daughter, oral history taped at Ostrey Hollow, 1979.

- Narrator born 1912 at top of Ostrey Hollow, in house built by her father's father; lived there her whole life except 2 years at Ashlin after first marriage.
- Her father hauled milk to the Ostrey Hollow station; nine families hauled to that station.
- Station: at the top of the Hollow where the road bends toward the ridge; low, whitewashed building, cold, smelled of milk/steam/hot iron.
- **Ansel Keddie** ran the station. Went up in 1895 (year before it opened) to prepare it; tested the first summer; still there when Vira was a girl.
- Kept a meticulous test book: ruled with his own straightedge, uniform line heights, figures "like print," written standing at a slope desk by the window. Never blotted. Vira's father said "if the rest of the world kept its accounts the way Ansel Keddie kept his there would be no lawyers in Ashlin."
- **1919** (Vira was seven): a printed letter from the Association, over the manager's signature, went to nine families saying "there was water in the cans coming down from Ostrey Hollow." Her father read it at the table with his hat on (unusual for him), read it twice, put it in the desk drawer — still there when he died; brother has it now. One woman kept hers pinned inside her trunk lid.
- The manager who signed the letter: **Selby Vose**, of Larrow Green — described by Vira as "old Gideon Vose's nephew." [CONFLICTS with r02 and r08, which both establish Selby Vose was Gideon Vose's SON, not nephew; r12 also calls him NEPHEW — see consolidated conflict list.]
- Gideon Vose started the Association originally; family "always thought the valley belonged to them a little."
- Local knowledge (unwritten): **Orra Keddie** (Ansel's wife) and "old Mrs. Vose" (presumably Gideon's wife) were first cousins — so the accusing letter was seen as "somebody's cousin" attacking the family.
- **Orra Keddie** was **Emmet Brant's sister**. Emmet Brant was the biggest patron on Hessel Bottom (most cows, most hay ground, most milk hauled) and was hurt worst by the low tests/letter.
- Before 1919 the Keddie and Brant families ate Sunday dinners together; after the letter, total estrangement — never spoke again, not at store or funeral; Orra would walk the long way to avoid passing her brother's lane. Both now dead, per Vira.
- After the letter, "all at once, one summer," test figures went up and stayed up — no one ever explained why, at the time, per Vira. Her father: milk tested better, but nothing had been done differently.
- A hearing was held "down at Ashlin," "that was the winter and spring after" [Vira implies the hearing followed shortly after the 1919 letter — i.e., winter 1919/spring 1920]. [CONFLICTS with r02/r03/r07/r08/r13, all of which place the actual petition/hearing in 1925 — a roughly five-to-six-year discrepancy.]
- Outcome per Vira: Ansel Keddie put out (fired), an old man, after all those years; patrons paid **$3,500** (thirty-five hundred dollars), split nine ways — "did not buy a hay rake" for her father's share. [CONFLICTS with r02, r03(implicit), r05, r07, r08, r13, which all give the official arbitration award as **$3,800**.]
- Vira's own explicit belief/accusation: "He was taking the cream. They caught him at it and they put him out, and that is why the numbers went right the summer after... it is not that he is a wicked man, it is that nobody ever came up that road and looked." She states this as fact, not rumor. [This directly conflicts with r04's eventual scientific finding of no theft, and with r03's own denial by Keddie, and with the "no cause established" arbitration outcome in r02/r07/r08/r13.]
- Her father, more charitably, said "Ansel Keddie was an honest man who had got into something" — Vira says she "never saw the difference" between that and an accusation.
- Keddie died in the 1930s "still under it" (under suspicion). [Note: r04 gives his death year precisely as 1934.] Orra outlived him a good while, carried the same stigma.
- 1958: a young woman researcher came from a college "away east," poked about the shut station building (used for fence wire and salt storage by then), asked questions, wrote everything down. She was "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed." [This is presumably Ivy Keddie of r04; r09 and r12's footnote both instead describe Ivy and Hazel as SECOND COUSINS, not first-cousins-once-removed — a further relationship-description conflict.] Vira told her about the letter in the drawer.
- Vira ties her memory of the letter to a physical/sensory detail: it rained that day; she remembers the wet on her father's shoulders coming in.

## R02 — "Notes toward a history of the Association": Effie Loomis, Association secretary 1901–1940, written 1941 for the Association's own files.

- Effie born ~1875 (was 14 in 1889); secretary from 1901–1940 (kept the minute book in her own hand throughout that period); wrote this history in 1941, the year after laying down the minute book.
- **1889**: Larrow Valley farmers met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. **Gideon Vose** elected first president, held office until 1911 (22 years, longest of anyone).
- The 1889–1892 minute book was LOST when the office moved in 1938 — carried out with everything else, never arrived, searched everywhere, not found. Effie states this limits what she can write about the Association's first 12 years (before she became secretary in 1901).
- **1891**: main creamery built at Larrow Green on Larrow Creek (same building still standing as of writing). Before it, no common test/price — "every man's arithmetic was his own."
- **Article VII** of the by-laws, drafted personally by Gideon Vose (not a lawyer), adopted at the **1892** annual meeting (the year after the creamery opened). [CONFLICTS with r15, Nyle Grigg's 1919 letter, which says "payment by test came in with the 1893 meeting."] Quoted in full:
  > "Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."
  - Effie stresses the last clause — "The test of the station where he delivers shall govern" — as the crucial, most-overlooked part: no appeal from the station's own test. She calls this "the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business." Gideon Vose, who wrote it, died in **1917** and could not be asked about it later.
- Officers in Effie's time: herself as secretary (1901–1940); **Wilbur Teague**, bookkeeper from 1905 (kept ledgers); **Hobart Sill**, buttermaker at Larrow Green (kept churn book and cream receiving book); **Selby Vose**, son of the late president [Gideon Vose], made manager in **1913**, served until **1938**. Effie notes none of the four of them ever personally went to the Ostrey Hollow station on a working day to watch the work — including herself.
- Ostrey Hollow skimming station: 7 miles up the valley from Larrow Green, at the head of the Hollow. Took in milk from farms up there, separated it, sent cream down to Larrow Green, returned skim to patrons. Best milk came from Hessel Bottom (wet meadow below the hamlet); **seven** farms had rights on Hessel Bottom [CONFLICTS with r09, r15, r16, all of which say SIX farms]; largest was **Emmet Brant's** — station's largest patron every year on record.
- **January 1925**: Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned the Association, claiming their milk had never tested as well as neighbors' and they'd been underpaid a long time; asked to be made whole.
- **Board of arbitration** sat at **Ashlin**, under **Alonzo Frick**, attorney, from **17 March to 4 May 1925**. [Note: r13, Frick's own account, clarifies only the FIRST session (17 March) was at Ashlin; the second and third sessions were held at Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's weigh room — a refinement/tension with Effie's blanket "sat at Ashlin" framing.] Three explanations were argued before the board; Effie declines to detail them ("they are in the minutes and the minutes are in the safe"); none of the three was accepted. [r13 supplies the missing attribution: water (Vose), theft (Teague), hay (1908 report).]
- The board's award, as Effie personally recorded it:
  > "The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account."
  - Effie notes the apparent contradiction of "finds no cause established" immediately followed by an award of $3,800 — she says it "was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once." [r13's Frick makes essentially the same observation independently: "An award without a finding is an unsatisfactory thing."]
- **Quantities from bookkeeper Wilbur Teague's ledgers** (Effie vouches only for the copying, not "the cows"): over the 25 years the patrons' claim covered, the Ostrey Hollow station received **40,000,000 lb** of milk total — average **1,600,000 lb/year**. Butterfat price averaged **25 cents/lb** over the period. [CONFLICTS with r05/r12's 44,000,000 lb and with Sill's/Kepp's 27 cents and Brant's 24-25 cents — see consolidated numeric conflicts.]
- Two gaps Effie flags explicitly in the record: (1) the lost 1889–1892 minute book (see above); (2) **"the crate"** — a crate of measures condemned by the state inspector in **June 1923**, replaced with new ones — was NEVER mentioned at all in any of the three arbitration hearing sessions, by patrons, officers, or the board — despite the board's language about "the books and appliances of the station." Effie: "the interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying."

## R03 — Ansel Keddie's own statement and unsent letter, spring 1925.

Two documents: (I) his 9-sentence oral statement to the board of arbitration at Ashlin, 17 March 1925; (II) an unsent letter written later that spring, found among his papers after his death.

**I. Statement (17 March 1925), quoted in full:**
> "I have kept the test book of this station in my own hand since the station opened, and there is not a figure in it that I did not put there. I have set down the date, the patron, the pounds, the test and the fat, and where I have made an error I have drawn a line through it and put my letters against the line, and I have never written a figure over a figure. I have made the tests in the same order and at the same hour of every working day of every season. I have heard what the patrons say of me and I have heard what the officers say of me. Neither of them has come up that road on a working morning and stood at my elbow and watched the work done. I am asked to account for figures, and the figures are what I read and what I wrote and nothing besides. I am an old man and I have no other trade. I have tested as I was taught and as the state man saw me do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, and I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are. I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself."

[NOTE — new conflict from r13: Alonzo Frick, the arbitration chairman, states that privately, in the corridor at Ashlin, Keddie told him "he had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the room" — i.e., contrary to his public "I cannot tell you why" stance quoted above, Keddie DID privately hold a theory (blaming the patrons) that he never disclosed publicly or in his own writings. Frick explicitly says it is false that Keddie "suspected nobody," even though it's true he "accused nobody."]

**II. Unsent letter, spring 1925 (found among his papers):**
- Explains his method in detail: day began 5:30 a.m.; wagons came up the Hollow in the dark most of the year; every can weighed and recorded before milk went anywhere. Scale was tested true by the state inspector; in 29 seasons no patron ever disputed a weight and was proven right. [Corroborated independently by r09: Roy Brant's father's own weigh book matched the station's weight figures exactly for decades — "the scales never fell out at all."]
- Sample taken from each patron's milk at weighing, into the patron's own numbered jar; jar made up over 10 days before testing. On test morning, jar warmed to blood-warm in warm water, poured repeatedly to mix cream through. "A sample that is not warmed and mixed is worth nothing and a boy could tell you so."
- Test procedure ("the charge"): milk drawn to a mark on the measure stem, tip wiped, blown into the patron's numbered bottle; acid poured down the side, bottle turned dark; four bottles in the hand tester, weighted against each other, cranked at the taught speed, no faster; fat read off the neck's column in per cent against rulings with a pair of dividers; figure written down. [r11's Bulletin 471 gives the same four-operation method in scientific language: draw charge into pipette, deliver into bottle, add sulfuric acid, spin in hand tester, read fat directly off graduated neck as a percentage — standard charge 17.6 c.c.]
- Glass (testing outfit) borrowed from **Larrow Green** starting 1896 (per the book's own first leaf, quoted below), used until the station's own outfit arrived "the spring following" (i.e., 1897). [NOTE: the r01–r08 notes' own prose summary of this had paraphrased this as "borrowed from Ashlin creamery" — that appears to be a paraphrase error in the earlier notes, since the book's own first leaf (quoted directly, both here and in r07 Item 3) says "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green." r14 (Hobart Sill) independently confirms and personalizes this: Sill says HE, the Larrow Green buttermaker, personally supplied his own glass from his own bench for the station's opening in May 1896, because the ordered outfit hadn't arrived; his glass was returned "the spring after" [1897], "one piece of it... chipped."]
- Kept the book in five columns: date, patron, pounds, test, fat. Corrections crossed and initialed, never overwritten. Tested **29 seasons, 1896 through 1924**. Did **not** test in the 1925 season.
- First leaf of the book, quoted:
  > "Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie."
- Summary/averages leaf made up in **1922**, quoted:
  > "Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80."
- The **1911 anomaly** — his account: station's own measure (pipette) broke **8 May 1911**; made shift with a borrowed measure; from that morning tests came up and stood at 3.80; on **10 July 1911** the station's own measure was back in use and tests dropped back down "the same day." [CONFIRMED independently and in identical duration/dates by r09 — Roy Brant's father's leaf recorded "nine weeks" too, and by "the whole run" of farms on the Bottom rising and falling together; and by r11, Vaught's bulletin, which also says "nine weeks."] Two entries quoted exactly:
  > "May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure."
  > "July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."
  - He says the question mark is his own, standing unanswered for 14 years (i.e., 1911 to 1925); he refuses to speculate.
- He stresses the gap was constant across seasons — present in July as in February, flush grass or dry hay, wet year or dry — never varying with weather/feed. [Corroborated independently by r09: Roy Brant makes the identical observation in nearly identical words — "it was seventeen hundredths in July and it was seventeen hundredths in February... it did not care whether the cows were on the meadow or on hay in the barn."]
- **1924** average of the station: **3.80** — he wrote it "with the same pen and same measure of care as all the others." [CONFIRMED by r06, Strawn's inspection note for 1924: "Their book gives the year at 3.80." CONFLICTS with r08, Vose's public claim of **3.90** for 1924.]
- History of complaints, per Keddie: complaints "began in 1902," when Larrow Green first said Ostrey Hollow milk didn't test with the rest of the valley. **1908**: a committee came up, sampled the herds through the winter, reported the wet hay of the Bottom thinned the cream, remedy lies with the land (NOT an accusation of fraud). **1919**: the Association's circular went to nine families saying there was water in their cans. He says he holds no charge against anyone who signed that letter but knows what came into the station each morning; notes "nine kitchens in this Hollow have had that letter in a drawer ever since" [corroborates r01's drawer detail].
- Confirms his wife **Orra is Emmet Brant's sister**; Emmet was his largest patron; Ansel weighed and tested every pound of Emmet's milk himself; the two men have not spoken since the circular [corroborates r01]. [r09 adds nuance: per Roy Brant, the estrangement was not over suspicion of water/theft per se, but because Emmet held that Keddie, as keeper of the test book, "ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not."]
- He was **put out (dismissed) on 30 April 1925** — notes the board "found no cause established" and dismissed him anyway on the last day of the month, and says he cannot reconcile those two facts in his mind. [CONFIRMED by r13: Frick says the dismissal came "four days before we made our award" (4 May 1925), and states plainly there was never any finding against Keddie by the board.] Ends: "I have tested as I was taught. I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are."

## R04 — "What the Crate Held": Ivy Keddie's talk to the Ordell County Historical Society, Ashlin, 1959 (investigation conducted 1958).

- **Ivy Keddie** is Ansel Keddie's granddaughter — daughter of **Duncan Keddie** (Ansel's son), who taught school at Ashlin. [Confirmed by r09: Duncan Keddie, Ansel and Orra's son, is Roy Brant's first cousin; Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are second cousins.] She explicitly disclaims that her findings depend on family loyalty: "nothing I am going to show you tonight depends in the smallest degree on my opinion of my grandfather. It depends on nine pieces of glass and a burette."
- Five record sets consulted, none of which had ever been in one room together before "last year" [1958]:
  1. The **station test book** — in the Association's office since 1925; came to the Historical Society "this year."
  2. The **Brant weigh book** — kept at Hessel Bottom from 1897 onward; lent to her by **Roy Brant**, Emmet Brant's son, who has kept it up himself since his father died. [Confirmed directly by r09, Roy Brant's own account, and by his 1958 postscript describing lending the book to Ivy.]
  3. **Alder Corners Cheese Company** test figures (over Redlow Ridge, Vessey County) — copied out for her by **Rosalie Cudd**, who runs that factory and is described here as **Jerome Cudd's niece**; the original book has never left Vessey County; she has never seen it herself. [CONFLICTS with r10 — Rosalie Cudd's own letter states plainly "My father, Jerome Cudd, built this factory" — she is his DAUGHTER, not his niece. r07 Item 13's provenance note ("passed from its founder to his daughter in 1941") and r12's pamphlet ("Jerome Cudd's daughter, Rosalie Cudd") both independently corroborate "daughter," making Ivy's "niece" description in r04 the outlier/likely error.]
  4. **Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks** (state dairy inspector for the county, from 1908) — held at her own college library at Farland since 1947; "eleven years in which nobody put a question to them" [1947+11=1958]. [CONFLICTS with r06's own covering note, in which Strawn says his circuit began in 1907, not 1908.]
  5. The Association's own annual ledgers — present at the talk.
- **February [1958]**: she went up to the station building (used for storage since closing), found — in the loft over the old intake room, under a tarpaulin — a wooden crate stenciled "**AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET**." Carried up there in **June 1923**. Contained, packed in straw, the station's condemned milk-test pipettes. [CONFLICTS with r07's Item 5 provenance, which says the crate was found "in the Association office at Ashlin," not the station building's loft.]
- Original outfit: **6 dozen (72)** pipettes bought new in **1897**, invoice totaling **$77.93** for the whole outfit (pipettes, bottles, acid measure, hand tester, freight). [CONFLICTS with r11, Bulletin 471 Section III, which states **FIVE DOZEN (60)** pipettes were purchased.] Of these, **nine** survived unbroken in the crate. [CONFLICTS with r12's 1959 footnote, which says ELEVEN pipettes were found.]
- **9 March [1958]**: she measured the nine pipettes at Farland against a calibrated burette. Her sheet, quoted:
  > "Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for."
  [CONFLICTS with r11, Bulletin 471 Section III, which gives the SAME nine-pipette measurement exercise but a different mean: "the mean delivery of the nine is 16.27 c.c." — likely a digit transposition (16.72 vs 16.27) between the two tellings of the identical experiment.]
- The math: pipettes delivered only 95% of their blown/rated volume (16.72 vs 17.6 c.c.), so every test made with that glass read 95% of the true value.
- Re-quotes her grandfather's 1922 summary leaf (identical to r03's quote: 1896=3.80; 1897 part year; 1898=3.61; 3.61 ± 0.03 every year since except nine weeks of 1911 at 3.80).
- Corrected math: recorded average 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **true average 3.80**. Gap of **0.19** points is **5% of the true value (3.80)**, and **5.25% of the recorded/credited value (3.61-based bookkeeping)** — she stresses both percentages are individually correct but not interchangeable, since "5%" and "5.25%" describe different denominators (true fat delivered vs. fat credited). [CONFLICTS with r11's parallel calculation, which — using its own different mean of 16.27 c.c. — computes ratio 0.9244, true average 3.905, and shortfall of 0.295, explicitly noting this "is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the glassware."]
- Over **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922**: station took in **40,000,000 lb** of milk, averaging 1,600,000 lb/year [matches r02]. At recorded 3.61%, patrons were credited **1,444,000 lb** of fat; at true 3.80%, they had actually delivered **1,520,000 lb**. Difference: **76,000 lb of butterfat**. At $0.25/lb average price, unpaid value = **$19,000**. The 1925 arbitration award of **$3,800** was one-fifth of that true value. [CONFLICTS with r11 Section V's own calculation of 118,000 lb (though r11's own Section VII conclusion later quotes 76,000 lb, contradicting its own Section V); also conflicts with r13's separate figure that "the patrons' own reckoning of what they had lost was sixty-seven thousand pounds of butter fat," and with r12's claim that the patrons' 1925 petition itself set the loss "to the dollar" at $19,600.]
- Because the error was proportional, it hit richer milk hardest: a herd recorded at 4.56 was short 0.24 points; a herd recorded at 3.42 was short 0.18 points. [Note: 4.56 matches Selby Vose's own herd test in r08; 3.42 matches the Brant weigh-book leaf for 1910 in r07's Item 4 AND in r09's own quote of his father's 1910 leaf.]
- **1911 anomaly re-explained**: same patrons, herds, pasture, season, acid, bottles, same tester — only the measure changed (borrowed from Larrow Green with the cream wagon) — and the average went to 3.80, then back to 3.61 the day the station's own (defective) measure returned. She says this is the only surviving natural experiment/proof and "no explanation... survives those nine weeks except the one I have given you." [NOTE: r16 reveals this exact argument — under a rounded "six weeks" framing — was raised as an objection to the wet-hay theory as early as 1926, thirty-two years before Ivy's talk, by an unnamed "gentleman" at Dr. Kepp's Farm Club paper; Kepp dismissed it rhetorically ("I could produce you six weeks in any year that would prove anything you liked") without addressing its logic.]
- Addresses the "wet hay" theory (the kindest of the old explanations, "offered in good faith"): the State Agricultural College tested it in 1911, **Bulletin No. 214**, quoted:
  > "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."
  - She notes this effect (max 0.05, winter only) is four times too small and the wrong seasonal shape to explain a flat, year-round 0.19 gap. [CONFLICTS with r15 (Nyle Grigg, 1919) and r16 (Dr. Kepp, 1926), both of which independently misquote/misremember this same bulletin as finding a depression of "two tenths" (0.20) in winter — a 4x-inflated, self-serving misquotation appearing in two unrelated tellings.]
- Cross-check: at Alder Corners factory, the same Hessel Bottom men's milk (14 overlapping seasons) tested ~0.19 points HIGHER there than at home — matching the calculated calibration gap almost exactly. [CONFIRMED with a full 16-season data table in r10, Rosalie Cudd's own letter, giving individual season averages 1915–1930, all in the 3.78–3.82 range.]
- **Bulletin No. 471**, issued October [1958], closing paragraph (Professor Vaught's wording, per Ivy):
  > "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."
  [This exact paragraph is reprinted verbatim by r11 (the bulletin's own author, Vaught, Section VII) and by r07 Item 15 — fully consistent across all three tellings. Yet r11's own Section V computes a DIFFERENT loss figure (118,000 lb) shortly before quoting this 76,000 lb conclusion — an internal inconsistency within the bulletin itself as reproduced in r11.]
- Timeline she closes on: glass condemned 1923, cause found 1958 = 35 years. Grandfather dismissed 1925 = 33 years before vindication. He died in **1934**, so had been dead **24 years** when the exonerating bulletin was issued (1958). "I have no remedy to propose. I have only the crate."

## R05 — "The Books of the Association," Wilbur Teague, retired bookkeeper (1905–1945), memoir chapter written at Ashlin, winter 1948.

- Teague kept the Association's books **1905–1945** (40 years); wrote this memoir chapter 3 years after retiring (i.e., 1948).
- Office at Ashlin: one room over a harness shop, cold at north end, too warm at south end; worked at a sloped desk with a brass rail.
- The Association in his time = three parts: creamery at Larrow Green, office at Ashlin, skimming station at Ostrey Hollow (7 miles up valley). **Selby Vose** was manager over the whole; **Effie Loomis** was secretary; Teague kept the ledger.
- Station's basic operation (as he explains it): patrons hauled milk to the station instead of driving 7 miles to Larrow Green; milk weighed, sampled, tested for butterfat; patron paid on pounds+test; station separated milk, sent cream to Larrow Green, returned skim to farmer.
- **The "station-gain" line**: on one side of the station account, butterfat CREDITED to patrons per the station's own test; on the other side, butterfat ACTUALLY RECEIVED at Larrow Green in the station's cream (weighed/tested into the vats there). The second figure was always larger. The difference ("station gain") was carried to the Association's general account. Teague states he personally INVENTED this bookkeeping line himself when he started in 1905 — denies it was any "old dodge of the founders." [CORROBORATED independently and dramatically by r14, Hobart Sill: as the buttermaker who physically re-weighed every batch of Hollow cream himself for 41 years, Sill independently confirms the exact same phenomenon from the receiving end — "that cream ran heavy. Every year it ran heavy... Always more. Never less" — matching Teague's ledger line from an entirely independent physical vantage point.]
- Example entry he quotes in full (for **1898**):
  > "Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account."
  - He notes this same form recurs every year, and the consistency of "five and a quarter percent" every year made it "furniture" — something that stopped him from scrutinizing it further, in hindsight. [This 5.25% figure matches Ivy Keddie's/r04's math of "5.25% of the fat credited" — independent ledger corroboration of the same calibration-driven gap. This exact 1898 example is reprinted WORD FOR WORD identically in r07 Item 6 and in r12's "Note Added While in Press" — three separate documents quote the identical ledger entry.]

## R11 — Bulletin No. 471, Introduction and Method, Leland Vaught, professor of dairy husbandry, State Agricultural College at Farland, October 1958.

*Sections I–VII, written for extension agents and county workers, closing with the bulletin's own concluding paragraph reproduced entire.*

- **I. Why written**: This station has been asked "at intervals for fifty years" why Ostrey Hollow's tests ran below the creamery's own seven miles down the valley. "It is answered here by reference to a piece of glass." Key takeaway for the extension worker: "the milk pipette is the one instrument in the butterfat test which the plant cannot check against anything else, and it is the instrument on which every figure in the plant depends."
- **II. The instrument**: the test = four operations — (1) a measured charge of milk drawn into a pipette and delivered into a test bottle; (2) sulfuric acid added, dissolves everything but the fat; (3) bottle spun in a hand tester, fat rises into the graduated neck; (4) operator reads the fat directly, as a percentage, off the neck. The operator "weighs nothing" — the reading is valid only because the charge is a fixed, known quantity (the standard charge in this state: **17.6 cubic centimeters**). If the pipette delivers less than its mark, the reading is low "in the exact proportion by which the charge was short."
  - **Three consequences**, emphasized because "in fifty years of this history nobody appears to have held all three in mind at once": (1) the error is PROPORTIONAL not additive — rich milk loses more absolutely; ratios between herds are preserved; the station's own figures look internally consistent and orderly, wrong only against the outside world; (2) the error is PRECISE — a short pipette delivers the identical wrong reading every time, for a quarter century, "has the appearance of excellent work" (unlike a scattered error, e.g. a leaking pipe, which attracts attention); (3) the error is INVISIBLE FROM INSIDE THE PLANT — every other element (scales, tester's speed, acid strength, bottle graduation) can be checked against an external standard, but the pipette IS the standard used to measure everything else — "an instrument that is itself the measure cannot be caught by measuring with it." Only an external burette can catch it, and burettes reached field men very late.
- **III. Material examined**: of the lot bought for Ostrey Hollow in 1897, "**five dozen**" pipettes were purchased [CONFLICTS with r04's and r07 Item 2's "6 doz. (72)" per the original invoice] — **nine** recovered from the condemned crate, unbroken and legible; stems blown **17.6 c.c.**; delivered against a calibrated burette, five runs each; mean delivery of the nine = **16.27 c.c.** [CONFLICTS with r04's and r07 Item 14's mean of **16.72 c.c.** for the same nine pipettes — likely a digit transposition between the two tellings]. States the station's own glass "went into use on **the first of May, 1898**" [CONFLICTS with this same document's own Section VII conclusion, which says "from the first of May, **1897**" — an internal self-contradiction within r11; also conflicts with r07 Item 2's 1897 invoice-use date].
  - Method described for repeatability: fill pipette to mark with room-temp water, wipe tip, deliver in the ordinary working manner into a certified burette; five deliveries per pipette, averaged; "occupies an afternoon," no special apparatus beyond the burette.
- **IV. The interval of 1911**: "For **nine weeks** in the summer of 1911" [matches r03/r04/r09's precise dating, contra r05's/r16's "six weeks"] the station's pipette was broken, a borrowed measure used; "one variable changed"; average rose from 3.61 to 3.80, held nine weeks, returned to 3.61 when the station's own measure resumed; recorded contemporaneously "by a man who did not know what he was recording and set a question mark against it." "Lay unread for forty-seven years" [1911+47=1958]. Vaught: "I have spent my working life urging students to prefer an experiment to a calculation. Here is one that ran itself... under conditions no designed trial could have bettered."
- **V. Magnitude**: ratio delivered/marked = 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = **0.9244**. Recorded average **3.61**; corresponding TRUE average = 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = **3.905**, a shortfall of **0.295** point. [CONFLICTS with r04's parallel calculation of a true average of 3.80 and shortfall of 0.19, using the different 16.72 c.c. mean.] Applied to 40,000,000 lb over 25 full delivery years: yields **118,000 lb** of butterfat delivered-and-not-credited. Explicitly flags its own inconsistency: "It should be said that this figure is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the glassware." Notes the "station gain" on the Association's ledgers is "five and a quarter percent of the butterfat actually delivered."
- **VI. The question of feed**: reprints Bulletin No. 214 (1911) verbatim, identically to r04 and r07 Item 12: "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Notes this discrepancy is "present in every month of the year and is some six times the largest effect the trial could produce."
- **VII. Conclusion** (quoted in full, identical to r04's and r07 Item 15's quotes):
  > "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."
  [NOTE the internal self-contradiction: this concluding paragraph's 76,000 lb figure directly conflicts with Section V's own computed 118,000 lb figure, within the very same document; the concluding paragraph's "first of May, 1897" also conflicts with Section III's own "first of May, 1898."]
- Signed **L. VAUGHT** — confirms "Professor Vaught," the attributed author named in r04.

Observed general stock condition: "thin in the flank at the end of the winter feeding," slow to condition in spring, rougher coats late-season; "it is our opinion, formed on the ground and not out of a book, that what such land gives in quantity it withholds in quality, and that a cow cannot put into her pail what her keep has never put into her." Explicit finding: **"We report that we found no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter, and we wish that finding to be printed in the same type as the rest."** Closing paragraph (matches r07 Item 11's quote exactly): "We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March. The milk is whole and the scales are honest. It is our opinion that the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station." Signed: **NYLE GRIGG, chairman, and the committee.**
- Closing (unsigned) editorial-style line: "That report was received and accepted, and the question was closed by it for eleven years — until this season, and the men who have reopened it have brought nothing new before us but their impatience." [Ambiguous whether this is Grigg's own continuing words or a compiler's added note.]

## R16 — "The Bog Meadows and the Milk," Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S., paper read before the Ordell County Farm Club, 1926, with floor discussion appended.

- **Dr. Roland Kepp**, veterinary surgeon of Ashlin, thirty years' practice. Frames his approach: "when I am called to a herd that is doing badly, I ask first what the cattle are standing on... the answer to nine cases in ten is under the animal's feet, and... the tenth is in the water she drinks."
- **The land**: elaborate soil-science argument — wet/undrained bottoms produce acid soil, poor in lime and phosphates; sweet grasses crowded out within a few seasons by sedge, rush, and coarse tussock grasses, which carry bulk and water but little lime or phosphorus; cut late, stripping remaining nutrients.
- **What the cow does with such keep**: a cow short on minerals "robs her own frame to make good... until she cannot." Consequences observed on wet-meadow herds: thin over the loin, rough coats, slow to condition in spring, prone to fall digestive disorders, later breeding, worse feet. "They will milk... wet land is generous of flow... but what is in the pail is another matter, and the fat is the first thing to suffer, because the fat is the dearest thing the cow makes."
- **The measure of it**: Kepp's own 30-year field observation — "the wet bottoms of this county yield a milk poorer in fat by **a fifth of a point [0.20]** and every practical man knows it," consistent regardless of breed, farmer, or barn, "depends on the ground."
- **Hessel Bottom**: **SIX** farms cut hay/pasture [matches r09/r15, conflicts with r02's seven]; springs run year-round at the foot of the slope; part under water every spring, unwalkable till "well into May"; sedge hay is "very nearly the whole" winter dependence. Cites the gap "as it has been given to me and as I have satisfied myself" = **twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21** [matches Vose's r08 claimed 0.21 RISE in 1924 tests, here reused/reinterpreted as a static Hollow-vs-valley gap figure]. Notes the 1908 committee "went over the ground... found the herds sound, the milk whole, and the scales honest, and laid the cause to the hay."
- **The evidence of the College**: cites Bulletin No. 214 as finding butterfat test "depressed by **two tenths [0.20]** in the winter feeding." **[Same misquotation as r15's Grigg — a SECOND, independent 1926 source repeating the identical wrong "two tenths" figure against the bulletin's actual verbatim "five hundredths," quoted correctly in r04/r07 Item 12/r11.]** Kepp explicitly triangulates three numbers as mutually corroborating: "Two tenths from the College, laid alongside the fifth of a point I have found in the field [0.20] and the twenty-one hundredths that station has been complaining of [0.21], and the three agree so nearly that I do not know what more a reasonable man requires." [All three of these figures are, per the true documentary record established elsewhere (Bulletin 214's real 0.05 finding; Ivy's 1958 calculated 0.19 true gap), either misquoted or otherwise inflated — a striking case of independently "corroborating" figures reinforcing a false conclusion.]
- **An objection**: Kepp addresses an objection "urged upon me, by a gentleman" (unnamed) — that in summer 1911 the station's testing was "broken off for **six weeks**" [matches r05's and this document's own "six weeks," contra r03/r04/r09/r11's precise "nine weeks"], during which figures stood higher. Kepp dismisses it rhetorically: "Six weeks in the flush of the season, gentlemen... I am astonished that anybody should found an argument on six weeks of summer figures. I could produce you six weeks in any year that would prove anything you liked." [This shows the single most decisive piece of evidence — later identified by Vaught's 1958 bulletin (r11/r04) as "the only surviving natural experiment" — was already known and discussed as early as 1926, but dismissed without engaging its logic, 32 years before it was properly recognized.]
- **The remedy**: drain, lime repeatedly, sow down, keep stock off wet spring ground, feed mixed ration with mineral supplement per the College. "Fat has been worth **twenty-seven cents** the pound through these years" [matches r14's Sill; conflicts with r02's/r09's 24-25 cents].
- **Discussion** (recorded by club secretary):
  - A member asks whether the difference might be in the cows/poorer stock of poorer men. Kepp rejects it: some of the best cattle in the county are on wet land; some indifferent cattle on hills test well; "a man may improve his herd for twenty years and never get his fat up while his ground is sour."
  - A member asks whether the trouble might lie in the STATION rather than the bottom, "as had been said in the valley for years." Kepp replies he has "no interest... and no competence in it," has "never in his life read a test and would not know how," but argues from convergence of evidence (soil, herbage, stock condition, the College's trial, and consistency "in every wet bottom in the county alike") that "it is not good sense to go looking for a second cause in a building." **[This is an explicit, on-the-record rejection of the equipment-defect hypothesis — which the 1958 bulletin later proves was in fact the ENTIRE and SOLE true cause. A striking instance of dramatic irony.]**
  - The chairman thanks Kepp; the paper is to be printed for the members.

## Cross-cutting conflicts and oddities (consolidated master list, R01–R16)

1. **Award amount**: $3,500 (r01, Vira's oral memory) vs. **$3,800** (r02, r03 implicitly, r05, r07 Item 7, r08, r13 — the consistent documentary figure).
2. **Timing of the hearing**: Vira (r01) implies the hearing followed almost immediately after the 1919 letter (winter 1919/spring 1920); the documentary record (r02, r03, r07, r08, r13) places the actual petition/hearing in **1925**, roughly 5–6 years later.
3. **Cause of the low tests — five competing theories, explicitly attributed by r13**: (a) THEFT — Vira (r01) asserts as settled fact; bookkeeper Teague (r05) suspects the same and testified so; buttermaker Sill (r14) independently endorses a version of it. (b) WATER/DILUTION — Manager Vose (r08) publicly insists on this; Sill (r14) publicly sides with Vose. (c) WET HAY/LAND — the 1908 committee under Nyle Grigg (r07 Item 11, r09, r15) and later Dr. Kepp (r16), who builds an elaborate scientific case for it. (d) KEDDIE'S OWN DENIAL (r03) — he claims no explanation, though r13 reveals he privately blamed the patrons. (e) THE TRUE ANSWER — Ivy Keddie's 1958 investigation and Bulletin No. 471 (r04, r07 Item 15, r11): a pure calibration defect in the station's pipettes (delivering only ~92-95% of rated volume); explicitly states "No milk was watered... and no cream was stolen." This is the central resolution/twist of the whole dossier — every contemporary theory (theft, dilution, land/feed) was wrong, and multiple independent witnesses (Teague's ledger, Sill's re-weighing, Rundle's retraction) had already stumbled onto physical evidence (cream arriving "heavy") that was more consistent with the true calibration explanation than with any of the theories they actually believed.
4. **Selby Vose's relation to Gideon Vose**: "nephew" (r01, r12 — both oral/secondhand) vs. **"son"** (r02, r08 — both authoritative/documentary/self-reported). An even split across four sources.
5. **Total lifetime milk intake at the station**: 40,000,000 lb (r02; r04's calculation; r07 Item 6's ledger citation) vs. **44,000,000 lb** (r05, Teague's memoir; independently repeated by r12, Rundle's pamphlet).
6. **Long-run average test**: ~3.61% recorded (r03, r04, r07 Item 3, r06's inspection notes, consistently) vs. **3.16%** (r05, Teague; independently corroborated by r13, Frick, the arbitration chairman himself).
7. **1924 corrected average**: 3.80 (r03 Keddie, r06 Strawn's inspection note) vs. **3.90** (r08, Vose — self-serving inflation, echoed nowhere else).
8. **Date pipettes were condemned**: 12 June 1923 (r02 [implied], r04, r06 Strawn's own notebook, r07 Item 9 photostat) vs. **12 July 1923** (r08 Vose; independently repeated by r12 Rundle).
9. **Duration of the 1911 measure substitution**: precisely 8 May–10 July 1911 (~nine weeks; r03's dated entries, r04, r09, r11) vs. **"six weeks"** (r05 Teague; independently repeated by r16 Kepp).
10. **Location the crate was found**: the station building's loft over the old intake room (r04's narrative; consistent with r06's 1928 note about the loft being "full of odds and ends") vs. **"the Association office at Ashlin"** (r07's Item 5 provenance note).
11. **Catalog mislabeling** (r07): Item 3 (station test book, actually signed/kept by A. Keddie — confirmed independently by r13's exhibit list) is headed "kept by E. Brant"; Item 4 (farm weigh book, provenanced entirely through the Brant family and independently confirmed as Emmet Brant's own book by r09's Roy Brant and by r13's exhibit list) is headed "kept by A. Keddie." These two headers appear to be swapped/crossed in the curator's own catalog.
12. **1919 circular's tone**: experienced by recipients as a severe, personal accusation kept for decades as evidence of grievance (r01; r03's "nine kitchens... have had that letter in a drawer ever since"; r09's account of permanent estrangement over it) vs. described by its own author (Vose, r08) as temperate, naming nobody, accusing nobody — vs. praised by Sill (r14) as "the only plain thing that office ever did."
13. **Merle Strawn's circuit start year**: "1907" by his own account (r06's covering note) vs. "**from 1908**" per Ivy Keddie's description of his notebooks (r04).
14. **1911 state inspection visit**: Strawn's own June 1911 circuit note says "Scales true. Watched a test through. Nothing here to note" (r06) — yet this visit falls within the window (8 May–10 July 1911) when the borrowed/substitute measure was in use — an unresolved oddity: the state inspector's contemporaneous check did not flag or remark on the anomaly at all.
15. **Alder Corners provenance/relationship**: r04 says Rosalie Cudd is Jerome Cudd's *niece*; r07's Item 13 provenance says the book passed from the founder to his *daughter* in 1941; **r10's own letter, in Rosalie's own voice, settles this definitively: she is Jerome Cudd's daughter** ("My father, Jerome Cudd, built this factory"). r12 independently and correctly also calls her "Jerome Cudd's daughter." r04's "niece" is thus the clear outlier/error.
16. **Number of farms with rights on Hessel Bottom**: **seven** (r02, Effie Loomis) vs. **six** (r09 Roy Brant; r15's reprinted 1908 report; r16 Dr. Kepp) — three independent sources now say six against Effie's lone seven.
17. **Alder Corners Cheese Company's founding/opening year**: **1913** (r10, Rosalie Cudd's own letter — presumably authoritative as her father's daughter) vs. **1914** (r09 Roy Brant; r12 Rundle's pamphlet — two independent sources).
18. **Payment-by-test / Article VII adoption year**: **1892** annual meeting (r02 Effie Loomis; r07 Item 1's curatorial cross-reference) vs. **1893** meeting (r15, Nyle Grigg, hedged as "as I have always understood it") vs. **1891**, "the year the churn was set up" (r12, Rundle, conflating adoption with the creamery's opening).
19. **Bulletin No. 214's actual finding vs. its community reputation**: the bulletin itself, quoted identically and verbatim in three places (r04, r07 Item 12, r11 Section VI), found bog hay depresses the test by "not more than **five hundredths** of one per cent [0.05] in winter, and by nothing... in summer." Yet TWO independent secondary sources — Nyle Grigg's 1919 covering letter (r15) and Dr. Kepp's 1926 paper (r16) — both cite the SAME wrong figure: "**two tenths**" [0.20], a 4x inflation, apparently because this exaggerated figure had become the version commonly repeated/believed in the valley, distinct from the bulletin's real (and much weaker) finding.
20. **The station's own glass entering service**: "1 May 1897" (r07 Item 2's invoice logic; r11's own Section VII conclusion) vs. **"1 May 1898"** (r11's own Section III) — a self-contradiction WITHIN r11 alone.
21. **Number of pipettes originally purchased in 1897**: "6 doz. (72)" per the Ammon & Sons invoice itself (r07 Item 2, quoted verbatim) and r04's retelling of it vs. **"five dozen" (60)** per r11 Section III (Vaught's own bulletin) — a conflict between the primary invoice document and the bulletin that supposedly analyzes the same purchase.
22. **Number of pipettes recovered from the crate in 1958**: **nine**, consistently, across r04, r07 Item 5/14, and r11 — vs. **"eleven"** per r12's 1959 footnote (likely Rundle's secondhand error).
23. **Mean delivery of the nine recovered pipettes**: **16.72 c.c.** (r04's and r07 Item 14's identical quoted measurement sheet) vs. **16.27 c.c.** (r11 Section III — likely digit transposition of the same underlying data), leading to two different "true average" and "shortfall" calculations (3.80/0.19/76,000 lb per r04, vs. 3.905/0.295/118,000 lb per r11 Section V) — with r11 itself then quoting the 76,000 lb figure in its own concluding paragraph (Section VII), contradicting its own Section V math.
24. **Patrons' claimed/calculated financial or quantity loss** — at least FOUR distinct figures circulate: **76,000 lb** / **$19,000** (r04's calculation, echoed in r11's concluding quote and r07 Item 6's curatorial note); **118,000 lb** (r11's own Section V calculation, self-flagged as irreconcilable with other data); **67,000 lb** (r13, Frick — "the patrons' own reckoning," as presented to the arbitration board); **$19,600** (r12, Rundle — "the patrons' petition set the figure out to the dollar"). None of these four figures agree, despite several purporting to describe the same underlying claim.
25. **The "gap" between Hollow milk and valley/true milk quality — at least SIX distinct figures**: **0.17** (r09, Roy Brant, "seventeen hundredths"); **0.19** (r04, Ivy Keddie's calculated true calibration gap, cross-validated by Alder Corners data in r10 and Larrow Green's ledger in r07 Item 16); **0.20** ("a fifth of a point," r16 Kepp's own field observation; also the misquoted Bulletin 214 figure per r15/r16); **0.21** (r08, Vose's claimed 1924 "rise"; also r16 Kepp's cited "gap... that station has been complaining of" — possibly the same number recycled in a different context); **0.295** (r11 Section V's own calculation); and Hobart Sill's separate claim (r14) that the Hollow's "real" test was **3.85** against Larrow Green's 3.78 (implying a gap of roughly 0.07, the smallest of all the estimates). None of these six figures are identical, though several cluster near 0.19–0.21.
26. **Price of butterfat**: **~25 cents/lb average** (r02 Effie Loomis; ~matches r09's 24 cents in 1910 / ~25 cents overall) vs. **27 cents** (r14 Sill; r16 Kepp — two independent sources).
27. **Emmet Brant's weigh-book start date**: **1 January 1897** (r07 Item 4's provenance note; r13's independent exhibit list) vs. **1 January 1898** (r09, Roy Brant's own account of his father's record-keeping) — a one-year discrepancy between the son's personal memory and the documentary/legal record.
28. **Ivy Keddie's and Hazel Brant's exact relationship**: "second cousins" (r09, Roy Brant; r12's 1959 footnote — two independent, family-sourced accounts) vs. the 1958 visiting researcher (presumed to be Ivy, though unnamed) being described in r01 as "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed."
29. **The 1908 committee's actual sampling period vs. its chairman's later recollection of it — an internal contradiction within r15 alone**: the reprinted 1908 report itself states sampling covered only "January, February and March" (matching r07 Item 11's curatorial note); but Nyle Grigg's own 1919 covering letter, introducing the very same reprint, claims the committee "sampled those herds through the whole of 1908 — every month of that year."
30. **The 1911 anomaly's true significance, publicly known but dismissed decades before Ivy's investigation**: r16 reveals that as early as 1926 — 32 years before Bulletin 471 — an unnamed "gentleman" raised the 1911 measure-substitution episode as an objection to the wet-hay theory at Dr. Kepp's Farm Club talk; Kepp dismissed it with a rhetorical flourish rather than engaging its logic, even though Vaught's 1958 bulletin (r11) would later call this exact episode "the only surviving natural experiment" and treat it as the single most decisive piece of evidence in the whole case.
31. **Keddie's private beliefs vs. his public/written statements**: his own written statement (r03) and unsent letter insist he has no explanation and no suspicion of anyone. r13 (Frick) reveals Keddie privately confided, in a corridor, that he "always thought the patrons were at fault" — directly contradicting the popular/subsequent characterization (which Frick says has "been said since") that Keddie "suspected nobody."
32. **Rundle's (r12) own internal reversal**: his pamphlet's Chapter IV endorses the theft theory as "the settled opinion of the valley... never heard it contradicted at the store," but a "Note Added While in Press" — seeing the same 1898 ledger entry independently cited by Teague (r05) — retracts the theory entirely: "Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy... I cannot make my paragraph stand."
33. **The cream-arriving-heavy phenomenon — three independent corroborating sources, three different interpretations**: Teague (r05) invents the "station gain" ledger line to describe it and privately suspects theft; Rundle (r12) initially believes theft, then retracts on realizing the direction of the discrepancy contradicts theft; Sill (r14), who physically re-weighed the cream himself for 41 years and has the strongest direct evidence of all, concludes instead that the PATRONS must have been adding water/help — none of the three arrives at the true explanation (a station-side calibration defect under-crediting the patrons), even though all three had the necessary physical evidence.
34. **Whether the arbitration board sat entirely "at Ashlin"**: r02, r05, and r08 all describe the board as sitting "at Ashlin" without qualification; r13 (Frick himself, the chairman) clarifies that only the first of three sessions was at Ashlin — the second and third were held at Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's own weigh room, at his personal insistence.
35. **Reason for the Keddie–Brant estrangement**: multiple sources (r01, r03) frame it simply as fallout from suspicion/accusation surrounding the 1919 circular; r09 (Roy Brant, the family's own account) specifies a more particular grievance — not that Emmet believed Keddie guilty, but that Emmet felt Keddie, as keeper of the test book, should have publicly defended the patrons against the circular and did not.
36. **Whether general valley hostility over the Hollow matter was permanent or time-limited**: r01/r03/r09 all imply the specific Keddie–Brant personal estrangement was lifelong/permanent (never spoke again, until both were dead); r12 (Rundle) describes the broader "two ends of this valley" hostility as lasting only "six years" (1919–1925) — possibly reconcilable as general-valley civility resuming after the arbitration while the specific family rift remained permanent, but stated as a flat duration in r12.
