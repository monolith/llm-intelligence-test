# Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

**Note on method:** I read notes covering r01–r16 (compiled by earlier readers) and then r17–r24 myself, in order, without being able to go back. All 24 retellings concern one connected history — the Ostrey Hollow butterfat-testing dispute in the Larrow Valley — so I have partitioned it into eight sub-stories by theme/episode rather than by narrator. Each story below uses corrected facts, with the conflict and my resolution method noted inline where it matters.

## Story 1: The Association and the Rule That Made the Test Final (1889–1896)

Between 1889 and 1896 the farmers of the Larrow Valley built the institution whose rule would later make the Ostrey Hollow dispute possible at all. In 1889 they met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, electing Gideon Vose — "heavy, quiet, obstinate" — as first president, an office he held 22 years until 1911 (r02, r12). In 1891 they built their main creamery at Larrow Green on Larrow Creek (r02, r12, r22); before it, "every man's arithmetic was his own" (r02). At a following annual meeting Gideon Vose personally drafted, and the membership adopted, Article VII of the by-laws: every patron would be paid strictly on the butterfat his milk tested at delivery, "and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" — no appeal from a station's own reading (r02, r07 Item 1, r12, r15, r22). The exact adoption year is contested: 1892 per Effie Loomis, the Association's own long-serving secretary (r02); 1891 per Dell Rundle's pamphlet (r12); 1893 per Nyle Grigg's recollection (r15); and county agent Orin Falke, checking the archives in 1959, found the printed by-laws undated and the minute book that would prove it lost in a 1938 office move, and declined to guess (r22). I favor 1892 on Effie's institutional authority, but given that even an archival check could not settle it, this cannot be fully resolved.

Over the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station seven miles up-valley at Ostrey Hollow to serve Hessel Bottom's farms, opening 4 May 1896 with Ansel Keddie as tester, who had gone up in 1895 to prepare it (r01, r22). Gideon Vose died in 1917, having authored the very rule — no appeal from a station's test — that would leave Ostrey Hollow's patrons with "no remedy on earth except to quarrel with a figure" once their own station's numbers turned out to be wrong (r13). His son Selby Vose (Story 6) inherited both the manager's chair (from 1913, per r02/r05/r08's own self-report — r23's clerk instead misremembers 1911, a lone conflicting figure I resolve against on the strength of three converging institutional accounts) and, in effect, the institutional stake in that rule's infallibility.

## Story 2: The Trouble at Ostrey Hollow (1896–1922)

From very nearly the station's opening, Ostrey Hollow's milk tested lower than any other in the county — lower than Larrow Green's own creamery patrons (whose average ran about 3.78, per buttermaker Hobart Sill, r14; "a little under two tenths" above the station's own figure per r07 Item 16), and constant across every season: "seventeen hundredths in July and... in February" per Roy Brant (r09), "nineteen hundredths... flat as a floor" per Hazel Brant (r17), independent of pasture or hay. Keddie's own test book (begun 4 May 1896, kept in five columns, corrections crossed and initialed, never overwritten) recorded a station average of 3.61% for essentially every year from 1898 on, against a true corrected figure later calculated at 3.80% (r03, r04, r07 Item 3) — though Teague's memoir and Frick's recollection instead cite a long-run average of 3.16% and a total intake of 44,000,000 lb rather than the ledger-documented 40,000,000 lb (r05, r13; possibly not fully independent of each other, since Rundle's pamphlet, r12, echoing 44 million, drew on the same valley oral tradition Teague represents).

The first complaints came in 1902 — Emmet Brant among the first (r09), one dispute reportedly over "his milk test[ing] under his brother-in-law's down the creek" on the "same feed and water" (r14). In January 1908 the Association sent a three-man committee under Nyle Grigg to sample the Hessel Bottom herds; visiting six farms through January–March (not "the whole of 1908," as Grigg's own 1919 covering letter mistakenly claimed — a self-contradiction within r15, also flagged by r07 Item 11), the committee found "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter" and blamed the wet, sedge-grown meadow hay, recommending drainage rather than any change at the station (r07 Item 11, r09, r15, r24's diary entry for January–March 1908). In 1926 veterinarian Dr. Roland Kepp gave the same wet-hay theory a fuller scientific gloss, citing a county-wide 0.20-point wet-bottom penalty and dismissing, without competence to judge it, a floor suggestion that "the trouble might lie in the station" (r16). Both wet-hay accounts, however, misquoted the State College's actual 1911 Bulletin No. 214 as finding a 0.20-point effect; its real text (quoted identically in r04, r07 Item 12, r11, and reconfirmed by Falke in 1959, r22) found at most 0.05 points in winter and nothing in summer — four times too small, and the wrong season, to explain the flat 0.19-point gap.

## Story 3: The Broken Measure of 1911

On 8 May 1911 the station's own measuring pipette broke; Keddie made shift with a borrowed one — specifically, per Roy Brant (r09), the state dairy inspector Merle Strawn's own calibrated instrument, left with the station that June — until the station's own (defective) measure returned to use on 10 July 1911, a span of about nine weeks (r03's dated book entries; corroborated by r04, r07, r11). Teague's looser recollection of "six weeks" (r05), echoed decades later by an unnamed objector at Kepp's 1926 talk (r16), is less precise than Keddie's own dated entries and is outweighed by them.

Through those nine weeks, with the herds, pasture, season, acid, bottles, and tester all unchanged, the station's average rose from its usual 3.61% to 3.80% — and fell back to 3.61% the same day the station's own measure resumed (r03's two quoted book entries: "Pipette broke this morning" / "Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."). Roy Brant's own farm ledger, kept independently at Hessel Bottom, shows the identical pattern that same summer: "our tests came up, all of us, every farm on the Bottom" (r09) — strong independent corroboration from an entirely separate record. Keddie set a question mark against his own July entry and, per his son Duncan, came home from that summer's work already saying "it was something in the glass," though he "never dared put it in the book" without proof (r18) — an early, private anticipation of the 1958 finding, forty-seven years ahead of it. Oddly, state inspector Strawn's own June 1911 circuit note from squarely within this window records nothing unusual ("Scales true. Watched a test through. Nothing here to note," r06) — an unresolved gap, since if Strawn's own measure was in use that day, his test that day should itself have read high. Dr. Kepp, presented with the anomaly in 1926 discussion, dismissed it as merely "six weeks of summer figures" proving nothing (r16) — a rebuttal that ignored both its precise nine-week duration and its status as a genuine substitution experiment rather than an arbitrary sample.

## Story 4: The 1919 Circular and the Family It Broke

In 1919 manager Selby Vose circulated a printed warning over his own signature to the Ostrey Hollow patrons about "water in the cans," in what he later described as "temperate language, naming nobody, accusing nobody" (r08). The patrons experienced it very differently: kept for decades in drawers and even a trunk lid as evidence of grievance (r01, r03), and answered by not one of the recipients — "which in that valley is a shout" (r17). Ansel Keddie himself delivered the circulars door to door rather than mail them (r24's diary), to nine families by his own later account ("nine kitchens in this Hollow have had that letter in a drawer ever since," r03; also r01, r17) — though his wife Orra's contemporaneous diary entry instead says he delivered to eleven families (r24), a genuine, unresolved conflict between two firsthand, contemporaneous accounts. Buttermaker Hobart Sill was the sole contemporary who approved of the letter — "the only plain thing that office ever did" (r14).

The circular's most lasting casualty was a family. Ansel Keddie's wife Orra was the sister of Emmet Brant, the Bottom's largest patron and hardest hit by the low tests (r01, r03, r09, r18, r24) — full brother and sister, "same mother and same father," per Roy Brant's explicit account (r09); Hazel Brant's 1981 claim that they were only half-siblings "by his father's second wife" (r17) is contradicted by this closer, more direct testimony and by her own admission that she is unreliable on exact family facts. Before 1919 the two families dined together every Sunday; after it, total estrangement until both men were dead (r01, r09, r24). Roy Brant specifies the cause was not the accusation itself but that Emmet felt "the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up... and did not" (r09) — Emmet confronted Keddie in the yard that June and never entered the house again (r24's diary). Orra outlived her husband, dying in 1948 without ever seeing him cleared (r18, r24).

## Story 5: The Glassware — Purchase, Manufacture, and Condemnation (1897–1923)

On 14 April 1897 the Association bought a complete testing outfit for Ostrey Hollow from Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet: 6 dozen (72) milk-test pipettes at $0.35 each ($25.20), 12 dozen test bottles ($31.68), an acid measure ($0.90), a four-bottle hand tester ($18.00), and freight ($2.15) — total $77.93, an invoice reproduced identically in r04, r07 Item 2, r19, and by the manufacturer's own historian in r20 (though both r19 and r20's prose narration misreads the total as "$77.39," a transposition of the same figures each has just quoted correctly). The outfit went into use "on the first of May, 1897," per the invoice date and Keddie's own book ("the spring following" the 1896 opening, r03) — though Vaught's 1958 bulletin (r11) and clerk Bertram Kyle (r23) both instead date it to 1898, a discrepancy I resolve toward 1897 on the invoice's own dating logic, without full certainty.

Each pipette was blown to deliver 17.6 c.c., the state's standard charge. In fact each delivered, on average, only 16.72 c.c. — short by 0.88 c.c., or 5% ("one part in twenty") of its marked capacity. Manufacturer Junia Ammon's 1949 firm history (r20) offers the only account of how: a reground glassblowing mold likely came back "a hair different" and ran a whole lot consistently short in a way no ordinary spot-check of a few pieces could catch — and admits the firm's 1923 reply to the Association's complaint ("our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot") was inadequate, though not dishonest. (Note: r20 elsewhere claims the firm "sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900," directly contradicted by the 1897 Ordell County invoice it reproduces in the same chapter.)

The defect went undetected until state dairy inspector Merle Strawn, newly issued a calibrated burette in 1921 (but not applied to this station's glass until two years later, per his own admitted delay, r06), ran the Ostrey Hollow pipettes against it on 12 June 1923 and condemned the whole crate — a date fixed by Strawn's own contemporaneous notebook (r06), a photostat of the same (r07 Item 9), and the bulletin (r11), against looser or self-serving misdatings elsewhere: Vose and Rundle both say July 1923 (r08, r12); Hazel Brant says "twenty-one" [1921] (r17); Kyle says "twenty-two" [1922] (r23). New glass was ordered and in use within the month (r06, r07 Item 10, r20's own reply letter).

## Story 6: The Petition and the Board of Arbitration (1925)

In January 1925 the Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned the Association, claiming a lifetime's underpayment. A three-man board of arbitration, chaired by Ashlin attorney Alonzo Frick — chosen, he says, "because I was the one of the three who kept no cow" (r13) — sat from 17 March to 4 May 1925: one session at the Association's Ashlin office, two more (Frick's own request) at the station itself, in its weigh room, so the board could see the plant rather than hear it described (r13, corroborated by cream-hauler's son Cleve Oram, who stood outside in the rain during one session, r21, and by Orra Keddie's diary, r24). Three explanations were formally argued: water, urged by manager Selby Vose "without heat and without proof"; theft, insinuated rather than charged by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague; and the 1908 committee's wet-hay report, read into the record "for the better part of an afternoon" (r13). Keddie's own 9-sentence statement (17 March 1925, quoted in full in r03) denied taking "a pound of cream," and — privately, per Frick — he told the chairman in the Ashlin corridor that he had always suspected the patrons themselves, though he never said so publicly (r13); this sits oddly against his son Duncan's account that Keddie privately suspected the glass, not the patrons, as early as 1911 (r18) — a conflict between two informants I cannot fully resolve, though Duncan's closer, sustained vantage as the man's own son living in the house arguably outweighs a single hallway remark Frick recorded 21 years after the fact.

The board found "no cause established" against anyone, yet awarded the patrons $3,800 "without admission" — a figure recorded in the arbitration's own minute book (r02, r07 Item 7) and independently confirmed by Effie Loomis, Wilbur Teague, Selby Vose, and Frick himself (five sources including the primary minute book); Vira Toland's $3,500 (r01) and Cleve Oram's matching $3,500 (r21) are two later oral-memory figures, possibly sharing common valley folklore rather than being fully independent. Keddie was dismissed 30 April 1925, four days before the board's own finding — meaning, as Frick stresses, "there was no finding against him from us and there never was one" (r13, r03, r18, r24's diary entry for that date). The crate of condemned pipettes was never raised at any of the three sessions (r02, confirmed by Frick's own four-item exhibit list, r13) despite sitting in the loft directly over the board's heads during the two station sessions (r13, r18, r21).

## Story 7: The View From Alder Corners (1913/14–1930)

From 1915, four (of the Bottom's six) farms hauled their summer-flush milk not to Ostrey Hollow but over Redlow Ridge to the Alder Corners Cheese Company in Vessey County, returning to Ostrey Hollow the rest of the year once the ridge road closed for winter (r09, r10, r12, r17). The factory was built by Jerome Cudd — in 1913 by his own daughter Rosalie's account (r10) or 1914 per two other independent sources (r09, r12) — and Cudd tested on his own bench, never once comparing his figures to the station's, seven miles and a mountain away (r10). His own end-of-summer 1919 entry for the four Hessel Bottom men reads 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82 (r10) — and his daughter Rosalie, who ran the factory from his death in March 1941 (matching r07 Item 13's provenance and r12, against r04's erroneous description of her as his "niece," a description r19's newspaper piece then independently repeats), supplied Ivy Keddie in 1958 with a full sixteen-season table (1915–1930) clustering 3.78–3.82 and averaging about 3.80.

That figure is the single most damaging piece of evidence against every "poor milk" theory of the Hollow's trouble: the very same farmers' milk, tested on an entirely different bench, by an entirely different man, using entirely different glassware, on the other side of a mountain, came out almost exactly at the "true" 3.80% that Ivy Keddie's 1958 pipette calibration would separately calculate for the station itself (r04) — an independent cross-check arrived at from a completely unconnected direction. Cudd himself dismissed the wet-hay theory as valley folklore, "the way some families have a ghost" (r10), and cheesemaking's own sensitivity to watered milk (curd fails to knit) meant that in sixteen summers he never once suspected or docked any of the four men (r10). Some of the four who "went over the ridge" felt themselves talked about "as though they had gone over to the enemy" (r09); Roy Brant, who did not go, says he regretted the decision for the rest of his working life, since it cost his farm money he never recovered (r09).

## Story 8: Ivy Keddie's Investigation and the 1958 Vindication

In 1958, Ivy Keddie — Ansel Keddie's granddaughter, daughter of his son Duncan (r04, r18; not the "great-niece" of r19's newspaper account and Falke's own first draft, corrected by Falke in r22) — a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, assembled five record sets that had never before been in one room: the station's own test book, the Brant family's weigh book (lent by Roy Brant), Alder Corners' figures (copied by Rosalie Cudd), Strawn's circuit notebooks, and the Association's own ledgers (r04). In February 1958 she found, in the loft over the station's old intake room — not, as one curatorial note mistakenly has it, the Ashlin office (r07 Item 5, against r04, r06, r12, r17, r18, r21's convergent testimony) — a crate stenciled "Ammon & Sons Dairy Supply Tarnet," containing nine surviving pipettes (r04, r07, r11, r22; not the "eleven" of r12's footnote and r19's newspaper piece). On 9 March 1958 she measured them against a calibrated burette at Farland: mean delivery 16.72 c.c. against a 17.6 c.c. mark — a figure verifiable by averaging her own nine quoted readings — confirming the pipettes delivered only 95% of their rated volume.

From this she recalculated the station's true average test at 3.80% against a recorded 3.61%, a 0.19-point shortfall, and — over the 25 full delivery years the patrons' claim covered (1898–1922, 40,000,000 lb of milk) — 76,000 lb of butterfat the patrons were never credited, worth about $19,000 against the $3,800 they actually received in 1925 (r04). Bulletin No. 471, issued jointly with Professor Leland Vaught in October 1958, concluded: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there" (r04, r07 Item 15, r11) — though the bulletin's own body-text math (a "five dozen" purchase figure, a 16.27 c.c. mean, a 3.905 true average, 118,000 lb shortfall) contradicts its own conclusion, an inconsistency Vaught himself flagged but never resolved (r11). The Ashlin Chronicle's front-page account (r19) and Falke's county report (r22) both publicized the finding; Ansel Keddie, dead since 1934, was cleared 24 years later, and his widow Orra, dead since 1948, did not live to see it (r18, r24).

# Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie: granddaughter.**
Ivy is Duncan Keddie's daughter (r04's own self-description at her talk, and Duncan's own 1968 family notes, r18: "I am Duncan... My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise"). Duncan is Ansel's son (r04, r18). This resolves two errors elsewhere: r19's newspaper calls her Ansel's "great-niece," and Falke's 1959 county report originally repeated the same "great-niece" error before explicitly correcting it in a postscript upon verification (r22). Both errors trace to secondhand, hastily-assembled outside accounts and are outweighed by Duncan's explicit, first-person, corrective statement.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant: second cousins.**
Duncan (Ansel's son) and Roy Brant (Emmet's son) are first cousins — children of siblings Orra and Emmet (r09, r18). Ivy is Duncan's daughter; Hazel is Roy's daughter. Children of first cousins are second cousins — stated explicitly by Duncan (r18: "the children of first cousins") and confirmed by Roy Brant's own reasoning (r09), Rundle's 1959 footnote (r12), and Hazel's own 1981 account ("Ivy Keddie and I are second cousins... it is not complicated," r17) — four sources, including both women themselves. Two narrators (Vira Toland, r01, describing an unnamed 1958 researcher presumed to be Ivy; Cleve Oram, r21) instead call it "first cousin once removed" — a secondhand error outweighed by the principals' own accounts. What the sources leave open: nothing about the generational chain itself, which is stated plainly and repeatedly, but the sources never address whether Orra or Emmet had other siblings who might complicate a simple two-line family tree — this detail is simply never raised and doesn't affect the cousin arithmetic as given.

**B3. Selby Vose's father: Gideon Vose.**
Gideon Vose was the Association's founding (1889) and long-serving (to 1911) president and author of Article VII (r02; r08's own self-report as Gideon's son; explicitly confirmed, with the "nephew" rumor rejected, by Ansel's own son Duncan Keddie, r18, and by office clerk Bertram Kyle, who calls the nephew story "nonsense," r23) — four independent, strong sources, including Selby's own public self-report. This matters because Gideon wrote the very rule (Article VII: "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern," no appeal) that made an uncorrected instrument error permanently binding on the patrons for 26 years. His son Selby then inherited both the manager's office (from 1913) and, arguably, a personal and dynastic stake in defending the Association's honesty rather than entertaining a flaw in the equipment his own father's rule had placed beyond challenge — plausibly why Selby pushed the watered-milk theory (blaming the patrons) rather than the equipment explanation, which he publicly dismissed while also misdating its key event by a month (r08).

**B4. The Vose–Keddie family relationship: none, per the most authoritative source.**
Two narrators record a persistent valley rumor of a family connection: Vira Toland (r01) says Orra Keddie and "old Mrs. Vose" were first cousins; Cleve Oram (r21), citing his mother, says "the Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort," though he admits he "could not draw you the line on paper." Duncan Keddie — Ansel and Orra's own son, and the family's explicit genealogist, writing specifically to correct rumors he'd "heard... twice in the last year" — states flatly: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses... My mother would have told me... and she never once put a Vose in it" (r18). Given Duncan's direct family standing, his stated purpose of correcting exactly this claim, and his mother's own extensive genealogical habits (which he cites as evidence she'd have known and told him), I resolve this as no actual relationship — though since a negative cannot be proven with certainty, I note the two oral claims are outweighed rather than disproven.
# Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math

**C1.**
(a) Ratio = mean measured delivery ÷ marked capacity = 16.72 c.c. ÷ 17.6 c.c. = **0.95**, i.e. the tests were low by **5% ("one part in twenty")**. Derivation: nine surviving pipettes, each blown to a 17.6 c.c. mark, were measured against a calibrated burette in 1958; their individual mean deliveries (16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 — r04/r07) average to exactly 16.72 c.c. Because the fat reading is taken directly off a bottle-neck scale calibrated for a full 17.6 c.c. charge, a pipette that delivers only 95% of that charge produces a fat reading that is also 95% of the truth (r11's technical explanation).

(b) True average = recorded average ÷ ratio = 3.61% ÷ 0.95 = **3.80%**. Shortfall = 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 points ("nineteen hundredths")** — independently matched by r17's "nineteen hundredths" and r22's "nineteen hundredths of a point," and close to (though not identical with) r09's separately-estimated "seventeen hundredths."

(c) Over 25 full delivery years (1898–1922), the station received 40,000,000 lb of milk (r02, r04, r07 Item 6). At the recorded 3.61%, patrons were credited 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb** of fat. At the true 3.80%, they had actually delivered 40,000,000 × 0.038 = **1,520,000 lb**. Difference = 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = **76,000 lb of butterfat not credited**.

(d) Value: 76,000 lb × $0.25/lb (average price over the period, r02) = **$19,000**. Patrons actually received the 1925 arbitration award of $3,800. $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = **0.20 = one-fifth** of the true value.

**C2.** Largest patron = Emmet Brant. 1910 leaf: delivered 200,000 lb, paid on a test of 3.42% (r07 Item 4, r09).
(a) Credited fat = 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb**.
(b) Correcting the same test by the 0.95 ratio: true test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60%. True fat = 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb. Owed but not credited = 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb**.
(c) At the 1910 price of 24 cents/lb (r09): 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40**.

**C3.** Over the same 25 full delivery years:
(a) Credited to patrons on the station's own test: **1,444,000 lb** (from C1c).
(b) Actually received at Larrow Green in the station's cream: the ledgers' own "station gain" line states this surplus as 5.25% of the fat credited (per the 1898 example: 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.264% ≈ "five and a quarter percent," r05/r07 Item 6/r12). Applied to the total: 1,444,000 × 1.0525 ≈ **1,520,000 lb** received — which, notably, matches the independently-calculated true total in C1c almost exactly, an arithmetic convergence between two unrelated methods (calibration math vs. ledger bookkeeping) worth flagging as strong corroboration.
(c) The difference (≈76,000 lb) is **5.26% (≈5.25%) of the fat credited** (1,444,000 lb) but **5.0% ("one part in twenty") of the fat actually delivered** (1,520,000 lb). Both percentages are correct, but only relative to their own denominator — a distinction the ledgers' own secretary flagged (r02, r07 Item 6) and which the two are not interchangeable.

**C4.**
(a) Invoice total: **$77.93** (14 April 1897, Ammon & Sons — quoted identically in r04, r07 Item 2, r19, r20). Note: both r19 and r20 misstate this in prose as "$77.39" despite quoting "$77.93" correctly in their own block quotations — a self-contradiction within each, not a genuine second figure.
(b) The invoice bought **6 dozen (72) pipettes** at $0.35 each. Each was blown to deliver 17.6 c.c. but measured (in 1958) to deliver only 16.72 c.c. — short by **17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c. per pipette**.

**C5.**
(a) The faulty glassware was in use from **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923** — about **26 years** (26 years, 1 month, 11 days). The money/butterfat calculation instead runs over **25 full delivery years (1898–1922)**. These differ because the "years in use" figure counts every calendar year the glass touched, including two partial years — 1897 (glass arrived in May, so only ~8 months of that year used it) and 1923 (glass condemned in June, so only ~5 months of that year used it) — while the retrospective calculation in r04 counts only complete calendar years for which a clean, whole-year total exists.
(b) The station's first-season (4 May–31 December 1896) intake, using borrowed Larrow Green glass, is **not recorded in any surviving ledger** — the curator explicitly says so (r07). Two personal-memory estimates conflict: Hobart Sill recalls "about nine hundred thousand pounds" (r14); clerk Bertram Kyle recalls "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" (r23). Both are secondhand personal recollections, not records, and diverge by over 20%. **This cannot be determined precisely from the sources** — only loosely bounded between roughly 900,000 and 1,100,000 lb.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran from **8 May 1911 to 10 July 1911, about nine weeks** (r03's dated book entries — the most precise source; corroborated by r04, r07, r11; against looser "six weeks" claims in r05 and r16, which I discount as imprecise recollection against exact dated primary entries). The variable: the measuring pipette itself — the station's own (later shown defective) measure was replaced by a borrowed one, specifically the state inspector's own calibrated instrument (r09). Held constant: the herds, pasture, season, acid, bottles, patrons, and the tester (the same man, Keddie, throughout). Result: the recorded average rose from ~3.61% to 3.80% for the nine weeks, then fell back to 3.61% the day the station's own measure returned (r03). What this establishes that calculation alone would not: it is a real, contemporaneous, naturally-controlled experiment proving the reading tracked the *instrument*, not the milk, the season, the feed, or the tester's honesty or skill — since only the instrument changed and everything else was held fixed, this directly rules out theories requiring some contemporaneous change (weather, feed, dishonesty). It does **not** establish the exact size of the true shortfall (that required the 1958 burette measurement), nor does it by itself identify the *mechanism* (a short pipette specifically, as opposed to some other difference between the two instruments) — it shows correlation between an instrument swap and the reading, a fact several contemporaries (notably Dr. Kepp, r16) had the evidence in hand for but declined to pursue.

**D2.** Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream:
1. The Brant family's own private weigh book, kept independently on their own farm scale, agreed with the station's scale figures every time for decades (r09) — this shows the *weighing* (the quantity received) was accurate, which a simple skimming-and-hiding scheme would have to disturb.
2. The Association's own "station-gain" ledger entries show that, every year, *more* fat physically arrived at Larrow Green in the station's cream than had been credited to patrons on the station's test (r05, r07 Item 6, r12) — the opposite of what theft would produce; cream stolen would arrive light, and it arrived heavy every year (a point Dell Rundle himself used, r12, to retract his own theft theory).
3. State inspector Merle Strawn's repeated, contemporaneous lactometer checks — including specifically after the 1919 water accusation — never once found anything abnormal at the station (r06), an outside, disinterested, repeat-visit witness finding nothing amiss in the physical operation.

These are independent because they come from entirely separate record-keepers with no shared authorship: a patron family's private ledger, the Association's own official bookkeeping (kept by a man, Teague, who himself believed the theft theory), and an outside state regulator. Reasons 1 and 3 (and the underlying ledger data behind 2) all predate and do not depend on the 1958 pipette measurement — they would refute the theft theory even if Ivy Keddie had never climbed the ladder.

Did the bookkeeper (Teague) ever question the ledger entries that refute his own accusation? **No.** Teague himself invented the "station-gain" ledger line in 1905 and personally recorded the very entries that contradict his later theft insinuation; by his own account (r05), the same recurring ~5.25% figure became "furniture" that "stopped him from scrutinizing it further," and he never connected this data to his own theory even while presenting it to the 1925 board (r13). It was Dell Rundle (r12) — not Teague — who later examined the same entries and retracted his own (separate) theft theory.

**D3.** Yes — the sources establish that manager Selby Vose had the information available and was directly told, whether or not he engaged with its meaning. Bookkeeper Teague states plainly that Vose "knew what the station-gain account was doing every year — he personally signed the annual sheet each spring" (r05), and that Teague himself raised the issue with Vose directly in 1916 and was told to "let it alone" (r05). Against this, clerk Bertram Kyle suggests Vose's engagement may have been passive — he "read the totals and never read the lines above them... take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign" (r23) — meaning he may have seen the number annually without registering its significance. On balance, I answer **yes, he knew** (or at minimum had the figures before him yearly and was explicitly warned once), based on Teague's direct, first-person account of both practices; Kyle's account softens the *degree* of Vose's engagement but does not contradict that the information reached him.

## Section F — Theory

**The single causal theory:** The Ostrey Hollow station's fat tests ran low for 26 years (1897–1923) because the pipettes purchased new from Ammon & Sons in 1897 delivered a fixed charge of milk about 5% (16.72 c.c. against a 17.6 c.c. mark) short of their rated volume. Because the test reads fat percentage directly off a bottle-neck scale calibrated for the full, correct charge, a pipette that consistently under-delivers produces a fat reading short by the same fixed proportion every time — regardless of the milk's true richness, the season, the feed, or the tester's skill or honesty. This explains: the *size* of the error (a constant ~0.19-point/5% shortfall, matching the flat, season- and herd-independent pattern every close observer reported); why *that* size (fixed by the specific manufacturing defect in that one glass lot); why it *began* when it did (the moment the new pipettes entered service in spring 1897, replacing accurate borrowed glass); why it *ended* when it did (the moment the state inspector, finally equipped with a calibrated burette, tested this station's glass against an outside standard in June 1923 and condemned it); and why *nothing at the station or on the farms had to change* for either — the defect was purely instrumental and invisible from inside the plant, since the pipette was itself the plant's own reference standard, uncheckable against anything else the station owned. Only an external check (the burette) could catch it, and none had been applied before 1923.

**The three competing explanations:**
1. **Theft** — advanced by Vira Toland (r01) and insinuated by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague (r05, r13). Defeated by the ledgers' own "station gain" entries showing *more* fat arriving at Larrow Green than was ever credited (the opposite of what theft would produce), by the Brant family's independently-agreeing weigh book, and by the 1911 substitution experiment showing the same man's readings changed with the instrument alone.
2. **Watered milk** — advanced by manager Selby Vose (r08) and believed by Hobart Sill (r14) and some of Kepp's informants (r16). Defeated by state inspector Strawn's repeated, contemporaneous lactometer tests, which never once found added water at the station, including specifically after the 1919 accusation (r06).
3. **Wet hay / poor land** — advanced by the 1908 committee under Nyle Grigg (r07 Item 11, r15) and given fuller scientific form by Dr. Kepp (r16). Defeated by the State College's own Bulletin No. 214, whose real text found an effect four to five times too small (and the wrong season) to explain the flat gap, and by the Alder Corners cross-check, where the same men's milk tested ~0.19–0.20 points higher on the other side of the ridge, proving the milk itself was never poor.

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware? No.** Effie Loomis (r02) states the crate was never mentioned at any of the three hearing sessions; Frick's own four-item exhibit list (r13) confirms it was never entered as evidence, despite sitting in the loft directly above two of the sessions.

**What did the tester himself believe?** Publicly, nothing — Keddie explicitly refused to theorize in both his statement and his unsent letter ("I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are," r03) and marked his own 1911 entry with an unanswered question mark. Privately, the sources conflict: per Alonzo Frick (r13), Keddie told him in 1925 that he had always suspected the patrons; per Duncan Keddie (r18), his father came home in 1911 already saying "it was something in the glass" but would not commit it to paper without proof. I cannot fully reconcile these — but Duncan's sustained, close vantage as the man's own son plausibly outweighs a single hallway remark Frick recorded 21 years after hearing it, suggesting Keddie's real private suspicion may have anticipated the true (glassware) answer decades early, whatever he told Frick in a different, later moment.

## Section G — Summary

For sixty years, Hessel Bottom farmers up the Larrow Valley were paid less than their milk was worth. The Ostrey Hollow skimming station, run by tester Ansel Keddie from 1896, recorded butterfat tests about a fifth of a point lower than anywhere else in the county. Neighbors blamed watered milk, officials suspected theft, a 1908 committee blamed wet hay — all argued at a bitter 1925 hearing that dismissed Keddie without proof, and all wrong. The real cause sat forgotten in a crate in the station loft: pipettes bought in 1897 that delivered five percent less milk than marked, making every test read low, invisibly, for twenty-six years. Keddie's granddaughter Ivy found and measured them in 1958, clearing him decades after his death. It took eight separate records — family memory, ledgers, an inspector's notebooks, a rival factory's figures, the invoice itself — because no single witness held enough evidence to see past their own theory.
