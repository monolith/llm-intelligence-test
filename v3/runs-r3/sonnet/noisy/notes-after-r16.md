# Retention notes — retellings r01 through r16 (Ostrey Hollow / Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association dossier)

All sixteen retellings concern the same underlying historical matter: a decades-long discrepancy in butterfat testing at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station (Hessel Bottom, up the Larrow Valley), operated by the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (creamery at Larrow Green, office at Ashlin). Sources span oral history, institutional memoir, personal testimony, bookkeeping memoir, curatorial catalog, inspector's field notes, manager's public address, farm-bureau reminiscence, a cheesemaker's letter, a scientific bulletin, a jubilee pamphlet, a lawyer's bar-yearbook recollection, a buttermaker's retirement remarks, a farmer's covering letter reprinting an old committee report, and a veterinarian's club paper — spanning 1854 to 1972 in persons'/production dates and covering events from 1889 to 1972. The retellings do NOT agree with each other on very many material points; conflicts are flagged explicitly below, both those carried forward from r01–r08 and many new ones surfaced in r09–r16.

---

## R01 — "What the Hollow Said": Vira Toland (b. 1912), Hessel Bottom patron's daughter, oral history taped at Ostrey Hollow, 1979.

- Narrator born 1912 at top of Ostrey Hollow, in house built by her father's father; lived there her whole life except 2 years at Ashlin after first marriage.
- Her father hauled milk to the Ostrey Hollow station; nine families hauled to that station.
- Station: at the top of the Hollow where the road bends toward the ridge; low, whitewashed building, cold, smelled of milk/steam/hot iron.
- **Ansel Keddie** ran the station. Went up in 1895 (year before it opened) to prepare it; tested the first summer; still there when Vira was a girl.
- Kept a meticulous test book: ruled with his own straightedge, uniform line heights, figures "like print," written standing at a slope desk by the window. Never blotted. Vira's father said "if the rest of the world kept its accounts the way Ansel Keddie kept his there would be no lawyers in Ashlin."
- **1919** (Vira was seven): a printed letter from the Association, over the manager's signature, went to nine families saying "there was water in the cans coming down from Ostrey Hollow." Her father read it at the table with his hat on (unusual for him), read it twice, put it in the desk drawer — still there when he died; brother has it now. One woman kept hers pinned inside her trunk lid.
- The manager who signed the letter: **Selby Vose**, of Larrow Green — described by Vira as "old Gideon Vose's nephew." [CONFLICTS with r02 and r08, which both establish Selby Vose was Gideon Vose's SON, not nephew. r12's pamphlet ALSO calls him "nephew," so the "nephew" error is repeated across two oral/vernacular sources (r01, r12) against two documentary/self-reported sources (r02, r08) saying "son."]
- Gideon Vose started the Association originally; family "always thought the valley belonged to them a little."
- Local knowledge (unwritten): **Orra Keddie** (Ansel's wife) and "old Mrs. Vose" (presumably Gideon's wife) were first cousins — so the accusing letter was seen as "somebody's cousin" attacking the family.
- **Orra Keddie** was **Emmet Brant's sister**. Emmet Brant was the biggest patron on Hessel Bottom (most cows, most hay ground, most milk hauled) and was hurt worst by the low tests/letter.
- Before 1919 the Keddie and Brant families ate Sunday dinners together; after the letter, total estrangement — never spoke again, not at store or funeral; Orra would walk the long way to avoid passing her brother's lane. Both now dead, per Vira.
- After the letter, "all at once, one summer," test figures went up and stayed up — no one ever explained why, at the time, per Vira. Her father: milk tested better, but nothing had been done differently.
- A hearing was held "down at Ashlin," "that was the winter and spring after" [Vira implies the hearing followed shortly after the 1919 letter — i.e., winter 1919/spring 1920]. [CONFLICTS with r02/r03/r07/r08, all of which place the actual petition/hearing in 1925 — a roughly five-to-six-year discrepancy in Vira's memory/telling vs. the documentary record.]
- Outcome per Vira: Ansel Keddie put out (fired), an old man, after all those years; patrons paid **$3,500** (thirty-five hundred dollars), split nine ways — "did not buy a hay rake" for her father's share. [CONFLICTS with r02, r03(implicit), r05, r07, r08, which all give the official arbitration award as **$3,800**.]
- Vira's own explicit belief/accusation: "He was taking the cream. They caught him at it and they put him out, and that is why the numbers went right the summer after... it is not that he is a wicked man, it is that nobody ever came up that road and looked." She states this as fact, not rumor. [This directly conflicts with r04's eventual scientific finding of no theft, and with r03's own denial by Keddie, and with the "no cause established" arbitration outcome in r02/r07/r08.]
- Her father, more charitably, said "Ansel Keddie was an honest man who had got into something" — Vira says she "never saw the difference" between that and an accusation.
- Keddie died in the 1930s "still under it" (under suspicion). Orra outlived him a good while, carried the same stigma.
- 1958: a young woman researcher came from a college "away east," poked about the shut station building (used for fence wire and salt storage by then), asked questions, wrote everything down. She was "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed." [This is presumably Ivy Keddie of r04, though Vira doesn't name her; note the family-connection description differs — r04 identifies the researcher as Ansel Keddie's own granddaughter (daughter of his son Duncan Keddie), not simply a Brant relation; r09 and r12 both independently state Ivy and Hazel are SECOND COUSINS, not "first cousin once removed" as Vira says here — a further, three-way conflict on the exact degree of the Ivy/Hazel relationship.] Vira told her about the letter in the drawer.
- Vira ties her memory of the letter to a physical/sensory detail: it rained that day; she remembers the wet on her father's shoulders coming in.

## R02 — "Notes toward a history of the Association": Effie Loomis, Association secretary 1901–1940, written 1941 for the Association's own files.

- Effie born ~1875 (was 14 in 1889); secretary from 1901–1940 (kept the minute book in her own hand throughout that period); wrote this history in 1941, the year after laying down the minute book.
- **1889**: Larrow Valley farmers met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. **Gideon Vose** elected first president, held office until 1911 (22 years, longest of anyone).
- The 1889–1892 minute book was LOST when the office moved in 1938 — carried out with everything else, never arrived, searched everywhere, not found. Effie states this limits what she can write about the Association's first 12 years (before she became secretary in 1901).
- **1891**: main creamery built at Larrow Green on Larrow Creek (same building still standing as of writing). Before it, no common test/price — "every man's arithmetic was his own."
- **Article VII** of the by-laws, drafted personally by Gideon Vose (not a lawyer), adopted at the **1892** annual meeting (the year after the creamery opened). [CONFLICTS: r12's pamphlet instead dates Article VII itself to "1891, the year the churn was set up"; r15 (Nyle Grigg) says payment by test "came in with the 1893 meeting." This is a THREE-WAY conflict on the founding date of the test-payment rule: 1891 (r12) / 1892 (r02, presumably the most authoritative — Effie was the long-serving secretary) / 1893 (r15, Grigg's recollection).] Quoted in full:
  > "Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."
  - Effie stresses the last clause — "The test of the station where he delivers shall govern" — as the crucial, most-overlooked part: no appeal from the station's own test. She calls this "the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business." Gideon Vose, who wrote it, died in **1917** and could not be asked about it later.
- Officers in Effie's time: herself as secretary (1901–1940); **Wilbur Teague**, bookkeeper from 1905 (kept ledgers); **Hobart Sill**, buttermaker at Larrow Green (kept churn book and cream receiving book); **Selby Vose**, son of the late president [Gideon Vose], made manager in **1913**, served until **1938**. Effie notes none of the four of them ever personally went to the Ostrey Hollow station on a working day to watch the work — including herself.
- Ostrey Hollow skimming station: 7 miles up the valley from Larrow Green, at the head of the Hollow. Took in milk from farms up there, separated it, sent cream down to Larrow Green, returned skim to patrons. Best milk came from Hessel Bottom (wet meadow below the hamlet); **seven farms** had rights on Hessel Bottom [CONFLICTS with r09's account (Roy Brant, a Hessel Bottom farmer himself) and with the 1908 committee report itself as reprinted in r15, BOTH of which say **six farms** cut hay/pastured stock on Hessel Bottom. Two farm-count figures: seven (r02) vs. six (r09, r15/1908 report).]; largest was **Emmet Brant's** — station's largest patron every year on record.
- **January 1925**: Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned the Association, claiming their milk had never tested as well as neighbors' and they'd been underpaid a long time; asked to be made whole.
- **Board of arbitration** sat at **Ashlin**, under **Alonzo Frick**, attorney, from **17 March to 4 May 1925**. Three explanations were argued before the board; Effie declines to detail them ("they are in the minutes and the minutes are in the safe"); none of the three was accepted. [r13, Frick's own recollection, later names the three explanations explicitly: water (Vose), theft (insinuated by Teague), hay (the 1908 report) — filling in what Effie declined to say.]
- The board's award, as Effie personally recorded it:
  > "The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account."
  - Effie notes the apparent contradiction of "finds no cause established" immediately followed by an award of $3,800 — she says it "was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once."
- **Quantities from bookkeeper Wilbur Teague's ledgers** (Effie vouches only for the copying, not "the cows"): over the 25 years the patrons' claim covered, the Ostrey Hollow station received **40,000,000 lb of milk** total — average **1,600,000 lb/year**. Butterfat price averaged **25 cents/lb** over the period.
- Two gaps Effie flags explicitly in the record: (1) the lost 1889–1892 minute book (see above); (2) **"the crate"** — a crate of measures condemned by the state inspector in **June 1923**, replaced with new ones — was NEVER mentioned at all in any of the three arbitration hearing sessions, by patrons, officers, or the board — despite the board's language about "the books and appliances of the station." Effie: "the interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying." [r13's exhibit list from Frick himself, the arbitration chairman, independently CONFIRMS this: the crate/pipettes are absent from his 4-item exhibit list.]

## R03 — Ansel Keddie's own statement and unsent letter, spring 1925.

Two documents: (I) his 9-sentence oral statement to the board of arbitration at Ashlin, 17 March 1925; (II) an unsent letter written later that spring, found among his papers after his death.

**I. Statement (17 March 1925), quoted in full:**
> "I have kept the test book of this station in my own hand since the station opened, and there is not a figure in it that I did not put there. I have set down the date, the patron, the pounds, the test and the fat, and where I have made an error I have drawn a line through it and put my letters against the line, and I have never written a figure over a figure. I have made the tests in the same order and at the same hour of every working day of every season. I have heard what the patrons say of me and I have heard what the officers say of me. Neither of them has come up that road on a working morning and stood at my elbow and watched the work done. I am asked to account for figures, and the figures are what I read and what I wrote and nothing besides. I am an old man and I have no other trade. I have tested as I was taught and as the state man saw me do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, and I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are. I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself."

- [NEW, from r13 (Alonzo Frick): Frick reports that privately, in the corridor at Ashlin, Keddie told him he had always thought the PATRONS were at fault and would not say so in the room — meaning Keddie's public "there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself" posture concealed a private suspicion directed at the patrons themselves, not a belief in his own innocence-plus-no-theory. Frick: "it has been said since that he went to his grave accusing nobody, which is true, and that he suspected nobody, which is not."]

**II. Unsent letter, spring 1925 (found among his papers):**
- Explains his method in detail: day began 5:30 a.m.; wagons came up the Hollow in the dark most of the year; every can weighed and recorded before milk went anywhere. Scale was tested true by the state inspector; in 29 seasons no patron ever disputed a weight and was proven right. [r09, Roy Brant's account of his father Emmet Brant's own farm weigh book, independently corroborates this: the Brant book's own scale weights agreed with the station's weights every single time across decades — "whatever was wrong at that station... it was not the weighing."]
- Sample taken from each patron's milk at weighing, into the patron's own numbered jar; jar made up over 10 days before testing. On test morning, jar warmed to blood-warm in warm water, poured repeatedly to mix cream through. "A sample that is not warmed and mixed is worth nothing and a boy could tell you so."
- Test procedure ("the charge"): milk drawn to a mark on the measure stem, tip wiped, blown into the patron's numbered bottle; acid poured down the side, bottle turned dark; four bottles in the hand tester, weighted against each other, cranked at the taught speed, no faster; fat read off the neck's column in per cent against rulings with a pair of dividers; figure written down.
- Glass (testing outfit) borrowed from Ashlin creamery starting **1896**, used until the station's own outfit arrived "the spring following" (i.e., 1897). [NOTE: this narrative line says "Ashlin creamery," but the first-leaf quotation immediately below says the glass was "borrowed of Larrow Green" — an internal inconsistency already present in the r01–r08 notes; likely "Ashlin creamery" is a slip for "Larrow Green creamery" (Ashlin has only the Association's office, not a creamery). r14 (Hobart Sill) now clarifies and specifies further: the loaned glass was Sill's OWN personal bench glassware from the Larrow Green creamery, made up by him and sent up by wagon because the ordered outfit had not yet arrived when the station was ready to open in May 1896; it was returned to him the following spring (1897) with one piece chipped.]
- Kept the book in five columns: date, patron, pounds, test, fat. Corrections crossed and initialed, never overwritten. Tested **29 seasons, 1896 through 1924**. Did **not** test in the 1925 season.
- First leaf of the book, quoted:
  > "Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie."
- Summary/averages leaf made up in **1922**, quoted:
  > "Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80."
- The **1911 anomaly** — his account: station's own measure (pipette) broke **8 May 1911**; made shift with a borrowed measure; from that morning tests came up and stood at 3.80; on **10 July 1911** the station's own measure was back in use and tests dropped back down "the same day." Two entries quoted exactly:
  > "May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure."
  > "July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."
  - He says the question mark is his own, standing unanswered for 14 years (i.e., 1911 to 1925); he refuses to speculate.
  - [NEW, from r09 (Roy Brant): specifies whose measure was borrowed in 1911 — "the state man left his own measure that June and that is what they used," i.e., the state dairy inspector's own calibrated instrument was used as the temporary substitute. This strengthens the calibration explanation: a presumably-accurate state instrument produced the higher (~3.80, closer to true) readings, while the station's own defective pipette produced the persistently low (3.61) readings. r06 (Merle Strawn, the inspector) does not mention lending his own measure in his 1911 circuit notes, an omission worth flagging.]
- He stresses the gap was constant across seasons — present in July as in February, flush grass or dry hay, wet year or dry — never varying with weather/feed. [r09 independently corroborates: "it was seventeen hundredths in July and it was seventeen hundredths in February... it did not care whether the cows were on the meadow or on hay in the barn."]
- **1924** average of the station: **3.80** — he wrote it "with the same pen and same measure of care as all the others." [CORROBORATED by r06 (Strawn's 1924 inspection note: "Their book gives the year at 3.80"). CONFLICTS with r08 (Vose's public claim of 3.90 for 1924).]
- History of complaints, per Keddie: complaints "began in 1902," when Larrow Green first said Ostrey Hollow milk didn't test with the rest of the valley. **1908**: a committee came up, sampled the herds through the winter, reported the wet hay of the Bottom thinned the cream, remedy lies with the land (NOT an accusation of fraud). **1919**: the Association's circular went to nine families saying there was water in their cans. He says he holds no charge against anyone who signed that letter but knows what came into the station each morning; notes "nine kitchens in this Hollow have had that letter in a drawer ever since" [corroborates r01's drawer detail].
- Confirms his wife **Orra is Emmet Brant's sister**; Emmet was his largest patron; Ansel weighed and tested every pound of Emmet's milk himself; the two men have not spoken since the circular [corroborates r01]. [r09 (Roy Brant, Emmet's son) gives a different nuance on WHY the estrangement happened: not the water accusation itself, but that Emmet held it against Keddie personally that "the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not" — i.e., Emmet was aggrieved by Keddie's public silence/failure to defend the patrons, not simply by association with the accusation. Also, r09 clarifies Orra and Emmet were FULL brother and sister, "same mother and same father," explicitly rebutting any story involving a stepmother.]
- He was **put out (dismissed) on 30 April 1925** — notes the board "found no cause established" and dismissed him anyway on the last day of the month, and says he cannot reconcile those two facts in his mind. Ends: "I have tested as I was taught. I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are." [r13, Frick, corroborates and adds: the dismissal came four days before the board's actual award (4 May 1925), and there was never any finding against Keddie by the board at all — the Association acted unilaterally and separately while the board was still sitting.]

## R04 — "What the Crate Held": Ivy Keddie's talk to the Ordell County Historical Society, Ashlin, 1959 (investigation conducted 1958).

- **Ivy Keddie** is Ansel Keddie's granddaughter — daughter of **Duncan Keddie** (Ansel's son), who taught school at Ashlin. She explicitly disclaims that her findings depend on family loyalty: "nothing I am going to show you tonight depends in the smallest degree on my opinion of my grandfather. It depends on nine pieces of glass and a burette."
- Five record sets consulted, none of which had ever been in one room together before "last year" [1958]:
  1. The **station test book** — in the Association's office since 1925; came to the Historical Society "this year."
  2. The **Brant weigh book** — kept at Hessel Bottom from 1897 onward; lent to her by **Roy Brant**, Emmet Brant's son, who has kept it up himself since his father died. [CONFLICTS with r09 itself (Roy Brant's own account), which states his father began the book "from the first of January, 1898" — a one-year discrepancy against both this r04 reference ("from 1897 onward") and r07 Item 4 / r13's exhibit list (both: "begun 1 January 1897"). So the weigh book's own author's son (Roy) gives a different start year than two independent documentary/catalog sources.]
  3. **Alder Corners Cheese Company** test figures (over Redlow Ridge, Vessey County) — copied out for her by **Rosalie Cudd**, who runs that factory and is **Jerome Cudd's niece**; the original book has never left Vessey County; she has never seen it herself. [CONFLICTS with r10 (Rosalie Cudd's own letter), which states explicitly and repeatedly that Jerome Cudd was her FATHER, not her uncle — "My father, Jerome Cudd, built this factory..." This also conflicts with r12 (Dell Rundle's pamphlet), which independently states "It is Jerome Cudd's daughter, Rosalie Cudd, who makes the cheese at Alder Corners now." So TWO sources (r10's own first-person letter, r12's pamphlet) say "daughter" against ONE source (r04, Ivy Keddie's own talk) saying "niece" — meaning Ivy Keddie appears to have gotten her own correspondent's family relationship wrong, or was misquoted/misremembered.]
  4. **Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks** (state dairy inspector for the county, from 1908) — held at her own college library at Farland since 1947; "eleven years in which nobody put a question to them" [1947+11=1958]. [Compare r06's own account of his career start: "the Ordell circuit from 1907" — a one-year discrepancy with Ivy Keddie's description of the notebooks as "from 1908."]
  5. The Association's own annual ledgers — present at the talk.
- **February [1958]**: she went up to the station building (used for storage since closing), found — in the loft over the old intake room, under a tarpaulin — a wooden crate stenciled "**AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET**." Carried up there in **June 1923**. Contained, packed in straw, the station's condemned milk-test pipettes. [CONFLICTS with r07's Item 5 provenance note, which says the crate was found by Ivy Keddie "in the Association office at Ashlin" — a location discrepancy between her own narrative (station building loft) and the catalog's provenance note (Ashlin office).]
- Original outfit: **6 dozen (72)** pipettes bought new in **1897**, invoice totaling **$77.93** for the whole outfit (pipettes, bottles, acid measure, hand tester, freight). Of these, **nine** survived unbroken in the crate. [CONFLICTS with r11 (Bulletin 471's own text, by Prof. Vaught), which states "five dozen" (60) pipettes were purchased for the same 1897 lot — a discrepancy of one dozen (12 pipettes) between the bulletin's own introduction and the invoice quoted in r04/r07 Item 2. Also CONFLICTS with r12's footnote, which states "eleven pipettes" — not nine — were found still in existence in the crate.]
- **9 March [1958]**: she measured the nine pipettes at Farland against a calibrated burette. Her sheet, quoted:
  > "Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for."
  - [CONFLICTS with r11's own quoted measurement summary: Vaught's bulletin text states the mean delivery of the same nine pipettes was **16.27 c.c.** (not 16.72 — an apparent digit transposition between the two retellings of the same underlying data) and computes the ratio as 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244 (92.44%), not 0.95 (95%).]
- The math: pipettes delivered only 95% of their blown/rated volume (16.72 vs 17.6 c.c.), so every test made with that glass read 95% of the true value.
- Re-quotes her grandfather's 1922 summary leaf (identical to r03's quote: 1896=3.80; 1897 part year; 1898=3.61; 3.61 ± 0.03 every year since except nine weeks of 1911 at 3.80).
- Corrected math: recorded average 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **true average 3.80**. Gap of **0.19** points is **5% of the true value (3.80)**, and **5.25% of the recorded/credited value (3.61-based bookkeeping)** — she stresses both percentages are individually correct but not interchangeable, since "5%" and "5.25%" describe different denominators (true fat delivered vs. fat credited). [CONFLICTS with r11's own body-text math, which — using its own 92.44% ratio — computes a true average of **3.905** (not 3.80), a shortfall of **0.295** (not 0.19). Vaught's bulletin even flags this itself: "It should be said that this figure is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the glassware" — an internal admission of inconsistency within the bulletin. Yet the bulletin's own final CONCLUSION paragraph (quoted identically in r04, r07 Item 15, and r11 itself) reverts to the "one part in twenty" (5%)/76,000 lb figures that match r04's math, not the bulletin's own body-text math — i.e., r11 is internally self-contradictory between its method/calculation sections and its own concluding paragraph.]
- Over **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922**: station took in **40,000,000 lb** of milk, averaging 1,600,000 lb/year [matches r02]. At recorded 3.61%, patrons were credited **1,444,000 lb** of fat; at true 3.80%, they had actually delivered **1,520,000 lb**. Difference: **76,000 lb of butterfat**. At $0.25/lb average price, unpaid value = **$19,000**. The 1925 arbitration award of **$3,800** was one-fifth of that true value. [CONFLICTS: r11's own body-text arithmetic instead yields **118,000 lb** of uncredited butterfat (using its own 92.44%/3.905 figures). r13 (Frick) states the patrons' OWN claimed loss, as put to the arbitration board in 1925, was **67,000 lb** of butterfat — a third, different figure (though this may be a different quantity: the patrons' contemporary claim vs. the 1958 retrospective calculation). r12's pamphlet gives the patrons' claimed dollar loss as **"nineteen thousand six hundred dollars" ($19,600)** — close to but not identical to r04's calculated $19,000.]
- Because the error was proportional, it hit richer milk hardest: a herd recorded at 4.56 was short 0.24 points; a herd recorded at 3.42 was short 0.18 points. [Note: 4.56 matches Selby Vose's own herd test in r08; 3.42 matches the Brant weigh-book leaf for 1910 in r07's Item 4 and r09's own quoted 1910 leaf.]
- **1911 anomaly re-explained**: same patrons, herds, pasture, season, acid, bottles, same tester — only the measure changed (borrowed from Larrow Green with the cream wagon) — and the average went to 3.80, then back to 3.61 the day the station's own (defective) measure returned. She says this is the only surviving natural experiment/proof and "no explanation... survives those nine weeks except the one I have given you." [r09 independently reports the SAME nine-week 1911 anomaly from the patron side — "Through those nine weeks our tests came up, all of us, every farm on the Bottom" — strong cross-corroboration from an entirely independent farm ledger.]
- Addresses the "wet hay" theory (the kindest of the old explanations, "offered in good faith"): the State Agricultural College tested it in 1911, **Bulletin No. 214**, quoted:
  > "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."
  - She notes this effect (max 0.05, winter only) is four times too small and the wrong seasonal shape to explain a flat, year-round 0.19 gap. [CONFLICTS: two later, independent sources — r15 (Nyle Grigg's 1919 covering letter) and r16 (Dr. Roland Kepp's 1926 veterinary paper) — both cite Bulletin No. 214's finding as "two tenths" (0.20) of a point, not "five hundredths" (0.05) — a four-fold misquotation of the actual bulletin text, and one that (not coincidentally) happens to support their own "it's the land/hay" arguments. The true bulletin text is independently and identically quoted three times (r04, r07 Item 12, r11), so the "two tenths" figure used by Grigg and Kepp is demonstrably a shared error/exaggeration circulating in valley advocacy, not a documentary variant.]
- Cross-check: at Alder Corners factory, the same Hessel Bottom men's milk (14 overlapping seasons) tested ~0.19 points HIGHER there than at home — matching the calculated calibration gap almost exactly. [r10, Rosalie Cudd's own letter, gives the primary source for this: 16 seasons (1915–1930) of data, all clustering 3.78–3.82, averaging ~3.80 — closely matching r04's calculated "true" average of 3.80, and directly contradicting the "watered milk" and "wet hay" theories via an entirely independent measuring instrument on the other side of the ridge.]
- **Bulletin No. 471**, issued October [1958], closing paragraph (Professor Vaught's wording, per Ivy):
  > "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."
- Timeline she closes on: glass condemned 1923, cause found 1958 = 35 years. Grandfather dismissed 1925 = 33 years before vindication. He died in **1934**, so had been dead **24 years** when the exonerating bulletin was issued (1958). "I have no remedy to propose. I have only the crate."

## R05 — "The Books of the Association," Wilbur Teague, retired bookkeeper (1905–1945), memoir chapter written at Ashlin, winter 1948.

- Teague kept the Association's books **1905–1945** (40 years); wrote this memoir chapter 3 years after retiring (i.e., 1948).
- Office at Ashlin: one room over a harness shop, cold at north end, too warm at south end; worked at a sloped desk with a brass rail.
- The Association in his time = three parts: creamery at Larrow Green, office at Ashlin, skimming station at Ostrey Hollow (7 miles up valley). **Selby Vose** was manager over the whole; **Effie Loomis** was secretary; Teague kept the ledger.
- Station's basic operation (as he explains it): patrons hauled milk to the station instead of driving 7 miles to Larrow Green; milk weighed, sampled, tested for butterfat; patron paid on pounds+test; station separated milk, sent cream to Larrow Green, returned skim to farmer.
- **The "station-gain" line**: on one side of the station account, butterfat CREDITED to patrons per the station's own test; on the other side, butterfat ACTUALLY RECEIVED at Larrow Green in the station's cream (weighed/tested into the vats there). The second figure was always larger. The difference ("station gain") was carried to the Association's general account. Teague states he personally INVENTED this bookkeeping line himself when he started in 1905 — denies it was any "old dodge of the founders." [r14 (Hobart Sill, buttermaker) independently pushes this phenomenon's observed history back BEFORE 1905: Sill says he personally weighed every incoming can of Hollow cream himself from 1893 onward and always found it heavier in fat than the station's own paperwork claimed — i.e., the underlying "station gain" physical reality long predates Teague's 1905 formal ledger line; Teague only formalized the bookkeeping of something that had apparently always been true.]
- Example entry he quotes in full (for **1898**):
  > "Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account."

[This 5.25% figure matches Ivy Keddie's/r04's math of "5.25% of the fat credited" — i.e., this is independent ledger corroboration of the same calibration-driven gap, though Teague at the time read it as suspicious surplus rather than a measurement artifact. r12's pamphlet quotes this exact same 1898 example WORD FOR WORD (also matching r07 Item 6), and r12's author uses this very entry, once he sees it, to retract his own pamphlet's theft accusation — see r12 below.]

- Teague explicitly told the **1925 board of arbitration**, and repeats here, his suspicion of deliberate theft: "Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons... a business which is arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken from it, and that no set of books ever made can prevent it. ## R11 — Bulletin No. 471, State Agricultural College at Farland, October 1958 — introduction and method sections, by Professor Leland Vaught.

- Written for extension agents and county workers, explaining WHY the Ostrey Hollow station's tests ran low for fifty years: "It is answered here by reference to a piece of glass."
- Core technical point: "the milk pipette is the one instrument in the butterfat test which the plant cannot check against anything else, and it is the instrument on which every figure in the plant depends."
- Explains the test procedure: measured charge of milk drawn into a pipette, delivered into a test bottle; sulfuric acid dissolves everything but the fat; bottle spun in a hand tester; fat rises into the graduated neck; operator reads the percentage DIRECTLY off the neck, trusting that the charge volume is fixed and correct. Standard charge in the state (and on this glassware): **17.6 cubic centimeters**.
- If the pipette under-delivers, the reading is low "in the exact proportion by which the charge was short." Three consequences stressed: (1) the error is **proportional, not additive** — it removes a fixed fraction, so rich milk loses more absolutely than poor milk, and a station's herds still rank correctly against EACH OTHER even though the whole station reads wrong against the outside world; (2) the error is **precise/consistent** — a short pipette delivers the same wrong amount every time, for decades, looking like excellent, careful work; (3) the error is **invisible from inside the plant** — every other component (scales, tester speed, acid strength, bottle graduation) can be checked against an external standard, but the pipette itself IS the standard the plant measures with, so it can only be caught by an external burette, and burettes reached field inspectors very late in this history.
- **Material examined**: of the lot bought for Ostrey Hollow in 1897, "**five dozen**" pipettes were purchased [CONFLICTS with r04's/r07 Item 2's "6 doz. (72)" per the original invoice — a discrepancy of 12 pipettes]; **nine** were recovered from the condemned crate, unbroken and legible. Stems blown **17.6 c.c.**; delivered against a calibrated burette, five runs each, mean delivery of the nine = **16.27 c.c.** [CONFLICTS with r04's/r07 Item 5's "16.72 c.c." for the same measurement — an apparent digit transposition between sources.]
- States the station's own glass "went into use on the first of May, 1898" [CONFLICTS with r03's "the spring following" 1896 (1897) and r07 Item 2's invoice-based "1 May 1897" — a one-year discrepancy], remaining in use until condemned in **June 1923**.
- Method for measuring: fill pipette to its mark with room-temperature water, wipe tip, deliver in the ordinary working manner into a certified burette; five deliveries per pipette, averaged; "occupies an afternoon" and needs no apparatus a creamery doesn't own besides the burette.
- **The 1911 interval** (Section IV): for nine weeks in summer 1911 the station's pipette was broken and a borrowed measure used; all other variables held constant; the station's average rose from 3.61 to 3.80 and returned to 3.61 the day the own measure came back — "recorded contemporaneously, in the operator's hand, by a man who did not know what he was recording and set a question mark against it." Calls it an experiment that "ran itself" and "lay unread for forty-seven years" (i.e., 1911 to 1958).
- **Magnitude** (Section V): ratio of delivered to marked capacity = 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = **0.9244**. Recorded average 3.61; corresponding "true" average = 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = **3.905**, a shortfall of **0.295** points [CONFLICTS with r04's calculated 3.80 true average / 0.19 shortfall, using r04's own 16.72/17.6=0.95 ratio]. Applied to the 40,000,000 lb received over 25 years, this yields **118,000 lb** of uncredited butterfat [CONFLICTS with r04's 76,000 lb figure]. Vaught himself flags the inconsistency: "It should be said that this figure is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the glassware" — an explicit, unresolved admission of internal inconsistency within the bulletin's own math.
- States the ledger "station gain" is "five and a quarter percent of the butterfat actually delivered" [this 5.25% figure matches r04's/r05's/r07 Item 6's/r12's figure, NOT the bulletin's own body-text 0.9244/118,000 lb calculation — a further internal inconsistency within r11 itself].
- **Bulletin No. 214** reproduced identically to r04/r07 Item 12 ("not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and nothing... in summer pasture") — confirming the TRUE bulletin text against Grigg's (r15) and Kepp's (r16) later "two tenths" misquotations.
- **Conclusion** (Section VII), quoted, IDENTICAL to r04's/r07 Item 15's closing paragraph:
  > "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."
  - This concluding paragraph's own numbers ("one part in twenty" = 5%; "seventy-six thousand pounds") FLATLY CONTRADICT the bulletin's own body-text calculations just above (92.44%/0.9244 ratio; 118,000 lb) — the single most significant internal (self-)contradiction found across all sixteen retellings, present within one single document.

- **The 1908 report itself**, reproduced in full: committee "appointed in January last" [i.e., January 1908]; describes Hessel Bottom (six farms, wet meadow, sedge/rush/coarse grasses, hay cut late and fed as main winter dependence); committee visited all six farms (some three or four times), drew and sealed their own samples, watched milking/hauling/weighing/testing, tried the station scales against a known weight AND against the patrons' own scales, checked water supply at each barn; observed herd condition (thin in the flank, slow to condition in spring, rough coats later in season); concluded: "We report that we found no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter, and we wish that finding to be printed in the same type as the rest," and that the wet hay thins the cream, remedy lying with the land not the station. - Confirms "six farms" cut hay/pastured on Hessel Bottom [corroborates r09's "six farms," conflicting with r02's "seven farms"].
- Closing line notes the report "was received and accepted, and the question was closed by it for eleven years — until this season" [i.e., 1908 to 1919].

## R16 — "The bog meadows and the milk," Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S., paper read before the Ordell County Farm Club, 1926 (with discussion appended).

- Kepp: veterinary surgeon, thirty years' practice, of Ashlin. Frames the Hollow question as one of "soil, herbage, and the mineral economy of the cow," to be "treated as such" rather than by opinion/grumbling/naming names.
- Theory: wet, undrained bottomland is acid, lime- and phosphate-poor soil; produces coarse, late-cut sedge/rush hay lacking minerals; a milking cow fed on such hay "robs her own frame" to make good the shortfall, sacrificing fat first (the "dearest thing the cow makes") while milk VOLUME is unaffected or even generous — hence Hessel Bottom cows give plenty of milk but poorer fat.
- General county-wide claim (not just Hessel Bottom): wet-bottom herds test "poorer in fat by **a fifth of a point** [0.20]" compared to hill herds across the whole county, independent of breed, farmer, or barn — "it depends on the ground."
- Hessel Bottom's specific cited gap: "**twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21**" [matches Vose's r08 figure of a 0.21 "rise" in 1924, though used here to describe the ongoing GAP rather than an improvement — both figures appear in 1926 sources, suggesting a shared, possibly-inflated contemporary local number].
- Cites Bulletin No. 214's finding as "**two tenths**" depression in winter feeding [the SAME misquotation as r15/Grigg's — see conflict noted there — versus the bulletin's true "five hundredths" quoted verbatim in r04/r07 Item 12/r11]. Argues his own 0.20 fieldwork figure, the Hollow's specific 0.21, and the College's (misquoted) "two tenths" "agree so nearly that I do not know what more a reasonable man requires" — his case actually rests on a shared, propagated misquotation.
- Fat price cited: "**twenty-seven cents** the pound through these years" [matches r14's/Sill's figure, conflicting with r02's/r09's ~24–25 cents].
- **The 1911 objection**, addressed and dismissed: an unnamed "gentleman" had noted that in summer 1911 testing was "broken off for **six weeks**" [matches r05's/Teague's "six weeks," not the more precise ~nine weeks of r03/r04/r11] with a borrowed apparatus, and that figures were higher during that window. Kepp dismisses this as insufficient: "Six weeks in the flush of the season... I am astonished that anybody should found an argument on six weeks of summer figures. I could produce you six weeks in any year that would prove anything you liked." [This rebuttal is logically weak/flawed: it ignores the documented year-round consistency of the gap (Keddie, r03; Brant, r09) and mischaracterizes the anomaly as merely "six weeks of summer" rather than a controlled substitution experiment against the SAME season's other weeks.]
- **Remedy** proposed: drain the bottom, open/cut ditches, lime the ground repeatedly, reseed, keep stock off it in spring wet, never feed the hay alone — feed it with a mixed ration and mineral supplement "as the College directs."
- **Discussion** (floor Q&A, appended by the club secretary):
  - A member asks whether the difference might be in the COWS rather than the land (poorer men, poorer stock). Kepp rejects this: some of the county's best-bred cattle stand on wet land; some indifferent cattle on hills test well; "a man may improve his herd for twenty years and never get his fat up while his ground is sour."
  - A member asks whether the trouble might lie in the STATION rather than the bottom, "as had been said in the valley for years." Kepp declines to engage: "he had no interest in that quarrel and no competence in it; that he had never in his life read a test and would not know how" — but nonetheless dismisses the idea by appeal to the (mutually reinforcing, but ultimately erroneous/misquoted) convergence of soil/herbage/herd-condition/College-trial evidence, concluding "it is not good sense to go looking for a second cause in a building." [A textbook case of an expert's confident non-engagement with the one line of inquiry — the testing apparatus itself — that eventually (1958) proved to be the actual cause.]
  - Chairman thanks Kepp; paper ordered printed for members.

## Cross-cutting conflicts and oddities (consolidated across ALL SIXTEEN retellings, R01–R16)

1. **Award amount**: $3,500 (r01, Vira's oral memory) vs. **$3,800** (r02, r03 implicitly consistent, r05, r07 Item 7, r08, r13 — the consistent documentary figure).
2. **Timing of the hearing**: Vira (r01) implies the hearing followed almost immediately after the 1919 letter (winter 1919/spring 1920); the documentary record (r02, r03, r07, r08, r13) places the actual petition/hearing in **1925**, roughly 5–6 years later — Vira's oral account appears to have compressed/conflated the timeline.
3. **Cause of the low tests** — at least FIVE distinct theories are on record: (a) Keddie stole cream (Vira, r01; Teague's insinuation, r05/r13); (b) patrons watered their milk (Vose, r08; Sill, r14; and indirectly Kepp's informants, r16); (c) the wet hay/land itself (1908 committee under Grigg, r07 Item 11/r09/r15; and Dr. Kepp's full mineral-deficiency theory, r16); (d) Keddie privately suspected the PATRONS of some fault, never voiced publicly (revealed only in r13, via Frick's private conversation with Keddie); (e) the TRUE, scientifically-established cause: a proportional calibration defect in the station's milk pipettes (only 92–95% of rated delivery), established by Ivy Keddie's 1958 investigation and Bulletin No. 471 (r04, r07 Items 14–15, r11) — explicitly stating "No milk was watered... and no cream was stolen." Keddie himself (r03) denies both theft and any explanation. This is the central resolution/twist of the whole set, though even the "definitive" bulletin (r11) contains its own significant internal numerical inconsistencies (see #16 below).
4. **Selby Vose's relation to Gideon Vose**: "nephew" (r01, oral/rumor; r12, pamphlet/oral tradition) vs. **"son"** (r02 and r08, both authoritative/documentary and self-reported) — two vernacular sources vs. two documentary/first-person sources.
5. **Total lifetime milk intake at the station**: 40,000,000 lb (r02; r04's calculation; r07's Item 6 ledger citation) vs. **44,000,000 lb** (r05, Teague's memoir; ALSO independently stated by r12, Rundle's pamphlet — now a two-source figure, not just an isolated Teague error as first thought).
