# Retention notes — retellings r01 through r08 (Ostrey Hollow / Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association dossier)

All eight retellings concern the same underlying historical matter: a decades-long discrepancy in butterfat testing at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station (Hessel Bottom, up the Larrow Valley), operated by the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (creamery at Larrow Green, office at Ashlin). Sources span oral history, institutional memoir, personal testimony, bookkeeping memoir, curatorial catalog, inspector's field notes, and a manager's public address — spanning 1896 to 1972 in production date and 1889 to 1972 in covered events. The retellings do NOT agree with each other on several material points; conflicts are flagged explicitly below.

---

## R01 — "What the Hollow Said": Vira Toland (b. 1912), Hessel Bottom patron's daughter, oral history taped at Ostrey Hollow, 1979.

- Narrator born 1912 at top of Ostrey Hollow, in house built by her father's father; lived there her whole life except 2 years at Ashlin after first marriage.
- Her father hauled milk to the Ostrey Hollow station; nine families hauled to that station.
- Station: at the top of the Hollow where the road bends toward the ridge; low, whitewashed building, cold, smelled of milk/steam/hot iron.
- **Ansel Keddie** ran the station. Went up in 1895 (year before it opened) to prepare it; tested the first summer; still there when Vira was a girl.
- Kept a meticulous test book: ruled with his own straightedge, uniform line heights, figures "like print," written standing at a slope desk by the window. Never blotted. Vira's father said "if the rest of the world kept its accounts the way Ansel Keddie kept his there would be no lawyers in Ashlin."
- **1919** (Vira was seven): a printed letter from the Association, over the manager's signature, went to nine families saying "there was water in the cans coming down from Ostrey Hollow." Her father read it at the table with his hat on (unusual for him), read it twice, put it in the desk drawer — still there when he died; brother has it now. One woman kept hers pinned inside her trunk lid.
- The manager who signed the letter: **Selby Vose**, of Larrow Green — described by Vira as "old Gideon Vose's nephew." [CONFLICTS with r02 and r08, which both establish Selby Vose was Gideon Vose's SON, not nephew.]
- Gideon Vose started the Association originally; family "always thought the valley belonged to them a little."
- Local knowledge (unwritten): **Orra Keddie** (Ansel's wife) and "old Mrs. Vose" (presumably Gideon's wife) were first cousins — so the accusing letter was seen as "somebody's cousin" attacking the family.
- **Orra Keddie** was **Emmet Brant's sister**. Emmet Brant was the biggest patron on Hessel Bottom (most cows, most hay ground, most milk hauled) and was hurt worst by the low tests/letter.
- Before 1919 the Keddie and Brant families ate Sunday dinners together; after the letter, total estrangement — never spoke again, not at store or funeral; Orra would walk the long way to avoid passing her brother's lane. Both now dead, per Vira.
- After the letter, "all at once, one summer," test figures went up and stayed up — no one ever explained why, at the time, per Vira. Her father: milk tested better, but nothing had been done differently.
- A hearing was held "down at Ashlin," "that was the winter and spring after" [Vira implies the hearing followed shortly after the 1919 letter — i.e., winter 1919/spring 1920]. [CONFLICTS with r02/r03/r07/r08, all of which place the actual petition/hearing in 1925 — a roughly five-to-six-year discrepancy in Vira's memory/telling vs. the documentary record.]
- Outcome per Vira: Ansel Keddie put out (fired), an old man, after all those years; patrons paid **$3,500** (thirty-five hundred dollars), split nine ways — "did not buy a hay rake" for her father's share. [CONFLICTS with r02, r03(implicit), r05, r07, r08, which all give the official arbitration award as **$3,800**.]
- Vira's own explicit belief/accusation: "He was taking the cream. They caught him at it and they put him out, and that is why the numbers went right the summer after... it is not that he is a wicked man, it is that nobody ever came up that road and looked." She states this as fact, not rumor. [This directly conflicts with r04's eventual scientific finding of no theft, and with r03's own denial by Keddie, and with the "no cause established" arbitration outcome in r02/r07/r08.]
- Her father, more charitably, said "Ansel Keddie was an honest man who had got into something" — Vira says she "never saw the difference" between that and an accusation.
- Keddie died in the 1930s "still under it" (under suspicion). Orra outlived him a good while, carried the same stigma.
- 1958: a young woman researcher came from a college "away east," poked about the shut station building (used for fence wire and salt storage by then), asked questions, wrote everything down. She was "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed." [This is presumably Ivy Keddie of r04, though Vira doesn't name her; note the family-connection description differs — r04 identifies the researcher as Ansel Keddie's own granddaughter (daughter of his son Duncan Keddie), not simply a Brant relation. Possible conflict/ambiguity about how the visiting researcher is related to the families.] Vira told her about the letter in the drawer.
- Vira ties her memory of the letter to a physical/sensory detail: it rained that day; she remembers the wet on her father's shoulders coming in.

## R02 — "Notes toward a history of the Association": Effie Loomis, Association secretary 1901–1940, written 1941 for the Association's own files.

- Effie born ~1875 (was 14 in 1889); secretary from 1901–1940 (kept the minute book in her own hand throughout that period); wrote this history in 1941, the year after laying down the minute book.
- **1889**: Larrow Valley farmers met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. **Gideon Vose** elected first president, held office until 1911 (22 years, longest of anyone).
- The 1889–1892 minute book was LOST when the office moved in 1938 — carried out with everything else, never arrived, searched everywhere, not found. Effie states this limits what she can write about the Association's first 12 years (before she became secretary in 1901).
- **1891**: main creamery built at Larrow Green on Larrow Creek (same building still standing as of writing). Before it, no common test/price — "every man's arithmetic was his own."
- **Article VII** of the by-laws, drafted personally by Gideon Vose (not a lawyer), adopted at the **1892** annual meeting (the year after the creamery opened). Quoted in full:
  > "Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."
  - Effie stresses the last clause — "The test of the station where he delivers shall govern" — as the crucial, most-overlooked part: no appeal from the station's own test. She calls this "the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business." Gideon Vose, who wrote it, died in **1917** and could not be asked about it later.
- Officers in Effie's time: herself as secretary (1901–1940); **Wilbur Teague**, bookkeeper from 1905 (kept ledgers); **Hobart Sill**, buttermaker at Larrow Green (kept churn book and cream receiving book); **Selby Vose**, son of the late president [Gideon Vose], made manager in **1913**, served until **1938**. Effie notes none of the four of them ever personally went to the Ostrey Hollow station on a working day to watch the work — including herself.
- Ostrey Hollow skimming station: 7 miles up the valley from Larrow Green, at the head of the Hollow. Took in milk from farms up there, separated it, sent cream down to Larrow Green, returned skim to patrons. Best milk came from Hessel Bottom (wet meadow below the hamlet); seven farms had rights on Hessel Bottom; largest was **Emmet Brant's** — station's largest patron every year on record.
- **January 1925**: Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned the Association, claiming their milk had never tested as well as neighbors' and they'd been underpaid a long time; asked to be made whole.
- **Board of arbitration** sat at **Ashlin**, under **Alonzo Frick**, attorney, from **17 March to 4 May 1925**. Three explanations were argued before the board; Effie declines to detail them ("they are in the minutes and the minutes are in the safe"); none of the three was accepted.
- The board's award, as Effie personally recorded it:
  > "The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account."
  - Effie notes the apparent contradiction of "finds no cause established" immediately followed by an award of $3,800 — she says it "was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once."
- **Quantities from bookkeeper Wilbur Teague's ledgers** (Effie vouches only for the copying, not "the cows"): over the 25 years the patrons' claim covered, the Ostrey Hollow station received **40,000,000 lb of milk** total — average **1,600,000 lb/year**. Butterfat price averaged **25 cents/lb** over the period.
- Two gaps Effie flags explicitly in the record: (1) the lost 1889–1892 minute book (see above); (2) **"the crate"** — a crate of measures condemned by the state inspector in **June 1923**, replaced with new ones — was NEVER mentioned at all in any of the three arbitration hearing sessions, by patrons, officers, or the board — despite the board's language about "the books and appliances of the station." Effie: "the interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying."

## R03 — Ansel Keddie's own statement and unsent letter, spring 1925.

Two documents: (I) his 9-sentence oral statement to the board of arbitration at Ashlin, 17 March 1925; (II) an unsent letter written later that spring, found among his papers after his death.

**I. Statement (17 March 1925), quoted in full:**
> "I have kept the test book of this station in my own hand since the station opened, and there is not a figure in it that I did not put there. I have set down the date, the patron, the pounds, the test and the fat, and where I have made an error I have drawn a line through it and put my letters against the line, and I have never written a figure over a figure. I have made the tests in the same order and at the same hour of every working day of every season. I have heard what the patrons say of me and I have heard what the officers say of me. Neither of them has come up that road on a working morning and stood at my elbow and watched the work done. I am asked to account for figures, and the figures are what I read and what I wrote and nothing besides. I am an old man and I have no other trade. I have tested as I was taught and as the state man saw me do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, and I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are. I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself."

**II. Unsent letter, spring 1925 (found among his papers):**
- Explains his method in detail: day began 5:30 a.m.; wagons came up the Hollow in the dark most of the year; every can weighed and recorded before milk went anywhere. Scale was tested true by the state inspector; in 29 seasons no patron ever disputed a weight and was proven right.
- Sample taken from each patron's milk at weighing, into the patron's own numbered jar; jar made up over 10 days before testing. On test morning, jar warmed to blood-warm in warm water, poured repeatedly to mix cream through. "A sample that is not warmed and mixed is worth nothing and a boy could tell you so."
- Test procedure ("the charge"): milk drawn to a mark on the measure stem, tip wiped, blown into the patron's numbered bottle; acid poured down the side, bottle turned dark; four bottles in the hand tester, weighted against each other, cranked at the taught speed, no faster; fat read off the neck's column in per cent against rulings with a pair of dividers; figure written down.
- Glass (testing outfit) borrowed from Ashlin creamery starting **1896**, used until the station's own outfit arrived "the spring following" (i.e., 1897).
- Kept the book in five columns: date, patron, pounds, test, fat. Corrections crossed and initialed, never overwritten. Tested **29 seasons, 1896 through 1924**. Did **not** test in the 1925 season.
- First leaf of the book, quoted:
  > "Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie."
- Summary/averages leaf made up in **1922**, quoted:
  > "Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80."
- The **1911 anomaly** — his account: station's own measure (pipette) broke **8 May 1911**; made shift with a borrowed measure; from that morning tests came up and stood at 3.80; on **10 July 1911** the station's own measure was back in use and tests dropped back down "the same day." Two entries quoted exactly:
  > "May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure."
  > "July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."
  - He says the question mark is his own, standing unanswered for 14 years (i.e., 1911 to 1925); he refuses to speculate.
- He stresses the gap was constant across seasons — present in July as in February, flush grass or dry hay, wet year or dry — never varying with weather/feed.
- **1924** average of the station: **3.80** — he wrote it "with the same pen and same measure of care as all the others."
- History of complaints, per Keddie: complaints "began in 1902," when Larrow Green first said Ostrey Hollow milk didn't test with the rest of the valley. **1908**: a committee came up, sampled the herds through the winter, reported the wet hay of the Bottom thinned the cream, remedy lies with the land (NOT an accusation of fraud). **1919**: the Association's circular went to nine families saying there was water in their cans. He says he holds no charge against anyone who signed that letter but knows what came into the station each morning; notes "nine kitchens in this Hollow have had that letter in a drawer ever since" [corroborates r01's drawer detail].
- Confirms his wife **Orra is Emmet Brant's sister**; Emmet was his largest patron; Ansel weighed and tested every pound of Emmet's milk himself; the two men have not spoken since the circular [corroborates r01].
- He was **put out (dismissed) on 30 April 1925** — notes the board "found no cause established" and dismissed him anyway on the last day of the month, and says he cannot reconcile those two facts in his mind. Ends: "I have tested as I was taught. I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are."

## R04 — "What the Crate Held": Ivy Keddie's talk to the Ordell County Historical Society, Ashlin, 1959 (investigation conducted 1958).

- **Ivy Keddie** is Ansel Keddie's granddaughter — daughter of **Duncan Keddie** (Ansel's son), who taught school at Ashlin. She explicitly disclaims that her findings depend on family loyalty: "nothing I am going to show you tonight depends in the smallest degree on my opinion of my grandfather. It depends on nine pieces of glass and a burette."
- Five record sets consulted, none of which had ever been in one room together before "last year" [1958]:
  1. The **station test book** — in the Association's office since 1925; came to the Historical Society "this year."
  2. The **Brant weigh book** — kept at Hessel Bottom from 1897 onward; lent to her by **Roy Brant**, Emmet Brant's son, who has kept it up himself since his father died.
  3. **Alder Corners Cheese Company** test figures (over Redlow Ridge, Vessey County) — copied out for her by **Rosalie Cudd**, who runs that factory and is **Jerome Cudd's niece**; the original book has never left Vessey County; she has never seen it herself.
  4. **Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks** (state dairy inspector for the county, from 1908) — held at her own college library at Farland since 1947; "eleven years in which nobody put a question to them" [1947+11=1958].
  5. The Association's own annual ledgers — present at the talk.
- **February [1958]**: she went up to the station building (used for storage since closing), found — in the loft over the old intake room, under a tarpaulin — a wooden crate stenciled "**AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET**." Carried up there in **June 1923**. Contained, packed in straw, the station's condemned milk-test pipettes.
- Original outfit: 6 dozen (72) pipettes bought new in **1897**, invoice totaling **$77.93** for the whole outfit (pipettes, bottles, acid measure, hand tester, freight). Of these, **nine** survived unbroken in the crate.
- **9 March [1958]**: she measured the nine pipettes at Farland against a calibrated burette. Her sheet, quoted:
  > "Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for."
- The math: pipettes delivered only 95% of their blown/rated volume (16.72 vs 17.6 c.c.), so every test made with that glass read 95% of the true value.
- Re-quotes her grandfather's 1922 summary leaf (identical to r03's quote: 1896=3.80; 1897 part year; 1898=3.61; 3.61 ± 0.03 every year since except nine weeks of 1911 at 3.80).
- Corrected math: recorded average 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **true average 3.80**. Gap of **0.19** points is **5% of the true value (3.80)**, and **5.25% of the recorded/credited value (3.61-based bookkeeping)** — she stresses both percentages are individually correct but not interchangeable, since "5%" and "5.25%" describe different denominators (true fat delivered vs. fat credited).
- Over **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922**: station took in **40,000,000 lb** of milk, averaging 1,600,000 lb/year [matches r02]. At recorded 3.61%, patrons were credited **1,444,000 lb** of fat; at true 3.80%, they had actually delivered **1,520,000 lb**. Difference: **76,000 lb of butterfat**. At $0.25/lb average price, unpaid value = **$19,000**. The 1925 arbitration award of **$3,800** was one-fifth of that true value.
- Because the error was proportional, it hit richer milk hardest: a herd recorded at 4.56 was short 0.24 points; a herd recorded at 3.42 was short 0.18 points. [Note: 4.56 matches Selby Vose's own herd test in r08; 3.42 matches the Brant weigh-book leaf for 1910 in r07's Item 4.]
- **1911 anomaly re-explained**: same patrons, herds, pasture, season, acid, bottles, same tester — only the measure changed (borrowed from Larrow Green with the cream wagon) — and the average went to 3.80, then back to 3.61 the day the station's own (defective) measure returned. She says this is the only surviving natural experiment/proof and "no explanation... survives those nine weeks except the one I have given you."
- Addresses the "wet hay" theory (the kindest of the old explanations, "offered in good faith"): the State Agricultural College tested it in 1911, **Bulletin No. 214**, quoted:
  > "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."
  - She notes this effect (max 0.05, winter only) is four times too small and the wrong seasonal shape to explain a flat, year-round 0.19 gap.
- Cross-check: at Alder Corners factory, the same Hessel Bottom men's milk (14 overlapping seasons) tested ~0.19 points HIGHER there than at home — matching the calculated calibration gap almost exactly.
- **Bulletin No. 471**, issued October [1958], closing paragraph (Professor Vaught's wording, per Ivy):
  > "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."
- Timeline she closes on: glass condemned 1923, cause found 1958 = 35 years. Grandfather dismissed 1925 = 33 years before vindication. He died in **1934**, so had been dead **24 years** when the exonerating bulletin was issued (1958). "I have no remedy to propose. I have only the crate."

## R05 — "The Books of the Association," Wilbur Teague, retired bookkeeper (1905–1945), memoir chapter written at Ashlin, winter 1948.

- Teague kept the Association's books **1905–1945** (40 years); wrote this memoir chapter 3 years after retiring (i.e., 1948).
- Office at Ashlin: one room over a harness shop, cold at north end, too warm at south end; worked at a sloped desk with a brass rail.
- The Association in his time = three parts: creamery at Larrow Green, office at Ashlin, skimming station at Ostrey Hollow (7 miles up valley). **Selby Vose** was manager over the whole; **Effie Loomis** was secretary; Teague kept the ledger.
- Station's basic operation (as he explains it): patrons hauled milk to the station instead of driving 7 miles to Larrow Green; milk weighed, sampled, tested for butterfat; patron paid on pounds+test; station separated milk, sent cream to Larrow Green, returned skim to farmer.
- **The "station-gain" line**: on one side of the station account, butterfat CREDITED to patrons per the station's own test; on the other side, butterfat ACTUALLY RECEIVED at Larrow Green in the station's cream (weighed/tested into the vats there). The second figure was always larger. The difference ("station gain") was carried to the Association's general account. Teague states he personally INVENTED this bookkeeping line himself when he started in 1905 — denies it was any "old dodge of the founders."
- Example entry he quotes in full (for **1898**):
  > "Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account."
  - He notes this same form recurs every year, and the consistency of "five and a quarter percent" every year made it "furniture" — something that stopped him from scrutinizing it further, in hindsight. [This 5.25% figure matches Ivy Keddie's/r04's math of "5.25% of the fat credited" — i.e., this is independent ledger corroboration of the same calibration-driven gap, though Teague at the time read it as suspicious surplus rather than a measurement artifact.]
- **His own totals, which CONFLICT with other sources**: "that station took in forty-four million pounds of milk" [44,000,000 lb — conflicts with r02's and r04's figure of 40,000,000 lb for the comparable period] and "the average test of all the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, taken over the whole period... was **3.16**" [conflicts sharply with r03/r04/r06/r07's consistently cited figure of ~3.61 recorded average]. He states Larrow Green "did better than that."
- Mentions the 1911 measure-break, but with a DIFFERENT duration: "made shift with a borrowed one for **six weeks** before their own came up from town" [conflicts with r03/r04's precise dates of 8 May–10 July 1911, which span roughly nine weeks, not six].
- Teague explicitly told the **1925 board of arbitration**, and repeats here, his suspicion of deliberate theft: "Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons... a business which is arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken from it, and that no set of books ever made can prevent it. The remedy is two men or a lock, and the Association had neither." He is careful to say he doesn't know what any particular person did on any particular day.
- Confirms: patrons petitioned in winter; board sat at Ashlin under **Alonzo Frick** ("as fair a chairman as ever a hearing had"); board found nothing established against anybody; awarded **$3,800**, without admission, charged to the station account. He entered it and "it balanced."
- Two additional claims: (1) Manager **Selby Vose** knew what the station-gain account was doing every year — he personally signed the annual sheet each spring; Teague never learned what Vose made of it privately. (2) Teague did NOT stay silent for 40 years as some have since claimed — he raised the issue with Vose in **1916** and was told to let it alone; he did so, reasoning "I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience."

## R06 — "Extracts from twenty-eight circuit books," Merle Strawn, retired state dairy inspector, copied out 1936 at the state dairy office's request.

- Strawn covered the **Ordell circuit from 1907** [note: r04 describes his notebooks as "from 1908" — a minor discrepancy in starting year between his own account (1907) and Ivy Keddie's description] until he retired in 1936 — "fifty-odd plants in four counties."
- His fixed four-part inspection routine every visit: (1) scales, against sealed weights (intake scale and cream scale, light and heavy loads); (2) lactometer test on the day's milk, patron by patron if in question, for added water; (3) boiler, water supply, cold room; (4) stood at the tester's elbow and watched a full test made from sample to reading, without letting the tester hurry.
- States plainly: "I put down what I found. I did not put down what I thought about it... every time in my life I have seen a field man paid for an opinion instead, the opinion has outlived the weighing and done harm after the man was dead." — explicit methodological contrast with Teague (r05) and Vose (r08), who both offer theories/opinions.
- Year-by-year Ostrey Hollow extracts (selected):
  - **1907**: first round; scales true; tester (Keddie) "works slow and clean," keeps book "in his own hand, and his hand is better than most."
  - **1909**: scales true, boiler in order; one man cautioned about a rusted seam.
  - **1911, June** [en route elsewhere]: "Scales true. Watched a test through. Nothing here to note." [Notably, this visit falls squarely within the 8 May–10 July 1911 borrowed-measure anomaly period described in r03/r04, yet Strawn recorded nothing amiss — an unresolved oddity: either the state inspector's own check didn't catch the discrepancy, or his visit's test happened not to reveal it.]
  - **1912**: scales true; lactometer, no added water; cream shipped same day as separated ("more than some do").
  - **1913**: new manager at the Association came out to the platform with him, unlike the previous one [this would be Selby Vose's first year as manager, 1913, matching r02/r05].
  - **1915**: wash room floor ordered graded.
  - **1916**: scales true; "The tester complains that his patrons blame him for their figures. I told him that is the trade and he said he knew it." [Direct quote of a remark from Ansel Keddie, unnamed here but clearly him.]
  - **1919**: scales true; lactometer, **no added water**; explicitly notes: "The manager of the Association has sent a circular round to the patrons of this station about water in the cans. I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so." [Strong independent exculpatory evidence against the "watered milk" accusation, contradicting Vose's public position in r08 and the substance of the 1919 circular itself.]
  - **1920**: visited twice (spring/fall); no added water both times; cold room short of ice in fall, corrected before he left the county.
  - **1921**: state began issuing calibrated burettes (in wooden cases, with printed directions) to field men this year, for checking delivery of measures/pipettes. His own arrived in spring.
  - **1922**: did NOT run the station's glass against the burette this year — ran out of time; explicitly notes he's recording this omission because "it is what happened."
  - **1923, 12 June**: ran the milk pipettes against the burette (having carried the burette two full seasons before finally checking). Quoted in full:
    > "Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk pipettes against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them."
  - **1923, October**: new glass in use since July; ran the new pipettes against the burette — they deliver their mark correctly; scales true.
  - **1924**: scales true, no added water, "tests improved. Their book gives the year at 3.80." [Corroborates r03's and r04's 1924 figure of 3.80; conflicts with Vose's r08 claim of 3.90.]
  - **1925, fall**: tests improved; "New man at the tester's bench this year. He is quick, and quick is not the thing I look for." [Confirms Keddie was replaced in 1925, and hints at some unease about the new tester's manner.]
  - **1926**: state's burette program discontinued this year; instruments kept by the men who have them, no more issued, none returned.
  - **1928**: "The loft over the intake room is full of odds and ends" — told the station staff a loft over a milk room should hold nothing but air; told in return it had been full "since before the war." [This is the same loft where Ivy Keddie found the crate 30 years later, in 1958 — per r04.]
  - **1931**: bad ice year across all four counties.
  - **1934**: separator bearing ran hot, shut down 2 days, repaired; "tester's book is kept up, though not in the hand it used to be kept in" [implying a different/less careful tester by then — Ansel Keddie died in 1934, per r04].
  - **1936, March**: his last round; nothing to note anywhere.
- Closing note: his 28 pocket notebooks (oilcloth covers, one per year) go to the state dairy office per retirement rules. Explicitly states he "never had a theory about a plant and I do not recommend having one. A theory is a thing a man carries out to the platform with him and it gets in the way of the weights."

## R07 — Accession notes for the Ordell County dairy collection, Halbert Nym, curator, Ordell County Historical Rooms, 1972 (Items 1–16, exact transcriptions).

- **Item 1**: By-laws pamphlet (gray wrappers) — Article VII quoted (identical wording to r02's quote). Curatorial note: the PRINTED pamphlet itself carries no year on its face, only "adopted at the annual meeting" (the 1892 date comes from Effie Loomis's separate account in r02, not from the pamphlet itself).
- **Item 2**: Invoice, **Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, Tarnet**, dated **14 April 1897**, quoted in full:

## R08 — Manager Selby Vose's annual address to the Association membership, printed in the 1926 annual report (delivered spring 1926).

- Vose: "It is thirteen years this spring, since 1913" that he has managed the Association — confirms he became manager in 1913 [matches r02, r05].
- Ordinary business of the past year (1925 season): wet season into June, heavy hauling; Larrow Green creamery ran the flush without lost time; butter held market position; ice supply good; no complaints of any employee. Ostrey Hollow station "ran its season out and its cream came down to us daily and sweet."
- Recounts (consistent with r02/r05/r07): January 1925 petition by Ostrey Hollow patrons; board of arbitration at Ashlin under **Alonzo Frick** ("an able and impartial man"); board heard everyone; found **no cause established** against the station, office, or any officer/servant; nevertheless awarded **$3,800**, without admission, charged to the station account. Vose says he supported the payment as "a generous settlement of an unproved claim," paid to preserve peace among the membership rather than as an admission of fault: "There is not a business house in Ordell County that would have done it. We did it because we are not a business house; we are the farmers of this valley in company together."
- Vose then states his own personal theory, publicly, "in this room": that water was deliberately being added to cans at the head of the valley for a generation, and it "stopped when the Association began to look." He cites the 1924 rise as proof: "the tests at that station rose by **twenty-one hundredths** and the average for 1924 stood at **3.90**." [CONFLICTS with r03 (Keddie's own letter: 1924 average = 3.80), r06 (Strawn's inspection note: "Their book gives the year at 3.80"), and r07's Item 3 summary leaf (implying 3.80 was the corrected/normal level) — Vose's numbers (a 0.21 rise to 3.90) appear inflated relative to the consistently corroborated figures elsewhere (a 0.19 rise to 3.80). This is a clean, isolated numerical discrepancy specifically from the one figure (the manager) who has a personal/institutional motive to maximize the apparent "improvement" and thereby support his watered-milk theory.]
- He explicitly rejects the equipment/apparatus explanation being offered to him "kindly... by men who mean well": he says the state inspector's visit "condemned the station's testing apparatus on the **twelfth of July, 1923**, and told us to order new. We ordered new inside the month." [CONFLICTS with r06 (Strawn's own notebook: condemned **12 June 1923**, not July) and r07's Item 9 (photostat confirming June 12). Vose gets the month wrong — either a memory error or motivated vagueness, since accepting the equipment explanation would undercut his "someone was watering milk" narrative.] He calls the apparatus explanation "a piece of housekeeping that appears in our own order book with a date and a price against it" — i.e., dismisses it as mundane and irrelevant, not a "mystery."
- He declines to name any suspected individual: "I do not know a name and I would not use it if I did... I have no wish to see anybody prosecuted, and nobody has been or will be."
- He defends his own 1919 circular (matching r01/r03/r06's references to this document), describing it as written in "temperate language," naming nobody, accusing nobody, merely warning that "added water would be looked for and would be dealt with" and that "this Association pays for butterfat and cannot pay for creek water." [This self-description of a measured, impersonal notice contrasts sharply with how the letter was actually experienced by recipients per r01 — kept in drawers/trunks for decades as a devastating personal accusation — an interesting gap between the author's self-perception and the recipients' lived experience.]
- Personal/family detail: his father — "your first president," who "wrote the by-law under which every patron in this Association is paid on the test of the station where he delivers" — i.e., confirms Gideon Vose (first president, author of Article VII per r02) is Selby Vose's FATHER. [This directly conflicts with r01's description of Selby Vose as Gideon Vose's "nephew."] Gideon Vose kept a herd of Jerseys at the home place; Selby kept them after him. In **1910** that herd tested **4.56** [matches the "herd recorded at 4.56" example of proportional error discussed in r04]. He cites this to rebut any claim that Larrow Valley is inherently "poor milk country."
- Closing charge to members for the coming season: "Bring it as it left the cow. Wash the cans, cool the milk, keep the dog out of the milk house." Expresses hope the Ostrey Hollow tests will hold at the 1924 level and the matter will be "dead and buried."
- Thanks members for "thirteen years of your patience" and asks for one more.

## Cross-cutting conflicts and oddities (summary, consolidated across R01–R08)

1. **Award amount**: $3,500 (r01, Vira's oral memory) vs. **$3,800** (r02, r03 implicitly consistent, r05, r07 Item 7, r08 — the consistent documentary figure).
2. **Timing of the hearing**: Vira (r01) implies the hearing followed almost immediately after the 1919 letter (winter 1919/spring 1920); the documentary record (r02, r03, r07, r08) places the actual petition/hearing in **1925**, roughly 5–6 years later — Vira's oral account appears to have compressed/conflated the timeline.
3. **Cause of the low tests**: Vira (r01) asserts as settled fact that Keddie was "caught" stealing cream. Bookkeeper Teague (r05) suspects the same (opportunity-based reasoning) and testified so to the 1925 board. Manager Vose (r08) publicly insists it was deliberately watered milk by an unnamed patron, not equipment. Keddie himself (r03) denies both theft and any explanation he can give. The 1908 committee (r07 Item 11) blamed wet hay/land, not fraud. The definitive scientific finding — Ivy Keddie's 1958 investigation and the resulting Bulletin No. 471 (r04, r07 Item 15) — concludes NONE of these: it was purely a calibration defect in the station's pipettes (delivering only 95% of rated volume), and states explicitly "No milk was watered... and no cream was stolen." This is the central resolution/twist of the whole set.
4. **Selby Vose's relation to Gideon Vose**: "nephew" (r01, oral/rumor) vs. **"son"** (r02 and r08, both authoritative/documentary and self-reported).
5. **Total lifetime milk intake at the station**: 40,000,000 lb (r02; r04's calculation; r07's Item 6 ledger citation) vs. **44,000,000 lb** (r05, Teague's memoir — an apparent misremembering by the very bookkeeper who kept the ledgers).
6. **Long-run average test**: ~3.61% recorded (r03, r04, r07 Item 3, consistently) vs. **3.16%** (r05, Teague — again apparently a memory error, sharply inconsistent with all other sources).
7. **1924 corrected average**: 3.80 (r03 Keddie, r06 Strawn's inspection note, consistent with r07 Item 3's implied pattern) vs. **3.90** (r08, Vose — self-serving inflation).
8. **Date pipettes were condemned**: 12 June 1923 (r02 [implied June], r04, r06 Strawn's own notebook, r07 Item 9 photostat — all consistent) vs. **12 July 1923** (r08, Vose — a factual error, again in the direction that supports his preferred narrative).
9. **Duration of the 1911 measure substitution**: precisely 8 May–10 July 1911 (~9 weeks; r03's dated entries, matching "nine weeks" in r03/r04/r07's summary-leaf quote) vs. **"six weeks"** (r05, Teague's looser recollection).
10. **Location the crate was found**: the station building's loft over the old intake room (r04's narrative) vs. **"the Association office at Ashlin"** (r07's Item 5 provenance note) — a location inconsistency between two otherwise-corroborating sources about the same crate.
11. **Catalog mislabeling** (r07): Item 3 (station test book, actually signed/kept by A. Keddie) is headed "kept by E. Brant"; Item 4 (farm weigh book, provenanced entirely through the Brant family — Roy Brant, Hazel Brant) is headed "kept by A. Keddie." These two headers appear to be swapped/crossed in the curator's own catalog.
12. **1919 circular's tone**: experienced by recipients as a severe, personal accusation kept for decades as evidence of grievance (r01, r03's "nine kitchens... have had that letter in a drawer ever since") vs. described by its own author (Vose, r08) as temperate, naming nobody, accusing nobody.
13. **Merle Strawn's circuit start year**: "1907" by his own account (r06's covering note) vs. "**from 1908**" per Ivy Keddie's description of his notebooks (r04).
14. **1911 state inspection visit**: Strawn's own June 1911 circuit note says "Scales true. Watched a test through. Nothing here to note" (r06) — yet this visit falls within the window (8 May–10 July 1911) when the borrowed/substitute measure was in use and testing 3.80 instead of the usual 3.61 — an unresolved oddity: the state inspector's contemporaneous check did not flag or remark on the anomaly at all.
15. **Alder Corners provenance**: r04 says Rosalie Cudd (Jerome Cudd's *niece*) currently runs the factory and copied the figures; r07's Item 13 provenance says the original test book passed from its founder to his *daughter* in 1941 — leaving ambiguous whether Rosalie is that daughter (contradicting "niece") or a distinct, later relative.
