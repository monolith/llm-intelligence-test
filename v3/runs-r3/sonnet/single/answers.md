# Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

*Method note: I read all 24 retellings once, in order, and partitioned them into eight underlying
stories by event-cluster rather than by narrator or theme. Many retellings contribute to several
stories at once; I have assigned each fact to the story it most directly belongs to. Corrected
facts only appear below; the reasoning behind each correction is in Sections B–F of Part 3, or
noted briefly inline where a story's own internal facts required resolving a conflict.*

---

## Story 1: The Association, the Creamery, and Article VII (1889–1892)

In 1889 the farmers of the Larrow Valley met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley
Cooperative Creamery Association, tired of hauling butter to Ashlin merchants on the merchants'
own terms. Gideon Vose was elected first president, a post he held twenty-two years, until 1911;
he drafted the by-laws himself at his own kitchen table. The main creamery was built at Larrow
Green on Larrow Creek in 1891, and the valley began to be paid in cash monthly for the first time
in its history.

The by-laws' Article VII — drafted by Vose and adopted, per the Association's own secretary Effie
Loomis quoting the minute book directly, "at the annual meeting of 1892" — established that every
patron would be paid for the butterfat found in his milk "by test," not by the milk's weight, and,
critically, that "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern," with no appeal to any
other test. (Dell Rundle's 1939 pamphlet instead gives 1891 for the article, apparently conflating
its adoption with the creamery's construction the year before; Loomis's citation of the minute
book itself is the better-sourced date and is preferred here.) This one clause is what later let a
single station's own glassware become the entire measure of what dozens of farmers were paid, for
decades, with no cross-check built into the system by design.

**Quantities:** creamery built 1891; Article VII adopted 1892; Gideon Vose president 1889–1911
(22 years).

**Documents:** Effie Loomis's 1941 "Notes toward a history of the Association" (R02, §§1–3,
quoting Article VII from the minutes, and noting the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in an office
move in 1938); Dell Rundle's 1939/1959 jubilee pamphlet (R12, §§I–II, quoting Article VII from the
printed by-laws); Halbert Nym's 1972 accession notes, Item 1 (R07, the printed by-laws pamphlet,
undated on its face); Alonzo Frick's 1946 recollection (R13); Orin Falke's 1959 county report
(R22, ¶3).

## Story 2: Building the Ostrey Hollow Station and Buying Its Glass (1895–1898)

To serve the farms at the head of the valley — chiefly the six farms with rights on Hessel
Bottom, a 160-acre wet meadow — the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven
miles up from Larrow Green, over the winter of 1895–96. It opened on 4 May 1896, with Ansel
Keddie, who had gone up in 1895 to ready it, as tester and separator man; he held the post
twenty-nine seasons.

The station had no testing equipment of its own on opening day. Larrow Green's buttermaker,
Hobart Sill, lent his own glassware to get the first season started, and the station kept
borrowing test glass from Larrow Green through 1896 and into early 1897. A proper outfit was then
ordered from Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet: the invoice, dated 14 April 1897, billed the
Association $77.93 for six dozen milk-test pipettes (each blown to 17.6 c.c.) at 35 cents apiece,
twelve dozen test bottles at 22 cents, one acid measure at 90 cents, and a four-bottle hand tester
at $18.00, plus $2.15 freight. That outfit went into use at the station on 1 May 1897 — confirmed
by the invoice's own terms, by Ammon & Sons' later company history, and by Orra Keddie's diary
entry of that exact date ("the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing").
(Bulletin No. 471 and clerk Bertram Kyle's memoir both instead give 1 May 1898 for this
changeover — a shared one-year error against three independent, closer-to-the-event sources.)
Those pipettes, manufactured with a defect that made them deliver short of their marked capacity,
remained the station's testing glass for the next twenty-six years.

**Quantities:** Hessel Bottom, 160 acres, six farms; station opened 4 May 1896; outfit invoice
$77.93, for six dozen pipettes at 35¢ each, twelve dozen bottles at 22¢, one acid measure (90¢),
one hand tester ($18.00), freight $2.15; own glass in use from 1 May 1897.

**Documents:** Ansel Keddie's test book, first leaf (quoted in R03, R04, R07 Item 3); the Ammon &
Sons invoice (R07 Item 2; reproduced again in R20); Orra Keddie's diary (R24, entries of 1897);
Hobart Sill's 1934 recollection (R14); Junia Ammon's 1949 firm history (R20); Orin Falke's 1959
report (R22, ¶4).

## Story 3: A Generation of Complaint and a Family Split Down the Middle (1902–1919)

From about 1902, patrons at Ostrey Hollow — Emmet Brant, the largest patron on Hessel Bottom,
foremost among them — complained publicly that their milk tested lower than milk delivered
anywhere else in the valley, for the same cows on the same feed, and were not believed; some were
laughed at. In January 1908 the Association sent a three-man committee under Nyle Grigg, a Larrow
Green farmer with no stake in the station, to investigate. The committee sampled the Hessel
Bottom herds through January–March 1908, found the scales honest and the milk whole, and
concluded the wet, sour hay of the bog meadow was thinning the cream — a finding it printed and
stood behind, and which Grigg reprinted with a covering letter in 1919 (there misquoting the
College's later Bulletin No. 214 as finding a "two tenths" effect, when the bulletin's own text
gives at most five hundredths). The complaint went unresolved for another eleven years.

In 1919, manager Selby Vose circulated a warning letter to the Hollow's patrons — nine families,
by most accounts (Orra Keddie's diary alone says eleven) — about "water in the cans." Ansel
Keddie, the station's own tester, delivered the letters himself rather than mail them. The
circular permanently split the valley's two ends and, within Keddie's own household, ended
relations between him and his brother-in-law Emmet Brant (Ansel's wife Orra was Emmet's sister):
Emmet confronted Ansel in the yard days later over why Ansel had personally carried a paper he
did not believe, and the two men — who had shared Sunday dinners for thirty years — never spoke
again before Emmet's death in 1929.

**Quantities:** complaints from 1902; 1908 committee sampled Jan–Mar 1908; 1919 circular sent to
nine families (Orra's diary: eleven); Ansel and Emmet estranged 1919–1929 (ten years, ended only
by Emmet's death).

**Documents:** Nyle Grigg's 1908 report and 1919 covering letter (R15); Roy Brant's 1955 account
(R09); Vira Toland's 1979 oral history (R01); Orra Keddie's diary (R24, entries of March 1902,
Jan/Mar 1908, June 1919); Ansel Keddie's unsent 1925 letter (R03); Hazel Brant's 1981 interview
(R17).

## Story 4: The Nine Weeks of 1911 — An Accidental Experiment

On 8 May 1911 the Ostrey Hollow station's own milk pipette broke. Tester Ansel Keddie made shift
with a measure borrowed from Larrow Green, used it for nine weeks, and put the station's own
(replacement) glass back into service on 10 July 1911. Every other factor at the station — the
patrons, the herds, the pasture, the season, the acid, the bottles, and the tester himself —
stayed exactly the same throughout. During those nine weeks the station's recorded average test
rose sharply, from its usual 3.61 to 3.80, and it fell straight back to 3.61 the day the station's
own measure returned.

Keddie recorded both dates and both results in his own book at the time, understanding neither,
and set a bare question mark against the entry — a mark he said, in 1925, he had "let stand for
fourteen years." His son Duncan later wrote that Ansel came home in 1911 already saying "it was
something in the glass," but would not commit an unproved suspicion to the book. No one else in
the valley connected the incident to anything at the time; it became evidence only when Ivy
Keddie and Professor Vaught examined it in 1958 and recognized it as an unplanned but conclusive
natural experiment, isolating the measuring glass as the one variable that mattered — something a
bare comparison of numbers, without the swap-and-return, could not have proven. (Roland Kepp's
1926 paper dismisses the episode, misdating it as six weeks and calling it merely a flush-season
artifact.)

**Quantities:** 8 May–10 July 1911, nine weeks; test rose 3.61 → 3.80 and back to 3.61.

**Documents:** Ansel Keddie's test book entries (R03); Ivy Keddie's 1959 talk (R04); Vaught's
Bulletin No. 471, §IV (R11); Duncan Keddie's notes (R18); Merle Strawn's circuit book (R06);
Roland Kepp's paper (R16).

## Story 5: The 1925 Petition, Arbitration, and the Dismissal of Ansel Keddie

In January 1925 the Ostrey Hollow patrons formally petitioned the Association, seeking to be
made whole for a quarter century of low tests. A three-member board of arbitration, chaired by
Ashlin attorney Alonzo Frick (chosen, he said, because he "kept no cow"), sat from 17 March to 4
May 1925 — the first session at the Association's Ashlin office, two further sessions at the
station itself so the board could see the plant firsthand. Only four documents were entered as
exhibits: the station's own test book (Keddie's hand, 1896–1924), its receiving book, Emmet
Brant's private farm weigh book, and the state inspector's 1923 circuit notebook. The condemned
glass crate itself, sitting in the loft directly overhead, was never produced or examined by the
board.

Three explanations were argued: watering (Selby Vose), tester theft (raised by bookkeeper Wilbur
Teague's insinuation), and the wet-hay theory (revived from 1908). Keddie made a nine-sentence
statement on 17 March denying he had ever taken cream and saying he could not explain the
figures; privately, in the corridor, he told Frick he suspected the patrons were somehow at
fault, though he would not say so publicly. On 30 April 1925 — four days before the board's
finding — the Association dismissed Keddie outright, with no finding against him from the board
at any point. On 4 May 1925 the board announced it found "no cause established" against anyone,
yet in the same breath awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800, "without admission," charged to
the station account. (Vira Toland's and Cleve Oram's recollections of "$3,500" repeat the same
misremembered figure, against the award as quoted verbatim and identically by Loomis's minutes,
Nym's catalogue, Vose's own address, Rundle, Frick, and Falke.) The patrons' own reckoning of
their loss, per Frick, was 67,000 lb of butterfat — a claim later superseded by the much larger
figure the 1958 investigation established. Keddie never worked at the station again; he took up
carpentry and died in 1934, still under the valley's suspicion.

**Quantities:** petition Jan 1925; hearing 17 Mar–4 May 1925; dismissal 30 Apr 1925; award $3,800;
patrons' 1925 claim, 67,000 lb butterfat.

**Documents:** Effie Loomis's minutes (R02, §6); Ansel Keddie's statement (R03); Alonzo Frick's
1946 recollection and exhibit list (R13); Selby Vose's 1926 address (R08); Bertram Kyle's memoir
(R23); Orra Keddie's diary (R24); Cleve Oram (R21); Hazel Brant (R17).

## Story 6: The 1923 Condemnation of the Glass, and Ammon & Sons' Silence

The state's dairy inspector for the Ordell circuit, Merle Strawn, visited Ostrey Hollow almost
every year from 1907; his pocket circuit-books record the scales tested true and (from 1908 on)
no added water found by lactometer at every visit. In 1921 the state began issuing inspectors
calibrated burettes for checking plant glassware against an outside standard — the pipette
itself, being what a plant measured with, had never before been checkable against anything. On 12
June 1923, on a routine visit, Strawn ran the Ostrey Hollow station's milk pipettes against his
burette for the first time and found "these measures will not deliver their mark." He condemned
the entire crate on the spot and told the manager to order new glass; the condemned pipettes went
into storage in the loft over the station's intake room rather than being destroyed. (Selby
Vose's 1926 address gives the date as 12 July, and Hazel Brant's 1981 recollection gives the year
as 1921 — both single-source errors against Strawn's own contemporaneous entry, quoted verbatim
twice.)

The Association wrote to Ammon & Sons on 28 June 1923; the firm's reply of 2 July, entered in its
own letter book, promised replacement glass within the month and stated flatly that "our books of
the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name." New glass was in
use by July 1923, and the station's tests rose immediately. Decades later, the firm's own
historian, Junia Ammon, speculated in print that a reground mold had quietly shrunk the pipette
bore in the 1890s and gone uncaught by routine spot-checks — "consistently wrong, which is the one
fault a check of that kind cannot find."

**Quantities:** burettes issued to state inspectors from 1921; pipettes condemned 12 June 1923;
Association's order letter 28 June 1923; Ammon's reply 2 July 1923; new glass in use by July 1923.

**Documents:** Merle Strawn's circuit book (R06); Halbert Nym's catalogue, Items 5, 9, 10 (R07);
Selby Vose's address (R08); Junia Ammon's firm history (R20); Orin Falke's report (R22).

## Story 7: Ivy Keddie's 1958 Investigation and Bulletin No. 471

Ivy Keddie, Ansel Keddie's granddaughter (born 1931, daughter of Duncan Keddie), was a graduate
student at the State Agricultural College at Farland. In February 1958 she climbed into the loft
over the old Ostrey Hollow station's intake room and found the condemned glass crate, still under
a tarpaulin where it had sat since 1923; nine of the original six dozen pipettes survived intact.
On 9 March 1958 she measured all nine against a calibrated burette at Farland, five runs each: the
readings averaged 16.72 c.c. against a stem blown for 17.6 c.c. — delivering only 95 hundredths of
the marked charge, a shortfall of 0.88 c.c. per pipette. (Professor Leland Vaught's own bulletin
elsewhere mistranscribes this mean as "16.27," an internal transposition against Ivy's own
measurement sheet, which is quoted, and checks out arithmetically, identically twice.)

She assembled five sets of records never before brought into one room: the station's own test
book; the Brant family's weigh book (lent by Roy Brant); the Alder Corners Cheese Company's
cross-check figures for four Hessel Bottom farmers, copied out by Rosalie Cudd; Merle Strawn's
state circuit notebooks, held at Farland's library since 1947; and the Association's own ledgers.
Working with Professor Vaught, she calculated that a pipette short by five percent produces a
proportionally low reading on every test, worst on the richest milk, invisible from inside the
plant because the pipette is the plant's own standard — and that this exactly matched both the
station's flat, all-season 0.19-point shortfall and the 1911 nine-week anomaly. Their joint
result, Bulletin No. 471 ("No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there"),
was published by the College in October 1958, thirty-three years after Keddie's dismissal and
twenty-four years after his death.

**Quantities:** crate found Feb 1958; nine pipettes measured 9 Mar 1958, mean delivery 16.72 c.c.
(95% of the 17.6 c.c. mark); Bulletin No. 471 published October 1958.

**Documents:** Ivy Keddie's 1959 talk (R04); her measurement sheet (R07, Item 14); Rosalie Cudd's
1958 letter (R10); Vaught's Bulletin No. 471 (R11); Roy Brant's 1955/1958 account (R09); Halbert
Nym's catalogue (R07, Items 5, 13, 14).

## Story 8: The Reckoning — Newspaper, County Report, Museum, and Family Record (1958–1972)

Word of Bulletin No. 471 spread through Ordell County within days. The Ashlin Chronicle ran it as
a front-page feature on 3 October 1958 (misstating the bulletin's own $19,000 shortfall figure as
$19,600, and calling Ivy Keddie Ansel's "great-niece" rather than his granddaughter), quoting Ivy
directly: "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not." In 1959 the county's agricultural
agent, Orin Falke, prepared an official report for the board of supervisors reviewing the whole
matter; his first draft repeated the "great-niece" error and gave some credit to the old wet-hay
theory, but on receiving the actual text of the 1911 feeding-trial bulletin (No. 214) he formally
withdrew that concession and separately corrected Ivy's relationship to Ansel to "granddaughter."

Also in 1959, the station's test book and the crate of pipettes were deposited at the Ordell
County Historical Rooms, joined by the Brant family's weigh book in 1963; curator Halbert Nym
catalogued all sixteen items in 1972, tracing each document's custody chain and noting gaps still
in the record (no ledger figure survives for the station's partial first season of 1896). Dell
Rundle's 1939 jubilee pamphlet, reissued in 1959, added a footnote pointing readers to the
college's findings and a "note added while in press" in which Rundle explicitly retracted his own
earlier suggestion that cream had been stolen, having realized his own quoted ledger entry showed
cream arriving heavy, not light. In 1968 (with an addition in 1970), Duncan Keddie wrote up the
family's genealogy for his descendants, correcting several errors in circulation — that Selby Vose
was Gideon Vose's nephew rather than son, and that the Voses and Keddies were related at all — and
recording where each of the five source-documents had ended up: three separate buildings, none of
them the family's own.

**Quantities:** Chronicle article 3 Oct 1958; documents deposited at the Historical Rooms in 1959
(test book, crate) and 1963 (weigh book); Nym's catalogue, 1972, sixteen items.

**Documents:** The Ashlin Chronicle (R19); Orin Falke's report (R22); Dell Rundle's pamphlet and
1959 footnote (R12); Duncan Keddie's notes (R18); Halbert Nym's accession notes (R07).

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**
Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter, not his great-niece. This is stated in her own voice
("Ansel Keddie... was my grandfather," R04) and confirmed by her father Duncan Keddie's genealogy
notes (R18: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter,"
adding he'd "already twice seen it printed otherwise"). Two retellings do print it otherwise: the
Ashlin Chronicle (R19) and county agent Orin Falke's report (R22, ¶2) both call her Ansel's
"great-niece." Falke himself catches and corrects the error in a note appended to his own report:
"I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the
great-niece." I resolve this to granddaughter on the strength of Ivy's own first-person statement
plus her father's explicit correction, against two copies of the same secondhand error, one of
which self-corrects.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**
Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. Duncan Keddie (R18) sets out the chain explicitly:
his mother Orra and Hazel's grandfather Emmet Brant were siblings, making Duncan and Roy Brant
(Emmet's son) first cousins, and making their children, Ivy and Hazel, second cousins — "the
children of first cousins." Hazel Brant herself independently states the same relationship in the
same terms (R17), and both explicitly reject a different label — "first cousin once removed" —
that circulates elsewhere (Vira Toland, R01; Cleve Oram, R21), which I treat as a shared error
from imprecise local reckoning, outweighed by two family members independently working the
generations out loud.

What the sources leave genuinely open, rather than merely mislabeled, is whether Orra and Emmet
were full or half siblings: Roy Brant (R09) insists they were "full brother and sister, same
mother and same father," explicitly pre-rejecting a rumor of a stepmother, while his own daughter
Hazel Brant (R17) later calls Orra "a half sister to my grandfather Emmet, by his father's second
wife." Nothing in the sources settles which is right — this is cannot be determined from the
sources — though it does not change the second-cousin arithmetic either way.

**B3. Selby Vose's father, and why it matters.**
Selby Vose was Gideon Vose's son, not his nephew. Duncan Keddie (R18) and clerk Bertram Kyle
(R23) both state this explicitly and both flag "nephew" as a specific, recurring, false rumor
they'd each heard repeated in town. Vira Toland's oral history (R01) is the source that gives the
wrong "nephew" version. It matters because Vira's version uses the supposed nephew relationship to
support a claim that Orra Keddie and "old Mrs. Vose" were first cousins — implying the 1919 water
accusation and the whole affair were shot through with Vose-family favoritism against the Keddies.
With Selby correctly identified as Gideon's own son, and with any Vose–Keddie family link
independently denied outright (see B4), that personal-vendetta reading loses its factual basis;
what happened at Ostrey Hollow traces to an unchecked measuring instrument, not a family grudge
running through the manager's office.

**B4. The Vose family and the Keddie family.**
No relationship is established between the two families. Duncan Keddie addresses this directly
and at length (R18, §III): "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses," he
writes, attributing the rumor to confusion with an unrelated Ashlin family also named Vose, and
noting that his mother — who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection" for
everyone else in the valley — never once claimed one. Against this stand only Vira Toland's
specific claim that Orra Keddie and "old Mrs. Vose" were first cousins (R01) and Cleve Oram's
vaguer, explicitly hearsay "the Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort... I could not
draw you the line on paper" (R21). I resolve this in favor of no relationship: Duncan's account is
a direct family member's explicit, reasoned denial addressing the claim by name, against two
unspecific oral-tradition claims that do not even agree with each other on what the relationship
was supposed to be.
# Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math

**C1(a). Proportion by which tests differed from truth.**
Ivy Keddie's own measurement sheet (quoted identically in R04 and R07 Item 14) gives the nine
surviving pipettes' mean delivery as 16.72 c.c. against a stem blown for 17.6 c.c.
Working: 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c. short; 0.88 ÷ 17.6 = 0.05 = 5%.
Equivalently, 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 — the pipette delivered 95% of its marked charge. Because the
test bottle's neck is ruled to read percent-fat correctly only for the full marked charge, every
reading came out at 95% of the true value, i.e., low by "one part in twenty" (5%) — the exact
phrase Bulletin No. 471 itself uses (R11 §VII, R07 Item 15).
*(Vaught's bulletin elsewhere states the mean as 16.27 c.c.; I reject that figure — see Section E,
item 9 — and use 16.72, which is the number on Ivy's own measurement sheet and sums/averages
correctly from the nine listed readings: 16.70+16.71+16.72+16.72+16.72+16.73+16.73+16.74+16.71 =
150.48; 150.48 ÷ 9 = 16.72.)*

**C1(b). True average test, and the shortfall in points.**
Recorded average (Keddie's own book, quoted verbatim three times: R03, R04, R07 Item 3) = 3.61.
True average = recorded ÷ 0.95 = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80.
Shortfall = 3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19 points (nineteen hundredths) — matching the flat, all-season gap
reported by Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant, and matching the level the station's own book recorded
during the 1911 nine-week interval when the borrowed (presumably true) measure was in use.

**C1(c). Pounds of butterfat not credited, over the affected full delivery years.**
Milk received, 1898–1922 (25 full delivery years) = 40,000,000 lb (resolved in Section E, item 1).
At the recorded 3.61%: credited fat = 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb.
At the true 3.80%: actual fat delivered = 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb.
Difference = 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 lb of butterfat not credited.
This matches Bulletin No. 471's own stated figure exactly (quoted in R04, R07 Item 15, R11 §VII,
R19).

**C1(d). Money value, and fraction actually paid.**
Average price of butterfat over the period = $0.25/lb (R02, R07 Item 6, R09, R23 — well
corroborated).
76,000 lb × $0.25 = $19,000.
The 1925 award actually paid was $3,800 (resolved in Section E, item 5).
$3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.2 = one-fifth. The patrons received one-fifth of what the shortfall was
worth — matching Ivy Keddie's own statement, "That is a fifth of it" (R04).

**C2. The largest patron (Emmet Brant), his 1910 year.**
From his own weigh book (quoted in R07 Item 4, R09, R17): 200,000 lb of milk delivered in 1910,
paid on a recorded test of 3.42.
(a) Butterfat credited = 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb.**
(b) True test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60. True fat delivered = 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb.
Owed and not credited = 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb.** (This matches R04's own worked example: "A
herd recorded at 3.42 was short 0.18" — 3.60 − 3.42 = 0.18, confirming the same pipette ratio.)
(c) Money value that year: Roy Brant's account (R09) gives the 1910 price of butterfat
specifically as 24 cents/lb (distinct from the ~25-cent multi-year average used above).
360 × $0.24 = **$86.40.**

**C3. Credited vs. actually-delivered fat, full period.**
(a) Fat credited to patrons on the station test, 1898–1922: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb.**
(b) Fat actually reaching Larrow Green in the station's cream (i.e., the true fat content):
40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lb.**
(c) Difference = 76,000 lb. As a percentage:
— of the fat actually delivered (1,520,000 lb): 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5%.**
— of the fat credited to patrons (1,444,000 lb): 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.26%** ("five and a
quarter percent," the figure the Association's own ledgers used every year, e.g., the 1898 entry:
gain of 3,040 lb on 57,760 lb credited = 5.26%, and on 60,800 lb received = 5%).
Both percentages are correct; they are not interchangeable because they use different
denominators, exactly as R04 and R07 Item 6 both explicitly caution.

**C4. The invoice for the station's outfit.**
(a) Total = **$77.93** (the figure printed on the invoice itself, reproduced identically in R07
Item 2 and in R20's own facsimile). Two narrators — Junia Ammon's own prose in R20, and the Ashlin
Chronicle (R19) — instead say "$77.39" in running text; I reject that figure because it
contradicts the invoice document each of them is purporting to summarize (Ammon's chapter even
reproduces the correct $77.93 invoice right alongside her own mistaken prose figure — see Section
E).
(b) The invoice bought **six dozen (72) pipettes**, each blown to 17.6 c.c. and delivering 16.72
c.c., i.e., each was short by **0.88 c.c.** (17.6 − 16.72).

**C5(a). Duration glass was in use, vs. the years the money calculation covers.**
The faulty glass was in physical use from **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923** — about 26 years and 6
weeks (this is Bulletin No. 471's own conclusion, R11 §VII / R07 Item 15, and it uses the correct
1897 start date despite the bulletin's own method section elsewhere misstating 1898 — see Section
E, item 10).
The money/pounds calculation, however, runs over **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922** (R02, R04,
R07 Item 6). These are different spans because the glass went into use partway through 1897 and
was condemned partway through 1923 — Keddie's own summary leaf marks "1897, part year" — so both
the first and last calendar years are partial and are excluded from the 25-year "full delivery
years" tally used for the pounds/dollars arithmetic, even though the glass itself was in use
across the longer 1897–1923 span.

**C5(b). Pounds of milk received in the first season (1896).**
Cannot be determined precisely from the sources. Curator Halbert Nym states explicitly that no
ledger figure exists: "the station's intake for its first season, 4 May to the end of 1896, is not
in the ledgers and is nowhere else... inquirers wanting a figure for 1896 must be told there is
none" (R07, Notes and queries). Two informal, non-ledger estimates exist and disagree: Bertram
Kyle recalls "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds... not in any book I ever ruled" (R23), while
Hobart Sill recalls "about nine hundred thousand pounds" (R14). Neither is documentary. The
sources establish only that no authoritative figure survives and that recollection puts the first
partial season somewhere between roughly 900,000 and 1,100,000 lb.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment.**
It ran from 8 May to 10 July 1911 — nine weeks (63 days) — established consistently across R03,
R04, R06, R09, R11, R17, and R22. The variable that changed was the measuring glass: the station's
own (defective) pipette broke and a measure borrowed from Larrow Green was substituted, then
withdrawn when the station's own replacement glass arrived. Held constant throughout: the same
patrons, the same herds, the same pasture and season overlap, the same acid, the same bottles, and
the same tester (Ansel Keddie) using the same technique. The result: the station's recorded
average test rose from 3.61 to 3.80 for the nine weeks and fell back to 3.61 the day the original
measure resumed. This establishes, by a real-time before/after/before comparison rather than by
retrospective arithmetic, that the discrepancy tracked the physical measuring instrument and
nothing else — ruling out explanations resting on the herds, the feed, the season, or the tester's
conduct, since none of those changed while the numbers moved twice, precisely in step with the
glass swap. A calculation performed on the 1958-recovered crate alone could show only what that
particular surviving glass delivered decades later; it could not, by itself, prove that swapping
the instrument was what moved the historical figures — the 1911 episode supplies that causal proof
contemporaneously, from inside the very years in question.
What it does **not** establish: it does not by itself identify the mechanism (a short pipette,
specifically, rather than some other apparatus defect such as a mismarked bottle neck); it does
not prove the same defect was present unchanged for the full 26 years, only that *a* difference in
measuring equipment produced *a* comparable-sized swing on this one occasion; and it does not
quantify the shortfall precisely — that required the 1958 physical measurement of the recovered
pipettes. (Roland Kepp's 1926 paper tries to dismiss the episode as merely "six weeks" of flush-
season variation — misstating the duration as well as its significance, against the well-
corroborated nine weeks.)

**D2. Independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream.**
(1) The physical cream record: buttermaker Hobart Sill, who received the station's cream daily at
Larrow Green for over forty years, testified that it consistently arrived "heavy" — containing
*more* fat than the station's own paperwork credited to patrons (R14). Theft of cream by the
tester would produce the opposite pattern (less arriving than credited).
(2) The Association's own ledgers, kept independently by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague from 1905 (and
matching entries reconstructed to 1898), record the identical fact from the institutional
accounting side: every year, more butterfat arrived at Larrow Green in the station's cream than
was credited to patrons on the station's test — the "station gain" line, running at a steady 5.25%
of credited fat (R05, R07 Item 6, R12).
(3) The 1911 natural experiment (D1): the same tester, under no different supervision, produced
sharply *higher* readings the moment a different instrument was substituted — inconsistent with a
deliberate, controlled skimming operation that a change of glass would not affect.
(4) Decades of independent, unannounced state inspections (Merle Strawn, from 1907) found the
scales true and, from 1908 onward, no added water and nothing amiss at every single visit (R06).
Reasons (1)/(2) and (3) are independent in kind — one is a physical/accounting record of fat
quantities, the other a controlled before/after comparison — and (4) is independent again, coming
from an outside official with no stake in the valley's quarrel. Reason (1)/(2), the "cream arrived
heavy" fact, would still hold even if the glassware had never been measured in 1958: Teague's
ledger entries existed from 1898 onward and Sill's observation dated from his own arrival in 1893,
decades before anyone connected either fact to a faulty pipette.
Did the bookkeeper ever question the entries that refute his own accusation? The sources indicate
no. Teague's own 1948 memoir (R05) presents the "station gain" figures and his theft-suspicion
theory side by side without ever noting that a *surplus* arriving at Larrow Green is inconsistent
with cream being stolen at the source — a surplus is what a competent skimming operation would
prevent, not produce. It was a different narrator, Dell Rundle, who caught this exact
inconsistency and explicitly retracted his own earlier theft suggestion once he examined the
ledger line himself (R12, "Note added while in press": "Cream stolen at the Hollow would have
reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph
stand").

**D3. Did the manager know, before 1923, that tests ran low?**
Yes. Bookkeeper Wilbur Teague states directly that he raised the discrepancy with manager Selby
Vose in 1916 and "was told to let it alone" (R05) — a specific, dated, first-person account from
someone with no evident motive to invent it against his former employer. Teague also states that
Vose signed the annual sheet carrying the "station gain" line every year of his tenure: "a man who
signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him." Vose's own 1926 address corroborates this
indirectly: he speaks of "figures going one way for twenty years" and says a manager who "sees his
figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing... is not being kind" — conceding, in his
own words, long awareness of the pattern, while disputing only its cause (he attributes it to
watering, not glass). Clerk Bertram Kyle's account complicates only the depth of Vose's scrutiny,
not his basic knowledge: Kyle recalls Vose reading "the totals" and signing "without reading the
lines above them" (R23) — consistent with knowing the pattern existed in a general way (from
Teague's direct 1916 warning and from twenty years of annual meetings) without having personally
parsed the ledger's line-by-line mechanics.

## Section E — Contradictions

1. **Total milk received, 1898–1922 (or "the years complained of").** 40,000,000 lb (Effie Loomis,
R02, citing Teague's own annual sheets, columns added twice; Halbert Nym's curatorial note, R07
Item 6, sourced directly from the ledgers; Bertram Kyle, R23, who personally extracted the figure
for the arbitration) vs. 44,000,000 lb (Wilbur Teague's own 1948 memoir, R05; Dell Rundle's
pamphlet, R12). Resolution: 40,000,000 lb, because it is the figure Bulletin No. 471 actually uses
in its own calculation (which reproduces exactly to 76,000 lb of shortfall) and it is attested by
three sources with direct ledger access, against two later, more informal recollections that
happen to agree with each other. **Two narrators share the same wrong value** (Teague, Rundle).

2. **Recorded average test.** 3.61 (Keddie's own test book, quoted verbatim identically in R03,
R04, R07 Item 3, and echoed in R22) vs. 3.16 (Wilbur Teague, R05; Alonzo Frick, R13). Resolution:
3.61, per a primary document quoted verbatim three times, most likely correct; 3.16 looks like a
digit transposition. **Two narrators share the same wrong value** (Teague, Frick).

3. **Mean delivery of the recovered pipettes.** 16.72 c.c. (Ivy Keddie's own measurement sheet,
quoted identically in R04 and R07 Item 14, and verified by averaging the nine listed readings) vs.
16.27 c.c. (Leland Vaught's Bulletin No. 471, R11 §III). Resolution: 16.72, by direct arithmetic
check against the primary data; the bulletin's figure is a single-source transposition error.

4. **Number of pipettes purchased.** Six dozen (the invoice itself, quoted identically in R07 Item
2 and R20; also R04, R19) vs. five dozen (Vaught's bulletin, R11 §III; Cleve Oram, R21).
Resolution: six dozen, per the invoice document quoted twice verbatim. **Two narrators share the
same wrong value** (Vaught, Oram).

5. **1925 award amount.** $3,800 (quoted verbatim in R02, R07 Item 7, R08, R12, R13, R22) vs.
$3,500 (Vira Toland, R01; Cleve Oram, R21). Resolution: $3,800, per the arbitration's own award
text, quoted identically six times. **Two narrators share the same wrong value** (Toland, Oram).

Sons' own history, R20; Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary entry of that exact date, R24) vs. 1
May 1898 (Vaught's bulletin, R11 §III; Bertram Kyle, R23). Resolution: 1897, by weight of three
largely independent, closer-to-the-event sources, including a live diary. **Two narrators share
the same wrong value** (Vaught, Kyle).

"nine families" and "eleven families" could in principle describe different counts (patrons of

note, R07 Item 4; Duncan Keddie, R18; Alonzo Frick's exhibit list, R13) vs. 1 January 1898 (Roy

not"). Resolution: 1897, by three largely institutional/documentary records against two family
recollections, one of which self-identifies as unreliable on years.

14. **Bulletin No. 214's finding on bog hay.** "Not more than five hundredths of one per cent in

corroborated by "the College's own trial" when it was not (see Section F).

15. **An apparent conflict that dissolves under closer reading.** Roy Brant states the Hollow-vs-
valley gap as "seventeen hundredths" (0.17, R09) while Hazel Brant and Ivy Keddie state it as
"nineteen hundredths" (0.19, R17, R04). These are not actually the same measurement: 0.17 is the
gap between the station's recorded average (3.61) and the Larrow Green creamery's recorded average
(3.78) — what farmers directly observed and were paid on (3.78 − 3.61 = 0.17) — while 0.19 is the
gap between the station's recorded average (3.61) and the true average (3.80) implied by the
pipette shortfall (3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19). Both figures are correct; they answer different questions.
This is offered as an example of a contradiction resolved by definition rather than by discarding
either source.

16. **Cleve Oram conflates two different figures.** Oram states that the 1958 investigation found
"sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat... that the Hollow men were never paid for" (R21)
immediately after describing Ivy Keddie's 1958 discovery. But 67,000 lb is Alonzo Frick's figure
for the patrons' own unproven claim at the 1925 hearing (R13); the actual 1958 scientific finding
was 76,000 lb (R04, R07 Item 15, R11, R19). Oram appears to have merged the two figures from
different stages of the story.

17. **Chronicle vs. bulletin's own arithmetic.** The Ashlin Chronicle states the bulletin's
shortfall was worth "$19,600" (R19), but the bulletin's own components (76,000 lb × $0.25/lb)

direct arithmetic; the Chronicle's figure appears to be a reporting error, possibly borrowed from
an unrelated $19,600 figure Dell Rundle's pamphlet attaches to the patrons' original 1925 petition
(R12).

18. **Full vs. half siblings.** Roy Brant states Orra and Emmet were "full brother and sister, same
mother and same father" (R09), explicitly pre-rejecting a stepmother story; his own daughter Hazel
Brant later calls Orra "a half sister... by his father's second wife" (R17). Cannot be determined
from the sources which is correct; noted in Section B2.

Resolved in Section B2. **Two narrators share the same wrong value** (Vira Toland, Cleve Oram).

## Section F — Theory

**The causal theory.** The Ostrey Hollow station's own butterfat-test pipettes — six dozen bought
new from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet in 1897, put into use 1 May 1897, each blown to deliver a
standard charge of 17.6 c.c. — were manufactured with a defect (per Ammon's own historian, most
plausibly a mold reground slightly undersized and never caught by routine spot-checks) that made
them deliver only about 16.72 c.c., roughly 95% of the marked charge. Because the test bottle's
graduated neck is calibrated to read fat percentage correctly only for the *full* marked charge,
every short charge produced a proportionally short reading, automatically, on every single test,
for as long as that pipette lot remained in service. Because the error was a fixed *proportion*
(about 5% low) rather than a fixed number of points, it fell hardest in absolute terms on the
richest milk while preserving the internal ranking of the station's own patrons relative to one
another — which is exactly why nobody inside the plant, checking scales, acid strength, or the
tester's technique, could ever catch it: the one instrument doing the actual measuring was never
itself checked against anything, because in ordinary practice the pipette *was* the standard. It
began precisely when the defective lot went into service (1 May 1897) and ended precisely when a
state inspector's newly issued burette — an outside standard, only available from 1921 and first
applied here in 1923 — caught it and the lot was condemned and replaced (12 June 1923). Nothing
about the cows, the feed, the land, the weather, or the tester's honesty needed to change for the
error to start or stop, because the sole operative variable was a piece of glass. This is not
established by 1958 arithmetic alone: the 1911 nine-week natural experiment (Section D1), in which
the station's own broken measure was replaced by a borrowed one for nine weeks with every other
factor held constant, shows the same relationship playing out in real time, decades before anyone
understood what they were seeing.

**The three competing explanations, who advanced each, and what defeats each:**

1. **Watering** — advanced by manager Selby Vose (his 1919 circular; his 1926 address). Defeated
by state inspector Merle Strawn's lactometer tests, which found no added water at essentially
every visit from 1908 onward (R06, R22); and by the fact that a fixed 5% proportional shortfall,
present equally in every month regardless of season, is not the signature of variable human
tampering. Bulletin No. 471 states flatly: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow."

2. **Cream theft by the tester** — advanced (by insinuation) by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague at the
1925 hearing, and repeated as valley gossip by Hobart Sill, Cleve Oram, and originally Dell
Rundle's pamphlet. Defeated by the very ledger entries Teague himself kept for decades, and by
Hobart Sill's independent daily observation, both showing that *more*, not less, fat consistently
arrived at Larrow Green than was credited to patrons (Section D2); and by the 1911 experiment,
where the same unsupervised tester produced *higher* readings the moment the instrument changed —
the opposite of what a deliberate skimming operation would produce. Bulletin No. 471: "no cream was
stolen there."

3. **Wet hay on Hessel Bottom** — advanced by the Association's 1908 committee under Nyle Grigg,
reaffirmed by Grigg's 1919 letter, and elaborated by veterinarian Roland Kepp's 1926 paper.
Defeated by the College's own controlled feeding trial, Bulletin No. 214, which found bog hay
depressed the test by at most 0.05 points in winter and nothing measurable in summer — far too
small (roughly a quarter of the 0.19-point gap, and the wrong shape, since the real gap was flat
across every month including summer) to account for it (Grigg's and Kepp's citation of this trial
as finding "two tenths" is itself a fourfold misstatement — see Section E, item 14); and directly
refuted by Rosalie Cudd's Alder Corners cross-check, showing the very same Hessel Bottom cows, on
the very same ground, testing about nineteen hundredths *higher* when hauled to the cheese factory
across the ridge than when hauled to Ostrey Hollow.

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?** No. Effie Loomis states this
explicitly: "The crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all... Not a word of it was spoken in
any of the three sessions" (R02, §8). Alonzo Frick's own exhibit list names only four items — the
test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book, and Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook — never
the crate or the pipettes themselves (R13). Cleve Oram independently recalls the crate sitting in
the loft directly above two of the hearing's sessions the entire time, unexamined (R21).

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?** His public position, in his sworn statement
and his unsent letter (R03), was that he did not know: "I cannot tell you why the figures are what
they are." Privately, the sources diverge: his son Duncan reports that Ansel came home in 1911
already saying "it was something in the glass," but would not commit an unproven suspicion to the
book (R18) — consistent with the bare question mark he left against the 1911 entry. Alonzo Frick,
separately, reports that Keddie told him privately in 1925 that he had "always thought the patrons
were at fault" (R13). The sources do not let this be fully reconciled — it may represent two
different private confidences at different times (an early hunch about the glass in 1911, a
different, vaguer suspicion voiced to the board's chairman fourteen years later after no answer
had emerged) — but they agree that his one committed, documented position, throughout, was that he
would not accuse anyone or claim an answer he could not prove.

## Section G — Summary

For decades, milk from Ostrey Hollow's six Hessel Bottom farms tested lower for butterfat than
milk anywhere else in the valley, costing those farmers money under the Association's pay-by-test
rule. Suspicion fell on the farmers (watering their milk), on the bottom's wet hay, and finally on
the station's own tester, Ansel Keddie, dismissed in 1925 after a hearing that paid the patrons
money without explaining why. He died in 1934, still suspected. In 1958 his granddaughter, a
graduate student, found the station's original 1897 test pipettes discarded in a loft and measured
them: they delivered five percent less milk than marked, so every test read five percent low, for
twenty-six years. Nobody had checked the glass, because the glass was what everything else was
checked against. It took a bookkeeper's ledgers, a rival cheese factory's figures, an accidental
nine-week experiment in 1911, and finally a burette to add it up.
