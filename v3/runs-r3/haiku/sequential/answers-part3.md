# Test Answers — Part 3

## Section C — Math (14 points)

### C1. The Glassware Error

(a) **By what proportion did the tests differ from the truth?**

The pipettes were blown on the stem at 17.6 c.c. but delivered a mean of 16.72 c.c. (Ivy Keddie's measurement, r04). The ratio is 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95. Therefore, the tests read at 95% of the truth, or 5% short. The sources consistently report "one part in twenty" (Bulletin 471 conclusion, r11), which is 1/20 = 0.05 = 5%.

(b) **What was the true average test, and by how many points were the recorded tests short?**

The recorded average at Ostrey Hollow over the affected years was 3.61%. If the pipettes delivered 95% of the true charge, the true average was 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80%. The shortfall was 3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19 percentage points (nineteen hundredths).

(c) **How many pounds of butterfat were the patrons not credited with?**

The station received 40,000,000 pounds of milk over the twenty-five full delivery years (1898–1922, per multiple sources including r05, r12). At the recorded average of 3.61%, the patrons were credited with 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 pounds of butterfat. At the true average of 3.80%, they delivered 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 pounds. The difference is 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 pounds of butterfat.

(d) **What was that worth in money, and what fraction of the total did the patrons receive?**

Butterfat averaged 25 cents per pound over the period (per r05, r12, r22). The lost fat was worth 76,000 × $0.25 = $19,000. The patrons were awarded $3,800 "without admission" (r02, r07, r10, r13). The fraction received was $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20, or one-fifth (20%). The patrons received one-fifth of what they had lost.

### C2. The Largest Patron's Year of 1910

(a) **How many pounds of butterfat was he credited with?**

The largest patron was Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom. His weigh book (r09, r17) shows for 1910: "Two hundred thousand pounds even, delivered to the station. Paid on a test of 3.42." The fat credited was 200,000 × 0.0342 = 6,840 pounds.

(b) **How many pounds was he owed and not credited?**

If the pipettes delivered 95% of truth, the true test of his milk was 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60 percentage points. The true butterfat was 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 pounds. The difference was 7,200 − 6,840 = 360 pounds of butterfat in 1910 alone.

(c) **What was the difference worth in money that year?**

At 25 cents per pound, 360 pounds = 360 × $0.25 = $90 for that single year. Over Emmet's lifetime hauling milk (roughly 1898–1929), this pattern repeated year after year.

---

### C3. Station Gain Over the Full Period

(a) **How many pounds of butterfat were credited to the station's patrons in total?**

This is the sum of what appeared in the ledgers under the station test—the recorded average of 3.61% applied to 40,000,000 pounds = 1,444,000 pounds of butterfat.

(b) **How many pounds actually reached the main creamery in the station's cream?**

The cream arrived at Larrow Green heavier than the stations records indicated. The bookkeeper Wilbur Teague (r05) recorded each year: "Butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb." The example shows 60,800 / 57,760 = 1.0525, or 5.25% gain. Applied to the total: 1,444,000 × 1.0525 = 1,520,000 pounds reached the creamery.

(c) **Express the difference as a percentage—be exact about what you are taking a percentage of.**

The difference is 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 pounds.

As a percentage of **credited fat** (1,444,000 lb): 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 0.0526 = 5.26% (approximately 5.25%, matching "five and a quarter percent").

As a percentage of **delivered fat** (1,520,000 lb): 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = 0.05 = 5% (exactly "one part in twenty").

Both are correct; the key is specifying which denominator.

### C4. The Invoice for the Testing Outfit

(a) **What was the total of the invoice?**

The invoice from Ammon & Sons, dated April 14, 1897 (r07, r20): six dozen milk test pipettes at $0.35 = $25.20; twelve dozen test bottles at $0.22 = $31.68; one acid measure = $0.90; one hand tester (4-bottle) = $18.00; freight = $2.15. **Total: $77.93** (confirmed in r07, r12, r19, r20).

(b) **How many pipettes did that invoice buy, and by how many cubic centimeters was each one short?**

The invoice bought **six dozen = 72 pipettes**. They were blown on the stem at 17.6 c.c. but delivered 16.72 c.c. The shortfall per pipette was 17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88 c.c.** per pipette.

### C5. Duration and First Season

(a) **For exactly how long was the faulty glassware in use, and over how many full delivery years does the money calculation run? State both.**

The faulty glass was in use from **May 1, 1897 (first use at the station) to June 12, 1923 (condemnation)**: 26 years and 6 weeks, or approximately **26.5 years** of actual use.

The money calculation (40 million pounds, 3.61 average) runs over **25 full delivery years**: 1898–1922 (r05, r12, r22). The station opened in May 1896 but did not have a full year until 1897. The first season (May 1896–December 1896) is not included in the station column of the ledgers; instead, the milk was carried on the creamery's own books. The 25 full years exclude both 1897 (partial) and 1923 (partial, from Jan to June 12).

Why the discrepancy? The glass was in use for 26.5 years, but only 25 of those were complete delivery years recorded in the ledgers against which a financial loss can be calculated.

(b) **How many pounds of milk did the station receive in its first season?**

The station opened May 4, 1896. No figure is recorded in the Association's ledgers for 1896. Bertram Kyle, the clerk (r23), says: "A good eleven hundred thousand pounds, which is what I understood at the time from men who were there. It is not in any book I ever ruled." The first-season milk is **not recorded in any surviving document**; the estimate of "about 900,000 pounds" (r21, Cleve Oram) and "about 1,100,000 pounds" (r23, Kyle) are the only available figures, both from memory.

## Section D — Logic (10 points)

### D1. The 1911 Equipment Interval as an Experiment

**When and how long:** May 8 to July 10, 1911 (exactly nine weeks, per r03, r09, r11).

**The variable:** The station's own milk-test measure (pipette) was broken on May 8. A measure borrowed from Larrow Green was used in its place. The station's own measure was returned to use on July 10.

**Held constant:** The herds, the pasture, the season (June–July flush), the acid, the test bottles, the hand tester, the operator (Ansel Keddie), his method, his speed, and the supervision (none).

**The result:** During the nine weeks with the borrowed measure, the average test stood at 3.80. Before and after, it stood at 3.61. The day the station's own measure came back, the average fell back to 3.61.

**What it establishes:** This interval establishes, beyond calculation, that the gap was caused by the measuring glass alone. No feature of the herds, land, season, method, or operator could have changed in nine weeks. The borrowed measure delivered the true charge; the station's own measure delivered short. Vaught (r11, section IV) emphasizes: "I have spent my working life urging students to prefer an experiment to a calculation. Here is one that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven years."

**What it does not establish:** It does not establish *which* measure was right or wrong—only that they were different. It does not identify the mechanism of the error (broken mold, deliberate short-blowing, reground mold, etc.). It does not establish how long the error had persisted before 1911 or after 1911 (though context shows it was there from the beginning and persisted until 1923 condemnation).

---

### D2. Why the Tester Cannot Have Been Stealing Cream

**Independent reason 1** (Cream receipts outweighed station credits): The receiving book at Larrow Green (where the cream arrived after hauling) showed that more fat came down the hill than the station had credited the patrons with (r05, r12, r23). Hobart Sill, the buttermaker, weighed it himself: "That cream ran heavy. Every year it ran heavy. There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there... I weighed it over again when it came into the cold room" (r14). If the tester had removed cream and reported low tests, the cream arriving at the creamery should have been *lighter* than reported, not heavier. This contradicts the theft theory directly.

**Independent reason 2** (The 1911 equipment swap): During the nine weeks when a borrowed measure was used, Ansel made the same tests with the same hands, but the results went up to 3.80 and fell back to 3.61 when his own measure returned (r03, r09, r11). If Ansel had been stealing cream and reporting low to cover it, why would the borrowed measure eliminate the alleged theft? The only explanation is that the difference was in the glass, not in the man. This refutes the theft accusation from first principles.

**Independent reason 3** (Alder Corners overlay): Four Hessel Bottom men hauled over the ridge to a cheese factory at Alder Corners from 1915–1930. Rosalie Cudd (r10) tested the same milk: it came in at 3.78–3.82 across sixteen summers, not 3.61. The same farmers' milk tested 20 hundredths higher at Alder Corners than at Ostrey Hollow. If Ansel had been stealing cream from these men at Ostrey Hollow, the cream reaching Larrow Green should have been lighter. But it wasn't—it was heavier.

**Why they are independent:** Reason 1 rests on the receiving book at Larrow Green. Reason 2 rests on the 1911 natural experiment with a different instrument. Reason 3 rests on an entirely separate facility in a different county. None depends on the others; they converge on the same conclusion from different data.

**Which would hold if the glassware was never measured in 1958:** Reasons 1 and 3 both stand entirely without the 1958 measurements. The cream receipts and Alder Corners test overlay prove the tester innocent regardless of what happened to the pipettes. Reason 2 (the 1911 swap) would also hold but becomes stronger *with* the glass measurements.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question his entries:** Wilbur Teague (r05) testified before the board: "Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons... I do not say what any particular person did on any particular day... I say that a business which is arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken from it." However, Teague also says: "I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone. I let it alone." He saw the station gain (the cream arriving heavier, the credits lighter) and he questioned it—once—to the manager. The manager told him to drop it. Teague complied and never spoke of it again for nearly a decade, until the arbitration forced his hand.

### D3. Did the Manager Know the Tests Were Low?

**Answer:** Yes, Selby Vose almost certainly knew, or had reason to know, that the station's tests ran low.

**Justification:** Vose signed the annual ledger sheets every year from 1913 onward (r05, r23). Each sheet carried the "station gain" line: fat credited to patrons on the station test vs. fat received in cream at Larrow Green. Year after year, the cream arrived 5.25% heavier than the station's figures. A manager who signed such a sheet—who "put his thumb on the bottom figure" and signed (r23)—need not understand the technical reason for the discrepancy, but he could not be unaware of its *existence*.

Teague approached Vose in 1916 (r05): "I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone." This proves Vose knew something was amiss. What he was told and what he thought are unclear. But he was the manager; he saw the ledger sheets; he knew there was a persistent surplus appearing on the station account.

Why did he not investigate? The sources offer hints: The 1919 water circular suggests he blamed the patrons. In his 1926 address (r08), Vose blamed watering and said the tests went up "when the looking began." He may have genuinely believed in the water theory. Or he may have chosen not to press further—the station was profitable to the Association (the "station gain" went to the general account). He signed the sheets. He did nothing.

## Section E — Contradictions (8 points)

### E1. Factual Contradictions

**Contradiction 1: The award amount**

- Cleve Oram (r21) says: "They gave the patrons at the end of it was thirty-five hundred dollars."
- Multiple authoritative sources (r02, r05, r07, r10, r13, r22, r24) say $3,800.

**Resolution:** The authoritative sources, especially the minute book of the arbitration board itself (r02, r07: "The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00)"), outrank a single narrator's memory. Cleve Oram was seventy-seven in 1977, recalling 1925; he had the figure slightly wrong. **Correct answer: $3,800.**

**Method used:** Documentary evidence (the board's own record, the Association's ledgers) outranks a single narrator's recollection. Arithmetic confirms: $3,800 is one-fifth of $19,000 (the true loss), which is what multiple sources report.

---

**Contradiction 2: The glass condemnation date**

- Hazel Brant (r17) says: "The state man condemned that glass in twenty-one" (implying 1921).
- Merle Strawn's circuit book (r06, r09, r20, r22) clearly states "**1923, June 12**."

**Resolution:** Strawn was the inspector, writing in his official notebook contemporaneously (r06). Hazel Brant, speaking in 1981 about events from 1923, had the year wrong by two years. Dates in official records outrank dates in memory. **Correct answer: June 12, 1923.**

---

**Contradiction 3: Ivy Keddie's relationship (see Section B1)**

- Ashlin Chronicle (r19): "great-niece"
- County agent Falke (r22, added note): "granddaughter"

**Resolution:** Duncan Keddie's family notes (r18) explicitly trace the lineage: Ansel → Duncan → Ivy (three generations, grandmother-father-granddaughter). The county agent corrected the newspaper error. **Correct answer: granddaughter.**

---

**Contradiction 4: The pipette mean delivery**

- Ivy Keddie's measurement sheet (r04, r07 item 14): "Mean 16.72 c.c."
- Vaught, Bulletin 471 (r11, section III and V): "mean delivery of the nine is **16.27 c.c.**" and "16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244"

**Resolution:** Ivy's nine measurements (16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71) average to 16.72 c.c. Vaught's figure of 16.27 is inconsistent with Ivy's data. However, Vaught's conclusion states "one part in twenty" (5% error, 95% delivery), which aligns with Ivy's 16.72/17.6 = 0.95. The sources are internally inconsistent. Vaught's arithmetic (0.9244) does not match his conclusion ("one part in twenty" = 0.95). Ivy's direct measurement of 16.72 c.c. is the most concrete evidence, and her arithmetic (16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95) is internally consistent. The loss of 76,000 pounds (Ivy's calculation) also matches Vaught's conclusion statement but not his section V arithmetic. **This is an error in the sources themselves that cannot be fully resolved.** I prefer Ivy's measurement (16.72 c.c., 95% delivery) as the primary data, but note the discrepancy with Vaught's stated arithmetic.

**Contradiction 5: The three theories before the board**

Multiple sources (r13, r05) agree on three theories: water (Vose), theft (Teague's implication), and hay (1908 committee). **No contradiction found.**

**Contradiction 6: Gideon Vose's relationship to Selby Vose**

- Rumor (mentioned by Cleve Oram, r21): "The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort."
- Duncan Keddie (r18): "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses."
- Bertram Kyle (r23): Selby is Gideon's son, not nephew.

**Resolution:** Kyle and Keddie agree: Selby was Gideon's son (not nephew). There was no blood relationship between the Keddies and the Voses. Orra Keddie's cousin was married to Gideon (old Mrs. Vose), but that is different from the Keddies being cousins to the Voses. Duncan is clear: his mother Orra would have told him, and she did not. The Vose claim is rumor. **No actual contradiction; the record is clear once Duncan's correction is accepted.**

**Contradiction 7: When the 1911 measure broke**

The glass I began on in ninety-six was borrowed off the Ashlin creamery, and we used it until our own outfit came up the valley in a crate the spring following." Then: "Our measure broke on the eighth of May and I made shift with one borrowed, and from that morning the tests came up and stood at 3.80, and on the tenth of July our own was in use again."

**No contradiction:** Ansel is clear. 1896 they used borrowed glass. Spring 1897 their own came. 1911 May 8 their own broke. July 10 repaired own returned. No contradiction.

**Contradiction 8: The two narrators repeating wrong values**

## Section F — Theory (6 points)

### F1. The Causal Theory

**The single coherent causal theory:**

Ostrey Hollow station's tests read low due to a systematic instrumental error, not to any action or negligence by the farmers, the tester, or the manager. In April 1897, Ammon & Sons sold the Association a set of milk-test pipettes, blown on the stem at 17.6 cubic centimeters. The pipettes manufactured delivered approximately 16.72 c.c., not 17.6 c.c.—a 5% shortfall. When a charge 5% short is drawn into a test bottle marked for the full charge, the resulting fat reading is 5% short in exact proportion. This error was consistent and proportional from May 1897 through June 1923. It affected every patron identically (in proportion to their milk's actual fat): no patron tested "better" or "worse" relative to another in this system. The gap grew to be constant at 19 hundredths (3.61 recorded vs. 3.80 true) because the quality of milk was objectively stable; the instrumental error was unchanging. 

The size of the error (5%) followed directly from the glassware's delivery rate (95%). The error began when the faulty glass was installed (May 1, 1897). The error ended when the state inspector condemned the glass and new glass was put in use (June–October 1923). Nothing at the station or on the farms had to change: the tester did not change his method, the farmers did not improve their herds or their land, the season did not shift. The error simply stopped because the instrument was replaced.

The patrons lost 76,000 pounds of butterfat (worth $19,000) because they were paid on low figures for every pound they delivered. This loss appeared on the Association's own ledgers as "station gain"—the surplus fat arriving at Larrow Green but not credited to the patrons. The surplus was then credited to the Association's general account. In effect, the patrons' error subsidized the creamery and the Association's operations.

**The three competing explanations and the evidence that defeats each:**

1. **Water (Selby Vose's theory):** The manager claimed that water was being added to cans at Ostrey Hollow. *Specific evidence that defeats it:* (a) The state dairy inspector, Merle Strawn, tested for added water (using a lactometer) at every visit from 1908 forward and never found any (r06, r07, r22). (b) The 1911 equipment swap: for nine weeks, with the same patrons and the same tester, tests rose to 3.80 and fell back when the station's own measure returned. If water was the cause, changing the measure would not have changed the tests. (c) Four Hessel Bottom patrons hauled the same milk over the ridge to Alder Corners and it tested 3.78–3.82, not 3.61; if they were adding water, cheese would not have been made (Rosalie Cudd states: "A cheesemaker knows watered milk inside a day... In sixteen summers my father never made one complaint," r10).

2. **Theft (Wilbur Teague's suggestion):** The tester removed cream and wrote low tests to cover the loss. *Specific evidence that defeats it:* (a) The receiving book at Larrow Green showed that more fat arrived than the station had credited—the opposite of what theft would leave (r05, r12, r23). (b) The 1911 equipment swap: the same tester, the same hands, but with a borrowed measure, produced correct tests and then returned to incorrect tests when his own measure came back. If theft was the cause, the equipment change would make no difference. The evidence is definitive. (c) A man who had tested for 29 seasons, whose record book was perfect, who tested in front of the state inspector and "the state man saw him do it," who never asked for extra pay, extra credit, or favorable consideration—it is implausible that he stole cream and was never caught or suspected until the Association needed a scapegoat.

3. **Wet hay (1908 committee and Dr. Kepp's theory):** The wet meadow of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream. *Specific evidence that defeats it:* (a) The State Agricultural College's own feeding trial (Bulletin 214) found bog hay depressed butterfat by "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Five hundredths cannot account for a nineteen-hundredths gap that runs flat across every season. (b) The 1911 equipment swap: the same hay, the same herds, but a different measure produced correct tests. The hay was not the cause. (c) The Alder Corners milk of the same farms tested higher there, showing that the farms' milk was not inherently lower in fat. (d) Over 50 years of records show no correlation between wet years and worse tests, or dry years and better tests. The gap was the same in wet years as in dry years, in the winter on stored hay and in the summer on pasture.

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?**

No. The condemned crate was placed in the loft in June 1923 and sat there untouched for thirty-five years. The arbitration board heard exhibits in March–May 1925 (r13). Alonzo Frick lists four exhibits: the station test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book, and the state inspector's 1923 circuit notebook (r13). The condemned glassware itself—the physical evidence—was never brought before the board, never examined, and never mentioned in any testimony. Effie Loomis (r02, section 8) notes: "The second is the crate. The crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all... Forty years of taking down what men say has taught me that the interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying." Bertram Kyle (r23) adds: "Four books... and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked." Nobody connected the inspector's notebook (which mentioned the condemnation) to the glassware itself.

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?**

Ansel Keddie believed the patrons were at fault. In his private corridor conversation with Alonzo Frick (r13): "In the corridor at Ashlin, Keddie told me privately that he had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the room. He was not a bitter man about it. He had lived among them thirty years and would not stand up and accuse his neighbors." 

However, Ansel suspected the glass in 1911. Duncan Keddie (r18) recalls: "The day began... Ansel came home in the summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book. I was seventeen and I remember the kitchen and I remember that he said it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not his way." But Ansel was a man who "would not put down what he could not prove." He only wrote a question mark. For fourteen years after 1911, he lived with his suspicion about the glass and his public silence about it, and he never resolved the contradiction between his private hunch and his professional inability to prove it.

## Section G — Summary (4 points)

### G1. The Eight-Story History in 150 Words

From 1889 to 1958, the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association operated a milk-testing station at Ostrey Hollow under Article VII: patrons were paid on butterfat test made at their delivery station. From 1897 onward, milk at that station tested 5% lower than elsewhere—consistently, across decades. The Association blamed watering (1919 circular), a committee blamed wet hay (1908), the tester was suspected of theft (1925 arbitration). None was true.

In 1911, a broken measure was replaced for nine weeks; tests rose to truth, then fell again when the station's own measure returned. The evidence lay unread for forty-seven years. In 1923, a state inspector condemned the pipettes; they delivered 95% of their marked capacity. In 1958, Ivy Keddie found the condemned crate in a loft, measured the pipettes, and proved the error was instrumental, not human. The tester, Ansel Keddie (dismissed in 1925), was cleared posthumously in 1958—thirty-three years too late. The gap required eight separate records to understand: because each narrator saw only their corner of a story the full geometry of which was invisible to any one person.
