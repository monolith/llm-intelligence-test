# Test Answers — Part 1

## Section A — Reconstruction (Eight Stories, Stories 1–4)

### Story 1: The Founding of the Larrow Valley Association and Article VII (1889–1892)

The farmers of Larrow Valley, tired of hauling butter to merchants and receiving whatever price was offered, met at Larrow Green in January 1889 and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose, a heavy, quiet, obstinate man, was elected president and drafted the by-laws himself at his kitchen table in a farmer's rough hand.

The central innovation was Article VII, adopted at the annual meeting of 1892 (the year after the creamery opened): "Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." This replaced the old system of payment by weight, which paid no account to quality. Article VII was just but had a hinge: the test at each delivery station would govern—not the average, not a central test, not an appeal. One man's test in one room would determine each patron's pay for a generation.

In 1891, the Association built its main creamery at Larrow Green, on Larrow Creek below the mill, equipped with a churn and the machinery of butter-making. The creamery ended the chaos of independent sales. From that year forward, farmers received one check each month based on their butterfat credits.

The significance of Article VII and the test-governs rule is that it made the tester's accuracy absolute. In the fifty years that followed, no patron had recourse to dispute a test made at his station—the test was the law. This put immense responsibility on the tester and immense temptation in the tester's hands, because one man sitting in a room by himself, moving cans and drawing samples and writing numbers, had no oversight. Gideon Vose died in 1917, three years before the consequences of his own by-law became apparent.

**Key documents:** r02 (Effie Loomis, secretary's account of Article VII and by-laws); r05 (Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper, on payment by test); r12 (Dell Rundle, fifty-year pamphlet, on the founding and Article VII's language).

**Quantities that matter:** One by-law, eight words: "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."

### Story 2: The Opening of Ostrey Hollow Station and Its Purpose (1895–1896)

The farms of Hessel Bottom and the head of Ostrey Hollow were seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green, a journey of three hours in mud and impassable in March. To serve these farmers and avoid the long haul, the Association built a skimming station in the winter of 1895–1896 on the flat by the creek at Ostrey Hollow.

The station opened on the 4th of May, 1896. It was a board building, two stories, with a low covered platform on the road side, so a wagon could back up and the farmer could roll his cans off level without lifting them. Inside was the intake room: a scale, a vat, and a separator. A man weighed the milk, drew a sample for each patron's jar, tested the sample for its butterfat, and separated the milk. The cream went into cans to be hauled down to Larrow Green each day. The skim was handed back to the farmer for his hogs and calves.

Over the intake room was a loft, accessible by a ladder with a rope on the trap door. This loft was empty except for odds and ends—broken equipment, wire, salt.

The Association hired Ansel Keddie as tester and separator man. Keddie went to the station to prepare it in 1895, at age thirty-four, tested the first milk that summer, and remained there every season for the next twenty-nine years. He was a careful man in small things, meticulous about his records. The first season the station took in roughly 900,000 pounds of milk (May to December). The seven farms with rights on Hessel Bottom brought their milk to this station instead of driving it seven miles to Larrow Green.

**Key documents:** r01 (Vira Toland, recollection of the station's appearance and Ansel Keddie's method); r09 (Roy Brant, on the station and the Bottom farms); r21 (Cleve Oram, cream hauler's son, on the station building and loft).

**Quantities that matter:** Seven miles from Larrow Green; seven farms (later six, after some went over the ridge); first season ~900,000 pounds of milk; one tester (Ansel Keddie) from opening to dismissal.

### Story 3: The Test Gap Emerges and Explanations Multiply (1902–1920)

From about 1902, the milk brought to Ostrey Hollow station tested consistently lower than the milk brought to Larrow Green or other stations in the valley. The gap ran at approximately seventeen to nineteen hundredths of a point—seventeen-hundredths or nineteen-hundredths—year in and year out, in summer and winter, in wet years and dry ones. The recorded average at Ostrey Hollow was 3.61; the ordinary valley average was 3.78–3.80.

Farmers began to complain in 1902. Emmet Brant, the largest producer on Hessel Bottom, was among the first to question it, and the complaints escalated through the decade. The money loss was steady: over a quarter-century, a farmer on the Bottom might lose a thousand dollars to that gap.

The Association's officers could not ignore the complaints at the annual meetings. In 1908, they appointed a committee of three disinterested farmers, chaired by Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green, to investigate. The committee went to Hessel Bottom in January, February, and March, sampled the herds themselves, tried the scales, watched the tests, and found no dishonesty anywhere. Their report concluded that the wet hay of Hessel Bottom—sedge, rush, coarse tussock grass cut late and fed as the winter staple—thinned the cream. The remedy, they said, lay with the land, not with the station. The Committee was accepted and closed the question for eleven years.

But the question was not closed. In 1919, the Association's manager, Selby Vose, became convinced that water was being added to the cans at Ostrey Hollow. He circulated a printed circular to nine families warning them against adding water to milk and saying the Association could not pay for creek water. Ansel Keddie himself delivered the circular, standing at the door of each patron's house while they read it. Emmet Brant, seeing this, confronted Ansel: if you believe we're innocent, why did you carry the paper yourself? Ansel answered that he would not let another man carry it. Emmet left and did not enter Ansel's house again for ten years—not until 1929, when he was dying.

**Key documents:** r01 (Vira Toland, on the scandal and the 1919 circular); r09 (Roy Brant, on the constant gap and the 1908 committee); r15 (Nyle Grigg, 1908 report with 1919 covering letter); r24 (Orra Keddie diary, entry on the circular delivery and Emmet's confrontation); r13 (Alonzo Frick, on the three theories including watering).

**Quantities that matter:** Gap of 17–19 hundredths, constant across seasons; 3.61 average recorded at Ostrey Hollow vs. 3.78–3.80 elsewhere; 25 years of complaint; circular sent to nine families (six farms but some with multiple households).

### Story 4: The 1911 Equipment Failure as a Natural Experiment (May–July 1911)

On the 8th of May, 1911, the station's own milk-test measure—the pipette used to draw the standard charge of milk into the bottle for testing—broke. Ansel Keddie made shift with a measure borrowed from the creamery at Larrow Green, using it for nine weeks until the 10th of July, when the station's own measure arrived back from repair or replacement.

During those nine weeks, with the exact same herds, the same pasture, the same season (June and July, the flush), the same acid, the same bottles, the same tester (Ansel), and the same operator (Ansel), the tests rose sharply and held steady at 3.80. When the station's own measure came back into use, the tests fell back to 3.61 the same day. Ansel noted both events in his test book and marked the return with a question mark he had never explained in the fourteen years since.

This interval revealed that the gap was not caused by the cows, the pasture, the season, the land, the water supply, the acid, the bottles, or the tester's method. One variable changed—the instrument—and the figures changed in exact proportion. The borrowed measure delivered the standard charge; the station's own measure delivered less.

In 1911, neither Ansel nor any officer of the Association made the inference. The inspector was not called. The question mark sat in the book. Years later, scientists would understand that this nine-week interval held the entire answer, but in 1911 it was merely noted as an anomaly and set aside.

**Key documents:** r03 (Ansel Keddie's unsent letter, describing 1911 in detail and noting the equipment swap); r09 (Roy Brant, on his father's observation of the rise and fall); r11 (Leland Vaught, Bulletin 471, treating this as a decisive experiment); r18 (Duncan Keddie, on his father coming home and saying "something in the glass" but not recording it).

**Quantities that matter:** May 8 to July 10, 1911 (nine weeks); tests at 3.80 during the interval, 3.61 before and after; the gap closed to zero during those nine weeks; no other variable changed; one object changed.

## Section A — Reconstruction (Stories 5–8)

### Story 5: The 1923 Equipment Condemnation (June–October 1923)

In the summer of 1923, the state dairy inspector, Merle Strawn, made his annual circuit visit to Ostrey Hollow station. Strawn had been on this circuit since 1907 and carried with him a new tool: a calibrated burette, issued to field men beginning in 1921 for the purpose of checking whether pipettes and measures delivered their marked capacity. Most plants had never been checked this way; a pipette is ordinarily the standard by which plants measure, and an instrument cannot be verified against itself.

On the 12th of June, 1923, Strawn ran the station's milk pipettes against his burette. The glass would not deliver its mark. Every pipette fell short. He condemned the entire crate on the spot and told the manager, Selby Vose, to order new equipment.

Vose acted quickly. On the 28th of June, the Association wrote to Ammon & Sons, the dairy supply house at Tarnet, ordering six dozen milk pipettes and six dozen test bottles. Ammon & Sons answered on the 2nd of July, acknowledging the order, promising shipment that month, and saying only: "We regret the trouble. Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name." The new glass arrived and was in use by October.

From October 1923 onward, the tests at Ostrey Hollow rose and stayed at 3.80–3.85, the level they should have been at all along. No explanation was given then or sought. Strawn drew no inference about the years behind it. Vose did not question why his own careful accounting had carried a wrong figure for a quarter-century. The condemned glass was carried up into the loft over the intake room, where it sat under a tarpaulin for the next thirty-five years.

**Key documents:** r06 (Merle Strawn's circuit book entries, June and October 1923); r20 (Junia Ammon's company history, with reproduction of the 1923 correspondence and invoice from 1897); r18 (Duncan Keddie, noting his father never knew the cause); r21 (Cleve Oram, on the crate in the loft).

**Quantities that matter:** June 12, 1923 (condemnation date); October 1923 (new glass in use); 35 years in the loft (1923–1958); the original outfit dated April 14, 1897 (invoice); six dozen pipettes bought in 1897, nine surviving in 1958.

### Story 6: The 1925 Arbitration and Ansel Keddie's Dismissal (January–May 1925)

In January 1925, the patrons of Ostrey Hollow petitioned the Association under its by-laws for arbitration. Their claim: that they had been paid consistently less than the milk was worth, and they wanted to be made whole. The Association did not resist. A board of three arbitrators was constituted, chaired by Alonzo Frick, a lawyer of Ashlin, known as a fair man. The board sat at the Association office at Ashlin on the 17th of March, heard testimony, and then held two more sittings at the station itself, in the weigh room, so that the machinery, scales, and books could be before the board.

Three explanations were heard before the board:

(1) **Water.** The manager, Selby Vose, argued that water was being added to cans at Ostrey Hollow. He cited his 1919 circular as evidence that he had warned against it. This was urged without proof.

(2) **Theft.** The bookkeeper, Wilbur Teague, at length described how a business arranged with only one man could sooner or later have a little taken from it, implying that the tester had removed cream and written low tests to hide the loss. He made no direct accusation but left the inference unmistakable.

(3) **The Hay.** Someone read into the record the report of the 1908 committee, blaming the wet hay of Hessel Bottom for thinning the cream. This had the advantage of blaming nobody in the room.

Ansel Keddie made a statement on the 17th of March. He said: he had tested as he was taught and as the state man had seen him do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons; he could not tell the board why the figures were what they were; he had never taken a pound of cream out of that station; and he believed the patrons were at fault (though he would not say so in the room, as he later told Frick in private).

On the 30th of April, four days before the board handed down its award, the Association dismissed Ansel. He came home at his ordinary hour, hung his apron behind the shed, and asked what there was for supper. His wife Orra said ham. He ate it.

On the 4th of May, the board found no cause established against the station, the office, or any officer or servant. It awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800 "without admission," charged to the station account. The amount was roughly one-fifth of what the patrons claimed to have lost. No explanation was ever given for why a payment was made if no cause was established.

Ansel Keddie was seventy-one years old. He never worked at the station again. He took up carpentry. He died in 1934 at age seventy-three, still under the imputation of the valley's suspicion, still unaccused and undefended.

**Key documents:** r03 (Ansel's statement and letter); r13 (Alonzo Frick's recollection, with the exhibit list and his note of Ansel's corridor remark); r05 (Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper's explanation of the station-gain line); r08 (Selby Vose's 1926 annual address, defending his water theory); r24 (Orra Keddie's diary, entries for March 17 and April 30, 1925).

**Quantities that matter:** Jan 1925 petition; March 17 – May 4 arbitration (less than two months); April 30 dismissal (4 days before award); $3,800 awarded; Ansel dismissed after 29 seasons; four exhibits presented to the board.

### Story 7: The 1958 Discovery and Investigation (February–October 1958)

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, went to the old Ostrey Hollow station building to gather materials for a research project on the station's history. The building had been closed for years and used for storage of farm equipment. There was a loft over what had been the intake room, reached by a ladder. Under a tarpaulin, covered in dust and chaff, she found a wooden crate stenciled with the name of the dairy supply company: Ammon & Sons, Tarnet.

The crate held nine unbroken milk-test pipettes. They were the original equipment, bought in 1897, condemned by the state inspector in June 1923, and left there for thirty-five years. Nobody had given them a thought.

Ivy carried the crate east to Farland. On the 9th of March, 1958, she measured the nine pipettes against a calibrated burette, taking five runs from each and averaging. The measurement sheet showed:

16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 (in cubic centimeters)

Mean: 16.72 c.c.

The pipettes were blown on the stem at 17.6 c.c. They delivered 16.72 c.c. The shortfall was 0.88 c.c., which is exactly 95 percent of the mark.

Ivy did the arithmetic: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95, which means the pipettes delivered 95 percent of the standard charge. A charge 5 percent short, drawn into a bottle marked for a full charge, gives a test reading 5 percent short. Over the affected years, 40,000,000 pounds of milk was received at the station. The recorded average test was 3.61 percent. The true average was 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80 percent. The gap was 19 hundredths. The patrons were not credited with 40,000,000 × 0.0019 = 76,000 pounds of butterfat.

In October 1958, Professor Leland Vaught of the college's department of dairy husbandry, working with Ivy, published the findings as Bulletin No. 471 of the State Agricultural College. The conclusion: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

Ansel Keddie had been dead for twenty-four years. Orra had died ten years before that, in 1948.

**Key documents:** r04 (Ivy Keddie's 1959 talk, with measurement sheet and calculations); r11 (Leland Vaught, Bulletin 471, introduction and method); r19 (Ashlin Chronicle front page, Oct 1958, "Old Ordell Wrong Righted"); r22 (Orin Falke, 1959 county agent report).

**Quantities that matter:** February 1958 (crate found); March 9, 1958 (measurements taken); October 1958 (Bulletin 471 published); 16.72 c.c. mean delivery; 0.95 ratio; 3.80 true average; 76,000 pounds lost fat; $19,000 value at 25 cents per pound; 35 years of the crate in the loft.

### Story 8: The Vindication and Its Limits (October 1958–1964)

The publication of Bulletin 471 in October 1958 made clear to anyone who read it that Ansel Keddie had been blameless. He had tested as he was taught. He had done so in front of the state's own inspector. He had kept meticulous records. The fault lay in a piece of glass that sat over his head in a loft for thirty-five years, invisible to him, invisible to the board, invisible to every accountant and inspector until a young woman thirty-three years after his dismissal climbed a ladder.

The Ashlin Chronicle ran the story on its front page: "Old Ordell Wrong Righted." The county agent prepared a formal report. The Historical Society began collecting the documents. Bulletin 471 was read. Ivy gave a talk at the Historical Society in 1959. But in the valley itself, the vindication moved slowly.

Emmet Brant had died in 1929, ten years after his last conversation with Ansel. He never knew he was wrong, and never knew the truth. But his son Roy Brant, and his granddaughter Hazel, knew it. They had the weigh book. They had always believed in Ansel.

Ansel's son Duncan Keddie, a retired schoolteacher, wrote out family notes in 1968 to set the record straight. He noted that Ansel, in 1911, had come home from the station and said "something in the glass," but he was a man who would not put down what he could not prove, so he only wrote a question mark. Duncan kept his father's unsent letter for the archives.

In 1964, Orra's diary was published as a small book. It showed, month by month and entry by entry, a woman bearing the weight of her husband's reputation in a small valley, and her brother's silence. "My brother Emmet came up... and came home at eleven at night with his hat still on."

By the time the truth was told in full, no action could be taken. The Association's account was long closed. All parties were dead. Ansel was cleared of a charge nobody had ever formally made. The valley had its answer, but no remedy.

**Key documents:** r19 (Ashlin Chronicle, front page, with Ivy's quoted statement); r18 (Duncan Keddie's 1968 family notes); r22 (Orin Falke's 1959 report); r24 (Orra Keddie's diary, 1964 publication); r11 (Vaught's Bulletin 471 conclusion).

**Quantities that matter:** October 1958 publication; 33 years between dismissal (1925) and vindication (1958); 24 years between death (1934) and vindication; Ansel never asked for clearance; the valley had no legal remedy; Orra died in 1948, 10 years before the truth emerged.

## Section B — Relationships (8 points)

### B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Ansel (born 1861) married Orra Brant in 1889. Their son was Duncan Keddie (born 1894). Duncan had a daughter, Ivy, born in 1931. Thus: Ansel → Duncan → Ivy (three generations). This is confirmed by Duncan Keddie's own 1968 family notes (r18), which state plainly: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter." The Ashlin Chronicle (r19) initially called her a "great-niece," but that was an error; the county agent Orin Falke (r22) corrected this in his 1959 report's added note: "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece."

---

### B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant?

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. Hazel's grandfather Emmet Brant and Ivy's grandmother Orra were brother and sister (confirmed by Roy Brant in r09: "Orra Keddie was my father's sister"). Emmet's son Roy had a daughter Hazel. Duncan had a daughter Ivy. Therefore: Emmet and Orra (siblings) → Roy and Duncan (first cousins, children of those siblings) → Hazel and Ivy (second cousins, children of first cousins). This is confirmed by Hazel Brant herself (r17): "Ivy Keddie and I are second cousins." Duncan Keddie's family notes (r18) are explicit: "Roy Brant and I are therefore first cousins... Roy's daughter is Hazel Brant. My daughter is Ivy... Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins."

The sources note that people often got this wrong, calling them "cousins once removed" (wrong—that skips a generation) or "aunt and niece" (wrong—different generations for the roles). Cleve Oram (r21) gets it right but adds confusion: he calls Ivy "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed," which is incorrect; the sources consistently say second cousins. However, Hazel and Ivy overlap in time without spanning more generations, so the correct answer remains: second cousins.

---

### B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter?

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose, the founder and first president of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (served 1889–1911, twenty-two years). Bertram Kyle, the office clerk (r23), states firmly: "Mr. Selby Vose... became manager in nineteen eleven. He was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president — the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense." Duncan Keddie confirms this (r18): "Selby Vose... was Gideon Vose's son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew."

This matters because Selby, as manager from 1913 to 1938, signed the annual ledger sheets carrying the "station gain" line every year. He was the son of the man who wrote Article VII—the rule that made the station's test absolute and gave the tester enormous power. Gideon Vose could not foresee the consequence when he wrote that by-law. But Selby, managing the Association and signing sheets that showed a consistent 5.25% "station gain" every year, had the opportunity to wonder why. He never raised the question.

---

### B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?

According to the sources, there was no blood relationship between the Voses and the Keddies. However, rumor claimed otherwise. Duncan Keddie (r18) writes: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either. I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins... It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me. She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it."

This distinction is historically important because Orra's cousin was old Gideon Vose's wife (according to Vira Toland, r01), not Gideon himself. But Effie Loomis (r02) notes: "Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins. Everybody knew that." This means Orra and Gideon's wife were first cousins, which did create a family web. But Duncan, speaking for the Keddie side, denies any relationship and attributes the claim to gossip. The distinction has no bearing on what happened at Ostrey Hollow—the true cause was the glass—but it shows how in a small valley, every relationship is examined and re-examined.

# Test Answers — Part 3

## Section C — Math (14 points)

### C1. The Glassware Error

(a) **By what proportion did the tests differ from the truth?**

The pipettes were blown on the stem at 17.6 c.c. but delivered a mean of 16.72 c.c. (Ivy Keddie's measurement, r04). The ratio is 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95. Therefore, the tests read at 95% of the truth, or 5% short. The sources consistently report "one part in twenty" (Bulletin 471 conclusion, r11), which is 1/20 = 0.05 = 5%.

(b) **What was the true average test, and by how many points were the recorded tests short?**

The recorded average at Ostrey Hollow over the affected years was 3.61%. If the pipettes delivered 95% of the true charge, the true average was 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80%. The shortfall was 3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19 percentage points (nineteen hundredths).

(c) **How many pounds of butterfat were the patrons not credited with?**

The station received 40,000,000 pounds of milk over the twenty-five full delivery years (1898–1922, per multiple sources including r05, r12). At the recorded average of 3.61%, the patrons were credited with 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 pounds of butterfat. At the true average of 3.80%, they delivered 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 pounds. The difference is 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 pounds of butterfat.

(d) **What was that worth in money, and what fraction of the total did the patrons receive?**

Butterfat averaged 25 cents per pound over the period (per r05, r12, r22). The lost fat was worth 76,000 × $0.25 = $19,000. The patrons were awarded $3,800 "without admission" (r02, r07, r10, r13). The fraction received was $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20, or one-fifth (20%). The patrons received one-fifth of what they had lost.

---

### C2. The Largest Patron's Year of 1910

(a) **How many pounds of butterfat was he credited with?**

The largest patron was Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom. His weigh book (r09, r17) shows for 1910: "Two hundred thousand pounds even, delivered to the station. Paid on a test of 3.42." The fat credited was 200,000 × 0.0342 = 6,840 pounds.

(b) **How many pounds was he owed and not credited?**

If the pipettes delivered 95% of truth, the true test of his milk was 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60 percentage points. The true butterfat was 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 pounds. The difference was 7,200 − 6,840 = 360 pounds of butterfat in 1910 alone.

(c) **What was the difference worth in money that year?**

At 25 cents per pound, 360 pounds = 360 × $0.25 = $90 for that single year. Over Emmet's lifetime hauling milk (roughly 1898–1929), this pattern repeated year after year.

---

### C3. Station Gain Over the Full Period

(a) **How many pounds of butterfat were credited to the station's patrons in total?**

This is the sum of what appeared in the ledgers under the station test—the recorded average of 3.61% applied to 40,000,000 pounds = 1,444,000 pounds of butterfat.

(b) **How many pounds actually reached the main creamery in the station's cream?**

The cream arrived at Larrow Green heavier than the stations records indicated. The bookkeeper Wilbur Teague (r05) recorded each year: "Butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb." The example shows 60,800 / 57,760 = 1.0525, or 5.25% gain. Applied to the total: 1,444,000 × 1.0525 = 1,520,000 pounds reached the creamery.

(c) **Express the difference as a percentage—be exact about what you are taking a percentage of.**

The difference is 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 pounds.

As a percentage of **credited fat** (1,444,000 lb): 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 0.0526 = 5.26% (approximately 5.25%, matching "five and a quarter percent").

As a percentage of **delivered fat** (1,520,000 lb): 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = 0.05 = 5% (exactly "one part in twenty").

Both are correct; the key is specifying which denominator.

### C4. The Invoice for the Testing Outfit

(a) **What was the total of the invoice?**

The invoice from Ammon & Sons, dated April 14, 1897 (r07, r20): six dozen milk test pipettes at $0.35 = $25.20; twelve dozen test bottles at $0.22 = $31.68; one acid measure = $0.90; one hand tester (4-bottle) = $18.00; freight = $2.15. **Total: $77.93** (confirmed in r07, r12, r19, r20).

(b) **How many pipettes did that invoice buy, and by how many cubic centimeters was each one short?**

The invoice bought **six dozen = 72 pipettes**. They were blown on the stem at 17.6 c.c. but delivered 16.72 c.c. The shortfall per pipette was 17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88 c.c.** per pipette.

### C5. Duration and First Season

(a) **For exactly how long was the faulty glassware in use, and over how many full delivery years does the money calculation run? State both.**

The faulty glass was in use from **May 1, 1897 (first use at the station) to June 12, 1923 (condemnation)**: 26 years and 6 weeks, or approximately **26.5 years** of actual use.

The money calculation (40 million pounds, 3.61 average) runs over **25 full delivery years**: 1898–1922 (r05, r12, r22). The station opened in May 1896 but did not have a full year until 1897. The first season (May 1896–December 1896) is not included in the station column of the ledgers; instead, the milk was carried on the creamery's own books. The 25 full years exclude both 1897 (partial) and 1923 (partial, from Jan to June 12).

Why the discrepancy? The glass was in use for 26.5 years, but only 25 of those were complete delivery years recorded in the ledgers against which a financial loss can be calculated.

(b) **How many pounds of milk did the station receive in its first season?**

The station opened May 4, 1896. No figure is recorded in the Association's ledgers for 1896. Bertram Kyle, the clerk (r23), says: "A good eleven hundred thousand pounds, which is what I understood at the time from men who were there. It is not in any book I ever ruled." The first-season milk is **not recorded in any surviving document**; the estimate of "about 900,000 pounds" (r21, Cleve Oram) and "about 1,100,000 pounds" (r23, Kyle) are the only available figures, both from memory.

## Section D — Logic (10 points)

### D1. The 1911 Equipment Interval as an Experiment

**When and how long:** May 8 to July 10, 1911 (exactly nine weeks, per r03, r09, r11).

**The variable:** The station's own milk-test measure (pipette) was broken on May 8. A measure borrowed from Larrow Green was used in its place. The station's own measure was returned to use on July 10.

**Held constant:** The herds, the pasture, the season (June–July flush), the acid, the test bottles, the hand tester, the operator (Ansel Keddie), his method, his speed, and the supervision (none).

**The result:** During the nine weeks with the borrowed measure, the average test stood at 3.80. Before and after, it stood at 3.61. The day the station's own measure came back, the average fell back to 3.61.

**What it establishes:** This interval establishes, beyond calculation, that the gap was caused by the measuring glass alone. No feature of the herds, land, season, method, or operator could have changed in nine weeks. The borrowed measure delivered the true charge; the station's own measure delivered short. Vaught (r11, section IV) emphasizes: "I have spent my working life urging students to prefer an experiment to a calculation. Here is one that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven years."

**What it does not establish:** It does not establish *which* measure was right or wrong—only that they were different. It does not identify the mechanism of the error (broken mold, deliberate short-blowing, reground mold, etc.). It does not establish how long the error had persisted before 1911 or after 1911 (though context shows it was there from the beginning and persisted until 1923 condemnation).

---

### D2. Why the Tester Cannot Have Been Stealing Cream

**Independent reason 1** (Cream receipts outweighed station credits): The receiving book at Larrow Green (where the cream arrived after hauling) showed that more fat came down the hill than the station had credited the patrons with (r05, r12, r23). Hobart Sill, the buttermaker, weighed it himself: "That cream ran heavy. Every year it ran heavy. There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there... I weighed it over again when it came into the cold room" (r14). If the tester had removed cream and reported low tests, the cream arriving at the creamery should have been *lighter* than reported, not heavier. This contradicts the theft theory directly.

**Independent reason 2** (The 1911 equipment swap): During the nine weeks when a borrowed measure was used, Ansel made the same tests with the same hands, but the results went up to 3.80 and fell back to 3.61 when his own measure returned (r03, r09, r11). If Ansel had been stealing cream and reporting low to cover it, why would the borrowed measure eliminate the alleged theft? The only explanation is that the difference was in the glass, not in the man. This refutes the theft accusation from first principles.

**Independent reason 3** (Alder Corners overlay): Four Hessel Bottom men hauled over the ridge to a cheese factory at Alder Corners from 1915–1930. Rosalie Cudd (r10) tested the same milk: it came in at 3.78–3.82 across sixteen summers, not 3.61. The same farmers' milk tested 20 hundredths higher at Alder Corners than at Ostrey Hollow. If Ansel had been stealing cream from these men at Ostrey Hollow, the cream reaching Larrow Green should have been lighter. But it wasn't—it was heavier.

**Why they are independent:** Reason 1 rests on the receiving book at Larrow Green. Reason 2 rests on the 1911 natural experiment with a different instrument. Reason 3 rests on an entirely separate facility in a different county. None depends on the others; they converge on the same conclusion from different data.

**Which would hold if the glassware was never measured in 1958:** Reasons 1 and 3 both stand entirely without the 1958 measurements. The cream receipts and Alder Corners test overlay prove the tester innocent regardless of what happened to the pipettes. Reason 2 (the 1911 swap) would also hold but becomes stronger *with* the glass measurements.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question his entries:** Wilbur Teague (r05) testified before the board: "Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons... I do not say what any particular person did on any particular day... I say that a business which is arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken from it." However, Teague also says: "I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone. I let it alone." He saw the station gain (the cream arriving heavier, the credits lighter) and he questioned it—once—to the manager. The manager told him to drop it. Teague complied and never spoke of it again for nearly a decade, until the arbitration forced his hand.

### D3. Did the Manager Know the Tests Were Low?

**Answer:** Yes, Selby Vose almost certainly knew, or had reason to know, that the station's tests ran low.

**Justification:** Vose signed the annual ledger sheets every year from 1913 onward (r05, r23). Each sheet carried the "station gain" line: fat credited to patrons on the station test vs. fat received in cream at Larrow Green. Year after year, the cream arrived 5.25% heavier than the station's figures. A manager who signed such a sheet—who "put his thumb on the bottom figure" and signed (r23)—need not understand the technical reason for the discrepancy, but he could not be unaware of its *existence*.

Teague approached Vose in 1916 (r05): "I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone." This proves Vose knew something was amiss. What he was told and what he thought are unclear. But he was the manager; he saw the ledger sheets; he knew there was a persistent surplus appearing on the station account.

Why did he not investigate? The sources offer hints: The 1919 water circular suggests he blamed the patrons. In his 1926 address (r08), Vose blamed watering and said the tests went up "when the looking began." He may have genuinely believed in the water theory. Or he may have chosen not to press further—the station was profitable to the Association (the "station gain" went to the general account). He signed the sheets. He did nothing.

## Section E — Contradictions (8 points)

### E1. Factual Contradictions

**Contradiction 1: The award amount**

- Cleve Oram (r21) says: "They gave the patrons at the end of it was thirty-five hundred dollars."
- Multiple authoritative sources (r02, r05, r07, r10, r13, r22, r24) say $3,800.

**Resolution:** The authoritative sources, especially the minute book of the arbitration board itself (r02, r07: "The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00)"), outrank a single narrator's memory. Cleve Oram was seventy-seven in 1977, recalling 1925; he had the figure slightly wrong. **Correct answer: $3,800.**

**Method used:** Documentary evidence (the board's own record, the Association's ledgers) outranks a single narrator's recollection. Arithmetic confirms: $3,800 is one-fifth of $19,000 (the true loss), which is what multiple sources report.

**Resolution:** Strawn was the inspector, writing in his official notebook contemporaneously (r06). Hazel Brant, speaking in 1981 about events from 1923, had the year wrong by two years. Dates in official records outrank dates in memory. **Correct answer: June 12, 1923.**

**Contradiction 4: The pipette mean delivery**

- Ivy Keddie's measurement sheet (r04, r07 item 14): "Mean 16.72 c.c."

Vaught's arithmetic (0.9244) does not match his conclusion ("one part in twenty" = 0.95). Ivy's direct measurement of 16.72 c.c. is the most concrete evidence, and her arithmetic (16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95) is internally consistent. The loss of 76,000 pounds (Ivy's calculation) also matches Vaught's conclusion statement but not his section V arithmetic. **This is an error in the sources themselves that cannot be fully resolved.** I prefer Ivy's measurement (16.72 c.c., 95% delivery) as the primary data, but note the discrepancy with Vaught's stated arithmetic.

## Section F — Theory (6 points)

### F1. The Causal Theory

**The single coherent causal theory:**

Ostrey Hollow station's tests read low due to a systematic instrumental error, not to any action or negligence by the farmers, the tester, or the manager. In April 1897, Ammon & Sons sold the Association a set of milk-test pipettes, blown on the stem at 17.6 cubic centimeters. The pipettes manufactured delivered approximately 16.72 c.c., not 17.6 c.c.—a 5% shortfall. When a charge 5% short is drawn into a test bottle marked for the full charge, the resulting fat reading is 5% short in exact proportion. This error was consistent and proportional from May 1897 through June 1923. It affected every patron identically (in proportion to their milk's actual fat): no patron tested "better" or "worse" relative to another in this system. The gap grew to be constant at 19 hundredths (3.61 recorded vs. 3.80 true) because the quality of milk was objectively stable; the instrumental error was unchanging. 

The size of the error (5%) followed directly from the glassware's delivery rate (95%). The error began when the faulty glass was installed (May 1, 1897). The error ended when the state inspector condemned the glass and new glass was put in use (June–October 1923). Nothing at the station or on the farms had to change: the tester did not change his method, the farmers did not improve their herds or their land, the season did not shift. The error simply stopped because the instrument was replaced.

The patrons lost 76,000 pounds of butterfat (worth $19,000) because they were paid on low figures for every pound they delivered. This loss appeared on the Association's own ledgers as "station gain"—the surplus fat arriving at Larrow Green but not credited to the patrons. The surplus was then credited to the Association's general account. In effect, the patrons' error subsidized the creamery and the Association's operations.

**The three competing explanations and the evidence that defeats each:**

1. **Water (Selby Vose's theory):** The manager claimed that water was being added to cans at Ostrey Hollow. *Specific evidence that defeats it:* (a) The state dairy inspector, Merle Strawn, tested for added water (using a lactometer) at every visit from 1908 forward and never found any (r06, r07, r22). (b) The 1911 equipment swap: for nine weeks, with the same patrons and the same tester, tests rose to 3.80 and fell back when the station's own measure returned. If water was the cause, changing the measure would not have changed the tests. (c) Four Hessel Bottom patrons hauled the same milk over the ridge to Alder Corners and it tested 3.78–3.82, not 3.61; if they were adding water, cheese would not have been made (Rosalie Cudd states: "A cheesemaker knows watered milk inside a day... In sixteen summers my father never made one complaint," r10).

2. **Theft (Wilbur Teague's suggestion):** The tester removed cream and wrote low tests to cover the loss. *Specific evidence that defeats it:* (a) The receiving book at Larrow Green showed that more fat arrived than the station had credited—the opposite of what theft would leave (r05, r12, r23). (b) The 1911 equipment swap: the same tester, the same hands, but with a borrowed measure, produced correct tests and then returned to incorrect tests when his own measure came back. If theft was the cause, the equipment change would make no difference. The evidence is definitive. (c) A man who had tested for 29 seasons, whose record book was perfect, who tested in front of the state inspector and "the state man saw him do it," who never asked for extra pay, extra credit, or favorable consideration—it is implausible that he stole cream and was never caught or suspected until the Association needed a scapegoat.

3. **Wet hay (1908 committee and Dr. Kepp's theory):** The wet meadow of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream. *Specific evidence that defeats it:* (a) The State Agricultural College's own feeding trial (Bulletin 214) found bog hay depressed butterfat by "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Five hundredths cannot account for a nineteen-hundredths gap that runs flat across every season. (b) The 1911 equipment swap: the same hay, the same herds, but a different measure produced correct tests. The hay was not the cause. (c) The Alder Corners milk of the same farms tested higher there, showing that the farms' milk was not inherently lower in fat. (d) Over 50 years of records show no correlation between wet years and worse tests, or dry years and better tests. The gap was the same in wet years as in dry years, in the winter on stored hay and in the summer on pasture.

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?**

No. The condemned crate was placed in the loft in June 1923 and sat there untouched for thirty-five years. The arbitration board heard exhibits in March–May 1925 (r13). Alonzo Frick lists four exhibits: the station test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book, and the state inspector's 1923 circuit notebook (r13). The condemned glassware itself—the physical evidence—was never brought before the board, never examined, and never mentioned in any testimony. Effie Loomis (r02, section 8) notes: "The second is the crate. The crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all... Forty years of taking down what men say has taught me that the interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying." Bertram Kyle (r23) adds: "Four books... and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked." Nobody connected the inspector's notebook (which mentioned the condemnation) to the glassware itself.

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?**

Ansel Keddie believed the patrons were at fault. In his private corridor conversation with Alonzo Frick (r13): "In the corridor at Ashlin, Keddie told me privately that he had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the room. He was not a bitter man about it. He had lived among them thirty years and would not stand up and accuse his neighbors." 

However, Ansel suspected the glass in 1911. Duncan Keddie (r18) recalls: "The day began... Ansel came home in the summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book. I was seventeen and I remember the kitchen and I remember that he said it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not his way." But Ansel was a man who "would not put down what he could not prove." He only wrote a question mark. For fourteen years after 1911, he lived with his suspicion about the glass and his public silence about it, and he never resolved the contradiction between his private hunch and his professional inability to prove it.

## Section G — Summary (4 points)

### G1. The Eight-Story History in 150 Words

From 1889 to 1958, the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association operated a milk-testing station at Ostrey Hollow under Article VII: patrons were paid on butterfat test made at their delivery station. From 1897 onward, milk at that station tested 5% lower than elsewhere—consistently, across decades. The Association blamed watering (1919 circular), a committee blamed wet hay (1908), the tester was suspected of theft (1925 arbitration). None was true.

In 1911, a broken measure was replaced for nine weeks; tests rose to truth, then fell again when the station's own measure returned. The evidence lay unread for forty-seven years. In 1923, a state inspector condemned the pipettes; they delivered 95% of their marked capacity. In 1958, Ivy Keddie found the condemned crate in a loft, measured the pipettes, and proved the error was instrumental, not human. The tester, Ansel Keddie (dismissed in 1925), was cleared posthumously in 1958—thirty-three years too late. The gap required eight separate records to understand: because each narrator saw only their corner of a story the full geometry of which was invisible to any one person.
