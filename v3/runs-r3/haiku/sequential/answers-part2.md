# Test Answers — Part 2

## Section A — Reconstruction (Stories 5–8)

### Story 5: The 1923 Equipment Condemnation (June–October 1923)

In the summer of 1923, the state dairy inspector, Merle Strawn, made his annual circuit visit to Ostrey Hollow station. Strawn had been on this circuit since 1907 and carried with him a new tool: a calibrated burette, issued to field men beginning in 1921 for the purpose of checking whether pipettes and measures delivered their marked capacity. Most plants had never been checked this way; a pipette is ordinarily the standard by which plants measure, and an instrument cannot be verified against itself.

On the 12th of June, 1923, Strawn ran the station's milk pipettes against his burette. The glass would not deliver its mark. Every pipette fell short. He condemned the entire crate on the spot and told the manager, Selby Vose, to order new equipment.

Vose acted quickly. On the 28th of June, the Association wrote to Ammon & Sons, the dairy supply house at Tarnet, ordering six dozen milk pipettes and six dozen test bottles. Ammon & Sons answered on the 2nd of July, acknowledging the order, promising shipment that month, and saying only: "We regret the trouble. Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name." The new glass arrived and was in use by October.

From October 1923 onward, the tests at Ostrey Hollow rose and stayed at 3.80–3.85, the level they should have been at all along. No explanation was given then or sought. Strawn drew no inference about the years behind it. Vose did not question why his own careful accounting had carried a wrong figure for a quarter-century. The condemned glass was carried up into the loft over the intake room, where it sat under a tarpaulin for the next thirty-five years.

**Key documents:** r06 (Merle Strawn's circuit book entries, June and October 1923); r20 (Junia Ammon's company history, with reproduction of the 1923 correspondence and invoice from 1897); r18 (Duncan Keddie, noting his father never knew the cause); r21 (Cleve Oram, on the crate in the loft).

**Quantities that matter:** June 12, 1923 (condemnation date); October 1923 (new glass in use); 35 years in the loft (1923–1958); the original outfit dated April 14, 1897 (invoice); six dozen pipettes bought in 1897, nine surviving in 1958.

### Story 6: The 1925 Arbitration and Ansel Keddie's Dismissal (January–May 1925)

In January 1925, the patrons of Ostrey Hollow petitioned the Association under its by-laws for arbitration. Their claim: that they had been paid consistently less than the milk was worth, and they wanted to be made whole. The Association did not resist. A board of three arbitrators was constituted, chaired by Alonzo Frick, a lawyer of Ashlin, known as a fair man. The board sat at the Association office at Ashlin on the 17th of March, heard testimony, and then held two more sittings at the station itself, in the weigh room, so that the machinery, scales, and books could be before the board.

Three explanations were heard before the board:

(1) **Water.** The manager, Selby Vose, argued that water was being added to cans at Ostrey Hollow. He cited his 1919 circular as evidence that he had warned against it. This was urged without proof.

(2) **Theft.** The bookkeeper, Wilbur Teague, at length described how a business arranged with only one man could sooner or later have a little taken from it, implying that the tester had removed cream and written low tests to hide the loss. He made no direct accusation but left the inference unmistakable.

(3) **The Hay.** Someone read into the record the report of the 1908 committee, blaming the wet hay of Hessel Bottom for thinning the cream. This had the advantage of blaming nobody in the room.

Ansel Keddie made a statement on the 17th of March. He said: he had tested as he was taught and as the state man had seen him do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons; he could not tell the board why the figures were what they were; he had never taken a pound of cream out of that station; and he believed the patrons were at fault (though he would not say so in the room, as he later told Frick in private).

On the 30th of April, four days before the board handed down its award, the Association dismissed Ansel. He came home at his ordinary hour, hung his apron behind the shed, and asked what there was for supper. His wife Orra said ham. He ate it.

On the 4th of May, the board found no cause established against the station, the office, or any officer or servant. It awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800 "without admission," charged to the station account. The amount was roughly one-fifth of what the patrons claimed to have lost. No explanation was ever given for why a payment was made if no cause was established.

Ansel Keddie was seventy-one years old. He never worked at the station again. He took up carpentry. He died in 1934 at age seventy-three, still under the imputation of the valley's suspicion, still unaccused and undefended.

**Key documents:** r03 (Ansel's statement and letter); r13 (Alonzo Frick's recollection, with the exhibit list and his note of Ansel's corridor remark); r05 (Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper's explanation of the station-gain line); r08 (Selby Vose's 1926 annual address, defending his water theory); r24 (Orra Keddie's diary, entries for March 17 and April 30, 1925).

**Quantities that matter:** Jan 1925 petition; March 17 – May 4 arbitration (less than two months); April 30 dismissal (4 days before award); $3,800 awarded; Ansel dismissed after 29 seasons; four exhibits presented to the board.

### Story 7: The 1958 Discovery and Investigation (February–October 1958)

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, went to the old Ostrey Hollow station building to gather materials for a research project on the station's history. The building had been closed for years and used for storage of farm equipment. There was a loft over what had been the intake room, reached by a ladder. Under a tarpaulin, covered in dust and chaff, she found a wooden crate stenciled with the name of the dairy supply company: Ammon & Sons, Tarnet.

The crate held nine unbroken milk-test pipettes. They were the original equipment, bought in 1897, condemned by the state inspector in June 1923, and left there for thirty-five years. Nobody had given them a thought.

Ivy carried the crate east to Farland. On the 9th of March, 1958, she measured the nine pipettes against a calibrated burette, taking five runs from each and averaging. The measurement sheet showed:

16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 (in cubic centimeters)

Mean: 16.72 c.c.

The pipettes were blown on the stem at 17.6 c.c. They delivered 16.72 c.c. The shortfall was 0.88 c.c., which is exactly 95 percent of the mark.

Ivy did the arithmetic: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95, which means the pipettes delivered 95 percent of the standard charge. A charge 5 percent short, drawn into a bottle marked for a full charge, gives a test reading 5 percent short. Over the affected years, 40,000,000 pounds of milk was received at the station. The recorded average test was 3.61 percent. The true average was 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80 percent. The gap was 19 hundredths. The patrons were not credited with 40,000,000 × 0.0019 = 76,000 pounds of butterfat.

In October 1958, Professor Leland Vaught of the college's department of dairy husbandry, working with Ivy, published the findings as Bulletin No. 471 of the State Agricultural College. The conclusion: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

Ansel Keddie had been dead for twenty-four years. Orra had died ten years before that, in 1948.

**Key documents:** r04 (Ivy Keddie's 1959 talk, with measurement sheet and calculations); r11 (Leland Vaught, Bulletin 471, introduction and method); r19 (Ashlin Chronicle front page, Oct 1958, "Old Ordell Wrong Righted"); r22 (Orin Falke, 1959 county agent report).

**Quantities that matter:** February 1958 (crate found); March 9, 1958 (measurements taken); October 1958 (Bulletin 471 published); 16.72 c.c. mean delivery; 0.95 ratio; 3.80 true average; 76,000 pounds lost fat; $19,000 value at 25 cents per pound; 35 years of the crate in the loft.

### Story 8: The Vindication and Its Limits (October 1958–1964)

The publication of Bulletin 471 in October 1958 made clear to anyone who read it that Ansel Keddie had been blameless. He had tested as he was taught. He had done so in front of the state's own inspector. He had kept meticulous records. The fault lay in a piece of glass that sat over his head in a loft for thirty-five years, invisible to him, invisible to the board, invisible to every accountant and inspector until a young woman thirty-three years after his dismissal climbed a ladder.

The Ashlin Chronicle ran the story on its front page: "Old Ordell Wrong Righted." The county agent prepared a formal report. The Historical Society began collecting the documents. Bulletin 471 was read. Ivy gave a talk at the Historical Society in 1959. But in the valley itself, the vindication moved slowly.

Emmet Brant had died in 1929, ten years after his last conversation with Ansel. He never knew he was wrong, and never knew the truth. But his son Roy Brant, and his granddaughter Hazel, knew it. They had the weigh book. They had always believed in Ansel.

Ansel's son Duncan Keddie, a retired schoolteacher, wrote out family notes in 1968 to set the record straight. He noted that Ansel, in 1911, had come home from the station and said "something in the glass," but he was a man who would not put down what he could not prove, so he only wrote a question mark. Duncan kept his father's unsent letter for the archives.

In 1964, Orra's diary was published as a small book. It showed, month by month and entry by entry, a woman bearing the weight of her husband's reputation in a small valley, and her brother's silence. "My brother Emmet came up... and came home at eleven at night with his hat still on."

By the time the truth was told in full, no action could be taken. The Association's account was long closed. All parties were dead. Ansel was cleared of a charge nobody had ever formally made. The valley had its answer, but no remedy.

**Key documents:** r19 (Ashlin Chronicle, front page, with Ivy's quoted statement); r18 (Duncan Keddie's 1968 family notes); r22 (Orin Falke's 1959 report); r24 (Orra Keddie's diary, 1964 publication); r11 (Vaught's Bulletin 471 conclusion).

**Quantities that matter:** October 1958 publication; 33 years between dismissal (1925) and vindication (1958); 24 years between death (1934) and vindication; Ansel never asked for clearance; the valley had no legal remedy; Orra died in 1948, 10 years before the truth emerged.

## Section B — Relationships (8 points)

### B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Ansel (born 1861) married Orra Brant in 1889. Their son was Duncan Keddie (born 1894). Duncan had a daughter, Ivy, born in 1931. Thus: Ansel → Duncan → Ivy (three generations). This is confirmed by Duncan Keddie's own 1968 family notes (r18), which state plainly: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter." The Ashlin Chronicle (r19) initially called her a "great-niece," but that was an error; the county agent Orin Falke (r22) corrected this in his 1959 report's added note: "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece."

---

### B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant?

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. Hazel's grandfather Emmet Brant and Ivy's grandmother Orra were brother and sister (confirmed by Roy Brant in r09: "Orra Keddie was my father's sister"). Emmet's son Roy had a daughter Hazel. Duncan had a daughter Ivy. Therefore: Emmet and Orra (siblings) → Roy and Duncan (first cousins, children of those siblings) → Hazel and Ivy (second cousins, children of first cousins). This is confirmed by Hazel Brant herself (r17): "Ivy Keddie and I are second cousins." Duncan Keddie's family notes (r18) are explicit: "Roy Brant and I are therefore first cousins... Roy's daughter is Hazel Brant. My daughter is Ivy... Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins."

The sources note that people often got this wrong, calling them "cousins once removed" (wrong—that skips a generation) or "aunt and niece" (wrong—different generations for the roles). Cleve Oram (r21) gets it right but adds confusion: he calls Ivy "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed," which is incorrect; the sources consistently say second cousins. However, Hazel and Ivy overlap in time without spanning more generations, so the correct answer remains: second cousins.

---

### B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter?

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose, the founder and first president of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (served 1889–1911, twenty-two years). Bertram Kyle, the office clerk (r23), states firmly: "Mr. Selby Vose... became manager in nineteen eleven. He was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president — the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense." Duncan Keddie confirms this (r18): "Selby Vose... was Gideon Vose's son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew."

This matters because Selby, as manager from 1913 to 1938, signed the annual ledger sheets carrying the "station gain" line every year. He was the son of the man who wrote Article VII—the rule that made the station's test absolute and gave the tester enormous power. Gideon Vose could not foresee the consequence when he wrote that by-law. But Selby, managing the Association and signing sheets that showed a consistent 5.25% "station gain" every year, had the opportunity to wonder why. He never raised the question.

---

### B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?

According to the sources, there was no blood relationship between the Voses and the Keddies. However, rumor claimed otherwise. Duncan Keddie (r18) writes: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either. I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins... It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me. She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it."

This distinction is historically important because Orra's cousin was old Gideon Vose's wife (according to Vira Toland, r01), not Gideon himself. But Effie Loomis (r02) notes: "Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins. Everybody knew that." This means Orra and Gideon's wife were first cousins, which did create a family web. But Duncan, speaking for the Keddie side, denies any relationship and attributes the claim to gossip. The distinction has no bearing on what happened at Ostrey Hollow—the true cause was the glass—but it shows how in a small valley, every relationship is examined and re-examined.
