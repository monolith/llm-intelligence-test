# Test Answers — Part 1

## Section A — Reconstruction (Eight Stories, Stories 1–4)

### Story 1: The Founding of the Larrow Valley Association and Article VII (1889–1892)

The farmers of Larrow Valley, tired of hauling butter to merchants and receiving whatever price was offered, met at Larrow Green in January 1889 and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose, a heavy, quiet, obstinate man, was elected president and drafted the by-laws himself at his kitchen table in a farmer's rough hand.

The central innovation was Article VII, adopted at the annual meeting of 1892 (the year after the creamery opened): "Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." This replaced the old system of payment by weight, which paid no account to quality. Article VII was just but had a hinge: the test at each delivery station would govern—not the average, not a central test, not an appeal. One man's test in one room would determine each patron's pay for a generation.

In 1891, the Association built its main creamery at Larrow Green, on Larrow Creek below the mill, equipped with a churn and the machinery of butter-making. The creamery ended the chaos of independent sales. From that year forward, farmers received one check each month based on their butterfat credits.

The significance of Article VII and the test-governs rule is that it made the tester's accuracy absolute. In the fifty years that followed, no patron had recourse to dispute a test made at his station—the test was the law. This put immense responsibility on the tester and immense temptation in the tester's hands, because one man sitting in a room by himself, moving cans and drawing samples and writing numbers, had no oversight. Gideon Vose died in 1917, three years before the consequences of his own by-law became apparent.

**Key documents:** r02 (Effie Loomis, secretary's account of Article VII and by-laws); r05 (Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper, on payment by test); r12 (Dell Rundle, fifty-year pamphlet, on the founding and Article VII's language).

**Quantities that matter:** One by-law, eight words: "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."

### Story 2: The Opening of Ostrey Hollow Station and Its Purpose (1895–1896)

The farms of Hessel Bottom and the head of Ostrey Hollow were seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green, a journey of three hours in mud and impassable in March. To serve these farmers and avoid the long haul, the Association built a skimming station in the winter of 1895–1896 on the flat by the creek at Ostrey Hollow.

The station opened on the 4th of May, 1896. It was a board building, two stories, with a low covered platform on the road side, so a wagon could back up and the farmer could roll his cans off level without lifting them. Inside was the intake room: a scale, a vat, and a separator. A man weighed the milk, drew a sample for each patron's jar, tested the sample for its butterfat, and separated the milk. The cream went into cans to be hauled down to Larrow Green each day. The skim was handed back to the farmer for his hogs and calves.

Over the intake room was a loft, accessible by a ladder with a rope on the trap door. This loft was empty except for odds and ends—broken equipment, wire, salt.

The Association hired Ansel Keddie as tester and separator man. Keddie went to the station to prepare it in 1895, at age thirty-four, tested the first milk that summer, and remained there every season for the next twenty-nine years. He was a careful man in small things, meticulous about his records. The first season the station took in roughly 900,000 pounds of milk (May to December). The seven farms with rights on Hessel Bottom brought their milk to this station instead of driving it seven miles to Larrow Green.

**Key documents:** r01 (Vira Toland, recollection of the station's appearance and Ansel Keddie's method); r09 (Roy Brant, on the station and the Bottom farms); r21 (Cleve Oram, cream hauler's son, on the station building and loft).

**Quantities that matter:** Seven miles from Larrow Green; seven farms (later six, after some went over the ridge); first season ~900,000 pounds of milk; one tester (Ansel Keddie) from opening to dismissal.

### Story 3: The Test Gap Emerges and Explanations Multiply (1902–1920)

From about 1902, the milk brought to Ostrey Hollow station tested consistently lower than the milk brought to Larrow Green or other stations in the valley. The gap ran at approximately seventeen to nineteen hundredths of a point—seventeen-hundredths or nineteen-hundredths—year in and year out, in summer and winter, in wet years and dry ones. The recorded average at Ostrey Hollow was 3.61; the ordinary valley average was 3.78–3.80.

Farmers began to complain in 1902. Emmet Brant, the largest producer on Hessel Bottom, was among the first to question it, and the complaints escalated through the decade. The money loss was steady: over a quarter-century, a farmer on the Bottom might lose a thousand dollars to that gap.

The Association's officers could not ignore the complaints at the annual meetings. In 1908, they appointed a committee of three disinterested farmers, chaired by Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green, to investigate. The committee went to Hessel Bottom in January, February, and March, sampled the herds themselves, tried the scales, watched the tests, and found no dishonesty anywhere. Their report concluded that the wet hay of Hessel Bottom—sedge, rush, coarse tussock grass cut late and fed as the winter staple—thinned the cream. The remedy, they said, lay with the land, not with the station. The Committee was accepted and closed the question for eleven years.

But the question was not closed. In 1919, the Association's manager, Selby Vose, became convinced that water was being added to the cans at Ostrey Hollow. He circulated a printed circular to nine families warning them against adding water to milk and saying the Association could not pay for creek water. Ansel Keddie himself delivered the circular, standing at the door of each patron's house while they read it. Emmet Brant, seeing this, confronted Ansel: if you believe we're innocent, why did you carry the paper yourself? Ansel answered that he would not let another man carry it. Emmet left and did not enter Ansel's house again for ten years—not until 1929, when he was dying.

**Key documents:** r01 (Vira Toland, on the scandal and the 1919 circular); r09 (Roy Brant, on the constant gap and the 1908 committee); r15 (Nyle Grigg, 1908 report with 1919 covering letter); r24 (Orra Keddie diary, entry on the circular delivery and Emmet's confrontation); r13 (Alonzo Frick, on the three theories including watering).

**Quantities that matter:** Gap of 17–19 hundredths, constant across seasons; 3.61 average recorded at Ostrey Hollow vs. 3.78–3.80 elsewhere; 25 years of complaint; circular sent to nine families (six farms but some with multiple households).

### Story 4: The 1911 Equipment Failure as a Natural Experiment (May–July 1911)

On the 8th of May, 1911, the station's own milk-test measure—the pipette used to draw the standard charge of milk into the bottle for testing—broke. Ansel Keddie made shift with a measure borrowed from the creamery at Larrow Green, using it for nine weeks until the 10th of July, when the station's own measure arrived back from repair or replacement.

During those nine weeks, with the exact same herds, the same pasture, the same season (June and July, the flush), the same acid, the same bottles, the same tester (Ansel), and the same operator (Ansel), the tests rose sharply and held steady at 3.80. When the station's own measure came back into use, the tests fell back to 3.61 the same day. Ansel noted both events in his test book and marked the return with a question mark he had never explained in the fourteen years since.

This interval revealed that the gap was not caused by the cows, the pasture, the season, the land, the water supply, the acid, the bottles, or the tester's method. One variable changed—the instrument—and the figures changed in exact proportion. The borrowed measure delivered the standard charge; the station's own measure delivered less.

In 1911, neither Ansel nor any officer of the Association made the inference. The inspector was not called. The question mark sat in the book. Years later, scientists would understand that this nine-week interval held the entire answer, but in 1911 it was merely noted as an anomaly and set aside.

**Key documents:** r03 (Ansel Keddie's unsent letter, describing 1911 in detail and noting the equipment swap); r09 (Roy Brant, on his father's observation of the rise and fall); r11 (Leland Vaught, Bulletin 471, treating this as a decisive experiment); r18 (Duncan Keddie, on his father coming home and saying "something in the glass" but not recording it).

**Quantities that matter:** May 8 to July 10, 1911 (nine weeks); tests at 3.80 during the interval, 3.61 before and after; the gap closed to zero during those nine weeks; no other variable changed; one object changed.
