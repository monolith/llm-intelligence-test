# Test Answers — Part 2

## Section A: Reconstruction of Eight Original Stories (continued)

### Story 5: The Glass Is Condemned, But No One Connects It to the Past (June 12, 1923)

Twelve years after the mysterious nine weeks of 1911, on June 12, 1923, Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector for the Ordell circuit, made his regular visit to the Ostrey Hollow station. Strawn was a careful man who had carried the Ordell circuit since 1907, traveling by train where there was a train and by livery where there was not. He had detailed routines: he always checked the scales first, then the lactometer for added water, then the boiler and cold room, and finally—the step that most men grudged him—he stood at the tester's elbow and watched him make a test through from the sample to the reading without hurrying him.

In 1921, the state had begun issuing calibrated burettes to field men so they could check whether the pipettes at each plant actually delivered the volume marked on them. It was a slow business, and most plants had never had their glass checked. Strawn carried one in his grip. On his visit to Ostrey Hollow on June 12, 1923, he ran the station's milk pipettes against his burette. The glass would not deliver its mark. Every pipette in the crate fell short. He condemned the whole lot on the spot and told the manager to order new. The manager, Selby Vose, did so within the week, writing to Ammon & Sons on June 28, ordering replacement pipettes and bottles. Ammon & Sons answered on July 2, regretted the trouble, said they would ship within the month, and offered the unhelpful fact that their records of the nineties would not tell them anything about the original lot.

The condemned glass—six dozen pipettes, test bottles, acid measure, and hand tester—was carried up into the loft over the intake room at the station, placed in a wooden crate stenciled with the supplier's name "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET," covered with a tarpaulin, and left there. New glass was in use by the following month. From July 1923 forward, the station's test average rose markedly and stayed there. In 1924 it stood at 3.80 and remained.

No person at the time made any connection between the condemned glass and the twenty-six-year history of low tests. The glass was old; the pattern was old; but no one appears to have stood in that station and thought: What if these numbers were wrong because the glass was wrong all along?

**Who:** Merle Strawn (state dairy inspector), Selby Vose (manager), Ammon & Sons (dairy supply firm)

**What:** State inspector condemned the station's pipettes on June 12, 1923, using a calibrated burette; they were replaced; no one recognized the implication for past tests

**When:** June 12, 1923 (condemnation); June 28 – July 2, 1923 (order for new glass); July 1923 onward (new glass in use)

**Quantities:** 72 original pipettes (6 dozen) in the condemned crate; original invoice cost $77.93; test average rose from 3.61 to 3.80 after replacement

**Documents:** Merle Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook entry; Ammon & Sons letter of July 2, 1923; Ammon & Sons order book entry

### Story 6: The Arbitration and the Dismissal (January 1925 – April 30, 1925)

In January 1925, the patrons of Ostrey Hollow petitioned the Association. They stated their complaint plainly: their milk had tested lower than milk elsewhere for a very long time, they had been paid accordingly, and they asked to be made whole. The Association did not resist arbitration. Under its by-laws, a board of arbitration could be constituted, and the officers agreed to one.

The board was made up of three men, with Alonzo Frick, an attorney of Ashlin, as chairman. Frick was fair and impartial—a man who kept no cows and had no interest in the outcome. The board sat three times. The first session was at the Association's office in Ashlin on March 17, 1925. The second and third sessions Frick insisted on holding at the station itself, in the weigh room, so that the board might see the plant and the appliances before it rather than hear them described by interested parties.

Three explanations were argued before the board. The first was water: Selby Vose, the manager, maintained that water had been going into the cans at the head of the valley and that the rise in 1924 was the measure of it. The second was theft: Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper, answered a question about the station account in a way that suggested—without directly stating—that a man alone in a station with a separator running could help himself and write down a little less. The third was the hay: the 1908 committee report was read into the record, and Hessel Bottom's wet ground was before the board for much of an afternoon.

Ansel Keddie made a statement to the board on March 17. He said he had tested as he was taught and as the state man had seen him do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, that he could not tell the board why the figures were what they were, and that he had never taken a pound of cream. In the corridor afterward, he told Frick privately that he had always thought the patrons themselves were at fault—that he believed water had been added—but he would not say so in the room because he had lived among them thirty years and would not stand up and accuse his neighbors.

On April 30, 1925, four days before the board was ready to issue its finding, the Association dismissed Ansel Keddie. He was told nothing; he came up the road at his ordinary hour, hung his apron behind the shed, and asked what there was for supper. There was ham.

On May 4, 1925, the board issued its finding and award. It found no cause established—none of the three explanations had been proven. Yet it awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800, "without admission," to be charged to the station account. "Without admission" meant the Association was paying without admitting that anyone had done anything wrong. The award was generous—the patrons' own calculation of what they had lost was $19,600—but it was a fifth of it.

**Who:** Alonzo Frick (board chairman), Selby Vose (manager), Wilbur Teague (bookkeeper), Ansel Keddie (tester), the nine families of Ostrey Hollow, the board of arbitration

**What:** Patrons petitioned for back pay; three-session arbitration held; board found no cause but awarded money without admission; tester dismissed before award

**When:** January 1925 petition; March 17 – May 4, 1925 hearings; April 30, 1925 dismissal; May 4, 1925 award

**Quantities:** $3,800 awarded (about 1/5 of the patrons' claimed loss of $19,600); Keddie's twenty-nine seasons ended

**Documents:** Minute book of the board of arbitration; Ansel Keddie's statement to the board (9 sentences); four exhibits submitted to the board (station test book, receiving book, Brant weigh book, state inspector's notebook); award document signed by the board

### Story 7: Thirty-Five Years in the Loft (1923–1958)

The condemned crate sat in the loft over the intake room of the closed Ostrey Hollow station for thirty-five years. The station itself eventually closed—by 1946 milk was being trucked down the valley and skimming had stopped. The building was used for storage, hay tools, mowers. Nobody thought about the loft. The crate sat there, covered by a tarpaulin and a layer of dust and chaff, containing the original six dozen pipettes, of which nine survived unbroken.

In February 1958, a young woman from Farland Agricultural College named Ivy Keddie came to the station building. She was the granddaughter of Ansel Keddie, the tester who had been dismissed in 1925. She was also a graduate student in dairy husbandry. She went up the ladder to the loft, found the crate under the tarpaulin, and brought it down.

On March 9, 1958, Ivy measured the nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette in the college laboratory. She made five runs with each pipette and recorded the results. They were: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, and 16.71 cubic centimeters. The mean was 16.72 c.c. The pipettes were blown on the stem at 17.6 c.c. They were short by 0.88 c.c. each—that is, they delivered 95 hundredths of their marked capacity. Every test made with that glass for twenty-six years had recorded 95% of the truth.

With this measurement and the ledgers of the Association, the arithmetic became plain. Forty million pounds of milk had been received over the affected years. The recorded average test was 3.61 percent. The true average, corrected for the 5% error in the measuring glass, was 3.80 percent. The difference was seventy-six thousand pounds of butterfat that was never credited to the patrons. At the average price of twenty-five cents a pound, they had been underpaid by nineteen thousand dollars.

**Who:** Ivy Keddie (graduate student, granddaughter of Ansel Keddie), Professor Leland Vaught (dairy husbandry), the crate and its nine pipettes

**What:** The condemned glass was discovered in the station loft; measured and found to deliver only 95% of marked capacity; arithmetic revealed 35-year error

**When:** June 1923 (glass condemned and put in loft); February 1958 (Ivy found it); March 9, 1958 (measured); October 1958 (bulletin published)

**Quantities:** 9 surviving pipettes; each short 0.88 c.c. (5% error); 76,000 lbs of butterfat underpaid worth $19,000

**Documents:** Ivy Keddie's measurement sheet dated March 9, 1958; the condemned crate and pipettes; State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958) co-authored by Vaught and Keddie

### Story 8: Vindication and Historical Record (1958 onward)

On October 3, 1958, the Ashlin Chronicle published the story under the headline "Old Ordell Wrong Righted." The State Agricultural College had issued Bulletin No. 471, co-authored by Professor Leland Vaught and Miss Ivy Keddie. The bulletin concluded: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference—seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat—stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

Ansel Keddie's name was cleared. He had been right all along. The figures he recorded were what the glass gave him, and nothing more. He had been honest, he had been careful, and he had been wrong about nothing except his suspicion of his neighbors. He was dead by then—he had died in 1934, twenty-four years before the paper was published. His wife Orra, who had kept his letters and never spoken of the business except once a year in April, was also dead, having passed in 1948 without knowing that her husband had been cleared.

But the record was set right. The books came to rest in various places: the station test book in the Association's office at Ashlin, then to the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1959; Emmet Brant's weigh book, which Roy Brant had lent to Ivy for her work, eventually to Hazel Brant and then to the Historical Rooms in 1963; Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks, which had gone to the state dairy office in 1936 and then to Farland in 1947, where they had sat unread for eleven years until Ivy found the 1923 volume; the Alder Corners test book, which had passed from Jerome Cudd to his daughter Rosalie to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970; and the crate of condemned glass, which was deposited at the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1959.

The story entered the county's memory in different forms. Ivy Keddie wrote notes for her family. A farmer's daughter, Hazel Brant, gave her account. A school teacher, Dell Rundle, published a note to his 1959 reprint of an earlier jubilee pamphlet. The county agent filed a report. A retired clerk wrote down what he had seen pass across his desk. The widow's diary was published with footnotes. A clerk's granddaughter spoke to a newspaper. A cream hauler's son was recorded. And the documents—the ledgers, the test books, the notebooks, the crate itself—remained, waiting for anyone who would look.

**Who:** Ivy Keddie, Professor Leland Vaught, the historians and narrators who would later record the story, Ansel Keddie (posthumously), the people of the Larrow Valley

**What:** The true cause was published; Ansel Keddie's reputation was cleared; the historical record was compiled and deposited; the story became known

**When:** October 1958 bulletin; 1959 onward publication and depositing of records; 1959–1981 various recorded accounts

**Quantities:** 24-year gap between dismissal and vindication; 34-year gap between Ansel's death and vindication; $3,800 of $19,000 loss ever recovered

**Documents:** Bulletin No. 471, Ashlin Chronicle article, historical society accessions, various recorded interviews and published memoirs

## Section B: Relationships (8 points)

### B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Ansel was her paternal grandfather. Ansel's son was Duncan Keddie (born 1894), who taught school at Ashlin, and Duncan's daughter was Ivy (born 1931). Duncan states this plainly in Retelling 18: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise." The Ashlin Chronicle incorrectly called her his "great-niece" in 1958; the county agent Orin Falke corrected this error in his report, noting that the error was his and that "Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece."

### B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant?

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. The connection runs as follows: Emmet Brant and Orra Brant (later Orra Keddie) were brother and sister. Emmet's son was Roy Brant; Orra's son was Duncan Keddie. This makes Roy and Duncan first cousins (children of a brother and sister). Roy's daughter was Hazel Brant; Duncan's daughter was Ivy Keddie. This makes Hazel and Ivy second cousins (children of first cousins). Duncan states this explicitly in Retelling 18: "Roy's daughter is Hazel Brant. My daughter is Ivy. Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation." Cleve Oram (Retelling 21) incorrectly called them "first cousin once removed," but this was valley gossip, not documented fact. The genealogical chain is firm from the multiple consistent accounts.

### B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter to what happened at Ostrey Hollow?

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose, the founder and first president of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. This relationship matters crucially because Gideon Vose drafted the Association's by-laws, and Article VII of those by-laws—which established that "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern"—became the hinge of the entire Ostrey Hollow matter. As Article VII's author, Gideon had created a system with no appeal from the station's test. When Selby took office as manager in 1913, he inherited both this by-law and de facto authority over its application. When the Ostrey Hollow patrons began their complaints and eventually petitioned for arbitration in 1925, they were appealing against a rule that Selby's own father had written. Effie Loomis (Retelling 2) notes that Selby "was made manager in 1913 and served until 1938," and Duncan Keddie (Retelling 18) emphasizes that Selby was Gideon's son, "and not, as I have heard it said, his nephew," correcting a persistent error in valley talk.

### B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?

According to the documentary record and consistent testimony from Duncan Keddie (Retelling 18), there was no family connection between the Vose and Keddie families. Duncan writes: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either. I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her sit down. It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me... and she never once put a Vose in it." Vira Toland (Retelling 1) states: "Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins. Everybody knew that." However, this conflicts with Duncan's explicit denial and his note that his mother kept detailed family genealogies. The weight of evidence falls to Duncan's documentary account: there was no Vose-Keddie connection, and the persistent valley rumor was baseless. Cleve Oram (Retelling 21) also mentions "the Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort" as something he "would hear anyway," but marks it as village gossip from his mother's time, not documented fact. The sources cannot entirely settle this, but Duncan's firsthand family knowledge and his mother's genealogical memory (which he documents) outrank valley rumor.
