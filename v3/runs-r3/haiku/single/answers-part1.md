# Test Answers — Part 1

## Section A: Reconstruction of Eight Original Stories

### Story 1: The Founding of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (1889–1897)

Before 1889, farmers in the Larrow Valley sold their milk to merchants at whatever prices those merchants chose to offer. In the winter of 1889, frustrated by this arrangement, the farmers of the valley met at Larrow Green in cold weather and voted to organize themselves into the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose, a heavy, quiet man who had once considered law but reconsidered, was elected the first president and drafted the by-laws himself in a careful hand at his own kitchen table. His by-laws established a simple principle: farmers would own the place that bought their milk, and would be paid not for the weight of milk but for the pounds of butterfat it contained, as ascertained by test. This principle was formalized as Article VII in the by-laws and adopted at the annual meeting of 1892, after the main creamery at Larrow Green had been built. It was a just rule and a controversial one; some farmers left the Association over it. The creamery, built in 1891 on Larrow Creek below the mill, began paying farmers in cash once a month—a thing that had never happened in the valley before, and which remained vivid in farmers' memories decades later.

In 1896, the Association built a skimming station seven miles up the valley at Ostrey Hollow to serve the farms of that end of the valley, particularly those of Hessel Bottom, a wet meadow below the hamlet. The station opened on May 4, 1896. It took in the milk of the farms up there, weighed it, took a sample for testing, separated the cream from the skim, and sent the cream down the valley to Larrow Green while returning the skim to the farmers for their hogs and calves. Because of Article VII, the test made at the station where a man delivered his milk would govern what he was paid for the rest of the year. This detail—"the test of the station where he delivers shall govern"—would become the hinge of the whole matter.

**Who:** Gideon Vose (founder, first president), the farmers of the valley, Ansel Keddie (tester at the new station from its opening)

**What:** Farmers organized a cooperative to collectively market milk at fair prices; by-laws established payment by butterfat test; main creamery built at Larrow Green; skimming station built at Ostrey Hollow

**When:** Organization meeting 1889; creamery built 1891; station opened May 4, 1896; station's own testing equipment arrived May 1, 1897

**Quantities:** 72 pipettes (6 dozen) purchased from Ammon & Sons at a total cost of $77.93; station received approximately 900,000 pounds of milk in its first partial season (May–December 1896)

**Documents:** Association by-laws including Article VII; minute books from 1892 onward (1889–1892 book lost in 1938 office move); Ammon & Sons invoice dated April 14, 1897

### Story 2: The Complaint Begins—Ostrey Hollow Milk Tests Low (1902–1908)

Within a few years of the Ostrey Hollow station opening, patrons of that station began to notice and complain that their milk tested lower than the milk of farmers elsewhere in the valley. The first such complaint on record came in 1902. By 1908, the complaints had persisted for six years, had appeared at annual meetings, and had become common talk at crossroads stores. The difference was not large—roughly seventeen to nineteen hundredths of a point—but on payment by test, small differences compound to significant money over a lifetime of farming.

In January 1908, the Association appointed a committee led by Nyle Grigg, a farmer of standing from Larrow Green who had no personal stake in the outcome (he hauled his own milk to Larrow Green), to investigate the matter. The committee visited the Hessel Bottom farms three and four times each. They drew their own samples, sealed them, observed the herds, saw the milk hauled to the station, and stood by while the test was made. They tried the station scales against known weights and the patrons' own scales against the station's. They found no dishonesty anywhere. The herds were thin in the flank from winter feeding, and the coats were rough later in the season—signs of the lower nutrition from wet-meadow hay—but the milk was whole and the scales were honest.

The committee concluded that "the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station." This became the official explanation, and the question was considered closed. The gap between Ostrey Hollow milk (averaging 3.61% butterfat) and Larrow Green milk (averaging 3.78%) persisted, but the theory—that Hessel Bottom's wet ground and coarse hay naturally produced slightly lower-testing milk—satisfied most of the valley.

**Who:** Nyle Grigg (committee chairman), the six farms on Hessel Bottom (particularly Emmet Brant's operation), the Association's leadership

**What:** Patrons of Ostrey Hollow complained their milk tested consistently lower; committee investigated and concluded the wet hay of Hessel Bottom naturally reduced butterfat

**When:** First complaint 1902; committee investigation January–March 1908

**Quantities:** Recorded gap of approximately 0.17–0.19 points between Ostrey Hollow average (3.61) and Larrow Green average (3.78); gap consistent year-round

**Documents:** Committee report of 1908 (signed by Grigg and committee); State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 214 (1911), reporting a feeding trial of bog hay that found a depression of only 0.05 points (not enough to explain the full gap)

### Story 3: Three Attempted Explanations and Mounting Tension (1908–1919)

As the years continued and the complaint persisted, three competing explanations took shape in the valley. The first, advanced periodically by some patrons, was that water was being added to the cans before delivery—an old trick in the trade. The second, whispered in conversations, was that the tester himself, Ansel Keddie, might be removing cream for his own benefit and writing low tests to conceal it. The third, advanced by the 1908 committee and repeated by Dr. Roland Kepp, a veterinary surgeon, in 1926, was that the wet hay and poor minerals of Hessel Bottom were genuinely responsible.

In 1919, as the war ended and farming resumed in earnest, the manager of the Association, Selby Vose (who had taken office in 1913), decided to act on the first explanation. He circulated a warning circular to the nine families that received milk from the Ostrey Hollow station. The circular was temperate in language but unmistakable in meaning: it warned against adding water to milk, stated that the Association paid for butterfat and could not pay for creek water, and said that added water would be looked for and dealt with. The circular named no man and accused no man, but in a valley the size of this one, its meaning was plain.

The effect was to fracture a family. Ansel Keddie's wife Orra was the sister of Emmet Brant, the largest patron at the station and the farmer with the most to lose by low test prices. For thirty-six years they had eaten Sunday dinner together, the two families in one kitchen and the men at the fence after. After the 1919 circular, they did not speak—not at the store, not at a funeral, not once in all the years that followed. Ansel carried the circular himself to each farm, standing at the door while each man read it. Two days after, Emmet came up to the station and asked his brother-in-law why he had carried a paper that accused the Ostrey Hollow patrons of cheating. Ansel said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. The brothers-in-law never again entered each other's house in all the years that remained to them.

**Who:** Selby Vose (manager), Ansel Keddie (tester), Emmet Brant (largest patron), the nine families of Ostrey Hollow, Orra Keddie (Ansel's wife, Emmet's sister), Dr. Roland Kepp, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn

**What:** Three explanations circulated (water in cans, theft by tester, poor hay); manager sent warning circular in 1919; family relations ruptured

**When:** Ongoing from 1908 onward; circular issued June 1919; public rupture June 1919

**Quantities:** 0.19-point persistent gap; circular sent to nine families on Ostrey Hollow

**Documents:** 1919 circular (typed by clerk Bertram Kyle); newspaper accounts; diary entries by Orra Keddie; Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks (1908–1923)

### Story 4: An Accidental Experiment Points to the Answer (May 8 – July 10, 1911)

In May of 1911, something happened that no one recognized at the time as decisive but which later proved to be the key to everything. On May 8, 1911, Ansel Keddie's testing measure—the pipette that he used to draw the exact charge of milk into the test bottle—broke. The station had its own measuring glass, brought from Tarnet by the Ammon & Sons dairy supply house in 1897, which had been in daily use for fourteen years. Rather than close the station or delay the milk, Keddie made shift with a borrowed measure from the creamery at Larrow Green.

For the next nine weeks, from May 8 to July 10, while the borrowed measure was in use and everything else remained exactly the same—the same patrons, the same herds, the same pasture in the summer flush, the same acid, the same test bottles, the same hand tester, the same man making the tests at the same hour every morning—the station's average test rose from 3.61 to 3.80, where it held steady. The moment the station's own measure came back into use on July 10, the average fell back to 3.61 the same day.

Keddie noted the fact in his test book. Against the entries for May 8 and July 10, he wrote brief remarks: "Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure" and "Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." That question mark, written by a man who had tested milk for twenty-nine seasons and would not write a figure without proof, stood in the book for the next fourteen years, asked and unanswered.

None of the explanations then current in the valley could account for this interval. The milk came from the same cows, the same pasture was producing (flush season), no diet had changed, no man had changed his practices, nothing on Hessel Bottom had changed. Only one variable had altered: the pipette. Yet no one in 1911, and no one for forty-seven years after, recognized what the nine weeks were trying to say.

**Who:** Ansel Keddie (tester), the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, the creamery at Larrow Green (source of the borrowed measure)

**What:** Station's measure broke; borrowed measure used for 9 weeks; tests rose 0.19 points; returned to original level when station's own measure restored

**When:** May 8 – July 10, 1911

**Quantities:** Test average rose from 3.61 to 3.80 (a 0.19-point rise); fell back to 3.61 when original measure restored

**Documents:** Ansel Keddie's test book, with his own hand-written notes and question mark; State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958), which treats this interval as decisive evidence
