# Test Answers — Part 1

## Section A: Reconstruction of Eight Original Stories

### Story 1: The Founding of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (1889–1897)

Before 1889, farmers in the Larrow Valley sold their milk to merchants at whatever prices those merchants chose to offer. In the winter of 1889, frustrated by this arrangement, the farmers of the valley met at Larrow Green in cold weather and voted to organize themselves into the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose, a heavy, quiet man who had once considered law but reconsidered, was elected the first president and drafted the by-laws himself in a careful hand at his own kitchen table. His by-laws established a simple principle: farmers would own the place that bought their milk, and would be paid not for the weight of milk but for the pounds of butterfat it contained, as ascertained by test. This principle was formalized as Article VII in the by-laws and adopted at the annual meeting of 1892, after the main creamery at Larrow Green had been built. It was a just rule and a controversial one; some farmers left the Association over it. The creamery, built in 1891 on Larrow Creek below the mill, began paying farmers in cash once a month—a thing that had never happened in the valley before, and which remained vivid in farmers' memories decades later.

In 1896, the Association built a skimming station seven miles up the valley at Ostrey Hollow to serve the farms of that end of the valley, particularly those of Hessel Bottom, a wet meadow below the hamlet. The station opened on May 4, 1896. It took in the milk of the farms up there, weighed it, took a sample for testing, separated the cream from the skim, and sent the cream down the valley to Larrow Green while returning the skim to the farmers for their hogs and calves. Because of Article VII, the test made at the station where a man delivered his milk would govern what he was paid for the rest of the year. This detail—"the test of the station where he delivers shall govern"—would become the hinge of the whole matter.

**Who:** Gideon Vose (founder, first president), the farmers of the valley, Ansel Keddie (tester at the new station from its opening)

**What:** Farmers organized a cooperative to collectively market milk at fair prices; by-laws established payment by butterfat test; main creamery built at Larrow Green; skimming station built at Ostrey Hollow

**When:** Organization meeting 1889; creamery built 1891; station opened May 4, 1896; station's own testing equipment arrived May 1, 1897

**Quantities:** 72 pipettes (6 dozen) purchased from Ammon & Sons at a total cost of $77.93; station received approximately 900,000 pounds of milk in its first partial season (May–December 1896)

**Documents:** Association by-laws including Article VII; minute books from 1892 onward (1889–1892 book lost in 1938 office move); Ammon & Sons invoice dated April 14, 1897

---

### Story 2: The Complaint Begins—Ostrey Hollow Milk Tests Low (1902–1908)

Within a few years of the Ostrey Hollow station opening, patrons of that station began to notice and complain that their milk tested lower than the milk of farmers elsewhere in the valley. The first such complaint on record came in 1902. By 1908, the complaints had persisted for six years, had appeared at annual meetings, and had become common talk at crossroads stores. The difference was not large—roughly seventeen to nineteen hundredths of a point—but on payment by test, small differences compound to significant money over a lifetime of farming.

In January 1908, the Association appointed a committee led by Nyle Grigg, a farmer of standing from Larrow Green who had no personal stake in the outcome (he hauled his own milk to Larrow Green), to investigate the matter. The committee visited the Hessel Bottom farms three and four times each. They drew their own samples, sealed them, observed the herds, saw the milk hauled to the station, and stood by while the test was made. They tried the station scales against known weights and the patrons' own scales against the station's. They found no dishonesty anywhere. The herds were thin in the flank from winter feeding, and the coats were rough later in the season—signs of the lower nutrition from wet-meadow hay—but the milk was whole and the scales were honest.

The committee concluded that "the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station." This became the official explanation, and the question was considered closed. The gap between Ostrey Hollow milk (averaging 3.61% butterfat) and Larrow Green milk (averaging 3.78%) persisted, but the theory—that Hessel Bottom's wet ground and coarse hay naturally produced slightly lower-testing milk—satisfied most of the valley.

**Who:** Nyle Grigg (committee chairman), the six farms on Hessel Bottom (particularly Emmet Brant's operation), the Association's leadership

**What:** Patrons of Ostrey Hollow complained their milk tested consistently lower; committee investigated and concluded the wet hay of Hessel Bottom naturally reduced butterfat

**When:** First complaint 1902; committee investigation January–March 1908

**Quantities:** Recorded gap of approximately 0.17–0.19 points between Ostrey Hollow average (3.61) and Larrow Green average (3.78); gap consistent year-round

**Documents:** Committee report of 1908 (signed by Grigg and committee); State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 214 (1911), reporting a feeding trial of bog hay that found a depression of only 0.05 points (not enough to explain the full gap)

---

### Story 3: Three Attempted Explanations and Mounting Tension (1908–1919)

As the years continued and the complaint persisted, three competing explanations took shape in the valley. The first, advanced periodically by some patrons, was that water was being added to the cans before delivery—an old trick in the trade. The second, whispered in conversations, was that the tester himself, Ansel Keddie, might be removing cream for his own benefit and writing low tests to conceal it. The third, advanced by the 1908 committee and repeated by Dr. Roland Kepp, a veterinary surgeon, in 1926, was that the wet hay and poor minerals of Hessel Bottom were genuinely responsible.

In 1919, as the war ended and farming resumed in earnest, the manager of the Association, Selby Vose (who had taken office in 1913), decided to act on the first explanation. He circulated a warning circular to the nine families that received milk from the Ostrey Hollow station. The circular was temperate in language but unmistakable in meaning: it warned against adding water to milk, stated that the Association paid for butterfat and could not pay for creek water, and said that added water would be looked for and dealt with. The circular named no man and accused no man, but in a valley the size of this one, its meaning was plain.

The effect was to fracture a family. Ansel Keddie's wife Orra was the sister of Emmet Brant, the largest patron at the station and the farmer with the most to lose by low test prices. For thirty-six years they had eaten Sunday dinner together, the two families in one kitchen and the men at the fence after. After the 1919 circular, they did not speak—not at the store, not at a funeral, not once in all the years that followed. Ansel carried the circular himself to each farm, standing at the door while each man read it. Two days after, Emmet came up to the station and asked his brother-in-law why he had carried a paper that accused the Ostrey Hollow patrons of cheating. Ansel said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. The brothers-in-law never again entered each other's house in all the years that remained to them.

**Who:** Selby Vose (manager), Ansel Keddie (tester), Emmet Brant (largest patron), the nine families of Ostrey Hollow, Orra Keddie (Ansel's wife, Emmet's sister), Dr. Roland Kepp, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn

**What:** Three explanations circulated (water in cans, theft by tester, poor hay); manager sent warning circular in 1919; family relations ruptured

**When:** Ongoing from 1908 onward; circular issued June 1919; public rupture June 1919

**Quantities:** 0.19-point persistent gap; circular sent to nine families on Ostrey Hollow

**Documents:** 1919 circular (typed by clerk Bertram Kyle); newspaper accounts; diary entries by Orra Keddie; Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks (1908–1923)

### Story 4: An Accidental Experiment Points to the Answer (May 8 – July 10, 1911)

In May of 1911, something happened that no one recognized at the time as decisive but which later proved to be the key to everything. On May 8, 1911, Ansel Keddie's testing measure—the pipette that he used to draw the exact charge of milk into the test bottle—broke. The station had its own measuring glass, brought from Tarnet by the Ammon & Sons dairy supply house in 1897, which had been in daily use for fourteen years. Rather than close the station or delay the milk, Keddie made shift with a borrowed measure from the creamery at Larrow Green.

For the next nine weeks, from May 8 to July 10, while the borrowed measure was in use and everything else remained exactly the same—the same patrons, the same herds, the same pasture in the summer flush, the same acid, the same test bottles, the same hand tester, the same man making the tests at the same hour every morning—the station's average test rose from 3.61 to 3.80, where it held steady. The moment the station's own measure came back into use on July 10, the average fell back to 3.61 the same day.

Keddie noted the fact in his test book. Against the entries for May 8 and July 10, he wrote brief remarks: "Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure" and "Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." That question mark, written by a man who had tested milk for twenty-nine seasons and would not write a figure without proof, stood in the book for the next fourteen years, asked and unanswered.

None of the explanations then current in the valley could account for this interval. The milk came from the same cows, the same pasture was producing (flush season), no diet had changed, no man had changed his practices, nothing on Hessel Bottom had changed. Only one variable had altered: the pipette. Yet no one in 1911, and no one for forty-seven years after, recognized what the nine weeks were trying to say.

**Who:** Ansel Keddie (tester), the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, the creamery at Larrow Green (source of the borrowed measure)

**What:** Station's measure broke; borrowed measure used for 9 weeks; tests rose 0.19 points; returned to original level when station's own measure restored

**When:** May 8 – July 10, 1911

**Quantities:** Test average rose from 3.61 to 3.80 (a 0.19-point rise); fell back to 3.61 when original measure restored

**Documents:** Ansel Keddie's test book, with his own hand-written notes and question mark; State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958), which treats this interval as decisive evidence

### Story 5: The Glass Is Condemned, But No One Connects It to the Past (June 12, 1923)

Twelve years after the mysterious nine weeks of 1911, on June 12, 1923, Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector for the Ordell circuit, made his regular visit to the Ostrey Hollow station. Strawn was a careful man who had carried the Ordell circuit since 1907, traveling by train where there was a train and by livery where there was not. He had detailed routines: he always checked the scales first, then the lactometer for added water, then the boiler and cold room, and finally—the step that most men grudged him—he stood at the tester's elbow and watched him make a test through from the sample to the reading without hurrying him.

In 1921, the state had begun issuing calibrated burettes to field men so they could check whether the pipettes at each plant actually delivered the volume marked on them. It was a slow business, and most plants had never had their glass checked. Strawn carried one in his grip. On his visit to Ostrey Hollow on June 12, 1923, he ran the station's milk pipettes against his burette. The glass would not deliver its mark. Every pipette in the crate fell short. He condemned the whole lot on the spot and told the manager to order new. The manager, Selby Vose, did so within the week, writing to Ammon & Sons on June 28, ordering replacement pipettes and bottles. Ammon & Sons answered on July 2, regretted the trouble, said they would ship within the month, and offered the unhelpful fact that their records of the nineties would not tell them anything about the original lot.

The condemned glass—six dozen pipettes, test bottles, acid measure, and hand tester—was carried up into the loft over the intake room at the station, placed in a wooden crate stenciled with the supplier's name "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET," covered with a tarpaulin, and left there. New glass was in use by the following month. From July 1923 forward, the station's test average rose markedly and stayed there. In 1924 it stood at 3.80 and remained.

No person at the time made any connection between the condemned glass and the twenty-six-year history of low tests. The glass was old; the pattern was old; but no one appears to have stood in that station and thought: What if these numbers were wrong because the glass was wrong all along?

**Who:** Merle Strawn (state dairy inspector), Selby Vose (manager), Ammon & Sons (dairy supply firm)

**What:** State inspector condemned the station's pipettes on June 12, 1923, using a calibrated burette; they were replaced; no one recognized the implication for past tests

**When:** June 12, 1923 (condemnation); June 28 – July 2, 1923 (order for new glass); July 1923 onward (new glass in use)

**Quantities:** 72 original pipettes (6 dozen) in the condemned crate; original invoice cost $77.93; test average rose from 3.61 to 3.80 after replacement

**Documents:** Merle Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook entry; Ammon & Sons letter of July 2, 1923; Ammon & Sons order book entry

### Story 6: The Arbitration and the Dismissal (January 1925 – April 30, 1925)

In January 1925, the patrons of Ostrey Hollow petitioned the Association. They stated their complaint plainly: their milk had tested lower than milk elsewhere for a very long time, they had been paid accordingly, and they asked to be made whole. The Association did not resist arbitration. Under its by-laws, a board of arbitration could be constituted, and the officers agreed to one.

The board was made up of three men, with Alonzo Frick, an attorney of Ashlin, as chairman. Frick was fair and impartial—a man who kept no cows and had no interest in the outcome. The board sat three times. The first session was at the Association's office in Ashlin on March 17, 1925. The second and third sessions Frick insisted on holding at the station itself, in the weigh room, so that the board might see the plant and the appliances before it rather than hear them described by interested parties.

Three explanations were argued before the board. The first was water: Selby Vose, the manager, maintained that water had been going into the cans at the head of the valley and that the rise in 1924 was the measure of it. The second was theft: Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper, answered a question about the station account in a way that suggested—without directly stating—that a man alone in a station with a separator running could help himself and write down a little less. The third was the hay: the 1908 committee report was read into the record, and Hessel Bottom's wet ground was before the board for much of an afternoon.

Ansel Keddie made a statement to the board on March 17. He said he had tested as he was taught and as the state man had seen him do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, that he could not tell the board why the figures were what they were, and that he had never taken a pound of cream. In the corridor afterward, he told Frick privately that he had always thought the patrons themselves were at fault—that he believed water had been added—but he would not say so in the room because he had lived among them thirty years and would not stand up and accuse his neighbors.

On April 30, 1925, four days before the board was ready to issue its finding, the Association dismissed Ansel Keddie. He was told nothing; he came up the road at his ordinary hour, hung his apron behind the shed, and asked what there was for supper. There was ham.

On May 4, 1925, the board issued its finding and award. It found no cause established—none of the three explanations had been proven. Yet it awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800, "without admission," to be charged to the station account. "Without admission" meant the Association was paying without admitting that anyone had done anything wrong. The award was generous—the patrons' own calculation of what they had lost was $19,600—but it was a fifth of it.

**Who:** Alonzo Frick (board chairman), Selby Vose (manager), Wilbur Teague (bookkeeper), Ansel Keddie (tester), the nine families of Ostrey Hollow, the board of arbitration

**What:** Patrons petitioned for back pay; three-session arbitration held; board found no cause but awarded money without admission; tester dismissed before award

**When:** January 1925 petition; March 17 – May 4, 1925 hearings; April 30, 1925 dismissal; May 4, 1925 award

**Quantities:** $3,800 awarded (about 1/5 of the patrons' claimed loss of $19,600); Keddie's twenty-nine seasons ended

**Documents:** Minute book of the board of arbitration; Ansel Keddie's statement to the board (9 sentences); four exhibits submitted to the board (station test book, receiving book, Brant weigh book, state inspector's notebook); award document signed by the board

### Story 7: Thirty-Five Years in the Loft (1923–1958)

The condemned crate sat in the loft over the intake room of the closed Ostrey Hollow station for thirty-five years. The station itself eventually closed—by 1946 milk was being trucked down the valley and skimming had stopped. The building was used for storage, hay tools, mowers. Nobody thought about the loft. The crate sat there, covered by a tarpaulin and a layer of dust and chaff, containing the original six dozen pipettes, of which nine survived unbroken.

In February 1958, a young woman from Farland Agricultural College named Ivy Keddie came to the station building. She was the granddaughter of Ansel Keddie, the tester who had been dismissed in 1925. She was also a graduate student in dairy husbandry. She went up the ladder to the loft, found the crate under the tarpaulin, and brought it down.

On March 9, 1958, Ivy measured the nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette in the college laboratory. She made five runs with each pipette and recorded the results. They were: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, and 16.71 cubic centimeters. The mean was 16.72 c.c. The pipettes were blown on the stem at 17.6 c.c. They were short by 0.88 c.c. each—that is, they delivered 95 hundredths of their marked capacity. Every test made with that glass for twenty-six years had recorded 95% of the truth.

With this measurement and the ledgers of the Association, the arithmetic became plain. Forty million pounds of milk had been received over the affected years. The recorded average test was 3.61 percent. The true average, corrected for the 5% error in the measuring glass, was 3.80 percent. The difference was seventy-six thousand pounds of butterfat that was never credited to the patrons. At the average price of twenty-five cents a pound, they had been underpaid by nineteen thousand dollars.

**Who:** Ivy Keddie (graduate student, granddaughter of Ansel Keddie), Professor Leland Vaught (dairy husbandry), the crate and its nine pipettes

**What:** The condemned glass was discovered in the station loft; measured and found to deliver only 95% of marked capacity; arithmetic revealed 35-year error

**When:** June 1923 (glass condemned and put in loft); February 1958 (Ivy found it); March 9, 1958 (measured); October 1958 (bulletin published)

**Quantities:** 9 surviving pipettes; each short 0.88 c.c. (5% error); 76,000 lbs of butterfat underpaid worth $19,000

**Documents:** Ivy Keddie's measurement sheet dated March 9, 1958; the condemned crate and pipettes; State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958) co-authored by Vaught and Keddie

### Story 8: Vindication and Historical Record (1958 onward)

On October 3, 1958, the Ashlin Chronicle published the story under the headline "Old Ordell Wrong Righted." The State Agricultural College had issued Bulletin No. 471, co-authored by Professor Leland Vaught and Miss Ivy Keddie. The bulletin concluded: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference—seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat—stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

Ansel Keddie's name was cleared. He had been right all along. The figures he recorded were what the glass gave him, and nothing more. He had been honest, he had been careful, and he had been wrong about nothing except his suspicion of his neighbors. He was dead by then—he had died in 1934, twenty-four years before the paper was published. His wife Orra, who had kept his letters and never spoken of the business except once a year in April, was also dead, having passed in 1948 without knowing that her husband had been cleared.

But the record was set right. The books came to rest in various places: the station test book in the Association's office at Ashlin, then to the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1959; Emmet Brant's weigh book, which Roy Brant had lent to Ivy for her work, eventually to Hazel Brant and then to the Historical Rooms in 1963; Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks, which had gone to the state dairy office in 1936 and then to Farland in 1947, where they had sat unread for eleven years until Ivy found the 1923 volume; the Alder Corners test book, which had passed from Jerome Cudd to his daughter Rosalie to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970; and the crate of condemned glass, which was deposited at the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1959.

The story entered the county's memory in different forms. Ivy Keddie wrote notes for her family. A farmer's daughter, Hazel Brant, gave her account. A school teacher, Dell Rundle, published a note to his 1959 reprint of an earlier jubilee pamphlet. The county agent filed a report. A retired clerk wrote down what he had seen pass across his desk. The widow's diary was published with footnotes. A clerk's granddaughter spoke to a newspaper. A cream hauler's son was recorded. And the documents—the ledgers, the test books, the notebooks, the crate itself—remained, waiting for anyone who would look.

**Who:** Ivy Keddie, Professor Leland Vaught, the historians and narrators who would later record the story, Ansel Keddie (posthumously), the people of the Larrow Valley

**What:** The true cause was published; Ansel Keddie's reputation was cleared; the historical record was compiled and deposited; the story became known

**When:** October 1958 bulletin; 1959 onward publication and depositing of records; 1959–1981 various recorded accounts

**Quantities:** 24-year gap between dismissal and vindication; 34-year gap between Ansel's death and vindication; $3,800 of $19,000 loss ever recovered

**Documents:** Bulletin No. 471, Ashlin Chronicle article, historical society accessions, various recorded interviews and published memoirs

## Section B: Relationships (8 points)

### B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Ansel was her paternal grandfather. Ansel's son was Duncan Keddie (born 1894), who taught school at Ashlin, and Duncan's daughter was Ivy (born 1931). Duncan states this plainly in Retelling 18: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise." The Ashlin Chronicle incorrectly called her his "great-niece" in 1958; the county agent Orin Falke corrected this error in his report, noting that the error was his and that "Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece."

### B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant?

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. The connection runs as follows: Emmet Brant and Orra Brant (later Orra Keddie) were brother and sister. Emmet's son was Roy Brant; Orra's son was Duncan Keddie. This makes Roy and Duncan first cousins (children of a brother and sister). Roy's daughter was Hazel Brant; Duncan's daughter was Ivy Keddie. This makes Hazel and Ivy second cousins (children of first cousins). Duncan states this explicitly in Retelling 18: "Roy's daughter is Hazel Brant. My daughter is Ivy. Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation." Cleve Oram (Retelling 21) incorrectly called them "first cousin once removed," but this was valley gossip, not documented fact. The genealogical chain is firm from the multiple consistent accounts.

### B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter to what happened at Ostrey Hollow?

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose, the founder and first president of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. This relationship matters crucially because Gideon Vose drafted the Association's by-laws, and Article VII of those by-laws—which established that "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern"—became the hinge of the entire Ostrey Hollow matter. As Article VII's author, Gideon had created a system with no appeal from the station's test. When Selby took office as manager in 1913, he inherited both this by-law and de facto authority over its application. When the Ostrey Hollow patrons began their complaints and eventually petitioned for arbitration in 1925, they were appealing against a rule that Selby's own father had written. Effie Loomis (Retelling 2) notes that Selby "was made manager in 1913 and served until 1938," and Duncan Keddie (Retelling 18) emphasizes that Selby was Gideon's son, "and not, as I have heard it said, his nephew," correcting a persistent error in valley talk.

### B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?

According to the documentary record and consistent testimony from Duncan Keddie (Retelling 18), there was no family connection between the Vose and Keddie families. Duncan writes: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either. I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her sit down. It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me... and she never once put a Vose in it." Vira Toland (Retelling 1) states: "Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins. Everybody knew that." However, this conflicts with Duncan's explicit denial and his note that his mother kept detailed family genealogies. The weight of evidence falls to Duncan's documentary account: there was no Vose-Keddie connection, and the persistent valley rumor was baseless. Cleve Oram (Retelling 21) also mentions "the Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort" as something he "would hear anyway," but marks it as village gossip from his mother's time, not documented fact. The sources cannot entirely settle this, but Duncan's firsthand family knowledge and his mother's genealogical memory (which he documents) outrank valley rumor.

## Section C: Math (14 points; show working)

### C1. Proportion and true average

(a) **Proportion of delivery error**: The pipettes were marked (blown) at 17.6 c.c. Ivy Keddie's measurement on March 9, 1958 (R4, R7, R14) showed nine pipettes delivering: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean of nine: (16.70 + 16.71 + 16.72 + 16.72 + 16.72 + 16.73 + 16.73 + 16.74 + 16.71) ÷ 9 = 150.48 ÷ 9 = **16.72 c.c.**

Ratio: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.9477** or **94.77%** (call it **95%** or **one part in twenty short**, i.e., 5% error)

[Note: Vaught's bulletin states 16.27 c.c. mean, yielding 0.9244. This conflicts with Ivy's measurements; Ivy's primary data is used here.]

(b) **True average test**: Recorded average was 3.61%. Correcting for the 5% shortfall: true average = 3.61 ÷ 0.9477 = **3.806%**, call it **3.81%**. Shortfall = 3.81 - 3.61 = **0.20 points**, or the commonly cited **0.19 points** (Hazel Brant R17, Ivy R4, County agent R22).

(c) **Butterfat not credited**: Station received 40,000,000 lbs of milk over the affected years (1898-1922).
- Credited at recorded 3.61%: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lbs butterfat
- Actually delivered at true 3.80%: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lbs butterfat  
- Difference: 1,520,000 - 1,444,000 = **76,000 lbs of butterfat**

(d) **Value and recovery**: Average price of butterfat over period was $0.25/lb (stated in R4, R9).
- Total value of unpaid fat: 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000**
- Patrons received: **$3,800** (board award, R10, R13, R5)
- Fraction recovered: $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = **0.20 or 1/5 (20%)**

### C2. Emmet Brant's 1910 (from his own weigh book, R9)

Delivered to station: 200,000 pounds even. Paid on test of 3.42%.

(a) **Credited butterfat**: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lbs**

(b) **True test and underpayment**: Correct for 5% glass error: true test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60%
- True butterfat: 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lbs
- Owed and not credited: 7,200 - 6,840 = **360 lbs of butterfat**

(c) **Value**: 360 × $0.25/lb = **$90.00 that year**

### C3. Station account: butterfat across affected years (1898–1922)

(a) **Butterfat credited to patrons on station test**: 40,000,000 lbs milk × 3.61% = **1,444,000 lbs** (per Ivy's calculation, R4)

(b) **Butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in station cream**: 40,000,000 lbs × true 3.80% = **1,520,000 lbs** (calculated from the true average)

(c) **Difference and percentage**:
- Difference: 76,000 lbs (as per C1.c above)
- As percentage of fat actually delivered: 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **0.05 = 5%**
- As percentage of fat credited to patrons: 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **0.0526 = 5.26% (five and a quarter percent, as the ledger stated "as usual")**

[Note: Wilbur Teague (R5) and the ledger accounts (R7, R23) both cite "five and a quarter per cent" for the station gain. Ivy (R4) clarifies: "Five percent is the one that is also the glass."]

### C5. Duration of faulty glassware and first season

(a) **Duration in use and calculation years**:
- In use: May 1, 1897 (when station's own glass arrived) to June 12, 1923 (condemned)
- Duration: **26 years, 1 month, 11 days** (approximately 26 years)
- Money calculation runs: **1898–1922 (25 full calendar delivery years)**
- Why different: The first partial year (May-December 1897) is not counted in the money calculation, which begins with 1898. The year 1923 is cut short at June 12, after which new glass was in use. The milk-delivery accounting year also did not align perfectly with the calendar year.

(b) **First season pounds (May 4 – December 31, 1896)**: **Approximately 900,000 pounds**. Cleve Oram (R21), who rode the cream wagon as a boy starting in 1909, reports: "That first season they took in about nine hundred thousand pounds up there. May to the end of the year, that is, so it was not even a whole one."

## Section D: Logic (10 points)

### D1. The 1911 experiment

**When**: May 8 – July 10, 1911 (9 weeks)

**What broke**: The station's own measure (pipette) broke on May 8, 1911. A borrowed measure from Larrow Green creamery was used for the interval. The station's own measure was restored on July 10, 1911.

**Variables**:
- **Changed**: The measuring pipette (borrowed vs. station's own)
- **Held constant**: Same patrons; same herds; same pasture (flush season June–July); same season (early summer); same acid; same test bottles; same hand tester; same operator (Ansel Keddie, the tester); same testing procedure; same time of day for testing; same milk intake procedures

**Result**: Average test rose from 3.61% to 3.80% (a 0.19-point increase) during the 9 weeks. The day the station's own measure returned (July 10), the average fell back to 3.61%.

**What it establishes**: That the source of the error is the measurement instrument, not the milk (herds), not the ground (pasture), not the tester's procedure (he was constant), not the season, not the acid or bottles. An instrumental error of approximately 5% is proven—exactly the magnitude later found in the glass. Ansel's own notation in the test book ("? — A.K.") shows he recognized something but could not account for it. This interval is self-contained evidence and would hold even if the glass had never been measured in 1958.

**What it does NOT establish**: It does not prove that the faulty glass caused the full 26-year pattern, only that a measuring instrument of that type caused this specific 5% error. However, combined with the later physical measurement of the glass, it becomes decisive.

---

### D2. Three independent reasons Ansel Keddie could not have been stealing cream

**Reason 1** (Hobart Sill, buttermaker, R14): The cream reached the Larrow Green creamery heavy. Sill, who received and worked the cream, states: "That cream ran heavy. Every year it ran heavy. There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there... I weighed it myself, most years... and you will find the Hollow's cream coming in heavy on page after page after page, in my own writing, for as long as I have been in this room." If Keddie had removed cream before separation, the arriving cream would have been light. This is independent evidence from a different location (the creamery) using weight records.

**Reason 2** (The 1911 experimental interval, R4, R11): When a borrowed measure was used for 9 weeks, the test average rose from 3.61% to 3.80%. Keddie was the same man; his opportunity to steal was the same; his access was the same; his ability was the same. Yet the figures rose automatically when the measurement device changed and fell back when it returned. This proves that the figures were controlled by the instrument, not by the operator's behavior. This is independent because it's an experimental isolation, not dependent on other evidence.

**Reason 3** (Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper, R5): The "station gain" (the difference between credited and received butterfat) ran at exactly 5.25% year after year, "as usual," from 1898 onward. The ledger entry was identical every year: "Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual." If cream had been stolen in significant quantity, the gain would have varied—some years high when theft was high, some years low or absent. But the gain was consistent, mechanical, invariant. A thief's behavior creates scatter; a systematic instrument error creates consistency. This is independent statistical evidence from the ledger record.

**Why independent**: These three reasons do not logically depend on each other. Reason 1 (weight records) could stand alone and be persuasive without 2 or 3. Reason 2 (the 1911 experiment) stands alone as a controlled condition and would hold even if Reason 1 or 3 were somehow wrong. Reason 3 (statistical consistency) is a separate logical proof. A reader might be convinced by any one of them in isolation.

**Did Teague question the entries?** Yes. Teague (R5) states: "I did not sit silent for forty years, whatever has since been said. I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone. I let it alone." He questioned it with the manager but was instructed to drop the matter.

### D3. Did the manager know the station's tests ran low before the glass was condemned?

**Answer: Highly likely yes, though the documentary record is not conclusive.**

**Evidence**: Selby Vose, the manager from 1913 onward, signed the annual ledger sheets every year. Wilbur Teague (R5) states: "I laid the annual sheet before him each spring and he put his name at the foot of it, and a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him." The sheet showed the "station gain" line year after year: "Butter fat credited to patrons on station test... Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream... Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual."

This entry was unusual—a consistent gain of exactly 5.25% every year is not normal accounting. Teague says he raised it with Vose: "I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone." This suggests Vose knew the account was anomalous but chose not to investigate.

Vose also sent the 1919 circular warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons about water, and in his 1926 address (R8) he states: "Water was going into cans at the head of that valley and it stopped when the Association began to look. The rise of 1924 is the measure of it." He believed added water was the cause and accepted that explanation.

**Conclusion**: Vose knew the figures were anomalous (he signed them annually and was told about the gain), but there is no evidence he ever connected the anomaly to faulty glassware or investigated the measuring apparatus. He may have accepted Teague's explanation or simply not inquired further. He certainly did not examine the condemned glass or connect its condemnation in 1923 to the long-standing pattern of low tests.

## Section F: Theory (6 points)

### The true cause

**Causal theory**: From May 1, 1897, to June 12, 1923, the Ostrey Hollow skimming station used a set of milk-test pipettes (measuring glasses) that had been manufactured short of their marked capacity. The pipettes, part of a six-dozen set purchased from Ammon & Sons on April 14, 1897, for $77.93, delivered on average 16.72 cubic centimeters when they were marked (blown) to deliver 17.6 c.c.—an error of approximately 5%. 

This was a proportional error, not an additive one. When milk is drawn into a pipette that delivers only 95% of the marked volume, the resulting test registers 95% of the true butterfat percentage. No milk was watered. No cream was stolen. No procedure was wrong. The tester worked correctly every day. The scale was honest. The acid was correct. The hands were clean. But because one variable changed at the manufacturing stage—the glass was short—every single test produced a figure 5% lower than the truth.

For 26 years, from 1897 to 1923, 40 million pounds of milk from the head of the valley was measured through this faulty glass. At a recorded average test of 3.61%, this should have been 1,444,000 pounds of butterfat, but the patrons actually delivered 1,520,000 pounds. The difference—76,000 pounds of butterfat worth $19,000 at the average price of 25 cents a pound—was never credited to them. Instead, it appeared in the Association's ledgers year after year as "station gain," a mysterious and consistent 5.25% premium on every pound of cream that arrived at the main creamery.

**Why this size error?** 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9477 = a 5% shortfall in the measured charge, which produces a 5% shortfall in every test.

**Why it began when it did?** The glass arrived in May 1897 and went into use immediately. The first season (May–December 1896) used borrowed glass from Larrow Green, which tested correctly, producing a 3.80% average for that partial year. When the faulty glass came into use in 1897, the recorded average dropped to 3.61% and stayed there.

**Why it ended when it did?** On June 12, 1923, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn tested the pipettes with a calibrated burette, found them short, and condemned the entire lot. New glass was ordered and in use by July 1923. From that moment, tests registered the true butterfat content. The 1924 average stood at 3.80%, matching the true content.

**Why nothing on the station or farms had to change?** The herds were the same. The pasture was the same. The feed was the same. The farmer's hands were the same. The tester's hands were the same. The procedure was the same. The only variable that changed was the measuring instrument. Therefore, only the measuring instrument needed to change to fix the problem.

### The three competing explanations and their defeat

**Explanation 1: Water in the cans (advanced by Selby Vose, manager, 1919)**
- **Claim**: The Ostrey Hollow patrons were adding water to their milk to increase weight, knowing they would be paid by test.
- **Evidence defeated by**:
  - Merle Strawn tested with a lactometer on every circuit visit from 1908 onward and "never found any" added water (R6, R22)
  - The 1911 experimental interval: when a borrowed measure (from Larrow Green) was used for 9 weeks, the test average rose from 3.61 to 3.80. Water behavior would not change just because the measuring device changed. The fact that the test rose proves the error was instrumental, not in the milk.
  - Rosalie Cudd (R10) reports that the same four Ostrey Hollow farmers who hauled to her cheese factory over the ridge in summer tested at 3.79–3.82%, right at the true level, not low. If they were adding water, it would have shown at Alder Corners.

**Explanation 2: The tester was stealing cream (advanced by Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper, obliquely at the 1925 hearing)**
- **Claim**: Ansel Keddie was removing cream before separation and writing low tests to conceal the loss.
- **Evidence defeated by**:
  - Hobart Sill (R14): The cream reached Larrow Green heavy, not light. "There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there... you will find the Hollow's cream coming in heavy on page after page after page."
  - The 1911 interval: Same tester, same opportunity, same access, same ability—yet when a different measure was used, the test rose. A thief's behavior is constant; an instrument's error is constant. When the instrument changed, the figures changed. The tester stayed the same.
  - Ledger consistency: The station gain was exactly 5.25% every year, "as usual." Theft would scatter high and low; an instrument error is mechanical and invariant.

**Explanation 3: The wet hay of Hessel Bottom thins the cream (advanced by the 1908 committee)**
- **Claim**: The coarse, wet-meadow hay naturally produces milk lower in butterfat, and this is the remedy that lay with the land, not the station.
- **Evidence defeated by**:
  - The 1911 experimental interval: Same cows, same pasture, same hay, same herd in the summer flush—yet when the measure changed, the test changed. If the hay were the cause, the test would not have changed when only the measuring device changed.
  - State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 214 (1911): Bog hay fed to cows "depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." This is 0.05 points, not the observed 0.19-point gap.
  - Rosalie Cudd's records (R10): The same four Hessel Bottom farms, when they hauled their milk to her cheese factory in summer over the same wet meadow, tested at 3.79–3.82%, not 3.61%. Same ground, same hay in other seasons, same cows—but different test at a different station.
  - Orin Falke (county agent, R22) explicitly revises his initial acceptance: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July. I withdraw the concession made on page four."

### Subsidiary questions

**Did the 1925 board of arbitration examine the condemned glassware?**

No. The board sat March 17 – May 4, 1925. The glass had been condemned and stored in the loft since June 12, 1923—almost two years earlier. Bertram Kyle (R23), the clerk, carried four exhibits to the board: "the station's own test book, in Keddie's hand. The receiving book. Mr. Emmet Brant's private weigh book, which he lent for the purpose and wanted back. And the state inspector's notebook for the year he condemned the glass. Four books... I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked."

The glass was not mentioned at the hearing. It was upstairs in the loft over the weigh room where the board sat. No one thought to look. Cleve Oram (R21) notes: "The crate was up the ladder over their heads the entire time. Twenty feet, if that. I have thought about that since, I will not pretend I thought about it then."

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?**

Ansel Keddie believed water was being added by the patrons. Alonzo Frick (R13) writes: "In the corridor at Ashlin, Keddie told me privately that he had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the room. He was not a bitter man about it. He had lived among them thirty years and would not stand up and accuse his neighbors." Also, in Keddie's own unsent letter (R3): "The complaints began in 1902... In 1919 a circular went out from the Association... I carry no charge against any man who put his name to that paper. I only say that I stood in that room every morning of those years and I know what came into it." He hints at suspicion but refuses to accuse. 

In May 1911, when his measure broke and the borrowed one produced higher tests, he wrote a question mark in the book: "Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." He suspected something was wrong with the measuring glass all along but could not prove it. Duncan Keddie (R18) recalls: "He came home that summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book... What he wrote in the book was a question mark. He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove, and I have thought since that this was the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had."

So Ansel suspected the glass but believed the patrons were adding water. He was wrong about the water but right about the glass.

## Section G: Summary (4 points; max 150 words)

For nearly a quarter-century, farmers at Ostrey Hollow in Larrow Valley brought their milk to a cooperative skimming station and were paid by butterfat test. Their milk consistently tested lower than milk elsewhere—a gap of roughly 0.19 percentage points that cost each family thousands of dollars over a lifetime of farming. Three explanations circulated: the farmers were adding water, the tester was stealing cream, or their wet meadow's hay naturally produced lower-fat milk. A 1925 board of arbitration found "no cause established" yet awarded the farmers $3,800—about one-fifth of their actual loss.

The true answer lay in a crate in the station's loft for thirty-five years. In 1958, a graduate student named Ivy Keddie measured the old, condemned testing pipettes and found them consistently short of their marked capacity by 5%. Every test made for 26 years had recorded 95% of the truth. The remaining records were scattered across three counties. Eight separate witnesses—tester, bookkeeper, manager, inspector, farmer, buttermaker, cream hauler, widow—held different pieces of the story. Only together, across decades, did they reveal what no single person in 1925 could have known.
