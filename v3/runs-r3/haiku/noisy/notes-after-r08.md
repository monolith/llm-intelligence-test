# Retention Notes: Retellings 1–8

## Retelling 1 — Vira Toland (born 1912, Ostrey Hollow, 1979 oral account)
- Born at top of Ostrey Hollow, 1912; lived there except 2 years at Ashlin when first married
- Father hauled milk to station with 8 other families
- Station: low whitewashed building at top of Hollow, cold year-round, smelled of milk/steam/iron
- Ansel Keddie ran station, arrived 1895 to prepare, tested that first summer
- Keddie kept ruled test book in own hand, figures like print, perfect 5-column accounting: never blot, no errors without line-through + initials
- 1919: printed letter from Association (manager Selby Vose, nephew of old Gideon Vose, first president) came to 9 families saying "water in the cans"
- Orra Keddie (Ansel's wife) was first cousin of old Mrs. Vose; also sister of Emmet Brant (biggest patron of the Bottom, most cows, most hay ground, most milk hauled)
- After 1919 letter: Emmet and Ansel never spoke; Orra walked to Larrow Green to avoid Emmet's lane; both families' Sunday dinners ended
- Then "all at once one summer" tests went up, stayed up; nobody said why
- "That was the queerest part...that the thing corrected itself and no man stood up and gave a reason for it"
- Hearing at Ashlin winter/spring after 1919; lawyers and Association officers; men went in wagons
- Result: Ansel Keddie put out; 9 families paid $3,500 total, split 9 ways (father's share didn't buy a hay rake)
- "He was taking the cream...caught him at it and put him out, and that is why the numbers went right the summer after"
- "I am not going to dress it up for you"—cream skimming, separator, door shut, nobody came up road
- Ansel died in the 1930s, still under the stigma; Orra outlived him, had it whole time
- 1958: young woman from college (East) came, Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed; visited station building (shut, held fence wire/salt), talked at table, asked about letter in father's drawer, went up and poked around
- "That is the whole of what I know. It rained the day of that letter, I remember the wet on my father's shoulders when he came in, and I have never in sixty years been able to think about the one without the other."

## Retelling 2 — Effie Loomis, secretary (1901–1940, written 1941)
- Secretary of Association 1901 to 1940; wrote numbers + own recollection, kept apart
- 1889: farmers at Larrow Green organized into Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association; Gideon Vose first president (held 22 years until 1911)
- Loomis came in 1901, so first 12 years (1889–1892) lost—minute book lost when office moved 1938
- Main creamery built at Larrow Green 1891 on Larrow Creek, same building stands
- Before creamery: milk sold where could, no common test, no common price, every man's arithmetic his own
- Creamery "began as a plain remedy for a plain grievance"
- Gideon Vose drafted by-laws himself (not lawyer, farmer's hand, to their credit)
- **Article VII** (adopted annual meeting 1892, year after creamery opened): "Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."
- "That clause is the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business"—station's test, no appeal, written 1892 by Gideon (died 1917, couldn't be asked about it afterward)
- Officers: Loomis secretary 1901–1940 (minute book in own hand); Wilbur Teague bookkeeper from 1905; Hobart Sill buttermaker at Larrow Green (churn book, cream receiving book); Selby Vose manager 1913–1938 (son of late president)
- "Not one of us ever went up the valley and stood in the Ostrey Hollow station on a working morning to watch the work being done"
- Station: 7 miles up valley, took in milk, separated it, sent cream down, gave skim back to farmer
- Hessel Bottom: wet meadow below hamlet; 7 farms had rights on it; largest was Emmet Brant's (largest patron every year on record)
- January 1925: Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned; complaint: milk never tested same as neighbors', paid accordingly "very long time"
- Board of arbitration: Ashlin, 17 March to 4 May 1925, under Alonzo Frick (attorney)
- Three explanations argued; none accepted; minutes in safe
- Award (read by Loomis herself on last day): "The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account."
- "I have written many things into that book with more pleasure. *Finds no cause established*, and then an award of thirty-eight hundred dollars: I took it down as it was given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once."
- Bookkeeper's ledgers: 25 years Ostrey Hollow received 40 million pounds of milk = 1.6M lb/year average; price paid for butterfat averaged 25 cents/lb; figures not Loomis's, Teague's from annual sheets; she added columns twice
- Secretary's authority is record; record stops in two places:
  1. Office move 1938 lost 1889–1892 minute book; looked everywhere; "what was resolved at the first four annual meetings" is a story from father
  2. **"The crate"**—never mentioned in 1925 hearing, not one word, not in transcript; crate of measures state's inspector condemned June 1923, set aside, new ones ordered; not spoken by patrons, officers, or board; award's phrase "books and appliances" is form of words, not list; "Forty years of taking down what men say has taught me that the interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying"

## Retelling 3 — Ansel Keddie (statement 17 March 1925 + unsent letter, spring 1925)
- Statement (9 sentences, standing before board, 17 March 1925):
  - Kept test book since station opened, every figure by his hand
  - Set date, patron, pounds, test, fat; errors crossed with letters & initials; never figure over figure
  - Tests same order, same hour every working day every season
  - Heard complaints from patrons and officers; neither came up road on working morning to watch
  - Asked to account for figures; figures are what he read and wrote, nothing besides
  - Old man, no other trade; tested as taught, state man saw him do it 31 times/week for 29 seasons; cannot tell why figures are what they are
  - "I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself"
- Unsent letter (spring 1925): "because I could not say it in that room, where a man is allowed to answer and not allowed to explain"
  - Day began 5:30 a.m., wagons up Hollow in dark most of year, intake room open, lamps lit, scale swinging free
  - Every can on scale, weight called and written before milk went anywhere
  - Scale tested by state man, never disputed by patron in 29 seasons
  - Sample taken from each patron's milk as weighed, own jar numbered, made up over 10 days before testing
  - Testing morning: jar warmed blood-warm through, poured vessel to vessel until cream through whole
  - "A sample that is not warmed and mixed is worth nothing and a boy could tell you so"
  - "Then the charge"—milk drawn to mark, wiped, charge blown into patron's bottle; acid poured down side; milk dark; 4 bottles in hand tester, weighted, cover down, crank turned at speed taught, no faster; fat up in clear column, read with dividers, figure written down
  - "It is not a hard trade. It is only a trade that must be done the same way every time, which is a harder thing than it sounds"
  - Glass: borrowed from Ashlin creamery 1896, used until "our own outfit came up the valley in a crate the spring following" (1897)
  - Book: 5 columns (date, patron, pounds, test, fat); errors crossed + initials, never figure over figure
  - 29 seasons, 1896–1924; did not test in 1925
  - First leaf reads: "Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie"
  - 1922 back leaf with averages: "Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80."
  - **1911 anomaly**: May 8 measure broke, made shift with borrowed one; May 8 to July 10 tests stood at 3.80; July 10 own measure back in use, tests back to 3.61 same day
  - Two entries: "May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." + "July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."
  - Question mark his own, let stand 14 years; could not answer then or now; will not put anything in place he does not know
  - "I will say one thing more about the figures and then let them alone. They did not come and go with the season. The gap was there in July as it was there in February, in the flush of the grass and in the dead of the winter on dry hay, and it was the same in a wet year as in a dry one."
  - In 1924 average 3.80, figure written with same pen and same care as all others
  - Complaints began 1902, said Ostrey Hollow milk did not test with rest
  - 1908: committee came up, sampled herds through winter, reported wet hay of Bottom thins cream, remedy with land
  - 1919: circular from Association to 9 families saying water in cans; carries no charge against any man, only says stood in room every morning, knows what came in, nine kitchens in Hollow have letter in drawer ever since
  - Wife Orra is Emmet Brant's sister; Emmet brought more milk than any other man, every pound weighed and tested by him, wrote in book; he and Emmet have not spoken since circular
  - "That is the plainest fact in this letter and it is the one I find I have the least to say about"
  - Put out April 30, 1925; found no cause established; put out last day of month; both things true at once; given up trying to hold in head together
  - "I have tested as I was taught. I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are."

## Retelling 4 — Ivy Keddie, granddaughter (1959 talk to Historical Society)
- Ansel Keddie's granddaughter; father Duncan Keddie taught school at Ashlin
- "Nothing I am going to show you tonight depends in the smallest degree on my opinion of my grandfather. It depends on nine pieces of glass and a burette."
- Five sets of records needed, never in one room before last year:
  1. Station test book (Association office since 1925, came to Rooms this year)
  2. Brant weigh book (Hessel Bottom from 1897 on, lent by Roy Brant, Emmet's son, who kept it up since father died)
  3. Alder Corners Cheese Company test figures (copied by Rosalie Cudd, Jerome's niece; book never left Vessey County)
  4. Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks (state dairy inspector, kept from 1908 onward; in Farland college library since 1947—11 years nobody questioned them)
  5. Association's own annual ledgers
- February 1958: went up Hollow to station building (used for storage since closed); loft over intake room (ladder); under tarpaulin: wooden crate stenciled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET," carried up June 1923, still there
- Inside: condemned milk-test pipettes, packed in straw
- Six dozen bought new 1897, invoice $77.93 total (pipettes, bottles, acid measure, hand tester, freight)
- Nine unbroken in crate
- March 9, 1959: measured 9 against calibrated burette at Farland
- Sheet: "Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for."
- "Everything else follows from that line, and I will go through it slowly, because it is arithmetic and not conjuring."
- Butterfat test: draw measured charge of milk into bottle whose neck ruled to read % for that charge only; short charge = short reading, same proportion
- Pipettes delivered 16.72 where blown for 17.6; 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly
- "Every test made at that station with that glass read ninety-five percent of the truth"
- Grandfather's summary leaf (1922): average 3.61; true average (÷ 0.95) = 3.8; gap = 0.19 points
- "And I want you to notice what kind of number nineteen hundredths is, because everyone who looked at this before treated it as a fixed quantity, and it is not one. Nineteen hundredths is five percent of three point eight. The station did not lose a set number of points. It lost a twentieth of whatever was there."
- 25 full delivery years (1898–1922): 40 million pounds milk, average 1.6M/year
- At recorded 3.61%: patrons credited with 1.444M lb fat
- At true 3.80%: delivered 1.52M lb fat
- Difference: 76,000 lb butterfat
- Butterfat average 25 cents/lb over period = unpaid fat worth $19,000
- Board of arbitration 1925 awarded $3,800 = one-fifth of it
- "Two percentages have been quoted at me and both are right, so long as you say what they are percentages of. Seventy-six thousand pounds is five percent of the fat actually delivered—that is, of the million five hundred twenty thousand. It is five and a quarter percent of the fat credited, of the million four hundred forty-four thousand. The Association's ledger line uses the second, because a bookkeeper naturally reckons a surplus against the figure he has already entered. Neither figure is an error. But five percent is the one that is also the glass."
- Error was proportion; fell hardest on best milk
- Herd recorded 4.56 short 0.24 point; 3.42 short 0.18; richer milk took more from it; no way of knowing good cows cost him more than poor ones
- 1911: experiment nobody knew; station's measure broke May 8, substitute used to July 10; same patrons, herds, pasture, season, acid, bottles, man at crank—one object changed—average went to 3.80, back to 3.61 day own measure returned
- "No explanation of this valley's twenty-six-year anomaly survives those nine weeks except the one I have given you."
- Kindest old explanation: wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinned cream
- State Agricultural College tested (1911, Bulletin 214): "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."
- 0.05% in winter, 0 in summer—against 0.19% standing flat every month, July & February alike; four times too small, wrong shape
- Alder Corners (over ridge in Vessey): tested 4 Hessel Bottom men summer flush; where books overlap (14 seasons), same milk off same wet ground ran about 0.19 higher at Alder Corners than at home
- Bulletin 471 (October 1958), closing paragraph (Professor Vaught's wording + Ivy's): "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."
- Glass condemned 1923; cause found 1958: 35 years
- Grandfather dismissed 1925: 33 years
- Died 1934, had been dead 24 years when sheet was read that clears him
- "I have no remedy to propose. I have only the crate, and it is on the table, and you may look into it on your way out."

## Retelling 5 — Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper (1905–1945, 1948 memoir chapter)
- Kept books 1905–1945, 40 years, never carried column that wouldn't prove
- Office: one room over harness shop, Ashlin; cold north end, warm south; ledgers on tall man's shelf; worked at sloped desk with brass rail; remembers paper (station sheets stiffer), ruling, smell of ink pad better than faces
- Association three things on paper: creamery Larrow Green, office Ashlin, station Ostrey Hollow 7 miles up
- Selby Vose manager, Effie Loomis secretary/minutes, Teague ledger
- Station: simplest business—patrons hauled milk up, weighed in, sample taken, tested for butterfat, paid on pounds + test, milk separated, cream sent down, skim back to farmer
- One column always stopped strangers: on one side, butterfat credited to patrons on station's own test (what station's books said bought); other side, butterfat actually came down valley in station's cream, weighed and tested at Larrow Green (what in hand)
- Two always different; second always larger; difference charged to station's account, carried to general Association account
- "**Station-gain line** was my own device; I began it when I came in 1905"
- "I have heard it said in the valley that the line was some old dodge of the founders. It was not. It was a piece of ordinary bookkeeping, invented at that desk by the man sitting at it, because a difference that will not be entered somewhere will come back and bite you at audit"
- Example entry (1898, exact): "Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account."
- "There is one like it for every year after, in the same terms and in the same hand for as long as the hand was mine. The gain ran at five and a quarter percent of the fat that was actually in the cream. It ran there year after year, so evenly that the words 'as usual' got into the entry and stayed there, and a man may as well be honest about what evenness does to a bookkeeper: it stops him looking. A figure that jumps about will keep you awake. A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture."
- Two large numbers: station received 44 million pounds milk over account's run; average test all patrons Ostrey Hollow (whole period, not picked year) was 3.16
- Larrow Green did better; not a season in 25 years Ostrey Hollow men weren't sore about it
- One break: spring 1911, station's own measure broke, made shift with borrowed one, six weeks before own came from town; entered as small charge for replacement, nothing else
- "I would not mention it at all except that in later years people came asking me about that year in particular, as though a bookkeeper kept the weather."
- To board of arbitration 1925 (same words here): "Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons. I said so to the board and I say it here. One man weighed the milk, one man drew the sample, one man made the test, one man wrote the figure that the pay slip was made from, and one man ran the separator. There was no second man in that building from the spring to the fall. I do not say what any particular person did on any particular day, because I was not there and I do not know. I say that a business which is arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken from it, and that no set of books ever made can prevent it. The remedy is two men or a lock, and the Association had neither."
- Patrons petitioned winter (farmer time to be angry), board sat Ashlin under Alonzo Frick (fair chairman), Teague called, gave evidence
- "The board found nothing established against anybody. It then awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800, without admission, and charged it to the station account, and I entered that too, and it balanced."
- Two more things:
  1. "Mr. Vose knew what that account was doing. He signed the sheet every year. I laid the annual sheet before him each spring and he put his name at the foot of it, and a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him. What he made of it he never told me."
  2. "I did not sit silent for forty years, whatever has since been said. I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone. I let it alone. I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience, and a bookkeeper who takes his employer's business up a second time after being told once has generally begun to look for another employer."
- Figures in ledgers at Ashlin, ink, dated; "anybody who wishes to quarrel with this chapter is at liberty to go and read them, which is more than most of the quarrelers have ever done"

## Retelling 6 — Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector (1936, retired, 28 circuit books 1908–1936)
- Took Ordell circuit 1907, laid down 1936; 50-odd plants in 4 counties (creameries, cheese factories, skimming stations, butter 3x/week places); state expected each seen at least once/year, doubtful ones oftener
- Traveled by train where was train, livery where not, spring by neither (roads); learned plants by smells before names—sour-sweet receiving platform July, lime/steam wash room, flat cold nothing well-run cold room (smell he liked, meant no letter to write)
- Work always same four things, same order (so wouldn't forget which done): (1) scales against sealed weights he carried (intake & cream scales, loaded light & heavy); (2) lactometer on day's milk (patron by patron if question) for added water; (3) boiler, water supply, cold room (pressure, gauge, cased well, hold overnight); (4) stood at tester's elbow, watched through sample to reading, didn't let hurry
- "I put down what I found. I did not put down what I thought about it, and I have not begun now."
- 1907: Ostrey Hollow station (7 miles up from Larrow Green, opened before his time); scales true, cold room fair; tester keeps book own hand (better than most), works slow and clean, reads own figure without coaxing; watched two tests
- 1909: Ostrey Hollow fall; scales true, boiler safe, tested; station takes from wet bottom farms & 3 hill places; milk sweet, cans clean, one man cautioned rusted seam
- 1911 June: Ostrey Hollow on way other side county; scales true, watched test, nothing to note
- 1912: Ostrey Hollow; scales true, lactometer no added water; cream shipped down valley same day separated; creek out over flat road, went round ridge, lost afternoon
- 1913: Larrow Green creamery, 2 days; scales true; new manager (came out platform, last one didn't)
- 1915: Ostrey Hollow & plants down county line; scales true; wash room floor holding water at drain, ordered graded
- 1916: Ostrey Hollow; scales true, lactometer no water; tester complains patrons blame him for figures; Strawn told him that's trade
- 1917: Cheese factory next county, then Larrow Green; scales true both; bad help year, two testers doing three men's work (where mistakes live)
- 1919: Ostrey Hollow; scales true, lactometer no water; manager sent circular around patrons about water in cans; Strawn tested for water every visit, never found any, told him so
- 1920: Ostrey Hollow twice (spring & fall); scales true both times, no water; cold room short ice fall, said so, seen to before out county
- 1921: State began issuing burettes to field men (wooden case, graduated, with printed directions) for checking delivery of measures & pipettes; "slow business to run plant's glass against it and most plants have never had it done"
- 1922: Ostrey Hollow; scales true, no water, boiler in order; "I did not run their glass against the burette this year, the day being short and the road home being what it was, and I have put that down because it is what happened"
- **June 12, 1923**: "Ostrey Hollow. I had carried the burette two seasons before this check." [entry quoted]: "Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk pipettes against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them."
- October 1923: Ostrey Hollow; new glass in use since July; ran new pipettes against burette; deliver their mark; scales true
- 1924: Ostrey Hollow; scales true, no added water, tests improved; book gives year at 3.80
- 1925: Ostrey Hollow fall; scales true, no water, tests improved; new man at tester's bench; quick (not what look for)
- 1926: Word from state office burette program discontinued; instruments kept by who have them but no more issued, no returns wanted; "Mine is in its case in my grip and has been used twice since"
- 1928: Ostrey Hollow; scales true, no water; station quieter than was; loft over intake room full odds and ends; told loft over milk room is place for nothing but air; told in return full since before war
- 1931: Larrow Green creamery & Ostrey Hollow same week; both in order; bad ice year; plants short, two buying from railroad
- 1934: Ostrey Hollow; scales true, no water; separator bearing run hot, shut 2 days, since repaired; tester's book kept up, though not in hand it used to be kept
- 1936 March: Last round; Ostrey Hollow, Larrow Green, 2 factories over county line; scales true all four; nothing to note anywhere, nothing left to say about it
- Closing note: 28 books (one/year, oilcloth covers, pocket size) go to state dairy office with copy; "nothing in them but what a field man puts in a field book: what I weighed, what I tested, what I told the plant to mend, and whether it was mended when I came back. They are dull reading and they were meant to be."
- Asked once or twice (by men who ought to know better) to say in general way how certain plant stood in estimation; always answered: "I do not keep an estimation, I keep a book, and the book is open to anybody the state sends for it. I never had a theory about a plant and I do not recommend having one. A theory is a thing a man carries out to the platform with him and it gets in the way of the weights."

## Retelling 8 — Selby Vose, manager (1926 annual address to membership)
- Manager 13 years (since 1913), standing up 13 times to give account
- "I have never had a year I liked less to report on than the one behind us, and I have never had one I was gladder to have behind us"
- Ordinary business: season wet, heavy on horses & men; creamery Larrow Green ran flush without day lost (creameries below us can't say same); butter held place with trade; churn room clean; ice in early, lasted; no complaint payroll; station Ostrey Hollow 7 miles up, ran season, cream daily & sweet
- "I would to heaven it were the whole of the address"
- January 1925: Ostrey Hollow patrons laid petition; board of arbitration constituted, sat Ashlin under Alonzo Frick (able, impartial); heard everybody at length, some cost in time
- Board's finding: "no cause established" against station, office, any officer/servant; only sentence "arrived at by men under oath with the books in front of them"
- Having found no cause, board awarded $3,800 without admission, charged station account
- Vose supported payment then and now: "generous settlement of unproved claim"; Association paid rather than body of own members go believing used badly by neighbors; "not a business house in Ordell County would have done it. We did it because we are not a business house; we are the farmers of this valley in company together, and a company that will not spend money to keep peace among its own members has not understood what it is for"
- "But I will not have peace bought a second time, and so I will say what I believe to be the cause of the whole affair, and say it in this room, where I have said everything else"
- **Vose's claim**: Water was going into cans at head of valley, stopped when Association began to look; rise 1924 measure
- For generation milk brought station tested below Larrow Green; every year told "wet hay, or tester, or office, or weather"
- Then Association attention came to rest on station—petition, hearing, men required answer in front of neighbors
- **Very next season** tests at station rose by 0.21 points; average 1924 stood at 3.90 ("as good a figure as any station in county")
- "Nothing was done to those cows. Nothing was done to that bottom land. Nobody changed their feed and nobody changed their pasture. The tests came up when the looking began, and any man who wants a different explanation of that is welcome to stand where I am standing and give us one."
- State man's visit: came through county every year or two (testing scales—found true), condemned testing apparatus **twelfth of July, 1923**, told order new; ordered within month, use before flush over
- "Apparatus wears. A station in daily use since 1896 will wear its fittings out twice over in that time, and it is the state man's business to say so before they fail him, and ours to replace them when he does. I decline to build a mystery on a piece of housekeeping that appears in our own order book with a date and a price against it."
- Not comfortable saying this room with Ostrey Hollow men, without naming name (doesn't know name, wouldn't use if did)
- "A little water in a can is not a felony in a man's own mind. It is a way of making a hard year come out even, and the man who does it tells himself he will stop when the price improves"
- No wish prosecute, nobody has been or will be
- Defends 1919 circular sent patrons of station: duty to write it; warned added water would be looked for & dealt with; named nobody, accused nobody; "This Association pays for butterfat and cannot pay for creek water, and I would write every word of it again tomorrow. A manager who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing to the men who make them is not being kind to them; he is being idle at their expense."
- Father (first president) kept Jersey herd at home place, tested 4.56 in 1910; not to boast (gone now, no better than others' now), but settles thing said about county—"Larrow Valley is poor milk country, land sour, cattle indifferent, figures what man expects off ground like ours. That is not true. There is no better milk in state than this valley can produce when comes to platform as it left cow, and proof stood in my own barn."
- Wants from every man coming season: "Bring it as it left the cow. Wash the cans, cool the milk, keep the dog out of the milk house, and bring it as it left the cow. Do that, and the Ostrey Hollow tests will stay where they went in 1924, and this business will be dead and buried where it belongs, and your manager can stand up here next spring and talk to you about butter prices, which is what he is paid for."
- "I thank you for thirteen years of your patience and I ask for one more."

## Central Conflict & Contradiction
**Narrative Problem**: Selby Vose (manager 1913–1938) claims in his 1926 address that water was intentionally added to the Ostrey Hollow station's milk by someone, stopped when Association investigated (1925), evidenced by 1924 tests rising 0.21 points to 3.80.

**Counter-Evidence**:
- Ansel Keddie's statement (March 17, 1925) denies taking cream; says he tested properly; points to 1911 measure break as only anomaly
- Ansel's unsent letter (spring 1925) details procedure; expresses bewilderment about 25-year consistent gap; says cannot explain figures
- Merle Strawn's 1919 visit: explicitly tested for water, found none (repeated every visit)
- **June 12, 1923 (before petition)**: Strawn condemned pipettes—they don't deliver their mark
- **October 1923 (before any petition)**: New glass installed; tests improved naturally
- **1924 (after new glass)**: Tests rise to 3.80—not because water stopped, but because old defective glass was replaced
- Ivy Keddie's 1958 measurements: 9 surviving pipettes deliver 16.72 c.c. (95% of 17.6 marked); means all prior tests read low by 5%
- **1911 anomaly explained**: May 8–July 10 borrowed pipette was presumably better-calibrated, so 3.80 appeared; own measure returned, tests dropped to 3.61; not anomaly, just switched from one defective to another (back to regularly defective)
- Alder Corners data (Vessey County): same 4 Hessel Bottom men's milk tested 0.19 higher there than at Ostrey Hollow over 14 overlapping seasons (other dairy was using better glass)
- Board finding (May 4, 1925): "finds no cause established" against any person or apparatus, yet awarded $3,800 "without admission"
- **Physical evidence now**: Condemned crate of defective glass (June 12, 1923), still intact, discovered 1958; measurement proves defect precisely

**Implications**:
- Vose's 1926 claim (intentional water addition) contradicted by all documentary and physical evidence
- Ansel's dismissal (April 30, 1925) was based on a conclusion contrary to facts; he was exonerated 34 years posthumously (died 1934, vindicated 1958)
- The defective glass had been in use May 1, 1897–June 12, 1923 (26.1 years), reading low by 5%; accounts for exactly the 0.19-point gap Ivy calculates
- Selby Vose knew about the glass condemnation (June 12, 1923, 9 months before petition); yet in 1926 blamed missing tester for low tests, not glass
- Teague knew about station gain (5.25%) and signed sheets; never questioned it directly after 1916 rebuff
- Loomis knew crate was never mentioned in hearing despite "books and appliances" language
- Strawn's testimony not introduced at hearing (his 1923 condemnation would have been exculpatory)

## Financial Calculations
- 40 million pounds milk (1898–1922, 25 years)
- Station gain consistently 5.25% of fat actually in cream
- Recorded average test: 3.61%
- True average test: 3.80% (3.61 ÷ 0.95)
- Gap: 0.19 points (5% of 3.80)
- Credited fat (at recorded 3.61%): 1,444,000 lb
- Delivered fat (at true 3.80%): 1,520,000 lb
- Lost fat: 76,000 lb
- Lost value (at 25 cents/lb): $19,000
- Award by arbitration (1925): $3,800 (1/5 of lost value)
- Pipette delivery: 16.72 c.c. vs. 17.6 marked = 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 = 95 hundredths
