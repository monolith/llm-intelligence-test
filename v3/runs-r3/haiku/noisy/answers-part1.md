# Answers to Test Questions

## Section A — Reconstruction (8 Stories)

### Story 1: The Association's Founding and Founding Rules (1889–1892)

In 1889, farmers of the Larrow Valley, frustrated with inconsistent payment for butter sold through middlemen, organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association at Larrow Green. Gideon Vose, a farmer, was elected first president and took the unusual step of drafting the bylaws himself—not by lawyer but by farmer's hand—codifying the cooperative's core principle into Article VII. This article, adopted at the annual meeting in 1892 (the year after the main creamery opened on Larrow Creek), established that every patron would be paid not according to the weight of milk delivered but exclusively according to the pounds of butterfat determined by test—and critically, that the test made at the station where he delivered would be the test that governed his pay absolutely, with no appeal. This frameless rule made the reliability of testing instruments not a matter of convenience but a matter of survival for farmers in the valley. By the time this history ends, no other single provision of the Association would matter more. Gideon Vose drafted the clause without understanding what it would cost when the instruments proved faulty; he died in 1917, before the full consequence became clear. The bylaw stands unchanged: the station's test governs. In a system where tests had been wrong for twenty-six years, this meant farmers had no defense except proof that the test itself was defective—a thing nobody thought to examine for decades.

**Narrators**: Effie Loomis (r02), Dell Rundle (r12), Orin Falke (r22), Bertram Kyle (r23); **Documents**: Article VII text appears in notes and multiple retellings; **Key point**: The rule created absolute dependence on instrument accuracy with no independent verification mechanism.

### Story 2: Opening of Ostrey Hollow Station and Installation of the First Glassware (1896–1898)

In the winter of 1895–1896, the Larrow Valley Association built a small skimming station seven miles up the valley at Ostrey Hollow to serve the six farms of Hessel Bottom and surrounding hamlets. The station opened on May 4, 1896, under Ansel Keddie, a man of fifty-five who had been teaching himself testing practice for several months and who would remain at the post for twenty-nine seasons. For the first season (May 1896 through the end of 1896), the station borrowed its complete testing outfit—pipettes, bottles, acid measure, hand tester—from the main creamery at Larrow Green, as the Association's own equipment had not yet arrived. Ansel kept a meticulous test book from the opening day forward, five columns: date, patron, pounds, test, fat, all in his own hand, errors crossed out and initialed, never a figure written over. On May 1, 1897, the station's own glassware arrived from Ammon & Sons dairy supply at Tarnet: six dozen milk-test pipettes blown to a mark of 17.6 cubic centimeters, twelve dozen test bottles, one acid measure, one four-bottle hand tester, all for a total invoice of $77.93 including freight. Ansel notes on the first leaf of his station test book: "Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes." On May 1, 1897, those words would be superseded. Ansel would test every farmer's milk against this glassware for the next twenty-six years and two weeks, until June 12, 1923, when the state dairy inspector condemned the entire crate without explaining what it had been doing wrong the whole time.

**Narrators**: Ivy Keddie (r04), Halbert Nym (r07), Junia Ammon (r20), Bertram Kyle (r23); **Documents**: Ammon & Sons invoice April 14, 1897 ($77.93); station test book first leaf; **Key quantities**: 6 doz pipettes @ $0.35 = $25.20; 26 years of use until June 1923.

### Story 3: The Low-Test Pattern Emerges and Early Investigations (1902–1911)

From nearly the day the station's own glass came into use, the milk of Ostrey Hollow tested lower than the milk from any other station in the valley. In June 1897, the average was 3.80; from 1898 onward, the average hovered consistently at 3.61, while the main creamery at Larrow Green averaged 3.78. The difference—0.17 to 0.19 points—ran flat every month, July and February alike, in wet years and dry ones. The Ostrey Hollow patrons began complaining in 1902, and complaints recurred at nearly every annual meeting. In 1908, a committee chaired by Nyle Grigg was appointed to investigate. The committee sampled the Hessel Bottom herds throughout January, February, and March; found the milk whole, the scales honest, the herds sound; and concluded that the wet hay and coarse grasses of the Hessel Bottom meadow—which stood under water every spring and was fed as the primary winter feed—must be thinning the cream. The report was accepted and filed. Eleven years later, in 1919, Grigg reprinted it with a covering letter, noting that the State Agricultural College's Bulletin No. 214 (on bog hay feeding) had since found that such hay depressed butterfat by "two tenths" in winter, which he took as vindication of his theory (though the bulletin actually stated "not more than five hundredths" in winter, or about 0.05 points). The matter seemed settled until May 8, 1911, when the station's own pipette broke. A borrowed measure was used until July 10. During those nine weeks, the station's average test rose to 3.80—exactly the level Ansel had recorded for 1896, the first year on borrowed glass. The day the station's own measure returned, tests fell back to 3.61. Ansel noted both events in his book and placed a question mark—the only uncertainty he ever recorded in twenty-nine seasons. Nobody in the valley drew an inference.

**Narrators**: Vira Toland (r01), Ivy Keddie (r04), Nyle Grigg (r15), Roland Kepp (r16), Orin Falke (r22), Orra Keddie (r24); **Documents**: Grigg's 1908 committee report, Grigg's 1919 covering letter, State Agr. College Bulletin 214 (1911), Ansel's notations for May 8 and July 10, 1911; **Key finding**: The 1911 anomaly (borrowed measure produced higher tests despite identical herds, season, and operator) suggests the defect was in the apparatus, not the cows or land—but this inference went unstated until 1958.

### Story 4: The 1919 Water-Warning Circular and the Rupture of the Brant-Keddie Household (1919)

In June 1919, Selby Vose, manager of the Association since 1913, composed and circulated a letter to the nine families at Ostrey Hollow warning them in civil but unmistakable terms that the Association had begun investigating water being added to milk, that milk with added water would be detected and dealt with, and that all patrons should ensure the purity of their supplies. The letter was signed by Vose and carried no accusation against any individual; it was a general warning. Ansel Keddie received it at the station and, rather than post it on a bulletin board or send it by mail, took it himself to each of the nine families, standing at their doors as each man read it. He came home at eleven at night with his hat still on. Two days later, Emmet Brant—Ansel's brother-in-law, the largest patron of the station, and Orra's brother—rode up to the station and confronted Ansel in the yard. Emmet said: "You have hauled whole milk to this station since the day it opened and you know it better than any man living, and then you ask him why he carried the paper himself if he did not believe it." Ansel replied: "I carried it because I would not have another man carry it." Emmet went home. According to Orra's diary, Emmet did not enter the house again for ten years, until Ansel had died and propriety allowed reconciliation after Emmet's own death in 1929. Orra notes: "My brother Emmet is dead. I went down. Roy met me at the gate and was kind." The circular, sent to prevent water fraud, succeeded only in making an enemy of the man who hauled the most milk into the station. Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, testified that he tested for water at every visit from 1908 onward and never found any.

**Narrators**: Effie Loomis (r02), Bertram Kyle (r23), Orra Keddie (r24); **Documents**: The circular itself; Orra's diary entries for June 1919; **Key consequence**: The circular caused a family rupture that silenced the largest patron and made it impossible for Ansel to be defended by the patron he was said to have wronged most.
