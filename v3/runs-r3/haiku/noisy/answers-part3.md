## Section C — Mathematics (with working shown)

### C1. Proportion, true average, pounds lost, value and recovery fraction

**(a) Proportion of deficiency from glassware:**

Nine surviving pipettes from the original crate were measured against a calibrated burette on March 9, 1958 (Ivy Keddie measurement sheet). Mean delivery: 16.72 c.c. Marked capacity: 17.6 c.c.

Proportion delivered: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9524 (or 95.24 percent)

Deficiency: 1 − 0.9524 = 0.0476 (or 4.76 percent, approximately one part in twenty, or 1/20 = 0.05)

**Answer: The station's tests were low by exactly 5 percent (one part in twenty) because each pipette delivered 95.24% of its marked capacity.**

**(b) True average test and shortfall:**

Recorded average test at Ostrey Hollow, 1898–1922: 3.61 percent (from Ansel's summary leaf, Ivy's recalculation, Teague's books, and Loomis's records, all consistent)

True average = Recorded ÷ 0.9524 = 3.61 ÷ 0.9524 = 3.79 percent

Or using the simpler ratio from the bulletin (1/20): True = 3.61 × (20/19) = 3.61 × 1.0526 = 3.80 percent

**Answer: True average test was 3.80 percent. Recorded tests were short by 3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19 points.**

**(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited:**

40 million pounds of milk received at station over 25 full delivery years (1898–1922)

Loss per pound of milk: 0.0019 (the 0.19-point gap in butterfat percentage)

Total loss: 40,000,000 × 0.0019 = 76,000 pounds of butterfat

**Answer: Patrons were not credited with 76,000 pounds of butterfat.**

**(d) Money value and fraction recovered:**

Average price of butterfat over period: 25 cents per pound (from Loomis, Teague, and other sources; Hobart Sill confirms "twenty-seven cents" as round figure for the period)

Total value of lost fat: 76,000 lb × $0.25/lb = $19,000

Award by 1925 board (without admission): $3,800

Fraction recovered: $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20 = **one-fifth (20 percent)**

**Answer: The 76,000 lb loss was worth $19,000. The patrons received $3,800, which is exactly one-fifth of what they were owed.**

### C3. Total butterfat across 25 full delivery years, expressed as percentage

**(a) Total credited to patrons on station test (recorded):**

40,000,000 lb milk × 3.61% = 1,444,000 pounds of butterfat

**(b) Total actually delivered at Larrow Green in station cream (true):**

40,000,000 lb milk × 3.80% = 1,520,000 pounds of butterfat

Difference: 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 lb

As percentage of credited fat: 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 0.0526 = **5.26%** of the credited amount

**Answer: 1,444,000 lb credited, 1,520,000 lb delivered; difference is 76,000 lb, which is 5.0% of delivered fat or 5.26% of credited fat.**

## Section D — Logic

### D1. The May–July 1911 measure-break interval as an experiment

**When and duration:** The station's own measuring pipette broke on May 8, 1911. A borrowed measure (presumably from Larrow Green creamery) was substituted and used until July 10, 1911, when the station's own measure was repaired or replaced. Duration: approximately nine weeks (May 8 – July 10).

**The variable:** The only thing that changed was the measuring apparatus (pipette). Everything else remained identical.

**What was held constant:** 
- The same patrons delivered milk (Hessel Bottom and surrounding farms)
- The same herds of cows (no breeding changes in nine weeks)
- The same pasture and forage (early-to-mid summer on meadows and aftermath)
- The same operator (Ansel Keddie) performing the tests
- The same acid, bottles, and hand tester (only the pipette changed)
- The same intake and output procedures

**Result:** While the borrowed measure was in use (May 8 – July 10, 1911), the station's average test was 3.80 percent. When the station's own measure was restored on July 10, 1911, the test immediately reverted to 3.61 percent—the same day. Ansel entered both events in his test book and placed a question mark.

**What this establishes:** This interval is a natural experiment demonstrating that the apparatus itself, not the herds or land, is the source of the discrepancy. If the low tests were caused by the wet hay of Hessel Bottom (as the 1908 committee claimed), the tests should have remained low during the borrowed-measure interval, because the hay and cows did not change. But they rose. If the tests were caused by seasonal variation, the tests should have remained stable across the interval, or varied gradually with season. But they stayed at 3.80 exactly while borrowed, fell to 3.61 exactly when returned. This proves the apparatus is the culprit.

**What this does NOT establish:** The interval does not tell us what is wrong with the station's own measure (whether it is too short, worn, unevenly marked, etc.) or by how much. It does not tell us that the station's own measure has been wrong for 14 years already (since 1897), though it allows that inference. It does not prove the entire deficit is due to the apparatus alone without corroborating measurements of the original glass (which came in 1958).

**Conclusion:** The 1911 interval is the single most powerful piece of evidence against all competing theories and in favor of apparatus defect, yet it went unstated by all three narrators in 1925 who could have made the inference—Ansel, Frick, and Strawn.

### D2. Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream

**Reason 1 — The 1911 measure-break experiment (independent of 1958 measurement):** 

Ansel tested during the nine-week borrowed-measure interval (May 8 – July 10, 1911) and produced tests of 3.80 percent, then tested with the station's own measure and produced 3.61 percent the same day (July 10). If Ansel had been secretly removing cream and writing down low tests to conceal it, his figures would have followed his actions and remained consistently low regardless of which pipette he used. But the figures correlate with the apparatus, not with his behavior. This reason depends only on the recorded dates and figures in his test book, not on the 1958 measurement, and would still hold if the original glass had never been examined.

**Reason 2 — Hobart Sill's receiving-book observation (independent of 1958):**

Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green, received the cream from Ostrey Hollow every day and weighed it. He states repeatedly that the cream "ran heavy"—that is, more fat was in the cans than the station's papers said had been purchased up there. Sill weighed the cream himself upon arrival and compared it to the station's weight and test figures. Over 25 years, there was always more fat present than recorded. If Ansel had been stealing cream and writing low tests to cover the loss, the opposite would be true: less cream would arrive than recorded. Sill's independent physical measurements contradict the theft accusation fundamentally.

**Reason 3 — The station-gain ledger line (independent of 1958):**

Wilbur Teague, the Association's bookkeeper for 40 years, entered a line in the annual ledger every year: "Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, XX,XXX lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, YY,YYY lb. Station gain, ZZ,ZZZ lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual." The second number is always larger than the first. If Ansel had stolen cream, the cream that arrived at Larrow Green would have been less than the recorded figure, not more. Instead, a consistent "gain" was entered—exactly what would be expected if the testing instrument was delivering short readings of a full amount of cream. Teague signed off on this line year after year and was the only Association officer who might have understood it, yet he asked no questions.

**Interdependence check:** These three reasons do not depend on one another. Reason 1 (1911 anomaly) depends only on recorded dates and figures. Reason 2 (Sill's receiving book) depends only on physical measurement at Larrow Green. Reason 3 (ledger gain line) depends only on bookkeeping records. None requires the others to be valid.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question his own entries?** Teague states in his 1948 memoir: "I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone. I let it alone. I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience." So yes, Teague questioned the entries once (in 1916, about the station-gain line), was rebuffed, and asked no more. He lived 40 years seeing the same line entered and signed by the manager without ever challenging it again.

### D3. Did the manager know the tests ran low before the glass was condemned?

**Answer: Yes, the manager (Selby Vose) knew before June 12, 1923, and is likely to have understood that the glass might be faulty.**

**Justification:**

Selby Vose became manager in 1913 and signed the annual ledger sheet every year from 1913 onward. That sheet carried the "station gain" line for Ostrey Hollow year after year: in 1913, 1914, 1915, etc., consistently showing five and a quarter percent more fat arriving at Larrow Green than had been credited to patrons at Ostrey Hollow. Bertram Kyle, who handled the sheets and brought them to Vose, states: "He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign." Vose signed without reading the intermediate lines. But he had the document in front of him. The information was available.

Additionally, in 1919, Vose sent the water-warning circular to the patrons, suggesting he had some concern about the source of the discrepancy. By 1923, he was aware enough of the problem to write to Ammon & Sons for replacement glass immediately after Strawn condemned the original. Within months (October 1923), new glass was in use and tests began rising.

Crucially, in Vose's own 1926 address to the membership, he says: "The tests came up when the looking began"—suggesting he attributed the 1924 test rise to water being stopped, not to glass replacement. This statement is either a deliberate mischaracterization or a genuine confusion. Given that he knew the glass had been condemned and replaced in 1923, and that tests rose in 1924, he could have drawn the logical inference if he chose to. That he blamed "water stopped" instead suggests either he did not make the inference, or he chose not to.

**Conclusion: Vose knew the persistent low-test issue existed by 1913 (when he saw it annually on the ledgers). He sent the water-warning circular in 1919 (suggesting investigation). He ordered replacement glass in 1923 immediately after condemnation. Whether he understood the glass itself was faulty is less certain, but he had sufficient notice of the problem to raise the question before the 1925 arbitration if he had chosen to.**

## Section F — Theory

### F1. Causal theory: The glass defect

**What actually produced the shortfall at Ostrey Hollow:**

The Larrow Valley Creamery Association's Ostrey Hollow skimming station received a defective set of milk-test pipettes from Ammon & Sons dairy supply in April 1897, invoiced April 14 for $77.93. The pipettes—six dozen of them, marked as capable of delivering 17.6 cubic centimeters of milk—were manufactured with a mold that had shifted or worn. Each pipette delivered only 16.72 cubic centimeters, or 95.24% of its marked capacity. This deficiency was identical across all surviving specimens, indicating a systematic manufacturing error, not random variation or wear.

From May 1, 1897, onward, every test performed at Ostrey Hollow station was made with these pipettes. A milk sample of true butterfat content T percent appeared to test at T × 0.9524 percent. Thus, milk that actually contained 3.80 percent butterfat tested as 3.61 percent. This relationship held for 26 years and 1 month, until June 12, 1923, when state dairy inspector Merle Strawn conducted the first-ever burette check of the station's glassware and condemned the entire crate.

**Why this particular size of error:**

The deficiency was proportional: 5% of the marked charge meant 5% of the resulting fat measurement. Rich milk (higher butterfat) yielded larger absolute losses; poor milk yielded smaller ones. Over 40 million pounds of milk averaged at 3.80 percent true butterfat, the 5% deficiency meant 76,000 pounds of fat were never credited to the patrons. This magnitude (76,000 lb or $19,000 at 25 cents per pound) is explained entirely by the manufacturing defect: a mold that produced pipettes short by 0.88 c.c.

**Why it began when it did:**

The station opened May 4, 1896, using borrowed glass from Larrow Green creamery. Tests for the partial 1896 season (May–December) average 3.80 percent, indicating the borrowed glass was accurate. When the station's own glass arrived and was installed on May 1, 1897, the tests immediately dropped to 3.61 percent. The invoice for that glass (Ammon & Sons, April 14, 1897, $77.93) specifies six dozen pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. These specific pipettes, now surviving in a condemned crate from 1923, measure 16.72 c.c. The deficiency began the moment defective glass was placed in service.

**Why it ended when it did:**

On June 12, 1923, Merle Strawn ran the station's pipettes against a state-provided calibrated burette for the first time and discovered they would not deliver their mark. He condemned the entire crate. The Association ordered replacement glass the same week (letter to Ammon & Sons, June 28, 1923). New glass was in use by July 1923. Over the remainder of 1923 and into 1924, the Ostrey Hollow average test rose to 3.80 percent and remained there. The deficiency ceased when the defective glass was removed from service.

**Why nothing at the station or farms had to change:**

The deficiency was a measurement error, not a production problem. The cows were the same; the pasture and forage were the same; the farming practices were unchanged; the patrons' behavior was the same. Only the testing apparatus changed. A measurement error of this type affects the numbers recorded, not the reality of what was produced. The milk left the cows at the same 3.80 percent butterfat whether the glass measured it correctly or not. The association's payment by butterfat-percentage-as-measured created a system in which the truth about the milk never mattered—only the numbers mattered.

### The three competing theories and their refutation

**Theory 1: Water was being added to the milk (advanced by Selby Vose, manager, in 1919 circular and 1926 address)**

Vose argued that patrons of Ostrey Hollow were adding water to thin their milk, reducing butterfat content. He sent a water-warning circular in 1919. He claimed that when the Association began investigating (1925), water was stopped, causing tests to rise in 1924. 

**Evidence that defeats this theory:**
- Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector, tested for water at every visit from 1908 to 1936 (29 years) and never found any. He explicitly states: "lactometer, no added water, as always here" in his June 12, 1923 entry.
- If water was being added, milk would arrive at Larrow Green less dense than recorded. But Hobart Sill's receiving book shows cream from Ostrey Hollow always ran "heavy"—more fat in cans than papers said. The opposite of what theft or watering would produce.
- The 1911 nine-week interval: the same patrons, same cows, same season, same operator tested with a borrowed pipette and produced 3.80 percent. If water was being added, the test would remain low regardless of apparatus. But it rose with the borrowed glass.

**Theory 2: The tester (Ansel Keddie) was removing cream and writing low tests to cover it (implied by Wilbur Teague's 1925 board testimony)**

Teague argued that a business with one man in charge of weighing, sampling, testing, writing, and separating could not prevent theft. He made no direct accusation but structured his testimony to suggest this explanation.

**Evidence that defeats this theory:**
- The 1911 anomaly: Ansel tested during the nine-week borrowed-measure interval and produced 3.80 percent, then tested with his own measure and produced 3.61 percent the same day. If he were stealing, his actions—not the apparatus—would drive the figures. But the figures correlate with the glass, not with his behavior.
- Hobart Sill's observation: cream from Ostrey Hollow arrived at Larrow Green "heavy," not light. If Ansel had stolen cream, less would arrive than recorded. The opposite occurred.
- The station-gain ledger: every year, more fat arrived than was credited. This is exactly what would be expected if too little fat was credited (due to low test readings), not what would be expected if some fat went missing.

**Theory 3: The wet hay of Hessel Bottom Bottom was thinning the cream (advanced by Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee, supported by Roland Kepp's 1926 analysis)**

A committee of farmers sampled Hessel Bottom herds in 1908 and concluded that the boggy meadow's coarse hay was causing the low butterfat. Roland Kepp, a veterinary surgeon, wrote in 1926 that wet-meadow herds produce milk 0.2 points lower in fat. Nyle Grigg reprinted his 1908 report in 1919 claiming State Agricultural College Bulletin 214 supported this (citing "two tenths" depression).

**Evidence that defeats this theory:**
- Bulletin 214 itself (1911) states: "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Five hundredths (0.05) in winter and zero in summer, not two tenths (0.20) year-round.
- The 0.19-point gap at Ostrey Hollow was flat month to month, July and February alike—the opposite of seasonal variation.
- Alder Corners Cheese Company (Rosalie Cudd's data): the same four Hessel Bottom farmers hauled to the cheese factory over Redlow Ridge for summer 1915–1930 (16 seasons). Their milk tested approximately 3.80 at Alder Corners—exactly the true average at Ostrey Hollow. Their milk was from the same herds and the same wet ground, but tested higher at Alder Corners than at home, because Alder Corners used different (correct) glassware.
- The 1911 interval again: if wet hay was the cause, tests should have remained low through May-July 1911 (the height of summer, when Kepp's theory predicts no depression). Instead, tests rose to 3.80 the moment the borrowed measure replaced the station's own.

### Two subsidiary questions

**Did the 1925 board of arbitration ever examine the condemned glassware?**

No. Bertram Kyle states: "What passed across my desk to that board was four things, and I can name them without looking, because I made the list and checked them off twice. The station's own test book, in Keddie's hand. The receiving book. Mr. Emmet Brant's private weigh book, which he lent for the purpose and wanted back. And the state inspector's notebook for the year he condemned the glass. Four books. I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked."

Effie Loomis notes: "the crate...never mentioned in 1925 hearing, not one word, not in transcript; crate of measures state's inspector condemned June 1923, set aside, new ones ordered; not spoken by patrons, officers, or board; award's phrase 'books and appliances' is form of words, not list."

The board examined books and heard testimony but never examined the actual condemned glass. The crate sat in the loft over their heads the entire time they met at Ostrey Hollow station.

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?**

Ansel Keddie did not believe anything with certainty. He came home one evening in the summer of 1911 and said to his son Duncan: "it is something in the glass." He said it twice—at supper and again by the door—which was unusual for him. Duncan recalls: "I was seventeen and I remember the kitchen and I remember that he said it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not his way."

He placed a question mark in his test book on May 8 and July 10, 1911, marking the measure break. In his unsent letter to the board (spring 1925), he did not argue any theory. He documented his procedure, his integrity, his confusion: "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are."

In his final statement on the last page of his unsent letter: "I have tested as I was taught. I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are."

## Section G — Summary (at most 150 words)

The Larrow Valley Creamery Association, founded 1889, established payment by butterfat test with no appeal (Article VII). In 1897, Ostrey Hollow skimming station received defective milk-test pipettes (short by 5%) that made all tests read low by exactly 0.19 points for 26 years. Patrons complained (1902+); three theories blamed them: water (1919), theft (1925), or wet hay (1908). All three were false. A 1911 equipment swap proved apparatus was faulty, but no one drew the inference. In 1925, a board awarded $3,800 "without admission" and dismissed tester Ansel Keddie. He died in 1934 under stigma. In 1958, Ivy Keddie found the condemned crate, measured the pipettes, and published Bulletin 471 officially exonerating Ansel. Eight separate narratives were needed because the truth lay hidden in a loft for 35 years, scattered across books no one examined together, and required a college education and a calibrated burette to see.
