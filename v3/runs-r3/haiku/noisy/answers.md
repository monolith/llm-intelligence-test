# Answers to Test Questions

## Section A — Reconstruction (8 Stories)

### Story 1: The Association's Founding and Founding Rules (1889–1892)

In 1889, farmers of the Larrow Valley, frustrated with inconsistent payment for butter sold through middlemen, organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association at Larrow Green. Gideon Vose, a farmer, was elected first president and took the unusual step of drafting the bylaws himself—not by lawyer but by farmer's hand—codifying the cooperative's core principle into Article VII. This article, adopted at the annual meeting in 1892 (the year after the main creamery opened on Larrow Creek), established that every patron would be paid not according to the weight of milk delivered but exclusively according to the pounds of butterfat determined by test—and critically, that the test made at the station where he delivered would be the test that governed his pay absolutely, with no appeal. This frameless rule made the reliability of testing instruments not a matter of convenience but a matter of survival for farmers in the valley. By the time this history ends, no other single provision of the Association would matter more. Gideon Vose drafted the clause without understanding what it would cost when the instruments proved faulty; he died in 1917, before the full consequence became clear. The bylaw stands unchanged: the station's test governs. In a system where tests had been wrong for twenty-six years, this meant farmers had no defense except proof that the test itself was defective—a thing nobody thought to examine for decades.

**Narrators**: Effie Loomis (r02), Dell Rundle (r12), Orin Falke (r22), Bertram Kyle (r23); **Documents**: Article VII text appears in notes and multiple retellings; **Key point**: The rule created absolute dependence on instrument accuracy with no independent verification mechanism.

### Story 2: Opening of Ostrey Hollow Station and Installation of the First Glassware (1896–1898)

In the winter of 1895–1896, the Larrow Valley Association built a small skimming station seven miles up the valley at Ostrey Hollow to serve the six farms of Hessel Bottom and surrounding hamlets. The station opened on May 4, 1896, under Ansel Keddie, a man of fifty-five who had been teaching himself testing practice for several months and who would remain at the post for twenty-nine seasons. For the first season (May 1896 through the end of 1896), the station borrowed its complete testing outfit—pipettes, bottles, acid measure, hand tester—from the main creamery at Larrow Green, as the Association's own equipment had not yet arrived. Ansel kept a meticulous test book from the opening day forward, five columns: date, patron, pounds, test, fat, all in his own hand, errors crossed out and initialed, never a figure written over. On May 1, 1897, the station's own glassware arrived from Ammon & Sons dairy supply at Tarnet: six dozen milk-test pipettes blown to a mark of 17.6 cubic centimeters, twelve dozen test bottles, one acid measure, one four-bottle hand tester, all for a total invoice of $77.93 including freight. Ansel notes on the first leaf of his station test book: "Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes." On May 1, 1897, those words would be superseded. Ansel would test every farmer's milk against this glassware for the next twenty-six years and two weeks, until June 12, 1923, when the state dairy inspector condemned the entire crate without explaining what it had been doing wrong the whole time.

**Narrators**: Ivy Keddie (r04), Halbert Nym (r07), Junia Ammon (r20), Bertram Kyle (r23); **Documents**: Ammon & Sons invoice April 14, 1897 ($77.93); station test book first leaf; **Key quantities**: 6 doz pipettes @ $0.35 = $25.20; 26 years of use until June 1923.

### Story 3: The Low-Test Pattern Emerges and Early Investigations (1902–1911)

From nearly the day the station's own glass came into use, the milk of Ostrey Hollow tested lower than the milk from any other station in the valley. In June 1897, the average was 3.80; from 1898 onward, the average hovered consistently at 3.61, while the main creamery at Larrow Green averaged 3.78. The difference—0.17 to 0.19 points—ran flat every month, July and February alike, in wet years and dry ones. The Ostrey Hollow patrons began complaining in 1902, and complaints recurred at nearly every annual meeting. In 1908, a committee chaired by Nyle Grigg was appointed to investigate. The committee sampled the Hessel Bottom herds throughout January, February, and March; found the milk whole, the scales honest, the herds sound; and concluded that the wet hay and coarse grasses of the Hessel Bottom meadow—which stood under water every spring and was fed as the primary winter feed—must be thinning the cream. The report was accepted and filed. Eleven years later, in 1919, Grigg reprinted it with a covering letter, noting that the State Agricultural College's Bulletin No. 214 (on bog hay feeding) had since found that such hay depressed butterfat by "two tenths" in winter, which he took as vindication of his theory (though the bulletin actually stated "not more than five hundredths" in winter, or about 0.05 points). The matter seemed settled until May 8, 1911, when the station's own pipette broke. A borrowed measure was used until July 10. During those nine weeks, the station's average test rose to 3.80—exactly the level Ansel had recorded for 1896, the first year on borrowed glass. The day the station's own measure returned, tests fell back to 3.61. Ansel noted both events in his book and placed a question mark—the only uncertainty he ever recorded in twenty-nine seasons. Nobody in the valley drew an inference.

**Narrators**: Vira Toland (r01), Ivy Keddie (r04), Nyle Grigg (r15), Roland Kepp (r16), Orin Falke (r22), Orra Keddie (r24); **Documents**: Grigg's 1908 committee report, Grigg's 1919 covering letter, State Agr. College Bulletin 214 (1911), Ansel's notations for May 8 and July 10, 1911; **Key finding**: The 1911 anomaly (borrowed measure produced higher tests despite identical herds, season, and operator) suggests the defect was in the apparatus, not the cows or land—but this inference went unstated until 1958.

### Story 4: The 1919 Water-Warning Circular and the Rupture of the Brant-Keddie Household (1919)

In June 1919, Selby Vose, manager of the Association since 1913, composed and circulated a letter to the nine families at Ostrey Hollow warning them in civil but unmistakable terms that the Association had begun investigating water being added to milk, that milk with added water would be detected and dealt with, and that all patrons should ensure the purity of their supplies. The letter was signed by Vose and carried no accusation against any individual; it was a general warning. Ansel Keddie received it at the station and, rather than post it on a bulletin board or send it by mail, took it himself to each of the nine families, standing at their doors as each man read it. He came home at eleven at night with his hat still on. Two days later, Emmet Brant—Ansel's brother-in-law, the largest patron of the station, and Orra's brother—rode up to the station and confronted Ansel in the yard. Emmet said: "You have hauled whole milk to this station since the day it opened and you know it better than any man living, and then you ask him why he carried the paper himself if he did not believe it." Ansel replied: "I carried it because I would not have another man carry it." Emmet went home. According to Orra's diary, Emmet did not enter the house again for ten years, until Ansel had died and propriety allowed reconciliation after Emmet's own death in 1929. Orra notes: "My brother Emmet is dead. I went down. Roy met me at the gate and was kind." The circular, sent to prevent water fraud, succeeded only in making an enemy of the man who hauled the most milk into the station. Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, testified that he tested for water at every visit from 1908 onward and never found any.

**Narrators**: Effie Loomis (r02), Bertram Kyle (r23), Orra Keddie (r24); **Documents**: The circular itself; Orra's diary entries for June 1919; **Key consequence**: The circular caused a family rupture that silenced the largest patron and made it impossible for Ansel to be defended by the patron he was said to have wronged most.

### Story 5: The State Inspector Condemns the Glassware (June 12, 1923)

In 1921, the State began issuing calibrated burettes to its dairy inspection circuit as a tool for field verification of testing equipment. Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector for the Ordell circuit (a post he held for twenty-nine years, 1907–1936), carried his burette through his regular rounds but rarely used it—the process was slow, and most plants had never been checked that way. On June 12, 1923, after twenty-six years of visiting Ostrey Hollow station and finding scales true, no water, and honest work, Strawn ran the station's milk pipettes against his calibrated burette. He discovered that the pipettes would not deliver their marked capacity. Each pipette was blown to a mark of 17.6 cubic centimeters, but when tested, they delivered approximately 16.27 cubic centimeters—short by about 0.88 c.c., or roughly 5 percent. Strawn condemned the entire crate of glassware on the spot and told the manager to order new. He made two crucial notes in his circuit book: the date, the finding (measures will not deliver their mark, condemn the lot), and the fact that he did not weigh what it had cost them. He made no inference about the twenty-six years of tests that had been made with this glass, and neither, apparently, did anyone else. The Association's manager wrote to Ammon & Sons on June 28, 1923, apologizing for the trouble and ordering replacement glass. Ammon & Sons replied on July 2, regretted the trouble, and stated that their records of the nineties were not such as to explain what had happened to the original lot. The new glass arrived before the summer flush ended, and by July 1923, the station had replacement pipettes in use. In the fall of 1924, the Ostrey Hollow average test suddenly rose from 3.61 to 3.80—a rise of 0.19 points. Nobody wrote down that this coincided with the installation of corrected glassware, and nine months later, a board of arbitration would sit to investigate how the tests had risen at all.

**Narrators**: Merle Strawn (r06), Halbert Nym (r07), Bertram Kyle (r23), Orin Falke (r22); **Documents**: Strawn's circuit notebook entry for June 12, 1923; Association letter to Ammon & Sons June 28, 1923; Ammon & Sons reply July 2, 1923; **Key finding**: The glass had been defective for 26 years 1 month (May 1, 1897 – June 12, 1923), but nobody drew the inference until after the original tester had been dismissed.

### Story 6: The 1925 Arbitration, the Award, and the Dismissal of Ansel Keddie (January–May 1925)

In January 1925, the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, frustrated that their milk continued to test below the valley average despite decades of complaint, petitioned the Association for redress. A board of arbitration was constituted under the Association's bylaws, with Alonzo Frick, an attorney of Ashlin, named chairman. The board sat three times: first at Ashlin on March 17, then twice at the Ostrey Hollow station itself (at Frick's direction, so the board could see the scales and watch a test being made). The board heard three competing explanations, each urged by a man of standing. Selby Vose (manager since 1913) argued that water was being added to the milk at the head of the valley and that it had stopped being added once the Association began investigating—evidenced by the 1924 rise in tests. Wilbur Teague (bookkeeper since 1905) made an accountant's argument: a business arranged so that one man weighed, sampled, tested, wrote, and separated—with no second man and no lock—would inevitably lose a little, and a man who could not account for a figure was presumed to be its author. Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee report (read into the record) blamed the wet hay of Hessel Bottom. Ansel Keddie, standing before the board on March 17, stated that he had tested as he was taught, that the state man had watched him 31 times per week for 29 seasons, that he had never taken a pound of cream, and that he could not tell them why the figures were as they were. He did not explain, did not defend—he only stated facts and his own uncertainty. The board deliberated for weeks, examined scales and books, watched tests performed. On May 4, 1925, it announced its finding: "no cause established" against any person, officer, or apparatus; and an award of $3,800 to the Ostrey Hollow patrons "without admission"—lawyer's language for paying money without explaining why. But on April 30, 1925—four days before the award, before the board had concluded—the Association dismissed Ansel Keddie from the post he had held for twenty-nine seasons. He came home at his ordinary hour, hung his apron behind the shed, and asked what there was for supper. Orra had ham ready. He ate it. He lived nine more years under the stigma of dismissal by an Association that had found no cause against him, and died in 1934 without the valley ever speaking the other thing—that he might not have done it.

**Narrators**: Effie Loomis (r02), Ansel Keddie (r03), Wilbur Teague (r05), Alonzo Frick (r13), Cleve Oram (r21), Orin Falke (r22), Bertram Kyle (r23), Orra Keddie (r24); **Documents**: Arbitration board minutes (partial); Ansel's statement March 17, 1925; Ansel's unsent letter (spring 1925); board award May 4, 1925; dismissal notice April 30, 1925; **Key contradiction**: Board found "no cause" yet dismissed Ansel days before announcing this finding.

### Story 7: The Discovery and Measurement of the Condemned Glass (February–March 1958)

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie, a graduate student in dairy husbandry at the State Agricultural College at Farland and the granddaughter of Ansel Keddie (through her father Duncan, Ansel's son), went to the Ostrey Hollow station building—by then used only for storage of hay equipment—and climbed into the loft above the intake room, where she found a wooden crate stenciled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET" under a tarpaulin and about an inch of chaff. The crate had lain there since June 12, 1923, the day it was condemned, thirty-five years, undisturbed. Inside were milk-test pipettes, packed in straw. Of the original six dozen, nine survived unbroken and legible. On March 9, 1958, Ivy Keddie measured these nine pipettes against a calibrated burette in a college laboratory at Farland, running five deliveries for each pipette. The results: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 cubic centimeters. Mean: 16.72 c.c. Short: 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for. Every test made at Ostrey Hollow station with this glass had read low by exactly 5 percent for 26 years and 1 month. A short charge of milk into a bottle whose neck is ruled for a full one yields a reading short in the same proportion, every single time. The arithmetic flows from physics: if 16.72 c.c. delivers where 17.6 was expected, then the fat column stands at 0.9524 of its true height. The true average at Ostrey Hollow over 1898–1922 was 3.61 ÷ 0.9524 = 3.80. The gap was 0.19 points. Over 40 million pounds of milk received at that station over 25 full years, at an average recorded test of 3.61 percent, patrons were credited with 1,444,000 pounds of butterfat. At the true test of 3.80 percent, they should have been credited with 1,520,000. The difference was 76,000 pounds of butterfat. At an average price of 25 cents per pound (the price throughout the period), this was worth $19,000. The patrons received $3,800.

**Narrators**: Ivy Keddie (r04), Halbert Nym (r07), Duncan Keddie (r18), Hazel Brant (r17), Cleve Oram (r21), Orin Falke (r22); **Documents**: Measurement sheet March 9, 1958 (nine pipettes, mean 16.72 c.c.); Ammon & Sons invoice April 14, 1897 (showing original 17.6 c.c. mark); **Key quantities**: 9 of original 72 pipettes surviving; measured mean 16.72 vs. marked 17.6; ratio 0.9524 = 5% deficiency; applied to 40M lb milk over 25 years = 76,000 lb butterfat = $19,000 lost.

### Story 8: Bulletin 471 and the Official Exoneration (October 1958 onward)

In October 1958, the State Agricultural College published Bulletin No. 471, "Butterfat Tests and Testing Equipment," prepared jointly by Professor Leland Vaught of the department of dairy husbandry and Miss Ivy Keddie, graduate student. The bulletin's closing paragraph, written by Vaught, states: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference—seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat—stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain." The bulletin showed that the 1911 anomaly (when a borrowed measure produced higher tests for nine weeks) was the only experimental proof needed: the variable was the measuring instrument; everything else—herds, pasture, season, men, operator—was held constant; the tests rose with borrowed glass and fell when the original was restored. The bulletin demolished all three competing theories: Vose's water theory (contradicted by Strawn's repeated negative tests and by Sill's observation that cream always ran heavy); the theft theory (contradicted by the 1911 anomaly and by the station-gain arithmetic); and Grigg's wet-hay theory (contradicted by the total magnitude and the month-to-month flatness). Within days, the Ashlin Chronicle published "Old Ordell Wrong Righted," a front-page feature noting that Ansel Keddie had been dead twenty-four years and his widow fourteen. Orin Falke, county agricultural agent, filed a formal report for the county board of supervisors, initially conceding the wet-hay theory but then, upon examining Bulletin 214 (the college's 1911 feeding trial), withdrawing that concession: bog hay caused "not more than five hundredths" depression, nowhere near the "nineteen hundredths" gap that stood flat every month. The record was set straight. Ansel's name was cleared. Nobody in the valley had needed to clear it; they had only needed to climb a ladder and examine a crate.

**Narrators**: Ivy Keddie (r04), Leland Vaught (r11), Dell Rundle (r12), Ashlin Chronicle (r19), Orin Falke (r22); **Documents**: Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958); Bulletin No. 214 (1911, cited); Ashlin Chronicle front page (October 3, 1958); Orin Falke's report and correction (1959); **Key finding**: All three theories defeated; glass defect fully accounts for the gap; physical evidence (condemned pipettes) corroborates arithmetic; exoneration is official and complete.

## Section B — Relationships

### B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie

Ivy Keddie is Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Ansel's son Duncan (born 1894, taught school at Ashlin) married and had a daughter Ivy (born 1931). This is a direct lineal descent through one generation. Duncan's notes confirm: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise." Multiple sources (Nym, Kyle, Falke, Oram) incorrectly call her Ansel's great-niece or describe the relationship wrongly, but Duncan and Ivy both state it clearly: granddaughter. This relationship matters because it gave Ivy both motivation (family honor) and standing (family archives, family memory) to investigate, and because her success vindicated her grandfather thirty-three years after his dismissal and twenty-four years after his death.

### B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant, and intervening generations

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant are second cousins. Hazel's father Roy Brant (born ~1898, died 1962) was Ivy's father Duncan's first cousin—the children of a brother (Emmet Brant) and sister (Orra Keddie). Therefore, Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are second cousins (children of first cousins). Duncan explicitly sets this out: "Roy Brant and I are therefore first cousins...Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed." The sources leave open whether Emmet Brant had other children besides Roy, and whether Roy was Emmet's only child. Roy's full lineage back is clear (son of Emmet, born 1854, died 1929), but Emmet's full family structure is not fully documented in the sources.

### B3. Selby Vose's father, and why this matters

Selby Vose was the son of Gideon Vose (first president 1889–1911, died 1917), not Gideon's nephew as sometimes stated in the valley. Bertram Kyle specifically corrects this: "Selby Vose, who managed the Association, was Gideon Vose's son...not, as I have heard it given, his nephew." This matters because Selby inherited not just the managing position but also his father's intimate knowledge of Article VII and the system's design. Gideon had drafted Article VII to depend absolutely on the station test. Selby, managing from 1913 onward, signed the annual ledger sheets every year that carried the "station gain" line—five and a quarter percent, year after year. Either Selby understood the line and said nothing, or he did not read it, or he misunderstood it. His 1926 address blamed water for the 1924 test rise, not glass replacement. His knowledge (or ignorance) of what he was signing shaped how the crisis unfolded.

### B4. Relationship, if any, between Vose and Keddie families

Duncan Keddie denies any family connection between the Voses and the Keddies. He states: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses...I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins...It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me. She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it." However, Cleve Oram (r21) states: "The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley. My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper." The sources conflict. Duncan's denial is direct and emphatic; Oram's affirmation is secondhand, vague ("of some sort"), and explicitly hedged ("I could not draw you the line"). Duncan's authority is greater (he is family and heard from his mother directly), and his position is that the rumor is false valley talk. I resolve in favor of no blood relationship, grounded on Duncan's explicit family knowledge and his mother's absence of any such connection in her recorded genealogy.

## Section C — Mathematics (with working shown)

### C1. Proportion, true average, pounds lost, value and recovery fraction

**(a) Proportion of deficiency from glassware:**

Nine surviving pipettes from the original crate were measured against a calibrated burette on March 9, 1958 (Ivy Keddie measurement sheet). Mean delivery: 16.72 c.c. Marked capacity: 17.6 c.c.

Proportion delivered: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9524 (or 95.24 percent)

Deficiency: 1 − 0.9524 = 0.0476 (or 4.76 percent, approximately one part in twenty, or 1/20 = 0.05)

**Answer: The station's tests were low by exactly 5 percent (one part in twenty) because each pipette delivered 95.24% of its marked capacity.**

**(b) True average test and shortfall:**

Recorded average test at Ostrey Hollow, 1898–1922: 3.61 percent (from Ansel's summary leaf, Ivy's recalculation, Teague's books, and Loomis's records, all consistent)

True average = Recorded ÷ 0.9524 = 3.61 ÷ 0.9524 = 3.79 percent

Or using the simpler ratio from the bulletin (1/20): True = 3.61 × (20/19) = 3.61 × 1.0526 = 3.80 percent

**Answer: True average test was 3.80 percent. Recorded tests were short by 3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19 points.**

**(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited:**

40 million pounds of milk received at station over 25 full delivery years (1898–1922)

Loss per pound of milk: 0.0019 (the 0.19-point gap in butterfat percentage)

Total loss: 40,000,000 × 0.0019 = 76,000 pounds of butterfat

**Answer: Patrons were not credited with 76,000 pounds of butterfat.**

**(d) Money value and fraction recovered:**

Average price of butterfat over period: 25 cents per pound (from Loomis, Teague, and other sources; Hobart Sill confirms "twenty-seven cents" as round figure for the period)

Total value of lost fat: 76,000 lb × $0.25/lb = $19,000

Award by 1925 board (without admission): $3,800

Fraction recovered: $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20 = **one-fifth (20 percent)**

**Answer: The 76,000 lb loss was worth $19,000. The patrons received $3,800, which is exactly one-fifth of what they were owed.**

### C2. Largest patron in 1910 (Emmet Brant, via weigh book)

Emmet Brant's weigh book entry for 1910 (shown in writing: "Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42.")

**(a) Butterfat credited:**

200,000 lb × 3.42% = 200,000 × 0.0342 = 6,840 pounds of butterfat

**(b) Butterfat owed but not credited:**

True test for same milk: 3.42 ÷ 0.9524 = 3.589 ≈ 3.59 percent

Shortfall: 3.59 − 3.42 = 0.17 percentage points

Pounds owed not credited: 200,000 × 0.0017 = 340 pounds of butterfat

**(c) Money value that year:**

340 lb × $0.25/lb = $85

**Answer: Emmet was credited with 6,840 lb fat, owed 340 lb more (worth $85) that year.**

### C3. Total butterfat across 25 full delivery years, expressed as percentage

**(a) Total credited to patrons on station test (recorded):**

40,000,000 lb milk × 3.61% = 1,444,000 pounds of butterfat

**(b) Total actually delivered at Larrow Green in station cream (true):**

40,000,000 lb milk × 3.80% = 1,520,000 pounds of butterfat

**(c) Difference expressed as percentage:**

Difference: 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 lb

As percentage of delivered fat: 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = 0.050 = **5.0%** of the true amount delivered

As percentage of credited fat: 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 0.0526 = **5.26%** of the credited amount

Note: Both are correct and represent the same loss viewed from different denominators. The glass defect removed 5% of what was actually there (the true amount), which equals 5.26% when measured against what was incorrectly recorded.

**Answer: 1,444,000 lb credited, 1,520,000 lb delivered; difference is 76,000 lb, which is 5.0% of delivered fat or 5.26% of credited fat.**

### C5. Duration of faulty glassware and first season intake

**(a) Duration of faulty glass:**

Installed: May 1, 1897 (confirmed by Orra diary "A. says the station's own glass came up...May 1, 1897")

Condemned: June 12, 1923 (Merle Strawn circuit notebook entry, repeated in all sources)

**Duration in use: 26 years and 1 month** (May 1, 1897 – June 12, 1923)

**Money calculation runs over: 25 full delivery years (1898–1922)**

Why different: The station's first season (May 4, 1896 – end of 1896, using borrowed glass) is partial and not included in the account books' station column. The full-year accounting begins 1897, but 1897 itself is partial (glass arrived May 1, so testing with new glass is from May 1, 1897 onward). The last full year of faulty glass is 1922 (before June 12, 1923 condemnation). Therefore, financial harm is calculated over 1898–1922, twenty-five consecutive full years, even though the glass was in use from mid-1897 through mid-1923 (26+ years). The discrepancy arises because the first partial season is not in the accounts, and the final partial season is excluded from the ledger calculation.

**(b) First season milk intake (1896):**

The station opened May 4, 1896. Milk received May 4, 1896 through December 31, 1896 (using borrowed glass).

**Sources give estimates, not exact figures:**
- Hobart Sill: "about 9 hundred thousand pounds" (900,000 lb)
- Duncan Keddie: "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" (1,100,000 lb)
- Bertram Kyle: (no figure given)

None of these figures appears in any surviving ledger. Duncan notes: "It is not in any book I ever ruled."

**Answer: First season intake estimated 900,000–1,100,000 lb, not precisely recorded in extant accounts.**

## Section D — Logic

### D1. The May–July 1911 measure-break interval as an experiment

**When and duration:** The station's own measuring pipette broke on May 8, 1911. A borrowed measure (presumably from Larrow Green creamery) was substituted and used until July 10, 1911, when the station's own measure was repaired or replaced. Duration: approximately nine weeks (May 8 – July 10).

**The variable:** The only thing that changed was the measuring apparatus (pipette). Everything else remained identical.

**What was held constant:** 
- The same patrons delivered milk (Hessel Bottom and surrounding farms)
- The same herds of cows (no breeding changes in nine weeks)
- The same pasture and forage (early-to-mid summer on meadows and aftermath)
- The same operator (Ansel Keddie) performing the tests
- The same acid, bottles, and hand tester (only the pipette changed)
- The same intake and output procedures

**Result:** While the borrowed measure was in use (May 8 – July 10, 1911), the station's average test was 3.80 percent. When the station's own measure was restored on July 10, 1911, the test immediately reverted to 3.61 percent—the same day. Ansel entered both events in his test book and placed a question mark.

**What this establishes:** This interval is a natural experiment demonstrating that the apparatus itself, not the herds or land, is the source of the discrepancy. If the low tests were caused by the wet hay of Hessel Bottom (as the 1908 committee claimed), the tests should have remained low during the borrowed-measure interval, because the hay and cows did not change. But they rose. If the tests were caused by seasonal variation, the tests should have remained stable across the interval, or varied gradually with season. But they stayed at 3.80 exactly while borrowed, fell to 3.61 exactly when returned. This proves the apparatus is the culprit.

**What this does NOT establish:** The interval does not tell us what is wrong with the station's own measure (whether it is too short, worn, unevenly marked, etc.) or by how much. It does not tell us that the station's own measure has been wrong for 14 years already (since 1897), though it allows that inference. It does not prove the entire deficit is due to the apparatus alone without corroborating measurements of the original glass (which came in 1958).

**Conclusion:** The 1911 interval is the single most powerful piece of evidence against all competing theories and in favor of apparatus defect, yet it went unstated by all three narrators in 1925 who could have made the inference—Ansel, Frick, and Strawn.

### D2. Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream

**Reason 1 — The 1911 measure-break experiment (independent of 1958 measurement):** 

Ansel tested during the nine-week borrowed-measure interval (May 8 – July 10, 1911) and produced tests of 3.80 percent, then tested with the station's own measure and produced 3.61 percent the same day (July 10). If Ansel had been secretly removing cream and writing down low tests to conceal it, his figures would have followed his actions and remained consistently low regardless of which pipette he used. But the figures correlate with the apparatus, not with his behavior. This reason depends only on the recorded dates and figures in his test book, not on the 1958 measurement, and would still hold if the original glass had never been examined.

**Reason 2 — Hobart Sill's receiving-book observation (independent of 1958):**

Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green, received the cream from Ostrey Hollow every day and weighed it. He states repeatedly that the cream "ran heavy"—that is, more fat was in the cans than the station's papers said had been purchased up there. Sill weighed the cream himself upon arrival and compared it to the station's weight and test figures. Over 25 years, there was always more fat present than recorded. If Ansel had been stealing cream and writing low tests to cover the loss, the opposite would be true: less cream would arrive than recorded. Sill's independent physical measurements contradict the theft accusation fundamentally.

**Reason 3 — The station-gain ledger line (independent of 1958):**

Wilbur Teague, the Association's bookkeeper for 40 years, entered a line in the annual ledger every year: "Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, XX,XXX lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, YY,YYY lb. Station gain, ZZ,ZZZ lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual." The second number is always larger than the first. If Ansel had stolen cream, the cream that arrived at Larrow Green would have been less than the recorded figure, not more. Instead, a consistent "gain" was entered—exactly what would be expected if the testing instrument was delivering short readings of a full amount of cream. Teague signed off on this line year after year and was the only Association officer who might have understood it, yet he asked no questions.

**Interdependence check:** These three reasons do not depend on one another. Reason 1 (1911 anomaly) depends only on recorded dates and figures. Reason 2 (Sill's receiving book) depends only on physical measurement at Larrow Green. Reason 3 (ledger gain line) depends only on bookkeeping records. None requires the others to be valid.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question his own entries?** Teague states in his 1948 memoir: "I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone. I let it alone. I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience." So yes, Teague questioned the entries once (in 1916, about the station-gain line), was rebuffed, and asked no more. He lived 40 years seeing the same line entered and signed by the manager without ever challenging it again.

### D3. Did the manager know the tests ran low before the glass was condemned?

**Answer: Yes, the manager (Selby Vose) knew before June 12, 1923, and is likely to have understood that the glass might be faulty.**

**Justification:**

Selby Vose became manager in 1913 and signed the annual ledger sheet every year from 1913 onward. That sheet carried the "station gain" line for Ostrey Hollow year after year: in 1913, 1914, 1915, etc., consistently showing five and a quarter percent more fat arriving at Larrow Green than had been credited to patrons at Ostrey Hollow. Bertram Kyle, who handled the sheets and brought them to Vose, states: "He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign." Vose signed without reading the intermediate lines. But he had the document in front of him. The information was available.

Additionally, in 1919, Vose sent the water-warning circular to the patrons, suggesting he had some concern about the source of the discrepancy. By 1923, he was aware enough of the problem to write to Ammon & Sons for replacement glass immediately after Strawn condemned the original. Within months (October 1923), new glass was in use and tests began rising.

Crucially, in Vose's own 1926 address to the membership, he says: "The tests came up when the looking began"—suggesting he attributed the 1924 test rise to water being stopped, not to glass replacement. This statement is either a deliberate mischaracterization or a genuine confusion. Given that he knew the glass had been condemned and replaced in 1923, and that tests rose in 1924, he could have drawn the logical inference if he chose to. That he blamed "water stopped" instead suggests either he did not make the inference, or he chose not to.

**Conclusion: Vose knew the persistent low-test issue existed by 1913 (when he saw it annually on the ledgers). He sent the water-warning circular in 1919 (suggesting investigation). He ordered replacement glass in 1923 immediately after condemnation. Whether he understood the glass itself was faulty is less certain, but he had sufficient notice of the problem to raise the question before the 1925 arbitration if he had chosen to.**

## Section F — Theory

### F1. Causal theory: The glass defect

**What actually produced the shortfall at Ostrey Hollow:**

The Larrow Valley Creamery Association's Ostrey Hollow skimming station received a defective set of milk-test pipettes from Ammon & Sons dairy supply in April 1897, invoiced April 14 for $77.93. The pipettes—six dozen of them, marked as capable of delivering 17.6 cubic centimeters of milk—were manufactured with a mold that had shifted or worn. Each pipette delivered only 16.72 cubic centimeters, or 95.24% of its marked capacity. This deficiency was identical across all surviving specimens, indicating a systematic manufacturing error, not random variation or wear.

From May 1, 1897, onward, every test performed at Ostrey Hollow station was made with these pipettes. A milk sample of true butterfat content T percent appeared to test at T × 0.9524 percent. Thus, milk that actually contained 3.80 percent butterfat tested as 3.61 percent. This relationship held for 26 years and 1 month, until June 12, 1923, when state dairy inspector Merle Strawn conducted the first-ever burette check of the station's glassware and condemned the entire crate.

**Why this particular size of error:**

The deficiency was proportional: 5% of the marked charge meant 5% of the resulting fat measurement. Rich milk (higher butterfat) yielded larger absolute losses; poor milk yielded smaller ones. Over 40 million pounds of milk averaged at 3.80 percent true butterfat, the 5% deficiency meant 76,000 pounds of fat were never credited to the patrons. This magnitude (76,000 lb or $19,000 at 25 cents per pound) is explained entirely by the manufacturing defect: a mold that produced pipettes short by 0.88 c.c.

**Why it began when it did:**

The station opened May 4, 1896, using borrowed glass from Larrow Green creamery. Tests for the partial 1896 season (May–December) average 3.80 percent, indicating the borrowed glass was accurate. When the station's own glass arrived and was installed on May 1, 1897, the tests immediately dropped to 3.61 percent. The invoice for that glass (Ammon & Sons, April 14, 1897, $77.93) specifies six dozen pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. These specific pipettes, now surviving in a condemned crate from 1923, measure 16.72 c.c. The deficiency began the moment defective glass was placed in service.

**Why it ended when it did:**

On June 12, 1923, Merle Strawn ran the station's pipettes against a state-provided calibrated burette for the first time and discovered they would not deliver their mark. He condemned the entire crate. The Association ordered replacement glass the same week (letter to Ammon & Sons, June 28, 1923). New glass was in use by July 1923. Over the remainder of 1923 and into 1924, the Ostrey Hollow average test rose to 3.80 percent and remained there. The deficiency ceased when the defective glass was removed from service.

**Why nothing at the station or farms had to change:**

The deficiency was a measurement error, not a production problem. The cows were the same; the pasture and forage were the same; the farming practices were unchanged; the patrons' behavior was the same. Only the testing apparatus changed. A measurement error of this type affects the numbers recorded, not the reality of what was produced. The milk left the cows at the same 3.80 percent butterfat whether the glass measured it correctly or not. The association's payment by butterfat-percentage-as-measured created a system in which the truth about the milk never mattered—only the numbers mattered.

### The three competing theories and their refutation

**Theory 1: Water was being added to the milk (advanced by Selby Vose, manager, in 1919 circular and 1926 address)**

Vose argued that patrons of Ostrey Hollow were adding water to thin their milk, reducing butterfat content. He sent a water-warning circular in 1919. He claimed that when the Association began investigating (1925), water was stopped, causing tests to rise in 1924. 

**Evidence that defeats this theory:**
- Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector, tested for water at every visit from 1908 to 1936 (29 years) and never found any. He explicitly states: "lactometer, no added water, as always here" in his June 12, 1923 entry.
- If water was being added, milk would arrive at Larrow Green less dense than recorded. But Hobart Sill's receiving book shows cream from Ostrey Hollow always ran "heavy"—more fat in cans than papers said. The opposite of what theft or watering would produce.
- The 1911 nine-week interval: the same patrons, same cows, same season, same operator tested with a borrowed pipette and produced 3.80 percent. If water was being added, the test would remain low regardless of apparatus. But it rose with the borrowed glass.

**Theory 2: The tester (Ansel Keddie) was removing cream and writing low tests to cover it (implied by Wilbur Teague's 1925 board testimony)**

Teague argued that a business with one man in charge of weighing, sampling, testing, writing, and separating could not prevent theft. He made no direct accusation but structured his testimony to suggest this explanation.

**Evidence that defeats this theory:**
- The 1911 anomaly: Ansel tested during the nine-week borrowed-measure interval and produced 3.80 percent, then tested with his own measure and produced 3.61 percent the same day. If he were stealing, his actions—not the apparatus—would drive the figures. But the figures correlate with the glass, not with his behavior.
- Hobart Sill's observation: cream from Ostrey Hollow arrived at Larrow Green "heavy," not light. If Ansel had stolen cream, less would arrive than recorded. The opposite occurred.
- The station-gain ledger: every year, more fat arrived than was credited. This is exactly what would be expected if too little fat was credited (due to low test readings), not what would be expected if some fat went missing.

**Theory 3: The wet hay of Hessel Bottom Bottom was thinning the cream (advanced by Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee, supported by Roland Kepp's 1926 analysis)**

A committee of farmers sampled Hessel Bottom herds in 1908 and concluded that the boggy meadow's coarse hay was causing the low butterfat. Roland Kepp, a veterinary surgeon, wrote in 1926 that wet-meadow herds produce milk 0.2 points lower in fat. Nyle Grigg reprinted his 1908 report in 1919 claiming State Agricultural College Bulletin 214 supported this (citing "two tenths" depression).

**Evidence that defeats this theory:**
- Bulletin 214 itself (1911) states: "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Five hundredths (0.05) in winter and zero in summer, not two tenths (0.20) year-round.
- The 0.19-point gap at Ostrey Hollow was flat month to month, July and February alike—the opposite of seasonal variation.
- Alder Corners Cheese Company (Rosalie Cudd's data): the same four Hessel Bottom farmers hauled to the cheese factory over Redlow Ridge for summer 1915–1930 (16 seasons). Their milk tested approximately 3.80 at Alder Corners—exactly the true average at Ostrey Hollow. Their milk was from the same herds and the same wet ground, but tested higher at Alder Corners than at home, because Alder Corners used different (correct) glassware.
- The 1911 interval again: if wet hay was the cause, tests should have remained low through May-July 1911 (the height of summer, when Kepp's theory predicts no depression). Instead, tests rose to 3.80 the moment the borrowed measure replaced the station's own.

### Two subsidiary questions

**Did the 1925 board of arbitration ever examine the condemned glassware?**

No. Bertram Kyle states: "What passed across my desk to that board was four things, and I can name them without looking, because I made the list and checked them off twice. The station's own test book, in Keddie's hand. The receiving book. Mr. Emmet Brant's private weigh book, which he lent for the purpose and wanted back. And the state inspector's notebook for the year he condemned the glass. Four books. I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked."

Effie Loomis notes: "the crate...never mentioned in 1925 hearing, not one word, not in transcript; crate of measures state's inspector condemned June 1923, set aside, new ones ordered; not spoken by patrons, officers, or board; award's phrase 'books and appliances' is form of words, not list."

The board examined books and heard testimony but never examined the actual condemned glass. The crate sat in the loft over their heads the entire time they met at Ostrey Hollow station.

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?**

Ansel Keddie did not believe anything with certainty. He came home one evening in the summer of 1911 and said to his son Duncan: "it is something in the glass." He said it twice—at supper and again by the door—which was unusual for him. Duncan recalls: "I was seventeen and I remember the kitchen and I remember that he said it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not his way."

He placed a question mark in his test book on May 8 and July 10, 1911, marking the measure break. In his unsent letter to the board (spring 1925), he did not argue any theory. He documented his procedure, his integrity, his confusion: "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are."

In his final statement on the last page of his unsent letter: "I have tested as I was taught. I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are."

Ansel suspected the glass might be faulty (based on the 1911 anomaly), but he would not assert what he could not prove. He carried the doubt with him until his death in 1934, under the stigma of dismissal, vindicat ed only 24 years after he died when someone finally climbed the ladder and measured the glass.

## Section G — Summary (at most 150 words)

The Larrow Valley Creamery Association, founded 1889, established payment by butterfat test with no appeal (Article VII). In 1897, Ostrey Hollow skimming station received defective milk-test pipettes (short by 5%) that made all tests read low by exactly 0.19 points for 26 years. Patrons complained (1902+); three theories blamed them: water (1919), theft (1925), or wet hay (1908). All three were false. A 1911 equipment swap proved apparatus was faulty, but no one drew the inference. In 1925, a board awarded $3,800 "without admission" and dismissed tester Ansel Keddie. He died in 1934 under stigma. In 1958, Ivy Keddie found the condemned crate, measured the pipettes, and published Bulletin 471 officially exonerating Ansel. Eight separate narratives were needed because the truth lay hidden in a loft for 35 years, scattered across books no one examined together, and required a college education and a calibrated burette to see.
