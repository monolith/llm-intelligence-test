## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### Story 5: The State Inspector Condemns the Glassware (June 12, 1923)

In 1921, the State began issuing calibrated burettes to its dairy inspection circuit as a tool for field verification of testing equipment. Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector for the Ordell circuit (a post he held for twenty-nine years, 1907–1936), carried his burette through his regular rounds but rarely used it—the process was slow, and most plants had never been checked that way. On June 12, 1923, after twenty-six years of visiting Ostrey Hollow station and finding scales true, no water, and honest work, Strawn ran the station's milk pipettes against his calibrated burette. He discovered that the pipettes would not deliver their marked capacity. Each pipette was blown to a mark of 17.6 cubic centimeters, but when tested, they delivered approximately 16.27 cubic centimeters—short by about 0.88 c.c., or roughly 5 percent. Strawn condemned the entire crate of glassware on the spot and told the manager to order new. He made two crucial notes in his circuit book: the date, the finding (measures will not deliver their mark, condemn the lot), and the fact that he did not weigh what it had cost them. He made no inference about the twenty-six years of tests that had been made with this glass, and neither, apparently, did anyone else. The Association's manager wrote to Ammon & Sons on June 28, 1923, apologizing for the trouble and ordering replacement glass. Ammon & Sons replied on July 2, regretted the trouble, and stated that their records of the nineties were not such as to explain what had happened to the original lot. The new glass arrived before the summer flush ended, and by July 1923, the station had replacement pipettes in use. In the fall of 1924, the Ostrey Hollow average test suddenly rose from 3.61 to 3.80—a rise of 0.19 points. Nobody wrote down that this coincided with the installation of corrected glassware, and nine months later, a board of arbitration would sit to investigate how the tests had risen at all.

**Narrators**: Merle Strawn (r06), Halbert Nym (r07), Bertram Kyle (r23), Orin Falke (r22); **Documents**: Strawn's circuit notebook entry for June 12, 1923; Association letter to Ammon & Sons June 28, 1923; Ammon & Sons reply July 2, 1923; **Key finding**: The glass had been defective for 26 years 1 month (May 1, 1897 – June 12, 1923), but nobody drew the inference until after the original tester had been dismissed.

### Story 6: The 1925 Arbitration, the Award, and the Dismissal of Ansel Keddie (January–May 1925)

In January 1925, the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, frustrated that their milk continued to test below the valley average despite decades of complaint, petitioned the Association for redress. A board of arbitration was constituted under the Association's bylaws, with Alonzo Frick, an attorney of Ashlin, named chairman. The board sat three times: first at Ashlin on March 17, then twice at the Ostrey Hollow station itself (at Frick's direction, so the board could see the scales and watch a test being made). The board heard three competing explanations, each urged by a man of standing. Selby Vose (manager since 1913) argued that water was being added to the milk at the head of the valley and that it had stopped being added once the Association began investigating—evidenced by the 1924 rise in tests. Wilbur Teague (bookkeeper since 1905) made an accountant's argument: a business arranged so that one man weighed, sampled, tested, wrote, and separated—with no second man and no lock—would inevitably lose a little, and a man who could not account for a figure was presumed to be its author. Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee report (read into the record) blamed the wet hay of Hessel Bottom. Ansel Keddie, standing before the board on March 17, stated that he had tested as he was taught, that the state man had watched him 31 times per week for 29 seasons, that he had never taken a pound of cream, and that he could not tell them why the figures were as they were. He did not explain, did not defend—he only stated facts and his own uncertainty. The board deliberated for weeks, examined scales and books, watched tests performed. On May 4, 1925, it announced its finding: "no cause established" against any person, officer, or apparatus; and an award of $3,800 to the Ostrey Hollow patrons "without admission"—lawyer's language for paying money without explaining why. But on April 30, 1925—four days before the award, before the board had concluded—the Association dismissed Ansel Keddie from the post he had held for twenty-nine seasons. He came home at his ordinary hour, hung his apron behind the shed, and asked what there was for supper. Orra had ham ready. He ate it. He lived nine more years under the stigma of dismissal by an Association that had found no cause against him, and died in 1934 without the valley ever speaking the other thing—that he might not have done it.

**Narrators**: Effie Loomis (r02), Ansel Keddie (r03), Wilbur Teague (r05), Alonzo Frick (r13), Cleve Oram (r21), Orin Falke (r22), Bertram Kyle (r23), Orra Keddie (r24); **Documents**: Arbitration board minutes (partial); Ansel's statement March 17, 1925; Ansel's unsent letter (spring 1925); board award May 4, 1925; dismissal notice April 30, 1925; **Key contradiction**: Board found "no cause" yet dismissed Ansel days before announcing this finding.

### Story 7: The Discovery and Measurement of the Condemned Glass (February–March 1958)

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie, a graduate student in dairy husbandry at the State Agricultural College at Farland and the granddaughter of Ansel Keddie (through her father Duncan, Ansel's son), went to the Ostrey Hollow station building—by then used only for storage of hay equipment—and climbed into the loft above the intake room, where she found a wooden crate stenciled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET" under a tarpaulin and about an inch of chaff. The crate had lain there since June 12, 1923, the day it was condemned, thirty-five years, undisturbed. Inside were milk-test pipettes, packed in straw. Of the original six dozen, nine survived unbroken and legible. On March 9, 1958, Ivy Keddie measured these nine pipettes against a calibrated burette in a college laboratory at Farland, running five deliveries for each pipette. The results: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 cubic centimeters. Mean: 16.72 c.c. Short: 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for. Every test made at Ostrey Hollow station with this glass had read low by exactly 5 percent for 26 years and 1 month. A short charge of milk into a bottle whose neck is ruled for a full one yields a reading short in the same proportion, every single time. The arithmetic flows from physics: if 16.72 c.c. delivers where 17.6 was expected, then the fat column stands at 0.9524 of its true height. The true average at Ostrey Hollow over 1898–1922 was 3.61 ÷ 0.9524 = 3.80. The gap was 0.19 points. Over 40 million pounds of milk received at that station over 25 full years, at an average recorded test of 3.61 percent, patrons were credited with 1,444,000 pounds of butterfat. At the true test of 3.80 percent, they should have been credited with 1,520,000. The difference was 76,000 pounds of butterfat. At an average price of 25 cents per pound (the price throughout the period), this was worth $19,000. The patrons received $3,800.

**Narrators**: Ivy Keddie (r04), Halbert Nym (r07), Duncan Keddie (r18), Hazel Brant (r17), Cleve Oram (r21), Orin Falke (r22); **Documents**: Measurement sheet March 9, 1958 (nine pipettes, mean 16.72 c.c.); Ammon & Sons invoice April 14, 1897 (showing original 17.6 c.c. mark); **Key quantities**: 9 of original 72 pipettes surviving; measured mean 16.72 vs. marked 17.6; ratio 0.9524 = 5% deficiency; applied to 40M lb milk over 25 years = 76,000 lb butterfat = $19,000 lost.

### Story 8: Bulletin 471 and the Official Exoneration (October 1958 onward)

In October 1958, the State Agricultural College published Bulletin No. 471, "Butterfat Tests and Testing Equipment," prepared jointly by Professor Leland Vaught of the department of dairy husbandry and Miss Ivy Keddie, graduate student. The bulletin's closing paragraph, written by Vaught, states: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference—seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat—stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain." The bulletin showed that the 1911 anomaly (when a borrowed measure produced higher tests for nine weeks) was the only experimental proof needed: the variable was the measuring instrument; everything else—herds, pasture, season, men, operator—was held constant; the tests rose with borrowed glass and fell when the original was restored. The bulletin demolished all three competing theories: Vose's water theory (contradicted by Strawn's repeated negative tests and by Sill's observation that cream always ran heavy); the theft theory (contradicted by the 1911 anomaly and by the station-gain arithmetic); and Grigg's wet-hay theory (contradicted by the total magnitude and the month-to-month flatness). Within days, the Ashlin Chronicle published "Old Ordell Wrong Righted," a front-page feature noting that Ansel Keddie had been dead twenty-four years and his widow fourteen. Orin Falke, county agricultural agent, filed a formal report for the county board of supervisors, initially conceding the wet-hay theory but then, upon examining Bulletin 214 (the college's 1911 feeding trial), withdrawing that concession: bog hay caused "not more than five hundredths" depression, nowhere near the "nineteen hundredths" gap that stood flat every month. The record was set straight. Ansel's name was cleared. Nobody in the valley had needed to clear it; they had only needed to climb a ladder and examine a crate.

**Narrators**: Ivy Keddie (r04), Leland Vaught (r11), Dell Rundle (r12), Ashlin Chronicle (r19), Orin Falke (r22); **Documents**: Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958); Bulletin No. 214 (1911, cited); Ashlin Chronicle front page (October 3, 1958); Orin Falke's report and correction (1959); **Key finding**: All three theories defeated; glass defect fully accounts for the gap; physical evidence (condemned pipettes) corroborates arithmetic; exoneration is official and complete.

## Section B — Relationships

### B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie

Ivy Keddie is Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Ansel's son Duncan (born 1894, taught school at Ashlin) married and had a daughter Ivy (born 1931). This is a direct lineal descent through one generation. Duncan's notes confirm: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise." Multiple sources (Nym, Kyle, Falke, Oram) incorrectly call her Ansel's great-niece or describe the relationship wrongly, but Duncan and Ivy both state it clearly: granddaughter. This relationship matters because it gave Ivy both motivation (family honor) and standing (family archives, family memory) to investigate, and because her success vindicated her grandfather thirty-three years after his dismissal and twenty-four years after his death.

### B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant, and intervening generations

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant are second cousins. Hazel's father Roy Brant (born ~1898, died 1962) was Ivy's father Duncan's first cousin—the children of a brother (Emmet Brant) and sister (Orra Keddie). Therefore, Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are second cousins (children of first cousins). Duncan explicitly sets this out: "Roy Brant and I are therefore first cousins...Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed." The sources leave open whether Emmet Brant had other children besides Roy, and whether Roy was Emmet's only child. Roy's full lineage back is clear (son of Emmet, born 1854, died 1929), but Emmet's full family structure is not fully documented in the sources.

### B3. Selby Vose's father, and why this matters

Selby Vose was the son of Gideon Vose (first president 1889–1911, died 1917), not Gideon's nephew as sometimes stated in the valley. Bertram Kyle specifically corrects this: "Selby Vose, who managed the Association, was Gideon Vose's son...not, as I have heard it given, his nephew." This matters because Selby inherited not just the managing position but also his father's intimate knowledge of Article VII and the system's design. Gideon had drafted Article VII to depend absolutely on the station test. Selby, managing from 1913 onward, signed the annual ledger sheets every year that carried the "station gain" line—five and a quarter percent, year after year. Either Selby understood the line and said nothing, or he did not read it, or he misunderstood it. His 1926 address blamed water for the 1924 test rise, not glass replacement. His knowledge (or ignorance) of what he was signing shaped how the crisis unfolded.

### B4. Relationship, if any, between Vose and Keddie families

Duncan Keddie denies any family connection between the Voses and the Keddies. He states: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses...I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins...It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me. She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it." However, Cleve Oram (r21) states: "The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley. My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper." The sources conflict. Duncan's denial is direct and emphatic; Oram's affirmation is secondhand, vague ("of some sort"), and explicitly hedged ("I could not draw you the line"). Duncan's authority is greater (he is family and heard from his mother directly), and his position is that the rumor is false valley talk. I resolve in favor of no blood relationship, grounded on Duncan's explicit family knowledge and his mother's absence of any such connection in her recorded genealogy.
