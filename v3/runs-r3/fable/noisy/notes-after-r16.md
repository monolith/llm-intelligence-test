## 0. The whole affair in one paragraph (synthesis across r01–r16)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (organized 1889 at Larrow Green in the schoolhouse; creamery built 1891 on Larrow Creek below the mill) ran a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley at the head of the Hollow, taking milk from the farms of Hessel Bottom (the 160-acre wet meadow below the hamlet; six or seven farms) and three places on the hill — nine families in all. Ansel Keddie tested there alone from 4 May 1896 through the 1924 season (29 seasons). For the first season he used borrowed glass (from Larrow Green per the first leaf — Hobart Sill says it was his own personal outfit; from "the Ashlin creamery" per Keddie's letter) and the station average was 3.80; from 1 May 1897 (Vaught's body text says 1898) the station used its own outfit bought from Ammon & Sons, Tarnet (invoice 14 April 1897, $77.93, six dozen pipettes blown at 17.6 c.c. — Vaught says five dozen), and from 1898 the average sat at 3.61 (within three hundredths) every year — except nine weeks in 1911 (8 May–10 July; Teague and Kepp say six weeks) when the station's pipette broke, a borrowed measure was used (from Larrow Green per Ivy; "the state man left his own measure that June" per Roy Brant), and the average jumped to 3.80 then fell back the day the station's own measure returned. Complaints that Ostrey Hollow milk "did not test with the rest" began 1902 (Emmet Brant among the first); a 1908 committee of three under Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green blamed the wet hay of the Bottom and found "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter"; the report was reprinted in 1919 with Grigg's covering letter claiming Bulletin 214 found "two tenths" (it found not more than five hundredths); that same year manager Selby Vose sent a printed circular to nine families saying there was water in their cans. From 1915 to 1930 (sixteen summers) four Hessel Bottom men hauled over Redlow Ridge to Jerome Cudd's Alder Corners Cheese Company in the June–September flush and tested 3.78–3.82 there every year. The Association's ledgers carried every year a "station gain" line: fat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream exceeded fat credited on the station's test by 5¼% of credited (= 5% of delivered), "as usual" (typical entry 57,760 lb credited / 60,800 lb received / gain 3,040 lb); buttermaker Hobart Sill weighed the Hollow's cream heavy on every page of his receiving book from 1893 and "never made anything of it." State dairy inspector Merle Strawn ran the station's pipettes against a burette on 12 June 1923 (Vose and Rundle say 12 July), found they "will not deliver their mark," and condemned "the whole crate of them"; new glass was ordered 28 June and in use from July 1923; the 1924 average rose to 3.80 (Vose says 3.90). The patrons petitioned in January 1925 (Rundle: loss set out "to the dollar" at $19,600; Frick: patrons' reckoning 67,000 lb of butterfat); a board of three under attorney Alonzo Frick sat 17 March–4 May 1925 — first session at the Ashlin office, second and third in the station's own weigh room — heard three explanations (water/Vose, cream theft/Teague, hay/1908 report), had four books before it (test book, station receiving book, Brant weigh book, Strawn's 1923 notebook), found "no cause established," and awarded the patrons $3,800 "without admission," charged to the station account; Keddie was put out on 30 April 1925, four days before the award, and died in 1934. Dr. Roland Kepp (1926) told the Farm Club the 0.21 gap was soil chemistry; Dell Rundle's 1939 jubilee pamphlet recorded the valley's "settled opinion" that cream left by the back door, then retracted it in press on seeing the ledger line ("It reached the Green heavy"). In February 1958 Keddie's granddaughter Ivy Keddie (State Agricultural College, Farland) found the crate of condemned pipettes; on 9 March 1958 she measured the nine unbroken ones (Rundle's footnote says eleven) at a mean delivery of 16.72 c.c. against the 17.6 mark — 95 hundredths — proving every test read 5% low; over the 25 full delivery years 1898–1922 (40,000,000 lb milk; Teague, Rundle: 44,000,000) that was 76,000 lb of butterfat worth $19,000 at the 25¢/lb average, five times the award. Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958, Professor Leland Vaught — whose body text gives 16.27 c.c., ratio 0.9244, true average 3.905, shortfall 0.295, 118,000 lb, and admits it is "not readily reconciled") cleared Keddie in its closing paragraph: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there." Ivy read her talk "What the Crate Held" to the Ordell County Historical Society at Ashlin in 1959; curator Halbert Nym catalogued the collection in 1972.

## 1. Cast — every name and relationship

- **Ansel Keddie** — tester at Ostrey Hollow skimming station; went up in 1895 to get it ready (r01); kept the test book in his own hand from 4 May 1896 through the 1924 season = 29 seasons (Frick's exhibit list: "4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924"); did not test in 1925; put out 30 April 1925, "four days before we made our award" (r13); died 1934 (r04; r01 says "in the thirties"); "an old man" with "no other trade" in 1925. Husband of Orra. Father of Duncan. Grandfather of Ivy. Brother-in-law of Emmet Brant. Frick (r13): "lived among them thirty years"; told Frick privately in the Ashlin corridor that "he had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the room"; "he suspected nobody, which is not [true]." Roy Brant (r09): his father stopped speaking to Keddie in 1919 "not from any quarrel over the water, but because my father held that the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not." Vira's father: "an honest man who had got into something."
- **Orra Keddie** (née Brant) — Ansel's wife; **Emmet Brant's sister** — "full brother and sister, same mother and same father, whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it" (r09); **first cousin of old Mrs. Vose** (r01, "everybody knew, nobody wrote down"); outlived Ansel "a good while"; after 1919 walked down to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane.
- **Emmet Brant** — born 1854, died 1929 (r09); biggest man on Hessel Bottom: most cows, most hay ground, hauled the most milk; the station's largest patron in every recorded year (r02); sixty-odd acres of the meadow and thirty-eight cows in a good year (r09); kept the weigh book "from the first of January, 1898 [sic — 1897 elsewhere], until the week before he died"; wrote at the kitchen table after the second milking, in the lamp, hat still on; one of the first men to complain out loud in 1902 "and they were laughed at"; read the 1908 report twice and put it in the drawer: "the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not"; noticed and entered the 1911 rise and fall; the 1919 letter "cost him the most" (r01); never forgave it; brother of Orra; brother-in-law of Ansel; ate Sunday dinners with the Keddies before 1919, never spoke to Ansel after the circular; father of Roy Brant; did not haul to Alder Corners ("the biggest herd is the one that cannot afford to be talked about" — Roy).
- **Roy Brant** — Emmet's son; born c. 1888 (67 in winter 1955); farmer at Hessel Bottom, still milking in 1955; kept up the weigh book since his father died (1929), "in the same book until it was filled and then in another"; wrote the 1955 account for the county farm bureau's local-history collection, in longhand, with a 1958 postscript; lent the book to Ivy Keddie in 1958 ("She is careful with paper"); book to go to Hazel; first cousin of Duncan Keddie; father of Hazel; has cut the meadow "with a scythe and with a mower behind two horses."
- **Hazel Brant** — Roy's daughter; Miss Hazel Brant received the farm weigh book in 1962, deposited to the Rooms 1963 (r07). Ivy Keddie is "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed" (r01) — but Roy (r09) and Rundle (r12 footnote) say Hazel and Ivy are **second cousins** (which the genealogy supports: Emmet→Roy→Hazel; Orra→Duncan→Ivy).
- **Duncan Keddie** — Ansel and Orra's son ("boy"); taught school at Ashlin; Ivy's father (r04, r09); Roy Brant's first cousin (r09).
- **Ivy Keddie** — Ansel's granddaughter; "Duncan's girl"; of the State Agricultural College at Farland ("the college away east" per r01; "a graduate student at Farland" per r10 header); her college's library holds Strawn's notebooks; found the crate February 1958; measured pipettes 9 March 1958; wrote to Rosalie Cudd in 1958 asking "in four lines" what four Ordell County farmers' milk tested over the ridge; borrowed Roy's book 1958; talk to Ordell County Historical Society, Ashlin, 1959; visited Vira Toland in 1958 ("nice girl, careful, wrote everything down"); "second cousin to Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom" (r12 footnote).
- **Gideon Vose** — "old Gideon"; started the Association; first president 1889–1911 (22 years, longest ever); "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the law and thought better of it" (r12); drafted the by-laws himself at his own kitchen table (not a lawyer) in a hand clerks complained of thirty years later; wrote Article VII (adopted 1892 per Effie; "dates from 1891" per Rundle); died 1917 (r02); kept a herd of Jerseys at the home place that tested 4.56 in 1910 (r08). Selby's **father** per r02 ("son of the late president") and r08 ("My father, who was your first president"); Selby's **uncle** per r01 ("old Gideon Vose's nephew") and r12 ("His nephew Selby Vose ... used to say that his uncle wrote by-laws the way other men build stone wall — slowly, and to last").
- **Old Mrs. Vose** — first cousin of Orra Keddie (r01).
- **Selby Vose** — manager of the Association 1913–1938 (r02); "down at Larrow Green" (r01); signed the 1919 printed circular; signed Teague's annual sheet every spring; told Teague in 1916 to "let it alone"; 1926 annual address; "the family always thought the valley belonged to them a little" (r01). Strawn 1913: "New manager at the Association this year and he came out with me to the platform, which the last one never did." Before the board (r13) he put the water explanation "without heat and without proof" and "stood by" the circular. Sill (r14): "I thought well of him for it. It was the only plain thing that office ever did."

## 2. Places

- **Larrow Valley** — the Association's valley; "poor milk country" reputation in the trade below (Vose denies).
- **Larrow Green** — hamlet with the main creamery (built 1891 on Larrow Creek "below the mill," "the same building yet"); the 1889 meeting was in the schoolhouse there; the manager is "down at Larrow Green" (r01); lent the glass in 1896–97 (first leaf; Sill's own outfit per r14); the 1911 borrowed measure "came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon" (r04); receiving/churn books there (Nym Item 16); Sill's churn room — plank floor in 1893, cooled with creek ice out of the pond; Larrow Green's own patrons ("the men on this creek") ran 3.78 (r14); Rundle taught the school there; Grigg hauls there.
- **Ostrey Hollow** — station at the very top where the road bends and goes to nothing but the ridge; low whitewashed building, cold as a spring house even in August, smelling of milk, steam, hot iron; engine audible from the last turn; "boarded and battened, with the intake room at the road end and a loft over it that I believe has never been cleared out since it was put up; and behind it the creek, which in a wet April comes up to the sill and has twice come over it" (r12); intake room with loft above reached by a ladder; slope desk by the window; weigh room used for board sessions two and three (r13); shut for years by 1958, used for fence wire and salt (r01) / storage (r04); seven miles up from Larrow Green (r02, r05, r06, r08, r11, r12, r13); "the pull down was three hours in mud and could not be done at all in March" (r12); cream wagon down every day but Sunday (r14).
- **Hessel Bottom** — the wet meadow below the hamlet, "a hundred and sixty acres of wet meadow lying under the hill at Ostrey Hollow" (r09); six farms had rights (r09, r15, r16) / seven (r02); the Brants had "sixty-odd acres"; water stands on the low end into May; "a soft place by the alders that has taken a horse to the hock"; springs come out at the foot of the slope and run year-round; part under water every spring, not walkable till well into May (r16); herbage sedge, rush, coarse sour grasses; hay cut late and heavy, fed November–April (r15); best milk of the station.
- **Ashlin** — town with lawyers; Association office (one room over a harness shop, cold at the north end, per Teague); site of the first 1925 session (r13); Ordell County Historical Society / Historical Rooms; Duncan Keddie taught school there; Vira lived there two years; "Ashlin creamery" lent glass per Keddie's letter; Kepp's practice; farmers hauled butter to Ashlin merchants before 1889 (r12).
- **Ordell County** — the county; Ordell circuit (Strawn); Ordell County Historical Society/Rooms; Ordell County Bar Association (Frick's yearbook); Ordell County Farm Club (Kepp's paper); county farm bureau (Roy's account).
- **Vessey County** — over Redlow Ridge; Alder Corners Cheese Company "on the flat below the church"; Vessey County Historical Society (holds the Alder Corners test book since 1970).
- **Redlow Ridge** — "four miles of switchback, cut for a lumber road and never much improved since. It shuts in November and it does not open before April, some years May" (r10); drifts sit in the cut below the top; cloud sits on the top till the sun is up.
- **Farland** — State Agricultural College; its library holds Strawn's notebooks since 1947; Ivy measured the pipettes there; Bulletin 214 and 471 issued there.
- **Tarnet** — Ammon & Sons Dairy Supply.
- **Larrow Creek** — the creamery stands on it; "the creek" also runs behind the station and along Hessel Bottom.
- **The mill** at Larrow Green — creamery built "on the creek below the mill" (r12).

## 3. Master timeline

- **1854** — Emmet Brant born (r09).

- **1889** — Larrow Valley farmers meet at Larrow Green, in the schoolhouse, in cold weather, late into the night, somebody's horse got loose (r12); Association organized; Gideon Vose president (to 1911). Effie 14 years old.

- **1910** — Brant delivers 200,000 lb even, paid on 3.42; butterfat 24¢ that year (r09); Vose's Jersey herd at the home place tests 4.56.

**Keddie's statement to the board, 17 March 1925** (r03, nine sentences):
> I have kept the test book of this station in my own hand since the station opened, and there is not a figure in it that I did not put there. I have set down the date, the patron, the pounds, the test and the fat, and where I have made an error I have drawn a line through it and put my letters against the line, and I have never written a figure over a figure. I have made the tests in the same order and at the same hour of every working day of every season. I have heard what the patrons say of me and I have heard what the officers say of me. Neither of them has come up that road on a working morning and stood at my elbow and watched the work done. I am asked to account for figures, and the figures are what I read and what I wrote and nothing besides. I am an old man and I have no other trade. I have tested as I was taught and as the state man saw me do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, and I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are. I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself.

> REPORT OF THE COMMITTEE APPOINTED TO INQUIRE INTO THE COMPLAINTS OF THE PATRONS OF THE OSTREY HOLLOW STATION.
>
> To the President and Directors of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association.
>
> Your committee, appointed in January last, respectfully reports as follows.
>
> The complaint before us is of some years' standing and is in substance this: that the milk delivered at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station is returned by the test at a figure below that of milk delivered elsewhere in this valley, and that the patrons of that station are in consequence paid less for the same labor and the same cows than their neighbors are paid. Since payment by test came in among us and the pound of butter fat took the place of the pound of milk as the measure of a man's month, a difference of this kind is no longer a matter of opinion. It is a matter of money, and it comes out of the same pocket every month, and your committee has approached it in that light and not as a quarrel.
>
> Hessel Bottom, where six farms cut hay and turn out stock, is a wet meadow lying along the creek under Ostrey Hollow. Its ground is springy the year round and is under water in part every spring. Its herbage is sedge, rush, and the coarse sour grasses that come in on such land, and its hay is cut late and heavy and is fed as the main dependence of those farms from November to April. There is no better winter keep to be had there. We say this without reproach to any man on that bottom; the land is what it is, and they took it as their fathers left it to them.
>
> Your committee visited each of the six farms, some of them three and four times. We drew our samples ourselves and we sealed them ourselves. We saw the herds milked, we saw the cans filled, and we saw them hauled to the station and weighed in at the intake, and we stood by while the test was made and saw nothing whatever in the doing of it to remark upon. We tried the station scales against a known weight and we tried the patrons' own scales against the station's. We inquired into the water supply at each barn and satisfied ourselves upon it.
>
> Your committee further observed the general condition of the stock upon that bottom, as men who have kept cattle all their lives are entitled to observe it. The herds are thin in the flank at the end of the winter feeding and slow to come to their flesh in the spring. Their coats are rough later in the season than the coats of hill herds. They do well enough in flow, for the wet land is generous of milk, but it is our opinion, formed on the ground and not out of a book, that what such land gives in quantity it withholds in quality, and that a cow cannot put into her pail what her keep has never put into her.
>
> We report that we found no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter, and we wish that finding to be printed in the same type as the rest.
>
> We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March. The milk is whole and the scales are honest. It is our opinion that the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station.
>
> NYLE GRIGG, chairman, and the committee.

## 7. Per-retelling detail

### r01 — Vira Toland (b. 1912), Hessel Bottom patron's daughter, taped at Ostrey Hollow, 1979
- Tape at her kitchen table; interviewer asked three questions in an hour; transcribed as spoken with doublings-back.
- Born 1912 at the top end of the Hollow in her grandfather's house; two years at Ashlin when first married, "would not count those two years."
- Nine families hauled to the station; "in and out of each other's kitchens."
- Station description (see Places). Keddie went up in '95, the year before they opened it, "and he tested that first summer too."
- The book: everybody knew about it; ruled with his own straightedge; every line the same height; figures like print; wrote standing at a slope desk by the window; could turn to any day and read it off; "tested a thousand cans a week and never a blot"; father's joke about lawyers in Ashlin ("There are still lawyers in Ashlin").
- 1919: "the year everything went sour"; she was seven; a printed letter over the manager's signature: water in the cans coming down from Ostrey Hollow; went to nine families; father read it twice with his hat still on; mother stood at the stove with her back turned; letter into the desk drawer in the front room, still there when he died, brother has it yet; one woman had hers pinned inside her trunk lid; "keep the paper forty years to prove what was said of them."
- Signer: Selby Vose, down at Larrow Green, old Gideon Vose's nephew; Gideon started the Association; "the family always thought the valley belonged to them a little."
- Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose first cousins — "it was somebody's cousin saying it."
- Orra = Ansel's wife = Emmet Brant's sister; Emmet biggest man on the Bottom; "the man that tested the milk had married the sister of the man that got the worst of it"; Sunday dinners together before 1919, two houses in one kitchen, men at the fence after; after 1919 not one word, not at the store, not at a funeral; Orra walked to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane; lived within sight of each other's chimney smoke.
- Then the tests went up, all at once one summer, and stayed up; nobody said why; father had done nothing different — "the queerest part" to her.

### r09 — Roy Brant, farmer at Hessel Bottom, account for the county farm bureau's local-history collection, 1955 (postscript 1958)

### r11 — Leland Vaught, introduction and method to Bulletin No. 471, State Agricultural College at Farland, October 1958
- Opening and method sections, "written for extension agents and county workers," closing with the concluding paragraph entire.
- §I: asked "at intervals for fifty years" why one skimming station ran below its creamery seven miles down the same valley; "answered at various times by reference to the land and by reference to the men. It is answered here by reference to a piece of glass"; "Nobody in that valley lacked the intelligence to find it. What they lacked was any occasion to suspect the one article in the plant that nobody in the trade is in the habit of suspecting." Bold thesis (see §6).
- §II: four operations — pipette charge, sulfuric acid, spin in hand tester, read fat directly in percent; "The operator weighs nothing"; neck ruled for the standard charge; standard charge 17.6 c.c.; short delivery → reading "low in the exact proportion by which the charge was short." Three consequences: (1) proportional not additive — rich milk loses more absolutely, ratios undisturbed, "Its herds will rank correctly against one another. Only against the outside world is it wrong"; (2) precise — "A pipette that delivers 16.27 c.c. where it is blown 17.6 delivers 16.27 c.c. every time, for a quarter of a century, in January and in July ... It has the appearance of excellent work"; (3) invisible from inside — scales/test weights, tester speed counted, acid strength, bottle graduation with mercury, "But the pipette is not checked against anything, because in the ordinary practice of the trade the pipette is the standard ... It can be caught only by a burette brought in from outside, and burettes reached the state's field men very late in this history."
- §III: lot bought 1897 — "five dozen pipettes were purchased"; nine recovered unbroken and legible; stems 17.6; five runs each; "mean delivery of the nine is 16.27 c.c."; "The station's own glass went into use on the first of May, 1898, and remained in use until the state inspector condemned the lot in June of 1923. Before the earlier date, and after the later, the station's tests were made on other glass." Method for county workers (water at room temperature, tip wiped, ordinary drainage, touch tip to wall, certified burette, five deliveries, an afternoon); "no reason why every station in this state should not be checked once in this way."
- §IV: 1911 nine weeks; "One variable changed"; 3.61 → 3.80 held nine weeks, back to 3.61 the day the measure returned; "recorded contemporaneously, in the operator's hand, by a man who did not know what he was recording and set a question mark against it"; "an experiment ... that ran itself ... and lay unread for forty-seven years"; disposes of herds, ground, men.
- §V: 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244; 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.905; shortfall 0.295; 40,000,000 lb; 118,000 lb "delivered and not credited"; "It should be said that this figure is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the glassware." Ledger "station gain" — "The gain is five and a quarter percent of the butterfat actually delivered."
- §VI: Bulletin 214 quoted; "Five hundredths in winter; nothing at all in summer. The discrepancy under examination is present in every month of the year and is some six times the largest effect the trial could produce under conditions harsher than any farmer would impose."
- §VII: conclusion paragraph (identical to r04/r07) — 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923; one part in twenty; 76,000 lb. Signed "— L. VAUGHT."

### r13 — Alonzo Frick, "Recollections of the Ordell Arbitration," Ordell County Bar Association yearbook, 1946
- Editor asked "the older men of this bar"; Frick 74; forty years of titles and boundaries, two homicides; "none of them has stayed with me as the Ostrey Hollow matter has. It was not litigation at all ... no appeal from it, no reported decision, and nothing whatever to cite it for."
- The complaint: payment by fat, station test governs; "it makes the test the whole of the bargain, and it leaves a dissatisfied man with no remedy on earth except to quarrel with a figure." Station seven miles up; patrons paid "year upon year, on an average test of 3.16"; "Three and sixteen is not a scandalous figure standing by itself. It becomes one when the man over the hill is paid on a better figure for milk out of cows no different from yours; when the difference holds steady for a quarter of a century; and when nobody in the office can tell you the cause of it."
- Petition January 1925; by-laws provided arbitration; officers did not resist; board of three; Frick chairman "because I was the one of the three who kept no cow."
- Three sessions: first at the Association's office at Ashlin, 17 March 1925; second and third at Ostrey Hollow "in the station's own weigh room, so that the board might see the plant it was to pass upon instead of hearing it described by men with an interest in the description"; "Every physical appliance of that station was before us and we satisfied ourselves upon it. We saw the scales tried. We saw the sampling done and the test made"; "I could see that it was done the same way twice, and that the men who did it were not hiding their hands."
- Three explanations: (1) water — Selby Vose, stood by his circular, "a little water in a can is the oldest trick in the trade, and that a station cannot test what is not there"; "without heat and without proof." (2) tester taking cream and writing low tests — Wilbur Teague, "in the way an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it," answered "at length about how such an account would look if a man were helping himself, and then observed that he made no charge against anybody"; "There is no cross-examination that reaches a witness of that kind." (3) the hay — "Somebody read the report of the 1908 committee into the record," bog meadow before them "the better part of an afternoon"; "it had the advantage of blaming nobody in the room."
- Keddie: tested since 1896, book in his own hand every season; statement 17 March; "I believed him then and I believe him now. His denial was accurate and it did him no good at all ... a man who cannot account for a figure is presumed by everybody in the room to be the figure's author." Corridor remark (not in the minutes): Keddie "had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the room. He was not a bitter man about it. He had lived among them thirty years and would not stand up and accuse his neighbors ... he went to his grave accusing nobody, which is true, and that he suspected nobody, which is not."
- Dismissal 30 April, four days before the award; "no finding against him from us and there never was one ... He was simply let go while the board was still sitting, and I have never been able to think well of the Association for it."
- Award 4 May 1925: no cause established; $3,800 without admission, charged to station account; "The patrons' own reckoning of what they had lost was sixty-seven thousand pounds of butter fat over the years complained of, and nothing was ever brought forward to disturb that figure"; "An award without a finding is an unsatisfactory thing and every member of that board knew it."
- Exhibits (see §6): four books; "Four books and nothing else. It is a short list for a matter of that size, and I have looked at it many times since."
- Reflection: "whether the answer was in that room and simply was not in any of the three stories, and whether a lawyer who understood chemistry might have found it ... I understood that an honest man was ruined by a figure nobody could explain, and that we paid his neighbors money without being able to tell them what they were being paid for."

### r14 — Hobart Sill, buttermaker (ret.), remarks at the Larrow Green creamery on the day he left, 1934
- "Forty-one years I have stood at that churn. Eighteen ninety-three I came here, and there was no floor in this room but plank, and we cooled with creek ice out of the pond"; "never sent out a tub of butter I would not eat myself, which is more than the office can say."
- Cream wagon down from Ostrey Hollow every day but Sunday, seven miles of hill road; warm in July; set off at the platform, weighed, sampled, into the cold room; Sill weighed it over again himself — "I have never in my life taken another man's figure for what was going into my churn"; book ruled and signed before he put his coat on; his hand on every page of the receiving book from '93.
- "That cream ran heavy. Every year it ran heavy. There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there ... Always more. Never less." "I have said it to men in this room a hundred times and none of them ever thought it was worth anything."
- "Cream is not milk and it will not stand still and be counted. It is a thick thing and it clings and it separates on you ... So it ran heavy. So does everybody's. I never made anything of it, and I will say plain, nobody ever asked me to."
- Larrow Green's own patrons "ran 3.78 taking one year with another right through those years ... Ordinary milk, ordinary county, ordinary cows."
- "The Hollow milk was really a 3.85 and no better ... Wet ground up there, but good cows, and I never had a word to say against the cows ... if the figures they were paid on came out under that, then somebody up that hill road was helping the cans along, and I said so at the time and I say it now."
- "You cannot tell me nine families all got honest in one summer. There was water in those cans ... one summer, along in the twenties, those figures came up and they stayed up, and nothing on this earth changed but the season ... Something got stopped. Something got said to somebody. I never knew what and I never asked."
- 1902: "a man in here shouting that his milk tested under his brother-in-law's down the creek and it was the same feed and the same water and what did we mean by it." 1919 circular — "I thought well of him for it. It was the only plain thing that office ever did ... a paper never emptied a can."
- "The Association has a paper for everything and butter for nothing"; men in collars; butter took premiums in three states; "Twenty-seven cents. That is what fat was through those years, take one year with another."
- Station opening: "ninety-six, in May, and there was a crowd went up for it — they had no outfit of their own. Not a piece. It was ordered and it was not come ... talk of holding off the whole season. And I said no, they can have mine. I went to my own bench in this room and I made up an outfit for them out of my own glass, my own, that I had used and knew, and it went up the valley on the wagon and they opened on it. Their own came the spring after and they sent mine back to me, and one piece of it was chipped and I never said a word about that either."
- First season "about nine hundred thousand pounds up there. May to the end of the year"; "the biggest thing that ever happened to Ostrey Hollow."
- Aged 64; "never went up there but twice in my life"; the butter "as good as any, and better than most, and there was never a season it was not."
- "Do not put in that I said anything against the cows up there ... The cows were never the trouble. Put in that Hobart Sill made the butter and it was good butter and he stood at the churn until the day they gave him a chair."

### r15 — Nyle Grigg, covering letter of 1919 to the reprinted 1908 committee report
- Header: "A farmer of standing sends his eleven-year-old report back into circulation because the same complaint has been raised again, and adds a letter saying that the intervening science has proved him right."
- Letter dated Larrow Green, 1919, "To the officers and patrons": asked to let the report be printed again, "the Ostrey Hollow question having been reopened this season by men who were boys when we made it"; "I have nothing in it to take back."
- Background: from about 1902 grumbling; "It was not one man and it was not one season"; annual meeting talk, crossroads stores between here and Ashlin; "somebody must be doing it on purpose"; board "could not let it alone any longer without seeming to have an interest in letting it alone"; "we three were named"; "I have never in my life been given a duty I liked less." "Payment by test came in with the 1893 meeting, as I have always understood it, and from that year a difference of this kind stopped being a matter of opinion and became a matter of money." Known the Bottom men since youth; "bought hay off two of them."
- Method: "We sampled those herds through the whole of 1908 — every month of that year, in the barn and on the pasture, out of the night's milk and the morning's, and we did not let a man know which day we were coming. We weighed. We watched the test made and we watched it made again."
- Standing: "I had no interest whatever in the answer. I am not a patron of that station. I haul to Larrow Green and always have"; "abused for the finding by men on both sides of it, which I take to be a fair sign that the finding was an honest one."
- Bulletin 214: "a feeding trial upon bog hay carried out under the College's own eye, with weighed rations and no interest in the outcome. That trial found the bog hay to depress the butter fat test by two tenths in the winter feeding. Two tenths, gentlemen ... There is our finding, arrived at by another road and by better men, three years after we set our names to it."
- Remedy: "Drain that bottom, sweeten it, keep the stock off it in the wet of the spring, and feed the coarse hay off it with a mixed ration and not by itself, and the Hollow will test with the rest of us. Go on as they are going and abuse the man who reads their figures for them, and they will be grumbling in twenty years' time."
- Report full text (see §6). Closing line: "That report was received and accepted, and the question was closed by it for eleven years — until this season, and the men who have reopened it have brought nothing new before us but their impatience."

### r16 — Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S., of Ashlin, "The bog meadows and the milk," Ordell County Farm Club, 1926
- Header: veterinary surgeon of thirty years' practice; discussion appended by the club's secretary.
- Opening: "ask first what the cattle are standing on ... nine cases in ten is under the animal's feet, and that the tenth is in the water she drinks"; the question "has been dealt with hitherto by opinion, by grumbling, and by the naming of names."
- The land: wet bottom = distinct chemistry; growth lies sour; acid soil poor in lime and phosphates; sweet grasses crowded out by sedge, rush, tussock; herbage carries bulk and water, not lime or phosphorus; cut late.
- The cow: "a manufactory working on a narrow margin"; robs her own frame; thin over the loin, rough coat, slow to condition, digestive disorders in fall, breed later, worse feet; "Wet land is generous of flow and no man on a bog meadow ever complained that his pail was light ... the fat is the first thing to suffer, because the fat is the dearest thing the cow makes and it is the first economy she practices."
- The measure: "The wet bottoms of this county yield a milk poorer in fat by a fifth of a point and every practical man knows it ... farm for farm ... It depends on the ground."
- Hessel Bottom: "completest example of wet meadow in this county"; six farms; along the creek; springs at the foot of the slope year-round; part under water every spring, not walkable till well into May; sedge hay the winter dependence. Patrons complained "something like a quarter of a century"; "Their complaint is perfectly just and I have never doubted it"; gap "twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21 — between the milk of that station and the milk of this valley at large"; 1908 committee found herds sound, milk whole, scales honest; "it will not be settled by men shouting at one another across a weigh room. It is settled already, in the literature."
- The College: Bulletin 214 "found the butter fat test depressed by two tenths in the winter feeding. That is the whole case. Two tenths from the College, laid alongside the fifth of a point I have found in the field and the twenty-one hundredths that station has been complaining of, and the three agree so nearly."
- An objection: "by a gentleman who I know will forgive my noticing it here, that in the summer of 1911 the testing at that station was broken off for six weeks, their own apparatus having failed them and a borrowed one being used, and that the figures stood higher during those six weeks ... Six weeks of June and July, when every herd in the county is at its best and the stock are off the wet ground and up on the aftermath ... I could produce you six weeks in any year that would prove anything you liked."
- The remedy: drain, open old ditches and cut new; lime and lime again; sow when it will carry a seeding; keep stock off in spring; "never feed that hay alone"; mixed ration and mineral supplement "as the College directs"; "Fat has been worth twenty-seven cents the pound through these years, and a fifth of a point taken off every hundredweight a farm ships ... will pay for a great deal of tile."
- Discussion: member — cows not land? Kepp: rejected; best-bred cattle on wet land, indifferent hill cattle test well; "a man may improve his herd for twenty years and never get his fat up while his ground is sour." Member — station rather than bottom? Kepp: "no interest in that quarrel and no competence in it; that he had never in his life read a test and would not know how; but that when a cause is found in the soil, in the herbage, in the condition of the stock, in the College's trial, and in every wet bottom in the county alike, it is not good sense to go looking for a second cause in a building." Chairman: paper to be printed for the members.

## 8. Conflicts and oddities — between and within sources

(1–34 carried forward from the r08 notes, updated where new sources bear on them; 35 onward new this segment.)

1. **Award amount**: $3,500 split nine ways (r01) vs $3,800.00 (r02 award text, r04, r05, r07, r08, r13).
2. **Selby's relation to Gideon**: nephew (r01; r12 "His nephew Selby Vose ... his uncle") vs son (r02 "son of the late president"; r08 "My father, who was your first president").
3. **Total milk**: 40,000,000 lb over 25 years (r02, r04, r07, r11) vs 44,000,000 lb (r05; r12 "over the years complained of").
4. **Station average**: 3.61 (r03, r04, r06, r07, r11) vs 3.16 "over the whole period" (r05; r13 "an average test of 3.16 ... Three and sixteen") — looks like a digit transposition that Frick took from Teague's evidence.
5. **1924 average / size of rise**: 3.80, up 0.19 (r03, r04, r06, r11 "the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following") vs 3.90, up "twenty-one hundredths" (r08) — 3.61 + 0.21 = 3.82, not 3.90. Kepp (r16) also uses 0.21 as the gap.
6. **Condemnation date**: 12 June 1923 (r06 entry, r07 Items 5 & 9, r04 and r11 conclusion "twelfth of June") vs "the twelfth of July, 1923" (r08; r12). The replacement order was 28 June and the supplier's letter 2 July (r07 Item 10), new glass in use July (r06) — impossible under the July date.
7. **1911 borrowed-measure episode**: nine weeks, 8 May–10 July (r03, averages leaf, r04, r09, r11) vs "six weeks" (r05; r16 "six weeks of June and July"); station's own measure "in use again" (r03) / "returned" (r04) / "came back into use" (r09) vs "before their own came up from town" i.e. a replacement (r05). Source of the borrowed measure: "came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon" (r04) vs "The state man left his own measure that June" (r09) — the break was 8 May, and Strawn's own June 1911 entry says "Nothing here to note" with no mention of lending anything.
8. **Source of the 1896 borrowed glass**: "borrowed off the Ashlin creamery" (r03 letter) vs "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green" (r03 first leaf, r07 Items 2–3) vs Hobart Sill's own personal outfit made up at his bench at Larrow Green (r14) — Sill's version is consistent with "Larrow Green" but says the glass was his, not the creamery's, and one piece came back chipped.
9. **Where the crate was found**: the station loft over the intake room, under a tarpaulin, February 1958 (r04; consistent with r06 1928 and r12 "a loft ... never been cleared out") vs "found in the Association office at Ashlin, in February 1958" (r07 Item 5).
10. **Strawn's start year**: 1907 (r06) vs "from 1908" (r04).
11. **Alder Corners overlap**: "fourteen seasons" of four Hessel Bottom men (r04) vs sixteen seasons 1915–1930 (r09, r10 table) vs figures copied for "the season of 1919" only (r07 Item 13). Rosalie Cudd: "Jerome Cudd's niece" (r04) vs daughter (r07 "founder to his daughter in 1941"; r10 "My father, Jerome Cudd"; r12 "Jerome Cudd's daughter").
12. **Origin of the station-gain line**: Teague says "my own device; I began it when I came in 1905," yet quotes "the entry for 1898" in that form, and Nym says the station column runs from 1897 with each year "in one form." Rundle (1939) saw the same entry form "every year."
13. **Denominator of "five and a quarter per cent"**: Teague says the gain ran at 5¼% "of the fat that was actually in the cream" (received); Vaught (r11 §V) says "five and a quarter percent of the butterfat actually delivered" — but 3,040/60,800 = 5.0%; 5¼% is of the credited 57,760 (Ivy, Nym both say so; Vaught's own conclusion says "one part in twenty").
14. **Test-book columns**: five (r03) vs four (r07 Item 3).
15. **Nym's swapped labels**: Item 3 "kept by E. Brant" / Item 4 "kept by A. Keddie" — reversed.
16. **Dismissal vs award dates**: Keddie put out 30 April 1925 (r03; r13 "four days before we made our award") while the board sat until 4 May — dismissed before the finding.
17. **Water in the cans**: Vira, Keddie, Roy, Sill, Rundle read the circular as saying there WAS water; Vose (r08) says it only warned water would be looked for and "named nobody" — though it went to nine specific families; Strawn (r06) never found added water on any visit and told the manager so in 1919; Rosalie (r10): cheese "is a witness," never a watered can in sixteen summers; Bulletin 471: "No milk was watered."
18. **Vira's internal chronology**: rise (1924) preceded the dismissal (1925), yet she says "they put him out, and that is why the numbers went right the summer after." Sill (r14) similarly: "Something got stopped. Something got said to somebody" — but the circular was 1919, five years before the 1924 rise.
19. **Vose's chronology**: petition → hearing → "in the very next season" the rise of 1924; but the petition was January 1925.
20. **Article VII's date**: adopted at the 1892 annual meeting (r02) vs "dates from 1891, the year the churn was set up" (r12) vs "Payment by test came in with the 1893 meeting, as I have always understood it" (r15 Grigg); the printed pamphlet carries no year (r07 Item 1); the 1889–1892 minute book is lost.
21. **Number of patron families/farms**: nine families (r01, r03, r09, r14, circular) vs seven farms with rights on Hessel Bottom (r02) vs six farms (r09 "Six farms had rights on it"; r15 report "six farms cut hay and turn out stock"; r16 "Six farms") plus "three places on the hill" (r06). Six + three = nine ✓ — Effie's seven is the odd one.
22. **The 4.56 herd**: Ivy's example implies a station herd; the only 4.56 is Vose's Jersey herd at the home place (r08). Ivy's 3.42 matches Brant's 1910 leaf.
23. **Strawn's books**: "twenty-eight ... one to the year" over 1907–1936 (30 calendar years); extracts list only 20 years.
24. **Strawn's June 1911 visit** falls inside the borrowed-measure window; "Nothing here to note" — and Roy says Strawn himself supplied the measure.
25. **Volume of work**: "a thousand cans a week" (r01) vs "thirty-one times a week" (r03, r13).
26. **Office location/move**: Teague's office at Ashlin over a harness shop 1905–1945; Vira places the manager "down at Larrow Green"; Effie "the office was moved in 1938"; Nym: test book "in the Association's office at Ashlin from 1925"; Frick: first session "at the Association's office at Ashlin"; Rundle: "the Association's old ledgers in the office" (1939).
27. **Loft "full since before the war"** (r06, 1928) vs crate carried up June 1923; Rundle (1939): loft "never been cleared out since it was put up."
28. **Period of the error**: Bulletin 471 conclusion 1 May 1897–12 June 1923 (26 years); the 76,000 lb covers only 1898–1922; Vaught's §III says glass went into use "the first of May, 1898" — contradicting his own conclusion and the 1897 invoice/office file.
29. **Keddie "tested that first summer too"** (r01, 1895?) vs book begun 4 May 1896.
30. **Effie's "three explanations argued"** — now named by Frick (r13): water (Vose), cream theft (Teague), hay (1908 report read into the record). None was the glass; Effie says the crate was never mentioned.
31. **Vose's "every year or two"** for the state man vs Strawn's near-annual visits.
32. **Ivy's father's name** Duncan Keddie in r04 and r09 only; Vira never names the 1958 visitor.
33. **Old Mrs. Vose = Orra's first cousin** appears only in r01.
34. **Teague's 1916 warning to Vose** (r05) vs Vose's 1926 claim of duty; Vose signed the sheet every year.
35. **Vaught's body vs. conclusion (r11, internal)**: mean delivery 16.27 c.c. (vs 16.72 on Ivy's sheet, Nym Item 5, and Vaught's own conclusion "one part in twenty" — 16.27/17.6 = 0.9244, not 0.95); "five dozen pipettes" (vs invoice 6 doz., Ivy "six dozen"); glass into use 1 May 1898 (vs 1 May 1897 in his conclusion and the office file); true average 3.905, shortfall 0.295, 118,000 lb (vs 3.80 / 0.19 / 76,000 lb in his conclusion); he himself flags that 118,000 lb "is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following." Also §II's illustrative pipette "delivers 16.27 c.c." — the 16.27 looks like a transposition of 16.72 propagated through the arithmetic. "Some six times" the Bulletin 214 effect (0.295/0.05) vs Ivy's "four times" (0.19/0.05).
36. **Size of the gap**: 0.19 (Ivy, Bulletin conclusion, Keddie's book) vs 0.17 (Roy: "seventeen hundredths ... in July and ... in February") vs 0.21 (Kepp; Vose's rise) vs 0.295 (Vaught body) vs Sill's implied 0.24 (Hollow "really a 3.85" vs 3.61) vs Kepp's county-wide bottom/hill "fifth of a point" (0.20) vs Nym's Larrow Green "a little under two tenths better" (~3.78–3.80, consistent with Sill's 3.78).
37. **Weigh-book start date**: 1 January 1897 (r07 Item 4; r13 Exhibit 3 "begun 1 January 1897") vs "From the first of January, 1898" (r09 Roy).
38. **Ivy–Hazel relationship**: "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed" (r01) vs "second cousins" (r09 Roy, who has "had to have it explained to them ... more than once"; r12 footnote). Genealogy from r09 (Emmet→Roy→Hazel; Orra→Duncan→Ivy; Roy and Duncan first cousins) makes Hazel and Ivy second cousins — Vira is wrong.
39. **Alder Corners founding**: "built this factory in nineteen thirteen" and first vat set that year (r10 Rosalie) vs "had opened in 1914" (r09) / "in 1914 the Alder Corners Cheese Company was opened" (r12).
40. **Why the Brant–Keddie silence**: Vira — "somebody's cousin saying it" (the Vose connection); Roy — "not from any quarrel over the water, but because my father held that the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not."
41. **Butterfat price**: 25¢ "one year with another" (r02, r04, r09) vs 24¢ in 1910 (r09) vs 27¢ "through those years" (r14 Sill; r16 Kepp).
42. **Patrons' loss claim**: petition "set the figure out to the dollar" — $19,600 (r12) vs "sixty-seven thousand pounds of butter fat" (r13) vs Ivy's 76,000 lb / $19,000 (r04). 67,000 lb × 25¢ = $16,750; $19,600 ÷ 67,000 lb = 29.25¢/lb; 67,000 × 27¢ = $18,090 — no pair reconciles. Effie (r02) said the patrons "asked to be made whole" without a figure. Frick: "nothing was ever brought forward to disturb that figure."
43. **Number of surviving pipettes**: nine (r04, r07 Item 5, r11) vs "Eleven pipettes out of the station's old condemned crate were found still to be in existence" (r12 footnote, 1959).
44. **1896 intake**: "about nine hundred thousand pounds ... May to the end of the year" (r14 Sill) vs "no 1896 intake figure exists anywhere; the station column of the ledgers begins with 1897" (r07 Nym).
45. **Hearing venue**: Frick (r13) — first session at Ashlin, second and third "at Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's own weigh room," where "We saw the scales tried. We saw the sampling done and the test made"; Vira — "in an office" at Ashlin, "the men going down in wagons," "nobody ever came up that road and looked"; Keddie's statement (first session) — "Neither of them has come up that road on a working morning"; Effie — "none of the four [officers] ever stood in the station on a working morning"; Rosalie — "I never once heard anybody go up there and look."
46. **Exhibit 4 = Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook** before the board in 1925 (r13) — yet Effie (r02) says the condemned crate was "never mentioned in any of the three sessions by patrons, officers, or board"; Strawn kept his 28 books until March 1936 (r06); Nym (r07 Item 9) says the 1923 volume was "found and copied 1958" at Farland, implying it was unknown; Frick calls the exhibit list "exactly as it was made up." If the 12 June 1923 entry was an exhibit, the condemnation was before the board and nobody drew the inference.
47. **Exhibit 2 "The receiving book of the said station"** — no station receiving book appears elsewhere; Nym Item 16 is the Larrow Green receiving and churn books (Sill's); Keddie described weighing into the test book.
48. **Bulletin 214 misquoted**: "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter" (r04, r07, r11 verbatim) vs "depress the butter fat test by two tenths in the winter feeding" (r15 Grigg 1919; r16 Kepp 1926, "That is the whole case") — both build their case on a figure four times the bulletin's ceiling.
49. **Grigg's sampling period (r15, internal)**: covering letter — "through the whole of 1908 — every month of that year, in the barn and on the pasture ... we did not let a man know which day we were coming" vs the report itself — "appointed in January last ... We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March" (also r02, r03, r09, r12 "through the winter").
50. **Grigg's committee size and signature**: "we three were named" (r15); signed "NYLE GRIGG, chairman, and the committee"; Nym Item 11 described the fair copy as signed by an unnamed chairman; Roy and Rundle name Grigg as head.
51. **Kepp's 1911 placement**: "six weeks of June and July ... the stock are off the wet ground and up on the aftermath" (r16) vs the actual window 8 May–10 July (nine weeks) and the fact that the 1911 rise was to 3.80 and the normal summer figures were also 3.61 (Keddie, Roy: same gap in July and February).
52. **Sill on "everybody's" cream running heavy** (r14: "So it ran heavy. So does everybody's") vs Teague's station-gain line being a station-specific, exactly 5¼%, every-year figure ("furniture") and Rundle's inference from it ("It reached the Green heavy, every year").
53. **Sill's "really a 3.85"** for the Hollow vs Ivy/Vaught's 3.80 true average vs Alder Corners 3.78–3.82 for the same Bottom cows.
54. **Keddie's private view**: Frick — Keddie "had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the room" vs Keddie's letter "I carry no charge against any man who put his name to that paper" and Vira's father's "an honest man who had got into something"; Frick's "lived among them thirty years" (1895–1925 ✓).
55. **Rundle's authority**: "what the valley remembers, tidied" — repeats the valley's opinion (back door), the nephew, 12 July, 44 million, then retracts the theft in press; the 1959 footnote adds "eleven" pipettes, not nine.
56. **Roy's "near on fifty years"** for 1911→1955 (44); Vaught "forty-seven years" 1911→1958 ✓; "fifty years" of questions to the station (1908→1958).
