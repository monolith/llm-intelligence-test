## Section C — Math

**C1(a). Proportion: the tests read 0.95 of the truth — one part in twenty (5%) low.** From the glassware: Ivy's sheet lists nine pipettes delivering 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 c.c. (sum 150.48 ÷ 9 = 16.72) against a stem mark of 17.6 c.c. 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly. The test bottle's neck is ruled for a 17.6 c.c. charge; a charge 5% short puts 5% less fat in the neck, and the per-cent reading falls in the same proportion. Rejected: Vaught's body text and the Chronicle give 16.27 c.c. → 0.9244; the nine listed values cannot average 16.27, Nym's Item 5 says 16.72, and Vaught's own conclusion says "one part in twenty" — 16.27 is a digit transposition (the Chronicle copied the bulletin, so it is not an independent source). Independent corroboration: 3.61 ÷ 3.80 = 0.95 in the 1911 nine weeks and in the 1924 rise, and Alder Corners at 3.80.

**C1(b). True average 3.80; recorded tests short by 0.19 points.** 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80 (check: 3.80 × 0.95 = 3.61). Rejected: Kepp's and Vose's 0.21 (3.61 + 0.21 = 3.82, not Vose's 3.90); Sill's "really a 3.85" and Falke's 3.85 (memory / unexplained); Vaught's 3.905 and 0.295 (from the transposed 16.27). Roy's "seventeen hundredths" is not wrong so much as a different comparison — Larrow Green's own patrons ran 3.78 (Sill, Falke), and 3.78 − 3.61 = 0.17.

**C1(c). 76,000 lb of butterfat.** Full delivery years on the faulty glass: 1898–1922, 25 years, 40,000,000 lb of milk (1,600,000 lb/yr — Effie from the annual sheets, added twice; Kyle, who got the figure out for the arbitration; Nym; Ivy; Vaught). Credited at 3.61%: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb. Delivered at 3.80%: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb. Difference 76,000 lb (= 0.0019 × 40,000,000). Rejected: Teague's and Rundle's 44,000,000 (Teague from memory in 1948, Rundle from Teague/valley memory; the ledger's typical year, 57,760 ÷ 0.0361 = 1,600,000, × 25 = 40,000,000); Vaught's 118,000 (transposition); the petition's 67,000 lb (Frick — the patrons' own 1925 reckoning; Oram's "67,000" is that figure misremembered as the 1958 result).

**C1(d). $19,000; the patrons received one-fifth of it.** 76,000 lb × $0.25 = $19,000. Butterfat "one year with another" averaged 25¢ (Effie and Kyle from the annual sheets, Roy, Ivy, Nym; Sill's and Kepp's 27¢ are round memories; 24¢ was 1910 alone). Award $3,800.00 (the award text, quoted verbatim by Effie and Nym; Frick, Vose, Teague, Falke, Kyle) ÷ $19,000 = 0.20 = one-fifth. (At 27¢ it would be $20,520 and the award 18.5%.) Vira's and Oram's $3,500 are their fathers' memories against a quoted document.

**C2. Emmet Brant, 1910: 200,000 lb "even," paid on 3.42.** (a) Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = 6,840 lb. (b) True test 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60; true fat 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb; owed and not credited 7,200 − 6,840 = 360 lb (check: Ivy's "3.42 short 0.18" → 0.0018 × 200,000 = 360). (c) Butterfat was 24¢ that year (Roy): 360 × $0.24 = $86.40. (At the 25¢ long-run average it would be $90.00; the question says "that year," so $86.40.)

**C3.** (a) Credited to the station's patrons over 1898–1922: 1,444,000 lb. (b) Received at Larrow Green in the station's cream: 1,520,000 lb — the ledger's typical entry (57,760 credited / 60,800 received / gain 3,040) is exactly the average year, and × 25 gives these totals. (c) The difference, 76,000 lb, is **5.00% of the fat received/delivered** (76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = 0.0500 — the same 1/20 as the glass) and **5.26% ≈ "five and a quarter per cent" of the fat credited** (76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 0.05263; 1/19). The ledger's "five and a quarter" is therefore a percentage of the credited figure. Teague ("of the fat that was actually in the cream") and Vaught §V ("of the butterfat actually delivered") name the wrong denominator: 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.0%, not 5¼% — arithmetic settles it; Ivy and Nym have it right.

**C4.** (a) **$77.93.** The invoice is reproduced verbatim by two independent holders — Nym from the Association's office file, Junia Ammon in facsimile from the firm's letter book — and the lines sum to it: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93. The "$77.39" of Junia's own prose and of the Chronicle is a transposition. (b) **Six dozen = 72 pipettes** (72 × $0.35 = $25.20, as the invoice line says; the "five dozen" of Vaught's body text and of Oram fails that arithmetic). Each was short by **0.88 c.c.** on the mean (17.6 − 16.72); the nine survivors individually ranged 16.70–16.74, i.e., short 0.86–0.90 c.c. Strictly only the nine measured pipettes are proven short; that the whole lot was follows from the uniform 3.61 average over 25 years and the 1911 episode.

**C5(a).** The faulty glass was in use from **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 — 26 years, 1 month and 12 days** (about 26 years and six weeks), less the nine weeks of 8 May–10 July 1911 on a borrowed measure. The money calculation runs over **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922.** They differ because 1897 and 1923 were part years — the glass arrived in May 1897 ("1897, part year" on the averages leaf) and was condemned in June 1923 with true glass in use from July — and the ledger's figures (1,600,000 lb, the station-gain line) are whole-year figures, so Ivy counted only complete years on the faulty glass. The result is a rounded, slightly conservative figure: the part years 1897 and 1923 add uncounted loss, while the nine weeks of 1911 (about 9/52 of one year's 3,040 lb, roughly 500 lb) are inside the 25 years and were not deducted.

**C5(b). Cannot be determined from the sources.** Nym, from the ledgers: no 1896 intake figure exists anywhere; the station column begins with 1897. Kyle explains why: the first season was carried on the creamery's own books and those sheets were never put with the station papers — "not in any book I ever ruled." Two memories conflict: Sill (1934) "about nine hundred thousand pounds, May to the end of the year"; Kyle (1953) "a good eleven hundred thousand," from "men who were there." Both are round hearsay; no document exists; nothing breaks the tie. What is established: a part season from 4 May to the end of 1896, of the order of a million pounds (a full year later ran 1,600,000 and May–December contains the flush).

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran from 8 May 1911, when the station's pipette broke ("A. home cross about a broken measure" — Orra, the same evening), to 10 July 1911, when "our own measure [was] in use again" — nine weeks (Keddie's dated entries; Teague's and Kepp's "six weeks" are wrong). The variable: the pipette alone — a borrowed measure (from Larrow Green per Ivy, or Strawn's own per Roy; unsettled, and immaterial) replaced the station's. Held constant: the tester, his method and hour, the scales, the acid, the bottles, the hand tester, the herds, the land, the feed, the water supply, the supervision (none), and the patrons. Result: every Hessel Bottom farm rose from about 3.61 to 3.80, held there nine weeks, and fell back to 3.61 the day the station's measure returned — a ratio of 0.95, recorded contemporaneously by the tester (who did not know what he was recording and wrote "?") and independently by Emmet Brant. It establishes what a calculation cannot: that the whole difference lived in the measure at that station in that period — cause, not merely sufficiency — and that the fault was present already in 1911, twelve years before the condemnation; it disposes at once of hay, water, cows and men, because none of them changed. It does not establish which of the two measures was right (only that they differed by 5% — the burette settled that in 1923 and 1958), that the fault reached back to 1897 or forward to 1923 (the averages leaf and the 1924 rise do that), that the pipette broken in May was the same piece used in every other year (Keddie had six dozen), the exact magnitude beyond a nine-week average, or why the glass was short.

**D2.** Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream:
(1) *The receiving end weighed the fat that arrived.* Every year the ledger's station-gain line showed more butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream than was credited on the station's test — 5¼% of credited, "as usual" (Teague, Kyle, Rundle) — and buttermaker Sill, reweighing the Hollow's cream himself in the cold room, found it "Always more. Never less." Stolen cream reaches the Green light; this reached it heavy (Rundle's retraction). Source: the office ledgers and the creamery receiving book, 1897 onward.
(2) *The nine weeks of 1911.* The same man, alone, unsupervised, at the same separator, produced tests 5% higher the moment the pipette changed and 5% lower the day it came back, recorded in his own book and in the book of the patron he was supposed to be robbing. A thief does not start and stop with a piece of glass. Source: the test book and the Brant weigh book.
(3) *The 1924 season.* After Strawn condemned the glass on 12 June 1923 and new glass came in July, the station average rose to 3.80 while Keddie was still alone at the station and tested the whole of 1924 "with the same pen and the same measure of care" — before the petition, the hearing and his dismissal. Source: Strawn's circuit book (June and October 1923, 1924), the averages leaf, Orra's diary (July 1923).
Supporting: the shortfall was exactly proportional to each herd's richness (4.56 short 0.24, 3.42 short 0.18) and identical in flush and dry hay — an instrument's signature, not a man's opportunity; and the state inspector and Frick's board watched the test done and found nothing to remark.
They are independent because they rest on different records in different buildings, in different hands, in different years: the accuser's own ledger at the office and the buttermaker's book at the Green; the tester's and the largest patron's books at the Hollow in 1911; the state inspector's book and the station's figures of 1923–24. None uses the 1958 measurement; all three existed before the board sat in 1925, and (2) and (3) were before it as Exhibits 1, 3 and 4. Any one of them would still hold if the crate had never been opened.
Did the bookkeeper ever question the entries that refute him? No. Teague invented the line (1905), called it "furniture," said its evenness "stops him looking," raised the station account once with Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone ("I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience"), advanced the theft theory before the board in 1925 with the refutation in his own hand, and in his 1948 memoir still did not connect the two. Kyle: "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years." The inference was drawn by Rundle in 1939 and Ivy in 1958, never by Teague.

**D3.** Yes — Selby Vose knew from the start that the station's tests ran low; that was never a secret. Complaints had been made at annual meetings since 1902; the 1908 committee reported to "the President and Directors" that Hollow milk "is returned by the test at a figure below" the rest of the valley; Teague put the station account to him in 1916; the question was reopened in 1919 and his own circular of that year presupposes the low figures and offers water as the cause; Strawn told him in 1919 that no water was ever found; and his 1926 address speaks of "excuses over a generation" — wet hay, tester, office, weather. What he did not know, or would not let himself learn, was that the low tests were the station's own error: he signed the station-gain sheet every spring but "read the totals and never the lines above them" (Kyle), and his answer to Teague in 1916 — "let it alone" — is the answer of a man who preferred not to look. There is no evidence he knew the cause before 12 June 1923, and after it he called the condemnation "a piece of housekeeping" and credited the 1924 rise to water stopping. So: knowledge of the low tests, certain from 1913; knowledge that they were wrong, none shown — the sources support incuriosity, not concealment.

## Section F — Theory

**The theory.** The six dozen pipettes Ammon & Sons shipped for the Ostrey Hollow outfit in April 1897 were blown short — a reground mold or a knocked gauge, as Junia Ammon supposes — and short alike: the nine survivors deliver 16.70–16.74 c.c. against a 17.6 mark, mean 16.72, 0.95 of the mark. The butterfat test charges a fixed volume of milk into a bottle whose graduated neck is ruled for 17.6 c.c.; the fat that rises is read directly as per cent. A charge 5% short puts 5% less fat in the neck, so every reading is 95% of the truth — proportional, not additive: 3.80 reads 3.61, 3.60 reads 3.42, 4.80 reads 4.56, and the station's herds rank correctly against one another while the whole station is wrong against the world. That is the size (0.19 on a 3.80 average) and why that size (16.72 ÷ 17.6). It began on 1 May 1897, the day the station's own glass replaced Sill's borrowed, correct outfit (1896 on borrowed glass: 3.80; 1898 on its own: 3.61), and ended in July 1923, when the new glass ordered on 28 June after Strawn's burette condemned the lot on 12 June went into use (1924: 3.80). Nothing at the station or on the farms had to change because nothing there was wrong: the scales were true, the acid right, the crank counted, the bottles good, the cows ordinary, the milk whole; the pipette is "the one instrument in the butterfat test which the plant cannot check against anything else" — it is the standard — and can be caught only by a burette from outside, which reached the state's field men in 1921. Glass does not vary with season, feed or weather, so the gap was identical in February and July, wet years and dry. The missing fat was never missing: it went down the road in the cream and was booked at Larrow Green, year after year, as "station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual," and carried to the Association's general account. The 1911 nine weeks, when a borrowed pipette put every farm at 3.80 and the station's own put them back at 3.61 the day it returned, is the theory run as an experiment by accident, twelve years before anyone condemned the glass.

**Three competing explanations and what defeats them.**
(1) *Added water* — Selby Vose (1919 circular; the board, 1925; annual address, 1926), echoed by Sill (1934). Defeated by: Strawn's lactometer on every visit 1907–1936, "no added water, as always here," and his telling Vose so in 1919; Rosalie Cudd's vat, which "knows watered milk inside a day" and never showed one in sixteen summers; the ledger — watered milk yields thin cream and a station *loss* at the Green, not a 5¼% gain every year; the 1911 interval, since water does not stop and start with a pipette; the exact proportionality of the shortfall to each herd's richness; and Bulletin 471's finding.
(2) *Cream stolen by the tester, low tests written to cover it* — Wilbur Teague (before the board, 1925; memoir, 1948); Oram's father; Vira; the valley's "settled opinion" in Rundle (1939, retracted in press). Defeated by: the station-gain line and Sill's reweighing — the cream "reached the Green heavy, every year"; the 1911 interval, the same man alone producing 3.80 with another man's glass; the 1924 rise to 3.80 with Keddie still alone and untouched for a full season before the petition; and the board's and Strawn's watching of the test.
(3) *Wet hay of Hessel Bottom* — the 1908 committee under Nyle Grigg, Grigg's 1919 letter, Dr. Kepp (1926), and Falke provisionally (1959). Defeated by: Bulletin 214 itself — not more than 0.05 in winter, nothing in summer — four times too small and the wrong shape; the gap's constancy in February and July (Keddie, Roy's book, Hazel); the same four herds at 3.78–3.82 over the ridge every summer for sixteen years; and the 1911 interval, in which the herds, the hay and the ground did not change and the tests did.

**Did the board examine the condemned glassware?** No. Effie, who took down every word: the crate "was never mentioned in any of the three sessions by patrons, officers, or board"; "books and appliances" was a form of words. Yet Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook was Exhibit 4 (Frick's list; Kyle carried it in), so the fact of the condemnation was before the board, and two of its three sessions sat in the weigh room with the crate in the loft some twenty feet overhead (Oram, Duncan). Frick "saw the scales tried ... the sampling done and the test made" — on the new glass. No one "laid the second beside the fourth and looked" (Kyle). The board had the record of the condemnation and never asked for, saw, or thought of the condemned glass.

**What the tester believed.** In writing, nothing he could not prove: "the figures are what I read and what I wrote and nothing besides ... I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are"; against the 1911 question mark, "I will not put anything in its place that I do not know." Privately, according to his son, he came home in the summer of 1911 and said twice that "it was something in the glass," and "never dared put it in the book" — a suspicion with nothing to hold in his hand against the outfit he had been taught to trust. Frick's 1946 recollection of a corridor remark — that Keddie "had always thought the patrons were at fault" and "suspected" someone — points the other way; it is a lawyer's twenty-one-year-old memory of an unrecorded aside, against a son's memory of the supper table at seventeen, and the two cannot both stand. On balance: he suspected the glass from 1911, would not say so without proof, and went to his grave accusing nobody — "the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had."

## Section G — Summary

From 1897 to 1923 the Larrow Valley Association's Ostrey Hollow station tested nine families' milk with pipettes blown five per cent short. Every test read one part in twenty low: 3.61 for milk that was 3.80 — 76,000 lb of butterfat, $19,000 over twenty-five years — while the missing fat arrived at the creamery and was booked each year as "station gain, as usual." The valley blamed wet hay (1908), watered cans (1919) and the tester (1925); an arbitration paid $3,800 without a finding, and Ansel Keddie was dismissed and died under suspicion in 1934. The proof was scattered in eight places that never met: his test book with its nine unexplained weeks of 1911, his brother-in-law's weigh book, the office ledgers, the buttermaker's scales, the cheese factory over the ridge, the inspector's 1923 condemnation, the supplier's invoice, and the crate in the loft — until his granddaughter measured nine pipettes in 1958.
