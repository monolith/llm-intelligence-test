## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1 — Article VII: the rule that turned a figure into money (1889–1938)

In 1889 the farmers of the Larrow Valley, tired of hauling butter to the Ashlin merchants "and taking whatever the merchant chose to allow them," met in the schoolhouse at Larrow Green in cold weather and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (Effie Loomis r02, Rundle r12, Falke r22). Gideon Vose — heavy, quiet, obstinate, not a lawyer though he had once thought of the law — was elected president and served 1889–1911, the longest term the Association ever had; he drafted the by-laws himself at his kitchen table. The main creamery was built on Larrow Creek below the mill at Larrow Green in 1891 and the valley was paid in cash once a month.

Article VII — "Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" (quoted identically by Effie, Nym Item 1 and Rundle) — made the butterfat test, not the pound of milk, the measure of a man's month. Its last clause allowed no appeal from the station's figure, which Effie, Rundle, Frick and Falke all call the hinge of the whole affair: "it makes the test the whole of the bargain, and it leaves a dissatisfied man with no remedy on earth except to quarrel with a figure" (Frick). The article's date cannot be fixed: Effie says adopted at the 1892 annual meeting, Rundle 1891, Grigg 1893; the printed pamphlet carries only "adopted at the annual meeting," and the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move.

The Association was three things (Teague r05): the creamery at Larrow Green (buttermaker Hobart Sill, 1893–1934, keeping the churn and cream-receiving books), the office (bookkeeper Wilbur Teague 1905–1945 with the ledgers; secretary Effie Loomis 1901–1940 with the minute book; clerk Bertram Kyle at the outer desk twenty-five years, ruling the fair copies), and the Ostrey Hollow station. Selby Vose was manager 1913–1938 — his own "thirteen years this spring" in 1926 and Strawn's 1913 note of a new manager outrank Kyle's "1911." He was Gideon's son (his own printed words, Effie, Duncan Keddie, Kyle); the "nephew" of Vira and Rundle is valley talk. Where the office stood is not settled: Teague, Frick, Nym and Duncan put it at Ashlin, one room over a harness shop, where the 1925 first session sat and the test book lay 1925–1959; Kyle and Vira put the manager's door at Larrow Green; the two may describe different rooms. Gideon died in 1917. None of the four officers, Effie says, ever stood in the station on a working morning.

Documents: Article VII; the by-laws pamphlet (Nym Item 1); Effie's "Notes toward a history" (1941); Rundle's jubilee pamphlet (1939); Kyle (1953); Teague's Chapter Nine (1948).

### Story 2 — Ostrey Hollow opens on borrowed glass, and the short lot arrives (1895–1898)

Seven miles up the valley, on the flat by the creek where the road bends toward the ridge, the Association built a skimming station in the winter of 1895–96 (Falke) to serve Hessel Bottom — a 160-acre wet meadow with six farms holding rights (Roy, Hazel, the 1908 report, Kepp; Effie's "seven" is the odd one out) — and three hill places (Strawn): nine families in all. Ansel Keddie, born 1861 (Duncan; Orra's "a carpenter at sixty-six" in 1927 agrees), went up in 1895 to get it ready and was tester and separator man alone from the opening on 4 May 1896 through the 1924 season — twenty-nine seasons. Milk was weighed at the intake, sampled into numbered ten-day composite jars, charged by pipette into a patron-numbered bottle, tested in a four-bottle hand tester, and separated; the cream went down daily by wagon (Cleve Oram's father drove it) and the skim went home for hogs and calves.

The station had no glass of its own at the opening. Keddie's first leaf reads "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes"; Hobart Sill says the borrowed outfit was his own personal glass, made up at his bench at Larrow Green and sent up on the wagon. Keddie's later "borrowed off the Ashlin creamery" is contradicted by his own first leaf and by Sill and is rejected. On that glass the 1896 average was 3.80. The first season's intake is recorded nowhere (Nym: the station column begins 1897); Sill's "about nine hundred thousand pounds" and Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand" are both hearsay and cannot be reconciled.

On 14 April 1897 Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, Tarnet, invoiced the outfit: 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 = 25.20; 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at .22 = 31.68; acid measure .90; 4-bottle hand tester 18.00; freight 2.15; total $77.93 (reproduced verbatim by Nym Item 2 and in facsimile by Junia Ammon; the "$77.39" of Junia's prose and of the Chronicle is a transposition, and the lines sum to 77.93). Seventy-two pipettes, not the "five dozen" of Vaught's body text and Oram (72 × .35 = 25.20). The outfit went into use on 1 May 1897 — Orra's diary entry of that date, the office file, Sill's "the spring after," Junia, and Bulletin 471's conclusion; Vaught's §III and Kyle's "1 May 1898" are wrong. Sill's glass went back with one piece chipped. From 1898 the station average was 3.61, and 3.61 within three hundredths every year after (averages leaf). Junia Ammon (1949), whose works records burned in 1931, believes a reground mold made the lot run consistently short and that sample checks cannot catch a consistent error; her claim that the house sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900 is refuted by her own invoice.

Documents: first leaf and averages leaf of the test book; the invoice; Orra's diary (1 May 1897); Sill (1934); Junia Ammon (1949); Keddie's unsent letter.

### Story 3 — The hay theory and the ridge that refuted it (1902–1930)

Talk began at the Larrow Green store in March 1902 that Hollow milk "did not test with the rest" (Orra's diary; Keddie; Roy — Emmet Brant among the first to say it aloud, and laughed at; Sill). In January 1908 the board named a committee of three under Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green, a non-patron who hauled to the Green. They came up in Grigg's sleigh, went to Emmet Brant's first, and "wanted to see the barns and not the station" (Orra). Their report (printed in full by Grigg in 1919) says they sampled the Hessel Bottom herds through January, February and March, visited each of six farms three and four times, tried the scales, stood by the test, found "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter" — "the milk is whole and the scales are honest" — and blamed the wet sedge hay of the Bottom: "the remedy lies with the land and not with the station." Grigg's 1919 covering letter claims sampling "through the whole of 1908 — every month"; his own report contradicts him and governs. The question "was closed by it for eleven years."

The College's Bulletin No. 214 (1911), a feeding trial, found bog hay "depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture" (quoted verbatim by Ivy, Nym, Vaught and Falke). Grigg (1919) and Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S., of Ashlin (Farm Club paper, 1926) both cite it as "two tenths in the winter feeding," four times the bulletin's ceiling; the verbatim text breaks the tie. Kepp, who had "never in my life read a test," put the gap at 0.21, the county's wet bottoms at "a fifth of a point" below the hills, and waved off the 1911 borrowed-measure interval as "six weeks of June and July." Falke (1959) first allowed the hay as a partial factor, then withdrew it on reading Bulletin 214.

Three facts defeat the hay. The gap was the same in February as in July, "flat as a floor" (Keddie, Roy's two-column book, Hazel) — not seasonal, as a feed effect must be. From 1915 to 1930 four of the six Hessel Bottom men hauled over Redlow Ridge every summer flush (about 1 June to mid-September; the ridge is shut November to April) to Jerome Cudd's Alder Corners Cheese Company in Vessey County — built 1913 per his daughter Rosalie (Roy and Rundle say opened 1914). Cudd tested on his own new glass and never compared figures with anyone. His 1919 entry gives the four men 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82 — "Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge" — and sixteen season averages run 3.78–3.82, mean 3.80, wet years and dry (Rosalie's table; sixteen seasons per Rosalie, Roy and Hazel, not Ivy's "fourteen"). And the true effect, 0.19, is four times Bulletin 214's ceiling "and the wrong shape" (Ivy).

Documents: the 1908 report; Grigg's 1919 letter; Bulletin 214; Kepp's paper; Cudd's test book and Rosalie's 1958 letter; Orra's diary (January and March 1908).

### Story 4 — Two columns that agreed: the Brant weigh book and the two families (1854–1963)

Emmet Brant (1854–1929) was the biggest man on Hessel Bottom — sixty-odd acres of the meadow, thirty-eight cows in a good year, the station's largest patron every recorded year. From 1 January 1897 (Frick's exhibit list, Nym's deposit record, Duncan Keddie; Roy's "1898" and Hazel's "1900" are memory — Hazel warns "years I have not") he weighed every can on his own barn scale before it went on the wagon and wrote that figure beside the station's returned weight, at the kitchen table after the second milking, hat still on, in a plain hand-ruled ledger with the front cover gone and the back held by a strip of harness leather. The two columns agreed for thirty years, "barring a pound or two ... when a can got slopped on the hill" — whatever was wrong at the station, "it was not the weighing" (Roy). The leaf for 1910: "Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42" — butterfat at 24¢ that year. Emmet also entered the 1911 rise and fall. He was among the first to complain in 1902, read the 1908 report twice and put it in the drawer ("the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not"), did not haul to Alder Corners because "the biggest herd is the one that cannot afford to be talked about," and his name was on the January 1925 petition (Orra).

The book passed to Roy (born c. 1888) in 1929, who kept it up in the same book and then another. Roy lent it — "lent, and I want the word" (Duncan; Hazel) — to Ivy Keddie in 1958 and had it back; at Roy's death in 1962 it went to Hazel, who deposited it at the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1963 (Nym Item 4, whose label "kept by A. Keddie" is swapped with the test book's).

The families: Ansel Keddie married Orra Brant, Emmet's sister, in 1889 — so the tester was brother-in-law to the patron who lost the most, and the two households ate Sunday dinner together for thirty years. Their son Duncan (born 1894; to the Ashlin school as a teacher in October 1911, aged seventeen) and Emmet's son Roy are first cousins; Duncan's daughter Ivy (born 1931) and Roy's daughter Hazel are second cousins (Roy, Duncan, Hazel, Rundle's 1959 footnote) — not "first cousin once removed" as Vira and Oram have it. Roy calls Orra Emmet's full sister and names the stepmother story a rumor; Hazel calls her a half-sister by a second wife; the sources leave that open. Orra was born 1866 (her own "sixty-four" in August 1930; Duncan's "eighty-two when she died in 1948"; Duncan's "1868" contradicts himself).

Documents: the weigh book and its 1910 leaf; Roy (1955); Hazel (1981); Duncan (1968); Orra's diary; Nym Item 4; Frick's Exhibit 3.

### Story 5 — The water letter and the silence (1919–1934)

In 1919 "men who were boys" reopened the question (Grigg). The Association answered with a printed circular over manager Selby Vose's signature, typed by clerk Bertram Kyle: a civil warning to the Ostrey Hollow patrons that added water would be looked for — "this Association pays for butterfat and cannot pay for creek water." Vose (1926) says it named nobody; every family that received it read it as saying there was water in the cans (Vira, Keddie, Roy, Sill, Rundle, Hazel). It went to nine families (Keddie, Vira, Roy, Sill, Hazel); Orra's diary says "eleven," the sole outlier, rejected against the man who carried them and the families that got them. It rained that day. Keddie would not mail the letters or leave them on the platform; he carried them round himself, stood at each door while the man read, and came home at eleven at night with his hat on (Orra, June 1919). Vira's father read his twice with his hat on and kept it in the desk drawer until he died; Hazel's mother put hers in the stove; one woman pinned hers inside her trunk lid. Two days later Emmet Brant stood in the Keddie yard — he did not come in — said he had hauled whole milk since the day the station opened and Ansel knew it, and asked why Ansel had carried the paper himself if he did not believe it; Ansel said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet never entered the house again; Orra walked to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane. Roy's version — his father held that the man who kept the test book "ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not" — is the same quarrel from the other kitchen; Vira's "somebody's cousin saying it" is talk. The two ends of the valley "did not speak civilly for six years" (Rundle).

The water charge had nothing behind it. Inspector Merle Strawn tested with the lactometer on every visit from 1907, never found added water, and told the manager so in 1919; Rosalie Cudd's vat — "Cheese is a witness" — never showed a watered can in sixteen summers; Bulletin 471 concluded "No milk was watered." Vose nonetheless defended the circular in 1926 ("would write every word of it again") and read the 1924 rise as proof the water stopped once the Association looked; Sill thought well of him for it — "nine families all got honest in one summer." Emmet died in spring 1929, "ten years and neither of them would go up the road first"; Ansel in February 1934, when men who had not spoken to him in nine years shook Orra's hand and praised his hand with a door.

Documents: the circular (never transcribed; described by seven narrators); Strawn's 1919 entry; Vose's 1926 address; Orra's diary (June 1919, 1929, 1934); Rosalie's letter; Roy (1955).

### Story 6 — Written down and never read: the ledger line, the nine weeks, and the condemned crate (1897–1924)

Three contemporaneous records held the answer. First, the ledgers. Every year the Ostrey Hollow station account carried, in the bookkeeper's hand: "butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account" (Teague's "entry for 1898"; Nym Item 6; Rundle 1939; Kyle copied it yearly). Teague says the line was his own device from 1905 yet quotes an 1898 entry in that form — unresolved. Hobart Sill reweighed the Hollow's cream himself in the cold room: it "ran heavy. Every year ... Always more. Never less," and he said so a hundred times. Vose signed the sheet every spring; Teague put the station account to him in 1916 and was told to let it alone. "A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture" (Teague); "It was in plain sight with a name on it" (Kyle). Rundle, seeing the ledgers in 1939 on another errand, drew the inference in press: stolen cream "would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy."

Second, the nine weeks. On 8 May 1911 the station's pipette broke — "A. home cross about a broken measure" (Orra, that day) — and a borrowed measure served until 10 July (Keddie's dated entries; Ivy, Roy, Vaught, Falke; Teague's and Kepp's "six weeks" fail against the dates). Its source is unsettled: from Larrow Green with the cream wagon (Ivy) or left by Strawn (Roy) — Strawn's own June 1911 entry says only "Nothing here to note." Every Bottom farm tested 3.80; the day the station's own measure returned the tests fell to 3.61 and Keddie wrote "? — A.K.", which stood fourteen years. Emmet Brant entered both movements. Keddie came home that summer saying "it was something in the glass," twice, but would not write what he could not prove (Duncan).

Third, the burette. The state issued burettes to field men in 1921. On 12 June 1923 Strawn ran the station's pipettes against his: "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new" (his circuit book; Nym's photostat). Vose's and Rundle's "12 July," Kyle's "'22" and Hazel's "'21" fall against the order of 28 June, Ammon & Sons' reply of 2 July, and new glass in use in July 1923 ("new glass at the station" — Orra). The crate went up the ladder into the loft over the intake room under a tarpaulin. In October Strawn noted the new pipettes delivered their mark; the 1924 average was 3.80 (averages leaf, Strawn; Vose's 3.90 and Falke's 3.85 are wrong). Nobody — not Strawn, who "did not weigh what it has cost them," not the office, which treated it "as a broken window" — connected the new glass with the old figures.

Documents: the ledger entry; Sill's receiving book; test-book entries of 8 May and 10 July 1911; Strawn's 12 June 1923 entry; the 28 June order and 2 July letter; Orra's diary.

### Story 7 — The arbitration of 1925 and what it cost the tester (1925–1934)

In January 1925 the Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned the Association — Emmet Brant's name on it (Orra) — to be made whole. The petition set the loss "to the dollar" at $19,600 (Rundle) or at 67,000 lb of butterfat (Frick); the two do not reconcile at any price and the petition is not transcribed, so its figure stays open. Under the by-laws a board of three sat, chaired by Alonzo Frick, attorney of Ashlin, "the one of the three who kept no cow." The first session was 17 March 1925 at the Association's office at Ashlin — Keddie went down in his good coat, up since three (Orra) — and, at Frick's request, the second and third in the station's own weigh room (the intake room, with the scale), where the board saw the scales tried and the test made while a dozen men, Cleve Oram among them, stood on the platform in the rain (Frick, Oram, Orra, Duncan, Kyle; Vira's and Falke's Ashlin-only account is incomplete). Keddie's nine-sentence statement ended: "I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself." Four exhibits, listed by Frick and carried in by Kyle: the test book, 4 May 1896–31 December 1924; the station receiving book; Emmet Brant's weigh book from 1 January 1897; Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook — so the condemnation was before the board, and no one "laid the second beside the fourth and looked." Effie: the crate itself was never mentioned. Three explanations were argued: water (Vose, standing by his circular, "without heat and without proof"); cream theft by the tester (Teague, "in the way an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it"); wet hay (the 1908 report read into the record). None was accepted.

On 30 April 1925, four days before the award, the Association dismissed Keddie — "Twenty-nine seasons and a letter" (Orra); he came home at his ordinary hour, hung his apron behind the shed door, and ate ham. On 4 May the board "finds no cause established" and awarded $3,800.00 "without admission, to be charged to the station account" (award text; Effie, Nym Item 7, Frick, Vose, Teague, Falke, Kyle; Vira's and Oram's "$3,500" are their fathers' memories) — one-fifth of the loss. Frick believed Keddie then and after: "an honest man was ruined by a figure nobody could explain." Keddie kept a few cows and turned carpenter — hung a barn door for the Orams that swung true thirty years, mended half the doors in the hollow "at a quarter of what the other paid," and whistled. He died at home in February 1934; the funeral was well attended and nobody said the other thing.

Documents: the award; Keddie's statement and unsent letter; Frick's exhibit list; Effie §6–8; Orra's diary (January–June 1925, 1934); Kyle; Oram.

### Story 8 — What the crate held (1958–1972)

Ivy Keddie — Ansel's granddaughter, Duncan's daughter, born 1931, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland — climbed the ladder into the station loft in February 1958 and found the Ammon & Sons crate under a tarpaulin (Ivy, Duncan, Hazel, Oram, Chronicle, Falke; Nym's "found in the Association office at Ashlin" stands alone and is rejected). Nine pipettes were unbroken (Ivy's sheet, Nym, Vaught, Falke, Oram; Rundle's and the Chronicle's "eleven" fall to the sheet, which lists nine values). She carried them east and on 9 March 1958 measured them against a calibrated burette: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 — mean 16.72 c.c. against a 17.6 mark, short 0.88, "95 hundredths of the mark." Every test made on that glass read one part in twenty low: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80. Over the 25 full delivery years 1898–1922, 40,000,000 lb of milk (Effie, Kyle, Nym, Vaught; the 44,000,000 of Teague and Rundle rejected) credited 1,444,000 lb of fat at 3.61 where 1,520,000 was delivered at 3.80 — 76,000 lb short, $19,000 at the 25¢ average, five times the award.

She brought the five record sets into one room: the test book; the Brant book, lent by Roy; Rosalie Cudd's copied Alder Corners figures ("Your letter came Tuesday"); Strawn's 1923 notebook, found in the Farland library where the state dairy office had sent the set in 1947; and the Association's ledgers. She visited Vira Toland. Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958), Professor Leland Vaught with Ivy, closed: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain." Vaught's body text (16.27 c.c., 0.9244, 3.905, 118,000 lb, "five dozen," 1 May 1898) is a transposition of 16.72 carried through the arithmetic, which he himself calls "not readily reconciled"; the Ashlin Chronicle (3 October 1958) copied 16.27, "eleven," "$19,600" and "$77.39," called Ivy his great-niece, and accused Ammon & Sons of knowing; county agent Orin Falke (1959) repeated "great-niece" and corrected it. Ivy read "What the Crate Held" at Ashlin in 1959: "It depends on nine pieces of glass and a burette." The test book and crate went to the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1959, the weigh book in 1963, Cudd's book to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970; Halbert Nym catalogued sixteen items in 1972. Ansel had been dead twenty-four years; Orra died in 1948.

Documents: Ivy's measurement sheet; Bulletin 471; the Chronicle; Falke's report; Rosalie's letter; Nym's accession notes; Duncan's list of the books.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** Derivation: Ansel and Orra's son was Duncan Keddie (Roy r09: "Ansel and Orra's boy"; Orra's diary, 1899: "Duncan five years old"; Duncan r18: "My father was Ansel Keddie"); Duncan's daughter is Ivy (Duncan: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter"; Roy: "Duncan's girl Ivy"; Ivy herself: "my grandfather"). Pointing elsewhere: the Ashlin Chronicle (1958) calls her "his great-niece," and Falke's county report (1959) repeats it — but Falke wrote from the newspaper notice and the same press release, so that is one source, not two, and Falke "verified" and corrected it to granddaughter on the same page. Duncan says he had "twice seen it printed otherwise." Resolution: the family (father, cousin, herself) and the corrected official report outrank a newspaper feature written "in a day and a half."

**B2. Ivy and Hazel Brant were second cousins.** Derivation: Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie were brother and sister (Roy, Duncan, Hazel, Vira, Keddie); Emmet's son Roy and Orra's son Duncan were therefore first cousins (Roy, Duncan); Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy, children of first cousins, are second cousins (Roy, Duncan, Hazel, Rundle's 1959 footnote). Vira and Oram say "first cousin once removed," which would require Ivy and Roy, or Hazel and Duncan, to be in the same generation — they are not; both are valley hearsay and Hazel says "people are forever telling me she is my niece or my cousin once removed." Left open: whether Emmet and Orra were full siblings (Roy: "full brother and sister, same mother and same father," dismissing a stepmother story) or half-siblings "by his father's second wife" (Hazel); Duncan says only "brother and sister ... the younger of the two." If Hazel is right they are half second cousins; the degree does not change. Also open: Orra's birth year — 1866 by her own diary and by Duncan's "eighty-two in 1948," 1868 by Duncan's stated figure.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose**, the Association's founder, first president (1889–1911) and author of the by-laws. Sources: Selby's own printed 1926 address ("My father, who was your first president"), Effie ("son of the late president"), Duncan ("his son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew"), Kyle ("the son, whatever is said at Ashlin"). Against: Vira and Rundle say nephew — Rundle's pamphlet is "what the valley remembers, tidied," and Vira is retailing valley talk; a man's own printed statement of who his father was outranks both. Why it matters: Gideon wrote Article VII, whose last clause made the station's test govern without appeal; his son sat as manager for the whole of the dispute's active years (1913–1938), signed the station-gain sheet every spring, told Teague in 1916 to let the station account alone, sent the 1919 water circular, put the water theory before the board in 1925, and in 1926 defended both the by-law and the circular. "The family always thought the valley belonged to them a little" (Vira): the rule and its enforcement stayed in one family, and the man who might have questioned the station's figure had every reason to stand by his father's clause.

**B4. Vose–Keddie: no established relationship; the best-placed source denies one.** Two narrators assert kinship: Vira ("old Mrs. Vose" was Orra's first cousin, "everybody knew, nobody wrote down" — "it was somebody's cousin saying it") and Oram ("cousins of some sort ... I could not draw you the line on paper," from his mother's mother). Duncan Keddie, Orra's son, flatly denies it: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses"; people confuse them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Gideon either; his mother, who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection," never put a Vose in it. The two affirmers are hearsay of hearsay and cannot draw the line; the denier is the son of the woman in question. Resolution: no relationship is established, and the weight of evidence is that there was none; the sources cannot prove a negative, but they give no reason to believe the rumor.

## Section C — Math

**C1(a). Proportion: the tests read 0.95 of the truth — one part in twenty (5%) low.** From the glassware: Ivy's sheet lists nine pipettes delivering 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 c.c. (sum 150.48 ÷ 9 = 16.72) against a stem mark of 17.6 c.c. 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly. The test bottle's neck is ruled for a 17.6 c.c. charge; a charge 5% short puts 5% less fat in the neck, and the per-cent reading falls in the same proportion. Rejected: Vaught's body text and the Chronicle give 16.27 c.c. → 0.9244; the nine listed values cannot average 16.27, Nym's Item 5 says 16.72, and Vaught's own conclusion says "one part in twenty" — 16.27 is a digit transposition (the Chronicle copied the bulletin, so it is not an independent source). Independent corroboration: 3.61 ÷ 3.80 = 0.95 in the 1911 nine weeks and in the 1924 rise, and Alder Corners at 3.80.

**C1(b). True average 3.80; recorded tests short by 0.19 points.** 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80 (check: 3.80 × 0.95 = 3.61). Rejected: Kepp's and Vose's 0.21 (3.61 + 0.21 = 3.82, not Vose's 3.90); Sill's "really a 3.85" and Falke's 3.85 (memory / unexplained); Vaught's 3.905 and 0.295 (from the transposed 16.27). Roy's "seventeen hundredths" is not wrong so much as a different comparison — Larrow Green's own patrons ran 3.78 (Sill, Falke), and 3.78 − 3.61 = 0.17.

**C1(c). 76,000 lb of butterfat.** Full delivery years on the faulty glass: 1898–1922, 25 years, 40,000,000 lb of milk (1,600,000 lb/yr — Effie from the annual sheets, added twice; Kyle, who got the figure out for the arbitration; Nym; Ivy; Vaught). Credited at 3.61%: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb. Delivered at 3.80%: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb. Difference 76,000 lb (= 0.0019 × 40,000,000). Rejected: Teague's and Rundle's 44,000,000 (Teague from memory in 1948, Rundle from Teague/valley memory; the ledger's typical year, 57,760 ÷ 0.0361 = 1,600,000, × 25 = 40,000,000); Vaught's 118,000 (transposition); the petition's 67,000 lb (Frick — the patrons' own 1925 reckoning; Oram's "67,000" is that figure misremembered as the 1958 result).

**C1(d). $19,000; the patrons received one-fifth of it.** 76,000 lb × $0.25 = $19,000. Butterfat "one year with another" averaged 25¢ (Effie and Kyle from the annual sheets, Roy, Ivy, Nym; Sill's and Kepp's 27¢ are round memories; 24¢ was 1910 alone). Award $3,800.00 (the award text, quoted verbatim by Effie and Nym; Frick, Vose, Teague, Falke, Kyle) ÷ $19,000 = 0.20 = one-fifth. (At 27¢ it would be $20,520 and the award 18.5%.) Vira's and Oram's $3,500 are their fathers' memories against a quoted document.

**C2. Emmet Brant, 1910: 200,000 lb "even," paid on 3.42.** (a) Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = 6,840 lb. (b) True test 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60; true fat 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb; owed and not credited 7,200 − 6,840 = 360 lb (check: Ivy's "3.42 short 0.18" → 0.0018 × 200,000 = 360). (c) Butterfat was 24¢ that year (Roy): 360 × $0.24 = $86.40. (At the 25¢ long-run average it would be $90.00; the question says "that year," so $86.40.)

**C3.** (a) Credited to the station's patrons over 1898–1922: 1,444,000 lb. (b) Received at Larrow Green in the station's cream: 1,520,000 lb — the ledger's typical entry (57,760 credited / 60,800 received / gain 3,040) is exactly the average year, and × 25 gives these totals. (c) The difference, 76,000 lb, is **5.00% of the fat received/delivered** (76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = 0.0500 — the same 1/20 as the glass) and **5.26% ≈ "five and a quarter per cent" of the fat credited** (76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 0.05263; 1/19). The ledger's "five and a quarter" is therefore a percentage of the credited figure. Teague ("of the fat that was actually in the cream") and Vaught §V ("of the butterfat actually delivered") name the wrong denominator: 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.0%, not 5¼% — arithmetic settles it; Ivy and Nym have it right.

**C4.** (a) **$77.93.** The invoice is reproduced verbatim by two independent holders — Nym from the Association's office file, Junia Ammon in facsimile from the firm's letter book — and the lines sum to it: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93. The "$77.39" of Junia's own prose and of the Chronicle is a transposition. (b) **Six dozen = 72 pipettes** (72 × $0.35 = $25.20, as the invoice line says; the "five dozen" of Vaught's body text and of Oram fails that arithmetic). Each was short by **0.88 c.c.** on the mean (17.6 − 16.72); the nine survivors individually ranged 16.70–16.74, i.e., short 0.86–0.90 c.c. Strictly only the nine measured pipettes are proven short; that the whole lot was follows from the uniform 3.61 average over 25 years and the 1911 episode.

**C5(a).** The faulty glass was in use from **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 — 26 years, 1 month and 12 days** (about 26 years and six weeks), less the nine weeks of 8 May–10 July 1911 on a borrowed measure. The money calculation runs over **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922.** They differ because 1897 and 1923 were part years — the glass arrived in May 1897 ("1897, part year" on the averages leaf) and was condemned in June 1923 with true glass in use from July — and the ledger's figures (1,600,000 lb, the station-gain line) are whole-year figures, so Ivy counted only complete years on the faulty glass. The result is a rounded, slightly conservative figure: the part years 1897 and 1923 add uncounted loss, while the nine weeks of 1911 (about 9/52 of one year's 3,040 lb, roughly 500 lb) are inside the 25 years and were not deducted.

**C5(b). Cannot be determined from the sources.** Nym, from the ledgers: no 1896 intake figure exists anywhere; the station column begins with 1897. Kyle explains why: the first season was carried on the creamery's own books and those sheets were never put with the station papers — "not in any book I ever ruled." Two memories conflict: Sill (1934) "about nine hundred thousand pounds, May to the end of the year"; Kyle (1953) "a good eleven hundred thousand," from "men who were there." Both are round hearsay; no document exists; nothing breaks the tie. What is established: a part season from 4 May to the end of 1896, of the order of a million pounds (a full year later ran 1,600,000 and May–December contains the flush).

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran from 8 May 1911, when the station's pipette broke ("A. home cross about a broken measure" — Orra, the same evening), to 10 July 1911, when "our own measure [was] in use again" — nine weeks (Keddie's dated entries; Teague's and Kepp's "six weeks" are wrong). The variable: the pipette alone — a borrowed measure (from Larrow Green per Ivy, or Strawn's own per Roy; unsettled, and immaterial) replaced the station's. Held constant: the tester, his method and hour, the scales, the acid, the bottles, the hand tester, the herds, the land, the feed, the water supply, the supervision (none), and the patrons. Result: every Hessel Bottom farm rose from about 3.61 to 3.80, held there nine weeks, and fell back to 3.61 the day the station's measure returned — a ratio of 0.95, recorded contemporaneously by the tester (who did not know what he was recording and wrote "?") and independently by Emmet Brant. It establishes what a calculation cannot: that the whole difference lived in the measure at that station in that period — cause, not merely sufficiency — and that the fault was present already in 1911, twelve years before the condemnation; it disposes at once of hay, water, cows and men, because none of them changed. It does not establish which of the two measures was right (only that they differed by 5% — the burette settled that in 1923 and 1958), that the fault reached back to 1897 or forward to 1923 (the averages leaf and the 1924 rise do that), that the pipette broken in May was the same piece used in every other year (Keddie had six dozen), the exact magnitude beyond a nine-week average, or why the glass was short.

**D2.** Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream:
(1) *The receiving end weighed the fat that arrived.* Every year the ledger's station-gain line showed more butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream than was credited on the station's test — 5¼% of credited, "as usual" (Teague, Kyle, Rundle) — and buttermaker Sill, reweighing the Hollow's cream himself in the cold room, found it "Always more. Never less." Stolen cream reaches the Green light; this reached it heavy (Rundle's retraction). Source: the office ledgers and the creamery receiving book, 1897 onward.
(2) *The nine weeks of 1911.* The same man, alone, unsupervised, at the same separator, produced tests 5% higher the moment the pipette changed and 5% lower the day it came back, recorded in his own book and in the book of the patron he was supposed to be robbing. A thief does not start and stop with a piece of glass. Source: the test book and the Brant weigh book.
(3) *The 1924 season.* After Strawn condemned the glass on 12 June 1923 and new glass came in July, the station average rose to 3.80 while Keddie was still alone at the station and tested the whole of 1924 "with the same pen and the same measure of care" — before the petition, the hearing and his dismissal. Source: Strawn's circuit book (June and October 1923, 1924), the averages leaf, Orra's diary (July 1923).
Supporting: the shortfall was exactly proportional to each herd's richness (4.56 short 0.24, 3.42 short 0.18) and identical in flush and dry hay — an instrument's signature, not a man's opportunity; and the state inspector and Frick's board watched the test done and found nothing to remark.
They are independent because they rest on different records in different buildings, in different hands, in different years: the accuser's own ledger at the office and the buttermaker's book at the Green; the tester's and the largest patron's books at the Hollow in 1911; the state inspector's book and the station's figures of 1923–24. None uses the 1958 measurement; all three existed before the board sat in 1925, and (2) and (3) were before it as Exhibits 1, 3 and 4. Any one of them would still hold if the crate had never been opened.
Did the bookkeeper ever question the entries that refute him? No. Teague invented the line (1905), called it "furniture," said its evenness "stops him looking," raised the station account once with Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone ("I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience"), advanced the theft theory before the board in 1925 with the refutation in his own hand, and in his 1948 memoir still did not connect the two. Kyle: "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years." The inference was drawn by Rundle in 1939 and Ivy in 1958, never by Teague.

**D3.** Yes — Selby Vose knew from the start that the station's tests ran low; that was never a secret. Complaints had been made at annual meetings since 1902; the 1908 committee reported to "the President and Directors" that Hollow milk "is returned by the test at a figure below" the rest of the valley; Teague put the station account to him in 1916; the question was reopened in 1919 and his own circular of that year presupposes the low figures and offers water as the cause; Strawn told him in 1919 that no water was ever found; and his 1926 address speaks of "excuses over a generation" — wet hay, tester, office, weather. What he did not know, or would not let himself learn, was that the low tests were the station's own error: he signed the station-gain sheet every spring but "read the totals and never the lines above them" (Kyle), and his answer to Teague in 1916 — "let it alone" — is the answer of a man who preferred not to look. There is no evidence he knew the cause before 12 June 1923, and after it he called the condemnation "a piece of housekeeping" and credited the 1924 rise to water stopping. So: knowledge of the low tests, certain from 1913; knowledge that they were wrong, none shown — the sources support incuriosity, not concealment.

## Section F — Theory

**The theory.** The six dozen pipettes Ammon & Sons shipped for the Ostrey Hollow outfit in April 1897 were blown short — a reground mold or a knocked gauge, as Junia Ammon supposes — and short alike: the nine survivors deliver 16.70–16.74 c.c. against a 17.6 mark, mean 16.72, 0.95 of the mark. The butterfat test charges a fixed volume of milk into a bottle whose graduated neck is ruled for 17.6 c.c.; the fat that rises is read directly as per cent. A charge 5% short puts 5% less fat in the neck, so every reading is 95% of the truth — proportional, not additive: 3.80 reads 3.61, 3.60 reads 3.42, 4.80 reads 4.56, and the station's herds rank correctly against one another while the whole station is wrong against the world. That is the size (0.19 on a 3.80 average) and why that size (16.72 ÷ 17.6). It began on 1 May 1897, the day the station's own glass replaced Sill's borrowed, correct outfit (1896 on borrowed glass: 3.80; 1898 on its own: 3.61), and ended in July 1923, when the new glass ordered on 28 June after Strawn's burette condemned the lot on 12 June went into use (1924: 3.80). Nothing at the station or on the farms had to change because nothing there was wrong: the scales were true, the acid right, the crank counted, the bottles good, the cows ordinary, the milk whole; the pipette is "the one instrument in the butterfat test which the plant cannot check against anything else" — it is the standard — and can be caught only by a burette from outside, which reached the state's field men in 1921. Glass does not vary with season, feed or weather, so the gap was identical in February and July, wet years and dry. The missing fat was never missing: it went down the road in the cream and was booked at Larrow Green, year after year, as "station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual," and carried to the Association's general account. The 1911 nine weeks, when a borrowed pipette put every farm at 3.80 and the station's own put them back at 3.61 the day it returned, is the theory run as an experiment by accident, twelve years before anyone condemned the glass.

**Three competing explanations and what defeats them.**
(1) *Added water* — Selby Vose (1919 circular; the board, 1925; annual address, 1926), echoed by Sill (1934). Defeated by: Strawn's lactometer on every visit 1907–1936, "no added water, as always here," and his telling Vose so in 1919; Rosalie Cudd's vat, which "knows watered milk inside a day" and never showed one in sixteen summers; the ledger — watered milk yields thin cream and a station *loss* at the Green, not a 5¼% gain every year; the 1911 interval, since water does not stop and start with a pipette; the exact proportionality of the shortfall to each herd's richness; and Bulletin 471's finding.
(2) *Cream stolen by the tester, low tests written to cover it* — Wilbur Teague (before the board, 1925; memoir, 1948); Oram's father; Vira; the valley's "settled opinion" in Rundle (1939, retracted in press). Defeated by: the station-gain line and Sill's reweighing — the cream "reached the Green heavy, every year"; the 1911 interval, the same man alone producing 3.80 with another man's glass; the 1924 rise to 3.80 with Keddie still alone and untouched for a full season before the petition; and the board's and Strawn's watching of the test.
(3) *Wet hay of Hessel Bottom* — the 1908 committee under Nyle Grigg, Grigg's 1919 letter, Dr. Kepp (1926), and Falke provisionally (1959). Defeated by: Bulletin 214 itself — not more than 0.05 in winter, nothing in summer — four times too small and the wrong shape; the gap's constancy in February and July (Keddie, Roy's book, Hazel); the same four herds at 3.78–3.82 over the ridge every summer for sixteen years; and the 1911 interval, in which the herds, the hay and the ground did not change and the tests did.

**Did the board examine the condemned glassware?** No. Effie, who took down every word: the crate "was never mentioned in any of the three sessions by patrons, officers, or board"; "books and appliances" was a form of words. Yet Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook was Exhibit 4 (Frick's list; Kyle carried it in), so the fact of the condemnation was before the board, and two of its three sessions sat in the weigh room with the crate in the loft some twenty feet overhead (Oram, Duncan). Frick "saw the scales tried ... the sampling done and the test made" — on the new glass. No one "laid the second beside the fourth and looked" (Kyle). The board had the record of the condemnation and never asked for, saw, or thought of the condemned glass.

**What the tester believed.** In writing, nothing he could not prove: "the figures are what I read and what I wrote and nothing besides ... I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are"; against the 1911 question mark, "I will not put anything in its place that I do not know." Privately, according to his son, he came home in the summer of 1911 and said twice that "it was something in the glass," and "never dared put it in the book" — a suspicion with nothing to hold in his hand against the outfit he had been taught to trust. Frick's 1946 recollection of a corridor remark — that Keddie "had always thought the patrons were at fault" and "suspected" someone — points the other way; it is a lawyer's twenty-one-year-old memory of an unrecorded aside, against a son's memory of the supper table at seventeen, and the two cannot both stand. On balance: he suspected the glass from 1911, would not say so without proof, and went to his grave accusing nobody — "the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had."

## Section G — Summary

From 1897 to 1923 the Larrow Valley Association's Ostrey Hollow station tested nine families' milk with pipettes blown five per cent short. Every test read one part in twenty low: 3.61 for milk that was 3.80 — 76,000 lb of butterfat, $19,000 over twenty-five years — while the missing fat arrived at the creamery and was booked each year as "station gain, as usual." The valley blamed wet hay (1908), watered cans (1919) and the tester (1925); an arbitration paid $3,800 without a finding, and Ansel Keddie was dismissed and died under suspicion in 1934. The proof was scattered in eight places that never met: his test book with its nine unexplained weeks of 1911, his brother-in-law's weigh book, the office ledgers, the buttermaker's scales, the cheese factory over the ridge, the inspector's 1923 condemnation, the supplier's invoice, and the crate in the loft — until his granddaughter measured nine pipettes in 1958.
