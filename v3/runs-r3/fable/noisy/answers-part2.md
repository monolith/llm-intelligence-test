## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### Story 5 — The water letter and the silence (1919–1934)

In 1919 "men who were boys" reopened the question (Grigg). The Association answered with a printed circular over manager Selby Vose's signature, typed by clerk Bertram Kyle: a civil warning to the Ostrey Hollow patrons that added water would be looked for — "this Association pays for butterfat and cannot pay for creek water." Vose (1926) says it named nobody; every family that received it read it as saying there was water in the cans (Vira, Keddie, Roy, Sill, Rundle, Hazel). It went to nine families (Keddie, Vira, Roy, Sill, Hazel); Orra's diary says "eleven," the sole outlier, rejected against the man who carried them and the families that got them. It rained that day. Keddie would not mail the letters or leave them on the platform; he carried them round himself, stood at each door while the man read, and came home at eleven at night with his hat on (Orra, June 1919). Vira's father read his twice with his hat on and kept it in the desk drawer until he died; Hazel's mother put hers in the stove; one woman pinned hers inside her trunk lid. Two days later Emmet Brant stood in the Keddie yard — he did not come in — said he had hauled whole milk since the day the station opened and Ansel knew it, and asked why Ansel had carried the paper himself if he did not believe it; Ansel said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet never entered the house again; Orra walked to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane. Roy's version — his father held that the man who kept the test book "ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not" — is the same quarrel from the other kitchen; Vira's "somebody's cousin saying it" is talk. The two ends of the valley "did not speak civilly for six years" (Rundle).

The water charge had nothing behind it. Inspector Merle Strawn tested with the lactometer on every visit from 1907, never found added water, and told the manager so in 1919; Rosalie Cudd's vat — "Cheese is a witness" — never showed a watered can in sixteen summers; Bulletin 471 concluded "No milk was watered." Vose nonetheless defended the circular in 1926 ("would write every word of it again") and read the 1924 rise as proof the water stopped once the Association looked; Sill thought well of him for it — "nine families all got honest in one summer." Emmet died in spring 1929, "ten years and neither of them would go up the road first"; Ansel in February 1934, when men who had not spoken to him in nine years shook Orra's hand and praised his hand with a door.

Documents: the circular (never transcribed; described by seven narrators); Strawn's 1919 entry; Vose's 1926 address; Orra's diary (June 1919, 1929, 1934); Rosalie's letter; Roy (1955).

### Story 6 — Written down and never read: the ledger line, the nine weeks, and the condemned crate (1897–1924)

Three contemporaneous records held the answer. First, the ledgers. Every year the Ostrey Hollow station account carried, in the bookkeeper's hand: "butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account" (Teague's "entry for 1898"; Nym Item 6; Rundle 1939; Kyle copied it yearly). Teague says the line was his own device from 1905 yet quotes an 1898 entry in that form — unresolved. Hobart Sill reweighed the Hollow's cream himself in the cold room: it "ran heavy. Every year ... Always more. Never less," and he said so a hundred times. Vose signed the sheet every spring; Teague put the station account to him in 1916 and was told to let it alone. "A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture" (Teague); "It was in plain sight with a name on it" (Kyle). Rundle, seeing the ledgers in 1939 on another errand, drew the inference in press: stolen cream "would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy."

Second, the nine weeks. On 8 May 1911 the station's pipette broke — "A. home cross about a broken measure" (Orra, that day) — and a borrowed measure served until 10 July (Keddie's dated entries; Ivy, Roy, Vaught, Falke; Teague's and Kepp's "six weeks" fail against the dates). Its source is unsettled: from Larrow Green with the cream wagon (Ivy) or left by Strawn (Roy) — Strawn's own June 1911 entry says only "Nothing here to note." Every Bottom farm tested 3.80; the day the station's own measure returned the tests fell to 3.61 and Keddie wrote "? — A.K.", which stood fourteen years. Emmet Brant entered both movements. Keddie came home that summer saying "it was something in the glass," twice, but would not write what he could not prove (Duncan).

Third, the burette. The state issued burettes to field men in 1921. On 12 June 1923 Strawn ran the station's pipettes against his: "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new" (his circuit book; Nym's photostat). Vose's and Rundle's "12 July," Kyle's "'22" and Hazel's "'21" fall against the order of 28 June, Ammon & Sons' reply of 2 July, and new glass in use in July 1923 ("new glass at the station" — Orra). The crate went up the ladder into the loft over the intake room under a tarpaulin. In October Strawn noted the new pipettes delivered their mark; the 1924 average was 3.80 (averages leaf, Strawn; Vose's 3.90 and Falke's 3.85 are wrong). Nobody — not Strawn, who "did not weigh what it has cost them," not the office, which treated it "as a broken window" — connected the new glass with the old figures.

Documents: the ledger entry; Sill's receiving book; test-book entries of 8 May and 10 July 1911; Strawn's 12 June 1923 entry; the 28 June order and 2 July letter; Orra's diary.

### Story 7 — The arbitration of 1925 and what it cost the tester (1925–1934)

In January 1925 the Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned the Association — Emmet Brant's name on it (Orra) — to be made whole. The petition set the loss "to the dollar" at $19,600 (Rundle) or at 67,000 lb of butterfat (Frick); the two do not reconcile at any price and the petition is not transcribed, so its figure stays open. Under the by-laws a board of three sat, chaired by Alonzo Frick, attorney of Ashlin, "the one of the three who kept no cow." The first session was 17 March 1925 at the Association's office at Ashlin — Keddie went down in his good coat, up since three (Orra) — and, at Frick's request, the second and third in the station's own weigh room (the intake room, with the scale), where the board saw the scales tried and the test made while a dozen men, Cleve Oram among them, stood on the platform in the rain (Frick, Oram, Orra, Duncan, Kyle; Vira's and Falke's Ashlin-only account is incomplete). Keddie's nine-sentence statement ended: "I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself." Four exhibits, listed by Frick and carried in by Kyle: the test book, 4 May 1896–31 December 1924; the station receiving book; Emmet Brant's weigh book from 1 January 1897; Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook — so the condemnation was before the board, and no one "laid the second beside the fourth and looked." Effie: the crate itself was never mentioned. Three explanations were argued: water (Vose, standing by his circular, "without heat and without proof"); cream theft by the tester (Teague, "in the way an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it"); wet hay (the 1908 report read into the record). None was accepted.

On 30 April 1925, four days before the award, the Association dismissed Keddie — "Twenty-nine seasons and a letter" (Orra); he came home at his ordinary hour, hung his apron behind the shed door, and ate ham. On 4 May the board "finds no cause established" and awarded $3,800.00 "without admission, to be charged to the station account" (award text; Effie, Nym Item 7, Frick, Vose, Teague, Falke, Kyle; Vira's and Oram's "$3,500" are their fathers' memories) — one-fifth of the loss. Frick believed Keddie then and after: "an honest man was ruined by a figure nobody could explain." Keddie kept a few cows and turned carpenter — hung a barn door for the Orams that swung true thirty years, mended half the doors in the hollow "at a quarter of what the other paid," and whistled. He died at home in February 1934; the funeral was well attended and nobody said the other thing.

Documents: the award; Keddie's statement and unsent letter; Frick's exhibit list; Effie §6–8; Orra's diary (January–June 1925, 1934); Kyle; Oram.

### Story 8 — What the crate held (1958–1972)

Ivy Keddie — Ansel's granddaughter, Duncan's daughter, born 1931, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland — climbed the ladder into the station loft in February 1958 and found the Ammon & Sons crate under a tarpaulin (Ivy, Duncan, Hazel, Oram, Chronicle, Falke; Nym's "found in the Association office at Ashlin" stands alone and is rejected). Nine pipettes were unbroken (Ivy's sheet, Nym, Vaught, Falke, Oram; Rundle's and the Chronicle's "eleven" fall to the sheet, which lists nine values). She carried them east and on 9 March 1958 measured them against a calibrated burette: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 — mean 16.72 c.c. against a 17.6 mark, short 0.88, "95 hundredths of the mark." Every test made on that glass read one part in twenty low: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80. Over the 25 full delivery years 1898–1922, 40,000,000 lb of milk (Effie, Kyle, Nym, Vaught; the 44,000,000 of Teague and Rundle rejected) credited 1,444,000 lb of fat at 3.61 where 1,520,000 was delivered at 3.80 — 76,000 lb short, $19,000 at the 25¢ average, five times the award.

She brought the five record sets into one room: the test book; the Brant book, lent by Roy; Rosalie Cudd's copied Alder Corners figures ("Your letter came Tuesday"); Strawn's 1923 notebook, found in the Farland library where the state dairy office had sent the set in 1947; and the Association's ledgers. She visited Vira Toland. Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958), Professor Leland Vaught with Ivy, closed: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain." Vaught's body text (16.27 c.c., 0.9244, 3.905, 118,000 lb, "five dozen," 1 May 1898) is a transposition of 16.72 carried through the arithmetic, which he himself calls "not readily reconciled"; the Ashlin Chronicle (3 October 1958) copied 16.27, "eleven," "$19,600" and "$77.39," called Ivy his great-niece, and accused Ammon & Sons of knowing; county agent Orin Falke (1959) repeated "great-niece" and corrected it. Ivy read "What the Crate Held" at Ashlin in 1959: "It depends on nine pieces of glass and a burette." The test book and crate went to the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1959, the weigh book in 1963, Cudd's book to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970; Halbert Nym catalogued sixteen items in 1972. Ansel had been dead twenty-four years; Orra died in 1948.

Documents: Ivy's measurement sheet; Bulletin 471; the Chronicle; Falke's report; Rosalie's letter; Nym's accession notes; Duncan's list of the books.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** Derivation: Ansel and Orra's son was Duncan Keddie (Roy r09: "Ansel and Orra's boy"; Orra's diary, 1899: "Duncan five years old"; Duncan r18: "My father was Ansel Keddie"); Duncan's daughter is Ivy (Duncan: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter"; Roy: "Duncan's girl Ivy"; Ivy herself: "my grandfather"). Pointing elsewhere: the Ashlin Chronicle (1958) calls her "his great-niece," and Falke's county report (1959) repeats it — but Falke wrote from the newspaper notice and the same press release, so that is one source, not two, and Falke "verified" and corrected it to granddaughter on the same page. Duncan says he had "twice seen it printed otherwise." Resolution: the family (father, cousin, herself) and the corrected official report outrank a newspaper feature written "in a day and a half."

**B2. Ivy and Hazel Brant were second cousins.** Derivation: Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie were brother and sister (Roy, Duncan, Hazel, Vira, Keddie); Emmet's son Roy and Orra's son Duncan were therefore first cousins (Roy, Duncan); Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy, children of first cousins, are second cousins (Roy, Duncan, Hazel, Rundle's 1959 footnote). Vira and Oram say "first cousin once removed," which would require Ivy and Roy, or Hazel and Duncan, to be in the same generation — they are not; both are valley hearsay and Hazel says "people are forever telling me she is my niece or my cousin once removed." Left open: whether Emmet and Orra were full siblings (Roy: "full brother and sister, same mother and same father," dismissing a stepmother story) or half-siblings "by his father's second wife" (Hazel); Duncan says only "brother and sister ... the younger of the two." If Hazel is right they are half second cousins; the degree does not change. Also open: Orra's birth year — 1866 by her own diary and by Duncan's "eighty-two in 1948," 1868 by Duncan's stated figure.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose**, the Association's founder, first president (1889–1911) and author of the by-laws. Sources: Selby's own printed 1926 address ("My father, who was your first president"), Effie ("son of the late president"), Duncan ("his son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew"), Kyle ("the son, whatever is said at Ashlin"). Against: Vira and Rundle say nephew — Rundle's pamphlet is "what the valley remembers, tidied," and Vira is retailing valley talk; a man's own printed statement of who his father was outranks both. Why it matters: Gideon wrote Article VII, whose last clause made the station's test govern without appeal; his son sat as manager for the whole of the dispute's active years (1913–1938), signed the station-gain sheet every spring, told Teague in 1916 to let the station account alone, sent the 1919 water circular, put the water theory before the board in 1925, and in 1926 defended both the by-law and the circular. "The family always thought the valley belonged to them a little" (Vira): the rule and its enforcement stayed in one family, and the man who might have questioned the station's figure had every reason to stand by his father's clause.

**B4. Vose–Keddie: no established relationship; the best-placed source denies one.** Two narrators assert kinship: Vira ("old Mrs. Vose" was Orra's first cousin, "everybody knew, nobody wrote down" — "it was somebody's cousin saying it") and Oram ("cousins of some sort ... I could not draw you the line on paper," from his mother's mother). Duncan Keddie, Orra's son, flatly denies it: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses"; people confuse them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Gideon either; his mother, who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection," never put a Vose in it. The two affirmers are hearsay of hearsay and cannot draw the line; the denier is the son of the woman in question. Resolution: no relationship is established, and the weight of evidence is that there was none; the sources cannot prove a negative, but they give no reason to believe the rumor.
