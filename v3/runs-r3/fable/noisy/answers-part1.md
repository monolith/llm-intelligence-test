## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1 — Article VII: the rule that turned a figure into money (1889–1938)

In 1889 the farmers of the Larrow Valley, tired of hauling butter to the Ashlin merchants "and taking whatever the merchant chose to allow them," met in the schoolhouse at Larrow Green in cold weather and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (Effie Loomis r02, Rundle r12, Falke r22). Gideon Vose — heavy, quiet, obstinate, not a lawyer though he had once thought of the law — was elected president and served 1889–1911, the longest term the Association ever had; he drafted the by-laws himself at his kitchen table. The main creamery was built on Larrow Creek below the mill at Larrow Green in 1891 and the valley was paid in cash once a month.

Article VII — "Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" (quoted identically by Effie, Nym Item 1 and Rundle) — made the butterfat test, not the pound of milk, the measure of a man's month. Its last clause allowed no appeal from the station's figure, which Effie, Rundle, Frick and Falke all call the hinge of the whole affair: "it makes the test the whole of the bargain, and it leaves a dissatisfied man with no remedy on earth except to quarrel with a figure" (Frick). The article's date cannot be fixed: Effie says adopted at the 1892 annual meeting, Rundle 1891, Grigg 1893; the printed pamphlet carries only "adopted at the annual meeting," and the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move.

The Association was three things (Teague r05): the creamery at Larrow Green (buttermaker Hobart Sill, 1893–1934, keeping the churn and cream-receiving books), the office (bookkeeper Wilbur Teague 1905–1945 with the ledgers; secretary Effie Loomis 1901–1940 with the minute book; clerk Bertram Kyle at the outer desk twenty-five years, ruling the fair copies), and the Ostrey Hollow station. Selby Vose was manager 1913–1938 — his own "thirteen years this spring" in 1926 and Strawn's 1913 note of a new manager outrank Kyle's "1911." He was Gideon's son (his own printed words, Effie, Duncan Keddie, Kyle); the "nephew" of Vira and Rundle is valley talk. Where the office stood is not settled: Teague, Frick, Nym and Duncan put it at Ashlin, one room over a harness shop, where the 1925 first session sat and the test book lay 1925–1959; Kyle and Vira put the manager's door at Larrow Green; the two may describe different rooms. Gideon died in 1917. None of the four officers, Effie says, ever stood in the station on a working morning.

Documents: Article VII; the by-laws pamphlet (Nym Item 1); Effie's "Notes toward a history" (1941); Rundle's jubilee pamphlet (1939); Kyle (1953); Teague's Chapter Nine (1948).

### Story 2 — Ostrey Hollow opens on borrowed glass, and the short lot arrives (1895–1898)

Seven miles up the valley, on the flat by the creek where the road bends toward the ridge, the Association built a skimming station in the winter of 1895–96 (Falke) to serve Hessel Bottom — a 160-acre wet meadow with six farms holding rights (Roy, Hazel, the 1908 report, Kepp; Effie's "seven" is the odd one out) — and three hill places (Strawn): nine families in all. Ansel Keddie, born 1861 (Duncan; Orra's "a carpenter at sixty-six" in 1927 agrees), went up in 1895 to get it ready and was tester and separator man alone from the opening on 4 May 1896 through the 1924 season — twenty-nine seasons. Milk was weighed at the intake, sampled into numbered ten-day composite jars, charged by pipette into a patron-numbered bottle, tested in a four-bottle hand tester, and separated; the cream went down daily by wagon (Cleve Oram's father drove it) and the skim went home for hogs and calves.

The station had no glass of its own at the opening. Keddie's first leaf reads "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes"; Hobart Sill says the borrowed outfit was his own personal glass, made up at his bench at Larrow Green and sent up on the wagon. Keddie's later "borrowed off the Ashlin creamery" is contradicted by his own first leaf and by Sill and is rejected. On that glass the 1896 average was 3.80. The first season's intake is recorded nowhere (Nym: the station column begins 1897); Sill's "about nine hundred thousand pounds" and Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand" are both hearsay and cannot be reconciled.

On 14 April 1897 Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, Tarnet, invoiced the outfit: 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 = 25.20; 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at .22 = 31.68; acid measure .90; 4-bottle hand tester 18.00; freight 2.15; total $77.93 (reproduced verbatim by Nym Item 2 and in facsimile by Junia Ammon; the "$77.39" of Junia's prose and of the Chronicle is a transposition, and the lines sum to 77.93). Seventy-two pipettes, not the "five dozen" of Vaught's body text and Oram (72 × .35 = 25.20). The outfit went into use on 1 May 1897 — Orra's diary entry of that date, the office file, Sill's "the spring after," Junia, and Bulletin 471's conclusion; Vaught's §III and Kyle's "1 May 1898" are wrong. Sill's glass went back with one piece chipped. From 1898 the station average was 3.61, and 3.61 within three hundredths every year after (averages leaf). Junia Ammon (1949), whose works records burned in 1931, believes a reground mold made the lot run consistently short and that sample checks cannot catch a consistent error; her claim that the house sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900 is refuted by her own invoice.

Documents: first leaf and averages leaf of the test book; the invoice; Orra's diary (1 May 1897); Sill (1934); Junia Ammon (1949); Keddie's unsent letter.

### Story 3 — The hay theory and the ridge that refuted it (1902–1930)

Talk began at the Larrow Green store in March 1902 that Hollow milk "did not test with the rest" (Orra's diary; Keddie; Roy — Emmet Brant among the first to say it aloud, and laughed at; Sill). In January 1908 the board named a committee of three under Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green, a non-patron who hauled to the Green. They came up in Grigg's sleigh, went to Emmet Brant's first, and "wanted to see the barns and not the station" (Orra). Their report (printed in full by Grigg in 1919) says they sampled the Hessel Bottom herds through January, February and March, visited each of six farms three and four times, tried the scales, stood by the test, found "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter" — "the milk is whole and the scales are honest" — and blamed the wet sedge hay of the Bottom: "the remedy lies with the land and not with the station." Grigg's 1919 covering letter claims sampling "through the whole of 1908 — every month"; his own report contradicts him and governs. The question "was closed by it for eleven years."

The College's Bulletin No. 214 (1911), a feeding trial, found bog hay "depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture" (quoted verbatim by Ivy, Nym, Vaught and Falke). Grigg (1919) and Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S., of Ashlin (Farm Club paper, 1926) both cite it as "two tenths in the winter feeding," four times the bulletin's ceiling; the verbatim text breaks the tie. Kepp, who had "never in my life read a test," put the gap at 0.21, the county's wet bottoms at "a fifth of a point" below the hills, and waved off the 1911 borrowed-measure interval as "six weeks of June and July." Falke (1959) first allowed the hay as a partial factor, then withdrew it on reading Bulletin 214.

Three facts defeat the hay. The gap was the same in February as in July, "flat as a floor" (Keddie, Roy's two-column book, Hazel) — not seasonal, as a feed effect must be. From 1915 to 1930 four of the six Hessel Bottom men hauled over Redlow Ridge every summer flush (about 1 June to mid-September; the ridge is shut November to April) to Jerome Cudd's Alder Corners Cheese Company in Vessey County — built 1913 per his daughter Rosalie (Roy and Rundle say opened 1914). Cudd tested on his own new glass and never compared figures with anyone. His 1919 entry gives the four men 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82 — "Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge" — and sixteen season averages run 3.78–3.82, mean 3.80, wet years and dry (Rosalie's table; sixteen seasons per Rosalie, Roy and Hazel, not Ivy's "fourteen"). And the true effect, 0.19, is four times Bulletin 214's ceiling "and the wrong shape" (Ivy).

Documents: the 1908 report; Grigg's 1919 letter; Bulletin 214; Kepp's paper; Cudd's test book and Rosalie's 1958 letter; Orra's diary (January and March 1908).

### Story 4 — Two columns that agreed: the Brant weigh book and the two families (1854–1963)

Emmet Brant (1854–1929) was the biggest man on Hessel Bottom — sixty-odd acres of the meadow, thirty-eight cows in a good year, the station's largest patron every recorded year. From 1 January 1897 (Frick's exhibit list, Nym's deposit record, Duncan Keddie; Roy's "1898" and Hazel's "1900" are memory — Hazel warns "years I have not") he weighed every can on his own barn scale before it went on the wagon and wrote that figure beside the station's returned weight, at the kitchen table after the second milking, hat still on, in a plain hand-ruled ledger with the front cover gone and the back held by a strip of harness leather. The two columns agreed for thirty years, "barring a pound or two ... when a can got slopped on the hill" — whatever was wrong at the station, "it was not the weighing" (Roy). The leaf for 1910: "Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42" — butterfat at 24¢ that year. Emmet also entered the 1911 rise and fall. He was among the first to complain in 1902, read the 1908 report twice and put it in the drawer ("the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not"), did not haul to Alder Corners because "the biggest herd is the one that cannot afford to be talked about," and his name was on the January 1925 petition (Orra).

The book passed to Roy (born c. 1888) in 1929, who kept it up in the same book and then another. Roy lent it — "lent, and I want the word" (Duncan; Hazel) — to Ivy Keddie in 1958 and had it back; at Roy's death in 1962 it went to Hazel, who deposited it at the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1963 (Nym Item 4, whose label "kept by A. Keddie" is swapped with the test book's).

The families: Ansel Keddie married Orra Brant, Emmet's sister, in 1889 — so the tester was brother-in-law to the patron who lost the most, and the two households ate Sunday dinner together for thirty years. Their son Duncan (born 1894; to the Ashlin school as a teacher in October 1911, aged seventeen) and Emmet's son Roy are first cousins; Duncan's daughter Ivy (born 1931) and Roy's daughter Hazel are second cousins (Roy, Duncan, Hazel, Rundle's 1959 footnote) — not "first cousin once removed" as Vira and Oram have it. Roy calls Orra Emmet's full sister and names the stepmother story a rumor; Hazel calls her a half-sister by a second wife; the sources leave that open. Orra was born 1866 (her own "sixty-four" in August 1930; Duncan's "eighty-two when she died in 1948"; Duncan's "1868" contradicts himself).

Documents: the weigh book and its 1910 leaf; Roy (1955); Hazel (1981); Duncan (1968); Orra's diary; Nym Item 4; Frick's Exhibit 3.
