## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1 — The By-law Without an Appeal (1889–1892)

**Who.** The farmers of the Larrow Valley, Ordell County; Gideon Vose, first president; and, as the keeper of the record, Effie Loomis, Association secretary 1901–1940.

**What happened.** In 1889 the farmers met at Larrow Green (in the schoolhouse, per Rundle's pamphlet) and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose was elected president and held the office until 1911 — twenty-two years, longer than anyone since (Loomis); he died in 1917. He drafted the by-laws himself, at his own kitchen table, "as a farmer wrote them." In 1891 the main creamery was built at Larrow Green on Larrow Creek, and for the first time the valley was paid in cash once a month.

The article that governs the whole later history is the seventh, which four sources quote in identical words: every patron is paid according to the pounds of butterfat in his milk, ascertained by test, not by weight — *and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.* Loomis, Rundle, Frick and Falke all point at that last clause: no appeal to the creamery's test, no valley average, no re-test anywhere. Before Article VII a hundred pounds of milk was a hundred pounds; after it, the station's own instrument became the entire bargain. Frick put it best: a dissatisfied man was left with "no remedy on earth except to quarrel with a figure."

**When adopted.** The printed by-laws say only "adopted at the annual meeting" (Nym, item 1; Falke). Loomis says 1892, Rundle 1891, Grigg "the 1893 meeting." The 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move. Loomis kept the office for thirty-seven years before that loss and is the only narrator who could have read the book, so 1892 is the most probable year — but the sources cannot settle it beyond that, and Falke was right to decline.

**Quantities that matter.** Twenty-two years of Gideon Vose's presidency; one clause; one lost minute book; four officers who never once stood in the Ostrey Hollow station on a working morning (Loomis, Teague, Sill, Selby Vose — Loomis's own confession).

**Why it belongs at the head.** Under payment by weight, nothing that follows would have cost any man a dollar (Falke). Under Article VII a five-percent-short pipette at one station converted directly into money, year after year, with no mechanism in the by-laws to catch it. And the man who wrote the clause was the father of the manager who defended it in 1926 (Story 5, and B3).

**Documents bearing on it.** r02 (Loomis, primary for the officers and the lost book), r07 item 1 (Nym's transcription of Article VII), r12 (Rundle), r13 (Frick), r15 (Grigg's "1893"), r22 (Falke), r08 (Vose: "my father … wrote the by-law").

### Story 2 — The Outfit from Tarnet (1896–1897, with its sequel in 1923)

**Who.** Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green 1893–1934; Ansel Keddie, the new tester; Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet; Junia Ammon, the firm's historian.

**What happened.** The Association built a skimming station at the head of Ostrey Hollow, seven miles above Larrow Green, in the winter of 1895–96; Keddie went up in 1895 to make it ready (Toland, Hazel Brant). It opened on 4 May 1896 — the date on the first leaf of the test book. It opened with no glass of its own: the order had not come, there was talk of losing the season, and Sill made up an outfit from his own bench at Larrow Green and sent it up on the wagon. The test book's first leaf agrees: "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie." (Keddie's unsent letter says "borrowed off the Ashlin creamery"; his own leaf, Sill, Orra's diary and Kyle all say Larrow Green, so that is a slip.) On Sill's glass the station's 1896 average was 3.80.

The invoice, transcribed identically by Nym (item 2) and reproduced in facsimile by Ammon, is dated 14 April 1897: six dozen milk-test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at 35¢ = $25.20; twelve dozen 8-percent-neck test bottles at 22¢ = $31.68; one acid measure, 90¢; one four-bottle hand tester, $18.00; freight $2.15; total **$77.93**. The Chronicle's "$77.39," and Ammon's own text beneath her facsimile, fail the line-sum. The outfit went into use at the station on **1 May 1897** — Nym's office file, Orra's diary entry of that very day ("done borrowing of Larrow Green"), Ammon's chapter and Bulletin 471's conclusion all agree; Vaught's introduction and Kyle say 1898 and are rejected (Part 3, E). Sill's glass went back, one piece chipped.

The lot was short. Every pipette blown at 17.6 delivered about 16.72 c.c. — 95 percent. Junia Ammon's guess (offered as hers, not as record): a mold reground a hair small, a gauge knocked off its nail; a boy checks the first, tenth and fiftieth and finds them consistent, "consistently wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind cannot find." The works books that could have said burned in 1931. Her chapter also claims the house sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900, which her own 1897 invoice contradicts.

**Sequel.** When the Association reordered on 28 June 1923, Ammon's reply of 2 July entered six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles, shipped that month, and said the books of the nineties could tell nothing of the old lot. The Chronicle's charge that the firm knowingly sold short glass has no support anywhere in the sources.

**Quantities.** 72 pipettes; $77.93; 17.6 c.c. marked, 16.72 delivered, 0.88 short; 1 May 1897.

**Documents.** r03, r07 (items 2, 10), r14, r20, r24, r04, r11, r19, r23.

### Story 3 — The Tester's Book and the Nine Weeks (1896–1925)

**Who.** Ansel Keddie (1861–1934), tester and separator man at Ostrey Hollow for twenty-nine seasons, 1896 through 1924; alone in the building from spring to fall.

**How the work was done.** The day began at half past five. Every can was weighed on a scale the state's man tested and always found true. A sample from each patron went into his own numbered jar, made up over ten days; on testing morning the jar was warmed blood-warm and poured vessel to vessel; the charge was drawn to the mark on the pipette stem, blown into the numbered bottle, acid added, four bottles spun in the hand tester at the speed he was taught, and the fat column read against the neck rulings with dividers. Thirty-one tests a week. Five columns — date, patron, pounds, test, fat; errors struck through and initialed, never written over. Strawn watched him in 1907 and 1911: "slow and clean," reads his own figure "without being coaxed."

**The figures.** The 1922 summary leaf, quoted identically by Keddie, Ivy Keddie and Nym: 1896, 3.80; 1897, part year; 1898, 3.61, "and 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 … when it stood at 3.80." The gap did not move with the season — July and February, wet year and dry (Keddie, Roy Brant, Hazel Brant, Falke). In 1924, on the new glass, the book gave 3.80; Strawn's 1924 entry confirms it (Vose's 3.90 and Falke's 3.85 are rejected).

**The nine weeks.** "May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." "July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." Eight May to ten July is sixty-three days — nine weeks; Teague's and Kepp's "six weeks" fail the dates. The question mark stood fourteen years; he would put nothing in its place he did not know. Where the borrowed measure came from cannot be settled: Ivy says up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon; Roy says the state man left his; Strawn's own June 1911 entry records no loan.

**The end.** Statement to the board, 17 March 1925, nine sentences: he had never taken a pound of cream and could not tell them why the figures were what they were. Dismissed 30 April 1925, four days before the award, with no finding. He kept a few cows, did carpentry that "swung true for thirty years" (Oram; Orra: "a carpenter at sixty-six"), and died in February 1934 with the imputation on him. The book was impounded in March 1925, lay in the Ashlin office to 1959, then went to the Historical Rooms (Nym's label "kept by E. Brant" is a catalogue slip; the leaf is signed A. Keddie).

**Documents.** r03, r04, r06, r07 (items 3, 8), r09, r11, r13, r18, r21, r22, r24, r01.

### Story 4 — The Wet Hay of Hessel Bottom (1902–1930)

**Who.** The patrons of Hessel Bottom; Nyle Grigg and his 1908 committee; the State Agricultural College (Bulletin 214, 1911); Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S. (1926); Jerome and Rosalie Cudd of Alder Corners, whose factory supplied the control.

**The complaint.** From 1902 the Hollow said its milk tested under the same class of milk at Larrow Green (Keddie, Roy, Rundle, Grigg, Sill; Orra's diary, March 1902). Hessel Bottom is 160 acres of wet meadow under the hamlet, six farms (Roy, the 1908 report, Kepp, Hazel; Loomis's "seven" is outvoted), nine families; Emmet Brant's was the largest — sixty-odd acres, thirty-eight cows in a good year — and he was the station's largest patron in every recorded year.

**The hay explanation.** A committee under Grigg — a Larrow Green patron with no interest in the station — was appointed January 1908 and sampled the six farms through **January, February and March** (the report's own words; Nym; Orra's diary places the sleigh in January and the return in March). It tried both sets of scales, found the milk whole and the scales honest, "no dishonesty of any kind," and gave its opinion that the wet hay thinned the cream and the remedy lay with the land. Grigg's 1919 covering letter claims a whole year's sampling and that Bulletin 214 found "two tenths" — contradicted by his own report and by the bulletin. Bulletin 214 actually found bog hay, fed as the whole roughage, depressed the test by not more than **0.05 in winter and nothing measurable on summer pasture**. Kepp in 1926 argued from soil chemistry for "a fifth of a point," repeated the "two tenths," and waved the 1911 interval away as six summer weeks. Falke in 1959 first allowed hay as a partial factor, then withdrew on reading Bulletin 214.

**What defeats it.** The gap — 0.19 against the truth — was flat in every month of the year, which a feed effect cannot be; 0.05 is roughly four times too small and the wrong shape (Ivy). And over Redlow Ridge, Jerome Cudd's cheese factory (built 1913) tested the same four Hessel Bottom herds off the same wet ground every summer flush, June to mid-September, 1915 through 1930 — sixteen seasons, 3.78 to 3.82, the four men in 1919 at 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82: "Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge." Larrow Green itself ran 3.78 (Sill, Falke), so Roy's "seventeen hundredths" is the Hollow's gap against the Green, not against the cows.

**Documents.** r15, r07 (items 11–12), r16, r09, r10, r04, r11, r22, r24, r17, r02, r06, r14.

## Section A (continued)

### Story 5 — The Water Letter (1919–1929)

**Who.** Selby Vose, manager 1913–1938; the nine patron families of the Hollow; Ansel Keddie; Emmet Brant (1854–1929) and his sister Orra Keddie (1866–1948).

**What happened.** In June 1919 a printed circular went out over Vose's name and no other (typed by the clerk Bertram Kyle), warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons that added water would be looked for and dealt with; it named nobody. It went to **nine** families (Toland, whose father was one; Keddie's letter; Roy; Hazel — Orra's diary says Ansel carried it to "eleven," a count I could not reconcile; nine is the settled figure, the diary's eleven possibly including hill patrons). Strawn's 1919 entry records that he had tested with the lactometer at every visit and never found water at that station, "and I told him so" — Vose sent it anyway, and in 1926 defended every word.

Keddie would not put the letters in the mail: he carried them round himself, stood at each door while it was read, and came home at eleven at night with his hat on (Orra). Two days later Emmet Brant stood in the yard — would not come in — said he had hauled whole milk since the day the station opened and Ansel knew it, and asked why Ansel had carried the paper himself if he did not believe it. Ansel said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet never entered the house again. Roy's version agrees: his father held that the man who kept the book should have stood up against the circular.

So the tester was estranged from his own wife's brother — the patron the station's error cost most — from 1919 until Emmet's death in the spring of 1929: ten years, neither going up the road first (Orra). Toland remembers Orra walking to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane. The valley kept the paper: Toland's father's in a desk drawer until he died; one woman's pinned inside a trunk lid; Hazel's mother read hers twice and put it in the stove. Rundle: the two ends of the valley did not speak civilly for six years and the children inherited it "the way a debt does." Grigg reprinted his 1908 report that season in reply. Sill thought well of the letter. Rosalie Cudd, from the other side of the ridge: a cheesemaker knows watered milk in a day, and in sixteen summers her father never docked those men a cent.

**Family facts settled here.** Orra was Emmet's full sister, the younger (Roy, Duncan); Hazel's "half sister by a second wife" is the stepmother story Roy explicitly warned against. She was born 1866 (diary preface; "I am sixty-four" in August 1930; Duncan's "eighty-two when she died in 1948" agrees, his "born 1868" does not). Married 1889.

**Documents.** r24 (contemporaneous), r01, r03, r06, r08, r09, r12, r14, r15, r17, r18, r23, r10.

### Story 6 — Station Gain (1898–1945)

**Who.** Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945; Hobart Sill at the receiving platform; Bertram Kyle, who copied the line; Selby Vose, who signed it.

**What happened.** The station bought fat on its own test, then sent the cream down the valley, where it was weighed and tested into the vats at Larrow Green. Teague, on arriving in 1905, invented a ledger line to hold the difference — his own device, he insists, not a founders' dodge. The 1898 entry, quoted word for word by Teague, Nym, Rundle and Kyle: butterfat credited to patrons on station test **57,760 lb**; received at Larrow Green in station cream **60,800 lb**; station gain **3,040 lb, "five and a quarter per cent, as usual."** Carried to the general account. The same words every year for forty years. 3,040 ÷ 57,760 is 5.26 percent of the credited fat; 3,040 ÷ 60,800 is exactly 5 percent of the fat delivered — Teague's own claim that it was five and a quarter "of the fat actually in the cream" is wrong by his own arithmetic.

Across the twenty-five full delivery years 1898–1922 the station took in **40,000,000 lb** of milk (1,600,000 a year; Loomis copied Teague's own annual sheets and added twice; Kyle got the figure out for the arbitration; Nym's ledgers agree — Teague's memoir "44,000,000" and his "3.16" are rejected). Credited 1,444,000 lb; received 1,520,000 lb; accumulated gain **76,000 lb**, worth **$19,000** at the 25¢ average.

Sill weighed the Hollow's cream over again himself, every year from 1893, and it always came in heavy, never light — "so does everybody's," and nobody asked him. Kyle ruled under the line every year and never wondered: "A figure with a name on it has been explained." Vose signed the annual sheet every spring (Teague), reading the totals and never the lines above them (Kyle). Teague put the account to Vose once, in 1916, and was told to let it alone; he let it alone. The line was read out at every annual meeting to fifty farmers with their hats on their knees. "It was not a secret. It was worse than a secret. It was in plain sight with a name on it."

The station column begins with 1897; the 1896 season was carried on the creamery's books and no figure survives (Nym, Kyle). Teague, who insinuated theft to the board in 1925, printed the refuting entry in his own 1948 chapter without seeing it; Rundle, a schoolteacher shown the ledger in 1939, saw it at once: stolen cream would have reached the Green light, and it reached the Green heavy.

**Documents.** r05, r07 (items 6, 16), r14, r23, r12, r02, r04, r11, r13.

### Story 7 — The Burette and the Board (1921–1926)

**Who.** Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector, Ordell circuit 1907–1936; Alonzo Frick, attorney of Ashlin, chairman of the board of arbitration; the officers and the patrons.

**The condemnation.** The state issued burettes to its field men in 1921. Strawn did not run the Hollow's glass in 1922 ("the day being short"). On **12 June 1923** he did: "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them." The Association ordered on 28 June; Ammon replied 2 July; new glass was in use from July (Strawn, October 1923: delivers its mark; Orra, July 1923: "new glass at the station"). The crate went up the ladder into the loft. The 1924 average stood at 3.80. Vose and Rundle's "12 July," Kyle's "'22" and Hazel's "'21" all fall to Strawn's dated entry and the June order. The burette program was discontinued in 1926; nobody drew the inference at the time, Strawn least of all — he kept a book, not an opinion.

**The arbitration.** The patrons petitioned in January 1925, Emmet Brant's name on it. A board of three under Frick sat from 17 March to 4 May 1925: first at the Association's office at Ashlin, then twice in the station's own weigh room (Frick, Oram, Orra, Duncan, Kyle) — twenty feet beneath the crate. Three explanations were argued: water (Vose), cream taken by the tester (Teague, "in the way an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it"), and the hay (the 1908 report read in). None was proved. Exhibits, from Frick's own file: four books — the station test book (4 May 1896–31 December 1924), the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book (begun 1 January 1897), and Strawn's 1923 notebook. The condemnation was thus before the board on paper; the crate itself was never mentioned in any session (Loomis, who took every word), and nobody laid the receiving book beside the notebook (Kyle).

Keddie was dismissed on **30 April 1925**, four days before the award, with no finding against him. On **4 May** the board "finds no cause established" and awarded **$3,800** without admission, charged to the station account (Loomis's minute book; Toland's and Oram's "$3,500" are memory). The patrons' own reckoning was 67,000 lb (Frick); Rundle says the petition put it at $19,600, which no figure in the sources reproduces. Roy Brant never went to another meeting. Vose told the 1926 annual meeting it was "a generous settlement of an unproved claim" and that water had stopped "when the looking began" — although the rise he cited came with the July 1923 glass, eighteen months before the petition.

**Documents.** r06, r07 (items 7–10), r02, r03, r05, r08, r13, r17, r18, r20, r21, r22, r23, r24, r01.

### Story 8 — The Crate in the Loft (1947–1959)

**Who.** Ivy Keddie (b. 1931), Duncan's daughter and Ansel's granddaughter, graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland; Professor Leland Vaught; Rosalie Cudd; Roy and Hazel Brant; Halbert Nym, curator.

**What happened.** Strawn's twenty-eight circuit notebooks went to the state dairy office in 1936 and to the Farland library in 1947, where nobody put a question to them for eleven years. In February 1958 Ivy went up the ladder into the loft over the station's intake room and found, under a tarpaulin, the crate stenciled AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET, there since June 1923. Nine of the seventy-two pipettes were unbroken (Ivy's sheet, Nym, Oram, Falke; Rundle's footnote and the Chronicle say "eleven"; Oram's "five dozen" is the outfit misremembered). On **9 March 1958** she delivered each against a calibrated burette, five runs apiece: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 — mean **16.72 c.c.** against a 17.6 mark, short 0.88, **95 hundredths**.

She brought five records into one room for the first time: the test book from the Ashlin office; the Brant weigh book, lent by Roy in 1958 (lent, not left — Roy, Hazel and Duncan all insist); the Alder Corners figures copied by Rosalie Cudd, Jerome's daughter (Ivy and the Chronicle wrongly say niece); Strawn's 1923 volume; and the Association's ledgers. She also sat at Vira Toland's table.

**Bulletin No. 471**, October 1958, under Vaught's and Keddie's names, concludes: no milk watered, no cream stolen; from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 the tests were low by one part in twenty because the pipettes were short by one part in twenty; 76,000 lb of butterfat stands in the Association's own ledgers as station gain. Vaught's introduction carries a different set of numbers (16.27 c.c., 0.9244, 3.905, 118,000 lb, five dozen, 1 May 1898) that contradict the sheet and his own conclusion; he admits they do not reconcile with 1924's 3.80. *The Ashlin Chronicle* of 3 October 1958 spread the story and several errors (Part 3, E). Ivy read "What the Crate Held" to the Ordell County Historical Society in 1959 with the crate on the table; Falke's county report followed; Rundle added a footnote to his reprint.

**Where the records went.** Test book and crate to the Historical Rooms, 1959; weigh book to Hazel at Roy's death in 1962 and to the Rooms in 1963; Cudd's book to the Vessey County Historical Society, 1970. Keddie had been dead twenty-four years, dismissed thirty-three; Orra died in 1948. The station itself had closed in 1946.

**Documents.** r04, r07, r10, r11, r12, r17, r18, r19, r21, r22, r01, r09, r06.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** Ansel (b. 1861) married Orra Brant in 1889; their son Duncan was born 1894 (Orra's diary has him five in September 1899 and seventeen in 1911); Duncan's daughter Ivy was born 1931 (Duncan; the Chronicle has her twenty-seven in 1958, which agrees). Ivy herself opens her 1959 talk "Ansel Keddie … was my grandfather. My father, Duncan Keddie … was his son." Roy Brant calls her "Duncan's girl." Against this stand the Chronicle's "great-niece" and Falke's first draft — but Falke, on verifying, corrected himself to granddaughter, and the Chronicle was assembled "in a day and a half from a college press release and two telephone calls." Family testimony from three generations, plus Falke's retraction, outranks a newspaper's guess.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins.** Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie were brother and sister; Emmet's son Roy and Orra's son Duncan were therefore first cousins (both say so); Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy, the children of first cousins, are second cousins — stated by Roy, Hazel, Duncan and Rundle's footnote. Toland and Oram say "first cousin once removed," which would require Ivy and Hazel to stand in different generations from the sibling pair; they do not. What the sources leave open: whether Emmet and Orra were full or half siblings (Hazel says half, by their father's second wife; Roy and Duncan say full — I take full, but if Hazel were right the two women would be half second cousins); Hazel's exact birth year (she was "small" in 1919, so roughly two decades older than Ivy, which is what tempts people to add "removed"); Roy's mother's name; and whether Emmet had other children.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose**, first president 1889–1911 and author of the by-laws. Selby says it himself in 1926 — "My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law under which every patron … is paid on the test of the station where he delivers." Loomis ("son of the late president"), Duncan and Kyle agree; Toland and Rundle say nephew, which Duncan and Kyle both identify as an Ashlin story. It matters because the man who managed the Association through the whole crisis — who signed the station-gain sheet every spring, told Teague in 1916 to let it alone, sent the 1919 circular, defended the by-law in 1926 and kept the Jerseys that tested 4.56 — was defending his father's rule and his father's Association, and the Association was the party that had received the 76,000 lb. Toland: "the family always thought the valley belonged to them a little." Loyalty to the clause his father wrote is the simplest account of why he looked at the patrons and never at the station.

**B4. No relationship between the Voses and the Keddies can be established, and the best-placed witness denies one.** Toland says Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins, "everybody knew that"; Oram says the families "were cousins of some sort," had it from his grandmother, and "could not draw you the line on paper." Duncan Keddie, Orra's son, says flatly there was none — his mother, who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection," never once put a Vose in it, and people confused Gideon's family with unrelated Ashlin Voses. Two admitted hearsay reports against a specific denial from inside the family: the relationship is unproven and probably nonexistent. A negative cannot be closed absolutely, but no source offers a name or a line.

## Section C — Math

**C1.**
(a) Nine surviving pipettes, blown 17.6 c.c., delivered (mean of five runs each) 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Sum 150.48; ÷ 9 = **16.72 c.c.** Short 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c. Ratio 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95**. The bottle neck is ruled for a 17.6 charge; a charge 95% of the mark yields a fat column 95% of the true one, so every test read **95% of the truth — low by one part in twenty.** (Vaught's introduction's 16.27 c.c. / 0.9244 is a transposition of the sheet's 16.72; the nine readings average 16.72, and 0.95 is the only ratio that reproduces the observed 3.61 → 3.80.)

(b) Recorded average 3.61 (summary leaf). True average 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. Short by **0.19 of a point**. Cross-checks: 3.80 on the borrowed Larrow Green glass in 1896, 3.80 in the nine weeks of 1911, 3.80 in 1924 on the new glass, 3.78–3.82 at Alder Corners.

(c) Milk over the 25 full delivery years 1898–1922: 40,000,000 lb. Credited 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb. Delivered 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb. Not credited: **76,000 lb** (= 40,000,000 × 0.0019).

(d) 76,000 × $0.25 (ledger average price) = **$19,000**. Award $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = **0.20 — one fifth.**

**C2.** Emmet Brant, 1910: 200,000 lb at a paid test of 3.42.
(a) Credited 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb**.
(b) True test 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60; 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb. Owed and not credited **360 lb** (= 200,000 × 0.0018).
(c) Butterfat that year 24¢ (Roy): 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40**. (At the 25-year average of 25¢ it would be $90.00; the 1910 price is the one the question asks for.)

**C3.** (a) Credited **1,444,000 lb**. (b) Reached Larrow Green **1,520,000 lb**. (c) Difference 76,000 lb = **5.00% of the fat delivered** (76,000 ÷ 1,520,000) and **5.26% of the fat credited** (76,000 ÷ 1,444,000) — the ledger's "five and a quarter per cent" is taken of the smaller, credited figure, which is the number a bookkeeper had already entered. Teague's and Vaught's "of the fat actually delivered/in the cream" is the wrong denominator: 3,040 ÷ 60,800 in the 1898 entry is exactly 5%.

**C4.** (a) $25.20 + $31.68 + $0.90 + $18.00 + $2.15 = **$77.93** (the invoice total; "$77.39" fails the sum). (b) Six dozen = **72 pipettes**, each short by **0.88 c.c.** (assuming, as Ammon's account of a reground mold implies and the nine survivors' spread of 0.04 c.c. supports, that the lot was uniform).

**C5.** (a) The faulty glass was in use from **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 — twenty-six years and six weeks** (26 years, 42 days). The money runs over **twenty-five full delivery years, 1898 through 1922.** The numbers differ because the ledgers count calendar years, and 1897 (short glass only from 1 May; "part year" on the summary leaf) and 1923 (short glass to 12 June, good glass from July) are mixed years that cannot be assigned to either instrument; 1896 was on Larrow Green's correct glass and is not in the station column at all. (b) **Cannot be determined.** The station column begins with 1897; the 1896 season was carried on the creamery's books and the sheets were never put with the station papers (Nym, Kyle). Sill remembers "about nine hundred thousand"; Kyle "a good eleven hundred thousand"; two conflicting recollections and no document.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran from **8 May to 10 July 1911 — nine weeks** (63 days), recorded in Keddie's own dated entries. The variable: the milk pipette — the station's own short measure replaced by a borrowed one that delivered its mark. Held constant: the patrons, herds, pasture and season; the acid, bottles and hand tester; the scales; the tester and his habits; the ten-day composite method. Result: the station average rose from 3.61 to 3.80 on 8 May and returned to 3.61 on 10 July, the day the station's own measure came back — by 0.19, the same 5% the burette found forty-seven years later. What it establishes that a calculation cannot: that the cause lived in the measure and nowhere else, since nothing else changed — it clears the cows, the land, the cans and the man's hands in one stroke, and shows the effect was already present in 1911 and was reversible within a day. What it does *not* establish: the size of the shortfall in cubic centimeters (there was no burette); that the borrowed measure was itself exactly true (it might have over-delivered — only the agreement with Larrow Green's 3.78, Alder Corners' 3.80 and 1924's 3.80 supports its accuracy); that the fault dated from 1897 (only that it existed in 1911); where the borrowed measure came from (Ivy: Larrow Green; Roy: the state man; unsettled); and, on its own, that a coincident summer effect was not at work (Kepp's objection) — that is answered by the gap's flatness across all months, which is a separate observation.

**D2.** Independent reasons the tester cannot have been taking cream:
1. *The ledger.* Cream that reached Larrow Green carried more fat than the station had credited, every year — 60,800 against 57,760 in 1898, 76,000 lb over 25 years (Teague's entries; Nym; Kyle). Stolen cream would have arrived light; it arrived heavy. Sill's independent re-weighing in the receiving book says the same. Rundle saw it in one reading. This reason needs no 1958 measurement.
2. *The nine weeks of 1911.* The same man, alone, under no different supervision, produced 3.80 for nine weeks when the measure changed and 3.61 when it changed back (Keddie's book; Falke's paragraph 8). A thief's figures do not rise because he borrows a pipette. Also independent of 1958.
3. *The 1923–24 rise.* The tests went to 3.80 in July 1923 and stayed there through 1924 with Keddie still at the bench (Strawn 1924: "Their book gives the year at 3.80") — before the petition and the hearing. If the man were the cause, replacing the glass would have changed nothing. Independent of 1958.
4. *The shape of the error.* It was proportional (rich herds lost more: 4.56 short 0.24, 3.42 short 0.18), precise to three hundredths for twenty-five years, and identical in every month (Vaught, Ivy, Roy, Hazel). Skimming by hand yields scatter and an additive loss, not a fixed fraction.
5. *The outside tests.* Alder Corners tested the same milk at 3.80 in summer (Rosalie Cudd); the gap was in the *test*, before any cream existed to steal, and Roy's weigh book agreed with the station scale, so nothing was lost at the intake either.
6. *The inspector.* Strawn stood at his elbow in 1907, 1911 and after; scales true, work slow and clean.
These are independent because they rest on different records kept by different people for different purposes — the bookkeeper's ledger, the tester's own book, the inspector's notebook, the cheesemaker's book, the farmer's weigh book — and on different logic (accounting balance, controlled interval, timing, error structure, external comparison). Reasons 1, 2, 3 and 5 all stand if the crate had never been opened.
*The bookkeeper.* Teague never questioned the entries. He called the line furniture, said evenness "stops him looking," raised "it" once with Vose in 1916 and dropped it, and in 1948 printed the 1898 entry in the same chapter in which he repeated the accusation, without noticing that the one refutes the other. Kyle: "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years."

**D3.** Yes. Vose knew from the start of his managership that the station's tests ran low — it was the standing complaint from 1902, the reason for his 1919 circular ("a manager who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing…"), and his own words in 1926: "for a generation the milk brought to that station tested below the milk brought to Larrow Green." He had also been told, twice, what would have explained it: Teague put the station-gain account to him in 1916 and was told to let it alone, and Strawn told him in 1919 that no water had ever been found. He signed the sheet carrying the gain line every spring (Teague), though Kyle says he read only the totals. What the sources do *not* establish is that he understood the gain line's meaning — Teague: "What he made of it he never told me." So: he knew the tests were low, chose watering as the reason, and had the refutation under his signature yearly without drawing it.

## Section F — Theory

**The theory.** In April 1897 the Association bought six dozen milk-test pipettes from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet, blown to a 17.6 c.c. mark. The lot ran uniformly short — the survivors deliver 16.72 c.c., 0.88 short, 95 percent — most plausibly from a reground mold that made every piece consistently wrong (Ammon's guess; unproven). The Babcock bottle neck is ruled for a 17.6 charge, so every test made with that glass read exactly 95 percent of the truth. **Size and why that size:** 3.80 × 0.95 = 3.61; a fixed fraction, hence 0.19 on the station average, 0.24 on a 4.56 herd, 0.18 on a 3.42 herd, and the same in February as in July. **Why it began when it did:** the station opened 4 May 1896 on Hobart Sill's correct Larrow Green glass (1896 = 3.80); its own outfit went into use 1 May 1897, and the 1898 average fell to 3.61. **Why it ended when it did:** the pipette was the standard the plant measured with and could be caught only by a burette from outside; the state issued burettes in 1921, Strawn skipped the check in 1922, ran it on 12 June 1923, condemned the lot, and new glass delivering its mark went in in July — 1924 = 3.80. **Why nothing had to change:** the error lived in the one instrument that was itself the measure; herds, land, cans, scales, acid and the man were unaltered at both ends, and the nine weeks of 1911 show the same switch in miniature. The missing fat was never missing: it went down the valley in the cream, was weighed at Larrow Green, and was booked as "station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual" — 76,000 lb, $19,000, over 1898–1922 — to the Association's general account.

**The three competing explanations.**
1. *Water in the cans* — Selby Vose (1919 circular; 1926 address), Hobart Sill. Defeated by Strawn's lactometer, which never once found added water at that station from 1907 on and said so to the manager in 1919; by Cudd's cheese vat, which would have shown watered milk within a day and never did in sixteen summers; by the weigh books agreeing (water adds weight, and the error was in the test proportion, not the pounds); and by Vose's own dates — the rise came with the new glass in July 1923, eighteen months before anyone "looked."
2. *The tester stealing cream and writing low tests* — Wilbur Teague before the board and in 1948; the valley's "settled opinion" (Rundle); Oram's father. Defeated by the ledger itself: cream reached the Green heavy every year, not light; by the 1911 interval, in which the same man's figures rose when only the measure changed; by 1924's 3.80 with Keddie still at the bench; by the error's precision and proportionality; and by Strawn watching the work.
3. *The wet hay of Hessel Bottom* — Grigg's 1908 committee; Dr. Kepp (1926); Falke's first draft. Defeated by Bulletin 214 (≤0.05 in winter, nothing in summer, against 0.19 flat all year); by Alder Corners testing the same herds off the same ground at 3.80 every summer; by the 1911 and 1923 changes with the land untouched; and by Larrow Green's own 3.78 — the Hollow's true 3.80 was ordinary milk.

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?** No. Strawn's 1923 notebook recording the condemnation was one of the four exhibits (Frick's list), and two sessions sat in the weigh room twenty feet under the crate; but the crate was never mentioned in any session (Loomis, who took every word), the "appliances" the board saw tried were the new glass of 1925, and nobody laid the receiving book beside the notebook (Kyle). Frick himself wonders whether the answer "was in that room and simply was not in any of the three stories."

**What the tester believed.** His own words settle only this much: he could not say why the figures were what they were, would write nothing he could not prove, left a question mark standing fourteen years, and in his unsent letter defended the patrons against the circular. Beyond that the sources conflict — Duncan remembers his father saying in 1911 "it was something in the glass" and never daring to write it; Frick remembers a corridor remark that the patrons were at fault. The letter tells against Frick's version and the 1911 entries fit Duncan's; but neither can be proven, and the honest answer is that he suspected the glass, could not prove it, and named no cause.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** In 1889 the Larrow Valley farmers formed a creamery cooperative whose by-law paid each patron on the butterfat test of the station where he delivered, with no appeal. In 1897 their Ostrey Hollow station received a lot of milk pipettes blown for 17.6 c.c. that delivered 16.72 — five percent short — so for twenty-six years every test there read 95 percent of the truth: 3.61 instead of 3.80. The unpaid fat, 76,000 pounds, went down the valley in the cream and was booked yearly as "station gain." The valley blamed the hay, the patrons' cans, and the tester, Ansel Keddie, who was dismissed in 1925 as a board awarded $3,800 without finding cause. In 1923 a state inspector had condemned the glass; nobody connected it. In 1958 Keddie's granddaughter found the crate in the loft, measured nine pipettes, and joined the tester's book, a farmer's weigh book, a cheesemaker's figures, the inspector's notebooks and the ledgers — records no one had ever read together — to clear him twenty-four years after his death.
