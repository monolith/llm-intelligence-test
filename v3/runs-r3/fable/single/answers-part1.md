# Answer sheet — part 1 of 3 (Section A, stories 1–4)

Conventions: r01–r24 are the retellings in bundle order. Where I resolve a conflict I say what decided it: a quoted document, arithmetic, a contemporaneous entry, or a narrator's own internal consistency. Where the sources do not decide, I say "cannot be determined."

## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1. The Rule and the Room — the Association, Article VII, and the station it built (1889–1897)

In 1889 the farmers of the Larrow Valley met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose, first president 1889–1911, drafted the by-laws himself; the creamery was built at Larrow Green in 1891. Article VII pays a patron on the pounds of butterfat found by test, not on weight, "and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" — no appeal, no cross-check. Its adoption year cannot be determined: Loomis says 1892, Rundle 1891, Grigg 1893; the printed by-laws say only "adopted at the annual meeting," and the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move. Selby Vose was Gideon's son — his own words (r08), Loomis, Duncan Keddie, Kyle; the "nephew" of Vira and Rundle is valley talk — and was manager 1913–1938 (Kyle's 1911 loses to Vose's own "thirteen years since 1913" and Strawn's 1913 entry).

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at the head of Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley. Ansel Keddie (born 1861; married Orra Brant 1889) went up in 1895 to ready it and was tester and separator man, alone in the building, for twenty-nine seasons, 1896–1924. It opened on 4 May 1896 with no outfit of its own: Hobart Sill made one up from his own glass at Larrow Green, and Keddie's first leaf reads "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes." That season averaged 3.80. Its intake is in no ledger — Sill recalls about 900,000 lb, Kyle "a good eleven hundred thousand" by hearsay — and cannot be determined.

The station's own outfit came on an Ammon & Sons (Tarnet) invoice of 14 April 1897: six dozen milk-test pipettes blown 17.6 c.c. at 35¢ ($25.20), twelve dozen test bottles ($31.68), acid measure (90¢), four-bottle hand tester ($18.00), freight ($2.15): total $77.93 — the lines sum to it; Ammon's prose and the Chronicle transpose it to $77.39. It went into use on 1 May 1897: Orra's diary that day, the office file, Ammon, and Bulletin 471's conclusion agree; Vaught's method section and Kyle say 1898, Hazel 1899 — wrong, and the test book's "1897, part year" fits a May switch. From 1898 the yearly average was 3.61, within three hundredths. Documents: printed by-laws (Nym Item 1); Loomis's minutes; the invoice (Item 2; r20 facsimile); the test book's first and summary leaves (Item 3); Orra's diary; Sill (r14).

### Story 2. Three Wrong Answers — the grievance and its explanations (1902–1926)

From 1902 the Ostrey Hollow men said their milk tested under the same class of milk at Larrow Green (Keddie, Roy Brant, Rundle, Grigg, Sill; Orra's diary, March 1902). The station averaged 3.61; Larrow Green's own milk ran 3.78 (Sill, Falke; Nym Item 16) — a gap of 0.17 to the creamery, the same in July and February, wet years and dry (Roy, Hazel, Keddie, Falke). Three explanations grew up; each blamed people or land.

Hay. In January 1908 a committee of three under Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green, none a station patron, sampled the six Hessel Bottom herds in January, February and March (the signed report and Orra's diary agree; Grigg's 1919 claim of "every month of that year" contradicts his own report). It found "no dishonesty of any kind," the milk whole, the scales honest, and blamed the wet hay: "the remedy lies with the land and not with the station." Bulletin No. 214 (1911) fed bog hay as the whole roughage and found the test depressed "by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Grigg (1919) and Dr. Roland Kepp (1926) both cite it as "two tenths" — wrong; the printed text survives (Nym Item 12). Falke conceded hay as a partial factor in 1959, then withdrew it on reading the bulletin: five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer "will not carry nineteen hundredths in July."

Water. In June 1919 manager Selby Vose sent a circular, typed by clerk Bertram Kyle and signed by Vose alone, warning the station's patrons — nine families (Vira, Keddie, Roy, Hazel, Sill; Orra's diary says eleven) — that added water would be looked for. Inspector Merle Strawn had run the lactometer at every visit since 1907, never found water, and told Vose so that year. Rosalie Cudd: watered milk cannot be hidden from a cheesemaker, and in sixteen summers her father never complained of the Bottom men. Vose still defended the circular in 1926.

Theft. Bookkeeper Wilbur Teague told the 1925 board that one man alone with a separator can "take a little and write down a little less"; the valley's settled opinion (Rundle 1939, Vira, Cleve Oram's father) was that cream left by the back door. The ledgers refute it (Story 5). Two days after the circular Emmet Brant stood in the Keddies' yard, asked why Ansel had carried the paper himself, and never entered the house again. Documents: the 1908 report (Nym Item 11; Grigg's reprint, r15); Bulletin 214 (Item 12); Kepp's paper (r16); Strawn's 1919 entry (r06); Orra's diary (r24); Vose's address (r08).

### Story 3. Nine Weeks in 1911 — the experiment nobody knew was one

On the morning of 8 May 1911 the station's milk pipette broke. Ansel Keddie entered it in the test book — "Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." — and Orra's diary that evening has him "home cross about a broken measure." He tested with the borrowed glass until 10 July, when he wrote: "Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." From 8 May to 10 July is sixty-three days: nine weeks, as Keddie, Ivy, Roy Brant, Vaught and Falke say. Teague and Kepp both say six weeks; the dated entries settle it, and Kepp's "June and July" is wrong too.

The station average, 3.61 in every year since 1898, stood at 3.80 from the first morning on the borrowed measure and fell back to 3.61 the day the station's own returned. Every farm on the Bottom rose together — Emmet Brant entered the rise and the fall in his weigh book and could make nothing of them. Where the borrowed measure came from cannot be determined: Ivy says from Larrow Green with the cream wagon; Roy says the state man left his own "that June"; Keddie says only "borrowed," and Strawn's June 1911 entry has "nothing here to note" — his visit came after the break, so Roy's version does not fit. Teague booked a small replacement charge and nothing else.

Keddie came home that summer saying, twice, that "it was something in the glass" — Duncan, then seventeen, remembers it — but he would not write what he could not prove; the question mark stood fourteen years and he went on with the same glass through 1924. Vaught (Bulletin 471, §IV) called it an experiment that ran itself: same patrons, herds, pasture, acid, bottles, hand tester and man; one variable changed; it "disposes of every explanation that looks to the herds, to the ground or to the men." The interval showed where the fault lay, and its size — 0.19, one part in twenty — forty-seven years before anyone measured a pipette. Documents: the two dated entries (Nym Item 3; quoted in r03, r04); Orra's diary, 8 May 1911; Brant weigh book; Teague's ledger; Duncan's notes (r18); Bulletin 471 (r11).

### Story 4. Two Honest Books — Emmet Brant's weigh book and the four men over the ridge (1897–1930)

Hessel Bottom is 160 acres of wet meadow under Ostrey Hollow: six farms (the 1908 report, Roy and Hazel Brant, Kepp, the Chronicle; Loomis's "seven" is wrong) and nine families. The largest was Emmet Brant's (1854–1929) — sixty-odd acres, thirty-eight cows, the station's biggest patron every year. Emmet began a weigh book on 1 January 1897 (Frick's 1925 exhibit list, Nym, Duncan, Ivy; Roy remembers 1898 and Hazel 1900 — memory against a document). Two columns a day: the weight off his own barn scale, and the station's weight for the same cans. They agreed for thirty years "barring a pound or two" — so whatever was wrong at the station, it was not the weighing. His 1910 leaf: "Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42." Butterfat was 24¢ that year, about 25¢ over the period. The book went to Roy in 1929, was lent to Ivy in 1958 and returned, to Hazel in 1962, and to the Historical Rooms in 1963.

Orra Keddie was Emmet's sister — full, by Roy's explicit account; Hazel says half, by a second wife; Roy, a generation nearer, is preferred, though no document decides it. The tester was married into the family the station cost the most.

Over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County, Jerome Cudd built the Alder Corners Cheese Company in 1913 (his daughter Rosalie; Roy and Rundle say it opened in 1914 — reconcilable, unsettled). From 1915, for sixteen summers through 1930, four of the six Bottom men hauled to him from about 1 June to mid-September and to Ostrey Hollow the rest of the year; Roy stayed home. Cudd tested on his own bench with his own glass and never compared a figure with anyone. His 1919 entry: "3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge." Every summer 1915–1930 the four together sat between 3.78 and 3.82 — about 3.80. A cheesemaker knows watered milk by the next morning's curd; in sixteen summers Cudd never docked or complained of them. Rosalie — Jerome's daughter, not his niece as Ivy and the Chronicle say — copied the sixteen seasons for Ivy in 1958; the book never left Vessey County. Against the station's 3.61, the same cows off the same wet ground read about 0.19 higher wherever the station's glass was not used. Documents: Brant weigh book (Nym Item 4; Frick exhibit 3); Cudd's letter and table (r10); Alder Corners test book (Nym Item 13).
