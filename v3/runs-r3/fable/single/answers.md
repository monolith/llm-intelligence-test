# Answer sheet — part 1 of 3 (Section A, stories 1–4)

Conventions: r01–r24 are the retellings in bundle order. Where I resolve a conflict I say what decided it: a quoted document, arithmetic, a contemporaneous entry, or a narrator's own internal consistency. Where the sources do not decide, I say "cannot be determined."

## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1. The Rule and the Room — the Association, Article VII, and the station it built (1889–1897)

In 1889 the farmers of the Larrow Valley met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose, first president 1889–1911, drafted the by-laws himself; the creamery was built at Larrow Green in 1891. Article VII pays a patron on the pounds of butterfat found by test, not on weight, "and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" — no appeal, no cross-check. Its adoption year cannot be determined: Loomis says 1892, Rundle 1891, Grigg 1893; the printed by-laws say only "adopted at the annual meeting," and the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move. Selby Vose was Gideon's son — his own words (r08), Loomis, Duncan Keddie, Kyle; the "nephew" of Vira and Rundle is valley talk — and was manager 1913–1938 (Kyle's 1911 loses to Vose's own "thirteen years since 1913" and Strawn's 1913 entry).

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at the head of Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley. Ansel Keddie (born 1861; married Orra Brant 1889) went up in 1895 to ready it and was tester and separator man, alone in the building, for twenty-nine seasons, 1896–1924. It opened on 4 May 1896 with no outfit of its own: Hobart Sill made one up from his own glass at Larrow Green, and Keddie's first leaf reads "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes." That season averaged 3.80. Its intake is in no ledger — Sill recalls about 900,000 lb, Kyle "a good eleven hundred thousand" by hearsay — and cannot be determined.

The station's own outfit came on an Ammon & Sons (Tarnet) invoice of 14 April 1897: six dozen milk-test pipettes blown 17.6 c.c. at 35¢ ($25.20), twelve dozen test bottles ($31.68), acid measure (90¢), four-bottle hand tester ($18.00), freight ($2.15): total $77.93 — the lines sum to it; Ammon's prose and the Chronicle transpose it to $77.39. It went into use on 1 May 1897: Orra's diary that day, the office file, Ammon, and Bulletin 471's conclusion agree; Vaught's method section and Kyle say 1898, Hazel 1899 — wrong, and the test book's "1897, part year" fits a May switch. From 1898 the yearly average was 3.61, within three hundredths. Documents: printed by-laws (Nym Item 1); Loomis's minutes; the invoice (Item 2; r20 facsimile); the test book's first and summary leaves (Item 3); Orra's diary; Sill (r14).

### Story 2. Three Wrong Answers — the grievance and its explanations (1902–1926)

From 1902 the Ostrey Hollow men said their milk tested under the same class of milk at Larrow Green (Keddie, Roy Brant, Rundle, Grigg, Sill; Orra's diary, March 1902). The station averaged 3.61; Larrow Green's own milk ran 3.78 (Sill, Falke; Nym Item 16) — a gap of 0.17 to the creamery, the same in July and February, wet years and dry (Roy, Hazel, Keddie, Falke). Three explanations grew up; each blamed people or land.

Hay. In January 1908 a committee of three under Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green, none a station patron, sampled the six Hessel Bottom herds in January, February and March (the signed report and Orra's diary agree; Grigg's 1919 claim of "every month of that year" contradicts his own report). It found "no dishonesty of any kind," the milk whole, the scales honest, and blamed the wet hay: "the remedy lies with the land and not with the station." Bulletin No. 214 (1911) fed bog hay as the whole roughage and found the test depressed "by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Grigg (1919) and Dr. Roland Kepp (1926) both cite it as "two tenths" — wrong; the printed text survives (Nym Item 12). Falke conceded hay as a partial factor in 1959, then withdrew it on reading the bulletin: five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer "will not carry nineteen hundredths in July."

Water. In June 1919 manager Selby Vose sent a circular, typed by clerk Bertram Kyle and signed by Vose alone, warning the station's patrons — nine families (Vira, Keddie, Roy, Hazel, Sill; Orra's diary says eleven) — that added water would be looked for. Inspector Merle Strawn had run the lactometer at every visit since 1907, never found water, and told Vose so that year. Rosalie Cudd: watered milk cannot be hidden from a cheesemaker, and in sixteen summers her father never complained of the Bottom men. Vose still defended the circular in 1926.

Theft. Bookkeeper Wilbur Teague told the 1925 board that one man alone with a separator can "take a little and write down a little less"; the valley's settled opinion (Rundle 1939, Vira, Cleve Oram's father) was that cream left by the back door. The ledgers refute it (Story 5). Two days after the circular Emmet Brant stood in the Keddies' yard, asked why Ansel had carried the paper himself, and never entered the house again. Documents: the 1908 report (Nym Item 11; Grigg's reprint, r15); Bulletin 214 (Item 12); Kepp's paper (r16); Strawn's 1919 entry (r06); Orra's diary (r24); Vose's address (r08).

### Story 3. Nine Weeks in 1911 — the experiment nobody knew was one

On the morning of 8 May 1911 the station's milk pipette broke. Ansel Keddie entered it in the test book — "Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." — and Orra's diary that evening has him "home cross about a broken measure." He tested with the borrowed glass until 10 July, when he wrote: "Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." From 8 May to 10 July is sixty-three days: nine weeks, as Keddie, Ivy, Roy Brant, Vaught and Falke say. Teague and Kepp both say six weeks; the dated entries settle it, and Kepp's "June and July" is wrong too.

The station average, 3.61 in every year since 1898, stood at 3.80 from the first morning on the borrowed measure and fell back to 3.61 the day the station's own returned. Every farm on the Bottom rose together — Emmet Brant entered the rise and the fall in his weigh book and could make nothing of them. Where the borrowed measure came from cannot be determined: Ivy says from Larrow Green with the cream wagon; Roy says the state man left his own "that June"; Keddie says only "borrowed," and Strawn's June 1911 entry has "nothing here to note" — his visit came after the break, so Roy's version does not fit. Teague booked a small replacement charge and nothing else.

Keddie came home that summer saying, twice, that "it was something in the glass" — Duncan, then seventeen, remembers it — but he would not write what he could not prove; the question mark stood fourteen years and he went on with the same glass through 1924. Vaught (Bulletin 471, §IV) called it an experiment that ran itself: same patrons, herds, pasture, acid, bottles, hand tester and man; one variable changed; it "disposes of every explanation that looks to the herds, to the ground or to the men." The interval showed where the fault lay, and its size — 0.19, one part in twenty — forty-seven years before anyone measured a pipette. Documents: the two dated entries (Nym Item 3; quoted in r03, r04); Orra's diary, 8 May 1911; Brant weigh book; Teague's ledger; Duncan's notes (r18); Bulletin 471 (r11).

### Story 4. Two Honest Books — Emmet Brant's weigh book and the four men over the ridge (1897–1930)

Hessel Bottom is 160 acres of wet meadow under Ostrey Hollow: six farms (the 1908 report, Roy and Hazel Brant, Kepp, the Chronicle; Loomis's "seven" is wrong) and nine families. The largest was Emmet Brant's (1854–1929) — sixty-odd acres, thirty-eight cows, the station's biggest patron every year. Emmet began a weigh book on 1 January 1897 (Frick's 1925 exhibit list, Nym, Duncan, Ivy; Roy remembers 1898 and Hazel 1900 — memory against a document). Two columns a day: the weight off his own barn scale, and the station's weight for the same cans. They agreed for thirty years "barring a pound or two" — so whatever was wrong at the station, it was not the weighing. His 1910 leaf: "Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42." Butterfat was 24¢ that year, about 25¢ over the period. The book went to Roy in 1929, was lent to Ivy in 1958 and returned, to Hazel in 1962, and to the Historical Rooms in 1963.

Orra Keddie was Emmet's sister — full, by Roy's explicit account; Hazel says half, by a second wife; Roy, a generation nearer, is preferred, though no document decides it. The tester was married into the family the station cost the most.

Over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County, Jerome Cudd built the Alder Corners Cheese Company in 1913 (his daughter Rosalie; Roy and Rundle say it opened in 1914 — reconcilable, unsettled). From 1915, for sixteen summers through 1930, four of the six Bottom men hauled to him from about 1 June to mid-September and to Ostrey Hollow the rest of the year; Roy stayed home. Cudd tested on his own bench with his own glass and never compared a figure with anyone. His 1919 entry: "3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge." Every summer 1915–1930 the four together sat between 3.78 and 3.82 — about 3.80. A cheesemaker knows watered milk by the next morning's curd; in sixteen summers Cudd never docked or complained of them. Rosalie — Jerome's daughter, not his niece as Ivy and the Chronicle say — copied the sixteen seasons for Ivy in 1958; the book never left Vessey County. Against the station's 3.61, the same cows off the same wet ground read about 0.19 higher wherever the station's glass was not used. Documents: Brant weigh book (Nym Item 4; Frick exhibit 3); Cudd's letter and table (r10); Alder Corners test book (Nym Item 13).

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### Story 5. Five and a Quarter Per Cent, As Usual — the station-gain line (1897–1948)

From 1897 the Association's annual ledgers carried a station column for Ostrey Hollow, and every year it held three figures and a sentence. For 1898: "butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account." The fat that came down the hill in the cream was always more than the station said it had bought — never less. The arithmetic the ledger never did: 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26 per cent of the credited fat; 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.00 per cent of the fat received; 57,760 ÷ 1,600,000 lb = 3.61 per cent; 60,800 ÷ 1,600,000 = 3.80. Both the recorded test and the true one stood in the book every year. Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945, wrote the line. He says the gain was five and a quarter per cent "of the fat that was actually in the cream"; Vaught repeats that denominator; both are wrong by the entry's own figures — five and a quarter of the credited, five of the delivered (Ivy, Nym).

Hobart Sill, buttermaker 1893–1934, re-weighed the Hollow cream himself as it came into the cold room: "That cream ran heavy. Every year… Always more. Never less." He made nothing of it — "so does everybody's" — and went on blaming water. Manager Selby Vose signed every annual sheet; clerk Bertram Kyle says he read the totals and never the lines. Teague put the account to Vose in 1916, was told to let it alone, and did.

Over the twenty-five full delivery years 1898–1922 the station took in 40,000,000 lb of milk (Loomis added Teague's columns twice; Kyle got the figure out for the arbitration; Teague's and Rundle's 44,000,000 do not fit 25 × 1,600,000), was credited 1,444,000 lb of fat, delivered 1,520,000, gain 76,000 lb — $19,000 at the ledger's 25¢ average. Teague's "3.16" average transposes the 3.61 his own entry implies. Dell Rundle, shown the ledger in 1939 while his pamphlet was in press, retracted the theft story on the page: stolen cream would have reached the Green light; it reached it heavy. Bulletin 471 ends there: the missing fat "stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain." Kyle: it was "in plain sight with a name on it." Documents: annual ledgers (Nym Item 6); Larrow Green receiving and churn books (Item 16); Teague's chapter (r05); Kyle (r23); Sill (r14); Rundle's note (r12).

### Story 6. Glass Is Glass — Merle Strawn and the condemnation (1907–1926)

Merle Strawn took the state's Ordell dairy circuit in 1907 — his own covering note and first entry; Ivy and Falke say 1908 — and laid it down in March 1936. Four checks at every plant, always in the same order: scales against sealed weights, lactometer for added water, boiler and cold room, and a test watched through at the tester's elbow. At Ostrey Hollow the scales were true at every visit, the lactometer never found water — "as always here" — and Keddie worked "slow and clean." In 1919 he told the manager the circular was wrong: he had tested for water at every visit and never found any. In 1921 the state issued burettes for checking pipettes; in 1922 he did not run Ostrey Hollow's glass, "the day being short."

On 12 June 1923 he ran the station's milk pipettes against the burette: "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them." The date is fixed by his entry and its photostat, by Ammon's letter of 2 July acknowledging the Association's order of 28 June, and by Orra's diary for July 1923 ("new glass at the station"); Vose and Rundle say 12 July, Kyle 1922, Hazel 1921 — all wrong. Ammon & Sons entered the order for six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles, shipped in July, and could say nothing of the 1897 lot: "our books of the nineties are not such." Their works books burned in 1931; Junia Ammon's guess is a reground mold and a lot consistently — therefore undetectably — short. The new glass delivered its mark when Strawn checked it in October. The condemned crate went up the ladder into the loft over the intake room. In 1924, Keddie still at the bench, "tests improved. Their book gives the year at 3.80." The burette program was discontinued in 1926.

Strawn drew no inference about the twenty-six years behind the glass — "I do not keep an estimation, I keep a book" — and nobody at the Association did either: no more was said of it "than of a broken window" (Kyle). His notebooks went to the state dairy office in 1936 and to the Farland college library in 1947, unopened for eleven years. Documents: Strawn's circuit books and 1936 extracts (r06); the 1923 photostat (Nym Item 9); the Ammon letter (Item 10; r20); Orra's diary (r24); Kyle's order entry (r23).

### Story 7. No Cause Established — the arbitration of 1925 and the ruin of Ansel Keddie (1925–1948)

In January 1925 the Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned the Association. A board of three was constituted, chaired by Alonzo Frick, attorney, of Ashlin. It sat from 17 March to 4 May 1925 in three sessions: the first at the Association's office at Ashlin, the second and third in the station's own weigh room at Ostrey Hollow, with the condemned crate in the loft twenty feet above. Four exhibits and nothing else: the station test book (4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924), the station receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book (lent, and wanted back), and Strawn's circuit notebook for 1923. The crate was never mentioned; the board saw the scales tried and a test made on the new glass. Three explanations were argued — water (Vose), theft (Teague, by insinuation), hay (the 1908 report read into the record) — and none proved.

On 17 March Keddie made a nine-sentence statement: tests made "as I was taught and as the state man saw me do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons"; "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are"; "I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station." Frick believed him. Frick also recalls a corridor remark that Keddie "had always thought the patrons were at fault" — contradicted by Keddie's own unsent letter ("I know what came into it") and by Orra's diary; I do not credit it. On 30 April 1925, four days before the award, the Association dismissed him. On 4 May the board "finds no cause established" and awarded the patrons $3,800, "without admission, to be charged to the station account" — the minute book's words; Vira and Oram remember $3,500, wrongly. The patrons' own reckoning had been 67,000 lb of fat — about 0.17 × 40,000,000, the gap to Larrow Green — and the award was a fifth of the true $19,000.

Keddie kept a few cows and turned carpenter. Emmet died in 1929, the two men still unspeaking; Ansel died at home in February 1934, uncleared. Orra died in 1948. Vose told the 1926 annual meeting it had been "a generous settlement of an unproved claim" and that the cause was water. Documents: the board's minute book (Nym Item 7; r02); Keddie's statement (Item 8; r03) and unsent letter; Frick's exhibit list (r13); Vose's address (r08); Orra's diary (r24); Teague (r05); Kyle (r23).

### Story 8. What the Crate Held — Ivy Keddie's rediscovery (1958–1959)

Ivy Keddie (born 1931; Ansel's granddaughter — the Chronicle's "great-niece" is wrong), a graduate student under Professor Leland Vaught at Farland, brought five records into one room: the station test book, Emmet Brant's weigh book (lent by Roy), the Alder Corners figures (copied by Rosalie Cudd), Strawn's circuit notebooks, and the Association's ledgers. In February 1958 she climbed the ladder into the loft over the station's intake room and found, under a tarpaulin, a crate stenciled AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET, there since June 1923 (not in the Ashlin office, as Nym's catalogue says). Nine pipettes were unbroken (not eleven). On 9 March 1958 she measured them at Farland against a calibrated burette, five runs each: readings from 16.70 to 16.74, mean 16.72 c.c., against stems blown 17.6 — short 0.88 c.c., 95 hundredths of the mark.

Everything follows: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80, a gap of 0.19 that is five per cent of whatever was there, so richer milk lost more (3.42 short 0.18; 4.56 short 0.24). Over 1898–1922, on 40,000,000 lb of milk: 1,444,000 lb credited, 1,520,000 delivered, 76,000 lb unpaid — $19,000 at 25¢; the 1925 award was a fifth of it. Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958, Vaught and Keddie) concludes: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty," and the difference stands in the ledgers as station gain. Vaught's method and magnitude sections carry 16.27 c.c., 0.9244, 3.905, 118,000 lb, "five dozen" and "1 May 1898" — contradicted by the sheet, the invoice, Orra's diary and his own conclusion.

The Ashlin Chronicle of 3 October 1958 spread the news with errors (great-niece, eleven pipettes, 16.27, $19,600, $77.39, "Cudd's niece," "the 1908 committee sat again") and accused Ammon of knowingly selling short glass, on no evidence. County agent Orin Falke's 1959 report is sound and withdrew its hay concession on receiving Bulletin 214. Ivy read "What the Crate Held" to the Historical Society in 1959; crate and test book were deposited that year, the weigh book in 1963. Thirty-five years from condemnation to cause; thirty-three from dismissal; Ansel dead twenty-four. Documents: measurement sheet (Nym Item 14; r04); Bulletin 471 (Item 15; r11); the crate (Item 5); r19; r22; r04; r18.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie and Ansel Keddie.** Ivy was Ansel's granddaughter. Chain: Ansel (born 1861) and Orra had a son, Duncan, born 1894 — Orra's diary has "Duncan five years old" in September 1899 and Duncan leaving to teach at Ashlin in October 1911 "seventeen"; Duncan's own notes say "I am Duncan, born in 1894, their son… My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter." Ivy's talk opens "Ansel Keddie… was my grandfather. My father, Duncan Keddie… was his son." Roy calls her "Duncan's girl Ivy," Hazel "Duncan's girl," Oram "the granddaughter." The Chronicle says "great-niece" and Falke's report repeats it, then corrects itself in the postscript ("granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine"). Those two are one source, not two: the Chronicle was written in a day and a half from a press release and phone calls, and Falke's report was prompted by that "newspaper notice." Duncan says he has "twice seen it printed otherwise." Ages agree with the family: 27 in October 1958 fits a 1931 birth.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant.** Second cousins. Orra Brant (Ansel's wife) and Emmet Brant were sister and brother. Orra's son Duncan and Emmet's son Roy are therefore first cousins. Duncan's daughter Ivy and Roy's daughter Hazel are the children of first cousins: second cousins. Roy, Hazel, Duncan and Rundle's 1959 footnote all say so and derive it the same way. Vira and Oram say "first cousin once removed," which would put Ivy and Hazel a generation apart; the generations are the same (grandchild of Orra, grandchild of Emmet), so both are wrong — an age gap of a dozen-odd years misled the valley. Left open by the sources: whether Orra and Emmet were full siblings (Roy: "same mother and same father, whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother") or half siblings by Emmet's father's second wife (Hazel) — if Hazel is right, the two women are half second cousins; nothing in Orra's hand survives from before 1889 to settle it, and Emmet (1854) was twelve years older than Orra (1866 by the diary and its editor; Duncan's "1868" contradicts his own "eighty-two when she died in 1948"). I prefer Roy, who is one generation nearer and rebuts the stepmother story explicitly, but it is preference, not proof.

**B3. Selby Vose's father.** Gideon Vose — the Association's organizer, first president 1889–1911 and author of its by-laws. Selby's own address ("My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law under which every patron… is paid on the test of the station where he delivers"), Loomis ("son of the late president"), Duncan and Kyle all say son; Vira and Rundle say nephew, and both are repeating store talk (Kyle: "the nephew story… is nonsense"). It matters three ways. First, the rule that made the whole affair possible — payment on the station's own test, with no appeal and no cross-check — was his father's sentence, and Selby administered it for twenty-five years with a family stake in the Association's account being right (Vira: "the family always thought the valley belonged to them a little"). Second, his water theory rested on his father's Jersey herd, kept on at the home place and testing 4.56 in 1910, as proof the valley's ground was good and low tests must be the patrons' doing — a true premise and a wrong conclusion. Third, he signed every annual sheet carrying the station-gain line and told Teague in 1916 to let it alone; the son of the man who wrote "the station's test shall govern" was the officer best placed to ask whether the station's test was true, and never did.

**B4. The Voses and the Keddies.** No relationship is established. Two narrators assert one: Vira ("Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins. Everybody knew that… nobody wrote down anywhere") and Oram ("cousins of some sort… My mother had it from her mother… I could not draw you the line on paper"). Both are hearsay from the same valley talk, so they count as one source, and neither can name the link. Against them stands Duncan Keddie, Orra's son: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses… I have heard it from two women in this town… It is talk"; he explains it as confusion with the Ashlin Voses, who were no kin to Gideon either, and notes that his mother, who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection," never once put a Vose in it. A specific denial from inside the family outweighs an unlocatable rumor. The sources cannot prove a negative, but they give no reason to believe the rumor, and the story does not turn on it.

## Section C — Math

**C1 (a) Proportion.** The nine surviving pipettes were blown 17.6 c.c. and delivered (mean of five runs each) 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Sum = 150.48; 150.48 ÷ 9 = 16.72 c.c. Ratio 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly (check: 17.6 × 0.95 = 16.72). Because the bottle neck is ruled in per cent for a 17.6 charge and nothing else, a charge 0.95 of the mark puts 0.95 of the fat in the neck and reads 0.95 of the truth. So every test at the station read 95 per cent of the true figure — low by one part in twenty. Vaught's method section (16.27 c.c., 0.9244) is rejected: it contradicts the measurement sheet (Nym Item 14, Ivy's reading of it), Nym's Item 5, and his own conclusion ("one part in twenty"); 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.905 matches nothing else in the record, while 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80 matches the 1896 borrowed-glass season, the 1911 borrowed-measure weeks, the 1924 new-glass year, and the Alder Corners figures. The Chronicle's 16.27 is the same error copied from the press release.

**C1 (b) True average and shortfall.** 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80. Recorded tests were short by 3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19 of a point (0.19 ÷ 3.80 = 5 per cent). Vose's 0.21 and Kepp's 0.21 do not fit the book; Roy's 0.17 is the gap to Larrow Green's 3.78, a different comparison.

**C1 (c) Pounds not credited.** Milk received 1898–1922: 40,000,000 lb (ledgers, Loomis §7; Nym Item 6; Kyle). Credited: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb. Delivered: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb. Difference: 76,000 lb (equivalently 40,000,000 × 0.0019 = 76,000). Vaught's 118,000 lb follows from his wrong ratio and is rejected; Frick's and Oram's 67,000 lb is the patrons' own 1925 reckoning (≈ 0.17 × 40,000,000 = 68,000, the gap to Larrow Green), not the true shortfall.

**C1 (d) Money and fraction received.** Price: the ledgers' period average of 25¢ (Loomis copying Teague, Kyle who got it out for the arbitration, Ivy, Roy; Sill's and Kepp's 27¢ is recollection, not ledger). 76,000 × $0.25 = $19,000. The award was $3,800: 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = 0.20 — one fifth. (The $19,600 of Rundle and the Chronicle cannot be produced from any figures in the sources: 19,600 ÷ 0.25 = 78,400 lb, which is nobody's number.)

**C2. Emmet Brant, 1910.** Weigh book leaf: 200,000 lb delivered, paid on 3.42. (a) Credited fat: 200,000 × 0.0342 = 6,840 lb. (b) True test 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60 (Ivy: "a herd recorded at 3.42 was short 0.18"); true fat 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb; owed and not credited: 7,200 − 6,840 = 360 lb (check: 6,840 × 0.05263 = 360; 5 per cent of 7,200 = 360). (c) Butterfat was 24¢ in 1910 (Roy): 360 × $0.24 = $86.40. (At the 25-year average of 25¢ it would be $90.00; the question asks for that year, so $86.40.)

**C3. Totals over 1898–1922.** (a) Credited to patrons: 1,444,000 lb. (b) Received at Larrow Green in the station's cream: 1,520,000 lb. (c) The difference, 76,000 lb, is 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = 5.00 per cent of the fat actually delivered (received), and 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 5.263 per cent ≈ five and a quarter per cent of the fat credited. The ledger's "five and a quarter per cent" is of the credited figure — proved by its own 1898 line: 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26 per cent, whereas 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.00 per cent. Teague ("of the fat that was actually in the cream") and Vaught ("of the butterfat actually delivered") name the wrong denominator; Ivy and Nym have it right. The two are the same error seen from two sides: 0.05 ÷ 0.95 = 0.0526.

**C4 (a) Invoice total.** $25.20 + $31.68 + $0.90 + $18.00 + $2.15 = $77.93 (Nym Item 2; Ammon's facsimile). Junia Ammon's prose and the Chronicle say $77.39 — a transposition that the invoice's own lines refute. **(b) Pipettes and shortage.** Six dozen = 72 pipettes, at 35¢: 72 × 0.35 = $25.20, which matches the invoice line (five dozen — Vaught, Oram — would be $21.00). Each was short 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c. (the nine survivors ranged 16.70–16.74, i.e., 0.86–0.90 short each; I take the survivors as representative of the lot, which Ammon's "consistently wrong" and the ledger's 26-year constancy support).

**C5 (a) Two different spans.** The faulty glass was in use from 1 May 1897 (Orra's diary that day; the office file; Ammon; the bulletin's conclusion) to 12 June 1923 (Strawn's entry): 26 years and 42 days — 26 years, 6 weeks. The money calculation runs over 25 full delivery years, 1898 to 1922. They differ because the ledger keeps annual totals and the patrons' claim was laid year by year: 1897 (glass only from May; the summary leaf calls it "part year") and 1923 (glass only to 12 June) are part-years and are left out; 1896 was on Sill's borrowed glass and is in no ledger anyway. The omission understates the loss by about 8 + 5½ months ≈ 1.1 years ≈ 3,400 lb ≈ $850; against that, the nine weeks of 1911 inside the period were tested on a borrowed measure and were not short, an overstatement of about 9/52 × 3,040 ≈ 530 lb. Net, 76,000 lb is slightly conservative.

**C5 (b) First-season intake.** Cannot be determined from the sources. Sill (1934) recalls "about nine hundred thousand pounds… May to the end of the year"; Kyle (1953) says "a good eleven hundred thousand," which he had "from men who were there" and which "is not in any book I ever ruled"; Nym states flatly that the 1896 intake "is not in the ledgers and is nowhere else — the station column begins with 1897." Two memories 200,000 lb apart and no record. What is established: the season ran 4 May to 31 December 1896 on borrowed glass, averaged 3.80, and was carried on the creamery's own books, whose sheets were never put with the station papers.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment.** When: 8 May to 10 July 1911 — 63 days, nine weeks (Keddie's two dated entries; Orra's diary for 8 May; Teague's and Kepp's "six weeks" are wrong, and Kepp's "June and July" misplaces it). Variable: the milk pipette — the station's own broke and a borrowed one was used, then the station's own returned. Held constant: the patrons, herds, land and feed, the acid, the test bottles, the hand tester, the scales, the method, and the man (Keddie tested throughout, under no different supervision). Result: the station average went from 3.61 to 3.80 on the first morning of the borrowed measure, held there, and dropped to 3.61 the day the station's own measure came back; every farm on the Bottom rose and fell together (Roy). What it establishes that a calculation cannot: causation by intervention. A calculation from the 1958 measurement shows the glass *could* account for the gap; the interval shows that changing that one object *did* move the figures, at the station, in the tester's own hand, decades before anyone suspected glass — and that the size of the effect (0.19, one part in twenty) was already there. It rules out the herds, the ground, the hay and the men in one stroke, because none of them changed and the figures did; it also clears the tester, since the same man produced both sets of figures. What it does not establish: that the borrowed measure was itself true (only that it differed — its 3.80 is corroborated by 1896, 1924 and Alder Corners, not by the interval); anything about winter, since it ran May–July (the flat all-year gap does that work); why the station's glass was short or who was to blame; where the borrowed measure came from (cannot be determined); the total money, which needs the ledgers; and, on its own, that hay has *no* effect — only that whatever effect it had was constant across the swap. It also did not establish anything to anyone at the time: Keddie wrote a question mark, Teague booked a replacement charge, and Kepp in 1926 dismissed it as summer figures.

**D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream.** (1) The Association's own ledger: every year the fat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream exceeded the fat credited on the station's test — "station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual," 3,040 lb on 57,760 in 1898, 76,000 lb over 1898–1922 (Teague's entries; Kyle copied them; Rundle's 1939 correction). Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green *light*; it reached it *heavy*. Sill's receiving book confirms it from the platform side: he re-weighed the Hollow cream himself and it "ran heavy every year… never less." (2) The 1911 interval: the same man, alone, with a borrowed pipette, produced tests 0.19 higher for nine weeks, and lower again the day his own glass returned (Keddie's entries; Roy's book). A man writing low tests to cover theft would not have his figures rise when a borrowed measure was put in his hand. (3) 1924: with the new glass, and Keddie still at the bench, the station's book gives the year at 3.80 — "tests improved," in the state inspector's own hand (Strawn 1924; Keddie's letter). Same man, honest figures; the change was the instrument. (4) The error was an exact constant fraction — 5¼ per cent every year for twenty-five years, and proportional to richness (3.42 short 0.18; 4.56 short 0.24, Ivy; "precise… proportional," Vaught): a hand skimming cans cannot produce a machine-constant ratio. (5) Emmet Brant's parallel scale agreed with the station's for thirty years (Roy, Hazel), so nothing was taken by weight. Independence: (1) is the creamery's side — what arrived, recorded by two different men in two different books; (2) is an intervention at the station, recorded by the tester and by a patron; (3) is a different year and a different instrument, witnessed by the state's inspector, who was kin to nobody; (4) is a property of the numbers themselves; (5) is a farmer's private record. Any one would stand if the others had been lost. Reasons (1), (2), (3), (4) and (5) all hold if the crate had never been measured in 1958 — (1) alone was in the office, in ink, from 1898. Did the bookkeeper ever question the entries? No. Teague wrote "as usual" for forty years and says evenness "stops him looking… is furniture"; he put the account to Vose once, in 1916, was told to let it alone, and did; Kyle: "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years." Before the board he used the account to describe "how such an account would look if a man were helping himself," not to test whether it did. In 1948 he quotes the entry, explains that the received figure "was always the larger," and in the next paragraphs repeats the theft theory unchanged — the refutation and the accusation on the same page, never brought together.

**D3. Did the manager know the tests ran low before 1923?** Yes, without doubt, and by his own account. Selby Vose's 1926 address: "For a generation the milk brought to that station tested below the milk brought to Larrow Green, and every year we were told it was the wet hay, or the tester, or the office, or the weather"; "A manager who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing to the men who make them… is being idle at their expense." His 1919 circular presupposes it — one does not warn nine families about water in cans that test normally. The complaints ran from 1902 and the 1908 committee sat under his father's presidency; the station-gain line came to him on every annual sheet from 1913, and Teague put the account to him in 1916 and was told to let it alone; in 1919 Strawn told him there was no water. What the sources do *not* establish is whether he ever grasped that the low tests were *false* rather than true readings of thin milk: Teague says "what he made of it he never told me," and Kyle says he "read the totals and never the lines above them." His conduct — the circular, the 1926 speech, "a piece of housekeeping" — shows a man who took the low figures as facts about the milk and the men, not about the glass. So: he knew the tests ran low from the start of his tenure; he had been handed, in 1916 and 1919, the two facts that together meant they were wrong; the sources give no sign he put them together.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. What produced the shortfall.** In April 1897 Ammon & Sons of Tarnet shipped the Ostrey Hollow station six dozen milk-test pipettes blown 17.6 c.c. The lot was short: each delivered about 16.72 c.c., 0.95 of its mark (Junia Ammon's best guess — a reground mold, so the whole lot was consistently wrong and no sample check could catch it). A butterfat test reads the length of a fat column in a bottle neck ruled for a 17.6 charge; a charge 0.95 of the mark yields a column 0.95 as long, so every test read 95 per cent of the truth — a fixed *fraction*, not a fixed number of points, which is why the recorded gap was 0.19 on an average of 3.80, 0.18 on a 3.42 herd, 0.24 on a 4.56 one, and why the ledger's surplus was always 5¼ per cent of the credited (5 per cent of the delivered), never varying. Under Article VII the station's test governed pay with no appeal, so every patron was paid 5 per cent short for every pound of fat; the uncredited fat went down the hill in the cream, was weighed into Larrow Green, and was booked as "station gain, as usual" to the Association's general account — 76,000 lb, $19,000, over 1898–1922. It began on 1 May 1897, the day the crate's glass replaced Sill's borrowed Larrow Green outfit (1896 on the borrowed glass: 3.80; 1898 on the crate's: 3.61), and it ended on 12 June 1923, when Merle Strawn, carrying the burette the state had issued in 1921 — the first instrument in the trade able to check a pipette, because "the pipette is the standard" and cannot be caught by measuring with itself — ran the lot against it and condemned it; new glass from July 1923; 1924 average 3.80. Nothing at the station or on the farms had to change for either the start or the end, because nothing at the station or on the farms was ever wrong: the milk left the farms at about 3.80 (Alder Corners 3.78–3.82 every summer 1915–1930; the gap flat in July and February), the scales agreed with Emmet Brant's for thirty years, the lactometer never found water, and the tester made the same test the same way for twenty-nine seasons — the only thing that ever changed was which piece of glass was in his hand, three times: May 1897, nine weeks in 1911, and July 1923, and the figures moved each time and only then.

**The three competing explanations and what defeats each.** *(1) Watered milk* — Selby Vose (1919 circular; 1926 address), Hobart Sill, Cleve Oram's father. Defeated by: Strawn's lactometer, "no added water, as always here," at every visit 1907–1936, told to Vose in 1919; the ledger — watered milk would bring *less* fat than credited, yet more arrived every year; Rosalie Cudd — a cheesemaker sees watered milk in the vat the next morning and never once did in sixteen summers; the flat all-year gap, where a farmer's water would come and go; and Vose's own chronology — the rise came in 1924, after the new glass and before the petition. *(2) Cream stolen by the tester* — Wilbur Teague before the board; the "settled opinion of the valley" (Rundle 1939, Vira, Oram's father). Defeated by: the station-gain line (stolen cream arrives light; it arrived heavy — Rundle's own retraction); the 1911 nine weeks (same man, borrowed pipette, figures up); 1924 (same man, new glass, figures right); the machine-constant 5¼ per cent; and, finally, nine pieces of short glass. *(3) The wet hay of Hessel Bottom* — Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee, Dr. Roland Kepp (1926), Falke's first draft. Defeated by: Bulletin 214 (1911) — at most 0.05 in winter, nothing in summer, against 0.19 in every month, "four times too small and the wrong shape"; Alder Corners — the same cows off the same wet ground tested 3.80; the 1911 interval, in which the hay did not change and the figures did; Larrow Green's own 3.78 being *below* the Hollow's true 3.80; and Grigg's and Kepp's reliance on a misquoted "two tenths."

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?** No. The exhibit list is four books — the test book, the receiving book, Brant's weigh book, Strawn's 1923 notebook — "and nothing else" (Frick, Kyle). Loomis, who took every word, says the crate "was never mentioned… in any of the three sessions," and the award's "books and appliances" is a form of words. What the board saw tried in the weigh room was the *replacement* glass of July 1923; the condemned crate sat in the loft above two of its three sittings (Duncan, Oram, Orra). It had, in exhibit 4, the entry condemning the glass, and nobody laid it beside the others.

**What the tester believed.** Publicly, nothing he could not prove: "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are," and a question mark in the book. Privately, from the summer of 1911, that "it was something in the glass" — said twice at home (Duncan), never written, and outweighed in his own mind by the outfit he had been taught to trust. He did not believe the patrons watered: his unsent letter says he stood in that room every morning "and I know what came into it," and Orra's diary has him carrying the circular only so that no other man would. Frick's recollection of a corridor remark blaming the patrons is contradicted by both and is not credited.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** In 1897 a Larrow Valley cooperative creamery bought pipettes for its Ostrey Hollow skimming station. The lot was short — 16.72 c.c. against a 17.6 mark — so every butterfat test read 95 per cent of the truth, and under a by-law making the station's test final its patrons were paid five per cent short for twenty-six years: 76,000 lb of fat, $19,000. The missing fat reached the creamery regardless and was booked as "station gain, as usual." The valley blamed wet hay, watered cans, and the tester, Ansel Keddie, dismissed in 1925 while an arbitration found "no cause" and paid $3,800. A state inspector condemned the glass in 1923 without drawing the inference. In 1958 Keddie's granddaughter found the crate in the station loft, measured it, and laid five records side by side — test book, farm weigh book, a cheesemaker's figures, inspector's notebooks, ledgers — each kept in different hands, none sufficient alone.
