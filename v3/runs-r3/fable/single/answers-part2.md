# Answer sheet — part 2 of 3 (Section A, stories 5–8; Section B)

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### Story 5. Five and a Quarter Per Cent, As Usual — the station-gain line (1897–1948)

From 1897 the Association's annual ledgers carried a station column for Ostrey Hollow, and every year it held three figures and a sentence. For 1898: "butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account." The fat that came down the hill in the cream was always more than the station said it had bought — never less. The arithmetic the ledger never did: 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26 per cent of the credited fat; 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.00 per cent of the fat received; 57,760 ÷ 1,600,000 lb = 3.61 per cent; 60,800 ÷ 1,600,000 = 3.80. Both the recorded test and the true one stood in the book every year. Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945, wrote the line. He says the gain was five and a quarter per cent "of the fat that was actually in the cream"; Vaught repeats that denominator; both are wrong by the entry's own figures — five and a quarter of the credited, five of the delivered (Ivy, Nym).

Hobart Sill, buttermaker 1893–1934, re-weighed the Hollow cream himself as it came into the cold room: "That cream ran heavy. Every year… Always more. Never less." He made nothing of it — "so does everybody's" — and went on blaming water. Manager Selby Vose signed every annual sheet; clerk Bertram Kyle says he read the totals and never the lines. Teague put the account to Vose in 1916, was told to let it alone, and did.

Over the twenty-five full delivery years 1898–1922 the station took in 40,000,000 lb of milk (Loomis added Teague's columns twice; Kyle got the figure out for the arbitration; Teague's and Rundle's 44,000,000 do not fit 25 × 1,600,000), was credited 1,444,000 lb of fat, delivered 1,520,000, gain 76,000 lb — $19,000 at the ledger's 25¢ average. Teague's "3.16" average transposes the 3.61 his own entry implies. Dell Rundle, shown the ledger in 1939 while his pamphlet was in press, retracted the theft story on the page: stolen cream would have reached the Green light; it reached it heavy. Bulletin 471 ends there: the missing fat "stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain." Kyle: it was "in plain sight with a name on it." Documents: annual ledgers (Nym Item 6); Larrow Green receiving and churn books (Item 16); Teague's chapter (r05); Kyle (r23); Sill (r14); Rundle's note (r12).

### Story 6. Glass Is Glass — Merle Strawn and the condemnation (1907–1926)

Merle Strawn took the state's Ordell dairy circuit in 1907 — his own covering note and first entry; Ivy and Falke say 1908 — and laid it down in March 1936. Four checks at every plant, always in the same order: scales against sealed weights, lactometer for added water, boiler and cold room, and a test watched through at the tester's elbow. At Ostrey Hollow the scales were true at every visit, the lactometer never found water — "as always here" — and Keddie worked "slow and clean." In 1919 he told the manager the circular was wrong: he had tested for water at every visit and never found any. In 1921 the state issued burettes for checking pipettes; in 1922 he did not run Ostrey Hollow's glass, "the day being short."

On 12 June 1923 he ran the station's milk pipettes against the burette: "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them." The date is fixed by his entry and its photostat, by Ammon's letter of 2 July acknowledging the Association's order of 28 June, and by Orra's diary for July 1923 ("new glass at the station"); Vose and Rundle say 12 July, Kyle 1922, Hazel 1921 — all wrong. Ammon & Sons entered the order for six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles, shipped in July, and could say nothing of the 1897 lot: "our books of the nineties are not such." Their works books burned in 1931; Junia Ammon's guess is a reground mold and a lot consistently — therefore undetectably — short. The new glass delivered its mark when Strawn checked it in October. The condemned crate went up the ladder into the loft over the intake room. In 1924, Keddie still at the bench, "tests improved. Their book gives the year at 3.80." The burette program was discontinued in 1926.

Strawn drew no inference about the twenty-six years behind the glass — "I do not keep an estimation, I keep a book" — and nobody at the Association did either: no more was said of it "than of a broken window" (Kyle). His notebooks went to the state dairy office in 1936 and to the Farland college library in 1947, unopened for eleven years. Documents: Strawn's circuit books and 1936 extracts (r06); the 1923 photostat (Nym Item 9); the Ammon letter (Item 10; r20); Orra's diary (r24); Kyle's order entry (r23).

### Story 7. No Cause Established — the arbitration of 1925 and the ruin of Ansel Keddie (1925–1948)

In January 1925 the Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned the Association. A board of three was constituted, chaired by Alonzo Frick, attorney, of Ashlin. It sat from 17 March to 4 May 1925 in three sessions: the first at the Association's office at Ashlin, the second and third in the station's own weigh room at Ostrey Hollow, with the condemned crate in the loft twenty feet above. Four exhibits and nothing else: the station test book (4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924), the station receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book (lent, and wanted back), and Strawn's circuit notebook for 1923. The crate was never mentioned; the board saw the scales tried and a test made on the new glass. Three explanations were argued — water (Vose), theft (Teague, by insinuation), hay (the 1908 report read into the record) — and none proved.

On 17 March Keddie made a nine-sentence statement: tests made "as I was taught and as the state man saw me do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons"; "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are"; "I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station." Frick believed him. Frick also recalls a corridor remark that Keddie "had always thought the patrons were at fault" — contradicted by Keddie's own unsent letter ("I know what came into it") and by Orra's diary; I do not credit it. On 30 April 1925, four days before the award, the Association dismissed him. On 4 May the board "finds no cause established" and awarded the patrons $3,800, "without admission, to be charged to the station account" — the minute book's words; Vira and Oram remember $3,500, wrongly. The patrons' own reckoning had been 67,000 lb of fat — about 0.17 × 40,000,000, the gap to Larrow Green — and the award was a fifth of the true $19,000.

Keddie kept a few cows and turned carpenter. Emmet died in 1929, the two men still unspeaking; Ansel died at home in February 1934, uncleared. Orra died in 1948. Vose told the 1926 annual meeting it had been "a generous settlement of an unproved claim" and that the cause was water. Documents: the board's minute book (Nym Item 7; r02); Keddie's statement (Item 8; r03) and unsent letter; Frick's exhibit list (r13); Vose's address (r08); Orra's diary (r24); Teague (r05); Kyle (r23).

### Story 8. What the Crate Held — Ivy Keddie's rediscovery (1958–1959)

Ivy Keddie (born 1931; Ansel's granddaughter — the Chronicle's "great-niece" is wrong), a graduate student under Professor Leland Vaught at Farland, brought five records into one room: the station test book, Emmet Brant's weigh book (lent by Roy), the Alder Corners figures (copied by Rosalie Cudd), Strawn's circuit notebooks, and the Association's ledgers. In February 1958 she climbed the ladder into the loft over the station's intake room and found, under a tarpaulin, a crate stenciled AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET, there since June 1923 (not in the Ashlin office, as Nym's catalogue says). Nine pipettes were unbroken (not eleven). On 9 March 1958 she measured them at Farland against a calibrated burette, five runs each: readings from 16.70 to 16.74, mean 16.72 c.c., against stems blown 17.6 — short 0.88 c.c., 95 hundredths of the mark.

Everything follows: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80, a gap of 0.19 that is five per cent of whatever was there, so richer milk lost more (3.42 short 0.18; 4.56 short 0.24). Over 1898–1922, on 40,000,000 lb of milk: 1,444,000 lb credited, 1,520,000 delivered, 76,000 lb unpaid — $19,000 at 25¢; the 1925 award was a fifth of it. Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958, Vaught and Keddie) concludes: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty," and the difference stands in the ledgers as station gain. Vaught's method and magnitude sections carry 16.27 c.c., 0.9244, 3.905, 118,000 lb, "five dozen" and "1 May 1898" — contradicted by the sheet, the invoice, Orra's diary and his own conclusion.

The Ashlin Chronicle of 3 October 1958 spread the news with errors (great-niece, eleven pipettes, 16.27, $19,600, $77.39, "Cudd's niece," "the 1908 committee sat again") and accused Ammon of knowingly selling short glass, on no evidence. County agent Orin Falke's 1959 report is sound and withdrew its hay concession on receiving Bulletin 214. Ivy read "What the Crate Held" to the Historical Society in 1959; crate and test book were deposited that year, the weigh book in 1963. Thirty-five years from condemnation to cause; thirty-three from dismissal; Ansel dead twenty-four. Documents: measurement sheet (Nym Item 14; r04); Bulletin 471 (Item 15; r11); the crate (Item 5); r19; r22; r04; r18.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie and Ansel Keddie.** Ivy was Ansel's granddaughter. Chain: Ansel (born 1861) and Orra had a son, Duncan, born 1894 — Orra's diary has "Duncan five years old" in September 1899 and Duncan leaving to teach at Ashlin in October 1911 "seventeen"; Duncan's own notes say "I am Duncan, born in 1894, their son… My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter." Ivy's talk opens "Ansel Keddie… was my grandfather. My father, Duncan Keddie… was his son." Roy calls her "Duncan's girl Ivy," Hazel "Duncan's girl," Oram "the granddaughter." The Chronicle says "great-niece" and Falke's report repeats it, then corrects itself in the postscript ("granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine"). Those two are one source, not two: the Chronicle was written in a day and a half from a press release and phone calls, and Falke's report was prompted by that "newspaper notice." Duncan says he has "twice seen it printed otherwise." Ages agree with the family: 27 in October 1958 fits a 1931 birth.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant.** Second cousins. Orra Brant (Ansel's wife) and Emmet Brant were sister and brother. Orra's son Duncan and Emmet's son Roy are therefore first cousins. Duncan's daughter Ivy and Roy's daughter Hazel are the children of first cousins: second cousins. Roy, Hazel, Duncan and Rundle's 1959 footnote all say so and derive it the same way. Vira and Oram say "first cousin once removed," which would put Ivy and Hazel a generation apart; the generations are the same (grandchild of Orra, grandchild of Emmet), so both are wrong — an age gap of a dozen-odd years misled the valley. Left open by the sources: whether Orra and Emmet were full siblings (Roy: "same mother and same father, whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother") or half siblings by Emmet's father's second wife (Hazel) — if Hazel is right, the two women are half second cousins; nothing in Orra's hand survives from before 1889 to settle it, and Emmet (1854) was twelve years older than Orra (1866 by the diary and its editor; Duncan's "1868" contradicts his own "eighty-two when she died in 1948"). I prefer Roy, who is one generation nearer and rebuts the stepmother story explicitly, but it is preference, not proof.

**B3. Selby Vose's father.** Gideon Vose — the Association's organizer, first president 1889–1911 and author of its by-laws. Selby's own address ("My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law under which every patron… is paid on the test of the station where he delivers"), Loomis ("son of the late president"), Duncan and Kyle all say son; Vira and Rundle say nephew, and both are repeating store talk (Kyle: "the nephew story… is nonsense"). It matters three ways. First, the rule that made the whole affair possible — payment on the station's own test, with no appeal and no cross-check — was his father's sentence, and Selby administered it for twenty-five years with a family stake in the Association's account being right (Vira: "the family always thought the valley belonged to them a little"). Second, his water theory rested on his father's Jersey herd, kept on at the home place and testing 4.56 in 1910, as proof the valley's ground was good and low tests must be the patrons' doing — a true premise and a wrong conclusion. Third, he signed every annual sheet carrying the station-gain line and told Teague in 1916 to let it alone; the son of the man who wrote "the station's test shall govern" was the officer best placed to ask whether the station's test was true, and never did.

**B4. The Voses and the Keddies.** No relationship is established. Two narrators assert one: Vira ("Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins. Everybody knew that… nobody wrote down anywhere") and Oram ("cousins of some sort… My mother had it from her mother… I could not draw you the line on paper"). Both are hearsay from the same valley talk, so they count as one source, and neither can name the link. Against them stands Duncan Keddie, Orra's son: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses… I have heard it from two women in this town… It is talk"; he explains it as confusion with the Ashlin Voses, who were no kin to Gideon either, and notes that his mother, who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection," never once put a Vose in it. A specific denial from inside the family outweighs an unlocatable rumor. The sources cannot prove a negative, but they give no reason to believe the rumor, and the story does not turn on it.
