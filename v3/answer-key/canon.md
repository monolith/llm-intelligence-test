# Canon — Ground-Truth Fact Sheet, v3 (SECRET — never show to the system under test)

The world of v3 is **the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, Ordell County**, and
the twenty-six years in which one of its skimming stations weighed honest milk and wrote down
a false number for it.

Everything below was constructed for this test. Every name, place, number, date and mechanism
is invented and interlocked so that every scored question has a single verifiable answer — or
is deliberately unanswerable and marked as such (see **Abstention facts, A01–A10**).

Nothing in this world overlaps with v1 ("The Selde Weir") or v2 ("Cadder Falls / the Sallow
Creek Viaduct"). No name, place, date, object, document, or mechanism is reused.

**Design order.** This canon was written first. The eight originals were written from it; the
twenty-four narrator briefs were written from it; the questions and the scoring key cite it by
fact id. Nothing in the retellings is true that is not here.

| F031 | **How the system worked.** Patrons hauled milk to a station. It was weighed at the intake and a composite sample was taken for each patron. The sample was tested for butterfat: a measured charge of milk from a **pipette** into a **test bottle**, sulfuric acid, a spin in the hand tester, and the fat read off in percent on the graduated neck. The station separated the milk, shipped the **cream** down to Larrow Green, and returned the **skim** to the patron. The patron was paid on **pounds of milk × percent of fat**. |
| F032 | **The standard milk pipette delivers 17.6 cc.** The acid measure is a separate 17.5 cc vessel and is not part of this history. |
| F033 | **The Ostrey Hollow pipettes.** Nine of the condemned lot survived and were measured on 9 March 1958 against a calibrated burette: individual deliveries **16.70 to 16.74 cc**, **mean 16.72 cc**, against the **17.6 cc** blown on the stem. |

| F035 | **The 1897 crate.** The station's own outfit arrived from Ammon & Sons in one crate on the invoice of **14 April 1897** and went into use **1 May 1897**. Until then the station used glassware **borrowed from Larrow Green**. |
| F036 | **The station loft.** The condemned crate sat in the loft over the station's intake room from June 1923 until February 1958. The 1925 arbitration sat in the Cooperative office at Ashlin, but its second and third sessions were held **in the station's own weigh room**, directly under the loft. |
| F037 | The **Ordell County Historical Rooms** at Ashlin hold, since 1959–1963: the station test book, the crate and its nine pipettes, the Cooperative minute books and ledgers, and the Brant weigh book. |
| F038 | The **Ammon & Sons works records burned in 1931**. Nothing survives about how the short lot was made — see **A04**. |
| F039 | The **Ostrey Hollow church register burned in 1912**, taking the local record of births and marriages before that date — see **A01** and **A08**. **World background only: no retelling states this, and no scored answer may rest on it.** In the test input the same gap appears as r24's editorial preface, which says the pages covering her girlhood and her family before 1889 were not preserved. |

## 4. Quantities and the arithmetic chains

### 4.1 The pipette chain (the spine of the test)

| id | Fact |
|---|---|
| F073 | Marked delivery **17.6 cc**; actual mean delivery **16.72 cc**; ratio **0.95**; short by **0.88 cc**, or **5 percent**. |
| F074 | **Recorded** station average test, 1898–1922: **3.61**. |
| F075 | **True** station average test: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. (Equivalently 3.61 × 20/19.) |
| F076 | Shortfall in test points: 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19**. And 0.19 ÷ 3.80 = **0.05** exactly — the shortfall is *proportional*, not a fixed number of points. |
| F077 | Milk received at the station in the **25 full delivery years 1898–1922**: **40,000,000 lb** — an average of **1,600,000 lb** a year. |
| F078 | Butterfat **credited** to patrons: 40,000,000 × 3.61 % = **1,444,000 lb**. |
| F079 | Butterfat **actually delivered** in the cream: 40,000,000 × 3.80 % = **1,520,000 lb**. |
| F080 | Difference: **76,000 lb** of butterfat. Equivalently 40,000,000 × 0.0019 = 76,000. |
| F081 | Average price paid for butterfat over the period: **25 cents a pound**. Value of the unpaid fat: 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000**. |
| F082 | The 1925 award was **$3,800** — exactly **20 percent** of $19,000. |
| F083 | **The two right percentages.** The shortfall is **5.00 percent of the true fat** (76,000 ÷ 1,520,000) and **5.26 percent of the credited fat** (76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 5.2632 %). The Cooperative's ledger line reads "five and a quarter percent," which is the *credited* denominator. Both figures are correct for their denominators; asserting one as *the* figure without saying of what is the designed trap. |

### 4.2 The single-farm chain

| id | Fact |
|---|---|

| F085 | Fat credited: 200,000 × 3.42 % = **6,840 lb**. True test 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = **3.60**; true fat 200,000 × 3.60 % = **7,200 lb**. Owed: **360 lb**. |
| F086 | Price for fat in 1910: **24 cents**. Owed in money: 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40**. |

## 5. The mechanism (the buried theory)

| id | Fact |
|---|---|
| F099 | **The cause.** The pipettes in the 1897 crate delivered 16.72 cc where their stems were blown 17.6. A short charge of milk into a bottle whose neck is graduated for the full charge yields a reading in the same proportion: **every test at Ostrey Hollow read 95 percent of the truth from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923.** Nothing else at the station was wrong. The scales were right, the acid was right, the tester was honest, the cows were ordinary and the milk was whole. |
| F100 | **Why the number is not a coincidence.** 0.19 is not a constant of nature: it is 5 percent of 3.80, and 5 percent is 0.88 ÷ 17.6. The same 5 percent appears three times over — in the glass, in the tests, and in the "station gain" in the Cooperative's ledger. |
| F101 | **Onset.** The 1896 season, on glassware borrowed from Larrow Green, averaged **3.80**. The first full year on the station's own glass, 1898, averaged **3.61**, and no year between 1898 and 1922 departed from 3.61 by more than three hundredths. The step is in the books, dated, and it is at the crate, not at the cows. |
| F102 | **The natural experiment.** For nine weeks in 1911 the station used a borrowed measure. Same patrons, same cows, same pasture, same season, same tester, same acid, same bottles — **only the pipette changed** — and the average went to 3.80 and came back to 3.61 the day the new station pipette was unpacked. This single interval refutes all three rival explanations at once. |
| F103 | **Cessation.** Strawn condemned the lot on 12 June 1923 for short delivery. New glass was in use from July. The 1924 average was **3.80** and stayed there. Nobody connected the rise to the condemnation. |
| F104 | **The mirror in the ledger.** Because the patrons were paid on a test 5 percent short, the cream shipped down to Larrow Green always contained more fat than the station's books said it had bought. Every year from 1898 the Cooperative's ledger carried a credit for the difference under the heading **"station gain."** Over the 25 full years it came to **76,000 lb** — the identical figure. **The proof of the fraud that never happened was written in the Cooperative's own books, annually, in the bookkeeper's own hand, for twenty-five years, under a name that explained it away.** |
| F105 | **DECOY 1 — WATERING.** Selby Vose (1919 circular, and again at the 1925 hearing) and Hobart Sill held that the Ostrey Hollow patrons watered their milk. **Refuted by record:** Strawn's lactometer readings, taken on every circuit visit from 1908, never showed added water at Ostrey Hollow; and the same four patrons' milk tested **normal** at Alder Corners in the same seasons. **Refuted by arithmetic:** a dilution sufficient to carry 3.80 down to 3.61 is a 5 percent dilution, which would have added **80,000 lb a year** to the station's intake (5 % of 1,600,000); the receiving book shows no such step in 1897 and no such drop in 1923. **And by the 1911 interval**, which no watering story survives. |
| F106 | **DECOY 2 — THE TESTER'S THEFT.** Wilbur Teague (at the 1925 hearing) and Cleve Oram held that Ansel Keddie skimmed cream for himself and covered it by writing low tests. **Refuted by record:** the Cooperative's own ledger shows a **surplus** of fat arriving at Larrow Green from the station, every year, not a deficit — and it was Teague who wrote the entries. **Refuted by arithmetic:** a theft removes fat, so the cream would have arrived **light** by the amount stolen; instead it arrived **heavy** by exactly the amount the short test failed to credit, 76,000 lb over 25 years. A thief cannot produce a surplus. **And by the 1911 interval**: the same man tested in those nine weeks and the figures went *up*. |
| F107 | **DECOY 3 — THE BOG HAY.** Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee and Dr. Roland Kepp (1926) held that the hay and pasture of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream. **Refuted by record:** Bulletin No. 214 (1911) fed bog hay under trial and found a depression of **at most 0.05 point in winter and none in summer**; and the four patrons who hauled over the ridge in summer tested 0.19 higher at Alder Corners **off the same pasture**. **Refuted by arithmetic:** a feed effect is seasonal, and the Ostrey Hollow shortfall was **flat at 0.19 in every month for 26 years**; further, the 1908 committee sampled only January–March, the one quarter in which the college found any effect at all. **And by the 1911 interval**: the cows ate the same hay for those nine weeks. |
| F108 | **What the 1923 condemnation did and did not do.** Strawn condemned the pipettes for short delivery. He wrote no figure, made no report, and drew no inference about the years before. The cure preceded the diagnosis by **35 years**. |
| F109 | **The moral arithmetic.** The board of arbitration dismissed the one man in the valley whose books, read against the Cooperative's own ledger, contained the answer; paid the patrons a fifth of what they were owed; and left the evidence in a crate twenty feet above the room in which two of its three sessions sat. |

## 6. Documents (verbatim — these outrank memory)

**D1 — By-laws of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, Article VII**
> *Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of
> pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of
> the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*

**D2 — Invoice, Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, Tarnet, 14 April 1897**
> *Sold to the Larrow Valley Co-op. Creamery Assn., for the Ostrey Hollow station:
> 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20
> 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at .22 — 31.68
> 1 acid measure — .90
> 1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00
> Freight — 2.15
> Total — 77.93*

**D3 — Station test book, first leaf (Ansel Keddie)**
> *Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until
> our own comes. A. Keddie.*

**D4 — Station test book, 8 May and 10 July 1911 (Ansel Keddie)**
> *May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure.*
> *July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.*

**D5 — Station test book, summary leaf (Ansel Keddie, made up 1922)**
> *Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61
> or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted
> in their place, when it stood at 3.80.*

**D6 — Merle Strawn, circuit notebook, 12 June 1923**
> *Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk
> pipettes against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot,
> the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not
> weigh what it has cost them.*

**D7 — Cooperative annual ledger, station account (Wilbur Teague), typical entry**
> *Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received
> at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per
> cent, as usual. Carried to the general account.*

**D8 — Minutes of the board of arbitration, Ashlin, 4 May 1925 (Effie Loomis, recording)**
> *The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and
> appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow
> patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged
> to the station account.*

**D9 — Statement of Ansel Keddie to the board, 17 March 1925**
> *I have tested as I was taught and as the state man saw me do it, thirty-one times a week for
> twenty-nine seasons, and I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are. I have never
> taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I
> have when he is by himself.*

**D10 — Emmet Brant's weigh book, leaf for 1910**
> *Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and
> theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42.*

**D11 — Alder Corners Cheese Company test book, season of 1919 (Jerome Cudd)**
> *The four Hessel Bottom men, summer of 1919: 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground,
> whatever they say over the ridge.*

**D12 — State Agricultural College, Bulletin No. 214 (1911), summary**
> *Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five
> hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture.*

**D13 — Ivy Keddie, measurement sheet, 9 March 1958**
> *Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette,
> mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean
> 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of
> the bottle was ruled for.*

**D14 — Bulletin No. 471 (1958), Vaught and Keddie, closing paragraph**
> *No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May,
> 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because
> its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds
> of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station
> gain.*

**D15 — Report of the investigating committee, 1908 (Nyle Grigg)**
> *We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March. The milk is
> whole and the scales are honest. It is our opinion that the wet hay of that bottom is what
> thins the cream, and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station.*

**D16 — Ammon & Sons to the Larrow Valley Cooperative, 2 July 1923**
> *Your order of the 28th ultimo for six dozen milk pipettes and six dozen test bottles is
> entered and will go forward this month. We regret the trouble. Our books of the nineties are
> not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name.*

| **I01** | The whole twenty-six-year anomaly is one short pipette: 0.88 cc, one part in twenty, and every number in the case follows from it. | S2 (the crate) + S3 (the test book) + S6 (the condemnation) + S8 (the measurement) |
| **I02** | The proof was in the Cooperative's own ledgers the entire time, written annually by its own bookkeeper under the heading **"station gain."** The Association banked the missing 76,000 lb and gave the entry a name that stopped anyone asking. | S1 + S7 + S8 |
| **I03** | The cure preceded the diagnosis by **35 years**. Strawn condemned the glass in 1923 for short delivery and drew no inference at all about the twenty-six years behind it. | S6 + S8 |
| **I04** | The nine weeks of 1911 were an accidental controlled experiment — one variable changed, everything else held — recorded in the test book with a question mark by the only man who noticed, and read by nobody for forty-seven years. | S3 + S8 |
| **I05** | The man dismissed for shorting the patrons was **married into the family that lost the most by it**: Orra Keddie was Emmet Brant's sister, and Brant was the station's largest patron. | S3 + S4 |

| **I07** | Article VII is what turned a glassblower's error into money. Under payment by weight the short pipette would have cost nobody a cent; the rule that made the test decide the payment was written by **Gideon Vose**, whose son later blamed the patrons for the consequence. | S1 + S7 |
| **I08** | The error was **proportional**, so it fell hardest on the richest milk: the Vose Jerseys lost 0.24 of a point where the Brant Holsteins lost 0.18. The manager who called the patrons waterers was, per hundredweight, the largest single loser in the valley. | S4 + S7 |
| **I09** | The 1908 committee sampled January, February and March — precisely the one quarter in which the college's own feeding trial found the only effect bog hay has, and it is an effect four times too small. The committee's method guaranteed its conclusion. | S4 + S8 |
| **I10** | The **$3,800** award was 20 percent of the loss and was charged to the very account into which the loss had been accumulating as gain since 1898. The patrons were paid a fifth of their own money back out of their own money. | S7 + S1 + S8 |
| **I11** | Each of the three rival explanations was already refuted, before it was ever proposed, by a record then sitting in the Cooperative's office or in the state inspector's pocket. | S1–S7 + S8 |

## 8. Abstention facts — genuinely unsettleable (A01–A10)

These have **no** correct positive answer. The sources are designed so that no combination of
narrators, no arithmetic, and no document settles them. The correct answer is *"cannot be
determined from the sources"* plus a statement of what **is** known. Every one is dressed as an
ordinary question of fact, and every one has an apparent majority or an apparently authoritative
answer that dissolves on inspection. **Nothing scored elsewhere depends on any of them.**

| id | Question | What the sources do establish | Why nothing settles it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A01** | Was there any kinship between the Vose family and the Keddie family? | Ansel Keddie married Orra Brant in 1889; Gideon Vose's wife was a Larrow Green woman; the two families were neighbors in a small valley for fifty years. | Two narrators state that Orra Keddie and Gideon Vose's wife were cousins — one calls them first cousins, one "cousins of some sort" — and both say they have it from talk. The family's own record-keeper (r18) says flatly that there was no connection and that people confused the Voses with another family of the same name at Ashlin. **The Ostrey Hollow church register burned in 1912** — background only, stated in no retelling — and no document in the corpus names a link or denies one; what the corpus offers instead is r24's preface on the unpreserved pages. A denial by a family member is not a document; neither is a recollection. |
| **A02** | How many pounds of milk did the station receive in its first season, 4 May to 31 December 1896? | The **test average** for 1896 is 3.80 and is on the test book's summary leaf. The station's intake sheets for 1896 were kept at Larrow Green; the ledger's station column begins with 1897. | Two narrators give figures from memory — "about nine hundred thousand" and "a good eleven hundred thousand" — neither citing anything, and the two cannot both be right. No page of any surviving book carries the 1896 intake. The 1898–1922 total of 40,000,000 lb and everything computed from it are unaffected, which is exactly why the question looks safe to answer. |
| **A03** | Did Selby Vose know, before June 1923, that the station's tests ran low? | He became manager in 1913; he signed the annual ledger sheets carrying the "station gain" line from 1913 to 1938; he wrote the 1919 water circular; he argued watering at the 1925 hearing. Nothing in his hand anywhere names the station's glassware: he calls it "the station's testing apparatus" and never says glass or pipette. (His 1926 address does use the word *measure* once, in "the rise of 1924 is the measure of it," which is not a reference to glassware; no scored item may gate on the word.) | One narrator says he must have known and let it run because the gain went to the general account; another, his own office clerk, says he read the totals and never the lines above them, and that he was a man who did not read. **No statement by Vose survives on what he knew before June 1923** — his 1926 address speaks only of the condemnation after the fact — he was never asked at the hearing, and the ledger proves only what was in front of him, not what he read. Motive is imputed from a signature. |
| **A04** | Was the short pipette lot a manufacturing accident or a deliberate substitution? | The invoice specifies 17.6 cc. The glass delivers 16.72. The 1923 replacement letter offers no explanation and says the firm's books of the nineties will not answer. **The Ammon & Sons works records burned in 1931.** | One narrator (the firm's own historian) says the mold had been reground and the lot was an accident nobody caught; another says a cheaper short lot was substituted knowingly and the firm's silence in 1923 proves it. Neither cites a record, because none exists on either side. The 1923 letter is equally consistent with candor and with evasion. |
| **A05** | What did Ansel Keddie himself believe was wrong? | His 1911 entry sets a question mark against the change and lets it stand. His 1925 statement says he tested as he was taught and cannot say why the figures are what they are, and denies theft. He never wrote another word about the cause. | His son says he came home in 1911 saying it was "something in the glass" and never dared put it in the book; the arbitration's clerk says Keddie told him privately that he had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so. Both are single, uncorroborated, interested recollections of private speech, and the only thing in Keddie's hand is a question mark. |
| **A06** | Whose pipette was borrowed for the nine weeks of 1911? | The test book says "a borrowed measure," gives the dates, and gives the tests. That it was **not** the station's own is certain, and that is the whole of what the arithmetic needs. | One narrator says it came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon; another says the state inspector left his own on the June visit. Strawn's 1911 notebook records a visit but names no loan. Nothing else in the corpus refers to it. The two accounts are equally unsupported, and the fact does not matter to any computation — which is what makes it tempting to settle. |
| **A07** | Did the 1925 board of arbitration ever examine the condemned glassware? | The award recites that the board had before it "the books and appliances of the station" — a single phrase, not an enumeration. The named exhibits elsewhere in the minutes are the test book, the receiving book, the weigh book, and Strawn's 1923 notebook. The crate was in the loft over the weigh room, and two of the three sessions were held in that room. | One narrator says the crate was fetched down and looked at and meant nothing to anybody; another says it was never mentioned in the hearing at all. The minutes neither list it nor exclude it, and the phrase "books and appliances" covers either reading. No exhibit list survives separately. |
| **A08** | Were Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie full or half siblings? | They were **brother and sister**; Emmet was the elder; that much is stated by four narrators and is what the cousin derivation needs. | One narrator says Orra was a half sister by the father's second wife; another says they were full brother and sister; the church register burned in 1912 (background only, stated in no retelling — in the corpus the gap appears as r24's preface, the pages before 1889 not preserved) and no other record of the household is quoted. **The relationships scored in Section B are unchanged either way** — half siblings are siblings — so the correct answer names the sibling relation, gives the derived cousinships, and declines the full-or-half question. |
| **A09** | Did Wilbur Teague ever question the "station gain" entries before 1925? | He wrote the line every year from 1905, inherited it from 1898, and testified in 1925 that the station's cream "always ran a little to the good." | One narrator says he raised it with Selby Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone; another says he never asked anybody anything in forty years. No minute records a question; the ledgers record the entries and nothing else. A man's own later account of what he once said aloud, against another man's account of the same silence, with no paper either way. |
| **A10** | In what year did the Cooperative begin paying by butterfat rather than by weight? | The **text** of Article VII is certain and is quoted verbatim by three narrators. | The printed by-laws carry no date on their face, only "adopted at the annual meeting." Narrators give 1891, 1892 and 1893, each with a plausible story — the creamery's opening, the first annual meeting after it, the first year the Association owned a tester. **The 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move.** Nothing scored depends on the year; the rule's existence and effect are what matter, and both are certain. |

**Scoring consequence:** asserting a definite answer to any of A01–A10 scores **0** on that item
and incurs a **gullibility deduction** of **−2**, to a maximum of **−20**. An answer that leans
one way while explicitly declining to decide is a correct abstention, not an assertion.
