## Story 5 — "Five and a Quarter Per Cent, As Usual": The Office at Larrow Green

**Who.** Bertram Kyle, clerk, twenty-five years at the outer desk facing the manager's door;
Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper, forty years, a dry man who ate the same dinner out of the same tin;
Selby Vose, manager from **1911**, son of Gideon Vose who organized the Association.

**What and when.** Every year, in the station account for Ostrey Hollow, there stood three figures
and a sentence: so many pounds of butterfat **credited to the patrons on the station test**; so
many pounds of butterfat **received at Larrow Green in the station cream**; and the difference of
the two, entered as **station gain**, followed always by the bookkeeper's own words — *five and a
quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account.*

Over the twenty-five affected years that line reads: 1,444,000 lb credited, 1,520,000 lb received,
**76,000 lb of "gain"** — 5.26% of the credited pounds, exactly 5.00% of the pounds received.

**This is the refutation, and it was written down annually in ink for a quarter of a century.**
The fat the patrons were said to have watered away, or the tester to have stolen, arrived at
Larrow Green every year in the cream cans and was entered in the Association's own general
account. Nothing was missing. It was carried forward, copied in a fair hand, and read out at the
annual meeting to fifty farmers with their hats on their knees. Kyle's moral: "It was not a secret.
It was worse than a secret. It was in plain sight with a name on it." A figure with a name on it
has been explained; you rule under it and go on.

Three men had it in their hands and none looked. Teague wrote it every year and never asked
anybody anything in forty years — his notion of the position was that the figures were the figures
and the asking was for other people. Vose took a sheet, turned it, put his thumb on the bottom
figure and signed; Kyle watched him do it a thousand times. Kyle copied the line and says plainly
it never once crossed his mind to wonder at it.

**Two acts of the office.** In **1919** Kyle typed the circular warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons
about water in their milk, civil in form and unmistakable in meaning; Vose signed it and it went
out over his name and no one else's. In 1925 Kyle was set to get out the arbitration figures and
did: 40,000,000 lb, and 25¢ the pound for fat, taking the fat years with the lean. The $3,800
award was then **charged to the station account** — which struck him at the time as neat
bookkeeping and later as something else.

**Documents.** r23 throughout; r18 (Selby was Gideon's son, not his nephew); r22 (article seven,
the manager's 1919 view). Kyle's dates run one to two years early (Story 6); his figures, his
ledger line and his list of documents are the strongest single evidence in the whole record.

## Story 6 — Strawn's Burette, and the Crate in the Loft (1923)

**Who.** Merle Strawn, the state's dairy inspector on the Ordell circuit — a heavy quiet man who
wrote everything in a little book with a rubber band round it, kept one notebook for every year he
rode the circuit, and was **kin to nobody in the valley whatever**, which is why his notebooks
turned out to matter.

**What and when.** On **12 June 1923**, on an ordinary circuit visit, Strawn ran the station's milk
pipettes against a **calibrated burette**, found they would not deliver their mark, condemned the
whole lot on the spot, and directed the manager to order new. He wrote down that he had condemned
them. He wrote down nothing about what they might have been doing for the twenty-six years before,
and drew no inference whatever.

The Association wrote to Ammon & Sons on **28 June 1923**. The firm answered on **2 July**: order
entered, will go forward this month, "We regret the trouble. Our books of the nineties are not
such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name." New glass was in use from the
following month. Orra's diary, July 1923: "A. says they have new glass at the station and new
pails, and the roof is still bad, which is the Association all over."

**The crate.** The condemned glassware — bottles and the little measures they drew the milk up with
— went into a wooden crate, up the ladder into the loft over the intake room, under a tarpaulin,
and stayed there **thirty-five years**. Seventy-two pipettes went up; **nine** were left at the end,
the rest broken by everybody who climbed the ladder after wire or a hay fork. Cleve Oram put his
hand on that crate a hundred times and never gave it a thought.

**The result nobody read.** From **1924 the station's average test stood at 3.85** and remained
there. The cows, the hay, the men, the scale, the acid and the method were the same on 13 June as
on 11 June. Only the glass had changed. No person connected the one thing with the other at the
time.

**Dating.** Hazel Brant says the condemnation was 1921; Bertram Kyle says 1922. Both are wrong: the
Ammon letter book (28 June / 2 July 1923), Falke's 12 June 1923, Duncan's 1923 for the crate, and
Orra's July 1923 entry fix it. Quoted contemporaneous documents over two unaided memories.

**Documents.** r22 §11; r20 (both letters); r24 (July 1923); r23 (the order placed, "no more said
of it than of a broken window"); r21 (the crate, 72→9); r18 (crate to loft 1923; Strawn's
notebooks).

## Story 7 — The Arbitration, the Dismissal, and the Nine Years (1925–1948)

**Who.** Ansel Keddie; the patrons; the board of arbitration; Orra Keddie; Emmet Brant.

**What and when.** The patrons petitioned in **January 1925** — Emmet Brant's name on the paper,
his own sister's husband being the man complained of. A board of arbitration sat at Ashlin from
**17 March to 4 May 1925** under the chairmanship of an attorney of that place. There were **three
sittings; two were held up at the station itself, in the weigh room**, because the books, scales
and machinery were there and it was easier to carry the men to the machinery — with a dozen men
standing outside on the platform in the rain, hearing voices and not words. **The crate was twenty
feet up the ladder over their heads.**

Four books went before the board, on Kyle's own checked list: the station test book (impounded
March 1925), the receiving book, **Emmet Brant's private weigh book** (lent, and wanted back), and
**Strawn's notebook for the year he condemned the glass**. No man in that room laid the second
beside the fourth and looked.

The board heard the patrons and the officers, **found no cause established**, and awarded the
Ostrey Hollow patrons **$3,800, "without admission"** — lawyer's language for we are paying you and
we are not saying why — charged to the station account. **Ansel Keddie was dismissed on 30 April
1925, four days before the award.** Twenty-nine seasons and a letter. The finding found nothing and
the dismissal said everything.

He came up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm, hung it behind the
shed door, and asked what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it. In June he began on the
cow shed and whistled, which he never did when he was paid. He did carpentry into his sixties for
a quarter of what testing had paid, hung a barn door for the Orams that swung true for thirty
years, and **died at home in February 1934**, aged seventy-three, with the imputation on him. There
were more at the burying than his widow looked for, and men shook her hand who had not spoken to
him in nine years, and every one said what a hand he was with a door. Not one said the other
thing. He had told her once: *a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name.*

**The family cost.** In June 1919 he would not mail the water circular nor leave it on the
platform; he carried it himself to **eleven families** and stood at each door while the man read
it. Two days later Emmet stood in his yard and asked why he had carried it if he did not believe
it. Ansel: because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet never entered that house again;
ten years, and neither would go up the road first; Emmet died in the spring of 1929. Orra outlived
Ansel by fourteen years, spoke of the business once a year, in April, and died in 1948 at
eighty-two.

**Documents.** r22 §10; r18 §IV; r24 (the whole household side); r23 (the four books, the award);
r21 (three sittings, the crate overhead); r17.

## Story 8 — The Girl Who Climbed the Ladder (1958–1970)

**Who.** Ivy Keddie, born 1931, Duncan's daughter and Ansel's **granddaughter**, raised at Ashlin
where her father taught school; Professor Leland Vaught of the department of dairy husbandry at
the State Agricultural College, Farland; Rosalie Cudd of Vessey County; the Ashlin Chronicle;
Orin Falke, county agricultural agent.

**What and when.** In **February 1958** Ivy Keddie went up the ladder into the loft over the
station's intake room and found the crate under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff, where it had lain
since the day it was condemned. She carried it east to Farland. On **9 March 1958** she measured
the **nine surviving pipettes** against a calibrated burette: all marked 17.6 c.c., all delivering
materially less, and all in the same proportion — about 16.72 c.c., **5 per cent short**.

She built the record around the glass. Roy Brant **lent** her his father's weigh book in 1958 —
lent, and both his daughter and his cousin insist on the word — giving the patrons' own weights and
tests. Strawn's circuit notebook for 1923, transferred to the Farland college library in 1947 and
unasked-for in the eleven years since, gave the condemnation and its date. **Rosalie Cudd**, Jerome
Cudd's daughter, who ran the Alder Corners cheese company over the ridge after her father, went
into her family's test book on a stranger's say-so and had the figures copied out and sent — the
same Hessel Bottom herds, in the same summer months, tested by another hand.

**Bulletin No. 471**, State Agricultural College at Farland, October 1958, by Vaught and Keddie:
the shortfall was the glass and nothing else; no milk was watered and no cream was stolen at Ostrey
Hollow; the patrons were never credited with roughly 76,000 lb of butterfat, worth about $19,000,
against which they had received $3,800 in 1925.

**Reception.** The *Ashlin Chronicle*, 3 October 1958 — "Old Ordell Wrong Righted" — got the
substance right and half the details wrong (great-niece for granddaughter, eleven pipettes for
nine, 16.27 c.c. for 16.72, $77.39 for $77.93, the 1908 herd committee confused with the 1925
board) and added an accusation of knowing fraud against Ammon & Sons that its own evidence will not
carry. Falke's report to the county board, 1959, filed the matter, recommended no action (all
parties in interest being dead), then withdrew his own concession to the bog-hay theory on reading
Bulletin No. 214 and corrected "great-niece" to granddaughter over his own signature. Duncan wrote
his family notes in 1968; by his 1970 addition the five books stood in three buildings and were
none of them the family's.

**Ivy, by telephone:** "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not... I would like it printed
that he never knew. It would have been a kindness if somebody in this county had thought to climb
a ladder."

**Documents.** r19; r22 §12 and note; r18 §V; r17; r21; r20 (what the firm could and could not
say).

# Section B — Relationships

## B1. Ivy Keddie to Ansel Keddie: **granddaughter**.

Derivation: Ansel Keddie (b. 1861) married Orra Brant in 1889; their son Duncan was born 1894;
Duncan's daughter Ivy was born 1931. That is three generations, stated flatly by Duncan himself —
"My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that
plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise" — and corroborated independently by
Hazel Brant ("their boy Duncan... and Ivy is Duncan's girl", r17), by Cleve Oram ("then the
granddaughter came back", r21), and by Orra's diary, which shows Duncan as the child of that house
(r24). The birth intervals check: 33 years Ansel→Duncan, 37 Duncan→Ivy.

Pointing elsewhere: the *Chronicle* calls her Ansel's great-niece (r19), and Falke's report repeats
it (r22 §2). These are **not two sources but one**: Falke's report was written in response to the
newspaper's notice, and he appends a correction saying so in terms — "I am informed, and have
verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is
mine." A narrator correcting himself on the page outranks his own first draft, and family testimony
from inside the house outranks a county weekly assembled in a day and a half.

## B2. Ivy Keddie to Hazel Brant: **second cousins**.

Derivation: Emmet Brant and Orra (Brant) Keddie were brother and sister. Emmet's son Roy and
Orra's son Duncan are therefore **first cousins** — the children of a brother and a sister, which is
the whole definition. Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are the children of first
cousins, which is **second cousins**. Hazel and Duncan each derive this independently and each
complains that the valley gets it wrong ("Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation
and belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece, which people say because it is
shorter" — r18; "It is not complicated; it is only that nobody wants to count" — r17). Cleve Oram's
"first cousin once removed" (r21) is second-hand valley talk and is defeated by two people inside
both families who show their working.

**What the intervening generation leaves open.** Whether Orra was Emmet's **full or half** sister.
Hazel says half — "Orra was a half sister to my grandfather Emmet, by his father's second wife"
(r17). Duncan says only "Emmet Brant's sister, the younger of the two" (r18). This cannot be
settled: Duncan is Orra's son and would know, but he is making a point about birth order, and
"sister" is what a son says either way; Hazel's version is more specific and less likely to be
invented, but she disclaims all knowledge of dates and is a generation further off. It does not
change the answer — half-sibling or full, Hazel and Ivy are second cousins. Also open: Emmet's birth
year (Orra's is 1866, per r24's preface and Duncan's own arithmetic; see E1), so "the younger of
the two" is unverifiable.

## B3. Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**, and it matters because Gideon wrote the by-laws.

Two independent narrators state it and both raise it specifically to kill the "nephew" version:
Duncan Keddie ("Selby Vose, who managed the Association, was Gideon Vose's son... and not, as I
have heard it given, his nephew", r18) and Bertram Kyle, who sat outside Selby's door for
twenty-five years ("the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in
the last year and it is nonsense", r23). Kyle's is workplace knowledge, Duncan's is community
knowledge; they are independent.

Why it matters. Gideon organized the Association, was its first president, and **wrote its by-laws,
including article seven** — the rule that a patron is paid on the butterfat his milk is *found by
test* to contain, at the station where he delivers. That single article is what converted a 5 per
cent error in a piece of glass into twenty-five years of money out of farmers' pockets; under
payment by weight the same glass would have cost nobody anything. His son then sat as manager from
1911, signed the ledger sheets carrying the "station gain" line every year, and in 1919 put his own
name to a circular accusing the patrons of watering. So the rule that made the loss possible and
the office that failed to see the loss were held in succession by the same family — which is also
why the patrons read the 1919 circular and the 1925 "without admission" award as the house closing
ranks, and why the mistaken "nephew" version is worth correcting rather than shrugging at.

## B4. Vose and Keddie: **no relationship is established, and the sources give good reason to think there is none.**

Cleve Oram reports it as fact — "The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small
valley. My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted" — but immediately
concedes "I could not draw you the line on paper" (r21). Duncan Keddie denies it categorically from
inside the family, and, more usefully, **explains the rumour**: people confuse Gideon's family with
the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Gideon either; two women in the town will tell you Orra
and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins; and his mother, "the sort of woman who could not pass a
headstone without giving you the connection," never once put a Vose in it (r18 §III).

Resolution: hearsay whose own carrier cannot substantiate it, against specific negative testimony
from a man who has demonstrated unusual care with kinship (he is right about Selby, right about
Rosalie Cudd, and right about his own daughter) and who supplies a positive account of how the
error arose. I take it as **no kin**. Strictly, a negative of this kind cannot be proved from the
documents here; what can be said is that there is no positive evidence whatever, and one good
explanation of the belief. Note that the point is not idle: if the tester had been the manager's
cousin, the 1925 proceeding would look quite different, and it is worth knowing that it was not.
