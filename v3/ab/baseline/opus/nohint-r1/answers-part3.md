# Section C — Math

## C1.

**(a) The proportion. The tests read 95% of the truth — the glass was 5% short.**

The pipettes were blown and marked **17.6 c.c.**, the standard charge of milk for a butterfat test
and the charge the graduated neck of the test bottle is ruled for. They delivered **16.72 c.c.**

> 17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88 c.c. short**; 0.88 ÷ 17.6 = **0.05 = 5.00%**
> recorded test = 16.72/17.6 × true test = **0.95 × true**
> true test = recorded ÷ 0.95 = recorded × 20/19; deficiency = recorded ÷ 19

Short a *fixed proportion* of the charge, the fat column in the neck stands a fixed proportion
short, and the reading is short in the same proportion — every sample, every month, every year.

**How I get 16.72 rather than the 16.27 c.c. the *Chronicle* prints (r19).** 16.27 is a
transposition, and three independent checks refuse it:

1. *The test figures.* Station average recorded 3.61 (r22). 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80, deficiency
   **0.19** — exactly the "nineteen hundredths" given independently by Hazel Brant from the weigh
   book (r17) and by Falke from the bulletin (r22). With 16.27: 3.61 ÷ 0.92443 = 3.905, deficiency
   0.295. Wrong.
2. *The ledger.* The Association's station gain ran at **5¼% as usual** (r23). If patrons are
   credited 0.95 of the fat that arrives, the gain is 0.05/0.95 = **5.26%** of the credited pounds.
   With 16.27 the gain would have to be 0.0756/0.9244 = 8.2%. Wrong.
3. *The largest patron's leaf.* 1910: recorded 3.42 (r17). 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = **3.60** exactly.
   With 16.27: 3.70. Less clean, and inconsistent with the 0.19 average.

Three independent bodies of evidence — the county agent's averages, the creamery's ledger, and a
farmer's private weigh book — all agree on 0.95, and the *Chronicle* is elsewhere demonstrably
careless with digits (it also prints $77.39 for an invoice that adds to $77.93). **16.72 c.c.**

**(b) True average test 3.80; recorded 3.61; short by 0.19 of a point.**

Recorded average over the period of complaint: 3.61 (r22 §5). True = 3.61 × 20/19 = **3.80**.
Corroboration: the Larrow Green creamery's own milk averaged 3.78 over the same years — an ordinary
figure for the county — and from 1924, with new glass, the same station's average stood at 3.85
(r22 §§5, 11). The Ostrey Hollow cows were never any different.

**(c) 76,000 lb of butterfat.**

Milk received over the affected years: **40,000,000 lb** (Kyle, who got the figure out for the
arbitration himself; 1,600,000 a year × 25 years, internally consistent — r23).

> credited fat = 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**
> true fat    = 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lb**
> not credited = **76,000 lb**  (check: 40,000,000 × 0.0019 = 76,000 ✔)

Cleve Oram's "sixty-seven thousand pounds" (r21) is a transposition of 76,000; he is repeating a
figure he heard, and says himself he cannot picture it.

**(d) About $19,000, of which the patrons received one fifth.**

Butterfat averaged **25¢ the pound** over the whole stretch, taking the fat years with the lean
(Kyle, r23, who was set to find that figure and found it).

> 76,000 lb × $0.25 = **$19,000**
> award actually paid, 1925 = **$3,800** "without admission"
> 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **0.20 — exactly one fifth**

*On the $19,600 in r19.* The *Chronicle* reports the bulletin's figure as $19,600. It cannot be
reproduced from any combination of figures in the record (it would need 78,400 lb at 25¢, or
76,000 lb at 25.8¢), whereas $19,000 falls out of two independently sourced numbers and yields an
exact 20% for the award — a coincidence too clean to discard. I therefore give **$19,000**, and
flag that the bulletin itself is not in evidence, only the newspaper's paraphrase of it; if the
bulletin used a slightly different milk total or fat price, its own figure may stand and the
discrepancy is unresolvable from here.

*A further point on the fraction:* the $3,800 was **charged to the station account** (r23), so the
patrons arguably contributed to their own award; that is not quantifiable from the sources.

## C2. The largest patron, Emmet Brant, 1910.

From the head of the 1910 leaf of the Brant weigh book, the one year Hazel Brant is certain of
because she has read the page a thousand times (r17): **200,000 lb even** delivered to the station,
his scale and the station's agreeing, **paid on a test of 3.42**.

**(a)** Credited butterfat = 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb**

**(b)** True test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60. True fat = 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb.
Not credited = 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb** (check: 6,840 ÷ 19 = 360 ✔)

**(c)** 360 lb × $0.25 = **$90.00** in 1910 money.

*Assumption stated:* 25¢ is the long-run average price of butterfat over the whole stretch (r23);
no price for 1910 specifically appears in any source. The pound figures in (a) and (b) are exact
given the leaf; the $90 is an estimate at the average price.

## C3. Over the same 25 full delivery years.

**(a)** Credited to the patrons: **1,444,000 lb** (40,000,000 × 3.61%).

**(b)** Actually reaching Larrow Green in the station cream: **1,520,000 lb** (40,000,000 × 3.80%).
These are precisely the first two of the three figures Kyle copied every year in the station
account (r23).

**(c)** Difference **76,000 lb**. Now, *of what*:

> 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.263%  of the pounds credited to the patrons**
> 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5.000%  of the pounds actually received**

The bookkeeper's standing entry — "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" — is the **first** of
these: the gain is struck as a percentage of the credited figure, which is why it reads 5¼ and not
5. The glass's own error is the **second**: exactly 5% of the true charge. The two numbers are the
same fact seen from either end (0.05/0.95 = 0.0526), and mistaking one for the other is the easiest
way to get this arithmetic wrong.

*Assumption:* that essentially all the fat left the station in the cream, i.e. that fat lost in the
skim or in transit is negligible. The sources support this indirectly — the ledger's gain sits right
at the figure the glass predicts, leaving no room for a material third term — but no source measures
it.

## C4. The invoice.

**(a) $77.93.** The invoice of 14 April 1897 is reproduced in facsimile in the firm's own history
(r20). Its column:

> 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = **77.93**

Junia Ammon's prose immediately under the facsimile says "Seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine
cents," and the *Chronicle* independently prints "$77.39" (r19). **Two narrators carrying the same
wrong value** — and the tie is broken not by counting narrators but by adding the quoted document's
own column. r20 contradicts itself on the page; r19 either copied the same slip or made it too.
The line items are further self-checking: 6 doz. × $0.35 = $25.20 ✔, 12 doz. × $0.22 = $31.68 ✔.

**(b) 72 pipettes (six dozen), each short 0.88 c.c.**

6 × 12 = 72; and 72 × $0.35 = $25.20, matching the invoice line exactly. Cleve Oram's "five dozen…
that was the whole outfit the station was set up with" (r21) fails the same arithmetic: 60 × 0.35
= $21.00, not $25.20. Shortfall per pipette: 17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88 c.c.**, i.e. 5% of the marked
capacity.

## C5.

**(a) The glass was in use for 26 years; the money runs over 25 full delivery years.**

*In use:* **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923** — twenty-six years and six weeks. Start: the outfit went
into use 1 May 1897 (r20, and Orra's diary of that very day, r24). End: condemned by Strawn on 12
June 1923 (r22 §11), new glass in use from the following month (r20's letters, r24's July entry).
Ammon's own phrase, "six and twenty years afterward," and the *Chronicle*'s "twenty-six years
before," both agree.

*The money calculation:* **25 full delivery years, 1898 through 1922.** The two numbers differ
because **1897 and 1923 are part-years**: the faulty glass came into service four months into the
1897 season (and the season before that, 1896, ran on glass borrowed from Larrow Green, so it is
unaffected altogether), and was condemned five and a half months into 1923, the rest of that year
running on sound glass. A loss calculation can only use years in which the station both received
milk for twelve months and tested it with the bad glass throughout.

The record confirms 25 independently: Kyle's arbitration figures are 40,000,000 lb total at
1,600,000 lb a year (r23) — 40,000,000 ÷ 1,600,000 = **25** exactly. Two different routes to the
same count.

**(b) About 1,100,000 lb in the first season (1896).**

Kyle: "People have asked me what the Hollow took in that first year and I have always said the
same: a good eleven hundred thousand pounds, which is what I understood at the time from men who
were there. It is not in any book I ever ruled" (r23). That season's milk was weighed at the Hollow
and carried on the creamery's own books before there was a station account, and those sheets were
never put with the station papers.

**Caveat, and it is a real one:** this is a single narrator, reporting hearsay, about a season
before his own time, and he says himself it is in no book. It is also markedly below the 1,600,000
lb annual average of the complaint years — plausible for a station's opening season (it opened 4
May, missing the spring), but not verifiable. Answer with low confidence: **≈1,100,000 lb**.

# Section D — Logic

## D1. The nine weeks of 1911, treated as an experiment.

**When and how long.** From **8 May 1911**, when the station's own measuring glass was broken, to
**10 July 1911**, when it was resumed: sixty-three days, **exactly nine weeks** (r22 §6, and Orra
Keddie's diary entry of 8 May 1911 recording the broken measure, r24 — an independent
contemporaneous fix on the start date).

**The variable:** the measuring pipette, and nothing else. A measure borrowed from elsewhere
replaced the station's own.

**Held constant:** the herds, the pasture, the season, the feed, the men, the machinery, the
scale, the acid, the method, the building, the supervision — and above all **the same tester
making the tests**.

**The result:** through those nine weeks the station's tests stood markedly higher; on the day the
station resumed its own glass they fell back to their former level. An ABA design: intervention and
return to baseline, which is stronger than a single change.

**What it establishes that a calculation could not.** Every calculation available from the books —
the 0.19 gap, the low averages, the station gain — establishes only that a *discrepancy* existed;
and all three theories of the valley (watering, theft, bog hay) were fully compatible with a
discrepancy of any size. Arithmetic cannot discriminate among causes that predict the same number.
The nine weeks change one physical object, hold the men and the milk fixed, and move the result —
so the cause is **in the apparatus**, not in the cows, the feed, the honesty of the farmers or the
honesty of the tester. It converts a correlation into a located cause, and it does so eight years
before the manager sent the water circular and fourteen before the hearing at which all three
theories were argued.

**What it does not establish.** (i) *Which* piece of glass was at fault: the borrowed measure was
never gauged either, so in principle the borrowed one could have read high rather than the
station's reading low — it takes Strawn's burette in 1923 to fix the direction. (ii) *By how much*:
"markedly higher" is not a number; nothing here gives 5% or 0.19. (iii) That the error was
**constant** across the other twenty-six years — that comes from the flatness of the 0.19 across
months and seasons and from the 1958 measurements. (iv) Anything about the manufacturer, the mold,
or the date the fault began. (v) Ansel Keddie's innocence in the eyes of anyone, since he did not
write the inference down — only a question mark — and the interval sat unread in the test book in
front of the 1925 board.

## D2. Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream.

**Reason 1 — the nine weeks of 1911 (r22 §6; r24; r18).** The same man made the tests, under no
different supervision, with a borrowed measure, and the figures **rose**; when his own glass came
back they fell. A thief does not steal less for exactly the interval in which his glass is broken
and resume on the day it is repaired.

**Reason 2 — the Association's own ledger (r23).** Every year the station account recorded the fat
credited to the patrons, the fat **received at Larrow Green in the station cream**, and the
difference as "station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual." The fat the tester was
supposed to have stolen **arrived at the creamery**, in the cream cans, every year, and was carried
to the general account in the bookkeeper's own hand. Fat cannot both be stolen at Ostrey Hollow and
delivered at Larrow Green. This is not an argument about character; it is a closed mass balance.

**Reason 3 — the flatness of the deficiency (r17, corroborated r22 §5).** Nineteen hundredths
lower, "every month, every season, wet years and dry… flat as a floor," including the June flush
and February. Human taking varies with opportunity, with need, with who is watching; a fixed
proportional error does not. Add the four Hessel Bottom farms that hauled over the ridge to Alder
Corners in the summer flush and to the station the rest of the year (r17): the same cows tested
higher at the other factory in the very months a thief at Ostrey Hollow would have had the most
cream to take.

**Reason 4 (supporting) — conduct (r24, r18, r17).** He carried the 1919 water circular to eleven
doors himself and stood while each man read it rather than let another man carry it; he set a
question mark in his own book against the one interval that pointed at his apparatus; and the
patron he was said to have robbed worst was his wife's brother, at his table on Sundays for
thirty-six years.

**Why these are independent.** They come from different custodians, different document types and
different narrators: (1) is a physical event at the station recorded in the tester's own book and in
a household diary; (2) is the Association's general accounting, kept by a different man in a
different building seven miles away and reported by a clerk who was hostile to nobody; (3) is the
pattern in the patrons' own weigh-book figures plus a third party's records over the ridge; (4) is
household testimony. No one of them depends on any other being true, and none depends on the 1958
measurement.

**Which survive without the 1958 measurement:** all four. Name two: **the 1911 interval** and **the
station-gain ledger line**. Either alone disposes of the theft charge, and both were in existence,
in writing, before the charge was ever made.

**Did the Association's bookkeeper ever question those ledger entries?** No — never once, in forty
years. Wilbur Teague wrote the "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" line every year of his
service and "never asked anybody anything in forty years"; his notion of the position was that the
figures were the figures and the asking was for other people, and if other people did not ask it
was not his affair (r23). He therefore wrote out, annually and in his own hand, the arithmetic that
refuted the Association's accusation, and filed it. *One qualification I must state:* the sources I
have attribute the cream-theft charge at the 1925 hearing to "an officer of the Association" (r22
§8) without naming him; that the officer was Teague himself is not established in these documents.
Whoever made it, the bookkeeper never questioned the entries.

## D3. Did the manager know, before 1923, that the station's tests ran low?

**Two distinct questions, and the sources answer them differently.**

*Did Selby Vose know the Ostrey Hollow tests ran low?* **Yes, certainly, and long before 1923.**
The complaint was of record from 1902 and was renewed at every annual meeting for a quarter of a
century (r22 §5, r19). He became manager in 1911 (r23). In June 1919 he did not merely know the
tests were low — he had adopted an explanation for it and acted on it, putting his own name and no
one else's to a circular warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons about water in their milk (r23, who
typed it; r24, whose husband delivered it to eleven doors). You do not send that letter unless you
know the tests are low.

*Did he know the tests were **wrong** — that the station's own accounts showed the missing fat
arriving at Larrow Green?* **There is no evidence that he ever drew that inference, and good
evidence that he never looked.** Kyle, who sat a foot from his door for twenty-five years and
watched everything pass: "he read the totals and he never read the lines above them… He would take
a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign. I watched him do it a thousand
times." And when Strawn came into the office in person to say he had condemned the station's
pipettes, Vose ordered new glass and there was "no more said of it in the office than there would
have been of a broken window" (r23).

**Answer.** He knew the fact and never read its meaning. He had in his hands every year, over his
own signature, the three figures that disproved the theory he was circulating over that same
signature — and the sources establish inattention, not concealment. What **cannot** be determined
is whether he ever consciously connected the station gain to the low tests and let it lie: Kyle's
evidence is behavioural and observed from outside the door, and no document records Vose's own
account of anything. On the evidence available the charitable reading and the damning reading are
not distinguishable; what is certain is that the information was in front of him annually and he
acted, in 1919, as though it were not.

# Section E — Contradictions

*Format: the claims → who → resolution (or "unsettled") → method.*

### E1a. Straight contradictions between narrators

1. **Invoice total: $77.93 vs $77.39.** r20's facsimile totals 77.93; r20's prose and r19 both say
   "seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents." → **$77.93.** *Method: arithmetic on the quoted
   document* (25.20+31.68+0.90+18.00+2.15), backed by the line items' own internal checks. See E1b.
2. **Pipettes bought: 72 (six dozen) vs 60 (five dozen).** r19 and r20's invoice vs r21 (Oram). →
   **72.** *Method: arithmetic* — 72 × $0.35 = $25.20, the invoice line; 60 would be $21.00.
3. **Pipettes surviving in the crate: nine vs eleven.** r22 §12 and r21 vs r19. → **nine.**
   *Method: reliability plus independence* — Falke takes it from the bulletin and Oram from having
   handled the crate before 1958, two different routes to nine; r19 is the least accurate document
   in the set on every checkable number.
4. **Average delivery of the glass: 16.72 vs 16.27 c.c.** Derived (r17+r22+r23) vs r19. → **16.72.**
   *Method: triangulation* — 16.27 is refused by the 0.19-point deficiency, by the 5¼% ledger line,
   and by the 3.42 leaf; 16.72 satisfies all three. A digit transposition.
5. **Fat never credited: 76,000 lb vs 67,000 lb.** Derived (r23 × r22) vs r21. → **76,000.**
   *Method: arithmetic* (40,000,000 × 0.0019); a second transposition, in a narrator repeating a
   figure he says he cannot picture.
6. **The 1925 award: $3,800 vs $3,500.** r19, r22 §10, r23 vs r21. → **$3,800.** *Method:
   independence, not majority* — Kyle got the arbitration figures out himself and Falke worked from
   the county record; Oram is quoting his father across fifty-two years. (Note r19 and r22 may both
   trace to the bulletin, so the "three sources" are really two.)
7. **Year of the award: 1925 vs 1924.** r18, r21, r22, r24 vs r23. → **1925.** *Method:
   contemporaneous document* — Orra's diary of March and 30 April 1925; and r18 and r22 agree
   independently that the dismissal of 30 April fell four days before the 4 May finding.
8. **Year the glass was condemned: 1923 vs 1922 vs 1921.** r18, r20, r22, r24 vs r23 vs r17. →
   **1923 (12 June).** *Method: quoted dated correspondence* — the Ammon letter book (order 28 June
   1923, reply 2 July 1923) and Orra's July 1923 entry. Two memories, two *different* wrong years,
   which is itself informative: they are independent slips, not a shared error.
9. **The outfit went into use 1 May 1897 vs 1 May 1898.** r19, r20, r24 vs r23. → **1897.**
   *Method: contemporaneous document* — the diary entry written that day, plus the invoice of 14
   April 1897. Kyle says himself the station "was opened before my time."
10. **The station/Ansel began in 1896 vs 1895.** r18, r22 vs r17. → **1896 (opened 4 May).**
    *Method: internal consistency* — 29 seasons ending in 1925 requires 1896; and Hazel opens by
    disclaiming all knowledge of years. Her 1895 is probably the winter of building.
11. **Ivy is Ansel's granddaughter vs great-niece.** r17, r18, r21, r22-as-corrected vs r19, r22-as-
    first-written. → **granddaughter.** See B1 and E1b.
12. **Ivy and Hazel are second cousins vs first cousins once removed.** r17, r18 vs r21. → **second
    cousins.** *Method: derivation from stated parentage*, which two narrators perform on the page.
13. **Keddies and Voses were kin vs no kin.** r21 vs r18. → **no kin established**; r18 also
    explains the rumour (confusion with the unrelated Ashlin Voses). *Method: specific negative
    testimony from inside the family, with a mechanism for the error, over unsupported hearsay.*
14. **Rosalie Cudd is Jerome Cudd's daughter vs niece.** r18 (and r18's provenance list: "it passed
    to his daughter Rosalie in 1941") vs r19. → **daughter.** *Method: the more careful narrator,
    who lists the relation precisely because it is "always got wrong," plus his internal
    consistency.*
15. **The 1919 circular went to nine families vs eleven.** r17 vs r24. → **eleven.** *Method:
    proximity to the event* — Orra's husband carried them by hand that night and came home at eleven
    o'clock; Hazel was a small child and remembers the stove, not the count. *Possible
    reconciliation:* eleven patron families in all, of which Hazel counts only the nine on Hessel
    Bottom ("nine families, six farms, if you are counting").
16. **The Brant weigh book was begun 1 January 1900 vs 1 January 1897.** r17 vs r18. → **1897, but
    only just.** *Method: demonstrated care with dates* — Duncan's provenance list is dated
    throughout and his other dates check (Emmet's death in 1929 is confirmed by Orra's diary);
    Hazel disclaims years outright. Low confidence; the sources do not decisively settle it.
17. **Total loss $19,000 vs $19,600.** Derived (r23 × r22) vs r19. → **$19,000**, with the
    reservation stated at C1(d). *Method: reproducibility* — $19,000 falls out of two independently
    sourced figures and makes the award exactly one fifth; $19,600 cannot be reconstructed from
    anything in the record. **This one I hold with less confidence than the others**, because the
    bulletin itself is not in evidence.
18. **Ammon & Sons cheated knowingly vs the lot was an accident.** r19 vs r20. → **unsettled, but
    the accusation is unsupported.** r19's whole argument is that the firm's 1923 silence proves
    1897 knowledge, which does not follow; r20 offers a mechanism (a reground mold; a uniformly
    wrong lot passing every consistency check) and is testimony against interest. But see E1b(4) —
    r20's own chronology leaves a crack. *Method: burden of proof and testimony against interest;
    declining to settle where neither side has evidence.*
19. **"The committee that sat in 1908 sat again in 1925."** r19 vs r22 §§9–10 and r24. → **two
    different bodies**: a committee of the Association sampled the herds in Jan–Mar 1908; a board of
    arbitration under an attorney sat 17 March–4 May 1925. *Method: the detailed account displaces
    the compressed one.*
20. **How many farms were affected: "a valley of six farms" (r19) vs the station serving "the farms
    of that end, Hessel Bottom principally" (r22) and eleven patron families (r24).** →
    **unsettled in detail.** Hessel Bottom was six farms; the station's patron list was larger. The
    total milk (1,600,000 lb a year) is hard to square with six farms of which four hauled elsewhere
    in the flush, which supports the larger list. r19 over-narrows by borrowing Hazel's figure.
21. **Ammon "sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900" vs the 1897 Ordell County invoice.** r20
    against itself — see E1b(4).

### E1b. Cases where two narrators carry the same wrong value

1. **"$77.39" — r19 and r20's prose.** What breaks the tie is not a third narrator but **the
   document both are describing**: the invoice's own column adds to $77.93, and its line items check
   independently (6 doz × .35 = 25.20; 12 doz × .22 = 31.68). Note they are probably not independent
   at all: r19 says "The Chronicle has seen the invoice," so both may be repeating one slip, or r19
   may be repeating r20's house history. *Counting narrators would have got this wrong; adding a
   column gets it right.*
2. **"Great-niece" — r19 and r22 §2.** Broken by (i) **r22 correcting itself in its own appended
   note** — "the error on page two is mine" — and (ii) three family sources deriving the descent from
   named parents. And these two are **not independent**: Falke's report was occasioned by the
   newspaper's notice, and he says he was later "informed, and have verified" otherwise. Two copies
   of one error.
3. **"Nine surviving pipettes" — r21 and r22.** Here the repeated value is the *right* one, and it
   still needs testing: Falke has it from the bulletin, Oram from having handled the crate and
   watched it lose glass over thirty-five years. Genuinely different routes, so the agreement counts.
4. **"Thirty-five years in the loft" — r18 and r21.** Both correct and arithmetically confirmed
   (1923→1958). Listed for completeness.

### E1c. Cases where a narrator contradicts itself on the page

1. **r20 (Junia Ammon), the invoice.** Prints a facsimile totalling **77.93** and says in the next
   sentence "Seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents." The document wins over its own editor.
2. **r20, the territory.** "The house sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900" — and then
   reproduces an **1897** invoice to the Larrow Valley Association for its Ostrey Hollow station,
   which is in Ordell County (Falke, the Ordell County agent, treats the whole valley as his
   county's business, and Ashlin is its town). The invoice wins. *Available reconciliation:* the
   "nothing before 1900" is the drummer's remembered territory, and the Larrow Valley order may have
   come in by mail or been taken at a depot outside the county — but that is speculation, and the
   contradiction stands as written.
3. **r20, the exculpation.** Junia excuses the 1923 letter on the ground that the works books — what
   glass, what gauge, what man, what day — would have answered the question but are gone; yet she
   also dates the fire that destroyed them to **1931, eight years after the letter**. So in 1923 the
   firm still had the records that could have identified the lot, and told a customer of
   twenty-six years' standing that its books could say nothing. **This cannot be settled from the
   sources**: either nobody looked, or somebody looked and found nothing identifiable, and Junia
   writing in 1949 no longer had the means to tell which. It does *not* establish the *Chronicle*'s
   charge of fraud in 1897, which concerns a different act at a different time.
4. **r22 (Falke), twice, deliberately.** He concedes the bog-hay theory in §9 ("this office is not
   prepared to dismiss it… the 1908 committee was not simply wrong") and withdraws the concession in
   his appended note on reading Bulletin No. 214; and he prints "great-niece" in §2 and corrects it
   to granddaughter at the end. Both are self-contradictions on the page, and both **raise** his
   standing rather than lowering it: he dates his sources, states where the record is thin, and
   corrects himself over his own signature.
5. **r19 (the Chronicle).** "Nobody watered anything. Nobody stole anything… a piece of glass out of
   a crate was wrong" — an accident — and, four paragraphs later, "A cheap short lot was put off on
   the Association knowingly." Not a strict contradiction (a firm can defraud while nobody at the
   station cheats), but the paper reaches for a villain after establishing there was none, and its
   proof — that a house which cannot say what became of a lot "is a house that would rather not" —
   is a non sequitur.
6. **r18 (Duncan), his mother's birth year.** "My mother was Orra Brant, born in eighteen
   sixty-eight" and "She was eighty-two when she died in 1948" cannot both be true: 1948 − 1868 = 80.
   His own arithmetic implies **1866**, which is exactly what r24's editorial preface gives. →
   **born 1866**; the "1868" is the slip. *Method: the narrator's internal arithmetic, confirmed by
   an outside document.*
7. **r17 (Hazel).** Opens by declaring "Quantities I have. Years I have not," and then supplies four
   years (1900, 1895, 1921, 1926), three of which are wrong, while every quantity she gives —
   200,000 lb, 3.42, six farms, four haulers, nineteen hundredths — survives every cross-check. Her
   own warning is the correct guide to her evidence, and it is a general lesson for this record:
   **weigh a narrator claim by claim, not document by document.**
8. **r23 (Kyle).** Says he can name the four books "without looking, because I made the list and
   checked them off twice" — and is right about them — in a document that puts the condemnation in
   1922, the award in 1924 and the outfit in 1898. His own explanation covers it: "People suppose a
   clerk lives by the calendar. He does not. He lives by what is on the desk." Trust his figures and
   his documents; date him from elsewhere.

It is not represented as exhaustive over all twenty-four.*

# Section F — Theory

## F1. What actually produced the shortfall.

**The theory, in one chain.** The Larrow Valley Association's by-law seven, written by Gideon Vose,
paid every patron on the butterfat his milk was *found by test* to contain, at the station where he
delivered — not on the weight of his milk. In April 1897 the Association bought a complete testing
outfit for its new Ostrey Hollow station from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet, $77.93 with freight, six
dozen milk-test pipettes among it. That lot was short: the mold, in all probability, had been
reground, and every pipette in the crate delivered about **16.72 c.c. against a mark of 17.6** —
0.88 c.c., **exactly 5 per cent**, under. Nobody in the works caught it, because a lot that is
uniformly wrong is *consistent*, and consistency is the only thing a spot-check of the first, the
tenth and the fiftieth can test.

**Why an error of that size, and why exactly that size.** In the Babcock test the graduated neck of
the test bottle is ruled on the assumption that exactly 17.6 c.c. of milk was charged into it.
Charge 95 per cent of that and the fat column stands 95 per cent as high, and the reading is 95 per
cent of the truth — not approximately, and not on average, but **every time, for every sample, in
every month**, because the error is a fixed ratio of a fixed instrument and has nothing to do with
the milk. The size of the error is therefore neither more nor less than the ratio the glassblower
missed by: 16.72/17.6. In the books it shows as a true 3.80 recorded as 3.61 — **0.19 of a point,
flat as a floor**, in the June flush and in February alike, wet years and dry. That flatness is the
fingerprint: feed effects are seasonal, theft is opportunistic, watering varies by farm and by day;
only an instrument is constant.

**Why it began when it did.** 1 May 1897, the day the station stopped borrowing Larrow Green's
glass and put its own into use. The opening season of 1896 ran on borrowed glass and shows no
deficiency — which is why the trouble dates from the station's second year and not its first.

**Why it ended when it did.** 12 June 1923, when Merle Strawn, on an ordinary circuit round, ran
the pipettes against a calibrated burette, condemned the lot on the spot and told the manager to
order new. New glass was in use within the month, and from 1924 the station's average test stood at
3.85 and stayed there.

**Why nothing had to change at the station or on the farms.** Because nothing did. The cows, the
pasture, the hay, the scale, the vat, the separator, the acid, the method and the man were the same
on 13 June 1923 as on 11 June. The entire cause was a piece of furniture, and replacing the
furniture was the entire cure — which is also why twenty-five years of argument about the character
of farmers and the character of a tester could never have arrived at it, and why the whole thing
could have been settled in an afternoon with a burette and a straight face.

**Quantities.** 25 full delivery years; 40,000,000 lb of milk; 1,444,000 lb of fat credited against
1,520,000 lb delivered; **76,000 lb never paid for, about $19,000**, against which the patrons
received $3,800.

### The three competing explanations, and what defeats each.

**(1) The patrons watered their milk.** Advanced by **Selby Vose**, the Association's manager, in a
circular over his own name in June 1919, and held "rather more quietly" by the officers for years
before. *Defeated by:* the state dairy inspector tested with the lactometer at every visit **from
1908 forward and never once found added water at that station** (r22 §7) — and watering is exactly
the adulteration a lactometer detects. Further, watering would vary farm by farm and day by day and
could not hold a valley-wide deficiency at a constant 0.19 for a quarter century; and the fat
"missing" from the patrons' credit was arriving at Larrow Green in the cream every year (r23), which
it could not do if it had been replaced with water at the farm.

**(2) The tester removed cream and wrote low tests to conceal it.** Urged at the 1925 hearing by
**an officer of the Association**, and believed independently on the road by the **cream hauler**,
who said less cream came off that platform than went onto it and said so on the wagon seat for
years. *Defeated by:* the nine weeks of 1911 — the same man, the same supervision, a borrowed
measure, and the tests **rose**, then fell back the day his own glass returned (r22 §6); and by the
Association's own ledger, where the supposedly stolen fat is recorded arriving at Larrow Green and
carried to the general account as "station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual" (r23). Both
defeaters predate the accusation and neither depends on the 1958 measurement. (The hauler's
impression was honest and is explained: he was eyeing cream cans, not measuring anything, and the
cream he hauled did in fact carry more fat than the patrons had been credited with.)

**(3) The wet hay of Hessel Bottom thins the cream.** Reported by **a committee of the Association**
that came up in Grigg's sleigh in January 1908, looked into the barns and the mangers — and
pointedly not into the station — and reported in March; supported by **a veterinarian at Ashlin in
1926**, and provisionally allowed by **Falke** in 1959. *Defeated by:* the college's own **Bulletin
No. 214**, the 1911 bog-hay feeding trial — "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the
butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that
could be measured on summer pasture." Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not
carry nineteen hundredths in July; Falke read it and withdrew his concession in writing. Defeated
also by the **four Hessel Bottom farms that hauled over the ridge to Alder Corners in the summer
flush** — the same cows on the same bog hay tested higher at the Cudds' factory — and by the 1911
interval, in which the hay did not change and the tests did.

### Two subsidiary questions.

**Did the 1925 board of arbitration ever examine the condemned glassware? No.** The glass was in a
crate under a tarpaulin in the loft over the intake room, and **two of the board's three sittings
were held in the room directly underneath it**, twenty feet and one nailed ladder away, with the
crate over the members' heads the whole of those afternoons. What the board did have was the
*paper* record of the condemnation: Kyle's list of the four books laid before it includes **the
state inspector's notebook for the year he condemned the glass**, alongside the station's own test
book — which contains the question mark of 1911. "I could not have told you then and cannot tell
you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked." The evidence was in
the room, in two forms, and went out again unread.

**What did the tester himself believe?** That it was the glass — and he had believed it since the
summer of **1911**. He said so at home, twice in one evening, at supper and again by the door, which
was not his way. But he would not write down what he could not prove: what went into the station
book was a **question mark**. He had nothing in his hand but a suspicion about a piece of glass, and
against it stood the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the
county. So he kept on for fourteen more years and wrote down every figure that glass gave him, and
those figures were what convicted him. His son's verdict: "the most expensive virtue anybody in that
valley ever had." His granddaughter's, in 1958: "I would like it printed that he never knew" — which
is the one thing in the record that is not quite right, and generously so. He suspected; he could
not prove; and no one gave him a burette.

# Section G — Summary

## G1. (149 words)

From 1897 to 1923 the Larrow Valley Cooperative's skimming station at Ostrey Hollow tested its
patrons' milk with pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. that delivered only 16.72. Because the by-laws paid by
test, every patron was paid 5% short — 0.19 of a point, every month, for twenty-five years: 76,000
lb of butterfat, about $19,000, of which they recovered $3,800. The valley blamed the farmers for
watering, the tester for stealing, and the bog hay for thinning. The tester, Ansel Keddie,
suspected the glass in 1911, could not prove it, was dismissed in 1925 and died disgraced in 1934.
His granddaughter Ivy found the condemned glass in the station loft in 1958 and measured it. It
took eight records because no one of them held both halves: the office ledger showed the missing
fat arriving, the station book showed the anomaly, the family knew the cost — and the crate sat
above them all.
