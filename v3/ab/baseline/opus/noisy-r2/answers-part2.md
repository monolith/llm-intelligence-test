### 5. The Nine Weeks of 1911 — An Experiment That Ran Itself

**Who:** Ansel Keddie; every patron of the station; Merle Strawn, who was there and saw nothing.

On **8 May 1911** the station's own pipette broke. Keddie entered it: *"May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure."* Orra Keddie's diary for the same day: "A. home cross about a broken measure." For nine weeks the station tested on other glass, and the average went to **3.80**. On **10 July** he wrote: *"Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."* The figures fell to 3.61 the same day. He let the question mark stand for fourteen years rather than write down a cause he could not prove.

Everything else was held constant: the same patrons, the same herds, the same pasture, the same season, the same acid, the same bottles, the same hand tester, the same man turning the crank at the speed he was taught. **One object changed.** That is what a calculation cannot give you. It disposes at a stroke of every explanation that looks to the ground, the feed, the cows or the men's honesty, because none of those was altered in those nine weeks and the figures were.

It does not establish everything. It does not fix the magnitude — a nine-week flush average is not a year, and the interval was later used, by a veterinarian who wanted it dismissed, precisely as "six weeks of June and July, when every herd in the county is at its best," an argument that dies on the station's other twenty-five Junes and Julies, which read 3.61. It does not prove the borrowed measure was *accurate*, only that it was different. And it does not name the mechanism: that the fault was short delivery, in the exact ratio 0.95, took a burette and an afternoon in 1958.

Its provenance is disputed. The measure came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon (Ivy Keddie); came "up from town" (Teague, of the replacement); or was the state inspector's own, left that June (Roy Brant). Strawn's own 1911 entry says he visited in June, watched a test through, and wrote **"Nothing here to note."** The duration is settled at nine weeks by Keddie's two dated entries; Teague and Kepp both say six, from memory.

Duncan Keddie adds the private half of it: his father came home that summer saying it was something in the glass, said it twice, at supper and again at the door, and would not put it in the book.

**Documents:** the two 1911 entries and the 1922 summary leaf; Orra Keddie's diary; Strawn's circuit book for 1911; Bulletin 471 §IV; Roy Brant's account; Kepp's rebuttal; Duncan Keddie's family notes.

### 6. Over Redlow Ridge: The Control Group Nobody Knew Was One (1913–1958)

**Who:** Jerome Cudd of Alder Corners, Vessey County; his daughter Rosalie Cudd; four Hessel Bottom men.

Jerome Cudd built the Alder Corners Cheese Company in 1913 on the flat below the church, for the spring water on the lot and the hard road east; the valley remembered it as opening in 1914. From **1915**, four of the six Hessel Bottom men hauled to him in the summer flush — about 1 June to mid-September — and delivered to Ostrey Hollow the rest of the year. Sixteen seasons, the last in **1930**. They were talked about at home as though they had gone over to the enemy. Roy Brant, who had the biggest herd on the meadow, stayed home out of pride and said afterward it was a poor reason and cost him money.

The ridge road is four miles of switchback cut for lumber: it shuts in November and does not open before April, some years May, so no man from Ordell ever hauled over it in winter, whatever the price. The comparison therefore existed only in the flush — the very season the wet-hay men dismissed as proving nothing.

Cudd tested himself, on his own bench, with his own glass bought new, and wrote his own book. He never compared a figure of his with anybody else's; it never entered his head. In 1919 he set down the four men's summer averages — 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82 — and added, "Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge," in a book where nobody would ever read it. Rosalie Cudd copied out sixteen season averages for Ivy Keddie in 1958: 1915–1930, never below 3.78, never above 3.82, wet years and dry, "whether the meadow had been under water in May or hard as a road." The series is **flat across 1923 and 1924**, the exact years the Ostrey Hollow figure rises by nineteen hundredths — because the Alder Corners glass was never short.

She also killed the water charge physically: watered milk cannot be hidden from a cheesemaker, because the curd comes soft and will not knit and the yield is down the next morning. "Butter is more forgiving that way. Cheese is a witness." In sixteen summers her father never docked one of those four men a cent.

**Documents:** Rosalie Cudd's letter of 1958 with the sixteen-season table; Jerome Cudd's book, 1919; the Vessey County correspondence file; Rundle ch. V; Roy Brant.

### 7. The Circular of 1919, and the Two Kitchens That Stopped Being One

**Who:** Selby Vose, manager; Ansel and Orra Keddie; Emmet Brant; the patrons at the head of the valley.

In June 1919 the manager sent round a printed circular over his own signature warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons that added water would be looked for and dealt with. He had it typed in the office by a clerk who thought at the time it was a hard thing to send to men who brought their milk in every morning of their lives, and typed it anyway. Vose defended every word of it in 1926: it named nobody, and a manager who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing is "being idle at their expense."

It was false. The state's inspector had run a lactometer at that station on every visit for twelve years, had never found added water, and told the manager so that year; his condemnation entry of 1923 still reads "no added water, as always here."

Ansel Keddie carried the papers himself. He would not put them in the mail nor leave them on the platform: he took them round to the households, stood at each door while the man read it, and came home at eleven at night with his hat still on. Two days later Emmet Brant — Orra Keddie's brother, the station's largest patron, at that table on Sundays for thirty years — stood in the yard, would not come in, said he had hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and asked why Ansel had carried the paper himself if he did not believe it. Ansel said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet went home and never entered that house again; the two men did not speak until Emmet died in the spring of 1929, and Orra sat an hour in the kitchen where she had been a girl and came home.

The circular went to nine patron households by the recollection of four independent witnesses and to **eleven families** by the contemporaneous count of the woman whose husband delivered them — reconcilable if some of the nine farms held more than one household, which the valley itself says was usual. Its real cost was neither: one woman burned hers in the stove, another kept hers pinned inside a trunk lid for forty years, and a schoolteacher watched the estrangement come down to the children of both ends of the valley "entire, without a word of it being explained, the way a debt does."

**Documents:** Orra Keddie's diary, June 1919; Kyle, who typed it; Vose's 1926 address; Strawn's 1919 entry; Vira Toland; Roy Brant; Hazel Brant; Sill; Rundle.

### 8. The Burette, the Four Books, and the Crate Over Their Heads (1921–1979)

**Who:** Merle Strawn; Alonzo Frick's board; Ansel Keddie; Ivy Keddie.

The state began issuing burettes to its field men in **1921**. Strawn carried his two seasons; in 1922 he did not run the station's glass against it, "the day being short and the road home being what it was," and wrote that down because it was what happened. On **12 June 1923** he did: *"Ran their milk pipettes against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new."* The order went to Tarnet on 28 June; the firm answered 2 July, entered it, and said its books of the nineties would tell nobody anything. New glass was in use in July; in October Strawn ran it against the burette and it delivered its mark. The condemned crate went up a ladder into the loft over the intake room. In **1924** the station averaged **3.80** and stayed there. Nobody connected the one thing with the other, and Strawn drew no inference about the twenty-six years behind it — a field man keeps a book, not an estimation.

In January 1925 the patrons petitioned, reckoning their loss at 67,000 lb of butterfat. A board of three sat under Frick, attorney of Ashlin, from 17 March to 4 May: the first session at the Ashlin office, the second and third in the station's own weigh room, at the chairman's request, with men standing outside on the platform in the rain. Three explanations were urged — water by the manager, theft by the bookkeeper, hay out of the 1908 report — and none was proved. Four books were before the board: the test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book, and **the state inspector's circuit notebook for 1923**, which said it outright. Nobody laid the second beside the fourth. The condemned crate was twenty feet up a ladder in the room they were sitting in, and was never mentioned in any of the three sessions.

On **30 April** the Association put Keddie out by letter, four days before the award and while the board still sat. On 4 May the award found "no cause established" and gave the patrons **$3,800 without admission**, charged to the station account. He kept four or five cows, hung doors well, and died in February 1934; more came to the burying than his widow looked for and every one of them said what a hand he was with a door, and not one said the other thing. "A man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."

In February 1958 his granddaughter climbed that ladder, brought down the crate from under its tarpaulin and an inch of chaff, and on 9 March measured nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette. Bulletin No. 471 followed in October 1958: no milk watered, no cream stolen, the tests low by one part in twenty for twenty-six years, and seventy-six thousand pounds of butterfat standing credited in the Association's own ledgers as station gain.

**Documents:** Strawn's circuit books 1921–1924; Frick's exhibit list and recollections; Loomis on the crate and the award; Keddie's nine-sentence statement; Kyle; Oram; Orra Keddie's diary, April 1925 and February 1934; Ivy Keddie's measurement sheet and talk; Bulletin 471; Falke's county report; the Ashlin Chronicle.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** The line is: Ansel Keddie (b. 1861, d. Feb 1934) married Orra Brant in 1889; their son Duncan was born in 1894 and taught school at Ashlin; Duncan's daughter is Ivy, born 1931. Duncan states all of this himself in family notes written expressly because he had "already twice seen it printed otherwise." It is corroborated by Roy Brant ("Duncan's girl Ivy"), by Ivy's own talk, and by the diary (Duncan five years old in 1899, seventeen and teaching at Ashlin in 1911). Two sources say "great-niece": the *Ashlin Chronicle*, assembled in a day and a half from a college press release and two telephone calls, and paragraph 2 of the county agent's 1959 report — and the county agent, having written to check, appended a correction to his own report: "Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." A narrator who verifies and retracts outranks one who does not; a participant's own family record outranks both.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins.** Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie were brother and sister; their children, Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie, are therefore first cousins; the children of first cousins — Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy — are second cousins. Duncan, Hazel and Roy all say so independently, and Rundle's 1959 footnote repeats it. Against this stand Vira Toland and Cleve Oram, who both say "first cousin once removed"; that is valley talk, it is the commonest way of getting a second-cousinship wrong, and Hazel complains that people tell her the same thing to her face. What the sources leave open about the intervening generation: whether Orra and Emmet were *full* siblings (Roy Brant insists they were, "whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it"; Duncan calls her Emmet's sister and the younger of the two; Hazel calls her a half sister by Emmet's father's second wife) — the degree of cousinship is the same either way; and Orra's birth year, which the diary's arithmetic fixes at 1866 while Duncan's list says 1868, though his own statement that she died at eighty-two in 1948 gives 1866. Nothing in Orra's hand survives from before her marriage, so the Brant parentage cannot be checked against any document.

**B3. Selby Vose was Gideon Vose's son.** Vose says it from the platform in 1926 — "My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law under which every patron in this Association is paid on the test of the station where he delivers" — and Loomis, Duncan Keddie and the office clerk all say son; only Vira Toland and Rundle say nephew, and Rundle was writing down what the valley remembered. It matters three ways. Gideon wrote Article VII, whose last clause made the station's test unappealable; so the manager who defended the station's figures for twenty-five years and signed the annual station sheet was the author's son, defending his father's rule as well as his own management. He kept his father's Jersey herd, and boasted in 1926 that it tested 4.56 in 1910 to prove the valley was not poor milk country — the same figure Ivy Keddie later used to show the error was proportional, since a 4.56 herd was robbed of 0.24 of a point where a 3.42 herd lost 0.18. And his defence of the 1919 circular was, in effect, a family defence of the whole arrangement.

**B4. No relationship between the Voses and the Keddies is established, and probably there was none.** Two narrators assert one: Vira Toland says old Mrs. Vose was first cousin to Orra Keddie — "everybody knew that and nobody wrote it down anywhere" — and Cleve Oram says the families "were cousins of some sort… I could not draw you the line on paper." Both describe unwritten talk and neither can trace it. Against them is Duncan Keddie, Orra's son, writing specifically to settle such questions: there was no connection whatever, people confuse them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Gideon either, and his mother — a woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection — never once put a Vose in it. A first-hand denial by the son of the woman in question, who states his reasons, outranks two admittedly untraceable rumours. I record that the rumour mattered even though it was false: Vira's sense that the 1919 circular was "somebody's cousin saying it" is a real fact about how the letter was received, whatever the genealogy.
