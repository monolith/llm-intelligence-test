# Retention notes after retellings 01–16 (Ostrey Hollow / Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association)

These notes carry forward the whole of the earlier notes covering r01–r08 (nothing dropped) and add r09–r16. Distractor documents are deliberately excluded.

## 0. THE CORE STORY IN ONE PARAGRAPH (the solution, established by r04/r06/r07 and confirmed by r10/r11/r14)

The Ostrey Hollow skimming station's milk-test pipettes were short. They were blown for 17.6 c.c. but delivered a mean of 16.72 c.c. — 95 hundredths (one part in twenty short). Because a butterfat test bottle's neck is ruled to read percent for one specific charge and no other, every test made with that glass read 95% of the truth. The station's recorded average of 3.61 was really 3.80. The shortfall was **proportional, not fixed** — the station lost a twentieth of whatever was there, so it fell hardest on the richest milk. The missing fat was never stolen: it went down the valley in the cream and was entered in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as "station gain." Nobody watered milk; nobody stole cream. The glass was condemned 12 June 1923 by state inspector Merle Strawn; tests rose immediately and permanently; Ansel Keddie was dismissed anyway in 1925; the cause was found by Ivy Keddie in 1958 and printed in Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958).

## 2. PEOPLE AND RELATIONSHIPS

- **Ansel Keddie** — tester at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station. Went up in **1895**, the year before it opened, to get it ready (r01); tested that first summer too. Kept the test book in his own hand for **29 seasons, 1896 through 1924**; "I did not test a pound in the season of 1925." Put out (dismissed) **30 April 1925**. Died **1934** (r04: "died in 1934," "dead twenty-four years when the sheet was read"); r01 says vaguely "he died in the thirties" and "he was still under it when he died." An old man at the time of the hearing: "I am an old man and I have no other trade." **r13 (Frick) adds: in the corridor at Ashlin Keddie privately told Frick that he had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the room — "He had lived among them thirty years and would not stand up and accuse his neighbors."**
- **Orra Keddie** — Ansel's wife. **Emmet Brant's sister**. r09 (Roy Brant) confirms and insists: "They were full brother and sister, same mother and same father, whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it." Also **first cousin to old Mrs. Vose** — "everybody knew that and nobody wrote it down anywhere" (r01). Outlived Ansel "a good while" and carried the stigma the whole time. After 1919 she "would walk down to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane."
- **Emmet Brant** — **born 1854, died 1929** (r09). Biggest man on Hessel Bottom; most cows, most hay ground; the station's **largest patron in every year of which there is a record** (r02); the man the 1919 letter cost the most. Brother of Orra Keddie, i.e. Ansel's brother-in-law. Before 1919 the two families ate Sunday dinners together, "the two houses in one kitchen and the men out at the fence after." After 1919 **they never spoke again** — "not one word, not at the store, not at a funeral." Both long dead by 1979. Keddie on this: "That is the plainest fact in this letter and it is the one I find I have the least to say about." **r09 gives the reason for the rupture from the Brant side: not the water charge itself but that "my father held that the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not."** He was one of the first men to complain out loud, in 1902, and was laughed at. He read the 1908 committee report twice, put it in the drawer, and said "that the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not." Wrote at the kitchen table after the second milking, in the lamp, **with his hat still on** (echo of Vira's father reading the 1919 letter with his hat on), and Roy's mother would not have the table cleared till he was done.
- **Roy Brant** — Emmet Brant's son; **born c. 1888** (sixty-seven in 1955); kept the Hessel Bottom weigh book up himself after his father died; lent it to Ivy Keddie (1958). Farms sixty-odd acres of the meadow, **thirty-eight cows in a good year**, "which for this valley was a herd." Never hauled over the ridge: "We had the biggest herd on the meadow, and the biggest herd is the one that cannot afford to be talked about, and I have thought since that this was a poor reason and cost us money." **First cousin to Duncan Keddie.**
- **Hazel Brant** — Roy Brant's **daughter**; the weigh book goes to her ("It goes to Hazel when I am done with it. That is all the arrangement I have made and all I intend to make"); it went "to Miss Hazel Brant in 1962." **Second cousin to Ivy Keddie** (r09, r12 footnote) — "I mention it because both of them have had to have it explained to them by me more than once." (r01 Vira called Ivy "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed" — wrong degree.)
- **Gideon Vose** — founder; elected **first president in 1889**, held office until **1911 (22 years, longer than any other man)**; **drafted the by-laws himself**, not a lawyer, "they read as a farmer wrote them, which is to their credit"; **died 1917**. Kept a herd of **Jerseys** at the home place. r12 (Rundle): "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the law and thought better of it," wrote the by-laws at his own kitchen table "in a hand that the Association's clerks were still complaining about thirty years later"; wrote by-laws "the way other men build stone wall — slowly, and to last."
- **old Mrs. Vose** — Gideon's wife; first cousin to Orra Keddie.
- **Selby Vose** — Gideon's **son** (r02 §4: "son of the late president"; r08: "My father, who was your first president"). BUT r01 says he was "old Gideon Vose's **nephew**," and **r12 (Rundle) also says nephew** — "His nephew Selby Vose, afterward manager of the Association, used to say that his uncle wrote by-laws…" **CONFLICT (now 2 sources for nephew vs. 2 for son, but Vose's own words settle it).** Manager **1913–1938**. Signed the 1919 circular. Signed the annual station sheet every year. Kept his father's Jersey herd after him. Put the water explanation to the 1925 board "without heat and without proof" (r13).
- **Effie Loomis** — secretary 1901–1940; **fourteen years old in 1889**; kept the minute book in her own hand throughout; recorded the 1925 award herself on the last day; laid down the minute book 1940, wrote her notes 1941. (r07 Item 7: the arbitration minute book is "in the hand of the Association's secretary.")
- **Wilbur Teague** — bookkeeper, came in **1905**, kept the ledgers to **1945** ("forty years by the calendar and a good deal more than that by the wrist"). Wrote his memoir 1948, "three years after I gave up the desk." Devised the station-gain line. Put the matter to Vose in **1916** and was told to let it alone. **r13: at the hearing he put the theft theory "in the way an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it — he was asked about the station's account and answered at length about how such an account would look if a man were helping himself, and then observed that he made no charge against anybody. There is no cross-examination that reaches a witness of that kind, and I did not attempt one."**
- **Hobart Sill** — buttermaker at Larrow Green; had the churn book and the cream receiving book. **r14: came in 1893, forty-one years at the churn, retired 1934, aged 64 (so b. c. 1870).** "There was no floor in this room but plank, and we cooled with creek ice out of the pond." Made butter that "took premiums in three states." Went up to the station **only twice in his life**. His parting instruction: "Put in about the butter… Put in that Hobart Sill made the butter and it was good butter and he stood at the churn until the day they gave him a chair."
- **Alonzo Frick** — attorney of Ashlin; chairman of the 1925 board of arbitration; "as fair a chairman as ever a hearing had" (Teague); "an able and impartial man" (Vose). **r13: born c. 1872 (seventy-four in 1946); forty years trying titles and boundaries, and two homicides; named chairman "I have always supposed because I was the one of the three who kept no cow."**
- **Merle Strawn** — state dairy inspector; took the Ordell circuit in **1907** and laid it down in **1936**; ~50-odd plants in four counties.
- **Ivy Keddie** — Ansel's granddaughter; of the State Agricultural College at **Farland**; found the crate February 1958, measured the pipettes 9 March 1958, gave the talk 1959. A **graduate student** in 1958 (r10). Rosalie Cudd on her: "here is a young woman from the college who asks one in four lines and apologizes for troubling me." Roy Brant on her: "She is careful with paper." Second cousin to Hazel Brant.
- **Duncan Keddie** — Ivy's father, Ansel and Orra's son; **taught school at Ashlin**; **first cousin to Roy Brant** (r09).
- **Rosalie Cudd** — runs the Alder Corners Cheese Company. **r10 settles the conflict in her own voice: she is Jerome Cudd's DAUGHTER.** "My father, Jerome Cudd, built this factory in nineteen thirteen." She was **a girl of twelve when the first vat was set** (so b. c. 1901), "drying hoops before I was tall enough to see into one." Took the factory over in the spring of 1941 when her father died and had had it **seventeen years** by 1958; "I have changed the boiler and the roof and nothing else." (r04 Ivy called her "Jerome Cudd's niece" — Ivy is wrong; r12 Rundle also says daughter.)
- **Jerome Cudd** — founder of the Alder Corners Cheese Company. **Built it in 1913 per his daughter (r10); r12 says the factory opened in 1914; r09 says "The factory over there had opened in 1914."** Died **March 1941**, "between the setting and the cutting of a vat, which he would have arranged that way if he had been asked." Tested himself, on his own bench, in the little room off the intake, with his own glassware bought new, washed in the evening, kept in a rack he had made, and wrote his own book. **Never compared a figure of his with anybody else's — "it never entered his head."** Said the Ordell people "had a theory about that bottom the way some families have a ghost: it explained things, and it cost nothing, and nobody was ever going to test it."
- **Professor Leland Vaught** — professor of dairy husbandry at Farland; author (with Ivy) of the closing paragraph of Bulletin No. 471; author of its introduction and method sections (r11). Signs "— L. VAUGHT." "I have spent my working life urging students to prefer an experiment to a calculation."
- **Halbert Nym** — curator, Ordell County Historical Rooms, 1972.
- **Vira Toland** — born **1912** at the top end of Ostrey Hollow, in the house her father's father built; lived there all her life except two years at Ashlin when first married ("I would not count those two years anyhow"); her father hauled milk to the station; has a **brother** who she believes still has the 1919 letter.
- **The young man from the county** — recorded Vira's tape in 1979; "asked three questions in an hour and let her have the rest of it."
- **Dell Rundle** — schoolteacher at Larrow Green; wrote the 1939 jubilee pamphlet; "I have taught the school at the Green a good many years now and I have listened to this history in pieces, at funerals and in the back of the store." Had the children of both ends of the valley in one schoolroom through the 1919–1925 estrangement: "it came down to them entire, without a word of it being explained, the way a debt does." Too old to rewrite the pamphlet in 1959.
- **Nyle Grigg** — farmer of Larrow Green; chairman of the 1908 investigating committee (a board of three); **not a patron of the Ostrey Hollow station** — "I haul to Larrow Green and always have… I was chosen for the work on that account and for no other, and I have been abused for the finding by men on both sides of it, which I take to be a fair sign that the finding was an honest one." Had known the men of the bottom since he was a young man and **had bought hay off two of them**. "I have never in my life been given a duty I liked less."
- **Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S.** — veterinary surgeon of Ashlin, thirty years in practice by 1926. "It is my habit, when I am called to a herd that is doing badly, to ask first what the cattle are standing on… the answer to nine cases in ten is under the animal's feet, and that the tenth is in the water she drinks." Admits in discussion: **"he had never in his life read a test and would not know how."**
- **The boy on Rosalie Cudd's scale platform** — one of the four Hessel Bottom haulers brought his boy, who "used to sit on my scale platform and be weighed for a joke. That boy is a man in the state road department now and does not remember me."

## 3. THE ASSOCIATION — ORGANIZATION, PLACES, DATES

- **1889** — farmers of the Larrow Valley meet at Larrow Green, organize the **Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association**. Gideon Vose elected first president. **r12 adds: they met in the schoolhouse, in cold weather; the older men remembered chiefly "that it went on very late and that somebody's horse got loose in the middle of it." Their grievance: "tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking whatever the merchant chose to allow them for it," having worked out "that the way to be paid properly for a thing is to own the place that buys it."**
- **1891** — main **creamery built at Larrow Green, on Larrow Creek**; still the same building in 1941. r12: "on the creek below the mill," the churn set up in it, "and the valley began to be paid in cash once a month, which had never happened to it before. There are men still living who can tell you what the first check did in their mother's kitchen, and they will tell you whether you ask or not." Before it, "the members sold their milk where they could get a price for it and there was no common test and no common price, and every man's arithmetic was his own." Loomis: "it began as a plain remedy for a plain grievance, and I have never heard anybody in this valley say we should have been better without it."
- **1892** — **Article VII adopted** at the annual meeting (Loomis). **CONFLICT: r12 says "Article VII dates from 1891, the year the churn was set up"; r15 (Grigg) says "Payment by test came in with the 1893 meeting, as I have always understood it."** Three different years from three sources; Loomis (secretary, working from minutes) says 1892, and Nym notes the printed copies **carry no year on their face**, only "adopted at the annual meeting" — which is exactly why nobody can date it.
- r12: Article VII "was thought a hard rule at the time and there were men who left the Association over it."
- **1896** — Ostrey Hollow **skimming station** opens (test book begun 4 May 1896). r14: "that was ninety-six, in May, and there was a crowd went up for it."
- Three things on paper in Teague's time: **a creamery at Larrow Green, an office at Ashlin, a skimming station seven miles up the valley at Ostrey Hollow.** "The station was the smallest of the three and it made the most noise."
- **Office at Ashlin**: one room over a **harness shop**, cold at the north end and never cold enough at the south; ledgers on a shelf a taller man had built; Teague's sloped desk with a **brass rail across the foot** to keep the sheets from sliding. Station sheets arrived in March on a stiffer paper than the creamery used.
- **1938** — office move; the **1889–1892 minute book was lost** in it.
- **Ordell County**; Ashlin is the county town with lawyers; **Vessey County** is over **Redlow Ridge** (Alder Corners Cheese Company); **Tarnet** is where Ammon & Sons Dairy Supply is; **Farland** is the State Agricultural College.
- **Hessel Bottom** — the wet meadow below the hamlet. **r09: a hundred and sixty acres; SIX farms had rights on it** (also six in r15's 1908 report and in r16 Kepp). Earlier notes recorded "seven farms" from r02/r03; **r09, r15 and r16 all say six — six is now the strongly attested figure.** Roy Brant's was the largest of the six, sixty-odd acres. "The water stands on the low end into May, and there is a soft place by the alders that has taken a horse to the hock in my time and my father's. The hay off it is coarse, and it cuts late, and a cow will eat it and do well enough, whatever has been said about that hay by men who never fed it… it never failed to grow something." Best milk of the station came off it.
- **Nine families** hauled milk from Ostrey Hollow / received the 1919 circular (r01, r03, r09 "Nine families at the head of the valley got that paper and nine families read it for what it was"; r14 "You cannot tell me nine families all got honest in one summer"). (Six farms on the Bottom + three places on the hill = nine; Strawn 1909: "the farms of the wet bottom below it and from three places on the hill" — **this now reconciles exactly**.)

### The station building
Stood at the very top of the Hollow "where the road bends and then goes to nothing but the ridge." Low, whitewashed, "cold as a spring house inside even in August," smelled of "milk and steam and hot iron"; you could hear the engine before you came round the last turn. Keddie wrote standing up at a **slope desk by the window** where the light came in. Seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green. **A loft over the intake room, reached by a ladder** (Strawn 1928: "The loft over the intake room is full of odds and ends and I told them a loft over a milk room is a place for nothing but air, and was told in return that it had been full since before the war"). Closed by 1958; used for storage — "they kept fence wire and salt in it." **r12 adds: "a plain one, boarded and battened, with the intake room at the road end and a loft over it that I believe has never been cleared out since it was put up; and behind it the creek, which in a wet April comes up to the sill and has twice come over it."** r12 also: in the nineties the pull down to the Green "was three hours in mud and could not be done at all in March."

### How a skimming station worked
Patrons hauled milk up to the station instead of driving seven miles down to Larrow Green. Milk weighed in; a sample taken from each man's can and tested for butterfat; the man paid on his pounds and his test together. The station then separated the milk, sent the **cream** down the valley to Larrow Green, and gave the **skim** back to the farmer for his hogs and calves. r14: **the cream wagon came down every day of the week but Sunday**, seven miles of hill road, "and in July it came down warm no matter how early they started"; set off at the platform, weighed, sampled, into the cold room.

### The Alder Corners Cheese Company and Redlow Ridge (r10)
- Built by Jerome Cudd **in 1913** (his daughter) / opened **1914** (Rundle and Roy Brant), "on the flat below the church, because there was spring water on the lot and a hard road out to the east," in Vessey County.
- **The ridge road: four miles of switchback, cut for a lumber road and never much improved. "It shuts in November and it does not open before April, some years May… the drifts sit in the cut below the top and stay there after everything else is bare. So no man from your valley ever hauled to me in winter, whatever the price was. It was not possible."**
- **From 1915, four men off Hessel Bottom hauled to Alder Corners in the summer flush — about 1 June to mid-September — and went down to Ostrey Hollow the rest of the year. Sixteen seasons; the last of those summers was 1930.** Reasons, per Rosalie: cheese paid better than butter in a flush month; she was buying hard in flush "because a cheese factory can use everything you can carry it"; and while the road was open her gate was a shorter pull for those four farms. r09 (Roy Brant) confirms sixteen seasons from 1915 and adds it "was talked about here as though they had gone over to the enemy, which shows you what a valley is."
- The haul: "They started before daylight to have the cans on my platform by seven, because milk that has been four miles down a switchback in a wagon on a July morning is not improving while it stands, and they came in with the horses steaming and the tarps wet with the cloud that sits on the top of the ridge till the sun is well up."

## 4. DOCUMENTS QUOTED (verbatim)

### Article VII (quoted identically by Loomis r02, Nym r07 Item 1, and Rundle r12)
> *Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*

Loomis's gloss: before it, a man was paid for what his cans weighed, "a hundred pounds of milk was a hundred pounds of milk whosever cow it came out of." After it, paid for fat found by a test made at the place of delivery. "The rule is a just rule and I have always said so: it pays a man for what he actually brings and not for the water that is naturally in it. But I would have the reader notice the last clause, which is the one nobody quotes. *The test of the station where he delivers shall govern.* Not the test of the creamery, not the average of the valley, not a test made over again anywhere else. The station's test, and no appeal from it. **That clause is the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business.**" She has had it misquoted at her across the desk for forty years. Nym notes: **the printed copies carry no year on their face, only the words "adopted at the annual meeting."**

**Rundle r12 independently identifies the same clause as the hinge:** "But mark the last clause, because the valley has had fifty years to learn what it means. *The test of the station where he delivers shall govern.* Not the test of the creamery. Not any test but the one made at his own door. **Gideon Vose put that in for convenience and it was convenient for thirty years, and it is the sentence on which the whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned.**" And: "A man who keeps good cows and feeds them ought not to be paid the same as the man who keeps poor ones, and **the pound of milk is a liar where the pound of butterfat is not.**"

**Frick r13 states the same point as a lawyer:** "it makes the test the whole of the bargain, and it leaves a dissatisfied man with **no remedy on earth except to quarrel with a figure**." Also: the arbitration was "under the by-laws of a creamery association, with **no appeal from it, no reported decision, and nothing whatever to cite it for**."

### First leaf of the station test book (quoted by Keddie r03 and Nym r07 Item 3, identically)
> *Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie.*

### The 1922 summary leaf (quoted by Keddie r03, Ivy r04, Nym r07 Item 3 — identical in all three)
> *Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80.*

Keddie made it up in 1922 "because I had been asked for them so often."
**NOTE the buried bombshell: 1896 — the first season, on the borrowed Larrow Green glass — reads 3.80, the true figure. The 3.61 begins in 1898, after the station's own short glass came into use (1 May 1897).**

### The two 1911 entries (Keddie r03)
> *May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure.*

> *July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.*

"That question mark is mine and I have let it stand for fourteen years. I could not answer it then and I cannot answer it now, and I will not put anything in its place that I do not know."

### The ledger station-gain entry for 1898 (quoted identically by Teague r05, Nym r07 Item 6, and **Rundle r12's "Note added while in press"**)
> *Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account.*

(Check: 1,600,000 lb × 3.61% = 57,760. 1,600,000 × 3.80% = 60,800. Difference 3,040. 3,040/57,760 = 5.26% ≈ "five and a quarter"; 3,040/60,800 = exactly 5.00%.)

**Rundle saw this entry in the ledgers "on another errand altogether" while his pamphlet was being set in type, and it destroyed his own Chapter IV:** "I must go back on Chapter IV. **Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand.** I leave it as written and correct it here." — This is a schoolteacher with no arithmetic beyond the ordinary reaching, in 1939, the one conclusion nobody else drew for another nineteen years.

### The arbitration award, 4 May 1925 (quoted identically by Loomis r02 and Nym r07 Item 7; paraphrased identically by Frick r13)
> *The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account.*

Loomis: "I have written many things into that book with more pleasure. *Finds no cause established*, and then an award of thirty-eight hundred dollars: I took it down as it was given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once."
Keddie: "They found no cause established and they put me out on the last day of the month, and both of those things are true at once, and I have given up trying to hold them in my head together."

>
> To the President and Directors of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association.
>
> Your committee, appointed in January last, respectfully reports as follows.
>
> The complaint before us is of some years' standing and is in substance this: that the milk delivered at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station is returned by the test at a figure below that of milk delivered elsewhere in this valley, and that the patrons of that station are in consequence paid less for the same labor and the same cows than their neighbors are paid. Since payment by test came in among us and the pound of butter fat took the place of the pound of milk as the measure of a man's month, a difference of this kind is no longer a matter of opinion. >

Its herbage is sedge, rush, and the coarse sour grasses that come in on such land, and its hay is cut late and heavy and is fed as the main dependence of those farms from November to April. There is no better winter keep to be had there. We say this without reproach to any man on that bottom; the land is what it is, and they took it as their fathers left it to them.
>
> Your committee visited each of the six farms, some of them three and four times. We drew our samples ourselves and we sealed them ourselves. We saw the herds milked, we saw the cans filled, and we saw them hauled to the station and weighed in at the intake, and we stood by while the test was made and saw nothing whatever in the doing of it to remark upon. We tried the station scales against a known weight and we tried the patrons' own scales against the station's. We inquired into the water supply at each barn and satisfied ourselves upon it.
>
> Your committee further observed the general condition of the stock upon that bottom, as men who have kept cattle all their lives are entitled to observe it. The herds are thin in the flank at the end of the winter feeding and slow to come to their flesh in the spring. Their coats are rough later in the season than the coats of hill herds. >

## 5. THE ARITHMETIC (Ivy Keddie, r04; confirmed by Nym r07 Item 6)

- Pipettes blown **17.6 c.c.**, delivering mean **16.72 c.c.**; short **0.88 c.c.**; ratio **16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly**.
- "A butterfat test is made by drawing a measured charge of milk into a bottle whose neck is ruled to read percent for that charge and no other. Put in a short charge and you get a short reading, in the same proportion."
- Recorded average **3.61**; 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80** true. Gap = **0.19** (nineteen hundredths of a point).
- **The gap was a proportion, not a fixed quantity.** "Everyone who looked at this before treated it as a fixed quantity, and it is not one. Nineteen hundredths is five percent of three point eight. The station did not lose a set number of points. It lost a twentieth of whatever was there."
- 25 full delivery years **1898–1922**: intake **40,000,000 lb of milk**, average **1,600,000 lb/year**.
- At recorded 3.61%: patrons credited with **1,444,000 lb of fat**. At true 3.80%: they had delivered **1,520,000 lb**. Difference = **76,000 lb of butterfat**.
- Butterfat averaged **25 cents a pound** over those years → unpaid fat worth **$19,000**.
- The 1925 award was **$3,800** = **one fifth** of $19,000.
- **The two percentages, both correct against their own denominator:** 76,000 is **5%** of the 1,520,000 delivered; it is **5¼%** of the 1,444,000 credited. "The Association's ledger line uses the second, because a bookkeeper naturally reckons a surplus against the figure he has already entered. Neither figure is an error. **But five percent is the one that is also the glass.**" Nym Item 6 gloss: "The two percentages in this file are not interchangeable: five and a quarter percent of the credited fat is five percent of the fat delivered, and each is right of its own denominator and of no other."
- **Regressivity:** "A herd whose milk was recorded at **4.56** was short **0.24** of a point. A herd recorded at **3.42** was short **0.18**. The richer the milk, the more was taken from it, and no man had any way of knowing that his good cows cost him more than his poor ones."
  - **These two numbers are not hypothetical.** 4.56 = Selby Vose's Jersey herd in 1910 (r08). 3.42 = the Hessel Bottom weigh-book test paid on in 1910 (r07 Item 4, r09). Ivy quietly used the manager's own herd and the Brants' own book as her two examples.
- **The 1911 natural experiment:** "Same patrons, same herds, same pasture, same season, same acid, same bottles, same man at the crank — **one object changed** — and the average went to 3.80 and came back to 3.61 the day the station's own measure returned. No explanation of this valley's twenty-six-year anomaly survives those nine weeks except the one I have given you." The measure borrowed in 1911 **came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon**. (**r09 says instead: "The state man left his own measure that June and that is what they used."** — a third account of the borrowed measure's origin.)
- **Disposing of the wet-hay theory:** Bulletin 214 measured bog hay at ≤0.05 in winter and nothing measurable on summer pasture, "against a gap of nineteen hundredths standing flat in every month of every year, July and February alike. **It is four times too small and it is the wrong shape.**"
- **The cross-check over the ridge:** the Alder Corners factory (Vessey County) tested the milk of **four Hessel Bottom men** in their summer flush; "where the two books overlap, **fourteen seasons** of them, that same milk off that same wet ground ran about **nineteen hundredths higher** at Alder Corners than at home." (**Rosalie Cudd's letter gives sixteen seasons, 1915–1930, not fourteen — see conflicts.**)
- **The delays:** glass condemned 1923, cause found 1958 = **35 years**. Grandfather dismissed 1925 → 1958 = **33 years**. He died 1934, "had been dead twenty-four years when the sheet was read that clears him." "I have no remedy to propose. I have only the crate, and it is on the table, and you may look into it on your way out."

## 5A. VAUGHT'S EXPOSITION IN BULLETIN 471 (r11) — the theory stated properly, with its own figures wrong

**§I Why the bulletin was written.** "This station has been asked, at intervals for fifty years, why the butterfat tests at one skimming station in Ordell County ran below those of its own creamery seven miles down the same valley. The question has been answered at various times by reference to the land and by reference to the men. **It is answered here by reference to a piece of glass.**"
"The answer is not complicated, and **its simplicity is itself part of the history. Nobody in that valley lacked the intelligence to find it. What they lacked was any occasion to suspect the one article in the plant that nobody in the trade is in the habit of suspecting.**"
The one-sentence takeaway for extension workers: "**the milk pipette is the one instrument in the butterfat test which the plant cannot check against anything else, and it is the instrument on which every figure in the plant depends.**"

**§II The instrument.** Four operations: a measured charge of milk drawn into a pipette and delivered into a test bottle; sulfuric acid added, dissolving everything but the fat; the bottle spun in a hand tester; the liberated fat rises into the graduated neck and is read **directly** in percent. "Notice the word *directly*. The operator weighs nothing. He reads the length of a column of fat in a narrow neck and calls it a percentage, and he is entitled to do so for one reason only: the charge of milk that went into that bottle is a fixed and known quantity. **The maker rules the neck for that quantity and no other.** Every mark on it means 'this much fat, out of the standard charge.'" The standard charge in this state, and on the glassware examined, is **17.6 cubic centimeters**. If the pipette delivers less than its mark, "The reading is not merely low. It is low *in the exact proportion by which the charge was short*."

**The three consequences "nobody appears to have held all three in mind at once" in fifty years:**
1. **"The error is proportional and not additive.** It does not remove a fixed number of points from every test. It removes a fixed *fraction*. Rich milk therefore loses more than poor milk, absolutely, and the ratio between any two tests made on the same short glass is undisturbed. **A station using such glass will show a perfectly orderly, internally consistent set of figures. Its herds will rank correctly against one another. Only against the outside world is it wrong.**"
2. **"The error is precise.** A leaking pipe or a slipping scale gives a scattered error, and scatter attracts attention. A pipette that delivers 16.27 c.c. where it is blown 17.6 delivers 16.27 c.c. every time, for a quarter of a century, in January and in July, for a careful operator and a careless one. **It has the appearance of excellent work.**"
3. **"The error is invisible from inside the plant.** Every other element of the test can be verified against a standard. The scales can be checked with test weights. The tester's speed can be counted. The acid can be checked for strength. The bottle's graduation can be checked with mercury. But the pipette is not checked against anything, because in the ordinary practice of the trade *the pipette is the standard*. It is what the plant measures with. **An instrument that is itself the measure cannot be caught by measuring with it.** It can be caught only by a burette brought in from outside, and burettes reached the state's field men very late in this history."

**§III The material examined** — and here the errors begin. Vaught writes: "Of the lot bought for the Ostrey Hollow station in 1897 — **five dozen** pipettes were purchased — **nine** were recovered… Their stems are blown 17.6 c.c… five runs to each pipette, and **the mean delivery of the nine is 16.27 c.c.**" And: "The station's own glass went into use on **the first of May, 1898**, and remained in use until the state inspector condemned the lot in June of 1923."
**Method, repeatable by any county worker:** the pipette is filled to its mark with water at room temperature, the tip wiped, the contents delivered in the ordinary working manner — the same interval of drainage, the same touch of the tip to the wall of the receiver — into a burette of certified graduation; five deliveries from each, averaged. "**The whole of it occupies an afternoon and requires no apparatus a creamery does not own, save the burette. There is no reason why every station in this state should not be checked once in this way, and several reasons in this history why it should be.**"

**§IV "The interval of 1911, which is worth more than the arithmetic."** "I would ask the reader to weigh what follows above anything computed in this bulletin… The patrons were the same patrons; the herds were the same herds; the pasture, the season, the acid, the bottles, the tester and the tester's habits were all unaltered. **One variable changed.** The station's average test rose from 3.61 to 3.80 and held there for the nine weeks, and returned to 3.61 on the day the station's own measure came back into service. This is recorded contemporaneously, in the operator's hand, **by a man who did not know what he was recording and set a question mark against it.**" And: "**I have spent my working life urging students to prefer an experiment to a calculation. Here is one that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven years.** It disposes of every explanation that looks to the herds, to the ground or to the men, for the sufficient reason that none of those was altered in those nine weeks, and the figures were."

**§V Magnitude** (all of it wrong, and he half-notices): ratio 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = **0.9244**; 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = **3.905**, a shortfall of **0.295** of a point; applied to 40,000,000 lb over twenty-five full delivery years, **118,000 lb** of butterfat delivered and not credited. Then, flatly: "**It should be said that this figure is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the glassware.**" He also writes: "**The gain is five and a quarter percent of the butterfat actually delivered**" — repeating Teague's exact denominator error, which Ivy and Nym had both corrected.

**§VI The question of feed** — quotes Bulletin 214 correctly (five hundredths in winter, nothing in summer) and says the discrepancy "is present in every month of the year and is **some six times** the largest effect the trial could produce" (six times fits his own inflated 0.295; Ivy's "four times too small" fits the true 0.19).

**§VII** reproduces the concluding paragraph, which says **one part in twenty** and **seventy-six thousand pounds** — irreconcilable with his own §III and §V.

## 7. HOW A TEST WAS ACTUALLY MADE (Ansel Keddie's unsent letter, r03) — the fullest account of the work

- Day began at **half past five**. Wagons came up the Hollow in the dark most of the year; intake room open, lamps lit, **scale swinging free** before the first wagon was at the door.
- Every can went on the scale; the weight **called and written before the milk went anywhere else**. The scale was tested by the state's man on his rounds and was true. "I have never in twenty-nine seasons had a patron dispute a weight with me and be right."
- A sample was taken from each patron's milk **as it was weighed**, into **his own jar, numbered to him and to nobody else**; the jar was **made up over ten days** before it was tested.
- On testing morning the jar was set in warm water **until blood-warm right through**, then poured from vessel to vessel and poured again "until the cream that had risen was carried down through the whole of it. A sample that is not warmed and mixed is worth nothing and a boy could tell you so."
- **The charge:** milk drawn up in the measure **to the mark on the stem**, tip wiped, charge blown out into the bottle carrying the patron's number.
- **The acid** poured down the side; the bottle turned until the milk was dark.
- **Four bottles in the hand tester**, weighted against each other, cover down, crank turned "at the speed I was taught to turn it and no faster, and no man ever stood beside me and made me hurry it."
- "Then the fat is up in the neck of the bottle in a clear column, and it is read off in per cent **against the rulings, with a pair of dividers**, and the figure is written down. That is the whole of it. It is not a hard trade. It is only a trade that must be done the same way every time, which is a harder thing than it sounds."
- **The book kept in five columns: the date, the patron, the pounds, the test, the fat.** Errors crossed with initials against the crossing; never a figure written over a figure, "because a figure written over another figure is a figure no man can swear to afterward."
- "**The glass I began on in ninety-six was borrowed off the Ashlin creamery**, and we used it until our own outfit came up the valley in a crate the spring following." (**r14 Sill definitively resolves this: the borrowed glass was Sill's own, from the Larrow Green bench.**)
- On seasonality: "They did not come and go with the season. The gap was there in July as it was there in February, in the flush of the grass and in the dead of the winter on dry hay, and it was the same in a wet year as in a dry one. **In 1924 the average of this station was 3.80**, and I wrote that figure with the same pen and the same measure of care as all the others."
- On his reason for writing: "because I could not say it in that room, where a man is allowed to answer and not allowed to explain, and because it seems to me that somebody ought to know how the work was done before everybody who did it is gone."

## 8. VIRA TOLAND'S TESTIMONY (r01) — texture and claims

- Nine families hauled to that station; "we were in and out of each other's kitchens the whole year round and I could name you every one of them, but you don't want names, you want the trouble, **they always want the trouble**."
- On Keddie's book: "It was ruled, and the ruling was his own, done with a straightedge, and every line in it was the same height as every other line, and his figures were like print." He never had to hunt: "He turned to the day and he read it off." Her claim: "He tested **a thousand cans a week** and never a blot in that book." Her father: "if the rest of the world kept its accounts the way Ansel Keddie kept his there would be no lawyers in Ashlin. Which was my father's way of talking. **There are still lawyers in Ashlin.**"
- **1919, the letter:** "A letter came from the Association, printed, over the manager's signature, and it said there was **water in the cans** coming down from Ostrey Hollow." She was seven; "I remember it the way you remember a smell." Her father read it **at the table with his hat still on, which he never did**; her mother stood at the stove with her back to him and did not turn around the whole time. He read it twice, put it in the drawer of the desk in the front room; it was still there when he died and she believes her brother has it yet. **One woman had hers pinned inside the lid of her trunk.** "You can tell people a thing like that is only business and they will still keep the paper forty years to prove what was said of them."
- The kinship point: "when the letter came it was not only the Association saying it, if you follow me, it was **somebody's cousin** saying it, and that is a different thing altogether in a place this size."
- **The unexplained rise:** "Then the tests went up. All at once, one summer, the figures came up and stayed up, and nobody ever said why. Not the station, not the Association, not anybody. My father came in from hauling and said the milk was testing better and he did not know what he had done different, and he had not done anything different, none of them had. **That was the queerest part of the whole business to me even as a child, that the thing corrected itself and no man stood up and gave a reason for it.**"
- **Her version of the hearing:** "after that there was the hearing down at Ashlin. That was the winter and spring after." Lawyers, officers, "our men going down in the wagons." "And at the end of it they put Ansel Keddie out. An old man, and out, after all those seasons in that cold room, and **they paid the patrons thirty-five hundred dollars** and called it settled. Thirty-five hundred dollars, split up nine ways over all those years — **my father's share of it did not buy a hay rake**."
- **Her verdict:** "He was taking the cream. They caught him at it and they put him out, and that is why the numbers went right the summer after… A man stands in a room by himself with the separator running and the door shut and **nobody in the valley can add**, and after a while he helps himself, and it is not that he is a wicked man, it is that **nobody ever came up that road and looked**." Her father would never say it out loud: "My father said Ansel Keddie was an honest man who had got into something, which is a nice way of saying it and I never saw the difference."
- Her unease: "he was still under it when he died. That is the part that sits badly with me now that I am old… he had all those years of this valley looking at him a certain way in church, and Orra had it too."
- **The 1958 visitor:** "a young woman came round here in nineteen fifty-eight from the college away east, asking after all this, and she went up and poked about the old station building, which was shut by then… She sat where you are sitting. Nice girl, careful, wrote everything down. She was **Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed**, so she was of the family both sides in a manner of speaking, and I remember thinking, well, here it comes round again after all this time and **there is nobody left alive that it can do any good to**. I told her about the letter in my father's drawer. **I don't know what she made of it.**"
- Closing: "It rained the day of that letter, I remember the wet on my father's shoulders when he came in, and I have never in sixty years been able to think about the one without the other."

"These two figures are not mine and I do not vouch for the cows, only for the copying: they are Mr. Teague's, out of the annual sheets, and **I have added the columns twice**."

Forty years of taking down what men say has taught me that **the interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying**."

## 10. WILBUR TEAGUE'S CHAPTER (r05) — the ledger side

- Opening: "in the whole of it I never carried a column that would not prove. That is not said to be admired. In my experience a bookkeeper who wants admiring has generally got something behind him that he would rather you did not turn over."
- **The station-gain line explained in his own words:** "On one side of the station account I entered the butterfat credited to the patrons on the station's own test — that is, what the station's books said it had bought. On the other side I entered the butterfat that actually came down the valley in the station's cream and was weighed and tested into our vats at Larrow Green — that is, what we had in hand. **The two were never the same. The second was always the larger.** The difference between them was the station's account to the good, and I carried it to the general account of the Association where it belonged, since the Association had received it."
- **"The station-gain line was my own device; I began it when I came in 1905.** I have heard it said in the valley that the line was some old dodge of the founders. It was not. It was a piece of ordinary bookkeeping, invented at that desk by the man sitting at it, because a difference that will not be entered somewhere will come back and bite you at audit."
- **The evenness that stopped inquiry:** "The gain ran at five and a quarter percent of the fat that was actually in the cream. It ran there year after year, so evenly that the words 'as usual' got into the entry and stayed there, and a man may as well be honest about what evenness does to a bookkeeper: **it stops him looking. A figure that jumps about will keep you awake. A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture.**"
- **His two large numbers:** "Over the years the account ran, that station took in **forty-four million pounds of milk**. And **the average test of all the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, taken over the whole period and not over some picked year that suits an argument, was 3.16.** Down at Larrow Green we did better than that, and there was not a season in twenty-five years when the Ostrey Hollow men were not sore about it."
- **His memory of 1911:** "The station's own measure broke in the spring of 1911, and they made shift with a borrowed one for **six weeks** before their own came **up from town**. It went into my accounts as a small charge for replacement and nothing else, and I would not mention it at all except that in later years people came asking me about that year in particular, **as though a bookkeeper kept the weather**."
- **His theory, given to the board in 1925 and repeated:** "**Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons.** One man weighed the milk, one man drew the sample, one man made the test, one man wrote the figure that the pay slip was made from, and one man ran the separator. **There was no second man in that building from the spring to the fall.** I do not say what any particular person did on any particular day, because I was not there and I do not know. I say that a business which is arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken from it, and that no set of books ever made can prevent it. **The remedy is two men or a lock, and the Association had neither.**"
- "The patrons petitioned in the winter, **when a farmer has time to be angry**."
- **On Vose's knowledge:** "Mr. Vose knew what that account was doing. **He signed the sheet every year.** I laid the annual sheet before him each spring and he put his name at the foot of it, and a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him. What he made of it he never told me."
- **On his own silence:** "I did not sit silent for forty years, whatever has since been said. **I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone. I let it alone. I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience**, and a bookkeeper who takes his employer's business up a second time after being told once has generally begun to look for another employer."
- Close: "anybody who wishes to quarrel with this chapter is at liberty to go and read them, which is more than most of the quarrelers have ever done."

## 11. MERLE STRAWN'S CIRCUIT BOOKS (r06) — year by year

**Covering note.** Took the Ordell circuit in **1907**, laid it down **1936**. Fifty-odd plants in four counties — creameries, cheese factories, skimming stations, "one or two little places that made butter three days a week and called themselves a creamery." Each to be seen at least once a year, the doubtful ones oftener. Traveled by train where there was one, by livery where not, "and in the spring by neither, the roads up the side valleys being what they are in April." "A man on this circuit learns the plants by their smells before he learns them by their names: the sour-sweet of a receiving platform in July, the lime and steam of a wash room, **the flat cold nothing of a well-run cold room, which is the smell I liked best because it meant I would have no letter to write**."

**His invariable four-step routine, always in the same order "so that I would not have to remember which of them I had done":**
1. **The scales**, against the sealed weights he carried — both intake scale and cream scale, loaded light and loaded heavy.
2. **The lactometer** on the day's milk, patron by patron where there was any question, **for added water**.
3. **The boiler, water supply and cold room** — pressure, gauge, the cased well if there was one, and whether the room would hold what they put in it overnight.
4. **"And the one most men grudged me, I stood at the tester's elbow and watched him make a test through from the sample to the reading, and I did not let him hurry it."**

**Note that the routine contains no check of the glass** — until the burette arrives in 1921.

His creed: "I put down what I found. I did not put down what I thought about it, and I have not begun now. The state pays a field man to weigh and to look, and **every time in my life I have seen a field man paid for an opinion instead, the opinion has outlived the weighing and done harm after the man was dead.**"

**Entries:**
- **1907** — First round of the new circuit. Ostrey Hollow, up seven miles from Larrow Green, "opened before my time." Scales true. Cold room fair. "Their tester keeps his book in his own hand, and his hand is better than most. Watched him through two tests. **He works slow and clean and reads his own figure without being coaxed to it.**"
- **1909** — Ostrey Hollow, in the fall. Scales true. Boiler in order, safety tried. "The station takes from the farms of the wet bottom below it and from **three places on the hill**." Milk sweet, cans clean, one man cautioned about a rusted seam.
- **1911** — Ostrey Hollow, **June**, on his way over to the other side of the county. Scales true. Watched a test through. **"Nothing here to note."** *(He was there during the nine borrowed-measure weeks and recorded nothing unusual. NB r09 claims the state man himself left the borrowed measure that June — if true, Strawn recorded neither the loan nor the effect.)*
- **1912** — Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. "Cream shipped down the valley the same day it is separated, which is more than some do." Creek out over the flat road; went round by the ridge and lost the afternoon.
- **1913** — Larrow Green creamery, two days, the churn room and the receiving books. Scales true. "**New manager at the Association this year** and he came out with me to the platform, which the last one never did." *(= Selby Vose taking over.)*
- **1915** — Ostrey Hollow and the plants down the county line. Scales true. Wash room floor holding water at the drain; ordered it graded. "A field man who lets a floor go will be shown a floor at every plant on his circuit inside of two years."
- **1916** — Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. "**The tester complains that his patrons blame him for their figures. I told him that is the trade and he said he knew it.**"
- **1917** — Cheese factory in the next county, then Larrow Green. Scales true at both. "A bad year for help; the plants are short-handed and I found two testers doing the work of three men, **which is where mistakes live**."
- **1919** — Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. "**The manager of the Association has sent a circular round to the patrons of this station about water in the cans. I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so.**"
- **1920** — Ostrey Hollow twice, spring and fall. Scales true both times. No added water. Cold room short of ice in the fall; "I said so and it was seen to before I was out of the county."
- **1921** — "**The state has begun issuing burettes to the field men this year**, graduated, in a wooden case, for checking the delivery of measures and pipettes at the plants. Mine came in the spring with a printed sheet of directions. **It is a slow business to run a plant's glass against it and most plants have never had it done.**"
- **1922** — Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. No added water. Boiler in order. "**I did not run their glass against the burette this year, the day being short and the road home being what it was, and I have put that down because it is what happened.**"
- **1923, June 12** — "I had carried the burette two seasons before this check." Then the condemnation entry (quoted in §4 above).
- **1923, October** — Ostrey Hollow. **New glass in use since July.** Ran the new pipettes against the burette. **They deliver their mark.** Scales true.
- **1924** — Ostrey Hollow. Scales true, no added water, **tests improved. "Their book gives the year at 3.80."**
- **1925, in the fall** — Scales true, no added water, tests improved. "**New man at the tester's bench this year. He is quick, and quick is not the thing I look for.**"
- **1926** — "Word from the state office that **the burette program is discontinued from this year**. The instruments are to be kept by the men who have them but no more are to be issued and no returns are wanted. Mine is in its case in my grip and **has been used twice since**."
- **1928** — Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. No added water. "The station is quieter than it was." The loft-over-the-milk-room exchange (see §3).
- **1931** — Larrow Green creamery and Ostrey Hollow in the same week. Both in order. "Bad ice year; every plant in the four counties short and complaining, and two of them buying from the railroad."
- **1934** — Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. No added water. A separator bearing run hot and shut down two days, since repaired. "**The tester's book is kept up, though not in the hand it used to be kept in.**"
- **1936, March** — Last round. Ostrey Hollow, Larrow Green, and the two factories over the county line. Scales true at all four. "Nothing to note anywhere and nothing left to say about it."

**Closing note.** "The books themselves — **twenty-eight of them, one to the year, oilcloth covers, pocket size** — go to the state dairy office with this copy, as the rules require of a man laying down a circuit. There is nothing in them but what a field man puts in a field book: what I weighed, what I tested, what I told the plant to mend, and whether it was mended when I came back. **They are dull reading and they were meant to be.** I have been asked once or twice, by men who ought to know better, to say in a general way how a certain plant stood in my estimation over the years, and I have always answered that **I do not keep an estimation, I keep a book**, and the book is open to anybody the state sends for it. **I never had a theory about a plant and I do not recommend having one. A theory is a thing a man carries out to the platform with him and it gets in the way of the weights.**"

## 12A. ALONZO FRICK'S RECOLLECTIONS (r13) — the hearing from the chair

- Opening: "Our editor has asked the older men of this bar for something out of their own practice, and at seventy-four I take him to mean me. I have tried titles and boundaries for forty years, and two homicides, and none of them has stayed with me as the Ostrey Hollow matter has. **It was not litigation at all.**"
- **The complaint stated:** the patrons "had been paid, year upon year, on an average test of **3.16**" *(Teague's erroneous figure, not the book's 3.61 — see conflicts)*, "and the milk of the rest of the valley tested better than theirs. Three and sixteen is not a scandalous figure standing by itself. It becomes one when the man over the hill is paid on a better figure for milk out of cows no different from yours; when the difference holds steady for a quarter of a century; and when nobody in the office can tell you the cause of it."
- **The board:** three members; Frick chairman. "The by-laws provided for arbitration, and the officers, whatever else may be said of them, did not resist it."
- **THE SITTINGS — new and important:** "**We sat three times. The first session was at the Association's office at Ashlin on the seventeenth of March, 1925. The second and the third I asked to have at Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's own weigh room**, so that the board might see the plant it was to pass upon instead of hearing it described by men with an interest in the description. **That was my doing and I am still content with it.**"
- **What they did there:** "Every physical appliance of that station was before us and we satisfied ourselves upon it. We saw the scales tried. **We saw the sampling done and the test made**, and I will say plainly that **I did not understand the test then and do not understand it now; I am a lawyer and the thing is chemistry.** But I could see that it was done the same way twice, and that the men who did it were not hiding their hands."
- **THE THREE EXPLANATIONS, at last attributed:**
  1. **Water — Selby Vose, the manager.** "He had circulated a warning to those patrons on the subject some years before and he stood by it before us: that the Hollow's milk came in thinned, that a little water in a can is the oldest trick in the trade, and that a station cannot test what is not there. **He put it without heat and without proof.**"
  2. **Theft by the tester — Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper**, insinuated rather than charged (see §2 above for the full quotation).
  3. **The hay** — "Somebody read the report of the 1908 committee into the record, and the bog meadow at Hessel Bottom was before us for the better part of an afternoon… **It is an old opinion in that valley and it had the advantage of blaming nobody in the room.**"
- **On Keddie:** "I believed him then and I believe him now. **His denial was accurate and it did him no good at all, and the reason it did him no good is that a man who cannot account for a figure is presumed by everybody in the room to be the figure's author.**"
- **The corridor confidence** (not in the minutes): Keddie privately told Frick "that he had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the room. He was not a bitter man about it… **and I record it here because it has been said since that he went to his grave accusing nobody, which is true, and that he suspected nobody, which is not.**"
- **On the dismissal:** "On the thirtieth of April, four days before we made our award, the Association dismissed him. There was no finding against him from us and there never was one; the board had not concluded and did not conclude that he had done anything whatever. He was simply let go while the board was still sitting, and **I have never been able to think well of the Association for it.**"
- **The patrons' own claim:** "**The patrons' own reckoning of what they had lost was sixty-seven thousand pounds of butter fat over the years complained of, and nothing was ever brought forward to disturb that figure.** We were not able to say who owed it or why, and we said so."
- **Reflection:** "A board of arbitration can only decide between the accounts that are put to it. Three were put to us; each of them was urged by a man of standing; each of them would have been believed by half the valley; and not one of them was proved. **I have wondered since whether the answer was in that room and simply was not in any of the three stories, and whether a lawyer who understood chemistry might have found it. I did not understand chemistry. I understood that an honest man was ruined by a figure nobody could explain, and that we paid his neighbors money without being able to tell them what they were being paid for.**"

## 13. SELBY VOSE'S 1926 ADDRESS (r08) — the official version

- "It is thirteen years this spring, since 1913, that you have had the management of this Association in my hands, and I have stood up before you thirteen times… I have never had a year I liked less to report on than the one behind us, and I have never had one I was gladder to have behind us." Ends: "I thank you for thirteen years of your patience and I ask for one more."
- **Ordinary business:** season opened wet and stayed wet into June; hauling heavy on the horses and heavier on the men. Larrow Green ran through the flush without a day lost, "which is more than the creameries below us can say." Butter has held its place with the trade; churn room clean; ice went into the house early and lasted; no complaint of any man on the payroll. The station "ran its season out and its cream came down to us daily and sweet."
- **On the finding:** "The board found no cause established. It found no cause established against the station, against the office, or against any officer or servant of this Association, and I ask you to sit with that sentence a moment, because **it is the only sentence in this business that was arrived at by men under oath with the books in front of them**."
- **On the payment:** "a generous settlement of an unproved claim. This Association paid thirty-eight hundred dollars rather than let a body of its own members go on believing they had been used badly by their neighbors. There is not a business house in Ordell County that would have done it. We did it because we are not a business house; **we are the farmers of this valley in company together, and a company that will not spend money to keep peace among its own members has not understood what it is for.**" But: "**I will not have peace bought a second time.**"
- **His theory:** "**Water was going into cans at the head of that valley and it stopped when the Association began to look. The rise of 1924 is the measure of it.**" "For a generation the milk brought to that station tested below the milk brought to Larrow Green, and every year we were told it was the wet hay, or the tester, or the office, or the weather. Then the Association's attention came to rest on the station, and there was a petition, and a hearing, and men were required to answer questions in front of their neighbors. **And in the very next season the tests at that station rose by twenty-one hundredths and the average for 1924 stood at 3.90**, which is as good a figure as any station in this county will show you. Nothing was done to those cows. Nothing was done to that bottom land. Nobody changed their feed and nobody changed their pasture. **The tests came up when the looking began**, and any man who wants a different explanation of that is welcome to stand where I am standing and give us one."
- **Dismissing the glass:** "I am told, and I have been told it kindly and by men who mean well, that the state man's visit of that period explains the whole thing. **It does not.** He came through the county as he came through it every year or two, he tried our scales against his weights and found them true, and **he condemned the station's testing apparatus on the twelfth of July, 1923**, and told us to order new. **We ordered new inside the month and had it in use before the flush was over. Apparatus wears. A station in daily use since 1896 will wear its fittings out twice over in that time**, and it is the state man's business to say so before they fail him, and ours to replace them when he does. **I decline to build a mystery on a piece of housekeeping that appears in our own order book with a date and a price against it.**"
- **On the unnamed waterer:** "I say it without naming a name, because I do not know a name and I would not use it if I did. **A little water in a can is not a felony in a man's own mind. It is a way of making a hard year come out even, and the man who does it tells himself he will stop when the price improves.** I have no wish to see anybody prosecuted, and nobody has been or will be."
- **Defending the 1919 circular:** "which has been thrown at me a good deal in the last two years. I wrote it because it was my duty to write it. **It warned the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, in temperate language, that added water would be looked for and would be dealt with. It named nobody. It accused nobody. It said that this Association pays for butterfat and cannot pay for creek water, and I would write every word of it again tomorrow.** A manager who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing to the men who make them is not being kind to them; **he is being idle at their expense.**"
- **The Jersey herd:** "My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law under which every patron in this Association is paid on the test of the station where he delivers, kept a herd of **Jerseys** at the home place, as some of you remember, and I kept them after him. **In 1910 that herd tested 4.56.** I do not bring that forward to boast of the cows; they are gone now and the ones I have are no better than yours. I bring it forward because it settles a thing that gets said about this county in the trade below us — **that the Larrow Valley is poor milk country**, that our land is sour and our cattle are indifferent and our figures are what a man must expect off ground like ours. That is not true."
- **His exhortation:** "**Bring it as it left the cow. Wash the cans, cool the milk, keep the dog out of the milk house, and bring it as it left the cow.** Do that, and the Ostrey Hollow tests will stay where they went in 1924, and this business will be dead and buried where it belongs, and your manager can stand up here next spring and talk to you about butter prices, which is what he is paid for."

## 13A. ROY BRANT ON HIS FATHER'S BOOK (r09) — the patron's ledger

- Written in longhand at Hessel Bottom, winter 1955, at the farm bureau's request. "I would rather write about my father's book, because the old days are gone and the book is on the shelf behind my chair, and **a man does better to talk about what he can put his hand on**."
- **The book:** a plain ledger, ruled by hand. **Two columns for each day of delivery: (1) the weight of every can as Emmet took it off his own scale in his own barn before the wagon went down the road; (2) the figure the station gave him back for that same milk.** "That is all it is. **There is no comment in it anywhere but three or four lines in thirty years, and no opinion at all.**" The cover is gone off the front, the back held by a strip of harness leather Emmet tacked on himself, and the first forty leaves are stained brown at one corner "where it lay in a leaky window a winter he was sick."
- **Kept from 1 January 1898** per Roy — **Nym's Item 4 and Frick's Exhibit 3 both say begun 1 January 1897.** Kept "until the week before he died" (1929), and by Roy since, "in the same book until it was filled and then in another, in the same way."
- **THE DECISIVE OBSERVATION:** "**The two columns agreed.** That is the thing about the book that people do not take in when I tell them, so I will say it slowly. Day after day, month after month, for the whole run of years my father kept it and for the years I have kept it, his weight and the station's weight came out the same, barring a pound or two here and there, and those were days when a can got slopped on the hill. **Two scales, his and the station's, kept by two men who by the end were not speaking to one another, and the scales never fell out at all. Whatever was wrong at that station — and something was wrong at that station for a very long time — it was not the weighing.**"
- **The size of the gap, from the patron's side: "Our milk at the Hollow ran seventeen hundredths of a point under what the same class of milk was making down at Larrow Green. Seventeen hundredths is nothing to look at on one pay slip. Set the slips in a row for a lifetime of farming and it is a farm."** (Compare Ivy's 0.19, Nym's "a little under two tenths," Vose's 0.21, Kepp's 0.21 and "a fifth of a point.")
- **The shape of the gap: "it was seventeen hundredths in July and it was seventeen hundredths in February. It did not come and go with the season. It did not care whether the cows were on the meadow or on hay in the barn. Nobody would take account of that, then or since, though it was in front of them."**
- **1902:** "The first men to say so out loud said it in 1902, and they were laughed at, and one of them was my father."
- **1911:** "There were nine weeks in the summer of 1911 when the station's own measure was out of use, it having been broken. **The state man left his own measure that June and that is what they used.** Through those nine weeks our tests came up, all of us, every farm on the Bottom; and then the station's own came back into use and the figures went down again to where they had been. **My father noticed the rise and he noticed the fall and he entered both, and he could make nothing of it, and I could make nothing of it either, and there it sat for near on fifty years.**"
- Prices: **twenty-four cents** for fat in 1910; "over the whole stretch of years the complaint covers, one year with another, **it came to about twenty-five cents**." (Agrees with Loomis/Teague/Ivy against Sill's and Kepp's 27 cents.)
- Family: Orra was Emmet's full sister; Duncan Keddie and Roy are **first cousins**; Hazel and Ivy are **second cousins**. "So Ansel Keddie, who kept the station's test book, was married into the family that had more to lose by that station than any other family on the meadow, and **I have never known what to do with that fact except set it down.**"
- Closing: "I am sixty-seven and **the book has outlived two of the three men who are in it.**" *(Postscript 1958:)* "Duncan's girl Ivy came out to the place this year and asked whether she might borrow the book for a piece of work she is doing at the college at Farland. I let her have it. **She is careful with paper.** It comes back to me when she is through, and after me it is Hazel's."

## 13B. ROSALIE CUDD'S LETTER (r10) — the outside measurement

- Reads Ivy's letter twice, "once standing up in the make room with my hands wet and once properly after supper." "**In thirty-odd years nobody from over the ridge has ever asked me a plain question about our books**, and here is a young woman from the college who asks one in four lines and apologizes for troubling me. You are not troubling me. Sit down and I will tell you the whole of it."
- **Why her father's figures are independent evidence:** "He tested himself, on his own bench, in the little room off the intake, with his own glassware that he bought new and washed in the evening and kept in a rack he had made, and he wrote his own book. **He never compared a figure of his with anybody else's, and I want to be honest with you about the reason: it never entered his head.** He had no business with what your station made of the same milk. A cheesemaker tests what comes in his door so he knows what he is paying for and what his vat will do. He is not keeping a check on another man's arithmetic seven miles off over a mountain, and if you had suggested it to my father he would have laughed at you."
- **THE SIXTEEN-SEASON TABLE (season averages of the four Hessel Bottom men taken together, copied straight off the leaves, nothing smoothed and no season thrown out):**

| Season | Test | Season | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1915 | 3.79 | 1923 | 3.81 |
| 1916 | 3.81 | 1924 | 3.80 |
| 1917 | 3.78 | 1925 | 3.79 |
| 1918 | 3.80 | 1926 | 3.82 |
| 1919 | 3.80 | 1927 | 3.78 |
| 1920 | 3.82 | 1928 | 3.80 |
| 1921 | 3.79 | 1929 | 3.81 |
| 1922 | 3.78 | 1930 | 3.79 |

  "Sixteen seasons, and the four men taken together, one summer with another, at about 3.80." Range 3.78–3.82. "They sit between 3.78 and 3.82, year in, year out, wet years and dry ones, **whether the meadow had been under water in May or hard as a road**." The four men separately are "within a few hundredths of each other and always were, which is what you would expect of four herds off one piece of ground."
  **NOTE: 1923 (3.81) and 1924 (3.80) show no jump at the condemnation — because the Alder Corners glass was never short. The over-the-ridge series is flat across the exact years the Ostrey Hollow series rises by 0.19. This is the control group.**
- **On watered milk — the best evidence in the whole file that nobody watered anything:** "A cheesemaker knows watered milk inside a day. He does not need a test to know it, though the test will tell him too. **The curd comes soft and it will not knit; you get a slack, weak body and your yield is down, and you can stand at the vat and see your money going out with the whey. It cannot be hidden from the man who makes the cheese, because the cheese tells him, and it tells him the next morning, not next year. Butter is more forgiving that way. Cheese is a witness.** In sixteen summers my father never made one complaint of those four men, never docked one of them a cent, and never — not once that I heard, and I was in the room — said a word about their milk except that **he wished he had more of it in July**."
- **On the valley's theory:** her father "used to say that the Ordell people had a theory about that bottom **the way some families have a ghost: it explained things, and it cost nothing, and nobody was ever going to test it**."
- **On the quarrel:** "I am aware, of course, that there was trouble in your valley for years about the milk at the head of it, because **trouble carries over a ridge better than anything else does**. It came to me the way weather comes, in pieces and late, and always with somebody's name attached to it. I paid no attention. It was not my county, they were not my patrons in September, and I had a make to get out. **If I sound short about Ordell it is only that I have listened to forty years of your valley explaining that meadow to itself, and I never once heard anybody go up there and look.**" (The fourth independent statement of the "nobody ever went and looked" theme — after Loomis, Keddie and Vira.)
- **Her disclaimer:** "**I do not know what your station made of the same cows, and I have not the first idea what your county decided about any of it. I never saw those figures and I would not know how to get them.** All I can give you is our side of the ridge, honestly copied, and you are welcome to it, and welcome to print it if it is any use to you." — i.e. the comparison series was assembled by someone with no stake in and no knowledge of the outcome.
- Invitation: "Come over in the summer and see the place. The road is open by then, mostly, and I will give you your dinner and show you my father's book, **which is a nicer object than anything I have said about it here**."

## 13C. DELL RUNDLE'S JUBILEE PAMPHLET (r12) — the valley's own memory, and its self-correction

- Framing, "TO THE MEMBERS": "Fifty years is not long for a country, but it is a long time for a valley to keep one thing going… **What follows is not a record. It is what the valley remembers, tidied, and where the valley is certain of a thing I have written it down as certain.**" — a precise self-description of folk history, and the reason nearly every figure in it is wrong.
- **Chapter IV, the verdict of the store:** "From 1902 the men of the Hollow complained… They were not believed. In 1908 the Association sent a committee up under Nyle Grigg… In 1919 the manager circulated a warning… **and after that the two ends of this valley did not speak civilly to one another for six years.**" Then: "**It is the settled opinion of the valley that cream left that station by the back door, and the figures went right when the man who kept it did not.** I set that down as I have heard it set down a hundred times, by men who were there and men who were not, and **I have never heard it contradicted at the store.**"
- **His account of the hearing:** "The state inspector condemned the station's glassware **on the twelfth of July, 1923**, **for reasons of his own that had nothing to do with the quarrel**, and new glass was put in. In January of 1925 the Hollow patrons petitioned the Association. The station took in, over the years complained of, **forty-four million pounds of milk**. The patrons' loss upon it was **nineteen thousand six hundred dollars, and their petition set the figure out to the dollar.** A board of arbitration sat at Ashlin that spring under Alonzo Frick, attorney, of that town. It heard everybody. It found nothing established, it made an award to the patrons without admitting anything, and **it sent this valley home to live with itself, which the valley has done, after a fashion, ever since.**"
- **Chapter V, over the ridge:** "in 1914 the Alder Corners Cheese Company was opened over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County, by Jerome Cudd, and… in the summers a few of our Hessel Bottom men hauled over the mountain to him rather than down to their own station. **That was resented here.** It is **Jerome Cudd's daughter, Rosalie Cudd**, who makes the cheese at Alder Corners now."
- **The 1959 footnote, added to the reprint:** "*Added to the 1959 reprint.* The Hollow matter has since been taken up again at the college at Farland by Miss Ivy Keddie, who is **second cousin to Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom**. **Eleven pipettes** out of the station's old condemned crate were found still to be in existence. I am too old to rewrite a pamphlet and I refer the reader to the college. — D.R."
- **Chapter VI, the balance sheet:** "Set against the quarrel, put the rest: fifty Junes of cream coming down that road in the early cool, fifty Januaries of it going down over ice with the horses roughshod, a check every month in kitchens that had known none, and a creamery still standing that was built by men with nothing but each other and a piece of paper they had written themselves. **Whatever this valley did wrong at the head of it, it did this right, and it did it for half a century, and the men of 1889 would think we had done well.**"
- **NOTE ADDED WHILE IN PRESS** — quoted in full in §4 above. The ledger entry he happened to see "on another errand altogether" made him withdraw his own chapter: cream stolen would have arrived light; it arrived heavy. **He leaves the paragraph standing and corrects it at the back, which is exactly Loomis's discipline of keeping record and recollection apart.**

## 13D. HOBART SILL'S REMARKS (r14) — the cream at the receiving end

- "Forty-one years I have stood at that churn. Eighteen ninety-three I came here, and there was no floor in this room but plank, and we cooled with creek ice out of the pond. You can write that down. **Forty-one years and I never sent out a tub of butter I would not eat myself, which is more than the office can say about anything it ever sent out.**"
- **THE HEAVY CREAM, stated plainer than anywhere else in the file:** "**That cream ran heavy. Every year it ran heavy. There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there.** The office would send down its sheet and the sheet would say so many pounds of fat bought at the station, and the fat that came into this room off that wagon was more than the sheet said. **Always more. Never less.**"
- **His double weighing:** "**And I weighed it myself, most years.** The platform man weighed it and I weighed it over again when it came into the cold room, because **I have never in my life taken another man's figure for what was going into my churn.** Cans off, cans on the scale, cans into the ice, and the book ruled and signed before I put my coat on. You may go and look at the receiving book if they will let you at it, and **you will find my hand on every page of it from ninety-three, and you will find the Hollow's cream coming in heavy on page after page after page, in my own writing, for as long as I have been in this room.**"
- **Why he made nothing of it — the second great "evenness stops you looking" moment:** "**Well, that is cream for you. Cream is not milk and it will not stand still and be counted.** It is a thick thing and it clings and it separates on you and any man who tells you he can put a figure on cream to the pound and hold it is a man who has never had his arm in a can up to the elbow. **So it ran heavy. So does everybody's. I never made anything of it, and I will say plain, nobody ever asked me to.**"
- **His two test figures:** "Our own milk, here at Larrow Green, our own patrons, the men on this creek — **that ran 3.78 taking one year with another right through those years. Three seventy-eight.** I know it because I made the butter out of it and a buttermaker knows what his cream is worth before anybody hands him a paper about it. Ordinary milk, ordinary county, ordinary cows. There is nothing wonderful about 3.78 and there is nothing shameful about it either." And: "**The Hollow milk was really a 3.85 and no better**, and I will say that to any man in this valley. Wet ground up there, but good cows, and I never had a word to say against the cows. A 3.85 and no better, and **if the figures they were paid on came out under that, then somebody up that hill road was helping the cans along, and I said so at the time and I say it now.**"
- **His version of the rise:** "You cannot tell me nine families all got honest in one summer. There was water in those cans and I said so at the time. Because I will tell you what happened — **one summer, along in the twenties, those figures came up and they stayed up, and nothing on this earth changed but the season**, and men do not suddenly start pouring better milk out of the same cows in the same barns. **Something got stopped. Something got said to somebody. I never knew what and I never asked, because the day a buttermaker starts asking about other men's cans is the day he stops making butter.**"
- **1902 remembered from the receiving end:** "Nineteen two is the first I remember of it, a man in here shouting that his milk tested under his brother-in-law's down the creek and it was the same feed and the same water and what did we mean by it."
- **On the office:** "That is the offices for you. Papers. **The Association has a paper for everything and butter for nothing.** I have had men come in here in a collar and tell me my churn was running slow when they could not have told you which end of a cow gets up first… **not one man in that office ever came down here to see how it was done, and every one of them could tell you to the half cent what the fat was worth. Twenty-seven cents.** That is what fat was through those years, take one year with another, twenty-seven cents the pound, **and there is not a man in the office could tell you what makes it churn.**"
- **THE 1896 GLASS — the single most important new fact in r14:** "When they opened that station up at Ostrey Hollow — that was ninety-six, in May, and there was a crowd went up for it — **they had no outfit of their own. Not a piece.** It was ordered and it was not come, and they were ready to start taking milk, and there was talk of holding off the whole season. **And I said no, they can have mine. I went to my own bench in this room and I made up an outfit for them out of my own glass, my own, that I had used and knew, and it went up the valley on the wagon and they opened on it. Their own came the spring after and they sent mine back to me, and one piece of it was chipped and I never said a word about that either.**" — This confirms the test book's first leaf ("Glass borrowed of Larrow Green"), refutes Keddie's later memory ("borrowed off the Ashlin creamery"), and establishes that **the 3.80 of 1896 was read on the same known-good Larrow Green glass that produced Larrow Green's own 3.78 average.**
- **THE MISSING 1896 INTAKE FIGURE:** "**That first season they took in about nine hundred thousand pounds up there. May to the end of the year, that is, so it was not even a whole one.** That is a good deal of milk for a hill station starting from nothing, and it was the biggest thing that ever happened to Ostrey Hollow, and I was glad enough to have had a hand in it." — Nym's "notes and queries" says this figure "is nowhere else"; it was in the buttermaker's head, and nobody asked him.
- Closing: "I am sixty-four years old and I have thought about butter. I never went up there but twice in my life. I took their cream off the platform and I made it into butter and the butter was good… **Whatever the Hollow was doing with its cans, and I have told you what I think about that, the fat came down that hill and it came into my churn and it went out of this room as good butter with this creamery's name on the tub.**" And: "**Do not put in that I said anything against the cows up there, because I did not. The cows were never the trouble.**"

## 13E. NYLE GRIGG'S 1919 COVERING LETTER (r15)

- Occasion: "I am asked to let the report of our committee be printed again, **the Ostrey Hollow question having been reopened this season by men who were boys when we made it.** I consent willingly and I have nothing in it to take back. I ask leave only to add a page, because a report is a dry thing and does not say why it was written, and because **eleven years have brought us some light from a quarter that had none to give us in 1908.**" (Note: 1908 + 11 = 1919, the year of the water circular — the reprint and the circular are the same season's business.)
- **On the origin of the quarrel:** "From about 1902 there had been grumbling out of Ostrey Hollow that the milk of that station tested under the milk of the rest of this valley. It was not one man and it was not one season. It went on and it grew, and it got into the talk at the annual meeting and into the talk at every crossroads store between here and Ashlin, and **it took the shape such things always take, which is that somebody must be doing it on purpose.** By the winter in which we were named, **the board could not let it alone any longer without seeming to have an interest in letting it alone**, and we three were named to look into it."
- "**Payment by test came in with the 1893 meeting, as I have always understood it**, and from that year a difference of this kind stopped being a matter of opinion and became a matter of money."
- **His account of the method — flatly contradicting his own report:** "**We sampled those herds through the whole of 1908 — every month of that year**, in the barn and on the pasture, out of the night's milk and the morning's, and we did not let a man know which day we were coming. We weighed. We watched the test made and we watched it made again."
- **His declaration of disinterest** (quoted in §2).
- **The misquotation that is the whole point of his letter:** "**But the State Agricultural College at Farland has since published its Bulletin No. 214**, which was a feeding trial upon bog hay carried out under the College's own eye, with weighed rations and no interest in the outcome. **That trial found the bog hay to depress the butter fat test by two tenths in the winter feeding. Two tenths, gentlemen, out of a College that had never heard of Hessel Bottom and had no reason on earth to flatter us. There is our finding, arrived at by another road and by better men, three years after we set our names to it.**" — The bulletin says **not more than five hundredths in winter and nothing measurable in summer**. Grigg has quadrupled it and dropped the summer half, which is the half that kills his theory.
- **His prescription:** "The remedy lies with the land and not with the station. Drain that bottom, sweeten it, keep the stock off it in the wet of the spring, and feed the coarse hay off it with a mixed ration and not by itself, and the Hollow will test with the rest of us. **Go on as they are going and abuse the man who reads their figures for them, and they will be grumbling in twenty years' time as they were grumbling when we began, and no better off.**" (They were grumbling in twenty years' time — 1939, when Rundle wrote — and for thirty-nine.)

## 13F. DR. ROLAND KEPP'S PAPER (r16) — the wet-hay theory at its most respectable

- **The land:** "A wet bottom is not a rich meadow with too much water in it. It is a distinct kind of ground with a distinct kind of chemistry, and the water is the least part of it. Where drainage is wanting, the year's growth does not rot down as it does on a hill field; **it lies sour**, and the soil that results is **an acid soil, poor in lime, and poor in the phosphates**. On such ground the sweet grasses will not hold. They are crowded out within a few seasons by **sedge and rush and the coarse tussock grasses**… That herbage carries bulk and it carries water. What it does not carry, in any quantity worth the name, is **lime or phosphorus**, and it is cut late because such ground cannot be got onto early, and late cutting takes out what little was in it."
- **The cow:** "A cow in milk is a manufactory working on a narrow margin… If her ration is short of the minerals, she does not simply give less; **she robs her own frame to make good, and she goes on robbing it until she cannot.**" Signs: thin over the loin and rough in the coat out of winter feeding; slow to condition in spring; ready subjects for digestive disorders in the fall; breed later; feet worse. "**They will milk, gentlemen. Wet land is generous of flow and no man on a bog meadow ever complained that his pail was light. But what is in the pail is another matter, and the fat is the first thing to suffer, because the fat is the dearest thing the cow makes and it is the first economy she practices when she is pressed.**"
- **His field measure:** "The wet bottoms of this county yield a milk **poorer in fat by a fifth of a point** and every practical man knows it. Set the hill herds of this county against the bottom herds and you will find that difference, farm for farm, wherever you look for it, and the difference does not depend on the breed, on the man, or on the barn. **It depends on the ground.**"
- **Hessel Bottom:** "the completest example of wet meadow in this county. **Six farms** cut hay and pasture stock upon it… the springs come out at the foot of the slope and run in it the year round; a part of it is under water every spring and is not fit to be walked on until well into May. Its hay is sedge hay, and on those farms it is the winter dependence and very nearly the whole of it."
- **His statement of the gap:** "The patrons of the skimming station at Ostrey Hollow have complained for something like a quarter of a century that their milk tests below the milk of the rest of the valley. **Their complaint is perfectly just and I have never doubted it.** The figure of the gap, as it has been given to me and as I have satisfied myself, is **twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21** — between the milk of that station and the milk of this valley at large."
- **The same misquotation as Grigg's:** "The College fed the bog hay under control and measured what it did, and **it found the butter fat test depressed by two tenths in the winter feeding. That is the whole case. Two tenths from the College, laid alongside the fifth of a point I have found in the field and the twenty-one hundredths that station has been complaining of, and the three agree so nearly that I do not know what more a reasonable man requires.**" — Three numbers agreeing beautifully, one of which is a fourfold misquotation and the other two of which are the artifact he is trying to explain.
- **His dismissal of the 1911 experiment — the worst reasoning in the file:** "It has been urged upon me, by a gentleman who I know will forgive my noticing it here, that in the summer of 1911 the testing at that station was broken off for **six weeks**, their own apparatus having failed them and a borrowed one being used, and that the figures stood higher during those six weeks than before or after. **Six weeks in the flush of the season, gentlemen. Six weeks of June and July, when every herd in the county is at its best and the stock are off the wet ground and up on the aftermath. I am astonished that anybody should found an argument on six weeks of summer figures. I could produce you six weeks in any year that would prove anything you liked.**" — He never asks why the other twenty-five Junes and Julies read 3.61.
- **The remedy:** "It is not a mystery and it is not cheap. Drain the bottom; open the old ditches and cut new; lime the ground and lime it again; sow it down when it will carry a seeding; keep the stock off it in the wet of the spring, **when a hoof does more harm in a day than a season will repair**; and never feed that hay alone. Feed it with a mixed ration and a mineral supplement, as the College directs. **Fat has been worth twenty-seven cents the pound through these years, and a fifth of a point taken off every hundredweight a farm ships, year in and year out, will pay for a great deal of tile.**"
- **DISCUSSION (taken down by the club's secretary):**
  - *A member* asked whether the difference might not be in the cows rather than the land, the bottom farms being poorer men with poorer stock. **Kepp:** he had considered and rejected it; "some of the best-bred cattle in the county stand on wet land, and some very indifferent cattle on the hills test well; and that **a man may improve his herd for twenty years and never get his fat up while his ground is sour.**"
  - *A member* asked whether the trouble might lie in the station rather than the bottom, as had been said in the valley for years. **Kepp:** "that he had no interest in that quarrel and no competence in it; that **he had never in his life read a test and would not know how**; but that when a cause is found in the soil, in the herbage, in the condition of the stock, in the College's trial, and in every wet bottom in the county alike, **it is not good sense to go looking for a second cause in a building.**" — The right question was asked from the floor in 1926 and was answered by a man who had just disqualified himself from answering it.
  - *The chairman* thanked Dr. Kepp and observed that the paper would be printed for the members.

## 15. CONFLICTS, ERRORS AND ODDITIES — MASTER LIST

**Numerical conflicts about the settlement and the hearing**
1. **Award amount: $3,500 (Vira Toland) vs. $3,800 (Loomis's minute, Teague, Nym's Item 7, Ivy, Vose, Frick).** Six sources give $3,800 with the award quoted verbatim; Vira's is a sixty-year-old memory.
2. **Date of the hearing: Vira places it "the winter and spring after" the 1919 letter; every other source puts it in 1925** — six years later. Vira has compressed 1919 and 1925 into one event, which also makes her believe the tests rose *after* the hearing, when in fact they rose after the June 1923 condemnation, well before the petition.
3. **Vira's causal chain is therefore inverted:** she says Keddie was caught, put out, and the numbers went right the summer after. In fact the numbers went right in the summer of 1923 (new glass, July) and 1924, and Keddie was put out on 30 April 1925 — nearly two years *after* the correction. **Rundle's pamphlet enshrines the same inversion as the settled opinion of the valley: "the figures went right when the man who kept it did not."**
4. **NEW — Where the board sat: Loomis and Nym both place the whole arbitration at Ashlin (Nym Item 7: "Minute book of the board of arbitration, Ashlin, 17 March to 4 May 1925"); Rundle says "A board of arbitration sat at Ashlin that spring"; but Frick, the chairman, says only the first session was at Ashlin and the second and third were held in the station's own weigh room at Ostrey Hollow, at his own request.** If Frick is right — and he was there, and it is the kind of thing a chairman remembers arranging — then the board *did* stand in the station on a working morning, saw the sampling and the test made, and still missed it; and Loomis, who took every word down, wrote her account of the hearing without mentioning that two thirds of it happened seven miles up the valley.
5. **NEW — The patrons' own claim, three ways: 67,000 lb of butterfat (Frick, "nothing was ever brought forward to disturb that figure"); $19,600 "set out to the dollar" (Rundle); against Ivy's computed 76,000 lb and $19,000.** The patrons undercounted the pounds by 9,000 and overcounted the money by $600. Note $19,600 ÷ 67,000 lb ≈ 29¼ cents, which matches no price anyone quotes.

**Numerical conflicts about the quantities**
6. **Milk intake: 40,000,000 lb (Loomis §7, "from Mr. Teague's own annual sheets"; Ivy; Nym Item 6; Vaught) vs. 44,000,000 lb (Teague himself, r05; and Rundle r12, who presumably had it from the same talk).** Loomis attributes her 40 million to Teague, and Teague gives 44 million. Nym's ledger note supports 40 million — but for the specific span 1898–1922; Teague says "over the years the account ran," which (ledger column beginning 1897, running past 1922) is a longer span. The two may be reconcilable by span, or Teague's memory may simply be off by a decade of his own figures. **Rundle repeating 44 million shows which figure was in circulation in the valley.**
7. **Average test: 3.16 (Teague) vs. 3.61 (the test book, in three independent quotations).** Teague's 3.16 looks like a transposition of 3.61. **NEW: Frick repeats 3.16 in 1946 as the figure the patrons "had been paid, year upon year" on — so Teague's transposition got into the chairman's understanding of the case and stayed there for twenty-one years.** Nobody else anywhere gives 3.16, and 3.16 ÷ 0.95 = 3.33, which fits nothing.
8. **The gain percentage: Teague says "the gain ran at five and a quarter percent *of the fat that was actually in the cream*." That is wrong by his own entry** — 3,040 of 60,800 is exactly 5.00%; 5¼% is against the *credited* 57,760. Ivy and Nym both make this exact point. Teague, the careful bookkeeper, misstated his own denominator. **NEW: Vaught repeats the identical error in Bulletin 471 §V — "The gain is five and a quarter percent of the butterfat actually delivered" — in the very publication that corrects everything else.**
9. **Teague says the station-gain line "was my own device; I began it when I came in 1905" — yet the entry he quotes as his own is for 1898, seven years before he arrived, and Nym records that the station column runs from 1897.** Either he has misdated his innovation or the founders' books already carried the line he insists was not "some old dodge of the founders." (r01/r05 both note valley gossip that it was old.)
10. **NEW — The price of butterfat: 25 cents (Loomis from Teague's sheets; Ivy; Roy Brant "about twenty-five cents") vs. 27 cents (Sill, "take one year with another"; Kepp, "through these years") vs. 24 cents for 1910 specifically (Roy Brant, from the weigh book).** The 25-cent figure is the documented one; 27 cents is what two men at the working end remembered. At 27 cents the 76,000 lb would be worth $20,520, not $19,000.
11. **NEW — The size of the gap, six ways: 0.17 (Roy Brant, from the only book that sets the two figures side by side day after day); 0.19 (Ivy, computed; also "a little under two tenths," Nym Item 16, from the creamery books); 0.20 / "a fifth of a point" (Kepp's field impression); 0.21 (Vose 1926; Kepp's second figure); 0.295 (Vaught's miscomputation); and, by Sill's two remembered averages, 3.85 − 3.61 = 0.24.** The documentary figures cluster at 0.17–0.19; the rhetorical ones inflate.
12. **NEW — The Larrow Green average: 3.78 (Sill, from making the butter) vs. ≈3.80 (Nym Item 16, "a little under two tenths of a point better" than 3.61) vs. 3.80 (the Alder Corners four-man average, and the station's own 1896 and 1924 figures).** All within two hundredths — the valley's true milk is ~3.78–3.80 everywhere, which is the whole point.
13. **NEW — Sill's "the Hollow milk was really a 3.85 and no better."** He is the only man who ever put an independent number on the Hollow's true test, and he did it by feel from the cream he handled. It is five hundredths above the true 3.80 — and he used it to conclude that the cans were watered, since 3.61 is short of 3.85. Had he trusted his own 3.78 and his own heavy-cream books instead, he would have had it.

**Conflicts about the 1911 episode**
14. **Duration: nine weeks (Keddie's own dated entries, 8 May – 10 July; the 1922 summary leaf; Ivy; Vaught; Roy Brant) vs. six weeks (Teague; and Kepp, who builds his whole rebuttal on the six).**
15. **Where the borrowed 1911 measure came from: "came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon" (Ivy) vs. "before their own came up from town" (Teague) vs. "The state man left his own measure that June" (Roy Brant, r09).** These may describe different objects (the borrowed one vs. the replacement), but Roy Brant's version is new and startling: if Strawn left his own measure at the station in June 1911, then **the state's own inspector personally supplied the instrument that produced the anomaly, visited that same month, and wrote "Nothing here to note."** Strawn's own extract for 1911 says nothing of lending anything, and he had no burette until 1921.
16. **Strawn visited in June 1911, in the middle of the anomaly, watched a test through, and wrote "Nothing here to note."** He had no burette then, and nothing about a borrowed measure struck him as worth an entry. The most consequential nine weeks in the story left no trace in the state's record.
17. **NEW — Kepp's dismissal of 1911 is self-refuting.** He says six weeks of June and July prove nothing because every herd is at its best then and the stock are off the wet ground. But the station's *other* twenty-five Junes and Julies also had the herds at their best and the stock off the wet ground, and they read 3.61. The seasonal argument requires the gap to vary with the season; every witness who kept a book (Keddie, Roy Brant) says flatly that it did not.

**Conflicts about the condemnation**
18. **Date: 12 June 1923 (Strawn's own book, quoted twice verbatim; Nym Item 9; Bulletin 471; Loomis "June of 1923"; Ivy "June of 1923"; Vaught "June of 1923") vs. 12 July 1923 (Vose's address AND Rundle's pamphlet).** Vose has the day right and the month wrong — and the error is convenient for him, because moving it into July puts the condemnation *after* the tests had begun to rise, and the true June date puts it immediately before. **Rundle repeating "the twelfth of July, 1923" in 1939 shows Vose's version had become the valley's version.** (Strawn's Oct 1923 entry: "New glass in use since July," i.e. ordered 28 June, shipped July — Vose's "we ordered new inside the month" is accurate to the June date, not the July one he gives.)
19. **Vose's explanation of the condemnation — "Apparatus wears" — cannot account for it.** A pipette blown at 17.6 does not wear its way to a uniform 16.72 across six dozen; the nine measured runs cluster within 0.04 c.c. of each other, which is a manufacturing fault, not wear. Also his "a station in daily use since 1896 will wear its fittings out twice over" ignores that the glass in question dates from 1897 and was the *original* outfit.
20. **NEW — Rundle's gloss: the inspector condemned the glassware "for reasons of his own that had nothing to do with the quarrel."** Perfectly true as a statement of Strawn's motive, and perfectly wrong as a statement of relevance; it is the sentence with which the valley filed away its own answer.

**Conflicts about the 1924 figures**
21. **The 1924 average: 3.80 (Keddie's book; Strawn's 1924 entry, "Their book gives the year at 3.80"; the summary leaf's own logic) vs. 3.90 (Vose).** And **the rise: 0.19 (3.61→3.80, per Ivy's arithmetic) vs. "twenty-one hundredths" (Vose).** Vose has inflated both the level and the jump; on the true figures the rise is precisely the 1/20 the glass predicts. **NEW: Kepp adopts Vose's 0.21 as "the figure of the gap… as it has been given to me and as I have satisfied myself" — a manager's rhetorical rounding becomes a veterinarian's field constant three years later.**

**Conflicts about the borrowed glass of 1896**
22. **"Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes" (the test book's own first leaf, quoted identically three times) vs. "The glass I began on in ninety-six was borrowed off the Ashlin creamery" (Keddie's unsent letter, 29 years later).** Nym's office-file gloss backs Larrow Green. **NEW AND DECISIVE: Sill (r14) says he made the outfit up out of his own glass at his own bench at Larrow Green and sent it up the valley, and got it back the spring after with one piece chipped.** Keddie was contradicting his own first leaf from memory; the Ashlin office had no creamery at all, only a room over a harness shop.
23. **NEW — the significance nobody drew:** the 1896 season's 3.80 was read on glass that Sill personally used, knew and trusted, and that also produced Larrow Green's own 3.78. The two "borrowed glass" episodes (1896 and 1911) both produce 3.80; the twenty-six years in between, on the station's own glass, produce 3.61. Three natural experiments, not one.

**Conflicts about kinship and identity**
24. **Selby Vose: Gideon's nephew (Vira r01; Rundle r12) vs. Gideon's son (Loomis §4 and Vose's own "My father, who was your first president").** Vose's own testimony settles it; the valley (and its schoolteacher) had it as "nephew."
25. **Rosalie Cudd is "Jerome Cudd's niece" (Ivy r04) vs. the founder's daughter (Nym Item 13; Rundle r12; and Rosalie herself, r10: "My father, Jerome Cudd").** **RESOLVED: daughter. Ivy — who corresponded with her and quoted her figures — got her own informant's relationship wrong in the 1959 talk.**
26. **Vira identifies the 1958 visitor as "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed."** **Roy Brant and Rundle's 1959 footnote both say Ivy and Hazel are SECOND cousins** (their fathers, Duncan Keddie and Roy Brant, being first cousins). Vira has the degree wrong. That visitor is Ivy Keddie, who is a Keddie by name and a Brant by way of Orra (Emmet Brant's sister). Vira never realized the young woman was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter — she was telling the tester's granddaughter that the tester was a thief. Ivy, for her part, never mentions the visit in her 1959 talk.
27. **Vira says Ivy went and "poked about the old station building" in 1958 — and Ivy says she found the crate in the loft of that building, in February 1958. But Nym's Item 5 says "The crate was found in the Association office at Ashlin, in February 1958."** The curator has the wrong building; Ivy's own first-hand account (a loft over the intake room, reached by a ladder, under a tarpaulin at the back, corroborated by Strawn's 1928 grumble and by **Rundle's "a loft over it that I believe has never been cleared out since it was put up"**) is the reliable one.
28. **NEW — Hessel Bottom: six farms (Roy Brant r09; the 1908 report r15; Kepp r16) vs. seven (earlier notes from r02/r03).** Six is now attested by three independent sources including the contemporaneous report. Six bottom farms + Strawn's "three places on the hill" = the nine families of the 1919 circular.

**The curator's swapped attributions (r07, the largest single error in the file) — now independently confirmed**
29. **Item 3 is headed "Station test book, kept by E. Brant" — but its first leaf is signed "A. Keddie," it is in one hand for twenty-nine seasons, and Item 8 identifies Keddie's signed statement as being "in the hand of Item 3."** The station test book was kept by **A. Keddie**. **Frick's Exhibit 1: "The test book of the Ostrey Hollow station, 4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924, in the hand of Ansel Keddie."**
30. **Item 4 is headed "Farm weigh book, kept by A. Keddie" — but its provenance is Hessel Bottom, "kept there by one household throughout," passing to Roy Brant in 1929 and Hazel Brant in 1962, and Ivy calls it "the Brant weigh book."** The farm weigh book was kept by **E. Brant**. **Frick's Exhibit 3: "The private weigh book of Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom, begun 1 January 1897." And r09 is Emmet Brant's son describing his father keeping it.**
31. **So the curator has exactly transposed the two keepers' names** while insisting his transcriptions are "exact and checked twice" — and the transcriptions *are* exact; it is the headings, which are his own, that are wrong. His self-deprecating preface ("The glosses are mine and are worth what an afternoon is worth") turns out to be precisely calibrated. **Two independent 1946/1955 sources settle it beyond argument.**
32. **NEW — the weigh book's start date: 1 January 1897 (Nym Item 4; Frick Exhibit 3) vs. 1 January 1898 (Roy Brant, who has the book on the shelf behind his chair).** The son is a year out, or the two documents are.

**Conflicts in the scope of evidence**
33. **The Alder Corners cross-check: Ivy claims "fourteen seasons" of overlap showing ~0.19 higher; Rosalie Cudd's letter supplies SIXTEEN seasons (1915–1930) and says so twice; Nym's Item 13 says the Vessey County correspondence file holds figures "for the season of 1919" only.** Rosalie is the source and gives sixteen; Ivy, quoting her, said fourteen; the Rooms kept one. **So Ivy understated her strongest corroboration by two seasons, and the archive preserved one-sixteenth of it.** Note also that Ivy never saw the Alder Corners book herself, and that Rosalie offered to copy the four men out separately and apparently was never asked.
34. **NEW — the overlap is not really sixteen seasons of comparison anyway.** The four men hauled to Alder Corners *in the flush and to Ostrey Hollow the rest of the year*, so the same herds were being tested at both places in alternation, which is stronger than a mere overlap: the same cows' milk read 3.80 over the ridge in July and 3.61 at home in August.
35. **Strawn's circuit notebooks: Ivy says they were "kept by the state's dairy inspector for this county from 1908"; Strawn says he took the circuit in 1907 and his first Ostrey Hollow entry is 1907.**
36. **Strawn's books: "twenty-eight of them, one to the year," covering 1907–1936, which is thirty years.** Two years are missing from the sequence (his extracts skip 1908, 1910, 1914, 1918, 1927, 1929, 1930, 1932, 1933, 1935, but he says one book to the year).
37. **Strawn says the books went to the state dairy office in 1936; Nym and Ivy say they were at Farland college library from 1947.** Nym's Item 9 provenance supplies the missing link (state office → Farland, 1947); no conflict, but the path matters.
38. **NEW — the number of surviving pipettes: nine (Ivy's measurement sheet, Nym Item 5, Vaught §III) vs. ELEVEN (Rundle's 1959 footnote).** And **the size of the original lot: six dozen (the 1897 invoice, quoted verbatim by Nym; Ivy) vs. five dozen (Vaught §III).** Rundle was writing at second hand from "the college"; Vaught was writing *at* the college, from the invoice, and got it wrong anyway.

**Bulletin No. 471's internal collapse (r11) — a whole new category**
39. **The bulletin's own introduction contradicts the bulletin's own conclusion, on the same pages.** §III gives the mean delivery as **16.27 c.c.**; the measurement sheet, Nym, and Ivy all give **16.72**. The digits are transposed. Everything downstream in §V is therefore wrong: 0.9244 instead of 0.95; 3.905 instead of 3.80; a shortfall of 0.295 instead of 0.19; **118,000 lb instead of 76,000 lb.** §VII then reprints the closing paragraph, which says "**low by one part in twenty**" (= 0.95, = 16.72) and "**seventy-six thousand pounds**." One part in twenty and 0.9244 cannot both be true.
40. **Vaught noticed the contradiction and published anyway.** §V: "**It should be said that this figure is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the glassware.**" That single sentence is the tell: 3.61 ÷ 3.80 = 0.95 exactly, which is the ratio he should have had. He treats the 1924 figure as an awkward outside fact rather than as the check that would have caught his transposition in ten seconds.
41. **Vaught also mis-states the date the short glass went into use — "the first of May, 1898" — against the invoice, the office file, Nym, and his own concluding paragraph ("From the first of May, 1897").** And he says **five dozen** where the invoice says six.
42. **And he repeats Teague's denominator error** ("five and a quarter percent of the butterfat actually delivered"), which Ivy and Nym had both explicitly corrected.
43. **The irony is total: the document that proves the valley was ruined by an uncheckable measure is itself full of unchecked figures, in a section headed "Magnitude," by an author who writes in the same bulletin that "the error is invisible from inside the plant" and that any county worker could repeat the measurement in an afternoon.** His §II, §IV and §VII are the best analysis in the whole file; his §III and §V are worthless. Ivy's talk (r04) and the closing paragraph — the parts she wrote or co-wrote — are correct throughout.
44. **NEW — Vaught's "some six times the largest effect the trial could produce"** is right for his wrong 0.295 and wrong for the true 0.19 (Ivy: "four times too small"). Even his rhetoric inherited the transposition.

**The wet-hay theory's paper trail (r15, r16) — how a finding became a fact**
45. **Grigg's covering letter of 1919 contradicts the report printed immediately beneath it.** He says "**We sampled those herds through the whole of 1908 — every month of that year**, in the barn and on the pasture, out of the night's milk and the morning's"; the report says "**We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March.**" Eleven years turned three winter months into a full year — and the expansion is exactly what the theory needed, since a January–March sample cannot speak to the summer at all.
46. **Grigg's and Kepp's "two tenths in the winter feeding" is a fourfold misquotation of Bulletin No. 214, which says "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."** Both men drop the summer clause entirely. Grigg (1919) presents the misquotation as independent confirmation "arrived at by another road and by better men"; Kepp (1926) presents it as "the whole case." **Neither had read the bulletin; Ivy, Nym and Vaught all quote it accurately and identically.**
47. **Kepp's "three figures that agree so nearly" are 0.20 (his own field impression), 0.21 (Vose's inflated rise, "as it has been given to me"), and 0.20 (his misquotation of the College).** Not one of the three is independent evidence about Hessel Bottom, and two of them are artifacts of the short glass. This is the most sophisticated-sounding argument in the file and it is a closed loop.
48. **The 1908 committee's own report expressly found "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter," and asked for that finding "to be printed in the same type as the rest" — and the valley remembered the hay and forgot the exoneration.** Seventeen years later the theft theory was the settled opinion at the store.
49. **The 1908 committee did check the two things that mattered and recorded that they were sound: "We tried the station scales against a known weight and we tried the patrons' own scales against the station's," and "we stood by while the test was made and saw nothing whatever in the doing of it to remark upon."** They watched the short pipette being used and had no way to see it. Nothing in the routine of that era could catch it — which is Vaught's §II point demonstrated in the field seventeen years before he wrote it.
50. **Kepp is asked the right question from the floor in 1926 — "whether the trouble might lie in the station rather than the bottom" — and answers that "he had never in his life read a test and would not know how," then rules the possibility out anyway.** The single best question anyone asked in the whole fifty years was asked by an unnamed farm club member and dismissed by an expert on the ground of his own incompetence to judge it.
51. **Kepp's clinical observations of the bottom herds are probably true and are irrelevant.** Thin flanks, rough coats, late breeding and bad feet are real consequences of sour ground; none of them explains why the same cows' milk read 3.80 four miles over a ridge in the same week.

**Oddities that are not conflicts but are the heart of the thing**
52. **Loomis's crate observation:** the condemned crate "was never mentioned in the hearing at all… Not a word of it was spoken in any of the three sessions, by the patrons, by the officers, or by the board." The board's phrase "the books and appliances of the station" is "a form of words the board used and not a list of anything." The single decisive object was 21 months old and lying in the loft of the very building under examination — **and, if Frick is right, the board sat twice in that building, directly beneath it.**
53. **NEW AND WORSE — Frick's Exhibit 4 was "The circuit notebook of the state dairy inspector for the year 1923."** The board had in its hands the volume containing "Ran their milk pipettes against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot." Four books were before them; one of them said it outright; nobody read it aloud, and the chairman spent thirty-one years wondering "whether the answer was in that room."
54. **Everybody's blindness is documented in their own words.** Loomis: not one of the four officers ever stood in the station on a working morning. Keddie: neither patrons nor officers ever came up that road and watched. Vira: "nobody ever came up that road and looked." Teague: evenness "stops him looking." Strawn 1922: he skipped the burette check that year and wrote down that he had. **Rosalie Cudd: "I never once heard anybody go up there and look." Sill: "not one man in that office ever came down here to see how it was done."** Six independent statements of the same sentence.
55. **Strawn's four-step routine had no glass check in it until 1921**, and the burette program was **discontinued in 1926** — a five-year window in which the whole thing could be found, and it was found in year three of it, by a man who had already skipped it once.
56. **The 1896 figure of 3.80 sat on the summary leaf of Keddie's own book from 1922 onward, in the hearing exhibits, plainly stating that the station tested 3.80 on borrowed glass and 3.61 on its own.** Together with the nine weeks of 1911 and the 1924 rise, the book contained the answer three times over — and the book was Exhibit 1.
57. **The question mark.** Keddie let "?" stand in his book for fourteen years rather than write down a cause he did not know — the same discipline (record what you found, not what you thought) that Strawn preached, and the reason the evidence survived intact for Ivy. **Vaught's tribute: "recorded contemporaneously, in the operator's hand, by a man who did not know what he was recording and set a question mark against it."**
58. **The award's self-contradiction** — "finds no cause established" plus $3,800 — noted independently as unbearable by Loomis, by Keddie, and by Frick ("An award without a finding is an unsatisfactory thing and every member of that board knew it"), and defended as generosity by Vose.
59. **Keddie was dismissed on 30 April, four days before the board that found no cause against him published its award on 4 May** — **and, per Frick, while the board was still sitting.** "I have never been able to think well of the Association for it."
60. **The money never left the Association.** The 76,000 lb was received at Larrow Green, weighed into the vats, entered as station gain and carried to the general account — the patrons' unpaid fat was quietly financing the cooperative that the patrons owned. **Sill weighed it in twice a day, in his own hand, on every page of the receiving book from 1893, and thought it was just what cream does.**
61. **Ivy's two illustrative herds (4.56 and 3.42) are Vose's Jerseys and the Brants' Hessel Bottom milk** — the manager's own boast about his cows is, in her arithmetic, the proof that the error was proportional and that his best-in-the-valley herd would have been the worst-robbed had it delivered to the station.
62. **Vose's proof that "the Larrow Valley is not poor milk country" is exactly Ivy's proof that the station's glass was short** — his herd at 4.56 and Larrow Green running "a little under two tenths of a point better" than the station (Nym Item 16) both say the same milk read low only where the short pipettes were.
63. **Vose's charge and Strawn's record are flatly incompatible:** Vose says water was going into cans until the Association began to look; Strawn ran a lactometer at that station on every visit across nearly thirty years and never once found added water, told Vose so in 1919, and wrote "no added water, as always here" in the condemnation entry itself. **Rosalie Cudd adds the independent physical refutation: watered milk cannot be hidden from a cheesemaker, and in sixteen summers her father never docked one of those four men a cent. "Cheese is a witness."**
64. **The 1908 committee's wet-hay finding was based on January–March only** — winter, on dry/bog hay — and was then used for seventeen years (and, through Grigg and Kepp, for at least eighteen more) to explain a gap that stood equally in July.
65. **Strawn's warning about opinion outliving weighing** ("every time in my life I have seen a field man paid for an opinion instead, the opinion has outlived the weighing and done harm after the man was dead") is exactly what happened to Keddie — Vira's opinion outlived every weight in the book, and did harm after Keddie was dead. **Rundle printed the opinion in 1939 as the valley's settled verdict and could not get it back.**
66. **Nobody's motive was corrupt.** No milk watered, no cream stolen, no false entry, no dishonest official: a manufacturing defect in six dozen pieces of glass, a bookkeeping line that absorbed the discrepancy too neatly to alarm anyone, and a by-law clause ("the test of the station where he delivers shall govern") that made the station's reading unappealable.
67. **The remedy that would have worked was never proposed by anyone in the story.** Teague proposed "two men or a lock" (against theft); Loomis proposed nothing; Vose proposed washing cans; **Grigg and Kepp proposed drainage, lime and tile.** The thing that actually solved it was one instrument — a graduated burette — and a person willing to spend an afternoon on nine pieces of glass. **Vaught: "There is no reason why every station in this state should not be checked once in this way, and several reasons in this history why it should be."**
68. **NEW — Rundle, a schoolteacher with no special competence, got there in 1939 and nobody noticed.** He saw one ledger entry by accident, understood in one sentence that stolen cream arrives light and this cream arrived heavy, retracted his own chapter at the back of his own pamphlet, and left it there for twenty years. The pamphlet was reprinted in 1959 — after Bulletin 471 — with a footnote about Ivy and the pipettes, and its author "too old to rewrite a pamphlet."
69. **NEW — Three men held decisive facts and each thought his fact was ordinary.** Sill knew the cream ran heavy every year ("So does everybody's… nobody ever asked me to") and that the 1896 glass was his own. Roy Brant knew the gap was identical in July and February ("Nobody would take account of that, then or since, though it was in front of them"). Rosalie Cudd knew the same milk ran 3.80 over the ridge ("I do not know what your station made of the same cows"). None of the three was ever asked until 1958, and two of them were never asked at all — Sill dictated his to a listener in 1934 who wanted the quarrel, and Roy Brant wrote his for a farm bureau local-history file in 1955.
70. **NEW — the family arithmetic of the injury.** Ansel Keddie married Orra Brant; Orra's brother Emmet was the station's largest patron; so the tester whose glass took a twentieth off every test was, by marriage, taking it hardest out of his own wife's brother — "the family that had more to lose by that station than any other family on the meadow, and I have never known what to do with that fact except set it down." The 1919 circular broke that marriage-tie permanently, not over the water charge but because Keddie did not stand up in front of it; and the granddaughter of one and the great-niece of the other put the case together in 1958 with the second cousin's book on her desk.
71. **NEW — the rupture's stated cause differs by side.** Vira thought the letter itself did it ("it was somebody's cousin saying it"). Keddie could not explain it ("the plainest fact in this letter and… the one I find I have the least to say about"). Roy Brant knows exactly: "not from any quarrel over the water, but because my father held that the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not."
72. **NEW — Keddie did privately blame the patrons** (Frick's corridor note), which he never wrote in the letter, never said in the room, and which — since no patron watered anything — was as wrong as everything else anybody believed. The one man who kept his opinions out of his book had an opinion, and it was mistaken.
73. **NEW — the four Hessel Bottom men who hauled over the ridge were "talked about here as though they had gone over to the enemy," and Roy Brant, the biggest herd on the meadow, stayed home out of pride and lost money by it** — "the biggest herd is the one that cannot afford to be talked about, and I have thought since that this was a poor reason and cost us money." The four defectors were the only patrons in the valley who were, half the year, being paid honestly.
74. **NEW — the ridge road's closure is why the fraud theory could never be tested locally: "no man from your valley ever hauled to me in winter, whatever the price was. It was not possible."** The control group existed only in the flush, which is exactly the season Kepp dismissed as proving nothing.
75. **NEW — Jerome Cudd wrote "Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge" in his own book in 1919, "where nobody would ever read it, which is where he generally put his opinions."** The rebuttal to the wet-hay theory was written down the same year the water circular went out, four miles away, and stayed unread for thirty-nine years.
76. **NEW — the pattern of the whole file: every honest observer recorded the anomaly accurately and drew the wrong conclusion or none, and every confident explainer misquoted his evidence.** Keddie, Strawn, Emmet Brant, Roy Brant, Sill, Jerome Cudd and Rundle all wrote down true things they could not interpret; Vose, Teague, Grigg, Kepp and Vira all interpreted freely on figures they had wrong. Vaught, uniquely, did both at once.
