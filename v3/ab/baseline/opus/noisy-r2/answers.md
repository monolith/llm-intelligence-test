### 1. The Rule That Turned a Reading Into Money (1889–1896)

**Who:** the farmers of the Larrow Valley; Gideon Vose, first president; Effie Loomis, secretary from 1901; Hobart Sill, buttermaker from 1893; Ansel Keddie, tester.

In 1889 farmers of the Larrow Valley met in the schoolhouse at Larrow Green — in cold weather, very late, and somebody's horse got loose — and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking what the merchant allowed. Gideon Vose, a heavy obstinate man who had once thought of the law, was elected president and held it twenty-two years, to 1911; he drafted the by-laws himself at his own kitchen table. In 1891 they built the creamery on Larrow Creek below the mill, set up the churn, and the valley began to be paid in cash once a month.

The consequential act was Article VII: *every patron shall be paid… according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in [his milk], ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.* The last clause is the hinge. It made one station's reading unappealable — no re-test at the creamery, no average of the valley — and it left a dissatisfied man, as the arbitration chairman put it, no remedy on earth except to quarrel with a figure. The year of adoption cannot be fixed: the printed copies carry no date, only "adopted at the annual meeting"; the secretary's minutes give 1892, the jubilee pamphlet 1891, the 1908 committee's chairman 1893; and the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move.

Ansel Keddie went up to Ostrey Hollow in 1895 to make the station ready. It opened 4 May 1896, seven miles up the valley: milk weighed in, sampled, tested, separated; cream down to Larrow Green daily except Sunday, skim back to the farmer. No testing outfit had arrived, so Sill made one up out of his own glass off the Larrow Green bench and sent it up. On that borrowed, known-good glass the station's first season averaged **3.80** — the valley's true figure, and the last honest average it would record for twenty-six years.

**Documents:** Article VII (quoted identically by Loomis, Nym, Rundle); test book first leaf, "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie"; Sill's dictated remarks; Rundle's pamphlet; Falke's county report; Loomis's notes.

### 2. Six Dozen Pipettes From Tarnet (1897–1923)

**Who:** Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet; the Association; Ansel Keddie; Ivy Keddie, who measured them sixty-one years later.

On **14 April 1897** the firm invoiced the Association, for the Ostrey Hollow station: 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20; 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at .22 — 31.68; 1 acid measure — .90; 1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00; freight — 2.15; **total 77.93**. The crate came up the valley in the spring; Sill's glass went back with one piece chipped; and on **1 May 1897** the station's own outfit went into use. Orra Keddie wrote it in her diary that day: "A. says the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green. He is pleased about it."

The pipettes were short. Blown on the stem at 17.6 c.c., they delivered a mean of **16.72** — 0.88 c.c. short, exactly 95 hundredths of the mark, one part in twenty. A butterfat test is read *directly*: the operator weighs nothing, he reads a column of fat in a neck that the maker ruled for one charge and no other. Put in 95 per cent of the charge and every reading is 95 per cent of the truth, forever, in January and July, for a careful operator and a careless one. From 1898 the station's recorded average stood at **3.61** and stayed within three hundredths of it for twenty-five years.

The firm's own historian, writing in 1949, reconstructed the cause honestly and without records: the mold had been reground and the lot ran short — an accident nobody caught, because a boy checks the first, the tenth and the fiftieth and finds them consistent, "which is the one fault a check of that kind cannot find." The works books that would have proved it burned in 1931. There is no evidence the house knew; a county newspaper's 1958 charge that a cheap short lot was knowingly put off on the Association is editorial invention.

**Documents:** the invoice (quoted verbatim by the curator and by the firm's own history); Orra Keddie's diary, 1 May 1897; the 1922 summary leaf; Ivy Keddie's measurement sheet of 9 March 1958 (nine pipettes, five runs each, 16.70–16.74, mean 16.72); Vaught's exposition of the instrument in Bulletin 471 §II.

### 3. "Station Gain": The Missing Fat, Entered in the Association's Own Books (1897–1948)

**Who:** Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945; Bertram Kyle, outer-desk clerk; Hobart Sill at the churn; Dell Rundle, schoolteacher.

Every year the Ostrey Hollow station account carried three figures and a sentence. Fat credited to the patrons on the station's test; fat actually weighed into the vats at Larrow Green in the station's cream; and the difference, entered as *station gain*, "five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account." The 1898 entry: credited 57,760 lb; received 60,800 lb; gain 3,040 lb.

The second figure was always the larger, because the fat was there — the glass only misread it. The patrons' unpaid butterfat went down the hill in the cream and financed the cooperative the patrons owned. Nothing was hidden. As the clerk who copied the line for twenty-five years put it: it had a name, and a figure with a name on it has been explained, so you rule under it and go on. Teague said the same from the other side: a figure that jumps about keeps you awake; a figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture.

Sill knew it physically. He weighed the cream twice — once on the platform, once into the cold room — and wrote it in the receiving book in his own hand from 1893: "There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there. Always more. Never less." He made nothing of it: cream is a thick thing that clings, everybody's runs heavy, and nobody ever asked him.

The one man who drew the inference was a schoolteacher. In 1939, with his jubilee pamphlet already in type, Rundle saw the 1898 ledger entry on another errand and withdrew his own chapter at the back of his own book: *cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand.* Nobody noticed for nineteen years.

**Documents:** the annual ledgers, station account (curator's Item 6); Teague's memoir, ch. IX; Kyle's "What I saw of the office"; Sill's remarks; Rundle's note added while in press; the Larrow Green receiving and churn books.

### 4. The Wet Hay of Hessel Bottom (1902–1926)

**Who:** the six farms of Hessel Bottom, Emmet Brant chief among them; Nyle Grigg's committee of three; Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S., of Ashlin.

From 1902 the men of Ostrey Hollow said out loud that their milk tested under everyone else's. They were laughed at; Emmet Brant was among the first. By the winter of 1908 the board could not let it alone without seeming to have an interest in doing so, and named three men under Grigg — a Larrow Green farmer who was not a patron of the station and was chosen for that reason.

The committee visited each of the six farms, drew and sealed its own samples, tried the station scales against a known weight and the patrons' scales against the station's, and stood by while tests were made. It found "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter," and asked that the finding be printed in the same type as the rest. Then it blamed the ground: Hessel Bottom is a springy meadow of sedge and rush, cut late, fed as the winter dependence; what such land gives in quantity it withholds in quality; the remedy lies with the land and not with the station. **It sampled January, February and March only** — three winter months, used thereafter to explain a gap that stood equally in July.

The theory hardened into fact by misquotation. The College's Bulletin No. 214 (1911) found bog hay depressed the test "by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Reprinting his report in 1919, Grigg cited it as "two tenths in the winter feeding" — a fourfold inflation with the summer clause dropped — and offered it as independent confirmation; in the same letter he remembered his three months as "the whole of 1908, every month of that year." Kepp repeated the same misquotation in 1926 as "the whole case," laying it beside his own field impression of a fifth of a point and the manager's inflated 0.21, three numbers agreeing beautifully, two of them artifacts of the short glass. Asked from the floor whether the trouble might lie in the station, he replied that he had never in his life read a test and would not know how — and ruled it out anyway.

**Documents:** the 1908 report, full text, with Grigg's 1919 covering letter; Bulletin No. 214; Kepp's paper and the appended discussion; Orra Keddie's diary of January and March 1908 ("They wanted to see the barns and not the station"); Falke's 1959 report and its withdrawn concession.

### 5. The Nine Weeks of 1911 — An Experiment That Ran Itself

**Who:** Ansel Keddie; every patron of the station; Merle Strawn, who was there and saw nothing.

On **8 May 1911** the station's own pipette broke. Keddie entered it: *"May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure."* Orra Keddie's diary for the same day: "A. home cross about a broken measure." For nine weeks the station tested on other glass, and the average went to **3.80**. On **10 July** he wrote: *"Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."* The figures fell to 3.61 the same day. He let the question mark stand for fourteen years rather than write down a cause he could not prove.

Everything else was held constant: the same patrons, the same herds, the same pasture, the same season, the same acid, the same bottles, the same hand tester, the same man turning the crank at the speed he was taught. **One object changed.** That is what a calculation cannot give you. It disposes at a stroke of every explanation that looks to the ground, the feed, the cows or the men's honesty, because none of those was altered in those nine weeks and the figures were.

It does not establish everything. It does not fix the magnitude — a nine-week flush average is not a year, and the interval was later used, by a veterinarian who wanted it dismissed, precisely as "six weeks of June and July, when every herd in the county is at its best," an argument that dies on the station's other twenty-five Junes and Julies, which read 3.61. It does not prove the borrowed measure was *accurate*, only that it was different. And it does not name the mechanism: that the fault was short delivery, in the exact ratio 0.95, took a burette and an afternoon in 1958.

Its provenance is disputed. The measure came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon (Ivy Keddie); came "up from town" (Teague, of the replacement); or was the state inspector's own, left that June (Roy Brant). Strawn's own 1911 entry says he visited in June, watched a test through, and wrote **"Nothing here to note."** The duration is settled at nine weeks by Keddie's two dated entries; Teague and Kepp both say six, from memory.

Duncan Keddie adds the private half of it: his father came home that summer saying it was something in the glass, said it twice, at supper and again at the door, and would not put it in the book.

**Documents:** the two 1911 entries and the 1922 summary leaf; Orra Keddie's diary; Strawn's circuit book for 1911; Bulletin 471 §IV; Roy Brant's account; Kepp's rebuttal; Duncan Keddie's family notes.

### 6. Over Redlow Ridge: The Control Group Nobody Knew Was One (1913–1958)

**Who:** Jerome Cudd of Alder Corners, Vessey County; his daughter Rosalie Cudd; four Hessel Bottom men.

Jerome Cudd built the Alder Corners Cheese Company in 1913 on the flat below the church, for the spring water on the lot and the hard road east; the valley remembered it as opening in 1914. From **1915**, four of the six Hessel Bottom men hauled to him in the summer flush — about 1 June to mid-September — and delivered to Ostrey Hollow the rest of the year. Sixteen seasons, the last in **1930**. They were talked about at home as though they had gone over to the enemy. Roy Brant, who had the biggest herd on the meadow, stayed home out of pride and said afterward it was a poor reason and cost him money.

The ridge road is four miles of switchback cut for lumber: it shuts in November and does not open before April, some years May, so no man from Ordell ever hauled over it in winter, whatever the price. The comparison therefore existed only in the flush — the very season the wet-hay men dismissed as proving nothing.

Cudd tested himself, on his own bench, with his own glass bought new, and wrote his own book. He never compared a figure of his with anybody else's; it never entered his head. In 1919 he set down the four men's summer averages — 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82 — and added, "Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge," in a book where nobody would ever read it. Rosalie Cudd copied out sixteen season averages for Ivy Keddie in 1958: 1915–1930, never below 3.78, never above 3.82, wet years and dry, "whether the meadow had been under water in May or hard as a road." The series is **flat across 1923 and 1924**, the exact years the Ostrey Hollow figure rises by nineteen hundredths — because the Alder Corners glass was never short.

She also killed the water charge physically: watered milk cannot be hidden from a cheesemaker, because the curd comes soft and will not knit and the yield is down the next morning. "Butter is more forgiving that way. Cheese is a witness." In sixteen summers her father never docked one of those four men a cent.

**Documents:** Rosalie Cudd's letter of 1958 with the sixteen-season table; Jerome Cudd's book, 1919; the Vessey County correspondence file; Rundle ch. V; Roy Brant.

### 7. The Circular of 1919, and the Two Kitchens That Stopped Being One

**Who:** Selby Vose, manager; Ansel and Orra Keddie; Emmet Brant; the patrons at the head of the valley.

In June 1919 the manager sent round a printed circular over his own signature warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons that added water would be looked for and dealt with. He had it typed in the office by a clerk who thought at the time it was a hard thing to send to men who brought their milk in every morning of their lives, and typed it anyway. Vose defended every word of it in 1926: it named nobody, and a manager who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing is "being idle at their expense."

It was false. The state's inspector had run a lactometer at that station on every visit for twelve years, had never found added water, and told the manager so that year; his condemnation entry of 1923 still reads "no added water, as always here."

Ansel Keddie carried the papers himself. He would not put them in the mail nor leave them on the platform: he took them round to the households, stood at each door while the man read it, and came home at eleven at night with his hat still on. Two days later Emmet Brant — Orra Keddie's brother, the station's largest patron, at that table on Sundays for thirty years — stood in the yard, would not come in, said he had hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and asked why Ansel had carried the paper himself if he did not believe it. Ansel said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet went home and never entered that house again; the two men did not speak until Emmet died in the spring of 1929, and Orra sat an hour in the kitchen where she had been a girl and came home.

The circular went to nine patron households by the recollection of four independent witnesses and to **eleven families** by the contemporaneous count of the woman whose husband delivered them — reconcilable if some of the nine farms held more than one household, which the valley itself says was usual. Its real cost was neither: one woman burned hers in the stove, another kept hers pinned inside a trunk lid for forty years, and a schoolteacher watched the estrangement come down to the children of both ends of the valley "entire, without a word of it being explained, the way a debt does."

**Documents:** Orra Keddie's diary, June 1919; Kyle, who typed it; Vose's 1926 address; Strawn's 1919 entry; Vira Toland; Roy Brant; Hazel Brant; Sill; Rundle.

### 8. The Burette, the Four Books, and the Crate Over Their Heads (1921–1979)

**Who:** Merle Strawn; Alonzo Frick's board; Ansel Keddie; Ivy Keddie.

The state began issuing burettes to its field men in **1921**. Strawn carried his two seasons; in 1922 he did not run the station's glass against it, "the day being short and the road home being what it was," and wrote that down because it was what happened. On **12 June 1923** he did: *"Ran their milk pipettes against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new."* The order went to Tarnet on 28 June; the firm answered 2 July, entered it, and said its books of the nineties would tell nobody anything. New glass was in use in July; in October Strawn ran it against the burette and it delivered its mark. The condemned crate went up a ladder into the loft over the intake room. In **1924** the station averaged **3.80** and stayed there. Nobody connected the one thing with the other, and Strawn drew no inference about the twenty-six years behind it — a field man keeps a book, not an estimation.

In January 1925 the patrons petitioned, reckoning their loss at 67,000 lb of butterfat. A board of three sat under Frick, attorney of Ashlin, from 17 March to 4 May: the first session at the Ashlin office, the second and third in the station's own weigh room, at the chairman's request, with men standing outside on the platform in the rain. Three explanations were urged — water by the manager, theft by the bookkeeper, hay out of the 1908 report — and none was proved. Four books were before the board: the test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book, and **the state inspector's circuit notebook for 1923**, which said it outright. Nobody laid the second beside the fourth. The condemned crate was twenty feet up a ladder in the room they were sitting in, and was never mentioned in any of the three sessions.

On **30 April** the Association put Keddie out by letter, four days before the award and while the board still sat. On 4 May the award found "no cause established" and gave the patrons **$3,800 without admission**, charged to the station account. He kept four or five cows, hung doors well, and died in February 1934; more came to the burying than his widow looked for and every one of them said what a hand he was with a door, and not one said the other thing. "A man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."

In February 1958 his granddaughter climbed that ladder, brought down the crate from under its tarpaulin and an inch of chaff, and on 9 March measured nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette. Bulletin No. 471 followed in October 1958: no milk watered, no cream stolen, the tests low by one part in twenty for twenty-six years, and seventy-six thousand pounds of butterfat standing credited in the Association's own ledgers as station gain.

**Documents:** Strawn's circuit books 1921–1924; Frick's exhibit list and recollections; Loomis on the crate and the award; Keddie's nine-sentence statement; Kyle; Oram; Orra Keddie's diary, April 1925 and February 1934; Ivy Keddie's measurement sheet and talk; Bulletin 471; Falke's county report; the Ashlin Chronicle.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** The line is: Ansel Keddie (b. 1861, d. Feb 1934) married Orra Brant in 1889; their son Duncan was born in 1894 and taught school at Ashlin; Duncan's daughter is Ivy, born 1931. Duncan states all of this himself in family notes written expressly because he had "already twice seen it printed otherwise." It is corroborated by Roy Brant ("Duncan's girl Ivy"), by Ivy's own talk, and by the diary (Duncan five years old in 1899, seventeen and teaching at Ashlin in 1911). Two sources say "great-niece": the *Ashlin Chronicle*, assembled in a day and a half from a college press release and two telephone calls, and paragraph 2 of the county agent's 1959 report — and the county agent, having written to check, appended a correction to his own report: "Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." A narrator who verifies and retracts outranks one who does not; a participant's own family record outranks both.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins.** Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie were brother and sister; their children, Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie, are therefore first cousins; the children of first cousins — Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy — are second cousins. Duncan, Hazel and Roy all say so independently, and Rundle's 1959 footnote repeats it. Against this stand Vira Toland and Cleve Oram, who both say "first cousin once removed"; that is valley talk, it is the commonest way of getting a second-cousinship wrong, and Hazel complains that people tell her the same thing to her face. What the sources leave open about the intervening generation: whether Orra and Emmet were *full* siblings (Roy Brant insists they were, "whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it"; Duncan calls her Emmet's sister and the younger of the two; Hazel calls her a half sister by Emmet's father's second wife) — the degree of cousinship is the same either way; and Orra's birth year, which the diary's arithmetic fixes at 1866 while Duncan's list says 1868, though his own statement that she died at eighty-two in 1948 gives 1866. Nothing in Orra's hand survives from before her marriage, so the Brant parentage cannot be checked against any document.

**B3. Selby Vose was Gideon Vose's son.** Vose says it from the platform in 1926 — "My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law under which every patron in this Association is paid on the test of the station where he delivers" — and Loomis, Duncan Keddie and the office clerk all say son; only Vira Toland and Rundle say nephew, and Rundle was writing down what the valley remembered. It matters three ways. Gideon wrote Article VII, whose last clause made the station's test unappealable; so the manager who defended the station's figures for twenty-five years and signed the annual station sheet was the author's son, defending his father's rule as well as his own management. He kept his father's Jersey herd, and boasted in 1926 that it tested 4.56 in 1910 to prove the valley was not poor milk country — the same figure Ivy Keddie later used to show the error was proportional, since a 4.56 herd was robbed of 0.24 of a point where a 3.42 herd lost 0.18. And his defence of the 1919 circular was, in effect, a family defence of the whole arrangement.

**B4. No relationship between the Voses and the Keddies is established, and probably there was none.** Two narrators assert one: Vira Toland says old Mrs. Vose was first cousin to Orra Keddie — "everybody knew that and nobody wrote it down anywhere" — and Cleve Oram says the families "were cousins of some sort… I could not draw you the line on paper." Both describe unwritten talk and neither can trace it. Against them is Duncan Keddie, Orra's son, writing specifically to settle such questions: there was no connection whatever, people confuse them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Gideon either, and his mother — a woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection — never once put a Vose in it. A first-hand denial by the son of the woman in question, who states his reasons, outranks two admittedly untraceable rumours. I record that the rumour mattered even though it was false: Vira's sense that the 1919 circular was "somebody's cousin saying it" is a real fact about how the letter was received, whatever the genealogy.

## Section C — Math

### C1.

**(a) The tests were 0.95 of the truth — short by one part in twenty (5 %).**
The pipettes were blown on the stem at **17.6 c.c.**, the standard charge in this state, and the graduated neck of a test bottle is ruled to read per cent for that charge *and no other*. Nine survivors were run against a certified burette, five deliveries each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 — **mean 16.72 c.c.**
17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88 c.c. short**; 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95 exactly**. A short charge into a neck ruled for a full one reads low in precisely that proportion, so every test was 95 % of the truth.

**(b) True average 3.80; recorded tests short by 0.19 of a point.**
3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19**.
Two independent checks: the station read 3.80 in 1896 on Sill's borrowed glass and 3.80 again in 1924 on new glass; and Larrow Green's own average over the same years was 3.78–3.80.

**(c) 76,000 lb of butterfat.**
Full delivery years 1898–1922 = 25 years; intake 40,000,000 lb of milk (1,600,000 lb/yr).
Credited: 40,000,000 × 3.61 % = **1,444,000 lb**.
Delivered: 40,000,000 × 3.80 % = **1,520,000 lb**.
Difference = **76,000 lb**.

**(d) $19,000, of which the patrons received one fifth.**
76,000 lb × $0.25 = **$19,000**. The 1925 award was **$3,800**, and 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **0.20 — exactly one fifth**. (At Sill's and Kepp's remembered 27 cents it would be $20,520; I use 25 cents, which is the figure taken off Teague's annual sheets by the secretary and found independently for the arbitration by the clerk, and which Roy Brant confirms from the weigh book.)

### C2. Hessel Bottom (Emmet Brant), 1910 — 200,000 lb even, paid on a test of 3.42.

**(a)** 200,000 × 3.42 % = **6,840 lb credited**.
**(b)** True test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = **3.60** exactly. 200,000 × 3.60 % = 7,200 lb owed. 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb not credited**. (Equivalently 6,840 × 5/95 = 360; the shortfall of 0.18 of a point is the figure Ivy Keddie cites for a 3.42 herd.)
**(c)** Butterfat was **24 cents** in 1910 (Roy Brant, from the weigh book, for that specific year — not the 25-cent long-run average): 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40** in that one year, off the largest patron on the meadow.

### C3. Over 1898–1922.

**(a)** Credited to the patrons on the station's test: **1,444,000 lb**.
**(b)** Actually weighed into the vats at Larrow Green in the station's cream: **1,520,000 lb**.
**(c)** Difference **76,000 lb**, and the denominator must be stated:
- as a percentage of the fat **delivered** (1,520,000): 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **exactly 5.00 %** — and this is the figure that is also the glass, one part in twenty;
- as a percentage of the fat **credited** (1,444,000): 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.263 %**, i.e. the ledger's "five and a quarter per cent, as usual."
Neither is an error; each is right against its own denominator and no other. The ledger uses the second because a bookkeeper reckons a surplus against the figure he has already entered. Teague himself misstated it — "the gain ran at five and a quarter per cent of the fat that was actually in the cream" — and Professor Vaught repeated the identical error in Bulletin 471 §V.

### C4.

**(a) $77.93.** The column proves it: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93. Both the *Ashlin Chronicle* and the supply firm's own historian render it in words as "seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents" — a digit transposition, and in both cases the correct itemized invoice is printed a few lines above the misreading.
**(b) 72 pipettes** (6 doz. at .35 = 25.20, which is itself a check: 72 × 0.35 = 25.20). Each was short **0.88 c.c.** on the measured mean; the nine survivors individually ran 16.70–16.74, i.e. short by 0.86 to 0.90 c.c. ("Five dozen" appears in Bulletin 471 §III and in Cleve Oram's memory; the invoice, quoted verbatim by the curator and reproduced in facsimile by the firm that issued it, says six.)

### C5.

**(a) In use 26 years and about 6 weeks; the money runs over 25 full delivery years.**
Into use **1 May 1897** (invoice 14 April 1897; office file; Orra Keddie's diary of that day). Condemned **12 June 1923**. That is 26 years, 1 month, 11 days — and it is the span Bulletin 471's concluding paragraph gives: "From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923."
The money calculation runs **1898–1922, 25 years**, because those are the *full* delivery years: 1897 is a part year on the station's own summary leaf, the glass having gone in on 1 May, and 1923 was broken in June by the condemnation. The 40,000,000 lb intake figure is the ledger total for exactly that span. The two numbers differ because one measures how long the fault existed and the other measures how many complete years can be priced.

**(b) Cannot be determined from the sources.** The station's intake for its first season (4 May to the end of 1896) is in no ledger: the station account column begins with 1897, and that first season's milk was carried on the creamery's own books, whose sheets were never put with the station papers. Two men supplied a figure from memory and they disagree — the buttermaker says "about nine hundred thousand pounds," the office clerk "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds, which is what I understood at the time from men who were there." Both are hearsay of a figure neither ever saw written; there is no third source and no arithmetic that constrains it. What the sources *do* establish is that the season ran from 4 May, was therefore not a whole one, and was tested on borrowed Larrow Green glass at an average of 3.80 — so whatever the intake was, it lies outside the affected years and does not enter the loss.

## Section D — Logic

### D1. The 1911 interval.

**8 May to 10 July 1911 — nine weeks.** Fixed by the tester's own two dated entries ("May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." / "July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.") and by his wife's diary for 8 May. Teague and Kepp both say six weeks, from memory and decades later; two dated contemporaneous entries outrank two recollections, and Kepp's "six" is doing rhetorical work in his argument.

**The variable** was one object: the milk pipette. **Held constant:** the same patrons, the same herds, the same ground and pasture, the same season, the same acid, the same test bottles, the same hand tester, the same operator with the same habits, and no change in supervision. **Result:** the station average rose from 3.61 to 3.80, held for nine weeks, and returned to 3.61 on the day the station's own measure came back.

**What it establishes that a calculation cannot:** causation, by intervention rather than by inference. Every explanation resting on the cows, the feed, the wet ground, the men's honesty or the office's arithmetic requires something about the herds or the men to have changed; nothing did, and the figures moved anyway, twice, on two known dates. It is a controlled trial that ran itself under conditions no designed experiment could have bettered — recorded, as Vaught put it, by a man who did not know what he was recording and set a question mark against it.

**What it does not establish:** (i) the magnitude — a nine-week flush average is not an annual one, and the ratio 0.95 comes from the burette, not from 1911; (ii) that the borrowed measure was *correct*, only that it differed from the station's; (iii) which measure was borrowed or from where — three incompatible accounts survive (up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon; up from town; left by the state inspector himself that June), and the inspector's own book for June 1911 says "Nothing here to note"; (iv) the mechanism — that the fault was short *delivery* into a neck ruled for a fixed charge; and (v) anything at all about the twenty-six years, except by the assumption that the station's own glass behaved in 1911 as it behaved before and after.

### D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream.

**Reason 1 — the cream arrived heavy, and was weighed twice at the far end.** The buttermaker at Larrow Green took the cream off the wagon and weighed it again himself into the cold room, in his own hand, on every page of the receiving book from 1893: "There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there. Always more. Never less." Stolen cream arrives light.

**Reason 2 — the surplus is entered in the Association's own ledgers, year by year.** The station-gain line sets the fat credited to patrons against the fat received at Larrow Green and carries the difference to the general account: 57,760 / 60,800 / gain 3,040 lb in 1898, and so on for a quarter of a century. The missing fat is not missing; it is accounted for, in ink, on an open shelf.

**Reason 3 — the nine weeks of 1911.** The same man, in the same building, with no additional supervision, produced tests 0.19 higher for nine weeks and lower again from a fixed date. A thief does not become honest on 8 May and relapse on 10 July.

**Reason 4 (a fourth, independent) — the milk read 3.80 over the ridge.** The same four men's milk, off the same wet ground, averaged 3.78–3.82 at Alder Corners for sixteen summers while reading 3.61 at home, tested by a man who never compared his figures with anyone's and did not know there was a quarrel.

**Why these are independent:** they come from four different people who did not communicate, in four different places (the Larrow Green churn room, the Ashlin ledger desk, the station's own test book, a cheese factory in another county), and they are four different *kinds* of evidence — a physical weighing, a bookkeeping identity, a time series with a dated intervention, and an outside control. Three of the four were in the hands of men who believed the tester was guilty or believed nothing at all.

**Which survive without the 1958 measurement:** Reasons 1, 2 and 3 all do, and 4 as well. Reason 1 with Reason 2 is decisive by itself and required no science: it is the inference the schoolteacher Rundle actually drew in 1939, from a single ledger entry seen by accident, nineteen years before anyone measured a pipette.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question the entries that refute his own accusation?** No. He devised the station-gain line himself in 1905, wrote it every year, watched it come in at five and a quarter "as usual," and said plainly what evenness does: "A figure that jumps about will keep you awake. A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture." He raised the station account with the manager once, in 1916, was told to let it alone, and let it alone — "I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience." Then in 1925 he put the theft theory to the board in the way an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it. He never questioned the entries; he misstated their denominator; and his own line refuted him for twenty years while he copied it.

### D3. Did the manager know, before 1923, that the station's tests ran low?

**Yes — that the tests ran low is established beyond argument, from his own mouth.** In his 1926 address he says: "For a generation the milk brought to that station tested below the milk brought to Larrow Green, and every year we were told it was the wet hay, or the tester, or the office, or the weather." He circulated the 1919 circular precisely because of it, and defended it on the ground that "a manager who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing to the men who make them… is being idle at their expense." He had been manager from 1913, and the bookkeeper put the station account to him directly in **1916** and was told to let it alone — so he was told, once, in terms, seven years before the condemnation.

**Whether he knew the further thing — that more fat was arriving at Larrow Green than the station had paid for — is not established, and the evidence points against it.** The bookkeeper's claim is inferential and he says so: "He signed the sheet every year, and a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him. What he made of it he never told me." The clerk who sat outside his door for twenty-five years and watched him do it a thousand times says what he actually did with a sheet: "He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign. He read the totals and he never read the lines above them." That is first-hand observation of the specific act against an inference from the act, and it wins.

The honest answer is therefore in two parts: he knew the tests ran low, he had been told once that the station account was odd, and he chose an explanation that blamed the patrons and required no inquiry; but there is no evidence he ever understood that the Association was banking the difference, and good evidence he never read the line that said so.

## Section E — Contradictions

### E1(i). Direct contradictions and their resolution

**Method used throughout, in order of force:** a document quoted verbatim and identically by two or more parties beats a paraphrase; a contemporaneous entry beats a later recollection; arithmetic that closes beats a figure that does not; a participant's own account of his own act beats another man's inference about it; a narrator who checks and retracts beats one who does not; and *independence* is counted, not numbers.

1. **The mean delivery of the pipettes: 16.72 (measurement sheet, curator, Ivy Keddie) vs 16.27 (Bulletin 471 §III, and the *Chronicle* repeating it).** Resolved to 16.72: the sheet lists nine individual runs whose mean is 16.72 and which cluster within 0.04 c.c.; and 3.61 ÷ 3.80 = 0.95 = 16.72/17.6, whereas 16.27/17.6 = 0.9244 predicts a true test of 3.905 that nothing supports. Digit transposition.
2. **Loss: 76,000 lb (computed; Bulletin's own concluding paragraph) vs 118,000 lb (Bulletin §V) vs 67,000 lb (the patrons' petition, repeated by Frick and by Oram).** 76,000 is right; 118,000 is the transposition carried downstream; 67,000 was the patrons' own reckoning, never computed, and Frick notes only that nothing was brought forward to disturb it.
3. **Money: $19,000 (76,000 × 25¢) vs $19,600 (the petition "set out to the dollar," Rundle; repeated by the *Chronicle*).** $19,600 ÷ 67,000 lb ≈ 29¼ cents, a price nobody quotes; arithmetic settles it.
4. **The award: $3,800 vs $3,500.** $3,800 — the award is quoted verbatim and identically by the secretary who wrote it and by the curator, paraphrased by the chairman who made it, and confirmed by the manager, the clerk and the county agent.
5. **Date of condemnation: 12 June 1923 vs 12 July 1923.** June — the inspector's own field book, quoted verbatim twice, plus the order of 28 June and the reply of 2 July, which cannot postdate a July condemnation.
6. **The 1924 average: 3.80 vs 3.90 (Vose) vs 3.85 "from 1924" (Falke).** 3.80 — the test book, confirmed by the inspector's 1924 entry, "Their book gives the year at 3.80," and required by 3.61 ÷ 0.95.
7. **The rise: 0.19 vs "twenty-one hundredths" (Vose, adopted by Kepp).** 0.19; Vose inflated both level and jump.
8. **The gap: 0.17 (Roy Brant, from the only book that sets both figures side by side) vs 0.19 (computed, and Nym's "a little under two tenths") vs 0.20/0.21 (Kepp, Vose) vs 0.24 (Sill's 3.85 − 3.61) vs 0.295 (Vaught).** The documentary figures cluster at 0.17–0.19; 0.19 is the one the glass predicts and the one Hazel Brant and Falke give flatly. Roy Brant's 0.17 is a real measurement against a slightly different comparator and I do not treat it as an error.
9. **Milk intake: 40,000,000 lb vs 44,000,000 lb.** 40,000,000 — the secretary added the columns twice off Teague's own annual sheets, the clerk got the figure out for the arbitration himself, and the curator's ledger note gives it for exactly 1898–1922. Teague's 44 million may cover a longer span, but he does not say so.
10. **Station average: 3.61 vs 3.16.** 3.61 — three independent verbatim quotations of the summary leaf. 3.16 is a transposition.
11. **Date the station's own glass went into use: 1 May 1897 vs 1 May 1898.** 1897 — the invoice of 14 April 1897, the office file, the Bulletin's own closing paragraph, and decisively Orra Keddie's diary entry *of that day*.
12. **Weigh book begun: 1 Jan 1897 vs 1 Jan 1898 vs 1 Jan 1900.** 1897 — the curator's accession record and the chairman's exhibit list agree, and Duncan Keddie's family notes give 1897; the son and the granddaughter are recalling.
13. **Pipettes surviving: nine vs eleven.** Nine — the measurement sheet lists nine runs, the curator accessioned nine, the county agent says nine, the hauler's son who handled the crate says nine. "Eleven" reaches the *Chronicle* and Rundle's footnote from "the college" at second hand.
14. **Lot size: six dozen vs five dozen.** Six dozen — the invoice, and 72 × $0.35 = $25.20 closes.
15. **Invoice total: $77.93 vs $77.39.** $77.93 — the column adds to it.
16. **Duration of the 1911 interval: nine weeks vs six weeks.** Nine — two dated entries.
17. **Origin of the borrowed 1911 measure: Larrow Green / "up from town" / left by the state inspector.** **Cannot be settled.** The inspector's own 1911 entry mentions no loan; Teague may be describing the replacement rather than the borrowed measure; Roy Brant's version is second-hand and startling. Nothing decides it, and nothing in the argument turns on it.
18. **Number of families receiving the 1919 circular: nine vs eleven.** Not settled outright, but reconcilable. Four independent recollections say nine, which matches six Hessel Bottom farms plus the inspector's "three places on the hill"; the contemporaneous diary of the man who carried them door to door says eleven. Hazel Brant supplies the reconciliation without being asked: "Nine families, six farms, if you are counting — a farm can hold more than one family and generally did." I take it as nine patron households on nine holdings and eleven papers actually delivered, and mark the inference as mine.
19. **The station's first-season intake: ~900,000 lb vs ~1,100,000 lb.** **Cannot be settled** (see C5b).
20. **The year Article VII was adopted: 1891 / 1892 / 1893.** **Cannot be settled** — the printed by-laws carry no year, and the minute book of those years was lost in 1938. The secretary's 1892, taken from minutes she had, is the best of the three but she was not there.
21. **Where the arbitration sat.** Loomis, the curator and Rundle place it at Ashlin; the chairman says session one at Ashlin and sessions two and three in the station's weigh room. Resolved in the chairman's favour — he arranged it — and now independently confirmed by two people who were outside the building: the hauler's son, who stood on the platform in the rain, and Orra Keddie, whose diary for April 1925 records "two of the sittings held up here at the station itself, in the weigh room, with the men standing outside in the road."
22. **Selby Vose: son vs nephew** — son (see B3). **Rosalie Cudd: daughter vs niece** — daughter, in her own words. **Ivy: granddaughter vs great-niece** — granddaughter. **Ivy and Hazel: second cousins vs first cousin once removed** — second cousins. **Keddie–Vose kinship** — not established (B4).
23. **Orra Brant: full sister vs half sister to Emmet.** Full — her son and her nephew both say so, and Roy Brant is expressly rebutting the stepmother version that Hazel repeats. **Orra's birth year: 1866 vs 1868** — 1866, by the diary's "I am sixty-four" in August 1930 and by Duncan's own "eighty-two when she died in 1948."
24. **When Vose became manager: 1913 vs 1911 (Kyle).** 1913 — the inspector's 1913 entry, "New manager at the Association this year," and Vose's own "thirteen years this spring, since 1913."
25. **The condemnation reaching the office "in twenty-two" (Kyle); the award "in twenty-four" (Kyle); the hearing "in twenty-six" and the condemnation "in twenty-one" (Hazel Brant); the hearing "the winter and spring after" 1919 (Vira Toland).** All wrong against dated documents; Hazel warns the interviewer in her first sentence that she has quantities and not years, and Vira has compressed 1919 and 1925 into one event, which is why she believes the tests rose *after* the dismissal when they rose two years before it.
26. **Where the Association's office was: Ashlin (Teague, the curator's provenances, the chairman's "the Association's office at Ashlin") vs Larrow Green (Kyle, who sat in it).** Unresolved in principle but I take Ashlin, since three records and the arbitration's own venue say so and Kyle is loose on every date he gives; it is possible the creamery at Larrow Green also had an office room and he means that one.
27. **The station's closing and later use:** closed 1946, used for hay tools (Oram) / used for fence wire and salt (Ivy). Compatible; both describe a building used for storage.
28. **"Less cream came off that platform than went onto it"** — the cream hauler's lifelong belief, reported by his son. Flatly refuted by the man who weighed it at the other end and by the ledger: the cream ran heavy every year.
29. **Ammon & Sons "sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900"** — contradicted by the 1897 invoice to an Ordell County association printed on the same page.
30. **The 1908 committee's sampling: "January, February and March" (the report) vs "the whole of 1908, every month" (its own chairman, eleven years later).** The report wins; the expansion is exactly what the theory needed.

### E1(ii). Cases where two narrators carry the same wrong value — and what breaks the tie

- **16.27 c.c.** — Vaught and the *Chronicle*. **Not two sources:** the paper worked from the college's press release. Broken by the nine individual runs on the measurement sheet and by 3.61/3.80 = 0.95.
- **"Eleven pipettes"** — the *Chronicle* and Rundle's 1959 footnote. Again one source ("the college"). Broken by the accession record and by two men who counted them.
- **$19,600** — Rundle (1939) and the *Chronicle* (1958). Both are the patrons' petition figure resurfacing. Broken by arithmetic: 76,000 × 25¢ = $19,000.
- **$77.39** — the *Chronicle* and the firm's own historian. Genuinely independent, and both are contradicted by the itemized invoice they each reprint verbatim on the same page.
- **"1 May 1898"** — Vaught and Kyle. Genuinely independent (a professor from the file, a clerk from memory). Broken by the invoice of 14 April 1897, the office file, Vaught's own concluding paragraph, and the diary entry written on the day.
- **"Five dozen"** — Vaught and Oram. Independent. Broken by the invoice and by 72 × $0.35 = $25.20.
- **"12 July 1923"** — Vose and Rundle. **Not independent:** Rundle is repeating what the valley had from Vose. Broken by the inspector's own dated entry and by the order/reply of 28 June and 2 July.
- **"$3,500"** — Vira Toland and Cleve Oram. Independent people, one shared source: valley talk at the two ends of the same road. Broken by the award quoted verbatim.
- **"First cousin once removed"** — Vira Toland and Oram. Same origin. Broken by three people inside the two families.
- **"Nephew"** — Vira Toland and Rundle. Same origin (Rundle is explicitly writing down what the valley remembers). Broken by Vose's own "My father, who was your first president."
- **"Great-niece"** — the *Chronicle* and Falke's page two. Broken by Falke himself, who checked and appended a correction, and by Duncan Keddie.
- **"Two tenths in the winter feeding"** — Grigg (1919) and Kepp (1926). **Not independent:** Kepp is repeating the valley's version of Bulletin 214, as Grigg had it. Broken by the bulletin's summary paragraph, quoted identically by four other people: five hundredths in winter, nothing measurable in summer. Both men drop the summer clause, which is the half that kills the theory.
- **"44,000,000 lb"** — Teague and Rundle. Not independent. Broken by two people who added the columns for the arbitration.
- **"3.16"** — Teague and Frick. Not independent: Frick heard it from Teague at the hearing and carried it for twenty-one years. Broken by three verbatim quotations of the test book.
- **"27 cents"** — Sill and Kepp. Independent, both at the working end. Broken by the documentary figure of 25 cents from the annual sheets, found separately by the clerk and confirmed by Roy Brant.
- **"67,000 lb"** — Frick and Oram. One origin: the patrons' petition.
- **"Six weeks" for 1911** — Teague and Kepp. Independent, both wrong; broken by two dated entries in the tester's hand.
- **The Keddie–Vose cousinship** — Vira and Oram; see B4.

### E1(iii). Narrators who contradict themselves on the page

- **Bulletin No. 471 (Vaught).** §III and §V give 16.27 c.c., a ratio of 0.9244, a true test of 3.905, a shortfall of 0.295 and 118,000 lb; §VII reprints the conclusion, "low by one part in twenty" and "seventy-six thousand pounds." One part in twenty and 0.9244 cannot both be true. He *notices* — "It should be said that this figure is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the glassware" — and publishes anyway. He also dates the glass to 1898 against his own conclusion, says five dozen against the invoice, repeats the bookkeeper's denominator error, and inflates "four times too small" into "some six times." The document that proves the valley was ruined by an unchecked measure is itself unchecked in its section headed "Magnitude."
- **The firm's own history (Junia Ammon).** Reprints an invoice totalling 77.93 and calls it seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents; states that the house sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900 while printing an 1897 invoice to an Ordell County association.
- **Grigg's 1919 covering letter** against the 1908 report printed beneath it: "the whole of 1908 — every month of that year" vs "January, February and March."
- **Rundle's pamphlet:** Chapter IV states the theft verdict as the settled opinion of the valley; the note added while in press demolishes it and he leaves both standing, which is the right thing to do.
- **Falke's county report:** paragraph 9 concedes the wet hay as "a real if partial factor"; the note appended after he obtained Bulletin 214 withdraws the concession — "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July" — and a second note corrects his own page two.
- **The curator's notes:** headings that transpose the two keepers ("Station test book, kept by E. Brant"; "Farm weigh book, kept by A. Keddie") against his own transcriptions and provenances — the first leaf signed A. Keddie, the weigh book kept at Hessel Bottom by one household and passing to Roy and then Hazel Brant. The chairman's exhibit list settles both the other way. The transcriptions are exact; only the glosses are wrong, which is exactly what his preface warns.
- **Kepp's paper:** he disclaims all competence to judge the station ("he had never in his life read a test and would not know how") and rules the possibility out in the next clause; and he dismisses the 1911 rise as an artefact of summer while his own theory requires a gap that varies with the season, which every man who kept a book says it did not.
- **Vose's address:** treats "no cause established" as vindication while defending a payment of $3,800; explains a uniform 5 % shortfall across six dozen pieces as "apparatus wears," of an outfit that was the station's original 1897 purchase.
- **The *Chronicle*:** prints "six dozen pipettes at thirty-five cents" and a total of $77.39 in adjacent paragraphs; says eleven pipettes were measured where the bulletin it is reporting says nine; and builds an accusation of fraud against the supply house on the firm's inability to trace a lot after thirty years.
- **Hazel Brant**, self-consciously: "Quantities I have. Years I have not" — and then gives four years, all wrong.

## Section F — Theory

### F1. What actually produced the shortfall

The Ostrey Hollow station bought its own testing outfit from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet on 14 April 1897 and put it into use on 1 May 1897. The six dozen milk pipettes in that crate were blown and marked at 17.6 c.c. but delivered a mean of 16.72 — almost certainly because a mold had been reground and a whole lot ran short, an error a glass house's own check cannot catch, since the pieces are consistent with each other and only wrong against the world.

A butterfat test reads the length of a fat column in a bottle neck that the maker ruled to read per cent **for one charge of milk and no other**. Deliver 95 % of that charge and the reading is 95 % of the truth. So the error was **proportional, not additive**: it did not subtract a fixed number of points, it removed one twentieth of whatever was there. That explains its size (0.19 of a point on a true 3.80), and why that particular size (0.19 = 5 % of 3.80, not a round figure but a ratio); it explains why the gap was identical in July and February, in wet years and dry, since a ratio does not vary with the season; it explains why the station's herds still ranked correctly against one another, so that nothing inside the plant ever looked disorderly; and it explains why the error was invisible — the scales could be checked with test weights, the acid for strength, the bottle's graduation with mercury, but the pipette is what the plant measures with, and an instrument that is itself the standard cannot be caught by measuring with it. It could be caught only by a burette brought in from outside, and burettes reached the state's field men in 1921.

It **began** on 1 May 1897, the day the station stopped borrowing Larrow Green's glass, which is why 1896 reads 3.80 and 1898 onwards reads 3.61. It **ended** on 12 June 1923, when the state inspector ran the pipettes against his burette, condemned the lot and ordered new; new glass was in use in July, and 1924 read 3.80. Nothing at the station or on the farms changed at either date, and nothing had to: the cows, the ground, the feed, the scales, the acid, the tester and his method were the same throughout. Only the glass changed, twice, and the figures moved both times. The fat itself was never lost — it went down the hill in the cream, was weighed into the vats at Larrow Green, and was entered in the Association's own ledgers as station gain and carried to the general account: 76,000 lb over 1898–1922, worth $19,000, of which the patrons eventually received $3,800.

### The three competing explanations, and what defeats each

**1. Water in the cans — advanced by Selby Vose, the manager**, in a printed circular of 1919 and again from the platform in 1926 ("Water was going into cans at the head of that valley and it stopped when the Association began to look").
*Defeated by:* the state inspector's lactometer, run at that station on every visit for nearly thirty years, never once finding added water, and recorded as such in the very entry condemning the glass — "Lactometer, no added water, as always here"; he told Vose so to his face in 1919. Independently, by the cheesemaker over the ridge: watered milk cannot be concealed from a man who makes cheese, because the curd comes soft and will not knit and the yield falls the next morning, and in sixteen summers Jerome Cudd never docked those four men a cent. And by chronology: the tests rose in 1923–24, immediately after the new glass, not after "the looking began."

**2. Theft of cream by the tester — insinuated by Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper**, at the 1925 hearing ("cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons… the remedy is two men or a lock"), and believed by the valley at large, by Vira Toland, and by the cream hauler.
*Defeated by:* the direction of the discrepancy. Stolen cream reaches the creamery light; this cream reached it heavy, every year, weighed twice by the buttermaker in his own hand, and the surplus was entered in the bookkeeper's own ledger line. Also by the 1911 interval — the same man, unsupervised, produced high tests for nine weeks and low ones from a fixed date; and by the fact that the tests corrected themselves in 1923–24 while Keddie was still at the bench and stayed corrected after he left.

**3. The wet hay of Hessel Bottom — advanced by the 1908 committee under Nyle Grigg, revived by Grigg in 1919 and given its most scientific dress by Dr. Roland Kepp in 1926.**
*Defeated by:* the College's own feeding trial, which found bog hay depressed the test by not more than five hundredths in winter and by nothing measurable on summer pasture — four times too small, and the wrong shape, against a gap of 0.19 standing flat in every month of every year. Grigg and Kepp both quoted it as "two tenths in the winter feeding," dropping the summer clause. Defeated also by the four Hessel Bottom men whose milk off that same wet ground averaged 3.80 at Alder Corners in the same summers it read 3.61 at home; and by the 1908 report's own sampling, which covered January, February and March only.

### Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?

**No.** The secretary took down every word of all three sessions and states flatly that the crate was never mentioned — "not a word of it was spoken in any of the three sessions, by the patrons, by the officers, or by the board" — and that the award's phrase "the books and appliances of the station" was a form of words the board used and not a list of anything. This is worse than it sounds three times over. The board had four exhibits, of which the fourth was the state inspector's circuit notebook **for 1923** — the volume containing "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot" — and nobody opened it to that page; the clerk who carried the books in and out says he could not tell you that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked. Two of the three sittings were held in the station's own weigh room, with the crate lying under a tarpaulin in the loft twenty feet above their heads. And the chairman spent the next thirty-one years wondering "whether the answer was in that room."

### What did the tester himself believe?

**On the record, he professed not to know, and refused to write down a cause he could not prove.** He let a question mark stand in his book for fourteen years and said so — "I could not answer it then and I cannot answer it now, and I will not put anything in its place that I do not know" — and told the board in nine sentences that the figures were what he read and wrote "and nothing besides," and that he could not tell them why.

**Two later reports of his private belief survive and they do not agree.** His son says he came home in the summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, said it twice, at supper and again at the door, and never dared put it in the book — having nothing to hold in his hand but a suspicion against the outfit he had been taught to trust. The chairman of the 1925 board says that in the corridor at Ashlin Keddie told him privately that he had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the room, having lived among them thirty years. Both are second-hand accounts of a private remark, they are fourteen years apart, and they can be sequenced — a suspicion of the glass in 1911 that he could not test, hardening by 1925 into the only other explanation available to a man who knew his own work was sound — but the sources do not settle which he held at the end, and I decline to choose. What is certain is the irony: if the son is right, the answer was spoken aloud in a kitchen in Ostrey Hollow in 1911, by the one man in the valley who would not write down what he could not prove, and that scruple is both why the case was lost and why the evidence survived intact for his granddaughter.

## Section G — Summary

### G1.

For twenty-six years one skimming station's milk tested a twentieth low, and a valley spent that time accusing its neighbours. The pipettes bought in 1897 were blown for 17.6 c.c. and delivered 16.72, so every butterfat reading was 95 % of the truth: 3.61 where the milk was 3.80. Nobody watered milk; nobody stole cream. The missing 76,000 lb of fat went down the valley in the cream and was entered in the cooperative's own ledgers as "station gain." The glass was condemned in 1923, the tester dismissed in 1925, the cause found in 1958 by his granddaughter with a burette.

It takes eight records because no one of them held two facts at once: the buttermaker knew the cream ran heavy, the patron knew the gap never varied with the season, the cheesemaker over the ridge knew the same milk read 3.80 — and none of them was ever asked.

*(148 words)*
