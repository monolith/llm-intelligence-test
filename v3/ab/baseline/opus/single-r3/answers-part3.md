# Answer Sheet — Part 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

### C1. The error, the true test, the lost fat and its value

**(a) Proportion.** The tests were low by **5% (one part in twenty)**; recorded = 0.95 × true.
- The pipettes were marked 17.6 c.c. The nine survivors averaged **16.72 c.c.**
- 16.72 / 17.6 = **0.95**.
- The test-bottle neck is ruled for a full 17.6 c.c. charge. A 95% charge puts 95% of the fat in the bottle, so every reading is 95% of the truth. The error is proportional, not a fixed deduction.

**(b) True average and shortfall.**
- True = 3.61 / 0.95 = **3.80%**.
- Short by 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 points**.
- This agrees with the 1896 borrowed glass (3.80), the 1911 interval (3.80), the 1924 new glass (3.80) and Alder Corners (about 3.80).

**(c) Fat not credited, 1898–1922 (40,000,000 lb of milk):**

| | Calculation | Pounds of fat |
|---|---|---|
| Delivered | 40,000,000 × 0.0380 | 1,520,000 |
| Credited | 40,000,000 × 0.0361 | 1,444,000 |
| **Not credited** | | **76,000** |

**(d) Money.**
- 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000**.
- The award was $3,800, and 3,800 / 19,000 = **1/5 (20%)** of what they lost.

### C2. The largest patron (Emmet Brant), 1910

**Inputs.** The weigh-book leaf (Hazel) shows 200,000 lb delivered, "paid on a test of three forty-two." Fat was 24¢ that year (Roy). Hazel admits she is poor on years but good on quantities, and she read that page "a thousand times," so I accept 3.42 as his own herd's recorded test.

**(a) Credited:** 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of fat.

**(b) Owed but not credited:**
- True test = 3.42 / 0.95 = 3.60, so the milk carried 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb.
- Owed = **360 lb**. (Check: 360 / 6,840 = 5.26% = 5¼% of credited.)

**(c) Worth:** 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40** in 1910 ($90.00 at the long-run 25¢).

*Alternative, stated for transparency:* if one used the station average of 3.61 instead of his own leaf, the figures would be 7,220 credited, 380 owed and $91.20. I prefer the documented leaf figure.

### C3. Credited, received, and the percentage (same 25 years)

**(a) Credited to patrons:** **1,444,000 lb** (40M × 3.61%).

**(b) Reached Larrow Green in the station cream:** **1,520,000 lb** (40M × 3.80%). The creamery found all of the fat, because its own testing was sound.

**(c) Difference: 76,000 lb.**
- As a share of the fat **credited**: 76,000 / 1,444,000 = **5.26% ≈ 5¼%**. This is the ledger's "station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual." Ledger check: 3,040 / 57,760 = 5.26%.
- As a share of the fat actually **delivered/received**: 76,000 / 1,520,000 = **exactly 5%**.
- Teague's "5¼% of the fat actually in the cream" and Vaught's "5¼% of fat actually delivered" use the wrong base.

### C4. The invoice and the pipettes

**(a) Total: $77.93.** The line items add up to it: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93. Junia Ammon's own text and the Chronicle both print $77.39, a transposition. The Chronicle is almost certainly repeating Ammon's history, and the arithmetic of the facsimile settles it.

**(b) Pipettes and shortfall.**
- **6 dozen = 72 pipettes**; "five dozen" (Vaught's introduction, Oram) is wrong.
- Each delivered on average 17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88 c.c. short**.
- That figure is the mean of the nine survivors, which Falke says were short "in the same proportion, one to another." Extending it to the 63 lost pipettes is an inference, but a well-supported one: the station's error stayed at exactly 5% for 26 years across whichever pipettes were in use.

### C5. How long the glass was in use; the first season

**(a) Two different spans.**
- **In use:** 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923, which is **26 years, 1 month and 11 days**.
- **Money calculation:** **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922.** That span carries the documented 40,000,000 lb (1.6M lb a year × 25) and Keddie's steady 3.61 average "from 1898."
- **Why they differ:**
  - 1897 was a part-year: the glass went in on 1 May, and the year began on other glass.
  - 1923 was cut off at 12 June.
  - Neither partial year is part of the 40M-lb total, so the $19,000 slightly *understates* the true loss.

**(b) First season (1896) intake: cannot be determined from the sources.**
- Nym, cataloguing the records, notes that no 1896 intake figure exists. Kyle says the first season was carried on the creamery's own books and "is not in any book I ever ruled."
- Sill's "about 900,000 lb" and Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand" are hearsay recollections. They disagree by 200,000 lb, and neither has a document behind it.
- 1896 was also a part-season (from 4 May).
- No figure can be given with confidence.

## Section D — Logic

### D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment

**When and how long:** 8 May to 10 July 1911, **nine weeks** (Orra's diary dates the breakage 8 May; Falke gives both dates). "Six weeks" (Teague, Kepp) is wrong.

**Design.**
- **The variable:** the measuring glass. The station's own Ammon pipette was replaced by one borrowed from Larrow Green.
- **Held constant:** the tester (Keddie), the herds, the Hessel Bottom pasture and hay, the farms and families, the scales, the bottles, the acid and the procedure, and the supervision.
- **Result:** the tests rose from 3.61 to about **3.80**, the valley's level, and fell straight back to 3.61 the day the station glass returned. The whole 0.19 gap switched on and off with the instrument.

**What it establishes that a calculation cannot.** A calculation shows only that a 5%-short pipette *would* produce a 0.19 gap. The interval shows that the station's instrument *actually was* the cause in operation, in real time, with milk and man unchanged. It rules out:
- the tester (the same man got the higher figure);
- water (the same families);
- the land (the same Bottom).

It also predates the 1958 measurement by 47 years.

**What it does not establish.**
- It covers only one summer. The hay objection is answered by other evidence: Bulletin 214's nil summer effect, the gap being the same in every season, and 1924.
- It does not show how much the glass was short or which piece was at fault. It points only at "the measure."
- It does not prove the borrowed measure was itself exact; it proves only that it agreed with the valley.
- It says nothing about when the fault began (1 May 1897), the money involved, or why the glass was short (Ammon's manufacturing).
- It is also one short run, and Keddie himself treated it as a suspicion, not a proof.

### D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream

**Three independent reasons.**

1. **The ledger's "station gain"** (Kyle r23, Teague's own entries; Rundle's press note). The theft theory needs the cream to reach Larrow Green *light*. Every year, 1897 onward, it arrived *heavier* than credited: 5¼% more fat, "as usual." A thief removing cream cannot deliver more fat than he recorded. Sill (r14), the buttermaker, confirms from the churn that the Hollow cream "always came down heavy."
2. **The 1911 interval** (Orra r24, Falke r22, Keddie's book). The same man, under the same supervision, recorded 3.80 for nine weeks with a borrowed measure and 3.61 before and after. His honesty did not change with the glass.
3. **The 1924 new glass** (Keddie's averages; Selby Vose). After the condemnation, the same Keddie tested the same patrons at 3.80 for all of 1924. A thief would not have stopped overnight because a pipette was replaced, and the rise came before any petition.

*(Supporting, but not needed: the 1908 committee watched the tests made and "found no dishonesty of any kind" (Grigg r15). The patron worst hurt was Keddie's own brother-in-law Emmet (Duncan, Hazel).)*

**Why these are independent.** Each rests on a different record kept by different people:
- Reason 1 is the Larrow Green office's bookkeeping, plus Sill's own work at the churn. It involves no station record at all.
- Reason 2 is a nine-week episode in the station test book, dated by Orra's private diary.
- Reason 3 is the station's post-1923 results on entirely different glass.

None depends on another, and none depends on Ivy's measurements. **All three would still hold if the glassware had never been measured in 1958.** The station gain in particular was in ink from 1897.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question it? No.**
- Wilbur Teague wrote the "station gain … five and a quarter per cent, as usual" line every year of his service. Kyle says he "never asked anybody anything in forty years."
- At the 1925 hearing Teague insinuated cream theft (Frick r13), a charge his own ledger entries refute.
- He later claimed to have invented the line in 1905, though the column runs from 1897, and he misdescribed its base.
- There is no record of him ever asking what the gain meant.

### D3. Did the manager know the tests ran low before 1923?

**Answer: No. As far as the sources show, Selby Vose did not know, although the information was in front of him every year.**

**The case against knowledge.**
- Kyle, who sat outside his door for 25 years, says Vose "read the totals and he never read the lines above them." He would put his thumb on the bottom figure and sign.
- Vose's actions show he believed the milk, not the test, was at fault. In 1919 he signed the water circular accusing the Hollow families, and in 1925 he pressed the water theory before the board. A man who knew the station's tests ran low had no reason to blame water. Watering cannot make the fat reach the creamery heavy; the line he signed showed exactly that.
- Even Strawn's 1923 condemnation drew no inference in the office; it was treated like "a broken window."

**The limit.** "Did not know" is an inference from conduct and from Kyle's testimony. There is no document recording his state of mind. What is certain is that the station gain was on every sheet he signed from 1911, so the fact was **available** to him, and he did not **see** it. The accurate judgment is negligence, not knowing concealment. Nothing in the sources supports the stronger charge.

## Section F — Theory

### F1. The cause of the shortfall

**The theory.** The Ostrey Hollow shortfall was produced by one lot of glass.

1. **What was wrong.** The six dozen pipettes that Ammon & Sons of Tarnet invoiced on 14 April 1897 were marked 17.6 c.c. but delivered on average only 16.72 c.c., which is **95%** of the charge. The bottle necks are ruled for a full charge, so every sample carried 95% of its true fat into the bottle.
2. **Why that size.** The error was **proportional**. True milk of 3.80% read 3.80 × 0.95 = **3.61**: a gap of **0.19 points**, a loss of one part in twenty. The gap was the same in every month and season because it came from a ratio, not from the feed.
3. **Why it began when it did.** The glass went into use on **1 May 1897**. Before that, the 1896 season was tested on borrowed Larrow Green glass and averaged 3.80.
4. **Why it ended when it did.** Merle Strawn condemned the glass on **12 June 1923**, and new glass was in use from July. The 1924 average returned to **3.80**.
5. **Why nothing else had to change.** The farms, cows, hay, families, scales and tester were the same throughout. The milk itself was always 3.80%; only the reading of it was short.
6. **Where the missing fat went.** The fat was never lost physically. It went down in the cream to Larrow Green, where honest testing found all of it. The ledger recorded the excess every year as "station gain, 5¼%, as usual": 76,000 lb over 1898–1922, worth $19,000.
7. **Corroboration.** The glass-swap experiment of 1911 (8 May–10 July, 3.80) and Jerome Cudd's summer tests of the same herds at Alder Corners (about 3.80) both confirm it.

**The three competing explanations, and what defeats each.**

1. **Added water**
   - **Advanced by** Selby Vose, the manager (the 1919 circular and his case at the 1925 hearing), with Sill agreeing.
   - **Defeated by:**
     - Strawn's lactometer tests at every visit from 1908 never found added water (Falke).
     - Cudd's cheese yields showed the Bottom milk whole.
     - Water cannot make the cream reach Larrow Green *heavier* in fat than credited.
     - Under payment by fat, watering earns nothing: weight goes up exactly as the test goes down.
     - The same families tested 3.80 in 1911 and 1924. Sill's "nine families all got honest in one summer" fails because the glass, not the families, changed.
2. **Cream theft by the tester**
   - **Advanced by** Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper, by insinuation at the 1925 hearing (Frick). It was also the valley's popular view (Rundle, before her retraction) and was urged by "an officer of the Association" (Falke).
   - **Defeated by:**
     - The station gain: the cream arrived heavier, not lighter.
     - The 1911 interval: the same man, recording higher on borrowed glass.
     - 1924: the same man, recording 3.80 on new glass.
     - The 1908 committee watched the tests made and found no dishonesty.
3. **Wet bog hay on Hessel Bottom**
   - **Advanced by** Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee ("remedy lies with the land"), repeated in his 1919 letter, and by Dr. Roland Kepp's 1926 paper. Falke initially conceded it as a partial factor.
   - **Defeated by:**
     - Bulletin 214 itself: at most 0.05 in winter and nothing in summer, against a flat 0.19 in every season.
     - The summer 1911 interval and Cudd's summer tests at 3.80.
     - 1924: the same hay, on new glass, at 3.80.

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware? No.**
- The crate lay in the loft over the intake room, about twenty feet above two of the board's three sittings, and was never brought down or produced.
- Strawn's 1923 notebook recording the condemnation was one of the four exhibits (Kyle, Frick). Nobody laid it beside the test book.
- The board found "no cause established."

**What the tester believed.**
- Ansel Keddie suspected **the glass**. In the summer of 1911 he came home saying "it was something in the glass," and said it twice (Duncan). He set a question mark against the 1911 figures in his book.
- He never put the suspicion in writing, because he would not record what he could not prove.
- He denied any wrongdoing, and Frick believed him. Frick's recollection that Keddie privately blamed the patrons conflicts with Keddie's own letter and his family's account, and is rejected.
- He died in 1934 without knowing he had been right.

## Section G — Summary

### G1. The whole history in 150 words

In 1897 the Larrow Valley creamery's Ostrey Hollow station began testing milk with six dozen Ammon & Sons pipettes that delivered only 95% of their marked 17.6 c.c. Patrons were paid on those tests, so readings of a true 3.80% came out 3.61, year after year. The ledger quietly recorded the missing 76,000 lb of butterfat ($19,000) as "station gain." For a generation people blamed water, a thieving tester, or wet hay. A committee, a college bulletin, a veterinarian and a 1925 arbitration each looked in the wrong place. The arbitration awarded $3,800, and the tester, Ansel Keddie, was dismissed. The inspector who condemned the glass in 1923 drew no conclusion. In 1958 Keddie's granddaughter Ivy found the crate in the station loft, measured it, and proved the glass was at fault. It took eight separate records: invoice, weigh book, diary, ledger, cheese-factory tests, inspector's notebook, test book and crate.
