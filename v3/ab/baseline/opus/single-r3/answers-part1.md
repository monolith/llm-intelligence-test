# Answer Sheet — Part 1 (Section A, stories 1–4)

## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1. Article VII and the station on the creek (1889–1897)

**Who:** the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, its founder Gideon Vose, and Ansel Keddie, the tester at Ostrey Hollow.

**What happened, when:**
- **1889.** Farmers meeting at Larrow Green organized the Association. Gideon Vose was its organizer, wrote its by-laws and was president from 1889 to 1911. He died in 1917.
- **1891.** The main creamery at Larrow Green was built.
- **Article VII.** Every patron is paid on the pounds of butterfat his milk is found by test to contain, not on the weight of the milk, and "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." This article is the hinge of the whole case: under payment by weight, nothing that followed would have cost anyone a dollar.
- **Date of Article VII: cannot be determined.** Loomis says 1892, Rundle 1891 and Grigg "the 1893 meeting." The printed by-laws carry no date, only "adopted at the annual meeting," and the early minute book was lost in an office removal (Falke, r22). Three memories disagree and no document decides between them.
- **Winter 1895–96.** The Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, 7 miles up the valley. It served Hessel Bottom, a wet meadow of six farms (the 1908 committee's report visited "each of the six farms"; Vira's and Ivy's "seven" are rejected).
- **4 May 1896.** The station opened. Milk was weighed in, sampled, tested and separated. The cream went down to Larrow Green on the Oram wagon, and the skim went back to the farmer.
- **Ansel Keddie (1861–1934)** was tester and separator man from the opening for 29 seasons (1896–1924). Hazel's "went up in '95" is a date slip; she admits she is weak on years.

**The first season (1896)** was tested on borrowed glass. It came from Larrow Green:
- Keddie's book says so.
- Orra's diary for 1 May 1897 says "done borrowing of Larrow Green."
- Kyle says the station borrowed from "the creamery here."
- Hobart Sill, the Larrow Green buttermaker, says he lent his own glass and got it back the next spring with one piece chipped.

Keddie's letter, which says "the Ashlin creamery," is the outlier. The 1896 average on that glass was **3.80**.

**The 1896 intake cannot be determined.** Nym notes that no figure exists. Sill's "about 900,000 lb" and Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand" are hearsay and disagree with each other.

**The station's own outfit.** Ammon & Sons of Tarnet invoiced it on 14 April 1897:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| 6 doz. pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at 35¢ | $25.20 |
| 12 doz. test bottles at 22¢ | $31.68 |
| Acid measure | $0.90 |
| Four-bottle hand tester | $18.00 |
| Freight | $2.15 |
| **Total** | **$77.93** |

The outfit went into use on **1 May 1897**.

**Documents:** Loomis; Rundle (r12); Grigg (r15); Falke (r22); Keddie's book and letter; Sill (r14); Orra's diary (r24); Kyle (r23); Nym's catalogue; the Ammon invoice (r20); Hazel (r17); Oram (r21).

### Story 2. Six dozen short pipettes: Ammon & Sons and the inspector (1897–1923)

**Who:** Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet (founded by Marcus Ammon; its history was written by Junia Ammon in 1949), and Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector on the Ordell circuit. Duncan says Strawn was kin to nobody in the valley.

**The glass.**
- **14 April 1897.** Ammon & Sons invoiced the outfit: 72 pipettes marked 17.6 c.c., the standard milk charge for which the test-bottle neck is ruled, with the total at **$77.93**.
- Hazel's "came from Tarnet in '99" and Junia Ammon's claim that the house "sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900" both fail against her own facsimile of the 1897 invoice.
- **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923.** The pipettes were in daily use.
- The pipettes delivered only 95% of their mark. A short charge in a bottle ruled for a full one reads low in exactly the same proportion, every time, in every season.

**How it happened: cannot be determined.** Ammon's works books burned in 1931. Junia Ammon's guess is that a reground mold ran the whole lot short, "consistently wrong," so a spot check could not catch it. The Chronicle's charge that the firm "knowingly" sold a short lot has no evidence behind it.

**The condemnation.**
- **12 June 1923.** On an ordinary circuit visit, Strawn ran the pipettes against his calibrated burette, found they would not deliver their mark, condemned the whole lot and told the manager to order new.
- Rundle's and Selby Vose's "12 July" date is impossible: the replacement order went out on **28 June 1923** (six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles). Kyle's "'22" and Hazel's "'21" are also wrong.
- **2 July 1923.** Ammon answered that the order was entered and they regretted the trouble, but that "our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot."
- New glass was in use from July 1923. The condemned crate went up into the loft over the intake room.
- Strawn wrote down the condemnation and drew no inference about the 26 years behind it. Nobody in the office treated it as more than "a broken window" (Kyle).
- On the new glass, the station's **1924** average was **3.80**. Falke's 3.85 and Selby Vose's 3.90 are rejected.

**Strawn's notebooks** went to the state dairy office when he retired in 1936 and to the Farland college library in 1947. Ivy found the 1923 volume there in 1958.

**Documents:** Ammon (r20); Strawn's notebook (via Duncan r18, Falke r22 and Kyle r23); the Chronicle (r19); Orra's diary (July 1923: "new glass at the station"); Hazel (r17); Selby Vose; Rundle (r12).

### Story 3. The complaint, the weigh book and the hay (1897–1926)

**Who:** the Hessel Bottom patrons, above all Emmet Brant; Nyle Grigg's committee; the State Agricultural College; and Dr. Roland Kepp.

**Emmet Brant's weigh book.**
- Emmet (1854–1929) had the largest farm on the Bottom. He began his weigh book on **1 January 1897**, per Frick's exhibit list, Nym's catalogue and Duncan. Roy's 1898 and Hazel's 1900 are rejected.
- He weighed on his own scale and entered the station's intake weight beside it. The two figures always agreed, so the scales were never the problem.
- The 1910 leaf shows 200,000 lb delivered, "paid on a test of three forty-two" (Hazel). Fat was 24¢ that year (Roy).

**The complaint.**
- It began in **1902**: Orra's diary for March 1902, Grigg and Sill all agree.
- The Hollow tested **0.19** below the valley, flat in every month and season.

**The Grigg committee, 1908.**
- Three men were named in January 1908 (Orra saw "four men" in Grigg's sleigh). Grigg was the chairman. He hauled to Larrow Green and was not a patron.
- They visited the six farms, drew and sealed samples, watched the tests made, tried the scales, looked into the water, and found "no dishonesty of any kind."
- They sampled **January, February and March** only; the report says so itself. Grigg's 1919 letter claiming "the whole of 1908" contradicts his own report.
- Their conclusion: wet sedge hay thins the cream, and "the remedy lies with the land."

**Bulletin 214 (Farland, 1911).** The College's bog-hay feeding trial found the test depressed "by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Falke (r22) quotes that summary word for word.

**The misquotations.**
- In 1919 Grigg reprinted the report and misquoted Bulletin 214 as "two tenths."
- In 1926 Kepp repeated "two tenths," estimated a "fifth of a point" from his field practice, gave the gap as 0.21, called the 1911 interval "six weeks of summer figures," and refused to look for a cause "in a building."

**Why the hay theory fails.**
- Bulletin 214 gives at most 0.05, and only in winter, against a 0.19 gap that ran all year.
- The same herds tested 3.80 on borrowed glass in 1911 (summer), on new glass in 1924, and at Alder Corners.
- In 1959 Falke withdrew his concession that hay was "a real if partial factor."

**Documents:** the Grigg report and letter (r15); Kepp (r16); Falke (r22); Orra (r24); Roy (r09); Hazel (r17); Frick (r13); Nym; Duncan (r18); Sill (r14).

### Story 4. Nine weeks in 1911: the accidental experiment

**Who:** Ansel Keddie, with his wife Orra and son Duncan as witnesses.

**What happened.**
- **8 May 1911.** The station's own measure broke. Orra's diary: "A. home cross about a broken measure."
- A measure was borrowed from Larrow Green and brought up on the cream wagon. It was used for **nine weeks**, until **10 July 1911**.
- Teague's and Kepp's "six weeks" is wrong: 8 May to 10 July is nine weeks. Roy's version, that the state man left his own measure, conflicts with the record of a measure borrowed from Larrow Green; it rests on memory alone and is rejected.

**The result.**
- Through those nine weeks the Hollow's tests stood at **3.80**, the valley's figure.
- The day Keddie went back to the station glass, they fell back to **3.61**.
- Nothing else changed: the same tester, the same herds and pasture, the same scales, acid and method, and the same supervision.

**What Keddie made of it.**
- He entered the figures and set **a question mark** against them.
- At home he said it was "something in the glass," twice: at supper and again by the door (Duncan, who was 17 and left to teach at Ashlin that October).
- He never wrote it down. He would not record what he could not prove, and he went on for another 14 years writing the figures the glass gave him.

**Why it was not followed up.**
- 1911 was also the year Bulletin 214 appeared, and the year Gideon Vose left the presidency and Selby Vose became manager.
- The interval was dismissed as "summer figures" (Kepp), even though the 0.19 gap was the same in every season.

**Its later use.**
- In 1958 Vaught and Ivy Keddie treated the interval as a natural experiment: only the instrument changed, and the test moved by the whole gap.
- In 1959 Falke called it decisive against the theft theory.
- It is independently confirmed by the 1896 average of 3.80 on borrowed Larrow Green glass and the 1924 average of 3.80 on new glass.

**Documents:** Orra's diary (r24); Duncan (r18); Falke (r22); Vaught (r11); Keddie's averages; Roy (r09); Teague; Kepp (r16).
