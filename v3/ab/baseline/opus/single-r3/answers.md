# Answer Sheet — Part 1 (Section A, stories 1–4)

## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1. Article VII and the station on the creek (1889–1897)

**Who:** the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, its founder Gideon Vose, and Ansel Keddie, the tester at Ostrey Hollow.

**What happened, when:**
- **1889.** Farmers meeting at Larrow Green organized the Association. Gideon Vose was its organizer, wrote its by-laws and was president from 1889 to 1911. He died in 1917.
- **1891.** The main creamery at Larrow Green was built.
- **Article VII.** Every patron is paid on the pounds of butterfat his milk is found by test to contain, not on the weight of the milk, and "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." This article is the hinge of the whole case: under payment by weight, nothing that followed would have cost anyone a dollar.
- **Date of Article VII: cannot be determined.** Loomis says 1892, Rundle 1891 and Grigg "the 1893 meeting." The printed by-laws carry no date, only "adopted at the annual meeting," and the early minute book was lost in an office removal (Falke, r22). Three memories disagree and no document decides between them.
- **Winter 1895–96.** The Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, 7 miles up the valley. It served Hessel Bottom, a wet meadow of six farms (the 1908 committee's report visited "each of the six farms"; Vira's and Ivy's "seven" are rejected).
- **4 May 1896.** The station opened. Milk was weighed in, sampled, tested and separated. The cream went down to Larrow Green on the Oram wagon, and the skim went back to the farmer.
- **Ansel Keddie (1861–1934)** was tester and separator man from the opening for 29 seasons (1896–1924). Hazel's "went up in '95" is a date slip; she admits she is weak on years.

**The first season (1896)** was tested on borrowed glass. It came from Larrow Green:
- Keddie's book says so.
- Orra's diary for 1 May 1897 says "done borrowing of Larrow Green."
- Kyle says the station borrowed from "the creamery here."
- Hobart Sill, the Larrow Green buttermaker, says he lent his own glass and got it back the next spring with one piece chipped.

Keddie's letter, which says "the Ashlin creamery," is the outlier. The 1896 average on that glass was **3.80**.

**The 1896 intake cannot be determined.** Nym notes that no figure exists. Sill's "about 900,000 lb" and Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand" are hearsay and disagree with each other.

**The station's own outfit.** Ammon & Sons of Tarnet invoiced it on 14 April 1897:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| 6 doz. pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at 35¢ | $25.20 |
| 12 doz. test bottles at 22¢ | $31.68 |
| Acid measure | $0.90 |
| Four-bottle hand tester | $18.00 |
| Freight | $2.15 |
| **Total** | **$77.93** |

The outfit went into use on **1 May 1897**.

**Documents:** Loomis; Rundle (r12); Grigg (r15); Falke (r22); Keddie's book and letter; Sill (r14); Orra's diary (r24); Kyle (r23); Nym's catalogue; the Ammon invoice (r20); Hazel (r17); Oram (r21).

### Story 2. Six dozen short pipettes: Ammon & Sons and the inspector (1897–1923)

**Who:** Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet (founded by Marcus Ammon; its history was written by Junia Ammon in 1949), and Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector on the Ordell circuit. Duncan says Strawn was kin to nobody in the valley.

**The glass.**
- **14 April 1897.** Ammon & Sons invoiced the outfit: 72 pipettes marked 17.6 c.c., the standard milk charge for which the test-bottle neck is ruled, with the total at **$77.93**.
- Hazel's "came from Tarnet in '99" and Junia Ammon's claim that the house "sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900" both fail against her own facsimile of the 1897 invoice.
- **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923.** The pipettes were in daily use.
- The pipettes delivered only 95% of their mark. A short charge in a bottle ruled for a full one reads low in exactly the same proportion, every time, in every season.

**How it happened: cannot be determined.** Ammon's works books burned in 1931. Junia Ammon's guess is that a reground mold ran the whole lot short, "consistently wrong," so a spot check could not catch it. The Chronicle's charge that the firm "knowingly" sold a short lot has no evidence behind it.

**The condemnation.**
- **12 June 1923.** On an ordinary circuit visit, Strawn ran the pipettes against his calibrated burette, found they would not deliver their mark, condemned the whole lot and told the manager to order new.
- Rundle's and Selby Vose's "12 July" date is impossible: the replacement order went out on **28 June 1923** (six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles). Kyle's "'22" and Hazel's "'21" are also wrong.
- **2 July 1923.** Ammon answered that the order was entered and they regretted the trouble, but that "our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot."
- New glass was in use from July 1923. The condemned crate went up into the loft over the intake room.
- Strawn wrote down the condemnation and drew no inference about the 26 years behind it. Nobody in the office treated it as more than "a broken window" (Kyle).
- On the new glass, the station's **1924** average was **3.80**. Falke's 3.85 and Selby Vose's 3.90 are rejected.

**Strawn's notebooks** went to the state dairy office when he retired in 1936 and to the Farland college library in 1947. Ivy found the 1923 volume there in 1958.

**Documents:** Ammon (r20); Strawn's notebook (via Duncan r18, Falke r22 and Kyle r23); the Chronicle (r19); Orra's diary (July 1923: "new glass at the station"); Hazel (r17); Selby Vose; Rundle (r12).

### Story 3. The complaint, the weigh book and the hay (1897–1926)

**Who:** the Hessel Bottom patrons, above all Emmet Brant; Nyle Grigg's committee; the State Agricultural College; and Dr. Roland Kepp.

**Emmet Brant's weigh book.**
- Emmet (1854–1929) had the largest farm on the Bottom. He began his weigh book on **1 January 1897**, per Frick's exhibit list, Nym's catalogue and Duncan. Roy's 1898 and Hazel's 1900 are rejected.
- He weighed on his own scale and entered the station's intake weight beside it. The two figures always agreed, so the scales were never the problem.
- The 1910 leaf shows 200,000 lb delivered, "paid on a test of three forty-two" (Hazel). Fat was 24¢ that year (Roy).

**The complaint.**
- It began in **1902**: Orra's diary for March 1902, Grigg and Sill all agree.
- The Hollow tested **0.19** below the valley, flat in every month and season.

**The Grigg committee, 1908.**
- Three men were named in January 1908 (Orra saw "four men" in Grigg's sleigh). Grigg was the chairman. He hauled to Larrow Green and was not a patron.
- They visited the six farms, drew and sealed samples, watched the tests made, tried the scales, looked into the water, and found "no dishonesty of any kind."
- They sampled **January, February and March** only; the report says so itself. Grigg's 1919 letter claiming "the whole of 1908" contradicts his own report.
- Their conclusion: wet sedge hay thins the cream, and "the remedy lies with the land."

**Bulletin 214 (Farland, 1911).** The College's bog-hay feeding trial found the test depressed "by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Falke (r22) quotes that summary word for word.

**The misquotations.**
- In 1919 Grigg reprinted the report and misquoted Bulletin 214 as "two tenths."
- In 1926 Kepp repeated "two tenths," estimated a "fifth of a point" from his field practice, gave the gap as 0.21, called the 1911 interval "six weeks of summer figures," and refused to look for a cause "in a building."

**Why the hay theory fails.**
- Bulletin 214 gives at most 0.05, and only in winter, against a 0.19 gap that ran all year.
- The same herds tested 3.80 on borrowed glass in 1911 (summer), on new glass in 1924, and at Alder Corners.
- In 1959 Falke withdrew his concession that hay was "a real if partial factor."

**Documents:** the Grigg report and letter (r15); Kepp (r16); Falke (r22); Orra (r24); Roy (r09); Hazel (r17); Frick (r13); Nym; Duncan (r18); Sill (r14).

### Story 4. Nine weeks in 1911: the accidental experiment

**Who:** Ansel Keddie, with his wife Orra and son Duncan as witnesses.

**What happened.**
- **8 May 1911.** The station's own measure broke. Orra's diary: "A. home cross about a broken measure."
- A measure was borrowed from Larrow Green and brought up on the cream wagon. It was used for **nine weeks**, until **10 July 1911**.
- Teague's and Kepp's "six weeks" is wrong: 8 May to 10 July is nine weeks. Roy's version, that the state man left his own measure, conflicts with the record of a measure borrowed from Larrow Green; it rests on memory alone and is rejected.

**The result.**
- Through those nine weeks the Hollow's tests stood at **3.80**, the valley's figure.
- The day Keddie went back to the station glass, they fell back to **3.61**.
- Nothing else changed: the same tester, the same herds and pasture, the same scales, acid and method, and the same supervision.

**What Keddie made of it.**
- He entered the figures and set **a question mark** against them.
- At home he said it was "something in the glass," twice: at supper and again by the door (Duncan, who was 17 and left to teach at Ashlin that October).
- He never wrote it down. He would not record what he could not prove, and he went on for another 14 years writing the figures the glass gave him.

**Why it was not followed up.**
- 1911 was also the year Bulletin 214 appeared, and the year Gideon Vose left the presidency and Selby Vose became manager.
- The interval was dismissed as "summer figures" (Kepp), even though the 0.19 gap was the same in every season.

**Its later use.**
- In 1958 Vaught and Ivy Keddie treated the interval as a natural experiment: only the instrument changed, and the test moved by the whole gap.
- In 1959 Falke called it decisive against the theft theory.
- It is independently confirmed by the 1896 average of 3.80 on borrowed Larrow Green glass and the 1924 average of 3.80 on new glass.

**Documents:** Orra's diary (r24); Duncan (r18); Falke (r22); Vaught (r11); Keddie's averages; Roy (r09); Teague; Kepp (r16).


# Answer Sheet — Part 2 (Section A, stories 5–8; Section B)

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### Story 5. Over the ridge: the Alder Corners cheese factory (1915–1930)

**Who:** Jerome Cudd, who founded the cheese company at Alder Corners in Vessey County, over the ridge; his daughter Rosalie Cudd; and four of the six Hessel Bottom farmers.

**The factory's dates.**
- Rosalie says it was built in 1913; Rundle and Roy say it opened in 1914.
- These may both be true (built 1913, opened 1914). That reading is my inference; the sources do not settle it.

**The milk.**
- The ridge road is closed from November to April.
- From **1915 to 1930 (16 seasons)**, four Bottom men hauled over the ridge in the summer flush only, June to mid-September. The rest of the year they delivered to the Ostrey Hollow station (Roy, Rosalie, Hazel).
- Hazel's father called it "the only honest arithmetic anybody in that valley ever did without meaning to."

**Cudd's tests.**
- Cudd tested that milk on his own, uncondemned glass. His 1919 figures for the four were **3.79, 3.81, 3.78 and 3.82**.
- His yearly averages ran 3.78–3.82, about **3.80**: the valley's figure, and the same figure the station got on borrowed glass in 1911 and new glass in 1924.
- The cheese yield itself showed there was no water in the milk.
- Jerome never compared his figures with the station's, and no one else did either.
- The same cows, on the same Bottom, in the same seasons, read 3.80 over the ridge and 3.61 at the station. That points to the station's instrument, not the land or the men.
- Because this was summer milk, it also answers the hay theory: Bulletin 214 found no measurable summer effect, yet the station's summer gap was still 0.19.

**How far the checks overlap.**
- Cudd's testing overlaps Keddie's for 1915–1924: **10 seasons**.
- It overlaps the short glass for **8 full seasons (1915–1922)**; the 1923 season was cut by the condemnation on 12 June.
- Ivy's "14 overlapping seasons" matches neither count and is rejected.

**The records.**
- Jerome Cudd died in March 1941. Rosalie (his daughter; the Chronicle's "niece" is wrong) took over the factory and the test book.
- In 1958 she had the figures copied out and sent to Ivy at Farland, "on a stranger's say-so."
- The book has never left Vessey County. It went to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970 (Duncan's 1970 addition).

**Documents:** Rosalie Cudd's letter (r10); Roy (r09); Hazel (r17); Rundle (r12); Duncan (r18); the Chronicle (r19); Ivy's account.

### Story 6. The office at Larrow Green: the water circular and the "station gain"

**Who:** Selby Vose (manager from 1911 for about a quarter-century; Gideon Vose's son, not his nephew); Wilbur Teague (bookkeeper); Bertram Kyle (clerk at the outer desk for 25 years); and Hobart Sill (buttermaker at Larrow Green, 1893–1934).

**The ledger line.**
- Every year the Ostrey Hollow station account carried three figures:
  - the fat credited to patrons on the station test;
  - the fat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream;
  - the difference, entered as "**station gain** … five and a quarter per cent, as usual," and carried to the general account.
- The column starts in 1897, when the short glass went in. Teague's claim to have invented the line in 1905 is wrong.
- A typical year: 57,760 lb credited against 60,800 lb received, a gain of 3,040 lb. That is 5¼% of the fat credited, and exactly what 1,600,000 lb of milk gives at 3.61% against 3.80%.
- Sill independently saw the same thing in the churn: the Hollow cream "always came down heavy," richer than its sheet. Yet he still blamed water, which cannot make cream heavier.

**Nobody read it.**
- Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years."
- Vose took each sheet, "put his thumb on the bottom figure, and signed."
- Kyle copied the line every year and read it to the annual meeting. "It was in plain sight with a name on it."

**The 1919 circular.**
- In June 1919 Vose signed, and Kyle typed, a circular warning the Hollow patrons against watering their milk. It went to **nine families** on six farms (Hazel and the other accounts; Orra's diary says eleven).
- Keddie would not mail the circulars. He carried them door to door and stood by while each man read his.
- Hazel's mother burned hers. None of the families answered.
- Emmet Brant came to the Keddies' yard and asked Ansel why he had carried the paper if he did not believe it. He did not set foot in the house again before his death in 1929.

**The office's figures for 1898–1922.**
- Kyle compiled them for the arbitration: **40,000,000 lb** of milk (1.6M lb a year), with fat averaging **25¢**.
- The 1925 award was later charged to the station account.

**Documents:** Kyle (r23); Teague; Sill (r14); Selby Vose; Orra's diary (r24); Hazel (r17); Frick (r13); Vaught (r11); Rundle's press note (r12).

### Story 7. The petition, the arbitration and the dismissal (1924–1934)

**Who:** the Hessel Bottom patrons; Alonzo Frick (Ashlin attorney and chairman of the board); Ansel Keddie; and the Association.

**Before the petition.**
- On the new glass, the station's 1924 average was 3.80: a rise of 0.19, which came **before** the petition.
- Selby Vose's claim that the rise came after the petition is wrong.

**The petition and hearing.**
- In **January 1925** the patrons petitioned, with Emmet Brant's name on the paper.
- A board of arbitration sat from **17 March to 4 May 1925**. The first sitting was at the Cooperative's office in Ashlin. The second and third were in the station weigh room itself (Frick, Oram, Orra, Duncan), with men standing outside in the rain and the condemned crate about twenty feet overhead in the loft.
- Three theories were argued:
  - water (Vose);
  - cream theft (Teague, by insinuation);
  - hay (the 1908 report).

**The four books before the board** (Kyle checked them off; Frick's list agrees):
- the station test book in Keddie's hand, 4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924;
- the receiving book;
- Emmet Brant's weigh book, lent for the purpose;
- Strawn's 1923 notebook, which recorded the condemnation.

Nobody laid the notebook beside the test book, and the crate was never produced.

**Dismissal and award.**
- Keddie was dismissed on **30 April 1925**, four days before the finding.
- On 4 May the board found "no cause established" and awarded **$3,800** "without admission." Vira's and Oram's $3,500 is rejected.
- Hazel's "hearing in '26," Kyle's "award in '24" and the Chronicle's "1908 committee sat again" are all wrong.
- Roy Brant never went to another Association meeting.

**Afterward.**
- The test book was impounded in March 1925 and lay in the Cooperative office at Ashlin until 1959.
- Keddie turned to carpentry: stairs, gates, doors, "a carpenter at sixty-six" in 1927.
- He died at home in **February 1934**. He had once told Orra that "a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."
- Frick (1946, aged 74) still believed Keddie's denial but got his figures wrong (3.16, 67,000 lb).
- The station closed in 1946 (Oram).

**Documents:** Frick (r13); Kyle (r23); Orra (r24); Duncan (r18); Oram (r21); Hazel (r17); Falke (r22); Selby Vose; Roy (r09).

### Story 8. The crate in the loft: Ivy Keddie and Bulletin 471 (1958)

**Who:**
- **Ivy Keddie**, born 1931, daughter of Duncan (a schoolteacher at Ashlin, born 1894) and granddaughter of Ansel and Orra. She was a graduate student in dairy husbandry at Farland.
- **Professor Leland Vaught**, her co-author.

**The research.**
- In 1958 Roy Brant **lent** her the weigh book; it went to Hazel at his death in 1962 and to the Historical Rooms in 1963.
- In **February 1958** she climbed the ladder into the station loft and found the condemned crate under a tarpaulin and chaff. **Nine** pipettes survived: Oram and Falke say nine, against the Chronicle's and Rundle's eleven.
- She took them to Farland. On **9 March 1958** she measured them against a calibrated burette: mean **16.72 c.c.** against 17.6 marked, so **0.95** of the mark, all in the same proportion.
- She also found Strawn's 1923 notebook in the college library, and Rosalie Cudd sent her the Alder Corners figures.

**Bulletin 471 (October 1958, Vaught and Keddie).**
- True average = 3.61 / 0.95 = **3.80**. The error was proportional, one part in twenty, from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923.
- Over the 25 full years 1898–1922, the 40M lb of milk was credited with 1,444,000 lb of fat but actually carried **1,520,000 lb**.
- The **76,000 lb** shortfall, worth **$19,000** at 25¢, sits in the ledgers as "station gain": 5% of the fat delivered, 5¼% of the fat credited.
- The 1925 award was one-fifth of the loss.
- Conclusion: no water, and no theft.
- Vaught's introduction contains slips (16.27 c.c., 118,000 lb, five dozen pipettes, 1898) that his own conclusion contradicts.

**Reception.**
- The Ashlin Chronicle (3 October 1958) announced the result with many errors: "great-niece," 11 pipettes, $19,600, $77.39, Rosalie as "niece."
- Rundle's 1959 reprint retracted the theft theory.
- Falke's 1959 report to the county board corrected "great-niece" to granddaughter and withdrew the hay concession.
- In 1959 the test book and the crate went to the Ordell County Historical Rooms at Ashlin.

**Kinship.** Orra Keddie was Emmet Brant's sister, so Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie were first cousins, and Hazel Brant and Ivy are second cousins. Ansel was cleared 24 years after his death. Orra had died in 1948.

**Documents:** Vaught (r11); the Chronicle (r19); Falke (r22); Duncan (r18); Hazel (r17); Roy (r09); Rundle (r12); Oram (r21); Kyle (r23); Rosalie (r10).

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie and Ansel Keddie: granddaughter.**
- Ansel and Orra's son was Duncan (born 1894). Ivy (born 1931) is Duncan's daughter. Duncan states this in his own family notes (r18) because he had "twice seen it printed otherwise."
- Hazel, Oram ("the granddaughter came back") and Falke's correction all agree.
- "Great-niece" appears in the Chronicle (r19) and in Falke's first draft (r22). Both drew on the same college press release and telephone calls, so they count as one source. Falke himself later verified the correction and retracted it.
- A son's written family record outranks a newspaper assembled in a day and a half.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant: second cousins.**
- Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie were siblings. Emmet's son Roy and Orra's son Duncan were therefore first cousins, and their daughters Hazel and Ivy are second cousins.
- Duncan, Hazel and Rundle all say second cousins. Vira's and Oram's "first cousin once removed" would require a one-generation offset that does not exist, since Hazel and Ivy are both great-grandchildren of the same Brant couple.
- **Left open:** whether Emmet and Orra were full or half siblings.
  - Hazel says Orra was a half-sister "by his father's second wife."
  - Duncan, Orra's own son, says simply "brother and sister … the younger of the two," and he is usually precise.
  - A 12-year age gap (Emmet born 1854, Orra born 1866) fits either account, and no document settles it.
  - If half-siblings, Hazel and Ivy are strictly half second cousins; the generation count is the same.
- **Also left open:** Orra's birth year. The diary preface says 1866 and has her 64 in 1930. Duncan says 1868 but also "eighty-two when she died in 1948," which implies 1866, so 1866 is preferred.
- The mothers of Roy and of Ivy are never named.

**B3. Selby Vose's father: Gideon Vose.**
- Gideon organized the Association in 1889, was its first president until 1911, and wrote its by-laws (Kyle, Duncan). Rundle's "nephew" is Ashlin gossip that both men dismiss.
- **Why it matters:** Gideon's Article VII made the *station's* test govern each patron's pay, with no appeal to another test. His son, manager from 1911, administered that rule for 14 years:
  - He signed the ledger sheets that carried the "station gain" every year without reading them.
  - He sent the 1919 water circular that blamed the patrons.
  - He was on the Association side at the 1925 arbitration, where the award was paid "without admission" and charged to the station account.
- The family that wrote the rule ran the institution that silently kept the 76,000 lb. The son had every reason to defend the father's system and to look for the fault in the farmers rather than in the station.

**B4. The Vose and Keddie families: no relationship established, and the best evidence says none.**
- Oram (r21) repeats that "the Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort." He had it from his mother's mother and admits he "could not draw you the line."
- Duncan (r18) states flatly that there was no connection. The rumor that Orra and Mrs. Gideon Vose were cousins comes from confusing Gideon's family with the unrelated Ashlin Voses, and Orra, who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection," never once named a Vose.
- A family member's direct knowledge outranks third-hand gossip that admits it cannot name the link.
- An absence of kinship cannot be proved absolutely, but nothing in the sources supports one.


# Answer Sheet — Part 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

### C1. The error, the true test, the lost fat and its value

**(a) Proportion.** The tests were low by **5% (one part in twenty)**; recorded = 0.95 × true.
- The pipettes were marked 17.6 c.c. The nine survivors averaged **16.72 c.c.**
- 16.72 / 17.6 = **0.95**.
- The test-bottle neck is ruled for a full 17.6 c.c. charge. A 95% charge puts 95% of the fat in the bottle, so every reading is 95% of the truth. The error is proportional, not a fixed deduction.

**(b) True average and shortfall.**
- True = 3.61 / 0.95 = **3.80%**.
- Short by 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 points**.
- This agrees with the 1896 borrowed glass (3.80), the 1911 interval (3.80), the 1924 new glass (3.80) and Alder Corners (about 3.80).

**(c) Fat not credited, 1898–1922 (40,000,000 lb of milk):**

| | Calculation | Pounds of fat |
|---|---|---|
| Delivered | 40,000,000 × 0.0380 | 1,520,000 |
| Credited | 40,000,000 × 0.0361 | 1,444,000 |
| **Not credited** | | **76,000** |

**(d) Money.**
- 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000**.
- The award was $3,800, and 3,800 / 19,000 = **1/5 (20%)** of what they lost.

### C2. The largest patron (Emmet Brant), 1910

**Inputs.** The weigh-book leaf (Hazel) shows 200,000 lb delivered, "paid on a test of three forty-two." Fat was 24¢ that year (Roy). Hazel admits she is poor on years but good on quantities, and she read that page "a thousand times," so I accept 3.42 as his own herd's recorded test.

**(a) Credited:** 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of fat.

**(b) Owed but not credited:**
- True test = 3.42 / 0.95 = 3.60, so the milk carried 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb.
- Owed = **360 lb**. (Check: 360 / 6,840 = 5.26% = 5¼% of credited.)

**(c) Worth:** 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40** in 1910 ($90.00 at the long-run 25¢).

*Alternative, stated for transparency:* if one used the station average of 3.61 instead of his own leaf, the figures would be 7,220 credited, 380 owed and $91.20. I prefer the documented leaf figure.

### C3. Credited, received, and the percentage (same 25 years)

**(a) Credited to patrons:** **1,444,000 lb** (40M × 3.61%).

**(b) Reached Larrow Green in the station cream:** **1,520,000 lb** (40M × 3.80%). The creamery found all of the fat, because its own testing was sound.

**(c) Difference: 76,000 lb.**
- As a share of the fat **credited**: 76,000 / 1,444,000 = **5.26% ≈ 5¼%**. This is the ledger's "station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual." Ledger check: 3,040 / 57,760 = 5.26%.
- As a share of the fat actually **delivered/received**: 76,000 / 1,520,000 = **exactly 5%**.
- Teague's "5¼% of the fat actually in the cream" and Vaught's "5¼% of fat actually delivered" use the wrong base.

### C4. The invoice and the pipettes

**(a) Total: $77.93.** The line items add up to it: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93. Junia Ammon's own text and the Chronicle both print $77.39, a transposition. The Chronicle is almost certainly repeating Ammon's history, and the arithmetic of the facsimile settles it.

**(b) Pipettes and shortfall.**
- **6 dozen = 72 pipettes**; "five dozen" (Vaught's introduction, Oram) is wrong.
- Each delivered on average 17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88 c.c. short**.
- That figure is the mean of the nine survivors, which Falke says were short "in the same proportion, one to another." Extending it to the 63 lost pipettes is an inference, but a well-supported one: the station's error stayed at exactly 5% for 26 years across whichever pipettes were in use.

### C5. How long the glass was in use; the first season

**(a) Two different spans.**
- **In use:** 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923, which is **26 years, 1 month and 11 days**.
- **Money calculation:** **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922.** That span carries the documented 40,000,000 lb (1.6M lb a year × 25) and Keddie's steady 3.61 average "from 1898."
- **Why they differ:**
  - 1897 was a part-year: the glass went in on 1 May, and the year began on other glass.
  - 1923 was cut off at 12 June.
  - Neither partial year is part of the 40M-lb total, so the $19,000 slightly *understates* the true loss.

**(b) First season (1896) intake: cannot be determined from the sources.**
- Nym, cataloguing the records, notes that no 1896 intake figure exists. Kyle says the first season was carried on the creamery's own books and "is not in any book I ever ruled."
- Sill's "about 900,000 lb" and Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand" are hearsay recollections. They disagree by 200,000 lb, and neither has a document behind it.
- 1896 was also a part-season (from 4 May).
- No figure can be given with confidence.

## Section D — Logic

### D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment

**When and how long:** 8 May to 10 July 1911, **nine weeks** (Orra's diary dates the breakage 8 May; Falke gives both dates). "Six weeks" (Teague, Kepp) is wrong.

**Design.**
- **The variable:** the measuring glass. The station's own Ammon pipette was replaced by one borrowed from Larrow Green.
- **Held constant:** the tester (Keddie), the herds, the Hessel Bottom pasture and hay, the farms and families, the scales, the bottles, the acid and the procedure, and the supervision.
- **Result:** the tests rose from 3.61 to about **3.80**, the valley's level, and fell straight back to 3.61 the day the station glass returned. The whole 0.19 gap switched on and off with the instrument.

**What it establishes that a calculation cannot.** A calculation shows only that a 5%-short pipette *would* produce a 0.19 gap. The interval shows that the station's instrument *actually was* the cause in operation, in real time, with milk and man unchanged. It rules out:
- the tester (the same man got the higher figure);
- water (the same families);
- the land (the same Bottom).

It also predates the 1958 measurement by 47 years.

**What it does not establish.**
- It covers only one summer. The hay objection is answered by other evidence: Bulletin 214's nil summer effect, the gap being the same in every season, and 1924.
- It does not show how much the glass was short or which piece was at fault. It points only at "the measure."
- It does not prove the borrowed measure was itself exact; it proves only that it agreed with the valley.
- It says nothing about when the fault began (1 May 1897), the money involved, or why the glass was short (Ammon's manufacturing).
- It is also one short run, and Keddie himself treated it as a suspicion, not a proof.

### D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream

**Three independent reasons.**

1. **The ledger's "station gain"** (Kyle r23, Teague's own entries; Rundle's press note). The theft theory needs the cream to reach Larrow Green *light*. Every year, 1897 onward, it arrived *heavier* than credited: 5¼% more fat, "as usual." A thief removing cream cannot deliver more fat than he recorded. Sill (r14), the buttermaker, confirms from the churn that the Hollow cream "always came down heavy."
2. **The 1911 interval** (Orra r24, Falke r22, Keddie's book). The same man, under the same supervision, recorded 3.80 for nine weeks with a borrowed measure and 3.61 before and after. His honesty did not change with the glass.
3. **The 1924 new glass** (Keddie's averages; Selby Vose). After the condemnation, the same Keddie tested the same patrons at 3.80 for all of 1924. A thief would not have stopped overnight because a pipette was replaced, and the rise came before any petition.

*(Supporting, but not needed: the 1908 committee watched the tests made and "found no dishonesty of any kind" (Grigg r15). The patron worst hurt was Keddie's own brother-in-law Emmet (Duncan, Hazel).)*

**Why these are independent.** Each rests on a different record kept by different people:
- Reason 1 is the Larrow Green office's bookkeeping, plus Sill's own work at the churn. It involves no station record at all.
- Reason 2 is a nine-week episode in the station test book, dated by Orra's private diary.
- Reason 3 is the station's post-1923 results on entirely different glass.

None depends on another, and none depends on Ivy's measurements. **All three would still hold if the glassware had never been measured in 1958.** The station gain in particular was in ink from 1897.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question it? No.**
- Wilbur Teague wrote the "station gain … five and a quarter per cent, as usual" line every year of his service. Kyle says he "never asked anybody anything in forty years."
- At the 1925 hearing Teague insinuated cream theft (Frick r13), a charge his own ledger entries refute.
- He later claimed to have invented the line in 1905, though the column runs from 1897, and he misdescribed its base.
- There is no record of him ever asking what the gain meant.

### D3. Did the manager know the tests ran low before 1923?

**Answer: No. As far as the sources show, Selby Vose did not know, although the information was in front of him every year.**

**The case against knowledge.**
- Kyle, who sat outside his door for 25 years, says Vose "read the totals and he never read the lines above them." He would put his thumb on the bottom figure and sign.
- Vose's actions show he believed the milk, not the test, was at fault. In 1919 he signed the water circular accusing the Hollow families, and in 1925 he pressed the water theory before the board. A man who knew the station's tests ran low had no reason to blame water. Watering cannot make the fat reach the creamery heavy; the line he signed showed exactly that.
- Even Strawn's 1923 condemnation drew no inference in the office; it was treated like "a broken window."

**The limit.** "Did not know" is an inference from conduct and from Kyle's testimony. There is no document recording his state of mind. What is certain is that the station gain was on every sheet he signed from 1911, so the fact was **available** to him, and he did not **see** it. The accurate judgment is negligence, not knowing concealment. Nothing in the sources supports the stronger charge.

## Section F — Theory

### F1. The cause of the shortfall

**The theory.** The Ostrey Hollow shortfall was produced by one lot of glass.

1. **What was wrong.** The six dozen pipettes that Ammon & Sons of Tarnet invoiced on 14 April 1897 were marked 17.6 c.c. but delivered on average only 16.72 c.c., which is **95%** of the charge. The bottle necks are ruled for a full charge, so every sample carried 95% of its true fat into the bottle.
2. **Why that size.** The error was **proportional**. True milk of 3.80% read 3.80 × 0.95 = **3.61**: a gap of **0.19 points**, a loss of one part in twenty. The gap was the same in every month and season because it came from a ratio, not from the feed.
3. **Why it began when it did.** The glass went into use on **1 May 1897**. Before that, the 1896 season was tested on borrowed Larrow Green glass and averaged 3.80.
4. **Why it ended when it did.** Merle Strawn condemned the glass on **12 June 1923**, and new glass was in use from July. The 1924 average returned to **3.80**.
5. **Why nothing else had to change.** The farms, cows, hay, families, scales and tester were the same throughout. The milk itself was always 3.80%; only the reading of it was short.
6. **Where the missing fat went.** The fat was never lost physically. It went down in the cream to Larrow Green, where honest testing found all of it. The ledger recorded the excess every year as "station gain, 5¼%, as usual": 76,000 lb over 1898–1922, worth $19,000.
7. **Corroboration.** The glass-swap experiment of 1911 (8 May–10 July, 3.80) and Jerome Cudd's summer tests of the same herds at Alder Corners (about 3.80) both confirm it.

**The three competing explanations, and what defeats each.**

1. **Added water**
   - **Advanced by** Selby Vose, the manager (the 1919 circular and his case at the 1925 hearing), with Sill agreeing.
   - **Defeated by:**
     - Strawn's lactometer tests at every visit from 1908 never found added water (Falke).
     - Cudd's cheese yields showed the Bottom milk whole.
     - Water cannot make the cream reach Larrow Green *heavier* in fat than credited.
     - Under payment by fat, watering earns nothing: weight goes up exactly as the test goes down.
     - The same families tested 3.80 in 1911 and 1924. Sill's "nine families all got honest in one summer" fails because the glass, not the families, changed.
2. **Cream theft by the tester**
   - **Advanced by** Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper, by insinuation at the 1925 hearing (Frick). It was also the valley's popular view (Rundle, before her retraction) and was urged by "an officer of the Association" (Falke).
   - **Defeated by:**
     - The station gain: the cream arrived heavier, not lighter.
     - The 1911 interval: the same man, recording higher on borrowed glass.
     - 1924: the same man, recording 3.80 on new glass.
     - The 1908 committee watched the tests made and found no dishonesty.
3. **Wet bog hay on Hessel Bottom**
   - **Advanced by** Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee ("remedy lies with the land"), repeated in his 1919 letter, and by Dr. Roland Kepp's 1926 paper. Falke initially conceded it as a partial factor.
   - **Defeated by:**
     - Bulletin 214 itself: at most 0.05 in winter and nothing in summer, against a flat 0.19 in every season.
     - The summer 1911 interval and Cudd's summer tests at 3.80.
     - 1924: the same hay, on new glass, at 3.80.

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware? No.**
- The crate lay in the loft over the intake room, about twenty feet above two of the board's three sittings, and was never brought down or produced.
- Strawn's 1923 notebook recording the condemnation was one of the four exhibits (Kyle, Frick). Nobody laid it beside the test book.
- The board found "no cause established."

**What the tester believed.**
- Ansel Keddie suspected **the glass**. In the summer of 1911 he came home saying "it was something in the glass," and said it twice (Duncan). He set a question mark against the 1911 figures in his book.
- He never put the suspicion in writing, because he would not record what he could not prove.
- He denied any wrongdoing, and Frick believed him. Frick's recollection that Keddie privately blamed the patrons conflicts with Keddie's own letter and his family's account, and is rejected.
- He died in 1934 without knowing he had been right.

## Section G — Summary

### G1. The whole history in 150 words

In 1897 the Larrow Valley creamery's Ostrey Hollow station began testing milk with six dozen Ammon & Sons pipettes that delivered only 95% of their marked 17.6 c.c. Patrons were paid on those tests, so readings of a true 3.80% came out 3.61, year after year. The ledger quietly recorded the missing 76,000 lb of butterfat ($19,000) as "station gain." For a generation people blamed water, a thieving tester, or wet hay. A committee, a college bulletin, a veterinarian and a 1925 arbitration each looked in the wrong place. The arbitration awarded $3,800, and the tester, Ansel Keddie, was dismissed. The inspector who condemned the glass in 1923 drew no conclusion. In 1958 Keddie's granddaughter Ivy found the crate in the station loft, measured it, and proved the glass was at fault. It took eight separate records: invoice, weigh book, diary, ledger, cheese-factory tests, inspector's notebook, test book and crate.
