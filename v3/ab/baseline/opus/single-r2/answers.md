## Section A — Reconstruction

### 1. The Association, Article VII and the station at Ostrey Hollow (1889–1897)

**Who:** the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association; Gideon Vose, its founder; Ansel Keddie, the station tester; Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green.

The Association was organized by valley farmers meeting at Larrow Green in 1889. Gideon Vose was its first president (1889–1911) and wrote its by-laws. He died in 1917. His son Selby managed the Association from 1913 to 1938. The main creamery was built at Larrow Green in 1891.

**Article VII** made every patron's pay depend on the pounds of butterfat his milk was found by test to contain, not on its weight. It also made the test at his own station the one that governed. That article is the hinge of the whole history: under payment by weight, a faulty test would have cost nobody anything.

The year Article VII was adopted **cannot be determined**:
- Rundle says 1891, Effie Loomis 1892 and Nyle Grigg 1893. None of them witnessed it (Loomis became secretary only in 1901).
- The printed by-laws carry no date.
- The 1889–92 minute book was lost in an office removal in 1938.

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, **seven miles** up the valley, chiefly to serve the wet farms of Hessel Bottom. Keddie went up in 1895. The station opened on **4 May 1896**, the date its test book begins. Keddie was tester and separator man there for **29 seasons (1896–1924)**.

The routine at the station:
1. Milk was weighed in and sampled.
2. It was tested by the Babcock method: a **17.6 c.c.** pipette charge went into a bottle whose neck is ruled for exactly that charge.
3. It was separated. The cream was hauled down to Larrow Green and the skim went back to the farmer.

In its first season the station had no glass of its own and tested with glass **borrowed from Larrow Green**:
- Sill says he lent it from his own bench, and one piece came back chipped.
- Keddie's first leaf reads "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green."
- Orra Keddie's diary for 1 May 1897 says they are "done borrowing of Larrow Green."
- Keddie's unsent letter says the Ashlin creamery. That is a slip against his own leaf and the two independent witnesses.

With that glass the **1896 tests averaged 3.80**. The station's own outfit went into use on 1 May 1897 (Story 2).

How much milk the station took in during its first season **cannot be determined**. Nym found no 1896 intake figure, because the sheets went onto the creamery's own books. Sill's "about 900,000 lb" and Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand" are unwritten recollections, and they disagree. A normal later year was about **1,600,000 lb** (40,000,000 lb over 25 years).

**Documents:** r02 (Loomis), r03 (Keddie), r12 (Rundle), r13 (Frick), r14 (Sill), r15 (Grigg), r21 (Oram), r22 (Falke), r23 (Kyle), r24 (Orra Keddie).

### 2. A crate of short glass: Ammon & Sons of Tarnet, 1897–1923

**Who:** Ammon & Sons, a dairy-glass house at Tarnet; the Association; Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector.

On **14 April 1897** Ammon & Sons invoiced the Association "for the Ostrey Hollow station":

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| 6 dozen milk-test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c., at 35¢ | $25.20 |
| 12 dozen test bottles, at 22¢ | $31.68 |
| Acid measure | $0.90 |
| Four-bottle hand tester | $18.00 |
| Freight | $2.15 |
| **Total** | **$77.93** |

The line items add to $77.93, which is also the figure the facsimile and Nym print. Junia Ammon's written-out "seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents", and the *Chronicle*'s $77.39, are a transposition. The outfit went into use on **1 May 1897**.

The **72 pipettes** came from one lot, and the lot ran short:
- The nine that survived to 1958 delivered on average **16.72 c.c.** against the 17.6 marked on them.
- That is **0.88 c.c. short, or 95%** of the charge.
- A short charge into a bottle ruled for a full one reads low in exactly that proportion, on every test.

Why the lot was short **cannot be determined**. The firm's works books burned in 1931. Junia Ammon guesses at a reground mold, which would make a lot "consistently wrong" and so pass any check that compares one piece with another. The *Chronicle*'s charge that the firm sold the lot knowingly has no evidence behind it.

The glass stayed in use for 26 years:
- **12 June 1923:** Strawn, inspector on the Ordell circuit since 1907, ran the pipettes against his calibrated burette. They would not deliver their mark, so he condemned the whole lot and told the manager to order new.
- **28 June 1923:** the Association ordered six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles.
- **2 July 1923:** Ammon replied that "our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name."
- **July 1923 onward:** the new glass was in use. The 1924 station average was **3.80**.

Nobody connected the condemnation with the 26 years of tests already in the book, and Strawn wrote down the condemnation and nothing more. The crate went up the ladder into the **loft over the intake room**. It sat there under a tarpaulin until February 1958; the cream hauler's son had his hand on it many times. In her 1949 chapter Junia Ammon says she regrets the firm's thin 1923 letter more than the accident itself.

**Documents:** r04 (Ivy Keddie), r06 (Strawn), r07 (Nym), r11 (Vaught), r19 (*Chronicle*), r20 (Junia Ammon), r21 (Oram), r22 (Falke), r23 (Kyle), r24 (Orra Keddie).

### 3. The Brants of Hessel Bottom and the weigh book

**Who:** Emmet Brant (1854–1929), his son Roy, Roy's daughter Hazel, and Emmet's sister Orra, who was Ansel Keddie's wife.

Hessel Bottom is a springy, sedge-grown wet meadow along the creek below Ostrey Hollow. **Six farms** worked it. Roy, Hazel, Grigg's 1908 report and Kepp all say six; Effie Loomis's seven stands alone. More than one family could live on a farm: Hazel counts nine families on the six farms. Emmet had the largest place and the largest herd (38 cows), and he hauled more milk to the station than any other patron.

On **1 January 1897** Emmet began a private weigh book. That date comes from Frick's exhibit list, Nym and Duncan. Roy's 1898 and Hazel's 1900 are later memories, and Hazel says herself that she has no memory for years. Emmet weighed his milk on his own scale and wrote the station's intake weight beside it. The two always agreed, which rules out short weighing.

The **1910 leaf** reads "200,000 lb even", scales agreeing, paid at a test of **3.42**, with fat at 24¢ that year:
- Emmet was credited with **6,840 lb** of fat.
- His true test was 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = **3.60**, which is **7,200 lb**.
- He was therefore **360 lb** short, worth **$86.40** at 24¢, in that one year.

Orra Brant married Ansel Keddie in **1889**. Her diary and its preface give her birth year as 1866. So the man later suspected of shorting the patrons was married to the sister of the patron who lost the most, and Emmet ate at his table on Sundays.

The quarrel between them:
- Emmet was among the first to complain, in 1902.
- In June 1919 the water circular came. Ansel carried it round himself rather than let another man do it. Emmet asked him why he had carried it if he did not believe it, and never entered the Keddie house again.
- Emmet's name was on the January 1925 petition.
- He died in the spring of 1929, still unreconciled.

The weigh book was **exhibit 3** at the 1925 arbitration; Emmet lent it for the purpose. Roy kept it the same way after 1929. In 1958 he **lent** it (lent, not left) to Ivy Keddie. Hazel had it from Roy's death in 1962 and gave it to the Historical Rooms at Ashlin in 1963.

The family relations:
- Roy (Emmet's son) and Duncan Keddie (Orra's son, born 1894) were first cousins.
- Their daughters Hazel and Ivy were therefore **second cousins**.
- Hazel alone calls Orra a *half*-sister. Roy and Duncan, one the son of each sibling, say sister.

**Documents:** r02, r04, r07, r09 (Roy Brant), r13, r15, r17 (Hazel Brant), r18 (Duncan Keddie), r24.

### 4. The hay theory: the 1908 committee, Bulletin 214 and Dr. Kepp (1908–1959)

**Who:** Nyle Grigg, a Larrow Green farmer who chaired the 1908 committee; the State Agricultural College at Farland; Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S., of Ashlin; Orin Falke, county agent.

Complaints that Ostrey Hollow milk tested under the rest of the valley began in **1902** and grew every year. In January 1908 the Association named a committee of three under Grigg, who was not a patron of the station.

The committee sampled the Hessel Bottom herds **through January, February and March 1908**. Grigg's 1919 covering letter says they sampled "the whole of 1908", but the report printed underneath it says January to March. The quoted report outranks his memory.

What the committee did:
- visited all six farms and drew and sealed its own samples;
- saw the cans hauled and weighed, and watched the test made;
- tried the station scales against a known weight, and the patrons' scales against the station's;
- looked into the water supply at each barn.

It found the milk whole, the scales honest and **no dishonesty anywhere**. It put the low test down to the late-cut sedge hay that was the Bottom's winter feed, and its report closed the question for eleven years.

In 1911 the College ran a feeding trial on bog hay and published it as **Bulletin No. 214**. Its summary, which Falke prints verbatim, says bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the test by "not more than **five hundredths** of one per cent in winter, and by **nothing** that could be measured on summer pasture."

Grigg in 1919 and Kepp in his 1926 Farm Club paper both misquote it as "two tenths in the winter feeding." Kepp added a field figure of a fifth of a point for wet bottoms and put the Hollow gap at 0.21. He brushed aside the 1911 interval as "six weeks" of summer figures, and said it was "not good sense to go looking for a second cause in a building."

Why the theory fails:
- The real deficiency was **0.19 in every month**, summer and winter alike. Bulletin 214's maximum is about a quarter of that and allows nothing in summer; Ivy Keddie called it "four times too small."
- The same herds' summer milk tested **3.80** at Alder Corners (Story 8).
- The tests jumped in 1911 and again in 1923 with the hay unchanged.
- Grigg sampled only in winter, so his committee could never have seen that the gap persisted on summer grass.

In his 1959 report Falke at first allowed hay as a "real if partial factor." He **withdrew** that concession once he had read Bulletin 214 itself.

**Documents:** r04, r07, r09, r11, r15 (Grigg), r16 (Kepp), r17, r22 (Falke), r24.

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### 5. The nine weeks of 1911

**Who:** Ansel Keddie; his family; Merle Strawn.

On **8 May 1911** the station's own measuring glass broke. The date is on Keddie's summary leaf, and Orra's diary for that day reads "A. home cross about a broken measure." A borrowed measure was used until Keddie's own replacement was back on **10 July 1911**. That is 63 days, exactly **nine weeks**, as Keddie's leaf, Roy, Vaught and Falke all say. Teague's and Kepp's "six weeks" is rejected, because the dated entries fix the length.

For those nine weeks the station's tests stood at **3.80**, the same figure the borrowed glass of 1896 had given. On the day Keddie's own glass came back they fell to **3.61** again. The herds, pasture, farms, scales, acid, tester and supervision were all the same; **the only thing that changed was the glass**. Keddie wrote in his book: "Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."

His son Duncan, then seventeen, remembers him coming home that summer and saying, twice, that it was "something in the glass." He never wrote it down: all he had was a suspicion about a piece of glass, set against an outfit he had been taught to trust.

Where the borrowed measure came from **cannot be settled**:
- Ivy Keddie says Larrow Green, which matches the 1896 precedent.
- Roy Brant says the "state man left his own measure that June", which could not cover a borrowing that began on 8 May.
- Teague says it "came up from town."

Strawn did visit in June 1911, watched a test and wrote "Nothing here to note." All that matters is that the borrowed glass was a different glass and read true.

The interval is a clean natural experiment:
- It shows the low figures were produced by the station's own apparatus, not by the cows, the land, water or the tester's hands.
- Because it fell in the flush of the season, it also answers Kepp: summer tests before 8 May and after 10 July were still 3.61.
- It does not show which piece of the outfit was at fault, how short it was, or how much was lost. Those needed the 1958 measurement and the ledger figures.

The entry was before the 1925 board, inside exhibit 1 (the test book). It then sat unread for **47 years**, until Vaught and Ivy Keddie called it decisive in Bulletin 471.

**Documents:** r03 (Keddie), r04, r05 (Teague), r06 (Strawn), r09, r11, r16, r18, r22, r24.

### 6. "Station gain, 5¼%, as usual": the ledger that knew (1897–1925)

**Who:** Wilbur Teague, the Association's bookkeeper; Bertram Kyle, clerk at the outer desk; Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green 1893–1934; Selby Vose, the manager.

From 1897 the Association's station account for Ostrey Hollow carried three figures every year:
1. the pounds of fat **credited** to the patrons on the station test;
2. the pounds of fat that **reached Larrow Green** in the station's cream;
3. the difference, entered as "**station gain**" and followed every year by the bookkeeper's words "five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account."

Teague quotes the **1898** entry: 57,760 lb credited, 60,800 received, a gain of 3,040. Those figures fit the short glass exactly:
- A normal year of 1,600,000 lb of milk at 3.61% is 57,760 lb; at 3.80% it is 60,800 lb.
- 3,040 is 5% of the fat received and 5.26% (the ledger's "5¼%") of the fat credited.

Over the 25 full years 1898–1922 (**40,000,000 lb** of milk):

| | Pounds of fat |
|---|---|
| Credited to patrons (at 3.61%) | 1,444,000 |
| Received at Larrow Green (at 3.80%) | 1,520,000 |
| Gain | **76,000** |

At Larrow Green, Sill saw the same thing from the other end: the Hollow cream "always came in heavier than the sheet said." He put it down to the nature of cream.

So the fat the patrons were not credited with never went missing. It travelled down to Larrow Green in the cream and was absorbed into the Association's general account under a name. Kyle copied the line in a fair hand for 25 years, and it was read out at every annual meeting. He says it never crossed his mind, or anyone's, to wonder at it: "a figure with a name on it has been explained."

The men who handled the line:
- **Vose** signed the sheets by putting his thumb on the bottom figure.
- **Teague**, by Kyle's account, "never asked anybody anything."
- In his 1948 memoir Teague claims he invented the line in 1905. His own quotation of the 1898 entry contradicts that, and the ledger column runs from 1897.
- At the 1925 hearing Teague insinuated that the tester was stealing ("Cream is portable"). His own ledger showed the creamery receiving *more* fat than was credited, not less.

The $3,800 award was charged to the same station account. The line is the most complete refutation of both theft and water on record. Rundle retracted his theft theory, in a note added in press to his 1959 reprint, after he saw it.

**Documents:** r04, r05 (Teague), r07 (Nym), r11, r12 (Rundle), r13, r14 (Sill), r23 (Kyle).

### 7. Water, theft and the arbitration of 1925

**Who:** Selby Vose, the manager; Wilbur Teague; Alonzo Frick, the Ashlin attorney who chaired the board; Ansel Keddie; the Hessel Bottom patrons.

**The 1919 circular.** In June 1919 a circular went out over Selby Vose's name warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons against adding water to their milk. Kyle typed it. Hazel and Vira say it went to nine families; Orra's diary says Ansel carried it by hand to eleven. The sources cannot settle that count. The circular split Ansel from his brother-in-law Emmet for good. The water theory had nothing under it: Strawn tested with the lactometer on every visit from 1908 and never found added water, and neither did Grigg's committee or Jerome Cudd.

**The hearing.** The patrons petitioned in **January 1925**. A board of arbitration under Frick sat from **17 March to 4 May 1925**. The first sitting was at Ashlin. The second and third were held in the station weigh room, where the board saw the scales tried and a test made (with the new 1923 glass). On 17 March Keddie stated that he had tested 31 times a week for 29 seasons and never taken cream.

Three theories were argued before the board:
- **water**, from Vose;
- **theft**, from Teague, by insinuation ("Cream is portable");
- **hay**, from the 1908 report.

The four exhibits, listed by both Frick and Kyle, were:
1. the station test book, 4 May 1896–31 December 1924;
2. the station receiving book;
3. Emmet Brant's weigh book;
4. Strawn's **1923 circuit notebook**, which held the condemnation entry.

Nobody laid the receiving book beside the notebook, and the crate sat about twenty feet above two of the sittings. Effie Loomis records that the crate was never mentioned. The patrons' claim rested on Teague's own figures: 40,000,000 lb of milk and fat at 25¢.

**The outcome.**
- Keddie was dismissed on **30 April 1925**.
- On **4 May** the board found no cause established and awarded the patrons **$3,800** "without admission." That figure is fixed by Frick as chairman, Loomis as secretary and Kyle; Vira's and Oram's $3,500 is rejected.
- In 1926 Vose called the award "a generous settlement of an unproved claim" and said "tests came up when the looking began." In fact they came up in July 1923, when the glass was replaced, eighteen months before the petition.

Keddie (1861–1934) worked as a carpenter up and down the Hollow. He died in February 1934, having said once that "a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name." Roy Brant never went to another Association meeting.

**Documents:** r01 (Toland), r02, r03, r05, r06, r08 (Vose), r12, r13 (Frick), r17, r18, r21, r23, r24.

### 8. The loft, the cheese factory and Bulletin 471 (1958–1970)

**Who:** Ivy Keddie (born 1931), Duncan's daughter and Ansel's granddaughter, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland; Professor Leland Vaught; Rosalie Cudd of Vessey County.

**Recovering the evidence.** In **February 1958** Ivy climbed into the loft over the old intake room and found the crate under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff. She took it to Farland. On **9 March** she measured the **nine** surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette. Their mean delivery was **16.72 c.c.** against the 17.6 marked, a ratio of **0.95**. She also:
- borrowed the Brant weigh book from Roy;
- found Strawn's 1923 notebook in the Farland library, where his notebooks had been since 1947;
- obtained the Alder Corners figures from Rosalie Cudd, Jerome Cudd's daughter.

**Alder Corners.** Jerome Cudd's cheese factory stood over the ridge, four miles of switchback road that was shut from November to April. It was built in 1913. From **1915 to 1930 (16 seasons)**, four Hessel Bottom men hauled their milk there each summer, June to mid-September, and Cudd tested it with his own new glassware. The same herds averaged about **3.80** every year (1919: 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82), while the station credited them 3.61 until the 1923 replacement. Cudd never found water.

**Bulletin 471.** The State College published Bulletin No. 471 in **October 1958** under Vaught's and Ivy's names. Its conclusion:
- The station's pipettes delivered one part in twenty short, from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923.
- A short charge into a bottle ruled for a full one reads low in the same proportion, so herds are shorted in proportion to their richness. A herd recorded at 4.56 was short 0.24; one recorded at 3.42 was short 0.18.
- The error was invisible from inside the plant, because the pipette is the plant's own standard.
- The true test was 3.80 against a recorded 3.61. That left **76,000 lb** of fat uncredited over 1898–1922, worth **$19,000**, of which the $3,800 award was **one-fifth**.
- No milk was watered and no cream was stolen.

The body of the bulletin carries figures that its own conclusion overrides: five dozen pipettes, a mean of 16.27, a start in 1898, 118,000 lb. Vaught notes himself that the 3.905 these give cannot square with the 1924 average of 3.80.

**Reception.**
- The *Ashlin Chronicle* ran the story on 3 October 1958, with many slips.
- Ivy gave a talk in 1959.
- Rundle retracted his theft theory in a note added in press, and county agent Falke withdrew his hay concession in his 1959 report to the county board.
- The test book and crate went to the Historical Rooms at Ashlin in 1959, the Brant book in 1963, and the Alder Corners book to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970.

Ansel had been dead 24 years.

**Documents:** r04, r09, r10 (Rosalie Cudd), r11 (Vaught), r12, r17, r18, r19 (*Chronicle*), r22.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie and Ansel Keddie: granddaughter.**

The derivation:
- Duncan Keddie, born 1894, is the son of Ansel and Orra Keddie (his own notes; Orra's diary names Duncan as their son).
- Duncan says Ivy, born 1931, is his daughter, "which makes her my father's granddaughter."
- Hazel says "Ivy is Duncan's girl," and Oram calls her "the granddaughter."

Pointing elsewhere is "great-niece," found in the *Chronicle* (1958) and on page 2 of Falke's 1959 report. Those two are not independent. The *Chronicle* piece was "put together in a day and a half from a press release and two telephone calls," and Falke wrote after it had run. Falke then withdrew the error himself: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter." Her own father's written statement settles it.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant: second cousins.**

The derivation:
1. Emmet Brant and Orra (Brant) Keddie were siblings.
2. Roy Brant (Emmet's son) and Duncan Keddie (Orra's son) were therefore first cousins.
3. Hazel (Roy's daughter) and Ivy (Duncan's daughter) are therefore second cousins.

Roy, Hazel, Duncan and Rundle's 1959 footnote all say so. "First cousin once removed" (Vira Toland, Cleve Oram) is wrong. Both of them are repeating valley talk, both get other kinship facts wrong, and the derivation from the three family members breaks the tie.

What the sources leave open:
- **Full or half siblings.** Hazel says Orra was Emmet's *half*-sister, "by his father's second wife." Roy calls her Emmet's full sister, and Duncan says his mother was "Emmet Brant's sister, the younger of the two." Their birth years, 1854 and 1866, fit either. Orra's pre-1889 papers do not survive, so the question cannot be finally settled, though the weight (the two sons, one from each side) is on full siblings. If they were half siblings, Roy and Duncan were half first cousins and Hazel and Ivy half second cousins. The degree is second cousin either way.
- **Orra's birth year.** Duncan says 1868; her own diary (64 in 1930, 82 at death in 1948) gives 1866.
- **Hazel's own birth year** is never stated.

**B3. Selby Vose's father: Gideon Vose.**

Selby calls himself Gideon's son (1926). So do Effie Loomis ("son of the late president"), Duncan Keddie and Bertram Kyle, who says outright that the nephew story "is nonsense." Only Vira Toland and Dell Rundle say "nephew." The man's own statement and the secretary's record outrank a recollection and a jubilee pamphlet.

Why it matters:
- Gideon organized the Association, wrote its by-laws (including Article VII, which made the station test govern pay) and was president from 1889 to 1911. That covers the opening of the station and the 1897 purchase of the Ammon outfit.
- Selby therefore managed, from 1913 to 1938, a system his father had built. He never located the fault in it:
  - He signed the station-gain sheets every year without reading the lines.
  - He put his name to the 1919 water circular.
  - In 1926 he called the award "a generous settlement of an unproved claim."
  - He offered his father's Jerseys testing 4.56 in 1910 as proof of good testing. That is exactly the recorded figure Ivy later showed to be short by 0.24 (true 4.80). This is my inference: it assumes the Vose herd was tested on the same glass, which is likely but not stated.
- His explanations always put the fault on the patrons, never on his father's apparatus or accounts.
- Establishing him as son, not nephew, also removes a link in the valley gossip that tied the Voses to the Keddies (B4).

**B4. The Vose and Keddie families: no relationship.**

Only two narrators claim a connection:
- Vira Toland says Orra Keddie and "old Mrs. Vose" were first cousins.
- Cleve Oram says the families "were cousins of some sort," from his mother's mother, adding "I could not draw you the line on paper."

Both are hearsay, and both narrators get other kinship facts wrong: Vira calls Selby a nephew and Ivy Hazel's "first cousin once removed," and Oram repeats the latter.

Against them, Duncan Keddie, Orra's son, writes that "there was no connection whatever between us and the Voses." He traces the rumor to confusion with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Gideon either. He says his mother, who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection," never once named a Vose. Nothing in Orra's diary mentions one.

A negative cannot be proved absolutely, but the best-placed witness denies it and the positive claims are unsourced. So no relationship is established.


# Answer Sheet — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math

### C1

**(a) Proportion.** The station tests read **5% low (one part in twenty): recorded = 0.95 × true.**
- Ivy Keddie measured the nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette. Their mean delivery was **16.72 c.c.** against the **17.6 c.c.** marked on them.
- 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95**.
- A Babcock bottle's neck is ruled for a full 17.6 c.c. charge, so a charge 5% short puts 5% less fat into the bottle and reads 5% low.
- Cross-check: 3.61 ÷ 3.80 = 0.95. The 1896 and 1911 borrowed-glass tests, the post-1923 tests and the Alder Corners tests all read 3.80.
- The bulletin body's 16.27 c.c. gives 0.9244, which is rejected (see E1).

**(b) True average and shortfall.** The true average was **3.80**; the recorded tests were **0.19 points** short.
- True average = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**.
- Shortfall = 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19** (equivalently, 3.80 × 0.05).

**(c) Uncredited fat.** **76,000 lb.**
- Full delivery years 1898–1922 (25 years): **40,000,000 lb** of milk.
- 40,000,000 × 0.0019 = **76,000 lb** of butterfat not credited.
- Cross-check: 40,000,000 × (0.0380 − 0.0361) = 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000.

**(d) Money.** **$19,000**, of which the patrons received **one-fifth (20%)**.
- 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000**, using the average fat price of 25¢ given by Kyle, Loomis and Ivy.
- The award was **$3,800**; 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **1/5 = 20%**.

### C2 — Emmet Brant, 1910 (200,000 lb at a recorded 3.42)

**(a) Credited:** 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of butterfat.

**(b) Owed but not credited:**
- True test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = **3.60**.
- 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb.
- 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb** (equivalently, 0.18 points × 200,000 lb).

**(c) Worth that year:** 360 × $0.24 (Roy's figure for 1910) = **$86.40**. At the 25-year average price of 25¢ it would be $90.00, but the year-specific price is the better one.

### C3 — Full delivery years 1898–1922 (40,000,000 lb of milk)

**(a) Credited to patrons:** 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb** of butterfat.

**(b) Reached Larrow Green in the station cream:** 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lb**.
- This assumes, as the ledger's "station gain" and Sill's observation show, that the fat arrived at its true value.
- Check against Teague's 1898 entry: 1,600,000 lb of milk → 57,760 credited and 60,800 received, exactly 3.61% and 3.80%.

**(c) Difference:** **76,000 lb**. As a percentage it depends on the base:
- Of the fat **credited**: 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.26%** (exactly 1/19, or 5 5⁄19 %). That is the ledger's "five and a quarter per cent."
- Of the fat **actually delivered and received**: 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5.00%** exactly.
- The patrons lost 5% of their true fat; the creamery's gain was 5¼% of what it had credited. Vaught's bulletin body, which calls the 5¼% a percentage "of fat actually delivered," has the base wrong.

### C4

**(a) Invoice total: $77.93.** The line items: $25.20 + $31.68 + $0.90 + $18.00 + $2.15 = **$77.93**. The written-out "seventy-seven thirty-nine" and the *Chronicle*'s $77.39 are a transposition.

**(b) Pipettes: 72.** The invoice lists 6 dozen = **72 pipettes** at 35¢ (= $25.20).
- Shortfall per pipette = 17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88 c.c.** on average.
- That is measured on the nine survivors, which were consistent with one another.
- The individual shortfall of the other 63 cannot be determined, because they no longer exist.

### C5

**(a) Duration of use: 26 years, 1 month and 11 days**, from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923.
- 26 years plus 42 days = 9,537 days (with five leap days: 1904, 1908, 1912, 1916, 1920).
- The **63 days** (8 May–10 July 1911) when a borrowed glass was used should be deducted: about 9,474 days actually in service.

**The money calculation runs over 25 full delivery years (1898–1922).** The two numbers differ because:
- 1897 (from 1 May) and 1923 (to 12 June) were **part-seasons**.
- The 40,000,000-lb milk figure compiled for the arbitration (Teague's figures, got out by Kyle) covers only the whole years 1898–1922. No separate intake figures for the two part-years are given, so they cannot be priced from the sources.

The effect on the $19,000: it slightly *overstates* the loss for the nine correct weeks of 1911, and *omits* the part-years. So it is an approximation, probably on the low side.

**(b) Milk received in the first season: cannot be determined from the sources.**
- Nym states that no 1896 intake figure exists; the first season was carried on the creamery's books and never filed with the station papers.
- Sill recalls "about 900,000 lb" and Kyle "a good eleven hundred thousand." Both are unwritten recollections, Kyle's explicitly secondhand ("not in any book I ever ruled"), and they disagree by 200,000 lb.
- The later average of 1.6 million lb a year does not fix a first season.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment.**

*When and how long.* From **8 May to 10 July 1911**: 63 days, exactly **nine weeks**. The dates are on Keddie's summary leaf and the breakage is in Orra's diary; "six weeks" (Teague, Kepp) is rejected.

*Design and result.*
- **Variable:** the measuring glass, borrowed instead of the station's own.
- **Held constant:** the tester, herds, farms, pasture and feed regime, scales, acid, bottles, method, place and supervision.
- **Result:** the tests rose from **3.61 to 3.80** and fell back to **3.61** on the day the station's own glass returned. It was a clean on–off–on reversal.

*What it establishes that a calculation cannot.* It shows **causation inside the real station**: the deficiency travelled with the station's own apparatus, not with the milk or the man.
- A calculation (16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 → 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80) shows only that a short pipette *would* produce the gap.
- It cannot show that nothing else was at work, or that the station's own instruments were the ones in use.
- The interval also rules out water, hay and theft without any arithmetic, since every one of those factors was held constant. It meets Kepp's "summer figures" objection too, because the summer weeks just before 8 May and just after 10 July read 3.61.

*What it does not establish.*
- **Which** piece of the outfit was faulty (pipettes, bottles or acid measure).
- **How** short it was, or that the error was proportional.
- How long the fault had lasted, or would last.
- The size of the loss.
- That the borrowed glass was itself perfectly true (only that it agreed with 1896 and, later, Alder Corners).
- Anything about intent or knowledge.
- Nine summer weeks cannot by themselves prove the effect held in winter; that comes from the constant year-round gap and the 1923 change.

Those needed the 1958 burette measurement, the ledger and the Alder Corners figures.

**D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream.**

The hypothesis (Teague's "Cream is portable," Rundle's pamphlet, Vira Toland, Oram's father) is that Keddie skimmed off cream and wrote low tests to hide it. Independent reasons against:

1. **The ledger (Teague and Kyle; Sill from the creamery end).** Every year the cream reaching Larrow Green carried **more** fat than the patrons were credited with, "station gain, 5¼%, as usual." Theft would make the creamery receive *less* fat than the tests implied. The Hollow cream "always came in heavier than the sheet said" (Sill).
2. **The 1911 interval (Keddie's own test book; Orra's diary).** The same man, under no different supervision, recorded 3.80 for nine weeks with borrowed glass and 3.61 before and after with his own.
3. **The 1923 replacement (Strawn's notebook and 1924 book).** Once the pipettes were condemned and new glass came in (July 1923), the tests rose to 3.80 while Keddie was still the tester. That was before the petition and before anyone accused him.
4. **Outside tests of the same milk (Rosalie Cudd's Alder Corners book).** The same herds' milk tested about 3.80 at another plant with other glass. So the milk was 3.80 milk and the station's figure was an instrument reading.
5. **Direct observation.** Grigg's 1908 committee watched the test made and found "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter." Strawn watched a test in June 1911 ("Nothing here to note"). Frick's board watched a test in 1925.

*Why they are independent.* Each comes from a different record, kept by different people, for different purposes, in different places:
- the creamery's accounts at Larrow Green (the bookkeeper and the buttermaker);
- the tester's own station book;
- a state inspector's circuit notebook;
- a cheese factory's book in another county;
- farmer and inspector eyewitnesses.

None was derived from another, and none depends on the 1958 measurement.

*Which would still hold without 1958.* All of 1, 2, 3 and 5. The **ledger** (1) is decisive on its own: it existed from 1897 and was read out at every annual meeting.

*Did the bookkeeper question the ledger entries that refute his accusation?* **No, so far as the sources show.**
- Teague wrote "as usual" under the gain every year.
- Kyle, who sat beside the work for 25 years, says Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years."
- At the 1925 hearing Teague insinuated theft ("Cream is portable") without ever pointing out that his own ledger showed the creamery receiving surplus fat.
- His 1948 memoir claims he raised "the matter" with Vose in 1916. That claim is uncorroborated, contradicted by Kyle, and comes from a memoir that is unreliable elsewhere (1905 vs. its own 1898 entry, 44M lb, 3.16, "six weeks"). Even on his own account, he never connected the gain to the accusation.

**D3. Did the manager know, before the condemnation, that the station's tests ran low?**

**Yes, he knew the Hollow's tests ran low compared with the rest of the valley.** There is **no evidence that he knew they were wrong**, that is, that the patrons were being under-credited. Whether he understood the ledger line cannot be established from his signature.

*He knew they ran low.*
- In June 1919 Selby Vose signed the circular warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons about adding water. The circular only makes sense if he knew their tests were low, and it shows he blamed the patrons for it.
- The complaints had been public since 1902, the 1908 committee had reported, and they came up at every annual meeting.
- In 1926 he said "tests came up when the looking began," so he had followed the figures.

*There is no good evidence that he knew they were wrong.*
- The ledger that proved it carried a named "station gain" line. Kyle, who watched him sign a thousand sheets, says Vose "read the totals and never read the lines above them," putting his thumb on the bottom figure.
- Signing a sheet does not show that he read the line.
- The only positive evidence is Teague's claim to have raised the matter in 1916, which is uncorroborated and contradicted by Kyle (D2).
- After the June 1923 condemnation, which Strawn reported in person at the office, Vose still ordered glass as routine ("no more said of it than of a broken window"). In 1926 he was still blaming water.

His conduct is that of a man who knew the Hollow tested low and misattributed it, not one who knew the fat was reaching the creamery. That knowledge was in his own books, unread.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. The causal theory: the short Ammon pipettes.**

*What happened.* The station's own milk-test pipettes, a single lot of six dozen bought from Ammon & Sons (invoice 14 April 1897), were marked 17.6 c.c. but delivered on average only **16.72 c.c.**, **95%** of the charge. In the Babcock test the bottle's neck is graduated for a full 17.6 c.c. charge, so a 5% short charge puts 5% less fat into the bottle and the column reads 5% low. The error is **proportional, precise, and invisible from inside the plant**:
- The pipette *is* the station's standard, so every pipette of the lot agreed with every other.
- Duplicate tests agreed, and the scales were true (Brant's book).
- Only an outside measure (a burette, another plant's glass) could reveal it.

*Why that size.* True 3.80 × 0.95 = 3.61, so every herd was short **0.19 points** on average, and richer herds by more (4.56 short 0.24, 3.42 short 0.18). Over 1898–1922 that came to **76,000 lb** of fat on 40M lb of milk, worth **$19,000**. The size is fixed by the glass, not by the season, which is why the gap was the same in June and in February.

*Why it began when it did.* The pipettes went into use on **1 May 1897**. In 1896 the station used borrowed Larrow Green glass and tested 3.80; from 1898 every year read 3.61.

*Why it ended when it did.* On **12 June 1923** Strawn ran the pipettes against his burette and condemned them. New glass was in use from July 1923, and the 1924 average was **3.80**. The one interruption, 8 May–10 July 1911, was a borrowed measure: the tests read 3.80 for exactly those nine weeks.

*Why nothing on the farms or at the station had to change.* The fat was never lost. All of it went down to Larrow Green in the cream, where it appeared every year as "station gain, 5¼%" (5.26% of the fat credited = 5% of the fat delivered). The patrons were simply credited with 95% of it, and the difference went into the Association's general account. The cows, hay, water, scales, man and methods stayed the same throughout, and so did the gap, until the one variable, the glass, changed.

**The three competing explanations, and what defeats each:**

1. **Water added by the patrons.** *Advanced by* Selby Vose (1919 circular; 1926 address), echoed by Sill. *Defeated by:*
   - Strawn's lactometer at every visit from 1908 found no added water, and neither did the 1908 committee ("the milk is whole") or Jerome Cudd at Alder Corners.
   - Watering adds weight and lowers the test in the same proportion, so the fat credited would be unchanged. It could not produce a *surplus* of fat at the creamery, yet the ledger shows 5¼% surplus every year.
   - The tests rose in July 1923 with the glass change, not "when the looking began": the looking had gone on since 1902 and 1908 without effect.

2. **Theft of cream by the tester.** *Advanced by* Wilbur Teague at the 1925 hearing ("Cream is portable"), and repeated as valley opinion by Vira Toland, Dell Rundle (later retracted) and Cleve Oram's father. *Defeated by:*
   - The ledger: the cream arrived at Larrow Green *heavier* than credited, the opposite of theft.
   - The 1911 interval: the same man got 3.80 with other glass.
   - The July 1923 rise under the same tester, and eyewitness tests by the 1908 committee, Strawn (1911) and the board.

3. **Wet bog hay on Hessel Bottom.** *Advanced by* the 1908 Grigg committee, Nyle Grigg again in 1919, Dr. Roland Kepp (1926), and provisionally Orin Falke (1959, withdrawn). *Defeated by:*
   - Bulletin 214's actual finding: not more than 0.05 in winter and nothing on summer pasture, against a gap of 0.19 in every month.
   - Alder Corners: the same herds' *summer* milk tested about 3.80.
   - The 1911 and 1923 changes with no change in feed.
   - Grigg's sampling was winter-only.
   - Hay, like water, could not create the ledger surplus.

**Did the 1925 board of arbitration examine the condemned glassware?**
**No.**
- Its four exhibits were books: the test book, the receiving book, the Brant weigh book and Strawn's 1923 notebook.
- The notebook contained the condemnation entry, but nobody read it against the ledger or receiving book (Kyle), and Effie Loomis records that the crate was never mentioned.
- The glass itself sat in its crate in the loft about twenty feet above the two sittings held in the weigh room.
- The test the board watched there was made with the new 1923 glass.

**What the tester himself believed.**
He **suspected the glass but could not prove it**, and would not write down what he could not prove. This is an inference, weighted as follows.
- His own contemporaneous note after the 1911 interval: "Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."
- His son Duncan's memory of him saying twice that summer that it was "something in the glass."
- Publicly he only denied theft: "never took cream," 17 March 1925.
- Frick's recollection (1946, aged 74) that Keddie privately told him in the corridor that the patrons were at fault conflicts with this. It is a memory 21 years old, from a narrator who misremembers other figures (3.16, 67,000 lb), so I give the contemporaneous "?" and Duncan's account more weight. But Duncan is family, so the belief cannot be called proved.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.**
From 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 the Ostrey Hollow skimming station tested its patrons' milk with Ammon & Sons pipettes that delivered 16.72 c.c. instead of 17.6. Every test read 5% low (3.61 instead of 3.80), and because the by-laws paid on the test, the patrons were short 76,000 lb of fat (about $19,000) over 1898–1922. The valley blamed water, theft and bog hay; in 1925 the tester, Ansel Keddie, was dismissed and the patrons got $3,800. The truth was split across separate records, each kept by someone who saw one piece: the invoice, Keddie's nine borrowed-glass weeks of 1911, Brant's weigh book, the 1908 hay report, the ledger's "station gain," the inspector's 1923 condemnation, the Alder Corners cheese tests and the crate in the loft. Only in 1958 did Keddie's granddaughter Ivy put them together.
