## Section A — Reconstruction

### 1. The Association, Article VII and the station at Ostrey Hollow (1889–1897)

**Who:** the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association; Gideon Vose, its founder; Ansel Keddie, the station tester; Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green.

The Association was organized by valley farmers meeting at Larrow Green in 1889. Gideon Vose was its first president (1889–1911) and wrote its by-laws. He died in 1917. His son Selby managed the Association from 1913 to 1938. The main creamery was built at Larrow Green in 1891.

**Article VII** made every patron's pay depend on the pounds of butterfat his milk was found by test to contain, not on its weight. It also made the test at his own station the one that governed. That article is the hinge of the whole history: under payment by weight, a faulty test would have cost nobody anything.

The year Article VII was adopted **cannot be determined**:
- Rundle says 1891, Effie Loomis 1892 and Nyle Grigg 1893. None of them witnessed it (Loomis became secretary only in 1901).
- The printed by-laws carry no date.
- The 1889–92 minute book was lost in an office removal in 1938.

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, **seven miles** up the valley, chiefly to serve the wet farms of Hessel Bottom. Keddie went up in 1895. The station opened on **4 May 1896**, the date its test book begins. Keddie was tester and separator man there for **29 seasons (1896–1924)**.

The routine at the station:
1. Milk was weighed in and sampled.
2. It was tested by the Babcock method: a **17.6 c.c.** pipette charge went into a bottle whose neck is ruled for exactly that charge.
3. It was separated. The cream was hauled down to Larrow Green and the skim went back to the farmer.

In its first season the station had no glass of its own and tested with glass **borrowed from Larrow Green**:
- Sill says he lent it from his own bench, and one piece came back chipped.
- Keddie's first leaf reads "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green."
- Orra Keddie's diary for 1 May 1897 says they are "done borrowing of Larrow Green."
- Keddie's unsent letter says the Ashlin creamery. That is a slip against his own leaf and the two independent witnesses.

With that glass the **1896 tests averaged 3.80**. The station's own outfit went into use on 1 May 1897 (Story 2).

How much milk the station took in during its first season **cannot be determined**. Nym found no 1896 intake figure, because the sheets went onto the creamery's own books. Sill's "about 900,000 lb" and Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand" are unwritten recollections, and they disagree. A normal later year was about **1,600,000 lb** (40,000,000 lb over 25 years).

**Documents:** r02 (Loomis), r03 (Keddie), r12 (Rundle), r13 (Frick), r14 (Sill), r15 (Grigg), r21 (Oram), r22 (Falke), r23 (Kyle), r24 (Orra Keddie).

### 2. A crate of short glass: Ammon & Sons of Tarnet, 1897–1923

**Who:** Ammon & Sons, a dairy-glass house at Tarnet; the Association; Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector.

On **14 April 1897** Ammon & Sons invoiced the Association "for the Ostrey Hollow station":

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| 6 dozen milk-test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c., at 35¢ | $25.20 |
| 12 dozen test bottles, at 22¢ | $31.68 |
| Acid measure | $0.90 |
| Four-bottle hand tester | $18.00 |
| Freight | $2.15 |
| **Total** | **$77.93** |

The line items add to $77.93, which is also the figure the facsimile and Nym print. Junia Ammon's written-out "seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents", and the *Chronicle*'s $77.39, are a transposition. The outfit went into use on **1 May 1897**.

The **72 pipettes** came from one lot, and the lot ran short:
- The nine that survived to 1958 delivered on average **16.72 c.c.** against the 17.6 marked on them.
- That is **0.88 c.c. short, or 95%** of the charge.
- A short charge into a bottle ruled for a full one reads low in exactly that proportion, on every test.

Why the lot was short **cannot be determined**. The firm's works books burned in 1931. Junia Ammon guesses at a reground mold, which would make a lot "consistently wrong" and so pass any check that compares one piece with another. The *Chronicle*'s charge that the firm sold the lot knowingly has no evidence behind it.

The glass stayed in use for 26 years:
- **12 June 1923:** Strawn, inspector on the Ordell circuit since 1907, ran the pipettes against his calibrated burette. They would not deliver their mark, so he condemned the whole lot and told the manager to order new.
- **28 June 1923:** the Association ordered six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles.
- **2 July 1923:** Ammon replied that "our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name."
- **July 1923 onward:** the new glass was in use. The 1924 station average was **3.80**.

Nobody connected the condemnation with the 26 years of tests already in the book, and Strawn wrote down the condemnation and nothing more. The crate went up the ladder into the **loft over the intake room**. It sat there under a tarpaulin until February 1958; the cream hauler's son had his hand on it many times. In her 1949 chapter Junia Ammon says she regrets the firm's thin 1923 letter more than the accident itself.

**Documents:** r04 (Ivy Keddie), r06 (Strawn), r07 (Nym), r11 (Vaught), r19 (*Chronicle*), r20 (Junia Ammon), r21 (Oram), r22 (Falke), r23 (Kyle), r24 (Orra Keddie).

### 3. The Brants of Hessel Bottom and the weigh book

**Who:** Emmet Brant (1854–1929), his son Roy, Roy's daughter Hazel, and Emmet's sister Orra, who was Ansel Keddie's wife.

Hessel Bottom is a springy, sedge-grown wet meadow along the creek below Ostrey Hollow. **Six farms** worked it. Roy, Hazel, Grigg's 1908 report and Kepp all say six; Effie Loomis's seven stands alone. More than one family could live on a farm: Hazel counts nine families on the six farms. Emmet had the largest place and the largest herd (38 cows), and he hauled more milk to the station than any other patron.

On **1 January 1897** Emmet began a private weigh book. That date comes from Frick's exhibit list, Nym and Duncan. Roy's 1898 and Hazel's 1900 are later memories, and Hazel says herself that she has no memory for years. Emmet weighed his milk on his own scale and wrote the station's intake weight beside it. The two always agreed, which rules out short weighing.

The **1910 leaf** reads "200,000 lb even", scales agreeing, paid at a test of **3.42**, with fat at 24¢ that year:
- Emmet was credited with **6,840 lb** of fat.
- His true test was 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = **3.60**, which is **7,200 lb**.
- He was therefore **360 lb** short, worth **$86.40** at 24¢, in that one year.

Orra Brant married Ansel Keddie in **1889**. Her diary and its preface give her birth year as 1866. So the man later suspected of shorting the patrons was married to the sister of the patron who lost the most, and Emmet ate at his table on Sundays.

The quarrel between them:
- Emmet was among the first to complain, in 1902.
- In June 1919 the water circular came. Ansel carried it round himself rather than let another man do it. Emmet asked him why he had carried it if he did not believe it, and never entered the Keddie house again.
- Emmet's name was on the January 1925 petition.
- He died in the spring of 1929, still unreconciled.

The weigh book was **exhibit 3** at the 1925 arbitration; Emmet lent it for the purpose. Roy kept it the same way after 1929. In 1958 he **lent** it (lent, not left) to Ivy Keddie. Hazel had it from Roy's death in 1962 and gave it to the Historical Rooms at Ashlin in 1963.

The family relations:
- Roy (Emmet's son) and Duncan Keddie (Orra's son, born 1894) were first cousins.
- Their daughters Hazel and Ivy were therefore **second cousins**.
- Hazel alone calls Orra a *half*-sister. Roy and Duncan, one the son of each sibling, say sister.

**Documents:** r02, r04, r07, r09 (Roy Brant), r13, r15, r17 (Hazel Brant), r18 (Duncan Keddie), r24.

### 4. The hay theory: the 1908 committee, Bulletin 214 and Dr. Kepp (1908–1959)

**Who:** Nyle Grigg, a Larrow Green farmer who chaired the 1908 committee; the State Agricultural College at Farland; Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S., of Ashlin; Orin Falke, county agent.

Complaints that Ostrey Hollow milk tested under the rest of the valley began in **1902** and grew every year. In January 1908 the Association named a committee of three under Grigg, who was not a patron of the station.

The committee sampled the Hessel Bottom herds **through January, February and March 1908**. Grigg's 1919 covering letter says they sampled "the whole of 1908", but the report printed underneath it says January to March. The quoted report outranks his memory.

What the committee did:
- visited all six farms and drew and sealed its own samples;
- saw the cans hauled and weighed, and watched the test made;
- tried the station scales against a known weight, and the patrons' scales against the station's;
- looked into the water supply at each barn.

It found the milk whole, the scales honest and **no dishonesty anywhere**. It put the low test down to the late-cut sedge hay that was the Bottom's winter feed, and its report closed the question for eleven years.

In 1911 the College ran a feeding trial on bog hay and published it as **Bulletin No. 214**. Its summary, which Falke prints verbatim, says bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the test by "not more than **five hundredths** of one per cent in winter, and by **nothing** that could be measured on summer pasture."

Grigg in 1919 and Kepp in his 1926 Farm Club paper both misquote it as "two tenths in the winter feeding." Kepp added a field figure of a fifth of a point for wet bottoms and put the Hollow gap at 0.21. He brushed aside the 1911 interval as "six weeks" of summer figures, and said it was "not good sense to go looking for a second cause in a building."

Why the theory fails:
- The real deficiency was **0.19 in every month**, summer and winter alike. Bulletin 214's maximum is about a quarter of that and allows nothing in summer; Ivy Keddie called it "four times too small."
- The same herds' summer milk tested **3.80** at Alder Corners (Story 8).
- The tests jumped in 1911 and again in 1923 with the hay unchanged.
- Grigg sampled only in winter, so his committee could never have seen that the gap persisted on summer grass.

In his 1959 report Falke at first allowed hay as a "real if partial factor." He **withdrew** that concession once he had read Bulletin 214 itself.

**Documents:** r04, r07, r09, r11, r15 (Grigg), r16 (Kepp), r17, r22 (Falke), r24.
