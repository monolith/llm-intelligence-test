# Answer sheet — Part 1 (Section A, stories 1–4)

## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1. Article VII and the station at Ostrey Hollow (1889–1897)

**Who:** Gideon Vose, the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, Ansel Keddie, Hobart Sill.

Valley farmers met at Larrow Green in 1889 and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose was its first president (1889–1911; died 1917) and wrote its by-laws. The main creamery at Larrow Green was built in 1891. **Article VII** is the hinge of everything that follows. Every patron is paid on the pounds of butterfat his milk shows by test, not on its weight, and "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." Under payment by weight, no error in testing would have cost anyone a cent.

**The year of adoption cannot be determined.** Rundle says 1891, Effie Loomis 1892, and Grigg says payment by test "came in with the 1893 meeting." The printed by-laws carry only "adopted at the annual meeting," and the 1889–92 minute book was lost in an office removal in 1938.

The Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow in the winter of 1895–96, seven miles up the valley. It served chiefly Hessel Bottom, a wet meadow of six farms. **It opened on 4 May 1896.** Vira and Hazel say Keddie "went up in '95," but his test book begins on 4 May 1896. At the station, milk was weighed, sampled and tested by the Babcock method. The test puts a 17.6 c.c. charge of milk from a pipette into a bottle whose neck is ruled for exactly that charge. The milk was then separated, the cream hauled down to Larrow Green, and the skim returned to the patron. **Ansel Keddie** (1861–1934) was tester and separator man from the opening for 29 seasons (1896–1924). Emmet Brant, whose sister Orra was Keddie's wife, was the largest patron.

In the first season the station had no testing glass of its own. **Hobart Sill**, buttermaker at Larrow Green from 1893 to 1934, lent his own bench glass, which came back with one piece chipped. Orra's diary (1 May 1897) says they were "done borrowing of Larrow Green." Keddie's letter says the glass came from the "Ashlin creamery," but Sill's and Orra's independent accounts outweigh it. The station's own outfit went into use on **1 May 1897**. So the first season was tested with correct glass.

**The first season's intake cannot be determined.** Sill remembers about 900,000 lb and Kyle "a good eleven hundred thousand." Both are memories that disagree, and Kyle admits the figure "is not in any book I ever ruled." The 1896 sheets were kept on the creamery's books and never filed with the station papers, and the curator Nym says no figure survives.

Gideon's son, **Selby Vose**, was manager from 1913 to 1938.

### Story 2. Seventy-two pipettes from Tarnet (1897–1923)

**Who:** Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet (founded by Marcus Ammon), Junia Ammon, and the Association.

The invoice of **14 April 1897**, reproduced by Junia Ammon and seen by Ivy and the *Chronicle*, was billed to the Association for the Ostrey Hollow station:

- 6 doz. milk-test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35: **25.20**
- 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at .22: **31.68**
- 1 acid measure: **.90**
- 1 hand tester, 4-bottle: **18.00**
- Freight: **2.15**
- **Total $77.93**

The line items add to $77.93. Junia's own prose and the *Chronicle* both say "$77.39," a transposition. The order was **72 pipettes** at 35¢ each. Vaught's body text and Oram say "five dozen," which is wrong.

The outfit went into use at the station on **1 May 1897** and stayed in use until the state inspector condemned it on **12 June 1923**, a little over 26 years. Every pipette was marked 17.6 c.c. The nine that survived and were measured in 1958 delivered **16.70–16.74 c.c., mean 16.72: 0.88 c.c. short, or 95% of the mark.** They were short by the same amount, one to another.

Junia's explanation is her own belief, not a record. On it, a reground mould or a gauge knocked off its nail produced a lot that was *consistently* wrong, which is exactly the fault a spot-check of the first, tenth and fiftieth piece cannot catch. The firm's works books, which would show the glass, gauge, man and day, burned in 1931.

When the Association re-ordered on **28 June 1923** (six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles), Ammon's letter of **2 July** said only that "our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name." Junia calls that answer thin. The *Chronicle* (1958) says the firm knowingly sold a short lot. **That charge cannot be settled.** No surviving record shows knowledge or intent, and a consistent shortfall fits an undetected accident equally well.

The price of the fault was $77.93 of glass against about $19,000 of unpaid butterfat (see Story 8). After condemnation the crate was carried up to the loft over the intake room in June 1923.

### Story 3. The wet-hay theory: the 1902 complaints, the 1908 committee, Bulletin 214 and Kepp

**Who:** the Hessel Bottom patrons, Nyle Grigg, the State Agricultural College at Farland, and Dr. Roland Kepp.

From **1902** there was talk that Ostrey Hollow milk tested below the rest of the valley (Orra's diary for March 1902; Sill; Grigg). Hessel Bottom is springy ground along the creek. Its sedge hay is cut late and is the winter feed from November to April.

In **January 1908** the board named a committee of three, chaired by **Nyle Grigg**, a Larrow Green farmer who was not a patron of the station. Orra saw "four men" arrive in Grigg's sleigh. They went to the barns rather than the station. They visited the six farms, sealed their own samples, tried the station scales against a known weight and against the patrons' own scales, watched the tests, and inquired into water. They found **"no dishonesty of any kind"** and laid the low test to the wet hay.

The report itself says they sampled **January, February and March** only. Grigg's 1919 covering letter says they sampled "the whole of 1908, every month," but his own report and Orra's diary (committee arrived in January, gone by March) contradict him. A winter-only sample could never show that the gap was the same in summer.

The College's **Bulletin No. 214 (1911)**, a controlled bog-hay feeding trial, found the test depressed by **"not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."** Falke prints that summary verbatim. Grigg (1919) and **Kepp** (Farm Club paper, 1926) both cite it as "two tenths in winter," a misquotation. Kepp, a veterinarian, added a soil-and-minerals theory. He put wet bottoms "a fifth of a point" poorer, the station gap at 0.21, and waved away the 1911 interval as "six weeks" of summer flush, which undercuts his own theory.

The true gap was **0.19, flat in every month**, February and July alike. That is nearly four times Bulletin 214's winter maximum, and it held in summer, when bog hay is not fed and 214 found no effect at all. The same Hessel Bottom milk tested about 3.80 at Alder Corners in summer (Story 5). The county agent Falke first conceded hay as "a real if partial factor," then withdrew the concession once he read 214.

### Story 4. Nine weeks in 1911: the accidental experiment

**Who:** Ansel Keddie, and his son Duncan (then 17).

On **8 May 1911** the station's own measure broke. Orra's diary for that day reads: "A. home cross about a broken measure." A measure borrowed from Larrow Green was used until **10 July 1911**, which is **nine weeks (63 days)**. Teague ("six weeks, from town") and Kepp ("six weeks") are contradicted by the dated entries. Roy Brant says the state man left his own measure, but Strawn's June 1911 circuit book has only "Nothing here to note," so that version is unsupported.

During those nine weeks the station's tests stood at about **3.80**, level with the true milk and the valley. The day the station went back to its own glass they fell back to **3.61**. Keddie set **"? — A.K."** against the entries.

Nothing else changed: the same tester, herds, farms, scales, season, acid and supervision. It was a one-variable swap, and it reversed. The rise came in the summer flush, when the hay theory predicts no difference to begin with, and the gap was flat year-round, so the season cannot explain the rise.

Duncan remembers his father coming home that summer saying "it was something in the glass." He said it twice, but he never put it in the book, because he would not write down what he could not prove. He went on for another fourteen years recording what his glass gave him. Those figures were later used against him.

The same year, Bulletin 214 was published and Gideon Vose left the presidency. The entries "lay unread for forty-seven years" (Vaught), until Ivy read them in 1958. Keddie's unsent 1925 letter points to them along with the constant gap and the 1924 figure of 3.80. The interval shows the error was in the station's glass and was present in 1911, and that the same man got right figures from different glass. It does not measure how short the glass was; the 1958 burette work does that.
