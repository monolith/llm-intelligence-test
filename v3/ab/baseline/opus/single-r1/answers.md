# Answer sheet — Part 1 (Section A, stories 1–4)

## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1. Article VII and the station at Ostrey Hollow (1889–1897)

**Who:** Gideon Vose, the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, Ansel Keddie, Hobart Sill.

Valley farmers met at Larrow Green in 1889 and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose was its first president (1889–1911; died 1917) and wrote its by-laws. The main creamery at Larrow Green was built in 1891. **Article VII** is the hinge of everything that follows. Every patron is paid on the pounds of butterfat his milk shows by test, not on its weight, and "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." Under payment by weight, no error in testing would have cost anyone a cent.

**The year of adoption cannot be determined.** Rundle says 1891, Effie Loomis 1892, and Grigg says payment by test "came in with the 1893 meeting." The printed by-laws carry only "adopted at the annual meeting," and the 1889–92 minute book was lost in an office removal in 1938.

The Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow in the winter of 1895–96, seven miles up the valley. It served chiefly Hessel Bottom, a wet meadow of six farms. **It opened on 4 May 1896.** Vira and Hazel say Keddie "went up in '95," but his test book begins on 4 May 1896. At the station, milk was weighed, sampled and tested by the Babcock method. The test puts a 17.6 c.c. charge of milk from a pipette into a bottle whose neck is ruled for exactly that charge. The milk was then separated, the cream hauled down to Larrow Green, and the skim returned to the patron. **Ansel Keddie** (1861–1934) was tester and separator man from the opening for 29 seasons (1896–1924). Emmet Brant, whose sister Orra was Keddie's wife, was the largest patron.

In the first season the station had no testing glass of its own. **Hobart Sill**, buttermaker at Larrow Green from 1893 to 1934, lent his own bench glass, which came back with one piece chipped. Orra's diary (1 May 1897) says they were "done borrowing of Larrow Green." Keddie's letter says the glass came from the "Ashlin creamery," but Sill's and Orra's independent accounts outweigh it. The station's own outfit went into use on **1 May 1897**. So the first season was tested with correct glass.

**The first season's intake cannot be determined.** Sill remembers about 900,000 lb and Kyle "a good eleven hundred thousand." Both are memories that disagree, and Kyle admits the figure "is not in any book I ever ruled." The 1896 sheets were kept on the creamery's books and never filed with the station papers, and the curator Nym says no figure survives.

Gideon's son, **Selby Vose**, was manager from 1913 to 1938.

**Documents:** r02 Loomis, r12 Rundle, r15 Grigg, r22 Falke, r14 Sill, r23 Kyle, r24 Orra Keddie, r03 Keddie, r07 Nym, r13 Frick, r01 Vira, r17 Hazel.

### Story 2. Seventy-two pipettes from Tarnet (1897–1923)

**Who:** Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet (founded by Marcus Ammon), Junia Ammon, and the Association.

The invoice of **14 April 1897**, reproduced by Junia Ammon and seen by Ivy and the *Chronicle*, was billed to the Association for the Ostrey Hollow station:

- 6 doz. milk-test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35: **25.20**
- 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at .22: **31.68**
- 1 acid measure: **.90**
- 1 hand tester, 4-bottle: **18.00**
- Freight: **2.15**
- **Total $77.93**

The line items add to $77.93. Junia's own prose and the *Chronicle* both say "$77.39," a transposition. The order was **72 pipettes** at 35¢ each. Vaught's body text and Oram say "five dozen," which is wrong.

The outfit went into use at the station on **1 May 1897** and stayed in use until the state inspector condemned it on **12 June 1923**, a little over 26 years. Every pipette was marked 17.6 c.c. The nine that survived and were measured in 1958 delivered **16.70–16.74 c.c., mean 16.72: 0.88 c.c. short, or 95% of the mark.** They were short by the same amount, one to another.

Junia's explanation is her own belief, not a record. On it, a reground mould or a gauge knocked off its nail produced a lot that was *consistently* wrong, which is exactly the fault a spot-check of the first, tenth and fiftieth piece cannot catch. The firm's works books, which would show the glass, gauge, man and day, burned in 1931.

When the Association re-ordered on **28 June 1923** (six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles), Ammon's letter of **2 July** said only that "our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name." Junia calls that answer thin. The *Chronicle* (1958) says the firm knowingly sold a short lot. **That charge cannot be settled.** No surviving record shows knowledge or intent, and a consistent shortfall fits an undetected accident equally well.

The price of the fault was $77.93 of glass against about $19,000 of unpaid butterfat (see Story 8). After condemnation the crate was carried up to the loft over the intake room in June 1923.

**Documents:** r20 Junia Ammon, r04 Ivy Keddie, r11 Vaught, r19 *Chronicle*, r21 Oram, r18 Duncan Keddie, r06 Strawn.

### Story 3. The wet-hay theory: the 1902 complaints, the 1908 committee, Bulletin 214 and Kepp

**Who:** the Hessel Bottom patrons, Nyle Grigg, the State Agricultural College at Farland, and Dr. Roland Kepp.

From **1902** there was talk that Ostrey Hollow milk tested below the rest of the valley (Orra's diary for March 1902; Sill; Grigg). Hessel Bottom is springy ground along the creek. Its sedge hay is cut late and is the winter feed from November to April.

In **January 1908** the board named a committee of three, chaired by **Nyle Grigg**, a Larrow Green farmer who was not a patron of the station. Orra saw "four men" arrive in Grigg's sleigh. They went to the barns rather than the station. They visited the six farms, sealed their own samples, tried the station scales against a known weight and against the patrons' own scales, watched the tests, and inquired into water. They found **"no dishonesty of any kind"** and laid the low test to the wet hay.

The report itself says they sampled **January, February and March** only. Grigg's 1919 covering letter says they sampled "the whole of 1908, every month," but his own report and Orra's diary (committee arrived in January, gone by March) contradict him. A winter-only sample could never show that the gap was the same in summer.

The College's **Bulletin No. 214 (1911)**, a controlled bog-hay feeding trial, found the test depressed by **"not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."** Falke prints that summary verbatim. Grigg (1919) and **Kepp** (Farm Club paper, 1926) both cite it as "two tenths in winter," a misquotation. Kepp, a veterinarian, added a soil-and-minerals theory. He put wet bottoms "a fifth of a point" poorer, the station gap at 0.21, and waved away the 1911 interval as "six weeks" of summer flush, which undercuts his own theory.

The true gap was **0.19, flat in every month**, February and July alike. That is nearly four times Bulletin 214's winter maximum, and it held in summer, when bog hay is not fed and 214 found no effect at all. The same Hessel Bottom milk tested about 3.80 at Alder Corners in summer (Story 5). The county agent Falke first conceded hay as "a real if partial factor," then withdrew the concession once he read 214.

**Documents:** r15 Grigg, r16 Kepp, r22 Falke, r24 Orra, r09 Roy Brant, r17 Hazel, r11 Vaught, r13 Frick, r14 Sill.

### Story 4. Nine weeks in 1911: the accidental experiment

**Who:** Ansel Keddie, and his son Duncan (then 17).

On **8 May 1911** the station's own measure broke. Orra's diary for that day reads: "A. home cross about a broken measure." A measure borrowed from Larrow Green was used until **10 July 1911**, which is **nine weeks (63 days)**. Teague ("six weeks, from town") and Kepp ("six weeks") are contradicted by the dated entries. Roy Brant says the state man left his own measure, but Strawn's June 1911 circuit book has only "Nothing here to note," so that version is unsupported.

During those nine weeks the station's tests stood at about **3.80**, level with the true milk and the valley. The day the station went back to its own glass they fell back to **3.61**. Keddie set **"? — A.K."** against the entries.

Nothing else changed: the same tester, herds, farms, scales, season, acid and supervision. It was a one-variable swap, and it reversed. The rise came in the summer flush, when the hay theory predicts no difference to begin with, and the gap was flat year-round, so the season cannot explain the rise.

Duncan remembers his father coming home that summer saying "it was something in the glass." He said it twice, but he never put it in the book, because he would not write down what he could not prove. He went on for another fourteen years recording what his glass gave him. Those figures were later used against him.

The same year, Bulletin 214 was published and Gideon Vose left the presidency. The entries "lay unread for forty-seven years" (Vaught), until Ivy read them in 1958. Keddie's unsent 1925 letter points to them along with the constant gap and the 1924 figure of 3.80. The interval shows the error was in the station's glass and was present in 1911, and that the same man got right figures from different glass. It does not measure how short the glass was; the 1958 burette work does that.

**Documents:** r03 Keddie, r24 Orra, r18 Duncan, r06 Strawn, r09 Roy, r11 Vaught, r22 Falke, r16 Kepp, r05 Teague, r04 Ivy.

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### Story 5. Two outside yardsticks: Emmet Brant's weigh book and the Alder Corners vats (1897–1930)

**Who:** Emmet Brant, Roy Brant, Hazel Brant, Jerome Cudd and Rosalie Cudd.

**Emmet Brant** (1854–1929) had the largest place on Hessel Bottom (160 acres, 38 cows) and was the station's largest patron. His sister **Orra** was Ansel Keddie's wife, so the man later accused of shorting the patrons was shorting his own brother-in-law worst of all.

On **1 January 1897** Emmet began a private weigh book. That date comes from Frick's exhibit list, Nym and Duncan; Roy says 1898 and Hazel 1900. He weighed his milk on his own scale, the station weighed it again at intake, and he wrote both figures side by side. **The two always agreed.** So the scales were honest and the weights were never the problem.

The **1910 leaf** reads: **200,000 lb delivered, "his scale and theirs agreeing," paid on a test of 3.42**, with fat at 24¢ that year. At 95% reading, the true test was 3.60. He was credited with 6,840 lb of fat against 7,200 lb delivered, a shortfall of 360 lb, worth $86.40. Roy gives the gap as 0.17, but his own leaf implies 0.18.

The book passed to Roy in 1929 and was an exhibit at the 1925 hearing. Roy **lent** it to Ivy in 1958. At his death in 1962 it went to Hazel, who gave it to the Historical Rooms at Ashlin in 1963.

**Alder Corners Cheese Co.**, in Vessey County over Redlow Ridge, was built by Jerome Cudd in **1913**. Roy and Rundle say it opened in 1914, which may simply be the first vat. From **1915 to 1930 (16 seasons)**, **four of the six** Hessel Bottom men hauled there each summer flush, roughly 1 June to mid-September, and returned to the station for the rest of the year. The ridge was shut from November to April, so there are no winter figures.

Their milk tested **3.78–3.82, average about 3.80**. The 1919 individual figures were 3.79, 3.81, 3.78 and 3.82. Rosalie's yearly table:

| Year | Test | Year | Test | Year | Test | Year | Test |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1915 | 3.79 | 1919 | 3.80 | 1923 | 3.81 | 1927 | 3.78 |
| 1916 | 3.81 | 1920 | 3.82 | 1924 | 3.80 | 1928 | 3.80 |
| 1917 | 3.78 | 1921 | 3.79 | 1925 | 3.79 | 1929 | 3.81 |
| 1918 | 3.80 | 1922 | 3.78 | 1926 | 3.82 | 1930 | 3.79 |

A cheesemaker detects watered milk at once, and **Jerome never docked them.** The summers that overlap the short glass are 1915–1922, plus part of 1923. In those same summers the same men's milk read about 3.61 at the station during the rest of the year.

Jerome died in March 1941. His daughter **Rosalie** ran the factory from then on, copied the figures for Ivy in 1958, and the book went to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970. It has never left Vessey County. Hazel's comment: "the only honest arithmetic anybody in that valley ever did without meaning to."

**Documents:** r09 Roy Brant, r10 Rosalie Cudd, r17 Hazel, r18 Duncan, r13 Frick, r07 Nym, r12 Rundle.

### Story 6. "Station gain, as usual": the ledger, the office and the water circular (1905–1925)

**Who:** Wilbur Teague (bookkeeper), Bertram Kyle (clerk), Selby Vose (manager), Hobart Sill, Ansel and Orra Keddie, Emmet Brant.

From **1905**, bookkeeper **Wilbur Teague** entered three figures and a sentence in the Ostrey Hollow station account every year:
- butterfat credited to patrons on the station test
- butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream
- the difference, as "**station gain**... five and a quarter per cent, as usual"

A typical year: **57,760 lb credited, 60,800 lb received, gain 3,040 lb.** That is 5.26% of the fat credited (Teague's "5¼%") and exactly 5% of the fat received. **Hobart Sill** weighed the Hollow's cream in at Larrow Green with the receiving book in his hand and found it always heavier than the sheet said; he put that down to "the nature of cream."

Clerk **Bertram Kyle** copied the line for 25 years without wondering at it: "a figure with a name on it has been explained." **Selby Vose**, manager from 1913 to 1938, signed the sheet every year, "his thumb on the bottom figure." By his own memoir, Teague raised the Hollow matter with Vose in 1916. But nobody, Teague included, ever asked what the gain meant: the station's test under-reported fat that physically arrived downstream. That refutes both theft and watering.

Vose held the **water** theory. In **June 1919** a circular warning against added water, typed by Kyle and signed by Vose alone, went to the patrons of the bottom: **nine families** on six farms, per Hazel and Sill; Orra writes eleven. Ansel would not mail it and carried it door to door himself. Emmet then broke with him and never entered the Keddie house again until his death in 1929 (Orra, Roy).

Strawn tested with the lactometer at every visit from 1908 and never found added water. Emmet's scales matched the station's. Sill thought "nine families all got honest," and Vira remembers tests rising after 1919. In fact the figures stayed at 3.61 until the glass was changed in 1923.

At the 1925 hearing Teague insinuated that Keddie was taking cream ("cream is portable"). He was still saying so in 1948, misreading his own 5¼% as a share of "fat actually in cream." The $3,800 award was charged to the station account.

**Documents:** r05 Teague, r23 Kyle, r14 Sill, r08 Vose, r12 Rundle, r13 Frick, r24 Orra, r09 Roy, r17 Hazel, r06 Strawn, r01 Vira.

### Story 7. Condemned glass and a hearing that never looked at it (1923–1925)

**Who:** Merle Strawn (state dairy inspector), Alonzo Frick (arbitration chairman), Ansel Keddie, and the patrons.

**Merle Strawn** rode the Ordell circuit from 1907 to 1936 and was kin to nobody in the valley. He carried burettes from 1921 but did not check the station's glass in 1922. On **12 June 1923** he ran the station's pipettes against his burette, found they would not deliver their mark, **condemned the whole lot**, and told the manager to order new. He drew no inference about the years before.

The Association ordered on **28 June**; Ammon replied on **2 July**. New glass was in use from **July 1923**, and Strawn found it in order in October. The old crate went up to the loft over the intake room, which Strawn saw full in 1928. Vose and Rundle date the condemnation 12 July, Kyle 1922 and Hazel 1921. All are contradicted by Strawn's own book and by the 28 June order.

In **1924** the station averaged **3.80**, with Keddie still testing. Nobody connected the jump to the glass.

The patrons petitioned in **January 1925**, with Emmet's name on the petition. A board of three under the Ashlin attorney **Alonzo Frick** sat from **17 March to 4 May 1925**. The first sitting was at the Ashlin office. The second and third were in the station's weigh room, directly under the loft and the crate, while men stood outside in the rain. The board heard three theories: Vose's water, Teague's insinuated theft, and the hay, with the 1908 report read in. The patrons claimed 67,000 lb.

The exhibits were **four books and nothing else** (Frick, confirmed by Kyle, who carried them):
- Keddie's test book, 4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924
- the station receiving book
- Emmet Brant's weigh book
- Strawn's 1923 notebook

No glass was examined and no ledgers were exhibited. Nobody "laid the second beside the fourth."

Keddie was **dismissed on 30 April 1925**. Four days later, on **4 May**, the board found **"no cause established"** and awarded the patrons **$3,800 without admission**. Frick believed Keddie. Keddie worked as a carpenter afterward and died in **February 1934** with the imputation unspoken but unlifted. Roy never attended another Association meeting.

**Documents:** r06 Strawn, r13 Frick, r23 Kyle, r02 Loomis, r24 Orra, r21 Oram, r03 Keddie, r20 Junia Ammon, r08 Vose, r12 Rundle, r09 Roy, r17 Hazel, r18 Duncan.

### Story 8. The crate in the loft: Ivy Keddie and Bulletin 471 (1958–1970)

**Who:** Ivy Keddie, Leland Vaught, and the newspaper, county and pamphlet responses.

**Ivy Keddie** (born 1931) is Duncan's daughter and Ansel's **granddaughter**. She was a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland. In **February 1958** she climbed the ladder into the loft over the intake room and found the crate under a tarpaulin and chaff, where it had sat since June 1923. **Nine** of the original 72 pipettes survived.

On **9 March 1958** she measured them against a calibrated burette. The runs were 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74 and 16.71, a **mean of 16.72 c.c. against 17.6 marked: 0.88 short, 95%**. A short charge in a bottle ruled for the full charge reads low in the same proportion, so 3.80 milk read **3.61**, 0.19 short, identically in every season.

She gathered the other records:
- Strawn's 1923 notebook, at the Farland library since 1947
- Emmet's weigh book, lent by Roy
- Rosalie Cudd's Alder Corners figures
- Keddie's test book, including the 1911 "? — A.K." entries
- the station-gain ledger

**The loss:** 1898–1922 is 25 full years and 40,000,000 lb of milk. At 3.61 that milk was credited with 1,444,000 lb of fat, against 1,520,000 lb delivered. The difference is **76,000 lb** (one part in twenty of the fat delivered), which at 25¢ is **$19,000**. The **$3,800** award was one-fifth of that.

**Bulletin 471** (October 1958, Vaught and Keddie) concluded that the glass was the only cause: no milk watered, no cream stolen. Vaught's body text carries slips: five dozen pipettes, a mean of 16.27, 118,000 lb, and 1898. His conclusion matches Ivy's figures.

The ***Ashlin Chronicle*** (3 October 1958) got much wrong: Ivy as "great-niece," eleven pipettes, 16.27, $19,600, $77.39, and Rosalie as "niece." Rundle's 1959 reprint retracted her theft chapter because the ledger showed the cream arriving heavy. County agent **Falke**'s 1959 report withdrew both his hay concession and his "great-niece." Ivy's 1959 talk asked that it be printed that Ansel "never knew." In fact he had suspected since 1911.

The records ended up in public hands:
- test book and crate: Historical Rooms, Ashlin, 1959
- weigh book: the same, 1963
- Cudd book: Vessey County Historical Society, 1970

Keddie's name was cleared 24 years after his death. Orra had died in 1948.

**Documents:** r04 Ivy, r11 Vaught, r19 *Chronicle*, r22 Falke, r12 Rundle, r17 Hazel, r18 Duncan, r21 Oram, r10 Rosalie, r07 Nym, r24 Orra.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy and Ansel: Ivy is Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** Duncan (born 1894) was the son of Ansel and Orra, and Ivy (born 1931) is Duncan's daughter. Duncan states this directly and says he has "twice seen it printed otherwise." Hazel, Oram ("the granddaughter came back") and Falke's correction all agree. "Great-niece" appears in the *Ashlin Chronicle* (1958) and in the body of Falke's 1959 report, and Falke retracts it in his own note ("the error on page two is mine"). Falke almost certainly copied the newspaper, so it is one wrong source, not two. Duncan's first-hand family account outranks a newspaper assembled "in a day and a half from a press release and two telephone calls."

**B2. Ivy and Hazel: second cousins.** Emmet Brant and Orra were siblings. Their children, Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie, are first cousins. Their children, Hazel and Ivy, are therefore second cousins. Duncan, Hazel, Rundle's 1959 footnote and Falke's framing all agree. Vira and Oram say "first cousin once removed," which is the relation between Ivy and Roy, a generation off; both are wrong.

**Left open:** whether Orra was Emmet's full or half sister. Duncan says simply "sister, the younger of the two." Hazel says "half sister, by his father's second wife." If Hazel is right, Roy and Duncan are half first cousins and Ivy and Hazel half second cousins. Duncan, as Orra's son, is the closer witness and I lean to full siblings, but the sources cannot settle it. Orra's birth year is also disputed: 1866 in the diary preface and her own "sixty-four" in 1930, 1868 in Duncan, whose own "eighty-two when she died in 1948" implies 1866. The mothers of Hazel and Ivy are unnamed.

**B3. Selby Vose's father: Gideon Vose.** Gideon organized the Association in 1889, was its first president until 1911, and wrote its by-laws, including Article VII. Selby is his son on the evidence of Effie Loomis, Selby himself, Duncan and Kyle, who calls "the nephew story... nonsense." "Nephew" appears only in Rundle (1939) and Vira (1979), and Vira most likely got it from Rundle's pamphlet, so that is one source.

It matters because Selby, manager from 1913, was guarding his father's institution and his father's rule that "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." Admitting the station test was wrong meant admitting the Association owed the patrons money under its founder's own by-law. So Selby blamed the patrons: the 1919 water circular, and a 1926 address calling the 1923 condemnation "housekeeping," with Gideon's Jerseys at 4.56 in 1910 offered as a yardstick. He signed the station-gain line every year without reading it.

**B4. Vose and Keddie families: no relationship is established, and the best evidence says there was none.** Oram (1977) reports second-hand, from his mother's mother, that the Keddies and Voses were "cousins of some sort," but he "could not draw you the line." Duncan (1968) says flatly there was no connection. He traces the rumour to confusion with the unrelated Ashlin Voses and to town gossip about Orra and Gideon's wife being cousins. He also notes that his mother, who gave the kinship for every headstone in the valley, never once put a Vose in it. First-hand family testimony outranks third-hand hearsay. Strictly, the sources cannot prove a negative, but nothing supports a link.


# Answer sheet — Part 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

### C1. The proportion, true test, pounds and money

**(a) Proportion.** The pipettes were marked 17.6 c.c. and delivered a mean of 16.72 c.c. (9 pipettes, runs from 16.70 to 16.74, measured 9 March 1958).

16.72 / 17.60 = **0.950**. Each charge was 0.88 c.c. short, **5%, one part in twenty**. The bottle neck is ruled for a 17.6 c.c. charge, so the fat reading scales with the milk actually delivered: recorded test = true test × 0.95. Every test at the station therefore read **5% below the truth, in proportion**.

**(b) True test and shortfall.** Recorded average 3.61. True = 3.61 / 0.95 = **3.80%**. The recorded tests were short by **0.19 points**. Three independent checks agree on 3.80: the 1911 borrowed-glass interval, the 1924 average with new glass, and Alder Corners. I reject Vaught's body figure of 16.27 c.c.: it gives a ratio of 0.9244, a true test of 3.905 and a shortfall of 0.295, and he himself notes it does not fit 1924's 3.80.

**(c) Pounds not credited, 1898–1922** (25 full years, 40,000,000 lb of milk):
- True fat: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb
- Credited: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb
- Not credited: **76,000 lb** (also 40,000,000 × 0.0019)

**(d) Money and fraction received.** 76,000 lb × $0.25 = **$19,000**. The patrons received **$3,800**, which is 3,800 / 19,000 = **one-fifth (20%)**. The 25¢ figure is Kyle's period average, compiled for the arbitration; Roy's 24¢ for 1910 is consistent with it. I reject 27¢ (Sill, Kepp), $19,600 (Rundle, *Chronicle*) and $3,500 (Vira, Oram).

### C2. Emmet Brant, 1910

200,000 lb of milk, paid on a test of 3.42, both scales agreeing.

**(a) Credited:** 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of butterfat.

**(b) Owed and not credited:** true test = 3.42 / 0.95 = 3.60%, so true fat = 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb. The shortfall is **360 lb**. For his milk the gap is 0.18 points; the error is proportional, so his leaner milk lost a little less than the station average of 0.19. Roy's "0.17" does not fit his own leaf.

**(c) Worth that year:** 360 × $0.24 (1910 price per the leaf) = **$86.40**. At the 25¢ period average it would be $90.00.

### C3. Credited versus received, 1898–1922

**(a) Credited on the station test:** **1,444,000 lb**.

**(b) Received at Larrow Green in the station cream:** **1,520,000 lb**, the fat actually delivered. The ledger year matches exactly: 57,760 × 25 = 1,444,000 and 60,800 × 25 = 1,520,000, with 1,600,000 lb of milk a year.

**(c) Percentage.** Difference 76,000 lb.
- As a percentage of the fat **credited**: 76,000 / 1,444,000 = **5.263%**. This is Teague's "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" (3,040 / 57,760 = 5.263%).
- As a percentage of the fat **received**, the true delivered fat: 76,000 / 1,520,000 = **5.000%**. This is the pipette's one part in twenty.

Teague (1948) and Vaught's body text wrongly describe the 5¼% as a percentage of fat actually delivered. It is a percentage of fat credited.

### C4. The invoice

**(a) Total:** 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = **$77.93**, which matches the invoice total. "$77.39" in Junia's prose and the *Chronicle* is a transposition.

**(b) Pipettes:** 6 dozen = **72 pipettes** at 35¢ each (72 × 0.35 = 25.20). Each was short **0.88 c.c.** (17.60 − 16.72) on the measured mean; the nine survivors ranged from 0.86 to 0.90 short. The shortfall of the other 63 is inferred from the consistency of the nine and from Strawn condemning the whole lot.

### C5. Duration and first season

**(a) Time in use:** 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923, which is **26 years, 1 month and 11 days** (26 years plus 42 days, 9,537 days).

**The money calculation** runs over **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922**.

They differ because the glass was in use for only part of 1897 (from May) and part of 1923 (to 12 June). The annual station accounts and the 40,000,000-lb total compiled for the arbitration cover complete calendar years only, so the partial years are left out. The award calculation therefore slightly *understates* the loss by omitting about 8 months of 1897 and 5½ months of 1923. It slightly *overstates* it by including the nine weeks of 1911, when borrowed correct glass was in use.

**(b) First-season intake: cannot be determined from the sources.** Sill remembers about 900,000 lb and Kyle "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds." Both are memories, they disagree, and Kyle admits it "is not in any book I ever ruled." The 1896 sheets were kept on the creamery's books and never filed with the station papers, and Nym's catalogue records that no 1896 figure exists. The first season does not matter to the loss in any case, because it was tested with Sill's borrowed, correct glass.

## Section D — Logic

### D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment

**When and how long.** The measure broke on 8 May 1911 and borrowed glass from Larrow Green was used until 10 July: **nine weeks**. The dates are in Keddie's book and Orra's diary. "Six weeks" (Teague, Kepp) is wrong.

**Design.** The single variable was the testing glass: the station's own versus a borrowed measure. Held constant were the tester (Keddie), the farms and herds, the scales, the acid and method, the season's feeding, and the supervision.

**Result.** The tests rose to about 3.80, level with the valley and the true milk. They fell back to 3.61 the day the station's own glass returned. Keddie marked "? — A.K."

**What it establishes that calculation cannot.** It is direct, contemporary evidence of cause:
- The station's own glass produced the low figures, and swapping it reversed them.
- The fault was already present in 1911, 14 years before the hearing and 47 before the measurement.
- The same man with different glass got correct figures, which rules out his dishonesty and his technique.
- A summer rise against a flat year-round gap rules out the season and the hay.

The 1958 arithmetic assumes the pipettes were always as short as they measured in 1958. The 1911 interval shows that they were already short then.

**What it does not establish.** The size of the shortfall in c.c. (that needs the burette). That every year from 1897 to 1923 was affected, which needs 1924 and the ledger as well. Whether the fault lay in manufacture or later wear. Who, if anyone, knew. The money. Which piece of borrowed glass was used and who lent it (Roy says the state man; Strawn's June 1911 book says nothing). Whether the borrowed measure was itself exactly right; it matched the valley, which is the best available indication.

### D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream

1. **The ledger's station gain** (Teague's own entries, copied by Kyle; Sill's weighing; noted in Rundle's retraction). Every year *more* butterfat reached Larrow Green than the patrons were credited with: 60,800 against 57,760, 5¼% of credited. A tester who skimmed off cream and wrote low tests to hide it would deliver less fat than credited, or at most the same. The cream arrived heavy.
2. **The 1911 interval** (Keddie's test book, Orra's diary, Duncan, Falke). Same man, same supervision, different glass: the tests rose to 3.80 for nine weeks and fell back to 3.61.
3. **1924** (Keddie's unsent letter, Ivy, Vaught). After the glass was replaced, the station averaged 3.80 for a full year with Keddie still testing. A thief does not stop because the pipettes were changed.
4. **Independent yardsticks.** Emmet's scales always agreed with the station's (Roy, Hazel). The same milk tested about 3.80 at Alder Corners (Rosalie). The error was a constant proportion across every patron and season (Vaught), which a thief's take would not be.

**Why these are independent.** They rest on different records kept by different people at different places: the Association's accounts at Larrow Green, weighed by a buttermaker at the far end of the haul; the tester's own book at the station; post-1923 station figures; a private farm book; and a cheese factory's books in another county. None depends on the 1958 measurement or on any other item in the list.

**Still valid without the 1958 measurement:** the station gain (reason 1), and equally 1911 and 1924. All three existed before the glass was ever measured. The ledger stood on an open shelf and was read aloud at every annual meeting.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question his own entries?** No. Teague invented the line in 1905 and wrote "as usual" every year. Kyle says Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years." Teague says he raised the Hollow matter with Vose in 1916, but he never asked what his gain figure implied. At the 1925 hearing he insinuated theft, and his 1948 memoir still says "cream is portable" while misreading the 5¼% as a share of "fat actually in cream." The entries that refute his accusation were in his own hand and he never saw it.

### D3. Did the manager know before 1923 that the tests ran low?

**Yes, he knew the station's tests ran low. He did not, on the evidence, know or grasp that the station's own testing was the cause.**

The low test was public from 1902, and the 1908 committee was appointed by the Association's board (his father was president). Vose himself sent the June 1919 circular warning the Hollow patrons about water, and that circular only makes sense as a response to low tests. Teague says he raised the matter with Vose in 1916.

What the sources do not show is that he understood the station-gain line he signed every year. Kyle, who sat outside his door for 25 years, says Vose "read the totals and he never read the lines above them," signing with "his thumb on the bottom figure." In his 1926 address, after the condemnation, Vose still argued water, gave wrong figures (a 0.21 gap, 1924 at 3.90, condemnation "12 July"), and called the condemnation "housekeeping" in a station whose fittings wore with use. That is the stance of a man who never connected the pieces, not one concealing them. **I infer** there was no guilty knowledge of the cause. The sources establish only that he knew of the shortfall, that he had the gain figure in front of him every year, and that he read no further than the totals. Whether he *should* have known is a separate matter, and the answer is plainly yes.

## Section F — Theory

### F1. What produced the shortfall

**Causal theory.** In April 1897 the Association bought from Ammon & Sons, Tarnet, a lot of 72 Babcock pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. that actually delivered about 16.72 c.c., short in the making and consistently so. A Babcock bottle's neck is ruled for a 17.6 c.c. charge, so a charge 5% short reads 5% low, for every sample, every patron and every season.

- **Size:** milk that truly tested 3.80 read **3.61**.
- **Why that size:** 16.72 / 17.6 = 0.95, and 0.95 × 3.80 = 3.61. The gap of **0.19** is one part in twenty of the true test. Richer milk lost more points, since the error is proportional.
- **Why it began then:** it began on **1 May 1897**, when the station's own outfit replaced the correct glass Sill had lent for the 1896 season.
- **Why it ended then:** it ended when Strawn condemned the lot on **12 June 1923** and new Ammon glass came into use in July. The 1924 average was 3.80.
- **Why nothing else had to change:** the error lived entirely in the glass. The cows, hay, scales, farmers, acid, method and tester (Keddie tested before, during and after) were all constant. The one time the glass was swapped by accident, for nine weeks in 1911, the figures rose to 3.80 and fell back.

The fat was never lost. It was delivered, skimmed and received at Larrow Green, which is why the ledger showed a "station gain" of 5¼% of credited fat every year. Over 1898–1922 the patrons were uncredited 76,000 lb, about $19,000.

**The three competing explanations**
1. **Added water.** Advanced by manager Selby Vose (the 1919 circular, the 1926 address) and believed by Sill; argued at the hearing. **Defeated by:** Strawn's lactometer at every visit from 1908, which never found water; Emmet's scales matching the station's; Jerome Cudd never docking the same milk at Alder Corners, where it tested 3.80; and the ledger, since water cannot make *more* fat arrive at Larrow Green than was credited. The gap also did not move after the circular. It moved in 1923 with the glass.
2. **Theft of cream by the tester.** Insinuated by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague at the 1925 hearing ("cream is portable"), and the valley's "settled opinion" per Rundle; also Vira and Oram's father. **Defeated by:** the station gain (cream arrived heavier than credited, as Sill weighed it); the 1911 interval (same man, correct figures); and 1924 (Keddie still testing, average 3.80).
3. **Wet hay / sour soil of Hessel Bottom.** Advanced by the 1908 Grigg committee and by Dr. Roland Kepp (1926), and partly conceded by Falke before he withdrew it. **Defeated by:** Bulletin 214 itself (at most 0.05, winter only, nothing on summer pasture); a gap that stayed a flat 0.19 in July as in February; the 1911 summer rise; the summer Alder Corners tests of 3.80 on the same bottom's milk; and the 1924 jump with the same land and hay.

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware? No.** The exhibits were four books only (Frick, Kyle). The crate sat in the loft above the weigh room where two of the three sittings were held. Strawn's 1923 notebook recording the condemnation was an exhibit, but nobody set it beside the receiving book, and Loomis records that the crate was never raised. Frick's claim that "every physical appliance" was before them is contradicted by his own exhibit list.

**What the tester himself believed.** From 1911 Keddie suspected the glass. He came home saying "it was something in the glass" (Duncan), wrote "? — A.K." against the entries, and his unsent 1925 letter points to the 1911 entries, the constant gap and 1924's 3.80. He would not put in writing what he could not prove, and he never said it publicly. Frick recalls that Keddie privately said he thought the patrons were at fault. That conflicts with the rest, and I give Keddie's own letter and his son's memory more weight. The honest summary: he suspected the glass, was never sure, and kept silent.

## Section G — Summary

### G1.

In 1897 the Larrow Valley creamery's skimming station at Ostrey Hollow began testing with a $77.93 outfit from Tarnet. Its pipettes, marked 17.6 c.c., delivered 16.72, five per cent short. Patrons were paid on that test, so for twenty-six years milk worth 3.80 read 3.61, costing the Hessel Bottom farmers 76,000 lb of butterfat, about $19,000. The valley blamed water, theft and wet hay. The tester, Ansel Keddie, was dismissed in 1925, and the patrons got $3,800 without admission.

The proof lay in eight separate places: the invoice, Keddie's 1911 test-book entries, Emmet Brant's weigh book, the Alder Corners cheese tests, the ledger's "station gain," inspector Strawn's 1923 condemnation, the arbitration record, and the crate left in the loft. It stayed scattered until his granddaughter Ivy measured the glass in 1958 and set the records side by side.
