# Answer sheet — Part 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

### C1. The proportion, true test, pounds and money

**(a) Proportion.** The pipettes were marked 17.6 c.c. and delivered a mean of 16.72 c.c. (9 pipettes, runs from 16.70 to 16.74, measured 9 March 1958).

16.72 / 17.60 = **0.950**. Each charge was 0.88 c.c. short, **5%, one part in twenty**. The bottle neck is ruled for a 17.6 c.c. charge, so the fat reading scales with the milk actually delivered: recorded test = true test × 0.95. Every test at the station therefore read **5% below the truth, in proportion**.

**(b) True test and shortfall.** Recorded average 3.61. True = 3.61 / 0.95 = **3.80%**. The recorded tests were short by **0.19 points**. Three independent checks agree on 3.80: the 1911 borrowed-glass interval, the 1924 average with new glass, and Alder Corners. I reject Vaught's body figure of 16.27 c.c.: it gives a ratio of 0.9244, a true test of 3.905 and a shortfall of 0.295, and he himself notes it does not fit 1924's 3.80.

**(c) Pounds not credited, 1898–1922** (25 full years, 40,000,000 lb of milk):
- True fat: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb
- Credited: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb
- Not credited: **76,000 lb** (also 40,000,000 × 0.0019)

**(d) Money and fraction received.** 76,000 lb × $0.25 = **$19,000**. The patrons received **$3,800**, which is 3,800 / 19,000 = **one-fifth (20%)**. The 25¢ figure is Kyle's period average, compiled for the arbitration; Roy's 24¢ for 1910 is consistent with it. I reject 27¢ (Sill, Kepp), $19,600 (Rundle, *Chronicle*) and $3,500 (Vira, Oram).

### C2. Emmet Brant, 1910

200,000 lb of milk, paid on a test of 3.42, both scales agreeing.

**(a) Credited:** 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of butterfat.

**(b) Owed and not credited:** true test = 3.42 / 0.95 = 3.60%, so true fat = 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb. The shortfall is **360 lb**. For his milk the gap is 0.18 points; the error is proportional, so his leaner milk lost a little less than the station average of 0.19. Roy's "0.17" does not fit his own leaf.

**(c) Worth that year:** 360 × $0.24 (1910 price per the leaf) = **$86.40**. At the 25¢ period average it would be $90.00.

### C3. Credited versus received, 1898–1922

**(a) Credited on the station test:** **1,444,000 lb**.

**(b) Received at Larrow Green in the station cream:** **1,520,000 lb**, the fat actually delivered. The ledger year matches exactly: 57,760 × 25 = 1,444,000 and 60,800 × 25 = 1,520,000, with 1,600,000 lb of milk a year.

**(c) Percentage.** Difference 76,000 lb.
- As a percentage of the fat **credited**: 76,000 / 1,444,000 = **5.263%**. This is Teague's "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" (3,040 / 57,760 = 5.263%).
- As a percentage of the fat **received**, the true delivered fat: 76,000 / 1,520,000 = **5.000%**. This is the pipette's one part in twenty.

Teague (1948) and Vaught's body text wrongly describe the 5¼% as a percentage of fat actually delivered. It is a percentage of fat credited.

### C4. The invoice

**(a) Total:** 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = **$77.93**, which matches the invoice total. "$77.39" in Junia's prose and the *Chronicle* is a transposition.

**(b) Pipettes:** 6 dozen = **72 pipettes** at 35¢ each (72 × 0.35 = 25.20). Each was short **0.88 c.c.** (17.60 − 16.72) on the measured mean; the nine survivors ranged from 0.86 to 0.90 short. The shortfall of the other 63 is inferred from the consistency of the nine and from Strawn condemning the whole lot.

### C5. Duration and first season

**(a) Time in use:** 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923, which is **26 years, 1 month and 11 days** (26 years plus 42 days, 9,537 days).

**The money calculation** runs over **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922**.

They differ because the glass was in use for only part of 1897 (from May) and part of 1923 (to 12 June). The annual station accounts and the 40,000,000-lb total compiled for the arbitration cover complete calendar years only, so the partial years are left out. The award calculation therefore slightly *understates* the loss by omitting about 8 months of 1897 and 5½ months of 1923. It slightly *overstates* it by including the nine weeks of 1911, when borrowed correct glass was in use.

**(b) First-season intake: cannot be determined from the sources.** Sill remembers about 900,000 lb and Kyle "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds." Both are memories, they disagree, and Kyle admits it "is not in any book I ever ruled." The 1896 sheets were kept on the creamery's books and never filed with the station papers, and Nym's catalogue records that no 1896 figure exists. The first season does not matter to the loss in any case, because it was tested with Sill's borrowed, correct glass.

## Section D — Logic

### D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment

**When and how long.** The measure broke on 8 May 1911 and borrowed glass from Larrow Green was used until 10 July: **nine weeks**. The dates are in Keddie's book and Orra's diary. "Six weeks" (Teague, Kepp) is wrong.

**Design.** The single variable was the testing glass: the station's own versus a borrowed measure. Held constant were the tester (Keddie), the farms and herds, the scales, the acid and method, the season's feeding, and the supervision.

**Result.** The tests rose to about 3.80, level with the valley and the true milk. They fell back to 3.61 the day the station's own glass returned. Keddie marked "? — A.K."

**What it establishes that calculation cannot.** It is direct, contemporary evidence of cause:
- The station's own glass produced the low figures, and swapping it reversed them.
- The fault was already present in 1911, 14 years before the hearing and 47 before the measurement.
- The same man with different glass got correct figures, which rules out his dishonesty and his technique.
- A summer rise against a flat year-round gap rules out the season and the hay.

The 1958 arithmetic assumes the pipettes were always as short as they measured in 1958. The 1911 interval shows that they were already short then.

**What it does not establish.** The size of the shortfall in c.c. (that needs the burette). That every year from 1897 to 1923 was affected, which needs 1924 and the ledger as well. Whether the fault lay in manufacture or later wear. Who, if anyone, knew. The money. Which piece of borrowed glass was used and who lent it (Roy says the state man; Strawn's June 1911 book says nothing). Whether the borrowed measure was itself exactly right; it matched the valley, which is the best available indication.

### D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream

1. **The ledger's station gain** (Teague's own entries, copied by Kyle; Sill's weighing; noted in Rundle's retraction). Every year *more* butterfat reached Larrow Green than the patrons were credited with: 60,800 against 57,760, 5¼% of credited. A tester who skimmed off cream and wrote low tests to hide it would deliver less fat than credited, or at most the same. The cream arrived heavy.
2. **The 1911 interval** (Keddie's test book, Orra's diary, Duncan, Falke). Same man, same supervision, different glass: the tests rose to 3.80 for nine weeks and fell back to 3.61.
3. **1924** (Keddie's unsent letter, Ivy, Vaught). After the glass was replaced, the station averaged 3.80 for a full year with Keddie still testing. A thief does not stop because the pipettes were changed.
4. **Independent yardsticks.** Emmet's scales always agreed with the station's (Roy, Hazel). The same milk tested about 3.80 at Alder Corners (Rosalie). The error was a constant proportion across every patron and season (Vaught), which a thief's take would not be.

**Why these are independent.** They rest on different records kept by different people at different places: the Association's accounts at Larrow Green, weighed by a buttermaker at the far end of the haul; the tester's own book at the station; post-1923 station figures; a private farm book; and a cheese factory's books in another county. None depends on the 1958 measurement or on any other item in the list.

**Still valid without the 1958 measurement:** the station gain (reason 1), and equally 1911 and 1924. All three existed before the glass was ever measured. The ledger stood on an open shelf and was read aloud at every annual meeting.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question his own entries?** No. Teague invented the line in 1905 and wrote "as usual" every year. Kyle says Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years." Teague says he raised the Hollow matter with Vose in 1916, but he never asked what his gain figure implied. At the 1925 hearing he insinuated theft, and his 1948 memoir still says "cream is portable" while misreading the 5¼% as a share of "fat actually in cream." The entries that refute his accusation were in his own hand and he never saw it.

### D3. Did the manager know before 1923 that the tests ran low?

**Yes, he knew the station's tests ran low. He did not, on the evidence, know or grasp that the station's own testing was the cause.**

The low test was public from 1902, and the 1908 committee was appointed by the Association's board (his father was president). Vose himself sent the June 1919 circular warning the Hollow patrons about water, and that circular only makes sense as a response to low tests. Teague says he raised the matter with Vose in 1916.

What the sources do not show is that he understood the station-gain line he signed every year. Kyle, who sat outside his door for 25 years, says Vose "read the totals and he never read the lines above them," signing with "his thumb on the bottom figure." In his 1926 address, after the condemnation, Vose still argued water, gave wrong figures (a 0.21 gap, 1924 at 3.90, condemnation "12 July"), and called the condemnation "housekeeping" in a station whose fittings wore with use. That is the stance of a man who never connected the pieces, not one concealing them. **I infer** there was no guilty knowledge of the cause. The sources establish only that he knew of the shortfall, that he had the gain figure in front of him every year, and that he read no further than the totals. Whether he *should* have known is a separate matter, and the answer is plainly yes.

## Section F — Theory

### F1. What produced the shortfall

**Causal theory.** In April 1897 the Association bought from Ammon & Sons, Tarnet, a lot of 72 Babcock pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. that actually delivered about 16.72 c.c., short in the making and consistently so. A Babcock bottle's neck is ruled for a 17.6 c.c. charge, so a charge 5% short reads 5% low, for every sample, every patron and every season.

- **Size:** milk that truly tested 3.80 read **3.61**.
- **Why that size:** 16.72 / 17.6 = 0.95, and 0.95 × 3.80 = 3.61. The gap of **0.19** is one part in twenty of the true test. Richer milk lost more points, since the error is proportional.
- **Why it began then:** it began on **1 May 1897**, when the station's own outfit replaced the correct glass Sill had lent for the 1896 season.
- **Why it ended then:** it ended when Strawn condemned the lot on **12 June 1923** and new Ammon glass came into use in July. The 1924 average was 3.80.
- **Why nothing else had to change:** the error lived entirely in the glass. The cows, hay, scales, farmers, acid, method and tester (Keddie tested before, during and after) were all constant. The one time the glass was swapped by accident, for nine weeks in 1911, the figures rose to 3.80 and fell back.

The fat was never lost. It was delivered, skimmed and received at Larrow Green, which is why the ledger showed a "station gain" of 5¼% of credited fat every year. Over 1898–1922 the patrons were uncredited 76,000 lb, about $19,000.

**The three competing explanations**
1. **Added water.** Advanced by manager Selby Vose (the 1919 circular, the 1926 address) and believed by Sill; argued at the hearing. **Defeated by:** Strawn's lactometer at every visit from 1908, which never found water; Emmet's scales matching the station's; Jerome Cudd never docking the same milk at Alder Corners, where it tested 3.80; and the ledger, since water cannot make *more* fat arrive at Larrow Green than was credited. The gap also did not move after the circular. It moved in 1923 with the glass.
2. **Theft of cream by the tester.** Insinuated by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague at the 1925 hearing ("cream is portable"), and the valley's "settled opinion" per Rundle; also Vira and Oram's father. **Defeated by:** the station gain (cream arrived heavier than credited, as Sill weighed it); the 1911 interval (same man, correct figures); and 1924 (Keddie still testing, average 3.80).
3. **Wet hay / sour soil of Hessel Bottom.** Advanced by the 1908 Grigg committee and by Dr. Roland Kepp (1926), and partly conceded by Falke before he withdrew it. **Defeated by:** Bulletin 214 itself (at most 0.05, winter only, nothing on summer pasture); a gap that stayed a flat 0.19 in July as in February; the 1911 summer rise; the summer Alder Corners tests of 3.80 on the same bottom's milk; and the 1924 jump with the same land and hay.

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware? No.** The exhibits were four books only (Frick, Kyle). The crate sat in the loft above the weigh room where two of the three sittings were held. Strawn's 1923 notebook recording the condemnation was an exhibit, but nobody set it beside the receiving book, and Loomis records that the crate was never raised. Frick's claim that "every physical appliance" was before them is contradicted by his own exhibit list.

**What the tester himself believed.** From 1911 Keddie suspected the glass. He came home saying "it was something in the glass" (Duncan), wrote "? — A.K." against the entries, and his unsent 1925 letter points to the 1911 entries, the constant gap and 1924's 3.80. He would not put in writing what he could not prove, and he never said it publicly. Frick recalls that Keddie privately said he thought the patrons were at fault. That conflicts with the rest, and I give Keddie's own letter and his son's memory more weight. The honest summary: he suspected the glass, was never sure, and kept silent.

## Section G — Summary

### G1.

In 1897 the Larrow Valley creamery's skimming station at Ostrey Hollow began testing with a $77.93 outfit from Tarnet. Its pipettes, marked 17.6 c.c., delivered 16.72, five per cent short. Patrons were paid on that test, so for twenty-six years milk worth 3.80 read 3.61, costing the Hessel Bottom farmers 76,000 lb of butterfat, about $19,000. The valley blamed water, theft and wet hay. The tester, Ansel Keddie, was dismissed in 1925, and the patrons got $3,800 without admission.

The proof lay in eight separate places: the invoice, Keddie's 1911 test-book entries, Emmet Brant's weigh book, the Alder Corners cheese tests, the ledger's "station gain," inspector Strawn's 1923 condemnation, the arbitration record, and the crate left in the loft. It stayed scattered until his granddaughter Ivy measured the glass in 1958 and set the records side by side.
