# Answer Sheet — Part 1 (Section A, stories 1–4)

*Note on sources: this session read retellings r17–r24 directly. No notes on r01–r16 were present in this session's context, so every answer below rests on r17–r24 alone. Where r01–r16 might settle a point, I say so rather than guess.*

## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1. The rule and the station (1889–1897)

**Who:** Gideon Vose, the farmers of the Larrow Valley, and Ansel Keddie.

**What happened and when:** Valley farmers meeting at Larrow Green organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association in **1889**. Gideon Vose organized it, was its first president and wrote its by-laws. The main creamery was built at Larrow Green in **1891**. By-law article **seven** is the hinge of the whole history: every patron is paid on the pounds of **butterfat** his milk is found by test to contain, not on the weight of the milk, and the test at the station where he delivers is the one that governs his pay. Under payment by weight, nothing that happened later would have cost anyone a dollar. The year the article was adopted **cannot be determined**: the printed by-laws are undated and the early minute book was lost (Falke).

In the winter of **1895–96** the Association built a skimming station at **Ostrey Hollow**, seven miles up the valley, on a road that climbs the whole way. The station served the farms of **Hessel Bottom**: **six farms** on wet ground, of which Emmet Brant's was the largest. It opened on **4 May 1896**. It was a two-floor board building with a covered platform. Inside were the intake (weigh) room with the scale, the vat and the separator, and over that room a loft reached by a ladder nailed to the wall. Milk was weighed, sampled, tested and separated. The cream was hauled down to Larrow Green, for more than twenty years by Cleve Oram's father, and the skim went back to the patrons.

The tester and separator man from the day it opened was **Ansel Keddie** (born 1861). In 1889 he had married **Orra**, Emmet Brant's sister. He held the post for **29 seasons (1896–1924)**. Hazel Brant's "went up in '95" fits the building winter.

For its **first season (1896)** the station **borrowed its testing glass from the Larrow Green creamery**. Its own outfit went into use on **1 May 1897**, which is confirmed by Orra's diary entry of that exact date and by the Ammon history. Kyle's "1 May 1898" is wrong: the contemporaneous diary and the dated invoice outrank a recollection. Hazel's "'99" is also wrong. That first borrowed-glass season is therefore **outside** the faulty-glass period. The first season's intake **cannot be established**: Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" is hearsay that he says is "not in any book".

**Documents:** Falke's report ¶¶3–4 (r22); Kyle (r23); Orra's diary (r24); Duncan's notes (r18); Cleve Oram (r21); the *Chronicle* (r19).

### Story 2. Six dozen pipettes from Tarnet (1897, with 1923, 1931 and 1949)

**Who:** Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet, founded by Marcus Ammon; the Association; and Junia Ammon, the firm's historian.

**What happened:** The firm blew the glass for about half the creameries in the state. A milk-test pipette is a *to-deliver* vessel: it must deliver **17.6 c.c.**, the charge that the neck of the test bottle is ruled for. The firm's invoice, dated **14 April 1897** and reproduced in facsimile, was made out to the Association for the Ostrey Hollow station:

- 6 doz. milk-test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at $0.35: **$25.20**
- 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at $0.22: **$31.68**
- 1 acid measure: **$0.90**
- 1 four-bottle hand tester: **$18.00**
- Freight: **$2.15**
- **Total: $77.93**

The lines add up to exactly $77.93. The "$77.39" in Junia's own prose and in the *Chronicle* is a transposition: the facsimile and the arithmetic outrank it. The outfit went into use on **1 May 1897**. It contained **72 pipettes**, not the "five dozen" Cleve Oram remembered.

That lot delivered **short, by about 5%**. A pipette marked 17.6 c.c. delivered about **16.72 c.c.**, roughly **0.88 c.c.** short (this figure is inferred; see C1/C4). A short charge in a bottle ruled for a full one makes the fat read low in the same proportion, every time.

The firm's works books, which recorded glass, gauge, man and day, **burned in 1931** when the old third floor caught fire. Only the office side survives: invoices, letter books and day books. When the lot was condemned, the Association wrote to the firm on **28 June 1923** ordering six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles. The reply of **2 July 1923** entered the order, "regret[ted] the trouble", and said that its books of the nineties could tell nothing about the lot.

Junia believes a **reground mold** made the lot run short by accident, and that spot checks could not catch an error that was *consistent*. That is her inference, not a record. The *Chronicle*'s charge that the firm knowingly sold a short lot is unsupported. Whether the fault was known **cannot be determined**, because the works records are gone. Junia also contradicts herself: she says the house sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900, yet she prints the 1897 invoice.

**Documents:** Junia Ammon's chapter IX (r20), the *Chronicle* (r19), Cleve Oram (r21).

### Story 3. Nineteen hundredths, the Brant weigh book and the hay theory (1896–1911, answered 1959)

**Who:** The Hessel Bottom patrons, chiefly Emmet Brant and later his son Roy; the Association's 1908 committee; and Jerome Cudd of the cheese factory at Alder Corners.

**What happened:** From the start, Ostrey Hollow milk tested low. The station averaged **3.61** against Larrow Green's **3.78**. The shortfall was **0.19 point, flat**, in every month: in the June flush and in February, in wet years and dry. A feed effect would not behave that way. The first complaint on record came in **1902** (Falke; Orra's diary, March 1902: store talk that "our milk up here runs poorer"). After that the patrons complained at every annual meeting.

**Emmet Brant** began his private **weigh book** on **1 January 1897**. I take Duncan's written date over Hazel's "1900", since Hazel says herself that she cannot hold years. He weighed his milk on his own scale and wrote the station's weight beside it. They agreed. His **1910** leaf reads: **200,000 lb even**, "his scale and theirs agreeing", paid on a test of **3.42**. That credited him with 6,840 lb of fat when he was owed about 7,200 lb, a shortfall of about **360 lb**, or **about $90** at the long-run price of 25¢ (see C2).

There was also a natural comparison. **Four of the six** Hessel Bottom farmers hauled over the ridge during the summer flush to Jerome Cudd's cheese factory at **Alder Corners** in Vessey County. The same milk tested **higher** there, by the same nineteen hundredths. Cudd's test book passed to his daughter **Rosalie** in 1941, and she had its figures copied for Ivy in 1958.

In **January 1908** a committee of four came up in Grigg's sleigh, dined at Emmet's, and looked at the barns rather than the station. In **March 1908** it reported that the **wet bog hay** of the bottom thinned the cream. An Ashlin veterinarian wrote the same in 1926, and Falke first conceded the point (¶9). That theory is defeated by the college's own **Bulletin No. 214**, a 1911 feeding trial: bog hay as the whole roughage lowered the test by "not more than five hundredths" in winter and by nothing measurable on summer pasture. That cannot produce 0.19 in July. Falke withdrew his concession, and the 1908 committee was simply wrong.

**Documents:** Hazel Brant's tape (r17), Falke (r22) with his note on Bulletin 214, Orra's diary (r24), Duncan's notes (r18) on the books.

### Story 4. Nine weeks in 1911: the borrowed measure

**Who:** Ansel Keddie, with his wife Orra and son Duncan as witnesses at home.

**What happened and when:** On **8 May 1911** the station's own measuring glass broke. Orra wrote that day: "A. home cross about a broken measure. Rain all day." A measure **borrowed from elsewhere** was used in its place until **10 July 1911**, exactly **nine weeks** (63 days). Throughout those nine weeks the station's tests stood **markedly higher**. On the day the station went back to its own glass, they **fell back** to their old level.

Nothing else changed: the same herds, pasture, season, patrons, tester, machinery, scales and procedure. Ansel recorded the fact in the station test book and set a **question mark** against it.

That summer he came home saying "it was something in the glass". He said it twice, at supper and again at the door, which was not his way, and seventeen-year-old Duncan remembered it. He never dared write it in the book, because he would not put down what he could not prove. He had only a suspicion about a piece of glass, set against the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county. So he went on testing with the same glass for fourteen more years and faithfully recorded every low figure it gave him. Those figures were later used to convict him. Duncan left in October 1911 to teach school at Ashlin. **Selby Vose** became the Association's manager the same year.

The 1958 bulletin treats this interval as **decisive**. It shows that the low readings travelled with the station's glass and not with the milk or the man: a controlled before-and-after within one season. The sources do **not** give the size of the rise ("markedly higher" only), and they do not show that the borrowed measure was itself exact. They also leave unclear exactly which piece of glass was swapped, since the outfit had one acid measure but 72 pipettes. No one acted on it for 47 years.

**Documents:** Falke ¶6 and ¶8 (r22), Orra's diary entry of 8 May 1911 (r24), Duncan's notes §IV (r18).
