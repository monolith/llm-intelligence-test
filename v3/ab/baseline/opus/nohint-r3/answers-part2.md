# Answer Sheet — Part 2 (Section A, stories 5–8; Section B)

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### Story 5. "Station gain, as usual": the office at Larrow Green and the water circular (1911–1925)

**Who:** Bertram Kyle, the outer-desk clerk for 25 years; Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper; **Selby Vose**, manager from **1911** for about a quarter-century and **son** (not nephew) of Gideon Vose; and the Ostrey Hollow patrons.

**What happened:** Every year the Ostrey Hollow station account carried three figures and a sentence. The first figure was the pounds of butterfat **credited** to the patrons on the station test. The second was the pounds of butterfat actually **received** at Larrow Green in the station's cream. The third was the difference, entered as **"station gain"**, followed always by Teague's words "**five and a quarter per cent, as usual**, carried to the general account."

More fat was reaching the creamery than the patrons were being paid for. Teague wrote that line every year and never asked about it. Vose signed each sheet with his thumb on the bottom figure and never read the lines above it. The account was read out at every annual meeting. It was in plain sight, and because it had a name nobody questioned it.

For the arbitration, Kyle extracted the long-run figures: **40,000,000 lb** of milk received at the station, **1,600,000 lb a year** (that is, **25 years**), with butterfat averaging **25¢ a pound**. The arithmetic:
- At the recorded 3.61, the fat credited is **1,444,000 lb**.
- At the true 3.80, the fat reaching the creamery is **1,520,000 lb**.
- The gap is **76,000 lb**, which is **5.26% of the credited fat**. That is Kyle's "five and a quarter per cent", and it is 5% of the fat actually received.

A thief skimming cream at the station would have produced a station **loss**, and watered milk produces no gain in fat at all. The ledger therefore refuted both accusations every year.

In **June 1919** Vose sent the Ostrey Hollow patrons a **circular warning against adding water**. Kyle typed it, and it went out over Vose's name alone. Ansel would not mail it or leave it on the platform. He carried it himself to **eleven families** and stood at each door while it was read, as Orra's contemporaneous diary records. Hazel, who was a small child then, remembered nine. Two days later **Emmet Brant** confronted Ansel in the yard. He never entered the Keddie house again, and the two men never reconciled before Emmet died in 1929.

The water theory was already disproved. The state inspector had tested with the **lactometer at every visit from 1908** and never once found added water at Ostrey Hollow.

**Documents:** Kyle (r23), Orra's diary (r24), Falke ¶7 (r22), Hazel (r17), Duncan (r18) on the Vose father–son relationship.

### Story 6. The inspector and the crate (1908–1958)

**Who:** **Merle Strawn**, the state dairy inspector on the Ordell circuit, who was kin to nobody in the valley. He was a heavy, quiet man who wrote everything in a little notebook with a rubber band round it.

**What happened:** Strawn tested Ostrey Hollow milk with the lactometer at every visit from 1908 and never found water. On **12 June 1923**, on an ordinary circuit visit, he ran the station's milk pipettes against a **calibrated burette**. They would not deliver their mark. He **condemned the whole lot** on the spot and told the manager to order new glass. The Association ordered replacements from Ammon & Sons on **28 June**, and the new glass was in use by **July 1923**; Orra wrote that month that "they have new glass at the station".

The year is **1923**. That is fixed by the Ammon letter book (28 June and 2 July), Orra's diary, Falke, Duncan and the *Chronicle*. Hazel's "'21" and Kyle's "'22" are memory errors.

Strawn noted the condemnation but drew **no inference** about the 26 years before it, and nobody connected the two things. **From 1924 the station's average test stood at 3.85 and stayed there.** The same tester was working the same herds, and only the glass had changed.

The condemned glassware was crated and put up in the **loft over the intake room** under a tarpaulin. It stayed there for **35 years (1923 to February 1958)**, about twenty feet above the room where two of the three arbitration sittings later met. People going up for wire broke pieces, and **nine pipettes** survived. This count is given independently by Cleve Oram, who handled the crate, and by Falke's report; the *Chronicle*'s "eleven" is an outlier.

Strawn kept one notebook for every year on the circuit. They went to the state dairy office when he retired in **1936** and to the library of the State Agricultural College at **Farland** in **1947**. There, eleven years later, Ivy found the **1923** volume; nobody had asked for them in between. The 1923 notebook had also been one of the four books placed before the 1925 board, and nobody read it against the receiving book.

**Documents:** Falke ¶¶7, 11, 12 (r22); Kyle (r23); Duncan §V (r18); Cleve Oram (r21); Junia Ammon (r20); Orra's diary (r24).

### Story 7. The arbitration and the dismissal (1925–1934)

**Who:** Ansel Keddie, the patrons (including Emmet Brant), the Association's officers, and a board chaired by an Ashlin attorney.

**What happened:** The patrons petitioned in **January 1925**, with Emmet's name on the paper. The board impounded the station test book in March. It sat from **17 March to 4 May 1925**. There were three sittings: the first at Ashlin, in the Cooperative's office, and **two in the weigh room at the station itself**, directly under the crate of condemned glass. Farmers stood outside on the platform and in the road, some of them in the rain.

Four books went before the board: the station **test book** in Keddie's hand, the **receiving book**, **Emmet Brant's weigh book** (lent and wanted back), and **Strawn's 1923 notebook**. Nobody laid the receiving book beside the notebook. The three theories argued were:
- water, the manager's view;
- cream theft by the tester, urged by an officer of the Association;
- bog hay.

Keddie was **dismissed on 30 April 1925** ("twenty-nine seasons and a letter"), **four days before** the board's finding of **4 May 1925**. The board found no cause established and awarded the patrons **$3,800 "without admission"**, charged to the station account. That sum is exactly **one-fifth** of the $19,000 later computed. Three sources give $3,800 (Falke, Kyle and the *Chronicle*); Cleve's $3,500 is hearsay from his father. Kyle's "1924" and Hazel's "'26" are wrong. Roy Brant never went to another Association meeting.

Ansel came home at his usual hour, hung up his apron and ate ham. He rebuilt the cow shed, whistling, and worked as a carpenter at sixty-six; he hung the Orams' barn door, which swung true for thirty years. He died at home in **February 1934**, nine years after the dismissal, with the imputation still on him. The funeral was well attended, and nobody mentioned the accusation. Orra records his one remark on it: "a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name." Emmet had died in the spring of 1929, and the rift between the two men was never closed. Orra died in **1948** at 82.

**Documents:** Falke ¶10 (r22), Kyle (r23), Orra's diary (r24), Duncan §IV (r18), Cleve Oram (r21), Hazel (r17), the *Chronicle* (r19).

### Story 8. Up the ladder: Ivy Keddie and Bulletin No. 471 (1958–1970)

**Who:** **Ivy Keddie** (born 1931), daughter of Duncan Keddie (born 1894, schoolteacher at Ashlin) and **granddaughter of Ansel**. She was a graduate student in dairy husbandry at the State Agricultural College at Farland, working with **Professor Leland Vaught**.

**What happened:** In **February 1958** Ivy climbed the ladder into the station loft and found the crate. She took it east to Farland, and on **9 March 1958** measured the **nine** surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette. Every one delivered less than the 17.6 c.c. blown on its stem, and all were short **in the same proportion**, about **5%** (roughly 16.72 c.c.).

She assembled the other records alongside the crate:
- **Roy Brant's weigh book**, which Roy **lent** her in 1958 (he did not give it);
- **Strawn's 1923 notebook** from the Farland library;
- the **Alder Corners figures**, copied for her by **Rosalie Cudd**, Jerome Cudd's **daughter**.

**Bulletin No. 471** (October 1958, by Vaught and Keddie) concluded that a short charge into a bottle ruled for the full charge reads short in the same proportion. That alone explained the low tests: no milk was watered and no cream was stolen. The bulletin puts the uncredited butterfat at **76,000 lb** over **25 full delivery years**, worth **$19,000** at 25¢. The patrons had received $3,800, or 20%. The *Chronicle*'s $19,600 and Cleve's 67,000 lb do not survive the arithmetic.

The *Ashlin Chronicle* ran the story on **3 October 1958**, 33 years after the dismissal and 24 after Ansel's death, and it got several facts wrong. County agent Orin Falke reported to the county board in **1959**. He added a note withdrawing his hay concession after reading Bulletin 214, and correcting "great-niece" to "granddaughter".

Where the records went:
- The **station test book** stayed in the Cooperative office at Ashlin from 1925 to **1959**, then went to the Ordell County Historical Rooms at Ashlin.
- The **crate** came back from Farland and was deposited there in **1959**.
- The **weigh book** passed to Hazel when Roy died in **1962**, and she gave it to the Rooms in **1963**.
- The **Alder Corners book** went to the Vessey County Historical Society in **1970**.
- **Strawn's notebooks** remain at Farland.

Ivy and Hazel Brant are **second cousins**.

**Documents:** the *Chronicle* (r19), Falke with his note (r22), Duncan's notes with the 1970 addition (r18), Hazel (r17), Cleve Oram (r21), the editor's note to Orra's diary (r24).

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** Ansel (1861–1934) married Orra in 1889, and their son Duncan was born in 1894. Duncan writes that "my daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter." Cleve Oram also calls her "the granddaughter". The *Chronicle* and Falke's main text both say "great-niece", but that is one source, not two: both evidently took it from the college press release and bulletin. Falke himself retracts it ("verified… the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine"). The *Chronicle*'s dating also fits a granddaughter: Ivy was 27 in 1958, so born about 1931. The father's first-hand written statement plus Falke's verified correction outrank the repeated error.

**B2. Ivy and Hazel Brant are second cousins.** Emmet Brant and Orra were siblings. Emmet's son Roy and Orra's son Duncan are therefore first cousins, and their daughters Hazel and Ivy are second cousins. Duncan and Hazel both say so explicitly. Cleve's "first cousin once removed" is wrong: the two women are the same generation, and "removed" describes a generational gap.

The sources leave one point open: whether Orra was Emmet's full sister or his half-sister. Duncan calls her "Emmet Brant's sister, the younger of the two", which implies a full sibling. Hazel says she was "a half sister… by his father's second wife." Orra's own pre-1889 pages were not preserved, and she writes only "my brother Emmet". The intervening generation cannot be settled from these sources. If Hazel is right, the cousinships are strictly "half" first cousins and "half" second cousins. Either way, the degree is second cousins.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose.** Gideon organized the Association, was its first president and **wrote its by-laws**, including article seven, which pays each patron on the butterfat found by the **station's own test**. Duncan and Kyle both reject the "nephew" story independently. This matters for three reasons:
- The rule that turned a short pipette into a lost payment was the father's.
- The son, as manager from 1911, signed the ledger line that showed a 5¼% "station gain" every year without reading it.
- The son sent the 1919 water circular, blaming the patrons for a shortfall that his own ledger contradicted and that his father's rule had made costly.

**B4. There was no relationship between the Vose and Keddie families.** Duncan states it flatly. His mother "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection" and never put a Vose in it. He says the talk arose from confusion with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Gideon either. Cleve Oram's "cousins of some sort" is family hearsay ("my mother had it from her mother"), and he admits he "could not draw you the line on paper." I prefer the insider's specific denial, which also explains where the rumour came from, over an unsupported rumour.
