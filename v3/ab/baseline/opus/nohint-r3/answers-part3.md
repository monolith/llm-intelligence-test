# Answer Sheet — Part 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

**C1.**

(a) **The tests were 5% (one-twentieth) below the truth.** The glass works like this. A pipette marked 17.6 c.c. delivers a short charge into a bottle whose neck is ruled for 17.6 c.c., so the fat reads low by the same proportion.

The station figures fix that proportion: 0.19 ÷ 3.80 = **0.05**. A 5% short pipette delivers 17.6 × 0.95 = **16.72 c.c.** Kyle's ledger confirms it independently: a gain of 5¼% on credited fat is 1/0.95 − 1 = 5.26%.

The *Chronicle*'s "average 16.27 c.c." would imply 7.6% short. That fits none of the other figures, so I read it as a transposition of 16.72, like its $77.39 for $77.93. This is an inference from arithmetic.

(b) **The true average test was 3.80.** The recorded average was 3.61, and 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80. That is **0.19 point short**, consistent with Hazel's and Falke's "nineteen hundredths". For comparison, Larrow Green averaged 3.78, and the station averaged 3.85 after 1923 with good glass.

(c) Over 25 full delivery years the milk received was 40,000,000 lb (Kyle: 1,600,000 lb × 25).
- True fat = 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb.
- Credited fat = 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb.
- **Uncredited = 76,000 lb** (the same as 40,000,000 × 0.0019).

Cleve's "67,000 lb" is a digit reversal.

(d) 76,000 lb × $0.25 = **$19,000**. The patrons received $3,800, and 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **one-fifth (20%)**. The *Chronicle*'s $19,600 is wrong on the arithmetic: it would require 78,400 lb.

**C2.** Emmet Brant, 1910 (Hazel's reading of the weigh-book leaf): 200,000 lb of milk, test 3.42.

(a) Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of butterfat.

(b) The error is proportional, so the true test was 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60. He was owed 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb, so **360 lb** went uncredited.

(c) 360 × $0.25 = **$90**. This uses the long-run average price, since no 1910 price is given, so it is an inference. Applying the flat station-average 0.19 instead would give 380 lb and $95. The proportional figure is the correct one for a single patron.

**C3.** Over the same 25 years:

(a) Credited: **1,444,000 lb**.

(b) Reaching Larrow Green in the station cream: **1,520,000 lb**.

(c) The difference is 76,000 lb:
- as a percentage **of the credited fat**: 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.26%**, which is Kyle's "five and a quarter per cent" station gain;
- as a percentage **of the fat actually received** (the true fat): 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5.00%**, which is the size of the glass error.

**C4.**

(a) **$77.93**:
- 25.20 (6 doz pipettes × $0.35)
- \+ 31.68 (12 doz bottles × $0.22)
- \+ 0.90 (acid measure)
- \+ 18.00 (hand tester)
- \+ 2.15 (freight)
- = 77.93

The "$77.39" in Junia Ammon's prose and in the *Chronicle* is a transposition.

(b) **72 pipettes** (6 dozen; not Cleve's five dozen). Each was short by about **0.88 c.c.** on average: 17.6 − 16.72. This is inferred from the 5% error. The *Chronicle*'s 16.27 would give 1.33 c.c. but conflicts with every other figure.

**C5.**

(a) **The glass was in use from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923**, when Strawn condemned it: **26 years, 1 month and 11 days**, roughly Junia's "six and twenty years". Replacement glass was in use in July 1923.

**The money calculation runs over 25 full delivery years** (Kyle: 40,000,000 lb at 1,600,000 lb a year). The two numbers differ because the service period starts and ends mid-year:
- 1897 was a part year, with deliveries on the station's own glass only from May;
- 1923 was a part year, ending in June;
- the first season, 1896, used borrowed Larrow Green glass and was never affected.

The full years in between, **1898–1922**, number 25. Treating calendar years as the delivery years is my inference.

(b) **Cannot be determined from these sources.** The only figure is Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" (1,100,000 lb). He heard it from men who were there, and he says it "is not in any book I ever ruled", because the first season was carried on the creamery's own books and those sheets were never filed with the station papers. That is hearsay with no document behind it. (Retellings r01–r16, which I did not have this session, might settle it.)

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran from **8 May to 10 July 1911, nine weeks**. Falke gives the dates, and Orra's diary independently notes the broken measure on 8 May.

- **Variable:** the measuring glass, which changed from the station's own to one borrowed from elsewhere.
- **Held constant:** the herds, feed and pasture, season, patrons, the tester himself, supervision, scales, acid and machinery.
- **Result:** the tests rose markedly for the whole interval and fell back on the day the station's own glass returned. That is a switch-on, switch-off reversal.

A calculation can only show that a 5% short pipette *would* produce 0.19. The interval shows **by intervention** that the station's own glass *was* the cause in practice. It disposes of the theft theory, because the same man was testing under the same supervision, and of the water and hay theories, because the same milk and the same feed were involved. It did all this in 1911, 47 years before anyone measured the pipettes.

It does **not** establish:
- the size of the error, since the sources give no figures for the rise;
- that the borrowed measure was itself accurate;
- which piece of glass was at fault (the "measure" could be read as the single acid measure rather than one of the 72 pipettes);
- that the error went back to 1897 or lasted until 1923;
- how or why the lot came to be short.

**D2.** Independent reasons Keddie cannot have been stealing cream:

1. **The ledger's "station gain" (Kyle, r23).** Every year Larrow Green received about 5¼% *more* butterfat in the station cream than the patrons were credited with. Theft at the station would have produced a shortfall at the creamery, not a surplus.
2. **The 1911 borrowed measure (Falke r22, corroborated by Orra r24 and Duncan r18).** The same man, under the same supervision, produced higher tests with a different glass.
3. **The post-1923 rise (Falke r22).** With new glass from July 1923, the average went to 3.85 and stayed there while Keddie was still testing, until April 1925.
4. **The Alder Corners comparison (Hazel r17 and Duncan r18 on Cudd's book).** The same Hessel Bottom milk tested 0.19 higher at a factory Keddie never touched. Emmet's own scale also agreed with the station's weights.
5. **The constancy of the error.** A fixed 0.19 in every month and every season is the signature of an instrument, not of opportunistic skimming.

These are independent because they come from different records kept by different people for different purposes: the Association's own ledger, the tester's test book, a rival firm's test book, and a patron's private scale. None depends on the 1958 measurement. Reasons 1, 2, 3 and 4 **would all still hold if the glass had never been measured in 1958**. The ledger is the strongest, because it is the accuser's own books.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question the entries?** No. Kyle says Wilbur Teague wrote the "station gain… as usual" line every year and "never asked anybody anything in forty years". The figures were the figures, and asking was for other people. Falke names only "an officer of the Association" as the man who urged the theft charge at the hearing, so it is by inference that I take him to be the bookkeeper. Either way, the entries that refuted the charge went unquestioned by the man who wrote them.

**D3.** **Yes, he knew the station's tests ran low, but he never grasped what his ledger said about why.**

- In June 1919 Selby Vose signed and sent, over his name alone, a circular warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons against watering their milk. That circular only makes sense if he knew their tests ran low.
- The patrons had complained at every annual meeting since about 1902.
- The 1908 committee and the 1911 interval were matters of Association record.
- The station account was read aloud at each annual meeting.

Kyle also records that Vose "read the totals and never read the lines above them": he signed with his thumb on the bottom figure. So he knew of the low tests but, on the evidence, never noticed that the "station gain" line contradicted his own water theory. The sources support *knowledge of the low tests*. They do not support *knowledge of the cause*, and nothing shows that he suspected the glass before Strawn condemned it.

## Section E — Contradictions

**Direct contradictions.** Each line gives the claims, who made them, the resolution and the method.

1. **Invoice total:** $77.39 (Junia's prose, *Chronicle*) vs $77.93 (Junia's facsimile). Resolved to **$77.93** by summing the invoice lines and by the rule that a quoted document outranks prose.
2. **Number of pipettes bought:** six dozen (invoice, *Chronicle*) vs five dozen (Cleve). Resolved to **72**: the document outranks memory.
3. **Pipettes surviving in 1958:** nine (Cleve, Falke) vs eleven (*Chronicle*). Resolved to **nine** on independent majority: an eyewitness who handled the crate plus an official report built from the bulletin, against a hasty press piece.
4. **Average delivered volume:** 16.27 c.c. (*Chronicle*) vs the 5% error implied by the test figures and the ledger. Inferred to be **16.72**, by arithmetic consistency.
5. **When the station's own glass went into use:** 1 May 1897 (Orra's diary, Junia, *Chronicle* "spring 1897") vs 1 May 1898 (Kyle) vs '99 (Hazel). Resolved to **1897**: the contemporaneous diary plus the dated invoice.
6. **Year the glass was condemned:** 1923 (Falke, Junia's letter book, Orra's diary, Duncan, *Chronicle*) vs 1922 (Kyle) vs 1921 (Hazel). Resolved to **1923**: dated documents and diary.
7. **Year of the hearing and award:** 1925 (Falke, Duncan, Orra, Cleve) vs 1924 (Kyle, for the award) vs '26 (Hazel). Resolved to **1925**, with the award on 4 May: dated documents and majority.
8. **Amount of the award:** $3,800 (Falke, Kyle, *Chronicle*) vs $3,500 (Cleve, from his father). Resolved to **$3,800**: independent majority including the clerk who charged it. It also equals exactly one-fifth of $19,000.
9. **Uncredited butterfat and its value:** 76,000 lb and $19,000 (arithmetic from Kyle's figures and 0.19) vs 67,000 lb (Cleve) vs $19,600 (*Chronicle*). Resolved **by arithmetic**.
10. **Ivy's relation to Ansel:** granddaughter (Duncan, Cleve, Falke's note) vs great-niece (*Chronicle*, Falke's main text). Resolved to **granddaughter**; see B1.
11. **Ivy's relation to Hazel:** second cousins (Duncan, Hazel) vs first cousin once removed (Cleve). Resolved to **second cousins** by the definition of the terms and by the two family insiders.
12. **Rosalie Cudd's relation to Jerome:** daughter (Duncan) vs niece (*Chronicle*). Resolved to **daughter**: Duncan's careful family record, and the book "passed to his daughter Rosalie in 1941 when she took the factory on".
13. **Selby Vose's relation to Gideon:** son (Duncan, Kyle) vs the nephew story, which both mention and reject. Resolved to **son**.
14. **Vose–Keddie kinship:** none (Duncan) vs "cousins of some sort" (Cleve). Resolved to **none**; see B4.
15. **Whether Orra was Emmet's full or half sister:** full sister, "the younger of the two" (Duncan) vs half-sister by the second wife (Hazel). **The sources cannot settle it.** Both are family memory of equal standing, and Orra's pre-marriage pages are lost.
16. **When the Brant weigh book began:** 1 January 1897 (Duncan) vs 1 January 1900 (Hazel). I prefer **1897**: Duncan's record of the books is written and precise, while Hazel says she cannot hold years and that she knows only 1910 for certain. This is a preference, not proof.
17. **Number of families who got the water circular:** eleven (Orra's diary) vs nine (Hazel). Resolved to **eleven**: a contemporaneous diary against a small child's memory. How the circular was delivered also conflicts: Ansel carrying it by hand (Orra) against "sent round" (Hazel).
18. **Emmet at the Keddie table:** "for thirty-six years" up to 1925 (Duncan) vs "has not been in this house since" June 1919 (Orra). Resolved **for the diary**, which is contemporaneous. Duncan had left home in 1911.
19. **The 1908 committee vs the 1925 board:** the *Chronicle* says "the committee that sat in 1908 sat again in 1925". Every other source treats them as separate bodies: the Association's herd committee in 1908 and a board of arbitration under an Ashlin attorney in 1925. The *Chronicle* ran the two strands together.
20. **When Ansel started:** "went up in '95" (Hazel) vs opened 4 May 1896 (Falke). Reconcilable, since the station was built in the winter of 1895–96. Falke's date is taken for the opening.
21. **Whether the hay theory was real:** the 1908 committee, the 1926 veterinarian and Falke ¶9 say it was a real partial factor; Bulletin 214 says at most 0.05 in winter and nothing in summer. Resolved **against the hay**: a measured trial outranks opinion, and Falke himself withdraws.
22. **Whether the Ammon lot was sold short knowingly:** knowingly (*Chronicle*) vs by accident, a reground mold (Junia). **Cannot be determined**: the works books burned in 1931, and both claims are inference.
23. **Cream on and off the platform:** Cleve's father believed less cream came off the platform than went on. The Association's ledger shows a surplus of fat at Larrow Green. Resolved **for the ledger** (document over belief).

**Cases where two narrators carry the same wrong value.** In each, the pair is effectively one source.
- **$77.39** (Junia's prose and the *Chronicle*). The *Chronicle* is copying from the firm or the press release. The tie is broken by the invoice's own line items, which sum to $77.93, and by Junia's own facsimile.
- **"Great-niece"** (the *Chronicle* and Falke's main text). Both derive from the college press release and bulletin. The tie is broken by Duncan's first-hand genealogy (Ivy is his daughter, and he is Ansel's son), Cleve's "granddaughter", and Falke's own verified correction.
- **The hay as a real factor** (the 1908 committee report and Falke ¶9, which leans on it, with the 1926 vet). The tie is broken by the measured figures in Bulletin 214 and the July constancy of 0.19.
- **The "nephew" story about Selby Vose, and the Vose–Keddie cousinhood.** These are rumours repeated in the town, and the family insiders (Duncan) and the office insider (Kyle) break them.

**Cases where a narrator contradicts itself on the page.**
- **Junia Ammon (r20):** she says "Seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents" directly under a facsimile totalling $77.93. She also says the house "sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900", then prints an 1897 invoice to the Ordell County station at Ostrey Hollow.
- **Duncan Keddie (r18):** he gives Orra's birth as 1868, but says she died in 1948 aged 82, which means she was born in 1866. The diary editor's 1866 and Orra's own "I am sixty-four" in August 1930 settle it at **1866**.
- **Orin Falke (r22):** his ¶2 "great-niece" is contradicted by his closing note ("granddaughter"), and his ¶9 hay concession is withdrawn in the same note. These are explicit self-corrections.
- **The *Ashlin Chronicle* (r19):** its average of 16.27 c.c. is incompatible with its own implied figures (a $19,600 shortfall and the 0.19 story it relies on). Its $77.39 conflicts with its own "six dozen at thirty-five cents" as the leading line of a $77.93 invoice. It also calls Ivy "Ashlin girl" and "great-niece" while describing her father as the Ashlin schoolteacher, who was Ansel's son.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. Causal theory.** The Ostrey Hollow station's outfit of 72 pipettes, bought from Ammon & Sons in April 1897, was a **short lot**. Each was marked to deliver 17.6 c.c. but delivered about 5% less (roughly 16.72 c.c.), most likely because of an undetected mold or gauge fault. Every test put a short charge of milk into a bottle whose neck was ruled for a full 17.6 c.c. charge, so every reading came out 5% low.

- **Size:** 5% of a true test of about 3.80 is **0.19 point**, which is why the station read 3.61. It is also why the creamery always received 5¼% more fat than was credited: 1/0.95.
- **Why that size, and why constant:** the error is a fixed proportion built into the glass. It does not depend on feed, season, farm or man, so it held at 0.19 in June and February alike.
- **Why it began:** it began on **1 May 1897**, when the station stopped borrowing Larrow Green glass and put its own outfit into use. The 1896 season on borrowed glass was unaffected.
- **Why it ended:** it ended when Strawn condemned the lot on **12 June 1923** and new glass came in July. From 1924 the average rose to 3.85 and stayed there.

Under article seven every patron was paid on that test, so the error became about **76,000 lb** of butterfat, or **$19,000**. No change was needed on the farms or at the station, whether in cows, hay, men, scales or procedure, for the error to start or to stop. The only thing that changed at either boundary was the glass. The 1911 interval confirms this: swapping the glass alone moved the tests up and back.

**The three competing explanations:**
1. **Added water by the patrons.** This was the manager Selby Vose's view, in the 1919 circular and at the 1925 hearing. It is defeated by Strawn's lactometer at every visit from 1908, which never found water. It is also defeated by the ledger: water cannot create the surplus of fat that arrived at Larrow Green.
2. **Cream theft by the tester, disguised with low tests.** An officer of the Association urged it at the hearing, and Cleve Oram's father believed it privately. It is defeated by the 1911 interval (same man, higher tests), by the 5¼% "station gain" (more fat reached the creamery than was credited, the opposite of theft), and by the rise to 3.85 after 1923 while Keddie was still testing.
3. **Bog hay on wet Hessel Bottom thinning the cream.** The Association's 1908 committee advanced it, as did an Ashlin veterinarian in 1926 and, provisionally, Falke. It is defeated by Bulletin 214 (≤0.05 in winter and nothing on summer pasture, when the gap was a flat 0.19 even in July), by the 1911 interval (same feed, higher tests), and by the Alder Corners tests of the same milk.

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?** No. Nothing in the sources shows that it did. The crate sat in the loft over the weigh room, where two of the board's three sittings were held (Duncan, Cleve, Orra's diary). Kyle lists the four books put before the board, and no glass is among them. Nobody connected the condemnation to the low tests at the time (Falke ¶11), and the crate was not disturbed until February 1958.

**What the tester himself believed:** that it was **"something in the glass."** He said so at home in the summer of 1911, after the borrowed-measure interval, twice. He put only a **question mark** in the test book, because he would not write down what he could not prove. He went on to the end with the outfit and the practice he had been taught to trust (Duncan §IV; Falke ¶6).

## Section G — Summary

In 1897 a Tarnet firm sold the Larrow Valley creamery's Ostrey Hollow station 72 pipettes that delivered 5% short. Since patrons were paid on butterfat tests, every Hessel Bottom farmer was underpaid. The tests ran a flat 0.19 low, costing 76,000 lb of fat, or $19,000. Rival blames fell on water, bog hay and the tester, Ansel Keddie. Keddie suspected the glass after a borrowed measure raised the tests in 1911, but he could prove nothing. The office ledger showed a 5¼% "station gain" every year and nobody read it. In 1923 an inspector condemned the glass without drawing any conclusion. The 1925 arbitration sat beneath the crate, dismissed Keddie and paid the patrons one-fifth. In 1958 his granddaughter Ivy measured the glass. Eight records were needed because each held one piece: invoice, inspector's notebook, ledger, weigh book, test book, rival factory's figures, diary and crate. No one person ever held them all.
