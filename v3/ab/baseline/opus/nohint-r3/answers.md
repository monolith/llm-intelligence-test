# Answer Sheet — Part 1 (Section A, stories 1–4)

*Note on sources: this session read retellings r17–r24 directly. No notes on r01–r16 were present in this session's context, so every answer below rests on r17–r24 alone. Where r01–r16 might settle a point, I say so rather than guess.*

## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1. The rule and the station (1889–1897)

**Who:** Gideon Vose, the farmers of the Larrow Valley, and Ansel Keddie.

**What happened and when:** Valley farmers meeting at Larrow Green organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association in **1889**. Gideon Vose organized it, was its first president and wrote its by-laws. The main creamery was built at Larrow Green in **1891**. By-law article **seven** is the hinge of the whole history: every patron is paid on the pounds of **butterfat** his milk is found by test to contain, not on the weight of the milk, and the test at the station where he delivers is the one that governs his pay. Under payment by weight, nothing that happened later would have cost anyone a dollar. The year the article was adopted **cannot be determined**: the printed by-laws are undated and the early minute book was lost (Falke).

In the winter of **1895–96** the Association built a skimming station at **Ostrey Hollow**, seven miles up the valley, on a road that climbs the whole way. The station served the farms of **Hessel Bottom**: **six farms** on wet ground, of which Emmet Brant's was the largest. It opened on **4 May 1896**. It was a two-floor board building with a covered platform. Inside were the intake (weigh) room with the scale, the vat and the separator, and over that room a loft reached by a ladder nailed to the wall. Milk was weighed, sampled, tested and separated. The cream was hauled down to Larrow Green, for more than twenty years by Cleve Oram's father, and the skim went back to the patrons.

The tester and separator man from the day it opened was **Ansel Keddie** (born 1861). In 1889 he had married **Orra**, Emmet Brant's sister. He held the post for **29 seasons (1896–1924)**. Hazel Brant's "went up in '95" fits the building winter.

For its **first season (1896)** the station **borrowed its testing glass from the Larrow Green creamery**. Its own outfit went into use on **1 May 1897**, which is confirmed by Orra's diary entry of that exact date and by the Ammon history. Kyle's "1 May 1898" is wrong: the contemporaneous diary and the dated invoice outrank a recollection. Hazel's "'99" is also wrong. That first borrowed-glass season is therefore **outside** the faulty-glass period. The first season's intake **cannot be established**: Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" is hearsay that he says is "not in any book".

**Documents:** Falke's report ¶¶3–4 (r22); Kyle (r23); Orra's diary (r24); Duncan's notes (r18); Cleve Oram (r21); the *Chronicle* (r19).

### Story 2. Six dozen pipettes from Tarnet (1897, with 1923, 1931 and 1949)

**Who:** Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet, founded by Marcus Ammon; the Association; and Junia Ammon, the firm's historian.

**What happened:** The firm blew the glass for about half the creameries in the state. A milk-test pipette is a *to-deliver* vessel: it must deliver **17.6 c.c.**, the charge that the neck of the test bottle is ruled for. The firm's invoice, dated **14 April 1897** and reproduced in facsimile, was made out to the Association for the Ostrey Hollow station:

- 6 doz. milk-test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at $0.35: **$25.20**
- 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at $0.22: **$31.68**
- 1 acid measure: **$0.90**
- 1 four-bottle hand tester: **$18.00**
- Freight: **$2.15**
- **Total: $77.93**

The lines add up to exactly $77.93. The "$77.39" in Junia's own prose and in the *Chronicle* is a transposition: the facsimile and the arithmetic outrank it. The outfit went into use on **1 May 1897**. It contained **72 pipettes**, not the "five dozen" Cleve Oram remembered.

That lot delivered **short, by about 5%**. A pipette marked 17.6 c.c. delivered about **16.72 c.c.**, roughly **0.88 c.c.** short (this figure is inferred; see C1/C4). A short charge in a bottle ruled for a full one makes the fat read low in the same proportion, every time.

The firm's works books, which recorded glass, gauge, man and day, **burned in 1931** when the old third floor caught fire. Only the office side survives: invoices, letter books and day books. When the lot was condemned, the Association wrote to the firm on **28 June 1923** ordering six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles. The reply of **2 July 1923** entered the order, "regret[ted] the trouble", and said that its books of the nineties could tell nothing about the lot.

Junia believes a **reground mold** made the lot run short by accident, and that spot checks could not catch an error that was *consistent*. That is her inference, not a record. The *Chronicle*'s charge that the firm knowingly sold a short lot is unsupported. Whether the fault was known **cannot be determined**, because the works records are gone. Junia also contradicts herself: she says the house sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900, yet she prints the 1897 invoice.

**Documents:** Junia Ammon's chapter IX (r20), the *Chronicle* (r19), Cleve Oram (r21).

### Story 3. Nineteen hundredths, the Brant weigh book and the hay theory (1896–1911, answered 1959)

**Who:** The Hessel Bottom patrons, chiefly Emmet Brant and later his son Roy; the Association's 1908 committee; and Jerome Cudd of the cheese factory at Alder Corners.

**What happened:** From the start, Ostrey Hollow milk tested low. The station averaged **3.61** against Larrow Green's **3.78**. The shortfall was **0.19 point, flat**, in every month: in the June flush and in February, in wet years and dry. A feed effect would not behave that way. The first complaint on record came in **1902** (Falke; Orra's diary, March 1902: store talk that "our milk up here runs poorer"). After that the patrons complained at every annual meeting.

**Emmet Brant** began his private **weigh book** on **1 January 1897**. I take Duncan's written date over Hazel's "1900", since Hazel says herself that she cannot hold years. He weighed his milk on his own scale and wrote the station's weight beside it. They agreed. His **1910** leaf reads: **200,000 lb even**, "his scale and theirs agreeing", paid on a test of **3.42**. That credited him with 6,840 lb of fat when he was owed about 7,200 lb, a shortfall of about **360 lb**, or **about $90** at the long-run price of 25¢ (see C2).

There was also a natural comparison. **Four of the six** Hessel Bottom farmers hauled over the ridge during the summer flush to Jerome Cudd's cheese factory at **Alder Corners** in Vessey County. The same milk tested **higher** there, by the same nineteen hundredths. Cudd's test book passed to his daughter **Rosalie** in 1941, and she had its figures copied for Ivy in 1958.

In **January 1908** a committee of four came up in Grigg's sleigh, dined at Emmet's, and looked at the barns rather than the station. In **March 1908** it reported that the **wet bog hay** of the bottom thinned the cream. An Ashlin veterinarian wrote the same in 1926, and Falke first conceded the point (¶9). That theory is defeated by the college's own **Bulletin No. 214**, a 1911 feeding trial: bog hay as the whole roughage lowered the test by "not more than five hundredths" in winter and by nothing measurable on summer pasture. That cannot produce 0.19 in July. Falke withdrew his concession, and the 1908 committee was simply wrong.

**Documents:** Hazel Brant's tape (r17), Falke (r22) with his note on Bulletin 214, Orra's diary (r24), Duncan's notes (r18) on the books.

### Story 4. Nine weeks in 1911: the borrowed measure

**Who:** Ansel Keddie, with his wife Orra and son Duncan as witnesses at home.

**What happened and when:** On **8 May 1911** the station's own measuring glass broke. Orra wrote that day: "A. home cross about a broken measure. Rain all day." A measure **borrowed from elsewhere** was used in its place until **10 July 1911**, exactly **nine weeks** (63 days). Throughout those nine weeks the station's tests stood **markedly higher**. On the day the station went back to its own glass, they **fell back** to their old level.

Nothing else changed: the same herds, pasture, season, patrons, tester, machinery, scales and procedure. Ansel recorded the fact in the station test book and set a **question mark** against it.

That summer he came home saying "it was something in the glass". He said it twice, at supper and again at the door, which was not his way, and seventeen-year-old Duncan remembered it. He never dared write it in the book, because he would not put down what he could not prove. He had only a suspicion about a piece of glass, set against the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county. So he went on testing with the same glass for fourteen more years and faithfully recorded every low figure it gave him. Those figures were later used to convict him. Duncan left in October 1911 to teach school at Ashlin. **Selby Vose** became the Association's manager the same year.

The 1958 bulletin treats this interval as **decisive**. It shows that the low readings travelled with the station's glass and not with the milk or the man: a controlled before-and-after within one season. The sources do **not** give the size of the rise ("markedly higher" only), and they do not show that the borrowed measure was itself exact. They also leave unclear exactly which piece of glass was swapped, since the outfit had one acid measure but 72 pipettes. No one acted on it for 47 years.

**Documents:** Falke ¶6 and ¶8 (r22), Orra's diary entry of 8 May 1911 (r24), Duncan's notes §IV (r18).


# Answer Sheet — Part 2 (Section A, stories 5–8; Section B)

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### Story 5. "Station gain, as usual": the office at Larrow Green and the water circular (1911–1925)

**Who:** Bertram Kyle, the outer-desk clerk for 25 years; Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper; **Selby Vose**, manager from **1911** for about a quarter-century and **son** (not nephew) of Gideon Vose; and the Ostrey Hollow patrons.

**What happened:** Every year the Ostrey Hollow station account carried three figures and a sentence. The first figure was the pounds of butterfat **credited** to the patrons on the station test. The second was the pounds of butterfat actually **received** at Larrow Green in the station's cream. The third was the difference, entered as **"station gain"**, followed always by Teague's words "**five and a quarter per cent, as usual**, carried to the general account."

More fat was reaching the creamery than the patrons were being paid for. Teague wrote that line every year and never asked about it. Vose signed each sheet with his thumb on the bottom figure and never read the lines above it. The account was read out at every annual meeting. It was in plain sight, and because it had a name nobody questioned it.

For the arbitration, Kyle extracted the long-run figures: **40,000,000 lb** of milk received at the station, **1,600,000 lb a year** (that is, **25 years**), with butterfat averaging **25¢ a pound**. The arithmetic:
- At the recorded 3.61, the fat credited is **1,444,000 lb**.
- At the true 3.80, the fat reaching the creamery is **1,520,000 lb**.
- The gap is **76,000 lb**, which is **5.26% of the credited fat**. That is Kyle's "five and a quarter per cent", and it is 5% of the fat actually received.

A thief skimming cream at the station would have produced a station **loss**, and watered milk produces no gain in fat at all. The ledger therefore refuted both accusations every year.

In **June 1919** Vose sent the Ostrey Hollow patrons a **circular warning against adding water**. Kyle typed it, and it went out over Vose's name alone. Ansel would not mail it or leave it on the platform. He carried it himself to **eleven families** and stood at each door while it was read, as Orra's contemporaneous diary records. Hazel, who was a small child then, remembered nine. Two days later **Emmet Brant** confronted Ansel in the yard. He never entered the Keddie house again, and the two men never reconciled before Emmet died in 1929.

The water theory was already disproved. The state inspector had tested with the **lactometer at every visit from 1908** and never once found added water at Ostrey Hollow.

**Documents:** Kyle (r23), Orra's diary (r24), Falke ¶7 (r22), Hazel (r17), Duncan (r18) on the Vose father–son relationship.

### Story 6. The inspector and the crate (1908–1958)

**Who:** **Merle Strawn**, the state dairy inspector on the Ordell circuit, who was kin to nobody in the valley. He was a heavy, quiet man who wrote everything in a little notebook with a rubber band round it.

**What happened:** Strawn tested Ostrey Hollow milk with the lactometer at every visit from 1908 and never found water. On **12 June 1923**, on an ordinary circuit visit, he ran the station's milk pipettes against a **calibrated burette**. They would not deliver their mark. He **condemned the whole lot** on the spot and told the manager to order new glass. The Association ordered replacements from Ammon & Sons on **28 June**, and the new glass was in use by **July 1923**; Orra wrote that month that "they have new glass at the station".

The year is **1923**. That is fixed by the Ammon letter book (28 June and 2 July), Orra's diary, Falke, Duncan and the *Chronicle*. Hazel's "'21" and Kyle's "'22" are memory errors.

Strawn noted the condemnation but drew **no inference** about the 26 years before it, and nobody connected the two things. **From 1924 the station's average test stood at 3.85 and stayed there.** The same tester was working the same herds, and only the glass had changed.

The condemned glassware was crated and put up in the **loft over the intake room** under a tarpaulin. It stayed there for **35 years (1923 to February 1958)**, about twenty feet above the room where two of the three arbitration sittings later met. People going up for wire broke pieces, and **nine pipettes** survived. This count is given independently by Cleve Oram, who handled the crate, and by Falke's report; the *Chronicle*'s "eleven" is an outlier.

Strawn kept one notebook for every year on the circuit. They went to the state dairy office when he retired in **1936** and to the library of the State Agricultural College at **Farland** in **1947**. There, eleven years later, Ivy found the **1923** volume; nobody had asked for them in between. The 1923 notebook had also been one of the four books placed before the 1925 board, and nobody read it against the receiving book.

**Documents:** Falke ¶¶7, 11, 12 (r22); Kyle (r23); Duncan §V (r18); Cleve Oram (r21); Junia Ammon (r20); Orra's diary (r24).

### Story 7. The arbitration and the dismissal (1925–1934)

**Who:** Ansel Keddie, the patrons (including Emmet Brant), the Association's officers, and a board chaired by an Ashlin attorney.

**What happened:** The patrons petitioned in **January 1925**, with Emmet's name on the paper. The board impounded the station test book in March. It sat from **17 March to 4 May 1925**. There were three sittings: the first at Ashlin, in the Cooperative's office, and **two in the weigh room at the station itself**, directly under the crate of condemned glass. Farmers stood outside on the platform and in the road, some of them in the rain.

Four books went before the board: the station **test book** in Keddie's hand, the **receiving book**, **Emmet Brant's weigh book** (lent and wanted back), and **Strawn's 1923 notebook**. Nobody laid the receiving book beside the notebook. The three theories argued were:
- water, the manager's view;
- cream theft by the tester, urged by an officer of the Association;
- bog hay.

Keddie was **dismissed on 30 April 1925** ("twenty-nine seasons and a letter"), **four days before** the board's finding of **4 May 1925**. The board found no cause established and awarded the patrons **$3,800 "without admission"**, charged to the station account. That sum is exactly **one-fifth** of the $19,000 later computed. Three sources give $3,800 (Falke, Kyle and the *Chronicle*); Cleve's $3,500 is hearsay from his father. Kyle's "1924" and Hazel's "'26" are wrong. Roy Brant never went to another Association meeting.

Ansel came home at his usual hour, hung up his apron and ate ham. He rebuilt the cow shed, whistling, and worked as a carpenter at sixty-six; he hung the Orams' barn door, which swung true for thirty years. He died at home in **February 1934**, nine years after the dismissal, with the imputation still on him. The funeral was well attended, and nobody mentioned the accusation. Orra records his one remark on it: "a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name." Emmet had died in the spring of 1929, and the rift between the two men was never closed. Orra died in **1948** at 82.

**Documents:** Falke ¶10 (r22), Kyle (r23), Orra's diary (r24), Duncan §IV (r18), Cleve Oram (r21), Hazel (r17), the *Chronicle* (r19).

### Story 8. Up the ladder: Ivy Keddie and Bulletin No. 471 (1958–1970)

**Who:** **Ivy Keddie** (born 1931), daughter of Duncan Keddie (born 1894, schoolteacher at Ashlin) and **granddaughter of Ansel**. She was a graduate student in dairy husbandry at the State Agricultural College at Farland, working with **Professor Leland Vaught**.

**What happened:** In **February 1958** Ivy climbed the ladder into the station loft and found the crate. She took it east to Farland, and on **9 March 1958** measured the **nine** surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette. Every one delivered less than the 17.6 c.c. blown on its stem, and all were short **in the same proportion**, about **5%** (roughly 16.72 c.c.).

She assembled the other records alongside the crate:
- **Roy Brant's weigh book**, which Roy **lent** her in 1958 (he did not give it);
- **Strawn's 1923 notebook** from the Farland library;
- the **Alder Corners figures**, copied for her by **Rosalie Cudd**, Jerome Cudd's **daughter**.

**Bulletin No. 471** (October 1958, by Vaught and Keddie) concluded that a short charge into a bottle ruled for the full charge reads short in the same proportion. That alone explained the low tests: no milk was watered and no cream was stolen. The bulletin puts the uncredited butterfat at **76,000 lb** over **25 full delivery years**, worth **$19,000** at 25¢. The patrons had received $3,800, or 20%. The *Chronicle*'s $19,600 and Cleve's 67,000 lb do not survive the arithmetic.

The *Ashlin Chronicle* ran the story on **3 October 1958**, 33 years after the dismissal and 24 after Ansel's death, and it got several facts wrong. County agent Orin Falke reported to the county board in **1959**. He added a note withdrawing his hay concession after reading Bulletin 214, and correcting "great-niece" to "granddaughter".

Where the records went:
- The **station test book** stayed in the Cooperative office at Ashlin from 1925 to **1959**, then went to the Ordell County Historical Rooms at Ashlin.
- The **crate** came back from Farland and was deposited there in **1959**.
- The **weigh book** passed to Hazel when Roy died in **1962**, and she gave it to the Rooms in **1963**.
- The **Alder Corners book** went to the Vessey County Historical Society in **1970**.
- **Strawn's notebooks** remain at Farland.

Ivy and Hazel Brant are **second cousins**.

**Documents:** the *Chronicle* (r19), Falke with his note (r22), Duncan's notes with the 1970 addition (r18), Hazel (r17), Cleve Oram (r21), the editor's note to Orra's diary (r24).

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** Ansel (1861–1934) married Orra in 1889, and their son Duncan was born in 1894. Duncan writes that "my daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter." Cleve Oram also calls her "the granddaughter". The *Chronicle* and Falke's main text both say "great-niece", but that is one source, not two: both evidently took it from the college press release and bulletin. Falke himself retracts it ("verified… the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine"). The *Chronicle*'s dating also fits a granddaughter: Ivy was 27 in 1958, so born about 1931. The father's first-hand written statement plus Falke's verified correction outrank the repeated error.

**B2. Ivy and Hazel Brant are second cousins.** Emmet Brant and Orra were siblings. Emmet's son Roy and Orra's son Duncan are therefore first cousins, and their daughters Hazel and Ivy are second cousins. Duncan and Hazel both say so explicitly. Cleve's "first cousin once removed" is wrong: the two women are the same generation, and "removed" describes a generational gap.

The sources leave one point open: whether Orra was Emmet's full sister or his half-sister. Duncan calls her "Emmet Brant's sister, the younger of the two", which implies a full sibling. Hazel says she was "a half sister… by his father's second wife." Orra's own pre-1889 pages were not preserved, and she writes only "my brother Emmet". The intervening generation cannot be settled from these sources. If Hazel is right, the cousinships are strictly "half" first cousins and "half" second cousins. Either way, the degree is second cousins.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose.** Gideon organized the Association, was its first president and **wrote its by-laws**, including article seven, which pays each patron on the butterfat found by the **station's own test**. Duncan and Kyle both reject the "nephew" story independently. This matters for three reasons:
- The rule that turned a short pipette into a lost payment was the father's.
- The son, as manager from 1911, signed the ledger line that showed a 5¼% "station gain" every year without reading it.
- The son sent the 1919 water circular, blaming the patrons for a shortfall that his own ledger contradicted and that his father's rule had made costly.

**B4. There was no relationship between the Vose and Keddie families.** Duncan states it flatly. His mother "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection" and never put a Vose in it. He says the talk arose from confusion with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Gideon either. Cleve Oram's "cousins of some sort" is family hearsay ("my mother had it from her mother"), and he admits he "could not draw you the line on paper." I prefer the insider's specific denial, which also explains where the rumour came from, over an unsupported rumour.


# Answer Sheet — Part 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

**C1.**

(a) **The tests were 5% (one-twentieth) below the truth.** The glass works like this. A pipette marked 17.6 c.c. delivers a short charge into a bottle whose neck is ruled for 17.6 c.c., so the fat reads low by the same proportion.

The station figures fix that proportion: 0.19 ÷ 3.80 = **0.05**. A 5% short pipette delivers 17.6 × 0.95 = **16.72 c.c.** Kyle's ledger confirms it independently: a gain of 5¼% on credited fat is 1/0.95 − 1 = 5.26%.

The *Chronicle*'s "average 16.27 c.c." would imply 7.6% short. That fits none of the other figures, so I read it as a transposition of 16.72, like its $77.39 for $77.93. This is an inference from arithmetic.

(b) **The true average test was 3.80.** The recorded average was 3.61, and 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80. That is **0.19 point short**, consistent with Hazel's and Falke's "nineteen hundredths". For comparison, Larrow Green averaged 3.78, and the station averaged 3.85 after 1923 with good glass.

(c) Over 25 full delivery years the milk received was 40,000,000 lb (Kyle: 1,600,000 lb × 25).
- True fat = 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb.
- Credited fat = 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb.
- **Uncredited = 76,000 lb** (the same as 40,000,000 × 0.0019).

Cleve's "67,000 lb" is a digit reversal.

(d) 76,000 lb × $0.25 = **$19,000**. The patrons received $3,800, and 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **one-fifth (20%)**. The *Chronicle*'s $19,600 is wrong on the arithmetic: it would require 78,400 lb.

**C2.** Emmet Brant, 1910 (Hazel's reading of the weigh-book leaf): 200,000 lb of milk, test 3.42.

(a) Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of butterfat.

(b) The error is proportional, so the true test was 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60. He was owed 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb, so **360 lb** went uncredited.

(c) 360 × $0.25 = **$90**. This uses the long-run average price, since no 1910 price is given, so it is an inference. Applying the flat station-average 0.19 instead would give 380 lb and $95. The proportional figure is the correct one for a single patron.

**C3.** Over the same 25 years:

(a) Credited: **1,444,000 lb**.

(b) Reaching Larrow Green in the station cream: **1,520,000 lb**.

(c) The difference is 76,000 lb:
- as a percentage **of the credited fat**: 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.26%**, which is Kyle's "five and a quarter per cent" station gain;
- as a percentage **of the fat actually received** (the true fat): 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5.00%**, which is the size of the glass error.

**C4.**

(a) **$77.93**:
- 25.20 (6 doz pipettes × $0.35)
- \+ 31.68 (12 doz bottles × $0.22)
- \+ 0.90 (acid measure)
- \+ 18.00 (hand tester)
- \+ 2.15 (freight)
- = 77.93

The "$77.39" in Junia Ammon's prose and in the *Chronicle* is a transposition.

(b) **72 pipettes** (6 dozen; not Cleve's five dozen). Each was short by about **0.88 c.c.** on average: 17.6 − 16.72. This is inferred from the 5% error. The *Chronicle*'s 16.27 would give 1.33 c.c. but conflicts with every other figure.

**C5.**

(a) **The glass was in use from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923**, when Strawn condemned it: **26 years, 1 month and 11 days**, roughly Junia's "six and twenty years". Replacement glass was in use in July 1923.

**The money calculation runs over 25 full delivery years** (Kyle: 40,000,000 lb at 1,600,000 lb a year). The two numbers differ because the service period starts and ends mid-year:
- 1897 was a part year, with deliveries on the station's own glass only from May;
- 1923 was a part year, ending in June;
- the first season, 1896, used borrowed Larrow Green glass and was never affected.

The full years in between, **1898–1922**, number 25. Treating calendar years as the delivery years is my inference.

(b) **Cannot be determined from these sources.** The only figure is Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" (1,100,000 lb). He heard it from men who were there, and he says it "is not in any book I ever ruled", because the first season was carried on the creamery's own books and those sheets were never filed with the station papers. That is hearsay with no document behind it. (Retellings r01–r16, which I did not have this session, might settle it.)

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran from **8 May to 10 July 1911, nine weeks**. Falke gives the dates, and Orra's diary independently notes the broken measure on 8 May.

- **Variable:** the measuring glass, which changed from the station's own to one borrowed from elsewhere.
- **Held constant:** the herds, feed and pasture, season, patrons, the tester himself, supervision, scales, acid and machinery.
- **Result:** the tests rose markedly for the whole interval and fell back on the day the station's own glass returned. That is a switch-on, switch-off reversal.

A calculation can only show that a 5% short pipette *would* produce 0.19. The interval shows **by intervention** that the station's own glass *was* the cause in practice. It disposes of the theft theory, because the same man was testing under the same supervision, and of the water and hay theories, because the same milk and the same feed were involved. It did all this in 1911, 47 years before anyone measured the pipettes.

It does **not** establish:
- the size of the error, since the sources give no figures for the rise;
- that the borrowed measure was itself accurate;
- which piece of glass was at fault (the "measure" could be read as the single acid measure rather than one of the 72 pipettes);
- that the error went back to 1897 or lasted until 1923;
- how or why the lot came to be short.

**D2.** Independent reasons Keddie cannot have been stealing cream:

1. **The ledger's "station gain" (Kyle, r23).** Every year Larrow Green received about 5¼% *more* butterfat in the station cream than the patrons were credited with. Theft at the station would have produced a shortfall at the creamery, not a surplus.
2. **The 1911 borrowed measure (Falke r22, corroborated by Orra r24 and Duncan r18).** The same man, under the same supervision, produced higher tests with a different glass.
3. **The post-1923 rise (Falke r22).** With new glass from July 1923, the average went to 3.85 and stayed there while Keddie was still testing, until April 1925.
4. **The Alder Corners comparison (Hazel r17 and Duncan r18 on Cudd's book).** The same Hessel Bottom milk tested 0.19 higher at a factory Keddie never touched. Emmet's own scale also agreed with the station's weights.
5. **The constancy of the error.** A fixed 0.19 in every month and every season is the signature of an instrument, not of opportunistic skimming.

These are independent because they come from different records kept by different people for different purposes: the Association's own ledger, the tester's test book, a rival firm's test book, and a patron's private scale. None depends on the 1958 measurement. Reasons 1, 2, 3 and 4 **would all still hold if the glass had never been measured in 1958**. The ledger is the strongest, because it is the accuser's own books.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question the entries?** No. Kyle says Wilbur Teague wrote the "station gain… as usual" line every year and "never asked anybody anything in forty years". The figures were the figures, and asking was for other people. Falke names only "an officer of the Association" as the man who urged the theft charge at the hearing, so it is by inference that I take him to be the bookkeeper. Either way, the entries that refuted the charge went unquestioned by the man who wrote them.

**D3.** **Yes, he knew the station's tests ran low, but he never grasped what his ledger said about why.**

- In June 1919 Selby Vose signed and sent, over his name alone, a circular warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons against watering their milk. That circular only makes sense if he knew their tests ran low.
- The patrons had complained at every annual meeting since about 1902.
- The 1908 committee and the 1911 interval were matters of Association record.
- The station account was read aloud at each annual meeting.

Kyle also records that Vose "read the totals and never read the lines above them": he signed with his thumb on the bottom figure. So he knew of the low tests but, on the evidence, never noticed that the "station gain" line contradicted his own water theory. The sources support *knowledge of the low tests*. They do not support *knowledge of the cause*, and nothing shows that he suspected the glass before Strawn condemned it.

## Section E — Contradictions

**Direct contradictions.** Each line gives the claims, who made them, the resolution and the method.

1. **Invoice total:** $77.39 (Junia's prose, *Chronicle*) vs $77.93 (Junia's facsimile). Resolved to **$77.93** by summing the invoice lines and by the rule that a quoted document outranks prose.
2. **Number of pipettes bought:** six dozen (invoice, *Chronicle*) vs five dozen (Cleve). Resolved to **72**: the document outranks memory.
3. **Pipettes surviving in 1958:** nine (Cleve, Falke) vs eleven (*Chronicle*). Resolved to **nine** on independent majority: an eyewitness who handled the crate plus an official report built from the bulletin, against a hasty press piece.
4. **Average delivered volume:** 16.27 c.c. (*Chronicle*) vs the 5% error implied by the test figures and the ledger. Inferred to be **16.72**, by arithmetic consistency.
5. **When the station's own glass went into use:** 1 May 1897 (Orra's diary, Junia, *Chronicle* "spring 1897") vs 1 May 1898 (Kyle) vs '99 (Hazel). Resolved to **1897**: the contemporaneous diary plus the dated invoice.
6. **Year the glass was condemned:** 1923 (Falke, Junia's letter book, Orra's diary, Duncan, *Chronicle*) vs 1922 (Kyle) vs 1921 (Hazel). Resolved to **1923**: dated documents and diary.
7. **Year of the hearing and award:** 1925 (Falke, Duncan, Orra, Cleve) vs 1924 (Kyle, for the award) vs '26 (Hazel). Resolved to **1925**, with the award on 4 May: dated documents and majority.
8. **Amount of the award:** $3,800 (Falke, Kyle, *Chronicle*) vs $3,500 (Cleve, from his father). Resolved to **$3,800**: independent majority including the clerk who charged it. It also equals exactly one-fifth of $19,000.
9. **Uncredited butterfat and its value:** 76,000 lb and $19,000 (arithmetic from Kyle's figures and 0.19) vs 67,000 lb (Cleve) vs $19,600 (*Chronicle*). Resolved **by arithmetic**.
10. **Ivy's relation to Ansel:** granddaughter (Duncan, Cleve, Falke's note) vs great-niece (*Chronicle*, Falke's main text). Resolved to **granddaughter**; see B1.
11. **Ivy's relation to Hazel:** second cousins (Duncan, Hazel) vs first cousin once removed (Cleve). Resolved to **second cousins** by the definition of the terms and by the two family insiders.
12. **Rosalie Cudd's relation to Jerome:** daughter (Duncan) vs niece (*Chronicle*). Resolved to **daughter**: Duncan's careful family record, and the book "passed to his daughter Rosalie in 1941 when she took the factory on".
13. **Selby Vose's relation to Gideon:** son (Duncan, Kyle) vs the nephew story, which both mention and reject. Resolved to **son**.
14. **Vose–Keddie kinship:** none (Duncan) vs "cousins of some sort" (Cleve). Resolved to **none**; see B4.
15. **Whether Orra was Emmet's full or half sister:** full sister, "the younger of the two" (Duncan) vs half-sister by the second wife (Hazel). **The sources cannot settle it.** Both are family memory of equal standing, and Orra's pre-marriage pages are lost.
16. **When the Brant weigh book began:** 1 January 1897 (Duncan) vs 1 January 1900 (Hazel). I prefer **1897**: Duncan's record of the books is written and precise, while Hazel says she cannot hold years and that she knows only 1910 for certain. This is a preference, not proof.
17. **Number of families who got the water circular:** eleven (Orra's diary) vs nine (Hazel). Resolved to **eleven**: a contemporaneous diary against a small child's memory. How the circular was delivered also conflicts: Ansel carrying it by hand (Orra) against "sent round" (Hazel).
18. **Emmet at the Keddie table:** "for thirty-six years" up to 1925 (Duncan) vs "has not been in this house since" June 1919 (Orra). Resolved **for the diary**, which is contemporaneous. Duncan had left home in 1911.
19. **The 1908 committee vs the 1925 board:** the *Chronicle* says "the committee that sat in 1908 sat again in 1925". Every other source treats them as separate bodies: the Association's herd committee in 1908 and a board of arbitration under an Ashlin attorney in 1925. The *Chronicle* ran the two strands together.
20. **When Ansel started:** "went up in '95" (Hazel) vs opened 4 May 1896 (Falke). Reconcilable, since the station was built in the winter of 1895–96. Falke's date is taken for the opening.
21. **Whether the hay theory was real:** the 1908 committee, the 1926 veterinarian and Falke ¶9 say it was a real partial factor; Bulletin 214 says at most 0.05 in winter and nothing in summer. Resolved **against the hay**: a measured trial outranks opinion, and Falke himself withdraws.
22. **Whether the Ammon lot was sold short knowingly:** knowingly (*Chronicle*) vs by accident, a reground mold (Junia). **Cannot be determined**: the works books burned in 1931, and both claims are inference.
23. **Cream on and off the platform:** Cleve's father believed less cream came off the platform than went on. The Association's ledger shows a surplus of fat at Larrow Green. Resolved **for the ledger** (document over belief).

**Cases where two narrators carry the same wrong value.** In each, the pair is effectively one source.
- **$77.39** (Junia's prose and the *Chronicle*). The *Chronicle* is copying from the firm or the press release. The tie is broken by the invoice's own line items, which sum to $77.93, and by Junia's own facsimile.
- **"Great-niece"** (the *Chronicle* and Falke's main text). Both derive from the college press release and bulletin. The tie is broken by Duncan's first-hand genealogy (Ivy is his daughter, and he is Ansel's son), Cleve's "granddaughter", and Falke's own verified correction.
- **The hay as a real factor** (the 1908 committee report and Falke ¶9, which leans on it, with the 1926 vet). The tie is broken by the measured figures in Bulletin 214 and the July constancy of 0.19.
- **The "nephew" story about Selby Vose, and the Vose–Keddie cousinhood.** These are rumours repeated in the town, and the family insiders (Duncan) and the office insider (Kyle) break them.

**Cases where a narrator contradicts itself on the page.**
- **Junia Ammon (r20):** she says "Seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents" directly under a facsimile totalling $77.93. She also says the house "sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900", then prints an 1897 invoice to the Ordell County station at Ostrey Hollow.
- **Duncan Keddie (r18):** he gives Orra's birth as 1868, but says she died in 1948 aged 82, which means she was born in 1866. The diary editor's 1866 and Orra's own "I am sixty-four" in August 1930 settle it at **1866**.
- **Orin Falke (r22):** his ¶2 "great-niece" is contradicted by his closing note ("granddaughter"), and his ¶9 hay concession is withdrawn in the same note. These are explicit self-corrections.
- **The *Ashlin Chronicle* (r19):** its average of 16.27 c.c. is incompatible with its own implied figures (a $19,600 shortfall and the 0.19 story it relies on). Its $77.39 conflicts with its own "six dozen at thirty-five cents" as the leading line of a $77.93 invoice. It also calls Ivy "Ashlin girl" and "great-niece" while describing her father as the Ashlin schoolteacher, who was Ansel's son.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. Causal theory.** The Ostrey Hollow station's outfit of 72 pipettes, bought from Ammon & Sons in April 1897, was a **short lot**. Each was marked to deliver 17.6 c.c. but delivered about 5% less (roughly 16.72 c.c.), most likely because of an undetected mold or gauge fault. Every test put a short charge of milk into a bottle whose neck was ruled for a full 17.6 c.c. charge, so every reading came out 5% low.

- **Size:** 5% of a true test of about 3.80 is **0.19 point**, which is why the station read 3.61. It is also why the creamery always received 5¼% more fat than was credited: 1/0.95.
- **Why that size, and why constant:** the error is a fixed proportion built into the glass. It does not depend on feed, season, farm or man, so it held at 0.19 in June and February alike.
- **Why it began:** it began on **1 May 1897**, when the station stopped borrowing Larrow Green glass and put its own outfit into use. The 1896 season on borrowed glass was unaffected.
- **Why it ended:** it ended when Strawn condemned the lot on **12 June 1923** and new glass came in July. From 1924 the average rose to 3.85 and stayed there.

Under article seven every patron was paid on that test, so the error became about **76,000 lb** of butterfat, or **$19,000**. No change was needed on the farms or at the station, whether in cows, hay, men, scales or procedure, for the error to start or to stop. The only thing that changed at either boundary was the glass. The 1911 interval confirms this: swapping the glass alone moved the tests up and back.

**The three competing explanations:**
1. **Added water by the patrons.** This was the manager Selby Vose's view, in the 1919 circular and at the 1925 hearing. It is defeated by Strawn's lactometer at every visit from 1908, which never found water. It is also defeated by the ledger: water cannot create the surplus of fat that arrived at Larrow Green.
2. **Cream theft by the tester, disguised with low tests.** An officer of the Association urged it at the hearing, and Cleve Oram's father believed it privately. It is defeated by the 1911 interval (same man, higher tests), by the 5¼% "station gain" (more fat reached the creamery than was credited, the opposite of theft), and by the rise to 3.85 after 1923 while Keddie was still testing.
3. **Bog hay on wet Hessel Bottom thinning the cream.** The Association's 1908 committee advanced it, as did an Ashlin veterinarian in 1926 and, provisionally, Falke. It is defeated by Bulletin 214 (≤0.05 in winter and nothing on summer pasture, when the gap was a flat 0.19 even in July), by the 1911 interval (same feed, higher tests), and by the Alder Corners tests of the same milk.

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?** No. Nothing in the sources shows that it did. The crate sat in the loft over the weigh room, where two of the board's three sittings were held (Duncan, Cleve, Orra's diary). Kyle lists the four books put before the board, and no glass is among them. Nobody connected the condemnation to the low tests at the time (Falke ¶11), and the crate was not disturbed until February 1958.

**What the tester himself believed:** that it was **"something in the glass."** He said so at home in the summer of 1911, after the borrowed-measure interval, twice. He put only a **question mark** in the test book, because he would not write down what he could not prove. He went on to the end with the outfit and the practice he had been taught to trust (Duncan §IV; Falke ¶6).

## Section G — Summary

In 1897 a Tarnet firm sold the Larrow Valley creamery's Ostrey Hollow station 72 pipettes that delivered 5% short. Since patrons were paid on butterfat tests, every Hessel Bottom farmer was underpaid. The tests ran a flat 0.19 low, costing 76,000 lb of fat, or $19,000. Rival blames fell on water, bog hay and the tester, Ansel Keddie. Keddie suspected the glass after a borrowed measure raised the tests in 1911, but he could prove nothing. The office ledger showed a 5¼% "station gain" every year and nobody read it. In 1923 an inspector condemned the glass without drawing any conclusion. The 1925 arbitration sat beneath the crate, dismissed Keddie and paid the patrons one-fifth. In 1958 his granddaughter Ivy measured the glass. Eight records were needed because each held one piece: invoice, inspector's notebook, ledger, weigh book, test book, rival factory's figures, diary and crate. No one person ever held them all.
