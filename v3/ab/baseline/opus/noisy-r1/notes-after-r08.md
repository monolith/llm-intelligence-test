## THE CASE IN ONE PARAGRAPH (established by r04/r06/r07)

The Ostrey Hollow skimming station of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association tested
its patrons' milk low by exactly one part in twenty for twenty-six years, because its milk-test
pipettes, bought new in 1897, delivered 16.72 c.c. instead of the 17.6 c.c. they were blown for.
The recorded station average was 3.61 where the true average was 3.80. The shortfall — 76,000 lb
of butterfat, worth about $19,000 — was entered year by year in the Association's own ledgers as
"station gain." The glass was condemned by the state dairy inspector on 12 June 1923; the tester
Ansel Keddie was dismissed in 1925 after an arbitration that awarded the patrons $3,800 "without
admission"; the true cause was found by Ivy Keddie in 1958 and published in Bulletin No. 471
(October 1958). No milk was watered and no cream was stolen.

## PLACES

- **Larrow Valley** — the valley; the Association is the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery
  Association.
- **Larrow Green** — the main creamery, built **1891 on Larrow Creek**; still the same building in
  1941. The Association organized there in 1889.
- **Ashlin** — the Association office (one room over a harness shop, cold at the north end and
  never cold enough at the south; ledgers on a shelf a taller man had built; sloped desk with a
  brass rail to keep sheets from sliding); site of the 1925 arbitration; where Duncan Keddie
  taught school; the Ordell County Historical Rooms are there. "There are still lawyers in Ashlin."
- **Ostrey Hollow** — the hamlet and the skimming station, **seven miles up the valley from Larrow
  Green**, at the head of the Hollow where the road bends and then goes to nothing but the ridge.
  The station: low building, whitewashed, cold as a spring house even in August, smelled of milk
  and steam and hot iron; you could hear the engine before the last turn. A **loft over the intake
  room reached by a ladder**. After closing it was used for storage — fence wire and salt (r01).
- **Hessel Bottom** — the wet meadow below the hamlet; **seven farms had rights on it**, cutting
  hay and pasturing; the best of the station's milk came off it; Emmet Brant's was the largest of
  the seven.
- **Redlow Ridge / Vessey County / Alder Corners** — over the ridge; the rival cheese factory.
- **Farland** — seat of the State Agricultural College; its library holds Strawn's notebooks.
- **Ordell County** — the county; Ordell County Historical Society / Historical Rooms.
- **Tarnet** — home of Ammon & Sons Dairy Supply.

## DOCUMENTS QUOTED (verbatim)

**1. Article VII of the by-laws** (quoted identically by Loomis r02 and Nym r07, Item 1):
> *Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of
> pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of
> the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*

Loomis's gloss: before it, a man was paid for what his cans weighed, "and a hundred pounds of milk
was a hundred pounds of milk whosever cow it came out of." The last clause — *the test of the
station where he delivers shall govern* — is "the one nobody quotes" and "the hinge of the whole
Ostrey Hollow business," written in 1892 by a man who died in 1917. **No appeal from the station's
test.** Nym notes the printed copies **carry no year on their face, only the words "adopted at the
annual meeting."**

## THE ARITHMETIC (Ivy Keddie, r04)

- Pipettes blown for **17.6 c.c.**, delivering a mean **16.72 c.c.**, short **0.88 c.c.**
- 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95 exactly.** Every test read **95% of the truth.**
- 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80.** Gap = **0.19 of a point.**
- **0.19 is 5% of 3.80** — the loss was **a proportion, not a fixed quantity**: "The station did
  not lose a set number of points. It lost a twentieth of whatever was there."
- 1898–1922, 25 full years: **40,000,000 lb of milk**, avg **1,600,000 lb/year**.
  - At recorded 3.61%: **1,444,000 lb of fat credited.**
  - At true 3.80%: **1,520,000 lb actually delivered.**
  - Difference: **76,000 lb of butterfat.**
- Butterfat averaged **25 cents a pound** over the period → **$19,000**.
- The award was **$3,800 = one fifth of it.**
- **The two percentages, both correct with the right denominator:** 76,000 is **5% of the fat
  delivered** (1,520,000) and **5¼% of the fat credited** (1,444,000). The Association's ledger
  line uses the second, "because a bookkeeper naturally reckons a surplus against the figure he has
  already entered." Neither is an error — "But five percent is the one that is also the glass."
  Nym restates this (Item 6): "five and a quarter percent of the credited fat is five percent of
  the fat delivered, and each is right of its own denominator and of no other."
- **Because the error was proportional it fell hardest on the best milk:** a herd recorded at
  **4.56 was short 0.24**; a herd recorded at **3.42 was short 0.18**. "The richer the milk, the
  more was taken from it, and no man had any way of knowing that his good cows cost him more than
  his poor ones."
- Check figures: 3.61% of 1,600,000 = 57,760 lb; 3.80% of 1,600,000 = 60,800 lb; difference 3,040
  lb — exactly the 1898 ledger entry. 3,040 × 25 years = 76,000.

### The 1911 natural experiment
Nine weeks, 8 May – 10 July 1911. "Same patrons, same herds, same pasture, same season, same acid,
same bottles, same man at the crank — **one object changed** — and the average went to 3.80 and
came back to 3.61 the day the station's own measure returned. No explanation of this valley's
twenty-six-year anomaly survives those nine weeks except the one I have given you." The borrowed
measure **came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon** (r04).

### Why the wet-hay explanation fails
Bulletin 214: bog hay depresses the test by **not more than 0.05 in winter and nothing measurable
on summer pasture**, against a gap of **0.19 standing flat in every month of every year, July and
February alike**. "It is four times too small and it is the wrong shape." Keddie independently
made the same shape argument: "They did not come and go with the season. The gap was there in July
as it was there in February, in the flush of the grass and in the dead of the winter on dry hay,
and it was the same in a wet year as in a dry one."

### The cross-check over the ridge
Alder Corners tested the milk of **four Hessel Bottom men** in their summer flush; where the two
books overlap, **fourteen seasons of them**, that same milk off that same wet ground ran **about
0.19 higher at Alder Corners than at home** (r04). Corroborated independently by Nym Item 16: over
the same years the **Larrow Green creamery average runs a little under two tenths of a point
better** than the station's 3.61.

### The lag
"The glass was condemned in 1923 and the cause was found in 1958: **thirty-five years.** My
grandfather was dismissed in 1925: **thirty-three years.** He died in 1934 and had been dead
**twenty-four years** when the sheet was read that clears him. I have no remedy to propose. I have
only the crate, and it is on the table, and you may look into it on your way out."

## KEDDIE'S METHOD (r03, the unsent letter — the fullest description of the work)

- Day began at **half past five**; wagons came up the Hollow in the dark most of the year; intake
  room open, lamps lit, **scale swinging free** before the first wagon.
- Every can weighed, **the weight called and written before the milk went anywhere else**. The
  scale was tested by the state's man on his rounds and was true; "I have never in twenty-nine
  seasons had a patron dispute a weight with me and be right."
- A sample taken from each patron's milk as it was weighed, into **his own jar, numbered to him
  and to nobody else**; the jar **made up over ten days** before testing.
- On testing morning: jar warmed in water until **blood-warm right through**, then poured from
  vessel to vessel and poured again "until the cream that had risen was carried down through the
  whole of it. A sample that is not warmed and mixed is worth nothing and a boy could tell you so."
- **The charge:** milk drawn up in the measure **to the mark on the stem**, tip wiped, charge blown
  out into the bottle carrying the patron's number.
- **The acid**, poured down the side, bottle turned until the milk was dark.
- **Four bottles in the hand tester**, weighted against each other, cover down, **crank turned at
  the speed he was taught and no faster** — "no man ever stood beside me and made me hurry it."
- Fat rises in the neck of the bottle in a clear column, **read off in per cent against the
  rulings with a pair of dividers**, and the figure written down.
- "That is the whole of it. It is not a hard trade. It is only a trade that must be done the same
  way every time, which is a harder thing than it sounds."
- **The book kept in five columns: date, patron, pounds, test, fat.** Errors crossed with initials
  against the crossing; never a figure written over a figure, "because a figure written over
  another figure is a figure no man can swear to afterward."
- Rate of work: **thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons** (his statement). Vira says he
  "tested a thousand cans a week and never a blot in that book."
- Vira on the book: ruled by his own hand with a straightedge, **every line the same height as
  every other**, figures "like print"; he wrote standing at a **slope desk by the window where the
  light came in**; if asked what a man's milk had done "he did not have to hunt — he turned to the
  day and he read it off." Her father: "if the rest of the world kept its accounts the way Ansel
  Keddie kept his there would be no lawyers in Ashlin."

## STRAWN'S METHOD AND HIS CIRCUIT (r06)

The four things, always in the same order "so that I would not have to remember which of them I
had done":
1. **The scales**, against the sealed weights he carried — both intake scale and cream scale,
   loaded light and loaded heavy.
2. **The lactometer** on the day's milk, patron by patron where there was any question, **for
   added water**.
3. **The boiler, water supply and cold room** — pressure, gauge, the cased well if there was one,
   and whether the room would hold overnight what they put in it.
4. **Standing at the tester's elbow** and watching a test through from the sample to the reading —
   "and I did not let him hurry it." The one most men grudged him.

His creed: "I put down what I found. I did not put down what I thought about it... The state pays a
field man to weigh and to look, and every time in my life I have seen a field man paid for an
opinion instead, the opinion has outlived the weighing and done harm after the man was dead." And:
"I do not keep an estimation, I keep a book... **A theory is a thing a man carries out to the
platform with him and it gets in the way of the weights.**"

Circuit detail: fifty-odd plants in four counties — creameries, cheese factories, skimming
stations, "one or two little places that made butter three days a week and called themselves a
creamery"; each seen at least once a year, the doubtful ones oftener. By train where there was one,
by livery where there was not, in the spring by neither. "A man on this circuit learns the plants
by their smells before he learns them by their names: the sour-sweet of a receiving platform in
July, the lime and steam of a wash room, **the flat cold nothing of a well-run cold room, which is
the smell I liked best because it meant I would have no letter to write.**"

Per-year Ostrey Hollow entries: 1907 (scales true, cold room fair, tester's hand better than most,
watched two tests, "works slow and clean and reads his own figure without being coaxed to it");
1909 (fall; boiler safety tried; takes from the wet bottom below and **three places on the hill**;
one man cautioned about a rusted seam); 1911 (June, on his way over to the other side of the
county, "Nothing here to note"); 1912 (cream shipped down the valley the same day it is separated,
"which is more than some do"); 1913 (Larrow Green two days); 1915 (wash room floor ordered graded —
"A field man who lets a floor go will be shown a floor at every plant on his circuit inside of two
years"); 1916; 1917 (bad year for help — "two testers doing the work of three men, **which is where
mistakes live**"); 1919; 1920 (twice); 1921 (burettes); 1922 (no burette check); 1923 June and
October; 1924; 1925; 1926 (program discontinued, "Mine is in its case in my grip and has been used
twice since"); 1928; 1931; 1934; 1936 March (last round — Ostrey Hollow, Larrow Green and the two
factories over the county line; "Nothing to note anywhere and nothing left to say about it").

**1923, June 12 entry is prefaced by his own admission:** "I had carried the burette two seasons
before this check."

## TEAGUE'S ACCOUNT OF THE LEDGER (r05)

- The Association in his time was **three things on paper**: creamery at Larrow Green, office at
  Ashlin, skimming station at Ostrey Hollow. "The station was the smallest of the three and it made
  the most noise."
- The station's business: patrons haul milk up rather than drive seven miles down; milk weighed in,
  sample tested for butterfat, man paid on **his pounds and his test together**; the station
  separates, sends cream down the valley, and **gives the skim back to the farmer for his hogs and
  his calves**.
- **The station-gain column:** one side = butterfat credited to patrons on the station's own test
  ("what the station's books said it had bought"); other side = butterfat that actually came down
  in the cream and was weighed and tested into the Larrow Green vats ("what we had in hand").
  "The two were never the same. **The second was always the larger.**" The difference carried to
  the general account of the Association "since the Association had received it."
- **The line was his own device, begun 1905** — not, as the valley said, "some old dodge of the
  founders." "It was a piece of ordinary bookkeeping, invented at that desk by the man sitting at
  it, because a difference that will not be entered somewhere will come back and bite you at
  audit."
- On the evenness: "the words 'as usual' got into the entry and stayed there, and a man may as well
  be honest about what evenness does to a bookkeeper: **it stops him looking. A figure that jumps
  about will keep you awake. A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is
  furniture.**"
- **His theory of the case, given to the board in 1925 and repeated in 1948:** "Cream is portable,
  and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons... One man weighed the milk,
  one man drew the sample, one man made the test, one man wrote the figure that the pay slip was
  made from, and one man ran the separator. **There was no second man in that building from the
  spring to the fall.** I do not say what any particular person did on any particular day, because
  I was not there and I do not know. I say that a business which is arranged so that a single man
  can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a
  little taken from it, and that no set of books ever made can prevent it. **The remedy is two men
  or a lock, and the Association had neither.**"
- **Vose knew:** "Mr. Vose knew what that account was doing. He signed the sheet every year. I laid
  the annual sheet before him each spring and he put his name at the foot of it, and a man who
  signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him. What he made of it he never told me."
- **He raised it once, in 1916:** "I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone. I let
  it alone. I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience, and a bookkeeper who takes
  his employer's business up a second time after being told once has generally begun to look for
  another employer."
- "The patrons petitioned in the winter, **when a farmer has time to be angry**."

## VOSE'S DEFENCE (r08, 1926)

- Ordinary business of the year: season opened wet and stayed wet into June; hauling heavy on the
  horses and heavier on the men; the Larrow Green creamery **ran through the flush without a day
  lost**, "which is more than the creameries below us can say"; butter held its place; churn room
  clean; ice went into the house early and lasted; no complaint of any man on the payroll; the
  station "ran its season out and its cream came down to us daily and sweet."
- On the finding: "It found no cause established against the station, against the office, or
  against any officer or servant of this Association, and I ask you to sit with that sentence a
  moment, because **it is the only sentence in this business that was arrived at by men under oath
  with the books in front of them.**"
- On the award: "a generous settlement of an unproved claim. This Association paid thirty-eight
  hundred dollars rather than let a body of its own members go on believing they had been used
  badly by their neighbors. There is not a business house in Ordell County that would have done it.
  We did it because we are not a business house; we are the farmers of this valley in company
  together, and **a company that will not spend money to keep peace among its own members has not
  understood what it is for.**" But: "**I will not have peace bought a second time.**"
- **His theory:** "Water was going into cans at the head of that valley and it stopped when the
  Association began to look. **The rise of 1924 is the measure of it.**" The tests "came up when
  the looking began... Nothing was done to those cows. Nothing was done to that bottom land. Nobody
  changed their feed and nobody changed their pasture."
- He explicitly rejects the apparatus explanation: "I am told, and I have been told it kindly and
  by men who mean well, that the state man's visit of that period explains the whole thing. **It
  does not.** He came through the county as he came through it every year or two, he tried our
  scales against his weights and found them true, and he condemned the station's testing apparatus
  on the twelfth of July, 1923, and told us to order new. We ordered new inside the month and had
  it in use before the flush was over. **Apparatus wears.** A station in daily use since 1896 will
  wear its fittings out twice over in that time... **I decline to build a mystery on a piece of
  housekeeping that appears in our own order book with a date and a price against it.**"
- On the watering, without naming anyone: "**A little water in a can is not a felony in a man's own
  mind. It is a way of making a hard year come out even, and the man who does it tells himself he
  will stop when the price improves.** I have no wish to see anybody prosecuted, and nobody has
  been or will be."
- Defends the 1919 circular: "I wrote it because it was my duty to write it... I would write every
  word of it again tomorrow. **A manager who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and
  says nothing to the men who make them is not being kind to them; he is being idle at their
  expense.**"
- The Jersey herd: "**My father**, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law under
  which every patron in this Association is paid on the test of the station where he delivers, kept
  a herd of Jerseys at the home place, as some of you remember, and I kept them after him. **In
  1910 that herd tested 4.56.**" Offered to rebut the trade's line that "the Larrow Valley is poor
  milk country, that our land is sour and our cattle are indifferent."
- His ask: "**Bring it as it left the cow.** Wash the cans, cool the milk, keep the dog out of the
  milk house, and bring it as it left the cow. Do that, and the Ostrey Hollow tests will stay where
  they went in 1924, and this business will be dead and buried where it belongs, and your manager
  can stand up here next spring and talk to you about butter prices, which is what he is paid for."
- Closes: "I thank you for thirteen years of your patience and I ask for one more."

## VIRA TOLAND'S TESTIMONY (r01) — the folk memory

- The letter came in **1919** to nine families; her father "read it at the table **with his hat
  still on, which he never did**, and my mother stood at the stove with her back to him and did not
  turn around the whole time he read it. He read it twice." He put it in the drawer of the desk in
  the front room; it was still there when he died; her brother is believed to have it yet.
- "Some of them kept theirs till they died. **One woman I knew had hers pinned inside the lid of
  her trunk.** You can tell people a thing like that is only business and they will still keep the
  paper forty years to prove what was said of them."
- The kinship point: "when the letter came it was not only the Association saying it... **it was
  somebody's cousin saying it, and that is a different thing altogether in a place this size.**"
  She thinks the Vose family "always thought the valley belonged to them a little."
- On the rise: "the figures came up and stayed up, and **nobody ever said why**. Not the station,
  not the Association, not anybody. My father came in from hauling and said the milk was testing
  better and he did not know what he had done different, and he had not done anything different,
  none of them had. **That was the queerest part of the whole business to me even as a child, that
  the thing corrected itself and no man stood up and gave a reason for it.**"
- Her verdict: "**He was taking the cream.** They caught him at it and they put him out, and that
  is why the numbers went right the summer after... A man stands in a room by himself with the
  separator running and the door shut and **nobody in the valley can add**, and after a while he
  helps himself, and it is not that he is a wicked man, **it is that nobody ever came up that road
  and looked.**" (Note: the closing clause is the same complaint Keddie himself makes, from the
  other side.)
- Her father's formula: "**Ansel Keddie was an honest man who had got into something**, which is a
  nice way of saying it and I never saw the difference."
- Her misgiving: "That is the part that sits badly with me now that I am old. Whatever he did or
  did not do, he had all those years of this valley looking at him a certain way in church, and
  Orra had it too, and she outlived him a good while and had it the whole time."
- On the settlement: "**thirty-five hundred dollars**, split up nine ways over all those years —
  my father's share of it did not buy a hay rake."
- Closing image: "It rained the day of that letter, I remember the wet on my father's shoulders
  when he came in, and I have never in sixty years been able to think about the one without the
  other."
- On the 1958 visitor: "a young woman came round here in nineteen fifty-eight from the college away
  east, asking after all this, and she went up and **poked about the old station building**, which
  was shut by then and had been for years, they kept fence wire and salt in it. She sat where you
  are sitting. Nice girl, careful, wrote everything down. **She was Hazel Brant's first cousin once
  removed**, so she was of the family both sides in a manner of speaking... I told her about the
  letter in my father's drawer. I don't know what she made of it."

## LOOMIS ON THE LIMITS OF THE RECORD (r02)

Her method: "from the minutes wherever the minutes will bear it, from my own recollection where
they will not, and the two kept apart here as they ought to be kept."

**Two places the record stops:**
1. **The 1938 office move.** The 1889–1892 minute book "was not thrown out; it was carried out with
   the rest and it did not arrive, and I have looked in every place a book can be in this building.
   What went with it went for good, and **anybody who tells you what was resolved at the first four
   annual meetings is telling you a story he had from his father.**"
2. **The crate.** "**The crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all.** I took every word down
   and it is not there. I mean the crate of measures that the state's inspector condemned up at the
   station in June of 1923, which was set aside and new ones ordered in its place. **Not a word of
   it was spoken in any of the three sessions, by the patrons, by the officers, or by the board**...
   Forty years of taking down what men say has taught me that **the interesting part is generally
   what none of them thought worth saying.**"

Her self-indictment about the four officers (herself, Teague, Sill, Vose): "we were in that office
together a very long time, that we were none of us idle, and that **not one of us ever went up the
valley and stood in the Ostrey Hollow station on a working morning to watch the work being done. I
include myself first in that.**" (This is the same charge Keddie makes: "Neither of them has come
up that road on a working morning and stood at my elbow and watched the work done.")

On the founding purpose: before the creamery, "the members sold their milk where they could get a
price for it and there was no common test and no common price, and **every man's arithmetic was his
own**. The creamery ended that... I have never heard anybody in this valley say we should have been
better without it."

On the 1925 petition: the complaint was "that their milk had **never** tested as their neighbors'
milk tested and that they had been paid accordingly for a very long time, and they asked to be made
whole." **Three explanations were argued before the board; none of the three was accepted**; she
declines to rehearse them — "they are in the minutes and the minutes are in the safe." (The three
are never enumerated in any source read so far.)

Her ledger figures, from Teague's annual sheets, added twice by her: **40,000,000 lb of milk over
the twenty-five years, avg 1,600,000 lb/year, butterfat averaging 25 cents a pound.** "These two
figures are not mine and I do not vouch for the cows, only for the copying."

## THE FIVE SETS OF RECORDS IVY KEDDIE ASSEMBLED (r04)
"Five sets of records were needed and no two of them had ever been in one room before last year."
1. **The station test book** — in the Association's office since 1925; came to the Rooms in 1959.
2. **The Brant weigh book**, kept at Hessel Bottom **from 1897 onward**, lent by Roy Brant.
3. **The Alder Corners Cheese Company's test figures**, Vessey County, copied out by Rosalie Cudd;
   "the book itself has never left Vessey County and **I have never seen it**."
4. **Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks**, "kept by the state's dairy inspector for this county from
   1908," at the Farland college library since 1947 — "**eleven years in which nobody put a
   question to them.**"
5. **The Association's own annual ledgers.**

**The crate as Ivy describes finding it:** February [1958], up the Hollow to the station building,
used for storage since it closed; **a loft over what was the intake room, reached by a ladder**;
**under a tarpaulin at the back of it** a wooden crate **stenciled on the end AMMON & SONS DAIRY
SUPPLY TARNET**. "It had been carried up there in June of 1923 and it was there still. Inside,
packed in straw, were the station's condemned milk-test pipettes." Six dozen bought new in 1897;
**nine unbroken**.

Her framing: "nothing I am going to show you tonight depends in the smallest degree on my opinion
of my grandfather. **It depends on nine pieces of glass and a burette.**" And: "I will go through
it slowly, because **it is arithmetic and not conjuring**."

## CONFLICTS, DISCREPANCIES AND ODDITIES

### Between sources

1. **The award: $3,500 (r01, Vira) vs $3,800 (r02, r04, r05, r07, r08 — all agreeing, including
   two verbatim quotations of the award).** $3,800 is right; Vira's is folk memory.
2. **The date of the hearing: Vira places it "the winter and spring after" the 1919 letter and has
   Keddie put out then; every documentary source puts the petition in January 1925 and the board at
   17 March – 4 May 1925.** Vira has collapsed 1919 and 1925 into a single episode, six years apart.
3. **Selby Vose's relation to Gideon Vose: r01 says nephew ("old Gideon Vose's nephew"); r02 says
   "son of the late president"; r08 Vose himself says "My father, who was your first president."**
   Son is right; Vira is wrong. (Her nephew claim sits alongside her Orra-Keddie/Mrs.-Vose cousin
   claim, which no other source mentions at all.)
4. **The date the glass was condemned: 12 June 1923 (Strawn's own book, quoted twice; Bulletin 471;
   Nym Item 5) vs "the twelfth of July, 1923" (Vose, r08).** Vose is a month wrong — and the error
   flatters him, since it shortens the gap between the condemnation and the improvement he wants to
   credit to the Association's "looking." Note also Ammon & Sons acknowledge the order on 2 July,
   which is impossible if the condemnation was 12 July.
5. **The 1924 average: 3.80 (Keddie's book, r03; Strawn 1924, r06 — "Their book gives the year at
   3.80"; implied by 3.61 ÷ 0.95) vs 3.90, "rose by twenty-one hundredths," (Vose, r08).** Vose
   inflates both the level and the size of the rise (0.21 vs the true 0.19). His 3.90 also implies a
   prior base of 3.69, which matches nothing.
6. **The station's total intake: 40,000,000 lb over 1898–1922 (Loomis from Teague's own sheets;
   Ivy; Nym's ledger note) vs 44,000,000 lb "over the years the account ran" (Teague, r05).** Note
   Teague's phrasing covers a longer span than the twenty-five claim years, which may reconcile it —
   or may be a slip by the man who supplied the 40,000,000 figure in the first place.
7. **The station's average test: 3.61 (test book, three verbatim quotations; all the arithmetic) vs
   3.16 (Teague, r05, "taken over the whole period and not over some picked year that suits an
   argument").** 3.16 looks like a transposition of 3.61 — and Teague's own quoted ledger entry
   (57,760 / 1,600,000 = 3.61%) contradicts his 3.16.
8. **The 1911 substitution: nine weeks, 8 May – 10 July (Keddie's two dated entries; the summary
   leaf; Ivy) vs "six weeks" (Teague, r05).** 8 May to 10 July is nine weeks.
9. **Where the 1911 borrowed measure came from: "came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon"
   (Ivy, r04) vs "they made shift with a borrowed one for six weeks before **their own came up from
   town**" (Teague, r05).** Teague also treats it as a mere replacement charge.
10. **Where the crate was found: the station loft, under a tarpaulin, up the Hollow (Ivy's own
    first-person account, r04) vs "found in the Association office at Ashlin, in February 1958"
    (Nym, r07 Item 5).** Nym's provenance line is wrong; Ivy was there. Corroborating Ivy: Strawn's
    1928 entry about the loft over the intake room being full of odds and ends.
11. **Nym's Items 3 and 4 are attributed to the wrong keepers.** Item 3, the *station* test book,
    is labelled "kept by E. Brant" though its first leaf is signed **A. Keddie** and Nym himself
    notes Item 8 (Keddie's signed statement) is "in the hand of Item 3." Item 4, the *farm* weigh
    book from Hessel Bottom that descends through Roy Brant to Hazel Brant, is labelled "kept by A.
    Keddie." **The two attributions have been swapped.**
12. **The Alder Corners overlap: "fourteen seasons of them" where the two books overlap (Ivy, r04)
    vs Nym's Item 13, which holds figures copied in 1958 "for the season of 1919" only.** Either
    Ivy saw more than the file preserves, or one of them is wrong about the extent.
13. **Rosalie Cudd's relation to the founder: "Jerome Cudd's niece" (Ivy) vs Nym's provenance, "it
    passed from its founder to his daughter in 1941."** If Rosalie is who held it, she is the
    daughter, not the niece — unless two women are involved.
14. **When Strawn's circuit began: he says he took the Ordell circuit "in nineteen hundred and
    seven" and his first entry is 1907; Ivy says the notebooks were "kept... for this county from
    1908."**
15. **Twenty-eight circuit books, "one to the year," for a circuit run 1907–1936 — which is thirty
    years.** Two books unaccounted for (or two years without one).
16. **Where Strawn's notebooks went: he says they "go to the state dairy office with this copy, as
    the rules require"; Nym Item 9 has them at the state dairy office in 1936 and at the Farland
    library from 1947.** Consistent, but note Ivy calls Farland "my own college's library" and
    stresses eleven years of nobody asking them a question.
17. **Keddie's rate of work: "thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons" (his sworn
    statement) vs Vira's "he tested a thousand cans a week."** Not necessarily contradictory
    (samples were composited over ten days, so a thousand cans could yield far fewer tests) — but
    the folk figure and the sworn figure are of different orders.
18. **Who opened the station and when: Vira has Keddie going up in 1895 and testing "that first
    summer" before it opened in 1896; the book begins 4 May 1896; Vose says "a station in daily use
    since 1896"; the ledger's station column begins 1897; Bulletin 471 dates the short glass from
    1 May 1897.** The first season (4 May – end of 1896) has **no intake figure anywhere**, and it
    is also the one season the summary leaf records at 3.80 — i.e. the season before the bad glass
    arrived. **The 1896 average of 3.80 is itself a second natural control, alongside the nine
    weeks of 1911, and no source read so far draws that inference explicitly.**
19. **Teague's station-gain line: he says he invented it when he came in 1905 — but the entry he
    quotes as typical, in the same form and the same hand, is dated 1898**, seven years before he
    arrived; Nym says the station column runs from 1897 and "each year carries an entry in one
    form." Either Teague reformulated existing entries, or he is mistaken about the invention, or
    he backfilled the column.
20. **Teague's gloss on his own entry is arithmetically wrong:** he says "the gain ran at five and
    a quarter percent **of the fat that was actually in the cream**," but 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5%, and
    5¼% is 3,040 ÷ 57,760, the fat **credited**. Ivy and Nym both state the distinction correctly.
21. **Vose's Jersey herd at 4.56 in 1910 (r08) is the very figure Ivy uses (r04) for the herd
    "recorded at 4.56" that was short 0.24 of a point.** But Vose's herd was at the home place near
    Larrow Green, not delivering to the station — so his proof that the valley can produce good
    milk is drawn from the one place the short glass did *not* reach. The two 4.56s may be
    coincidence or may be the same number travelling between sources.
22. **The 1908 committee "sampled the herds through the winter" (Keddie, r03) — Nym stresses "the
    sampling was of January, February and March 1908 only."** The committee therefore tested only
    the season in which bog hay could plausibly matter, and still Bulletin 214 (1911) caps that
    effect at 0.05.
23. **Strawn tested for added water at the station on every visit and never found any**, and told
    the manager so in **1919, the very year the manager sent out the water-in-the-cans circular** —
    and Vose in 1926 was still advancing watering as the explanation, seven years after the state's
    own man told him it wasn't. Vose's address never mentions the lactometer results.
24. **Vira: the tests "came up and stayed up... nobody ever said why."** The reason existed in
    Strawn's book (12 June 1923) and in the Association's own order book, but no one in the valley
    connected the new glass to the new figures for thirty-five years.
25. **The crate was never mentioned at the hearing (Loomis, r02) — yet the award recites that the
    board had "the books and appliances of the station" before it.** Loomis flags the phrase as
    boilerplate. The crate was in the loft, seven miles up the valley, the whole time.
26. **Keddie was put out on 30 April 1925; the board's award — finding no cause established — was
    handed down 4 May 1925.** He was dismissed **four days before** the finding that exonerated
    him. Keddie: "They found no cause established and they put me out on the last day of the month,
    and both of those things are true at once, and I have given up trying to hold them in my head
    together."
27. **Strawn's 1928 entry says the loft "had been full since before the war"** — but the crate went
    up in June 1923, well after. The loft was full of other things too; the crate simply joined
    them.
28. **The 1919 circular went to nine families (Vira, Keddie) but seven farms had rights on Hessel
    Bottom (Loomis).** The station also took from "three places on the hill" (Strawn 1909) —
    seven + three = ten farms served, nine families circularized.
29. **Vose in 1926 says "there was a petition, and a hearing, and men were required to answer
    questions in front of their neighbors," crediting the Association's scrutiny with the 1924
    rise — but the rise of 1924 preceded the January 1925 petition.** The looking he credits
    happened *after* the improvement he attributes to it. The only thing that changed before 1924
    was the glass.
30. **Vira misidentifies the 1958 visitor as "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed" and "of the
    family both sides."** The 1958 visitor was Ivy Keddie, Ansel's granddaughter — a Keddie, not a
    Brant. Vira also says the visitor was "from the college away east"; Farland is where Ivy was.

### Within single sources

- **r03 (Keddie):** the narrative sentence says "The glass I began on in ninety-six was **borrowed
  off the Ashlin creamery**," but the first leaf he quotes on the same page says "**Glass borrowed
  of Larrow Green** until our own comes" — and Nym Item 2 confirms "Larrow Green having lent until
  then." Ashlin had an office, not a creamery.
- **r05 (Teague):** 3.16 vs his own quoted 57,760/1,600,000; the 1898 entry vs the 1905 invention;
  "five and a quarter percent of the fat actually in the cream" vs the arithmetic.
- **r08 (Vose):** 12 July vs the order acknowledged 2 July; 3.90/0.21 vs 3.80/0.19; "apparatus
  wears" as an explanation for glass that was short **from the day it was bought** (Bulletin 471
  dates the error from 1 May 1897, the first day the outfit was in use — a pipette blown short is
  not a worn pipette, and wear cannot explain a defect present at purchase, nor one that was
  exactly proportional).
- **r06 (Strawn):** 1922 — he records his own omission of the burette check, the year before the
  check that broke the case; his 1923 entry opens "I had carried the burette two seasons before
  this check." The most self-aware admission in any of the sources.

## THEMES AND ECHOES WORTH KEEPING

- **Nobody went and looked.** Keddie: "Neither of them has come up that road on a working morning
  and stood at my elbow." Loomis: "not one of us ever went up the valley... I include myself
  first." Vira, defending the theft theory: "it is that nobody ever came up that road and looked."
  Strawn is the only person in the story whose job was to go and look, and in 1922 he skipped the
  one check that mattered, and wrote down that he had skipped it.
- **Evenness hides error.** Teague: "A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is
  furniture." The constancy of the gap — flat across seasons, wet years and dry — is simultaneously
  the strongest evidence against every biological explanation and the reason nobody investigated.
- **Article VII's last clause** made the station's test unappealable, so a defective pipette became
  law for twenty-six years.
- **Three explanations were argued at the hearing and none accepted; the true one was never
  argued** — the crate was never mentioned.
- **Proportional, not fixed:** the single analytic key. It explains why the best milk lost most,
  why "5%" and "5¼%" both circulate, and why the gap never varied with feed or season.
- **Two natural experiments existed in the record all along:** the 1896 season at 3.80 (before the
  glass) and the nine weeks of 1911 at 3.80 (without the glass). Both are on the same summary leaf
  Keddie made up in 1922 and handed to everyone.
- **The cost fell on people, not just money:** two families who ate Sunday dinner together never
  spoke again; a letter kept forty years pinned inside a trunk lid; an old man dismissed four days
  before he was cleared, dead twenty-four years before anyone said so out loud.
