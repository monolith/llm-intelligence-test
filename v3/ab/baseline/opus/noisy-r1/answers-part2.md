### Story 5. Station Gain: Seventy-Six Thousand Pounds in Plain Sight (1897–1939)

**Who.** Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper; Bertram Kyle, clerk; Hobart Sill, buttermaker; Dell Rundle.

The station weighed a patron's milk, tested a sample, separated, sent the cream down to Larrow
Green and gave the skim back for his hogs. So there were two measurements of the same fat: what
the station credited to patrons on its own test, and what actually arrived in the cream and was
weighed into the creamery's vats. **The two were never the same. The second was always the
larger.**

Teague built a ledger line for the difference — "a piece of ordinary bookkeeping, invented at that
desk by the man sitting at it, because a difference that will not be entered somewhere will come
back and bite you at audit." The 1898 entry, quoted identically by Teague, Nym and Rundle:
*"Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at
Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as
usual. Carried to the general account."*

3,040 lb a year × 25 years = **76,000 lb of butterfat**, worth about **$19,000**, entered year by
year in the Association's own books as a gain. Teague on why nobody looked: "the words 'as usual'
got into the entry and stayed there... A figure that jumps about will keep you awake. A figure
that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture." Kyle, who copied the line for
twenty-five years in a fair hand: "It had a name. That is the thing about a name. A figure with a
name on it has been explained, and you rule under it and go on to the next."

At the far end of the road the same fact was handled physically every day. Sill weighed the
Hollow's cream in over again himself, forty-one years, "because I have never in my life taken
another man's figure for what was going into my churn": "There was more fat in the cans than the
papers said had been bought up there... Always more. Never less." He explained it away — "that is
cream for you" — and added, "I never made anything of it, and I will say plain, nobody ever asked
me to."

This is the standing disproof of theft, and it was in the books the whole time. Rundle found it by
accident in 1939 while his jubilee pamphlet was in type, having printed in Chapter IV that the
valley's settled opinion was that cream left the station by the back door. His press note:
"**Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy,
every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand.**" He left the accusation standing and printed
the correction at the back, and reprinted both unaltered in 1959.

Kyle's moral: "It was not a secret. It was worse than a secret. It was in plain sight with a name
on it."

**Documents:** the annual ledgers, station account (the station column runs from 1897); Sill's
receiving and churn books, his hand on every page from 1893; Rundle's press note.

### Story 6. The Circular, and Two Kitchens That Were One (1919–1929)

**Who.** Selby Vose; Ansel and Orra Keddie; Emmet and Roy Brant; nine (or eleven) families.

In 1919 the manager sent a printed circular over his own signature to the patrons at the head of
the valley warning that added water would be looked for and dealt with. Vose in 1926: "It named
nobody. It accused nobody... A manager who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and
says nothing to the men who make them is not being kind to them; he is being idle at their
expense. I would write every word of it again tomorrow." Strawn had tested for water with the
lactometer at that station on every visit and never found any, and told the manager so **in 1919**.

Orra Keddie's diary gives what no other source has. Ansel "would not put them in the mail nor
leave them on the platform. He took them round himself... and stood at the door while each man
read it, and came home at eleven at night with his hat still on." Two days later Emmet Brant came
up, would not come in, stood in the yard, said he had hauled whole milk to that station since the
day it opened and Ansel knew it better than any man living, and asked why he had carried the paper
himself if he did not believe it. "A. said he carried it because he would not have another man
carry it. He went home. He has not been in this house since, and he is my brother."

Roy Brant, independently: the cause of the silence was not the water charge and not a quarrel —
it was that "the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and
did not." Before 1919 the two families ate Sunday dinner together, two houses in one kitchen, the
men at the fence after. After it they never spoke again — not at the store, not at a funeral —
living within sight of each other's chimney smoke, for ten years, until Emmet died in **1929**.
Orra: "Ten years and neither of them would go up the road first, and now it is settled the way
those things get settled."

Vira Toland was seven: her father read his letter at the table with his hat on, which he never
did, and read it twice; her mother stood at the stove and did not turn round. One woman kept hers
pinned inside the lid of her trunk. Hazel Brant's mother read hers twice and put it in the stove.
Rundle taught the children of both ends of the valley in one schoolroom and watched the quarrel
come down to them entire, unexplained, "the way a debt does."

The count of letters is contested: **nine families** (Vira, Roy Brant, Hazel Brant) against
**eleven** (Orra's contemporaneous entry, written from the man who delivered them). Nine fits six
Hessel Bottom farms plus Strawn's "three places on the hill."

**Documents:** the circular itself (never quoted verbatim anywhere); Orra's diary, June 1919;
Strawn's 1919 circuit entry; Roy Brant's account; Rundle's trouble chapter.

### Story 7. Condemned in 1923, Ruined in 1925 (1923–1934)

**Who.** Merle Strawn; the board of arbitration; Ansel Keddie.

The state began issuing **burettes** to its field men in 1921. Strawn's came in the spring in a
wooden case with a printed sheet of directions. In 1922 he did not run the station's glass against
it — "the day being short and the road home being what it was, and I have put that down because it
is what happened." On **12 June 1923** he did: *"Ran their milk pipettes against the burette. These
measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the
manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them."* The crate went
up the ladder into the loft over the intake room. The order went to Tarnet on 28 June; Ammon &
Sons acknowledged 2 July; new glass was in use in July; in October Strawn ran the new pipettes and
wrote, "They deliver their mark." Kyle, in the office: "There was no more said of it in the office
than there would have been of a broken window." **In 1924 the station's average stood at 3.80.**

In January 1925 the patrons petitioned — "in the winter, when a farmer has time to be angry." A
board of three sat under Alonzo Frick, chairman "because I was the one of the three who kept no
cow": first session at Ashlin **17 March**, then two more at Frick's request in the station's own
weigh room, "so that the board might see the plant it was to pass upon instead of hearing it
described by men with an interest in the description." Cleve Oram stood outside on the platform in
the rain and could hear the voices but not the words. Three explanations were urged — water by
Vose, theft by Teague by insinuation, the hay by way of the 1908 report read into the record — and
none was proved. Four exhibits only: the station test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's
weigh book, and Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook. **The crate was never mentioned in any of the
three sessions**, and it was twenty feet above the room in two of them.

Keddie's nine-sentence statement stands entire: "I have tested as I was taught and as the state
man saw me do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, and I cannot tell you why the
figures are what they are. I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station."

He was put out on **30 April 1925**, four days before the award. On **4 May** the board found "no
cause established" and awarded the patrons **$3,800, without admission**, charged to the station
account. Frick: "There was no finding against him from us and there never was one... He was simply
let go while the board was still sitting, and I have never been able to think well of the
Association for it." And: "a man who cannot account for a figure is presumed by everybody in the
room to be the figure's author."

Orra, 30 April: "Twenty-nine seasons and a letter. He came up the road at his ordinary hour with
his apron rolled under his arm and hung it behind the shed door and asked what there was for
supper. There was ham. He ate it." He did carpentry after — a barn door that swung true for thirty
years — and worked at the cow shed "from light to dark and whistles, which he never did when he
was paid." He died in **February 1934**. "Men shook my hand who had not spoken to him in nine
years, and every one of them said what a hand he was with a door. Not one of them said the other
thing... He said to me once and only once that a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will
name."

**Documents:** Strawn's 1923 notebook (Exhibit 4); the order book and the Ammon letter; the
arbitration minute book and the award; Keddie's statement; Frick's exhibit list; Orra's diary.

### Story 8. The Crate in the Loft (1958–1972)

**Who.** Ivy Keddie, Ansel's granddaughter, born 1931, graduate student at Farland.

In **February 1958** she went up the Hollow to the shut station, up a ladder nailed to the wall,
and found under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff a deal crate stenciled AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY
TARNET, packed in straw. Six dozen had been bought; nine survived. Cleve Oram, who had put his
hand on that crate a hundred times after wire and hay forks, explains the attrition: "a thing in a
loft for thirty-five years gets broken by everybody who goes up after wire."

On **9 March 1958** she measured them at Farland against a calibrated burette, five deliveries
each: mean **16.72 c.c.** against a blown 17.6 — short 0.88, "95 hundredths of the mark, and the
mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for." Every test the station ever made on that glass
read 95% of the truth. 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80.

She assembled five sets of records that had never been in one room: the station test book; the
Brant weigh book (lent by Roy Brant, and *lent* — Hazel insists on the word, and so does Duncan);
the Alder Corners figures copied out by Rosalie Cudd; Strawn's notebooks, at Farland since 1947,
"eleven years in which nobody put a question to them"; and the Association's ledgers. Her framing:
"nothing I am going to show you tonight depends in the smallest degree on my opinion of my
grandfather. It depends on nine pieces of glass and a burette."

**Bulletin No. 471** (October 1958) closes: *"No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was
stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were
low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the
difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own
ledgers, year by year, as station gain."* Vaught's body text contradicts the conclusion he signed
throughout, having transposed 16.72 into 16.27 and propagated it (0.9244, 3.905, 0.295, 118,000
lb) — and he notices in print that his figure "is not readily reconciled with the station's own
average of 3.80" and publishes anyway.

The retellings then multiply the errors. The *Ashlin Chronicle* (3 Oct 1958) got the shape right
and the details wrong: "great-niece," eleven pipettes, 16.27, $19,600, $77.39, the 1908 committee
"sitting again" in 1925, and a fraud charge against Ammon & Sons. Falke's county report (1959)
repeated the great-niece, then verified and corrected it. Rundle added one footnote to his 1959
reprint and declined to rewrite the pamphlet. Ivy gave her talk to the Historical Society in 1959;
the test book and crate went to the Historical Rooms that year, the weigh book by way of Hazel
Brant in 1962–63, the Alder Corners book to Vessey County in 1970, and Nym catalogued them in
1972. Duncan's 1970 postscript: "the five books are now in three buildings and are none of them
ours."

The lag: glass condemned 1923, cause found 1958 — thirty-five years; dismissed 1925 — thirty-three;
dead since 1934 — twenty-four. Hazel Brant: "A thing you could have settled in an afternoon with a
burette and a straight face, and it took fifty years and a girl from the college who thought to
climb a ladder."

**Documents:** the measurement sheet of 9 March 1958; Bulletin No. 471; Nym's accession notes;
Duncan Keddie's list of the books; the *Chronicle*; Falke's report.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** Duncan Keddie's 1968 family notes settle it
from inside the household: "I am Duncan, born in 1894, their son... My daughter is Ivy, born in
1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already
twice seen it printed otherwise." Ivy herself says "my grandfather" throughout her 1959 talk and
dates the lag as "he died in 1934 and had been dead twenty-four years when the sheet was read that
clears him." Against this stand the *Ashlin Chronicle* ("Miss Keddie is his great-niece") and
Falke's report ¶2 ("the great-niece of the tester") — but these are **not two sources**: both were
written within months of the same college press release, the *Chronicle* admittedly "put together
in a day and a half from a college press release and two telephone calls," and Falke, having
written to Farland, appended a correction: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is
the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." Method: a participant's
own genealogy plus a source that checked and retracted, against a single derived error printed
twice.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins.** The derivation is given identically by
both families. Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie were brother and sister; Emmet's son Roy and Orra's son
Duncan are therefore first cousins, "the children of a brother and a sister, which is the whole
definition"; Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy, being the children of first cousins,
are second cousins. Roy Brant explained it twice, Duncan sets it out to be copied, and Hazel says
it herself: "People are forever telling me she is my niece or my cousin once removed... It is not
complicated; it is only that nobody wants to count." Rundle's 1959 footnote agrees. Vira Toland's
"first cousin once removed" and Cleve Oram's repetition of the same phrase are the valley's
version, and they are not independent of each other in any way that matters — neither can state
the line.

**What the sources leave open in the intervening generations:** (i) whether Orra was Emmet's
**full** sister or half sister. Roy Brant says full — "same mother and same father, whatever you
may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it" — and Duncan says "Emmet Brant's
sister, the younger of the two," and Orra herself writes "my brother Emmet" without qualification;
but Hazel Brant, Roy's own daughter, gives the stepmother version explicitly ("a half sister to my
grandfather Emmet, by his father's second wife"). I resolve for full siblings — Roy rebuts the
story by name, and Orra's son is a generation nearer — but note that half-blood would not alter
the degree of cousinship either way. (ii) Orra's birth year: the diary's preface and her own
"I am sixty-four" in August 1930 give **1866**; Duncan writes 1868 and then refutes himself by
saying she was eighty-two when she died in 1948.

**B3. Selby Vose was Gideon Vose's son.** Selby says so from the platform in 1926 — "My father, who
was your first president and who wrote the by-law under which every patron in this Association is
paid on the test of the station where he delivers" — and Loomis, Duncan Keddie and Bertram Kyle
all say son; Kyle adds, "the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story
twice in the last year and it is nonsense." Vira and Rundle carry the nephew version, Rundle even
attributing to Selby a saying about "his uncle" — the valley's version, twice attested and wrong.

**Why it matters:** the by-law that made the station's test unappealable and the manager who
enforced its consequences were father and son. Selby signed the annual station sheet carrying the
station-gain line every year, told Teague in 1916 to let the question alone, sent the 1919 water
circular, put the watering theory to the board in 1925 "without heat and without proof," and in
1926 refused the apparatus explanation as "a piece of housekeeping." His proof that the valley
could make rich milk was his father's Jersey herd testing 4.56 in 1910 — a herd at the home place
near Larrow Green, the one place the short glass never reached.

**B4. There was no relationship between the Voses and the Keddies that the sources establish.**
Two narrators assert one: Vira Toland says Orra Keddie was first cousin to old Mrs. Vose —
"everybody knew that and nobody wrote it down" — and Cleve Oram says "the Keddies and the Voses
were cousins of some sort... I could not draw you the line on paper. My mother had it from her
mother." Both are hearsay and neither can state the connection. Against them Duncan Keddie devotes
a numbered section to denying it: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses.
People confused them with the Ashlin Voses... My mother would have told me. She told me a great
deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone
without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it."

Method: a specific, sourced denial by the son of the woman in question, with an explanation of how
the confusion arose, outranks two unspecified traditions. Strictly a negative cannot be proved, so
I record it as: no kinship established, and the affirmative claims are valley talk. It matters
that the *belief* was live, though — Vira's account of why 1919 cut so deep depends on it: "it was
somebody's cousin saying it, and that is a different thing altogether in a place this size."
