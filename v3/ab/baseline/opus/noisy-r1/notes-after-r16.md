## THE CASE IN ONE PARAGRAPH

The Ostrey Hollow skimming station of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association tested
its patrons' milk low by exactly one part in twenty for twenty-six years, because its milk-test
pipettes, bought new in 1897, delivered 16.72 c.c. instead of the 17.6 c.c. they were blown for.
The recorded station average was 3.61 where the true average was 3.80. The shortfall — 76,000 lb
of butterfat, worth about $19,000 — was entered year by year in the Association's own ledgers as
"station gain." The glass was condemned by the state dairy inspector on 12 June 1923; the tester
Ansel Keddie was dismissed in 1925 after an arbitration that awarded the patrons $3,800 "without
admission"; the true cause was found by Ivy Keddie in 1958 and published in Bulletin No. 471
(October 1958). No milk was watered and no cream was stolen.

## PEOPLE AND RELATIONSHIPS

- **Ansel Keddie** — tester at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station. Kept the station test book in
  his own hand for **twenty-nine seasons, 1896 through 1924**; did not test a pound in 1925.
  Per r01 he went up in **1895**, the year before the station opened, to get it ready, and tested
  that first summer too. Put out / dismissed **30 April 1925** ("I was put out on the thirtieth of
  April"). **Died in the thirties** (r01) / **died 1934** (r04: "He died in 1934 and had been dead
  twenty-four years when the sheet was read that clears him"). Still under the cloud when he died.
  "I am an old man and I have no other trade." Frick (r13) believed him then and in 1946, and adds
  that Keddie privately blamed the patrons (see conflicts).
- **Orra Keddie** — Ansel's wife. **Emmet Brant's sister** — and per r09 **full brother and
  sister, same mother and same father**, "whatever you may hear from people who like a story with
  a stepmother in it." Also **first cousin to old Mrs. Vose** (r01 only: "everybody knew that and
  nobody wrote it down"). Outlived Ansel "a good while" and bore the valley's opinion the whole
  time. Would walk down to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane.
- **Emmet Brant** — **born 1854, died 1929** (r09). Biggest man on Hessel Bottom; most cows, most
  hay ground; **the station's largest patron in every year of which there is a record** (r02);
  **60-odd acres of the meadow and thirty-eight cows in a good year** (r09). Orra's brother, so
  Ansel Keddie's brother-in-law. Kept the farm weigh book from **1 Jan 1898** (his own son) /
  **1 Jan 1897** (Nym, Frick) until the week before he died. One of the first men to say out loud
  in **1902** that the Hollow tested low, and was laughed at. The 1919 letter "cost him the most."
  Before 1919 the two families ate Sunday dinners together, the two houses in one kitchen, men at
  the fence after; **after 1919 they never spoke again — not at the store, not at a funeral** —
  though they lived within sight of each other's chimney smoke. **r09 gives the reason: not the
  water charge itself, but that "the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of
  that circular and did not."** Read the 1908 report twice, put it in the drawer, and said "the
  land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not."
- **Roy Brant** — Emmet's son; **sixty-seven in the winter of 1955 and still milking** (b. ~1888).
  Kept up the weigh book after his father died; the book came **to Roy Brant in 1929**; he lent it
  to Ivy Keddie in 1958 and it was returned. Never hauled over the ridge: "We had the biggest herd
  on the meadow, and the biggest herd is the one that cannot afford to be talked about, and I have
  thought since that this was a poor reason and cost us money." **First cousin to Duncan Keddie.**
- **Hazel Brant** — Roy's daughter. The weigh book was always to go to her; passed **to Miss Hazel
  Brant in 1962**; received by the Historical Rooms 1963. **Second cousin to Ivy Keddie** (r09,
  r12 footnote) — not "first cousin once removed" (r01).
- **Duncan Keddie** — Ansel's son, Ivy's father; **taught school at Ashlin**; Roy Brant's first
  cousin.
- **Ivy Keddie** — Ansel's granddaughter; graduate student of the **State Agricultural College at

## PLACES

- **Larrow Valley** — the valley; the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association.
- **Larrow Green** — the main creamery, built **1891 on Larrow Creek, below the mill**; the
  Association organized there in 1889 in the schoolhouse. Sill's churn room had no floor but plank
  in 1893 and cooled with creek ice out of the pond.
- **Ashlin** — the Association office (one room over a harness shop, cold at the north end and
  never cold enough at the south; sloped desk with a brass rail); the merchants the valley hauled
  butter to before 1889; site of the first arbitration session; where Duncan Keddie taught school
  and Kepp practiced; the Ordell County Historical Rooms are there. "There are still lawyers in
  Ashlin."
- **Ostrey Hollow** — hamlet and skimming station, **seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green**;
  in the nineties the pull down was **three hours in mud and could not be done at all in March**.
  Low building, boarded and battened, whitewashed, cold as a spring house even in August, smelled
  of milk and steam and hot iron; the **intake room at the road end** and a **loft over it reached
  by a ladder**, which Rundle believes "has never been cleared out since it was put up." Behind it
  the creek, which in a wet April **comes up to the sill and has twice come over it**. After
  closing, storage — fence wire and salt.
- **Hessel Bottom** — the wet meadow below the hamlet; **a hundred and sixty acres** (r09);
  **six farms** had rights on it (r09, the 1908 report itself, Kepp) — Loomis says seven. Springs
  come out at the foot of the slope and run the year round; water stands on the low end into May;
  a soft place by the alders "that has taken a horse to the hock in my time and my father's."
  Herbage sedge, rush, coarse sour tussock grasses; hay cut late and heavy, the winter dependence
  from November to April. Emmet Brant's was the largest of the farms.
- **Redlow Ridge** — between the valleys: **four miles of switchback, cut for a lumber road and
  never much improved. Shuts in November and does not open before April, some years May; the
  drifts sit in the cut below the top and stay after everything else is bare.** No Ordell man ever
  hauled over it in winter — "It was not possible."
- **Alder Corners, Vessey County** — the rival cheese factory, on the flat below the church.
- **Farland** — seat of the State Agricultural College; its library holds Strawn's notebooks.
- **Ordell County** — the county; Historical Society / Historical Rooms; the Farm Club; the Bar
  Association.
- **Tarnet** — home of Ammon & Sons Dairy Supply.

## CHRONOLOGY

- **1889** — farmers meet at Larrow Green (in the schoolhouse, cold weather, went on very late,
  somebody's horse got loose) and organize the Association; Gideon Vose elected first president.
- **1889–1892** — minute book covering these years; **lost in the office move of 1938**.
- **1891** — main creamery built at Larrow Green, on the creek below the mill; the churn set up;
  the valley paid in cash once a month for the first time. Rundle dates **Article VII to 1891**.
- **1892** — Article VII adopted at the annual meeting, the year after the creamery opened
  (Loomis). Grigg instead says payment by test "came in with the **1893** meeting."
- **1893** — Hobart Sill comes to Larrow Green as buttermaker.
- **1895** — Ansel Keddie goes up to the station to get it ready (r01).
- **1896, May** — the station opens; **a crowd went up for it**. Its own outfit was ordered and had
  not come; there was talk of holding off the whole season; **Sill made up an outfit out of his own
  glass off his own bench and sent it up the valley on the wagon**, and they opened on it.
- **1896, 4 May** — station test book begun. "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes."
- **1896, May to end of year** — **about 900,000 lb of milk taken in** (Sill) — the only figure
  anywhere for the first season, which the ledgers omit. The summary leaf records the year at
  **3.80**.
- **1897, spring** — the station's own glass arrives; **Sill's glass is sent back to him, one piece
  chipped, and he never said a word about it.**
- **1897, 1 January** — the farm weigh book at Hessel Bottom begun (Nym Item 4; Frick Exhibit 3).
  Roy Brant says **1 January 1898**.
- **1897, 14 April** — Ammon & Sons invoice for the station outfit, $77.93.
- **1897, 1 May** — the station's own (short) pipettes go into use; Bulletin 471's conclusion dates
  the low tests "from the first of May, 1897." (Vaught's body text says 1 May **1898**.)
- **1898** — first full delivery year; recorded average settles at 3.61; first quoted station-gain
  ledger entry.
- **1898–1922** — the twenty-five full delivery years of the claim: 40,000,000 lb of milk.
- **1901** — Effie Loomis becomes secretary.
- **1902** — complaints begin. Sill remembers "a man in here shouting that his milk tested under his
  brother-in-law's down the creek and it was the same feed and the same water and what did we mean
  by it." Emmet Brant was one of the first to say it out loud and was laughed at.
- **1905** — Wilbur Teague comes in as bookkeeper; begins the station-gain line.
- **1907** — Strawn takes the Ordell circuit; first round; Ostrey Hollow already open.
- **1908, January** — the investigating committee appointed, three men, Nyle Grigg chairman.
- **1908 (Jan–Mar)** — the committee samples the Hessel Bottom herds and reports; the wet hay thins
  the cream and the remedy lies with the land. Received and accepted; "the question was closed by
  it for eleven years."
- **1909** — Strawn at Ostrey Hollow in the fall; notes the station takes from the wet bottom below
  and **three places on the hill**.
- **1910** — the weigh-book leaf: 200,000 lb delivered, paid on a test of 3.42; **butterfat that
  year was twenty-four cents** (r09). Vose's Jersey herd tests 4.56.
- **1911** — Gideon Vose's presidency ends. **Bulletin No. 214 published** (the College's feeding
  trial on bog hay). **8 May: the station's pipette breaks; a borrowed measure is used; tests rise
  to 3.80. 10 July: their own measure back in use; tests fall the same day.** Strawn visits in June
  on his way over to the other side of the county and notes "nothing here to note."
- **1912** — Strawn at Ostrey Hollow; creek out over the flat road.
- **1913** — Selby Vose becomes manager. **Jerome Cudd builds the Alder Corners factory** (r10).
- **1914** — the Alder Corners Cheese Company opens (r12).
- **1915** — Strawn orders the wash room floor graded. **Four Hessel Bottom men begin hauling over
  Redlow Ridge to Alder Corners in the summer flush** (about 1 June to mid-September), returning to
  Ostrey Hollow the rest of the year.
- **1916** — Strawn: "the tester complains that his patrons blame him for their figures." Teague
  puts the station-gain question to Vose and is told to let it alone.
- **1917** — Gideon Vose dies. Strawn: a bad year for help, two testers doing the work of three.
- **1919** — **the circular about water in the cans** goes to nine families. Strawn tests for water
  and finds none, and tells the manager so. **Grigg's 1908 report is reprinted with his covering
  letter**, the question "having been reopened this season by men who were boys when we made it."
  Jerome Cudd writes his 1919 season note at Alder Corners.
- **1920** — Strawn visits twice; cold room short of ice in the fall.
- **1921** — the state begins issuing **burettes** to field men, graduated, in a wooden case.
  Strawn's arrives in the spring with a printed sheet of directions.
- **1922** — Keddie makes up the summary leaf at the back of his book. Strawn **does not** run the
  glass against the burette — "the day being short and the road home being what it was, and I have
  put that down because it is what happened."
- **1923, 12 June** — Strawn runs the pipettes against the burette and **condemns the whole crate**;
  tells the manager to order new. The crate is carried up to the loft.
- **1923, 28 June** — the Association's order for replacement pipettes and bottles.
- **1923, 2 July** — Ammon & Sons letter acknowledging the order, promising shipment that month,
  **declining to say anything of the earlier lot**.
- **1923, July** — new glass in use.
- **1923, October** — Strawn runs the new pipettes against the burette: "They deliver their mark."
- **1924** — tests improved; the book gives the year at **3.80**.
- **1925, January** — the Ostrey Hollow patrons petition the Association.
- **1925, 17 March** — first session of the board of arbitration, at the Association's office at
  Ashlin. Keddie's statement. Test book impounded.
- **1925, spring** — **second and third sessions held at Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's own
  weigh room, at Frick's request**; the board sees the scales tried and a test made.
- **1925, 30 April** — Ansel Keddie put out, four days before the award, while the board was still
  sitting.
- **1925, 4 May** — the award recorded: no cause established; $3,800 without admission.
- **1925, fall** — Strawn: "New man at the tester's bench this year. He is quick, and quick is not
  the thing I look for."
- **1926** — the burette program discontinued. Selby Vose gives his annual address. **Kepp reads
  "The bog meadows and the milk" to the Ordell County Farm Club.**
- **1928** — Strawn tells them a loft over a milk room is a place for nothing but air, and is told
  it has been full "since before the war."
- **1929** — Emmet Brant dies; the farm weigh book passes to Roy Brant.
- **1930** — **the last of the sixteen Alder Corners summers.**
- **1931** — bad ice year; every plant in the four counties short, two buying from the railroad.
- **1934** — Ansel Keddie dies. **Hobart Sill retires from the churn** and gives his remarks.
  Strawn notes a separator bearing run hot; "the tester's book is kept up, though not in the hand
  it used to be kept in."
- **1936, March** — Strawn's last round; the books go to the state dairy office.
- **1938** — the office move in which the 1889–1892 minute book is lost. Selby Vose's management
  ends.
- **1939** — **Rundle's jubilee pamphlet**, with the retraction added while in press.
- **1940** — Effie Loomis lays down the minute book.
- **1941, March** — **Jerome Cudd dies**; Rosalie takes the factory over that spring. Loomis writes
  her notes.
- **1945** — Teague gives up the desk.
- **1946** — **Frick's bar-association article.**
- **1947** — Strawn's notebooks go from the state dairy office to the Farland college library.
- **1948** — Teague writes Chapter Nine at Ashlin.
- **1955, winter** — **Roy Brant writes his account for the farm bureau.**
- **1958, February** — Ivy Keddie finds the crate in the station loft.
- **1958, 9 March** — she measures the nine pipettes at Farland against a calibrated burette.
- **1958** — **Rosalie Cudd's letter**; Strawn's 1923 volume found and copied at Farland; Roy Brant
  lends the weigh book (his 1958 postscript); Vira Toland visited.
- **1958, October** — **Bulletin No. 471** issued.
- **1959** — Ivy's talk to the Ordell County Historical Society; the test book and the crate come to
  the Historical Rooms. **Rundle's pamphlet reprinted with its one added footnote.**
- **1962/1963** — weigh book to Hazel Brant, then to the Rooms.
- **1970** — the Alder Corners test book to the Vessey County Historical Society.
- **1972** — Nym's accession notes.
- **1979** — Vira Toland's tape.

## DOCUMENTS QUOTED (verbatim)

**1. Article VII of the by-laws** (quoted identically by Loomis r02, Nym r07 Item 1, and Rundle
r12 — three times, word for word):
> *Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of
> pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of
> the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*

Loomis's gloss: before it, a man was paid for what his cans weighed, "and a hundred pounds of milk
was a hundred pounds of milk whosever cow it came out of." The last clause — *the test of the
station where he delivers shall govern* — is "the one nobody quotes" and "the hinge of the whole
Ostrey Hollow business." **No appeal from the station's test.** Nym: the printed copies **carry no
year on their face, only the words "adopted at the annual meeting."** Rundle: "It was thought a
hard rule at the time and there were men who left the Association over it. It is a just rule... the
pound of milk is a liar where the pound of butterfat is not." And: "Gideon Vose put that in for
convenience and it was convenient for thirty years, and it is the sentence on which the whole
trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned." Frick: "it makes the test the whole of the
bargain, and it leaves a dissatisfied man with no remedy on earth except to quarrel with a figure."

**2. The Ammon & Sons invoice, 14 April 1897** (r07 Item 2):
> *Sold to the Larrow Valley Co-op. Creamery Assn., for the Ostrey Hollow station:*
> *6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20*
> *12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at .22 — 31.68*
> *1 acid measure — .90*
> *1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00*
> *Freight — 2.15*
> *Total — 77.93*

**3. The test book's first leaf** (Keddie r03, Nym r07):
> *Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until
> our own comes. A. Keddie.*

**4. The 1922 summary leaf** (r03, r04, r07 — identical all three times):
> *Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61
> or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in
> their place, when it stood at 3.80.*

**5. The two 1911 entries** (r03):
> *May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure.*

> *July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.*

Keddie: "That question mark is mine and I have let it stand for fourteen years."

**6. The station-gain ledger entry for 1898** (Teague r05, Nym r07 Item 6, **Rundle r12's press
note** — identical all three times):
> *Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received
> at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per
> cent, as usual. Carried to the general account.*

**7. The award of the board of arbitration, 4 May 1925** (Loomis r02, Nym r07 Item 7):
> *The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and
> appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow
> patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to
> the station account.*

Loomis: "*Finds no cause established*, and then an award of thirty-eight hundred dollars: I took it
down as it was given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be
true at once." She flags "the books and appliances of the station" as **"a form of words the board
used and not a list of anything."** Frick: "An award without a finding is an unsatisfactory thing
and every member of that board knew it."

**8. Keddie's statement to the board, 17 March 1925 — nine sentences, entire** (r03):
> I have kept the test book of this station in my own hand since the station opened, and there is
> not a figure in it that I did not put there. I have set down the date, the patron, the pounds,
> the test and the fat, and where I have made an error I have drawn a line through it and put my
> letters against the line, and I have never written a figure over a figure. I have made the tests
> in the same order and at the same hour of every working day of every season. I have heard what

## THE ARITHMETIC (Ivy Keddie, r04 — the correct version)

- Pipettes blown for **17.6 c.c.**, delivering a mean **16.72 c.c.**, short **0.88 c.c.**
- 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95 exactly.** Every test read **95% of the truth.**
- 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80.** Gap = **0.19 of a point.**
- **0.19 is 5% of 3.80** — the loss was **a proportion, not a fixed quantity**: "The station did
  not lose a set number of points. It lost a twentieth of whatever was there."
- 1898–1922, 25 full years: **40,000,000 lb of milk**, avg **1,600,000 lb/year**.
  - At recorded 3.61%: **1,444,000 lb of fat credited.**
  - At true 3.80%: **1,520,000 lb actually delivered.**
  - Difference: **76,000 lb of butterfat.**
- Butterfat averaged **25 cents a pound** over the period → **$19,000**.
- The award was **$3,800 = one fifth of it.**
- **The two percentages:** 76,000 is **5% of the fat delivered** (1,520,000) and **5¼% of the fat
  credited** (1,444,000). The ledger line uses the second, "because a bookkeeper naturally reckons
  a surplus against the figure he has already entered." Neither is an error — "But five percent is
  the one that is also the glass." Nym: "five and a quarter percent of the credited fat is five
  percent of the fat delivered, and each is right of its own denominator and of no other."
- **Because the error was proportional it fell hardest on the best milk:** a herd recorded at
  **4.56 was short 0.24**; a herd recorded at **3.42 was short 0.18**. "The richer the milk, the
  more was taken from it, and no man had any way of knowing that his good cows cost him more than
  his poor ones."
- Check figures: 3.61% of 1,600,000 = 57,760 lb; 3.80% of 1,600,000 = 60,800 lb; difference 3,040
  lb — exactly the 1898 ledger entry. 3,040 × 25 years = 76,000.

### Vaught's parallel arithmetic (r11 §V) — the same method on transposed numbers
- 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = **0.9244**; 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = **3.905**; shortfall **0.295**; on 40,000,000 lb over
  the twenty-five years, **118,000 lb** of butterfat delivered and not credited.
- He then writes: "**It should be said that this figure is not readily reconciled with the
  station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the glassware.**" He
  notices the contradiction, records it, and leaves it standing — while the bulletin's own closing
  paragraph, which he signs, gives 16.72's answers (one part in twenty, 76,000 lb).
- He also states "**The gain is five and a quarter percent of the butterfat actually delivered**" —
  the same wrong denominator Teague used, and the one Ivy and Nym both correct.

### The 1911 natural experiment
Nine weeks, 8 May – 10 July 1911. Ivy: "Same patrons, same herds, same pasture, same season, same
acid, same bottles, same man at the crank — **one object changed** — and the average went to 3.80
and came back to 3.61 the day the station's own measure returned." Vaught puts it above all his own
arithmetic (§IV, "The interval of 1911, which is worth more than the arithmetic"): "The patrons
were the same patrons; the herds were the same herds; the pasture, the season, the acid, the
bottles, the tester and the tester's habits were all unaltered. **One variable changed.**... This is
recorded contemporaneously, in the operator's hand, by a man who did not know what he was recording
and set a question mark against it. I have spent my working life urging students to prefer an
experiment to a calculation. Here is one that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could
have bettered, and **lay unread for forty-seven years**. It disposes of every explanation that looks
to the herds, to the ground or to the men, for the sufficient reason that none of those was altered
in those nine weeks, and the figures were." Roy Brant (r09), from the farm side: "Through those nine
weeks our tests came up, all of us, every farm on the Bottom; and then the station's own came back
into use and the figures went down again to where they had been. My father noticed the rise and he
noticed the fall and he entered both, and he could make nothing of it, and I could make nothing of
it either, and there it sat for near on fifty years."

### Why the wet-hay explanation fails
Bulletin 214: bog hay depresses the test by **not more than 0.05 in winter and nothing measurable
on summer pasture**, against a gap standing flat in every month of every year. Ivy: "It is four
times too small and it is the wrong shape." Vaught: "some six times the largest effect the trial
could produce under conditions harsher than any farmer would impose" (six, because he is working
from his inflated 0.295). Keddie independently: "They did not come and go with the season. The gap
was there in July as it was there in February, in the flush of the grass and in the dead of the
winter on dry hay, and it was the same in a wet year as in a dry one." Roy Brant independently:
"it was seventeen hundredths in July and it was seventeen hundredths in February. It did not come
and go with the season. It did not care whether the cows were on the meadow or on hay in the barn.
Nobody would take account of that, then or since, though it was in front of them."

### The cross-check over the ridge
Alder Corners tested the milk of **four Hessel Bottom men** in their summer flush; Ivy says that
where the two books overlap, **fourteen seasons of them**, that same milk ran **about 0.19 higher at
Alder Corners than at home**. Rosalie's own table gives **sixteen seasons, 1915–1930, all between
3.78 and 3.82**. Corroborated independently by Nym Item 16 (the Larrow Green creamery average runs
"a little under two tenths of a point better" than the station's 3.61) and by Sill (r14), who says
Larrow Green's own patrons ran **3.78** one year with another right through those years.

### The lag
"The glass was condemned in 1923 and the cause was found in 1958: **thirty-five years.** My
grandfather was dismissed in 1925: **thirty-three years.** He died in 1934 and had been dead
**twenty-four years** when the sheet was read that clears him. I have no remedy to propose. I have
only the crate, and it is on the table, and you may look into it on your way out."

## WHY THE PIPETTE COULD NOT BE CAUGHT (Vaught, r11 — the analytic core)

The four operations of the test: a measured charge of milk drawn into a pipette and delivered into
a test bottle; sulfuric acid added, dissolving everything but the fat; the bottle spun in a hand
tester so the liberated fat rises into the graduated neck; the operator reads the fat off
**directly**, in percent.

"Notice the word *directly*. **The operator weighs nothing.** He reads the length of a column of fat
in a narrow neck and calls it a percentage, and he is entitled to do so for one reason only: the
charge of milk that went into that bottle is a fixed and known quantity. The maker rules the neck
for that quantity and no other. Every mark on it means 'this much fat, out of the standard charge.'"
The standard charge in this state, and on the glassware examined, is **17.6 c.c.** If the pipette
delivers less: less milk, less fat liberated, a shorter column, "and the neck, which knows nothing
of the substitution, is read off in the ordinary way. The reading is not merely low. **It is low in
the exact proportion by which the charge was short.**"

The three consequences, "which in fifty years of this history nobody appears to have held all three
in mind at once":

1. **The error is proportional and not additive.** It removes a fixed *fraction*. "Rich milk
   therefore loses more than poor milk, absolutely, and the ratio between any two tests made on the
   same short glass is undisturbed. **A station using such glass will show a perfectly orderly,
   internally consistent set of figures. Its herds will rank correctly against one another. Only
   against the outside world is it wrong.**"
2. **The error is precise.** "A leaking pipe or a slipping scale gives a scattered error, and
   scatter attracts attention. A pipette that delivers 16.27 c.c. where it is blown 17.6 delivers
   16.27 c.c. every time, for a quarter of a century, in January and in July, for a careful
   operator and a careless one. **It has the appearance of excellent work.**"
3. **The error is invisible from inside the plant.** "Every other element of the test can be
   verified against a standard. The scales can be checked with test weights. The tester's speed can
   be counted. The acid can be checked for strength. The bottle's graduation can be checked with
   mercury. But the pipette is not checked against anything, because **in the ordinary practice of
   the trade the pipette is the standard. It is what the plant measures with. An instrument that is
   itself the measure cannot be caught by measuring with it.** It can be caught only by a burette
   brought in from outside, and burettes reached the state's field men very late in this history."

His framing sentences: "The question has been answered at various times by reference to the land and
by reference to the men. **It is answered here by reference to a piece of glass.**" — "Nobody in that
valley lacked the intelligence to find it. What they lacked was any occasion to suspect the one
article in the plant that nobody in the trade is in the habit of suspecting." — "The extension
worker who reads no further should carry away this: **the milk pipette is the one instrument in the
butterfat test which the plant cannot check against anything else, and it is the instrument on which
every figure in the plant depends.**"

**The repeatable method** (§III): the pipette is filled to its mark with water at room temperature,
the tip wiped, the contents delivered in the ordinary working manner — the same interval of
drainage, the same touch of the tip to the wall of the receiver — into a burette of certified
graduation; five deliveries from each pipette, readings averaged. "The whole of it occupies an
afternoon and requires no apparatus a creamery does not own, save the burette. **There is no reason
why every station in this state should not be checked once in this way, and several reasons in this
history why it should be.**"

## KEDDIE'S METHOD (r03, the unsent letter)

- Day began at **half past five**; wagons came up the Hollow in the dark most of the year; intake
  room open, lamps lit, **scale swinging free** before the first wagon.
- Every can weighed, **the weight called and written before the milk went anywhere else**. "I have
  never in twenty-nine seasons had a patron dispute a weight with me and be right."
- A sample taken from each patron's milk as it was weighed, into **his own jar, numbered to him and
  to nobody else**; the jar **made up over ten days** before testing.
- On testing morning: jar warmed in water until **blood-warm right through**, then poured from
  vessel to vessel and poured again "until the cream that had risen was carried down through the
  whole of it. A sample that is not warmed and mixed is worth nothing and a boy could tell you so."
- **The charge:** milk drawn up in the measure **to the mark on the stem**, tip wiped, charge blown
  out into the bottle carrying the patron's number.
- **The acid**, poured down the side, bottle turned until the milk was dark.
- **Four bottles in the hand tester**, weighted against each other, cover down, **crank turned at
  the speed he was taught and no faster** — "no man ever stood beside me and made me hurry it."
- Fat rises in the neck in a clear column, **read off in per cent against the rulings with a pair
  of dividers**, and the figure written down.
- "That is the whole of it. It is not a hard trade. It is only a trade that must be done the same
  way every time, which is a harder thing than it sounds."
- **The book kept in five columns: date, patron, pounds, test, fat.** Errors crossed with initials
  against the crossing; never a figure written over a figure, "because a figure written over another
  figure is a figure no man can swear to afterward."
- Rate: **thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons.** Vira says he "tested a thousand cans a
  week and never a blot in that book."
- Vira on the book: ruled by his own hand with a straightedge, **every line the same height**,
  figures "like print"; he wrote standing at a **slope desk by the window**; if asked what a man's
  milk had done "he did not have to hunt — he turned to the day and he read it off." Her father: "if
  the rest of the world kept its accounts the way Ansel Keddie kept his there would be no lawyers in
  Ashlin."

## STRAWN'S METHOD AND HIS CIRCUIT (r06)

The four things, always in the same order "so that I would not have to remember which of them I had
done":
1. **The scales**, against the sealed weights he carried — both intake and cream scale, loaded
   light and loaded heavy.
2. **The lactometer** on the day's milk, patron by patron where there was any question, **for added
   water**.
3. **The boiler, water supply and cold room** — pressure, gauge, the cased well if there was one,
   and whether the room would hold overnight what they put in it.
4. **Standing at the tester's elbow** and watching a test through from the sample to the reading —
   "and I did not let him hurry it." The one most men grudged him.

His creed: "I put down what I found. I did not put down what I thought about it... The state pays a
field man to weigh and to look, and every time in my life I have seen a field man paid for an
opinion instead, the opinion has outlived the weighing and done harm after the man was dead." And:
"I do not keep an estimation, I keep a book... **A theory is a thing a man carries out to the
platform with him and it gets in the way of the weights.**"

Circuit detail: fifty-odd plants in four counties — creameries, cheese factories, skimming stations,
"one or two little places that made butter three days a week and called themselves a creamery"; each
seen at least once a year, the doubtful ones oftener. By train where there was one, by livery where
there was not, in the spring by neither. "A man on this circuit learns the plants by their smells
before he learns them by their names: the sour-sweet of a receiving platform in July, the lime and
steam of a wash room, **the flat cold nothing of a well-run cold room, which is the smell I liked
best because it meant I would have no letter to write.**"

Per-year Ostrey Hollow entries: 1907 (scales true, cold room fair, tester's hand better than most,
watched two tests, "works slow and clean and reads his own figure without being coaxed to it"); 1909
(fall; boiler safety tried; takes from the wet bottom below and **three places on the hill**; one man
cautioned about a rusted seam); 1911 (June, on his way over to the other side of the county,
"Nothing here to note"); 1912 (cream shipped down the valley the same day it is separated); 1913
(Larrow Green two days); 1915 (wash room floor ordered graded — "A field man who lets a floor go will
be shown a floor at every plant on his circuit inside of two years"); 1916; 1917 ("two testers doing
the work of three men, **which is where mistakes live**"); 1919; 1920 (twice); 1921 (burettes); 1922
(no burette check); 1923 June and October; 1924; 1925; 1926 ("Mine is in its case in my grip and has
been used twice since"); 1928; 1931; 1934; 1936 March (last round). **The 12 June 1923 entry is
prefaced by his own admission: "I had carried the burette two seasons before this check."**

## TEAGUE'S ACCOUNT OF THE LEDGER (r05)

- The Association was **three things on paper**: creamery at Larrow Green, office at Ashlin, station
  at Ostrey Hollow. "The station was the smallest of the three and it made the most noise."
- The station's business: patrons haul milk up rather than drive seven miles down; milk weighed in,
  sample tested, man paid on **his pounds and his test together**; the station separates, sends cream
  down, **gives the skim back to the farmer for his hogs and his calves**.
- **The station-gain column:** one side = butterfat credited to patrons on the station's own test;
  other side = butterfat that actually came down in the cream and was weighed and tested into the
  Larrow Green vats. "The two were never the same. **The second was always the larger.**"
- **The line was his own device, begun 1905** — "a piece of ordinary bookkeeping, invented at that
  desk by the man sitting at it, because a difference that will not be entered somewhere will come
  back and bite you at audit."
- On the evenness: "the words 'as usual' got into the entry and stayed there... **it stops him
  looking. A figure that jumps about will keep you awake. A figure that comes in at five and a
  quarter every year is furniture.**"
- **His theory, given to the board in 1925 and repeated in 1948:** "Cream is portable, and that
  station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons... One man weighed the milk, one man drew
  the sample, one man made the test, one man wrote the figure that the pay slip was made from, and
  one man ran the separator. **There was no second man in that building from the spring to the
  fall.** I do not say what any particular person did on any particular day... **The remedy is two
  men or a lock, and the Association had neither.**"
- **Vose knew:** "He signed the sheet every year... a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in
  front of him. What he made of it he never told me."
- **He raised it once, in 1916, and was told to let it alone.** "I was the Association's bookkeeper
  and not its conscience."
- "The patrons petitioned in the winter, **when a farmer has time to be angry**."

## VOSE'S DEFENCE (r08, 1926)

- Ordinary business of the year: wet season into June; the Larrow Green creamery **ran through the
  flush without a day lost**; butter held its place; ice went in early and lasted; the station "ran
  its season out and its cream came down to us daily and sweet."
- On the finding: "It found no cause established against the station, against the office, or against
  any officer or servant of this Association... **it is the only sentence in this business that was
  arrived at by men under oath with the books in front of them.**"
- On the award: "a generous settlement of an unproved claim... **a company that will not spend money
  to keep peace among its own members has not understood what it is for.**" But: "**I will not have
  peace bought a second time.**"
- **His theory:** "Water was going into cans at the head of that valley and it stopped when the
  Association began to look. **The rise of 1924 is the measure of it.**" "Nothing was done to those
  cows. Nothing was done to that bottom land. Nobody changed their feed and nobody changed their
  pasture."
- He rejects the apparatus explanation: "he condemned the station's testing apparatus on the twelfth
  of July, 1923, and told us to order new. We ordered new inside the month... **Apparatus wears.** A
  station in daily use since 1896 will wear its fittings out twice over in that time... **I decline
  to build a mystery on a piece of housekeeping that appears in our own order book with a date and a
  price against it.**"
- On watering: "**A little water in a can is not a felony in a man's own mind. It is a way of making
  a hard year come out even, and the man who does it tells himself he will stop when the price
  improves.** I have no wish to see anybody prosecuted."
- Defends the 1919 circular: "I would write every word of it again tomorrow. **A manager who sees his
  figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing to the men who make them is not being kind
  to them; he is being idle at their expense.**"
- The Jersey herd: "**My father**, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law under which
  every patron in this Association is paid on the test of the station where he delivers, kept a herd
  of Jerseys at the home place, as some of you remember, and I kept them after him. **In 1910 that
  herd tested 4.56.**"
- His ask: "**Bring it as it left the cow.** Wash the cans, cool the milk, keep the dog out of the
  milk house... and this business will be dead and buried where it belongs, and your manager can
  stand up here next spring and talk to you about butter prices, which is what he is paid for."
- Closes: "I thank you for thirteen years of your patience and I ask for one more."

## VIRA TOLAND'S TESTIMONY (r01) — the folk memory

- The letter came **1919** to nine families; her father "read it at the table **with his hat still
  on, which he never did**, and my mother stood at the stove with her back to him and did not turn
  around the whole time he read it. He read it twice." Put in the drawer of the desk in the front
  room; still there when he died; her brother is believed to have it yet.
- "Some of them kept theirs till they died. **One woman I knew had hers pinned inside the lid of her
  trunk.** You can tell people a thing like that is only business and they will still keep the paper
  forty years to prove what was said of them."
- The kinship point: "it was somebody's cousin saying it, and that is a different thing altogether in
  a place this size." She thinks the Vose family "always thought the valley belonged to them a
  little."
- On the rise: "the figures came up and stayed up, and **nobody ever said why**... **That was the
  queerest part of the whole business to me even as a child, that the thing corrected itself and no
  man stood up and gave a reason for it.**"
- Her verdict: "**He was taking the cream.**... A man stands in a room by himself with the separator
  running and the door shut and **nobody in the valley can add**, and after a while he helps himself,
  and it is not that he is a wicked man, **it is that nobody ever came up that road and looked.**"
- Her father's formula: "**Ansel Keddie was an honest man who had got into something.**"
- Her misgiving: "he had all those years of this valley looking at him a certain way in church, and
  Orra had it too, and she outlived him a good while and had it the whole time."
- On the settlement: "**thirty-five hundred dollars**, split up nine ways over all those years — my
  father's share of it did not buy a hay rake."
- Closing image: "It rained the day of that letter, I remember the wet on my father's shoulders when
  he came in, and I have never in sixty years been able to think about the one without the other."
- On the 1958 visitor: "a young woman came round here in nineteen fifty-eight from the college away
  east... she went up and **poked about the old station building**, which was shut by then... She sat
  where you are sitting. Nice girl, careful, wrote everything down. **She was Hazel Brant's first
  cousin once removed**, so she was of the family both sides in a manner of speaking... I told her
  about the letter in my father's drawer. I don't know what she made of it."

## LOOMIS ON THE LIMITS OF THE RECORD (r02)

Her method: "from the minutes wherever the minutes will bear it, from my own recollection where they
will not, and the two kept apart here as they ought to be kept."

**Two places the record stops:**
1. **The 1938 office move.** The 1889–1892 minute book "was not thrown out; it was carried out with
   the rest and it did not arrive... **anybody who tells you what was resolved at the first four
   annual meetings is telling you a story he had from his father.**"
2. **The crate.** "**The crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all.** I took every word down
   and it is not there... **Not a word of it was spoken in any of the three sessions, by the patrons,
   by the officers, or by the board**... Forty years of taking down what men say has taught me that
   **the interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying.**"

Her self-indictment about the four officers (herself, Teague, Sill, Vose): "**not one of us ever went
up the valley and stood in the Ostrey Hollow station on a working morning to watch the work being
done. I include myself first in that.**"

On the founding purpose: before the creamery, "the members sold their milk where they could get a
price for it and there was no common test and no common price, and **every man's arithmetic was his
own**."

On the 1925 petition: the complaint was "that their milk had **never** tested as their neighbors'
milk tested and that they had been paid accordingly for a very long time, and they asked to be made
whole." **Three explanations were argued; none accepted**; she declines to rehearse them — "they are
in the minutes and the minutes are in the safe." **(Frick, r13, now names all three — see below.)**

Her ledger figures, from Teague's annual sheets, added twice by her: **40,000,000 lb over the
twenty-five years, avg 1,600,000 lb/year, butterfat averaging 25 cents a pound.** "These two figures
are not mine and I do not vouch for the cows, only for the copying."

## THE BRANT WEIGH BOOK (r09) — the outside check on the weighing

- A plain ledger, ruled by hand. **Two columns for each day of delivery.** Column 1: the weight of
  every can as Emmet took it off **his own scale in his own barn before the wagon went down the
  road**. Column 2: **the figure the station gave him back for that same milk.** "That is all it is.
  There is no comment in it anywhere but three or four lines in thirty years, and no opinion at all."
- Physical: cover gone off the front; the back held by a strip of harness leather Emmet tacked on
  himself; the first forty leaves stained brown at one corner "where it lay in a leaky window a
  winter he was sick."
- **The two columns agreed.** "Day after day, month after month, for the whole run of years my father
  kept it and for the years I have kept it, his weight and the station's weight came out the same,
  barring a pound or two here and there, and those were days when a can got slopped on the hill. Two
  scales, his and the station's, kept by two men who by the end were not speaking to one another, and
  the scales never fell out at all. **Whatever was wrong at that station — and something was wrong at
  that station for a very long time — it was not the weighing.**"
- Emmet wrote it "at the kitchen table after the second milking, in the lamp, with his hat still on,
  and my mother would not have the table cleared till he was done. It took him no time at all, and it
  is the only reason there is anything left to argue about now."
- On the gap: "Our milk at the Hollow ran seventeen hundredths of a point under what the same class
  of milk was making down at Larrow Green. **Seventeen hundredths is nothing to look at on one pay
  slip. Set the slips in a row for a lifetime of farming and it is a farm.**"
- On the family fact: "Ansel Keddie, who kept the station's test book, was married into the family
  that had more to lose by that station than any other family on the meadow, and **I have never known
  what to do with that fact except set it down.**"
- On the hay: he grants the hay is coarse and cuts late, "and a cow will eat it and do well enough,
  whatever has been said about that hay by men who never fed it... I will say for it that it never
  failed to grow something."

## ROSALIE CUDD'S LETTER (r10) — the outside check on the milk

- Opening: "Your letter came Tuesday and I have read it twice, once standing up in the make room with
  my hands wet and once properly after supper... **In thirty-odd years nobody from over the ridge has
  ever asked me a plain question about our books**, and here is a young woman from the college who
  asks one in four lines and apologizes for troubling me. You are not troubling me."
- The four men's habit: started before daylight to have the cans on her platform by seven, "because
  milk that has been four miles down a switchback in a wagon on a July morning is not improving while
  it stands, and they came in with the horses steaming and the tarps wet with the cloud that sits on
  the top of the ridge till the sun is well up." One of them brought his boy, "who used to sit on my
  scale platform and be weighed for a joke. That boy is a man in the state road department now and
  does not remember me."
- Why they came: cheese paid better than butter in a flush month; she and her father were buying hard
  in flush "because a cheese factory can use everything you can carry it"; and while the road was open
  her gate was a shorter pull for those four farms. "Their reasons were plain enough and none of them
  was mysterious."
- On her father's practice and why no one ever compared: "He had no business with what your station
  made of the same milk. **A cheesemaker tests what comes in his door so he knows what he is paying
  for and what his vat will do. He is not keeping a check on another man's arithmetic seven miles off
  over a mountain**, and if you had suggested it to my father he would have laughed at you."
- On the Ordell theory: he "thought it was nonsense, and he wrote so in his own book where nobody
  would ever read it, which is where he generally put his opinions. He used to say that **the Ordell
  people had a theory about that bottom the way some families have a ghost: it explained things, and
  it cost nothing, and nobody was ever going to test it.**"
- **On water — the flat refutation:** "A cheesemaker knows watered milk inside a day. He does not
  need a test to know it, though the test will tell him too. **The curd comes soft and it will not
  knit; you get a slack, weak body and your yield is down, and you can stand at the vat and see your
  money going out with the whey.** It cannot be hidden from the man who makes the cheese, because the
  cheese tells him, and it tells him the next morning, not next year. **Butter is more forgiving that
  way. Cheese is a witness.** In sixteen summers my father never made one complaint of those four
  men, never docked one of them a cent, and never — not once that I heard, and I was in the room —
  said a word about their milk except that he wished he had more of it in July."
- On the valley: "trouble carries over a ridge better than anything else does. It came to me the way
  weather comes, in pieces and late, and always with somebody's name attached to it. I paid no
  attention. It was not my county, they were not my patrons in September, and I had a make to get
  out. If I sound short about Ordell it is only that **I have listened to forty years of your valley
  explaining that meadow to itself, and I never once heard anybody go up there and look.**"
- Her limits, stated plainly: "**I do not know what your station made of the same cows**, and I have
  not the first idea what your county decided about any of it. I never saw those figures and I would
  not know how to get them. All I can give you is our side of the ridge, honestly copied."
- Closing: come over in the summer, "the road is open by then, mostly, and I will give you your
  dinner and show you my father's book, which is a nicer object than anything I have said about it
  here."

## FRICK ON THE ARBITRATION (r13)

- Why it stayed with him: "It was not litigation at all. It was an arbitration under the by-laws of a
  creamery association, with no appeal from it, no reported decision, and nothing whatever to cite it
  for; and I have thought about it oftener than about anything I ever argued in a courthouse."
- "Three and sixteen is not a scandalous figure standing by itself. It becomes one when the man over
  the hill is paid on a better figure for milk out of cows no different from yours; **when the
  difference holds steady for a quarter of a century; and when nobody in the office can tell you the
  cause of it.**"
- The by-laws provided for arbitration and "the officers, whatever else may be said of them, did not
  resist it." A board of three; Frick chairman "because I was the one of the three who kept no cow."
- **Three sessions.** First at Ashlin, 17 March 1925. "The second and the third I asked to have at
  Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's own weigh room, **so that the board might see the plant it
  was to pass upon instead of hearing it described by men with an interest in the description.** That
  was my doing and I am still content with it."
- **"Every physical appliance of that station was before us and we satisfied ourselves upon it. We
  saw the scales tried. We saw the sampling done and the test made, and I will say plainly that I did
  not understand the test then and do not understand it now; I am a lawyer and the thing is
  chemistry. But I could see that it was done the same way twice, and that the men who did it were
  not hiding their hands."** — note: by 1925 the station had been on new glass for nearly two years,
  so a test made in front of the board would have shown nothing wrong.
- **THE THREE EXPLANATIONS, at last named** (Loomis declined to rehearse them; Frick sets them down
  "because the three did not sit easily together and nobody seemed to mind that they did not"):
  1. **Water — put by the manager, Selby Vose.** He had circulated the warning some years before and
     stood by it: "that the Hollow's milk came in thinned, that a little water in a can is the oldest
     trick in the trade, and that a station cannot test what is not there. **He put it without heat
     and without proof.**"
  2. **Theft by the tester — put by the bookkeeper, Wilbur Teague**, "in the way an accountant puts a
     thing he wants said without saying it — he was asked about the station's account and answered at
     length about how such an account would look if a man were helping himself, and then observed
     that he made no charge against anybody. **There is no cross-examination that reaches a witness
     of that kind, and I did not attempt one.**"
  3. **The hay.** "Somebody read the report of the 1908 committee into the record, and the bog meadow
     at Hessel Bottom was before us for the better part of an afternoon... **It is an old opinion in
     that valley and it had the advantage of blaming nobody in the room.**"
- On Keddie: "I believed him then and I believe him now. **His denial was accurate and it did him no
  good at all, and the reason it did him no good is that a man who cannot account for a figure is
  presumed by everybody in the room to be the figure's author.**"
- **The corridor conversation, expressly not in the minutes:** "In the corridor at Ashlin, Keddie told
  me privately that **he had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the
  room.** He was not a bitter man about it. He had lived among them thirty years and would not stand
  up and accuse his neighbors, and I record it here because it has been said since that he went to
  his grave accusing nobody, which is true, and that he suspected nobody, **which is not**."
- On the dismissal: "**There was no finding against him from us and there never was one; the board had
  not concluded and did not conclude that he had done anything whatever. He was simply let go while
  the board was still sitting, and I have never been able to think well of the Association for it.**"
- The patrons' own reckoning: **sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat** over the years complained
  of, "and nothing was ever brought forward to disturb that figure."
- Reflection: "A board of arbitration can only decide between the accounts that are put to it. Three
  were put to us; each of them was urged by a man of standing; each of them would have been believed
  by half the valley; and not one of them was proved. **I have wondered since whether the answer was
  in that room and simply was not in any of the three stories, and whether a lawyer who understood
  chemistry might have found it.** I did not understand chemistry. **I understood that an honest man
  was ruined by a figure nobody could explain, and that we paid his neighbors money without being
  able to tell them what they were being paid for.**"

## SILL AT THE CHURN (r14)

- "Forty-one years I have stood at that churn. Eighteen ninety-three I came here, and there was no
  floor in this room but plank, and we cooled with creek ice out of the pond... Forty-one years and I
  never sent out a tub of butter I would not eat myself, which is more than the office can say about
  anything it ever sent out."
- The cream wagon came down from the Hollow **every day of the week but Sunday**, seven miles of hill
  road, "and in July it came down warm no matter how early they started." Set off at the platform,
  weighed, sampled, into the cold room.
- **The heavy cream — the disproof of theft, handled daily for forty-one years:** "That cream ran
  heavy. Every year it ran heavy. **There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been
  bought up there.** The office would send down its sheet and the sheet would say so many pounds of
  fat bought at the station, and the fat that came into this room off that wagon was more than the
  sheet said. **Always more. Never less.**"
- And he weighed it himself: "The platform man weighed it and I weighed it over again when it came
  into the cold room, because I have never in my life taken another man's figure for what was going
  into my churn. Cans off, cans on the scale, cans into the ice, and the book ruled and signed before
  I put my coat on... you will find my hand on every page of it from ninety-three, and you will find
  the Hollow's cream coming in heavy on page after page after page, in my own writing, for as long as
  I have been in this room."
- **How he explained it away:** "Well, that is cream for you. Cream is not milk and it will not stand
  still and be counted. It is a thick thing and it clings and it separates on you and any man who
  tells you he can put a figure on cream to the pound and hold it is a man who has never had his arm
  in a can up to the elbow. So it ran heavy. **So does everybody's. I never made anything of it, and
  I will say plain, nobody ever asked me to.**"
- Larrow Green's own patrons: "**that ran 3.78** taking one year with another right through those
  years. Three seventy-eight... Ordinary milk, ordinary county, ordinary cows. There is nothing
  wonderful about 3.78 and there is nothing shameful about it either."
- His estimate of the Hollow: "**The Hollow milk was really a 3.85 and no better**, and I will say
  that to any man in this valley. Wet ground up there, but good cows, and I never had a word to say
  against the cows. A 3.85 and no better, and if the figures they were paid on came out under that,
  then somebody up that hill road was helping the cans along, and I said so at the time and I say it
  now."
- His theory: "**You cannot tell me nine families all got honest in one summer.** There was water in
  those cans and I said so at the time... one summer, along in the twenties, those figures came up
  and they stayed up, and nothing on this earth changed but the season, and men do not suddenly start
  pouring better milk out of the same cows in the same barns. **Something got stopped. Something got
  said to somebody. I never knew what and I never asked, because the day a buttermaker starts asking
  about other men's cans is the day he stops making butter.**"
- On 1902: "a man in here shouting that his milk tested under his brother-in-law's down the creek and
  it was the same feed and the same water and what did we mean by it."
- On the 1919 circular: "I thought well of him for it. It was the only plain thing that office ever
  did, and of course they hated him for it up the Hollow, and of course it changed nothing, **because
  a paper never emptied a can.**"
- On offices: "The Association has a paper for everything and butter for nothing. I have had men come
  in here in a collar and tell me my churn was running slow when they could not have told you which
  end of a cow gets up first. I made butter that took premiums in three states out of this room, and
  **not one man in that office ever came down here to see how it was done, and every one of them could
  tell you to the half cent what the fat was worth. Twenty-seven cents.**"
- **The lent glass, May 1896:** "When they opened that station up at Ostrey Hollow — that was
  ninety-six, in May, and there was a crowd went up for it — **they had no outfit of their own. Not a
  piece.** It was ordered and it was not come, and they were ready to start taking milk, and there was
  talk of holding off the whole season. And I said no, they can have mine. **I went to my own bench in
  this room and I made up an outfit for them out of my own glass, my own, that I had used and knew,
  and it went up the valley on the wagon and they opened on it. Their own came the spring after and
  they sent mine back to me, and one piece of it was chipped and I never said a word about that
  either.**"
- **The missing 1896 figure:** "That first season they took in **about nine hundred thousand pounds**
  up there. May to the end of the year, that is, so it was not even a whole one. That is a good deal
  of milk for a hill station starting from nothing, and it was the biggest thing that ever happened to
  Ostrey Hollow, and I was glad enough to have had a hand in it."
- "I am sixty-four years old and I have thought about butter. **I never went up there but twice in my
  life.**"
- Closing: "Put in about the butter. **Do not put in that I said anything against the cows up there,
  because I did not. The cows were never the trouble.** Put in that Hobart Sill made the butter and it
  was good butter and he stood at the churn until the day they gave him a chair."

## GRIGG'S 1919 COVERING LETTER (r15)

- Why reprinted: "the Ostrey Hollow question having been reopened this season by men who were boys
  when we made it. I consent willingly and I have nothing in it to take back. I ask leave only to add
  a page, because a report is a dry thing and does not say why it was written."
- On the pressure in 1908: "From about 1902 there had been grumbling out of Ostrey Hollow... It was
  not one man and it was not one season. It went on and it grew, and it got into the talk at the
  annual meeting and into the talk at every crossroads store between here and Ashlin, and **it took
  the shape such things always take, which is that somebody must be doing it on purpose.** By the
  winter in which we were named, **the board could not let it alone any longer without seeming to have
  an interest in letting it alone**, and we three were named to look into it. I have never in my life
  been given a duty I liked less."
- On when payment by test began: "**Payment by test came in with the 1893 meeting, as I have always
  understood it**, and from that year a difference of this kind stopped being a matter of opinion and
  became a matter of money."
- His disclosure and his disclaimer, in adjacent breaths: "**I had known the men of that bottom since
  I was a young man and I have bought hay off two of them**" ... "I am not a patron of that station. I
  haul to Larrow Green and always have, and **whatever the Hollow was paid or not paid never put a
  cent into my pocket nor took one out of it.** I was chosen for the work on that account and for no
  other, and I have been abused for the finding by men on both sides of it, which I take to be a fair
  sign that the finding was an honest one."
- **His account of the sampling, contradicting the report he is reprinting:** "We sampled those herds
  **through the whole of 1908 — every month of that year**, in the barn and on the pasture, out of the
  night's milk and the morning's, and we did not let a man know which day we were coming. We weighed.
  We watched the test made and we watched it made again."
- **The misquotation of Bulletin 214:** "the State Agricultural College at Farland has since published
  its Bulletin No. 214, which was a feeding trial upon bog hay carried out under the College's own
  eye, with weighed rations and no interest in the outcome. **That trial found the bog hay to depress
  the butter fat test by two tenths in the winter feeding.** Two tenths, gentlemen, out of a College
  that had never heard of Hessel Bottom and had no reason on earth to flatter us. **There is our
  finding, arrived at by another road and by better men, three years after we set our names to it.**"
  (The bulletin says *not more than five hundredths* in winter and *nothing measurable* on summer
  pasture. Grigg inflates it four-fold and drops the summer clause.)
- His prescription and his prophecy: "Drain that bottom, sweeten it, keep the stock off it in the wet
  of the spring, and feed the coarse hay off it with a mixed ration and not by itself, and the Hollow
  will test with the rest of us. **Go on as they are going and abuse the man who reads their figures
  for them, and they will be grumbling in twenty years' time as they were grumbling when we began, and
  no better off.**"
- Closing: "That report was received and accepted, and the question was closed by it for eleven years
  — until this season, and **the men who have reopened it have brought nothing new before us but their
  impatience.**"

## KEPP'S PAPER (r16)

- Opening habit: "when I am called to a herd that is doing badly, to ask first what the cattle are
  standing on. I have found in thirty years that the answer to nine cases in ten is under the animal's
  feet, and that the tenth is in the water she drinks." The question "has been dealt with hitherto by
  opinion, by grumbling, and by the naming of names. It deserves better."
- **The land:** "A wet bottom is not a rich meadow with too much water in it. It is a distinct kind of
  ground with a distinct kind of chemistry, and the water is the least part of it." Growth does not rot
  down; it lies sour; the soil is acid, poor in lime and in the phosphates. Sweet grasses crowded out
  by sedge, rush, coarse tussock grasses. "That herbage carries bulk and it carries water. What it does
  not carry, in any quantity worth the name, is lime or phosphorus, and it is cut late because such
  ground cannot be got onto early, and late cutting takes out what little was in it."
- **The cow:** "A cow in milk is a manufactory working on a narrow margin... If her ration is short of
  the minerals, she does not simply give less; **she robs her own frame to make good, and she goes on
  robbing it until she cannot.**" Signs: thin over the loin and rough in the coat out of winter
  feeding; slow to condition in spring; ready subjects for digestive disorder in the fall; breed
  later; feet worse.
- "**They will milk, gentlemen.** Wet land is generous of flow and no man on a bog meadow ever
  complained that his pail was light. But what is in the pail is another matter, and **the fat is the
  first thing to suffer, because the fat is the dearest thing the cow makes and it is the first economy
  she practices when she is pressed.**"
- **His measure:** "The wet bottoms of this county yield a milk poorer in fat by **a fifth of a point**
  and every practical man knows it. Set the hill herds of this county against the bottom herds and you
  will find that difference, farm for farm, wherever you look for it, and the difference does not
  depend on the breed, on the man, or on the barn. It depends on the ground."
- **His figure for the gap:** "as it has been given to me and as I have satisfied myself, is
  **twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21** — between the milk of that station and the milk of this
  valley at large." He grants the complaint entirely: "**Their complaint is perfectly just and I have
  never doubted it.**"
- Hessel Bottom "is the completest example of wet meadow in this county. **Six farms** cut hay and
  pasture stock upon it... a part of it is under water every spring and is not fit to be walked on
  until well into May. Its hay is sedge hay, and on those farms it is the winter dependence and very
  nearly the whole of it."
- "The matter has been before committees since and before boards, and it is not settled, and it will
  not be settled by men shouting at one another across a weigh room. **It is settled already, in the
  literature, for anybody who will read.**"
- **The spurious triple convergence:** "The College fed the bog hay under control and measured what it
  did, and it found the butter fat test depressed by **two tenths** in the winter feeding. That is the
  whole case. **Two tenths from the College, laid alongside the fifth of a point I have found in the
  field and the twenty-one hundredths that station has been complaining of, and the three agree so
  nearly that I do not know what more a reasonable man requires.**"
- **His dismissal of 1911:** "It has been urged upon me, by a gentleman who I know will forgive my
  noticing it here, that in the summer of 1911 the testing at that station was broken off for **six
  weeks**, their own apparatus having failed them and a borrowed one being used, and that the figures
  stood higher during those six weeks than before or after. Six weeks in the flush of the season,
  gentlemen. **Six weeks of June and July, when every herd in the county is at its best and the stock
  are off the wet ground and up on the aftermath.** I am astonished that anybody should found an
  argument on six weeks of summer figures. **I could produce you six weeks in any year that would prove
  anything you liked.**"
- **The remedy:** "Drain the bottom; open the old ditches and cut new; lime the ground and lime it
  again; sow it down when it will carry a seeding; keep the stock off it in the wet of the spring, when
  a hoof does more harm in a day than a season will repair; and never feed that hay alone. Feed it with
  a mixed ration and a mineral supplement, as the College directs. **Fat has been worth twenty-seven
  cents the pound through these years**, and a fifth of a point taken off every hundredweight a farm
  ships, year in and year out, will pay for a great deal of tile."
- **DISCUSSION** (taken down by the club's secretary):
  - *A member* asked whether the difference might not be in the cows rather than the land, the bottom
    farms being poorer men with poorer stock. *Kepp*: considered and rejected — "some of the
    best-bred cattle in the county stand on wet land, and some very indifferent cattle on the hills
    test well; and... a man may improve his herd for twenty years and never get his fat up while his
    ground is sour."
  - *A member* asked **whether the trouble might lie in the station rather than the bottom, as had been
    said in the valley for years.** *Kepp*: "he had no interest in that quarrel and no competence in
    it; that **he had never in his life read a test and would not know how**; but that when a cause is
    found in the soil, in the herbage, in the condition of the stock, in the College's trial, and in
    every wet bottom in the county alike, **it is not good sense to go looking for a second cause in a
    building.**"
  - *The chairman* thanked Dr. Kepp and observed that the paper would be printed for the members.

## RUNDLE'S PAMPHLET (r12) — the valley's own version, and its retraction

- Method and disclaimer: compiled "from the recollections of old members and two winters of asking
  questions at the store... **What follows is not a record. It is what the valley remembers, tidied,
  and where the valley is certain of a thing I have written it down as certain.**" He has taught the
  school at the Green "a good many years" and "listened to this history in pieces, at funerals and in
  the back of the store, for the whole of them."
- 1889: "They were tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking whatever the merchant chose to allow
  them for it, and they had worked out — the way farmers do, slowly and then all at once — that **the
  way to be paid properly for a thing is to own the place that buys it.**" The meeting in the
  schoolhouse; it went on very late; somebody's horse got loose.
- 1891: the creamery; "the valley began to be paid in cash once a month, which had never happened to
  it before. There are men still living who can tell you what the first check did in their mother's
  kitchen, and they will tell you whether you ask or not."
- **The trouble chapter:** "I would rather not write this chapter and it cannot be left out." After the
  1919 circular "the two ends of this valley did not speak civilly to one another for six years. **I
  have had the children of both ends in one schoolroom through those years and I can tell you that it
  came down to them entire, without a word of it being explained, the way a debt does.**"
- **The valley's verdict, printed as certain:** "**It is the settled opinion of the valley that cream
  left that station by the back door, and the figures went right when the man who kept it did not.** I
  set that down as I have heard it set down a hundred times, by men who were there and men who were
  not, and **I have never heard it contradicted at the store.**"
- On the condemnation: "The state inspector condemned the station's glassware on the twelfth of July,
  1923, **for reasons of his own that had nothing to do with the quarrel**, and new glass was put in."
- The claim: "The station took in, over the years complained of, **forty-four million pounds of milk.
  The patrons' loss upon it was nineteen thousand six hundred dollars, and their petition set the
  figure out to the dollar.**"
- The board "heard everybody. It found nothing established, it made an award to the patrons without
  admitting anything, and **it sent this valley home to live with itself, which the valley has done,
  after a fashion, ever since.**"
- The jubilee coda: "fifty Junes of cream coming down that road in the early cool, fifty Januaries of
  it going down over ice with the horses roughshod, a check every month in kitchens that had known
  none, and a creamery still standing that was built by men with nothing but each other and a piece of
  paper they had written themselves. **Whatever this valley did wrong at the head of it, it did this
  right, and it did it for half a century.**"
- **THE PRESS NOTE — the theft theory refuted in 1939, nineteen years before Bulletin 471.** While the
  pamphlet was being set in type he was allowed to look at the Association's old ledgers "on another
  errand altogether," found the station-gain entry, quoted it, and wrote: "**I must go back on Chapter
  IV. Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every
  year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand. I leave it as written and correct it here.**" The 1959
  reprint carries both the paragraph and the correction unaltered.

  the note added while in press demolishes it from the Association's own ledgers; both stand in the
  1959 reprint. Also Article VII dated 1891 in a pamphlet that dates the by-laws to Gideon Vose's
  kitchen table in 1889.
- **r13 (Frick):** "an average test of 3.16" against every documentary figure; and "Every physical
  appliance of that station was before us and we satisfied ourselves upon it" against his own exhibit
  list of four books and Loomis's record that the crate was never named.
- **r14 (Sill):** the heavy cream on every page of his own book against his conclusion that water was
  going in at the top; "nothing on this earth changed but the season" in a year when the glass changed;

- **r15 (Grigg):** "the whole of 1908 — every month of that year" against the report printed

## THEMES AND ECHOES WORTH KEEPING

- **Nobody went and looked — and then people did look, and looking was not enough.** Keddie: "Neither
  of them has come up that road on a working morning and stood at my elbow." Loomis: "not one of us
  ever went up the valley... I include myself first." Vira, defending the theft theory: "it is that
  nobody ever came up that road and looked." Rosalie, from the far side of the ridge: "I never once
  heard anybody go up there and look." **But the 1908 committee visited each of the six farms, some
  three and four times, drew and sealed its own samples, tried the station's scales against a known
  weight and the patrons' scales against the station's, and stood by while the test was made. Frick's
  board sat twice in the station's own weigh room and watched a test through. Strawn stood at the
  tester's elbow for sixteen visits over twenty-nine years. Four separate lookings, and not one of them
  could have found it**, because — Vaught — "the pipette is the standard... an instrument that is
  itself the measure cannot be caught by measuring with it."
- **The one uncheckable instrument.** Scales against test weights, acid against a titration, bottle
  necks against mercury, crank speed by counting — and the pipette against nothing at all until a
  burette comes in from outside, which for this state was 1921 and for this station 12 June 1923.
- **Evenness hides error.** Teague: "A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is
  furniture." Vaught: a short pipette "delivers 16.27 c.c. every time, for a quarter of a century, in
  January and in July, for a careful operator and a careless one. **It has the appearance of excellent
  work.**" The constancy of the gap is both the strongest evidence against every biological explanation
  and the reason nobody investigated.
- **Internally consistent, externally wrong.** "Its herds will rank correctly against one another. Only
  against the outside world is it wrong." Every check the valley was capable of running was a check
  *inside* the system; the three records that could have broken it — the Brant weigh book, the Alder
  Corners book, Strawn's notebooks — were all kept by people with no stake in the quarrel, and all
  three were sitting in drawers.
- **A misquotation became the county's science.** Grigg 1919 turns 0.05 into 0.20 and drops the summer
  clause; Kepp 1926 repeats it as the College's finding and stacks two more numbers on it; the paper is
  printed for the members. Meanwhile the actual Bulletin 214 sentence is quoted correctly and
  identically by Ivy, Nym and Vaught.
- **Article VII's last clause** made the station's test unappealable, so a defective pipette became law
  for twenty-six years. Frick: it "leaves a dissatisfied man with no remedy on earth except to quarrel
  with a figure." Rundle: "the sentence on which the whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward
  turned."
- **A man who cannot account for a figure is presumed to be the figure's author** (Frick) — the exact
  mechanism by which an accurate denial ruined the man who made it.
- **Three explanations were argued and none accepted; the true one was never argued.** The crate was
  never mentioned; the 1923 notebook that condemned it was Exhibit 4.
- **Proportional, not fixed:** the single analytic key. It explains why the best milk lost most, why
  "5%" and "5¼%" both circulate, why the gap never varied with feed or season, and why the station's
  own figures looked immaculate.
- **Three natural controls existed in the record all along:** the 1896 season at 3.80 on Sill's
  borrowed glass; the nine weeks of 1911 at 3.80 on a borrowed measure; and 1924 onward at 3.80 on new
  glass. Two of the three are on the same summary leaf Keddie made up in 1922 and handed to everyone.
  Add the sixteen Alder Corners summers at 3.78–3.82 and Larrow Green's own 3.78, and the true figure
  is written in five places.
- **The disproof of theft was in the books the whole time.** Cream stolen would have arrived light; it
  arrived heavy, every year, and the Association banked the difference and called it station gain.
  Sill weighed it twice a day and called it the nature of cream; Teague made a ledger line of it and
  called it furniture; Rundle found it by accident in 1939 and printed the retraction in a note at the
  back; Ivy made it the last sentence of Bulletin 471.
- **The cost fell on people, not just money:** two families who ate Sunday dinner together never spoke
  again, and the son of one of them could only "set it down"; a letter kept forty years pinned inside a
  trunk lid; children of both ends of the valley carrying an unexplained quarrel into one schoolroom
  "the way a debt does"; the largest patron on the meadow staying loyal to the station that cost him
  most because "the biggest herd is the one that cannot afford to be talked about"; an old man
  dismissed four days before he was cleared, dead twenty-four years before anyone said so out loud.
