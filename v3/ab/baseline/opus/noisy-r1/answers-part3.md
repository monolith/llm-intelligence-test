## Section C — Math

### C1.

**(a) The tests were low by exactly one part in twenty — 5%; every reading was 0.95 of the truth.**

From the glassware alone. The pipettes were blown on the stem for **17.6 c.c.**, the standard
charge of milk in this state and the charge the graduated neck of the test bottle is ruled for.
Ivy Keddie's signed sheet of 9 March 1958 gives five runs each from nine pipettes: 16.70, 16.71,
16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71.

Sum = 150.48; 150.48 ÷ 9 = **16.72 c.c.** mean.

16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95 exactly.** Short by 17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88 c.c.**

Why this transfers straight to the reading: the operator weighs nothing. He reads a column of fat
in a ruled neck and calls it a percentage, and he may do that only because the charge is a fixed
known quantity. A charge 5% short liberates 5% less fat into a neck ruled for the full charge, so
the reading is low **in the exact proportion by which the charge was short** — proportional, not
additive.

*(I reject 16.27 c.c. — Vaught's body text and the* Chronicle*. See E1.)*

**(b) True average 3.80; the recorded tests were short 0.19 of a point.**

3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80.** 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19.** And 0.19 ÷ 3.80 = 5%, confirming that the loss was a
proportion of whatever was there, not a fixed quantity.

Independently corroborated in five places: the 1896 season on borrowed glass (3.80); the nine
weeks of 1911 on a borrowed measure (3.80); 1924 onward on new glass (3.80); the sixteen Alder
Corners summers, 1915–1930, all between 3.78 and 3.82; and Larrow Green's own patrons at 3.78.

**(c) 76,000 lb of butterfat.**

Over the twenty-five full delivery years 1898–1922 the station received **40,000,000 lb of milk**,
averaging 1,600,000 lb a year.

- Credited at 3.61%: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**
- Actually delivered at 3.80%: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lb**
- Difference: **76,000 lb**

Check against the ledger: 1,600,000 × 0.0361 = 57,760 lb; × 0.0380 = 60,800 lb; difference 3,040
lb — exactly the 1898 station-gain entry. 3,040 × 25 = 76,000. The arithmetic and the Association's
own bookkeeping agree to the pound.

**(d) About $19,000; the patrons received one fifth of it.**

Butterfat averaged **25 cents a pound** over the period (Loomis, adding twice from Teague's annual
sheets; Ivy; Roy Brant "one year with another, about twenty-five cents").

76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000.** The award was **$3,800** — exactly **one fifth (20%)**.

*(At Sill's and Kepp's 27 cents the loss would be about $20,500; I use 25 cents because it comes
from the ledger sheets rather than from recollection. I reject $19,600 — Rundle and the* Chronicle
*— see E1.)*

### C2. Emmet Brant, 1910

From the weigh book leaf, quoted identically by Nym and Roy Brant and recited from memory by
Hazel: *"Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and
theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42."*

**(a) Credited:** 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb of butterfat.**

**(b) Owed and not credited:** his true test was 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = **3.60**; 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb.
7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb.**

(Check by proportion: 5% of 7,200 = 360. ✓)

**(c)** Roy Brant gives butterfat at **24 cents in 1910** specifically: 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40.**
At the period average of 25 cents it is $90.00. I prefer $86.40 as the year-specific figure.

Note what this shows: the largest patron on the meadow, in a single ordinary year, lost about the
price of a good implement — "Seventeen hundredths is nothing to look at on one pay slip. Set the
slips in a row for a lifetime of farming and it is a farm."

### C3. Over 1898–1922

**(a) Credited to the patrons: 1,444,000 lb.**
**(b) Reached Larrow Green in the station's cream: 1,520,000 lb.**
**(c) The difference is 76,000 lb, and the percentage depends entirely on the denominator:**

- 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5.00% of the butterfat actually delivered**
- 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.26% — "five and a quarter per cent" — of the butterfat credited**

The ledger's recurring phrase "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" is the **second**: a
bookkeeper reckons a surplus against the figure he has already entered. Neither figure is an
arithmetical error, and Nym states the relation correctly — "five and a quarter percent of the
credited fat is five percent of the fat delivered, and each is right of its own denominator and of
no other."

But **5% is the one that is also the glass.** 16.72/17.6 = 0.95, and the fat missing is exactly
5% of what came down the hill. Teague glossed his own entry as "five and a quarter percent of the
fat that was actually in the cream," which is the wrong denominator, and Vaught repeated the
identical error in Bulletin 471 §V — the one place where getting the denominator right would have
pointed straight at the pipette.

### C4.

**(a) $77.93.** Verify: 25.20 + 31.68 = 56.88; + 0.90 = 57.78; + 18.00 = 75.78; + 2.15 = **77.93.**
Both Junia Ammon and the *Chronicle* render this in words as "seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine
cents," which contradicts the invoice each of them reproduces or has seen. The line items settle
it.

**(b) Six dozen = 72 pipettes**, at .35 each (72 × 0.35 = $25.20 ✓). Each was short **0.88 c.c.**
(17.6 blown − 16.72 delivered). Vaught's "five dozen" and Cleve Oram's "five dozen" are both wrong
against the invoice, which is quoted verbatim three times.

### C5.

**(a) The glass was in use 26 years and about six weeks; the money runs over 25 full delivery
years.**

In use from **1 May 1897** to **12 June 1923** — twenty-six years and six weeks, less the nine-week
interruption of 1911. The money calculation runs over **1898–1922**, twenty-five full delivery
years.

They differ because the two end years are broken. 1897 is a part year — the station's own summary
leaf says "1897, part year," the glass having gone in on 1 May — and 1923 was cut in half on 12
June, with new glass from July. Neither is a whole year of deliveries at the recorded 3.61, so
neither can carry a full year's 1,600,000 lb through the arithmetic. The Bulletin's *conclusion*
dates the fault by the glass (1 May 1897 – 12 June 1923); the *loss* is reckoned by whole years.
Both figures are correct about different things, and they are not in conflict.

**(b) Cannot be determined from the sources.**

The intake for 4 May – 31 December 1896 is in no ledger. Nym is explicit: it "is not in the
ledgers and is nowhere else... inquirers wanting a figure for 1896 must be told there is none" —
the station account column does not begin until 1897, and the first season was carried on the
creamery's own books, whose sheets were never put with the station papers (Kyle confirms this from
the office side). Two recollections survive and they do not agree: **Sill says about 900,000 lb**,
**Kyle says "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds."** Both are recollection, not record. Sill's is
the better-placed of the two — he lent the glass the station opened on and weighed its cream in
daily from that season — while Kyle came into the office after the station opened and had his
figure "from men who were there." So: no determinable figure; if one must be quoted, Sill's
~900,000 lb, flagged as recollection.

## Section D — Logic

### D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment

**When and how long.** From **8 May 1911**, when the station's own pipette broke and a borrowed
measure was put in its place, to **10 July 1911**, when its own measure returned: **nine weeks.**
Both ends are dated entries in the tester's own hand, and both are independently confirmed —
Orra's diary for 8 May ("A. home cross about a broken measure") and the 1922 summary leaf, which
records the whole valley's average standing at 3.80 for "the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in
their place."

**The variable:** exactly one object, the measuring pipette. **Held constant:** the same patrons,
the same herds, the same pasture, the same season, the same acid, the same test bottles, the same
scales, the same building, the same tester and the same habits, the same hour and order of work.
**The result:** the station average rose from 3.61 to 3.80 and fell back to 3.61 *on the day* the
station's own measure returned. Roy Brant confirms it from the receiving end — every farm on the
Bottom came up together and went down together.

**What it establishes that calculation cannot.** The 1958 measurement tells you what nine surviving
pipettes deliver in 1958 and lets you compute what such glass would have done. The 1911 interval
shows the effect being switched off and switched back on in real time, under a contemporaneous
record, by a man who did not know what he was recording and set a question mark against it. It is
a controlled intervention rather than an inference, and it disposes at a stroke of every
explanation that looks to the herds, the ground, the feed, the water in the cans or the men — for
the sufficient reason that none of those was altered in those nine weeks, and the figures were.
Vaught: "Here is one that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and
lay unread for forty-seven years."

**What it does not establish.** (i) It does not prove the borrowed measure was *accurate*; it
proves the two measures differed. That 3.80 is the true figure comes from elsewhere — 1896, 1924,
Alder Corners, Larrow Green. (ii) It does not identify the mechanism: it shows the instrument
matters, not that the fault was short *delivery* rather than some other defect. (iii) It fixes no
magnitude for any other year; the constancy of 0.19 across 1898–1922 is established by the test
book and the pay slips, not by these nine weeks. (iv) It says nothing about which measure was
borrowed or from where — Ivy, Teague and Roy Brant give three incompatible accounts and Strawn's
own June 1911 entry reads "Nothing here to note." (v) It cannot, by itself, exclude that some
*second* cause also operated; it only shows one cause sufficient to produce the whole swing.

### D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream

**Reason 1 — the cream arrived heavy, every year (Sill, at the churn; and the Association's own
ledger).** Sill weighed the Hollow's cream in himself, over again after the platform man, for
forty-one years: "There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there...
Always more. Never less." Stolen cream arrives light. This one **holds without the 1958
measurement**, and it held every day from 1897; Rundle proved the point from the ledgers in 1939,
nineteen years before Bulletin 471: "Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green
light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand."

**Reason 2 — the nine weeks of 1911.** The same man, in the same room, alone, under no different
supervision, produced 3.80 for nine weeks and 3.61 on either side of them, with the changeover
falling on the day a piece of glass was substituted. No theft turns itself off in May and on again
in July at the convenience of a broken pipette.

**Reason 3 — the milk itself tested 3.80 over the ridge.** Jerome Cudd, in another county, on his
own bench, with his own glass, tested the same four Hessel Bottom men's milk at 3.78–3.82 across
sixteen summers. That shows the milk really was about 3.80 — so the deficiency was in the
*reading*, not in a missing quantity of fat. Combined with Reason 1, which shows the fat was all
present at Larrow Green, there is no quantity anywhere for a thief to have taken.

**Reason 4 (supporting) — the shape of the error.** The shortfall was an exact proportion, 5% of
every test, so that the richest herds lost most and the ratios between herds stayed perfect. Theft
by dipping is quantitative and scattered; it does not scale itself to the fat content of each
patron's sample for twenty-six years.

**Why these are independent.** They come from four custodians who had no contact and no common
interest: Sill at Larrow Green's churn; Keddie's own book at the station; Cudd's book in Vessey
County (Rosalie: nobody from over the ridge ever asked a plain question about their books in
thirty-odd years, and comparing figures with a station seven miles off "never entered his head");
and the 1958 glass. Roy Brant's weigh book adds a fifth strand — two scales kept by two men who
had stopped speaking agreed for thirty years — which eliminates false weighing.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question the entries that refute him?** Essentially no. Teague *created*
the station-gain line in 1905 and wrote it every year of his service; he raised the question with
Vose **once, in 1916**, was told to let it alone, and never returned to it — "I was the
Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience." He then explained his own evenness away ("A
figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture") and in 1925 put the theft
theory to the board by insinuation, while the column in his own ledger proved that the fat had all
arrived. Kyle copied the same line for twenty-five years and says it "never once crossed my mind
to wonder at it, and I do not believe it crossed anybody else's either." Nobody laid Exhibit 2, the
receiving book, beside Exhibit 4, the inspector's notebook.

### D3. Did the manager know the tests ran low before 1923?

**Yes — that the Ostrey Hollow tests ran persistently low he certainly knew, and had known for
years; what he did not know was why, and he had decided in advance what the answer was.**

The evidence: (i) He signed the annual station sheet every year — Teague: "a man who signs a sheet
has had the sheet in front of him. What he made of it he never told me." (ii) Teague put the
station-gain question to him directly in **1916** and was told to let it alone; that is knowledge
plus a decision. (iii) The **1919 circular** is itself proof: a warning that added water "would be
looked for and would be dealt with," sent to the head of the valley, presupposes that he knew the
Hollow's figures ran thin — he says so in 1926: "A manager who sees his figures going one way for
twenty years and says nothing to the men who make them is not being kind to them." (iv) Strawn
told him in 1919 that he had tested for water and found none; Vose was still advancing watering in
1926, and his address never mentions the lactometer. (v) He put the water theory to the board in
1925 "without heat and without proof."

The qualification comes from Kyle, who sat outside his door for twenty-five years: "he read the
totals and he never read the lines above them... He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on
the bottom figure, and sign." So the honest answer is layered: he knew the Hollow tested low, he
knew the question had been raised in his office and had closed it, and there is no evidence he
ever grasped what the station-gain line *meant* — that the missing fat was arriving in his own
creamery every day and being banked as a gain. Knowing the low tests, he chose an explanation that
put the fault on the patrons, and after 1923 he defended it against the apparatus: "Apparatus
wears... I decline to build a mystery on a piece of housekeeping."

## Section E — Contradictions

### E1(a). Cases where TWO narrators carry the SAME wrong value

These are the traps, and in every case what breaks the tie is that the two carriers are **not
independent** — they are drawing from one bad channel — while the correct value is fixed by a
document quoted verbatim, or by arithmetic that closes.

1. **Mean delivery 16.27 c.c.** — Vaught's body text (Bulletin 471 §V) and the *Ashlin Chronicle*.
   **Broken by:** Ivy's signed measurement sheet, quoted identically twice, listing nine readings
   that average 16.72; and by arithmetic — 16.27 gives 3.905 as the true average, which cannot be
   reconciled with the 3.80 recorded in 1896, in 1911 and from 1924. Vaught *himself flags* the
   irreconcilability in print and publishes anyway; the *Chronicle* took its figure from the
   college release, i.e. from Vaught. One source, printed twice. **16.72 is right.**

2. **Eleven pipettes recovered** — Rundle's 1959 footnote and the *Chronicle*. **Broken by:** the
   measurement sheet (nine readings), Nym Item 5, Vaught's own §III, Falke's report ("the nine
   surviving pipettes"), and Cleve Oram, who had climbed to the crate ("there were nine of them
   left in it at the end"). **Nine.**

3. **The award of $3,500** — Vira Toland and Cleve Oram. **Broken by:** the award itself, quoted
   verbatim by Loomis and Nym, and given as $3,800 by Ivy, Teague, Vose, Frick and Falke. Both
   carriers are recalling valley talk decades later. **$3,800.**

4. **67,000 lb of butterfat** — Frick (as "the patrons' own reckoning") and Cleve Oram. **Broken
   by:** it is a digit transposition of 76,000, and 76,000 closes against the ledger (3,040 × 25).
   Notable, though, that the patrons in 1925 reached within 12% of the truth from their own pay
   slips, thirty-three years before anyone could say why.

5. **$19,600 loss** — Rundle and the *Chronicle*. **Broken by:** 76,000 lb × $0.25 = $19,000. The
   *Chronicle* also compounds it by attaching the figure to "a valley of six farms," conflating the
   bottom farms with the nine circularized families.

6. **"Seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents"** — Junia Ammon and the *Chronicle*. **Broken
   by:** the invoice both of them reproduce or have seen; the line items sum to **$77.93**. A
   transposition of the last two digits, made twice.

7. **Glass into use 1 May 1898** — Vaught's body text and Bertram Kyle. **Broken by:** the invoice
   of 14 April 1897, Bulletin 471's own conclusion, Sill's account of his glass being returned in
   the spring of 1897, and decisively by **Orra Keddie's diary entry of 1 May 1897**: "A. says the
   station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green." A contemporaneous
   record beats two later reconstructions. **1 May 1897.**

8. **Five dozen pipettes bought** — Vaught's body text and Cleve Oram. **Broken by:** the invoice,
   "6 doz. milk test pipettes... at .35 — 25.20," and by 72 × 0.35 = 25.20. **Six dozen.**

9. **Condemned 12 July 1923** — Vose and Rundle. **Broken by:** Strawn's own dated entry (12 June),
   quoted twice, plus Bulletin 471 and Nym; and it is *impossible*, since Ammon & Sons acknowledged
   the replacement order on 2 July. Note the error flatters the Association by shortening the gap
   between condemnation and remedy.

10. **The station average 3.16** — Teague and Frick. **Broken by:** the test book, quoted verbatim
    three times, and by Teague's own ledger entry (57,760 ÷ 1,600,000 = 3.61%), which refutes his
    own text. A transposition that evidently circulated among the Ashlin men. **3.61.**

11. **"Six weeks" for the 1911 interval** — Teague and Kepp. **Broken by:** the two dated entries,
    8 May and 10 July, which are nine weeks. Kepp compounds it into "six weeks of June and July,"
    moving the start date to fit his flush argument.

12. **Total intake 44,000,000 lb** — Teague and Rundle. **Broken by:** Loomis, who added it twice
    from Teague's own annual sheets, plus Ivy, Nym, Vaught and Kyle, all at 40,000,000; and by
    consistency — 40,000,000 is what makes 3,040 lb/yr and 76,000 lb close.

13. **Bulletin 214 found "two tenths in the winter feeding"** — Grigg (1919) and Kepp (1926).
    **Broken by:** the bulletin's summary quoted verbatim and identically by Ivy, Nym, Vaught *and*
    Falke: "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be
    measured on summer pasture." Kepp is not independent of Grigg; he is repeating the county's
    circulating version. This single misquotation, with the summer clause dropped, is what let the
    hay theory survive for fifty years.

14. **Selby Vose as Gideon's nephew** — Vira and Rundle (Kyle reports hearing it twice more).
    **Broken by:** Selby's own words from the platform, plus Loomis, Duncan and Kyle. **Son.**

15. **Ivy as "first cousin once removed" to Hazel** — Vira and Cleve Oram. **Broken by:** Roy
    Brant, Duncan Keddie, Hazel Brant and Rundle's footnote. **Second cousins.**

16. **Ivy as Ansel's "great-niece"** — the *Chronicle* and Falke. **Broken by:** Duncan Keddie's
    family notes and Ivy's own usage; and Falke retracts on verification. Both carriers derive from
    the same press release.

17. **A Keddie–Vose kinship** — Vira ("first cousin to old Mrs. Vose") and Cleve Oram ("cousins of
    some sort"). **Broken by:** Duncan Keddie's explicit denial and his account of the confusion
    with the Ashlin Voses. Neither carrier can state the line.

18. **The post-1923 average as 3.85** — Sill (as his estimate of Hollow milk) and Falke (as a
    statement of fact, "From 1924 the station's average test stood at three eighty-five"). **Broken
    by:** the test book's 3.80 for 1924, and by 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80 exactly. Falke has probably
    absorbed the valley's figure; Sill's was always an impression.

### E1(b). Narrators who contradict themselves on the page

- **Junia Ammon (r20):** prints an invoice totaling **77.93** and reads it out in the next line as
  "seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents"; states "the house sold nothing into Ordell County
  before 1900" on the page above an 1897 invoice to an Ordell County association; and reproduces a
  1923 letter pleading that "our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything"
  after telling us that the works books — glass, gauge, man, day — did not burn until **1931**.
- **Vaught (r11):** body text and signed conclusion give different numbers throughout (16.27/16.72,
  five dozen/six dozen, 1898/1897, 0.295/0.19, 118,000/76,000, "six times"/four times Bulletin
  214); he notes in print that his own figure "is not readily reconciled with the station's own
  average of 3.80" and publishes; and he repeats Teague's denominator error while explaining
  proportionality better than anyone alive.
- **Teague (r05):** 3.16 against his own quoted 57,760/1,600,000; 44,000,000 against the 40,000,000
  he himself supplied to Loomis; "five and a quarter percent of the fat actually in the cream"
  against his own arithmetic; a line he says he invented in 1905 illustrated by an 1898 entry.
- **Vose (r08):** 12 July against an order acknowledged 2 July; 3.90 and "twenty-one hundredths"
  against 3.80 and 0.19; and "apparatus wears" offered for glass that was short **from the day it
  was bought** — wear cannot be present at purchase and cannot produce an exactly proportional
  defect.
- **Grigg (r15):** "the whole of 1908 — every month of that year" directly above his own report's
  "January, February and March"; "two tenths" against Bulletin 214's five hundredths; and a
  declaration of perfect disinterest two sentences after "I have bought hay off two of them."
- **Kepp (r16):** three "agreeing" numbers that are one misquotation, one impression and one
  rumour; "six weeks of June and July" for nine weeks beginning 8 May; and an admission that he
  cannot read a test, offered as a reason not to examine the test.
- **Rundle (r12):** Chapter IV prints theft as the valley's settled and uncontradicted opinion; the
  note added in press demolishes it from the ledgers; both stand unaltered in the 1959 reprint.
  Also dates Article VII to 1891 in a pamphlet that has Gideon Vose writing the by-laws in 1889.
- **Frick (r13):** "Every physical appliance of that station was before us and we satisfied
  ourselves upon it" against his own exhibit list of four books and Loomis's record that the crate
  was never named; plus 3.16.
- **Sill (r14):** heavy cream on every page of his own book against his conclusion that water was
  going in at the top; "nothing on this earth changed but the season" in the year the glass
  changed; and 3.85 as his estimate of Hollow milk, above his own creamery's 3.78.
- **Duncan Keddie (r18):** gives his mother's birth as 1868, then says she was eighty-two when she
  died in 1948 (→ 1866, which the diary confirms).
- **Hazel Brant (r17):** warns that she has no years and then supplies four wrong ones (weigh book
  begun 1900; crate out from Tarnet in 1899; condemnation in 1921; hearing in 1926) alongside
  quantities that are all correct.
- **Bertram Kyle (r23):** dates the condemnation to "twenty-two," the award to "twenty-four," and
  Vose's appointment to 1911 — while naming the four exhibits without looking and getting all four
  right.
- **Cleve Oram (r21):** relays his father's belief that less cream came off the platform than went
  on, in the same account that credits the college's finding — and the belief, if true, would
  implicate the hauler himself.
- **Nym (r07):** labels the *station* test book "kept by E. Brant" while noting Item 8 is in the
  hand of Item 3 and signed A. Keddie, and labels the *farm* weigh book "kept by A. Keddie."

## Section F — Theory

### F1. What actually happened

**The theory in one causal chain.** At Tarnet in the winter of 1896–97 a mold at Ammon & Sons was
reground, and a lot of milk-test pipettes was blown a little small. Nobody caught it, because the
one check a glass house runs — gauge the first, the tenth, the fiftieth — tests for *scatter*, and
this lot was perfectly consistent. It was consistently wrong. Six dozen of them were invoiced to
the Larrow Valley Association on 14 April 1897 for its Ostrey Hollow station, at $77.93 for the
whole outfit, and went into use on 1 May 1897.

Each pipette was blown for 17.6 c.c. and delivered 16.72 — **0.95 of its mark, one part in twenty
short.** In the Babcock test the operator weighs nothing: he reads a column of fat off a neck ruled
for a *standard charge*, and calls the length a percentage. Put in 5% less milk and 5% less fat
rises into a neck that knows nothing of the substitution. **The reading is not merely low; it is
low in the exact proportion by which the charge was short.**

- **Why that size.** 0.88 c.c. out of 17.6 — because that is how much small the mold was. Nothing
  about the farms, the season or the tester enters into it. 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80; the 0.19 gap is
  5% of 3.80.
- **Why it began when it did.** 1 May 1897, the day the station stopped borrowing Sill's glass and
  started on its own. That is why 1896 — the borrowed year — averaged 3.80.
- **Why it ended when it did.** 12 June 1923, when Merle Strawn, carrying a state-issued burette
  since 1921, finally ran the station's pipettes against it and condemned the crate. New glass in
  July; 3.80 from 1924. Not because anything changed on the farms, and not because of the petition
  — the rise preceded the January 1925 petition by a year.
- **Why nothing had to change at either end.** The cause was in the measure itself. **An instrument
  that is itself the standard cannot be caught by measuring with it.** Scales can be checked against
  test weights, acid against a titration, bottle necks with mercury, crank speed by counting — but
  in the ordinary practice of the trade the pipette *is* what the plant measures with. So the
  station's figures were orderly, internally consistent, and beautifully kept: its herds ranked
  correctly against one another, and only against the outside world was it wrong. The error was
  proportional (so the best milk lost most — a herd at 4.56 short 0.24, one at 3.42 short 0.18),
  precise (so it never scattered and never drew attention — "It has the appearance of excellent
  work"), and invisible from inside the building.

Over the twenty-five full delivery years 1898–1922, on 40,000,000 lb of milk, the patrons were
credited 1,444,000 lb of butterfat and delivered 1,520,000 lb. The 76,000 lb difference — about
$19,000 at 25 cents — arrived at Larrow Green in the cream every year, was weighed twice by the
buttermaker, was entered by the bookkeeper as "station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as
usual," and was carried to the general account. **The Association banked the shortfall and gave it
a name, and a figure with a name on it has been explained.**

### The three competing explanations, and what defeats each

**1. Water in the cans — advanced by Selby Vose**, the manager, who circulated the 1919 warning,
put it to the board in 1925 "without heat and without proof," and was still advancing it in 1926;
seconded by Sill in 1934 ("You cannot tell me nine families all got honest in one summer").
*Defeated by:* Strawn's lactometer, run at that station on every visit from 1907 and never once
finding added water — and he told the manager so in 1919, the very year the circular went out;
Rosalie Cudd's flat refutation from the vat ("The curd comes soft and it will not knit... Butter is
more forgiving that way. **Cheese is a witness**") over sixteen summers with never a complaint or a
docking; the 1911 interval, in which the same nine families' milk tested 3.80 for nine weeks and
3.61 on either side; and the sheer implausibility of nine households starting and stopping in
concert on the day a pipette broke.

**2. Theft by the tester — advanced by Wilbur Teague**, the bookkeeper, at the 1925 hearing, "in
the way an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it"; it was also the valley's
settled opinion, printed as certain by Rundle in 1939 and held to the end by Vira Toland and by
Cleve Oram's father. *Defeated by:* the cream arriving **heavy** at Larrow Green every year, never
light — Sill's own hand on every page from 1893, and the Association's own station-gain column,
from which Rundle demolished his own Chapter IV in 1939; by the 1911 interval, where the same man
alone in the same room produced honest figures for nine weeks; and by the proportional shape of
the error, which no thief could counterfeit.

**3. The wet hay of Hessel Bottom — advanced by the 1908 committee under Nyle Grigg**, read into
the record at the 1925 hearing, and given a scientific dress by Roland Kepp in 1926. *Defeated by:*
Bulletin No. 214's actual finding — "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and
nothing that could be measured on summer pasture" — against a gap that stood at a flat 0.19 in
July as in February, in wet years and dry, for twenty-six years. It is four times too small and it
is the wrong shape. Also by Rosalie Cudd's sixteen summers at 3.78–3.82 off that same ground; by
the 1911 interval; and by the exposure of Kepp's "triple convergence" as one misquotation (Grigg's
inflation of 0.05 into 0.20, with the fatal summer clause silently dropped), one field impression
and one rumour. Falke read the bulletin in 1959 and withdrew his concession in print.

### Subsidiary questions

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware? No — and it came remarkably close.**
The board sat three times, two of them in the station's own weigh room at Frick's request, and
watched a test made — but the station had been on **new** glass for nearly two years, so what it
watched was faultless. Its exhibits were four books, one of which, Exhibit 4, was Strawn's 1923
circuit notebook containing "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the
whole crate of them." Loomis, who took down every word, records that **the crate was never
mentioned in any of the three sessions, by the patrons, by the officers, or by the board.** The
award's phrase "the books and appliances of the station" she calls "a form of words the board used
and not a list of anything," and Frick's own four-item exhibit list confirms her. The crate itself
was up a ladder in the loft, twenty feet above the men's heads, in the two sittings held at the
station.

**What did the tester himself believe?** The sources give two answers and do not reconcile them.
Duncan Keddie, who was seventeen and in the kitchen, says his father came home in the summer of
1911 **saying it was something in the glass, and said it twice** — and would not write it in the
book, because he would not set down what he could not prove; what he wrote was a question mark,
and he let it stand for fourteen years. Frick, expressly off the minutes, says that in the corridor
at Ashlin in 1925 Keddie told him privately that **he had always thought the patrons were at
fault** and would not say so in the room, having lived among them thirty years. Both are private
statements reported at second hand by men with no motive to invent. The best reading is that they
are fourteen years apart: an unprovable suspicion of the glass in 1911, hardening into the
manager's version — the one that ruined him — by the time he had spent another fourteen years
writing down figures he could not explain. The contemporaneous trace, the "?" in his own hand,
belongs to the first.

## Section G — Summary (150 words max)

**148 words.**

A cooperative creamery paid its farmers by butterfat test, and its by-law made each station's test
final. In 1897 the Ostrey Hollow station bought pipettes blown for 17.6 c.c. that delivered 16.72
— exactly a twentieth short. For twenty-six years every test there read 95% of the truth: 3.61
where the milk was 3.80. The missing 76,000 lb of butterfat, about $19,000, came down in the cream
anyway and was banked as "station gain." Three explanations were argued — watered milk, a thieving
tester, wet hay — and each was refuted by a record nobody consulted. The tester was dismissed in
1925 and died under it in 1934. In 1958 his granddaughter found the condemned crate in a loft and
measured it. It took eight records because no single one contained the answer: the proof lay
scattered across a farm ledger, a rival cheesemaker's book, an inspector's notebook and a glass
firm's invoice.
