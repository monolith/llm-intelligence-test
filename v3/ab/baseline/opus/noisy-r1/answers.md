### Story 1. The By-Law That Made a Figure Into Money (1889–1896)

**Who.** Gideon Vose and the farmers of the Larrow Valley; Hobart Sill; Ansel Keddie.

In 1889 the farmers met in the schoolhouse at Larrow Green — cold weather, the meeting went very
late, somebody's horse got loose — and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery
Association. They were tired of hauling butter to the Ashlin merchants and taking what was
allowed them. Gideon Vose was elected first president and held the office until 1911; he drafted
the by-laws himself at his own kitchen table, not being a lawyer, and they "read as a farmer
wrote them." He died in 1917.

In 1891 the creamery was built on Larrow Creek below the mill, and the valley was paid in cash
once a month for the first time. Then came Article VII: every patron paid on the pounds of
butterfat his milk is found by test to contain, *not* on the weight of the milk — "and the test
of the station where he delivers shall govern." That last clause is the hinge of everything
after it: no appeal from the station's test. Loomis calls it "the one nobody quotes"; Frick says
it "leaves a dissatisfied man with no remedy on earth except to quarrel with a figure."

**When Article VII was adopted cannot be determined.** Rundle says 1891, Loomis 1892, Grigg "the
1893 meeting"; Nym notes the printed by-laws carry no year on their face, only "adopted at the
annual meeting," and the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the office move of 1938.

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles
up the valley. It opened 4 May 1896; a crowd went up for it. Ansel Keddie (b. 1861) was tester
and separator man from the opening, having gone up in 1895 to get the place ready. Its own
glassware had not arrived, and rather than lose the season Hobart Sill made up an outfit out of
his own personal glass at his own bench and sent it up the valley on the wagon. The test book's
first leaf: *"Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own
comes."*

**The quantity that matters: the 1896 season averaged 3.80** — on borrowed glass. That is the
first of three natural controls, and no source ever drew the inference. First-season intake is
disputed and unrecorded (see Story 8 / C5b).

**Documents:** Article VII (quoted verbatim by Loomis, Nym, Rundle, and Falke); the test book's
first leaf; the 1922 summary leaf; Sill's 1934 remarks.

### Story 2. The Lot That Ran Short (Tarnet, 1897 — told in 1949)

**Who.** Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet; Junia Ammon, the firm's historian.

Marcus Ammon's house supplied half the glass in the state's creameries — bottles, pipettes, acid
measures, burettes. A milk-test pipette must *deliver* a stated quantity, not hold it, which is
the harder measurement because milk clings to the glass. A man blows to a gauge hanging on a nail
at his elbow; the piece is annealed overnight, gauged next morning, and packed or thrown in the
cullet barrel.

On **14 April 1897** the firm billed the Larrow Valley Association for the Ostrey Hollow
station's outfit: 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20; 12 doz. test bottles,
8 per cent neck, at .22 — 31.68; 1 acid measure — .90; 1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00;
freight — 2.15. **Total $77.93.**

Those seventy-two pipettes were blown for 17.6 c.c. and delivered a mean of **16.72 c.c.** — short
0.88 c.c., exactly 0.95 of the mark. They went into use at the station on **1 May 1897**; Orra
Keddie's diary records that day, "A. says the station's own glass came up and they are done
borrowing of Larrow Green. He is pleased about it."

Junia Ammon's reconstruction, offered fifty years later because "nobody else is going to": the
mold had been reground, the lot ran short, and nobody in the works caught it — "a boy checks the
first of a lot and the tenth and the fiftieth and finds them consistent, because they are
consistent — they are consistently wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind cannot
find." That is Vaught's analysis arrived at independently from the blowing floor.

It was an accident, not a fraud. The *Ashlin Chronicle* charged in 1958 that a cheap short lot was
"put off on the Association knowingly," resting the charge on the firm's 1923 silence. Nothing
supports it. But the silence was worse than she admits: her own chapter says the **works books —
what glass, what gauge, what man, what day — survived until the fire of 1931**, eight years after
the Association wrote. In 1923 the firm still had the record of that lot and answered that its
books "are not such that we can tell you anything."

**Documents:** the invoice (reproduced in facsimile by Junia Ammon, catalogued by Nym as Item 2,
seen by the *Chronicle*); the letter book copy of 2 July 1923; Orra Keddie's diary, 1 May 1897.

### Story 3. How a Misquotation Became the County's Science (1902–1959)

**Who.** Nyle Grigg's committee; Roland Kepp, V.S.; Orin Falke, county agent.

From 1902 the Ostrey Hollow patrons said their milk tested under everyone else's. Sill remembers
a man in the churn room "shouting that his milk tested under his brother-in-law's down the creek
and it was the same feed and the same water." Emmet Brant said it out loud and was laughed at.
Orra Keddie, March 1902: "A. came home and would not eat... he said no, but it is my figures they
will look at."

In **January 1908** the board named three men — Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green chairman, chosen
because he was not a patron of that station. They visited each of the six Hessel Bottom farms,
drew and sealed their own samples, tried the station's scales against a known weight and the
patrons' scales against the station's, and stood by while the test was made. Their report
(Jan–Mar 1908): "The milk is whole and the scales are honest. It is our opinion that the wet hay
of that bottom is what thins the cream, and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the
station." They found no dishonesty and asked that the finding be printed in the same type as the
rest. The question was closed by it for eleven years.

Then the corruption of the evidence. In 1911 the College published **Bulletin No. 214**: bog hay as
the whole of the roughage "depressed the butter fat test by not more than **five hundredths** of
one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Reprinting his
report in **1919**, Grigg quoted it as **"two tenths in the winter feeding"** and dropped the summer
clause entirely — a fourfold inflation, and the deletion of the sentence that makes the hay
theory impossible. (In the same letter he claims the committee sampled "the whole of 1908 — every
month of that year," contradicting the report printed beneath his signature, and discloses two
sentences after asserting his disinterest that he had bought hay off two of the six farms.)

In **1926** Kepp read "The bog meadows and the milk" to the Farm Club: genuine soil chemistry —
sour ground, no lime or phosphorus, the cow robbing her own frame, fat the first economy she
practices. Then he laid the misquoted 0.20 beside his own field impression ("a fifth of a point")
and the station's reported gap (0.21) and announced "the three agree so nearly that I do not know
what more a reasonable man requires." Three confirmations that were one misquotation, one
impression and one rumour. Asked from the floor whether the trouble might lie in the station
rather than the bottom, he answered that he had never in his life read a test and would not know
how, and that "it is not good sense to go looking for a second cause in a building."

In **1959** Falke's county report conceded the hay as "a real if partial factor" — then wrote to
Farland for Bulletin 214, read the actual summary, and printed a note: "Five hundredths in winter
and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July. I withdraw the concession made
on page four."

**Documents:** the 1908 report (short and full forms); Bulletin 214; Grigg's 1919 covering letter;
Kepp's paper with its floor discussion; Falke's report and note.

### Story 4. Nine Weeks in 1911

**Who.** Ansel Keddie, and every herd on Hessel Bottom.

On **8 May 1911** the station's pipette broke. Keddie entered it: *"May 8. Pipette broke this
morning. Using a borrowed measure."* Orra's diary the same day: "A. home cross about a broken
measure." For nine weeks the station tested on glass that was not its own, and the average went
from 3.61 to **3.80**. On **10 July** he wrote: *"July 10. Our own measure in use again from today.
Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."* The figures fell the same day. "That question mark is
mine and I have let it stand for fourteen years."

This is the experiment the whole case turns on. **The variable:** one object, the measuring
pipette. **Held constant:** the same patrons, the same herds, the same pasture, the same season,
the same acid, the same bottles, the same tester and the same habits, the same scales, the same
building. **Result:** the deficit switched off and switched back on, on two dated days, recorded
contemporaneously by a man who did not know what he was recording.

Roy Brant, from the farm side: "Through those nine weeks our tests came up, all of us, every farm
on the Bottom; and then the station's own came back into use and the figures went down again...
My father noticed the rise and he noticed the fall and he entered both, and he could make nothing
of it, and I could make nothing of it either, and there it sat for near on fifty years."

Duncan Keddie supplies what is nowhere else: **his father came home that summer saying it was
something in the glass, and said it twice, at supper and again by the door, "which was not his
way."** He was seventeen and remembers the kitchen. Ansel would not put in the book what he could
not prove, and wrote a question mark instead — "the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley
ever had." He then kept on for fourteen more years, writing down every figure the glass gave him,
and the figures were what convicted him.

Vaught put the interval above all his own arithmetic: it "lay unread for forty-seven years."
Against it stand Teague's and Kepp's "six weeks" — 8 May to 10 July is nine — and Kepp's flush
argument, which is self-refuting, since if summer flush explained the rise it would have risen
every summer, and in twenty-six years no other six weeks ever did this.

**Where the borrowed measure came from cannot be determined:** Ivy says Larrow Green with the
cream wagon, Teague says "their own came up from town," Roy Brant says the state man left his
own that June. Strawn's own 1911 entry reads "Nothing here to note."

**Documents:** the two dated entries in the test book; the 1922 summary leaf; Orra's diary;
Bulletin 471 §IV; Roy Brant's account; Duncan Keddie's family notes.

### Story 5. Station Gain: Seventy-Six Thousand Pounds in Plain Sight (1897–1939)

**Who.** Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper; Bertram Kyle, clerk; Hobart Sill, buttermaker; Dell Rundle.

The station weighed a patron's milk, tested a sample, separated, sent the cream down to Larrow
Green and gave the skim back for his hogs. So there were two measurements of the same fat: what
the station credited to patrons on its own test, and what actually arrived in the cream and was
weighed into the creamery's vats. **The two were never the same. The second was always the
larger.**

Teague built a ledger line for the difference — "a piece of ordinary bookkeeping, invented at that
desk by the man sitting at it, because a difference that will not be entered somewhere will come
back and bite you at audit." The 1898 entry, quoted identically by Teague, Nym and Rundle:
*"Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at
Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as
usual. Carried to the general account."*

3,040 lb a year × 25 years = **76,000 lb of butterfat**, worth about **$19,000**, entered year by
year in the Association's own books as a gain. Teague on why nobody looked: "the words 'as usual'
got into the entry and stayed there... A figure that jumps about will keep you awake. A figure
that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture." Kyle, who copied the line for
twenty-five years in a fair hand: "It had a name. That is the thing about a name. A figure with a
name on it has been explained, and you rule under it and go on to the next."

At the far end of the road the same fact was handled physically every day. Sill weighed the
Hollow's cream in over again himself, forty-one years, "because I have never in my life taken
another man's figure for what was going into my churn": "There was more fat in the cans than the
papers said had been bought up there... Always more. Never less." He explained it away — "that is
cream for you" — and added, "I never made anything of it, and I will say plain, nobody ever asked
me to."

This is the standing disproof of theft, and it was in the books the whole time. Rundle found it by
accident in 1939 while his jubilee pamphlet was in type, having printed in Chapter IV that the
valley's settled opinion was that cream left the station by the back door. His press note:
"**Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy,
every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand.**" He left the accusation standing and printed
the correction at the back, and reprinted both unaltered in 1959.

Kyle's moral: "It was not a secret. It was worse than a secret. It was in plain sight with a name
on it."

**Documents:** the annual ledgers, station account (the station column runs from 1897); Sill's
receiving and churn books, his hand on every page from 1893; Rundle's press note.

### Story 6. The Circular, and Two Kitchens That Were One (1919–1929)

**Who.** Selby Vose; Ansel and Orra Keddie; Emmet and Roy Brant; nine (or eleven) families.

In 1919 the manager sent a printed circular over his own signature to the patrons at the head of
the valley warning that added water would be looked for and dealt with. Vose in 1926: "It named
nobody. It accused nobody... A manager who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and
says nothing to the men who make them is not being kind to them; he is being idle at their
expense. I would write every word of it again tomorrow." Strawn had tested for water with the
lactometer at that station on every visit and never found any, and told the manager so **in 1919**.

Orra Keddie's diary gives what no other source has. Ansel "would not put them in the mail nor
leave them on the platform. He took them round himself... and stood at the door while each man
read it, and came home at eleven at night with his hat still on." Two days later Emmet Brant came
up, would not come in, stood in the yard, said he had hauled whole milk to that station since the
day it opened and Ansel knew it better than any man living, and asked why he had carried the paper
himself if he did not believe it. "A. said he carried it because he would not have another man
carry it. He went home. He has not been in this house since, and he is my brother."

Roy Brant, independently: the cause of the silence was not the water charge and not a quarrel —
it was that "the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and
did not." Before 1919 the two families ate Sunday dinner together, two houses in one kitchen, the
men at the fence after. After it they never spoke again — not at the store, not at a funeral —
living within sight of each other's chimney smoke, for ten years, until Emmet died in **1929**.
Orra: "Ten years and neither of them would go up the road first, and now it is settled the way
those things get settled."

Vira Toland was seven: her father read his letter at the table with his hat on, which he never
did, and read it twice; her mother stood at the stove and did not turn round. One woman kept hers
pinned inside the lid of her trunk. Hazel Brant's mother read hers twice and put it in the stove.
Rundle taught the children of both ends of the valley in one schoolroom and watched the quarrel
come down to them entire, unexplained, "the way a debt does."

The count of letters is contested: **nine families** (Vira, Roy Brant, Hazel Brant) against
**eleven** (Orra's contemporaneous entry, written from the man who delivered them). Nine fits six
Hessel Bottom farms plus Strawn's "three places on the hill."

**Documents:** the circular itself (never quoted verbatim anywhere); Orra's diary, June 1919;
Strawn's 1919 circuit entry; Roy Brant's account; Rundle's trouble chapter.

### Story 7. Condemned in 1923, Ruined in 1925 (1923–1934)

**Who.** Merle Strawn; the board of arbitration; Ansel Keddie.

The state began issuing **burettes** to its field men in 1921. Strawn's came in the spring in a
wooden case with a printed sheet of directions. In 1922 he did not run the station's glass against
it — "the day being short and the road home being what it was, and I have put that down because it
is what happened." On **12 June 1923** he did: *"Ran their milk pipettes against the burette. These
measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the
manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them."* The crate went
up the ladder into the loft over the intake room. The order went to Tarnet on 28 June; Ammon &
Sons acknowledged 2 July; new glass was in use in July; in October Strawn ran the new pipettes and
wrote, "They deliver their mark." Kyle, in the office: "There was no more said of it in the office
than there would have been of a broken window." **In 1924 the station's average stood at 3.80.**

In January 1925 the patrons petitioned — "in the winter, when a farmer has time to be angry." A
board of three sat under Alonzo Frick, chairman "because I was the one of the three who kept no
cow": first session at Ashlin **17 March**, then two more at Frick's request in the station's own
weigh room, "so that the board might see the plant it was to pass upon instead of hearing it
described by men with an interest in the description." Cleve Oram stood outside on the platform in
the rain and could hear the voices but not the words. Three explanations were urged — water by
Vose, theft by Teague by insinuation, the hay by way of the 1908 report read into the record — and
none was proved. Four exhibits only: the station test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's
weigh book, and Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook. **The crate was never mentioned in any of the
three sessions**, and it was twenty feet above the room in two of them.

Keddie's nine-sentence statement stands entire: "I have tested as I was taught and as the state
man saw me do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, and I cannot tell you why the
figures are what they are. I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station."

He was put out on **30 April 1925**, four days before the award. On **4 May** the board found "no
cause established" and awarded the patrons **$3,800, without admission**, charged to the station
account. Frick: "There was no finding against him from us and there never was one... He was simply
let go while the board was still sitting, and I have never been able to think well of the
Association for it." And: "a man who cannot account for a figure is presumed by everybody in the
room to be the figure's author."

Orra, 30 April: "Twenty-nine seasons and a letter. He came up the road at his ordinary hour with
his apron rolled under his arm and hung it behind the shed door and asked what there was for
supper. There was ham. He ate it." He did carpentry after — a barn door that swung true for thirty
years — and worked at the cow shed "from light to dark and whistles, which he never did when he
was paid." He died in **February 1934**. "Men shook my hand who had not spoken to him in nine
years, and every one of them said what a hand he was with a door. Not one of them said the other
thing... He said to me once and only once that a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will
name."

**Documents:** Strawn's 1923 notebook (Exhibit 4); the order book and the Ammon letter; the
arbitration minute book and the award; Keddie's statement; Frick's exhibit list; Orra's diary.

### Story 8. The Crate in the Loft (1958–1972)

**Who.** Ivy Keddie, Ansel's granddaughter, born 1931, graduate student at Farland.

In **February 1958** she went up the Hollow to the shut station, up a ladder nailed to the wall,
and found under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff a deal crate stenciled AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY
TARNET, packed in straw. Six dozen had been bought; nine survived. Cleve Oram, who had put his
hand on that crate a hundred times after wire and hay forks, explains the attrition: "a thing in a
loft for thirty-five years gets broken by everybody who goes up after wire."

On **9 March 1958** she measured them at Farland against a calibrated burette, five deliveries
each: mean **16.72 c.c.** against a blown 17.6 — short 0.88, "95 hundredths of the mark, and the
mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for." Every test the station ever made on that glass
read 95% of the truth. 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80.

She assembled five sets of records that had never been in one room: the station test book; the
Brant weigh book (lent by Roy Brant, and *lent* — Hazel insists on the word, and so does Duncan);
the Alder Corners figures copied out by Rosalie Cudd; Strawn's notebooks, at Farland since 1947,
"eleven years in which nobody put a question to them"; and the Association's ledgers. Her framing:
"nothing I am going to show you tonight depends in the smallest degree on my opinion of my
grandfather. It depends on nine pieces of glass and a burette."

**Bulletin No. 471** (October 1958) closes: *"No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was
stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were
low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the
difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own
ledgers, year by year, as station gain."* Vaught's body text contradicts the conclusion he signed
throughout, having transposed 16.72 into 16.27 and propagated it (0.9244, 3.905, 0.295, 118,000
lb) — and he notices in print that his figure "is not readily reconciled with the station's own
average of 3.80" and publishes anyway.

The retellings then multiply the errors. The *Ashlin Chronicle* (3 Oct 1958) got the shape right
and the details wrong: "great-niece," eleven pipettes, 16.27, $19,600, $77.39, the 1908 committee
"sitting again" in 1925, and a fraud charge against Ammon & Sons. Falke's county report (1959)
repeated the great-niece, then verified and corrected it. Rundle added one footnote to his 1959
reprint and declined to rewrite the pamphlet. Ivy gave her talk to the Historical Society in 1959;
the test book and crate went to the Historical Rooms that year, the weigh book by way of Hazel
Brant in 1962–63, the Alder Corners book to Vessey County in 1970, and Nym catalogued them in
1972. Duncan's 1970 postscript: "the five books are now in three buildings and are none of them
ours."

The lag: glass condemned 1923, cause found 1958 — thirty-five years; dismissed 1925 — thirty-three;
dead since 1934 — twenty-four. Hazel Brant: "A thing you could have settled in an afternoon with a
burette and a straight face, and it took fifty years and a girl from the college who thought to
climb a ladder."

**Documents:** the measurement sheet of 9 March 1958; Bulletin No. 471; Nym's accession notes;
Duncan Keddie's list of the books; the *Chronicle*; Falke's report.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** Duncan Keddie's 1968 family notes settle it
from inside the household: "I am Duncan, born in 1894, their son... My daughter is Ivy, born in
1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already
twice seen it printed otherwise." Ivy herself says "my grandfather" throughout her 1959 talk and
dates the lag as "he died in 1934 and had been dead twenty-four years when the sheet was read that
clears him." Against this stand the *Ashlin Chronicle* ("Miss Keddie is his great-niece") and
Falke's report ¶2 ("the great-niece of the tester") — but these are **not two sources**: both were
written within months of the same college press release, the *Chronicle* admittedly "put together
in a day and a half from a college press release and two telephone calls," and Falke, having
written to Farland, appended a correction: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is
the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." Method: a participant's
own genealogy plus a source that checked and retracted, against a single derived error printed
twice.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins.** The derivation is given identically by
both families. Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie were brother and sister; Emmet's son Roy and Orra's son
Duncan are therefore first cousins, "the children of a brother and a sister, which is the whole
definition"; Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy, being the children of first cousins,
are second cousins. Roy Brant explained it twice, Duncan sets it out to be copied, and Hazel says
it herself: "People are forever telling me she is my niece or my cousin once removed... It is not
complicated; it is only that nobody wants to count." Rundle's 1959 footnote agrees. Vira Toland's
"first cousin once removed" and Cleve Oram's repetition of the same phrase are the valley's
version, and they are not independent of each other in any way that matters — neither can state
the line.

**What the sources leave open in the intervening generations:** (i) whether Orra was Emmet's
**full** sister or half sister. Roy Brant says full — "same mother and same father, whatever you
may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it" — and Duncan says "Emmet Brant's
sister, the younger of the two," and Orra herself writes "my brother Emmet" without qualification;
but Hazel Brant, Roy's own daughter, gives the stepmother version explicitly ("a half sister to my
grandfather Emmet, by his father's second wife"). I resolve for full siblings — Roy rebuts the
story by name, and Orra's son is a generation nearer — but note that half-blood would not alter
the degree of cousinship either way. (ii) Orra's birth year: the diary's preface and her own
"I am sixty-four" in August 1930 give **1866**; Duncan writes 1868 and then refutes himself by
saying she was eighty-two when she died in 1948.

**B3. Selby Vose was Gideon Vose's son.** Selby says so from the platform in 1926 — "My father, who
was your first president and who wrote the by-law under which every patron in this Association is
paid on the test of the station where he delivers" — and Loomis, Duncan Keddie and Bertram Kyle
all say son; Kyle adds, "the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story
twice in the last year and it is nonsense." Vira and Rundle carry the nephew version, Rundle even
attributing to Selby a saying about "his uncle" — the valley's version, twice attested and wrong.

**Why it matters:** the by-law that made the station's test unappealable and the manager who
enforced its consequences were father and son. Selby signed the annual station sheet carrying the
station-gain line every year, told Teague in 1916 to let the question alone, sent the 1919 water
circular, put the watering theory to the board in 1925 "without heat and without proof," and in
1926 refused the apparatus explanation as "a piece of housekeeping." His proof that the valley
could make rich milk was his father's Jersey herd testing 4.56 in 1910 — a herd at the home place
near Larrow Green, the one place the short glass never reached.

**B4. There was no relationship between the Voses and the Keddies that the sources establish.**
Two narrators assert one: Vira Toland says Orra Keddie was first cousin to old Mrs. Vose —
"everybody knew that and nobody wrote it down" — and Cleve Oram says "the Keddies and the Voses
were cousins of some sort... I could not draw you the line on paper. My mother had it from her
mother." Both are hearsay and neither can state the connection. Against them Duncan Keddie devotes
a numbered section to denying it: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses.
People confused them with the Ashlin Voses... My mother would have told me. She told me a great
deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone
without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it."

Method: a specific, sourced denial by the son of the woman in question, with an explanation of how
the confusion arose, outranks two unspecified traditions. Strictly a negative cannot be proved, so
I record it as: no kinship established, and the affirmative claims are valley talk. It matters
that the *belief* was live, though — Vira's account of why 1919 cut so deep depends on it: "it was
somebody's cousin saying it, and that is a different thing altogether in a place this size."

## Section C — Math

### C1.

**(a) The tests were low by exactly one part in twenty — 5%; every reading was 0.95 of the truth.**

From the glassware alone. The pipettes were blown on the stem for **17.6 c.c.**, the standard
charge of milk in this state and the charge the graduated neck of the test bottle is ruled for.
Ivy Keddie's signed sheet of 9 March 1958 gives five runs each from nine pipettes: 16.70, 16.71,
16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71.

Sum = 150.48; 150.48 ÷ 9 = **16.72 c.c.** mean.

16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95 exactly.** Short by 17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88 c.c.**

Why this transfers straight to the reading: the operator weighs nothing. He reads a column of fat
in a ruled neck and calls it a percentage, and he may do that only because the charge is a fixed
known quantity. A charge 5% short liberates 5% less fat into a neck ruled for the full charge, so
the reading is low **in the exact proportion by which the charge was short** — proportional, not
additive.

*(I reject 16.27 c.c. — Vaught's body text and the* Chronicle*. See E1.)*

**(b) True average 3.80; the recorded tests were short 0.19 of a point.**

3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80.** 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19.** And 0.19 ÷ 3.80 = 5%, confirming that the loss was a
proportion of whatever was there, not a fixed quantity.

Independently corroborated in five places: the 1896 season on borrowed glass (3.80); the nine
weeks of 1911 on a borrowed measure (3.80); 1924 onward on new glass (3.80); the sixteen Alder
Corners summers, 1915–1930, all between 3.78 and 3.82; and Larrow Green's own patrons at 3.78.

**(c) 76,000 lb of butterfat.**

Over the twenty-five full delivery years 1898–1922 the station received **40,000,000 lb of milk**,
averaging 1,600,000 lb a year.

- Credited at 3.61%: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**
- Actually delivered at 3.80%: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lb**
- Difference: **76,000 lb**

Check against the ledger: 1,600,000 × 0.0361 = 57,760 lb; × 0.0380 = 60,800 lb; difference 3,040
lb — exactly the 1898 station-gain entry. 3,040 × 25 = 76,000. The arithmetic and the Association's
own bookkeeping agree to the pound.

**(d) About $19,000; the patrons received one fifth of it.**

Butterfat averaged **25 cents a pound** over the period (Loomis, adding twice from Teague's annual
sheets; Ivy; Roy Brant "one year with another, about twenty-five cents").

76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000.** The award was **$3,800** — exactly **one fifth (20%)**.

*(At Sill's and Kepp's 27 cents the loss would be about $20,500; I use 25 cents because it comes
from the ledger sheets rather than from recollection. I reject $19,600 — Rundle and the* Chronicle
*— see E1.)*

### C2. Emmet Brant, 1910

From the weigh book leaf, quoted identically by Nym and Roy Brant and recited from memory by
Hazel: *"Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and
theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42."*

**(a) Credited:** 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb of butterfat.**

**(b) Owed and not credited:** his true test was 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = **3.60**; 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb.
7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb.**

(Check by proportion: 5% of 7,200 = 360. ✓)

**(c)** Roy Brant gives butterfat at **24 cents in 1910** specifically: 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40.**
At the period average of 25 cents it is $90.00. I prefer $86.40 as the year-specific figure.

Note what this shows: the largest patron on the meadow, in a single ordinary year, lost about the
price of a good implement — "Seventeen hundredths is nothing to look at on one pay slip. Set the
slips in a row for a lifetime of farming and it is a farm."

### C3. Over 1898–1922

**(a) Credited to the patrons: 1,444,000 lb.**
**(b) Reached Larrow Green in the station's cream: 1,520,000 lb.**
**(c) The difference is 76,000 lb, and the percentage depends entirely on the denominator:**

- 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5.00% of the butterfat actually delivered**
- 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.26% — "five and a quarter per cent" — of the butterfat credited**

The ledger's recurring phrase "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" is the **second**: a
bookkeeper reckons a surplus against the figure he has already entered. Neither figure is an
arithmetical error, and Nym states the relation correctly — "five and a quarter percent of the
credited fat is five percent of the fat delivered, and each is right of its own denominator and of
no other."

But **5% is the one that is also the glass.** 16.72/17.6 = 0.95, and the fat missing is exactly
5% of what came down the hill. Teague glossed his own entry as "five and a quarter percent of the
fat that was actually in the cream," which is the wrong denominator, and Vaught repeated the
identical error in Bulletin 471 §V — the one place where getting the denominator right would have
pointed straight at the pipette.

### C4.

**(a) $77.93.** Verify: 25.20 + 31.68 = 56.88; + 0.90 = 57.78; + 18.00 = 75.78; + 2.15 = **77.93.**
Both Junia Ammon and the *Chronicle* render this in words as "seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine
cents," which contradicts the invoice each of them reproduces or has seen. The line items settle
it.

**(b) Six dozen = 72 pipettes**, at .35 each (72 × 0.35 = $25.20 ✓). Each was short **0.88 c.c.**
(17.6 blown − 16.72 delivered). Vaught's "five dozen" and Cleve Oram's "five dozen" are both wrong
against the invoice, which is quoted verbatim three times.

### C5.

**(a) The glass was in use 26 years and about six weeks; the money runs over 25 full delivery
years.**

In use from **1 May 1897** to **12 June 1923** — twenty-six years and six weeks, less the nine-week
interruption of 1911. The money calculation runs over **1898–1922**, twenty-five full delivery
years.

They differ because the two end years are broken. 1897 is a part year — the station's own summary
leaf says "1897, part year," the glass having gone in on 1 May — and 1923 was cut in half on 12
June, with new glass from July. Neither is a whole year of deliveries at the recorded 3.61, so
neither can carry a full year's 1,600,000 lb through the arithmetic. The Bulletin's *conclusion*
dates the fault by the glass (1 May 1897 – 12 June 1923); the *loss* is reckoned by whole years.
Both figures are correct about different things, and they are not in conflict.

**(b) Cannot be determined from the sources.**

The intake for 4 May – 31 December 1896 is in no ledger. Nym is explicit: it "is not in the
ledgers and is nowhere else... inquirers wanting a figure for 1896 must be told there is none" —
the station account column does not begin until 1897, and the first season was carried on the
creamery's own books, whose sheets were never put with the station papers (Kyle confirms this from
the office side). Two recollections survive and they do not agree: **Sill says about 900,000 lb**,
**Kyle says "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds."** Both are recollection, not record. Sill's is
the better-placed of the two — he lent the glass the station opened on and weighed its cream in
daily from that season — while Kyle came into the office after the station opened and had his
figure "from men who were there." So: no determinable figure; if one must be quoted, Sill's
~900,000 lb, flagged as recollection.

## Section D — Logic

### D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment

**When and how long.** From **8 May 1911**, when the station's own pipette broke and a borrowed
measure was put in its place, to **10 July 1911**, when its own measure returned: **nine weeks.**
Both ends are dated entries in the tester's own hand, and both are independently confirmed —
Orra's diary for 8 May ("A. home cross about a broken measure") and the 1922 summary leaf, which
records the whole valley's average standing at 3.80 for "the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in
their place."

**The variable:** exactly one object, the measuring pipette. **Held constant:** the same patrons,
the same herds, the same pasture, the same season, the same acid, the same test bottles, the same
scales, the same building, the same tester and the same habits, the same hour and order of work.
**The result:** the station average rose from 3.61 to 3.80 and fell back to 3.61 *on the day* the
station's own measure returned. Roy Brant confirms it from the receiving end — every farm on the
Bottom came up together and went down together.

**What it establishes that calculation cannot.** The 1958 measurement tells you what nine surviving
pipettes deliver in 1958 and lets you compute what such glass would have done. The 1911 interval
shows the effect being switched off and switched back on in real time, under a contemporaneous
record, by a man who did not know what he was recording and set a question mark against it. It is
a controlled intervention rather than an inference, and it disposes at a stroke of every
explanation that looks to the herds, the ground, the feed, the water in the cans or the men — for
the sufficient reason that none of those was altered in those nine weeks, and the figures were.
Vaught: "Here is one that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and
lay unread for forty-seven years."

**What it does not establish.** (i) It does not prove the borrowed measure was *accurate*; it
proves the two measures differed. That 3.80 is the true figure comes from elsewhere — 1896, 1924,
Alder Corners, Larrow Green. (ii) It does not identify the mechanism: it shows the instrument
matters, not that the fault was short *delivery* rather than some other defect. (iii) It fixes no
magnitude for any other year; the constancy of 0.19 across 1898–1922 is established by the test
book and the pay slips, not by these nine weeks. (iv) It says nothing about which measure was
borrowed or from where — Ivy, Teague and Roy Brant give three incompatible accounts and Strawn's
own June 1911 entry reads "Nothing here to note." (v) It cannot, by itself, exclude that some
*second* cause also operated; it only shows one cause sufficient to produce the whole swing.

### D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream

**Reason 1 — the cream arrived heavy, every year (Sill, at the churn; and the Association's own
ledger).** Sill weighed the Hollow's cream in himself, over again after the platform man, for
forty-one years: "There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there...
Always more. Never less." Stolen cream arrives light. This one **holds without the 1958
measurement**, and it held every day from 1897; Rundle proved the point from the ledgers in 1939,
nineteen years before Bulletin 471: "Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green
light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand."

**Reason 2 — the nine weeks of 1911.** The same man, in the same room, alone, under no different
supervision, produced 3.80 for nine weeks and 3.61 on either side of them, with the changeover
falling on the day a piece of glass was substituted. No theft turns itself off in May and on again
in July at the convenience of a broken pipette.

**Reason 3 — the milk itself tested 3.80 over the ridge.** Jerome Cudd, in another county, on his
own bench, with his own glass, tested the same four Hessel Bottom men's milk at 3.78–3.82 across
sixteen summers. That shows the milk really was about 3.80 — so the deficiency was in the
*reading*, not in a missing quantity of fat. Combined with Reason 1, which shows the fat was all
present at Larrow Green, there is no quantity anywhere for a thief to have taken.

**Reason 4 (supporting) — the shape of the error.** The shortfall was an exact proportion, 5% of
every test, so that the richest herds lost most and the ratios between herds stayed perfect. Theft
by dipping is quantitative and scattered; it does not scale itself to the fat content of each
patron's sample for twenty-six years.

**Why these are independent.** They come from four custodians who had no contact and no common
interest: Sill at Larrow Green's churn; Keddie's own book at the station; Cudd's book in Vessey
County (Rosalie: nobody from over the ridge ever asked a plain question about their books in
thirty-odd years, and comparing figures with a station seven miles off "never entered his head");
and the 1958 glass. Roy Brant's weigh book adds a fifth strand — two scales kept by two men who
had stopped speaking agreed for thirty years — which eliminates false weighing.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question the entries that refute him?** Essentially no. Teague *created*
the station-gain line in 1905 and wrote it every year of his service; he raised the question with
Vose **once, in 1916**, was told to let it alone, and never returned to it — "I was the
Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience." He then explained his own evenness away ("A
figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture") and in 1925 put the theft
theory to the board by insinuation, while the column in his own ledger proved that the fat had all
arrived. Kyle copied the same line for twenty-five years and says it "never once crossed my mind
to wonder at it, and I do not believe it crossed anybody else's either." Nobody laid Exhibit 2, the
receiving book, beside Exhibit 4, the inspector's notebook.

### D3. Did the manager know the tests ran low before 1923?

**Yes — that the Ostrey Hollow tests ran persistently low he certainly knew, and had known for
years; what he did not know was why, and he had decided in advance what the answer was.**

The evidence: (i) He signed the annual station sheet every year — Teague: "a man who signs a sheet
has had the sheet in front of him. What he made of it he never told me." (ii) Teague put the
station-gain question to him directly in **1916** and was told to let it alone; that is knowledge
plus a decision. (iii) The **1919 circular** is itself proof: a warning that added water "would be
looked for and would be dealt with," sent to the head of the valley, presupposes that he knew the
Hollow's figures ran thin — he says so in 1926: "A manager who sees his figures going one way for
twenty years and says nothing to the men who make them is not being kind to them." (iv) Strawn
told him in 1919 that he had tested for water and found none; Vose was still advancing watering in
1926, and his address never mentions the lactometer. (v) He put the water theory to the board in
1925 "without heat and without proof."

The qualification comes from Kyle, who sat outside his door for twenty-five years: "he read the
totals and he never read the lines above them... He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on
the bottom figure, and sign." So the honest answer is layered: he knew the Hollow tested low, he
knew the question had been raised in his office and had closed it, and there is no evidence he
ever grasped what the station-gain line *meant* — that the missing fat was arriving in his own
creamery every day and being banked as a gain. Knowing the low tests, he chose an explanation that
put the fault on the patrons, and after 1923 he defended it against the apparatus: "Apparatus
wears... I decline to build a mystery on a piece of housekeeping."

## Section E — Contradictions

### E1(a). Cases where TWO narrators carry the SAME wrong value

These are the traps, and in every case what breaks the tie is that the two carriers are **not
independent** — they are drawing from one bad channel — while the correct value is fixed by a
document quoted verbatim, or by arithmetic that closes.

1. **Mean delivery 16.27 c.c.** — Vaught's body text (Bulletin 471 §V) and the *Ashlin Chronicle*.
   **Broken by:** Ivy's signed measurement sheet, quoted identically twice, listing nine readings
   that average 16.72; and by arithmetic — 16.27 gives 3.905 as the true average, which cannot be
   reconciled with the 3.80 recorded in 1896, in 1911 and from 1924. Vaught *himself flags* the
   irreconcilability in print and publishes anyway; the *Chronicle* took its figure from the
   college release, i.e. from Vaught. One source, printed twice. **16.72 is right.**

2. **Eleven pipettes recovered** — Rundle's 1959 footnote and the *Chronicle*. **Broken by:** the
   measurement sheet (nine readings), Nym Item 5, Vaught's own §III, Falke's report ("the nine
   surviving pipettes"), and Cleve Oram, who had climbed to the crate ("there were nine of them
   left in it at the end"). **Nine.**

3. **The award of $3,500** — Vira Toland and Cleve Oram. **Broken by:** the award itself, quoted
   verbatim by Loomis and Nym, and given as $3,800 by Ivy, Teague, Vose, Frick and Falke. Both
   carriers are recalling valley talk decades later. **$3,800.**

4. **67,000 lb of butterfat** — Frick (as "the patrons' own reckoning") and Cleve Oram. **Broken
   by:** it is a digit transposition of 76,000, and 76,000 closes against the ledger (3,040 × 25).
   Notable, though, that the patrons in 1925 reached within 12% of the truth from their own pay
   slips, thirty-three years before anyone could say why.

5. **$19,600 loss** — Rundle and the *Chronicle*. **Broken by:** 76,000 lb × $0.25 = $19,000. The
   *Chronicle* also compounds it by attaching the figure to "a valley of six farms," conflating the
   bottom farms with the nine circularized families.

6. **"Seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents"** — Junia Ammon and the *Chronicle*. **Broken
   by:** the invoice both of them reproduce or have seen; the line items sum to **$77.93**. A
   transposition of the last two digits, made twice.

7. **Glass into use 1 May 1898** — Vaught's body text and Bertram Kyle. **Broken by:** the invoice
   of 14 April 1897, Bulletin 471's own conclusion, Sill's account of his glass being returned in
   the spring of 1897, and decisively by **Orra Keddie's diary entry of 1 May 1897**: "A. says the
   station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green." A contemporaneous
   record beats two later reconstructions. **1 May 1897.**

8. **Five dozen pipettes bought** — Vaught's body text and Cleve Oram. **Broken by:** the invoice,
   "6 doz. milk test pipettes... at .35 — 25.20," and by 72 × 0.35 = 25.20. **Six dozen.**

9. **Condemned 12 July 1923** — Vose and Rundle. **Broken by:** Strawn's own dated entry (12 June),
   quoted twice, plus Bulletin 471 and Nym; and it is *impossible*, since Ammon & Sons acknowledged
   the replacement order on 2 July. Note the error flatters the Association by shortening the gap
   between condemnation and remedy.

10. **The station average 3.16** — Teague and Frick. **Broken by:** the test book, quoted verbatim
    three times, and by Teague's own ledger entry (57,760 ÷ 1,600,000 = 3.61%), which refutes his
    own text. A transposition that evidently circulated among the Ashlin men. **3.61.**

11. **"Six weeks" for the 1911 interval** — Teague and Kepp. **Broken by:** the two dated entries,
    8 May and 10 July, which are nine weeks. Kepp compounds it into "six weeks of June and July,"
    moving the start date to fit his flush argument.

12. **Total intake 44,000,000 lb** — Teague and Rundle. **Broken by:** Loomis, who added it twice
    from Teague's own annual sheets, plus Ivy, Nym, Vaught and Kyle, all at 40,000,000; and by
    consistency — 40,000,000 is what makes 3,040 lb/yr and 76,000 lb close.

13. **Bulletin 214 found "two tenths in the winter feeding"** — Grigg (1919) and Kepp (1926).
    **Broken by:** the bulletin's summary quoted verbatim and identically by Ivy, Nym, Vaught *and*
    Falke: "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be
    measured on summer pasture." Kepp is not independent of Grigg; he is repeating the county's
    circulating version. This single misquotation, with the summer clause dropped, is what let the
    hay theory survive for fifty years.

14. **Selby Vose as Gideon's nephew** — Vira and Rundle (Kyle reports hearing it twice more).
    **Broken by:** Selby's own words from the platform, plus Loomis, Duncan and Kyle. **Son.**

15. **Ivy as "first cousin once removed" to Hazel** — Vira and Cleve Oram. **Broken by:** Roy
    Brant, Duncan Keddie, Hazel Brant and Rundle's footnote. **Second cousins.**

16. **Ivy as Ansel's "great-niece"** — the *Chronicle* and Falke. **Broken by:** Duncan Keddie's
    family notes and Ivy's own usage; and Falke retracts on verification. Both carriers derive from
    the same press release.

17. **A Keddie–Vose kinship** — Vira ("first cousin to old Mrs. Vose") and Cleve Oram ("cousins of
    some sort"). **Broken by:** Duncan Keddie's explicit denial and his account of the confusion
    with the Ashlin Voses. Neither carrier can state the line.

18. **The post-1923 average as 3.85** — Sill (as his estimate of Hollow milk) and Falke (as a
    statement of fact, "From 1924 the station's average test stood at three eighty-five"). **Broken
    by:** the test book's 3.80 for 1924, and by 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80 exactly. Falke has probably
    absorbed the valley's figure; Sill's was always an impression.

## Section F — Theory

### F1. What actually happened

**The theory in one causal chain.** At Tarnet in the winter of 1896–97 a mold at Ammon & Sons was
reground, and a lot of milk-test pipettes was blown a little small. Nobody caught it, because the
one check a glass house runs — gauge the first, the tenth, the fiftieth — tests for *scatter*, and
this lot was perfectly consistent. It was consistently wrong. Six dozen of them were invoiced to
the Larrow Valley Association on 14 April 1897 for its Ostrey Hollow station, at $77.93 for the
whole outfit, and went into use on 1 May 1897.

Each pipette was blown for 17.6 c.c. and delivered 16.72 — **0.95 of its mark, one part in twenty
short.** In the Babcock test the operator weighs nothing: he reads a column of fat off a neck ruled
for a *standard charge*, and calls the length a percentage. Put in 5% less milk and 5% less fat
rises into a neck that knows nothing of the substitution. **The reading is not merely low; it is
low in the exact proportion by which the charge was short.**

- **Why that size.** 0.88 c.c. out of 17.6 — because that is how much small the mold was. Nothing
  about the farms, the season or the tester enters into it. 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80; the 0.19 gap is
  5% of 3.80.
- **Why it began when it did.** 1 May 1897, the day the station stopped borrowing Sill's glass and
  started on its own. That is why 1896 — the borrowed year — averaged 3.80.
- **Why it ended when it did.** 12 June 1923, when Merle Strawn, carrying a state-issued burette
  since 1921, finally ran the station's pipettes against it and condemned the crate. New glass in
  July; 3.80 from 1924. Not because anything changed on the farms, and not because of the petition
  — the rise preceded the January 1925 petition by a year.
- **Why nothing had to change at either end.** The cause was in the measure itself. **An instrument
  that is itself the standard cannot be caught by measuring with it.** Scales can be checked against
  test weights, acid against a titration, bottle necks with mercury, crank speed by counting — but
  in the ordinary practice of the trade the pipette *is* what the plant measures with. So the
  station's figures were orderly, internally consistent, and beautifully kept: its herds ranked
  correctly against one another, and only against the outside world was it wrong. The error was
  proportional (so the best milk lost most — a herd at 4.56 short 0.24, one at 3.42 short 0.18),
  precise (so it never scattered and never drew attention — "It has the appearance of excellent
  work"), and invisible from inside the building.

Over the twenty-five full delivery years 1898–1922, on 40,000,000 lb of milk, the patrons were
credited 1,444,000 lb of butterfat and delivered 1,520,000 lb. The 76,000 lb difference — about
$19,000 at 25 cents — arrived at Larrow Green in the cream every year, was weighed twice by the
buttermaker, was entered by the bookkeeper as "station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as
usual," and was carried to the general account. **The Association banked the shortfall and gave it
a name, and a figure with a name on it has been explained.**

### The three competing explanations, and what defeats each

**1. Water in the cans — advanced by Selby Vose**, the manager, who circulated the 1919 warning,
put it to the board in 1925 "without heat and without proof," and was still advancing it in 1926;
seconded by Sill in 1934 ("You cannot tell me nine families all got honest in one summer").
*Defeated by:* Strawn's lactometer, run at that station on every visit from 1907 and never once
finding added water — and he told the manager so in 1919, the very year the circular went out;
Rosalie Cudd's flat refutation from the vat ("The curd comes soft and it will not knit... Butter is
more forgiving that way. **Cheese is a witness**") over sixteen summers with never a complaint or a
docking; the 1911 interval, in which the same nine families' milk tested 3.80 for nine weeks and
3.61 on either side; and the sheer implausibility of nine households starting and stopping in
concert on the day a pipette broke.

**2. Theft by the tester — advanced by Wilbur Teague**, the bookkeeper, at the 1925 hearing, "in
the way an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it"; it was also the valley's
settled opinion, printed as certain by Rundle in 1939 and held to the end by Vira Toland and by
Cleve Oram's father. *Defeated by:* the cream arriving **heavy** at Larrow Green every year, never
light — Sill's own hand on every page from 1893, and the Association's own station-gain column,
from which Rundle demolished his own Chapter IV in 1939; by the 1911 interval, where the same man
alone in the same room produced honest figures for nine weeks; and by the proportional shape of
the error, which no thief could counterfeit.

**3. The wet hay of Hessel Bottom — advanced by the 1908 committee under Nyle Grigg**, read into
the record at the 1925 hearing, and given a scientific dress by Roland Kepp in 1926. *Defeated by:*
Bulletin No. 214's actual finding — "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and
nothing that could be measured on summer pasture" — against a gap that stood at a flat 0.19 in
July as in February, in wet years and dry, for twenty-six years. It is four times too small and it
is the wrong shape. Also by Rosalie Cudd's sixteen summers at 3.78–3.82 off that same ground; by
the 1911 interval; and by the exposure of Kepp's "triple convergence" as one misquotation (Grigg's
inflation of 0.05 into 0.20, with the fatal summer clause silently dropped), one field impression
and one rumour. Falke read the bulletin in 1959 and withdrew his concession in print.

### Subsidiary questions

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware? No — and it came remarkably close.**
The board sat three times, two of them in the station's own weigh room at Frick's request, and
watched a test made — but the station had been on **new** glass for nearly two years, so what it
watched was faultless. Its exhibits were four books, one of which, Exhibit 4, was Strawn's 1923
circuit notebook containing "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the
whole crate of them." Loomis, who took down every word, records that **the crate was never
mentioned in any of the three sessions, by the patrons, by the officers, or by the board.** The
award's phrase "the books and appliances of the station" she calls "a form of words the board used
and not a list of anything," and Frick's own four-item exhibit list confirms her. The crate itself
was up a ladder in the loft, twenty feet above the men's heads, in the two sittings held at the
station.

**What did the tester himself believe?** The sources give two answers and do not reconcile them.
Duncan Keddie, who was seventeen and in the kitchen, says his father came home in the summer of
1911 **saying it was something in the glass, and said it twice** — and would not write it in the
book, because he would not set down what he could not prove; what he wrote was a question mark,
and he let it stand for fourteen years. Frick, expressly off the minutes, says that in the corridor
at Ashlin in 1925 Keddie told him privately that **he had always thought the patrons were at
fault** and would not say so in the room, having lived among them thirty years. Both are private
statements reported at second hand by men with no motive to invent. The best reading is that they
are fourteen years apart: an unprovable suspicion of the glass in 1911, hardening into the
manager's version — the one that ruined him — by the time he had spent another fourteen years
writing down figures he could not explain. The contemporaneous trace, the "?" in his own hand,
belongs to the first.

## Section G — Summary (150 words max)

**148 words.**

A cooperative creamery paid its farmers by butterfat test, and its by-law made each station's test
final. In 1897 the Ostrey Hollow station bought pipettes blown for 17.6 c.c. that delivered 16.72
— exactly a twentieth short. For twenty-six years every test there read 95% of the truth: 3.61
where the milk was 3.80. The missing 76,000 lb of butterfat, about $19,000, came down in the cream
anyway and was banked as "station gain." Three explanations were argued — watered milk, a thieving
tester, wet hay — and each was refuted by a record nobody consulted. The tester was dismissed in
1925 and died under it in 1934. In 1958 his granddaughter found the condemned crate in a loft and
measured it. It took eight records because no single one contained the answer: the proof lay
scattered across a farm ledger, a rival cheesemaker's book, an inspector's notebook and a glass
firm's invoice.
