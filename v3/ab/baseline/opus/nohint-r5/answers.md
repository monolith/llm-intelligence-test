## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1. The Association, its paying rule, and the station at Ostrey Hollow (1889–1896)

**Who:** Gideon Vose, who organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, served as its first president and wrote its by-laws; the farmers of the Larrow Valley; and Ansel Keddie (1861–1934), the station's tester and separator man.

**What happened:** Valley farmers met at Larrow Green and organized the Association in 1889. It built its main creamery there in 1891. By-law Article 7 made every patron's pay depend on the pounds of butterfat his milk contained, as found by the test at the station where he delivered, and not on the weight of the milk (Falke, r22). The printed by-laws carry no date and the early minute book was lost, so the year Article 7 was adopted cannot be determined. Duncan Keddie (r18) says Gideon wrote the by-laws. So the rule that later turned a short piece of glass into lost money came from the Voses. Under payment by weight, the glass would have cost nobody anything.

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green, to serve Hessel Bottom and the upper valley. It opened on **4 May 1896** (Falke). Milk was weighed in, sampled, tested for fat and separated. The cream was hauled down to Larrow Green, and the skim went back to the farmer (Falke; Chronicle r19; Cleve Oram r21, who describes the building, the waist-high platform, the intake/weigh room and the loft reached by a ladder). Ansel Keddie was the tester from the opening and held the post for **twenty-nine seasons**, 1896 through 1924 (Falke; Orra's diary preface r24). Hazel's "went up in ninety-five" (r17) probably refers to his appointment while the station was being built. Duncan (r18) says Ansel kept the test book from 1896.

For the first season the station borrowed its testing glass from the Larrow Green creamery (Kyle r23; Orra, 1 May 1897: "done borrowing of Larrow Green"). Kyle says the first season's milk was carried on the creamery's own books and never put with the station papers. His "good eleven hundred thousand pounds" is admitted hearsay and "not in any book I ever ruled."

**Quantities:** seven miles, station to creamery; opening 4 May 1896; a 29-season tenure; first-season receipts undocumented.

**Documents:** r22 (Falke), r18 (Duncan), r19 (Chronicle), r21 (Oram), r23 (Kyle), r24 (Orra).

### Story 2. Six dozen pipettes from Tarnet: Ammon & Sons and the station's outfit (1897; 1923; 1949)

**Who:** Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet (founder Marcus Ammon; the firm's historian Junia Ammon, r20), and the Larrow Valley Association.

**What happened:** On **14 April 1897** Ammon & Sons invoiced the Association "for the Ostrey Hollow station" for a complete testing outfit. The invoice is reproduced in facsimile in Junia Ammon's 1949 firm history (r20): 6 doz. milk-test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. at $0.35 = $25.20; 12 doz. test bottles (8% neck) at $0.22 = $31.68; one acid measure, $0.90; one four-bottle hand tester, $18.00; freight, $2.15. **Total $77.93.** The lines add to $77.93 and the invoice's own total line reads $77.93. Junia's running text and the Chronicle both say "$77.39," which is a transposition. The outfit went into use on **1 May 1897** (Junia; Orra's diary of that date). Kyle's "1898" is rejected.

The pipettes were marked 17.6 c.c., the standard milk charge for which the test bottle's neck is ruled, but they delivered only about **16.72 c.c.**, **0.88 c.c. (5%) short**. That figure is reconstructed by arithmetic (see C1). The Chronicle's "16.27" is another transposition. A short charge in a correctly ruled bottle reads low by exactly the same proportion, every time.

After the state inspector condemned the pipettes (Story 7), the Association wrote to Ammon on **28 June 1923** for six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles. The reply of **2 July 1923** entered the order and "regret[ted] the trouble," but said the firm's "books of the nineties" could tell nothing about the lot. The works books, which recorded glass, gauge, man and day, burned in **1931**. Junia regrets the letter and offers a guess, not a finding: a reground mold or a re-hung gauge made the lot run consistently short, and the checks missed it because the pieces were consistent with one another. The Chronicle's charge that the firm "knowingly" sold a short lot is editorial and unsupported. Whether anyone at Ammon knew cannot be determined. Junia also writes that the house sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900, which her own 1897 invoice disproves.

**Documents:** r20 (primary: the invoice and letter quoted), r19, r24, r23, r22.

### Story 3. Hessel Bottom, the Brant weigh book, and the two families

**Who:** Emmet Brant, the largest patron; Orra Brant Keddie (b. 1866, d. 1948), his sister and Ansel's wife; Roy Brant, Emmet's son; Duncan Keddie (b. 1894), Ansel and Orra's son and a schoolteacher at Ashlin; Hazel Brant, Roy's daughter; Ivy Keddie (b. 1931), Duncan's daughter.

**What happened:** Hessel Bottom was **six farms** on wet ground, and Emmet farmed the largest (Hazel r17; Duncan r18). Ansel married Orra in **1889**, so the tester accused of shorting the patrons was married to the sister of the patron who lost most by it. Emmet kept a private **weigh book**, weighing his milk on his own scale before the station weighed it and writing both figures side by side. Duncan says he began it on 1 January 1897; Hazel says 1900, though she admits she keeps no years. I accept Duncan's date. The book passed to Roy on Emmet's death in **1929**. Roy **lent** it to Ivy in **1958**, and it went to Hazel at his death in **1962**. She gave it to the Historical Rooms at Ashlin in **1963**.

The **1910 leaf**, the one year Hazel is sure of, records **200,000 lb** of milk delivered, the scales agreeing, paid on a test of **3.42**. Scale weights always agreed, so the weight was never the problem; the fat test was. At the true test of 3.60 (3.42 ÷ 0.95), Emmet was credited with 6,840 lb of fat that year against 7,200 lb actually delivered, **360 lb short**, about $90 at the $0.25 long-run average price.

Emmet was a sibling of Orra, so Roy and Duncan are first cousins and **Hazel and Ivy are second cousins**. Cleve Oram's "first cousin once removed" is wrong. Hazel calls Orra Emmet's *half* sister; Duncan calls her his younger full sister. The sources cannot settle which. Kinship did not stop the quarrel. Orra's diary shows Emmet leaving the house in June 1919 over the water circular, never returning, and signing the January 1925 petition. The two families were reconciled only by his death in spring 1929.

**Documents:** r17 (Hazel), r18 (Duncan), r24 (Orra), r21 (Oram, the erroneous kinship), r23 (Kyle: the weigh book was lent for the arbitration).

### Story 4. Thirty years of suspicion: complaint, the hay committee, the water circular (1902–1919)

**Who:** the Ostrey Hollow patrons; an Association committee (1908); Selby Vose, manager from 1911 and Gideon's son; Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector.

**What happened:** From early on the station's milk tested low. The long-run average was **3.61**, against 3.78 at Larrow Green, and the true value was **3.80**. The deficit was **0.19 points**, the same in every month, flush and winter alike (Falke; Hazel: "flat as a floor"). The first recorded complaint came in **1902** (Falke). Orra's March 1902 entry has store talk, and Ansel: "it is my figures they will look at." The complaints were renewed at every annual meeting for about a quarter century (Chronicle).

In **January–March 1908** four men from an Association committee came up in Grigg's sleigh, examined the herds starting with Emmet's, looked at barns and not at the station, and blamed the **wet bog hay** of the bottom (Orra; Falke). A veterinarian at Ashlin repeated the hay theory in 1926. The college's own feeding trial, **Bulletin No. 214 (1911)**, found that bog hay lowered the test by at most 0.05 in winter and by nothing measurable on summer pasture. That cannot account for 0.19 in July, and Falke withdrew his concession to the hay theory.

Four of the six Hessel Bottom farms sent their milk over the ridge to Jerome Cudd's cheese factory at Alder Corners during the summer flush and to the station the rest of the year (Hazel). The same milk tested normally there, so it was never the cows. Rosalie Cudd, Jerome's daughter, later copied that test book for Ivy.

In **June 1919** manager Selby Vose sent the patrons a **circular warning against watering** the milk. Kyle typed it and Vose alone signed it. Ansel would not mail it and carried it door to door himself, to **eleven families** according to Orra's contemporaneous diary (Hazel remembers nine). Hazel's mother burned hers. Strawn tested with the lactometer at every visit from 1908 on and **never found added water** (Falke).

**Documents:** r22, r24, r17, r19, r23, r18.

### Story 5. Nine weeks in 1911: the borrowed measure

**Who:** Ansel Keddie, with his wife Orra and son Duncan as witnesses at home.

**What happened:** On **8 May 1911** the station's own measuring glass broke (Falke r22; Orra's diary that day: "A. home cross about a broken measure"). A measure borrowed from elsewhere was used until **10 July 1911**, about nine weeks. Throughout those weeks the station's tests stood **markedly higher**. On the day the station went back to its own glass, they **fell back to their former level**. The herds, pasture, season, tester, machinery and supervision were all the same. Ansel noted the rise in his test book and put a **question mark** against it (Falke; Duncan).

Duncan, then seventeen, remembers his father coming home that summer saying "it was something in the glass," twice, at supper and again by the door. He never wrote the suspicion in the book, because he would not record what he could not prove. Set against it he had only the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county. He went on testing with the same glass for fourteen more years and recorded every figure it gave, and those figures were later used to convict him (Duncan r18).

This was, in effect, a controlled experiment. Only the glass changed, and the result followed the glass both ways, up when it was removed and down when it came back. It rules out water, theft and hay as the cause of the deficit, since the same men, cows and hay were present throughout. It did not identify the fault (pipette or bottle) or measure it, and nobody outside the Keddie household acted on it. Kyle, who handled the correspondence for the hearing, says nothing of it. Orra's October 1911 entry records Duncan leaving to teach at Ashlin at seventeen, which fits Duncan's age at the time.

**Quantities:** 8 May – 10 July 1911 (about nine weeks); higher tests during the interval, which Falke does not quantify; the standing deficit it exposed was 0.19 points.

**Documents:** r22, r24, r18.

### Story 6. The "station gain": the answer in the ledger (1897–1923)

**Who:** Bertram Kyle, the clerk at the outer desk of the Association office at Larrow Green for twenty-five years; Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper; Selby Vose, manager 1911 to about 1936.

**What happened:** Every year the Ostrey Hollow station account carried three figures and a sentence: the butterfat credited to patrons on the station test; the butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in the station's cream; and the difference, entered as **"station gain … five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account"** (Kyle r23). More fat reached the creamery than the station test said the patrons had delivered, and the difference was booked as the Association's gain.

For the arbitration Kyle assembled the long-run totals: **40,000,000 lb of milk** (1,600,000 lb a year over 25 full years), and butterfat at an average of **$0.25/lb**. At the recorded 3.61 test, patrons were credited with **1,444,000 lb** of fat. At the true 3.80, the cream carried **1,520,000 lb**. The **76,000 lb** difference is 5.26% of the credited fat ("five and a quarter") and exactly 5% of the fat received, which is the pipettes' 5% shortfall. The uncredited fat was worth **$19,000**.

This entry refutes the theft charge outright. A tester stealing cream would make the cream at Larrow Green come in *below* the credited fat, not above it. Teague wrote the line every year and "never asked anybody anything in forty years." Vose read only totals ("thumb on the bottom figure, and sign"). The line was read out at every annual meeting. "It was not a secret. It was worse than a secret. It was in plain sight with a name on it." Kyle's own dates drift, because he keeps time by the work on his desk (1898, '22, '24). The corrected dates are 1897, 1923 and 1925.

**Documents:** r23 (primary), with the arithmetic checked against r22 (3.61, 0.19) and r19.

### Story 7. Condemnation (1923), arbitration and dismissal (1925), and Ansel's last years

**Who:** Merle Strawn, the state inspector, related to no one in the valley; the board of arbitration, chaired by an Ashlin attorney; Ansel and Orra Keddie; Emmet Brant.

**What happened:** On **12 June 1923**, on an ordinary circuit visit, Strawn ran the station's pipettes against a calibrated burette, found they did not deliver their mark, **condemned the whole lot**, and told the manager to order new ones. He recorded the condemnation in his notebook but drew no inference about the years before. The order went to Tarnet on 28 June, and new glass was in use from **July 1923** (Falke; Junia; Orra, July 1923: "new glass at the station"). The old glass was crated and put in the loft over the intake room. **From 1924 the station tested 3.85**, with the same tester, farms and cows. Nobody connected the two events.

The patrons petitioned in **January 1925**, Emmet among the signers. The board sat from **17 March to 4 May 1925**, first at Ashlin and then for two of its three sittings in the station's own weigh room, twenty feet below the crate (Orra; Oram; Duncan). Four books went before it (Kyle): Keddie's test book (impounded in March), the receiving book, Emmet's weigh book, and Strawn's 1923 notebook. Nobody set the receiving book beside Strawn's notebook. An Association officer urged the theft theory. Ansel was **dismissed on 30 April 1925**. Four days later the board found "no cause established" and awarded the patrons **$3,800 "without admission,"** charged to the station account. That award is **one-fifth** of the $19,000 actually lost.

Ansel went home and hung his apron behind the shed door, then worked as a carpenter for a quarter of his old pay. He died in **February 1934** with the charge never withdrawn and never named: "a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name." Orra died in 1948.

**Documents:** r22, r24, r23, r18, r21, r20, r19. Rejected: Hazel's 1921/1926, Kyle's 1922/1924, Oram's $3,500.

### Story 8. The crate in the loft: Ivy Keddie and Bulletin 471 (1958–1970)

**Who:** Ivy Keddie, Ansel's granddaughter, a 27-year-old graduate student at the State Agricultural College, Farland; Professor Leland Vaught; Rosalie Cudd; Orin Falke, the county agent.

**What happened:** Ivy borrowed the Brant weigh book from Roy. She found Strawn's 1923 notebook in the college library, where Strawn's notebooks had lain since 1947 after going to the state dairy office in 1936. Rosalie Cudd, Jerome's daughter, sent copies of the Alder Corners test figures. In **February 1958** Ivy climbed the ladder into the station loft and found the condemned glass still crated, where it had lain since 1923. She took it to Farland and on **9 March 1958** measured the surviving pipettes (**nine**, per Falke and Oram; the Chronicle says eleven) against a calibrated burette. Every one delivered short of its 17.6 c.c. mark, all in the same proportion: about 16.72 c.c., 5% short.

**Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958)**, by Vaught and Keddie, concluded that the short charge alone explained the low tests: no water, no theft, no bog hay. It valued the patrons' loss over the affected years in the tens of thousands of pounds of fat; by the arithmetic above that is 76,000 lb, worth $19,000, of which the $3,800 award was a fifth. The *Ashlin Chronicle* ran the story on 3 October 1958 and got the kinship ("great-niece") and several figures wrong. In 1959 Falke reported to the county board, withdrew his hay concession after reading Bulletin 214, and corrected "great-niece" to "granddaughter." He recommended that no county action be taken, since all interested parties were dead.

Custody afterward: the test book and the crate went to the Ordell County Historical Rooms at Ashlin in 1959, and the weigh book in 1963. The Alder Corners book went to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970. Ansel was cleared 24 years after his death and 33 years after his dismissal. Ivy: "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not."

**Documents:** r19, r22, r18, r17, r21.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy is Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** Duncan (r18), Ansel's son, states that Ivy, born 1931, is his daughter and "my father's granddaughter." Hazel (r17) calls Ivy "Duncan's girl," Oram (r21) calls her "the granddaughter," and Falke's corrective note (r22) says he verified it. The contrary "great-niece" appears in the Chronicle (r19) and in Falke's first draft. Falke's report came after the newspaper coverage and used the same press material, so the two are one source, not two. His own verified correction, plus firsthand family testimony, breaks the tie.

**B2. Ivy and Hazel are second cousins.** Emmet (Hazel's grandfather) and Orra (Ivy's grandmother) were siblings. Their children Roy and Duncan were first cousins, and the children of first cousins are second cousins (Duncan r18, Hazel r17, agreeing). Oram's "first cousin once removed" is wrong: that relation spans two generations, and Ivy and Hazel are in the same generation. Left open: Hazel says Orra was Emmet's *half* sister by his father's second wife, while Duncan says she was his younger full sister. Orra's diary does not settle it, and her pre-1889 pages were not preserved. If Hazel is right, the two are half second cousins. Ivy's and Hazel's mothers are never named.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose**, who organized the Association, served as its first president and wrote its by-laws (Duncan r18 and Kyle r23, independently; both reject the "nephew" story). This matters because the father's by-laws, specifically Article 7 on payment by the station's fat test (Falke), turned a 5% short pipette into a 5% cut in pay. The son, as manager from 1911, signed the 1919 water circular and each year signed the ledger that carried the "station gain" showing the fat arrived. That the Voses held the office on both ends of this, rule and ledger, is what made it an institutional failure and not a personal crime.

**B4. No relationship is established.** The only claim is Oram's hearsay (r21): "cousins of some sort," from his mother's mother, and he "could not draw you the line." Duncan (r18), whose mother gave the connection at every headstone and "never once put a Vose in it," flatly denies any tie and explains the confusion with the unrelated Ashlin Voses. The firsthand family denial outweighs untraceable hearsay. On the evidence there was no kinship, though a negative cannot be proved absolutely.

## Section C — Math

**C1.**
(a) The pipettes were marked 17.6 c.c. and delivered 16.72 c.c., a shortfall of 0.88 c.c. 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95**, so every test read **95% of the truth (5% low)**. The bottle neck is ruled for a 17.6 c.c. charge, so a charge 5% short yields a fat reading 5% short. *Resolution:* the Chronicle's 16.27 would mean 7.56% short, which fits neither the 0.19-point deficit on 3.80 nor Kyle's 5¼% "station gain." 16.72 fits both (3.61 ÷ 3.80 = 0.95; 0.19 ÷ 3.61 = 5.26%). I read 16.27 as a transposition, the same kind the Chronicle made in $77.39 for $77.93. This is an inference by arithmetic.
(b) Recorded average 3.61 (Falke). True average = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. The recorded tests were **0.19 points short** (Falke, Hazel).
(c) 40,000,000 lb of milk (Kyle) × 0.0019 = **76,000 lb of butterfat** not credited. Check: 40,000,000 × 3.80% = 1,520,000 lb; 40,000,000 × 3.61% = 1,444,000 lb; the difference is 76,000 lb. Oram's "67,000" is a transposition of this figure.
(d) 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000**. The patrons received $3,800, and 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **1/5 (20%)**. The Chronicle's "$19,600" does not follow from these inputs. It would require 78,400 lb, which no source supports, so I reject it on arithmetic.

**C2.** Emmet Brant, 1910: 200,000 lb of milk at a recorded 3.42 test.
(a) Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of fat.
(b) True test 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60, giving 7,200 lb delivered. Owed and not credited: **360 lb**. The error is proportional, not a flat 0.19 points; on his 3.42 it comes to 0.18 points.
(c) 360 × $0.25 = **$90**. This uses Kyle's long-run average price; the 1910 price itself is not given in the sources.

**C3.**
(a) Credited: 40,000,000 × 3.61% = **1,444,000 lb**.
(b) Reached Larrow Green: 40,000,000 × 3.80% = **1,520,000 lb**.
(c) The difference is 76,000 lb. **As a percentage of the fat credited, it is 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 5.26%.** That is Kyle's "station gain … five and a quarter per cent," which the bookkeeper took on the credited figure. As a percentage of the fat actually received, it is 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5.00%**, exactly the pipettes' shortfall.

**C4.**
(a) **$77.93**: $25.20 + $31.68 + $0.90 + $18.00 + $2.15, matching the invoice's total line (r20). "$77.39" (Junia's text, the Chronicle) is a transposition.
(b) **72 pipettes** (6 dozen at $0.35 = $25.20), each **0.88 c.c. short** (17.6 − 16.72).

**C5.**
(a) **Duration in use:** 1 May 1897 to condemnation on 12 June 1923, about **26 years and 6 weeks**, with replacement glass in use from July 1923, less the nine weeks of 1911 (8 May – 10 July) when a borrowed measure was used. **Money calculation:** **25 full delivery years**, 1898–1922 (40,000,000 lb ÷ 1,600,000 lb a year). The numbers differ because 1897 (from May) and 1923 (to June) are partial seasons. The Association's full-year station accounts, which Kyle compiled for the arbitration, cover only the complete years. The 1911 interval also slightly overstates the loss for that year.
(b) **Cannot be determined from the sources.** The first season was 1896, from the opening on 4 May on borrowed Larrow Green glass. Its milk was carried on the creamery's books, and those sheets never joined the station papers. The only figure, Kyle's "good eleven hundred thousand pounds" (1.1 million lb), is by his own account hearsay "not in any book I ever ruled," and nothing corroborates it. It does not affect the loss calculation, because the faulty glass was not yet in use.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran from 8 May to 10 July 1911, about nine weeks (Falke; Orra dates the break to 8 May). **Variable:** the measuring glass, the station's own versus a borrowed one. **Held constant:** tester, herds, pasture, feed, season, machinery, acid, scales and supervision. **Result:** tests rose markedly with the borrowed glass and fell back the day the station's glass returned. The effect reversed both ways. **What it establishes that calculation cannot:** cause. A calculation only shows that the numbers are consistent with a 5% short charge. The interval shows the deficit follows the glass and not the men, cows or hay, and so rules out water, theft and bog hay as the cause. **What it does not establish:** which piece was at fault (pipette or bottle); the size of the error (the rise is not quantified); that the borrowed measure was itself accurate; when the fault began; and anything about winter conditions, since it ran in May–July only. It also proves nothing about anyone's knowledge or intent. Ansel himself only put a question mark.

**D2.** Four reasons the tester was not stealing cream:
1. **The ledger (Kyle, r23):** every year Larrow Green received *more* fat in the station's cream than the station test credited, the 5¼% "station gain." Cream stolen at the station would make the delivered fat fall *below* the credited fat.
2. **The 1911 interval (Falke r22; Orra r24; Duncan r18):** the same man, under no different supervision, tested higher with a borrowed measure and lower again with the station's own.
3. **The 1923–24 recovery (Falke):** after new glass arrived in July 1923, the station tested 3.85 from 1924 while Keddie was still the tester.
4. **Alder Corners (Hazel r17; the Cudd test book):** the same Hessel Bottom milk tested normally at the cheese factory over the ridge.

These are independent. They come from different records (the Association's own ledger, the tester's test book, the post-1923 station figures, and a different company's books), kept by different people with opposite interests. None depends on the 1958 measurement, so reasons 1, 3 and 4 would hold even if the glass had never been measured.

**The bookkeeper:** Kyle says Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years" and wrote the "station gain … as usual" line every year. On that basis, **no**, the bookkeeper never questioned the ledger entries that refuted the accusation. Falke says only that the theft charge was urged at the hearing "by an officer of the Association." That this officer was the bookkeeper is the question's premise; r17–r24 do not name him.

**D3.** **Yes, he knew the tests ran low. What cannot be shown is that he understood what his own ledger said about it.** The low tests were complained of at every annual meeting from 1902 on (Chronicle, Falke), and Vose was manager from 1911. In June 1919 he signed, alone, the water circular to the Ostrey Hollow patrons (Kyle typed it; Orra: "over the manager's name"). That circular only makes sense as a response to low tests, so he knew. The ledger, however, showed that the fat arrived in full, and he signed the station accounts reading only the bottom figure (Kyle: "he read the totals and he never read the lines above them"). He knew the tests were low. On the record, he did not register that his own books contradicted the circular he signed. The sources cannot show that he knew the cause.

**Two narrators carrying the same wrong value:**
- **$77.39**, in Junia's text and the Chronicle. The Chronicle claims to have seen the invoice, but it probably took the figure from the firm history or the press release, so the two are one source. The tie is broken by the invoice's own total line and the line-by-line sum, $77.93.
- **"Great-niece,"** in the Chronicle and Falke's paragraph 2. Falke drew on the same press notice, so this too is one source. The tie is broken by Falke's own verified correction and by Duncan (Ansel's son), Hazel and Oram, each independent.
- **"Nine" pipettes** (Falke, Oram) against the Chronicle's eleven is not a case of a shared wrong value. It is two independent sources agreeing, and I accept it.

**Narrators contradicting themselves on the page:**
- **Junia Ammon (r20):** her text says $77.39 while the invoice she reproduces says $77.93. She also says the house sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900, while reproducing an 1897 Ordell invoice.
- **Duncan Keddie (r18):** he gives Orra's birth as 1868 but says she died in 1948 aged eighty-two, which implies 1866. Orra's diary (64 in August 1930) and its preface confirm 1866.
- **Falke (r22):** "great-niece" in paragraph 2, corrected in his note; the hay concession of paragraph 9, withdrawn in his note; and in paragraph 5 his averages differ by 0.17 (3.61 vs 3.78) while he states the deficiency as 0.19. ## Section F — Theory

**F1. Causal theory.** The Ostrey Hollow outfit bought from Ammon & Sons in April 1897 contained 72 pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. that actually delivered about 16.72 c.c., 5% short, probably from an accidentally reground mold. Every test bottle was ruled for a 17.6 c.c. charge, so each test read 95% of the true fat. **Size:** the true station average of 3.80 read as 3.61, a deficit of 0.19 points. **Why that size:** it is exactly 5% of 3.80, the pipettes' shortfall, and the ledger's "station gain" of 5¼% of credited fat matches it. **Why it began then:** the glass went into use on 1 May 1897. The first season, 1896, used borrowed Larrow Green glass. **Why it ended then:** Strawn condemned the pipettes on 12 June 1923, new glass arrived in July, and from 1924 the station tested 3.85. **Why nothing else had to change:** the error was mechanical and proportional, the same every month in every season, independent of cows, hay, water or tester. It vanished for nine weeks in 1911 when a borrowed measure was used, and it returned with the station's own glass.

**The three competing explanations, and what defeats each:**
1. **Watering by the patrons.** Advanced by manager Selby Vose in the 1919 circular. Defeated by Strawn's lactometer tests at every visit from 1908, which never found water, and by the 1911 interval.
2. **Theft of cream by the tester.** Urged at the 1925 hearing by an Association officer and believed by the cream hauler (Oram's father). Defeated by the ledger's station gain (more fat arrived than was credited), the 1911 interval, and the 3.85 tests from 1924 under the same tester.
3. **Wet bog hay.** Advanced by the 1908 Association committee, a veterinarian at Ashlin in 1926, and at first by Falke. Defeated by Bulletin 214 (at most 0.05 in winter, nothing on summer pasture, against a flat 0.19 all year), by the normal tests at Alder Corners on the same milk, and by the 1911 and 1923 changes with the hay unchanged.

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?** **No.** It lay crated in the loft from 1923 until February 1958. Two of the three sittings were held in the weigh room directly under it (Duncan, Oram, Orra). None of the four books before the board was glass, and although Strawn's 1923 notebook recording the condemnation was among them, no one connected it (Kyle).

**What did the tester believe?** That the cause was **in the glass**. He said so at home in summer 1911 after the borrowed-measure weeks (Duncan), but he wrote only a question mark in the test book, because he would not record what he could not prove.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** In 1897 the Larrow Valley creamery's Ostrey Hollow station bought a $77.93 testing outfit from Ammon & Sons. Its pipettes delivered 5% less milk than marked, so for twenty-six years every patron was paid on tests reading 3.61 instead of 3.80. The patrons lost 76,000 lb of butterfat, worth $19,000. The valley blamed watering, the tester Ansel Keddie, or bog hay. The answer lay scattered: a broken measure in 1911, the office ledger's yearly "station gain," an inspector's 1923 condemnation, and a crate in the loft. In 1925 the board sat beneath that crate. It awarded $3,800 and dismissed Keddie, who died uncleared. In 1958 his granddaughter Ivy measured the glass. No single witness held more than one piece: family memory, firm, clerk, hauler, newspaper, county agent and diary each held a part, and each got something wrong.
