# Answer Sheet — Part 2 (Section A, stories 5–8; Section B)

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### Story 5. "Station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual" (1898–1925)

**About:** the Association's station ledger, its bookkeeper Wilbur Teague (1905–1945), office clerk Bertram Kyle, and manager Selby Vose (1913–1938).

**The ledger line.**
- Every year the Ostrey Hollow station account carried three figures:
  - the pounds of butterfat credited to patrons on the station test;
  - the pounds of butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in the station's cream;
  - the difference, entered as **"station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual"** and carried to the general account.
- Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green from 1893 to 1934, saw the same thing at the churn: the Hollow cream "always came in heavier than the sheet said."

**The 1898 entry.**
- Credited: 57,760 lb. Received: 60,800 lb. Gain: 3,040 lb.
- 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = **5.26% of the fat credited**, which is the bookkeeper's "5¼".
- The same gain is **5.00% of the fat received**.
- On 1.6 million lb of milk the entry corresponds to a recorded test of 3.61 and a true test of 3.80.

**The whole run.**
- For the arbitration Kyle compiled **40,000,000 lb of milk for 1898–1922**: 25 full years at 1.6 million lb a year.
- The 40 million lb is correct and Teague's and Rundle's 44 million is wrong: Kyle compiled the figure himself, it equals 1.6 million × 25 years, and 3.61% and 3.80% of 40 million come out exactly to the credited and received totals below.
- Fat credited: 40,000,000 × 3.61% = **1,444,000 lb**.
- Fat received: 40,000,000 × 3.80% = **1,520,000 lb**.
- Gain: **76,000 lb**. At the average price of **25c** (Kyle, who was set to find it; Roy) that is **$19,000**.

**Who looked and who didn't.**
- The line was in plain sight, read out at every annual meeting, and explained by nothing but its name.
- Teague says he raised the line with Vose in **1916** and was told to let it alone. Kyle, sitting outside the door, says Teague never asked anyone anything.
- Vose "read the totals and never read the lines above them."

**The ledger against the accusation.**
- At the 1925 hearing Teague insinuated that the tester was stealing cream. His own ledger refutes him: theft would leave *less* fat reaching the creamery than was credited, but year after year *more* arrived.
- Teague's 1948 account compounds the error. He gives 44 million lb and a 3.16 average, calls the 5¼% a share "of the fat actually in the cream," and claims he invented the line in 1905 while quoting an entry from 1898, before he joined.

**The award.**
- The **$3,800** award was **charged to the station account**, so the patrons were in effect repaid a fifth of their own uncredited fat.

**Documents:** r05, r23, r14, r11, r07, r13, r08, r12, r09.

### Story 6. Alder Corners: the same milk over the ridge (1913–1930, copied 1958)

**About:** Jerome Cudd's cheese factory at Alder Corners in Vessey County, his daughter Rosalie Cudd, and the four Hessel Bottom men who hauled there.

**The factory.**
- Rosalie says the factory was built in **1913**; Roy and Rundle say it opened in **1914**. Both can be true, and the sources do not force a choice.

**The hauling.**
- From **1915 to 1930, 16 seasons**, four of the six Hessel Bottom farmers hauled their milk over the ridge to Cudd each summer flush, roughly June to mid-September.
- The ridge road was closed from November to April. For the rest of the year the same men delivered the same cows' milk to the Ostrey Hollow station.
- Hazel quotes Roy: "the only honest arithmetic anybody in that valley ever did without meaning to."

**The tests.**
- Jerome tested with his own new glass and never compared figures with anyone, so his book is independent of the station.
- His summer averages for 1915–1930 were 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.80, 3.80, 3.82, 3.79, 3.78, 3.81, 3.80, 3.79, 3.82, 3.78, 3.80, 3.81, 3.79. The range is 3.78–3.82 and the mean is **3.80** (3.798).
- In 1919 the four patrons individually tested 3.79, 3.81, 3.78 and 3.82.

**What it proves.**
- A cheese factory's yield would show watered milk at once, and there was none.
- Summer cows off the bog hay tested 3.80 at Cudd's while the station was still recording about 3.61 for the rest of the year. Neither water nor hay can explain that. Only the station's measure can.

**Overlap with the short glass.**
- Only **1915–1922 are full overlapping seasons (8)**. Summer 1923 overlaps at most briefly, since the glass was condemned on 12 June 1923.
- Ivy's "fourteen overlapping seasons" is wrong.
- The 1924–1930 seasons act as a control: with new glass the station's test (1924: 3.80) matched Cudd's.

**What became of the book.**
- Jerome died in March 1941, and Rosalie took the factory and the book.
- On Ivy's request in 1958 she had the figures copied and sent to Farland. Bulletin 471 acknowledges her.
- The book never left Vessey County and went to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970.

**Sources rejected.**
- The *Chronicle*'s "niece" is wrong: Rosalie is Jerome's daughter by her own letter and by Rundle and Duncan.
- Nym's claim that the figures cover 1919 only is wrong.

**Documents:** r10, r04, r09, r12, r17, r18, r07, r19, r11.

### Story 7. The Brants, the Keddies, the weigh book and the water circular (1889–1934)

**About:** Emmet Brant (1854–1929), the largest patron; his sister Orra; her husband Ansel Keddie; and the next generations.

**Emmet and his weigh book.**
- Emmet farmed the largest place on Hessel Bottom: 60-odd acres and up to 38 cows.
- Orra (born 1866) married Ansel in 1889, so the tester weighed and tested his brother-in-law's milk for 29 years.
- Emmet began a private **weigh book on 1 January 1897**. Frick's exhibit list, Nym and Duncan give 1897; Roy says 1898 and Hazel 1900.
- In the book he entered his own farm scale weight and the station's intake weight side by side. They **always agreed**, so the scales were honest.
- **The 1910 leaf:** 200,000 lb delivered, scales agreeing, paid on a test of **3.42**. The truth was 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = **3.60**.
  - Credited: 6,840 lb. Owed: 7,200 lb. Shortfall: **360 lb**, worth **$86.40 at 1910's 24c**.

**The water circular, 1919.**
- In June 1919 Selby Vose sent a circular warning the Hessel Bottom patrons against adding water to their milk. It went to **nine families** on the six farms (Vose, Hazel; Orra's "eleven" is rejected). Kyle typed it.
- Strawn's lactometer never found water at the station, the weigh book showed nothing odd, and the cheese at Alder Corners would have shown it.
- Ansel would not leave the circulars on the platform and carried them round himself. Emmet came up, stood in the yard and asked why he had carried a paper he did not believe. Emmet held that Ansel should have stood up against it.
- The two men **did not speak from 1919 until Emmet's death in 1929**.
- Emmet's name was on the January 1925 petition.

**Kinship.**
- Emmet and Orra were siblings, so Emmet's son **Roy** (born about 1888) and Orra's son **Duncan** (1894–, a schoolteacher at Ashlin) were first cousins.
- Roy's daughter **Hazel** and Duncan's daughter **Ivy** (born 1931) were **second cousins**.
- Duncan and Roy say Orra was Emmet's full sister; Hazel says half-sister. That point is not fully settled.

**What became of the book.**
- It passed to Roy in 1929, and he kept it the same way.
- He **lent** it (not left it) to Ivy in 1958.
- At Roy's death in 1962 it went to Hazel, who gave it to the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1963.

**Ansel's last years.**
- After his dismissal he worked as a carpenter and died in February 1934. Orra died in 1948.

**Documents:** r09, r17, r18, r24, r08, r06, r02, r13, r23, r01, r21.

### Story 8. The arbitration, the crate in the loft, and the clearing (1925–1959)

**About:** Alonzo Frick's board of arbitration, Ansel Keddie's dismissal, and Ivy Keddie's investigation.

**The hearing.**
- The patrons petitioned in **January 1925**. A board chaired by the Ashlin attorney **Alonzo Frick** sat from **17 March to 4 May 1925**.
- Where it sat:
  - Three sittings: the first at Ashlin, then **two in the Ostrey Hollow weigh room**, where the board watched the test made.
  - Frick, Oram, Orra's diary, Duncan and Kyle agree on this. Effie's account that it never went to the station is rejected.
  - The condemned crate was in the loft about 20 feet overhead.
- The board heard three theories: Vose's water, Teague's insinuated theft, and the 1908 hay report.
- Its exhibits were four books:
  - the test book (4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924);
  - the receiving book;
  - Emmet's weigh book;
  - Strawn's 1923 notebook, which recorded the condemnation.
- **No glassware** was examined, and no one laid the receiving book beside Strawn's notebook.
- Frick believed Ansel's denial. His story that Ansel privately blamed the patrons is uncorroborated.

**The outcome.**
- Ansel was **dismissed on 30 April 1925**, four days before the award.
- On 4 May the board awarded **$3,800**, "no cause established… without admission," charged to the station account. That is one-fifth of the $19,000 actually lost.
- Ansel died in 1934 with the imputation on him.

**The investigation.**
- Ivy, Ansel's granddaughter, was a graduate student at Farland. In **February 1958** she climbed to the loft and found the crate under a tarpaulin.
- On **9 March 1958** she measured the **nine surviving pipettes** against a calibrated burette. The mean was **16.72 c.c. against a 17.6 mark (0.95)**.
- She found Strawn's 1923 notebook in the college library, where his notebooks had been sent in 1947. She borrowed Roy's weigh book, and got Rosalie's Alder Corners figures.
- **Bulletin 471** (October 1958, Vaught and Keddie) concluded:
  - Recorded 3.61 against a true 3.80, a gap of 0.19.
  - 76,000 lb of fat uncredited over 1898–1922, worth $19,000.
  - No water, no theft, no hay: the glass.
- Its introduction is corrupted by figures that contradict its own conclusion: 16.27 c.c., five dozen pipettes, 1898 and 118,000 lb.

**Aftermath.**
- The *Ashlin Chronicle* ran the story on 3 October 1958, with many errors.
- Ivy gave her talk in 1959.
- The crate and test book went to the Historical Rooms in 1959.
- Falke's 1959 county report found that "no action lies."

**Documents:** r13, r02, r21, r24, r18, r23, r04, r11, r19, r22, r12, r07, r03, r09, r10.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy and Ansel.**
- **Ivy is Ansel's granddaughter.**
- The line runs Ansel Keddie (1861–1934) and Orra, to their son Duncan (born 1894), to Duncan's daughter Ivy (born 1931).
- The evidence:
  - Duncan (r18), her father, states it and says he has seen it "printed otherwise."
  - Hazel says "Ivy is Duncan's girl."
  - Oram calls her "the granddaughter."
- The *Chronicle* (r19) called her Ansel's "great-niece." It was put together in a day and a half from a press release and two phone calls.
- Falke (r22) copied "great-niece," then in his end note corrected it to granddaughter, "informed, and have verified." That makes it one error with one origin, withdrawn by one of its two carriers. Vira's garbled version carries no weight against the father's statement.

**B2. Ivy and Hazel.**
- **They are second cousins.**
- Emmet Brant and Orra (Brant) Keddie were brother and sister, so their children Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie were first cousins. Their daughters Hazel and Ivy are second cousins (r09, r17, r18).
- Oram's "first cousin once removed" and Vira's garbled version are rejected, because the two women are in the same generation.
- **Left open:** whether Orra and Emmet were full or half siblings.
  - Duncan (her son, who says she gave him every connection in the valley) and Roy both say full sister.
  - Hazel says half-sister, "by his father's second wife."
  - Orra's own diary pages from before 1889 were not preserved.
  - Her diary ("that kitchen where I was a girl"; "the ones my mother had") and the birth years (Emmet 1854, Orra 1866) fit either version.
- The weight of two independent sources favors full siblings, but the sources cannot strictly settle it. If they were half siblings, Roy and Duncan were half first cousins and Hazel and Ivy half second cousins. Either way they are in the second-cousin degree.
- Also open: Orra's birth year. The diary's 1866 is consistent with "sixty-four" in 1930 and with dying at 82 in 1948; Duncan's 1868 is not.

**B3. Selby Vose's father.**
- **Gideon Vose** was Selby's father. He organized the Association in 1889, was its president from 1889 to 1911, wrote the by-laws, and died in 1917.
- Selby himself (r08) says so, and so do Effie, Duncan and Kyle, who calls the "nephew" story "nonsense." Vira and Rundle say nephew; Selby's own statement outranks them.
- **Why it matters:**
  - Gideon wrote **Article VII**, which made the station's test final ("the test of the station where he delivers shall govern"). The patrons' complaint challenged that rule and so the station test itself.
  - As manager from 1913, the founder's son defended his father's system and blamed the patrons. He sent the 1919 water circular, told Teague in 1916 to let the station-gain line alone, and signed ledger sheets on the totals alone.
  - The $3,800 was settled "without admission."
  - That filial stake is an **inference**: no source says Vose acted from loyalty. What the sources do establish is that the by-law was Gideon's, that it barred any appeal from the station test, and that his son ran the office that kept the evidence.

**B4. Voses and Keddies.**
- **No relationship is established, and the best evidence says there was none.**
- Duncan (r18) denies any connection. He attributes the talk to confusion with the unrelated Ashlin Voses and to gossip that Orra and Gideon's wife were cousins. He notes that Orra, a precise genealogist of the valley, never once put a Vose in it.
- The only claim for a link is Oram's (r21) hearsay from his mother's mother: "cousins of some sort… I could not draw you the line."
- A negative cannot be proven absolutely, but a named family member's specific denial outranks untraceable hearsay.
