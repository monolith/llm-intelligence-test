## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1. Article VII and the opening of the Ostrey Hollow station (1889–1896)

**About:** the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, its founder Gideon Vose, and its first tester, Ansel Keddie.

**Founding and Article VII.**
- The Association was organized in 1889 by valley farmers meeting at Larrow Green. It built its main creamery there in 1891 and kept its office at Ashlin.
- Gideon Vose was president from 1889 to 1911 and wrote the by-laws. He died in 1917.
- Article VII pays each patron on the pounds of butterfat his milk contains by test, not on the weight of the milk. It also says that "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." Those words left a patron no appeal to any other test, and they are the hinge of everything that follows.
- The year Article VII was adopted **cannot be determined**:
  - Rundle gives 1891, Effie 1892 and Grigg "the 1893 meeting."
  - The printed by-laws say only "adopted at the annual meeting," and the early minute book was lost (r22).
  - It was in force well before 1896.

**The station.**
- In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, 7 miles up the valley. It served the six farms of Hessel Bottom, a wet meadow along the creek. The 1908 report counts six farms, as do Roy, Hazel and Kepp; Effie's "seven" is rejected.
- The station **opened 4 May 1896**, and the test book begins that day (r13 exhibit list; r22). Vira's and Hazel's "went up in '95" is a conflation with the building winter.
- The station worked like this:
  - Milk was weighed in at the intake platform and sampled.
  - The sample was tested for butterfat with a pipette charge of 17.6 c.c. into a bottle with a graduated neck.
  - The milk was separated. The cream was hauled 7 miles down to Larrow Green (Cleve Oram's father drove the wagon), and the skim went back to the farmer.

**Ansel Keddie.**
- Ansel (1861–1934) was tester and separator man from the opening day for 29 seasons, 1896 through 1924.
- In 1889 he had married Orra Brant, sister of Emmet Brant, the biggest patron on Hessel Bottom.

**The first season.**
- The station used **borrowed glass**. Ansel's first leaf says it was "borrowed of Larrow Green," and Orra's diary for 1 May 1897 says "done borrowing of Larrow Green." Kyle agrees.
- Sill, the Larrow Green buttermaker from 1893, says it was his own glass and came back chipped the next spring. That is compatible: it was the creamery's glass, in his care.
- Ansel's unsent letter says "the Ashlin creamery." That is a slip, since no creamery at Ashlin appears anywhere else.
- The 1896 milk was carried on the creamery's books, and no station figure survives (r07, r23). The first season's intake is therefore **unknown**: Sill's 900,000 lb and Kyle's 1,100,000 lb are unsupported memories.
- Because the 1896 glass was sound, the first season is not part of the loss.

**Documents:** r02, r03, r07, r12, r14, r15, r22, r23, r24, r21, r01, r17.

### Story 2. The Ammon outfit: seventy-two short pipettes (1897–1923)

**About:** Ammon & Sons of Tarnet, the station's own testing outfit, and state inspector Merle Strawn.

**The purchase.**
- Ammon's invoice is dated **14 April 1897** and reproduced in facsimile in r20:

  | Item | Amount |
  |---|---|
  | 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at $0.35 | $25.20 |
  | 12 doz. test bottles, 8% neck, at $0.22 | $31.68 |
  | Acid measure | $0.90 |
  | Four-bottle hand tester | $18.00 |
  | Freight | $2.15 |
  | **Total** | **$77.93** |

- The items add to $77.93. Junia's prose and the *Chronicle* give $77.39, a transposition. Twenty-five dollars twenty at 35 cents means **72 pipettes**, so "five dozen" (r11, r21) is wrong.
- The outfit went into use on **1 May 1897** (invoice date; r20; Orra's diary; Ivy). Vaught's and Kyle's 1898 is rejected.

**Why it mattered.**
- The pipettes were marked 17.6 c.c. but, as measured in 1958, delivered on average **16.72 c.c.: 0.88 c.c. short, 95 per cent of the mark**.
- A short charge in a bottle ruled for a full charge reads low in the same proportion. Every test the station made therefore recorded 95 per cent of the true fat:
  - the true 3.80 read 3.61;
  - richer milk lost more, so a true 4.80 read 4.56.
- The error was consistent from pipette to pipette. Junia's inference is that a reground mold ran the whole lot short, and a spot-check cannot find an error that is always the same. Ammon's works books burned in 1931, so this is **inference, not evidence**. The *Chronicle*'s charge that Ammon sold a short lot knowingly is unsupported.

**Inspection and condemnation.**
- Strawn rode the circuit from 1907 to 1936. Burettes were issued to field men in 1921, and Strawn skipped the burette check in 1922.
- On **12 June 1923** he ran the station's pipettes against his burette and condemned the whole crate.
- This date comes from Strawn's own 1923 notebook. It is also confirmed by the order date: the Association ordered six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles on 28 June, and Ammon replied on 2 July ("your order of the 28th ultimo… Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything"). Vose's and Rundle's "12 July" is impossible, and Hazel's 1921 and Kyle's 1922 are wrong.
- New glass was in use from July 1923, and the 1924 average was **3.80**.
- The condemned crate went up into the loft over the intake room. No one, Strawn included, asked what the glass had been doing for 26 years. The burette program was dropped in 1926.

**Documents:** r20, r19, r11, r04, r06, r22, r23, r24, r18, r08, r12, r21, r17.

### Story 3. The 1908 committee and the hay theory (1902–1926, withdrawn 1959)

**About:** Nyle Grigg's investigating committee, Bulletin 214, and Dr. Roland Kepp.

**The complaint and the committee.**
- Complaints that Ostrey Hollow milk tested low began about **1902**. Orra's March 1902 diary records the talk at the store and Ansel's "it is my figures they will look at."
- In January 1908 the board named a committee of three, chaired by **Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green**, who was not a patron of the station. Orra saw them arrive in Grigg's sleigh and go first to Emmet Brant's farm, the largest.
- The committee:
  - visited all **six** farms and sealed its own samples;
  - checked the station scales against a known weight and the farm scales against the station's;
  - asked about water;
  - stood by while the test was made.
- It found **no dishonesty of any kind**. The report says it sampled **January, February and March only**, and Orra's diary has the men gone by March.
- It concluded that the wet sedge hay of Hessel Bottom "thins the cream" and that "the remedy lies with the land and not with the station." The report closed the question for eleven years.

**The science.**
- The State Agricultural College at Farland published **Bulletin 214 in 1911**, a controlled feeding trial with bog hay. Its summary, quoted in full by Falke, found the test depressed by **not more than 0.05 in winter and by nothing measurable on summer pasture**.

**The theory repeated.**
- In 1919 Grigg reprinted the report with a cover letter that misstates both his own work ("through the whole of 1908, every month") and the Bulletin ("two tenths in the winter feeding").
- Kepp's 1926 paper to the Ordell County Farm Club repeated the "two tenths." It blamed sour, lime- and phosphate-poor soil for "a fifth of a point," used the wrong gap (0.21) and the wrong price (27c), and dismissed the 1911 weeks as "six weeks" of summer flush.
- At the 1925 arbitration the hay report was one of the three theories.

**Why it fails.**
- The true gap was **0.19, the same in every month**, February and July alike (r09, r17, r22). A winter-sampling committee could not have seen that the gap persisted in summer, when the cows were off the hay.
- 0.05 cannot carry 0.19 in winter, and nothing cannot carry it in summer.
- The same Hessel Bottom milk tested about 3.80 at Alder Corners in summer.
- The station test rose in 1911 and again after July 1923 with no drainage at all.
- Falke conceded the hay as a "partial factor" in 1959, then withdrew the concession on reading Bulletin 214's figures.

**Documents:** r15, r16, r22, r11, r24, r09, r13, r02, r04.

### Story 4. The nine weeks of 1911

**About:** Ansel Keddie's test book in the summer of 1911, the one natural experiment in the record.

**What happened.**
- On **8 May 1911** the station's own measuring glass broke. Orra's diary for that day: "A. home cross about a broken measure."
- A borrowed measure was used until **10 July 1911**, which is **nine weeks** (63 days). Teague's and Kepp's "six weeks" is wrong.
- Where the borrowed measure came from is not agreed. Most accounts say Larrow Green; Roy says the state man left his own. Either way it was a sound measure from outside the station's lot.

**The result.**
- For those nine weeks the Ostrey Hollow tests rose to about **3.80**, the true level of the milk and the figure later seen at Alder Corners and after 1923.
- When the station went back to its own glass on 10 July, in midsummer, the tests fell to the old low level at once.
- Nothing else changed: the same tester, the same herds and patrons, the same scales, acid, bottles and procedure.
- Ansel entered the figures and wrote a **"?"** in the test book. At home he said, twice, that it was "something in the glass." Duncan, then seventeen, remembered it. Ansel never put that in the book, because he could not prove it and "would not put down what he could not prove."
- Strawn inspected the station in June 1911, in the middle of the interval, and noted nothing.

**Later readings.**
- Kepp waved the weeks away as a summer fluke. That fails because the gap was constant across seasons and because the tests fell back in July, not in autumn.
- Bulletin 471 called the interval a natural experiment that "lay unread for forty-seven years" (1911 to 1958).
- Falke's 1959 report calls it decisive and uses it against the theft theory: the same man made the tests, under no different supervision, and the figures rose.

**The quantities.**
- Borrowed measure: about 3.80. Own glass: about 3.61. Difference: 0.19, which is 5 per cent.
- This matches the 0.95 ratio measured in 1958.

**Documents:** r03, r04, r11, r18, r22, r24, r16, r05, r09, r06, r12.

### Story 5. "Station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual" (1898–1925)

**About:** the Association's station ledger, its bookkeeper Wilbur Teague (1905–1945), office clerk Bertram Kyle, and manager Selby Vose (1913–1938).

**The ledger line.**
- Every year the Ostrey Hollow station account carried three figures:
  - the pounds of butterfat credited to patrons on the station test;
  - the pounds of butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in the station's cream;
  - the difference, entered as **"station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual"** and carried to the general account.
- Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green from 1893 to 1934, saw the same thing at the churn: the Hollow cream "always came in heavier than the sheet said."

**The 1898 entry.**
- Credited: 57,760 lb. Received: 60,800 lb. Gain: 3,040 lb.
- 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = **5.26% of the fat credited**, which is the bookkeeper's "5¼".
- The same gain is **5.00% of the fat received**.
- On 1.6 million lb of milk the entry corresponds to a recorded test of 3.61 and a true test of 3.80.

**The whole run.**
- For the arbitration Kyle compiled **40,000,000 lb of milk for 1898–1922**: 25 full years at 1.6 million lb a year.
- The 40 million lb is correct and Teague's and Rundle's 44 million is wrong: Kyle compiled the figure himself, it equals 1.6 million × 25 years, and 3.61% and 3.80% of 40 million come out exactly to the credited and received totals below.
- Fat credited: 40,000,000 × 3.61% = **1,444,000 lb**.
- Fat received: 40,000,000 × 3.80% = **1,520,000 lb**.
- Gain: **76,000 lb**. At the average price of **25c** (Kyle, who was set to find it; Roy) that is **$19,000**.

**Who looked and who didn't.**
- The line was in plain sight, read out at every annual meeting, and explained by nothing but its name.
- Teague says he raised the line with Vose in **1916** and was told to let it alone. Kyle, sitting outside the door, says Teague never asked anyone anything.
- Vose "read the totals and never read the lines above them."

**The ledger against the accusation.**
- At the 1925 hearing Teague insinuated that the tester was stealing cream. His own ledger refutes him: theft would leave *less* fat reaching the creamery than was credited, but year after year *more* arrived.
- Teague's 1948 account compounds the error. He gives 44 million lb and a 3.16 average, calls the 5¼% a share "of the fat actually in the cream," and claims he invented the line in 1905 while quoting an entry from 1898, before he joined.

**The award.**
- The **$3,800** award was **charged to the station account**, so the patrons were in effect repaid a fifth of their own uncredited fat.

**Documents:** r05, r23, r14, r11, r07, r13, r08, r12, r09.

### Story 6. Alder Corners: the same milk over the ridge (1913–1930, copied 1958)

**About:** Jerome Cudd's cheese factory at Alder Corners in Vessey County, his daughter Rosalie Cudd, and the four Hessel Bottom men who hauled there.

**The factory.**
- Rosalie says the factory was built in **1913**; Roy and Rundle say it opened in **1914**. Both can be true, and the sources do not force a choice.

**The hauling.**
- From **1915 to 1930, 16 seasons**, four of the six Hessel Bottom farmers hauled their milk over the ridge to Cudd each summer flush, roughly June to mid-September.
- The ridge road was closed from November to April. For the rest of the year the same men delivered the same cows' milk to the Ostrey Hollow station.
- Hazel quotes Roy: "the only honest arithmetic anybody in that valley ever did without meaning to."

**The tests.**
- Jerome tested with his own new glass and never compared figures with anyone, so his book is independent of the station.
- His summer averages for 1915–1930 were 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.80, 3.80, 3.82, 3.79, 3.78, 3.81, 3.80, 3.79, 3.82, 3.78, 3.80, 3.81, 3.79. The range is 3.78–3.82 and the mean is **3.80** (3.798).
- In 1919 the four patrons individually tested 3.79, 3.81, 3.78 and 3.82.

**What it proves.**
- A cheese factory's yield would show watered milk at once, and there was none.
- Summer cows off the bog hay tested 3.80 at Cudd's while the station was still recording about 3.61 for the rest of the year. Neither water nor hay can explain that. Only the station's measure can.

**Overlap with the short glass.**
- Only **1915–1922 are full overlapping seasons (8)**. Summer 1923 overlaps at most briefly, since the glass was condemned on 12 June 1923.
- Ivy's "fourteen overlapping seasons" is wrong.
- The 1924–1930 seasons act as a control: with new glass the station's test (1924: 3.80) matched Cudd's.

**What became of the book.**
- Jerome died in March 1941, and Rosalie took the factory and the book.
- On Ivy's request in 1958 she had the figures copied and sent to Farland. Bulletin 471 acknowledges her.
- The book never left Vessey County and went to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970.

**Sources rejected.**
- The *Chronicle*'s "niece" is wrong: Rosalie is Jerome's daughter by her own letter and by Rundle and Duncan.
- Nym's claim that the figures cover 1919 only is wrong.

**Documents:** r10, r04, r09, r12, r17, r18, r07, r19, r11.

### Story 7. The Brants, the Keddies, the weigh book and the water circular (1889–1934)

**About:** Emmet Brant (1854–1929), the largest patron; his sister Orra; her husband Ansel Keddie; and the next generations.

**Emmet and his weigh book.**
- Emmet farmed the largest place on Hessel Bottom: 60-odd acres and up to 38 cows.
- Orra (born 1866) married Ansel in 1889, so the tester weighed and tested his brother-in-law's milk for 29 years.
- Emmet began a private **weigh book on 1 January 1897**. Frick's exhibit list, Nym and Duncan give 1897; Roy says 1898 and Hazel 1900.
- In the book he entered his own farm scale weight and the station's intake weight side by side. They **always agreed**, so the scales were honest.
- **The 1910 leaf:** 200,000 lb delivered, scales agreeing, paid on a test of **3.42**. The truth was 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = **3.60**.
  - Credited: 6,840 lb. Owed: 7,200 lb. Shortfall: **360 lb**, worth **$86.40 at 1910's 24c**.

**The water circular, 1919.**
- In June 1919 Selby Vose sent a circular warning the Hessel Bottom patrons against adding water to their milk. It went to **nine families** on the six farms (Vose, Hazel; Orra's "eleven" is rejected). Kyle typed it.
- Strawn's lactometer never found water at the station, the weigh book showed nothing odd, and the cheese at Alder Corners would have shown it.
- Ansel would not leave the circulars on the platform and carried them round himself. Emmet came up, stood in the yard and asked why he had carried a paper he did not believe. Emmet held that Ansel should have stood up against it.
- The two men **did not speak from 1919 until Emmet's death in 1929**.
- Emmet's name was on the January 1925 petition.

**Kinship.**
- Emmet and Orra were siblings, so Emmet's son **Roy** (born about 1888) and Orra's son **Duncan** (1894–, a schoolteacher at Ashlin) were first cousins.
- Roy's daughter **Hazel** and Duncan's daughter **Ivy** (born 1931) were **second cousins**.
- Duncan and Roy say Orra was Emmet's full sister; Hazel says half-sister. That point is not fully settled.

**What became of the book.**
- It passed to Roy in 1929, and he kept it the same way.
- He **lent** it (not left it) to Ivy in 1958.
- At Roy's death in 1962 it went to Hazel, who gave it to the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1963.

**Ansel's last years.**
- After his dismissal he worked as a carpenter and died in February 1934. Orra died in 1948.

**Documents:** r09, r17, r18, r24, r08, r06, r02, r13, r23, r01, r21.

### Story 8. The arbitration, the crate in the loft, and the clearing (1925–1959)

**About:** Alonzo Frick's board of arbitration, Ansel Keddie's dismissal, and Ivy Keddie's investigation.

**The hearing.**
- The patrons petitioned in **January 1925**. A board chaired by the Ashlin attorney **Alonzo Frick** sat from **17 March to 4 May 1925**.
- Where it sat:
  - Three sittings: the first at Ashlin, then **two in the Ostrey Hollow weigh room**, where the board watched the test made.
  - Frick, Oram, Orra's diary, Duncan and Kyle agree on this. Effie's account that it never went to the station is rejected.
  - The condemned crate was in the loft about 20 feet overhead.
- The board heard three theories: Vose's water, Teague's insinuated theft, and the 1908 hay report.
- Its exhibits were four books:
  - the test book (4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924);
  - the receiving book;
  - Emmet's weigh book;
  - Strawn's 1923 notebook, which recorded the condemnation.
- **No glassware** was examined, and no one laid the receiving book beside Strawn's notebook.
- Frick believed Ansel's denial. His story that Ansel privately blamed the patrons is uncorroborated.

**The outcome.**
- Ansel was **dismissed on 30 April 1925**, four days before the award.
- On 4 May the board awarded **$3,800**, "no cause established… without admission," charged to the station account. That is one-fifth of the $19,000 actually lost.
- Ansel died in 1934 with the imputation on him.

**The investigation.**
- Ivy, Ansel's granddaughter, was a graduate student at Farland. In **February 1958** she climbed to the loft and found the crate under a tarpaulin.
- On **9 March 1958** she measured the **nine surviving pipettes** against a calibrated burette. The mean was **16.72 c.c. against a 17.6 mark (0.95)**.
- She found Strawn's 1923 notebook in the college library, where his notebooks had been sent in 1947. She borrowed Roy's weigh book, and got Rosalie's Alder Corners figures.
- **Bulletin 471** (October 1958, Vaught and Keddie) concluded:
  - Recorded 3.61 against a true 3.80, a gap of 0.19.
  - 76,000 lb of fat uncredited over 1898–1922, worth $19,000.
  - No water, no theft, no hay: the glass.
- Its introduction is corrupted by figures that contradict its own conclusion: 16.27 c.c., five dozen pipettes, 1898 and 118,000 lb.

**Aftermath.**
- The *Ashlin Chronicle* ran the story on 3 October 1958, with many errors.
- Ivy gave her talk in 1959.
- The crate and test book went to the Historical Rooms in 1959.
- Falke's 1959 county report found that "no action lies."

**Documents:** r13, r02, r21, r24, r18, r23, r04, r11, r19, r22, r12, r07, r03, r09, r10.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy and Ansel.**
- **Ivy is Ansel's granddaughter.**
- The line runs Ansel Keddie (1861–1934) and Orra, to their son Duncan (born 1894), to Duncan's daughter Ivy (born 1931).
- The evidence:
  - Duncan (r18), her father, states it and says he has seen it "printed otherwise."
  - Hazel says "Ivy is Duncan's girl."
  - Oram calls her "the granddaughter."
- The *Chronicle* (r19) called her Ansel's "great-niece." It was put together in a day and a half from a press release and two phone calls.
- Falke (r22) copied "great-niece," then in his end note corrected it to granddaughter, "informed, and have verified." That makes it one error with one origin, withdrawn by one of its two carriers. Vira's garbled version carries no weight against the father's statement.

**B2. Ivy and Hazel.**
- **They are second cousins.**
- Emmet Brant and Orra (Brant) Keddie were brother and sister, so their children Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie were first cousins. Their daughters Hazel and Ivy are second cousins (r09, r17, r18).
- Oram's "first cousin once removed" and Vira's garbled version are rejected, because the two women are in the same generation.
- **Left open:** whether Orra and Emmet were full or half siblings.
  - Duncan (her son, who says she gave him every connection in the valley) and Roy both say full sister.
  - Hazel says half-sister, "by his father's second wife."
  - Orra's own diary pages from before 1889 were not preserved.
  - Her diary ("that kitchen where I was a girl"; "the ones my mother had") and the birth years (Emmet 1854, Orra 1866) fit either version.
- The weight of two independent sources favors full siblings, but the sources cannot strictly settle it. If they were half siblings, Roy and Duncan were half first cousins and Hazel and Ivy half second cousins. Either way they are in the second-cousin degree.
- Also open: Orra's birth year. The diary's 1866 is consistent with "sixty-four" in 1930 and with dying at 82 in 1948; Duncan's 1868 is not.

**B3. Selby Vose's father.**
- **Gideon Vose** was Selby's father. He organized the Association in 1889, was its president from 1889 to 1911, wrote the by-laws, and died in 1917.
- Selby himself (r08) says so, and so do Effie, Duncan and Kyle, who calls the "nephew" story "nonsense." Vira and Rundle say nephew; Selby's own statement outranks them.
- **Why it matters:**
  - Gideon wrote **Article VII**, which made the station's test final ("the test of the station where he delivers shall govern"). The patrons' complaint challenged that rule and so the station test itself.
  - As manager from 1913, the founder's son defended his father's system and blamed the patrons. He sent the 1919 water circular, told Teague in 1916 to let the station-gain line alone, and signed ledger sheets on the totals alone.
  - The $3,800 was settled "without admission."
  - That filial stake is an **inference**: no source says Vose acted from loyalty. What the sources do establish is that the by-law was Gideon's, that it barred any appeal from the station test, and that his son ran the office that kept the evidence.

**B4. Voses and Keddies.**
- **No relationship is established, and the best evidence says there was none.**
- Duncan (r18) denies any connection. He attributes the talk to confusion with the unrelated Ashlin Voses and to gossip that Orra and Gideon's wife were cousins. He notes that Orra, a precise genealogist of the valley, never once put a Vose in it.
- The only claim for a link is Oram's (r21) hearsay from his mother's mother: "cousins of some sort… I could not draw you the line."
- A negative cannot be proven absolutely, but a named family member's specific denial outranks untraceable hearsay.


# Answer Sheet — Part 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

### C1

**(a) Proportion.**
- The nine surviving pipettes, all marked 17.6 c.c., delivered a mean of **16.72 c.c.**
- 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95**.
- A short charge into a bottle whose neck is ruled for 17.6 c.c. reads low in the same proportion. Every recorded test was **95% of the true test, 5% low**. Equivalently, true = recorded ÷ 0.95 = recorded × 1.0526.
- Each pipette was short by 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c.
- Bulletin 471's introduction and the *Chronicle* give 16.27 c.c. That is rejected: it gives a ratio of 0.9244 and a true test of 3.905, which the Bulletin itself admits cannot square with the 1924 figure of 3.80.

**(b) True average and shortfall.**
- The recorded average was **3.61**.
- True average: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**.
- The recorded tests were **0.19 point short**.
- Independent checks on 3.80:
  - the 1911 borrowed measure gave about 3.80;
  - the 1924 average with new glass was 3.80;
  - Alder Corners averaged 3.80.
- The 3.16 given by Teague and Frick is a transposition of 3.61.

**(c) Uncredited butterfat, 1898–1922.**
- Milk: 40,000,000 lb.
- True fat: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb.
- Credited: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb.
- **Uncredited: 76,000 lb.**

**(d) Money.**
- 76,000 lb × $0.25 = **$19,000**.
- The award was **$3,800**: 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **one-fifth (20%)**.
- It was also charged to the station account, which the uncredited fat itself had fed.

### C2. Emmet Brant, 1910

- He delivered 200,000 lb of milk and was paid on a test of 3.42.

**(a) Credited:** 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of butterfat.

**(b) Owed and not credited:**
- True test: 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60.
- True fat: 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb.
- **Not credited: 360 lb.**

**(c) Value that year:**
- At 1910's price of 24c (Roy's leaf): 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40**.
- At the 25c long-run average it would be $90.00.

### C3. The same 1898–1922 years

**(a) Credited to patrons:** 40,000,000 × 3.61% = **1,444,000 lb**.

**(b) Reached Larrow Green in the station cream:** 40,000,000 × 3.80% = **1,520,000 lb**. This is the ledger's received figure, which equals the true fat in the milk.

**(c) The difference is 76,000 lb.**
- As a share of the **fat credited**: 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.26%**. This is the ledger's "five and a quarter per cent, as usual."
- As a share of the **fat actually delivered and received**: 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5.00%**.
- Teague's own 1898 entry confirms the base: 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26%, and 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.00%.
- Teague ("of the fat actually in the cream") and Bulletin 471's introduction ("of the fat actually delivered") put the 5¼% on the wrong base.

### C4. The invoice

**(a) Total:** **$77.93**.

### C5

**(a) Two different spans.**
- **Glass in use:** from **1 May 1897** until condemnation on **12 June 1923**, which is **26 years, 1 month and 11 days**. Replacement glass was in use from July 1923, so a few weeks more at most.
- **Money calculation:** runs over **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922**, on Kyle's arbitration figure of 40,000,000 lb (1.6 million lb a year).
- **Why they differ:** 1897 (short glass only from May) and 1923 (short glass only until June) were partial years. The station-account run compiled for the arbitration covers whole years only, from the first station account in 1898. The losses in those two partial years are real but outside the figure, so **76,000 lb and $19,000 are a floor**.

**(b) First-season intake (1896):** **cannot be determined from the sources.**
- Nym, as curator, notes that no 1896 intake figure exists. That season's milk was carried on the creamery's own books and never filed with the station papers (Kyle).
- Sill says about 900,000 lb and Kyle "a good eleven hundred thousand." The two memories disagree, and Kyle admits his is "not in any book I ever ruled."
- In any case 1896 used sound borrowed glass, so it is outside the loss.

## Section D — Logic

### D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment

**When and how long.** From **8 May to 10 July 1911: nine weeks** (63 days). The start date is fixed by Orra's diary entry for 8 May.

**The variable.** The measuring glass: the station's own short measure versus a borrowed sound one.

**Held constant.**
- the tester (Ansel);
- the patrons, herds, feed and land;
- the scales, acid, bottles, method and supervision.

**The result.** Tests rose to about 3.80. They dropped back to the old level the day the station's own glass returned, which was in midsummer. Ansel marked the book "?".

**Why the season does not explain it.**
- The gap was constant in every month.
- Bulletin 214 found zero hay effect in summer.
- The reversal came on 10 July, still in the flush. That rules out Kepp's summer-flush explanation.

**What it establishes that calculation alone would not.** It is an intervention: switching one variable on and off moved the result both ways. That shows **causation located in the station's measuring apparatus**, not in the milk, the men, the land or the scales. A calculation only shows that a 0.95 ratio *fits* the numbers. The interval also:
- rules out the tester as the cause, since the same man produced the higher figures;
- rules out any gradual cause.

**What it does not establish.**
- which glass item was at fault, or by exactly how many c.c.;
- that every pipette in the lot was short;
- when the fault began (it does not by itself prove 1 May 1897);
- the size of the loss in pounds or dollars;
- the accuracy of the borrowed measure itself;
- whether a supplier or anyone else was at fault.

Nine weeks of one season also cannot prove the gap was the same in winter. That comes from the test book, the ledger and the constant 0.19.

### D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream

**Four independent reasons:**

1. **The station gain** (Teague's and Kyle's ledger, and Sill at the churn). More butterfat reached Larrow Green than was credited: 5¼% of the credited fat, every year. A tester skimming cream would produce a *shortfall* at the creamery, not a surplus. Sill independently says the Hollow cream always "came in heavier than the sheet said."
2. **The 1911 interval** (Ansel's test book, Orra's diary, Falke). Tests rose for nine weeks while the same man was testing, under no extra supervision.
3. **July 1923 onward** (Vose, Falke, Hazel). With new glass, Ansel's tests rose to 3.80 and stayed there through 1924. A thief covering a theft would not suddenly start recording the truth without any accusation having changed his situation. The rise came before the 1925 petition, contrary to Vose's "after the looking."
4. **Outside observers found nothing.**
   - The 1908 committee watched the test made and found "no dishonesty of any kind."
   - Frick's board watched it made in the weigh room in 1925.
   - Strawn inspected the station for years.
   - The Brant weigh book shows the scales always agreed.

**Why these are independent.** They rest on different records kept by different people for different purposes:
- the office ledger at Larrow Green, and the buttermaker's eye at the churn;
- the tester's own book in one particular summer;
- the post-1923 record after the glass was changed;
- outside witnesses at the station.

None of them depends on any of the others.

**Still valid without the 1958 measurement.** Reasons 1, 2 and 3 all hold even if the crate had never been measured. The ledger gain alone refutes theft.

**Did the bookkeeper question his own entries?**
- By his own account, **Teague raised the gain line once, with Vose in 1916, and was told to let it alone.** Kyle, who sat outside the manager's door, says Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years." The 1916 exchange therefore rests on Teague's word alone and cannot be confirmed.
- In either case Teague **never set his own "as usual" line against the theft he insinuated at the 1925 hearing**. It continued in his 1948 account, where he misstates it as a share "of the fat actually in the cream."
- He did not question the ledger in any way that bore on the accusation.

### D3. Did Vose know, before June 1923, that the tests ran low?

**He knew of the complaint.**
- The Hollow's tests had been complained of since 1902.
- The 1908 committee had investigated them.
- In 1919 he himself sent a circular attributing the low tests to water, which shows he knew they were low relative to the valley.

**Whether he knew they were low against the *truth* cannot be established.**
- The evidence that they were, the station gain, was on every ledger sheet he signed, and Teague says he raised it with Vose in 1916.
- But Kyle, who watched him sign "a thousand times," says he put his thumb on the bottom figure and signed without reading the lines above it.
- No source records him acknowledging what the gain meant.
- His water theory is incompatible with understanding it: water cannot make extra fat appear at the creamery.

**Answer.** Before June 1923 Vose had *the means* to know, and was pointed at it (per Teague), but the sources show inattention and a refusal to look, not proven knowledge. Anything firmer would be a guess. His 1926 account (r08) also misdates the rise to after the inquiry.

## Section F — Theory

### F1. What produced the shortfall

**The causal theory.**
- The station's own testing outfit arrived from Ammon & Sons and went into use on **1 May 1897**. Its 72 pipettes were marked 17.6 c.c. but delivered **16.72 c.c., 95% of the mark**.
- The Babcock bottle's neck is ruled for a full 17.6 c.c. charge, so each test measured the fat in a short charge and read it as if the charge were full. **Every test read 95% of the truth.**
- This explains the size and the shape of the error:
  - The true 3.80 read **3.61**, a gap of **0.19**.
  - Richer milk lost more (a true 4.80 read 4.56).
  - The error was the same in every month, every season and for every farm, because it was proportional and came from one consistent lot of glass.
- **Why it began when it did:** the borrowed 1896 glass was sound, and the error began the day the Ammon glass went into use.
- **Why it ended when it did:** Strawn condemned the lot on **12 June 1923**. With new glass from July 1923 the tests read **3.80** (the 1924 average).
- **Why nothing else had to change:** the milk, cows, land, scales, acid, man and method were the same throughout. The only thing that changed at either end was the glass, and the brief swap of 1911 moved the tests the same way.
- **Where the fat went:** it was never lost. It went on down in the cream and appeared at Larrow Green as the "station gain" of 5¼% of the credited fat. That is 76,000 lb over 1898–1922, worth $19,000, of which the patrons recovered $3,800.

**The three competing explanations.**

1. **The patrons watered their milk.**
   - *Advanced by* Selby Vose (the 1919 circular), echoed by Sill.
   - *Defeated by:*
     - Strawn's lactometer never found water;
     - Emmet's weigh book shows the farm and station scales always agreed;
     - the Alder Corners cheese yield would have exposed water, and the same men's milk tested 3.80 there;
     - water cannot make extra fat arrive at the creamery, yet the station gain shows it did;
     - the 1908 committee checked the water supply and found nothing.
2. **The tester was stealing cream and writing low tests to cover it.**
   - *Advanced by* Wilbur Teague at the 1925 hearing, and carried in valley talk (Vira, Oram's father, Rundle's "back door").
   - *Defeated by:*
     - the ledger's station gain, since more fat reached the creamery than was credited;
     - the 1911 interval, when the same man's tests rose;
     - the rise after July 1923 while Ansel was still testing;
     - the 1908 committee and the 1925 board both watched the test made.
3. **Wet sedge hay and sour ground thin the milk.**
   - *Advanced by* Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee, restated in 1919, and by Dr. Roland Kepp in 1926. Falke partly conceded it before withdrawing.
   - *Defeated by:*
     - Bulletin 214: not more than 0.05 in winter and nothing in summer;
     - the 0.19 gap persisted in summer;
     - the committee sampled only January–March;
     - summer milk from the same farms tested 3.80 at Alder Corners;
     - the tests rose in 1911 and after 1923 with no drainage or change of feed.

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?**
- **No.** Its exhibits were the test book, the receiving book, Emmet's weigh book and Strawn's 1923 notebook, which recorded the condemnation (Frick, Kyle).
- The crate was never raised (Effie). It sat in the loft about 20 feet above the weigh room where two of the three sittings were held.

**What did the tester believe?**
- Ansel **suspected the glass**. In the summer of 1911 he twice told his family it was "something in the glass" (Duncan) and wrote only a "?" in the book, because he could not prove it.
- Before the board he denied any theft, which Frick believed, and he recorded that the gap was the same in every season.
- Frick's later claim that Ansel privately blamed the patrons is uncorroborated hearsay. It contradicts Ansel's own statement and his family's testimony, and is rejected.

## Section G — Summary

### G1

In 1897 the Larrow Valley creamery's Ostrey Hollow station began testing with 72 pipettes that delivered only 95% of their marked 17.6 c.c. Every test read 5% low: 3.61 instead of 3.80. Under a by-law making the station's test final, Hessel Bottom farmers were underpaid 76,000 lb of butterfat, $19,000, until an inspector condemned the glass in 1923. The fat itself arrived at the creamery and was booked annually as "station gain." Water, theft and bog hay were blamed, and in 1925 tester Ansel Keddie was dismissed while the patrons got $3,800. In 1958 his granddaughter Ivy found the crate in the station loft and measured it. The truth took eight records to assemble:
- the invoice;
- the ledger;
- the 1911 borrowed-glass weeks;
- a farmer's weigh book;
- Alder Corners' tests of the same milk;
- the inspector's notebook;
- the refuted hay report;
- the crate.

No one had laid them side by side.
