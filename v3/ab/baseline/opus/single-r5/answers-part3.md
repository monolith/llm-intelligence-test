# Answer Sheet — Part 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

### C1

**(a) Proportion.**
- The nine surviving pipettes, all marked 17.6 c.c., delivered a mean of **16.72 c.c.**
- 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95**.
- A short charge into a bottle whose neck is ruled for 17.6 c.c. reads low in the same proportion. Every recorded test was **95% of the true test, 5% low**. Equivalently, true = recorded ÷ 0.95 = recorded × 1.0526.
- Each pipette was short by 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c.
- Bulletin 471's introduction and the *Chronicle* give 16.27 c.c. That is rejected: it gives a ratio of 0.9244 and a true test of 3.905, which the Bulletin itself admits cannot square with the 1924 figure of 3.80.

**(b) True average and shortfall.**
- The recorded average was **3.61**.
- True average: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**.
- The recorded tests were **0.19 point short**.
- Independent checks on 3.80:
  - the 1911 borrowed measure gave about 3.80;
  - the 1924 average with new glass was 3.80;
  - Alder Corners averaged 3.80.
- The 3.16 given by Teague and Frick is a transposition of 3.61.

**(c) Uncredited butterfat, 1898–1922.**
- Milk: 40,000,000 lb.
- True fat: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb.
- Credited: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb.
- **Uncredited: 76,000 lb.**

**(d) Money.**
- 76,000 lb × $0.25 = **$19,000**.
- The award was **$3,800**: 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **one-fifth (20%)**.
- It was also charged to the station account, which the uncredited fat itself had fed.

### C2. Emmet Brant, 1910

- He delivered 200,000 lb of milk and was paid on a test of 3.42.

**(a) Credited:** 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of butterfat.

**(b) Owed and not credited:**
- True test: 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60.
- True fat: 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb.
- **Not credited: 360 lb.**

**(c) Value that year:**
- At 1910's price of 24c (Roy's leaf): 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40**.
- At the 25c long-run average it would be $90.00.

### C3. The same 1898–1922 years

**(a) Credited to patrons:** 40,000,000 × 3.61% = **1,444,000 lb**.

**(b) Reached Larrow Green in the station cream:** 40,000,000 × 3.80% = **1,520,000 lb**. This is the ledger's received figure, which equals the true fat in the milk.

**(c) The difference is 76,000 lb.**
- As a share of the **fat credited**: 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.26%**. This is the ledger's "five and a quarter per cent, as usual."
- As a share of the **fat actually delivered and received**: 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5.00%**.
- Teague's own 1898 entry confirms the base: 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26%, and 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.00%.
- Teague ("of the fat actually in the cream") and Bulletin 471's introduction ("of the fat actually delivered") put the 5¼% on the wrong base.

### C5

**(a) Two different spans.**
- **Glass in use:** from **1 May 1897** until condemnation on **12 June 1923**, which is **26 years, 1 month and 11 days**. Replacement glass was in use from July 1923, so a few weeks more at most.
- **Money calculation:** runs over **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922**, on Kyle's arbitration figure of 40,000,000 lb (1.6 million lb a year).
- **Why they differ:** 1897 (short glass only from May) and 1923 (short glass only until June) were partial years. The station-account run compiled for the arbitration covers whole years only, from the first station account in 1898. The losses in those two partial years are real but outside the figure, so **76,000 lb and $19,000 are a floor**.

**(b) First-season intake (1896):** **cannot be determined from the sources.**
- Nym, as curator, notes that no 1896 intake figure exists. That season's milk was carried on the creamery's own books and never filed with the station papers (Kyle).
- Sill says about 900,000 lb and Kyle "a good eleven hundred thousand." The two memories disagree, and Kyle admits his is "not in any book I ever ruled."
- In any case 1896 used sound borrowed glass, so it is outside the loss.

## Section D — Logic

### D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment

**When and how long.** From **8 May to 10 July 1911: nine weeks** (63 days). The start date is fixed by Orra's diary entry for 8 May.

**The variable.** The measuring glass: the station's own short measure versus a borrowed sound one.

**Held constant.**
- the tester (Ansel);
- the patrons, herds, feed and land;
- the scales, acid, bottles, method and supervision.

**The result.** Tests rose to about 3.80. They dropped back to the old level the day the station's own glass returned, which was in midsummer. Ansel marked the book "?".

**Why the season does not explain it.**
- The gap was constant in every month.
- Bulletin 214 found zero hay effect in summer.
- The reversal came on 10 July, still in the flush. That rules out Kepp's summer-flush explanation.

**What it establishes that calculation alone would not.** It is an intervention: switching one variable on and off moved the result both ways. That shows **causation located in the station's measuring apparatus**, not in the milk, the men, the land or the scales. A calculation only shows that a 0.95 ratio *fits* the numbers. The interval also:
- rules out the tester as the cause, since the same man produced the higher figures;
- rules out any gradual cause.

**What it does not establish.**
- which glass item was at fault, or by exactly how many c.c.;
- that every pipette in the lot was short;
- when the fault began (it does not by itself prove 1 May 1897);
- the size of the loss in pounds or dollars;
- the accuracy of the borrowed measure itself;
- whether a supplier or anyone else was at fault.

Nine weeks of one season also cannot prove the gap was the same in winter. That comes from the test book, the ledger and the constant 0.19.

### D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream

**Four independent reasons:**

1. **The station gain** (Teague's and Kyle's ledger, and Sill at the churn). More butterfat reached Larrow Green than was credited: 5¼% of the credited fat, every year. A tester skimming cream would produce a *shortfall* at the creamery, not a surplus. Sill independently says the Hollow cream always "came in heavier than the sheet said."
2. **The 1911 interval** (Ansel's test book, Orra's diary, Falke). Tests rose for nine weeks while the same man was testing, under no extra supervision.
3. **July 1923 onward** (Vose, Falke, Hazel). With new glass, Ansel's tests rose to 3.80 and stayed there through 1924. A thief covering a theft would not suddenly start recording the truth without any accusation having changed his situation. The rise came before the 1925 petition, contrary to Vose's "after the looking."
4. **Outside observers found nothing.**
   - The 1908 committee watched the test made and found "no dishonesty of any kind."
   - Frick's board watched it made in the weigh room in 1925.
   - Strawn inspected the station for years.
   - The Brant weigh book shows the scales always agreed.

**Why these are independent.** They rest on different records kept by different people for different purposes:
- the office ledger at Larrow Green, and the buttermaker's eye at the churn;
- the tester's own book in one particular summer;
- the post-1923 record after the glass was changed;
- outside witnesses at the station.

None of them depends on any of the others.

**Still valid without the 1958 measurement.** Reasons 1, 2 and 3 all hold even if the crate had never been measured. The ledger gain alone refutes theft.

**Did the bookkeeper question his own entries?**
- By his own account, **Teague raised the gain line once, with Vose in 1916, and was told to let it alone.** Kyle, who sat outside the manager's door, says Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years." The 1916 exchange therefore rests on Teague's word alone and cannot be confirmed.
- In either case Teague **never set his own "as usual" line against the theft he insinuated at the 1925 hearing**. It continued in his 1948 account, where he misstates it as a share "of the fat actually in the cream."
- He did not question the ledger in any way that bore on the accusation.

### D3. Did Vose know, before June 1923, that the tests ran low?

**He knew of the complaint.**
- The Hollow's tests had been complained of since 1902.
- The 1908 committee had investigated them.
- In 1919 he himself sent a circular attributing the low tests to water, which shows he knew they were low relative to the valley.

**Whether he knew they were low against the *truth* cannot be established.**
- The evidence that they were, the station gain, was on every ledger sheet he signed, and Teague says he raised it with Vose in 1916.
- But Kyle, who watched him sign "a thousand times," says he put his thumb on the bottom figure and signed without reading the lines above it.
- No source records him acknowledging what the gain meant.
- His water theory is incompatible with understanding it: water cannot make extra fat appear at the creamery.

**Answer.** Before June 1923 Vose had *the means* to know, and was pointed at it (per Teague), but the sources show inattention and a refusal to look, not proven knowledge. Anything firmer would be a guess. His 1926 account (r08) also misdates the rise to after the inquiry.

## Section F — Theory

### F1. What produced the shortfall

**The causal theory.**
- The station's own testing outfit arrived from Ammon & Sons and went into use on **1 May 1897**. Its 72 pipettes were marked 17.6 c.c. but delivered **16.72 c.c., 95% of the mark**.
- The Babcock bottle's neck is ruled for a full 17.6 c.c. charge, so each test measured the fat in a short charge and read it as if the charge were full. **Every test read 95% of the truth.**
- This explains the size and the shape of the error:
  - The true 3.80 read **3.61**, a gap of **0.19**.
  - Richer milk lost more (a true 4.80 read 4.56).
  - The error was the same in every month, every season and for every farm, because it was proportional and came from one consistent lot of glass.
- **Why it began when it did:** the borrowed 1896 glass was sound, and the error began the day the Ammon glass went into use.
- **Why it ended when it did:** Strawn condemned the lot on **12 June 1923**. With new glass from July 1923 the tests read **3.80** (the 1924 average).
- **Why nothing else had to change:** the milk, cows, land, scales, acid, man and method were the same throughout. The only thing that changed at either end was the glass, and the brief swap of 1911 moved the tests the same way.
- **Where the fat went:** it was never lost. It went on down in the cream and appeared at Larrow Green as the "station gain" of 5¼% of the credited fat. That is 76,000 lb over 1898–1922, worth $19,000, of which the patrons recovered $3,800.

**The three competing explanations.**

1. **The patrons watered their milk.**
   - *Advanced by* Selby Vose (the 1919 circular), echoed by Sill.
   - *Defeated by:*
     - Strawn's lactometer never found water;
     - Emmet's weigh book shows the farm and station scales always agreed;
     - the Alder Corners cheese yield would have exposed water, and the same men's milk tested 3.80 there;
     - water cannot make extra fat arrive at the creamery, yet the station gain shows it did;
     - the 1908 committee checked the water supply and found nothing.
2. **The tester was stealing cream and writing low tests to cover it.**
   - *Advanced by* Wilbur Teague at the 1925 hearing, and carried in valley talk (Vira, Oram's father, Rundle's "back door").
   - *Defeated by:*
     - the ledger's station gain, since more fat reached the creamery than was credited;
     - the 1911 interval, when the same man's tests rose;
     - the rise after July 1923 while Ansel was still testing;
     - the 1908 committee and the 1925 board both watched the test made.
3. **Wet sedge hay and sour ground thin the milk.**
   - *Advanced by* Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee, restated in 1919, and by Dr. Roland Kepp in 1926. Falke partly conceded it before withdrawing.
   - *Defeated by:*
     - Bulletin 214: not more than 0.05 in winter and nothing in summer;
     - the 0.19 gap persisted in summer;
     - the committee sampled only January–March;
     - summer milk from the same farms tested 3.80 at Alder Corners;
     - the tests rose in 1911 and after 1923 with no drainage or change of feed.

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?**
- **No.** Its exhibits were the test book, the receiving book, Emmet's weigh book and Strawn's 1923 notebook, which recorded the condemnation (Frick, Kyle).
- The crate was never raised (Effie). It sat in the loft about 20 feet above the weigh room where two of the three sittings were held.

**What did the tester believe?**
- Ansel **suspected the glass**. In the summer of 1911 he twice told his family it was "something in the glass" (Duncan) and wrote only a "?" in the book, because he could not prove it.
- Before the board he denied any theft, which Frick believed, and he recorded that the gap was the same in every season.
- Frick's later claim that Ansel privately blamed the patrons is uncorroborated hearsay. It contradicts Ansel's own statement and his family's testimony, and is rejected.

## Section G — Summary

### G1

In 1897 the Larrow Valley creamery's Ostrey Hollow station began testing with 72 pipettes that delivered only 95% of their marked 17.6 c.c. Every test read 5% low: 3.61 instead of 3.80. Under a by-law making the station's test final, Hessel Bottom farmers were underpaid 76,000 lb of butterfat, $19,000, until an inspector condemned the glass in 1923. The fat itself arrived at the creamery and was booked annually as "station gain." Water, theft and bog hay were blamed, and in 1925 tester Ansel Keddie was dismissed while the patrons got $3,800. In 1958 his granddaughter Ivy found the crate in the station loft and measured it. The truth took eight records to assemble:
- the invoice;
- the ledger;
- the 1911 borrowed-glass weeks;
- a farmer's weigh book;
- Alder Corners' tests of the same milk;
- the inspector's notebook;
- the refuted hay report;
- the crate.

No one had laid them side by side.
