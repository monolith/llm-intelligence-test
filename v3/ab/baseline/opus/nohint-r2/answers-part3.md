# Answer Sheet — Part 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

**C1.**

(a) **The tests read 5 per cent low.** The recorded test was 0.95 × the true test.
- Working from the glass: each pipette was marked 17.6 c.c. but delivered on average 16.72 c.c. So 16.72 / 17.6 = 0.95, and each was short by 0.88 c.c., which is 5.0%.
- Why that carries through: the neck of the test bottle is ruled for a full 17.6 c.c. charge, so a charge that is 5% short gives a fat reading 5% low.
- The *Chronicle* prints "16.27". That would mean 7.56% short, a true test of 3.905 and a deficiency of 0.295, which contradicts the 0.19 given by r17 and r22.
- 16.72 is confirmed twice over: 0.19 / 3.80 = 5%, and the ledger's gain of 5¼% on the credited figure equals 0.05 / 0.95 = 5.26%. So "16.27" is transposed digits.

(b) **True average test = 3.80.** Recorded 3.61 / 0.95 = 3.80, so the recorded tests were **0.19 point** short (3.80 − 3.61). For comparison, Larrow Green averaged 3.78, and the same station averaged 3.85 after 1924.

(c) Over the 25 full delivery years: 40,000,000 lb of milk × 0.0019 = **76,000 lb of butterfat** not credited. Equivalently, 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000. Oram's "67,000 lb" transposes the digits.

(d) 76,000 lb × $0.25 = **$19,000**. The patrons received $3,800, and 3,800 / 19,000 = **1/5 (20%)**.
- The *Chronicle*'s "$19,600" cannot be derived from any of the figures in the sources.
- The clean one-fifth also supports $3,800 over Oram's $3,500, which would give 18.4%.

**C2.** This is Emmet Brant's 1910 leaf: 200,000 lb of milk at a recorded test of 3.42.

(a) Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of butterfat.

(b) True test: 3.42 / 0.95 = 3.60. Owed: 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb. So **360 lb** was owed and not credited.
- The error is proportional, so a herd testing below the station average loses slightly less than 0.19 point (here 0.18).
- Adding a flat 0.19 instead would give 380 lb, but a flat addition is not how a short charge behaves.

(c) 360 lb × $0.25 = **$90.00**.
- The 1910 butterfat price itself is not in the sources. I used the long-run average of 25¢ that Kyle gives, and that is an inference.

**C3.** Over the same 25 full delivery years:

(a) Credited to patrons: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**.

(b) Reached Larrow Green in the station cream: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lb**. Larrow Green tested the cream with sound glass.

(c) The difference is 76,000 lb.
- **As a percentage of the fat credited:** 76,000 / 1,444,000 = **5.26%**. This is the ledger's "station gain … five and a quarter per cent, as usual".
- **As a percentage of the fat actually received:** 76,000 / 1,520,000 = **5.00%**.
- The ledger's 5¼% is a percentage of the smaller, *credited* figure. The 5% pipette shortfall is a percentage of the *true* figure. They are the same error expressed on two different bases.

**C4.**

(a) The invoice total is **$77.93**: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15. The "$77.39" in Junia Ammon's prose and in the *Chronicle* is a transposition.

(b) The invoice bought **72 pipettes** (6 dozen × $0.35 = $25.20), each **0.88 c.c. short** on average (17.6 − 16.72). Oram's "five dozen" is contradicted by the invoice arithmetic.

**C5.**

(a) The faulty glass was in use from **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923**. That is **26 years, 1 month and 11 days** (about 26 years and 6 weeks), less the nine weeks in 1911 when a borrowed measure was used. Replacement glass was in use from July 1923.
- The money calculation runs over **25 full delivery years**: Kyle's 40,000,000 lb ÷ 1,600,000 lb a year = 25. I take these to be the complete account years **1898–1922**.
- The two numbers differ because the glass came in and went out in the middle of a year. In 1897 the station was still on borrowed Larrow Green glass until 1 May. In 1923 the faulty glass was used only until 12 June. Only years in which the faulty glass was used from start to finish count as full delivery years.
- The exact year boundary (calendar or account year) is my inference. The count of 25 comes from Kyle's figures.

(b) **This cannot be determined from the sources.**
- The only figure is Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" (1,100,000 lb). He had it from men who were there, and he says himself that it "is not in any book I ever ruled." The first season's sheets were kept on the creamery's own books and never filed with the station papers.
- That makes it a single hearsay source with no document behind it. It is plausible, since a first season that began on 4 May would fall below the 1.6M-a-year average, but it is not a finding.
- (The unread retellings r01–r16 may have held a documented figure; I could not use them.)

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment.**

*When and how long:* From **8 May to 10 July 1911**, exactly **nine weeks** (63 days). Sources: r22's dates, Orra's diary for 8 May 1911, and Duncan's "that summer of 1911."

*Variable:* only the measuring glass. The station's own measure was replaced by a borrowed one.

*Held constant:* the herds, pasture, season, patrons, tester (Ansel himself), supervision, scale, separator and procedure.

*Result:* the tests stood markedly higher throughout, and fell back to their old level on the day the station's own glass returned.

*What it establishes that calculation alone cannot:*
- It is a before-during-after test in actual use, with the glass as the only variable. It shows that the station's own glass *caused* the low readings at the station itself.
- It rules out the tester (same man, same honesty, higher figures) and rules out hay or water (same farms, same milk).
- The 1958 lab measurement shows the pipettes were short in a laboratory. The 1911 interval shows that the shortness actually drove the station's tests.

*What it does not establish:*
- How large the error was. "Markedly higher" is not quantified in these sources, and the borrowed measure's own accuracy is unknown.
- Which piece of the glass was at fault (pipette or another measure), or that every piece in the lot was short.
- Why the glass was short, or whether Ammon knew.
- That the error ran back to 1897. That needs the 1897 start date, the constant deficiency and the 1958 measurement.
- It was also only nine weeks in one season, so on its own it cannot show that the deficiency was constant all year.

**D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream.**

Three independent reasons:
1. **The 1911 interval** (Falke r22; Orra r24; Duncan r18). The same man made the tests under the same supervision, and his figures rose as soon as the glass changed.
2. **The ledger** (Kyle, r23). Every year *more* butterfat reached Larrow Green in the station's cream than the station test had credited to the patrons: "station gain, 5¼ per cent, as usual." A tester skimming cream would leave *less* fat arriving than credited, not 5¼% more. The "concealing" low tests would have to be matched by a surplus at the main creamery, which is the opposite of what theft produces.
3. **The 1923–24 changeover** (Falke r22; Orra r24). New glass came in from July 1923, and from 1924 the station averaged 3.85. Ansel was still the tester until April 1925, so the same man produced normal figures once the glass was changed.

A fourth, which confirms the others: the 1958 measurement of the pipettes (r19, r22) accounts for the whole deficiency.

*Why the three are independent:* each rests on a different record kept by a different person for a different purpose.
- The first is the station test book and a wife's diary, from 1911.
- The second is the Association's own ledger, written by its bookkeeper at Larrow Green.
- The third is the post-1923 station averages, following an inspector's action.
None of them depends on the others or on the 1958 laboratory work.

*Reasons that would still hold if the glass had never been measured in 1958:* the ledger (2), the 1911 interval (1) and the 1924 rise (3) all would.

*Did the bookkeeper ever question the ledger entries?* **No.** Wilbur Teague wrote the station-gain line every year of his service and "never asked anybody anything in forty years" (r23).
- These entries refute the theft accusation.
- My sources name the accuser at the hearing only as "an officer of the Association" (r22). If, as the question implies, that officer was Teague, he urged theft while his own annual entries showed surplus fat arriving, and he never questioned them.

**D3. Did the manager know, before 1923, that the tests ran low?**

**Yes, he knew the tests ran low. What he did not see, as far as the sources show, was that his own ledger explained it.**
- In June 1919 Selby Vose signed and sent the circular warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons about water (r23, r24, r22). That warning only makes sense if he knew the station's tests were low.
- Complaints had been made "at every annual meeting for a quarter of a century" (r19), with the first in 1902 (r22). Vose was manager from 1911, and the station account was read out at the annual meeting.
- On the other hand, Kyle says Vose "read the totals and he never read the lines above them." He put his thumb on the bottom figure and signed. So there is no evidence that he understood the station-gain line, which shows the fat arriving in full.
- He knew about the symptom and blamed the patrons. He did not connect it to the ledger, or, before Strawn's visit, to the glass.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. The causal theory.**

The Ostrey Hollow station's own pipettes, a lot of 72 marked 17.6 c.c. and bought from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet in 1897, each delivered only about 16.72 c.c., which is 5 per cent less.

- **Why an error at all:** Every Babcock-type test put a short charge of milk into a bottle whose neck was graduated for the full 17.6 c.c., so every reading came out 5 per cent low.
- **Why that size:** Five per cent of the true average of 3.80 is exactly **0.19 point**, which gives the recorded 3.61. The same 5% on the credited figure is the ledger's "station gain, 5¼ per cent" of fat arriving at Larrow Green, where it was tested with sound glass. Over the 25 full years that comes to **76,000 lb, or $19,000**. The error was proportional and built into the instrument, so it was the same "flat as a floor" in every month, wet years and dry. A feed effect would have been seasonal.
- **Why it began when it did:** It started on **1 May 1897**, when this lot replaced the glass borrowed from Larrow Green for the first season.
- **Why it ended when it did:** It ended when Strawn condemned the lot on **12 June 1923** and new glass came in during July. The station then averaged 3.85 from 1924.
- **Why nothing on the farms or at the station had to change:** The scales were true (Emmet's scale agreed with the station's), and the cows, hay, tester and procedure were unchanged. The fat was never lost. It went down the valley in the cream, and the ledger recorded it. The 1911 interval, when a borrowed measure raised the tests for nine weeks, is the natural experiment that confirms the theory.

**The three competing explanations:**

1. **Watered milk.** Advanced by the manager, Selby Vose, in his June 1919 circular.
   - Defeated by Strawn's lactometer tests at every visit from 1908, which never found water.
   - Also, pay was per pound of fat, and water does not reduce the pounds of fat (my inference).
   - The ledger shows full fat arriving at Larrow Green.

2. **Theft by the tester.** Urged at the 1925 hearing by an officer of the Association; believed privately by the cream hauler, Oram's father.
   - Defeated by the 1911 nine weeks (same man, higher tests).
   - By the ledger surplus: more fat arrived than was credited, the opposite of theft.
   - By the rise to 3.85 in 1924 under the same tester.

3. **Wet bog hay.** Advanced by the Association's 1908 herd committee and an Ashlin veterinarian in 1926, and briefly conceded by Falke.
   - Defeated by Bulletin No. 214, the college's 1911 feeding trial: at most 0.05 in winter and nothing measurable in summer, whereas the deficiency was 0.19 in July too.
   - By the same cows and hay testing normally over the ridge at Alder Corners in summer.
   - By the jump in 1911 and again in 1924 with no change of feed.

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glass? No.**
- The glass sat crated in the loft over the weigh room from 1923 until Ivy found it in February 1958. Duncan says it had been there 35 years undisturbed, and Falke says it had lain there "since the day it was condemned."
- Two of the board's three sittings were held in the room directly beneath it (r18, r21, r24).
- The board had Strawn's 1923 notebook recording the condemnation (r23) but never connected it to the tests. It "found no cause established."

**What did the tester himself believe? That the glass was at fault.**
- After the 1911 interval he told his family "it was something in the glass" (r18).
- He never dared write it down, since he could not prove it, and entered only a question mark (r22).
- He kept on testing faithfully with the only outfit he had, and those figures were later used against him.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** (148 words)

For twenty-six years (1897–1923) the Ostrey Hollow station tested its patrons' milk with 72 pipettes from Ammon & Sons that delivered 5 per cent less milk than their 17.6 c.c. mark. Every test read 5 per cent low (3.61 against a true 3.80). Over 25 full years the patrons went uncredited for 76,000 lb of butterfat, worth $19,000, which the Association's ledger booked as "station gain, as usual." Neighbours blamed the tester, the manager blamed water, a committee blamed hay. In 1925 an arbitration paid $3,800, a fifth of the loss, and dismissed tester Ansel Keddie, who died under suspicion. In 1958 his granddaughter Ivy found the condemned glass in the station loft and measured it. The proof lay in eight separate places: an invoice, a test book, a weigh book, a ledger, an inspector's notebook, a cheese factory's records, a diary and a crate — never read together.
