## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1 — A Short Lot from Tarnet (1897–1923)

**Who:** Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, glassblowers of Tarnet (founded by Marcus Ammon; firm history written by Junia Ammon, 1949), and their customer, the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association.

**What happened and when:** On **14 April 1897** Ammon & Sons invoiced the Association for a complete testing outfit for its Ostrey Hollow skimming station:
- 6 dozen (**72**) milk-test pipettes marked **17.6 c.c.**, at 35¢ each: $25.20
- 12 dozen (144) test bottles, 8-per-cent neck, at 22¢ each: $31.68
- one acid measure: $0.90
- one four-bottle hand tester: $18.00
- freight: $2.15

The line items add up to **$77.93**, which is the total printed on the invoice. Junia Ammon's prose and the *Ashlin Chronicle* both say "$77.39". That is a transposition, and the quoted document's own arithmetic governs. The outfit went into use on **1 May 1897**. Until then, through the station's first season, glass had been borrowed from the Larrow Green creamery. Orra Keddie's diary entry for 1 May 1897 confirms the date; Bertram Kyle's "1 May 1898" is wrong.

**The fault:** The pipettes were short. Measured in 1958, they delivered about **16.72 c.c.** on average against the 17.6 marked on the stems. That is **0.88 c.c.**, or exactly **5 per cent**, short, and every piece was short in the same proportion.
- The *Chronicle* printed "16.27". Only 16.72 fits two independent checks: the 0.19-point deficiency on a true 3.80 test, and the ledger's 5¼ per cent "station gain". So I take 16.27 as transposed digits.
- Junia Ammon thinks a reground mould made the lot run short by accident. Spot checks could not catch it because the pieces were "consistently wrong."
- The firm's works books burned in 1931, so **whether anyone at Ammon knew cannot be determined**. The *Chronicle*'s claim that the lot was put off "knowingly" is an assertion without evidence.
- Junia also says the firm "sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900". The 1897 invoice she reproduces contradicts that.

**Aftermath:** The state inspector condemned the lot on 12 June 1923. The Association wrote on **28 June 1923** for six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles. Ammon replied on **2 July** that the order was entered: "We regret the trouble. Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name." In 1949 the firm printed the invoice and this letter side by side as its own "bad morning", and said its regret was for the letter, not the accident.

**Documents:** r20 (invoice and letter book, quoted in facsimile), r19, r24 (diary, 1 May 1897), r23.

### Story 2 — The Ostrey Hollow Station and Its Tester (1889–1925)

**Who:** Ansel Keddie (1861–1934), tester and separator man; his wife Orra (née Brant, 1866–1948); their son Duncan (b. 1894); the station's patrons.

**Background:** The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was organized at Larrow Green in 1889 and built its creamery there in 1891.
- **By-law article seven** (the printed by-laws are undated, and the early minute book is lost) paid each patron for the pounds of butterfat found in his milk, not for its weight. The test that counted was the one made at his own station. This rule is what turned a measuring fault into lost money.
- In winter 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley, to serve the six farms of Hessel Bottom. It **opened on 4 May 1896**.
- Milk was weighed, sampled, tested and separated there. The cream went down to Larrow Green on the Orams' wagon, and the skim went back to the farms.
- The building had a covered platform, an intake room (the "weigh room") with scale, vat and separator, and a loft above it reached by a ladder nailed to the wall.
- Ansel Keddie was the tester from the opening, for **29 seasons** (1896–1924). He kept the station test book in his own hand from 1896.

**The low tests:** From early on the station tested low.
- Its long-run average was **3.61**, against **3.78** at Larrow Green.
- The deficiency was **0.19 point**, the same in every month, in the June flush and in February alike.
- The first complaint on record came in **1902**. In Orra's diary for March 1902, Ansel says: "it is my figures they will look at." The complaint came back every decade after that.

**The 1911 interval:**
- On **8 May 1911** the station's own measure broke (Orra: "A. home cross about a broken measure").
- A borrowed measure was used for **nine weeks, until 10 July 1911**, and the tests stood markedly higher.
- When the station went back to its own glass, the tests fell back to their old level.
- Ansel put a question mark in the book. At home he said, twice, that it was "something in the glass," but he never wrote that down because he could not prove it. He went on testing with that glass for fourteen more years, and those figures were later used against him.

**End:** Ansel was dismissed on **30 April 1925** (Story 7). He worked as a carpenter afterwards and died at home in **February 1934**. His name was not cleared until 1958. The station closed in 1946 and was later used to store hay tools.

**Documents:** r22, r18, r24, r21, r19.

### Story 3 — Hessel Bottom and the Brant Weigh Book (1897–1963)

**Who:**
- Emmet Brant (d. spring 1929), the largest farmer on Hessel Bottom and Orra Keddie's brother.
- His son Roy Brant (d. 1962), and Roy's daughter Hazel (taped 1981).
- Jerome Cudd and his daughter Rosalie, of the Alder Corners cheese factory in Vessey County.

**The weigh book:** Hessel Bottom was six wet farms, holding more than six families. Emmet had the biggest place and hauled the most milk.
- On **1 January 1897** he began a weigh book. That date is Duncan Keddie's. Hazel says 1900, but she says herself that she does not keep years.
- He entered his own scale weight and the station's intake weight side by side. The two agreed, so the station's *scale* was never at fault.

**The 1910 leaf**, which is the one year Hazel is sure of:
- **200,000 lb** of milk, "even," with "his scale and theirs agreeing," paid on a test of **3.42**.
- That credits him with **6,840 lb** of butterfat.
- At the true test of 3.42/0.95 = **3.60**, he was owed **7,200 lb**, so he was **360 lb** short.
- That is worth about **$90** at the long-run average price of 25¢/lb. Applying that average to 1910 is my inference.

**Cross-check over the ridge:** Four of the six farms hauled their milk over the ridge to the Cudds' cheese factory at Alder Corners during the summer flush, and to the station the rest of the year. The same milk tested **0.19 lower at the station** "than anywhere else," every season.
- Jerome Cudd's Alder Corners test book is the independent record of this. His daughter Rosalie kept it from 1941.
- In 1958 Rosalie had the figures copied for Ivy Keddie. The book went to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970.

**The family:** Emmet signed the patrons' petition of January 1925. He and Ansel had quarrelled over the June 1919 water circular, and they did not speak from then until Emmet died ten years later (Orra's diary). So Duncan's picture of Emmet at the Keddie Sunday table "for thirty-six years" holds only up to 1919.

**Where the book went:**
- It was lent to the 1925 arbitration (Kyle).
- It passed to Roy in 1929.
- Roy **lent** it to Ivy in 1958 and got it back ("lent, and I want the word").
- At Roy's death in 1962 it went to Hazel, who gave it to the Ordell County Historical Rooms at Ashlin in **1963**.

**Documents:** r17, r18, r23, r24.

### Story 4 — The Office Ledger: "Station Gain, as Usual" (1911–1925)

**Who:**
- Gideon Vose, who organized the Association, was its first president and wrote its by-laws.
- His **son** Selby Vose, manager from **1911** for about a quarter century.
- Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper.
- Bertram Kyle, clerk at the outer desk of the Larrow Green office for 25 years (memoir written 1953).

**What happened:** Every year the Ostrey Hollow station account held three figures and a sentence:
1. the butterfat credited to patrons on the station test;
2. the butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream, tested there with sound glass;
3. the difference, entered as "station gain … five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account."

Teague wrote that line every year and "never asked anybody anything in forty years." Selby Vose put his thumb on the bottom figure and signed. The account was read out every year at the annual meeting.

**Quantities:** Kyle compiled the figures for the arbitration: **40,000,000 lb** of milk over the years complained of, at **1,600,000 lb a year** (so **25 years**), with butterfat averaging **25¢/lb**.
- At the recorded 3.61 test, **1,444,000 lb** of fat was credited.
- At the true 3.80, **1,520,000 lb** reached Larrow Green.
- The "gain" was **76,000 lb**. That is **5.26 per cent of the fat credited** (Kyle's "five and a quarter"), or 5.0 per cent of the fat received. It is exactly the pipette error.
- In plain sight, the Association booked about **$19,000** of its patrons' butterfat as station gain.

**The 1919 circular:** In **June 1919** Selby Vose signed a circular, typed by Kyle, warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons against watering their milk. Ansel Keddie refused to mail it. He carried it himself to **eleven families** and stood at each door while it was read. I follow Orra's diary here, written at the time, over Hazel's childhood memory of "nine". This was the start of the quarrel between Emmet and Ansel.

**1923 and after:**
- The office ordered replacement glass from Tarnet the same week the inspector condemned the lot, in June 1923. Kyle says "twenty-two", but he admits he does not keep dates. The office treated it like "a broken window."
- The 1925 award of $3,800 was charged to the station account. Kyle's "twenty-four" is wrong.
- Kyle's moral: nothing was hidden. It "was in plain sight with a name on it."

**Documents:** r23, r24, r17, r18, r22.
