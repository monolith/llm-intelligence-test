## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1 — A Short Lot from Tarnet (1897–1923)

**Who:** Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, glassblowers of Tarnet (founded by Marcus Ammon; firm history written by Junia Ammon, 1949), and their customer, the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association.

**What happened and when:** On **14 April 1897** Ammon & Sons invoiced the Association for a complete testing outfit for its Ostrey Hollow skimming station:
- 6 dozen (**72**) milk-test pipettes marked **17.6 c.c.**, at 35¢ each: $25.20
- 12 dozen (144) test bottles, 8-per-cent neck, at 22¢ each: $31.68
- one acid measure: $0.90
- one four-bottle hand tester: $18.00
- freight: $2.15

The line items add up to **$77.93**, which is the total printed on the invoice. Junia Ammon's prose and the *Ashlin Chronicle* both say "$77.39". That is a transposition, and the quoted document's own arithmetic governs. The outfit went into use on **1 May 1897**. Until then, through the station's first season, glass had been borrowed from the Larrow Green creamery. Orra Keddie's diary entry for 1 May 1897 confirms the date; Bertram Kyle's "1 May 1898" is wrong.

**The fault:** The pipettes were short. Measured in 1958, they delivered about **16.72 c.c.** on average against the 17.6 marked on the stems. That is **0.88 c.c.**, or exactly **5 per cent**, short, and every piece was short in the same proportion.
- The *Chronicle* printed "16.27". Only 16.72 fits two independent checks: the 0.19-point deficiency on a true 3.80 test, and the ledger's 5¼ per cent "station gain". So I take 16.27 as transposed digits.
- Junia Ammon thinks a reground mould made the lot run short by accident. Spot checks could not catch it because the pieces were "consistently wrong."
- The firm's works books burned in 1931, so **whether anyone at Ammon knew cannot be determined**. The *Chronicle*'s claim that the lot was put off "knowingly" is an assertion without evidence.
- Junia also says the firm "sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900". The 1897 invoice she reproduces contradicts that.

**Aftermath:** The state inspector condemned the lot on 12 June 1923. The Association wrote on **28 June 1923** for six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles. Ammon replied on **2 July** that the order was entered: "We regret the trouble. Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name." In 1949 the firm printed the invoice and this letter side by side as its own "bad morning", and said its regret was for the letter, not the accident.

**Documents:** r20 (invoice and letter book, quoted in facsimile), r19, r24 (diary, 1 May 1897), r23.

### Story 2 — The Ostrey Hollow Station and Its Tester (1889–1925)

**Who:** Ansel Keddie (1861–1934), tester and separator man; his wife Orra (née Brant, 1866–1948); their son Duncan (b. 1894); the station's patrons.

**Background:** The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was organized at Larrow Green in 1889 and built its creamery there in 1891.
- **By-law article seven** (the printed by-laws are undated, and the early minute book is lost) paid each patron for the pounds of butterfat found in his milk, not for its weight. The test that counted was the one made at his own station. This rule is what turned a measuring fault into lost money.
- In winter 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley, to serve the six farms of Hessel Bottom. It **opened on 4 May 1896**.
- Milk was weighed, sampled, tested and separated there. The cream went down to Larrow Green on the Orams' wagon, and the skim went back to the farms.
- The building had a covered platform, an intake room (the "weigh room") with scale, vat and separator, and a loft above it reached by a ladder nailed to the wall.
- Ansel Keddie was the tester from the opening, for **29 seasons** (1896–1924). He kept the station test book in his own hand from 1896.

**The low tests:** From early on the station tested low.
- Its long-run average was **3.61**, against **3.78** at Larrow Green.
- The deficiency was **0.19 point**, the same in every month, in the June flush and in February alike.
- The first complaint on record came in **1902**. In Orra's diary for March 1902, Ansel says: "it is my figures they will look at." The complaint came back every decade after that.

**The 1911 interval:**
- On **8 May 1911** the station's own measure broke (Orra: "A. home cross about a broken measure").
- A borrowed measure was used for **nine weeks, until 10 July 1911**, and the tests stood markedly higher.
- When the station went back to its own glass, the tests fell back to their old level.
- Ansel put a question mark in the book. At home he said, twice, that it was "something in the glass," but he never wrote that down because he could not prove it. He went on testing with that glass for fourteen more years, and those figures were later used against him.

**End:** Ansel was dismissed on **30 April 1925** (Story 7). He worked as a carpenter afterwards and died at home in **February 1934**. His name was not cleared until 1958. The station closed in 1946 and was later used to store hay tools.

**Documents:** r22, r18, r24, r21, r19.

### Story 3 — Hessel Bottom and the Brant Weigh Book (1897–1963)

**Who:**
- Emmet Brant (d. spring 1929), the largest farmer on Hessel Bottom and Orra Keddie's brother.
- His son Roy Brant (d. 1962), and Roy's daughter Hazel (taped 1981).
- Jerome Cudd and his daughter Rosalie, of the Alder Corners cheese factory in Vessey County.

**The weigh book:** Hessel Bottom was six wet farms, holding more than six families. Emmet had the biggest place and hauled the most milk.
- On **1 January 1897** he began a weigh book. That date is Duncan Keddie's. Hazel says 1900, but she says herself that she does not keep years.
- He entered his own scale weight and the station's intake weight side by side. The two agreed, so the station's *scale* was never at fault.

**The 1910 leaf**, which is the one year Hazel is sure of:
- **200,000 lb** of milk, "even," with "his scale and theirs agreeing," paid on a test of **3.42**.
- That credits him with **6,840 lb** of butterfat.
- At the true test of 3.42/0.95 = **3.60**, he was owed **7,200 lb**, so he was **360 lb** short.
- That is worth about **$90** at the long-run average price of 25¢/lb. Applying that average to 1910 is my inference.

**Cross-check over the ridge:** Four of the six farms hauled their milk over the ridge to the Cudds' cheese factory at Alder Corners during the summer flush, and to the station the rest of the year. The same milk tested **0.19 lower at the station** "than anywhere else," every season.
- Jerome Cudd's Alder Corners test book is the independent record of this. His daughter Rosalie kept it from 1941.
- In 1958 Rosalie had the figures copied for Ivy Keddie. The book went to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970.

**The family:** Emmet signed the patrons' petition of January 1925. He and Ansel had quarrelled over the June 1919 water circular, and they did not speak from then until Emmet died ten years later (Orra's diary). So Duncan's picture of Emmet at the Keddie Sunday table "for thirty-six years" holds only up to 1919.

**Where the book went:**
- It was lent to the 1925 arbitration (Kyle).
- It passed to Roy in 1929.
- Roy **lent** it to Ivy in 1958 and got it back ("lent, and I want the word").
- At Roy's death in 1962 it went to Hazel, who gave it to the Ordell County Historical Rooms at Ashlin in **1963**.

**Documents:** r17, r18, r23, r24.

### Story 4 — The Office Ledger: "Station Gain, as Usual" (1911–1925)

**Who:**
- Gideon Vose, who organized the Association, was its first president and wrote its by-laws.
- His **son** Selby Vose, manager from **1911** for about a quarter century.
- Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper.
- Bertram Kyle, clerk at the outer desk of the Larrow Green office for 25 years (memoir written 1953).

**What happened:** Every year the Ostrey Hollow station account held three figures and a sentence:
1. the butterfat credited to patrons on the station test;
2. the butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream, tested there with sound glass;
3. the difference, entered as "station gain … five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account."

Teague wrote that line every year and "never asked anybody anything in forty years." Selby Vose put his thumb on the bottom figure and signed. The account was read out every year at the annual meeting.

**Quantities:** Kyle compiled the figures for the arbitration: **40,000,000 lb** of milk over the years complained of, at **1,600,000 lb a year** (so **25 years**), with butterfat averaging **25¢/lb**.
- At the recorded 3.61 test, **1,444,000 lb** of fat was credited.
- At the true 3.80, **1,520,000 lb** reached Larrow Green.
- The "gain" was **76,000 lb**. That is **5.26 per cent of the fat credited** (Kyle's "five and a quarter"), or 5.0 per cent of the fat received. It is exactly the pipette error.
- In plain sight, the Association booked about **$19,000** of its patrons' butterfat as station gain.

**The 1919 circular:** In **June 1919** Selby Vose signed a circular, typed by Kyle, warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons against watering their milk. Ansel Keddie refused to mail it. He carried it himself to **eleven families** and stood at each door while it was read. I follow Orra's diary here, written at the time, over Hazel's childhood memory of "nine". This was the start of the quarrel between Emmet and Ansel.

**1923 and after:**
- The office ordered replacement glass from Tarnet the same week the inspector condemned the lot, in June 1923. Kyle says "twenty-two", but he admits he does not keep dates. The office treated it like "a broken window."
- The 1925 award of $3,800 was charged to the station account. Kyle's "twenty-four" is wrong.
- Kyle's moral: nothing was hidden. It "was in plain sight with a name on it."

**Documents:** r23, r24, r17, r18, r22.

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### Story 5 — The Inspector's Burette and Notebooks (1908–1958)

**Who:** Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector on the Ordell circuit, related to nobody in the valley. He was a "heavy quiet man" who kept one notebook a year, with a rubber band round it.

**What happened and when:**
- From **1908** Strawn tested Ostrey Hollow milk with a lactometer at every visit. He **never once found added water**.
- On **12 June 1923**, on an ordinary circuit visit, he ran the station's milk pipettes against a calibrated burette. They would not deliver their mark. He **condemned the whole lot** on the spot and told the manager to order new ones.
- He recorded the condemnation but drew no inference about the 26 years before it.
- The office ordered from Ammon & Sons (letter of 28 June, reply of 2 July 1923). New glass was in use from **July 1923**. Orra's diary: "new glass at the station and new pails, and the roof is still bad."
- From **1924** the station's average test was **3.85**, level with the rest of the county. The tester and the herds were the same; only the glass had changed.
- The condemned glass was crated and put in the **loft over the intake room**, where it stayed for 35 years.

**Date conflict:** Hazel Brant says the glass was condemned in "twenty-one", and Kyle says "twenty-two". Both admit they are poor with years. **1923** is fixed by:
- the dated Ammon letter book,
- Orra's entry for July 1923,
- Duncan's chronology of the crate (1923 plus 35 years = 1958),
- the county agent and the *Chronicle*.

**The notebooks:**
- The 1923 volume was one of the four books before the 1925 arbitration. Nobody set it beside the station records.
- The notebooks went to the state dairy office when Strawn retired in **1936**, then to the library of the State Agricultural College at **Farland in 1947**.
- Ivy Keddie found the 1923 volume there in **1958**. Nobody had asked for the notebooks in the meantime.
- The *Chronicle* thanks "the state dairy office" for lending them. I read that as loose press-release wording; Duncan's specific account of where they went is preferred.

**Why it matters:** Strawn's records answer two separate questions, and he had no tie to any family or faction. His lactometer entries refute the water charge. His 1923 condemnation dates exactly when the error stopped.

**Documents:** r18, r22, r23, r20, r24, r19, r17.

### Story 6 — Hay, Water and Theft: the Wrong Explanations (1908–1959)

**Who and what:** For a quarter century the valley blamed people, never the equipment (the *Chronicle* calls it "furniture"). Three explanations were current, and all three were argued before the 1925 board.

**1. Wet hay (1908):**
- In **January 1908** four men of an Association committee came up in Grigg's sleigh to inspect the Hessel Bottom herds. They looked at barns, not at the station, and ate dinner at Emmet Brant's.
- In **March 1908** they reported that the bog hay of the bottom thinned the cream. An Ashlin veterinarian said the same in **1926**. Emmet brushed it off: "the bottom fed his father."
- In 1959 county agent Orin Falke at first allowed hay as "a real if partial factor."
- He then read the college's own **Bulletin No. 214**, a 1911 feeding trial. Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage lowered the test by **no more than 0.05 in winter and by nothing measurable on summer pasture**. His words: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July." He withdrew the concession.
- The four farms that sent the same milk over the ridge in summer also tested normally there.

**2. Water (1919):** This was the charge in manager Selby Vose's June 1919 circular.
- Strawn's lactometer tests at every visit from 1908 never found water.
- Pay was on pounds of fat, so watering could not lower a patron's credit. It only dilutes the percentage while the pounds of fat stay the same (my inference).

**3. Theft by the tester (1925):** An Association officer urged this at the hearing. Cleve Oram's father, the cream hauler, privately believed "less came off that platform than went onto it." Three things refute it:
- the 1911 interval (same man, higher tests);
- the ledger, which shows *more* fat arriving at Larrow Green than was credited, not less;
- the rise to 3.85 in 1924, while Ansel was still the tester.

**Quantities that decide it:** The deficiency was 0.19 point and constant in every month. A feed effect would be seasonal and small. Water or theft would leave less fat at Larrow Green, not 5¼ per cent more.

**Documents:** r22 (including the Bulletin 214 note), r24, r17, r21, r23, r19.

### Story 7 — The Arbitration of 1925 and the Tester's Dismissal

**Who:** The Ostrey Hollow patrons (Emmet Brant among the signers), the Association's officers, a board of arbitration chaired by an Ashlin attorney, and Ansel Keddie.

**What happened and when:**
- The patrons petitioned in **January 1925**.
- The board impounded the station test book in **March 1925** and sat from **17 March to 4 May 1925**.
- Ansel went down to the first sitting in his good coat, which Orra had ironed twice.
- There were three sittings. The first was at Ashlin. **Two, in April, were held in the station's weigh room**, with a dozen men standing on the platform in the rain. Twenty feet above them, in the loft, was the crate of condemned glass.
- Kyle carried in four books: the station test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book, and Strawn's notebook for the year the glass was condemned. Nobody compared them.
- An Association officer argued that the tester had stolen cream.

**Dismissal and award:**
- Ansel was **dismissed on 30 April 1925**, four days before the finding: "Twenty-nine seasons and a letter." He came home at his usual hour, hung up his apron, and ate ham.
- On **4 May** the board found no cause established and awarded the patrons **$3,800 "without admission"**. The award was charged to the station account.
- $3,800 is the figure given by the *Chronicle*, the county agent, and Kyle, who handled the papers. It outweighs Cleve Oram's "$3,500", which he had from his father, and it is exactly **one-fifth of the $19,000** later computed.
- Hazel's "1926" and Kyle's "1924" are date slips.
- The *Chronicle* says the 1908 committee "sat again in 1925". That confuses two different bodies: the 1908 herd committee was the Association's own, while the 1925 board was an arbitration chaired by an attorney.
- Orra's diary never mentions the award, and its 1926 leaves are torn out.

**Afterwards:**
- Roy Brant never went to another Association meeting.
- Ansel rebuilt his cow shed and did carpentry around the hollow, mending gates, doors and stairs at sixty-six for a quarter of his old pay. He hung the Orams' barn door, which "swung true for thirty years."
- He died in **February 1934**. He had once told Orra that "a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."
- The board never looked at the glass, even though the inspector's notebook recording its condemnation was on the table.

**Documents:** r18, r21, r22, r23, r24, r17, r19.

### Story 8 — The Granddaughter and the Crate (1958–1981)

**Who:** Ivy Keddie (b. 1931), Duncan's daughter and Ansel's **granddaughter**, a graduate student in dairy husbandry at the State Agricultural College at Farland; Professor Leland Vaught; Rosalie Cudd; Roy and Hazel Brant.

**What happened and when:** In 1958 Ivy brought the scattered records together.
- She borrowed the Brant weigh book from Roy.
- She found Strawn's 1923 notebook in the college library.
- She obtained Rosalie Cudd's copied Alder Corners figures.
- In **February 1958** she climbed the ladder into the loft of the old station and found the crate under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff, where it had sat since 1923.
- She carried it east to Farland. On **9 March 1958** she measured the **nine** surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette.
  - The count of nine comes from Cleve Oram, who had handled the crate, and from the county agent. The *Chronicle*'s "eleven" is outweighed.
  - Nine survived out of the original **72** (not Oram's "five dozen").
  - All nine delivered short in the same proportion, averaging about **16.72 c.c. against 17.6**, which is **5 per cent** short.

**The bulletin:** **Bulletin No. 471** (October 1958, by Vaught and Keddie) concluded that the short charge alone explained the low tests: no water, no theft.
- The patrons went uncredited for **76,000 lb** of butterfat. Oram's "67,000" swaps the digits.
- That was worth **$19,000** at 25¢/lb. The *Chronicle*'s "$19,600" does not follow from any of the figures.
- The 1925 award of $3,800 had paid a fifth of it.

**Coverage and corrections:**
- The *Ashlin Chronicle* ran "Old Ordell Wrong Righted" on 3 October 1958. It got several things wrong: it called Ivy a "great-niece" and Rosalie a "niece", and printed $77.39, 16.27 c.c. and $19,600.
- In 1959 Orin Falke reported to the county board. His note corrected his own "great-niece" and dropped the hay theory.

**Where the evidence ended up:**
- The test book and the crate went to the Ordell County Historical Rooms at Ashlin in 1959.
- The weigh book followed in 1963.
- The Alder Corners book went to Vessey County in 1970.
- In Duncan's words, "the five books are now in three buildings."

**Family:**
- Ansel was cleared 24 years after his death and 33 years after his dismissal.
- Orra had died in 1948 and did not see it. Her diary was published in 1964.
- Duncan wrote his family notes in 1968.
- In 1981 Hazel, Ivy's **second cousin**, said it could have been "settled in an afternoon with a burette". Instead it took fifty years and "a girl from the college who thought to climb a ladder."

**Documents:** r19, r22, r18, r21, r17, r24.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.**
- Ansel and Orra's son was Duncan, born 1894. Duncan says so himself ("I am Duncan … their son"), and Orra's diary names Duncan as their child and gives matching ages: five in 1899, seventeen in 1911.
- Duncan's daughter is Ivy, born 1931 (r18). The *Chronicle* backs this up: it says her father taught school at Ashlin for many years, which is Duncan, and gives her age as 27 in 1958.
- Cleve Oram also calls her the granddaughter (r21).
- The "great-niece" claim appears in the *Chronicle* (r19) and in the body of Falke's report (r22). These are not two independent sources: both came from the college bulletin or press release. Falke retracted it in his note after checking ("the granddaughter and not the great-niece"), and Duncan says he had seen it "printed otherwise" twice.
- For Ivy to be a great-niece, one of her parents would have to be a nephew or niece of Ansel's, and no such person appears anywhere. Resolved by first-hand family testimony plus the source's own correction.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins.**
- Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie were brother and sister (r17, r18, r24).
- Emmet's son Roy and Orra's son Duncan were therefore first cousins.
- Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy were therefore second cousins. Hazel (r17) and Duncan (r18) each state this independently, from each side of the family.
- Oram's "first cousin once removed" (r21) is wrong: it needs a generation gap, and both women are grandchildren of the same pair of siblings.
- **Left open by the sources:**
  - Whether Emmet and Orra were **full or half siblings**. Hazel says Orra was a half-sister "by his father's second wife"; Duncan says simply "sister, the younger of the two"; Orra's diary pages from before 1889 were not preserved. If they were half siblings, Roy and Duncan were half first cousins, and Hazel and Ivy half second cousins.
  - The birth years of Emmet, Roy and Hazel.
  - The spouses in the intervening generations (Roy's wife, Duncan's wife).

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose.** Duncan (r18) and Kyle (r23) say so independently, and both reject the "nephew" story by name.
- Gideon organized the Association, was its first president and wrote its by-laws. That includes article seven, which paid by the test at the patron's own station. That rule is what turned a 5 per cent glass fault into a money loss.
- From 1911 his son ran the office under his father's rules. Selby signed the station-gain sheets on the totals alone, blamed the patrons with the 1919 water circular, and presided over an Association that kept about $19,000 as "station gain."
- At the 1925 hearing it was the founder's own system and outfit that were really at issue. The office's instinct to blame men (patrons and tester) rather than the Association's rule or equipment fits a son defending his father's institution. **That last point is my inference, not something a source states.**

**B4. No relationship between the Vose and Keddie families is established. The best evidence says there was none.**
- Duncan (r18) denies any connection outright. He explains the rumour as confusion with the Ashlin Voses, who were not related to Gideon either, and as gossip that Orra and Gideon's wife were cousins. He adds that Orra, who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection," never put a Vose in it.
- Oram's "cousins of some sort" (r21) is hearsay from his mother's mother, and he admits he "could not draw you the line."
- A first-hand family denial that explains where the rumour came from outweighs undocumented hearsay. Strictly, a negative cannot be proved, so the answer is: no known relationship; the cousin story is unsupported rumour.


# Answer Sheet — Part 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

**C1.**

(a) **The tests read 5 per cent low.** The recorded test was 0.95 × the true test.
- Working from the glass: each pipette was marked 17.6 c.c. but delivered on average 16.72 c.c. So 16.72 / 17.6 = 0.95, and each was short by 0.88 c.c., which is 5.0%.
- Why that carries through: the neck of the test bottle is ruled for a full 17.6 c.c. charge, so a charge that is 5% short gives a fat reading 5% low.
- The *Chronicle* prints "16.27". That would mean 7.56% short, a true test of 3.905 and a deficiency of 0.295, which contradicts the 0.19 given by r17 and r22.
- 16.72 is confirmed twice over: 0.19 / 3.80 = 5%, and the ledger's gain of 5¼% on the credited figure equals 0.05 / 0.95 = 5.26%. So "16.27" is transposed digits.

(b) **True average test = 3.80.** Recorded 3.61 / 0.95 = 3.80, so the recorded tests were **0.19 point** short (3.80 − 3.61). For comparison, Larrow Green averaged 3.78, and the same station averaged 3.85 after 1924.

(c) Over the 25 full delivery years: 40,000,000 lb of milk × 0.0019 = **76,000 lb of butterfat** not credited. Equivalently, 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000. Oram's "67,000 lb" transposes the digits.

(d) 76,000 lb × $0.25 = **$19,000**. The patrons received $3,800, and 3,800 / 19,000 = **1/5 (20%)**.
- The *Chronicle*'s "$19,600" cannot be derived from any of the figures in the sources.
- The clean one-fifth also supports $3,800 over Oram's $3,500, which would give 18.4%.

**C2.** This is Emmet Brant's 1910 leaf: 200,000 lb of milk at a recorded test of 3.42.

(a) Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of butterfat.

(b) True test: 3.42 / 0.95 = 3.60. Owed: 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb. So **360 lb** was owed and not credited.
- The error is proportional, so a herd testing below the station average loses slightly less than 0.19 point (here 0.18).
- Adding a flat 0.19 instead would give 380 lb, but a flat addition is not how a short charge behaves.

(c) 360 lb × $0.25 = **$90.00**.
- The 1910 butterfat price itself is not in the sources. I used the long-run average of 25¢ that Kyle gives, and that is an inference.

**C3.** Over the same 25 full delivery years:

(a) Credited to patrons: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**.

(b) Reached Larrow Green in the station cream: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lb**. Larrow Green tested the cream with sound glass.

(c) The difference is 76,000 lb.
- **As a percentage of the fat credited:** 76,000 / 1,444,000 = **5.26%**. This is the ledger's "station gain … five and a quarter per cent, as usual".
- **As a percentage of the fat actually received:** 76,000 / 1,520,000 = **5.00%**.
- The ledger's 5¼% is a percentage of the smaller, *credited* figure. The 5% pipette shortfall is a percentage of the *true* figure. They are the same error expressed on two different bases.

**C4.**

(a) The invoice total is **$77.93**: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15. The "$77.39" in Junia Ammon's prose and in the *Chronicle* is a transposition.

(b) The invoice bought **72 pipettes** (6 dozen × $0.35 = $25.20), each **0.88 c.c. short** on average (17.6 − 16.72). Oram's "five dozen" is contradicted by the invoice arithmetic.

**C5.**

(a) The faulty glass was in use from **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923**. That is **26 years, 1 month and 11 days** (about 26 years and 6 weeks), less the nine weeks in 1911 when a borrowed measure was used. Replacement glass was in use from July 1923.
- The money calculation runs over **25 full delivery years**: Kyle's 40,000,000 lb ÷ 1,600,000 lb a year = 25. I take these to be the complete account years **1898–1922**.
- The two numbers differ because the glass came in and went out in the middle of a year. In 1897 the station was still on borrowed Larrow Green glass until 1 May. In 1923 the faulty glass was used only until 12 June. Only years in which the faulty glass was used from start to finish count as full delivery years.
- The exact year boundary (calendar or account year) is my inference. The count of 25 comes from Kyle's figures.

(b) **This cannot be determined from the sources.**
- The only figure is Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" (1,100,000 lb). He had it from men who were there, and he says himself that it "is not in any book I ever ruled." The first season's sheets were kept on the creamery's own books and never filed with the station papers.
- That makes it a single hearsay source with no document behind it. It is plausible, since a first season that began on 4 May would fall below the 1.6M-a-year average, but it is not a finding.
- (The unread retellings r01–r16 may have held a documented figure; I could not use them.)

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment.**

*When and how long:* From **8 May to 10 July 1911**, exactly **nine weeks** (63 days). Sources: r22's dates, Orra's diary for 8 May 1911, and Duncan's "that summer of 1911."

*Variable:* only the measuring glass. The station's own measure was replaced by a borrowed one.

*Held constant:* the herds, pasture, season, patrons, tester (Ansel himself), supervision, scale, separator and procedure.

*Result:* the tests stood markedly higher throughout, and fell back to their old level on the day the station's own glass returned.

*What it establishes that calculation alone cannot:*
- It is a before-during-after test in actual use, with the glass as the only variable. It shows that the station's own glass *caused* the low readings at the station itself.
- It rules out the tester (same man, same honesty, higher figures) and rules out hay or water (same farms, same milk).
- The 1958 lab measurement shows the pipettes were short in a laboratory. The 1911 interval shows that the shortness actually drove the station's tests.

*What it does not establish:*
- How large the error was. "Markedly higher" is not quantified in these sources, and the borrowed measure's own accuracy is unknown.
- Which piece of the glass was at fault (pipette or another measure), or that every piece in the lot was short.
- Why the glass was short, or whether Ammon knew.
- That the error ran back to 1897. That needs the 1897 start date, the constant deficiency and the 1958 measurement.
- It was also only nine weeks in one season, so on its own it cannot show that the deficiency was constant all year.

**D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream.**

Three independent reasons:
1. **The 1911 interval** (Falke r22; Orra r24; Duncan r18). The same man made the tests under the same supervision, and his figures rose as soon as the glass changed.
2. **The ledger** (Kyle, r23). Every year *more* butterfat reached Larrow Green in the station's cream than the station test had credited to the patrons: "station gain, 5¼ per cent, as usual." A tester skimming cream would leave *less* fat arriving than credited, not 5¼% more. The "concealing" low tests would have to be matched by a surplus at the main creamery, which is the opposite of what theft produces.
3. **The 1923–24 changeover** (Falke r22; Orra r24). New glass came in from July 1923, and from 1924 the station averaged 3.85. Ansel was still the tester until April 1925, so the same man produced normal figures once the glass was changed.

A fourth, which confirms the others: the 1958 measurement of the pipettes (r19, r22) accounts for the whole deficiency.

*Why the three are independent:* each rests on a different record kept by a different person for a different purpose.
- The first is the station test book and a wife's diary, from 1911.
- The second is the Association's own ledger, written by its bookkeeper at Larrow Green.
- The third is the post-1923 station averages, following an inspector's action.
None of them depends on the others or on the 1958 laboratory work.

*Reasons that would still hold if the glass had never been measured in 1958:* the ledger (2), the 1911 interval (1) and the 1924 rise (3) all would.

*Did the bookkeeper ever question the ledger entries?* **No.** Wilbur Teague wrote the station-gain line every year of his service and "never asked anybody anything in forty years" (r23).
- These entries refute the theft accusation.
- My sources name the accuser at the hearing only as "an officer of the Association" (r22). If, as the question implies, that officer was Teague, he urged theft while his own annual entries showed surplus fat arriving, and he never questioned them.

**D3. Did the manager know, before 1923, that the tests ran low?**

**Yes, he knew the tests ran low. What he did not see, as far as the sources show, was that his own ledger explained it.**
- In June 1919 Selby Vose signed and sent the circular warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons about water (r23, r24, r22). That warning only makes sense if he knew the station's tests were low.
- Complaints had been made "at every annual meeting for a quarter of a century" (r19), with the first in 1902 (r22). Vose was manager from 1911, and the station account was read out at the annual meeting.
- On the other hand, Kyle says Vose "read the totals and he never read the lines above them." He put his thumb on the bottom figure and signed. So there is no evidence that he understood the station-gain line, which shows the fat arriving in full.
- He knew about the symptom and blamed the patrons. He did not connect it to the ledger, or, before Strawn's visit, to the glass.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. The causal theory.**

The Ostrey Hollow station's own pipettes, a lot of 72 marked 17.6 c.c. and bought from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet in 1897, each delivered only about 16.72 c.c., which is 5 per cent less.

- **Why an error at all:** Every Babcock-type test put a short charge of milk into a bottle whose neck was graduated for the full 17.6 c.c., so every reading came out 5 per cent low.
- **Why that size:** Five per cent of the true average of 3.80 is exactly **0.19 point**, which gives the recorded 3.61. The same 5% on the credited figure is the ledger's "station gain, 5¼ per cent" of fat arriving at Larrow Green, where it was tested with sound glass. Over the 25 full years that comes to **76,000 lb, or $19,000**. The error was proportional and built into the instrument, so it was the same "flat as a floor" in every month, wet years and dry. A feed effect would have been seasonal.
- **Why it began when it did:** It started on **1 May 1897**, when this lot replaced the glass borrowed from Larrow Green for the first season.
- **Why it ended when it did:** It ended when Strawn condemned the lot on **12 June 1923** and new glass came in during July. The station then averaged 3.85 from 1924.
- **Why nothing on the farms or at the station had to change:** The scales were true (Emmet's scale agreed with the station's), and the cows, hay, tester and procedure were unchanged. The fat was never lost. It went down the valley in the cream, and the ledger recorded it. The 1911 interval, when a borrowed measure raised the tests for nine weeks, is the natural experiment that confirms the theory.

**The three competing explanations:**

1. **Watered milk.** Advanced by the manager, Selby Vose, in his June 1919 circular.
   - Defeated by Strawn's lactometer tests at every visit from 1908, which never found water.
   - Also, pay was per pound of fat, and water does not reduce the pounds of fat (my inference).
   - The ledger shows full fat arriving at Larrow Green.

2. **Theft by the tester.** Urged at the 1925 hearing by an officer of the Association; believed privately by the cream hauler, Oram's father.
   - Defeated by the 1911 nine weeks (same man, higher tests).
   - By the ledger surplus: more fat arrived than was credited, the opposite of theft.
   - By the rise to 3.85 in 1924 under the same tester.

3. **Wet bog hay.** Advanced by the Association's 1908 herd committee and an Ashlin veterinarian in 1926, and briefly conceded by Falke.
   - Defeated by Bulletin No. 214, the college's 1911 feeding trial: at most 0.05 in winter and nothing measurable in summer, whereas the deficiency was 0.19 in July too.
   - By the same cows and hay testing normally over the ridge at Alder Corners in summer.
   - By the jump in 1911 and again in 1924 with no change of feed.

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glass? No.**
- The glass sat crated in the loft over the weigh room from 1923 until Ivy found it in February 1958. Duncan says it had been there 35 years undisturbed, and Falke says it had lain there "since the day it was condemned."
- Two of the board's three sittings were held in the room directly beneath it (r18, r21, r24).
- The board had Strawn's 1923 notebook recording the condemnation (r23) but never connected it to the tests. It "found no cause established."

**What did the tester himself believe? That the glass was at fault.**
- After the 1911 interval he told his family "it was something in the glass" (r18).
- He never dared write it down, since he could not prove it, and entered only a question mark (r22).
- He kept on testing faithfully with the only outfit he had, and those figures were later used against him.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** (148 words)

For twenty-six years (1897–1923) the Ostrey Hollow station tested its patrons' milk with 72 pipettes from Ammon & Sons that delivered 5 per cent less milk than their 17.6 c.c. mark. Every test read 5 per cent low (3.61 against a true 3.80). Over 25 full years the patrons went uncredited for 76,000 lb of butterfat, worth $19,000, which the Association's ledger booked as "station gain, as usual." Neighbours blamed the tester, the manager blamed water, a committee blamed hay. In 1925 an arbitration paid $3,800, a fifth of the loss, and dismissed tester Ansel Keddie, who died under suspicion. In 1958 his granddaughter Ivy found the condemned glass in the station loft and measured it. The proof lay in eight separate places: an invoice, a test book, a weigh book, a ledger, an inspector's notebook, a cheese factory's records, a diary and a crate — never read together.
