# Answer sheet — Part 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

**C1.**

**(a)** The tests read **5 per cent low**, so every recorded test was 0.95 of the true value. The reason is the glassware. The bottles' necks are ruled for a 17.6 c.c. charge of milk. A pipette that delivers only a fraction *f* of 17.6 c.c. puts fraction *f* of the fat into the bottle, so the reading comes out at fraction *f* of the truth. The pipettes delivered about 16.72 c.c.:

> 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95

The delivered volume is inferred rather than read from a source. It is fixed by three figures that agree:
- 0.19 ÷ 3.80 = 0.05.
- The ledger's station gain: 1 ÷ 0.95 − 1 = 5.26 per cent, which the ledger writes as "five and a quarter per cent".
- $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = exactly 1/5.

The Chronicle's 16.27 c.c. would give 7.6 per cent, which matches none of these. It is a transposition of 16.72.

**(b)** True average = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80 per cent**. The recorded tests were **0.19 of a point** short. As a check, Larrow Green averaged 3.78, and the station averaged 3.85 once new glass was in use from 1924.

**(c)** 40,000,000 lb of milk over the 25 full delivery years:

> 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb of fat actually delivered
> 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb credited
> Shortfall = **76,000 lb of butterfat**

**(d)** 76,000 lb × $0.25 = **$19,000**. The patrons received $3,800:

> 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **1/5 (20 per cent)**

The Chronicle's $19,600 and Oram's 67,000 lb cannot be reconciled with the component figures. The first is an error, and the second is a transposition of 76,000.

**C2.** Emmet Brant's 1910 year: 200,000 lb of milk, recorded test 3.42.

**(a)** Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of butterfat.

**(b)** True test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60, so 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb. He was owed and not credited **360 lb**. The error is proportional, so on his lower test it comes to 0.18 of a point, not the average 0.19. A flat 0.19 would give 380 lb.

**(c)** 360 × $0.25 = **$90**. This uses Kyle's long-run average price, because no price specific to 1910 is given. On the flat-0.19 reading it would be $95.

**C3.** Over the 25 full delivery years (1898–1922):

**(a)** Credited to the patrons: **1,444,000 lb**.

**(b)** Reached Larrow Green in the station's cream: **1,520,000 lb**.

**(c)** The difference is 76,000 lb:
- **5.26 per cent of the fat credited** (76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 0.05263). This is the ledger's "station gain, five and a quarter per cent", computed on the credited figure.
- **Exactly 5.00 per cent of the fat received**, the true fat (76,000 ÷ 1,520,000).

The two percentages differ only because they are taken of different bases.

**C4.**

**(a)** **$77.93**: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15, the total on the facsimile invoice. The "$77.39" in Junia's prose and in the Chronicle is a transposition.

**(b)** **72 pipettes** (6 dozen at 35¢ = $25.20). Each was short by about **0.88 c.c.** (17.6 − 16.72, on the average delivery). The 0.88 is inferred from the 5 per cent proportion, as in C1(a). The Chronicle's 16.27 would have meant 1.33 c.c., and I reject it.

**C5.**

**(a)** **How long the glass was in use:** from **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923**, about **26 years and 6 weeks**. That includes the nine weeks of 8 May–10 July 1911, when borrowed glass replaced it, so actual use was about 26 years less nine weeks.

**The money calculation runs over 25 full delivery years**, 1898–1922. Kyle's figures: 40,000,000 ÷ 1,600,000 = 25.

The numbers differ because the two ends are partial seasons. 1897 began on 1 May, and 1923 ended on 12 June. The milk and fat totals were compiled by whole years, so those two part-years fall outside the figures used.

**(b)** **Cannot be determined from the sources.** The 1896 season, with borrowed glass, was carried on the creamery's own books, and the sheets were never put with the station papers. The only figure is Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds", which he says he "understood at the time from men who were there" and which is "not in any book I ever ruled". That is one unverified hearsay figure. It is also the one season the faulty glass did not touch, so nothing else in the record checks it.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran from **8 May to 10 July 1911**, exactly **nine weeks (63 days)**. It is dated by Orra's diary and by Falke.

- **Variable:** the measuring glass. A borrowed measure replaced the station's own.
- **Held constant:** the herds, the pasture and season, the men (the same tester), the machinery, and the supervision.
- **Result:** the tests stood markedly higher for the whole nine weeks and fell back on the day the station's own glass returned. It was a clean on/off/on reversal.

**What it establishes that a calculation cannot:** causation. The arithmetic (0.19 ÷ 3.80, the 5¼ per cent gain) shows that a 5 per cent error in the glass *would fit* the figures. The 1911 interval shows that changing the glass *changed the figures* while everything else stayed the same. That rules out the tester's honesty, the hay and water as causes all at once, since none of them changed.

**What it does not establish:**
- The size of the error. The sources give only "markedly".
- Which piece of the outfit was at fault, or that the borrowed measure was itself accurate. It shows only a difference between the two.
- That the error was constant through every year, 1897–1923, or in winter.
- Anything about how the glass came to be short, or whether Ammon knew.

Nor did it prove the cause to anyone at the time. Ansel put a question mark in the book and nothing more.

**D2.** Four reasons, from different narrators, why the tester cannot have been stealing cream:

1. **The ledger's "station gain"** (Kyle, the office clerk). Every year *more* fat reached Larrow Green in the station's cream than the patrons were credited with, about 5¼ per cent more. A skimming tester who wrote low tests to cover the theft would leave *less* fat arriving than the tests showed, or at most the same.
2. **The nine weeks of 1911** (Falke, confirmed by Orra's diary and Duncan). The same man, under the same supervision, recorded higher tests as soon as the glass changed.
3. **The tests after 1923** (Falke ¶11). Ansel stayed tester until April 1925. Once the new glass was in, from 1924, the station averaged 3.85, normal and above Larrow Green, with no change of man or method.
4. **The Alder Corners comparison** (Hazel, with the Cudd test book). The same milk from four of the six farms tested about 0.19 higher at the cheese factory in the summer flush. The low reading belonged to the station's measurement, not to the milk or the cream that left the station.

**Why they are independent:** they rest on different records kept by different people for different purposes. The Association's general ledger was kept by Teague and copied by Kyle. The station test book is Ansel's. The post-1923 averages came to Falke. The Cudd company kept its own test book in another county. None of them derives from another, and none depends on the 1958 bulletin.

**Holding even without the 1958 measurement:** the ledger station gain (1), the 1911 interval (2) and the post-1923 averages (3) all stand without it.

**Did the bookkeeper question the ledger?** No. Wilbur Teague wrote "five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account" every year of his service and "never asked anybody anything in forty years" (Kyle). He never questioned the very entries that refute the skimming charge. The Association's side urged that charge at the hearing (Falke says "an officer of the Association").

**D3.** **Yes, he knew the tests ran low. He did not know why, and nothing shows he understood what the station-gain line meant.**

- He became manager in 1911. The Ostrey Hollow patrons had complained that their tests ran low since 1902, at every annual meeting, where the station account itself was read out.
- In June 1919 he signed a circular warning those patrons about water in their milk. Kyle typed it, and it went out over Vose's name alone. That circular only makes sense if he knew the station's tests were low: he had settled on water as the reason.
- What he did not take in was the line above the total. Kyle watched him "put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign" a thousand times. The station gain showed that the fat was reaching the creamery, which contradicts his water theory as much as the theft theory.

So he knew of the low tests before 1923, but he blamed the patrons. The sources give no sign that he connected the gain line, or Strawn's condemnation, to the complaints.

## Section E — Contradictions

**E1.** Direct factual contradictions, each with its resolution and method:

1. **When the station opened.** Hazel: Ansel went up in '95. Falke: opened 4 May 1896; Duncan: test book from 1896. **Resolution: 1896.** Method: a dated official report plus a book record outrank a memory the narrator herself says is weak on years; '95 is the building winter.
2. **When the station's own outfit went into use.** 1 May 1897: Junia (from the invoice of 14 April 1897), Orra's diary for that day, and the Chronicle ("spring of 1897"). 1 May 1898 and "first season or two": Kyle. 1899: Hazel. **Resolution: 1 May 1897.** Method: a dated document plus a contemporaneous diary.
3. **Invoice total.** $77.93 on the facsimile total and by the line items; $77.39 in Junia's prose and the Chronicle. **Resolution: $77.93.** Method: arithmetic and the quoted document.
4. **Number of pipettes bought.** 6 dozen (invoice, Chronicle) against 5 dozen (Oram). **Resolution: 72.** Method: the document, and $25.20 ÷ $0.35 = 72.
5. **Number of pipettes surviving in 1958.** Nine (Falke, Oram) against eleven (Chronicle). **Resolution: nine.** Method: two independent witnesses (an official report and a man who handled the crate) against a hurried newspaper.
6. **What the pipettes delivered.** The Chronicle's 16.27 c.c. against the 5 per cent proportion implied by Falke's 0.19 on 3.80, Kyle's 5¼ per cent gain, and the one-fifth award. **Resolution: about 16.72 c.c.** This is inferred, by arithmetic consistency; the Chronicle transposed two digits.
7. **Year of condemnation.** 1923: Falke (12 June 1923), Junia (summer 1923, letter of 28 June), Duncan (crate to the loft in 1923), Orra (July 1923, new glass), Chronicle. 1921: Hazel. 1922: Kyle. **Resolution: 1923.** Method: dated documents and a contemporaneous diary.
8. **Amount of the award.** $3,800 (Falke, Kyle, Chronicle) against $3,500 (Oram, from his father). **Resolution: $3,800.** Method: independent majority, including the clerk who charged it to the account; it also makes exactly one-fifth of $19,000.
9. **Date of the award or hearing.** 1925 (Falke: 17 March–4 May 1925; Duncan; Orra; Oram) against 1924 (Kyle) and '26 (Hazel). **Resolution: 1925.** Method: dated diary entries and the report.
10. **The money and fat owed.** $19,000 and 76,000 lb by arithmetic (40,000,000 lb × 0.19 per cent; × $0.25), against the Chronicle's $19,600 and Oram's 67,000 lb. **Resolution: 76,000 lb and $19,000.** Method: arithmetic from Kyle's and Falke's component figures. The caveat is that the arithmetic depends on those components.
11. **Ivy's relationship to Ansel.** Granddaughter (Duncan, Hazel, Oram, Falke's correction) against great-niece (Chronicle; Falke page 2). **Resolution: granddaughter.**
12. **Ivy's relationship to Hazel.** Second cousins (Hazel, Duncan) against first cousin once removed (Oram). **Resolution: second cousins.** Method: working through the generations.
13. **Whether Orra was Emmet's full or half sister.** "Sister, the younger" (Duncan) against "half sister, by his father's second wife" (Hazel). **Cannot be settled**: no document survives, and Orra's writings before 1889 are lost.
14. **Rosalie Cudd's relationship to Jerome.** Daughter (Duncan) against niece (Chronicle). **Resolution: daughter.** Method: the careful family chronicler over a hurried newspaper; Duncan also records her taking over the factory "after her father".
15. **Whether the Voses and Keddies were related.** Cousins "of some sort" (Oram, secondhand) against no relation (Duncan). **Resolution: no established relation** (see B4).
16. **Whether Selby Vose was Gideon's son or nephew.** Son (Kyle, Duncan) against the nephew story reported at Ashlin. **Resolution: son**, on two independent insiders.
17. **When Emmet began the weigh book.** 1 January 1897 (Duncan) against 1 January 1900 (Hazel). **Leaning 1897, but not firmly settled.** Method: narrator reliability (Hazel disclaims years and misdates four other events); no independent document is quoted.
18. **Orra's birth year.** 1866 (diary preface; her own "sixty-four" in 1930) against 1868 (Duncan). **Resolution: 1866.** Method: her own contemporaneous statement and arithmetic.
19. **Emmet's visits to the Keddie house.** Duncan: Emmet sat at their Sunday table for 36 years, up to 1925. Orra's diary: he never entered the house after June 1919, and was estranged ten years until his death in 1929. **Resolution: the diary.** Method: a contemporaneous record outranks a memory 49 years later.
20. **How many families received the water circular.** Nine (Hazel) against eleven (Orra's diary, the night Ansel delivered them). **Resolution: eleven, with moderate confidence.** Method: a same-day diary over the memory of a small child. Both agree that six farms were involved.
21. **Who sat on the 1925 matter.** Chronicle: "the committee that sat in 1908 sat again in 1925". Falke: the 1908 body was an Association committee on the herds; 1925 was a board of arbitration under an Ashlin attorney. **Resolution: two different bodies.** Method: the official report's specific dates and chairmanship.
22. **Whether Ammon knowingly sold a short lot.** Knowingly (Chronicle) against an accidental reground mold (Junia). **Cannot be settled.** The works books burned in 1931. The Chronicle's only evidence is the 1923 silence, and Junia's account is belief, not record.
23. **Whether Ansel knew about the glass.** Ivy, in the Chronicle: "he never knew". Duncan: in 1911 his father said "it was something in the glass". **Resolution:** he suspected but could not prove it. Both hold in that sense.
24. **Cream "lost" between platform and wagon.** Oram's father believed less cream came off the platform than went onto it. The ledger shows *more* fat reached Larrow Green than was credited. **Resolution: the ledger**, a written record against one man's belief.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. Causal theory.** In 1897 Ammon & Sons of Tarnet supplied the Ostrey Hollow station with 72 test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. They came from one lot, probably blown in a reground mold, and each delivered only about 95 per cent of its mark (about 16.72 c.c.). Every test sample was therefore 5 per cent short, in a bottle whose neck was ruled for a full charge, so every reading came out at 95 per cent of the truth.

- **The size of the error:** 3.80 true read as 3.61, a shortfall of 0.19. Over the 25 full delivery years 1898–1922 that was 76,000 lb of fat, $19,000, of which the patrons recovered one-fifth.
- **Why that size:** it is the ratio of delivered to marked volume, 16.72 ÷ 17.6. Because the error is a proportion of the reading, it stayed "flat as a floor" in every month and on wet and dry years alike. A feed effect would have varied with the season.
- **Why it began when it did:** on 1 May 1897, when the station stopped borrowing Larrow Green's glass and put its own outfit into use.
- **Why it ended when it did:** Strawn condemned the lot on 12 June 1923. New glass was in use from July 1923, and from 1924 the station tested 3.85.
- **Why nothing else had to change:** the error lived entirely in the glass. The scales were true (Emmet's weigh book agreed), and the acid, the cows, the pasture and the tester were unchanged. The same fat went down to Larrow Green all along, which is why the ledger showed a steady "station gain". The 1911 swap of glass confirms the whole theory: figures up with borrowed glass, down with the station's own.

**The three competing explanations:**
1. **The patrons watered their milk.** Advanced by the manager, Selby Vose, in his 1919 circular. **Defeated by:** Strawn's lactometer tests at every visit from 1908 onward, which never found water; the 1911 interval (same milk, higher tests); and Alder Corners (same milk, about 0.19 higher).
2. **The tester skimmed cream and wrote low tests to hide it.** Urged at the 1925 hearing by an officer of the Association; Oram's father believed it too. **Defeated by:** the ledger's station gain (more fat arrived, not less); the 1911 interval; and Ansel's own normal tests of 3.85 after 1923.
3. **Wet bog hay on Hessel Bottom thinned the cream.** Advanced by the Association committee of 1908 and an Ashlin veterinarian in 1926, and briefly conceded by Falke. **Defeated by:** Bulletin No. 214, the college's 1911 feeding trial: at most 0.05 in winter and nothing on summer pasture, so it cannot produce 0.19 in July. Also by the 1911 interval (same pasture, higher tests) and by the constancy of the gap across seasons.

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glass?** **No.** The crate sat in the loft over the weigh room, where two of the three sittings were held, and nobody went up. Strawn's 1923 notebook recording the condemnation was among the four books before the board, but Kyle says no one laid it beside the station or receiving books. No narrator records any examination of the glass before Ivy's in 1958.

**What did the tester believe?** **That it was something in the glass.** He said so at home in the summer of 1911, twice (Duncan), and marked the 1911 jump with a question mark in the test book. He never wrote the suspicion down or pressed it, because he could not prove it. So he suspected the cause but never knew it, which is the sense of Ivy's "he never knew".

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** From 1897 to 1923 the Ostrey Hollow skimming station tested its patrons' milk with a lot of pipettes from Ammon & Sons that delivered 5 per cent short of their mark. Because the Association paid by butterfat test, every reading ran 0.19 low: 3.61 instead of 3.80. Over 25 years that cost the Hessel Bottom farmers 76,000 lb of fat, or $19,000. The patrons blamed the tester, Ansel Keddie; the manager blamed water; a committee blamed the hay. In 1925 a board sat beneath the crate of condemned glass, dismissed Keddie and paid out one-fifth. The truth was spread across separate records: the supplier's invoice, the tester's 1911 question mark, a farmer's weigh book, the clerk's yearly "station gain" line, an inspector's notebook, a cheese factory's tests, and a wife's diary. No one put them together until his granddaughter Ivy climbed to the loft in 1958 and measured the glass.
