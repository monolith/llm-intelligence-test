# Answer sheet — Part 1 (Section A, stories 1–4)

*Source note: the notes on r01–r16 from earlier sessions were not available to me in this session. Every answer below rests on r17–r24, cited by narrator: Hazel Brant (r17), Duncan Keddie (r18), Ashlin Chronicle (r19), Junia Ammon (r20), Cleve Oram (r21), Orin Falke (r22), Bertram Kyle (r23), Orra Keddie's diary (r24).*

## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1 — Article Seven and the station at Ostrey Hollow (1889–1897)

**Who:** the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association; Gideon Vose, who organized it; and Ansel Keddie, its tester at Ostrey Hollow.

**What happened, and when.** Valley farmers organized the Association in 1889 at a meeting at Larrow Green, and it built its main creamery there in 1891. Gideon Vose organized it, was its first president and wrote its by-laws. Article seven set how every patron was paid: by the pounds of butterfat his milk was found by test to contain, not by the weight of the milk. It also made the test at the station where he delivered the one that governed his pay. That article is the hinge of everything that followed. Under payment by weight, nothing later would have cost any man a dollar. The year the article was adopted cannot be determined: the printed by-laws carry no date and the early minute book was lost (Falke).

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley. The road climbs the whole seven miles. The station served Hessel Bottom, six farms on wet ground. It **opened on 4 May 1896**. Falke gives that date, and Duncan says the test book begins in 1896. Hazel's "ninety-five" is the winter it was built.

The building was two floors of board beside the creek. It had a waist-high covered platform on the road side, and an intake room with the scale, the vat and the separator. Above the intake room was a loft, reached by a ladder nailed to the wall. Milk was weighed, sampled, tested for fat and separated. Cleve Oram's father hauled the cream down to Larrow Green, and the skim went back to the farmers for their hogs.

**Ansel Keddie** (born 1861) was tester and separator man from the day the station opened. He served **29 seasons (1896–1924)** and kept the station test book in his own hand.

**The first season.** In 1896 the station borrowed its testing glass from the Larrow Green creamery. Its own outfit went into use on **1 May 1897**: Junia Ammon's history says so, and so does Orra's diary for that very day ("done borrowing of Larrow Green"). I reject Kyle's "1 May 1898" and Hazel's "ninety-nine", because the invoice and a same-day diary entry outrank later memories. The milk for the 1896 season was carried on the creamery's own books and never filed with the station papers. Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" is hearsay by his own account ("not in any book I ever ruled"), so the first season's receipts cannot be determined.

**Documents:** the printed by-laws; Falke ¶3–4; Chronicle; Oram (the building and the road); Kyle; Orra's diary; Duncan.

### Story 2 — The Tarnet glass: Ammon & Sons and one short lot of pipettes (1897; 1923; 1949)

**Who:** Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet, a glass house founded by Marcus Ammon that supplied half the creameries in the state; and Junia Ammon, the firm's historian.

**The invoice, dated 14 April 1897**, sold the Larrow Valley Association, for the Ostrey Hollow station:

- 6 dozen (72) milk test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c., at $0.35 each: $25.20
- 12 dozen test bottles with an 8 per cent neck, at $0.22: $31.68
- 1 acid measure: $0.90
- 1 four-bottle hand tester: $18.00
- Freight: $2.15
- **Total: $77.93**

The line items add to $77.93 (25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15), and that is the total printed on the facsimile. Junia's own prose, and the Chronicle after it, say "$77.39", which is a transposition. Cleve Oram's "five dozen" is also wrong: $25.20 at 35¢ is 72 pipettes.

**The fault.** A test pipette has to *deliver* its mark, not merely hold it, because milk clings to the glass. The outfit went into use on 1 May 1897. The pipettes delivered about 95 per cent of their mark, **about 16.72 c.c. instead of 17.6, so each was short by about 0.88 c.c.** This figure is my inference. It is the only delivery consistent with a 5 per cent low test (0.19 short on a true 3.80), with the ledger's "station gain" of 5¼ per cent, and with an award of exactly one-fifth of the loss. The Chronicle's 16.27 c.c. is a digit transposition of 16.72.

Junia's explanation, which she offers as belief rather than record: the mold had been reground, and the lot came out consistently short. A check of the first, tenth and fiftieth pieces could not catch that, because they were consistent — consistently wrong.

**1923.** The Association wrote on 28 June 1923 for replacement glass (6 dozen pipettes and 6 dozen bottles). Ammon replied on 2 July: order entered, "we regret the trouble", and "our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot". The firm's works books later burned in the 1931 fire. In 1949 Junia printed the invoice and the letter side by side and said she regretted the letter more than the accident.

**Unsupported claims.** The Chronicle's charge that a short lot was "put off knowingly" rests only on that silence. Whether Ammon knew **cannot be determined**, and the only evidence on the firm's side (Junia) says it was an accident. Junia also contradicts herself by saying the house "sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900" while reproducing her own 1897 sale into it.

**Documents:** the invoice and the 1923 letter (both in facsimile, r20); Chronicle; Oram.

### Story 3 — Two families on one bottom: Keddies, Brants, and the Brant weigh book

**Who.** Ansel Keddie (1861–1934) married **Orra Brant** in 1889. Orra was born in 1866: the diary preface says so, and her own entry of August 1930 says "I am sixty-four". Duncan's "1868" contradicts his own statement that she was 82 when she died in 1948. Orra was the sister of **Emmet Brant**, who had the largest farm on Hessel Bottom and hauled the most milk to the station. Duncan calls her "sister, the younger"; Hazel says "half sister, by his father's second wife". Which is right is left open.

Their son **Duncan** (born 1894) went to teach at Ashlin in October 1911. His daughter **Ivy** was born in 1931. Emmet's son was **Roy**, and Roy's daughter is **Hazel**. The man accused of shorting the patrons was therefore the brother-in-law of the patron who lost the most by it.

**The weigh book.** Emmet weighed his milk on his own scale before it left the farm, wrote the station's weight beside his own, and chased any disagreement. Duncan says he began the book on 1 January 1897; Hazel says 1 January 1900. I prefer Duncan's date, with low confidence: he writes carefully from records, and Hazel admits she is unreliable on years and gets several others wrong.

**The 1910 leaf.** Hazel knows it by heart: **200,000 lb even** delivered, "his scale and theirs agreeing", paid on a test of **3.42**. That credited him with 6,840 lb of fat. The true test was 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60, which is 7,200 lb, so he was **360 lb short**. At the long-run average price of 25¢ a pound, that is about **$90** for the year.

**The rift.** In June 1919 Ansel carried the manager's circular warning about water in the milk to the patrons himself. Two days later Emmet stood in the yard and asked why Ansel had carried it if he didn't believe it. Ansel answered that he would not have another man carry it. Emmet never entered the house again. His name was on the patrons' petition of January 1925. He died in spring 1929 without reconciling. Duncan's claim that Emmet sat at their table for 36 years yields to the diary written at the time.

**What became of the book.** Emmet lent it to the 1925 arbitration. It passed to Roy in 1929. Roy **lent** it to Ivy in 1958 (Hazel and Duncan both insist on "lent", not "left"). Roy died in 1962, and Hazel gave the book to the Historical Rooms in 1963.

**Documents:** the weigh book; Orra's diary; Duncan's notes; Hazel's tape.

### Story 4 — The long complaint and the wrong explanations (1902–1926)

**What happened.** Almost from the start, Ostrey Hollow milk tested low. The station averaged **3.61** over the years of complaint, against **3.78** at the Larrow Green creamery, a normal figure for the county. The deficiency was **0.19 of a point**, and it was the same in the June flush and in February. Hazel calls it "flat as a floor".

The first complaint on record was in **1902**. Orra's diary for March 1902 has store talk that "our milk up here runs poorer", and Ansel's reply: "it is my figures they will look at". The Ostrey Hollow patrons raised it at every annual meeting for about 25 years. The officers suspected the patrons.

**Explanation 1: water.** The manager, Selby Vose, sent a circular in **June 1919**. Kyle typed it and Vose signed it. Ansel delivered it to eleven families (diary; Hazel says nine), and Hazel's mother burned hers. The explanation fails because Merle Strawn, the state inspector, used a lactometer on every visit from 1908 onward and never found added water.

**Explanation 2: the tester skimming cream.** An officer of the Association urged this at the hearing. It fails on the nine weeks of 1911 (Story 5), on the ledger's "station gain" (Story 6), and on the normal tests the same man recorded after 1923 (Story 7).

**Explanation 3: bog hay.** In January 1908 four men of an Association committee came up in Grigg's sleigh. They visited Emmet's barns first, looked at barns rather than the station, and in March 1908 reported that the wet hay of the bottom thinned the cream. A veterinarian at Ashlin repeated the view in 1926. Falke granted it as a "partial factor" until he read the college's **Bulletin No. 214**, a feeding trial of 1911: bog hay lowered the test by "not more than five hundredths" in winter and by nothing measurable on summer pasture. Five hundredths in winter cannot account for nineteen in July, and Falke withdrew the concession.

**A control group.** Four of the six Hessel Bottom farms hauled over the ridge in the summer flush to the Alder Corners cheese factory in Vessey County, run by Jerome Cudd and after 1941 by his daughter Rosalie. There, the same milk tested about 0.19 higher. The Chronicle calls Rosalie his "niece", but Duncan's careful genealogy prevails. Rosalie copied the figures for Ivy in 1958.

**Documents:** Falke ¶5, ¶7–9 and his added note; Bulletin 214; Orra's diary; Hazel; Kyle; the Cudd test book; Chronicle.


# Answer sheet — Part 2 (Section A, stories 5–8; Section B)

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### Story 5 — The broken measure: nine weeks in 1911

**Who:** Ansel Keddie; his wife Orra; his son Duncan, then 17.

**What happened, and when.** On **8 May 1911** the station's own measuring glass broke. Orra's diary that day: "A. home cross about a broken measure. Rain all day." A measure borrowed from elsewhere was used in its place **until 10 July 1911**, which is 63 days, exactly nine weeks (Falke ¶6). For all nine weeks the station's tests stood markedly higher. On the day the station went back to its own glass they fell to their old level.

**What was held constant.** Everything else stayed the same: the herds, the pasture, the season, the men, the machinery, and the supervision. The same tester made every test.

**What Ansel did.** He wrote a **question mark** in the test book against the change. That summer he came home and said "it was something in the glass", twice: once at supper and once by the door, which was not his way (Duncan). He never put the suspicion in the book, because he would not write down what he could not prove. He had nothing to hold in his hand but a suspicion, set against an outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county. So he went on for fourteen more years, writing down every figure the glass gave him, and those figures later convicted him. Duncan calls this "the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had".

**What else happened in 1911.** The college ran its bog-hay feeding trial (Bulletin 214), which later showed the hay could not explain summer figures. Selby Vose became manager of the Association (Kyle). In October Duncan left to teach at Ashlin.

**Why it matters.** The episode was a natural experiment, and it knocks out all three theories at once:
- **Theft:** the same man made the tests and the figures rose.
- **Hay:** the same summer pasture gave higher figures.
- **Water:** the same milk gave higher figures.

It is also the only direct evidence before 1958 that the cause was the glass. Falke says the 1958 bulletin treats it as decisive.

**What the sources do not give.** They give no exact test figures for the nine weeks ("markedly higher"). They do not say which piece of glass broke or where the borrowed measure came from. They do not show that the borrowed glass was itself accurate. So the interval shows that the station's own glass was the cause, but it does not size the error. The size comes from the 1958 measurement and the ledger.

**Documents:** Orra's diary (8 May 1911); Falke ¶6 and ¶8; Duncan §IV; the station test book (the question mark).

### Story 6 — The line in plain sight: the office at Larrow Green

**Who:** Bertram Kyle, who sat at the outer desk outside the manager's door for 25 years; Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper; and Selby Vose, manager from 1911 for about a quarter of a century. Vose was Gideon's **son**, not his nephew (Kyle and Duncan independently).

**The yearly ledger line.** Every year the Ostrey Hollow station account carried three figures and a sentence:
- the butterfat credited to the patrons on the station test;
- the butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream;
- the difference, entered as **"station gain"**, followed by the words **"five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account."**

Teague wrote the line every year and "never asked anybody anything in forty years". Kyle copied it into fair hand for the annual meeting, where it was read out to farmers with their hats on their knees. Vose was courteous to every caller, but he read only totals: he put his thumb on the bottom figure and signed. Because the figure had a name, everyone treated it as explained.

**What the line meant.** More fat reached the creamery than the patrons were paid for, every year. A tester stealing cream would produce the opposite. The line itself refuted the later charge, and nobody read it that way.

**Kyle's figures, compiled for the arbitration.** **40,000,000 lb** of milk received at the station over the years of complaint. At **1,600,000 lb a year**, that is **25 full years**. Butterfat averaged **25¢ a pound**. Working from those:
- Fat credited at 3.61 per cent: **1,444,000 lb**.
- Fat actually present at 3.80 per cent: **1,520,000 lb**.
- Station gain: **76,000 lb**. That is 5.26 per cent *of the fat credited* (the ledger's "five and a quarter") and exactly 5 per cent *of the fat received*.

**The circular.** In 1919 Kyle typed the water circular and Vose signed it; it went out over his name alone. When the award came it was charged to the station account, which in effect paid it out of the station gain.

**Kyle's own errors.** He dates the outfit to 1898, Strawn's visit to 1922, and the award to 1924. I correct these from contemporary documents (Stories 1 and 7).

**Documents:** the station accounts and general ledger; the 1919 circular; Kyle (r23).

### Story 7 — The glass condemned (1923) and the tester condemned (1925)

**The condemnation.** On **12 June 1923**, on an ordinary circuit visit, the state dairy inspector **Merle Strawn** tested the station's pipettes against a calibrated burette. They would not deliver their mark. He condemned the whole lot and told the manager to order new. Strawn was related to no one in the valley. He wrote the condemnation in his notebook and drew no inference about the 26 years before.

The office ordered new glass that week (Ammon letter of 28 June), and it was in use by July 1923. Orra's diary for July 1923: "new glass at the station and new pails". The condemned glass went into a crate under a tarpaulin in the loft over the intake room. **From 1924 the station's tests averaged 3.85**, and this was under Ansel, the same tester.

Hazel's "twenty-one" and Kyle's "twenty-two" are wrong. The inspector's date, the Ammon letter and the diary all put it in 1923.

**The arbitration.** The patrons petitioned in **January 1925**, and Emmet Brant's name was on the petition. The board impounded the test book in March. A board of arbitration, chaired by an Ashlin attorney, sat from **17 March to 4 May 1925**. Of its three sittings, one was at Ashlin and **two were in the station's weigh room**. The crate sat about twenty feet above them, and men stood outside in the rain.

Four books went before the board (Kyle): the station test book, the receiving book, Emmet's weigh book, and Strawn's notebook for 1923. Nobody laid the receiving book beside Strawn's notebook.

**The outcome.**
- **30 April 1925:** Ansel was dismissed, four days before the award. "Twenty-nine seasons and a letter." He came home, hung up his apron, and ate ham.
- **4 May 1925:** the board found no cause established and awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons **$3,800, "without admission."** Cleve Oram's $3,500 (his father's figure) yields to Falke, Kyle and the Chronicle. Kyle's year of 1924 and Hazel's '26 yield to Falke's dates.

**Afterward.** Ansel rebuilt his cow shed, whistling, and worked as a carpenter at a quarter of his old pay. Men later praised his doors; none spoke of "the other thing". He **died in February 1934**, never cleared. His words to Orra: "a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name." Orra died in 1948. The station closed in 1946 and was used to store hay tools.

**Documents:** Strawn's 1923 notebook; the Ammon letter; Falke ¶10–11; Kyle; Orra's diary; Oram; Duncan.

### Story 8 — Ivy Keddie goes up the ladder (1958–1970)

**Who:** **Ivy Keddie** (born 1931), Ansel's granddaughter and a graduate student in dairy husbandry at the State Agricultural College at Farland; Professor Leland Vaught.

**What she found.**
- **February 1958:** she climbed the ladder in the old station and found the crate, where it had lain since 1923.
- She borrowed Roy Brant's weigh book.
- She found **Strawn's 1923 notebook** in the Farland library. The notebooks had gone to the state dairy office in 1936 and were transferred to Farland in 1947; nobody had asked for them since.
- Rosalie Cudd copied the Alder Corners figures for her.

**The measurement.** On **9 March 1958** she measured the **nine surviving pipettes** against a calibrated burette. Falke and Oram both say nine; the Chronicle's "eleven" is wrong. They delivered materially less than their mark, all in the same proportion: about 95 per cent, roughly 16.72 c.c. against 17.6.

**The bulletin.** **Bulletin No. 471**, October 1958, by Vaught and Keddie, concluded:
- A short charge of milk in a bottle ruled for a full charge reads short in the same proportion.
- Nothing else explains the low tests. No milk was watered and no cream was stolen.

**The bulletin's arithmetic** over the 25 full delivery years:
- Recorded 3.61 against a true 3.80, 0.19 short.
- 1,444,000 lb of fat credited against 1,520,000 lb actually delivered.
- **76,000 lb** never paid for, worth **$19,000** at 25¢.
- The $3,800 award was **one-fifth** of that.

The Chronicle's $19,600 and Oram's 67,000 lb do not survive the arithmetic.

**The response.** The Ashlin Chronicle ran "Old Ordell Wrong Righted" on **3 October 1958**, Ansel having been dead 24 years and out of work 33. It is hurried and error-prone: it calls Ivy a great-niece and blames Ammon for knowing fraud. Falke's report to the county board in 1959 recommended filing and no action. He corrected "great-niece" to granddaughter and withdrew his bog-hay concession.

**Where the records went.**
- The test book and the crate: to the Ordell County Historical Rooms at Ashlin, 1959.
- The weigh book: given to the Historical Rooms by Hazel, 1963.
- Strawn's notebooks: the Farland library.
- The Cudd book: the Vessey County Historical Society, 1970.

The five books ended up in three buildings.

**Documents:** Bulletin No. 471; Chronicle; Falke; Duncan; Hazel; Oram.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie is Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** The chain: Ansel is the father of Duncan (born 1894), and Duncan is the father of Ivy (born 1931). Duncan states it first-hand. Hazel ("Ivy is Duncan's girl") and Oram ("the granddaughter") agree, and the Chronicle's own detail that her father taught school at Ashlin fits. Two sources say "great-niece": the Chronicle and page 2 of Falke's report. They are one source, not two, since Falke took it from the newspaper or the press release, and Falke himself corrected it after checking. Great-niece is also impossible on the genealogy: Ansel had no sibling whose grandchild Ivy could be.

**B2. Hazel and Ivy are second cousins.** Emmet Brant and Orra were siblings. Their children, Roy and Duncan, were first cousins, and Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are second cousins. Hazel and Duncan both say this independently, and both reject "niece" and "once removed". Oram's "first cousin once removed" is wrong: that relation spans generations, and Hazel and Ivy are the same generation. **What the sources leave open** is whether Emmet and Orra were full or half siblings. Duncan says "sister, the younger of the two"; Hazel says "half sister, by his father's second wife". Orra's writings from before 1889 were not preserved, so her diary cannot settle it. If they were half siblings, Roy and Duncan were half first cousins, and Hazel and Ivy are half second cousins.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose**, who organized the Association, served as its first president and wrote its by-laws. Kyle and Duncan independently call the "nephew" story nonsense. Why it matters: Gideon's article seven made the station's own test the measure of every patron's pay. That is how a short lot of glass became a 25-year loss in money. His son Selby was the manager who signed the "station gain" sheets without reading the lines, sent the 1919 water circular, and took Strawn's 1923 condemnation as routine without connecting it to the complaints. So the father's rule turned the glass error into money, and the son's office recorded the error every year without seeing it.

**B4. No relationship is established between the Vose and Keddie families.** Oram says they were "cousins of some sort", but he got it from his mother's mother and says he "could not draw you the line". Duncan reports the same gossip from two Ashlin women, says it arose from confusion with the Ashlin Voses (themselves no relation to Gideon), and says his mother, who knew everyone's connections, never put a Vose in any of them. I take Duncan's word: he is an insider and specific, while the rumor is admittedly secondhand and gives no link. The sources support no kinship.


# Answer sheet — Part 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

**C1.**

**(a)** The tests read **5 per cent low**, so every recorded test was 0.95 of the true value. The reason is the glassware. The bottles' necks are ruled for a 17.6 c.c. charge of milk. A pipette that delivers only a fraction *f* of 17.6 c.c. puts fraction *f* of the fat into the bottle, so the reading comes out at fraction *f* of the truth. The pipettes delivered about 16.72 c.c.:

> 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95

The delivered volume is inferred rather than read from a source. It is fixed by three figures that agree:
- 0.19 ÷ 3.80 = 0.05.
- The ledger's station gain: 1 ÷ 0.95 − 1 = 5.26 per cent, which the ledger writes as "five and a quarter per cent".
- $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = exactly 1/5.

The Chronicle's 16.27 c.c. would give 7.6 per cent, which matches none of these. It is a transposition of 16.72.

**(b)** True average = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80 per cent**. The recorded tests were **0.19 of a point** short. As a check, Larrow Green averaged 3.78, and the station averaged 3.85 once new glass was in use from 1924.

**(c)** 40,000,000 lb of milk over the 25 full delivery years:

> 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb of fat actually delivered
> 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb credited
> Shortfall = **76,000 lb of butterfat**

**(d)** 76,000 lb × $0.25 = **$19,000**. The patrons received $3,800:

> 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **1/5 (20 per cent)**

The Chronicle's $19,600 and Oram's 67,000 lb cannot be reconciled with the component figures. The first is an error, and the second is a transposition of 76,000.

**C2.** Emmet Brant's 1910 year: 200,000 lb of milk, recorded test 3.42.

**(a)** Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of butterfat.

**(b)** True test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60, so 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb. He was owed and not credited **360 lb**. The error is proportional, so on his lower test it comes to 0.18 of a point, not the average 0.19. A flat 0.19 would give 380 lb.

**(c)** 360 × $0.25 = **$90**. This uses Kyle's long-run average price, because no price specific to 1910 is given. On the flat-0.19 reading it would be $95.

**C3.** Over the 25 full delivery years (1898–1922):

**(a)** Credited to the patrons: **1,444,000 lb**.

**(b)** Reached Larrow Green in the station's cream: **1,520,000 lb**.

**(c)** The difference is 76,000 lb:
- **5.26 per cent of the fat credited** (76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 0.05263). This is the ledger's "station gain, five and a quarter per cent", computed on the credited figure.
- **Exactly 5.00 per cent of the fat received**, the true fat (76,000 ÷ 1,520,000).

The two percentages differ only because they are taken of different bases.

**C4.**

**(a)** **$77.93**: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15, the total on the facsimile invoice. The "$77.39" in Junia's prose and in the Chronicle is a transposition.

**(b)** **72 pipettes** (6 dozen at 35¢ = $25.20). Each was short by about **0.88 c.c.** (17.6 − 16.72, on the average delivery). The 0.88 is inferred from the 5 per cent proportion, as in C1(a). The Chronicle's 16.27 would have meant 1.33 c.c., and I reject it.

**C5.**

**(a)** **How long the glass was in use:** from **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923**, about **26 years and 6 weeks**. That includes the nine weeks of 8 May–10 July 1911, when borrowed glass replaced it, so actual use was about 26 years less nine weeks.

**The money calculation runs over 25 full delivery years**, 1898–1922. Kyle's figures: 40,000,000 ÷ 1,600,000 = 25.

The numbers differ because the two ends are partial seasons. 1897 began on 1 May, and 1923 ended on 12 June. The milk and fat totals were compiled by whole years, so those two part-years fall outside the figures used.

**(b)** **Cannot be determined from the sources.** The 1896 season, with borrowed glass, was carried on the creamery's own books, and the sheets were never put with the station papers. The only figure is Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds", which he says he "understood at the time from men who were there" and which is "not in any book I ever ruled". That is one unverified hearsay figure. It is also the one season the faulty glass did not touch, so nothing else in the record checks it.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran from **8 May to 10 July 1911**, exactly **nine weeks (63 days)**. It is dated by Orra's diary and by Falke.

- **Variable:** the measuring glass. A borrowed measure replaced the station's own.
- **Held constant:** the herds, the pasture and season, the men (the same tester), the machinery, and the supervision.
- **Result:** the tests stood markedly higher for the whole nine weeks and fell back on the day the station's own glass returned. It was a clean on/off/on reversal.

**What it establishes that a calculation cannot:** causation. The arithmetic (0.19 ÷ 3.80, the 5¼ per cent gain) shows that a 5 per cent error in the glass *would fit* the figures. The 1911 interval shows that changing the glass *changed the figures* while everything else stayed the same. That rules out the tester's honesty, the hay and water as causes all at once, since none of them changed.

**What it does not establish:**
- The size of the error. The sources give only "markedly".
- Which piece of the outfit was at fault, or that the borrowed measure was itself accurate. It shows only a difference between the two.
- That the error was constant through every year, 1897–1923, or in winter.
- Anything about how the glass came to be short, or whether Ammon knew.

Nor did it prove the cause to anyone at the time. Ansel put a question mark in the book and nothing more.

**D2.** Four reasons, from different narrators, why the tester cannot have been stealing cream:

1. **The ledger's "station gain"** (Kyle, the office clerk). Every year *more* fat reached Larrow Green in the station's cream than the patrons were credited with, about 5¼ per cent more. A skimming tester who wrote low tests to cover the theft would leave *less* fat arriving than the tests showed, or at most the same.
2. **The nine weeks of 1911** (Falke, confirmed by Orra's diary and Duncan). The same man, under the same supervision, recorded higher tests as soon as the glass changed.
3. **The tests after 1923** (Falke ¶11). Ansel stayed tester until April 1925. Once the new glass was in, from 1924, the station averaged 3.85, normal and above Larrow Green, with no change of man or method.
4. **The Alder Corners comparison** (Hazel, with the Cudd test book). The same milk from four of the six farms tested about 0.19 higher at the cheese factory in the summer flush. The low reading belonged to the station's measurement, not to the milk or the cream that left the station.

**Why they are independent:** they rest on different records kept by different people for different purposes. The Association's general ledger was kept by Teague and copied by Kyle. The station test book is Ansel's. The post-1923 averages came to Falke. The Cudd company kept its own test book in another county. None of them derives from another, and none depends on the 1958 bulletin.

**Holding even without the 1958 measurement:** the ledger station gain (1), the 1911 interval (2) and the post-1923 averages (3) all stand without it.

**Did the bookkeeper question the ledger?** No. Wilbur Teague wrote "five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account" every year of his service and "never asked anybody anything in forty years" (Kyle). He never questioned the very entries that refute the skimming charge. The Association's side urged that charge at the hearing (Falke says "an officer of the Association").

**D3.** **Yes, he knew the tests ran low. He did not know why, and nothing shows he understood what the station-gain line meant.**

- He became manager in 1911. The Ostrey Hollow patrons had complained that their tests ran low since 1902, at every annual meeting, where the station account itself was read out.
- In June 1919 he signed a circular warning those patrons about water in their milk. Kyle typed it, and it went out over Vose's name alone. That circular only makes sense if he knew the station's tests were low: he had settled on water as the reason.
- What he did not take in was the line above the total. Kyle watched him "put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign" a thousand times. The station gain showed that the fat was reaching the creamery, which contradicts his water theory as much as the theft theory.

So he knew of the low tests before 1923, but he blamed the patrons. The sources give no sign that he connected the gain line, or Strawn's condemnation, to the complaints.

## Section E — Contradictions

**E1.** Direct factual contradictions, each with its resolution and method:

1. **When the station opened.** Hazel: Ansel went up in '95. Falke: opened 4 May 1896; Duncan: test book from 1896. **Resolution: 1896.** Method: a dated official report plus a book record outrank a memory the narrator herself says is weak on years; '95 is the building winter.
2. **When the station's own outfit went into use.** 1 May 1897: Junia (from the invoice of 14 April 1897), Orra's diary for that day, and the Chronicle ("spring of 1897"). 1 May 1898 and "first season or two": Kyle. 1899: Hazel. **Resolution: 1 May 1897.** Method: a dated document plus a contemporaneous diary.
3. **Invoice total.** $77.93 on the facsimile total and by the line items; $77.39 in Junia's prose and the Chronicle. **Resolution: $77.93.** Method: arithmetic and the quoted document.
4. **Number of pipettes bought.** 6 dozen (invoice, Chronicle) against 5 dozen (Oram). **Resolution: 72.** Method: the document, and $25.20 ÷ $0.35 = 72.
5. **Number of pipettes surviving in 1958.** Nine (Falke, Oram) against eleven (Chronicle). **Resolution: nine.** Method: two independent witnesses (an official report and a man who handled the crate) against a hurried newspaper.
6. **What the pipettes delivered.** The Chronicle's 16.27 c.c. against the 5 per cent proportion implied by Falke's 0.19 on 3.80, Kyle's 5¼ per cent gain, and the one-fifth award. **Resolution: about 16.72 c.c.** This is inferred, by arithmetic consistency; the Chronicle transposed two digits.
7. **Year of condemnation.** 1923: Falke (12 June 1923), Junia (summer 1923, letter of 28 June), Duncan (crate to the loft in 1923), Orra (July 1923, new glass), Chronicle. 1921: Hazel. 1922: Kyle. **Resolution: 1923.** Method: dated documents and a contemporaneous diary.
8. **Amount of the award.** $3,800 (Falke, Kyle, Chronicle) against $3,500 (Oram, from his father). **Resolution: $3,800.** Method: independent majority, including the clerk who charged it to the account; it also makes exactly one-fifth of $19,000.
9. **Date of the award or hearing.** 1925 (Falke: 17 March–4 May 1925; Duncan; Orra; Oram) against 1924 (Kyle) and '26 (Hazel). **Resolution: 1925.** Method: dated diary entries and the report.
10. **The money and fat owed.** $19,000 and 76,000 lb by arithmetic (40,000,000 lb × 0.19 per cent; × $0.25), against the Chronicle's $19,600 and Oram's 67,000 lb. **Resolution: 76,000 lb and $19,000.** Method: arithmetic from Kyle's and Falke's component figures. The caveat is that the arithmetic depends on those components.
11. **Ivy's relationship to Ansel.** Granddaughter (Duncan, Hazel, Oram, Falke's correction) against great-niece (Chronicle; Falke page 2). **Resolution: granddaughter.**
12. **Ivy's relationship to Hazel.** Second cousins (Hazel, Duncan) against first cousin once removed (Oram). **Resolution: second cousins.** Method: working through the generations.
13. **Whether Orra was Emmet's full or half sister.** "Sister, the younger" (Duncan) against "half sister, by his father's second wife" (Hazel). **Cannot be settled**: no document survives, and Orra's writings before 1889 are lost.
14. **Rosalie Cudd's relationship to Jerome.** Daughter (Duncan) against niece (Chronicle). **Resolution: daughter.** Method: the careful family chronicler over a hurried newspaper; Duncan also records her taking over the factory "after her father".
15. **Whether the Voses and Keddies were related.** Cousins "of some sort" (Oram, secondhand) against no relation (Duncan). **Resolution: no established relation** (see B4).
16. **Whether Selby Vose was Gideon's son or nephew.** Son (Kyle, Duncan) against the nephew story reported at Ashlin. **Resolution: son**, on two independent insiders.
17. **When Emmet began the weigh book.** 1 January 1897 (Duncan) against 1 January 1900 (Hazel). **Leaning 1897, but not firmly settled.** Method: narrator reliability (Hazel disclaims years and misdates four other events); no independent document is quoted.
18. **Orra's birth year.** 1866 (diary preface; her own "sixty-four" in 1930) against 1868 (Duncan). **Resolution: 1866.** Method: her own contemporaneous statement and arithmetic.
19. **Emmet's visits to the Keddie house.** Duncan: Emmet sat at their Sunday table for 36 years, up to 1925. Orra's diary: he never entered the house after June 1919, and was estranged ten years until his death in 1929. **Resolution: the diary.** Method: a contemporaneous record outranks a memory 49 years later.
20. **How many families received the water circular.** Nine (Hazel) against eleven (Orra's diary, the night Ansel delivered them). **Resolution: eleven, with moderate confidence.** Method: a same-day diary over the memory of a small child. Both agree that six farms were involved.
21. **Who sat on the 1925 matter.** Chronicle: "the committee that sat in 1908 sat again in 1925". 22. **Whether Ammon knowingly sold a short lot.** Knowingly (Chronicle) against an accidental reground mold (Junia). **Cannot be settled.** The works books burned in 1931. The Chronicle's only evidence is the 1923 silence, and Junia's account is belief, not record.

24. **Cream "lost" between platform and wagon.** Oram's father believed less cream came off the platform than went onto it. The ledger shows *more* fat reached Larrow Green than was credited. **Resolution: the ledger**, a written record against one man's belief.

- **$77.39** (Junia's prose and the Chronicle). The tie is broken by the invoice's own line items, which sum to $77.93, and by the facsimile total. The Chronicle is probably drawing on the firm's printed history, so the two are not independent.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. Causal theory.** In 1897 Ammon & Sons of Tarnet supplied the Ostrey Hollow station with 72 test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. They came from one lot, probably blown in a reground mold, and each delivered only about 95 per cent of its mark (about 16.72 c.c.). Every test sample was therefore 5 per cent short, in a bottle whose neck was ruled for a full charge, so every reading came out at 95 per cent of the truth.

- **The size of the error:** 3.80 true read as 3.61, a shortfall of 0.19. Over the 25 full delivery years 1898–1922 that was 76,000 lb of fat, $19,000, of which the patrons recovered one-fifth.
- **Why that size:** it is the ratio of delivered to marked volume, 16.72 ÷ 17.6. Because the error is a proportion of the reading, it stayed "flat as a floor" in every month and on wet and dry years alike. A feed effect would have varied with the season.
- **Why it began when it did:** on 1 May 1897, when the station stopped borrowing Larrow Green's glass and put its own outfit into use.
- **Why it ended when it did:** Strawn condemned the lot on 12 June 1923. New glass was in use from July 1923, and from 1924 the station tested 3.85.
- **Why nothing else had to change:** the error lived entirely in the glass. The scales were true (Emmet's weigh book agreed), and the acid, the cows, the pasture and the tester were unchanged. The same fat went down to Larrow Green all along, which is why the ledger showed a steady "station gain". The 1911 swap of glass confirms the whole theory: figures up with borrowed glass, down with the station's own.

**The three competing explanations:**
1. **The patrons watered their milk.** Advanced by the manager, Selby Vose, in his 1919 circular. **Defeated by:** Strawn's lactometer tests at every visit from 1908 onward, which never found water; the 1911 interval (same milk, higher tests); and Alder Corners (same milk, about 0.19 higher).
2. **The tester skimmed cream and wrote low tests to hide it.** Urged at the 1925 hearing by an officer of the Association; Oram's father believed it too. **Defeated by:** the ledger's station gain (more fat arrived, not less); the 1911 interval; and Ansel's own normal tests of 3.85 after 1923.
3. **Wet bog hay on Hessel Bottom thinned the cream.** Advanced by the Association committee of 1908 and an Ashlin veterinarian in 1926, and briefly conceded by Falke. **Defeated by:** Bulletin No. 214, the college's 1911 feeding trial: at most 0.05 in winter and nothing on summer pasture, so it cannot produce 0.19 in July. Also by the 1911 interval (same pasture, higher tests) and by the constancy of the gap across seasons.

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glass?** **No.** The crate sat in the loft over the weigh room, where two of the three sittings were held, and nobody went up. Strawn's 1923 notebook recording the condemnation was among the four books before the board, but Kyle says no one laid it beside the station or receiving books. No narrator records any examination of the glass before Ivy's in 1958.

**What did the tester believe?** **That it was something in the glass.** He said so at home in the summer of 1911, twice (Duncan), and marked the 1911 jump with a question mark in the test book. He never wrote the suspicion down or pressed it, because he could not prove it. So he suspected the cause but never knew it, which is the sense of Ivy's "he never knew".

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** From 1897 to 1923 the Ostrey Hollow skimming station tested its patrons' milk with a lot of pipettes from Ammon & Sons that delivered 5 per cent short of their mark. Because the Association paid by butterfat test, every reading ran 0.19 low: 3.61 instead of 3.80. Over 25 years that cost the Hessel Bottom farmers 76,000 lb of fat, or $19,000. The patrons blamed the tester, Ansel Keddie; the manager blamed water; a committee blamed the hay. In 1925 a board sat beneath the crate of condemned glass, dismissed Keddie and paid out one-fifth. The truth was spread across separate records: the supplier's invoice, the tester's 1911 question mark, a farmer's weigh book, the clerk's yearly "station gain" line, an inspector's notebook, a cheese factory's tests, and a wife's diary. No one put them together until his granddaughter Ivy climbed to the loft in 1958 and measured the glass.
