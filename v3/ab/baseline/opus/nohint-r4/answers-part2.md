# Answer sheet — Part 2 (Section A, stories 5–8; Section B)

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### Story 5 — The broken measure: nine weeks in 1911

**Who:** Ansel Keddie; his wife Orra; his son Duncan, then 17.

**What happened, and when.** On **8 May 1911** the station's own measuring glass broke. Orra's diary that day: "A. home cross about a broken measure. Rain all day." A measure borrowed from elsewhere was used in its place **until 10 July 1911**, which is 63 days, exactly nine weeks (Falke ¶6). For all nine weeks the station's tests stood markedly higher. On the day the station went back to its own glass they fell to their old level.

**What was held constant.** Everything else stayed the same: the herds, the pasture, the season, the men, the machinery, and the supervision. The same tester made every test.

**What Ansel did.** He wrote a **question mark** in the test book against the change. That summer he came home and said "it was something in the glass", twice: once at supper and once by the door, which was not his way (Duncan). He never put the suspicion in the book, because he would not write down what he could not prove. He had nothing to hold in his hand but a suspicion, set against an outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county. So he went on for fourteen more years, writing down every figure the glass gave him, and those figures later convicted him. Duncan calls this "the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had".

**What else happened in 1911.** The college ran its bog-hay feeding trial (Bulletin 214), which later showed the hay could not explain summer figures. Selby Vose became manager of the Association (Kyle). In October Duncan left to teach at Ashlin.

**Why it matters.** The episode was a natural experiment, and it knocks out all three theories at once:
- **Theft:** the same man made the tests and the figures rose.
- **Hay:** the same summer pasture gave higher figures.
- **Water:** the same milk gave higher figures.

It is also the only direct evidence before 1958 that the cause was the glass. Falke says the 1958 bulletin treats it as decisive.

**What the sources do not give.** They give no exact test figures for the nine weeks ("markedly higher"). They do not say which piece of glass broke or where the borrowed measure came from. They do not show that the borrowed glass was itself accurate. So the interval shows that the station's own glass was the cause, but it does not size the error. The size comes from the 1958 measurement and the ledger.

**Documents:** Orra's diary (8 May 1911); Falke ¶6 and ¶8; Duncan §IV; the station test book (the question mark).

### Story 6 — The line in plain sight: the office at Larrow Green

**Who:** Bertram Kyle, who sat at the outer desk outside the manager's door for 25 years; Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper; and Selby Vose, manager from 1911 for about a quarter of a century. Vose was Gideon's **son**, not his nephew (Kyle and Duncan independently).

**The yearly ledger line.** Every year the Ostrey Hollow station account carried three figures and a sentence:
- the butterfat credited to the patrons on the station test;
- the butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream;
- the difference, entered as **"station gain"**, followed by the words **"five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account."**

Teague wrote the line every year and "never asked anybody anything in forty years". Kyle copied it into fair hand for the annual meeting, where it was read out to farmers with their hats on their knees. Vose was courteous to every caller, but he read only totals: he put his thumb on the bottom figure and signed. Because the figure had a name, everyone treated it as explained.

**What the line meant.** More fat reached the creamery than the patrons were paid for, every year. A tester stealing cream would produce the opposite. The line itself refuted the later charge, and nobody read it that way.

**Kyle's figures, compiled for the arbitration.** **40,000,000 lb** of milk received at the station over the years of complaint. At **1,600,000 lb a year**, that is **25 full years**. Butterfat averaged **25¢ a pound**. Working from those:
- Fat credited at 3.61 per cent: **1,444,000 lb**.
- Fat actually present at 3.80 per cent: **1,520,000 lb**.
- Station gain: **76,000 lb**. That is 5.26 per cent *of the fat credited* (the ledger's "five and a quarter") and exactly 5 per cent *of the fat received*.

**The circular.** In 1919 Kyle typed the water circular and Vose signed it; it went out over his name alone. When the award came it was charged to the station account, which in effect paid it out of the station gain.

**Kyle's own errors.** He dates the outfit to 1898, Strawn's visit to 1922, and the award to 1924. I correct these from contemporary documents (Stories 1 and 7).

**Documents:** the station accounts and general ledger; the 1919 circular; Kyle (r23).

### Story 7 — The glass condemned (1923) and the tester condemned (1925)

**The condemnation.** On **12 June 1923**, on an ordinary circuit visit, the state dairy inspector **Merle Strawn** tested the station's pipettes against a calibrated burette. They would not deliver their mark. He condemned the whole lot and told the manager to order new. Strawn was related to no one in the valley. He wrote the condemnation in his notebook and drew no inference about the 26 years before.

The office ordered new glass that week (Ammon letter of 28 June), and it was in use by July 1923. Orra's diary for July 1923: "new glass at the station and new pails". The condemned glass went into a crate under a tarpaulin in the loft over the intake room. **From 1924 the station's tests averaged 3.85**, and this was under Ansel, the same tester.

Hazel's "twenty-one" and Kyle's "twenty-two" are wrong. The inspector's date, the Ammon letter and the diary all put it in 1923.

**The arbitration.** The patrons petitioned in **January 1925**, and Emmet Brant's name was on the petition. The board impounded the test book in March. A board of arbitration, chaired by an Ashlin attorney, sat from **17 March to 4 May 1925**. Of its three sittings, one was at Ashlin and **two were in the station's weigh room**. The crate sat about twenty feet above them, and men stood outside in the rain.

Four books went before the board (Kyle): the station test book, the receiving book, Emmet's weigh book, and Strawn's notebook for 1923. Nobody laid the receiving book beside Strawn's notebook.

**The outcome.**
- **30 April 1925:** Ansel was dismissed, four days before the award. "Twenty-nine seasons and a letter." He came home, hung up his apron, and ate ham.
- **4 May 1925:** the board found no cause established and awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons **$3,800, "without admission."** Cleve Oram's $3,500 (his father's figure) yields to Falke, Kyle and the Chronicle. Kyle's year of 1924 and Hazel's '26 yield to Falke's dates.

**Afterward.** Ansel rebuilt his cow shed, whistling, and worked as a carpenter at a quarter of his old pay. Men later praised his doors; none spoke of "the other thing". He **died in February 1934**, never cleared. His words to Orra: "a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name." Orra died in 1948. The station closed in 1946 and was used to store hay tools.

**Documents:** Strawn's 1923 notebook; the Ammon letter; Falke ¶10–11; Kyle; Orra's diary; Oram; Duncan.

### Story 8 — Ivy Keddie goes up the ladder (1958–1970)

**Who:** **Ivy Keddie** (born 1931), Ansel's granddaughter and a graduate student in dairy husbandry at the State Agricultural College at Farland; Professor Leland Vaught.

**What she found.**
- **February 1958:** she climbed the ladder in the old station and found the crate, where it had lain since 1923.
- She borrowed Roy Brant's weigh book.
- She found **Strawn's 1923 notebook** in the Farland library. The notebooks had gone to the state dairy office in 1936 and were transferred to Farland in 1947; nobody had asked for them since.
- Rosalie Cudd copied the Alder Corners figures for her.

**The measurement.** On **9 March 1958** she measured the **nine surviving pipettes** against a calibrated burette. Falke and Oram both say nine; the Chronicle's "eleven" is wrong. They delivered materially less than their mark, all in the same proportion: about 95 per cent, roughly 16.72 c.c. against 17.6.

**The bulletin.** **Bulletin No. 471**, October 1958, by Vaught and Keddie, concluded:
- A short charge of milk in a bottle ruled for a full charge reads short in the same proportion.
- Nothing else explains the low tests. No milk was watered and no cream was stolen.

**The bulletin's arithmetic** over the 25 full delivery years:
- Recorded 3.61 against a true 3.80, 0.19 short.
- 1,444,000 lb of fat credited against 1,520,000 lb actually delivered.
- **76,000 lb** never paid for, worth **$19,000** at 25¢.
- The $3,800 award was **one-fifth** of that.

The Chronicle's $19,600 and Oram's 67,000 lb do not survive the arithmetic.

**The response.** The Ashlin Chronicle ran "Old Ordell Wrong Righted" on **3 October 1958**, Ansel having been dead 24 years and out of work 33. It is hurried and error-prone: it calls Ivy a great-niece and blames Ammon for knowing fraud. Falke's report to the county board in 1959 recommended filing and no action. He corrected "great-niece" to granddaughter and withdrew his bog-hay concession.

**Where the records went.**
- The test book and the crate: to the Ordell County Historical Rooms at Ashlin, 1959.
- The weigh book: given to the Historical Rooms by Hazel, 1963.
- Strawn's notebooks: the Farland library.
- The Cudd book: the Vessey County Historical Society, 1970.

The five books ended up in three buildings.

**Documents:** Bulletin No. 471; Chronicle; Falke; Duncan; Hazel; Oram.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie is Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** The chain: Ansel is the father of Duncan (born 1894), and Duncan is the father of Ivy (born 1931). Duncan states it first-hand. Hazel ("Ivy is Duncan's girl") and Oram ("the granddaughter") agree, and the Chronicle's own detail that her father taught school at Ashlin fits. Two sources say "great-niece": the Chronicle and page 2 of Falke's report. They are one source, not two, since Falke took it from the newspaper or the press release, and Falke himself corrected it after checking. Great-niece is also impossible on the genealogy: Ansel had no sibling whose grandchild Ivy could be.

**B2. Hazel and Ivy are second cousins.** Emmet Brant and Orra were siblings. Their children, Roy and Duncan, were first cousins, and Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are second cousins. Hazel and Duncan both say this independently, and both reject "niece" and "once removed". Oram's "first cousin once removed" is wrong: that relation spans generations, and Hazel and Ivy are the same generation. **What the sources leave open** is whether Emmet and Orra were full or half siblings. Duncan says "sister, the younger of the two"; Hazel says "half sister, by his father's second wife". Orra's writings from before 1889 were not preserved, so her diary cannot settle it. If they were half siblings, Roy and Duncan were half first cousins, and Hazel and Ivy are half second cousins.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose**, who organized the Association, served as its first president and wrote its by-laws. Kyle and Duncan independently call the "nephew" story nonsense. Why it matters: Gideon's article seven made the station's own test the measure of every patron's pay. That is how a short lot of glass became a 25-year loss in money. His son Selby was the manager who signed the "station gain" sheets without reading the lines, sent the 1919 water circular, and took Strawn's 1923 condemnation as routine without connecting it to the complaints. So the father's rule turned the glass error into money, and the son's office recorded the error every year without seeing it.

**B4. No relationship is established between the Vose and Keddie families.** Oram says they were "cousins of some sort", but he got it from his mother's mother and says he "could not draw you the line". Duncan reports the same gossip from two Ashlin women, says it arose from confusion with the Ashlin Voses (themselves no relation to Gideon), and says his mother, who knew everyone's connections, never put a Vose in any of them. I take Duncan's word: he is an insider and specific, while the rumor is admittedly secondhand and gives no link. The sources support no kinship.
