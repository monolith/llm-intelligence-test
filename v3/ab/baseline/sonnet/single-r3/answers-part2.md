# Answers — Part 2: Section A (Stories 5–8) and Section B

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### 5. Suspicion of Watering and the 1919 Circular

The first of the three theories, chronologically dominant in official Association thinking, was
that the Ostrey Hollow patrons were adding water to their milk. The Association's manager, Selby
Vose (son of founder Gideon Vose, manager from 1911, see B3), circulated a written warning about
added water to the Hessel Bottom patrons in 1919 (r22, r23 — Kyle, the office clerk, typed it
himself and recalled its "no doubt at all... about what was meant"). Accounts differ on how many
families received it: Duncan Keddie recalls nine letters to six farms (a farm sometimes holding
more than one family, r18), while Orra Keddie's contemporaneous 1919 diary entry states Ansel
personally carried the circular to eleven families, refusing to mail it or leave it on the
platform, and returning home at eleven at night "with his hat still on" (r24). This is not fully
resolved (see Section E).

The circular's most lasting effect was personal: Orra's brother Emmet Brant, the largest Hessel
Bottom patron, confronted Ansel Keddie in the Keddie yard two days later, asking why he had
carried a paper accusing men he had tested milk for since the station opened. Keddie replied that
he "would not have another man carry it." Brant did not enter the house, and the two men — who had
shared Sunday dinners for thirty-six years — did not speak again until Brant's death in 1929 (r09,
r18, r24). The state dairy inspector's own lactometer testing, performed at every circuit visit
from 1908 onward, never once found added water at the station (r22) — the theory was current for
years before being formally disproven.

Documents bearing on this story: r09, r17, r18, r22, r23, r24.

### 6. The Station-Gain Ledger and the Skimming Accusation

The second theory — that Keddie himself skimmed cream and falsified low tests to conceal it — was
urged at the 1925 hearing by an unnamed officer of the Association (r22). It is contradicted by
the Association's own routine bookkeeping: the annual "station gain" ledger line (Story 2) shows
that the butterfat actually reaching Larrow Green in the station's cream consistently *exceeded*
what patrons were credited for, not the reverse a thief would produce (r05, r23). Buttermaker
Hobart Sill's own receiving book shows the same "heavy" pattern, even though Sill himself
personally believed the patrons were watering their milk — his own records undercut his own
belief (r14). Dell Rundle's 1939 jubilee pamphlet asserts the valley's "settled opinion" that
Keddie skimmed cream, then undercuts itself with a same-year note added while in press, conceding
that the ledger showed the station's gain arriving heavy, not light (r12).

A dissenting voice: Cleve Oram, recalling his father's decades as the cream hauler between the
station and Larrow Green, reports his father's lifelong belief that less cream came off the
platform than went onto his wagon (r21) — an account based on impression rather than ledger
figures, and outweighed by the documentary "station gain" evidence from three independent
record-keepers (Teague/Kyle, Sill, and Rundle's own retracted concession).

Documents bearing on this story: r05, r12, r14, r21, r23.

### 7. The 1925 Arbitration and Ansel Keddie's Dismissal

The Hessel Bottom patrons petitioned the Association in January 1925. A board of arbitration,
chaired by attorney Alonzo Frick, sat at Ashlin from 17 March to 4 May 1925, with two of its three
sittings held at the station itself so the books and machinery would not have to be moved (r09,
r13, r21, r22, r24). It heard all three theories (watering, skimming, wet hay), found none proven,
and awarded the patrons **$3,800**, "without admission" of any cause — a figure independently
given by both the county report (r22) and the office clerk who processed it (r23; Oram's
secondhand family recollection of "$3,500," r21, is outweighed by these two document-based
accounts). Ansel Keddie was dismissed on 30 April 1925, four days before the award was announced
(r18, r22) — Orra's diary records his return home that day, apron rolled under his arm, asking
what there was for supper: "There was ham. He ate it" (r24).

Only four exhibits were placed before the board: the station's own test book (in Keddie's hand),
the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book (lent for the purpose), and the state
inspector's notebook for the year he had condemned the station's glass — a list independently
reproduced by both the clerk who assembled it (r23) and the board's own chairman (r13). The crate
of condemned glassware itself, sitting in the loft directly over the hearing room for two of the
three sessions, was never brought in or examined (r13, r21, r23; corroborated by an account
summarized earlier in this session, r02). Keddie lived nine more years, working as a carpenter,
and died in 1934 with the accusation unresolved (r18, r21, r24).

Documents bearing on this story: r02, r09, r13, r18, r21, r22, r23, r24.

### 8. The Crate in the Loft: Rediscovery and Vindication (1923, 1958)

In 1923 the state dairy inspector, Merle Strawn, ran the station's milk pipettes against a
calibrated burette on an ordinary circuit visit and found they would not deliver their marked
volume. He condemned the entire lot on **12 June 1923** (r06, corroborated by r22's independent
report) — a date given as 12 July by two other narrators (r08, r12), addressed in Section E. New
glass, ordered the same month from Ammon & Sons, was in service within weeks; the station's
recorded average immediately rose to 3.85% and held there (r22). The condemned crate went into the
loft above the intake room and was forgotten for thirty-five years.

In February 1958, Ansel Keddie's granddaughter Ivy Keddie, a graduate student at the State
Agricultural College at Farland, found the crate under a tarpaulin. On 9 March 1958 she measured
the nine surviving pipettes (a count given by r06, r07, r11, and r22, against a conflicting count
of eleven in r12 and r18 — see Section E) against a calibrated burette. Each was blown to deliver
17.6 c.c. but delivered on average only **16.72 c.c.** — about 5% short — explaining the entire
0.19-point gap arithmetically (Section C). She and Professor Leland Vaught published the finding
as State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958), crediting Rosalie Cudd's Alder
Corners records and the state dairy office's loan of Strawn's old notebooks (r04, r11, r19). The
Ashlin Chronicle (r19) and county agent Falke's report (r22) publicized the finding, clearing
Ansel Keddie's name twenty-four years after his death.

Documents bearing on this story: r04, r06, r07, r08, r11, r18, r19, r20, r21, r22.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1.** Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's **granddaughter**. This is derived directly from Duncan
Keddie's own 1968 family notes: Duncan states plainly that he is Ansel's son and that his daughter
Ivy "makes her my father's granddaughter," adding that he has "already twice seen it printed
otherwise" (r18) — a complaint borne out by two other sources. The Ashlin Chronicle's 1958 report
calls her his "great-niece" (r19), and Orin Falke's 1959 county report repeats the same error in
its body text before appending an explicit correction: "I am informed, and have verified, that
Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine" (r22).
I resolve this in favor of granddaughter on the strength of Duncan's first-person paternity claim,
which Falke's own retraction independently confirms.

**B2.** Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were **second cousins**. Working from Duncan Keddie's explicit
chart (r18): Orra Brant (Ansel Keddie's wife) and Emmet Brant were brother and sister; their
children, Duncan Keddie and Roy Brant, were therefore first cousins; and their children in turn —
Ivy (Duncan's daughter) and Hazel (Roy's daughter) — are second cousins, "the children of first
cousins." Hazel Brant independently gives the identical reasoning and conclusion from the other
branch of the family (r17), which I treat as meaningful (if not fully arithmetically independent)
corroboration since both women worked out and stated the same relationship without apparent
reliance on the other's account. The sources leave nothing substantial open about the intervening
generations — both links (siblings, then first cousins) are stated plainly by family members on
both sides. Cleve Oram's secondhand claim that they were "first cousins once removed" (r21) is an
outsider's error, not evidence of genuine ambiguity in the family record.

**B3.** Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**, who organized the Association, served as its
first president, and wrote its by-laws (r18, r23 — the latter from the office clerk who worked
under Selby for twenty-five years and explicitly rebuts a competing "nephew" rumor he says he has
"heard... twice in the last year"). This matters because it makes Selby Vose an institutional
insider by birth, inheriting the manager's chair from the Association's own founder. Clerk Kyle's
account of Vose as a manager who signed sheets by the bottom total without reading the lines above
(r23) is consistent with a founding-family manager inclined to trust the institution's instruments
and personnel rather than suspect them — a plausible partial explanation for why the Association's
formal response to the long-running complaint (the 1919 circular, the skimming accusation at the
hearing) targeted the patrons and the tester rather than the equipment.

**B4.** The sources do not establish any real connection between the Vose and Keddie families.
Duncan Keddie addresses this directly and denies it: "There was no connection whatever between us
and the Voses," attributing the persistent rumor of a first-cousin tie through the mothers to
valley talk, and noting that his own mother — by his account a woman who "could not pass a
headstone without giving you the connection" — never once included a Vose in her genealogical
accounts (r18). Against this, Cleve Oram repeats unspecified valley hearsay that "the Keddies and
the Voses were cousins of some sort," sourced to what his mother had from her mother (r21). Weighed
against a direct family member's explicit, reasoned denial, I resolve that no relationship is
established — though, as with all negative claims, the documents cannot fully prove the absence of
one, only that the best-positioned witness denies it and the only witness asserting it is relaying
secondhand valley gossip of the very kind being denied.
