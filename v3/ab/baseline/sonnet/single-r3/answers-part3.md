# Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math (show working)

**C1.**

*(a) Proportion by which tests differed from the truth, from the glassware.* The pipettes were
blown to deliver 17.6 c.c. (r20's invoice; the graduated neck's calibration figure, r19). Measured
against a calibrated burette in 1958, they delivered on average **16.72 c.c.** (r04, r07 — I
reject r11's figure of 16.27 c.c. as an error; it is the same digits transposed, and it is not
consistent with the rest of the arithmetic below, whereas 16.72 is). Shortfall = 17.6 − 16.72 =
0.88 c.c. Proportion short = 0.88 / 17.6 = **0.05, i.e. 5.0%**. Because the test bottle's neck is
calibrated assuming the full 17.6 c.c. charge, a 5%-short charge produces a fat reading that is
also 5% short, in every test, regardless of herd or season.

*(b) True average test, and points short.* The station's recorded average was **3.61%** (r02, r07,
r22 — three independent citations). If recorded = 0.95 × true, then true = 3.61 / 0.95 = **3.80%**.
Points short = 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 points**, which matches the gap figure given independently by
two other sources (r17, r22) and closely matches the corrected post-1923 station average of 3.85%
(r22) and the independent Alder Corners cross-station average of ~3.80% (r10). This convergence of
four independently sourced numbers is why I trust 3.80/0.19 over r11's outlier figures (3.905 /
implied ~0.30 gap).

*(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited.* Total milk received at the station over the affected years
was **40,000,000 lb** (r02, r07, r23 — I reject the competing 44,000,000 lb figure of r05/r12; see
below). Shortfall in pounds = 40,000,000 × (3.80% − 3.61%) = 40,000,000 × 0.0019 = **76,000 lb**.
This matches the total independently reported by Ivy Keddie's own account and by r07's separate
citation of the bulletin — a clean arithmetic confirmation, and the reason I reject r11's 118,000
lb and r13/r21's 67,000 lb as the bulletin's true total (see Section E).

*(d) Money value, and fraction actually received.* Butterfat averaged **$0.25/lb** over the whole
stretch, per the office clerk's own ledger-derived figure (r23; I prefer this over Kepp's rhetorical
"twenty-seven cents," r16, since Kyle had actual ledger access and states he was "set to find" the
figure). Value = 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000** — again matching Ivy Keddie's own figure (r04)
exactly. The 1925 arbitration award was $3,800 (Section A, Story 7). Fraction actually received =
3,800 / 19,000 = **0.20, i.e. exactly 20%**. (Rundle's $19,600, r12, describes the patrons' 1925
petition amount — a different quantity, asked for before the true cause or size was known — not
the corrected 1958 total; I do not treat it as contradicting the $19,000 figure.)

**C2.** For Emmet Brant, 1910 (the largest patron; his weigh-book entry reads "Two hundred thousand
pounds even... paid on a test of three forty-two," r17).

(a) Credited butterfat = 200,000 × 3.42% = **6,840 lb**.

(b) True test = 3.42 / 0.95 = 3.6%. True butterfat = 200,000 × 3.6% = 7,200 lb. Owed and not
credited = 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb**.

(c) Value = 360 × $0.25 = **$90.00** (using the ledger's whole-period average price; no 1910-specific
price is given in any source, so this figure should be read as an estimate using the best available
rate, not a precise 1910 market price).

**C3.**

(a) Credited to patrons in total = 40,000,000 × 3.61% = **1,444,000 lb**.

(b) Actually reaching the main creamery in the station's cream: since the cream physically carries
the true fat content of the milk regardless of what the flawed test recorded, this equals the true
total = 40,000,000 × 3.80% = **1,520,000 lb**.

(c) Difference = 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 lb. As a **percentage of what was credited** (the
ledger's own base — this is how "station gain" was actually recorded): 76,000 / 1,444,000 =
**5.26%**, which matches the ledger's own recurring annotation of "five and a quarter per cent, as
usual" (r23) almost exactly. As a **percentage of what actually arrived** (the true total): 76,000
/ 1,520,000 = exactly **5.00%**, matching the pipette shortfall ratio from C1(a) precisely. Both are
correct; they differ only in denominator, and the near-exact match of the first to the ledger's own
long-standing "5¼%" note is itself independent confirmation of the whole reconstruction.

**C4.**

(a) Total invoice for the station's original testing outfit = **$77.93** (r20's reproduced
facsimile invoice: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93, verified by direct addition). Note
that r20's own prose elsewhere gives "$77.39," and r19 repeats that same wrong figure — I take the
itemized, arithmetically self-consistent invoice as authoritative over the prose paraphrase; see
Section E.

(b) The invoice bought **72 pipettes** (six dozen, marked 17.6 c.c. each), each short by **0.88
c.c.** (17.6 − 16.72, per C1(a)).

**C5.**

(a) The faulty glassware was in continuous use for **26 calendar years**, 1 May 1897 to 12 June
1923 (r19, r20 both independently state "twenty-six years"), interrupted only by the nine-week
borrowed-measure interval in summer 1911. The money/pounds calculation in C1(c)/C3, however, runs
over **25 full delivery years** (40,000,000 lb ÷ 1,600,000 lb/year = 25, both figures given
together in r23). These numbers differ because the glass touched parts of 27 calendar years in
total (1897 and 1923 both being partial seasons — the outfit arrived partway through 1897, and was
condemned partway through 1923) but only 25 of those years were complete delivery seasons under the
faulty glass; the partial first and last years are excluded from the full-year reckoning. This is
my inference from the two independently stated figures (26 years of use, 25 full years of intake
data); no source states the reconciliation explicitly.

(b) The station's first season (1896) received an estimated **1,100,000 lb** of milk, per the
office clerk's recollection of what he was told by men who were there — explicitly not drawn from
any surviving book, since the first season's milk was carried on the main creamery's books, not the
station's own account (r23). This figure should be treated as an approximation, not a ledger fact.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran from 8 May to 10 July 1911 — nine weeks (r22, dated precisely; independently
corroborated by Orra Keddie's diary entry of 8 May 1911, r24). Treated as an experiment: the
variable was the measuring instrument (the station's own glass, versus a borrowed substitute); held
constant were the tester (Ansel Keddie throughout), the herds, the pasture, and the general season
(spring into midsummer, on both sides of the interval). The result was that recorded tests rose
during the nine weeks and fell back to their former level the same day the station's own glass
resumed (r22). What this establishes, that a calculation alone (like the 1958 burette measurement)
cannot: a real-time, causal link from *within* the disputed decades themselves — proof that
whatever was wrong tracked the instrument, not the calendar, the cows, or the tester, at the actual
time the low tests were being recorded and paid on, independent of and decades prior to the 1958
forensic work. What it does **not** establish: the size or mechanism of the fault (no cubic
centimeters or percentage were measured in 1911 — only a relative rise); nor that the borrowed
measure was itself perfectly accurate (only better than the station's own); nor a full exclusion of
any wet-hay contribution in general, though the abruptness of both transitions (up in May, down
again in July) is hard to square with a gradual pasture-driven effect.

**D2.** Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream: (1) The 1911 natural
experiment (r22, r24, D1): if Keddie were personally diverting cream, changing the measuring
instrument he used should not have changed the outcome, yet it did, immediately and reversibly.
(2) The "station gain" ledger evidence (r05, r14, r23; Story 6): the cream that actually reached
Larrow Green consistently contained *more* fat than patrons were credited for, the opposite of what
skimming would produce — and this evidence comes from ordinary annual bookkeeping wholly
independent of the 1958 investigation. (3) The Alder Corners cross-check (r10): the same farms,
hauling the same milk to a different station in summer, tested normally there — showing the
deficiency belonged to the Ostrey Hollow station as a place, not to Keddie as a person, since when
he was not the tester the same milk still would have had to pass through the same hands to reach a
different result, yet did not need to. These are independent because they rest on different bodies
of evidence (a contemporaneous instrument swap; routine ledger bookkeeping; a separate company's
records) collected by different people for different original purposes. Reason (2) is the one that
would still hold even if the glassware had never been measured in 1958, since it is drawn from
routine annual ledger entries that predate and do not depend on Ivy Keddie's forensic work at all.
As for whether the bookkeeper ever questioned these entries: the sources do not identify the
bookkeeper, Wilbur Teague, as the one who made the theft accusation (that came from an unnamed
"officer of the Association" at the hearing, r22, and separately from the manager's watering
accusation, r22/r23) — so the premise that he was answering for "his own" accusation is not
supported. On the substance, though: no. Per clerk Bertram Kyle's direct account, Teague "never
asked anybody anything in forty years," wrote the exculpatory "station gain... as usual" line
every year without remark, and Kyle himself, who copied it, says it "never once crossed my mind to
wonder at it" either (r23).

**D3.** Selby Vose almost certainly knew the station's tests ran chronically low, but there is no
evidence he understood the ledger clue that would have explained why. He became manager in 1911
(r23), inheriting a complaint already a decade old and discussed at annual meetings; he personally
signed and circulated the 1919 warning about watering (r22, r23), which presupposes he knew there
was a persistent shortfall to explain — he simply had the wrong theory. But clerk Kyle's direct,
long-term observation of Vose's working habits — "he read the totals and he never read the lines
above them... take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign" (r23) — indicates
he was not a close reader of the itemized station-gain figures that, in hindsight, refuted his own
accusation. My answer: yes to the fact of the low tests (public and acted upon); no clear evidence
of awareness of the specific corroborating ledger detail that sat, in Kyle's words, "in plain sight
with a name on it" for the whole of his tenure.

## Section E — Contradictions

   arithmetic consistency (see C1) and majority; 16.27 is likely a digit-transposition error.
2. **True average test:** 3.80% (r04, r07, r10) vs 3.905% (r11). *Resolved to 3.80* — arithmetic
   derivation from the recorded average and independent cross-station match (r10).
3. **Total shortfall in pounds:** 76,000 lb (r04, r07) vs 118,000 lb (r11) vs 67,000 lb (r13, r21).

   contemporary claims) rather than simple errors; flagged as such rather than dismissed outright.

## Section F — Theory

**F1.** The shortfall was caused entirely by a manufacturing defect in the pipettes the station
bought new in 1897: blown to mark 17.6 c.c., they delivered on average only 16.72 c.c. — about 5%
short. Because the test bottle's graduated neck assumes a full 17.6 c.c. charge, every short charge
produced a proportionally short fat reading, regardless of herd, hay, season, or tester. This
explains the size of the error (0.19 points is 5% of the true 3.80% average, matching the pipette's
own physical shortfall exactly); why that particular size (fixed by the glass's own dimensions, not
by anything biological); why it began when it did (1 May 1897, the day the defective outfit went
into service, replacing accurate borrowed glass; the sole exception, nine weeks in 1911, tracks a
second instrument swap exactly); why it ended when it did (12 June 1923, the day the inspector
condemned and replaced the glass, after which the recorded average immediately rose to 3.85%); and
why nothing at the station or the farms needed to change for either transition, since the cause was
a fixed physical property of specific glassware, not a behavior or a biological condition — the
number moved the instant the instrument did, with no adjustment period.

Three competing explanations, and what defeats each:
1. **Watering**, advanced by manager Selby Vose (1919 circular, r22/r23) — defeated by the state
   inspector's own lactometer testing, which never once found added water at the station from 1908
   onward (r22).
2. **Skimming/theft by the tester**, urged by an unnamed Association officer at the 1925 hearing
   (r22) — defeated by the "station gain" ledger showing cream arriving at Larrow Green heavier
   than credited, the opposite of what theft would produce (r05, r14, r23), and by the 1911 natural
   experiment showing the same tester's figures moved with the instrument, not with him (D1).
3. **Wet hay/soil deficiency**, advanced by Grigg's 1908 committee and elaborated by Kepp in 1926
   (r15, r16) — defeated by Bulletin 214's actual, directly quoted finding of at most a 0.05-point
   winter effect and none in summer (r22), far too small for a flat 0.19-point year-round gap, and
   by the Alder Corners cross-station data showing the same hay-fed herds testing normally
   elsewhere (r10).

Subsidiary questions: The 1925 board of arbitration **never examined** the condemned glassware —
only four exhibits were placed before it (test book, receiving book, Brant's weigh book, inspector's
notebook), independently confirmed by the clerk who assembled the list (r23) and the board's own
chairman (r13), with an eyewitness noting the crate sat in the loft directly above two of the three
hearing sessions the entire time (r21). The tester himself privately believed, from as early as
1911, that the cause was "something in the glass" — his son's direct testimony — but he never
recorded this suspicion formally, only a question mark, because he would not put down what he could
not prove (r18); he went on using the same glass and recording what it gave him for another fourteen
years.

## Section G — Summary

For twenty-six years, a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow paid its farmers on milk tests that read
about 5% low, because the glass pipettes it bought in 1897 quietly delivered less than their marked
volume. No one suspected the glass: the valley blamed watered milk, a dishonest tester, or the wet
hay of Hessel Bottom, and a 1925 arbitration board, hearing all three theories, exonerated no one
and dismissed the tester anyway — without ever looking at the actual condemned pipettes, sitting
forgotten in the station's loft since 1923. Only in 1958, when the tester's own granddaughter
climbed that ladder and measured the glass against a proper burette, did the true, purely mechanical
cause emerge. It took eight separate, partial records — ledgers, diaries, a coroner-style hearing
transcript, a supply house's own invoice, competing scientific claims, and family memory — because
no single participant ever held all the pieces (the glass, the ledger, the dates, and the science)
at once; each account is honest and incomplete in a different way.
