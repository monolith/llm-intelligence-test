## Section A — Reconstruction

### 1. The Founding of the Association and the Opening of Ostrey Hollow (1889–1897)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was organized in 1889 by valley farmers
meeting at Larrow Green, which built the main creamery there in 1891 (r22). Its by-laws' Article
Seven established payment by test — every patron paid on the butterfat his milk tested at, not
on its weight — which is, as county agent Orin Falke observed, "the hinge of the whole matter"
(r22): under payment by weight, none of what follows would have cost anyone a dollar. Gideon
Vose organized the Association, served as its first president, and wrote its by-laws (r18, r23).

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles
up-valley, to serve the six farms of Hessel Bottom. It opened 4 May 1896 (r22). Ansel Keddie was
its tester and separator man from the day it opened until his 1925 dismissal — twenty-nine
seasons (r18, r22, r24). For its first season the station had no glassware of its own: buttermaker
Hobart Sill loaned it his own testing glass until the station's own outfit arrived (r14).

That outfit was ordered from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet, a dairy-supply house that had not sold into
Ordell County before 1900 generally but made an exception for this order; the invoice, reproduced
in facsimile by the firm's own historian, is dated 14 April 1897 and totals **$77.93** (six dozen
milk-test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. at 35 cents, twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, a
four-bottle hand tester, and freight) (r20). The outfit went into use at the station on **1 May
1897** — corroborated independently by Sill's recollection (r14), by Ammon's own narrative (r20),
and, most tellingly, by Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary entry of that exact date: "A. says the
station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green. He is pleased about it"
(r24). This date is contradicted by two later, secondary sources (r11, r23), addressed under
Story 8 and Section E.

Documents bearing on this story: r14, r18, r20, r22, r23, r24.

### 2. The Long Complaint: A Quarter-Century of Low Tests (1902–1924)

From soon after opening, Ostrey Hollow's milk tested below the rest of the valley. The first
complaint of record came in 1902 (r22, corroborated by Orra Keddie's diary entry of that same
month: "Talk at the store that our milk up here runs poorer than theirs down the valley," r24).
The complaint recurred at valley crossroads and Association annual meetings for over two decades.

The station's recorded average over the affected years was **3.61%** butterfat, against the
Larrow Green creamery's own average of 3.78% (an ordinary county figure) — both figures given
independently by two narrators (r02, r07) and confirmed by Falke's 1959 report (r22). The
station's true average, once corrected (see Section C), was 3.80%, and the gap between recorded
and true was a flat **0.19 points**, holding steady across every month and season, in winter and
summer alike (r17, r22) — a detail that told against any seasonal/feed explanation from the start.

Every year, the Association's own ledger for the station recorded a "station gain" line — the
difference between butterfat credited to patrons and butterfat that actually reached the main
creamery in the station's cream — annotated routinely "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" by
bookkeeper Wilbur Teague and copied without remark for decades by clerk Bertram Kyle (r23). No one
in the office connected this figure to the patrons' complaint until 1958.

Three theories circulated throughout this period — watering, skimming, and wet hay — treated in
Stories 4–6 below. The complaint persisted, unresolved, until the 1925 arbitration (Story 7) and
was only causally explained in 1958 (Story 8).

Documents bearing on this story: r02, r05, r07, r15, r16, r17, r22, r23, r24.

### 3. The Broken Measure: The 1911 Natural Experiment

On 8 May 1911, the station's own measuring glass broke, and a borrowed measure was used in its
place for nine weeks, until 10 July 1911 — precise dates given in Falke's 1959 report (r22) and
independently corroborated by Orra Keddie's diary, which records "A. home cross about a broken
measure" under the entry for that same May (r24). During those nine weeks, with the same tester,
the same herds, and the same pasture, the station's recorded tests rose markedly; the day the
station resumed its own glass, they fell back to their former level (r22). Ansel Keddie himself
noted the anomaly in the station's test book and set a question mark against it, but — according
to his son Duncan — privately said at the time that he thought "it was something in the glass,"
though he "never dared put it in the book" without proof (r18).

This episode is independently reported by at least three narrators (r03, r09, r22) besides
Duncan's family account (r18) and Orra's diary (r24), and its duration is disputed by only one
source: veterinarian Roland Kepp, writing in 1926, calls it "six weeks" and dismisses it as summer
flush-season noise (r16) — a claim the precisely dated county report (r22) and the diary (r24)
together outweigh.

The episode functions as a natural experiment: it isolates the testing instrument as the variable
while holding tester, herds, land, and season constant, and shows the result (tests) tracking the
instrument rather than any of those other factors. What it does not establish on its own is the
mechanism or magnitude of the fault, or that the borrowed measure was itself perfectly accurate;
that required the 1958 direct measurement (Story 8). See Section D1 for fuller analysis.

Documents bearing on this story: r03, r09, r16, r18, r22, r24.

### 4. The Wet-Hay Theory and the Misread Bulletin

The third and most durable theory held that the sour, sedge-hay diet of Hessel Bottom's six wet
farms thinned the milkfat of the cows pastured there. An Association committee chaired by Nyle
Grigg sampled the Hessel Bottom herds through January–March 1908 only, found the herds and scales
honest, and concluded that wet hay was the cause (r15). Veterinarian Roland Kepp elaborated the
same theory at length before the county Farm Club in 1926, arguing from soil chemistry and citing
"every wet bottom in the county" as corroboration (r16).

Both men cited the State Agricultural College's Bulletin No. 214 — a controlled feeding trial on
bog hay — as scientific proof, and both quoted its finding as a depression of "two tenths" of a
point (0.20) in winter testing (r15, r16). This is a shared error: when county agent Orin Falke
obtained and quoted the bulletin's actual summary sentence verbatim in 1959, it read "not more
than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing... on summer pasture" (r22) — a
figure independently consistent with the same passage as characterized in r07. Five hundredths
(0.05) cannot explain a flat, year-round 0.19-point gap; Falke explicitly retracted his own
provisional concession to the wet-hay theory on learning this ("I withdraw the concession made on
page four," r22).

The theory is further undercut by cross-station evidence: the same four Hessel Bottom farms that
hauled to the Alder Corners cheese factory (over the ridge) in summer tested normally there,
3.78–3.82%, across sixteen summers, 1915–1930 (r10) — showing the deficiency traveled with the
Ostrey Hollow station, not with the hay or the cows.

Documents bearing on this story: r07, r10, r15, r16, r22.


# Answers — Part 2: Section A (Stories 5–8) and Section B

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### 5. Suspicion of Watering and the 1919 Circular

The first of the three theories, chronologically dominant in official Association thinking, was
that the Ostrey Hollow patrons were adding water to their milk. The Association's manager, Selby
Vose (son of founder Gideon Vose, manager from 1911, see B3), circulated a written warning about
added water to the Hessel Bottom patrons in 1919 (r22, r23 — Kyle, the office clerk, typed it
himself and recalled its "no doubt at all... about what was meant"). Accounts differ on how many
families received it: Duncan Keddie recalls nine letters to six farms (a farm sometimes holding
more than one family, r18), while Orra Keddie's contemporaneous 1919 diary entry states Ansel
personally carried the circular to eleven families, refusing to mail it or leave it on the
platform, and returning home at eleven at night "with his hat still on" (r24). This is not fully
resolved (see Section E).

The circular's most lasting effect was personal: Orra's brother Emmet Brant, the largest Hessel
Bottom patron, confronted Ansel Keddie in the Keddie yard two days later, asking why he had
carried a paper accusing men he had tested milk for since the station opened. Keddie replied that
he "would not have another man carry it." Brant did not enter the house, and the two men — who had
shared Sunday dinners for thirty-six years — did not speak again until Brant's death in 1929 (r09,
r18, r24). The state dairy inspector's own lactometer testing, performed at every circuit visit
from 1908 onward, never once found added water at the station (r22) — the theory was current for
years before being formally disproven.

Documents bearing on this story: r09, r17, r18, r22, r23, r24.

### 6. The Station-Gain Ledger and the Skimming Accusation

The second theory — that Keddie himself skimmed cream and falsified low tests to conceal it — was
urged at the 1925 hearing by an unnamed officer of the Association (r22). It is contradicted by
the Association's own routine bookkeeping: the annual "station gain" ledger line (Story 2) shows
that the butterfat actually reaching Larrow Green in the station's cream consistently *exceeded*
what patrons were credited for, not the reverse a thief would produce (r05, r23). Buttermaker
Hobart Sill's own receiving book shows the same "heavy" pattern, even though Sill himself
personally believed the patrons were watering their milk — his own records undercut his own
belief (r14). Dell Rundle's 1939 jubilee pamphlet asserts the valley's "settled opinion" that
Keddie skimmed cream, then undercuts itself with a same-year note added while in press, conceding
that the ledger showed the station's gain arriving heavy, not light (r12).

A dissenting voice: Cleve Oram, recalling his father's decades as the cream hauler between the
station and Larrow Green, reports his father's lifelong belief that less cream came off the
platform than went onto his wagon (r21) — an account based on impression rather than ledger
figures, and outweighed by the documentary "station gain" evidence from three independent
record-keepers (Teague/Kyle, Sill, and Rundle's own retracted concession).

Documents bearing on this story: r05, r12, r14, r21, r23.

### 7. The 1925 Arbitration and Ansel Keddie's Dismissal

The Hessel Bottom patrons petitioned the Association in January 1925. A board of arbitration,
chaired by attorney Alonzo Frick, sat at Ashlin from 17 March to 4 May 1925, with two of its three
sittings held at the station itself so the books and machinery would not have to be moved (r09,
r13, r21, r22, r24). It heard all three theories (watering, skimming, wet hay), found none proven,
and awarded the patrons **$3,800**, "without admission" of any cause — a figure independently
given by both the county report (r22) and the office clerk who processed it (r23; Oram's
secondhand family recollection of "$3,500," r21, is outweighed by these two document-based
accounts). Ansel Keddie was dismissed on 30 April 1925, four days before the award was announced
(r18, r22) — Orra's diary records his return home that day, apron rolled under his arm, asking
what there was for supper: "There was ham. He ate it" (r24).

Only four exhibits were placed before the board: the station's own test book (in Keddie's hand),
the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book (lent for the purpose), and the state
inspector's notebook for the year he had condemned the station's glass — a list independently
reproduced by both the clerk who assembled it (r23) and the board's own chairman (r13). The crate
of condemned glassware itself, sitting in the loft directly over the hearing room for two of the
three sessions, was never brought in or examined (r13, r21, r23; corroborated by an account
summarized earlier in this session, r02). Keddie lived nine more years, working as a carpenter,
and died in 1934 with the accusation unresolved (r18, r21, r24).

Documents bearing on this story: r02, r09, r13, r18, r21, r22, r23, r24.

### 8. The Crate in the Loft: Rediscovery and Vindication (1923, 1958)

In 1923 the state dairy inspector, Merle Strawn, ran the station's milk pipettes against a
calibrated burette on an ordinary circuit visit and found they would not deliver their marked
volume. He condemned the entire lot on **12 June 1923** (r06, corroborated by r22's independent
report) — a date given as 12 July by two other narrators (r08, r12), addressed in Section E. New
glass, ordered the same month from Ammon & Sons, was in service within weeks; the station's
recorded average immediately rose to 3.85% and held there (r22). The condemned crate went into the
loft above the intake room and was forgotten for thirty-five years.

In February 1958, Ansel Keddie's granddaughter Ivy Keddie, a graduate student at the State
Agricultural College at Farland, found the crate under a tarpaulin. On 9 March 1958 she measured
the nine surviving pipettes (a count given by r06, r07, r11, and r22, against a conflicting count
of eleven in r12 and r18 — see Section E) against a calibrated burette. Each was blown to deliver
17.6 c.c. but delivered on average only **16.72 c.c.** — about 5% short — explaining the entire
0.19-point gap arithmetically (Section C). She and Professor Leland Vaught published the finding
as State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958), crediting Rosalie Cudd's Alder
Corners records and the state dairy office's loan of Strawn's old notebooks (r04, r11, r19). The
Ashlin Chronicle (r19) and county agent Falke's report (r22) publicized the finding, clearing
Ansel Keddie's name twenty-four years after his death.

Documents bearing on this story: r04, r06, r07, r08, r11, r18, r19, r20, r21, r22.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1.** Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's **granddaughter**. This is derived directly from Duncan
Keddie's own 1968 family notes: Duncan states plainly that he is Ansel's son and that his daughter
Ivy "makes her my father's granddaughter," adding that he has "already twice seen it printed
otherwise" (r18) — a complaint borne out by two other sources. The Ashlin Chronicle's 1958 report
calls her his "great-niece" (r19), and Orin Falke's 1959 county report repeats the same error in
its body text before appending an explicit correction: "I am informed, and have verified, that
Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine" (r22).
I resolve this in favor of granddaughter on the strength of Duncan's first-person paternity claim,
which Falke's own retraction independently confirms.

**B2.** Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were **second cousins**. Working from Duncan Keddie's explicit
chart (r18): Orra Brant (Ansel Keddie's wife) and Emmet Brant were brother and sister; their
children, Duncan Keddie and Roy Brant, were therefore first cousins; and their children in turn —
Ivy (Duncan's daughter) and Hazel (Roy's daughter) — are second cousins, "the children of first
cousins." Hazel Brant independently gives the identical reasoning and conclusion from the other
branch of the family (r17), which I treat as meaningful (if not fully arithmetically independent)
corroboration since both women worked out and stated the same relationship without apparent
reliance on the other's account. The sources leave nothing substantial open about the intervening
generations — both links (siblings, then first cousins) are stated plainly by family members on
both sides. Cleve Oram's secondhand claim that they were "first cousins once removed" (r21) is an
outsider's error, not evidence of genuine ambiguity in the family record.

**B3.** Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**, who organized the Association, served as its
first president, and wrote its by-laws (r18, r23 — the latter from the office clerk who worked
under Selby for twenty-five years and explicitly rebuts a competing "nephew" rumor he says he has
"heard... twice in the last year"). This matters because it makes Selby Vose an institutional
insider by birth, inheriting the manager's chair from the Association's own founder. Clerk Kyle's
account of Vose as a manager who signed sheets by the bottom total without reading the lines above
(r23) is consistent with a founding-family manager inclined to trust the institution's instruments
and personnel rather than suspect them — a plausible partial explanation for why the Association's
formal response to the long-running complaint (the 1919 circular, the skimming accusation at the
hearing) targeted the patrons and the tester rather than the equipment.

**B4.** The sources do not establish any real connection between the Vose and Keddie families.
Duncan Keddie addresses this directly and denies it: "There was no connection whatever between us
and the Voses," attributing the persistent rumor of a first-cousin tie through the mothers to
valley talk, and noting that his own mother — by his account a woman who "could not pass a
headstone without giving you the connection" — never once included a Vose in her genealogical
accounts (r18). Against this, Cleve Oram repeats unspecified valley hearsay that "the Keddies and
the Voses were cousins of some sort," sourced to what his mother had from her mother (r21). Weighed
against a direct family member's explicit, reasoned denial, I resolve that no relationship is
established — though, as with all negative claims, the documents cannot fully prove the absence of
one, only that the best-positioned witness denies it and the only witness asserting it is relaying
secondhand valley gossip of the very kind being denied.


# Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math (show working)

**C1.**

*(a) Proportion by which tests differed from the truth, from the glassware.* The pipettes were
blown to deliver 17.6 c.c. (r20's invoice; the graduated neck's calibration figure, r19). Measured
against a calibrated burette in 1958, they delivered on average **16.72 c.c.** (r04, r07 — I
reject r11's figure of 16.27 c.c. as an error; it is the same digits transposed, and it is not
consistent with the rest of the arithmetic below, whereas 16.72 is). Shortfall = 17.6 − 16.72 =
0.88 c.c. Proportion short = 0.88 / 17.6 = **0.05, i.e. 5.0%**. Because the test bottle's neck is
calibrated assuming the full 17.6 c.c. charge, a 5%-short charge produces a fat reading that is
also 5% short, in every test, regardless of herd or season.

*(b) True average test, and points short.* The station's recorded average was **3.61%** (r02, r07,
r22 — three independent citations). If recorded = 0.95 × true, then true = 3.61 / 0.95 = **3.80%**.
Points short = 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 points**, which matches the gap figure given independently by
two other sources (r17, r22) and closely matches the corrected post-1923 station average of 3.85%
(r22) and the independent Alder Corners cross-station average of ~3.80% (r10). This convergence of
four independently sourced numbers is why I trust 3.80/0.19 over r11's outlier figures (3.905 /
implied ~0.30 gap).

*(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited.* Total milk received at the station over the affected years
was **40,000,000 lb** (r02, r07, r23 — I reject the competing 44,000,000 lb figure of r05/r12; see
below). Shortfall in pounds = 40,000,000 × (3.80% − 3.61%) = 40,000,000 × 0.0019 = **76,000 lb**.
This matches the total independently reported by Ivy Keddie's own account and by r07's separate
citation of the bulletin — a clean arithmetic confirmation, and the reason I reject r11's 118,000
lb and r13/r21's 67,000 lb as the bulletin's true total (see Section E).

*(d) Money value, and fraction actually received.* Butterfat averaged **$0.25/lb** over the whole
stretch, per the office clerk's own ledger-derived figure (r23; I prefer this over Kepp's rhetorical
"twenty-seven cents," r16, since Kyle had actual ledger access and states he was "set to find" the
figure). Value = 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000** — again matching Ivy Keddie's own figure (r04)
exactly. The 1925 arbitration award was $3,800 (Section A, Story 7). Fraction actually received =
3,800 / 19,000 = **0.20, i.e. exactly 20%**. (Rundle's $19,600, r12, describes the patrons' 1925
petition amount — a different quantity, asked for before the true cause or size was known — not
the corrected 1958 total; I do not treat it as contradicting the $19,000 figure.)

**C2.** For Emmet Brant, 1910 (the largest patron; his weigh-book entry reads "Two hundred thousand
pounds even... paid on a test of three forty-two," r17).

(a) Credited butterfat = 200,000 × 3.42% = **6,840 lb**.

(b) True test = 3.42 / 0.95 = 3.6%. True butterfat = 200,000 × 3.6% = 7,200 lb. Owed and not
credited = 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb**.

(c) Value = 360 × $0.25 = **$90.00** (using the ledger's whole-period average price; no 1910-specific
price is given in any source, so this figure should be read as an estimate using the best available
rate, not a precise 1910 market price).

**C3.**

(a) Credited to patrons in total = 40,000,000 × 3.61% = **1,444,000 lb**.

(b) Actually reaching the main creamery in the station's cream: since the cream physically carries
the true fat content of the milk regardless of what the flawed test recorded, this equals the true
total = 40,000,000 × 3.80% = **1,520,000 lb**.

(c) Difference = 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 lb. As a **percentage of what was credited** (the
ledger's own base — this is how "station gain" was actually recorded): 76,000 / 1,444,000 =
**5.26%**, which matches the ledger's own recurring annotation of "five and a quarter per cent, as
usual" (r23) almost exactly. As a **percentage of what actually arrived** (the true total): 76,000
/ 1,520,000 = exactly **5.00%**, matching the pipette shortfall ratio from C1(a) precisely. Both are
correct; they differ only in denominator, and the near-exact match of the first to the ledger's own
long-standing "5¼%" note is itself independent confirmation of the whole reconstruction.

**C4.**

(a) Total invoice for the station's original testing outfit = **$77.93** (r20's reproduced
facsimile invoice: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93, verified by direct addition). Note
that r20's own prose elsewhere gives "$77.39," and r19 repeats that same wrong figure — I take the
itemized, arithmetically self-consistent invoice as authoritative over the prose paraphrase; see
Section E.

(b) The invoice bought **72 pipettes** (six dozen, marked 17.6 c.c. each), each short by **0.88
c.c.** (17.6 − 16.72, per C1(a)).

**C5.**

(a) The faulty glassware was in continuous use for **26 calendar years**, 1 May 1897 to 12 June
1923 (r19, r20 both independently state "twenty-six years"), interrupted only by the nine-week
borrowed-measure interval in summer 1911. The money/pounds calculation in C1(c)/C3, however, runs
over **25 full delivery years** (40,000,000 lb ÷ 1,600,000 lb/year = 25, both figures given
together in r23). These numbers differ because the glass touched parts of 27 calendar years in
total (1897 and 1923 both being partial seasons — the outfit arrived partway through 1897, and was
condemned partway through 1923) but only 25 of those years were complete delivery seasons under the
faulty glass; the partial first and last years are excluded from the full-year reckoning. This is
my inference from the two independently stated figures (26 years of use, 25 full years of intake
data); no source states the reconciliation explicitly.

(b) The station's first season (1896) received an estimated **1,100,000 lb** of milk, per the
office clerk's recollection of what he was told by men who were there — explicitly not drawn from
any surviving book, since the first season's milk was carried on the main creamery's books, not the
station's own account (r23). This figure should be treated as an approximation, not a ledger fact.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran from 8 May to 10 July 1911 — nine weeks (r22, dated precisely; independently
corroborated by Orra Keddie's diary entry of 8 May 1911, r24). Treated as an experiment: the
variable was the measuring instrument (the station's own glass, versus a borrowed substitute); held
constant were the tester (Ansel Keddie throughout), the herds, the pasture, and the general season
(spring into midsummer, on both sides of the interval). The result was that recorded tests rose
during the nine weeks and fell back to their former level the same day the station's own glass
resumed (r22). What this establishes, that a calculation alone (like the 1958 burette measurement)
cannot: a real-time, causal link from *within* the disputed decades themselves — proof that
whatever was wrong tracked the instrument, not the calendar, the cows, or the tester, at the actual
time the low tests were being recorded and paid on, independent of and decades prior to the 1958
forensic work. What it does **not** establish: the size or mechanism of the fault (no cubic
centimeters or percentage were measured in 1911 — only a relative rise); nor that the borrowed
measure was itself perfectly accurate (only better than the station's own); nor a full exclusion of
any wet-hay contribution in general, though the abruptness of both transitions (up in May, down
again in July) is hard to square with a gradual pasture-driven effect.

**D2.** Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream: (1) The 1911 natural
experiment (r22, r24, D1): if Keddie were personally diverting cream, changing the measuring
instrument he used should not have changed the outcome, yet it did, immediately and reversibly.
(2) The "station gain" ledger evidence (r05, r14, r23; Story 6): the cream that actually reached
Larrow Green consistently contained *more* fat than patrons were credited for, the opposite of what
skimming would produce — and this evidence comes from ordinary annual bookkeeping wholly
independent of the 1958 investigation. (3) The Alder Corners cross-check (r10): the same farms,
hauling the same milk to a different station in summer, tested normally there — showing the
deficiency belonged to the Ostrey Hollow station as a place, not to Keddie as a person, since when
he was not the tester the same milk still would have had to pass through the same hands to reach a
different result, yet did not need to. These are independent because they rest on different bodies
of evidence (a contemporaneous instrument swap; routine ledger bookkeeping; a separate company's
records) collected by different people for different original purposes. Reason (2) is the one that
would still hold even if the glassware had never been measured in 1958, since it is drawn from
routine annual ledger entries that predate and do not depend on Ivy Keddie's forensic work at all.
As for whether the bookkeeper ever questioned these entries: the sources do not identify the
bookkeeper, Wilbur Teague, as the one who made the theft accusation (that came from an unnamed
"officer of the Association" at the hearing, r22, and separately from the manager's watering
accusation, r22/r23) — so the premise that he was answering for "his own" accusation is not
supported. On the substance, though: no. Per clerk Bertram Kyle's direct account, Teague "never
asked anybody anything in forty years," wrote the exculpatory "station gain... as usual" line
every year without remark, and Kyle himself, who copied it, says it "never once crossed my mind to
wonder at it" either (r23).

**D3.** Selby Vose almost certainly knew the station's tests ran chronically low, but there is no
evidence he understood the ledger clue that would have explained why. He became manager in 1911
(r23), inheriting a complaint already a decade old and discussed at annual meetings; he personally
signed and circulated the 1919 warning about watering (r22, r23), which presupposes he knew there
was a persistent shortfall to explain — he simply had the wrong theory. But clerk Kyle's direct,
long-term observation of Vose's working habits — "he read the totals and he never read the lines
above them... take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign" (r23) — indicates
he was not a close reader of the itemized station-gain figures that, in hindsight, refuted his own
accusation. My answer: yes to the fact of the low tests (public and acted upon); no clear evidence
of awareness of the specific corroborating ledger detail that sat, in Kyle's words, "in plain sight
with a name on it" for the whole of his tenure.

## Section E — Contradictions

For each item: claims / narrators / resolution / method.

1. **Pipette mean delivery:** 16.72 c.c. (r04, r07) vs 16.27 c.c. (r11). *Resolved to 16.72* —
   arithmetic consistency (see C1) and majority; 16.27 is likely a digit-transposition error.
2. **True average test:** 3.80% (r04, r07, r10) vs 3.905% (r11). *Resolved to 3.80* — arithmetic
   derivation from the recorded average and independent cross-station match (r10).
3. **Total shortfall in pounds:** 76,000 lb (r04, r07) vs 118,000 lb (r11) vs 67,000 lb (r13, r21).
   *Resolved to 76,000* — the only figure that survives independent arithmetic reconstruction

## Section F — Theory

**F1.** The shortfall was caused entirely by a manufacturing defect in the pipettes the station
bought new in 1897: blown to mark 17.6 c.c., they delivered on average only 16.72 c.c. — about 5%
short. Because the test bottle's graduated neck assumes a full 17.6 c.c. charge, every short charge
produced a proportionally short fat reading, regardless of herd, hay, season, or tester. This
explains the size of the error (0.19 points is 5% of the true 3.80% average, matching the pipette's
own physical shortfall exactly); why that particular size (fixed by the glass's own dimensions, not
by anything biological); why it began when it did (1 May 1897, the day the defective outfit went
into service, replacing accurate borrowed glass; the sole exception, nine weeks in 1911, tracks a
second instrument swap exactly); why it ended when it did (12 June 1923, the day the inspector
condemned and replaced the glass, after which the recorded average immediately rose to 3.85%); and
why nothing at the station or the farms needed to change for either transition, since the cause was
a fixed physical property of specific glassware, not a behavior or a biological condition — the
number moved the instant the instrument did, with no adjustment period.

Three competing explanations, and what defeats each:
1. **Watering**, advanced by manager Selby Vose (1919 circular, r22/r23) — defeated by the state
   inspector's own lactometer testing, which never once found added water at the station from 1908
   onward (r22).
2. **Skimming/theft by the tester**, urged by an unnamed Association officer at the 1925 hearing
   (r22) — defeated by the "station gain" ledger showing cream arriving at Larrow Green heavier
   than credited, the opposite of what theft would produce (r05, r14, r23), and by the 1911 natural
   experiment showing the same tester's figures moved with the instrument, not with him (D1).
3. **Wet hay/soil deficiency**, advanced by Grigg's 1908 committee and elaborated by Kepp in 1926
   (r15, r16) — defeated by Bulletin 214's actual, directly quoted finding of at most a 0.05-point
   winter effect and none in summer (r22), far too small for a flat 0.19-point year-round gap, and
   by the Alder Corners cross-station data showing the same hay-fed herds testing normally
   elsewhere (r10).

Subsidiary questions: The 1925 board of arbitration **never examined** the condemned glassware —
only four exhibits were placed before it (test book, receiving book, Brant's weigh book, inspector's
notebook), independently confirmed by the clerk who assembled the list (r23) and the board's own
chairman (r13), with an eyewitness noting the crate sat in the loft directly above two of the three
hearing sessions the entire time (r21). The tester himself privately believed, from as early as
1911, that the cause was "something in the glass" — his son's direct testimony — but he never
recorded this suspicion formally, only a question mark, because he would not put down what he could
not prove (r18); he went on using the same glass and recording what it gave him for another fourteen
years.

## Section G — Summary

For twenty-six years, a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow paid its farmers on milk tests that read
about 5% low, because the glass pipettes it bought in 1897 quietly delivered less than their marked
volume. No one suspected the glass: the valley blamed watered milk, a dishonest tester, or the wet
hay of Hessel Bottom, and a 1925 arbitration board, hearing all three theories, exonerated no one
and dismissed the tester anyway — without ever looking at the actual condemned pipettes, sitting
forgotten in the station's loft since 1923. Only in 1958, when the tester's own granddaughter
climbed that ladder and measured the glass against a proper burette, did the true, purely mechanical
cause emerge. It took eight separate, partial records — ledgers, diaries, a coroner-style hearing
transcript, a supply house's own invoice, competing scientific claims, and family memory — because
no single participant ever held all the pieces (the glass, the ledger, the dates, and the science)
at once; each account is honest and incomplete in a different way.
