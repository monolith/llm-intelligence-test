# Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

*Method note: all 24 retellings ultimately concern one connected saga (the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association and its Ostrey Hollow skimming station), plus one satellite thread (Alder Corners). I have partitioned that saga into eight stories by phase of the underlying events, since no topic list was given. Each story draws only on facts established or correctable from the retellings, using quoted primary documents (invoices, ledger entries, dated diary/test-book leaves) over secondhand recollection wherever they conflict.*

---

## Story 1 — The Rule That Made the Trouble Possible (1889–1896)

**Who:** Gideon Vose and the farmers of Larrow Valley.
**What:** In 1889 the valley's farmers met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (r02, r12, r22). Gideon Vose was elected first president, serving until 1911 (r02), and personally drafted the by-laws (r02, r08, r12, r23) rather than hiring a lawyer. The main creamery was built at Larrow Green on the creek in 1891 (r02, r12, r22). Article VII of the by-laws established payment by butterfat test rather than by weight, with the decisive clause that "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" — no appeal, no re-test, no averaging (quoted identically in r02, r07, r12, r22). Effie Loomis, the Association's secretary, called this clause "the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business" (r02); the county agent's 1959 report independently calls it "the hinge of the whole matter" (r22).
**When:** 1889 (founding); 1891 (creamery built). The exact year Article VII was adopted **cannot be determined**: r02 says 1892, r12 says 1891, r15 says 1893 — three sources, three years, and the 1889–1892 minute book that would settle it was lost in an office move in 1938 (r02). The county agent's own 1959 report explicitly declines to state a year for this reason (r22).
**Quantities:** No dollar or pound figures attach to this story; its significance is purely structural.
**Documents:** r02 (Effie Loomis's history, quoting Article VII verbatim), r07 Item 1 (printed by-laws pamphlet), r12 (Rundle's jubilee pamphlet), r15 (Grigg's 1919 letter, third date), r22 (county agent's report, declines to resolve the date), r18 (confirms Gideon as founder/author of the by-laws).

This story matters only because of what it made possible later: a payment rule with no cross-check built in, administered at a remote station with a single tester and no second observer, was the precondition for every later dispute — not because the rule was unfair, but because it gave a single station's apparatus total and unreviewable authority over every patron's pay.

## Story 2 — The Station and Its Glass (1895–1898)

**Who:** Ansel Keddie (tester), Ammon & Sons Dairy Supply of Tarnet, Hobart Sill (buttermaker, Larrow Green).
**What:** The Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up-valley from Larrow Green, over the winter of 1895–96, to serve Hessel Bottom's farms (r22, r12). Ansel Keddie went up in 1895 to prepare it (r01, r17) and began testing on 4 May 1896 (quoted first leaf, r03/r04/r07). For its first partial season the station had no glassware of its own; accounts of the borrowed source conflict (see Story 8/Section E). Ammon & Sons of Tarnet supplied a full testing outfit — six dozen (72) milk-test pipettes at 17.6 c.c., bottles, an acid measure, and a four-bottle hand tester — under an invoice dated 14 April 1897, total $77.93 (quoted identically in r04, r07, and the supplier's own firm history, r20). This outfit went into use on 1 May 1897 (r02 implication, r04, r07, r20 — the supplier's own account is explicit on this date; r11's and r23's claims of 1898 are treated below as errors, since three independent quotations of the invoice and the supplier's own ledger agree on 1897). Unknown to anyone, the pipettes were slightly short in the mold — a manufacturing accident later explained by the firm's own historian, Junia Ammon, as a mold reground a hair small and never caught because a systematic error looks exactly like a correct one on every spot-check (r20).
**When:** Station built winter 1895–96; opened 4 May 1896; own glass in service from 1 May 1897.
**Quantities:** Invoice total $77.93 (quoted in three independent sources; r20's own narrative text mis-states it as "$77.39," a digit transposition against its own quoted invoice); 6 dozen (72) pipettes at $0.35 each = $25.20, arithmetic that only balances at six dozen, not the "five dozen" r11 and r21 separately claim.
**Documents:** r03 (Keddie's test book first leaf), r04 (Ivy Keddie's talk), r07 Item 2 (curator's invoice transcription), r14 (Sill's claim he supplied temporary 1896 glass from his own bench), r17 (gives a conflicting 1899 delivery date), r20 (Ammon & Sons' own firm history, the closest thing to a primary account of the manufacturing side), r23 (clerk's account, gives 1898).

## Story 3 — A Generation of Suspicion (1902–1919)

**Who:** The Hessel Bottom patrons (chiefly Emmet Brant), Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee, Selby Vose (manager from 1913).
**What:** From 1902 the Hessel Bottom patrons complained their milk tested lower than the rest of the valley's, at a gap that ran a constant amount year-round rather than varying by season (independently stated by Keddie r03, Roy Brant r09, Rundle r12, and Grigg's own report r15). In January 1908 a committee under Nyle Grigg sampled the Hessel Bottom herds — for three months only (January–March), per the committee's own reprinted report (r15, r07 Item 11), though Grigg's covering 1919 letter misremembers this as "the whole of 1908," an internal self-contradiction within r15. The committee blamed the wet hay of the bottom, exonerating the station's honesty. The complaint was not resolved and, per Grigg himself, was reopened in 1919. That year the manager, Selby Vose, circulated a printed warning to the Ostrey Hollow patrons alleging "water in the cans" (r01, r03, r08, r09, r14, r23 as the typist, r24 as an eyewitness diary). This letter caused a lasting rift: Ansel Keddie's wife Orra was Emmet Brant's sister, and Emmet — the station's largest patron — never spoke to Keddie again after Keddie personally delivered the circular door-to-door (r09, r24, confirmed independently from both the Brant and Keddie sides).
**When:** Complaints from 1902; committee report 1908; circular 1919; family rift 1919 onward, ending only at Emmet's death in 1929 (r24).
**Quantities:** The test gap is variously given as 0.17 (r09), 0.19 (r04, r17, r22), 0.20/"two tenths" (a shared misquotation of Bulletin 214 by r15 and r16, whose actual finding was 0.05), and 0.21 (r08, r16) — resolved in Section C as 0.19, the figure independently derived from documented averages and confirmed by the county agent's separate calculation.
**Documents:** r03, r07 Item 11, r09, r14, r15 (self-contradictory), r16, r24 (diary, dated entries), r23.

## Story 4 — Nine Weeks in 1911

**Who:** Ansel Keddie, the Hessel Bottom patrons, incidentally Merle Strawn (state inspector).
**What:** On 8 May 1911 the station's own measuring glass broke; a borrowed measure was used until 10 July 1911, when the station's own (defective) glass was restored (dated entries in Keddie's own book, quoted in r03/r04; corroborated by Orra Keddie's diary entry of 8 May 1911 and by Roy Brant's account, r09). Nothing else about the operation changed — same tester, same patrons, same herds, same season. For those nine weeks the recorded average rose from its usual 3.61 to 3.80, then fell back to 3.61 the instant the station's own measure returned. Keddie recorded the fact and set an unanswered question mark against it in his book (r03), which stood for 47 years until Ivy Keddie's 1958 analysis explained it. Duncan Keddie separately recalls his father coming home in 1911 saying "it was something in the glass" — a private suspicion Ansel never dared enter in the book and could not act on for another fourteen years (r18).
**When:** 8 May – 10 July 1911, roughly nine weeks.
**Quantities:** Duration is contested — r03, r04, r11, r22, and r24's dated diary entries all support nine weeks; r05 (Teague, writing decades later from memory) and r16 (Kepp, addressing the point secondhand) both say six weeks. The dated, contemporaneous entries outrank undated retrospective memory here.
**Documents:** r03 (Keddie's own dated book entries, quoted), r04 (Ivy Keddie's analysis of the anomaly), r05 (conflicting "six weeks"), r06 (Strawn's own June 1911 circuit note, which — tellingly — found nothing amiss, because everything *looked* normal with a correct measure in temporary use), r09, r11, r16, r22, r24.

This episode is the single most important piece of evidence in the whole saga: a genuine natural experiment, isolating the measuring instrument as the only variable, that pre-dates and does not depend on the 1958 physical discovery of the crate.
