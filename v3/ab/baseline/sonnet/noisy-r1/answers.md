# Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

*Method note: all 24 retellings ultimately concern one connected saga (the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association and its Ostrey Hollow skimming station), plus one satellite thread (Alder Corners). I have partitioned that saga into eight stories by phase of the underlying events, since no topic list was given. Each story draws only on facts established or correctable from the retellings, using quoted primary documents (invoices, ledger entries, dated diary/test-book leaves) over secondhand recollection wherever they conflict.*

---

## Story 1 — The Rule That Made the Trouble Possible (1889–1896)

**Who:** Gideon Vose and the farmers of Larrow Valley.
**What:** In 1889 the valley's farmers met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (r02, r12, r22). Gideon Vose was elected first president, serving until 1911 (r02), and personally drafted the by-laws (r02, r08, r12, r23) rather than hiring a lawyer. The main creamery was built at Larrow Green on the creek in 1891 (r02, r12, r22). Article VII of the by-laws established payment by butterfat test rather than by weight, with the decisive clause that "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" — no appeal, no re-test, no averaging (quoted identically in r02, r07, r12, r22). Effie Loomis, the Association's secretary, called this clause "the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business" (r02); the county agent's 1959 report independently calls it "the hinge of the whole matter" (r22).
**When:** 1889 (founding); 1891 (creamery built). The exact year Article VII was adopted **cannot be determined**: r02 says 1892, r12 says 1891, r15 says 1893 — three sources, three years, and the 1889–1892 minute book that would settle it was lost in an office move in 1938 (r02). The county agent's own 1959 report explicitly declines to state a year for this reason (r22).
**Quantities:** No dollar or pound figures attach to this story; its significance is purely structural.
**Documents:** r02 (Effie Loomis's history, quoting Article VII verbatim), r07 Item 1 (printed by-laws pamphlet), r12 (Rundle's jubilee pamphlet), r15 (Grigg's 1919 letter, third date), r22 (county agent's report, declines to resolve the date), r18 (confirms Gideon as founder/author of the by-laws).

This story matters only because of what it made possible later: a payment rule with no cross-check built in, administered at a remote station with a single tester and no second observer, was the precondition for every later dispute — not because the rule was unfair, but because it gave a single station's apparatus total and unreviewable authority over every patron's pay.

## Story 2 — The Station and Its Glass (1895–1898)

**Who:** Ansel Keddie (tester), Ammon & Sons Dairy Supply of Tarnet, Hobart Sill (buttermaker, Larrow Green).
**What:** The Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up-valley from Larrow Green, over the winter of 1895–96, to serve Hessel Bottom's farms (r22, r12). Ansel Keddie went up in 1895 to prepare it (r01, r17) and began testing on 4 May 1896 (quoted first leaf, r03/r04/r07). For its first partial season the station had no glassware of its own; accounts of the borrowed source conflict (see Story 8/Section E). Ammon & Sons of Tarnet supplied a full testing outfit — six dozen (72) milk-test pipettes at 17.6 c.c., bottles, an acid measure, and a four-bottle hand tester — under an invoice dated 14 April 1897, total $77.93 (quoted identically in r04, r07, and the supplier's own firm history, r20). This outfit went into use on 1 May 1897 (r02 implication, r04, r07, r20 — the supplier's own account is explicit on this date; r11's and r23's claims of 1898 are treated below as errors, since three independent quotations of the invoice and the supplier's own ledger agree on 1897). Unknown to anyone, the pipettes were slightly short in the mold — a manufacturing accident later explained by the firm's own historian, Junia Ammon, as a mold reground a hair small and never caught because a systematic error looks exactly like a correct one on every spot-check (r20).
**When:** Station built winter 1895–96; opened 4 May 1896; own glass in service from 1 May 1897.
**Quantities:** Invoice total $77.93 (quoted in three independent sources; r20's own narrative text mis-states it as "$77.39," a digit transposition against its own quoted invoice); 6 dozen (72) pipettes at $0.35 each = $25.20, arithmetic that only balances at six dozen, not the "five dozen" r11 and r21 separately claim.
**Documents:** r03 (Keddie's test book first leaf), r04 (Ivy Keddie's talk), r07 Item 2 (curator's invoice transcription), r14 (Sill's claim he supplied temporary 1896 glass from his own bench), r17 (gives a conflicting 1899 delivery date), r20 (Ammon & Sons' own firm history, the closest thing to a primary account of the manufacturing side), r23 (clerk's account, gives 1898).

## Story 3 — A Generation of Suspicion (1902–1919)

**Who:** The Hessel Bottom patrons (chiefly Emmet Brant), Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee, Selby Vose (manager from 1913).
**What:** From 1902 the Hessel Bottom patrons complained their milk tested lower than the rest of the valley's, at a gap that ran a constant amount year-round rather than varying by season (independently stated by Keddie r03, Roy Brant r09, Rundle r12, and Grigg's own report r15). In January 1908 a committee under Nyle Grigg sampled the Hessel Bottom herds — for three months only (January–March), per the committee's own reprinted report (r15, r07 Item 11), though Grigg's covering 1919 letter misremembers this as "the whole of 1908," an internal self-contradiction within r15. The committee blamed the wet hay of the bottom, exonerating the station's honesty. The complaint was not resolved and, per Grigg himself, was reopened in 1919. That year the manager, Selby Vose, circulated a printed warning to the Ostrey Hollow patrons alleging "water in the cans" (r01, r03, r08, r09, r14, r23 as the typist, r24 as an eyewitness diary). This letter caused a lasting rift: Ansel Keddie's wife Orra was Emmet Brant's sister, and Emmet — the station's largest patron — never spoke to Keddie again after Keddie personally delivered the circular door-to-door (r09, r24, confirmed independently from both the Brant and Keddie sides).
**When:** Complaints from 1902; committee report 1908; circular 1919; family rift 1919 onward, ending only at Emmet's death in 1929 (r24).
**Quantities:** The test gap is variously given as 0.17 (r09), 0.19 (r04, r17, r22), 0.20/"two tenths" (a shared misquotation of Bulletin 214 by r15 and r16, whose actual finding was 0.05), and 0.21 (r08, r16) — resolved in Section C as 0.19, the figure independently derived from documented averages and confirmed by the county agent's separate calculation.
**Documents:** r03, r07 Item 11, r09, r14, r15 (self-contradictory), r16, r24 (diary, dated entries), r23.

## Story 4 — Nine Weeks in 1911

**Who:** Ansel Keddie, the Hessel Bottom patrons, incidentally Merle Strawn (state inspector).
**What:** On 8 May 1911 the station's own measuring glass broke; a borrowed measure was used until 10 July 1911, when the station's own (defective) glass was restored (dated entries in Keddie's own book, quoted in r03/r04; corroborated by Orra Keddie's diary entry of 8 May 1911 and by Roy Brant's account, r09). Nothing else about the operation changed — same tester, same patrons, same herds, same season. For those nine weeks the recorded average rose from its usual 3.61 to 3.80, then fell back to 3.61 the instant the station's own measure returned. Keddie recorded the fact and set an unanswered question mark against it in his book (r03), which stood for 47 years until Ivy Keddie's 1958 analysis explained it. Duncan Keddie separately recalls his father coming home in 1911 saying "it was something in the glass" — a private suspicion Ansel never dared enter in the book and could not act on for another fourteen years (r18).
**When:** 8 May – 10 July 1911, roughly nine weeks.
**Quantities:** Duration is contested — r03, r04, r11, r22, and r24's dated diary entries all support nine weeks; r05 (Teague, writing decades later from memory) and r16 (Kepp, addressing the point secondhand) both say six weeks. The dated, contemporaneous entries outrank undated retrospective memory here.
**Documents:** r03 (Keddie's own dated book entries, quoted), r04 (Ivy Keddie's analysis of the anomaly), r05 (conflicting "six weeks"), r06 (Strawn's own June 1911 circuit note, which — tellingly — found nothing amiss, because everything *looked* normal with a correct measure in temporary use), r09, r11, r16, r22, r24.

This episode is the single most important piece of evidence in the whole saga: a genuine natural experiment, isolating the measuring instrument as the only variable, that pre-dates and does not depend on the 1958 physical discovery of the crate.


# Answers — Part 2: Section A, Stories 5–8; Section B

## Story 5 — The Petition and the Hearing (1925)

**Who:** The Ostrey Hollow patrons, Alonzo Frick (arbitration chairman), Ansel Keddie, Selby Vose, Wilbur Teague.
**What:** In January 1925 the Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned the Association, claiming chronic underpayment (r02, r12, r22). A three-person board of arbitration, chaired by attorney Alonzo Frick, sat in three sessions: the first at the Association's Ashlin office on 17 March 1925, the second and third at the station itself in its own weigh room, at Frick's specific request, so the board could see the equipment rather than hear it described (r13, corroborated by eyewitness Cleve Oram, r21, and by Orra Keddie's diary, r24). Three theories were argued: watering (Vose), cream theft by the tester (Teague), and wet hay (the 1908 report re-read into the record) — detailed in Story 6/Section F. Keddie gave a nine-sentence statement denying guilt and could not explain his own figures (r03, r07 Item 8). He was dismissed on 30 April 1925, four days before the board's finding (r03, r13, r18, r24). On 4 May 1925 the board found "no cause established" yet awarded the patrons $3,800 "without admission," charged to the station account — the wording and figure quoted verbatim from the minute book itself (r02, r07 Item 7, matching r05, r08, r12, r13, r19, r22, r23). Vira Toland (r01) and Cleve Oram (r21) both instead recall $3,500 — two independent oral family traditions against one quoted primary document; the document prevails (see Section E).
**When:** Petition January 1925; hearing 17 March–4 May 1925; dismissal 30 April 1925; award 4 May 1925.
**Quantities:** Award $3,800 (documentary); patrons' claimed shortfall variously $19,000 (r04's calculation), $19,600 (patrons' own petition figure, r12), or 67,000 lb of butterfat (patrons' own reckoning, per r13) — see Section C for reconciliation.
**Documents:** r01, r02, r03, r05, r07 (Items 3, 7, 8), r08, r12, r13 (fullest account, by the chairman himself), r17, r18, r21, r22, r23, r24.

## Story 6 — Over the Ridge: Alder Corners (1913/14–1930)

**Who:** Four of the six Hessel Bottom farmers, Jerome Cudd and his daughter Rosalie Cudd.
**What:** Jerome Cudd built the Alder Corners Cheese Company in Vessey County, over Redlow Ridge from Hessel Bottom (founding year contested: 1913 per Rosalie's own letter, r10; 1914 per r09 and r12). From 1915, four of the six Hessel Bottom farmers hauled their milk over the ridge to Alder Corners during the summer flush (roughly June–mid-September), returning to Ostrey Hollow the rest of the year, for sixteen seasons through 1930 (r09, r10 — these two independently agree on sixteen seasons, 1915–1930, which should be preferred over Ivy Keddie's own "fourteen seasons" reference in r04). Jerome Cudd tested this milk on his own bench with his own glass, never comparing figures with the station, and found it consistently good — around 3.78–3.82 every season, averaging about 3.80 across the sixteen years (full table quoted in r10). He explicitly dismissed the wet-hay theory as "nonsense... an Ordell theory." In 1958 Rosalie supplied these figures to Ivy Keddie by letter (r10).
**When:** Factory built 1913/1914; dual-hauling 1915–1930.
**Quantities:** Sixteen seasons of test data, 3.79–3.82 per season, averaging ~3.80 — independently matching the corrected "true" Ostrey Hollow figure derived from the pipette shortfall (Story 7), by a wholly separate testing operation using different equipment.
**Documents:** r09 (Roy Brant, from the farmers' side), r10 (Rosalie Cudd's letter and table, the primary source), r12 (Rundle, secondhand, gives a different founding year).

This story functions as an independent control for the whole saga: the same cows, same land, same men, tested elsewhere with different glass, came out normal. It quietly refutes the wet-hay theory before anyone knew to state the refutation in those terms.

## Story 7 — The Crate in the Loft (1958)

**Who:** Ivy Keddie (Ansel's granddaughter), Professor Leland Vaught.
**What:** In February 1958, Ivy Keddie — a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland — climbed into the loft over the station's old intake room and found, under a tarpaulin, the crate of pipettes condemned in 1923 (r04, r17, r18, r19, r21, r22; against the curator's claim in r07 that it was found in the Ashlin office — the loft version is corroborated independently by Strawn's own 1928 circuit note describing that loft as "full of odds and ends... since before the war," by Rundle's pamphlet describing the loft as apparently never cleared, and by three separate eyewitness/family narrators, so it is preferred). Nine pipettes survived intact (r04, r07, r21, r22; against r12's "eleven" and r19's "eleven," both of which appear to trace to the same confused press coverage). On 9 March 1958 Ivy measured them against a calibrated burette at Farland: mean delivery 16.72 c.c. against a stem blown for 17.6 c.c. — short 0.88 c.c., or 95% of the mark (quoted measurement sheet, r04, r07). She assembled five independent record sets (the station's test book, the Brant weigh book lent by Roy Brant, Alder Corners' figures via Rosalie Cudd, Merle Strawn's archived 1923 notebook, and the Association's own ledgers) to build the case. In October 1958 Bulletin No. 471, co-authored with Professor Vaught, was published, concluding that no milk was watered and no cream was stolen — the tests were low by one part in twenty because the pipettes were short by one part in twenty (verbatim conclusion, quoted identically in r04, r07, r11). The bulletin's own "Magnitude" section contains an unresolved internal contradiction (a different measurement, 16.27 c.c., and different downstream arithmetic, 118,000 lb) that its own final conclusion abandons in favor of the standard figures — an error within the bulletin itself, not a genuine alternative finding.
**When:** Found February 1958; measured 9 March 1958; published October 1958. In 1959 Ivy gave a talk, "What the Crate Held," to the county historical society.
**Quantities:** 9 surviving pipettes; mean delivery 16.72 c.c.; shortfall 0.88 c.c. (5%); see Section C for the derived money and pound figures.
**Documents:** r04, r07 (Items 5, 14, 15), r11 (internally inconsistent), r18, r19, r21, r22.

## Story 8 — What the Family Kept (1934–1981)

**Who:** Orra Keddie, Duncan Keddie, Roy and Hazel Brant, Vira Toland, Cleve Oram, Dell Rundle, Bertram Kyle, Orin Falke.
**What:** Ansel Keddie died in 1934, nine years after his dismissal, still under suspicion in the valley, "cleared of a thing nobody will name" (r24). His widow Orra died in 1948, ten years before the exoneration (r18, r24). Over the following decades the story was kept alive and pieced together in fragments: by Orra's own diary (published 1964, r24); by her son Duncan's 1968/1970 family notes, written specifically to correct errors already "printed" about the family — Selby Vose's parentage, Ivy's relationship to Hazel, and the false Vose–Keddie kinship rumor (r18); by the physical passage of five separate documents/objects (station test book, Brant weigh book, Strawn's notebooks, the Alder Corners test book, and the crate itself) through different hands and buildings until they came to rest in three different institutions by 1970 (r18's own careful provenance list); by oral history tapes recorded with Vira Toland (1979, r01) and Hazel Brant (1981, r17) and Cleve Oram (1977, r21), each preserving family memory with its own errors of date and figure; by Dell Rundle's own 1939 pamphlet, which endorsed the cream-theft theory and then explicitly retracted it in a 1959 "note added while in press" upon seeing the Association's ledgers (r12); and by two independent official retrospectives — Bertram Kyle's 1953 clerk's memoir (r23) and Orin Falke's 1959 county report (r22), the latter itself correcting its own initial error about Ivy's relation to Ansel.
**When:** 1934 (Keddie's death) through 1981 (Hazel Brant's tape), with the bulk of documentary consolidation occurring 1958–1970.
**Quantities:** None specific to this story; its significance is in provenance and correction, not measurement.
**Documents:** r01, r09 (postscript), r12 (footnote and retraction), r17, r18, r21, r22 (correction), r23, r24.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**
Granddaughter. Ivy is the daughter of Duncan Keddie (r04, r18, r24), and Duncan is Ansel and Orra Keddie's son (r03, r18, r24). Duncan states this plainly and pointedly in his own 1968 family notes, noting he has "already twice seen it printed otherwise" (r18) — a direct rebuttal aimed at exactly the error that appears in *The Ashlin Chronicle* (r19, "great-niece," sourced from a college press release) and in Orin Falke's initial 1959 draft (r22), which Falke himself explicitly retracts in an appended correction: "Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." Since both erroneous instances trace to the same press-release-era source rather than being independent errors, and since the correction comes both from Ivy's own father and from a self-correcting official report, "granddaughter" is settled beyond reasonable doubt.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**
Second cousins. Ivy's father Duncan and Hazel's father Roy are first cousins, being the children of siblings Orra Keddie (née Brant) and Emmet Brant (r09, r18); children of first cousins are second cousins. This is independently affirmed by Duncan himself (r18, the most explicit and deliberate source, written specifically "so that they can be copied"), by Roy Brant (r09), by Dell Rundle's 1959 corrective footnote (r12), and by Hazel Brant herself (r17), who states flatly, "People are forever telling me she is my niece or my cousin once removed or some such, and she is not. Second cousins." Against this stand two secondhand, non-family oral accounts — Vira Toland's "first cousin once removed" (r01) and Cleve Oram's identical phrase (r21), which may simply be the same village imprecision repeated rather than two independent errors. Four family/documentary sources beat two oral ones. What the sources leave open: whether Orra and Emmet were full or half siblings (r09 and r18 say full; r17, Hazel herself, says half, "by his father's second wife") is unresolved — but it does not change the cousin arithmetic, since siblingship of either kind supports the same first-cousin/second-cousin chain.

**B3. Selby Vose's father, and why it matters.**
Gideon Vose, the Association's founding president (1889–1911) and sole author of its by-laws, including Article VII (r02, r08, r18, r23). Selby himself says so directly in his 1926 address ("my father was your first president," r08); Effie Loomis, the Association's own secretary, independently calls him "son of the late president" (r02); Duncan Keddie and clerk Bertram Kyle both separately and explicitly reject the rival "nephew" claim as error/"nonsense" (r18, r23). Against this stand two secondhand accounts, Vira Toland's (r01) and Dell Rundle's (r12). Four independent sources, including Selby's own first-person testimony and two Association insiders, outweigh two oral traditions: son is settled. It matters because Article VII — "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern," with no appeal — was Gideon's own rule, and it was that very rule, inherited and administered without complaint by his son as manager, that allowed a silent 5% instrument error to stand unquestioned and unappealable for 26 years. Selby's fierce, personal defense of the Association's integrity (the 1919 circular, the 1926 defense of it, his rejection of the "faulty apparatus" theory even after Strawn's 1923 condemnation) reads differently once his father's authorship of the underlying rule is understood: he was, in effect, defending his father's system.

**B4. The Vose–Keddie family relationship.**
None, on the best evidence. Duncan Keddie (Orra's own son) states this as a deliberate correction: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses... My mother would have told me... and she never once put a Vose in it" (r18). Against this, a persistent village rumor holds that Orra and "old Mrs. Vose" were first cousins (Vira Toland, r01) or that "the Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort" (Cleve Oram, r21) — both secondhand, oral, and vague ("some sort"), and neither from a family member. Given Duncan's direct, motivated, and detailed denial against two imprecise oral traditions, the connection is most likely village legend rather than fact, though the sources cannot fully prove a negative — a small residual uncertainty remains.


# Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math

**C1.**
(a) The tests differed from the truth by a constant proportion of **5% (one part in twenty)**, low. This comes from the glassware measurement: the pipettes were blown to deliver 17.6 c.c. but, measured against a calibrated burette in 1958, delivered a mean of only 16.72 c.c. — 0.88 c.c. short. 0.88 / 17.6 = 0.05 exactly. Because a test-bottle neck is ruled to read percent-fat for one specific charge volume, a charge short by 5% produces a fat reading short by 5% of whatever the true value is (proportional, not fixed) — this is stated identically in r04, r07, and r11's own final conclusion (Section VII), which is why it is used here in preference to the same bulletin's internally inconsistent "Magnitude" section (16.27 c.c., ratio 0.9244), which even Vaught admits "is not readily reconciled" with the station's own recorded figures.

(b) True average = **3.80**; recorded average = 3.61; shortfall = **0.19 points**. Check: 3.61 / 0.95 = 3.80 exactly. This is corroborated four ways: Keddie's own averages leaf (r03/r04/r07), Strawn's 1924 field note (r06), the independent Alder Corners cross-check of the same farmers' milk (~3.80 across sixteen seasons, r10), and the county agent's own independent 1959 restatement of "nineteen hundredths" (r22). It is also the figure Hazel Brant recites from family memory (r17). Rival figures — Teague's memory of 3.16 (r05, r13), Vose's claimed 3.90 (r08), Sill's estimate of 3.85 (r14), Falke's later "3.85" (r22) — are all single, non-quoted-document recollections and are set aside in favor of the documentary/cross-checked 3.80/3.61 pair.

(c) **76,000 lb** of butterfat. Working: over 25 full delivery years (1898–1922) the station received 40,000,000 lb of milk (r02, r04, r07's own ledger citation — preferred over Teague's memoir figure of 44,000,000 lb, since the curator is quoting the ledgers directly rather than recalling them decades later). 40,000,000 × 0.0019 (the 0.19-point gap, as a fraction) = 76,000 lb. This matches Bulletin 471's own final conclusion (quoted identically in r04, r07, r11 despite r11's internal "118,000 lb" error) and the curator's independent ledger tally (r07 Item 6).

(d) At the period's average price of $0.25/lb (r02, r04), 76,000 lb = **$19,000**. The patrons actually received $3,800 from the 1925 award — **one-fifth (1/5)** of what they were owed (3,800/19,000 = 0.2), a fraction Ivy Keddie herself notes explicitly in r04 ("a fifth of it"). Note the patrons' own 1925 petition claimed a somewhat different figure ($19,600, r12, or 67,000 lb per r13) — these reflect the patrons' contemporaneous estimate before the mechanism was understood, not the corrected 1958 figure, and are not the same calculation.

**C2. Largest patron, 1910 (Emmet Brant).**
(a) Credited: 200,000 lb delivered, paid on a recorded test of 3.42% (dated leaf, quoted identically in r07 Item 4, r09, and recalled by r17) → 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of butterfat credited.
(b) Owed but not credited: true test = 3.42 / 0.95 = 3.60%; true fat = 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb; difference = 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb** not credited (equivalently, 200,000 × 0.0018, the 0.18-point shortfall r04 itself cites for exactly this test value as a worked example).
(c) At the 1910 butterfat price of $0.24/lb specifically (r09), 360 × 0.24 = **$86.40** for that year alone.

**C3. Station-wide, full delivery years.**
(a) Credited to patrons in total: 40,000,000 lb × 3.61% = **1,444,000 lb**.
(b) Actually received at Larrow Green in the station's cream: 40,000,000 lb × 3.80% = **1,520,000 lb** (equivalently 1,444,000 + 76,000).
(c) The difference (76,000 lb) as a percentage depends on the denominator, and both are "correct" relative to their own base:
 - As a percentage of the true/delivered fat (1,520,000): 76,000/1,520,000 = **5.0%** — this equals the pipette's own shortfall ratio.
 - As a percentage of the credited fat (1,444,000), which is how the Association's own ledgers computed and recorded it every year ("station gain... five and a quarter per cent, as usual" — quoted identically in r05, r07 Item 6, r12, r23): 76,000/1,444,000 = **5.26%** (≈5.25%).
 Neither is an error; they are the same absolute difference expressed against two different bases, and the ledger convention naturally used the higher (5.25%) figure because it is reckoned against the number already entered in the books (r04's own explanation of "the two percentages").

**C4.**
(a) Invoice total: **$77.93** (quoted identically in r04, r07, and the supplier's own history r20 — r20's narrative text mis-states this as "$77.39," a digit transposition against its own quoted document, which is preferred here).
(b) **Six dozen (72) pipettes**, each short **0.88 c.c.** (17.6 c.c. marked, 16.72 c.c. delivered). Six dozen is confirmed by the invoice arithmetic itself: 72 pipettes × $0.35 = $25.20, exactly the line total given; five dozen (60 × $0.35 = $21.00) does not match the quoted line total, so r11's and r21's "five dozen" are arithmetic errors, not a genuine alternative count.

**C5.**
(a) The faulty glassware was in service for **just over 26 years** — from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 (the condemnation date fixed by Merle Strawn's own contemporaneous 1923 circuit notebook, r06, and its photostat, r07 Item 9; this outranks the later, non-contemporaneous "12 July 1923" given by Vose, r08, and Rundle, r12). The money/pound calculation instead runs over **25 full delivery years** (1898–1922) because those are the only complete calendar years lying entirely within the affected period for which the ledgers give whole-year totals; the partial first year (1897, only 8 months under the new glass) and the partial final year (1923, only about 5½ months before condemnation) are excluded from the "40,000,000 lb / 25 years" figure, so the two numbers — "how long the glass was bad" and "how many years the arithmetic covers" — are different by design, not by error.
(b) **Cannot be determined from the sources.** The curator's own accession notes state plainly that no figure survives for the station's first partial season (May–December 1896): "inquirers wanting a figure for 1896 must be told there is none" (r07). Two secondhand recollections offer numbers — Hobart Sill's "about nine hundred thousand pounds" (r14) and Bertram Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" (r23) — but both are decades-later lay estimates ("which is what I understood at the time from men who were there," "it is not in any book I ever ruled"), they disagree with each other by roughly 20%, and neither claims documentary support. The archival record itself is explicit that the figure does not exist; that outranks two conflicting guesses.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 experiment.**
When/how long: 8 May – 10 July 1911, about nine weeks (established from Keddie's own dated book entries, corroborated by Orra Keddie's diary entry of the same date and by Roy Brant's independent account; preferred over Teague's and Kepp's later "six weeks," which come from undated retrospective memory). Variable: only the measuring instrument (pipette) changed — the station's own measure broke and a borrowed one was substituted. Held constant: the same tester, the same patrons and herds, the same season/pasture, the same scale, acid, and procedure, the same level of (non-)supervision. Result: the recorded average rose from its usual 3.61 to 3.80 for exactly the nine weeks, then fell back to 3.61 the same day the original measure returned. What this establishes that arithmetic alone would not: it is a genuine natural experiment demonstrating causation, not mere correlation — it directly rules out any theory requiring a change in the men, the cows, or the land (since none of those changed), and it does so independently of and years before the 1958 physical measurement of the crate. What it does **not** establish: it does not by itself identify the mechanism (that the error was proportional rather than fixed), does not quantify the shortfall precisely, and does not prove the *specific* pipettes later found in the crate were the same ones at fault — those required the 1958 measurement to confirm.

**D2. Why Keddie cannot have been stealing cream.**
Three independent reasons: (1) The Brant family's own private weigh book, kept independently of the Association, shows the farm scale and the station scale always agreed, ruling out any manipulation at intake (r09). (2) The Association's own "station gain" ledger line — an internal bookkeeping device, unrelated to family records — shows that *more* butterfat consistently arrived at Larrow Green in the station's cream than had been credited to patrons, every single year (r05, corroborated independently by Hobart Sill's decades-earlier lay observation that the cream always "ran heavy," r14, and by Rundle's own 1959 retraction of his cream-theft claim once he saw this same ledger logic, r12). Theft would make the cream *lighter* leaving the station, not heavier arriving downstream — the opposite of what the books show. (3) The 1911 natural experiment (D1): Keddie's opportunity and behavior were unchanged throughout, yet the readings moved with the instrument alone. These three are independent because they come from three different, unconnected record-keepers (a farm family, the Association's bookkeeper, and the tester's own book) reasoning from entirely different kinds of evidence (weight comparison, ledger accounting, and a substitution experiment). Reason (2) — the ledger logic — would still hold even if the glassware had never been measured in 1958, since Rundle himself worked it out from the ledgers alone, without knowing about the pipette defect. Did the bookkeeper (Teague) ever question the ledger entries that refute his own accusation? **No.** By his own account he found the consistent 5.25% "station gain" figure so regular that it became "furniture" and he stopped scrutinizing it (r05); yet he was also the one who put the cream-theft theory to the 1925 board (r13), and clerk Bertram Kyle later observed that no one in that room ever "laid the second [book] beside the fourth and looked" (r23).

**D3. Did the manager know the tests ran low before the glassware was condemned?**
**Yes.** The chronically low test average was public, valley-wide knowledge argued at annual meetings for a quarter century (r02, r08, r22) — Vose could not plausibly have been unaware of the bare fact. More specifically: he personally signed and defended the 1919 circular accusing patrons of watering, which itself presupposes he knew the tests were low and needed explaining (r08, r23); he reviewed and signed the station's annual "station gain" sheet every year of his tenure (r05); and when bookkeeper Teague raised the underlying issue with him directly in 1916, Vose told him to "let it alone" (r05) — direct evidence of knowledge, not ignorance. Clerk Bertram Kyle's independent account that Vose "read the totals and never read the lines above them" (r23) suggests he knew the outcome (low tests) but did not — or chose not to — engage with the ledger detail that would have pointed to the true, non-sinister cause. So: he knew the fact of the low tests for years; what he did not know, or did not act on, was the mechanism.

## Section F — Theory

**The single causal theory:** From 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923, the Ostrey Hollow station used a set of milk-test pipettes that a manufacturing accident at Ammon & Sons had left short by 5% of their marked capacity (16.72 c.c. delivered against a 17.6 c.c. mark). Because a test-bottle neck is ruled to read percent-fat for one exact charge volume, every charge drawn with these pipettes produced a fat reading exactly 5% below the truth, regardless of what the milk actually contained — which is why richer herds lost more in absolute points than poorer ones, and why the error was invisible from inside the plant (the pipette was itself the standard against which everything else was checked). The size of the error (5%, not some other fraction) is fixed entirely by how short that particular mold ran, an accident of glassblowing with no connection to any local condition (r20's own account of a reground mold). It began the instant the flawed glass replaced the station's borrowed/temporary glass (1 May 1897) and ended the instant the state inspector's burette caught it and new glass replaced it (12 June 1923) — bracketed exactly by two documentary dates. Nothing on the farms or at the station needed to change for the error to appear or vanish, because it was a property of a physical object, not of behavior; this is proven directly by the nine-week natural experiment of 1911 (Section D1), in which substituting a correct borrowed measure — with everything else held constant — made the "problem" disappear for exactly as long as the substitution lasted.

**Three competing explanations, and what defeats each:**
1. **Watering**, advanced by manager Selby Vose (1919 circular; 1926 address). Defeated by Merle Strawn's inspection notebooks: he tested with a lactometer at every visit from 1908 on and never once found added water (r06) — a direct, repeated, contemporaneous negative from the one disinterested outside observer with the instrument to check.
2. **Cream theft by the tester**, advanced by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague at the 1925 hearing, and long believed as valley consensus (r01, r12's original text). Defeated by the Association's own "station gain" ledger entries, which show *more* fat arriving at the creamery than was ever credited to patrons — the opposite signature of theft — a point Dell Rundle himself used to retract his own published accusation (r12), and by the 1911 experiment, in which the same tester's readings moved with the instrument alone.
3. **Wet hay / mineral deficiency of Hessel Bottom**, advanced by the 1908 committee (Grigg) and reinforced by Dr. Kepp's 1926 paper. Defeated by the college's own Bulletin No. 214 (1911), whose actual finding — a winter-only depression of at most 0.05 points, nothing measurable in summer — is roughly a quarter of the observed, constant, year-round 0.19-point gap (both Grigg and Kepp separately misquote this bulletin as finding 0.20, an error, not a genuine second reading); and by the Alder Corners cross-check (Story 6), showing the same Hessel Bottom cows and land testing normally elsewhere.

**Subsidiary questions:**
- Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware? **No.** Effie Loomis's own notes record that the crate condemned in June 1923 was never mentioned in any of the three 1925 sessions by anyone (r02); Cleve Oram independently recalls the crate sitting in the loft directly above the room where two of the three sessions were held, unmentioned the entire time (r21).
- What did the tester himself believe the cause was? Publicly, nothing — Keddie denied guilt and offered no theory (r03). Privately, per arbitration chairman Alonzo Frick's own recollection, Keddie confided in the Ashlin corridor that he had always suspected the *patrons* of watering their milk, contradicting the popular later story that "he suspected nobody" (r13). This sits in tension with Duncan Keddie's separate account that his father came home in 1911 saying "it was something in the glass" but never dared record it (r18) — the sources suggest Keddie's private suspicion shifted or was never fully settled in his own mind, torn between distrusting his equipment and not wanting to accuse either his employer's apparatus or his own neighbors.

## Section G — Summary

A valley creamery paid its farmers strictly on the butterfat their milk tested at, station by station, with no appeal. For 26 years one remote station's tests ran mysteriously low, and everyone blamed people: the patrons were accused of watering their milk, the tester was accused of stealing cream, and a committee blamed the wet hay of a low meadow. Each theory poisoned a friendship or a reputation — a manager's circular, a bookkeeper's insinuation, a tester dismissed in disgrace, two brothers-in-law who stopped speaking. The true cause was a crate of pipettes, bought new in 1897, blown very slightly short by an unnoticed accident at the glass factory — a fixed 5% error invisible from inside the plant and undetected until a state inspector's burette caught it in 1923, and not explained until the tester's own granddaughter found the discarded crate in a loft in 1958 and measured it herself. It took eight separate stories — statutes, invoices, ledgers, a broken measure, family diaries, a rival cheese factory's records, and one woman's climb up a ladder — because no single record-keeper had ever assembled them together.
