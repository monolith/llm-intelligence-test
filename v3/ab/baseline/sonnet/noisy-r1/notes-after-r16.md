# Exhaustive notes on Retellings 1–16 (Ostrey Hollow / Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association cycle)

## Overview / core subject
All sixteen retellings concern the Ostrey Hollow skimming station, seven miles up the Larrow Valley from the Larrow Green creamery, operated by the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (Ordell County; nearby towns Ashlin, Larrow Green; the county seat area includes Farland, home of a State Agricultural College). The central mystery: for decades the Ostrey Hollow station's butterfat tests ran chronically low compared to neighboring stations, provoking accusations of watered milk (by the Association) and suspected cream-skimming by the tester (by some patrons/bookkeeper), a 1925 arbitration that found "no cause established" yet paid $3,800, and a final 1958/1959 scientific exoneration showing the true cause was defective (short) pipettes bought in 1897, which read low by a constant proportion (5%) rather than a fixed amount. Multiple retellings directly contradict each other on facts, dates, and figures — these conflicts are cataloged below (original catalog from r01–r08, PLUS new conflicts surfaced by r09–r16).

---

## Retelling 01 — Vira Toland, b. 1912, Ostrey Hollow patron's daughter; tape recorded 1979 at Ostrey Hollow by a young man from the county (asked 3 questions in an hour); transcribed with her verbal doublings-back left in.
- Vira born 1912 at top of Ostrey Hollow, house built by her father's father; lived there her whole life except 2 years at Ashlin after first marriage (which she says shouldn't count).
- Her father hauled milk to the station; 9 families hauled to that station, in and out of each other's kitchens year-round.
- Station: at the very top of the Hollow where the road bends toward the ridge; low, whitewashed building, cold as a spring house even in August; smelled of milk, steam, hot iron; engine audible before rounding the last turn.
- Ansel Keddie ran the station. Went up in 1895 (year before it opened) to prepare it; tested first summer (1896); still there when Vira was old enough to ride the wagon.
- Keddie's book: ruled by his own straightedge, uniform line height, figures "like print"; wrote standing at a slope desk by the window. Tested ~1,000 cans a week, "never a blot." Vira's father said if the world kept accounts like Keddie there'd be no lawyers in Ashlin (joking — "there are still lawyers in Ashlin").
- 1919 (Vira age 7): a printed letter came from the Association, over the manager's signature, sent to the 9 Hessel Bottom families, stating there was "water in the cans" coming from Ostrey Hollow. Her father read it at the table with his hat on (unusual); read it twice; put it in the desk drawer in the front room; still there when he died; Vira believes her brother has it yet. Some patrons kept their letters till they died; one woman kept hers pinned inside her trunk lid.
- The manager who signed the letter: Selby Vose, of Larrow Green — described by Vira as "old Gideon Vose's nephew" (CONFLICTS with r02 and r08, which state Selby was Gideon's SON; r12 also says "nephew" — see updated conflict list below). Gideon Vose founded/started the Association.
- Orra Keddie (Ansel's wife) and "old Mrs. Vose" (Gideon's wife, per Vira) were first cousins — an unwritten but widely known fact; meant the accusing letter felt like "somebody's cousin saying it."
- Orra Keddie was Emmet Brant's sister. Emmet Brant: biggest man on Hessel Bottom, most cows, most hay ground, hauled the most milk to the station, and thus lost the most from the "water in the cans" letter.
- Family rupture: before 1919 the Keddie and Brant households shared Sunday dinners (two houses eating in one kitchen, men at the fence after); after 1919 they never spoke again — not at store, not at a funeral; Orra would walk out of her way to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane; both families lived within sight of each other's chimney smoke for years without speaking; both now (1979) dead.
- After the letter, tests "went up" suddenly one summer and stayed up; no official explanation given by station, Association, or anyone. Vira's father noticed his milk tested better without changing anything.
- A hearing was held at Ashlin the following winter/spring: lawyers, Association officers, and the valley's men attended in wagons; went on and on.
- Outcome per Vira: Ansel Keddie was "put out" (fired) — an old man, after all those years. Patrons were paid **$3,500** total, split nine ways ("did not buy a hay rake" for her father's share). [CONFLICTS with r02/r05/r07/r08/r12, which give the arbitration award as $3,800.]
- Vira's own explanation: "He was taking the cream. They caught him at it and they put him out, and that is why the numbers went right the summer after." She says this is not because Keddie was wicked, but because he stood alone in the room with the separator running, unsupervised. Her father wouldn't say it outright — called Keddie "an honest man who had got into something," which Vira says amounts to the same thing.
- Keddie died in the 1930s, still under the cloud/suspicion. Orra outlived him a good while, carrying the same stigma.
- In 1958, a young woman from a college "away east" came to Ostrey Hollow asking about all this; she poked around the old (by then shut) station building — used for storing fence wire and salt. She sat where the interviewer/Vira sat. She was "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed" — related to the family on both sides. Vira told her about the letter in the drawer; doesn't know what she made of it. [Almost certainly Ivy Keddie's 1958 research visit — note per r09/r12, Ivy Keddie is actually Hazel Brant's SECOND COUSIN, not "first cousin once removed" — a further precision/possible discrepancy, though loosely reconcilable given imprecise village genealogy.]
- Vira closes: it rained the day the letter came; she's never separated the memory of the rain from the letter in 60 years.

## Retelling 02 — Effie Loomis, Association secretary 1901–1940, "Notes toward a history of the Association," written 1941 (the year after she gave up the minute book).
- Written in numbered sections, distinguishing what comes from the minutes vs. her own recollection.
- **§1 Organizing**: 1889 — farmers of Larrow Valley met at Larrow Green, organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose elected first president; held office until 1911 (22 years), longest of any president. Effie was 14 in 1889; became secretary in 1901; for 1889–1901 she only knows the Association from its papers. The 1889–1892 minute book was LOST when the office was moved in 1938 (carried out with the rest, "did not arrive"; searched everywhere in the building; never found).
- **§2 Creamery**: Main creamery built at Larrow Green in 1891 on Larrow Creek (same building still standing as of 1941). Before it, no common test/price — "every man's arithmetic was his own." She calls it "a plain remedy for a plain grievance."
- **§3 By-laws / Article VII**: Gideon Vose drafted the by-laws himself (not a lawyer). Article VII adopted at the annual meeting of 1892 (year after creamery opened) [CONFLICTS: r12 says Article VII dates from 1891, "the year the churn was set up"; r15 (Grigg) says "payment by test came in with the 1893 meeting" — THREE different years now given for Article VII/payment-by-test: 1891, 1892, 1893]. Full text (quoted):
  > "Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."
  - Effie stresses the last clause — "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" — no appeal, no re-test elsewhere, no average. Calls this clause "the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business." Written by Gideon Vose, who died in 1917 (so couldn't be asked about it later).
- **§4 Officers in her time**: Effie secretary 1901–1940 (kept minute book by hand). Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper, came in 1905 (kept the ledgers). Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green (kept churn book and cream receiving book). Selby Vose, "son of the late president" [Gideon], made manager in 1913, served until 1938. Effie notes: none of the four of them ever personally went up to Ostrey Hollow to watch the work on a working morning — "I include myself first in that."
- **§5 Station**: Skimming station, 7 miles up valley at head of the Hollow; took milk from farms up there, separated it, sent cream down to Larrow Green, returned skim to patrons. Best milk came off Hessel Bottom (wet meadow below the hamlet). 7 farms had rights on Hessel Bottom [CONFLICTS with r09/r12/r15, all of which say SIX farms]. Emmet Brant's farm was the largest of the seven and the station's largest patron every year on record.
- **§6 Petition of 1925 and arbitration**: January 1925 — Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned the Association, claiming their milk had never tested as well as their neighbors' and they'd been underpaid accordingly for a long time; asked to be made whole. Board of arbitration sat at Ashlin under Alonzo Frick, attorney, from **17 March to 4 May 1925** [r13/Frick's own recollection clarifies: only the FIRST session was at Ashlin (17 March); the second and third sessions were held at Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's own weigh room, at Frick's own request]. Three explanations were argued before the board; none accepted; not detailed here (in the minutes, in the safe). Award (quoted, recorded by Effie herself on the last day):
  > "The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account."
  - Effie notes the irony/tension of "finds no cause established" immediately followed by an $3,800 award — "it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once."
- **§7 Quantities (from Teague's ledgers)**: Over the 25 years the patrons' claim covered, the station received **40,000,000 lb of milk** total, averaging **1,600,000 lb/year**. Average price paid for butterfat over the period: **25 cents/lb**. Effie says these are Teague's figures from the annual sheets; she added the columns twice but "does not vouch for the cows."
- **§8 What the record lacks**: Two gaps: (1) the lost 1889–1892 minute book (see above); (2) "the crate" — the crate of measures (pipettes) condemned by the state inspector in **June 1923**, replaced with new ones — was NEVER mentioned at all in the 1925 hearing, in any of the 3 sessions, by patrons, officers, or the board. The award's phrase "the books and appliances of the station" was "a form of words... not a list of anything." Effie's closing remark: "the interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying."

## Retelling 03 — Ansel Keddie, tester at Ostrey Hollow; (I) his 9-sentence statement to the board of arbitration, Ashlin, 17 March 1925 (taken down verbatim); (II) an unsent letter written at Ostrey Hollow later that spring, found among his papers after his death.
- **I. Statement (quoted in full)**: He kept the test book in his own hand since the station opened; every figure is his; errors are crossed out and initialed, never overwritten. Tested in the same order/same hour every working day/season. Neither patrons nor officers ever came and watched him work. "I am asked to account for figures, and the figures are what I read and what I wrote and nothing besides." Tested "thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons." Cannot explain the figures. Denies ever taking a pound of cream from the station, and says no one in the room privately believes he did. [CONTRAST: r13/Frick reveals privately, in the corridor at Ashlin, Keddie told him he had always thought the PATRONS were at fault (i.e., watering the milk) but would not say so in the room — Keddie suspected somebody, contrary to the popular later claim (echoed even in r13 itself) that "he suspected nobody."]
- **II. Unsent letter** — his own detailed description of the testing procedure:
  - Day began at 5:30 am; wagons came up in the dark most of the year; intake room open, lamps lit, scale swinging free before first wagon arrived.
  - Every can weighed and weight recorded before milk went anywhere; scale tested true by the state's man on rounds; in 29 seasons no patron ever disputed a weight and was right.
  - Sample taken from each patron's milk as weighed, placed in his own numbered jar, made up over 10 days before testing.
  - On testing morning: jar warmed in warm water until blood-warm throughout, poured vessel to vessel repeatedly to mix cream through.
  - Charge: milk drawn to the mark on the measure's stem, tip wiped, blown into the bottle bearing the patron's number; then acid poured down the side, bottle turned until milk darkened; four bottles in the hand tester, weighted against each other, crank turned at taught speed, never hurried.
  - Fat read off in percent against the rulings using a pair of dividers, then written down.
  - "The glass I began on in ninety-six was borrowed off the Ashlin creamery, and we used it until our own outfit came up the valley in a crate the spring following [i.e., spring 1897]." [DIRECT CONFLICT with r14 (Hobart Sill), who claims HE personally supplied the station's original 1896 glassware out of his OWN bench at Larrow Green creamery — not glass "borrowed off the Ashlin creamery." Sill says the Association's own outfit (ordered but not arrived) came the following spring, and his own glass (with a chipped piece) was sent back to him then — same timeline, different source of the 1896 glass (Larrow Green/Sill vs. Ashlin creamery).]
  - Book kept in 5 columns: date, patron, pounds, test, fat. Tested 29 seasons, **1896 through 1924** — did NOT test in 1925 (put out before that season).
  - First leaf of the book (quoted): "Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie." [NOTE: this first-leaf quotation actually says the glass was borrowed OF LARROW GREEN, which is INTERNALLY INCONSISTENT with the letter text quoted just above it in the same retelling, which says the glass was "borrowed off the Ashlin creamery" — an internal contradiction within r03 itself, and one that happens to align the book's own first leaf with Sill's account (r14) rather than Keddie's own letter text.]
  - Averages leaf, made 1922 (quoted): "Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80."
  - The 1911 anomaly (quoted entries): "May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." / "July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." Measure broke 8 May 1911; borrowed measure used until 10 July 1911 (roughly 9 weeks — matches his summary leaf's "nine weeks"); tests read 3.80 during that period, reverting to ~3.61 the same day the station's own measure returned. He calls this "the strangest thing in my book," never explained; his own question mark stood unanswered for 14 years.
  - He stresses the discrepancy was constant regardless of season (July same as February, wet year same as dry) — i.e., not seasonal/feed-related. In 1924, station average was 3.80 (his own final season's recorded figure) [matches r06/Strawn's 1924 note; CONFLICTS with r08/Vose's claimed 3.90 for 1924].
  - History of the grievance (his account): complaints "began in 1902" that Ostrey Hollow milk didn't test with the rest [matches r09, r12, r15]. In 1908 a committee came up, sampled herds through the winter, and reported the wet hay of the Bottom thinned the cream, blaming the land. In 1919 a circular went from the Association to the 9 families saying there was water in their cans. He states he carries no charge against whoever signed that (Selby Vose, per other retellings), but insists he knows what came into that station every morning and that "nine kitchens in this Hollow have had that letter in a drawer ever since" [echoes/confirms Vira Toland's account].
  - His wife Orra is Emmet Brant's sister; Emmet was his largest patron; Keddie personally weighed and tested every pound of Emmet's milk. He and Emmet have not spoken since the circular (1919) — confirms Vira's account of the family rift, from Keddie's own side [also independently confirmed by Roy Brant, r09].
  - He was "put out on the thirtieth of April" [1925] [confirmed by r13/Frick: dismissed 30 April, four days before the board's award of 4 May 1925]. States plainly the contradiction: "They found no cause established and they put me out on the last day of the month, and both of those things are true at once." Ends: "I have tested as I was taught. I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are."

## Retelling 04 — Ivy Keddie, granddaughter of Ansel Keddie (her father was Duncan Keddie, Ansel's son, a schoolteacher at Ashlin); talk "What the Crate Held," given to the Ordell County Historical Society at Ashlin, 1959, read from typescript with the crate itself displayed.
- Ivy identifies herself as Ansel Keddie's granddaughter but insists her findings rest on evidence ("nine pieces of glass and a burette"), not family loyalty.
- Five record sets consulted, none of which had ever been assembled together before 1958:
  1. The station test book — in the Association's office since 1925 [i.e., since it was impounded by the arbitration board], came to the Historical Society's Rooms in 1959 (per r07 provenance).
  2. The Brant weigh book, kept at Hessel Bottom from 1897, lent to Ivy by Roy Brant (Emmet Brant's son, who kept it up himself since his father died) [r09 confirms: Roy Brant lent his father's book to Ivy in 1958, it was returned to him].
  3. Alder Corners Cheese Company test figures (over Redlow Ridge, in Vessey County) — copied out for Ivy by Rosalie Cudd (runs the factory, Jerome Cudd's [her father's] niece — actually per r10, Rosalie is Jerome Cudd's DAUGHTER, not niece; NOTE: r04 calls her "Jerome Cudd's niece" while r10 (Rosalie's own letter) and r12 both identify her as Jerome Cudd's DAUGHTER — a direct conflict, with r10/r12 (closer to the source) more likely correct); the original book has never left Vessey County; Ivy never saw it directly.
  4. Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks (state dairy inspector for the county from 1908) — in Ivy's own college library at Farland (State Agricultural College) since 1947; sat unused/unquestioned for 11 years until Ivy consulted them.
  5. The Association's own annual ledgers (present at the talk).
- **The crate**: In February 1958, Ivy went to the old station building (used for storage since closing), found in the loft over the intake room, under a tarpaulin, a wooden crate stenciled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET." Carried up there in June 1923 and untouched since. Contained, packed in straw, the station's condemned milk-test pipettes. Six dozen (72) were bought new for the station in 1897 on an invoice totaling **$77.93** for the whole outfit (pipettes, bottles, acid measure, hand tester, freight) [CONFLICTS with r11/Bulletin 471's own "method" section by Prof. Vaught, which says "five dozen" pipettes were purchased — internal inconsistency, since r11's own final quoted conclusion paragraph matches r04's account]. Nine pipettes survived unbroken in the crate [CONFLICTS with r12's footnote, which says "eleven pipettes" were found].
- **Measurement, 9 March 1958** (quoted): "Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for." [CONFLICTS with r11's "Magnitude" section, which instead states the mean delivery is **16.27 c.c.**, not 16.72 — apparently a digit transposition, but treated in r11 as a distinct real figure with its own downstream (also conflicting) arithmetic.]
- Explanation: a butterfat test bottle's neck is ruled to read percent for one specific charge volume. A short charge produces a proportionally short reading. 16.72/17.6 = 0.95 exactly — every test at that station read 95% of the true value.
- Quotes Keddie's own 1922 summary leaf (same text as in r03/r07): average 3.80 in 1896, 3.61 "or within three hundredths" every year since except the nine weeks of 1911 (3.80).
- Arithmetic: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80 (true average). The gap (0.19 points) is NOT fixed — it is 5% of whatever the true value is ("nineteen hundredths is five percent of three point eight... The station did not lose a set number of points. It lost a twentieth of whatever was there").
- Over 25 full delivery years, **1898 to 1922**, the station took in **40,000,000 lb of milk** (avg 1,600,000 lb/year — matches r02's figures exactly; CONFLICTS with r05/Teague's 44,000,000 lb, and also with r12/Rundle's pamphlet, which also says 44,000,000 lb). At the recorded 3.61%, patrons were credited with 1,444,000 lb of fat. At the true 3.80%, they had actually delivered 1,520,000 lb. Difference: **76,000 lb of butterfat**. At $0.25/lb (avg over the period, matches r02), that's **$19,000** in unpaid value [CONFLICTS with r12/Rundle, whose pamphlet gives the patrons' petition figure as "nineteen thousand six hundred dollars" ($19,600) "to the dollar"; also CONFLICTS with r13/Frick, whose exhibit reflects the patrons' OWN reckoning as "sixty-seven thousand pounds of butter fat," and with r11/Vaught's "Magnitude" section, which computes 118,000 lb instead of 76,000 lb]. The 1925 board's award was $3,800 — "a fifth of it" ($19,000/5 = $3,800).
- Clarifies the "two percentages" confusion: 76,000 lb is 5% of the true/delivered fat (1,520,000) but 5.25% of the credited fat (1,444,000) — the Association's ledger convention naturally used the higher (5.25%) figure since it's reckoned against the already-entered number. "Neither figure is an error... But five percent is the one that is also the glass" [i.e., matches the pipette shortfall ratio exactly].
- Because the shortfall was proportional, richer milk lost more in absolute terms: a herd recorded at 4.56 was short 0.24 points; one at 3.42 was short 0.18 points — "the richer the milk, the more was taken from it." [NOTE: the 4.56 figure is exactly the figure Selby Vose (r08) cites as his OWN family herd's 1910 test — meaning Vose's prized herd was itself among the pipette's victims, an irony the sources never state outright. The 3.42 figure exactly matches the Brant weigh book's 1910 entry, quoted in both r07 Item 4 and r09.]
- The 1911 anomaly explained: station's own measure broke 8 May 1911, replaced by a borrowed measure (came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon) until 10 July 1911. Same patrons/herds/pasture/season/acid/bottles/tester — only the measuring instrument changed — and the average shifted to 3.80 (the TRUE, accurate figure) for those nine weeks, then reverted to 3.61 (the flawed figure) the instant the station's own (defective) measure returned. Ivy: "No explanation of this valley's twenty-six-year anomaly survives those nine weeks except the one I have given you."
- Addresses and refutes the "wet hay" theory (from the 1908 committee, see r07 Item 11): quotes State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 214 (1911) (quoted): "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." [NOTE: r15 (Grigg) and r16 (Kepp) both separately MISQUOTE this bulletin as finding a depression of "two tenths" (0.2) in winter feeding, not "five hundredths" (0.05) — an eight-fold misstatement common to both pro-wet-hay advocates.] This is four times too small to explain the ~0.19 gap, and wrong in "shape" (the observed gap was constant year-round, in winter and summer alike, whereas the wet-hay effect, if real, would appear only in winter). Also cites the Alder Corners factory's records: where its book overlaps with Hessel Bottom farmers' milk (14 seasons, 4 men [CONFLICTS with r10/Rosalie Cudd's own letter, which documents SIXTEEN seasons, 1915–1930, of four Hessel Bottom men's milk at Alder Corners — not 14]), the SAME milk tested ~0.19 points higher at Alder Corners than at home — further disproving any land/feed-based cause.
- Closes by quoting Bulletin No. 471, issued **October 1958** (Professor Vaught's wording, jointly with Ivy) (quoted): "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain." [This explicitly uses the dates 1 May 1897 – 12 June 1923 for the flawed-glass period, and calls the recovered surplus "station gain" in the ledgers — this directly connects to and corroborates Wilbur Teague's ledger entries in r05, and is quoted VERBATIM in r11's own concluding paragraph, notwithstanding r11's earlier, conflicting internal arithmetic.]
- Timeline recap: glass condemned 1923, true cause found 1958 — 35 years. Keddie dismissed 1925, cleared 1958 — 33 years. Keddie died 1934; had been dead 24 years when the exonerating bulletin was issued (1958).

## Retelling 05 — Wilbur Teague, Association bookkeeper 1905–1945 (40 years), retired 1945; unpublished memoir "The Books of the Association," Chapter Nine, written at Ashlin, winter 1948 (three years after retiring).
- Office at Ashlin: one room over a harness shop, cold at north end, too warm at south; ledgers on a shelf; sloped desk with brass rail.
- Association had three parts in his time: creamery at Larrow Green, office at Ashlin, skimming station at Ostrey Hollow (7 miles up valley). Selby Vose was manager over the whole; Effie Loomis was secretary/kept minutes; Teague kept the ledger.
- Explains the station's business: patrons hauled milk to the station instead of driving 7 miles to Larrow Green; milk weighed, sampled, tested for butterfat; farmer paid on pounds+test; station separated the milk, sent cream to Larrow Green, returned skim to farmers for hogs/calves.
- **The "station-gain" ledger line** — Teague's own invention, begun when he started in 1905 (he explicitly denies it was "some old dodge of the founders" — it was his own bookkeeping device, because "a difference that will not be entered somewhere will come back and bite you at audit"). On one side: butterfat credited to patrons per the station's own test. On the other: butterfat actually received/tested at Larrow Green from the station's cream. The second figure was ALWAYS larger; the difference ("station gain") was credited to the Association's general account. Example entry, for 1898 (quoted): "Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account." [This exact 1898 entry is independently quoted/reproduced in r07 Item 6 and again reproduced in r12's "Note added while in press," where Dell Rundle uses it to retract his own pamphlet's cream-theft accusation.] The gain ran consistently at **5.25%** every year — so consistent the phrase "as usual" became part of the boilerplate. Teague reflects that this very consistency made him stop scrutinizing it ("A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture").
- **Teague's own aggregate figures** (CONFLICTS with r02/r04/r07/r13; matches r12/Rundle's pamphlet on the 44M figure): "that station took in **forty-four million pounds** of milk" total, and "the average test of all the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, taken over the whole period... was **3.16**." [Contradicts r02/r04's 40,000,000 lb and 3.61 average, and r07's curatorial confirmation of 40,000,000 lb from the ledgers themselves; the 3.16 figure is later cited by Alonzo Frick himself in r13's recollection as the average he understood the patrons to have been paid on, suggesting Teague's figure, however anomalous, had some circulation/acceptance at the time — possibly reflecting Teague's faulty/selective memory decades later, or a different date range than "1898–1922."] Larrow Green tested better; Ostrey Hollow patrons were "sore about it" every season for 25 years.
- Notes the 1911 break: station's own measure broke spring 1911; borrowed one used for **six weeks** [CONFLICTS with r03/r04/r11's ~nine weeks, 8 May–10 July; matches r16/Kepp's "six weeks" reference] before their own came up from town; entered in his accounts merely as a "small charge for replacement," nothing more remarked at the time.
- Repeats what he told the 1925 board of arbitration (his own words, unchanged since; corroborated as "the tester had been taking cream" theory attributed to him by r13/Frick's recollection): cream is portable; the station was in one pair of hands (Keddie's) for 29 seasons; one man weighed, sampled, tested, wrote the figure, and ran the separator, with no second man present spring through fall. He does not accuse Keddie of any specific act ("I do not say what any particular person did on any particular day, because I was not there and I do not know") but argues structurally that such an arrangement "will sooner or later have a little taken from it," and that the remedy (two men or a lock) was never implemented by the Association.
- Confirms: petition came in winter; board sat at Ashlin under Alonzo Frick ("as fair a chairman as ever a hearing had"); Teague testified; board found nothing established against anybody; awarded **$3,800**, charged to the station account (matches r02/r07/r08/r12/r13).
- Two additional claims: (1) Manager Selby Vose knew what the station-gain account showed — he signed the annual sheet every year, which Teague laid before him each spring. Vose never told Teague what he made of it. (2) Teague did raise the issue with Vose once, in **1916**, and was told to "let it alone" — and did, for the rest of his career, reasoning "I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience."

## Retelling 06 — Merle Strawn, retired state dairy inspector for the Ordell circuit (1907–1936, i.e. laid down the circuit in 1936); "Extracts from twenty-eight circuit books," copied out spring 1936 for the state dairy office, with a covering note.
- Covering note: circuit covered ~50-odd plants across 4 counties (creameries, cheese factories, skimming stations, small butter operations); traveled by train/livery/on foot depending on season and roads. His fixed routine at every plant, in order: (1) scales, checked against his own sealed weights, both intake and cream scales, light and heavy loads; (2) lactometer test on the day's milk for added water; (3) boiler, water supply, cold room; (4) watched the tester perform an actual test start-to-finish, without letting him hurry. States he recorded only observations, never opinions ("every time in my life I have seen a field man paid for an opinion instead, the opinion has outlived the weighing and done harm after the man was dead").
- Year-by-year extracts on Ostrey Hollow (selected/relevant):

## Retelling 11 — Leland Vaught, professor of dairy husbandry, introduction and method sections of Bulletin No. 471, State Agricultural College at Farland, October 1958.
- **I. Why written**: the station has been asked for 50 years why Ostrey Hollow's tests ran below the creamery's own, 7 miles down the valley. Answered here "by reference to a piece of glass," not land or men.
- Key thesis: "the milk pipette is the one instrument in the butterfat test which the plant cannot check against anything else, and it is the instrument on which every figure in the plant depends."
- **II. The instrument**: explains the 4-step butterfat test (measured charge via pipette → acid dissolves non-fat → spin in hand tester → read fat percent directly off graduated neck). The neck is ruled for one exact charge volume; if the pipette delivers less than its mark, the reading is proportionally low, not just low by a fixed amount. Standard charge in the state (and on this glassware): **17.6 c.c.**
- Three points: (1) error is proportional/fractional, not additive — richer milk loses more absolutely, but a plant's own internal herd rankings stay consistent; (2) error is precise/consistent, not scattered, so it "has the appearance of excellent work"; (3) error is invisible from inside the plant, because the pipette IS the standard against which everything else (scales, bottle graduation, acid strength) is checked — it can only be caught by an external burette, which reached field men very late.
- **III. Material examined**: Of the lot bought for Ostrey Hollow in 1897 — **"five dozen" pipettes were purchased** [CONFLICTS directly with r04's/r07's invoice figure of "6 doz." (six dozen/72) pipettes] — **nine** were recovered unbroken and legible. Stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against a calibrated burette, five runs each, mean delivery of the nine = **16.27 c.c.** [CONFLICTS with r04's/r07's measured mean of **16.72 c.c.** — likely intended as the same figure with a possible digit transposition, but treated as its own distinct value with its own (also conflicting) downstream arithmetic in this document]. Station's own glass went into use **"the first of May, 1898"** [CONFLICTS with r02/r03/r04/r07, all of which give **1 May 1897** as the date the station's own glass came into use] and remained until condemned June 1923.
- Method of measurement described (fill to mark with room-temp water, wipe tip, deliver in ordinary working manner into a certified burette, 5 deliveries per pipette, average).
- **IV. The interval of 1911**: for nine weeks in summer 1911, station's own pipette was broken, borrowed measure used; all other variables held constant; average test rose from 3.61 to 3.80 and held for nine weeks, then reverted to 3.61 the day the station's own measure returned. Recorded contemporaneously by the operator (Keddie), who set a question mark against it and "did not know what he was recording." Vaught calls this "worth more than the arithmetic" and says it disposes of herd/ground/men explanations since nothing else changed in those nine weeks.
- **V. Magnitude** [INTERNALLY INCONSISTENT WITH SECTION VII, below]: ratio of delivered to marked capacity = 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = **0.9244**. ## Retelling 16 — Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S. (veterinary surgeon) of Ashlin, paper "The bog meadows and the milk," read before the Ordell County Farm Club, 1926, with floor discussion appended.
- Kepp, 30 years of veterinary practice in the county. Argues the wet-hay/mineral-deficiency theory on scientific/clinical grounds (not simply "opinion" as before).
- **The land**: wet bottoms produce acid, lime-and-phosphate-poor soil; sour hay grows there (sedge, rush, coarse tussock grasses); cut late, low in minerals.
- **The cow's physiology**: a cow short on dietary minerals "robs her own frame" to make milk; consequences include thin flank, rough coat, slow spring conditioning, digestive disorders in fall, later breeding, worse feet. Milk YIELD (volume) is not reduced on wet land ("no man on a bog meadow ever complained that his pail was light") but butterfat is the first thing sacrificed.
- **General claim**: across the county, wet-bottom herds test poorer in fat "by a fifth of a point" (0.20) than hill herds, regardless of breed, farmer, or barn — "It depends on the ground."
- **Hessel Bottom specifically**: SIX farms [matches r09/r12/r15], lying along the creek, springs year-round, under water each spring till May, sedge hay as the "winter dependence." Patrons have complained "for something like a quarter of a century." States the gap "as it has been given to me and as I have satisfied myself" is **twenty-one hundredths of a point (0.21)** [matches Vose's r08 "rise of twenty-one hundredths" figure, though applied to a different specific claim — Vose used it for the 1924 "rise," Kepp uses it as the general historical test gap].
- Cites Bulletin No. 214 as finding the test depressed by "**two tenths**" (0.2) in winter feeding [again MISQUOTES the bulletin exactly as Grigg does in r15 — actual bulletin text elsewhere is "five hundredths"/0.05]. Calls the three figures (his 0.20 field estimate, the bulletin's claimed "two tenths," and the station's "twenty-one hundredths" complaint) mutually corroborating ("the three agree so nearly that I do not know what more a reasonable man requires").
- **Addresses the 1911 objection** (raised by "a gentleman" in advance, presumably referring to the Ivy Keddie/Bulletin 471 pipette-substitution argument, though Bulletin 471 itself would not be published until 1958 — so this may be an ANACHRONISM, or Kepp may be addressing an earlier, less formal version of the same argument circulating in 1926): claims the 1911 substitution period lasted only **"six weeks"** [matches r05/Teague's six-week figure, not r03/r04/r11's nine weeks], dismisses it as "six weeks of June and July, when every herd in the county is at its best," arguing six summer weeks can't outweigh a broader pattern.
- **Remedy**: drain the bottom, lime it repeatedly, reseed, keep stock off in spring wet, never feed the hay alone — mixed ration plus mineral supplement. Butterfat at 27 cents/lb through these years [matches r14/Sill's price figure].
- **Discussion (floor questions, taken down by club secretary)**:
  - Member asks whether the difference might be in the COWS rather than the land (poorer farmers, poorer stock). Kepp rejects this: some of the best cattle in the county are on wet land, some poor cattle on hills test well; "a man may improve his herd for twenty years and never get his fat up while his ground is sour."
  - Member asks whether the trouble might lie in the STATION rather than the bottom, "as had been said in the valley for years." Kepp explicitly declines to engage: "he had no interest in that quarrel and no competence in it; he had never in his life read a test and would not know how" — but insists that when a cause is found consistently in soil/herbage/stock condition/the College's trial/every wet bottom in the county alike, "it is not good sense to go looking for a second cause in a building."
  - Chairman thanks Kepp; paper to be printed for members.

(short 0.88 c.c., 95% of mark); r11 (Vaught, "Magnitude" section) instead says 16.27 c.c., with completely different downstream arithmetic (ratio 0.9244, true average 3.905, shortfall 0.295, 118,000 lb) — yet r11's own final "Conclusion" paragraph (Section VII) reverts to the standard 76,000 lb / "one part in twenty" figures, an unresolved internal self-contradiction within Bulletin 471 as reproduced in r11.
18. **Date station's own glass went into use**: r02/r03/r04/r07 all say 1 May 1897; r11 (Vaught) says "the first of May, 1898" — a one-year discrepancy within material nominally quoting/summarizing the same underlying facts as r04/r07.
19. **Total butterfat shortfall / patrons' financial loss**: r04 calculates $19,000 (76,000 lb at $0.25/lb); r12 (Rundle) cites the patrons' own petition figure as $19,600; r13 (Frick) cites the patrons' own reckoning as 67,000 lb of butterfat (not a dollar figure, and not reconciled with either 76,000 lb or 118,000 lb elsewhere); r11 calculates 118,000 lb in its "Magnitude" section. At least four different, mutually unreconciled quantifications of the same underlying loss.
20. **Source of the station's original (1896) glassware, pre-dating the 1897 purchase**: Keddie's own letter (r03) states the glass was "borrowed off the Ashlin creamery" in his narrative text, but the SAME retelling's quoted first leaf of his own test book says "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes" — an internal contradiction within r03. Hobart Sill (r14) independently and emphatically claims HE personally supplied the original 1896 glass from his own Larrow Green creamery bench (later returned to him, one piece chipped) — this account agrees with Keddie's own quoted first-leaf text (Larrow Green) rather than with Keddie's narrative letter text (Ashlin creamery).
21. **1896 partial-season milk intake figure**: r07 (curator) explicitly states no figure survives for the station's first partial season (May–Dec 1896) — "inquirers wanting a figure for 1896 must be told there is none." r14 (Hobart Sill) provides a specific figure: "about nine hundred thousand pounds."
22. **Alder Corners Cheese Company founding date**: r10 (Rosalie Cudd, presumably the most authoritative/primary source, being Jerome Cudd's own daughter) says 1913; r09 (Roy Brant) and r12 (Rundle) both say 1914.
23. **Rosalie Cudd's relationship to Jerome Cudd**: r04 (Ivy Keddie's talk) mistakenly calls her Jerome Cudd's "niece"; r10 (Rosalie's own letter) and r12 (Rundle's pamphlet) both correctly/consistently identify her as his DAUGHTER.
24. **Number of overlapping seasons of Alder Corners/Hessel Bottom data**: r04 (Ivy Keddie) cites "14 seasons, 4 men" of overlap; r10 (Rosalie Cudd's own letter and table) documents a full 16 seasons (1915–1930) of the same four men's milk.
25. **1908 committee's actual sampling period**: internally self-contradictory within r15 itself — Grigg's 1919 covering letter claims the committee sampled "through the whole of 1908... every month of that year," while the reprinted 1908 report (in the same document) states sampling was done only "through January, February and March." r07's curatorial note on Item 11 also confirms the Jan–Mar-only sampling.
26. **Bulletin No. 214's actual finding vs. how it is cited**: the bulletin's own text (quoted verbatim identically in r04, r07, r11) states a winter depression of "not more than five hundredths of one per cent" (0.05) and nothing measurable in summer; but BOTH Nyle Grigg (r15) and Dr. Kepp (r16), independently, cite the bulletin as finding a depression of "two tenths" (0.20) — an eightfold, shared misquotation by the wet-hay theory's two chief proponents.
27. **Keddie's true private opinion on the cause**: publicly, in every account, Keddie offers no explanation and denies personal guilt; Alonzo Frick's 1946 recollection (r13) reveals that Keddie privately told him, in confidence, that he believed the PATRONS were at fault (i.e., watering their milk) — directly contradicting the popular assumption (voiced even within r13 itself, as something "said since") that Keddie "suspected nobody."
28. **Emmet Brant's estrangement from Keddie — motive nuance**: Roy Brant's account (r09) specifies that his father's anger was not that Keddie was necessarily guilty of anything but that Keddie "ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not" — a more nuanced motive than a simple accusation of guilt.
29. **The "cream ran heavy" observation**: Hobart Sill (r14) independently, and decades before the 1958 finding, observed that cream arriving at Larrow Green from the Hollow was consistently richer than the station's own paperwork indicated — functionally the same phenomenon later explained by Teague's "station gain" ledger line (r05, r07 Item 6, r12's "note added while in press") and ultimately by the pipette shortfall (r04, r11). Sill, however, drew the opposite conclusion from Vose/valley opinion, i.e., that it proved WATERING, when in fact (per the 1958 finding) it was proof of nothing sinister at all.
30. **Fat price per pound cited across retellings**: r02/r04 give ~25 cents average; r09 gives 24 cents (1910) rising to ~25 cents average; r14 and r16 both independently cite 27 cents "through these years" — a higher, seemingly later-period figure, not clearly reconciled with the 24-25 cent figures (possibly reflecting different date ranges within the same multi-decade dispute).
