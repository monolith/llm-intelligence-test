# Answers — Part 2: Section A, Stories 5–8; Section B

## Story 5 — The Petition and the Hearing (1925)

**Who:** The Ostrey Hollow patrons, Alonzo Frick (arbitration chairman), Ansel Keddie, Selby Vose, Wilbur Teague.
**What:** In January 1925 the Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned the Association, claiming chronic underpayment (r02, r12, r22). A three-person board of arbitration, chaired by attorney Alonzo Frick, sat in three sessions: the first at the Association's Ashlin office on 17 March 1925, the second and third at the station itself in its own weigh room, at Frick's specific request, so the board could see the equipment rather than hear it described (r13, corroborated by eyewitness Cleve Oram, r21, and by Orra Keddie's diary, r24). Three theories were argued: watering (Vose), cream theft by the tester (Teague), and wet hay (the 1908 report re-read into the record) — detailed in Story 6/Section F. Keddie gave a nine-sentence statement denying guilt and could not explain his own figures (r03, r07 Item 8). He was dismissed on 30 April 1925, four days before the board's finding (r03, r13, r18, r24). On 4 May 1925 the board found "no cause established" yet awarded the patrons $3,800 "without admission," charged to the station account — the wording and figure quoted verbatim from the minute book itself (r02, r07 Item 7, matching r05, r08, r12, r13, r19, r22, r23). Vira Toland (r01) and Cleve Oram (r21) both instead recall $3,500 — two independent oral family traditions against one quoted primary document; the document prevails (see Section E).
**When:** Petition January 1925; hearing 17 March–4 May 1925; dismissal 30 April 1925; award 4 May 1925.
**Quantities:** Award $3,800 (documentary); patrons' claimed shortfall variously $19,000 (r04's calculation), $19,600 (patrons' own petition figure, r12), or 67,000 lb of butterfat (patrons' own reckoning, per r13) — see Section C for reconciliation.
**Documents:** r01, r02, r03, r05, r07 (Items 3, 7, 8), r08, r12, r13 (fullest account, by the chairman himself), r17, r18, r21, r22, r23, r24.

## Story 6 — Over the Ridge: Alder Corners (1913/14–1930)

**Who:** Four of the six Hessel Bottom farmers, Jerome Cudd and his daughter Rosalie Cudd.
**What:** Jerome Cudd built the Alder Corners Cheese Company in Vessey County, over Redlow Ridge from Hessel Bottom (founding year contested: 1913 per Rosalie's own letter, r10; 1914 per r09 and r12). From 1915, four of the six Hessel Bottom farmers hauled their milk over the ridge to Alder Corners during the summer flush (roughly June–mid-September), returning to Ostrey Hollow the rest of the year, for sixteen seasons through 1930 (r09, r10 — these two independently agree on sixteen seasons, 1915–1930, which should be preferred over Ivy Keddie's own "fourteen seasons" reference in r04). Jerome Cudd tested this milk on his own bench with his own glass, never comparing figures with the station, and found it consistently good — around 3.78–3.82 every season, averaging about 3.80 across the sixteen years (full table quoted in r10). He explicitly dismissed the wet-hay theory as "nonsense... an Ordell theory." In 1958 Rosalie supplied these figures to Ivy Keddie by letter (r10).
**When:** Factory built 1913/1914; dual-hauling 1915–1930.
**Quantities:** Sixteen seasons of test data, 3.79–3.82 per season, averaging ~3.80 — independently matching the corrected "true" Ostrey Hollow figure derived from the pipette shortfall (Story 7), by a wholly separate testing operation using different equipment.
**Documents:** r09 (Roy Brant, from the farmers' side), r10 (Rosalie Cudd's letter and table, the primary source), r12 (Rundle, secondhand, gives a different founding year).

This story functions as an independent control for the whole saga: the same cows, same land, same men, tested elsewhere with different glass, came out normal. It quietly refutes the wet-hay theory before anyone knew to state the refutation in those terms.

## Story 7 — The Crate in the Loft (1958)

**Who:** Ivy Keddie (Ansel's granddaughter), Professor Leland Vaught.
**What:** In February 1958, Ivy Keddie — a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland — climbed into the loft over the station's old intake room and found, under a tarpaulin, the crate of pipettes condemned in 1923 (r04, r17, r18, r19, r21, r22; against the curator's claim in r07 that it was found in the Ashlin office — the loft version is corroborated independently by Strawn's own 1928 circuit note describing that loft as "full of odds and ends... since before the war," by Rundle's pamphlet describing the loft as apparently never cleared, and by three separate eyewitness/family narrators, so it is preferred). Nine pipettes survived intact (r04, r07, r21, r22; against r12's "eleven" and r19's "eleven," both of which appear to trace to the same confused press coverage). On 9 March 1958 Ivy measured them against a calibrated burette at Farland: mean delivery 16.72 c.c. against a stem blown for 17.6 c.c. — short 0.88 c.c., or 95% of the mark (quoted measurement sheet, r04, r07). She assembled five independent record sets (the station's test book, the Brant weigh book lent by Roy Brant, Alder Corners' figures via Rosalie Cudd, Merle Strawn's archived 1923 notebook, and the Association's own ledgers) to build the case. In October 1958 Bulletin No. 471, co-authored with Professor Vaught, was published, concluding that no milk was watered and no cream was stolen — the tests were low by one part in twenty because the pipettes were short by one part in twenty (verbatim conclusion, quoted identically in r04, r07, r11). The bulletin's own "Magnitude" section contains an unresolved internal contradiction (a different measurement, 16.27 c.c., and different downstream arithmetic, 118,000 lb) that its own final conclusion abandons in favor of the standard figures — an error within the bulletin itself, not a genuine alternative finding.
**When:** Found February 1958; measured 9 March 1958; published October 1958. In 1959 Ivy gave a talk, "What the Crate Held," to the county historical society.
**Quantities:** 9 surviving pipettes; mean delivery 16.72 c.c.; shortfall 0.88 c.c. (5%); see Section C for the derived money and pound figures.
**Documents:** r04, r07 (Items 5, 14, 15), r11 (internally inconsistent), r18, r19, r21, r22.

## Story 8 — What the Family Kept (1934–1981)

**Who:** Orra Keddie, Duncan Keddie, Roy and Hazel Brant, Vira Toland, Cleve Oram, Dell Rundle, Bertram Kyle, Orin Falke.
**What:** Ansel Keddie died in 1934, nine years after his dismissal, still under suspicion in the valley, "cleared of a thing nobody will name" (r24). His widow Orra died in 1948, ten years before the exoneration (r18, r24). Over the following decades the story was kept alive and pieced together in fragments: by Orra's own diary (published 1964, r24); by her son Duncan's 1968/1970 family notes, written specifically to correct errors already "printed" about the family — Selby Vose's parentage, Ivy's relationship to Hazel, and the false Vose–Keddie kinship rumor (r18); by the physical passage of five separate documents/objects (station test book, Brant weigh book, Strawn's notebooks, the Alder Corners test book, and the crate itself) through different hands and buildings until they came to rest in three different institutions by 1970 (r18's own careful provenance list); by oral history tapes recorded with Vira Toland (1979, r01) and Hazel Brant (1981, r17) and Cleve Oram (1977, r21), each preserving family memory with its own errors of date and figure; by Dell Rundle's own 1939 pamphlet, which endorsed the cream-theft theory and then explicitly retracted it in a 1959 "note added while in press" upon seeing the Association's ledgers (r12); and by two independent official retrospectives — Bertram Kyle's 1953 clerk's memoir (r23) and Orin Falke's 1959 county report (r22), the latter itself correcting its own initial error about Ivy's relation to Ansel.
**When:** 1934 (Keddie's death) through 1981 (Hazel Brant's tape), with the bulk of documentary consolidation occurring 1958–1970.
**Quantities:** None specific to this story; its significance is in provenance and correction, not measurement.
**Documents:** r01, r09 (postscript), r12 (footnote and retraction), r17, r18, r21, r22 (correction), r23, r24.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**
Granddaughter. Ivy is the daughter of Duncan Keddie (r04, r18, r24), and Duncan is Ansel and Orra Keddie's son (r03, r18, r24). Duncan states this plainly and pointedly in his own 1968 family notes, noting he has "already twice seen it printed otherwise" (r18) — a direct rebuttal aimed at exactly the error that appears in *The Ashlin Chronicle* (r19, "great-niece," sourced from a college press release) and in Orin Falke's initial 1959 draft (r22), which Falke himself explicitly retracts in an appended correction: "Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." Since both erroneous instances trace to the same press-release-era source rather than being independent errors, and since the correction comes both from Ivy's own father and from a self-correcting official report, "granddaughter" is settled beyond reasonable doubt.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**
Second cousins. Ivy's father Duncan and Hazel's father Roy are first cousins, being the children of siblings Orra Keddie (née Brant) and Emmet Brant (r09, r18); children of first cousins are second cousins. This is independently affirmed by Duncan himself (r18, the most explicit and deliberate source, written specifically "so that they can be copied"), by Roy Brant (r09), by Dell Rundle's 1959 corrective footnote (r12), and by Hazel Brant herself (r17), who states flatly, "People are forever telling me she is my niece or my cousin once removed or some such, and she is not. Second cousins." Against this stand two secondhand, non-family oral accounts — Vira Toland's "first cousin once removed" (r01) and Cleve Oram's identical phrase (r21), which may simply be the same village imprecision repeated rather than two independent errors. Four family/documentary sources beat two oral ones. What the sources leave open: whether Orra and Emmet were full or half siblings (r09 and r18 say full; r17, Hazel herself, says half, "by his father's second wife") is unresolved — but it does not change the cousin arithmetic, since siblingship of either kind supports the same first-cousin/second-cousin chain.

**B3. Selby Vose's father, and why it matters.**
Gideon Vose, the Association's founding president (1889–1911) and sole author of its by-laws, including Article VII (r02, r08, r18, r23). Selby himself says so directly in his 1926 address ("my father was your first president," r08); Effie Loomis, the Association's own secretary, independently calls him "son of the late president" (r02); Duncan Keddie and clerk Bertram Kyle both separately and explicitly reject the rival "nephew" claim as error/"nonsense" (r18, r23). Against this stand two secondhand accounts, Vira Toland's (r01) and Dell Rundle's (r12). Four independent sources, including Selby's own first-person testimony and two Association insiders, outweigh two oral traditions: son is settled. It matters because Article VII — "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern," with no appeal — was Gideon's own rule, and it was that very rule, inherited and administered without complaint by his son as manager, that allowed a silent 5% instrument error to stand unquestioned and unappealable for 26 years. Selby's fierce, personal defense of the Association's integrity (the 1919 circular, the 1926 defense of it, his rejection of the "faulty apparatus" theory even after Strawn's 1923 condemnation) reads differently once his father's authorship of the underlying rule is understood: he was, in effect, defending his father's system.

**B4. The Vose–Keddie family relationship.**
None, on the best evidence. Duncan Keddie (Orra's own son) states this as a deliberate correction: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses... My mother would have told me... and she never once put a Vose in it" (r18). Against this, a persistent village rumor holds that Orra and "old Mrs. Vose" were first cousins (Vira Toland, r01) or that "the Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort" (Cleve Oram, r21) — both secondhand, oral, and vague ("some sort"), and neither from a family member. Given Duncan's direct, motivated, and detailed denial against two imprecise oral traditions, the connection is most likely village legend rather than fact, though the sources cannot fully prove a negative — a small residual uncertainty remains.
