# Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math

**C1.**
(a) The tests differed from the truth by a constant proportion of **5% (one part in twenty)**, low. This comes from the glassware measurement: the pipettes were blown to deliver 17.6 c.c. but, measured against a calibrated burette in 1958, delivered a mean of only 16.72 c.c. — 0.88 c.c. short. 0.88 / 17.6 = 0.05 exactly. Because a test-bottle neck is ruled to read percent-fat for one specific charge volume, a charge short by 5% produces a fat reading short by 5% of whatever the true value is (proportional, not fixed) — this is stated identically in r04, r07, and r11's own final conclusion (Section VII), which is why it is used here in preference to the same bulletin's internally inconsistent "Magnitude" section (16.27 c.c., ratio 0.9244), which even Vaught admits "is not readily reconciled" with the station's own recorded figures.

(b) True average = **3.80**; recorded average = 3.61; shortfall = **0.19 points**. Check: 3.61 / 0.95 = 3.80 exactly. This is corroborated four ways: Keddie's own averages leaf (r03/r04/r07), Strawn's 1924 field note (r06), the independent Alder Corners cross-check of the same farmers' milk (~3.80 across sixteen seasons, r10), and the county agent's own independent 1959 restatement of "nineteen hundredths" (r22). It is also the figure Hazel Brant recites from family memory (r17). Rival figures — Teague's memory of 3.16 (r05, r13), Vose's claimed 3.90 (r08), Sill's estimate of 3.85 (r14), Falke's later "3.85" (r22) — are all single, non-quoted-document recollections and are set aside in favor of the documentary/cross-checked 3.80/3.61 pair.

(c) **76,000 lb** of butterfat. Working: over 25 full delivery years (1898–1922) the station received 40,000,000 lb of milk (r02, r04, r07's own ledger citation — preferred over Teague's memoir figure of 44,000,000 lb, since the curator is quoting the ledgers directly rather than recalling them decades later). 40,000,000 × 0.0019 (the 0.19-point gap, as a fraction) = 76,000 lb. This matches Bulletin 471's own final conclusion (quoted identically in r04, r07, r11 despite r11's internal "118,000 lb" error) and the curator's independent ledger tally (r07 Item 6).

(d) At the period's average price of $0.25/lb (r02, r04), 76,000 lb = **$19,000**. The patrons actually received $3,800 from the 1925 award — **one-fifth (1/5)** of what they were owed (3,800/19,000 = 0.2), a fraction Ivy Keddie herself notes explicitly in r04 ("a fifth of it"). Note the patrons' own 1925 petition claimed a somewhat different figure ($19,600, r12, or 67,000 lb per r13) — these reflect the patrons' contemporaneous estimate before the mechanism was understood, not the corrected 1958 figure, and are not the same calculation.

**C2. Largest patron, 1910 (Emmet Brant).**
(a) Credited: 200,000 lb delivered, paid on a recorded test of 3.42% (dated leaf, quoted identically in r07 Item 4, r09, and recalled by r17) → 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of butterfat credited.
(b) Owed but not credited: true test = 3.42 / 0.95 = 3.60%; true fat = 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb; difference = 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb** not credited (equivalently, 200,000 × 0.0018, the 0.18-point shortfall r04 itself cites for exactly this test value as a worked example).
(c) At the 1910 butterfat price of $0.24/lb specifically (r09), 360 × 0.24 = **$86.40** for that year alone.

**C3. Station-wide, full delivery years.**
(a) Credited to patrons in total: 40,000,000 lb × 3.61% = **1,444,000 lb**.
(b) Actually received at Larrow Green in the station's cream: 40,000,000 lb × 3.80% = **1,520,000 lb** (equivalently 1,444,000 + 76,000).
(c) The difference (76,000 lb) as a percentage depends on the denominator, and both are "correct" relative to their own base:
 - As a percentage of the true/delivered fat (1,520,000): 76,000/1,520,000 = **5.0%** — this equals the pipette's own shortfall ratio.
 - As a percentage of the credited fat (1,444,000), which is how the Association's own ledgers computed and recorded it every year ("station gain... five and a quarter per cent, as usual" — quoted identically in r05, r07 Item 6, r12, r23): 76,000/1,444,000 = **5.26%** (≈5.25%).
 Neither is an error; they are the same absolute difference expressed against two different bases, and the ledger convention naturally used the higher (5.25%) figure because it is reckoned against the number already entered in the books (r04's own explanation of "the two percentages").

**C4.**
(a) Invoice total: **$77.93** (quoted identically in r04, r07, and the supplier's own history r20 — r20's narrative text mis-states this as "$77.39," a digit transposition against its own quoted document, which is preferred here).
(b) **Six dozen (72) pipettes**, each short **0.88 c.c.** (17.6 c.c. marked, 16.72 c.c. delivered). Six dozen is confirmed by the invoice arithmetic itself: 72 pipettes × $0.35 = $25.20, exactly the line total given; five dozen (60 × $0.35 = $21.00) does not match the quoted line total, so r11's and r21's "five dozen" are arithmetic errors, not a genuine alternative count.

**C5.**
(a) The faulty glassware was in service for **just over 26 years** — from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 (the condemnation date fixed by Merle Strawn's own contemporaneous 1923 circuit notebook, r06, and its photostat, r07 Item 9; this outranks the later, non-contemporaneous "12 July 1923" given by Vose, r08, and Rundle, r12). The money/pound calculation instead runs over **25 full delivery years** (1898–1922) because those are the only complete calendar years lying entirely within the affected period for which the ledgers give whole-year totals; the partial first year (1897, only 8 months under the new glass) and the partial final year (1923, only about 5½ months before condemnation) are excluded from the "40,000,000 lb / 25 years" figure, so the two numbers — "how long the glass was bad" and "how many years the arithmetic covers" — are different by design, not by error.
(b) **Cannot be determined from the sources.** The curator's own accession notes state plainly that no figure survives for the station's first partial season (May–December 1896): "inquirers wanting a figure for 1896 must be told there is none" (r07). Two secondhand recollections offer numbers — Hobart Sill's "about nine hundred thousand pounds" (r14) and Bertram Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" (r23) — but both are decades-later lay estimates ("which is what I understood at the time from men who were there," "it is not in any book I ever ruled"), they disagree with each other by roughly 20%, and neither claims documentary support. The archival record itself is explicit that the figure does not exist; that outranks two conflicting guesses.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 experiment.**
When/how long: 8 May – 10 July 1911, about nine weeks (established from Keddie's own dated book entries, corroborated by Orra Keddie's diary entry of the same date and by Roy Brant's independent account; preferred over Teague's and Kepp's later "six weeks," which come from undated retrospective memory). Variable: only the measuring instrument (pipette) changed — the station's own measure broke and a borrowed one was substituted. Held constant: the same tester, the same patrons and herds, the same season/pasture, the same scale, acid, and procedure, the same level of (non-)supervision. Result: the recorded average rose from its usual 3.61 to 3.80 for exactly the nine weeks, then fell back to 3.61 the same day the original measure returned. What this establishes that arithmetic alone would not: it is a genuine natural experiment demonstrating causation, not mere correlation — it directly rules out any theory requiring a change in the men, the cows, or the land (since none of those changed), and it does so independently of and years before the 1958 physical measurement of the crate. What it does **not** establish: it does not by itself identify the mechanism (that the error was proportional rather than fixed), does not quantify the shortfall precisely, and does not prove the *specific* pipettes later found in the crate were the same ones at fault — those required the 1958 measurement to confirm.

**D2. Why Keddie cannot have been stealing cream.**
Three independent reasons: (1) The Brant family's own private weigh book, kept independently of the Association, shows the farm scale and the station scale always agreed, ruling out any manipulation at intake (r09). (2) The Association's own "station gain" ledger line — an internal bookkeeping device, unrelated to family records — shows that *more* butterfat consistently arrived at Larrow Green in the station's cream than had been credited to patrons, every single year (r05, corroborated independently by Hobart Sill's decades-earlier lay observation that the cream always "ran heavy," r14, and by Rundle's own 1959 retraction of his cream-theft claim once he saw this same ledger logic, r12). Theft would make the cream *lighter* leaving the station, not heavier arriving downstream — the opposite of what the books show. (3) The 1911 natural experiment (D1): Keddie's opportunity and behavior were unchanged throughout, yet the readings moved with the instrument alone. These three are independent because they come from three different, unconnected record-keepers (a farm family, the Association's bookkeeper, and the tester's own book) reasoning from entirely different kinds of evidence (weight comparison, ledger accounting, and a substitution experiment). Reason (2) — the ledger logic — would still hold even if the glassware had never been measured in 1958, since Rundle himself worked it out from the ledgers alone, without knowing about the pipette defect. Did the bookkeeper (Teague) ever question the ledger entries that refute his own accusation? **No.** By his own account he found the consistent 5.25% "station gain" figure so regular that it became "furniture" and he stopped scrutinizing it (r05); yet he was also the one who put the cream-theft theory to the 1925 board (r13), and clerk Bertram Kyle later observed that no one in that room ever "laid the second [book] beside the fourth and looked" (r23).

**D3. Did the manager know the tests ran low before the glassware was condemned?**
**Yes.** The chronically low test average was public, valley-wide knowledge argued at annual meetings for a quarter century (r02, r08, r22) — Vose could not plausibly have been unaware of the bare fact. More specifically: he personally signed and defended the 1919 circular accusing patrons of watering, which itself presupposes he knew the tests were low and needed explaining (r08, r23); he reviewed and signed the station's annual "station gain" sheet every year of his tenure (r05); and when bookkeeper Teague raised the underlying issue with him directly in 1916, Vose told him to "let it alone" (r05) — direct evidence of knowledge, not ignorance. Clerk Bertram Kyle's independent account that Vose "read the totals and never read the lines above them" (r23) suggests he knew the outcome (low tests) but did not — or chose not to — engage with the ledger detail that would have pointed to the true, non-sinister cause. So: he knew the fact of the low tests for years; what he did not know, or did not act on, was the mechanism.

## Section F — Theory

**The single causal theory:** From 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923, the Ostrey Hollow station used a set of milk-test pipettes that a manufacturing accident at Ammon & Sons had left short by 5% of their marked capacity (16.72 c.c. delivered against a 17.6 c.c. mark). Because a test-bottle neck is ruled to read percent-fat for one exact charge volume, every charge drawn with these pipettes produced a fat reading exactly 5% below the truth, regardless of what the milk actually contained — which is why richer herds lost more in absolute points than poorer ones, and why the error was invisible from inside the plant (the pipette was itself the standard against which everything else was checked). The size of the error (5%, not some other fraction) is fixed entirely by how short that particular mold ran, an accident of glassblowing with no connection to any local condition (r20's own account of a reground mold). It began the instant the flawed glass replaced the station's borrowed/temporary glass (1 May 1897) and ended the instant the state inspector's burette caught it and new glass replaced it (12 June 1923) — bracketed exactly by two documentary dates. Nothing on the farms or at the station needed to change for the error to appear or vanish, because it was a property of a physical object, not of behavior; this is proven directly by the nine-week natural experiment of 1911 (Section D1), in which substituting a correct borrowed measure — with everything else held constant — made the "problem" disappear for exactly as long as the substitution lasted.

**Three competing explanations, and what defeats each:**
1. **Watering**, advanced by manager Selby Vose (1919 circular; 1926 address). Defeated by Merle Strawn's inspection notebooks: he tested with a lactometer at every visit from 1908 on and never once found added water (r06) — a direct, repeated, contemporaneous negative from the one disinterested outside observer with the instrument to check.
2. **Cream theft by the tester**, advanced by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague at the 1925 hearing, and long believed as valley consensus (r01, r12's original text). Defeated by the Association's own "station gain" ledger entries, which show *more* fat arriving at the creamery than was ever credited to patrons — the opposite signature of theft — a point Dell Rundle himself used to retract his own published accusation (r12), and by the 1911 experiment, in which the same tester's readings moved with the instrument alone.
3. **Wet hay / mineral deficiency of Hessel Bottom**, advanced by the 1908 committee (Grigg) and reinforced by Dr. Kepp's 1926 paper. Defeated by the college's own Bulletin No. 214 (1911), whose actual finding — a winter-only depression of at most 0.05 points, nothing measurable in summer — is roughly a quarter of the observed, constant, year-round 0.19-point gap (both Grigg and Kepp separately misquote this bulletin as finding 0.20, an error, not a genuine second reading); and by the Alder Corners cross-check (Story 6), showing the same Hessel Bottom cows and land testing normally elsewhere.

**Subsidiary questions:**
- Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware? **No.** Effie Loomis's own notes record that the crate condemned in June 1923 was never mentioned in any of the three 1925 sessions by anyone (r02); Cleve Oram independently recalls the crate sitting in the loft directly above the room where two of the three sessions were held, unmentioned the entire time (r21).
- What did the tester himself believe the cause was? Publicly, nothing — Keddie denied guilt and offered no theory (r03). Privately, per arbitration chairman Alonzo Frick's own recollection, Keddie confided in the Ashlin corridor that he had always suspected the *patrons* of watering their milk, contradicting the popular later story that "he suspected nobody" (r13). This sits in tension with Duncan Keddie's separate account that his father came home in 1911 saying "it was something in the glass" but never dared record it (r18) — the sources suggest Keddie's private suspicion shifted or was never fully settled in his own mind, torn between distrusting his equipment and not wanting to accuse either his employer's apparatus or his own neighbors.

## Section G — Summary

A valley creamery paid its farmers strictly on the butterfat their milk tested at, station by station, with no appeal. For 26 years one remote station's tests ran mysteriously low, and everyone blamed people: the patrons were accused of watering their milk, the tester was accused of stealing cream, and a committee blamed the wet hay of a low meadow. Each theory poisoned a friendship or a reputation — a manager's circular, a bookkeeper's insinuation, a tester dismissed in disgrace, two brothers-in-law who stopped speaking. The true cause was a crate of pipettes, bought new in 1897, blown very slightly short by an unnoticed accident at the glass factory — a fixed 5% error invisible from inside the plant and undetected until a state inspector's burette caught it in 1923, and not explained until the tester's own granddaughter found the discarded crate in a loft in 1958 and measured it herself. It took eight separate stories — statutes, invoices, ledgers, a broken measure, family diaries, a rival cheese factory's records, and one woman's climb up a ladder — because no single record-keeper had ever assembled them together.
