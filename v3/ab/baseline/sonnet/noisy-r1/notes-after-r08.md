# Exhaustive notes on Retellings 1–8 (Ostrey Hollow / Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association cycle)

## Overview / core subject
All eight retellings concern the Ostrey Hollow skimming station, seven miles up the Larrow Valley from the Larrow Green creamery, operated by the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (Ordell County; nearby towns Ashlin, Larrow Green; the county seat area includes Farland, home of a State Agricultural College). The central mystery: for decades the Ostrey Hollow station's butterfat tests ran chronically low compared to neighboring stations, provoking accusations of watered milk (by the Association) and suspected cream-skimming by the tester (by some patrons/bookkeeper), a 1925 arbitration that found "no cause established" yet paid $3,800, and a final 1958/1959 scientific exoneration showing the true cause was defective (short) pipettes bought in 1897, which read low by a constant proportion (5%) rather than a fixed amount. Multiple retellings directly contradict each other on facts, dates, and figures — these conflicts are cataloged below.

## Retelling 01 — Vira Toland, b. 1912, Ostrey Hollow patron's daughter; tape recorded 1979 at Ostrey Hollow by a young man from the county (asked 3 questions in an hour); transcribed with her verbal doublings-back left in.
- Vira born 1912 at top of Ostrey Hollow, house built by her father's father; lived there her whole life except 2 years at Ashlin after first marriage (which she says shouldn't count).
- Her father hauled milk to the station; 9 families hauled to that station, in and out of each other's kitchens year-round.
- Station: at the very top of the Hollow where the road bends toward the ridge; low, whitewashed building, cold as a spring house even in August; smelled of milk, steam, hot iron; engine audible before rounding the last turn.
- Ansel Keddie ran the station. Went up in 1895 (year before it opened) to prepare it; tested first summer (1896); still there when Vira was old enough to ride the wagon.
- Keddie's book: ruled by his own straightedge, uniform line height, figures "like print"; wrote standing at a slope desk by the window. Tested ~1,000 cans a week, "never a blot." Vira's father said if the world kept accounts like Keddie there'd be no lawyers in Ashlin (joking — "there are still lawyers in Ashlin").
- 1919 (Vira age 7): a printed letter came from the Association, over the manager's signature, sent to the 9 Hessel Bottom families, stating there was "water in the cans" coming from Ostrey Hollow. Her father read it at the table with his hat on (unusual); read it twice; put it in the desk drawer in the front room; still there when he died; Vira believes her brother has it yet. Some patrons kept their letters till they died; one woman kept hers pinned inside her trunk lid.
- The manager who signed the letter: Selby Vose, of Larrow Green — described by Vira as "old Gideon Vose's nephew" (CONFLICTS with r02 and r08, which state Selby was Gideon's SON). Gideon Vose founded/started the Association.
- Orra Keddie (Ansel's wife) and "old Mrs. Vose" (Gideon's wife, per Vira) were first cousins — an unwritten but widely known fact; meant the accusing letter felt like "somebody's cousin saying it."
- Orra Keddie was Emmet Brant's sister. Emmet Brant: biggest man on Hessel Bottom, most cows, most hay ground, hauled the most milk to the station, and thus lost the most from the "water in the cans" letter.
- Family rupture: before 1919 the Keddie and Brant households shared Sunday dinners (two houses eating in one kitchen, men at the fence after); after 1919 they never spoke again — not at store, not at a funeral; Orra would walk out of her way to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane; both families lived within sight of each other's chimney smoke for years without speaking; both now (1979) dead.
- After the letter, tests "went up" suddenly one summer and stayed up; no official explanation given by station, Association, or anyone. Vira's father noticed his milk tested better without changing anything.
- A hearing was held at Ashlin the following winter/spring: lawyers, Association officers, and the valley's men attended in wagons; went on and on.
- Outcome per Vira: Ansel Keddie was "put out" (fired) — an old man, after all those years. Patrons were paid **$3,500** total, split nine ways ("did not buy a hay rake" for her father's share). [CONFLICTS with r02/r05/r07/r08, all of which give the arbitration award as $3,800.]
- Vira's own explanation: "He was taking the cream. They caught him at it and they put him out, and that is why the numbers went right the summer after." She says this is not because Keddie was wicked, but because he stood alone in the room with the separator running, unsupervised. Her father wouldn't say it outright — called Keddie "an honest man who had got into something," which Vira says amounts to the same thing.
- Keddie died in the 1930s, still under the cloud/suspicion. Orra outlived him a good while, carrying the same stigma.
- In 1958, a young woman from a college "away east" came to Ostrey Hollow asking about all this; she poked around the old (by then shut) station building — used for storing fence wire and salt. She sat where the interviewer/Vira sat. She was "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed" — related to the family on both sides. Vira told her about the letter in the drawer; doesn't know what she made of it. [This is almost certainly Ivy Keddie's 1958 research visit, described from r04/r07 — note Vira frames the visitor as connected to the Brant family via Hazel Brant, while r04 reveals the visitor was Ansel Keddie's own granddaughter, Ivy Keddie, and r07 also names "Miss Hazel Brant" as a later owner (1962) of Item 4, the farm weigh book.]
- Vira closes: it rained the day the letter came; she's never separated the memory of the rain from the letter in 60 years.

## Retelling 02 — Effie Loomis, Association secretary 1901–1940, "Notes toward a history of the Association," written 1941 (the year after she gave up the minute book).
- Written in numbered sections, distinguishing what comes from the minutes vs. her own recollection.
- **§1 Organizing**: 1889 — farmers of Larrow Valley met at Larrow Green, organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose elected first president; held office until 1911 (22 years), longest of any president. Effie was 14 in 1889; became secretary in 1901; for 1889–1901 she only knows the Association from its papers. The 1889–1892 minute book was LOST when the office was moved in 1938 (carried out with the rest, "did not arrive"; searched everywhere in the building; never found).
- **§2 Creamery**: Main creamery built at Larrow Green in 1891 on Larrow Creek (same building still standing as of 1941). Before it, no common test/price — "every man's arithmetic was his own." She calls it "a plain remedy for a plain grievance."
- **§3 By-laws / Article VII**: Gideon Vose drafted the by-laws himself (not a lawyer). Article VII adopted at the annual meeting of 1892 (year after creamery opened). Full text (quoted):
  > "Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."
  - Effie stresses the last clause — "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" — no appeal, no re-test elsewhere, no average. Calls this clause "the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business." Written by Gideon Vose, who died in 1917 (so couldn't be asked about it later).
- **§4 Officers in her time**: Effie secretary 1901–1940 (kept minute book by hand). Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper, came in 1905 (kept the ledgers). Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green (kept churn book and cream receiving book). Selby Vose, "son of the late president" [Gideon], made manager in 1913, served until 1938. Effie notes: none of the four of them ever personally went up to Ostrey Hollow to watch the work on a working morning — "I include myself first in that."
- **§5 Station**: Skimming station, 7 miles up valley at head of the Hollow; took milk from farms up there, separated it, sent cream down to Larrow Green, returned skim to patrons. Best milk came off Hessel Bottom (wet meadow below the hamlet). 7 farms had rights on Hessel Bottom. Emmet Brant's farm was the largest of the seven and the station's largest patron every year on record.
- **§6 Petition of 1925 and arbitration**: January 1925 — Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned the Association, claiming their milk had never tested as well as their neighbors' and they'd been underpaid accordingly for a long time; asked to be made whole. Board of arbitration sat at Ashlin under Alonzo Frick, attorney, from **17 March to 4 May 1925**. Three explanations were argued before the board; none accepted; not detailed here (in the minutes, in the safe). Award (quoted, recorded by Effie herself on the last day):
  > "The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account."
  - Effie notes the irony/tension of "finds no cause established" immediately followed by an $3,800 award — "it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once."
- **§7 Quantities (from Teague's ledgers)**: Over the 25 years the patrons' claim covered, the station received **40,000,000 lb of milk** total, averaging **1,600,000 lb/year**. Average price paid for butterfat over the period: **25 cents/lb**. Effie says these are Teague's figures from the annual sheets; she added the columns twice but "does not vouch for the cows."
- **§8 What the record lacks**: Two gaps: (1) the lost 1889–1892 minute book (see above); (2) "the crate" — the crate of measures (pipettes) condemned by the state inspector in **June 1923**, replaced with new ones — was NEVER mentioned at all in the 1925 hearing, in any of the 3 sessions, by patrons, officers, or the board. The award's phrase "the books and appliances of the station" was "a form of words... not a list of anything." Effie's closing remark: "the interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying."

## Retelling 03 — Ansel Keddie, tester at Ostrey Hollow; (I) his 9-sentence statement to the board of arbitration, Ashlin, 17 March 1925 (taken down verbatim); (II) an unsent letter written at Ostrey Hollow later that spring, found among his papers after his death.
- **I. Statement (quoted in full)**: He kept the test book in his own hand since the station opened; every figure is his; errors are crossed out and initialed, never overwritten. Tested in the same order/same hour every working day/season. Neither patrons nor officers ever came and watched him work. "I am asked to account for figures, and the figures are what I read and what I wrote and nothing besides." Tested "thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons." Cannot explain the figures. Denies ever taking a pound of cream from the station, and says no one in the room privately believes he did.
- **II. Unsent letter** — his own detailed description of the testing procedure:
  - Day began at 5:30 am; wagons came up in the dark most of the year; intake room open, lamps lit, scale swinging free before first wagon arrived.
  - Every can weighed and weight recorded before milk went anywhere; scale tested true by the state's man on rounds; in 29 seasons no patron ever disputed a weight and was right.
  - Sample taken from each patron's milk as weighed, placed in his own numbered jar, made up over 10 days before testing.
  - On testing morning: jar warmed in warm water until blood-warm throughout, poured vessel to vessel repeatedly to mix cream through.
  - Charge: milk drawn to the mark on the measure's stem, tip wiped, blown into the bottle bearing the patron's number; then acid poured down the side, bottle turned until milk darkened; four bottles in the hand tester, weighted against each other, crank turned at taught speed, never hurried.
  - Fat read off in percent against the rulings using a pair of dividers, then written down.
  - "The glass I began on in ninety-six was borrowed off the Ashlin creamery, and we used it until our own outfit came up the valley in a crate the spring following [i.e., spring 1897]."
  - Book kept in 5 columns: date, patron, pounds, test, fat. Tested 29 seasons, **1896 through 1924** — did NOT test in 1925 (put out before that season).
  - First leaf of the book (quoted): "Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie."
  - Averages leaf, made 1922 (quoted): "Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80."
  - The 1911 anomaly (quoted entries): "May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." / "July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." Measure broke 8 May 1911; borrowed measure used until 10 July 1911 (roughly 9 weeks — matches his summary leaf's "nine weeks"); tests read 3.80 during that period, reverting to ~3.61 the same day the station's own measure returned. He calls this "the strangest thing in my book," never explained; his own question mark stood unanswered for 14 years (i.e., as of ~1925/1938 writing... actually he's writing in spring 1925, so 14 years after 1911).
  - He stresses the discrepancy was constant regardless of season (July same as February, wet year same as dry) — i.e., not seasonal/feed-related. In 1924, station average was 3.80 (his own final season's recorded figure).
  - History of the grievance (his account): complaints "began in 1902" that Ostrey Hollow milk didn't test with the rest. In 1908 a committee came up, sampled herds through the winter, and reported the wet hay of the Bottom thinned the cream, blaming the land. In 1919 a circular went from the Association to the 9 families saying there was water in their cans. He states he carries no charge against whoever signed that (Selby Vose, per other retellings), but insists he knows what came into that station every morning and that "nine kitchens in this Hollow have had that letter in a drawer ever since" [echoes/confirms Vira Toland's account].
  - His wife Orra is Emmet Brant's sister; Emmet was his largest patron; Keddie personally weighed and tested every pound of Emmet's milk. He and Emmet have not spoken since the circular (1919) — confirms Vira's account of the family rift, from Keddie's own side.
  - He was "put out on the thirtieth of April" [1925]. States plainly the contradiction: "They found no cause established and they put me out on the last day of the month, and both of those things are true at once." Ends: "I have tested as I was taught. I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are."

## Retelling 04 — Ivy Keddie, granddaughter of Ansel Keddie (her father was Duncan Keddie, Ansel's son, a schoolteacher at Ashlin); talk "What the Crate Held," given to the Ordell County Historical Society at Ashlin, 1959, read from typescript with the crate itself displayed.
- Ivy identifies herself as Ansel Keddie's granddaughter but insists her findings rest on evidence ("nine pieces of glass and a burette"), not family loyalty.
- Five record sets consulted, none of which had ever been assembled together before 1958:
  1. The station test book — in the Association's office since 1925 [i.e., since it was impounded by the arbitration board], came to the Historical Society's Rooms in 1959 (per r07 provenance).
  2. The Brant weigh book, kept at Hessel Bottom from 1897, lent to Ivy by Roy Brant (Emmet Brant's son, who kept it up himself since his father died).
  3. Alder Corners Cheese Company test figures (over Redlow Ridge, in Vessey County) — copied out for Ivy by Rosalie Cudd (runs the factory, Jerome Cudd's niece); the original book has never left Vessey County; Ivy never saw it directly.
  4. Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks (state dairy inspector for the county from 1908) — in Ivy's own college library at Farland (State Agricultural College) since 1947; sat unused/unquestioned for 11 years until Ivy consulted them.
  5. The Association's own annual ledgers (present at the talk).
- **The crate**: In February 1958, Ivy went to the old station building (used for storage since closing), found in the loft over the intake room, under a tarpaulin, a wooden crate stenciled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET." Carried up there in June 1923 and untouched since. Contained, packed in straw, the station's condemned milk-test pipettes. Six dozen (72) were bought new for the station in 1897 on an invoice totaling **$77.93** for the whole outfit (pipettes, bottles, acid measure, hand tester, freight). Nine pipettes survived unbroken in the crate.
- **Measurement, 9 March 1958** (quoted): "Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for."
- Explanation: a butterfat test bottle's neck is ruled to read percent for one specific charge volume. A short charge produces a proportionally short reading. 16.72/17.6 = 0.95 exactly — every test at that station read 95% of the true value.
- Quotes Keddie's own 1922 summary leaf (same text as in r03/r07): average 3.80 in 1896, 3.61 "or within three hundredths" every year since except the nine weeks of 1911 (3.80).
- Arithmetic: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80 (true average). The gap (0.19 points) is NOT fixed — it is 5% of whatever the true value is ("nineteen hundredths is five percent of three point eight... The station did not lose a set number of points. It lost a twentieth of whatever was there").
- Over 25 full delivery years, **1898 to 1922**, the station took in **40,000,000 lb of milk** (avg 1,600,000 lb/year — matches r02's figures exactly). At the recorded 3.61%, patrons were credited with 1,444,000 lb of fat. At the true 3.80%, they had actually delivered 1,520,000 lb. Difference: **76,000 lb of butterfat**. At $0.25/lb (avg over the period, matches r02), that's **$19,000** in unpaid value. The 1925 board's award was $3,800 — "a fifth of it" ($19,000/5 = $3,800).
- Clarifies the "two percentages" confusion: 76,000 lb is 5% of the true/delivered fat (1,520,000) but 5.25% of the credited fat (1,444,000) — the Association's ledger convention naturally used the higher (5.25%) figure since it's reckoned against the already-entered number. "Neither figure is an error... But five percent is the one that is also the glass" [i.e., matches the pipette shortfall ratio exactly].
- Because the shortfall was proportional, richer milk lost more in absolute terms: a herd recorded at 4.56 was short 0.24 points; one at 3.42 was short 0.18 points — "the richer the milk, the more was taken from it."
- The 1911 anomaly explained: station's own measure broke 8 May 1911, replaced by a borrowed measure (came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon) until 10 July 1911. Same patrons/herds/pasture/season/acid/bottles/tester — only the measuring instrument changed — and the average shifted to 3.80 (the TRUE, accurate figure) for those nine weeks, then reverted to 3.61 (the flawed figure) the instant the station's own (defective) measure returned. Ivy: "No explanation of this valley's twenty-six-year anomaly survives those nine weeks except the one I have given you."
- Addresses and refutes the "wet hay" theory (from the 1908 committee, see r07 Item 11): quotes State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 214 (1911) (quoted): "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." This is four times too small to explain the ~0.19 gap, and wrong in "shape" (the observed gap was constant year-round, in winter and summer alike, whereas the wet-hay effect, if real, would appear only in winter). Also cites the Alder Corners factory's records: where its book overlaps with Hessel Bottom farmers' milk (14 seasons, 4 men), the SAME milk tested ~0.19 points higher at Alder Corners than at home — further disproving any land/feed-based cause.
- Closes by quoting Bulletin No. 471, issued **October 1958** (Professor Vaught's wording, jointly with Ivy) (quoted): "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain." [Note: this explicitly uses the dates 1 May 1897 – 12 June 1923 for the flawed-glass period, and calls the recovered surplus "station gain" in the ledgers — this directly connects to and corroborates Wilbur Teague's ledger entries in r05.]
- Timeline recap: glass condemned 1923, true cause found 1958 — 35 years. Keddie dismissed 1925, cleared 1958 — 33 years. Keddie died 1934; had been dead 24 years when the exonerating bulletin was issued (1958).

## Retelling 05 — Wilbur Teague, Association bookkeeper 1905–1945 (40 years), retired 1945; unpublished memoir "The Books of the Association," Chapter Nine, written at Ashlin, winter 1948 (three years after retiring).
- Office at Ashlin: one room over a harness shop, cold at north end, too warm at south; ledgers on a shelf; sloped desk with brass rail.
- Association had three parts in his time: creamery at Larrow Green, office at Ashlin, skimming station at Ostrey Hollow (7 miles up valley). Selby Vose was manager over the whole; Effie Loomis was secretary/kept minutes; Teague kept the ledger.
- Explains the station's business: patrons hauled milk to the station instead of driving 7 miles to Larrow Green; milk weighed, sampled, tested for butterfat; farmer paid on pounds+test; station separated the milk, sent cream to Larrow Green, returned skim to farmers for hogs/calves.
- **The "station-gain" ledger line** — Teague's own invention, begun when he started in 1905 (he explicitly denies it was "some old dodge of the founders" — it was his own bookkeeping device, because "a difference that will not be entered somewhere will come back and bite you at audit"). On one side: butterfat credited to patrons per the station's own test. On the other: butterfat actually received/tested at Larrow Green from the station's cream. The second figure was ALWAYS larger; the difference ("station gain") was credited to the Association's general account. Example entry, for 1898 (quoted): "Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account." The gain ran consistently at **5.25%** every year — so consistent the phrase "as usual" became part of the boilerplate. Teague reflects that this very consistency made him stop scrutinizing it ("A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture").
- **Teague's own aggregate figures** (NOTE: CONFLICTS with r02/r04/r07): "that station took in **forty-four million pounds** of milk" total, and "the average test of all the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, taken over the whole period... was **3.16**." [Contradicts r02/r04's 40,000,000 lb and 3.61 average, and r07's curatorial confirmation of 40,000,000 lb from the ledgers themselves — an internal inconsistency in the source material, possibly reflecting Teague's faulty/selective memory decades later, or a different date range than "1898–1922."] Larrow Green tested better; Ostrey Hollow patrons were "sore about it" every season for 25 years.
- Notes the 1911 break: station's own measure broke spring 1911; borrowed one used for **six weeks** [conflicts with r03/r04's ~nine weeks, 8 May–10 July] before their own came up from town; entered in his accounts merely as a "small charge for replacement," nothing more remarked at the time.
- Repeats what he told the 1925 board of arbitration (his own words, unchanged since): cream is portable; the station was in one pair of hands (Keddie's) for 29 seasons; one man weighed, sampled, tested, wrote the figure, and ran the separator, with no second man present spring through fall. He does not accuse Keddie of any specific act ("I do not say what any particular person did on any particular day, because I was not there and I do not know") but argues structurally that such an arrangement "will sooner or later have a little taken from it," and that the remedy (two men or a lock) was never implemented by the Association.
- Confirms: petition came in winter; board sat at Ashlin under Alonzo Frick ("as fair a chairman as ever a hearing had"); Teague testified; board found nothing established against anybody; awarded **$3,800**, charged to the station account (matches r02/r07/r08).
- Two additional claims: (1) Manager Selby Vose knew what the station-gain account showed — he signed the annual sheet every year, which Teague laid before him each spring. Vose never told Teague what he made of it. (2) Teague did raise the issue with Vose once, in **1916**, and was told to "let it alone" — and did, for the rest of his career, reasoning "I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience."

## Retelling 06 — Merle Strawn, retired state dairy inspector for the Ordell circuit (1907–1936, i.e. laid down the circuit in 1936); "Extracts from twenty-eight circuit books," copied out spring 1936 for the state dairy office, with a covering note.
- Covering note: circuit covered ~50-odd plants across 4 counties (creameries, cheese factories, skimming stations, small butter operations); traveled by train/livery/on foot depending on season and roads. His fixed routine at every plant, in order: (1) scales, checked against his own sealed weights, both intake and cream scales, light and heavy loads; (2) lactometer test on the day's milk for added water; (3) boiler, water supply, cold room; (4) watched the tester perform an actual test start-to-finish, without letting him hurry. States he recorded only observations, never opinions ("every time in my life I have seen a field man paid for an opinion instead, the opinion has outlived the weighing and done harm after the man was dead").
- Year-by-year extracts on Ostrey Hollow (selected/relevant):
  - **1907** (first round of new circuit): station "opened before my time" [i.e., before 1907 — consistent with the 1896 founding date elsewhere]. Scales true. Tester (Keddie) keeps his book well, works slow and clean, reads figures unprompted.
  - **1909**: scales true; boiler in order; one man cautioned about a rusted seam on a can.
  - **1911, June** (on his way to the other side of the county — this visit falls WITHIN the 8 May–10 July 1911 anomaly window described in r03/r04!): "Scales true. Watched a test through. Nothing here to note." [He did NOT detect anything unusual during the very period when — per Ivy Keddie's later analysis — the tests were actually reading TRUE/accurate (3.80) rather than the usual flawed 3.61. He noticed nothing amiss because everything about the test procedure looked normal; he had no way to know the everyday glass was defective, since a borrowed correct measure was in use at that very time.]
  - **1912**: scales true; no added water; cream shipped down same day as separated ("more than some do").
  - **1913**: at Larrow Green (not Ostrey Hollow this entry) — new Association manager (Selby Vose) came out to the platform with him, "which the last one never did."
  - **1915**: wash room floor ordered graded/fixed.
  - **1916**: tester (Keddie) complains patrons blame him for their figures; Strawn told him "that is the trade" and Keddie agreed.
  - **1919**: notes the manager (Vose) "has sent a circular round to the patrons of this station about water in the cans." Strawn states flatly: "I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so." [Directly undercuts/contradicts the premise of Vose's 1919 circular and his 1926 address defense of it.]
  - **1920**: visited twice (spring & fall); no added water either time; cold room short of ice in fall, fixed before he left the county.
  - **1921**: state begins issuing calibrated burettes to field men (in wooden cases) for checking delivery of plant glass/pipettes against the true mark. Strawn received his in spring 1921.
  - **1922**: did NOT run the Ostrey Hollow glass against his new burette this year (day too short, road home bad) — explicitly notes this "because it is what happened." [This is the near-miss — one year before the pipettes were finally checked and condemned.]
  - **1923, 12 June** (quoted, matches r07 Item 9's photostat exactly): "Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk pipettes against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them." [Note: this date, 12 June 1923, conflicts with Selby Vose's 1926 address, which states the apparatus was condemned "on the twelfth of July, 1923" — a one-month discrepancy; Strawn's contemporaneous notebook and Ivy Keddie's bulletin both independently corroborate 12 JUNE, making Vose's "July" date likely an error or misstatement.]
  - **1923, October**: new glass in use since July 1923; ran new pipettes against the burette — they deliver their mark correctly. Scales true.
  - **1924**: scales true, no added water, "tests improved"; the station's own book gives the year's average as **3.80** [conflicts with Vose's 1926 claim of 3.90 for 1924 — see r08].
  - **1925, fall**: tests still improved, no added water; notes a NEW man at the tester's bench this year (Keddie's replacement) — "he is quick, and quick is not the thing I look for."
  - **1926**: state discontinues the burette program this year; inspectors keep their own burettes but no more issued, none to be returned. Strawn's used only twice since.
  - **1928**: "loft over the intake room is full of odds and ends" — told them a loft should hold nothing but air; told it "had been full since before the war" [this loft is where Ivy Keddie found the crate in 1958].
  - **1931**: bad ice year across all four counties.
  - **1934**: separator bearing ran hot, shut down 2 days, since repaired; tester's book "kept up, though not in the hand it used to be kept in" [i.e., not Keddie's hand — he'd died that year].
  - **1936, March** (last round): nothing to note anywhere.
- Closing note: 28 pocket-sized oilcloth-covered books (one per year, 1907–1936... though his extracts start at 1907) submitted to the state dairy office per the rules on retiring from a circuit. Insists he "never had a theory about a plant" and doesn't recommend having one — "A theory is a thing a man carries out to the platform with him and it gets in the way of the weights."

## Retelling 07 — Halbert Nym, curator, accession notes for the Ordell County dairy collection, Ordell County Historical Rooms, 1972; catalogue items 1–16 with exact transcriptions and curatorial glosses.

## Retelling 08 — Selby Vose, manager of the Association, annual address to the membership, 1926 (printed in that year's annual report).
- Vose has been manager 13 years as of spring 1926, i.e., since **1913** (matches r02's statement that he was made manager in 1913, served until 1938).
- Calls the past year's business (referring to 1925, the arbitration year) the one he "liked least to report on" but was "gladdest to have behind" the Association.
- Ordinary business: wet season into June; heavy hauling; Larrow Green creamery ran the flush without losing a day; butter held its market position; ice supply good; no complaints about staff; the Ostrey Hollow station "ran its season out and its cream came down to us daily and sweet."
- Recaps the 1925 affair for the membership: January 1925 petition; board of arbitration at Ashlin under Alonzo Frick ("an able and impartial man"); board heard everyone; found "no cause established" against the station, the office, or any officer/servant of the Association. Vose stresses this finding was reached "by men under oath with the books in front of them."
- Despite "no cause established," the board awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons **$3,800**, without admission, charged to the station account (matches other sources). Vose says he supported the payment then and still does — calls it "a generous settlement of an unproved claim," done to "keep peace among its own members," since the Association is "the farmers of this valley in company together," not an ordinary business.
- Vose then gives his OWN theory of the cause, contradicting both Keddie's innocence claim and (implicitly) the eventual 1958 pipette explanation: he insists "water was going into cans at the head of that valley and it stopped when the Association began to look." Cites the "rise of 1924" as proof: after the petition/hearing process began, tests at the station "rose by twenty-one hundredths and the average for 1924 stood at **3.90**" [conflicting with Keddie's own book, quoted in r03/r04/r07, which gives the 1924 average as **3.80**, and with Strawn's 1924 circuit note also confirming "3.80" per the station's own book — so Vose's 3.90 figure appears to be his own error or exaggeration]. Argues nothing changed about the cows, feed, or pasture, so the change must reflect patrons no longer being able to get away with adding water once scrutiny increased.
- Addresses (and dismisses) the alternative "state man's visit" explanation: acknowledges the state inspector (Strawn) tried the scales (found true) and "condemned the station's testing apparatus on the twelfth of July, 1923" [NOTE: this date directly conflicts with Strawn's own contemporaneous notebook entry and Ivy Keddie's bulletin, both of which independently give **12 June 1923** — Vose's "twelfth of July" appears to be an error, or possibly a conflation with the 2 July 1923 Ammon & Sons acknowledgment letter mentioned in r07 Item 10]. States new apparatus was ordered within the month and in use before the flush was over. Vose argues wear-and-tear on apparatus in continuous use since 1896 is mere "housekeeping," not a "mystery," and specifically REJECTS the idea that faulty equipment explains the low tests — ironic in light of the 1958 bulletin's ultimate finding that faulty pipettes were exactly the cause.
- States he does not know and would not name any individual patron he suspects of watering milk; insists no one has been or will be prosecuted; frames added water as a common, low-level, non-criminal temptation ("a way of making a hard year come out even").
- Explicitly defends and takes ownership of the 1919 circular: "I wrote it because it was my duty to write it. It warned the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, in temperate language, that added water would be looked for and would be dealt with. It named nobody... I would write every word of it again tomorrow." This confirms Selby Vose as the author/signer of the 1919 "water in the cans" letter (as stated by Vira Toland in r01 and referenced in Keddie's letter in r03).
- Personal/family detail: his father was "your first president" (i.e., Gideon Vose) — this makes Selby Vose Gideon's SON, contradicting Vira Toland's claim in r01 that Selby was Gideon's NEPHEW (r02 also calls him "son of the late president," so r01 is the outlier/likely mistaken on this point, or reflects imprecise village gossip). Gideon Vose "wrote the by-law under which every patron... is paid on the test of the station where he delivers" (i.e., Article VII) — matches r02's attribution.
- Vose's father kept a herd of Jerseys at the home place, later kept by Selby himself; in **1910** that herd tested **4.56** [this exact figure, 4.56, is cited in Ivy Keddie's 1959 talk (r04) as an example of a herd whose recorded test was 0.24 points short of true due to the defective pipettes — meaning Vose's own family herd, which he holds up as proof of the valley's excellent milk quality, was ITSELF a victim of the same station's flawed measurement, an irony the retelling does not point out but which the numbers imply]. Vose uses this figure to argue Larrow Valley milk/land is NOT inferior, contra a reputation "in the trade below us."
- Closing exhortation: wash cans, cool milk, keep the dog out of the milk house, "bring it as it left the cow" — asks for one more year of patience after 13 years of service, hopes to return next year to discuss butter prices instead.

## Master list of cross-retelling conflicts and oddities to track going forward
1. **Award amount**: r01 (Vira) says $3,500; r02, r05, r07, r08 all say $3,800. (r01 is the outlier.)
2. **Selby Vose's relation to Gideon Vose**: r01 says nephew; r02 and r08 say son. (r01 likely wrong.)
3. **Total station milk intake**: r02 and r04 (and r07's curatorial note on the ledgers themselves) say 40,000,000 lb (1898–1922); r05 (Teague's memoir) says 44,000,000 lb (unspecified exact range). Direct numeric conflict, flagged explicitly by the curator in r07.
4. **Average butterfat test figure**: r02/r03/r04/r06/r07 consistently show recorded average as 3.61 (with 1896 at 3.80, and the true corrected value also 3.80 per Ivy's 1958 analysis); r05 (Teague) instead states the average was 3.16 — a further, unreconciled conflict.
5. **Duration of the 1911 measure-substitution anomaly**: r03 (Keddie's letter, contemporaneous entries) and r04 (Ivy's analysis) give 8 May–10 July 1911 (~9 weeks, matching Keddie's own "nine weeks" summary); r05 (Teague) says "six weeks."
6. **Date the pipettes were condemned**: r06 (Strawn's notebook, contemporaneous) and r07 Item 9 (photostat of same) and r04/r07 Item 15 (Bulletin 471) all agree on **12 June 1923**; r08 (Vose's 1926 address) says "the twelfth of July, 1923" — a one-month discrepancy, with Vose almost certainly wrong (or conflating it with the 2 July replacement-order acknowledgment, r07 Item 10).
7. **1924 average test value**: Keddie's own book (quoted in r03/r04/r07) and Strawn's 1924 circuit note (r06) both give **3.80**; Vose's 1926 address claims **3.90**, with a stated "rise" of "twenty-one hundredths" that doesn't arithmetically reconcile with the other documented figures (3.61+0.21=3.82, not 3.90; and the actual documented value for 1924 is 3.80, not 3.90).
8. **Cause of the low tests — three competing/sequential theories**: (a) 1908 committee: wet hay of Hessel Bottom thins the cream (r07 Item 11); (b) 1919 Association circular / Vose's 1926 address: patrons were adding water to the milk (r01, r03, r08); (c) Vira Toland's account (r01) and Teague's structural suspicion (r05): Keddie himself was skimming/stealing cream; (d) the definitive 1958 finding (Ivy Keddie/Bulletin 471, r04/r07): none of the above — the station's 1897 pipettes were mechanically short by 5%, producing a constant proportional under-reading; no water added, no cream stolen. Merle Strawn's inspection notes (r06) independently corroborate that he never found added water at any visit, undermining theory (b); the 1911 accuracy-during-substitution episode undermines theories (a) and (c) since nothing about patron behavior or feed changed, only the measuring instrument.
9. **Location where the crate was found in 1958**: Ivy Keddie's own talk (r04) says she found it in the station building's loft at Ostrey Hollow, under a tarpaulin; the curator's accession note (r07, Item 5) says it was found "in the Association office at Ashlin." These two primary/near-primary sources disagree on the find location.
10. **Curatorial labeling oddity in r07**: Item 3 (station test book, clearly Ansel Keddie's, signed "A. Keddie") is introduced by the curator as "kept by E. Brant," and Item 4 (farm weigh book from Hessel Bottom, provenance shows it passed through the Brant family/Roy Brant/Hazel Brant) is introduced as "kept by A. Keddie" — the two attributions appear to be swapped/possibly a cataloguing error, since the internal content and provenance details point the other way (Item 3 = Keddie's station book; Item 4 = a Brant family farm book).
11. **Timing/memory oddity**: Vira Toland (r01) recalls the visiting researcher in 1958 as connected to the Brant family ("Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed"), consistent with Ivy Keddie's real family being the Keddies — she may be conflating the researcher's identity, or there may be an actual Keddie-Brant kinship link via Ivy's family not otherwise spelled out (note Orra Keddie was a Brant by birth, so Ivy Keddie, as Ansel and Orra's granddaughter, WOULD also be a blood relation of the Brants — potentially reconciling this apparent conflict rather than contradicting it).
