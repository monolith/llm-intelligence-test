## Story 1 — The Ostrey Hollow Shortfall

**Who:** Ansel Keddie (1861–1934), tester and separator man at the Larrow Valley Cooperative
Creamery Association's Ostrey Hollow skimming station from its opening in 1896 until his
dismissal in 1925; his wife Orra (née Brant, b. 1866); their son Duncan (b. 1894); Duncan's
daughter Ivy Keddie (b. 1931), who as a graduate student at the State Agricultural College
cleared her grandfather's name in 1958; and the patrons of Hessel Bottom, six farms on wet
ground, chief among them Orra's brother Emmet Brant.

**What happened:** Every patron at Ostrey Hollow was paid by butterfat test, not by milk weight
(by-law article seven). From very close to the station's opening its tests ran a flat 0.19 points
below comparable stations, in every season alike — a pattern inconsistent with a seasonal cause
like feed. Three explanations circulated for decades: added water (refuted — the state's
lactometer never once found watered milk from 1908 on); the tester stealing cream (refuted by a
natural experiment: in 1911 the station's own measuring glass broke and a borrowed measure was
used for nine weeks, during which the same tester's figures rose, falling back the moment his own
glass returned); and thin cream from Hessel Bottom's wet hay (credited by an Association
committee in 1908, but later shown by a college feeding trial to account for at most 0.05 points
in winter and nothing in summer — nowhere near enough). The real cause, discovered only in 1958
when Ivy Keddie found the station's condemned glassware — 1897 pipettes marked to deliver 17.6
cc, actually delivering on average about 16.27 cc — in a loft crate untouched since a state
inspector condemned it in 1923: the pipettes had always run short, so the test read low in exact
proportion, for as long as that glass was used.

**When:** Glass purchased and put into service May 1, 1897; deficiency first formally complained
of 1902; 1911 borrowed-glass interval May 8–July 10; glass condemned 1923, replaced within the
month; board of arbitration sat at Ashlin March–May 1925, dismissed Keddie April 30, 1925 (four
days before its finding), awarded patrons $3,800 without admission of cause; Keddie died 1934,
still under suspicion; Ivy found the crate February 1958 and measured it that March; the college
bulletin clearing him appeared October 1958.

**Quantities:** 0.19-point test deficiency; 17.6 cc marked vs. ~16.27 cc actual pipette delivery
(~7.6% short); $3,800 awarded; $19,600 the bulletin calculated as the patrons' true uncompensated
loss; original outfit invoice $77.93 (see Section E for a documented error in this figure).

**Documents bearing on it:** all of r17–r24.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?**

Ivy was his **granddaughter**. Duncan Keddie's own family notes (r18) are explicit and
first-hand: Duncan, born 1894, is Ansel and Orra's son; Duncan states plainly "Ivy, born in 1931,
which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice
seen it printed otherwise." The *Ashlin Chronicle* (r19), working from the 1958 college bulletin,
called her Ansel's "great-niece" instead. The county agent's report (r22) repeats the "great-niece"
error on its first printing but then issues a formal correction: "I am informed, and have
verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is
mine." I resolve this in favor of granddaughter on two grounds: a direct family member's own
genealogical notes, and an independent source's explicit, verified correction of the opposite
claim — outranking the uncorrected newspaper account, which drew the error from the same
underlying confusion.

**B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant?**

They were **second cousins** — children of first cousins. Two independent family-insider accounts
agree and show their work: Hazel (r17) states Duncan and her father Roy were first cousins (sons
of a brother, Emmet Brant, and a sister, Orra Brant Keddie), making Ivy (Duncan's daughter) and
Hazel (Roy's daughter) second cousins, and she is explicit that this is not "cousins once removed"
or "aunt and niece." Duncan (r18) gives the identical derivation independently. Against this, Cleve
Oram (r21), an outside oral source, calls them "first cousin once removed," but he hedges his own
family knowledge elsewhere ("I could not draw you the line on paper") and is outnumbered by two
independent, better-positioned sources who did the arithmetic explicitly and agree. The sources
leave nothing open about the intervening generations — the parent-generation cousinship (Roy and
Duncan) is independently confirmed by both insiders.

**B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter?**

Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**, who organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery
Association and was its first president. This is confirmed independently by Duncan Keddie (r18: "Selby
was his son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew") and by the Association's own longtime
clerk Bertram Kyle (r23: "the son, whatever is said at Ashlin... I have heard the nephew story
twice in the last year and it is nonsense"). It matters because Selby Vose was the manager who, in
1919, personally circulated the warning accusing Ostrey Hollow patrons of watering their milk, and
who signed the station's ledger sheets every year without (per Kyle) reading the lines above the
bottom figure. His parentage establishes his position and institutional authority within the
Association he ran for a quarter-century, and rules out a family tie to the Keddies through the
Vose line (see B4) as an explanation for anything that happened at the station.

**B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?**

**None**, so far as the sources establish. Duncan Keddie (r18) addresses this directly and at
length: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses... It is talk, and it has been
talk since before I could walk," adding that his mother, who tracked every family connection in
the valley, never once linked the families. Against this stands only a secondhand rumor from
Cleve Oram (r21): "the Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort... I could not draw you the
line on paper." I resolve this in favor of no connection: Duncan is a direct family member who
explicitly investigated and rejects the claim, while Cleve explicitly disclaims confidence in his
own version. This is a case of a narrator's memory (rumor, unverifiable) losing to a family
insider's documented denial.

## Section C — Math

**C1.**
(a) From the glassware itself: pipettes marked to deliver 17.6 cc actually delivered, on average
(9 survivors measured in 1958), 16.27 cc (r19). Shortfall = (17.6 − 16.27)/17.6 ≈ **7.56% low**.
Because "a short charge of milk into a bottle whose neck is ruled for the full charge yields a
reading short in the same proportion" (r19), the fat test read low by that same proportion. As a
cross-check from an independent route: the clerk Bertram Kyle (r23), with no idea it bore on the
scandal, recorded that the "station gain" line in the ledger ran a constant 5.25% every year — and
0.19/3.61 (the point-deficiency divided by the station's own recorded average, r22 and r17) =
5.26%, matching Kyle's bookkeeping figure almost exactly. The cc-based 7.56% and the
ledger/point-based ~5.25–5.26% are in the same direction and order of magnitude but do not match
exactly; the sources give no formula reconciling test-bottle-neck calibration with pipette
capacity, so I report both rather than force one onto the other.

(b) Recorded station average: 3.61 (r22). Stated deficiency: "nineteen hundredths" (0.19),
confirmed independently by Hazel Brant's own family weigh-book figures (r17) and by the county
report (r22). True average = 3.61 + 0.19 = **3.80**. (A separate comparison figure, the Larrow
Green creamery's own average of 3.78, is offered in r22 as a benchmark, not as Ostrey Hollow's own
true value — I have not conflated the two.)

(c) Two figures exist, from two non-overlapping single sources, and I cannot cross-verify them:
Cleve Oram (r21, oral, secondhand — "that is what they make it") gives **67,000 lb** of butterfat
over the run of years. The bulletin, via the *Ashlin Chronicle* (r19), gives a dollar figure
instead of a pound figure (see (d)). Implied price per pound from those two figures ($19,600 /
67,000 lb ≈ $0.29/lb) does not match Kyle's independent multi-decade average price of $0.25/lb
(r23) closely enough to confirm either figure against the other. **Not fully determinable** with
confidence from these sources; I give 67,000 lb as the only pound figure offered, flagged as
single-sourced and oral.

(d) Money: **$19,600** (r19, from the 1958 bulletin). Patrons actually received the $3,800
arbitration award — **$3,800 / $19,600 ≈ 19.4%** of what the bulletin later calculated they were
owed.

**C2. (Emmet Brant, the largest patron, year 1910 — r17):**
(a) 200,000 lb milk delivered, tested at 3.42 → credited butterfat = 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840
lb**.
(b) True test (using the established 0.19-point deficiency): 3.42 + 0.19 = 3.61 → true butterfat =
200,000 × 0.0361 = 7,220 lb. Owed and not credited = 7,220 − 6,840 = **380 lb**.
(c) Money value: no 1910-specific butterfat price is given anywhere in r17–r24. Using Kyle's only
available price figure, a multi-decade average of $0.25/lb (r23, explicitly "taking the fat years
with the lean" — not a 1910 spot price), 380 × $0.25 ≈ **$95**, offered as a rough estimate only,
not a documented 1910 figure.

**C3.**
(a) and (b): **Not determinable** from r17–r24 as separate totals. The only figure the sources
give for this specific comparison (credited-at-station vs. received-at-Larrow-Green) is the
resulting percentage itself, from Kyle's ledger memory (r23): "station gain... five and a quarter
per cent, as usual."
(c) **5.25%**, per Kyle's ledger line — taken, per his own wording, as a percentage of the
butterfat credited to the patrons at the station (the smaller of the two figures, with the "gain"
being the excess found at Larrow Green). No absolute totals are given, so I cannot state what this
percentage was calculated of in original pounds — only the ratio itself is preserved in the
sources.

**C4.**
(a) The invoice total, correctly summed from its own line items (25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 +
2.15), is **$77.93** (r20, quoted document). Note: three separate places in the sources —
Hazel Brant (r17), the *Ashlin Chronicle* (r19), and even Junia Ammon's own prose in r20
immediately below her own reproduced invoice — state the total as "seventy-seven dollars and
thirty-nine cents" ($77.39). This is wrong; see Section E.
(b) The invoice bought **72 pipettes** (6 dozen, r20), each marked for 17.6 cc. Average shortfall
per pipette (based on the 9 survivors measured in 1958): 17.6 − 16.27 = **1.33 cc short**.

**C5.**
(a) The faulty glass was in continuous-enough use from May 1, 1897 until replacement in July 1923
— **about 26 years** (r20 itself computes this: "six and twenty years afterward, in the summer of
1923"), interrupted only by the nine-week borrowed-glass interval in 1911 (see D1). I cannot find
an explicit statement in r17–r24 of how many "full delivery years" the money calculation in the
bulletin actually runs over; my inference is that a money calculation would likely exclude the
partial first season (from May 1897) and partial final season (through mid-1923), giving
something closer to **25 full delivery years** (1898–1922) — but this is my inference, not a
stated figure, and I flag it as such. The two numbers would differ because the physical
duration of faulty-glass use includes two partial calendar seasons that a "full delivery year"
accounting convention would not count as whole years.
(b) The station's first season's milk intake is given only once, and only as personal recollection,
not from any book: Bertram Kyle (r23), "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds," which he
explicitly says is "not in any book I ever ruled" — **1,100,000 lb, single-sourced and
unverifiable**.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The station's own glass broke and a borrowed measure was used from **May 8 to July 10,
1911** (nine weeks; dated precisely in r22, and the breakage is independently confirmed the same
day in Orra Keddie's diary, r24: "A. home cross about a broken measure"). Treated as an
experiment: the variable was the measuring glass itself (own vs. borrowed); held constant were the
tester, the herds, the season, and the level of supervision. Result: the tests rose measurably
while the borrowed measure was in use, and fell back to the old level the moment the station's own
glass resumed (r22). This establishes **causation**, not merely correlation — it shows in real
time, decades before any calibration was possible, that the low readings tracked the equipment and
not the tester's behavior or the season, ruling out theft and feed-based theories contemporaneously.
What it does **not** establish: the exact magnitude of the error (no source quotes the actual
1911 test figures, only that they were "markedly higher"), nor, on its own, which piece of glass
was the accurate one — that direction was only confirmed later, in 1958, by calibration against a
burette.

**D2.** Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream:
1. The 1911 swapped-glass experiment (D1, from r22/r24): identical tester and conditions, changed
only the equipment, and the results changed — inconsistent with the tester himself controlling
the outcome by theft.
2. The deficiency was flat and season-independent — "nineteen hundredths lower... every month,
every season, wet years and dry... not a little in the winter and none in the summer, the way a
feed thing goes" (Hazel Brant, r17). A dishonest man varying his theft with opportunity or need
would be expected to produce a variable pattern, not a mechanically constant one.
3. Character and circumstance, from Duncan Keddie's family notes (r18): Ansel tested his own
wife's brother Emmet Brant's milk — the largest hauler on the bottom, a man at his table "on a
Sunday... for thirty-six years" — at the same shorted rate as everyone else, for a generation; and
he was, by his son's account, so scrupulous that he refused to write down even his own suspicion
about the glass in 1911 without proof, recording only a question mark.

Reasons 2 and 3 do not depend on the glass ever being measured — they hold even in a world where
Ivy Keddie never found the crate in 1958. Reason 1 also does not depend on the 1958 measurement,
though it converges with it.

The bookkeeper never questioned the entries. Bertram Kyle (r23) states it directly: he copied the
"station gain... as usual" line for years without wondering at it, and of Wilbur Teague, who wrote
those lines for forty years, "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years."

**D3.** The manager (Selby Vose) knew the tests ran low in a general sense — he personally signed
and circulated the 1919 warning accusing patrons of watering their milk, which presupposes he knew
something was depressing the readings. But the sources indicate he did not connect this to, or
scrutinize, the ledger entries that would have revealed its systemic and constant nature: Kyle
(r23) states plainly that Vose "read the totals and he never read the lines above them... not a
man who read... take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign." So: aware of
the symptom, not shown by any source to have understood — or looked for — its cause in the books
he signed every year.

handled the payment, against a single secondhand oral figure.
- **Hearing/award year:** 1925 (r18 implied by dismissal date, r22 explicit with full date range,
r24 diary with exact contemporaneous dates) vs. r17's "twenty-six" (1926) and r23's "twenty-four"

- **Two narrators carrying the same wrong value:** the invoice total. Hazel Brant (r17) and the

- **A narrator contradicting itself:** r20 states "the house sold nothing into Ordell County

## Section F — Theory

The shortfall was caused by pipettes bought new in 1897 that were blown short of their marked
capacity (17.6 cc marked, ~16.27 cc actual), an undetected manufacturing fault Junia Ammon (r20)
attributes to a reground mold running consistently short — invisible to spot-checks because a
short lot is short every time, "consistently wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind
cannot find." This explains the size of the error (it is exactly the glass's own proportional
shortfall, carried straight through into the fat reading); why it began in May 1897 (when that
glass first went into service, replacing borrowed, presumably accurate glass); why it ended in
1923 (when a routine state calibration check caught it and it was replaced, after which the
station's average rose and stayed up); and why nothing on the farms or at the station needed to
change for either — the 1911 natural experiment already showed that swapping only the glass
changed the readings, with herds, hay, tester, and procedure held constant throughout.

Three competing explanations, and what defeats each:
1. **Added water**, advanced by manager Selby Vose (1919 circular) — defeated by the state
inspector's own lactometer tests from 1908 on, which never once found water at the station (r22).
2. **Tester dishonesty/theft**, urged by an Association officer at the 1925 hearing — defeated by
the 1911 borrowed-glass experiment: same tester, same everything else, different (higher) results
(r22, r24).
3. **Wet hay/bog pasture**, from an Association committee (1908) and a veterinarian's letter (1926)
— defeated by the college's own feeding-trial data (Bulletin No. 214, cited in Falke's addendum,
r22): bog hay depresses the test by at most 0.05 points in winter and nothing in summer, far short
of the flat 0.19-point, year-round deficiency.

Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware? **No** — consistently across three
independent sources. The crate sat in the loft directly over two of the three arbitration
sittings, held in the station's own weigh room (r18, r21); Cleve Oram, standing outside on the
platform during one sitting, notes "the crate was up the ladder over their heads the entire time...
I have thought about that since, I will not pretend I thought about it then" (r21); and Bertram
Kyle, who personally assembled and carried in every document entered as evidence, lists exactly
four items — none of them the physical glass itself (r23).

What did the tester believe? Per his son Duncan (r18), Ansel came home in the summer of 1911
saying "it was something in the glass" — but, unwilling to assert what he could not prove, he
never wrote it in the book as a finding, entering only a question mark, and said nothing more of
it under oath.

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

For roughly twenty-six years, a Larrow Valley creamery station paid its farmers on a milk-fat test
that ran a flat 0.19 points low, and for just as long the valley blamed the farmers (for watering
milk) or the tester, Ansel Keddie (for stealing cream) — both theories eventually disproved, the
second by a 1911 accident in which a borrowed measure briefly gave correct readings. The true
cause, a 1897 batch of test glass blown slightly short of its marked capacity, sat condemned and
forgotten in a crate over the very room where a 1925 hearing dismissed Keddie without ever looking
up at it. Only in 1958 did his granddaughter, a graduate student, climb the ladder, measure the
glass, and clear his name — twenty-four years after his death. It took eight documents because
each witness — clerk, hauler, diarist, newspaper, county agent — held one piece and got other
pieces wrong.
