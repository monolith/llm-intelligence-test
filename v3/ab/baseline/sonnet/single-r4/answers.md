# Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

Method note: throughout, "resolved by document" means a quoted primary record (invoice,
diary, minute book, circuit book, measurement sheet) was preferred over a later memory;
"resolved by arithmetic" means two or more attested figures were cross-checked against each
other and one reproduced the others exactly; "resolved by independence" means a claim
repeated by two sources was downweighted once shown to trace to one underlying source.

## Story 1 — The Founding and the Payment-by-Test Rule

**Who/what:** The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was organized in 1889 by
farmers meeting at the Larrow Green schoolhouse (r02, r12, r22, all agreeing). Gideon Vose was
the first president and wrote the by-laws himself (r02, r12, r18, r23). The main creamery was
built at Larrow Green in 1891 (r02, r12, r22 agree). Article VII of the by-laws established
payment by butterfat test rather than by milk weight — the rule that made a testing error into
a financial injury rather than a curiosity.

**When:** Founded 1889; creamery built 1891. The date Article VII was *adopted* is genuinely
contested: 1892 (r02, Effie Loomis, secretary from 1901 with access to early records), 1891
(r12, Dell Rundle's pamphlet, claiming to reprint the article's own dateline), 1893 (r15, Nyle
Grigg, "as I have always understood it" — an explicit personal recollection, not a document
check). The Association's own 1889–1892 minute book is reported lost (r02, r22), so no primary
document survives to settle this. Orin Falke's 1959 report (r22), the most careful
investigation in the set, explicitly declines to resolve it: the printed by-laws "carry no date
upon their face." **This is left unresolved** — three narrators, no surviving primary record,
and the most rigorous investigator's own refusal to guess.

**Quantities:** No disputed quantities attach to this story itself, only the adoption date.

**Documents:** r02 (Loomis), r12 (Rundle), r15 (Grigg, in passing), r18 (Duncan Keddie, on
Gideon/Selby Vose), r22 (Falke, declining to resolve), r23 (Kyle, confirming Gideon as founder
and first president).

Why it matters to everything else: without payment-by-test, a chronically low reading at one
station would have cost nobody anything, and none of the other seven stories would have had
stakes.

## Story 2 — Building the Station and Its Glassware, 1895–1898

**Who/what:** The Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the
valley, over the winter of 1895–96, to serve the six farms of Hessel Bottom (r22). It opened on
4 May 1896 (r22), with Ansel Keddie as tester and separator man from the start — some sources
say he "went up" in 1895 (r17), consistent with construction beginning that year. For its first
season the station borrowed testing glass from the Larrow Green creamery; buttermaker Hobart
Sill (r14) confirms he personally lent his own bench glassware (one piece came back chipped),
matching a note in the curator's accession record (r07, Item 3: "glass borrowed of Larrow Green
until our own comes").

**When the station's own glass arrived:** the best-attested date is 1 May 1897 — given
independently by Orra Keddie's diary, written in real time ("May 1, 1897. A. says the station's
own glass came up," r24), and corroborated by the manufacturer's invoice itself, dated 14 April
1897 (r07 Item 2; r20). Two later, secondary sources place it in 1898 instead (r11, r23) — both
written decades afterward with no document cited. Resolved in favor of 1897 on the strength of
the contemporaneous diary and the invoice date.

**Quantities:** The invoice from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet, dated 14 April 1897, totaled $77.93
(itemized: 6 doz. pipettes at 17.6 c.c. rated capacity, $25.20; 12 doz. test bottles, $31.68; 1
acid measure, $0.90; 1 hand tester, $18.00; freight, $2.15 — sums exactly to $77.93). This
figure and the "six dozen" pipette count are independently confirmed by two facsimile
reproductions of the same invoice (r07 Item 2, and Ammon's own firm history r20), against a
"five dozen" claim appearing only in r11 and r21. The station's first season (May–Dec 1896, on
borrowed glass) took in "about nine hundred thousand pounds" per Sill (r14, a direct
participant), against "eleven hundred thousand pounds" in Kyle's account (r23), which Kyle
himself flags as secondhand ("not in any book I ever ruled"). Sill's figure is preferred as
firsthand.

**Documents:** r07 (Items 2–3), r11, r14, r17, r20, r22, r23, r24.

## Story 3 — The Chronic Complaint and Three Rival Theories, 1902–1926

**Who/what:** From about 1902, patrons at Ostrey Hollow complained that their milk tested lower
than milk anywhere else in the valley — a complaint raised at every annual meeting for a
quarter-century (r15, r22). Three explanations circulated and were all argued before the 1925
board of arbitration (r13): (1) watered milk, the official theory of manager Selby Vose,
publicized in a 1919 circular sent to the Hollow's patrons (r08, r24, r01, r17); (2) cream
skimming by the tester, implied at the hearing via bookkeeper Wilbur Teague's testimony (r13)
and privately believed by some patrons (r01, r21); (3) wet hay/soil chemistry, first advanced by
a three-man committee chaired by Nyle Grigg in 1908 (r15) and revived scientifically by
veterinarian Dr. Roland Kepp in a 1926 paper (r16).

**Quantities:** The recorded station average was 3.61 (best-attested; see Section C), against
Larrow Green's own average of 3.78 (r14, r22). The chronic gap is best given as "nineteen
hundredths" of a point (r17, r22, and matching the arithmetic in Section C), constant in every
season — a fact several narrators note specifically rules out a hay-based, seasonal theory
(r03, r09, r22). The 1908 committee sampled Hessel Bottom herds only from January through March
(confirmed by the report's own text in r15 and the curator's note in r07 Item 11) — despite
Grigg's own 1919 covering letter claiming, in the same document, that sampling ran "through the
whole of 1908." The wet-hay theory's key scientific support, Bulletin 214, actually found a
depression of only "five hundredths of one per cent" in winter and nothing in summer (r07 Item
12, r11, and r22's postscript, all quoting the bulletin directly) — a figure both Grigg (r15)
and Kepp (r16) independently overstated fourfold as "two tenths."

**Documents:** r01, r03, r07 (Items 11–12), r08, r09, r13, r15, r16, r17, r21, r22, r24.

## Story 4 — The 1911 Broken-Pipette Interval

**Who/what:** On 8 May 1911 the station's own measuring glass broke, and a borrowed measure was
used in its place until 10 July 1911 — nine weeks (r03, Ansel Keddie's own 1925 statement; r04;
r11; r22, cross-checking independently). Through those nine weeks the recorded tests rose
markedly, and fell back to the prior level the day the station's own glass resumed. Keddie noted
the fact in his book and entered only a question mark, being unwilling to record a conclusion he
could not prove (r03, r18). He told his family that year he believed "something in the glass"
was wrong, but "never dared put it in the book" (r18, his son Duncan's recollection).

**When/quantities:** 8 May–10 July 1911 (nine weeks) is corroborated by four independent
sources; a single dissenting account, veterinarian Kepp's 1926 paper (r16), calls it "six
weeks" of June–July while rhetorically dismissing its significance — the least reliable version,
since it appears designed to minimize an episode inconvenient to Kepp's own hay theory.

**Why it matters:** this interval functions as a natural experiment (tester, herds, season, and
supervision held constant; only the measuring instrument changed) and is treated at length in
Section D1. It independently defeats both the watered-milk and cream-theft theories, decades
before the 1958 discovery explained the mechanism.

**Documents:** r03, r04, r11, r16 (dissenting), r18, r22.


# Answers — Part 2: Section A, Stories 5–8; Section B

## Story 5 — The 1925 Hearing and Ansel Keddie's Dismissal

**Who/what:** Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned in January 1925. A board of arbitration sat at
Ashlin, chaired by attorney Alonzo Frick, from 17 March to 4 May 1925, with two of its three
sittings held at the station itself (r13, r21, r24). It heard all three rival theories (Story
3). Tester Ansel Keddie gave his statement on 17 March 1925 (r13, r03) and was dismissed on 30
April 1925 — four days *before* the board's own award, which found "no cause established" (r02,
r03, r13, r18, r22, r24 — the dismissal-before-finding sequence is one of the best-corroborated
facts in the whole record, and several narrators note the irony directly). Keddie went home,
worked on his farm and took up carpentry, and died in 1934 without public vindication (r18,
r21, r24); he privately told Frick, off the record, that he believed the patrons themselves
were somehow at fault, though he would not say so publicly (r13) — even as he had privately
suspected the glass since 1911 (Story 4; r18).

**Quantities:** The award was $3,800, "without admission of any cause," charged to the station's
own account (r02, r05, r07 Item 7 — the arbitration minute book itself — r13, r22: five sources,
including the primary minute book and the chairman's own account). Two oral-history sources
(r01, r21), both decades-later village memory, instead give $3,500 — a clear case of two
narrators sharing the same wrong figure, resolved in favor of $3,800 by document and by the
chairman's firsthand testimony. The exhibits before the board numbered exactly four — the
station test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book, and the 1923 circuit
notebook (r13, confirmed independently by clerk Bertram Kyle's near-identical list in r23) —
and, crucially, did **not** include the crate of condemned pipettes, which had already been
sitting in the station loft for nearly two years (see Story 7; this omission is central to
Section F).

**Documents:** r01, r02, r03, r05, r07 (Item 7), r13, r17, r18, r21, r22, r23, r24.

## Story 6 — The Ledger's Hidden Evidence: Station Gain and the Brant Weigh Book

**Who/what:** Bookkeeper Wilbur Teague invented a ledger line in 1905 called "station gain,"
recording the persistent surplus between the butterfat credited to patrons on the station's own
test and the butterfat actually received in cream at Larrow Green — a surplus he carried forward
every year as "5¼ per cent, as usual" (r05; confirmed independently by clerk Bertram Kyle, r23,
who copied the same line for decades). Teague raised the anomaly with manager Selby Vose in
1916 and was told to "let it alone" (r05) — after which he simply kept entering it without
further question, even though, as jubilee-pamphlet author Dell Rundle discovered on re-checking
the ledgers in 1959, this entry directly disproves both the watered-milk and cream-theft
theories: more fat arrived at Larrow Green than the station ever credited, which is the opposite
of what either theory predicts (r12's added press-time retraction of its own earlier cream-theft
narrative). Buttermaker Hobart Sill corroborates from the receiving end: the Hollow's cream
physically "weighed in heavy" at Larrow Green every year (r14).

Separately, Emmet Brant — the largest single patron, and Ansel Keddie's brother-in-law by
marriage to his sister Orra — kept his own farm weigh book from 1897, recording his own scale
figures beside the station's; the two consistently agreed, ruling out any weight-based
shorting (r09, his son Roy's account). The 1910 leaf of that book, quoted identically in two
independent family accounts (r07 Item 4's curatorial transcription and r09, Roy's own memoir),
reads: 200,000 lb delivered, "paid on a test of 3.42" — the figure used in Section C2.

**Quantities:** ~5.25% station gain (r05, r23); 200,000 lb / test of 3.42 for Emmet Brant, 1910
(r07 Item 4, r09).

**Documents:** r05, r07 (Item 4), r09, r12, r14, r17 (custody of the Brant book), r18 (full
custody chain), r23.

## Story 7 — The 1923 Condemnation of the Glassware

**Who/what:** State dairy inspector Merle Strawn, on an ordinary circuit visit, for the first
time ran the station's pipettes against a calibrated burette (rather than only checking for
added water with a lactometer, as he had every year since 1908) and found they would not deliver
their marked capacity. He condemned the entire crate and told the manager to order new glass
(r06, his own circuit-book extracts — a primary log). New glass was ordered from Ammon & Sons of
Tarnet the same week and in use within the month (r06, r20, r23).

**Date:** the best-attested date is 12 June 1923 — from Strawn's own photostatted circuit-book
entry (r07 Item 9, a primary document), matching Bulletin 471's own conclusion date (r04, r07
Item 15) and Falke's independent 1959 report (r22). Two sources instead give "the twelfth of
July, 1923" (r08, Vose's own address; r12, Rundle's pamphlet) — likely not independent of each
other, since Rundle's pamphlet may simply repeat the sitting manager's public figure. Two further
outliers place it even earlier, in 1921 (r17) or 1922 (r23's own account, elsewhere in the same
document referencing 1923 dates for other facts) — single, decades-later personal recollections
with no documentary support, least reliable.

**Aftermath:** the condemned crate itself went into the loft above the station's intake room and
was not examined again for 35 years (r18's custody chain; corroborated by r14 and r21, both of
whom describe seeing it there as children/young adults). Ammon & Sons' own reply letter (2 July
1923, quoted in full in r20) said only that "our books of the nineties are not such that we can
tell you anything about the lot"; firm historian Junia Ammon later speculated (1949, r20) that a
reground mold had produced a quietly undersized lot that passed ordinary spot-checks because the
shortfall was uniform across the whole lot.

**Documents:** r02, r06, r07 (Items 2, 9, 15), r08, r12, r17, r18, r20, r22, r23.

## Story 8 — Ivy Keddie's 1958 Discovery and Vindication

**Who/what:** In February 1958, Ansel Keddie's granddaughter Ivy Keddie, a graduate student at
the State Agricultural College at Farland, found the crate of condemned pipettes still sitting
in the loft over the station's intake room (r04, r18, r22 — the loft location is attested far
more broadly than a rival claim in r07 Item 5 that it was found "in the Association office at
Ashlin," most plausibly a curatorial mix-up with the *test book's* separate, well-documented
custody there from 1925–1959, per r18). On 9 March 1958 she measured the nine surviving pipettes
against a calibrated burette: mean delivery 16.72 c.c. against a rated 17.6 c.c. — 95% of the
mark (r04, r07 Items 5 & 14, her own primary measurement sheet). She cross-checked against
independent Alder Corners test records supplied by Rosalie Cudd, showing four Hessel Bottom
farmers' milk, delivered elsewhere, tested a normal 3.78–3.82 across sixteen summers (r10),
confirming the land and cattle were never at fault. She and Professor Leland Vaught published
Bulletin 471 in October 1958, concluding the pipette shortfall alone explained the deficiency:
no watering, no theft (r11, r22). Press coverage followed (r19), garbling some family details
(calling Ivy Ansel's "great-niece" rather than granddaughter, and Rosalie Cudd Jerome's "niece"
rather than daughter — both corrected by insider family sources: r18, r22's own postscript
correction).

**Quantities:** true average recomputed at 3.80 (matching the recorded 3.80 figure preserved
from 1896, before the faulty glass arrived — r07 Item 3); shortfall 76,000 lb of butterfat over
25 full delivery years (1898–1922), worth about $19,000 (see Section C for full derivation).

**Documents:** r04, r07 (Items 5, 6, 14, 15), r09, r10, r11, r18, r19, r21, r22.

# Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**
Granddaughter. Derivation: Ansel and Orra Keddie's son was Duncan Keddie (r18), and Duncan
states explicitly of his own daughter, "Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's
granddaughter" — a direct, first-hand genealogical statement from her own father, who also notes
he has "already twice seen it printed otherwise." Ivy's own 1959 talk (r04) is consistent with
this. The only contrary claim, "great-niece," appears in a single secondhand source, the 1958
newspaper feature (r19), evidently from a rushed phone interview; even Falke's own 1959 county
report initially repeated the newspaper's error before explicitly verifying and correcting it in
a postscript (r22): "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and
not the great-niece." Two independent, well-placed corrections against one uncorrected
secondhand report settle this cleanly.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**
Second cousins. Ansel Keddie married Orra Brant, sister (per r09, r18) or half-sister by their
father's second wife (per r17, Hazel's own account) of Emmet Brant. Emmet's son Roy Brant and
Ansel/Orra's son Duncan Keddie are therefore first cousins (children of siblings) — stated
identically by Roy himself (r09), Duncan himself (r18), and Hazel herself (r17). Roy's daughter
Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are consequently second cousins (children of first cousins) —
the same conclusion reached independently, with the same reasoning shown, by three different
family members writing decades apart. A single outside account (r21, Cleve Oram, citing village
gossip "from my mother... from her mother") instead calls them "first cousin once removed," but
Oram himself admits he "could not draw you the line on paper." Resolved in favor of second
cousins on the strength of three independent, reasoned insider sources against one unreasoned
outsider claim. Left open: whether Orra was Emmet's full or half sister (r09/r18 say full sister,
r17 says half-sister by a second wife) — this does not change the cousin arithmetic either way,
so the sources leave it open without needing to be resolved for B2's purposes.

**B3. Selby Vose's father, and its significance.**
Gideon Vose — the Association's founding president and the author of its by-laws, including
Article VII, the payment-by-test rule that gave the testing dispute its financial stakes in the
first place (r02, r12, r18, r23; "son, not nephew" explicitly confirmed by both Duncan Keddie,
r18, and clerk Bertram Kyle, r23, against a circulating "nephew" rumor). This matters because the
same family that wrote the rule creating the stakes later, in the person of the founder's own
son, became the institutional voice insisting (wrongly, and for years) that patrons were
cheating rather than that the Association's own equipment was at fault — suggesting continuity
of institutional self-interest rather than a fresh, disinterested inquiry each time the question
was raised.

**B4. Relationship between the Vose and Keddie families.**
None. Duncan Keddie's own family notes (r18) state flatly, "There was no connection whatever
between us and the Voses," attributing a circulating rumor of cousinhood to confusion with an
unrelated Ashlin family also named Vose, and noting his mother — who "could not pass a headstone
without giving you the connection" on everyone else in the valley — never once made this claim.
Cleve Oram (r21) repeats the rumor secondhand but explicitly cannot substantiate it. Resolved in
favor of no relationship: a direct, reasoned denial from the family itself outweighs unverified
village gossip.


# Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math (show working)

**C1(a). Proportion by which the tests differed from the truth, and how it follows from the
glassware.**
Two rival measurements of the recovered pipettes exist: mean delivery 16.72 c.c. (r04; r07
Items 5 & 14, Ivy's own primary measurement sheet, "95 hundredths of the mark") versus 16.27 c.c.
(r11, Vaught's bulletin introduction only). Resolved in favor of 16.72 c.c.: it is attested by
Ivy's own measurement sheet (a document, not a memory) and by her own later talk, and — decisively
— the bulletin's own final, twice-quoted conclusion (r04, r07 Item 15) states the shortfall as
76,000 lb, a figure that only reproduces exactly (see C1c) using the 16.72 c.c./95% ratio, not
the 16.27 c.c. figure appearing earlier in that same bulletin's introduction. A document
contradicting its own stated conclusion is the weaker reading.
**Working:** 16.72 / 17.6 = 0.95. The pipettes delivered 95% of their rated charge, so every test
made with them read 5% low, proportionally, regardless of the milk's actual richness.

**C1(b). True average test, and the shortfall in points.**
Recorded average: 3.61 (best-attested; see E3). True average: attested directly at 3.80 by two
sources — Ivy's recomputation (r04) and the curator's note that the station's *own* 1896 test
book (before the faulty glass arrived) averaged 3.80 (r07 Item 3) — and confirmed arithmetically:
recorded ÷ proportion = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**, an exact match. (Vaught's rival "3.905," r11, uses
the rejected 16.27 c.c. ratio and is set aside for the same reason as in C1a; Sill's "3.85 and no
better," r14, is an informal buttermaker's field estimate, in the right neighborhood but not
independently verified.)
**Shortfall:** 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 points ("nineteen hundredths")** — matching independent
figures from Hazel Brant (r17) and county agent Falke (r22). Roy Brant's "seventeen hundredths"
(r09) is treated as an imprecise decades-later recollection, outweighed by the arithmetic.

**C1(c). Pounds of butterfat never credited, over the affected years.**
Using total milk intake of 40,000,000 lb (r02, r23 — chosen over a rival 44,000,000 lb figure in
r05/r12; see E2) and the 0.19-point shortfall: 40,000,000 × 0.0019 = **76,000 lb**. This
reproduces exactly the figure independently given by Ivy (r04) and the curator's notes (r07
Items 6 & 15), which is strong confirmation of both the 40-million-pound intake figure and the
0.19-point gap simultaneously. (A rival 118,000 lb figure appears only in r11's introduction and
is rejected along with its underlying 16.27 c.c./3.905% figures, per C1a–b. A separate 67,000 lb
figure, r13, is explicitly the patrons' own 1925 estimate of their loss — a different quantity,
made before the mechanism was known, not a competing calculation of the same thing.)

**C1(d). Money value, and fraction actually received.**
76,000 lb × $0.25/lb (the average price attested in r02 and r23) = **$19,000**. Patrons actually
received the 1925 arbitration award of $3,800 (best-attested figure; see E19/Story 5).
**Fraction received:** $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = **1/5, or 20%**.

**C2. Emmet Brant, largest patron, year 1910.**
1910 leaf (r07 Item 4; r09, identical wording): 200,000 lb delivered, "paid on a test of 3.42."
Because the pipette error was proportional, true test = recorded ÷ 0.95 = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60.
(a) **Credited:** 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** butterfat.
(b) **Owed but not credited:** 200,000 × (0.0360 − 0.0342) = 200,000 × 0.0018 = **360 lb**.
(c) **Value that year:** 360 × $0.25 = **$90.00**.

**C3. Over the full delivery years (1898–1922, 25 years; 40,000,000 lb milk).**
(a) **Credited to patrons** (at the recorded 3.61% average): 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000
lb** butterfat.
(b) **Actually reaching Larrow Green in the station's cream** (at the true 3.80% average):
40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lb**.
(c) **Difference:** 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 lb (matching C1c exactly — a further
cross-check). Expressed as a percentage, the answer depends on the base:
 - As a percentage of what was **credited** (the ledger's own "station gain" framing):
   76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.26%** — matching Teague's and Kyle's independently attested "5¼ per
   cent, as usual" almost exactly.
 - As a percentage of what was **actually delivered** (the pipette-shortfall framing):
   76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5.00%** — matching the glassware's own 5% shortfall from C1a.
This resolves what looks like two different numbers ("5%" vs. "5.25%") scattered across the
sources: they are the same 76,000 lb, expressed as a percentage of two different bases.

**C4. The testing outfit's invoice.**
(a) **Total: $77.93** — the itemized sum (25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93) as
reproduced in two independent facsimiles of the same document (r07 Item 2; r20, Ammon & Sons'
own firm history). A rival prose figure, "$77.39," appears in both r19 and r20 — but in r20 this
contradicts that same document's own quoted invoice table (see E10/self-contradictions below),
so it is a transcription slip, not a rival total.
(b) **Six dozen (72) pipettes**, per the same two independent invoice reproductions (against a
"five dozen" figure appearing only in r11 and r21, both weaker sources — see E9). Each was short
by 17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88 c.c.** (5% of the rated 17.6 c.c. mark).

**C5(a). Duration of faulty glass in use vs. years in the money calculation.**
The glass was in use from 1 May 1897 (r20; r24's contemporaneous diary entry) until condemnation
on 12 June 1923 (Story 7) — about **26 years**. The money calculation (Story 8; C1–C3) instead
runs over **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922** (explicitly stated by the curator's note, r07
Item 6). These differ because the reckoning excludes the two partial calendar years at each end
— 1897 (glass arrived partway through the year) and 1923 (glass condemned partway through the
year) — counting only whole delivery years in between. This is a matter of accounting convention
(inclusive full-year counting), not a factual conflict.

**C5(b). Milk received in the station's first season.**
"About nine hundred thousand pounds," May–December 1896 (r14, Hobart Sill, a direct participant
who lent the glass used that season) — preferred over "eleven hundred thousand pounds" (r23,
Bertram Kyle, who explicitly flags his own figure as secondhand: "It is not in any book I ever
ruled").

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment.**
When/duration: 8 May – 10 July 1911, nine weeks (r03, r04, r11, r22 — four independent sources,
including Ansel Keddie's own contemporaneous notation; a single dissenting account, r16, calling
it six weeks, is treated as a motivated minimization — see E8). **Variable:** the measuring
glass/pipette (the station's own broken; a borrowed measure substituted). **Held constant:** the
same tester, the same herds and patrons, the same season transition, the same procedure
otherwise. **Result:** recorded tests rose during the nine weeks and fell back to the prior level
the day the station's own glass returned (r03). **What it establishes:** since every other
variable was held constant and only the instrument changed, this isolates the measuring glass as
the causal factor and rules out theories resting on the herds, the season, or the tester's
honesty — a real-world, before/after demonstration that a laboratory recalculation alone (done
47 years later) could not supply, because a calculation can only show a defective pipette
*would* produce a low reading, not that this mechanism was *actually* live and operating at the
time. **What it does not establish:** it does not by itself identify the specific mechanism
(an undersized pipette, as opposed to some other measuring fault); that specificity required the
1958 physical measurement. Nor does it establish that the shortfall's magnitude was constant
across all 26 years, though no source suggests otherwise.

**D2. Three independent reasons the tester was not stealing cream.**
1. The 1911 anomaly itself (r03/r04/r11/r22): the same tester, unsupervised any differently,
   produced higher readings the moment the instrument changed — impossible if he were the cause.
2. The ledger's "station gain" entry (r05, r23; Rundle's 1959 retraction in r12): decades of
   ordinary bookkeeping show *more* fat consistently reached Larrow Green than was ever credited
   to patrons — the opposite of what theft by the tester would produce.
3. Buttermaker Hobart Sill's independent physical observation (r14) that the Hollow's cream
   "weighed in heavy" at Larrow Green every year — corroborating the ledger from an entirely
   different person, role, and location.
These are independent because they rest on different observers (the tester himself; a
bookkeeper keeping routine records for an unrelated purpose; a receiving buttermaker's personal
sensory judgment), different evidence types (a temporal experiment; ledger arithmetic; physical
observation), and different locations (the station; the Larrow Green office; the Larrow Green
creamery floor). Reasons 2 and 3 would still hold even if the glassware had never been measured
in 1958, since both existed as evidence for decades before Ivy's discovery.
**Did the bookkeeper ever question the entries that refute his own accusation?** Only once: Teague
raised the persistent "gain" with manager Vose in 1916 and was told to "let it alone" (r05);
thereafter he entered the same line for the rest of his career without further question, and
even his own 1925 hearing testimony was read as implicating the tester (r13) — a documented
failure to connect his own ledger to his own testimony, later noted with regret by pamphleteer
Dell Rundle (r12) and clerk Bertram Kyle (r23, "I could not have told you then... that any man in
that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked").

**D3. Did the manager know, before the glassware was condemned, that tests ran low?**
Yes, as to the *effect* — no, as to the *cause*. Selby Vose signed the annual sheets carrying the
"station gain" line every single year (r05, r23), and clerk Kyle's firsthand observation is that
Vose "read the totals and he never read the lines above them... put his thumb on the bottom
figure, and sign" — meaning he saw the surplus entry yet never examined what produced it. He was
also told directly by Teague in 1916 that the gain persisted and was unexplained, and instructed
him to drop it (r05). And he personally issued the 1919 circular accusing patrons of watering
their milk — an act that presupposes he already knew there was an unexplained shortfall requiring
public explanation, since the complaint itself had been aired at annual meetings since 1902
(r15, r22). So: he knew of the chronic low readings for years, and was handed direct internal
evidence (the gain line, and Teague's warning) that his own preferred explanation could not be
right, but the sources give no indication he — or anyone — knew the specific mechanism (faulty
pipettes) before 1958.

## Section F — Theory

**Single causal theory.** The chronic shortfall at Ostrey Hollow (1897–1923) was produced
entirely by one defective lot of milk-test pipettes, bought from Ammon & Sons in 1897, blown to
the correct 17.6 c.c. mark but physically delivering only about 16.72 c.c. (95% of the mark) —
most plausibly, per the manufacturer's own historian (r20), because a reground mold quietly
produced an undersized lot that passed ordinary spot-checks precisely because the shortfall was
uniform across the whole lot ("consistently wrong," in her words). Because the test reads the fat
content of whatever volume the pipette actually delivers, a pipette that under-delivers by a
fixed proportion makes *every* test read low by that same proportion, regardless of season,
herd, or the tester's competence — which is exactly why the gap was constant year-round rather
than worse in winter, as a hay theory would require, and why it computes as a proportional ~5%
shortfall rather than a fixed offset. It began the moment the new (flawed) glass replaced the
genuinely accurate glass borrowed from Larrow Green (spring 1897) — which is also why the
station's very first, pre-1897 samples read 3.80%, matching the later-computed true average
almost exactly. It ended in 1923 when inspector Strawn, for the first time, checked the
pipettes' delivery volume against a calibrated burette rather than only checking for added water
with a lactometer, found and condemned them. Nothing on the farms or at the station ever needed
to change, at either end, because the fault was never in the milk, the cows, the land, or the
people — only in one crate of laboratory glassware.

**Three competing explanations, proponents, and defeating evidence:**
1. **Watered milk** — Selby Vose (1919 circular, r08). Defeated by inspector Strawn's own
   circuit-book record (r06): he tested with a lactometer at every visit from 1908 on and never
   once found added water.
2. **Cream skimming by the tester** — implied via bookkeeper Teague's hearing testimony (r13);
   believed informally by some patrons (r01, r21). Defeated by the 1911 anomaly (same tester,
   higher readings the moment the glass changed) and by the ledger's own "station gain" entry
   (r05, r23), which shows more fat arriving at Larrow Green than was credited — the opposite of
   theft.
3. **Wet hay / Hessel Bottom soil chemistry** — the 1908 Grigg committee (r15); Dr. Kepp's 1926
   paper (r16). Defeated by Bulletin 214's actual finding of only a five-hundredths depression in
   winter and none in summer (r22's direct quotation) — far too small, and too seasonal, to
   explain a constant 0.19-point year-round gap.

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?** No. Only four exhibits were before
it — the test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book, and the 1923 circuit
notebook (r13, r23, both giving essentially the same list) — and the crate itself, already
condemned and shelved nearly two years earlier, was never entered into evidence (also flagged as
a gap by r02). It remained unexamined for another 33 years.

**What did the tester himself believe?** He privately suspected the glass as early as summer
1911, saying so twice at home that year, though he would not commit an unproven conclusion to
his own book, entering only a question mark (r18, his son Duncan's recollection). By the 1925
hearing, however, he told the chairman privately that he thought the patrons themselves were
somehow at fault, though he would not say so publicly (r13) — a striking, unresolved shift from
his earlier private suspicion about the equipment.

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

From 1897 to 1923, the Ostrey Hollow testing station in the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery
Association chronically under-reported its patrons' butterfat, quietly costing them about
$19,000. The real cause was a defective lot of milk-test pipettes that delivered about 5% less
milk than their marked capacity, making every test read proportionally low regardless of season
or herd. For a generation, patrons and officers blamed each other instead: the manager suspected
watered milk, some patrons suspected the tester was skimming cream, and an official committee
blamed wet hay — while a 1911 equipment mishap and the Association's own ledgers had already,
quietly, disproved both the water and the theft theories, unnoticed. Tester Ansel Keddie was
dismissed in disgrace in 1925 and died in 1934. Only in 1958, when his granddaughter found the
condemned pipettes in a station loft and measured them, was the truth confirmed — decades too
late for him to hear it.
