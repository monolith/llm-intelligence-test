# Answers — Part 2: Section A, Stories 5–8, and Section B

## Story 5: The Brant and Cudd Ledgers — Independent Witnesses on the Bottom (1897–1930)

Two private, non-institutional records, kept for entirely private reasons by people with no stake in the Association's dispute, turned out decades later to hold the proof of what was really happening. Emmet Brant, the largest Hessel Bottom patron, began a personal weigh book in **January 1897** (per the 1925 arbitration's own exhibit list, r13, and the historical-society catalog — two documentary sources; his son Roy's 1955 recollection of "1898," r09, is a single, later memory and is outweighed). In it Emmet recorded his own barn-scale weight beside the station's returned weight for every delivery; the two "agreed... barring a pound or two" year after year, proving the *weighing* was never the problem (r09). His 1910 leaf records 200,000 pounds delivered, paid on a test of 3.42 (quoted identically in r07, r09, r17). Emmet kept the book until his death in 1929; his son Roy continued it; Roy's daughter Hazel inherited it in 1962 and gave it to the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1963 (r18).

Separately, from 1915 to 1930 — sixteen consecutive summers — four of the six Hessel Bottom farmers hauled their milk over Redlow Ridge to Rosalie Cudd's father's cheese factory at Alder Corners instead of to Ostrey Hollow, returning to the station the rest of each year (r09, r10, both independently confirming "sixteen seasons," 1915–1930). At Alder Corners, on the very same wet ground and the very same wet-hay feed the 1908 committee blamed, this milk tested normally: sixteen summer averages ranging 3.78–3.82, averaging about **3.80** — a table Rosalie Cudd copied out for Ivy Keddie in 1958 (r10). This is the single strongest piece of evidence against the wet-hay theory, because it holds the land and the cows constant and changes only the station.

Documents: r09 (Roy Brant's 1955 account), r10 (Rosalie Cudd's 1958 letter and table), r13 (Frick's exhibit list), r17 (Hazel Brant), r18 (Duncan Keddie).

## Story 6: The 1925 Arbitration and the Dismissal of Ansel Keddie

The patrons petitioned in January 1925. A three-member board of arbitration, chaired by Ashlin attorney Alonzo Frick, sat from **17 March to 4 May 1925** in three sessions: the first at the Association's Ashlin office, the second and third at the station itself, in its own weigh room, at Frick's request, so the board could see the plant directly (r13; independently corroborated by Cleve Oram's eyewitness account of standing outside on the platform during those sessions, r21, and by Orra Keddie's diary, r24, which records "two of the sittings held up here at the station itself... men standing outside in the road"). Three explanations were formally argued: water added by patrons (manager Selby Vose); cream taken by the tester (bookkeeper Wilbur Teague, obliquely); and wet hay (the 1908 report, read into the record). Frick's own 1946 recollection states plainly: "not one of them was proved," and that the state inspector's own condemned glassware — already sitting in the loft directly above where two of the three sessions were held — was never connected to the case.

Ansel Keddie was dismissed **30 April 1925**, four days before the board's finding was handed down (r18; confirmed to the exact date by Orra's own diary, r24: "April 30, 1925. They have put him out. Twenty-nine seasons and a letter."). The board awarded the patrons **$3,800**, "without admission of any cause" — the documentary consensus of five largely independent sources (r02, r08/Vose's own address, r19, r22, and r23/Kyle, the clerk who personally charged it to the station account), against a single outlier of $3,500 from Cleve Oram's secondhand account of his father's memory (r21). Keddie went home, took up carpentry, and died in 1934 never publicly cleared.

Documents: r13 (Frick), r18, r21 (Oram), r23 (Kyle), r24 (Orra Keddie's diary).

## Story 7: The State Inspector's Condemnation of the Glass (1923)

On **12 June 1923**, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn, on an ordinary circuit visit, ran the station's pipettes against a calibrated burette and found they would not deliver their marked capacity. He condemned the whole lot and directed the manager to order new glass. This date rests on Strawn's own dated notebook (a primary, contemporaneous record, cited in r06/r07) and is independently reconstructed in the county's 1959 report (r22). A rival date, 12 July 1923, appears in two sources — Selby Vose's 1926 address (r08) and Rundle's 1939 pamphlet (r12) — but these likely share a common origin or an echo of the Association's own order letter, which Ammon & Sons' history (r20) shows was sent "the twenty-eighth of June" and answered "the second of July"; a July date for the condemnation itself is most plausibly a conflation with this correspondence. New glass was in use within the month, and from 1924 the station's recorded average rose to about 3.85 (r22).

No one at the time connected the condemnation to the twenty-six years of complaints behind it. Bertram Kyle, the office clerk who entered the replacement order, recorded: "There was no more said of it in the office than there would have been of a broken window" (r23). The condemned crate went into the loft over the intake room and stayed there.

Documents: r06/r07 (Strawn's notebook), r20 (Ammon & Sons letter book), r22 (Falke's report), r23 (Kyle).

## Story 8: Ivy Keddie's Discovery and the Vindication of 1958

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie — Ansel Keddie's granddaughter, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland — found the condemned crate still in the station loft, under a tarpaulin, and carried it east. On 9 March 1958 she measured the surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette: **nine** of them, each blown to 17.6 c.c., delivering a mean of **16.72 c.c.** — short by exactly one part in twenty. (Three independent sources give nine survivors — r04, r07, r21's eyewitness account of the crate over thirty-five years; an outlying count of eleven appears in only two related sources, r12's 1959 footnote and the newspaper account in r19, which likely share a single garbled origin.) Bulletin No. 471, published October 1958 under Ivy Keddie's and Professor Leland Vaught's names, concluded: no milk was watered and no cream was stolen; the tests were low because the glass was short, and the resulting shortfall — 76,000 pounds of butterfat — stood credited in the Association's own ledgers every year as "station gain."

The Ashlin Chronicle publicized the finding on 3 October 1958 (r19), reaching Ivy Keddie by telephone; the county agricultural agent issued an official summary in 1959, at first crediting the wet-hay theory in part before retracting that concession on reading Bulletin 214's actual figures (r22). Ansel Keddie's name was cleared twenty-four years after his death; his widow Orra had died in 1948 and did not live to see it (r24).

Documents: r11 (Bulletin 471), r19 (Ashlin Chronicle), r22 (Falke's report and its correction), r24 (editor's closing note).

# Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**
Granddaughter. Duncan Keddie's own 1968 family record (r18) is explicit and purpose-written to correct the record: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise." Independently, the county agent's 1959 report (r22) first repeated a newspaper error calling her "great-niece" (traceable to the Ashlin Chronicle's telephone interview, r19) but appended a verified correction: "Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece." Two independent corrections converging on "granddaughter," against a single traceable newspaper error, settle this.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**
Second cousins. Orra Keddie (née Brant) and Emmet Brant were siblings; their children, Duncan Keddie and Roy Brant, were first cousins; their children, Ivy and Hazel, are therefore second cousins. This is stated explicitly and independently by four sources: Roy Brant himself (r09), Rundle's 1959 footnote (r12), Hazel Brant in her own 1981 tape — who specifically rebuts people calling her Ivy's "niece" or "cousin once removed" (r17) — and Duncan Keddie's family notes, which give the identical rebuttal (r18). What the sources leave open is *not* the cousin degree, which is unanimous among family sources, but whether Orra and Emmet were full or half siblings (see B4's neighboring conflict, unresolved as between r09/r18's "full brother and sister" and r17's "half sister... by his father's second wife"); this does not change the second-cousin conclusion, since the sibling relationship holds either way.

**B3. Selby Vose's father.**
Gideon Vose. Three independent sources converge: Duncan Keddie's family record, written specifically to correct genealogical errors (r18); Bertram Kyle, who worked in the Association office adjacent to Selby Vose for twenty-five years and states flatly "I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense" (r23); and Selby Vose's own words in his 1926 address, "My father, who was your first president..." (r08). Against these, the "nephew" claim (r01, r12) rests on secondhand valley pamphlet material. This matters to Ostrey Hollow because it means the Association's founder, who wrote the payment-by-test rule the whole dispute turned on, and the manager who spent decades defending the water-in-milk theory rather than questioning the equipment, were father and son — institutional authority over the station's interpretation stayed within one family across two generations.

**B4. Relationship between the Vose and Keddie families.**
None, per the most authoritative source. Duncan Keddie's family record devotes a section ("A thing that is said and is not so," r18) specifically to denying it, citing his mother Orra's own thorough genealogical knowledge: "she never once put a Vose in it." This is set against unsourced valley talk in r01 ("old Mrs. Vose... first cousin of Orra Keddie") and Cleve Oram's admittedly uncertain hearsay ("cousins of some sort... I could not draw you the line on paper," r21). A direct, sourced family denial outweighs two vague or unsourced claims; resolved as no relationship, the claim being valley folklore.
