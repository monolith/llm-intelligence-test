# Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

*Method note: throughout, "resolved" means I chose among conflicting sources using one of: a quoted primary document (invoice, dated log entry, contemporaneous diary) outranking memory; arithmetic that closes a loop between two independently-stated figures; a document's own internal consistency; or convergence of genuinely independent witnesses (not two retellings drawing on the same underlying source).*

---

## Story 1: The Founding of the Association and the Rule of Article VII (1889–1896)

In 1889 the farmers of the Larrow Valley met at the schoolhouse at Larrow Green, in cold weather, late into the night, and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (r12, r22). Gideon Vose, a "heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the law" (r12), organized the meeting, drafted the by-laws at his own kitchen table, and served as the Association's first president from 1889 to 1911. The Association built its main creamery at Larrow Green in 1891 (r12, r22 — two independent, consistent sources), with Hobart Sill starting as buttermaker there that same year per his own retirement remarks (r14 gives 1893 for Sill's start, a minor discrepancy not central to this story).

The by-laws included Article VII, under which every patron was paid according to the pounds of butterfat his milk tested, not according to the milk's weight — "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" (quoted identically in r12 and elsewhere). This single rule is the hinge of the entire eight-story history: under payment by weight, nothing that follows would have cost anyone money.

The exact year Article VII was adopted **cannot be determined from the sources**. Three dates circulate — 1891 (r12), 1892 (earlier notes, r02), 1893 (r15, Grigg's 1919 letter) — and none is a primary document. The county agricultural agent's own 1959 investigation (r22) went looking for the answer and reported explicitly that "the printed by-laws carry no date upon their face" and "the minute book of the Association's first years was lost in an office removal," so that his office was "unable to state the year of adoption and does not attempt to." That is the most authoritative statement available on the question, and it is a statement of unresolvability, not a fourth date.

Documents bearing on this story: r12 (Rundle's pamphlet), r22 (Falke's county report), and the by-laws text itself as reproduced in r12.

## Story 2: The Opening of the Ostrey Hollow Station and Its Flawed Glassware (1896–1897)

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley, to serve Hessel Bottom's six farms (r22). It opened on 4 May 1896 (r02, r22 — precise, consistent date), with Ansel Keddie as tester and separator man from the first day. The station's own ordered equipment had not yet arrived for the opening; buttermaker Hobart Sill personally lent his own creamery glassware from Larrow Green so the season would not have to be postponed (r14), and this glass was used through the station's partial first season, taking in roughly 900,000 pounds of milk by Sill's estimate (r14) — though clerk Bertram Kyle separately recalled "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" for "that first year" (r23), a figure he admits is oral tradition, "not in any book I ever ruled." These two figures likely describe different windows (Sill's partial 1896 season versus Kyle's fuller first operating year) and cannot be reconciled from the sources.

The station's own outfit was ordered from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet. The firm's own 1949 history (r20) reproduces the actual invoice, dated 14 April 1897: six dozen (72) milk test pipettes at 17.6 c.c., 35 cents each ($25.20); twelve dozen test bottles ($31.68); one acid measure ($0.90); one hand tester ($18.00); freight ($2.15); **total $77.93**. This resolves a persistent conflict: Vaught's 1958 bulletin (r11) says "five dozen" pipettes were purchased, but the primary invoice and the historical-society catalog both independently say six dozen; six dozen is correct.

The new glass went into use on **1 May 1897** — confirmed by the invoice narrative itself (r20) and, decisively, by Orra Keddie's own contemporaneous diary entry of that exact date: "A. says the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green" (r24). This overrides Bertram Kyle's secondhand recollection of 1898 (r23) and Vaught's own bulletin, which self-contradicts between 1897 and 1898 in two of its own sections.

Documents: r20 (Ammon & Sons invoice, facsimile), r24 (Orra Keddie's diary), r14 (Sill), r23 (Kyle).

## Story 3: Thirty Years of Suspicion — the 1908 Committee and the 1919 Water Circular (1902–1919)

From 1902 the Ostrey Hollow milk tested persistently lower than milk anywhere else in the valley — the first complaint of record came that year (r22, r24: Orra's diary independently dates it to March 1902). In January 1908 the Association appointed a committee under Nyle Grigg to investigate. It sampled all six Hessel Bottom farms through the year, tried the scales, checked water supplies, and concluded the milk itself was honest but that the bottom's wet, sour hay "thins the cream" (full report reprinted in r15; independently corroborated in outline by Orra's diary, r24, which places the committee's visit in January–March 1908). Grigg reprinted this report in a 1919 covering letter that also misquoted a college feeding-trial bulletin (No. 214) as finding a "two tenths" test depression from bog hay — a fourfold overstatement of the bulletin's actual finding of "not more than five hundredths," a misquote independently repeated by veterinarian Dr. Roland Kepp in 1926 (r16).

In 1919, manager Selby Vose circulated a warning letter to the Hessel Bottom patrons accusing them of adding water to their milk. Orra Keddie's diary (r24, the only eyewitness, contemporaneous source) records that Ansel refused to have the circular mailed or left on the platform and instead delivered it personally to **eleven families**, standing at each door while the man read it, returning home at eleven at night. (Hazel Brant's 1981 recollection, r17, gives "nine families, six farms" — a later, secondhand oral figure; Orra's diary, written by the deliverer's own wife on the day in question, is preferred.) No patron ever answered the letter. Two days later, Orra's brother Emmet Brant — the largest patron on the bottom — came to the yard, asked Ansel why he had carried the letter himself if he didn't believe its charge, and never entered the house again; the rupture lasted until Emmet's death in 1929.

Documents: r15 (Grigg's 1908 report and 1919 letter, reprinted in full), r24 (Orra Keddie's diary), r17 (Hazel Brant), r16 (Kepp).

## Story 4: The Nine Weeks of 1911 — An Accidental Experiment

On 8 May 1911 the station's own measuring glass broke, and a borrowed measure was used in its place until 10 July 1911 — **nine weeks** (Keddie's own dated log entries; independently confirmed in the same precise terms by the 1959 county report, r22, which had access to the bulletin's underlying record). Two later, secondary sources — Teague's 1948 memoir and Kepp's 1926 paper — instead say "six weeks," but neither is a contemporaneous record; the nine-week figure, resting on the tester's own dated entries, is preferred.

Through those nine weeks the station's tests rose sharply, matching the true valley-wide level; the day the station's own glass resumed, they fell back at once. Keddie recorded the fact and set a question mark against it rather than an explanation, because — per his son Duncan's 1968 family notes (r18) — he privately said at home, twice, "it was something in the glass," but would not commit an unproved suspicion to the official book.

This interval functions as a natural experiment: the tester, the herds, the season, and the procedure were all held constant; the only variable was which measuring glass was used; the result was a clean, reversible swing in the recorded test. It establishes that the fault lay in equipment, not in the patrons, the tester, or the land — years before anyone measured the glass directly. It does *not* establish the mechanism (short delivery), the magnitude of the error, or why the station's particular 1897 lot was bad from the start; those required the 1958 direct calibration.

Documents: Keddie's own test book (via r22, r11), r18 (Duncan Keddie's family notes), r13 (Frick), contrasted with r05 (Teague) and r16 (Kepp).

## Story 5: The Brant and Cudd Ledgers — Independent Witnesses on the Bottom (1897–1930)

Two private, non-institutional records, kept for entirely private reasons by people with no stake in the Association's dispute, turned out decades later to hold the proof of what was really happening. Emmet Brant, the largest Hessel Bottom patron, began a personal weigh book in **January 1897** (per the 1925 arbitration's own exhibit list, r13, and the historical-society catalog — two documentary sources; his son Roy's 1955 recollection of "1898," r09, is a single, later memory and is outweighed). In it Emmet recorded his own barn-scale weight beside the station's returned weight for every delivery; the two "agreed... barring a pound or two" year after year, proving the *weighing* was never the problem (r09). His 1910 leaf records 200,000 pounds delivered, paid on a test of 3.42 (quoted identically in r07, r09, r17). Emmet kept the book until his death in 1929; his son Roy continued it; Roy's daughter Hazel inherited it in 1962 and gave it to the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1963 (r18).

Separately, from 1915 to 1930 — sixteen consecutive summers — four of the six Hessel Bottom farmers hauled their milk over Redlow Ridge to Rosalie Cudd's father's cheese factory at Alder Corners instead of to Ostrey Hollow, returning to the station the rest of each year (r09, r10, both independently confirming "sixteen seasons," 1915–1930). At Alder Corners, on the very same wet ground and the very same wet-hay feed the 1908 committee blamed, this milk tested normally: sixteen summer averages ranging 3.78–3.82, averaging about **3.80** — a table Rosalie Cudd copied out for Ivy Keddie in 1958 (r10). This is the single strongest piece of evidence against the wet-hay theory, because it holds the land and the cows constant and changes only the station.

Documents: r09 (Roy Brant's 1955 account), r10 (Rosalie Cudd's 1958 letter and table), r13 (Frick's exhibit list), r17 (Hazel Brant), r18 (Duncan Keddie).

## Story 6: The 1925 Arbitration and the Dismissal of Ansel Keddie

The patrons petitioned in January 1925. A three-member board of arbitration, chaired by Ashlin attorney Alonzo Frick, sat from **17 March to 4 May 1925** in three sessions: the first at the Association's Ashlin office, the second and third at the station itself, in its own weigh room, at Frick's request, so the board could see the plant directly (r13; independently corroborated by Cleve Oram's eyewitness account of standing outside on the platform during those sessions, r21, and by Orra Keddie's diary, r24, which records "two of the sittings held up here at the station itself... men standing outside in the road"). Three explanations were formally argued: water added by patrons (manager Selby Vose); cream taken by the tester (bookkeeper Wilbur Teague, obliquely); and wet hay (the 1908 report, read into the record). Frick's own 1946 recollection states plainly: "not one of them was proved," and that the state inspector's own condemned glassware — already sitting in the loft directly above where two of the three sessions were held — was never connected to the case.

Ansel Keddie was dismissed **30 April 1925**, four days before the board's finding was handed down (r18; confirmed to the exact date by Orra's own diary, r24: "April 30, 1925. They have put him out. Twenty-nine seasons and a letter."). The board awarded the patrons **$3,800**, "without admission of any cause" — the documentary consensus of five largely independent sources (r02, r08/Vose's own address, r19, r22, and r23/Kyle, the clerk who personally charged it to the station account), against a single outlier of $3,500 from Cleve Oram's secondhand account of his father's memory (r21). Keddie went home, took up carpentry, and died in 1934 never publicly cleared.

Documents: r13 (Frick), r18, r21 (Oram), r23 (Kyle), r24 (Orra Keddie's diary).

## Story 7: The State Inspector's Condemnation of the Glass (1923)

On **12 June 1923**, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn, on an ordinary circuit visit, ran the station's pipettes against a calibrated burette and found they would not deliver their marked capacity. He condemned the whole lot and directed the manager to order new glass. This date rests on Strawn's own dated notebook (a primary, contemporaneous record, cited in r06/r07) and is independently reconstructed in the county's 1959 report (r22). A rival date, 12 July 1923, appears in two sources — Selby Vose's 1926 address (r08) and Rundle's 1939 pamphlet (r12) — but these likely share a common origin or an echo of the Association's own order letter, which Ammon & Sons' history (r20) shows was sent "the twenty-eighth of June" and answered "the second of July"; a July date for the condemnation itself is most plausibly a conflation with this correspondence. New glass was in use within the month, and from 1924 the station's recorded average rose to about 3.85 (r22).

No one at the time connected the condemnation to the twenty-six years of complaints behind it. Bertram Kyle, the office clerk who entered the replacement order, recorded: "There was no more said of it in the office than there would have been of a broken window" (r23). The condemned crate went into the loft over the intake room and stayed there.

Documents: r06/r07 (Strawn's notebook), r20 (Ammon & Sons letter book), r22 (Falke's report), r23 (Kyle).

## Story 8: Ivy Keddie's Discovery and the Vindication of 1958

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie — Ansel Keddie's granddaughter, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland — found the condemned crate still in the station loft, under a tarpaulin, and carried it east. On 9 March 1958 she measured the surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette: **nine** of them, each blown to 17.6 c.c., delivering a mean of **16.72 c.c.** — short by exactly one part in twenty. (Three independent sources give nine survivors — r04, r07, r21's eyewitness account of the crate over thirty-five years; an outlying count of eleven appears in only two related sources, r12's 1959 footnote and the newspaper account in r19, which likely share a single garbled origin.) Bulletin No. 471, published October 1958 under Ivy Keddie's and Professor Leland Vaught's names, concluded: no milk was watered and no cream was stolen; the tests were low because the glass was short, and the resulting shortfall — 76,000 pounds of butterfat — stood credited in the Association's own ledgers every year as "station gain."

The Ashlin Chronicle publicized the finding on 3 October 1958 (r19), reaching Ivy Keddie by telephone; the county agricultural agent issued an official summary in 1959, at first crediting the wet-hay theory in part before retracting that concession on reading Bulletin 214's actual figures (r22). Ansel Keddie's name was cleared twenty-four years after his death; his widow Orra had died in 1948 and did not live to see it (r24).

Documents: r11 (Bulletin 471), r19 (Ashlin Chronicle), r22 (Falke's report and its correction), r24 (editor's closing note).

# Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**
Granddaughter. Duncan Keddie's own 1968 family record (r18) is explicit and purpose-written to correct the record: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise." Independently, the county agent's 1959 report (r22) first repeated a newspaper error calling her "great-niece" (traceable to the Ashlin Chronicle's telephone interview, r19) but appended a verified correction: "Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece." Two independent corrections converging on "granddaughter," against a single traceable newspaper error, settle this.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**
Second cousins. Orra Keddie (née Brant) and Emmet Brant were siblings; their children, Duncan Keddie and Roy Brant, were first cousins; their children, Ivy and Hazel, are therefore second cousins. This is stated explicitly and independently by four sources: Roy Brant himself (r09), Rundle's 1959 footnote (r12), Hazel Brant in her own 1981 tape — who specifically rebuts people calling her Ivy's "niece" or "cousin once removed" (r17) — and Duncan Keddie's family notes, which give the identical rebuttal (r18). What the sources leave open is *not* the cousin degree, which is unanimous among family sources, but whether Orra and Emmet were full or half siblings (see B4's neighboring conflict, unresolved as between r09/r18's "full brother and sister" and r17's "half sister... by his father's second wife"); this does not change the second-cousin conclusion, since the sibling relationship holds either way.

**B3. Selby Vose's father.**
Gideon Vose. Three independent sources converge: Duncan Keddie's family record, written specifically to correct genealogical errors (r18); Bertram Kyle, who worked in the Association office adjacent to Selby Vose for twenty-five years and states flatly "I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense" (r23); and Selby Vose's own words in his 1926 address, "My father, who was your first president..." (r08). Against these, the "nephew" claim (r01, r12) rests on secondhand valley pamphlet material. This matters to Ostrey Hollow because it means the Association's founder, who wrote the payment-by-test rule the whole dispute turned on, and the manager who spent decades defending the water-in-milk theory rather than questioning the equipment, were father and son — institutional authority over the station's interpretation stayed within one family across two generations.

**B4. Relationship between the Vose and Keddie families.**
None, per the most authoritative source. Duncan Keddie's family record devotes a section ("A thing that is said and is not so," r18) specifically to denying it, citing his mother Orra's own thorough genealogical knowledge: "she never once put a Vose in it." This is set against unsourced valley talk in r01 ("old Mrs. Vose... first cousin of Orra Keddie") and Cleve Oram's admittedly uncertain hearsay ("cousins of some sort... I could not draw you the line on paper," r21). A direct, sourced family denial outweighs two vague or unsourced claims; resolved as no relationship, the claim being valley folklore.


# Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math

**C1.**
(a) The pipettes were blown to deliver 17.6 c.c. but the nine surviving examples averaged **16.72 c.c.** (Ivy Keddie's own 1958 measurement, r04/r07). 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95** — the glass delivered 95% of its marked charge, i.e., short by one part in twenty. I take 16.72 c.c. as correct over Vaught's own bulletin figure of 16.27 c.c. (r11) because 16.72 is the figure that reproduces the bulletin's *own* headline conclusion — "low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty" — exactly (16.72/17.6 = 0.9500 precisely), whereas 16.27/17.6 = 0.9244, which does not; the bulletin's Section V (built on 16.27) is thus internally inconsistent with its own Section VII, and I resolve in favor of the version that is arithmetically self-consistent.

(b) Recorded average test = 3.61 (documentary consensus: Keddie's book, Strawn, Bulletin 471's conclusion). True average = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. Shortfall = 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 points**. This is independently corroborated by the Alder Corners cross-check data (r10): the same Hessel Bottom farms' milk, tested elsewhere for sixteen summers, averaged "about 3.80" — a wholly independent confirmation of the true figure via a different route.

(c) Total milk received over the affected delivery years = 40,000,000 lb (see C5 for why 25 years). Credited butterfat = 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb. True butterfat = 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb. Shortfall = 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = **76,000 lb**. This matches the bulletin's own conclusion and r04/r07 exactly, and is preferred over Vaught's own Section V figure of 118,000 lb (which stems from the erroneous 16.27 c.c. figure, see C1a) and over Frick's "patrons' own reckoning" of 67,000 lb (r13), which was a contemporary 1925 estimate made without knowledge of the mechanism and is not a measured figure.

(d) 76,000 lb × $0.25/lb (the documented long-run average price, r02/r04/r07/r09) = **$19,000**. The patrons actually received the arbitration award of $3,800 (see Story 6 for sourcing). $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = **0.20, i.e., one-fifth (20%)** of what they were actually owed. (Note: Rundle's pamphlet and the newspaper separately cite a patrons' petition figure of $19,600 "to the dollar" — this is the patrons' own 1925 claimed loss, not the same computation, and the two are close but not identical; I have not forced them to agree.)

**C2.** Largest patron = Emmet Brant, 1910: 200,000 lb delivered, paid on a test of 3.42 (r07/r09/r17, unanimous).
(a) Credited butterfat = 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb**.
(b) True test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60. True butterfat = 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb. Owed and not credited = 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb**.
(c) 1910's specific price was 24 cents/lb (r07/r09, a single-year figure distinct from the multi-year average). 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40**.
(Note: 3.60 − 3.42 = 0.18 points, slightly below the "flat 0.19" figure quoted elsewhere — expected, since the true gap is a constant *proportion* (5%) of the test, not a constant number of points, so it shrinks slightly at lower test values.)

**C3.** Using the station-gain ledger's own sample entry (r07/r12/r23, quoted identically: credited 57,760 lb, received 60,800 lb, gain 3,040 lb, "five and a quarter per cent, as usual") as a check on the aggregate figures:
(a) Total credited over the full affected period = **1,444,000 lb** (see C1c).
(b) Total actually received at Larrow Green in the station's cream = **1,520,000 lb**.
(c) Difference = 76,000 lb = 76,000 / 1,444,000 = **5.26%, "as usual"** — and I am explicit that this percentage is taken **of the credited (recorded) total**, not of the true/delivered total. The sample ledger line itself confirms this base (3,040/57,760 = 5.26%, matching "five and a quarter per cent" applied to the *credited* figure of 57,760, not the delivered 60,800). This directly contradicts Vaught's own bulletin wording (r11), which describes the 5.25% as a fraction of butterfat "actually delivered" — the ledger's own arithmetic shows the bulletin's own wording is backwards; I resolve using the ledger's arithmetic, which is unambiguous and self-checking.

**C4.**
(a) Invoice total = **$77.93** (Ammon & Sons' own facsimile invoice, r20, itemized and summing exactly: $25.20+$31.68+$0.90+$18.00+$2.15 = $77.93).
(b) The invoice bought **six dozen (72) pipettes** at 35 cents each — resolving the "five dozen" figure in Vaught's bulletin (r11) against two independent documentary sources (the invoice itself and the historical-society catalog) that both say six dozen. Each was blown to 17.6 c.c. but delivered a mean of 16.72 c.c. — short by 17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88 c.c.**

**C5.**
(a) The faulty glass was in service from **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923** — about 26 years and six weeks. The money/butterfat calculation, however, runs over **25 years**, matching clerk Bertram Kyle's own figure of "forty million pounds... sixteen hundred thousand pounds a year" (r23) computed for the 1925 arbitration. These are different numbers because the physical service period includes two partial calendar years — roughly eight months of 1897 and five and a half months of 1923 — while the monetary calculation appears to count only **full delivery years**, 1898 through 1922 inclusive, which is exactly 25 years.
(b) The station's first-season intake is not reliably fixed: Hobart Sill's firsthand estimate gives "about nine hundred thousand pounds" for the partial May–December 1896 season on borrowed glass (r14), while clerk Bertram Kyle separately recalls "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" for "that first year" (r23), a figure he admits is oral tradition and "not in any book I ever ruled." These may describe different windows (partial 1896 season vs. a fuller first operating year through April 1897) and **cannot be reconciled from the sources**; I report both rather than choose.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran **8 May–10 July 1911, nine weeks** (Keddie's own dated log entries, corroborated independently by the 1959 county report, r22, against a weaker "six weeks" figure found only in two later secondary memoirs, r05 and r16). The variable was which measuring glass was used (the station's own, versus a borrowed measure); held constant were the tester, the herds, the pasture, the season, and the procedure. The result: tests rose to the true level for the nine weeks and fell back the moment the station's own glass returned. This establishes, years before any direct measurement, that the cause of the shortfall was located in the equipment rather than in the patrons, the tester, or the land — a controlled natural experiment isolating one variable. It does **not** establish the specific mechanism (short delivery from a bad mold), the exact magnitude of the error, or why the station's original 1897 lot was bad in the first place; those required the 1958 calibration against a burette.

**D2.** Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream:
1. Emmet Brant's private weigh book shows his own barn-scale weight agreeing with the station's returned weight "day after day... barring a pound or two" for over a decade (r09) — a patron-side record, independent of the station's own books.
2. The 1911 interval itself: tests rose while Keddie remained the sole tester throughout, which is inconsistent with a theft theory pinned on him personally, and this evidence is drawn from the contemporaneous test book, not from anything measured in 1958.
3. Hobart Sill's independent creamery-side weighing at Larrow Green found Hollow cream consistently arrived "heavy" — more fat arriving than was credited to patrons, the opposite of what theft would produce (r14) — a third, physically separate record kept by a third person at a different location.
These are independent because they come from three different people, three different physical records, and three different points in the delivery chain (patron's farm, the station's own log, and the receiving creamery), none derived from another. Reason (3) — Sill's "heavy cream" observation — would still hold even if the 1958 glassware had never been measured, since it is a direct physical counter-indicator on its own terms.
Did bookkeeper Wilbur Teague, who advanced the theft theory at the 1925 hearing, ever question the ledger entries that refute it? No evidence that he did. He personally wrote the "station gain... as usual" line into the ledger every year for forty years (r02, r05), and clerk Bertram Kyle, who copied those same figures for a quarter-century, states plainly that it never crossed his own mind to wonder at them and that he "could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second [book] beside the fourth and looked" at the 1925 hearing (r23). The inconsistency between Teague's own recorded ledger and his oral theory appears to have gone unexamined by everyone, including Teague.

**D3.** Yes, manager Selby Vose knew the tests ran low before the glassware was condemned — that is precisely why he circulated the 1919 water-accusation circular, and why his 1926 address discusses the "1924 average as 3.90" and a subsequent "rise" as known facts. However, the sources indicate he likely never scrutinized the ledger lines that might have pointed to the true cause: Bertram Kyle, who sat outside his office door for a quarter-century, describes him reading only the bottom total of each sheet — "He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign" — and never the "station gain" lines above it (r23). So: he was aware of the symptom for years, but there is no evidence he examined the evidence that, read carefully, contradicted his own preferred theory.

## Section E — Contradictions

*(A representative, not exhaustive, list — the corpus contains dozens of minor numeric variances; these are the substantive ones.)*

1. **Pipettes purchased: "6 doz." (invoice, catalog — r20, r07) vs. "five dozen" (Vaught's bulletin, r11).** Resolved for six dozen: two independent documentary sources against one bulletin passage that is itself internally inconsistent (see C1a).
2. **Pipette mean delivery: 16.72 c.c. (r04/r07, Ivy's own measurement) vs. 16.27 c.c. (r11, Vaught's write-up of the same measurement).** A likely digit transposition. Resolved for 16.72 c.c. by arithmetic self-consistency with the bulletin's own headline "one part in twenty" claim (method: arithmetic).
3. **Pipettes surviving to 1958: nine (r04, r07, r21) vs. eleven (r12's 1959 footnote, r19 the newspaper).** *Two narrators carry the same wrong value here*: r12 and r19 both say eleven, but they are not independent — the newspaper (r19) was assembled "from a college press release and two telephone calls" days before Rundle's 1959 footnote citing ongoing work at the college; both likely trace to the same secondhand, garbled report reaching the valley. Against them stand three independent sources for nine, including Cleve Oram's decades-long personal familiarity with the physical crate itself (r21). Resolved for nine; tie broken by counting independent origins, not raw count of repetitions.
4. **Condemnation date of pipettes: 12 June 1923 (Strawn's own dated notebook, r06/r07; independently reconstructed in r22) vs. 12 July 1923 (Vose's 1926 address, r08; Rundle's pamphlet, r12).** *Two narrators carry the same wrong value*: Vose and Rundle agree on July, but Rundle's valley pamphlet is plausibly dependent on Association lore originating with Vose's own address rather than an independent record. I resolve for June, the only date resting on a contemporaneous primary document (Strawn's notebook), and note that Ammon & Sons' own letter book (r20) shows the Association's order was sent "the twenty-eighth of June," which is a very plausible source of the July-date confusion (people misremembering the order date as the condemnation date).
5. **Total station intake: 40,000,000 lb over 25 years (r02, r07, and independently Kyle's own clerical recollection, r23) vs. 44,000,000 lb (Teague's memoir, r05; Rundle's pamphlet, r12).** Resolved for 40,000,000 by arithmetic: it is the only figure that reproduces the bulletin's own stated 76,000 lb shortfall exactly when combined with the 0.19-point gap (see C1c); Teague and Rundle's 44,000,000 may share a common valley-lore origin.
6. **Butterfat shortfall: 76,000 lb (r04, r07, Bulletin 471's own conclusion) vs. 118,000 lb (Vaught's own Section V, r11) vs. 67,000 lb (patrons' 1925 petition estimate, per Frick, r13).** Resolved for 76,000 lb by arithmetic self-consistency (see C1c); the 118,000 lb figure is *Vaught's own document contradicting itself* (see next item); the 67,000 lb figure is not a measurement at all but a pre-1958 patron estimate and is not expected to match the later forensic figure.
7. **Self-contradiction: Bulletin 471 (r11) gives the glass's in-service start date as 1 May 1898 in its own Section III, but as 1 May 1897 in its own Section VII conclusion.** Resolved for 1897 using Orra Keddie's independent, contemporaneous 1 May 1897 diary entry (r24) — the strongest possible primary source, external to the bulletin's own internal inconsistency.
8. **Self-contradiction: Dell Rundle's 1939 pamphlet (r12) endorses the cream-theft theory in its main text, then retracts it in a "note added while in press" the same year**, after seeing the station-gain ledger entry showing cream arriving heavy rather than light — an explicit, dated, single-author reversal within one document.
9. **Self-contradiction: county agent Orin Falke's 1959 report (r22) initially calls Ivy Keddie Ansel's "great-niece" and partially credits the wet-hay theory, then appends a note "added on receipt of Bulletin No. 214" retracting both:** correcting her to "granddaughter" and withdrawing the wet-hay concession on seeing the bulletin's actual (not misquoted) figures.
10. **Award to patrons: $3,800 (r02, r08, r19, r22, r23 — five sources, several independent) vs. $3,500 (r21, Cleve Oram, secondhand from his father).** Resolved for $3,800 by count of genuinely independent witnesses, including Kyle, the clerk who processed the payment.
11. **1919 circular delivered to nine families (r17, Hazel Brant, 1981 oral recollection) vs. eleven families (r24, Orra Keddie's own diary, written the week it happened).** Resolved for eleven: a contemporaneous first-person diary entry outranks an oral recollection recorded sixty-two years later.
12. **Emmet Brant's weigh book begun January 1897 (arbitration exhibit list, r13; historical-society catalog, r07) vs. January 1900 (Hazel Brant, r17) vs. January 1898 (Roy Brant's own 1955 memory, r09).** Three different dates from three sources of declining documentary weight; resolved for 1897, the only date attached to a contemporaneous exhibit list compiled for a legal hearing at the time.
13. **Orra Keddie's relationship to Emmet Brant: full siblings, "same mother and same father" (r09, explicit rebuttal of a stepmother story) vs. half-siblings "by his father's second wife" (r17, Hazel Brant).** **Cannot be settled from the sources.** Roy Brant's account is emphatic and clearly aimed at rebutting exactly the claim Hazel later makes, but Hazel's account is equally direct and from a family member of the next generation; neither is a documentary record, and I decline to guess between two contradicting firsthand family claims.

## Section F — Theory

**F1.** The Ostrey Hollow shortfall was produced by a single manufacturing defect: the lot of pipettes Ammon & Sons sold the Association in 1897, blown to a mark of 17.6 c.c., in fact delivered a mean of 16.72 c.c. — 95% of their stated charge, "one part in twenty" short (the firm's own historian, r20, speculates this came from a reground mold that ran short without anyone on the blowing floor catching it). Because every butterfat test computed a percentage from the milk this glass delivered, every reading from this station was multiplied by that same 0.95 factor, regardless of the true fat content — which explains why the error was proportional and nearly constant across seasons (about 0.19–0.18 points, shrinking slightly at lower test values, exactly as the 5% ratio predicts) rather than varying with feed, weather, or individual patron as a real quality problem would. It began on 1 May 1897, the day the new (defective) glass went into service, and ended on 12 June 1923, the day a routine state inspection finally checked the glass itself against a calibrated burette rather than against the reasonable assumption that manufactured glassware delivers what is marked on it. Nothing at the station or on the farms needed to change at either end, because nothing there was ever wrong — the herds, the hay, the scales, and the tester's technique were constant throughout, as the 1911 natural experiment (Story 4) demonstrated years before anyone understood why.

Three competing explanations were current, and each is defeated by specific evidence:
- **Added water** (advanced by manager Selby Vose, 1919 circular and 1925 hearing): defeated by the state inspector's own lactometer tests at every visit from 1908 onward, which never once found water; also by the 1911 interval, where the same patrons' milk read correctly under different glass.
- **Cream theft by the tester** (advanced obliquely by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague, 1925 hearing): defeated by the Association's own "station gain" ledger, which Teague himself recorded, showing cream consistently arriving heavier than credited — the reverse of what theft produces — and by the 1911 interval, since Keddie remained the sole tester throughout.
- **Wet hay / bog meadow feed** (advanced by the 1908 committee under Nyle Grigg, and defended scientifically by Dr. Roland Kepp in 1926): defeated by Bulletin 214's actual findings (a depression of at most 0.05 in winter and nothing in summer — far too small, and wrongly quoted as "0.20" by both Grigg and Kepp), and decisively by the Alder Corners data (Story 5), showing the same farms' milk on the same ground testing normally when delivered elsewhere.

Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware? **No, so far as the sources show.** The crate sat in the loft directly above the room where two of the board's three sessions were held (r13, r21, r23 all place it there), yet Frick's own 1946 recollection discusses only the three human/land theories and concludes the true answer "may have been in the room all along" — an admission, not a claim of having looked.

What did the tester himself believe caused it? The sources conflict, and I resolve in favor of the more primary account: Duncan Keddie's 1968 family notes (r18) record his father saying, at the actual time of the 1911 break, "it was something in the glass," though he never dared write it down unproved — a contemporaneous, dated, first-person family memory. This contradicts Alonzo Frick's secondhand 1946 account of a private courthouse-corridor remark that Keddie suspected the patrons (r13), reported decades later by a lawyer who admits he never understood the chemistry. I give more weight to Duncan's account for its proximity to the actual 1911 event and its status as a direct family memory rather than reported hearsay.

## Section G — Summary (150 words)

For sixty years, milk from six Hessel Bottom farms delivered to the Larrow Valley Cooperative's Ostrey Hollow skimming station tested lower in butterfat than milk anywhere else in the valley, costing patrons real money under the co-op's pay-by-test rule. Neighbors blamed watered milk, an embezzling tester, or the bottom's wet, sour hay; a 1925 arbitration board heard all three theories, proved none, and dismissed tester Ansel Keddie regardless. The true cause — pipettes from a flawed 1897 batch that delivered five percent less than marked — sat forgotten in a station-loft crate from the day a state inspector condemned it in 1923 until Keddie's granddaughter climbed the ladder in 1958 and measured the glass herself. No single witness — clerk, hauler, bookkeeper, buttermaker, widow, or lawyer — ever held the ledger, the diary, the private weigh book, and the crate together at once; only assembling all eight strands of family and institutional memory reveals the whole mechanism.
