# Exhaustive notes — retellings r01–r16 (Ostrey Hollow / Larrow Valley milk-testing saga)

All sixteen retellings concern the same core historical episode: the Ostrey Hollow skimming station (part of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, Larrow Green, Ashlin, Ordell County) and the decades-long dispute over its low butterfat test readings, tester Ansel Keddie's dismissal, and the eventual 1958 forensic discovery that the station's test pipettes were mechanically short, exonerating Keddie. Sources conflict heavily on dates, dollar amounts, percentages, durations, and genealogy — all conflicts are flagged below. This file carries forward everything established after r01–r08 and adds everything learned from r09–r16.

## Cast of people (with relationships) — carried forward + updated

- **Ansel Keddie** — tester at Ostrey Hollow skimming station from its opening (1896) until dismissed 30 April 1925. Kept meticulous test book in five columns (date, patron, pounds, test, fat) from 4 May 1896. Married to **Orra Keddie** (née Brant). Died 1934 (r04); Strawn's 1934 circuit note independently confirms a change of hand in the book that year, consistent with his death. Grandfather of **Ivy Keddie** (r04's narrator) through his son **Duncan Keddie**, who taught school at Ashlin. **New (r13):** privately told arbitration chairman Alonzo Frick, in the Ashlin courthouse corridor, that he had always thought the patrons were at fault (i.e., suspected them of watering) but would not say so publicly out of thirty years' loyalty to his neighbors — meaning Keddie went to his grave "accusing nobody" (true) but not "suspecting nobody" (false), per Frick. This nuances but does not overturn the 1958 forensic exoneration (which concerns the *mechanism*, not what Keddie personally believed).
- **Orra Keddie** (née Brant) — Ansel Keddie's wife; sister of Emmet Brant. **r09 (Roy Brant, her nephew) states explicitly: "Orra Keddie was my father's sister. They were full brother and sister, same mother and same father, whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it"** — a direct, pointed rebuttal aimed at unspecified other accounts suggesting a half-sibling/stepmother relationship (no such claim appears in r01–r08's notes as transcribed, so this rebuttal's target is unclear/unconfirmed, but it is explicitly flagged by Roy Brant as something people say). Per r01, also first cousin of "old Mrs. Vose." After the 1919 circular, she and Emmet never spoke again.
- **Emmet Brant** — biggest patron on Hessel Bottom; Orra's brother; born **1854**, died **1929** (r09 — new exact dates). Kept a personal farm ledger (see "Emmet Brant's weigh book" below) from 1 January 1898 (per r09's own text) — **note internal r09 date conflict**: r09's body text says "From the first of January, 1898... he wrote down every can," but Frick's exhibit list (r13) describes it as "The private weigh book of Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom, begun 1 January 1897" — a one-year discrepancy (1897 vs 1898) between Roy Brant's own account of his father's book and the arbitration exhibit list description of the same book. Stopped speaking to Ansel Keddie after the 1919 circular — per r09, not over the water accusation itself but because Emmet felt Keddie (who kept the test book) should have publicly objected to the circular and did not.
- **Roy Brant** — Emmet Brant's son; **born such that he is 67 years old in 1955** (r09, so born c. 1888); farmer at Hessel Bottom; inherited/kept his father's weigh book from 1929 (his father's death) and continued it "in the same book until it was filled and then in another"; wrote a 1955 account for the county farm bureau (r09) with a 1958 postscript describing lending the book to Ivy Keddie ("Duncan's girl Ivy") for her Farland college research; the book returns to him and afterward goes to his daughter Hazel. First cousin to Duncan Keddie (Ansel/Orra's son). His daughter **Hazel** and Duncan's daughter **Ivy** are second cousins (matches r12 footnote calling Ivy "second cousin to Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom").
- **Hazel Brant** — Roy Brant's daughter; will inherit the Brant weigh book after Roy; also received it in 1962 before it went to the Historical Rooms in 1963 (r07). r01 described a 1958 researcher as "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed" — this description does not cleanly match "second cousin" (r09, r12), a further wrinkle in the identity-of-the-1958-researcher ambiguity (see Oddities).
- **Gideon Vose** — organized/founded the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association in 1889 at Larrow Green; first president, served 1889–1911 (22 years); personally drafted the by-laws including Article VII; died 1917 (r02). **r12 (Rundle's pamphlet) describes him as "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the law and thought better of it," who wrote the by-laws "at his own kitchen table," and explicitly calls Selby Vose "his nephew"** — agreeing with r01 but conflicting with r02/r08 which call Selby his son (see persistent unresolved genealogical conflict below). Kept a herd of Jerseys at "the home place."
- **Selby Vose** — manager of the Association from 1913–1938 (r02); signed the 1919 circular; gave the 1926 annual address (r08). **CONFLICT (now three-way):** "nephew" of Gideon (r01, r12) vs. "son" of Gideon (r02, and Vose's own words in r08: "My father, who was your first president..."). Note r12 is itself internally consistent with r01 on this point, strengthening the "nephew" camp numerically (two sources) against the "son" camp (also two sources, r02 and r08/Vose's own first-person claim) — the conflict remains unresolved, now evenly split 2-2 rather than 1-2.
- **"Old Mrs. Vose"** — Gideon Vose's wife; first cousin of Orra Keddie (r01).
- **Effie Loomis** — secretary of the Association 1901–1940; wrote "Notes toward a history of the Association" in 1941 (r02).
- **Wilbur Teague** — bookkeeper 1905–1945; wrote memoir "The Books of the Association" (r05) in winter 1948. **New (r13):** at the 1925 arbitration hearing, Teague was the one who put forward, obliquely, the theory that "the tester had been taking cream for himself and writing low tests to cover the loss" — per Frick, Teague did this "in the way an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it," answering at length about how the account would look if a man were helping himself, then noting he made no charge against anybody. This is a more pointed characterization than earlier notes had (which described Teague's testimony as more general, about the one-man arrangement inviting embezzlement "without accusing anyone specifically" — r13 suggests his testimony, while not a formal accusation, was in substance the theft theory, one of the three explanations before the board).
- **Hobart Sill** — buttermaker at Larrow Green creamery for **41 years**, from **1893** to his retirement in **1934** (r14, new firsthand retirement remarks). Kept the churn book and receiving book. **Major new content (r14):** it was Sill's own glassware, from his own creamery bench, that was lent to open the Ostrey Hollow station in May 1896 when the station's own ordered outfit had not yet arrived — corroborating the station's own first leaf ("Glass borrowed of Larrow Green") over Keddie's own letter's claim that it was borrowed "off the Ashlin creamery" (r03). Sill also mentions "one piece of it was chipped and I never said a word about that" when his glass was returned to him the following spring — possibly explaining, or at least echoing, the later finding (r07 Item 5) that two of the nine surviving 1958 pipettes were chipped at the tip (though these are different lots of glass — Sill's personal loaner glass in 1896 vs. the 1897 Ammon & Sons purchase that later became the condemned crate — so this may be a coincidental echo rather than the same objects; flagged as a possible connection, not a confirmed one). Sill independently weighed Hollow cream at the creamery every year and found it consistently "ran heavy" — i.e., more fat arrived at Larrow Green than the station's paperwork credited to patrons — matching (from an independent, non-bookkeeping source) the "station gain" ledger phenomenon. Sill's own trained-eye estimate: Larrow Green's own patrons' milk tested **3.78** "one year with another"; he believed the Hollow's true milk was "a 3.85 and no better." He personally believed patrons were watering their milk despite the heavy-cream evidence he himself observed, attributing a mid-1920s rise in recorded tests to patrons having been "caught" and stopping — a folk theory paralleling Selby Vose's and different from the (correct) pipette explanation.
- **Alonzo Frick** — attorney of Ashlin; chaired the 1925 board of arbitration. **New (r13):** wrote his own 1946 recollection for the Ordell County Bar Association yearbook, at age 74, describing himself as "the one of the three who kept no cow" (implying the board had three members total, with Frick as an outside/impartial chair). Revealed the hearing was held in **three sessions**: the first at the Association's office at Ashlin on 17 March 1925; a second and third that Frick specifically arranged to hold at Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's own weigh room, so the board could see the plant directly rather than have it described to them (no exact dates given for sessions 2–3, only that the overall hearing period is 17 March – 4 May 1925 per prior sources). Frick candidly states he "did not understand the test then and do not understand it now," being a lawyer and not a chemist, and speculates in hindsight that "a lawyer who understood chemistry might have found" the true answer that eluded the board. Reprints the arbitration's exhibit list (see Documents below) — this resolves an earlier-flagged cataloging oddity (see Oddities, updated).
- **Vira Toland** — narrator of r01; the account of settlement as $3,500 (not $3,800) tied to a "caught stealing cream" theory remains uncorroborated by any of r09–r16 and continues to conflict with the documentary consensus.
- **Ivy Keddie** — Ansel Keddie's granddaughter; gave 1959 talk "What the Crate Held"; State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958), co-authored with Professor (now given full name) **Leland Vaught** (r11). Borrowed Emmet Brant's weigh book from Roy Brant in 1958 (per both r04/r07 and now r09 directly, in Roy's own words, confirming the loan and its return). Wrote to Rosalie Cudd at Alder Corners (r10) asking what four named Ordell County farmers' milk had tested there — Rosalie's letter (r10) is presented as the direct reply to this inquiry, quoted in full below. Described in r12's 1959-reprint footnote as "second cousin to Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom" (matching r09's genealogy).
- **Rosalie Cudd** — runs the Alder Corners Cheese Company in Vessey County, over Redlow Ridge. **Major new content (r10, her own 1958 letter to Ivy Keddie, quoted extensively below):** her father, **Jerome Cudd**, "built this factory in nineteen thirteen" (**CONFLICT:** r12/Rundle's pamphlet instead states "in 1914 the Alder Corners Cheese Company was opened... by Jerome Cudd" — a one-year discrepancy between Rosalie's own firsthand account of her father's factory and Rundle's secondhand pamphlet account). Also, **CONFLICT on relationship:** r10 (Rosalie's own letter) plainly states Jerome Cudd was "my father," and r12 independently agrees ("Jerome Cudd's daughter, Rosalie Cudd" runs it now) — both consistent with each other as "daughter," but this directly contradicts the r01-derived note in the prior (r01–r08) summary, which had described Jerome Cudd as Rosalie's *uncle* ("niece of Jerome Cudd... the factory's founder"). This is a clear three-source-vs-one conflict: r10 and r12 (both new) agree Rosalie is Jerome's **daughter**; the earlier note (attributed to r04 in the prior summary) called her his **niece**. Jerome Cudd died in March 1941, "between the setting and the cutting of a vat" (r10); Rosalie took over the factory that same spring and had run it "seventeen years now" as of the 1958 letter (1941+17=1958, internally consistent). She was "a girl of twelve when the first vat was set" (1913+12 would put her birth c. 1901, if the 1913 founding date is used). Her letter to Ivy Keddie provides 16 seasons (1915–1930) of test data (see Documents/Tables below) for the four Hessel Bottom farmers who hauled to Alder Corners in summer.
- **Merle Strawn** — state dairy inspector, Ordell circuit, 1907–1936.
- **Professor Leland Vaught** — full name now known (r11); professor of dairy husbandry at the State Agricultural College at Farland; wrote the introduction and method sections of Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958), reproduced in r11, closing with the bulletin's conclusion (quoted identically to earlier sources).
- **Halbert Nym** — curator, 1972 accession notes, Ordell County Historical Rooms.
- **Duncan Keddie** — Ansel Keddie's son; taught school at Ashlin; father of Ivy Keddie; first cousin to Roy Brant (r09).
- **Nyle Grigg** — of Larrow Green; chaired the 1908 investigating committee into Hessel Bottom milk complaints (confirmed by name across r09, r12, and now r15 in full, his own 1919 covering letter reprinting the 1908 report). Explicitly states in his 1919 letter that he is "not a patron of that station," hauls to Larrow Green, and had "no interest whatever in the answer," offered as proof of impartiality. States that payment-by-test "came in with the 1893 meeting" (see date conflict below). States the committee "sampled those herds through the whole of 1908... every month of that year," visiting "each of the six farms, some of them three and four times," without advance warning of visits.
- **Dell Rundle** — schoolteacher at Larrow Green; author of the 1939 jubilee pamphlet "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years" (r12), reprinted 1959 with one added footnote and the author's own retraction note "added while in press" in 1939 (see Documents below). Describes himself as having "taught the school at the Green a good many years" and compiled the pamphlet "from the recollections of old members and two winters of asking questions at the store." Explicitly endorses (in the main 1939 text) the folk belief that cream was stolen at the station, then explicitly retracts this belief in a same-year "note added while in press" after seeing the station-gain ledger entry — a rare instance of a single author holding and then reversing a position within one document.
- **Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S.** — veterinary surgeon of Ashlin, thirty years' practice as of 1926; author of "The bog meadows and the milk," a paper read to the Ordell County Farm Club in 1926 (r16), arguing the scientific/mineral-deficiency case for the wet-hay theory. Explicitly states he has "never in his life read a test and would not know how" and disclaims any competence or interest in the "quarrel" about the station itself, confining himself to soil/herbage/animal physiology. Dismisses the 1911 nine/six-week anomaly (he says "six weeks") as too short a window (mid-flush summer) to prove anything.

## Timeline — new and updated entries from r09–r16

(Carrying forward all r01–r08 timeline entries; only new information and new conflicts are detailed here to avoid repetition, but nothing from the prior file is superseded unless explicitly marked CONFLICT/UPDATE below.)

- **1891, 1892, or 1893 — three-way conflict on when payment-by-test (Article VII) began.** Prior note (r02) said adopted 1892 at the annual meeting. **r12 (Rundle's pamphlet) says "Article VII dates from 1891, the year the churn was set up"** (and separately states the creamery/churn was built in 1891). **r15 (Grigg's 1919 letter) says "Payment by test came in with the 1893 meeting."** All three dates (1891, 1892, 1893) now appear across different sources for the same by-law's adoption.
- **1893** — Hobart Sill begins as buttermaker at Larrow Green creamery (r14); at that time "there was no floor in this room but plank, and we cooled with creek ice out of the pond."
- **1896, May** — station opens. **New detail (r14):** the station's own ordered equipment had not yet arrived when the crowd gathered for the opening; there was talk of postponing the whole season; Hobart Sill personally lent his own creamery glassware from Larrow Green to open the station, and it was used until the station's own outfit arrived "the spring after" (1897), at which point Sill's glass was returned to him, one piece chipped. This corroborates the "borrowed of Larrow Green" wording on the station book's own first leaf, and stands against Keddie's own later letter claiming the glass was borrowed "off the Ashlin creamery."
- **1896, May–December (partial first season)** — the station took in "about nine hundred thousand pounds" of milk (r14, new figure, not given in any earlier source).
- **1897, 1 January or 1898, 1 January — one-year conflict on when Emmet Brant's farm weigh book begins.** r09 (Roy Brant's own account) states his father began the book "from the first of January, 1898." Frick's arbitration exhibit list (r13) describes the same book (explicitly: "The private weigh book of Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom") as "begun 1 January 1897." Note: the prior (r01–r08) notes file had this book (there mislabeled/misattributed in the historical-society catalog, r07 Item 4) beginning "1 January 1897" as well — so the documentary sources (r07, r13) agree on 1897, while Roy Brant's own personal recollection (r09) gives 1898. This is now resolved as to *whose* book it is (Emmet Brant's, confirmed independently by r09 and r13 — see Oddities/Resolved below) but a new one-year date conflict has appeared regarding its start.
- **1902** — first complaints. **r14 (Sill) adds a specific anecdotal memory**: "a man in here shouting that his milk tested under his brother-in-law's down the creek and it was the same feed and the same water" — this is presented as the first (1902) complaint Sill recalls; it is not clearly identified whose complaint this was, and its "brother-in-law" framing does not obviously map onto known relationships (Keddie and Emmet Brant were brothers-in-law, but Keddie was the tester, not a patron competing on the same station's figures against a specific brother-in-law elsewhere "down the creek") — flagged as a loose/unexplained anecdote, possibly referring to a different, otherwise unnamed pair of patrons.
- **1905** — Hessel Bottom farms hold rights on the bottom: r09 explicitly says "Six farms had rights on it," and r15 (Grigg's 1908 report, reprinted) also says "Hessel Bottom, where six farms cut hay and turn out stock." r16 (Kepp) also says "Six farms cut hay and pasture stock upon it." **This directly conflicts with the earlier (r01–r08-derived) note describing Hessel Bottom as having "seven farms holding hay/pasture rights."** Three new sources (r09, r15, r16) all independently agree on six farms; the "seven farms" figure now appears to be an outlier possibly traceable to r02, worth treating with suspicion.
- **1908** — Nyle Grigg named chairman of the investigating committee (confirmed explicitly by name in r09, r12, r15). r15 gives the fullest version of the 1908 report yet (quoted in full below), including new details: the committee visited "each of the six farms, some of them three and four times," drew and sealed their own samples, watched the test made, tried the station scales against a known weight and the patrons' own scales against the station's, and inquired into water supply at each barn. New physical/husbandry observations: "The herds are thin in the flank at the end of the winter feeding and slow to come to their flesh in the spring. Their coats are rough later in the season than the coats of hill herds." Report signed simply "NYLE GRIGG, chairman, and the committee" (other two members not named anywhere).
- **1910** — Selby Vose's Jersey herd tested 4.56 (carried forward). No new information.
- **1911, the pipette-break interval — duration now three ways disputed.** Keddie's own dated entries and r03/r04/r11 give "nine weeks" (8 May–10 July). Teague's 1948 memoir (r05) says "six weeks." **New: both r16 (Kepp, 1926) and, by implication, Kepp's unnamed interlocutor ("a gentleman... urged upon me") also use "six weeks."** So the "six weeks" figure now has two independent sources (Teague's memoir and Kepp's 1926 paper/discussion) against the "nine weeks" of the primary log and r03/r04/r11 — the conflict is not resolved but the six-weeks camp has grown.
- **1913 or 1914 — Alder Corners Cheese Company founding date conflict (new).** Rosalie Cudd's own letter (r10) says her father Jerome Cudd "built this factory in nineteen thirteen." Rundle's pamphlet (r12) says "in 1914 the Alder Corners Cheese Company was opened... by Jerome Cudd." One-year conflict between the proprietor's own firsthand account and the secondhand valley pamphlet.
- **1915–1930 — Hessel Bottom farmers hauling to Alder Corners in summer (elaborated).** r09 (Roy Brant) states: "From 1915, four of the Bottom men hauled their milk over Redlow Ridge to the cheese factory in the summer flush and back to the Hollow the rest of the year... They went on that way for sixteen seasons." Roy Brant states he did not go over ("We had the biggest herd on the meadow, and the biggest herd is the one that cannot afford to be talked about"). r10 (Rosalie Cudd) confirms: "From 1915, four men off Hessel Bottom hauled their milk over to me in the summer flush... They came for sixteen seasons. The last of those summers was 1930." (1915+16 seasons ending 1930 is internally consistent: 1915,1916,...,1930 = 16 years.) Rosalia's letter includes a full season-by-season test table for 1915–1930 (reproduced in Documents/Tables below), all readings 3.78–3.82, averaging about 3.80 across the sixteen summers — this independently and closely matches the pipette-corrected "true" value (3.80–3.905 depending on source) established decades later, and directly contradicts the wet-hay theory (same farms' milk, same wet ground, tested normally at Alder Corners). Her father's own 1919 season note, copied verbatim: "*The four Hessel Bottom men, summer of 1919: 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge.*" Rosalie adds that cheese-making reveals watered milk immediately (soft curd, poor yield) and that in sixteen summers her father "never made one complaint of those four men, never docked one of them a cent."
- **1919** — Nyle Grigg's covering letter (r15) reprints the 1908 report this year because "the Ostrey Hollow question having been reopened this season by men who were boys when we made it," and adds a claim that Bulletin No. 214 (published sometime before 1919, per Grigg — note this predates the bulletin's associated 1911 feeding trial being formally cited elsewhere as from "1911") found the bog hay depressed the test "by two tenths in the winter feeding" — **CONFLICT: this directly contradicts the bulletin's own quoted text** (given identically in r04, r07, and r11): "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Two tenths (0.20) vs. five hundredths (0.05) is a four-fold misstatement — and this same "two tenths" misquote is independently repeated by Dr. Kepp in his 1926 paper (r16): "it found the butter fat test depressed by two tenths in the winter feeding" — meaning two separate wet-hay-theory proponents (Grigg in 1919, Kepp in 1926) both cite Bulletin 214 as showing four times the depression it actually reported, suggesting either a shared misreading circulating in the valley or a rhetorical inflation by hay-theory advocates.
- **1925 — new details on the arbitration hearing's structure (r13).** The board sat in three sessions, not necessarily all at Ashlin: first session 17 March 1925 at the Association's office, Ashlin; second and third sessions (undated individually) at Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's own weigh room, at Frick's specific request, so the board could observe the physical plant directly. Frick reports the board watched the scales tried, watched sampling and testing done twice, and could not fault the physical operation despite not understanding the chemistry. **Three explanations were formally put to the board, one per identified party:** (1) water added by patrons — put by manager Selby Vose, "without heat and without proof"; (2) cream theft by the tester — put obliquely by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague; (3) wet hay — put via the 1908 committee report, read into the record, occupying "the better part of an afternoon." Frick states "not one of them was proved" and reflects that the true answer (the pipette flaw) may have been in the room all along but unrecognized by any of the three theories.
- **1925 — new/conflicting figure for the patrons' claimed loss (r13).** Frick states: "The patrons' own reckoning of what they had lost was sixty-seven thousand pounds of butter fat over the years complained of, and nothing was ever brought forward to disturb that figure." This is a *third* distinct butterfat-shortfall figure, joining the earlier 76,000 lb (r04/r07/Bulletin 471 conclusion, quoted identically in r11) and the 118,000 lb computed in r11's own body text (Section V) — see Quantitative Conflicts below for full three-way comparison.
- **1926 — Selby Vose's address; carried forward** (13th year as manager, defends 1919 circular and $3,800 payment, cites 1924 average as 3.90, condemnation date as 12 July 1923). **New corroboration/conflict (r16):** Dr. Kepp's 1926 paper independently cites the test gap as **0.21 (twenty-one hundredths)** — matching Vose's own 1926 figure for the "rise" almost exactly — while other sources (Keddie's book, Strawn, Bulletin 471) put the true gap nearer 0.19. Kepp's figure of "twenty-one hundredths" thus aligns with Vose's account rather than with Keddie's/Strawn's/Vaught's figures.
- **1926 or 1939 — condemnation date of the pipettes, now three dates in circulation.** 12 June 1923 (Strawn's own notebook, r06/r07 Item 9) vs. 12 July 1923 (Selby Vose's 1926 address, r08) — carried forward — and **now also 12 July 1923 again independently in r12 (Rundle's 1939 pamphlet): "The state inspector condemned the station's glassware on the twelfth of July, 1923"** — meaning the July date now has two independent sources (Vose's speech and Rundle's pamphlet) against the June date's one primary source (Strawn's own dated notebook entry, itself the presumptively most reliable contemporaneous record).
- **1934** — Hobart Sill retires after 41 years as buttermaker at Larrow Green (new exact figure, r14); his retirement remarks are the source text of r14.
- **1939** — Dell Rundle's original pamphlet "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years" is printed at Larrow Green for the Association's 50th anniversary (r12). Contains the "note added while in press" in which Rundle retracts his own chapter IV claim that cream was stolen, having just seen the station-gain ledger entry showing cream arrived at Larrow Green *heavier*, not lighter, than credited — "Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand."
- **1946** — Alonzo Frick, now 74, writes his recollection of the arbitration for the Ordell County Bar Association yearbook (r13) — the source text of that retelling.
- **1955, winter** — Roy Brant, 67, writes his account of his father's weigh book for the county farm bureau's local-history collection (r09) — the source text of that retelling.
- **1958** — Ivy Keddie's research year, elaborated: she also wrote to Rosalie Cudd at Alder Corners asking specifically what four named Ordell County farmers' milk had tested there (r10, Rosalie's reply is the source text of that retelling); she borrowed Emmet Brant's weigh book directly from Roy Brant, per Roy's own account (r09), confirming and personalizing the r04/r07 record of this loan.
- **1958, October — Bulletin No. 471, now with an authored introduction (r11, by Leland Vaught).** Vaught's method section adds substantial new technical content and numbers, several of which conflict with everything established previously — see Quantitative Conflicts below. His own conclusion paragraph (Section VII) is word-for-word identical to the one already quoted in r04/r07: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."
- **1959 (reprint)** — Rundle's 1939 pamphlet is reissued with one new footnote (quoted below) referencing Ivy Keddie's ongoing work at Farland and stating "**Eleven** pipettes out of the station's old condemned crate were found still to be in existence" — **CONFLICT: this contradicts every other source's count of nine surviving pipettes** (r04, r07 Item 5, and r11 Section III, which all independently say nine of the original lot were recovered unbroken/legible). Rundle's "eleven" appears to be an error (possibly conflating it with something else, or simply mistaken secondhand information reaching the aging schoolteacher), unresolved.

9. **The full 1908 committee report, reprinted by Grigg in 1919 (r15), quoted in full** — supersedes/expands the partial version previously logged (which only had the closing lines):
   > "REPORT OF THE COMMITTEE APPOINTED TO INQUIRE INTO THE COMPLAINTS OF THE PATRONS OF THE OSTREY HOLLOW STATION.
   > To the President and Directors of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association.
   > Your committee, appointed in January last, respectfully reports as follows.
   > The complaint before us is of some years' standing and is in substance this: that the milk delivered at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station is returned by the test at a figure below that of milk delivered elsewhere in this valley, and that the patrons of that station are in consequence paid less for the same labor and the same cows than their neighbors are paid. Since payment by test came in among us and the pound of butter fat took the place of the pound of milk as the measure of a man's month, a difference of this kind is no longer a matter of opinion. It is a matter of money, and it comes out of the same pocket every month, and your committee has approached it in that light and not as a quarrel.
   > Hessel Bottom, where six farms cut hay and turn out stock, is a wet meadow lying along the creek under Ostrey Hollow. Its ground is springy the year round and is under water in part every spring. Its herbage is sedge, rush, and the coarse sour grasses that come in on such land, and its hay is cut late and heavy and is fed as the main dependence of those farms from November to April. There is no better winter keep to be had there. We say this without reproach to any man on that bottom; the land is what it is, and they took it as their fathers left it to them.
   > Your committee visited each of the six farms, some of them three and four times. We drew our samples ourselves and we sealed them ourselves. We saw the herds milked, we saw the cans filled, and we saw them hauled to the station and weighed in at the intake, and we stood by while the test was made and saw nothing whatever in the doing of it to remark upon. We tried the station scales against a known weight and we tried the patrons' own scales against the station's. We inquired into the water supply at each barn and satisfied ourselves upon it.
   > Your committee further observed the general condition of the stock upon that bottom, as men who have kept cattle all their lives are entitled to observe it. The herds are thin in the flank at the end of the winter feeding and slow to come to their flesh in the spring. Their coats are rough later in the season than the coats of hill herds. They do well enough in flow, for the wet land is generous of milk, but it is our opinion, formed on the ground and not out of a book, that what such land gives in quantity it withholds in quality, and that a cow cannot put into her pail what her keep has never put into her.
   > We report that we found no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter, and we wish that finding to be printed in the same type as the rest.
   > We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March. The milk is whole and the scales are honest. It is our opinion that the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station.
   > NYLE GRIGG, chairman, and the committee."

## Quantitative/statistical claims and conflicts — expanded with r09–r16

- **Pipette mean delivery:** 16.72 c.c. (r04, r07 Item 14 — Ivy Keddie's own 1958 measurement sheet) **vs.** 16.27 c.c. (r11, Vaught's bulletin introduction, describing what should be the same nine pipettes) — a stark, unresolved numerical conflict between two sections of what is nominally the same research (Keddie's field measurement vs. Vaught's write-up of it), differing not just slightly but by transposed digits.
- **Pipettes purchased in the original lot:** "6 doz." (six dozen) per the Ammon & Sons invoice itself (r07 Item 2, quoted verbatim) **vs.** "five dozen" per Vaught's bulletin (r11, Section III) describing the same original purchase.
- **Pipettes surviving to 1958:** nine (r04; r07 Item 5; r11 Section III — three independent, consistent sources) **vs.** eleven (r12's 1959 footnote) — the "eleven" figure is an outlier against three consistent "nine" sources.
- **Butterfat shortfall over the affected period — now three figures:** 76,000 lb (r04's calculation; r07 Item 6; Bulletin 471's own Conclusion/Section VII in r11) **vs.** 118,000 lb (Vaught's own Section V computation in the very same bulletin, r11) **vs.** 67,000 lb ("the patrons' own reckoning," per Frick, r13, presented at the 1925 arbitration, predating and independent of the 1958 bulletin). All three figures purport to describe substantially the same quantity (total butterfat shorted to Hollow patrons over the long dispute) but none agree.
- **True/corrected average test:** 3.80 (r04's calculation, 3.61÷0.95) **vs.** 3.905 (r11 Section V, 3.61÷0.9244) — stemming directly from the 16.72-vs-16.27 c.c. discrepancy above.
- **Size of the "gap" between Hollow and valley-at-large milk, now four-to-five figures in play:** ~0.19 (documentary/forensic consensus: Keddie's book, Strawn, Bulletin 471 conclusion) vs. 0.21 (Selby Vose's 1926 address; independently matched by Kepp's 1926 paper) vs. 0.20/"two tenths" (Grigg's and Kepp's shared misquote of Bulletin 214, and also Kepp's own independent field estimate "a fifth of a point") vs. 0.07 (implied by Hobart Sill's own estimate: Larrow Green 3.78 vs. his believed true Hollow figure of 3.85, r14) vs. 0.295 (Vaught's Section V computation, r11).
- **Condemnation date of pipettes, now with two sources for July:** 12 June 1923 (Strawn's own dated notebook, r06/r07 Item 9; also cited as this date in Bulletin 471's conclusion, r04/r07/r11) vs. 12 July 1923 (Selby Vose's 1926 address, r08, AND independently Dell Rundle's 1939 pamphlet, r12) — the July date now has two sources against the June date's (still probably more reliable, being a contemporaneous primary record) single source.
- **Farms with rights on Hessel Bottom:** six (r09, r15's 1908 report, r16 — three independent, mutually consistent sources) vs. seven (the r01–r08-derived prior note, sourced to r02) — six now appears to be the better-supported figure.

- **Overall average test across the whole period, per Teague's memoir:** 3.16% (r05) — now independently corroborated by **Frick's 1946 recollection (r13): "The patrons of that station had been paid, year upon year, on an average test of 3.16"** — this is the first time the otherwise-outlier 3.16 figure (previously only in Teague's memoir, conflicting with the more common 3.61) receives independent corroboration from a second, differently-motivated source (the arbitration chairman). ## Oddities, internal inconsistencies, and unresolved ambiguities — updated

- **RESOLVED (partially):** The r07 Item 3/Item 4 mislabeling flagged in the prior notes (test book captioned "kept by E. Brant" but signed by Keddie; farm weigh book captioned "kept by A. - **NEW — Selby Vose's relationship to Gideon Vose, now evenly contested:** "nephew" (r01 and now also r12) vs. "son" (r02 and Vose's own words in r08) — 2 sources each way, unresolved.
