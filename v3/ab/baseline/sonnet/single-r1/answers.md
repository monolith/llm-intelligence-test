# Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

Method note: throughout, I prefer (in descending order) quoted primary documents (invoices, bulletin text, diary entries written at the time) over later memoirs; documents written by the person with direct custody of the fact (a clerk's ledger, a firm's own invoice) over secondhand valley report; and I flag every place two narrators share what is probably a single garbled source rather than treating them as independent confirmation.

---

## Story 1 — The founding of the Association and the Ostrey Hollow station (1889–1897)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was organized in 1889 by farmers meeting at Larrow Green, who built their main creamery there in 1891 (Falke, r22). Its by-laws' Article 7 fixed payment by butterfat test rather than milk weight — the provision that made every later dispute a matter of money rather than opinion; the exact adoption date and the Association's earliest minute book are lost, and Falke's report candidly says so rather than guessing (r22). Gideon Vose organized the Association, was its first president, and wrote its by-laws (Duncan Keddie, r18; confirmed independently by clerk Bertram Kyle, r23, who explicitly rejects a rumor that Selby Vose was Gideon's nephew rather than son).

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up-valley, to serve Hessel Bottom's six farms. The station opened 4 May 1896 (Falke, r22), initially using glass borrowed from the Larrow Green creamery (Kyle, r23; corroborated by buttermaker Hobart Sill, r14, who says he personally lent his own glassware to open the station before its own outfit arrived). Ansel Keddie was hired as tester and separator man from the opening, serving 29 seasons (Duncan, r18; Orra's diary, r24; Falke, r22 — three independent, mutually agreeing sources).

The station's own testing outfit was ordered from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet: an invoice dated 14 April 1897, totaling $77.93, for six dozen pipettes (marked 17.6 c.c.) at $0.35, twelve dozen test bottles, one acid measure, one hand tester, and freight (Ammon's own company history, r20 — a reproduced primary document, the strongest possible source for this fact). It went into use 1 May 1897 (r20), corroborated independently by Orra Keddie's diary entry of the same date (r24) — two contemporaneous, unrelated sources agreeing exactly, against later secondary accounts (r11, r23) that place it in 1898.

Documents: r18, r20, r22, r23, r24, r14; background r02/r07/r09.

## Story 2 — The chronic shortfall and the three folk theories (1902–1926)

From close to the station's opening, its milk tested lower than the rest of the valley: a flat deficiency of 0.19 points, present in every season alike, not just winter (Falke, r22; Hazel Brant, r17). The first complaint of record came in 1902 (Falke, r22, corroborated independently by Orra Keddie's diary entry of March 1902, r24 — an eyewitness account written at the time).

Three explanations competed for a generation, all argued out at the 1925 hearing (Falke, r22; Frick, r13):

1. **Patrons watering their milk** — the manager's theory (Selby Vose's 1919 circular, defended again in his 1926 address). Refuted by the state inspector's lactometer, which found no added water at any circuit visit from 1908 on (Falke, r22).
2. **Tester Ansel Keddie stealing cream** — urged by an Association officer at the 1925 hearing. Refuted by the 1911 broken-glass interval (Story 3) and by independent testimony that the Hollow's cream consistently "ran heavy" at Larrow Green (Sill, r14; Kyle's ledger, r23).
3. **Wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinning the cream** — Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee report (r15), reaffirmed in his 1919 covering letter and again by veterinarian Roland Kepp's 1926 paper (r16), both citing Bulletin No. 214 as vindication and both misquoting its finding as "two tenths." Refuted when Falke obtained the bulletin's actual text: a maximum 0.05-point depression in winter only, nothing measurable in summer — far too small, and wrong in seasonal pattern, to explain a flat, year-round 0.19-point gap (r22's appended note, quoting the bulletin verbatim).

Documents: r15, r16, r22 (incl. appended note), r19, r09/r10 background.

## Story 3 — The 1911 broken-glass interval (the natural experiment)

On 8 May 1911 the station's own measuring glass broke; a borrowed measure was used for nine weeks, until 10 July 1911, when the station's own glass (presumably repaired or replaced) was restored (Falke, r22 — precise dates; duration corroborated independently by r03/r04/r07/r09/r11's "nine weeks"). During those nine weeks the station's tests rose markedly; the day the station resumed its own glass, they fell back to the former level (Falke, r22). Same tester, same herds, same general season — only the glass changed.

Ansel Keddie noted the fact in the station book only as a bare question mark, unwilling to commit an unprovable accusation to the official record, but told his family at home, twice, that "it was something in the glass" (Duncan Keddie, r18, an eyewitness account from Ansel's son, then seventeen). Orra Keddie's diary independently corroborates the date: "May 8, 1911. A. home cross about a broken measure" (r24).

Dr. Kepp's 1926 paper (r16) misremembers the interval as lasting only six weeks and dismisses it as an unrepresentative flush-season anomaly — an error refuted by the exact, sourced dates in Falke's county report and in Orra's contemporaneous diary.

The interval matters because it isolates the glassware as the sole variable while holding tester, herds, and season constant — something no bulletin or ledger calculation, however careful, could establish after the fact; a real-time reversal is direct evidence in a way statistics are not.

Documents: r22, r18, r24, r16 (contradicted), r15 (ignores it); background r03/r04/r07/r09/r11.

## Story 4 — The 1925 petition, hearing, and Keddie's dismissal

The Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned in January 1925 (Falke, r22; corroborated by Orra's diary, r24: "My brother Emmet's name is on it"). A board of arbitration, chaired by attorney Alonzo Frick, sat at Ashlin from 17 March to 4 May 1925 (Falke, r22), with sessions held partly at Ashlin and partly at the station's own weigh room (Frick, r13; Cleve Oram, r21; Kyle, r23; Orra's diary, r24 — four sources agreeing on the split venue).

Clerk Bertram Kyle compiled and personally carried four exhibits before the board: the station's own test book (in Keddie's hand), the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book (lent for the purpose), and the state inspector's notebook for the year the glass was condemned (r23). The board found "no cause established" for any of the three competing theories, yet awarded the patrons money "without admission" (Falke, r22; Kyle, r23). Keddie was dismissed 30 April 1925, four days before the finding was announced — an odd sequence corroborated by four independent sources (Frick r13, Falke r22, Duncan r18, Orra's diary r24).

The award figure is disputed: $3,800 (Falke's official report, r22, and Kyle the clerk, r23 — the two most documentary sources, agreeing independently) versus $3,500 (Oram, r21, secondhand from his father) versus $19,600 (Rundle's pamphlet, r12, and the Chronicle, r19 — almost certainly a conflation with the later shortfall valuation rather than the 1925 award itself, and likely sharing a common source). Resolution: $3,800.

Dismissed after 29 seasons, Keddie took up carpentry and modest farming, dying in 1934 with the imputation still on him (Orra's diary, r24; Duncan, r18; Oram, r21).

Documents: r22, r23, r13, r21, r18, r24, r12, r19.


# Answers — Part 2: Section A Stories 5–8, and Section B

## Story 5 — The manager's 1919 water circular and its cost to the Brant-Keddie family

In 1919, manager Selby Vose circulated a written warning to Ostrey Hollow patrons about adding water to their milk (typed by clerk Kyle, r23; recorded by Orra Keddie, r24). Ansel Keddie insisted on delivering the letters himself rather than mailing them or leaving them on the intake platform — he walked them to every family and stood at the door while each man read it (Orra's diary, r24, a firsthand account by his wife).

The number of families reached is disputed: nine (Hazel Brant, r17, secondhand family/valley memory recorded 1981) versus eleven (Orra's diary, r24, a contemporaneous firsthand entry written by the woman whose husband delivered them, the same year). Resolution: eleven, on the strength of the contemporaneous diary over a later secondhand recollection.

The circular caused a lasting rift between Ansel and his brother-in-law Emmet Brant, the station's largest patron: Emmet confronted Ansel in the yard, asking why he had carried the paper himself if he did not believe its accusation; Ansel replied he would not have let another man carry it. The two did not speak again for years (Orra's diary, r24); Roy Brant's account (r09) corroborates the rift's lasting effect on the extended family, and Emmet's name later appears on the 1925 patrons' petition (Orra's diary, r24).

Manager Vose defended the water theory as late as his 1926 address despite the state inspector's lactometer never once finding added water at the station from 1908 forward (Falke, r22).

Documents: r24, r23, r17, r09, r08 (background), r22.

## Story 6 — The 1958 discovery: Ivy Keddie, the loft crate, and Bulletin No. 471

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie — a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, doing a piece of work on the station — climbed a ladder into the loft over the Ostrey Hollow station's intake room and found the station's original, condemned glassware, crated since 1923 (Chronicle, r19; Falke, r22; Hazel Brant, r17; Duncan Keddie, r18 — four independent sources agreeing on the discovery itself).

Of the six dozen pipettes originally bought, nine survived intact enough to measure (Falke, r22; Cleve Oram, r21, an independent oral source unconnected to the official investigation — against the Chronicle's r19 "eleven," likely a press error). She measured them against a calibrated burette at Farland on 9 March 1958 (Falke, r22, the most precise date given). Every pipette was blown to a nominal 17.6 c.c. mark but delivered an average of only 16.27 c.c. (Falke, r22, and the Chronicle, r19 — though these likely both derive from the same college press release, so this counts as one source, not two).

Published jointly with Professor Leland Vaught as Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958), the conclusion was that the 26-year shortfall came from proportional under-delivery by short-blown pipettes — not water, not theft, not hay.

Ivy's relationship to Ansel was misreported in the press as "great-niece" (Chronicle, r19; also Falke's own initial report, r22) but corrected by Falke's own appended note, after verification, to granddaughter (r22) — matching her father Duncan's explicit family notes (r18).

Documents: r19, r22, r17, r18, r11 (the bulletin itself).

## Story 7 — The independent evidence that the milk itself was never short

Several separate record-keepers, working independently of the 1958 investigation, had already shown the cream was never actually deficient:

- Roy Brant's account of his father Emmet's private weigh book (r09): station and farm scales always agreed — the problem was never weight.
- Rosalie Cudd's Alder Corners Cheese Company records (r10): the four Hessel Bottom farmers who hauled there in summer tested consistently around 3.78–3.82, no different from anywhere else.
- Hobart Sill's buttermaker recollections (r14, 1934): the Hollow's cream always "ran heavy" at Larrow Green — more fat arrived than was ever credited to patrons — even though Sill himself still believed the patrons were watering their milk.
- Bertram Kyle, the Association's own clerk (r23): the annual ledger openly carried a "station gain" line, routinely noted "five and a quarter per cent, as usual," and carried to the general account for decades, unremarked.
- Dell Rundle's pamphlet (r12) originally repeated the folk "cream theft" belief but appended a retraction after examining the actual ledgers and seeing the same "gain" pattern herself.

None connected their own evidence to the pipette explanation at the time; only in combination, after 1958, do they corroborate Bulletin 471.

Documents: r09, r10, r14, r23, r12.

## Story 8 — The family relationships and the record of who kept which book

Ansel Keddie married Orra Brant in 1889; their son Duncan (b. 1894), a retired schoolteacher, wrote the definitive family genealogy in 1968 (r18), stating: Ivy (b. 1931) is Ansel's granddaughter, not great-niece as variously reported; Selby Vose was Gideon Vose's son, not nephew; and there was no blood connection whatsoever between the Keddie and Vose families, despite persistent valley gossip.

Orra was Emmet Brant's sister; Emmet's son Roy and Duncan are therefore first cousins, and Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are second cousins (children of first cousins) — a relationship Hazel herself confirms exactly in her own words (r17), while outsider Cleve Oram (r21) garbles it as "first cousins once removed."

Duncan's notes (r18) also track where the five key documents ended up: the station test book (impounded 1925, Cooperative office until 1959, then Historical Rooms); the Brant weigh book (lent to Ivy 1958, to the Historical Rooms 1963); Strawn's circuit notebooks (state dairy office 1936, Farland college library 1947, where Ivy found the 1923 volume); the Alder Corners book (copied for Ivy 1958, the book itself to Vessey County Historical Society in 1970); and the condemned glassware crate (loft 1923–1958, Farland for measurement, Historical Rooms 1959).

Kyle (r23) independently corroborates Selby Vose as Gideon's son, confirming Duncan's correction against the "nephew" rumor.

Documents: r18, r21, r17, r23, r24 (birth-year discrepancy on Orra: 1866 vs. Duncan's 1868, unresolved).

# Section B — Relationships

**B1.** Ivy Keddie is Ansel Keddie's **granddaughter**. Duncan Keddie — Ansel's son and Ivy's father — states this explicitly and pointedly in his 1968 family notes (r18), noting he has "already twice seen it printed otherwise." The Ashlin Chronicle (r19) and Falke's original county report (r22) both called her "great-niece," but Falke's own appended note retracts this after verification: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine" (r22). Since the Chronicle and Falke's initial error both likely trace to the same college press release, this is really one erroneous source, not two, against Duncan's direct family testimony and Falke's own corrected, verified statement. Resolution: granddaughter.

**B2.** Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant are **second cousins** — children of first cousins. Duncan's genealogy (r18) lays out the chain precisely: his mother Orra and Emmet Brant were brother and sister; Emmet's son Roy and Duncan are first cousins; Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are therefore second cousins. Hazel confirms this exactly in her own words (r17): "Ivy Keddie and I are second cousins... it is not complicated; it is only that nobody wants to count." Cleve Oram (r21) instead says "first cousins once removed," but Oram is relying on secondhand valley hearsay, while Duncan and Hazel are the family members themselves, and they agree with each other exactly. What the sources leave open: Orra's own birth year is given as 1868 by her son Duncan (r18) but as 1866 in the editorial preface to her own diary (r24) — a two-year discrepancy that does not affect the cousin relationship but is not resolved by anything in the sources.

**B3.** Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**, who organized the Association, was its first president, and wrote its by-laws (Duncan, r18; independently confirmed by clerk Kyle, r23, who explicitly calls the rival "nephew" story "nonsense"). This matters because Selby, as Gideon's son, inherited both the founding family's institutional authority and the manager's post (from 1911, per Kyle r23); it was this Selby Vose who circulated the 1919 water-blame circular and who, as manager, signed the annual ledger sheets carrying the "station gain" line every year without apparently reading the figures above the bottom total (Kyle, r23) — meaning the man whose family had founded the Association, and who publicly blamed the patrons, was also the man who annually signed off on the very paper trail that, read closely, undercut his own accusation.

**B4.** There was **no relationship** between the Vose and Keddie families. Duncan Keddie's notes (r18, Section III) explicitly address and reject a persistent valley rumor of a cousin connection via Orra and Gideon's wife, attributing it to confusion with the unrelated "Ashlin Voses," and stating his mother — who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection" — never once included a Vose in her account of the family. Against this, Cleve Oram (r21) says "the Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort... my mother had it from her mother" — precisely the kind of secondhand gossip Duncan's notes were written to correct. Resolution: no relationship, on the strength of Duncan's direct first-person family authority against Oram's admittedly vague, thirdhand hearsay ("cousins of some sort," "I could not draw you the line on paper").


# Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math

**C1.**

*(a) Proportion.* Pipettes were blown to a mark of 17.6 c.c. but delivered an average of 16.27 c.c. (Falke, r22; Chronicle, r19 — treated as one source, both apparently drawing on the bulletin/press release). Shortfall = 17.6 − 16.27 = 1.33 c.c. Proportion short = 1.33 / 17.6 ≈ **7.6%**. Since the fat percentage is read off a scale calibrated for a full 17.6 c.c. charge, a 16.27 c.c. charge produces a reading low by roughly this same proportion.

*(b) True average and point-shortfall.* The recorded station average was 3.61 (Falke, r22 — the consensus figure across most sources). The deficiency is independently and repeatedly stated as a flat 0.19 points, in every season (Falke, r22; Hazel Brant, r17). True average = 3.61 + 0.19 = **3.80**, short by **0.19 points**. This matches closely with Larrow Green's own average of 3.78 (r22) and Alder Corners' independent summer figures of ~3.78–3.82 (r10) — strong triangulation. *Note:* applying the proportional method from (a) instead (3.61 / 0.9244) gives ≈3.90, which does not reconcile as neatly; I flag this as an internal inconsistency in the underlying sources rather than silently resolving it, and use the flat 0.19-point figure below because it is directly, repeatedly measured rather than derived.

*(c) Total pounds not credited.* Disputed: 76,000 lb (r11's own conclusion) vs. 118,000 lb (r11's own body text — a self-contradiction within the same document) vs. 67,000 lb (Frick, r13, and Oram, r21 — not independent of each other, both reflecting the patrons' 1925-era estimate). Using the sources' own separately-confirmed figures: total intake over the affected years was 40,000,000 lb (Kyle, r23, the clerk who compiled this exact figure for the arbitration — preferred over the competing 44,000,000 lb of r05/r12, which are not independent of each other). 40,000,000 × 0.19% (the point-shortfall) = 40,000,000 × 0.0019 = **76,000 lb**. This reconciles exactly with r11's conclusion figure, not its body-text figure, and is the number I use going forward. Working shown in C3 below.

*(d) Money value and fraction received.* At the butterfat price Kyle gives, $0.25/lb (r23), 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000**, which reconciles closely with Rundle's and the Chronicle's $19,600 (r12, r19 — likely the same figure rounded/adjusted differently, not independent). The patrons actually received the arbitration award of $3,800 (see Story 4). Fraction received = 3,800 / 19,000 = **0.20, i.e., one-fifth (20%)**.

**C2.** For Emmet Brant (the largest patron), year 1910: 200,000 lb delivered, credited test 3.42 (Hazel Brant's diary-page recollection, r17 — a precise, directly quoted figure).

(a) Butterfat credited = 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb**.
(b) True test = 3.42 + 0.19 = 3.61%. True butterfat = 200,000 × 0.0361 = 7,220 lb. Not credited = 7,220 − 6,840 = **380 lb**.
(c) At $0.25/lb: 380 × $0.25 = **$95.00** for that single year.

*(Alternate check using the proportional method of C1a: true test = 3.42 / 0.9244 ≈ 3.70%; difference ≈ 0.28 points ≈ 560 lb ≈ $140. I lead with the flat-point method because it is the sources' own directly measured figure, not a derived one, but note the two methods diverge by roughly 200 lb / $45 — another instance of the underlying sources not perfectly reconciling.)*

**C3.** Over the full affected years, using 40,000,000 lb total intake (Kyle, r23):

(a) Credited to patrons = 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**.
(b) Actually delivered in cream to Larrow Green = 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lb**.
(c) Difference = 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 lb. As a **percentage of what patrons were actually credited/paid on** (1,444,000 lb) = 76,000 / 1,444,000 ≈ **5.26%** — which matches, almost exactly, clerk Kyle's own independently-recorded ledger notation of "station gain… five and a quarter per cent, as usual" (r23). I take the percentage of the *credited* total, not the true total (which would give 5.0%), because that is what "station gain" meant on the Association's own books — the excess expressed as a fraction of what patrons were paid on, which is the quantity the ledger itself tracked.

**C4.**

(a) Invoice total = **$77.93** (Ammon & Sons' own reproduced invoice, r20 — the primary document; the Chronicle's r19 "$77.39" is a digit-transposition error against it).
(b) The invoice bought **six dozen (72) pipettes** (r20, corroborated independently by the Chronicle r19 and the original invoice quoted in earlier retellings — against r11's and Oram's "five dozen," which I treat as one shared error, since r21 gives no independent basis beyond a figure matching r11's). Each was marked 17.6 c.c. and delivered on average 16.27 c.c., short by **1.33 c.c. per pipette**.

**C5.**

(a) The faulty glass was in use from 1 May 1897 (r20, r24) to 12 June 1923 (Falke, r22), a span of just over **26 years**. The money calculation, however, runs over **25 full delivery years**: 40,000,000 lb total intake ÷ 1,600,000 lb/year (the steady annual rate Kyle states explicitly, r23) = 25 years. These numbers differ because the glass was installed partway through calendar 1897 and pulled partway through calendar 1923, leaving two partial "fringe" years at either end that are excluded from a calculation requiring whole delivery years.

(b) The station's first season's intake is not from any account book — Kyle explicitly flags it as recollection only, "not in any book I ever ruled" — but he gives **approximately 1,100,000 lb** for that first year (r23). I report this with the same caveat Kyle gives: it is oral testimony, not a documented figure, and should be weighted accordingly.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran **8 May to 10 July 1911, nine weeks** (Falke, r22, exact dates; duration corroborated by five other retellings). The variable was the measuring glass — the station's own broken, a borrowed substitute used in its place. Held constant: same tester (Ansel Keddie), same herds, same general season, same procedure. Result: tests rose markedly for the nine weeks and fell back the day the station's own glass was restored (Falke, r22). This establishes that the deficiency tracked the specific glassware, not the tester's conduct, the herds, or seasonal feed — something no bulletin-based statistical inference could show as directly, since it isolates the causal variable in real time rather than inferring it after the fact from aggregate figures. What it does **not** establish: the specific mechanism (that the glass was short-blown, as opposed to some other defect); the size of the true, steady-state deficiency (that required the 1958 burette measurement); or that wet hay played no role whatsoever — Falke's report initially credited wet hay "a real if partial factor" before withdrawing even that concession once he obtained Bulletin 214's actual text (r22).

**D2.** Three independent reasons the tester was not stealing cream:
1. The 1911 broken-glass interval (Falke, r22; Duncan, r18; Orra's diary, r24) — same tester, different glass, correct results; theft would not have been fixed by changing the glass.
2. Buttermaker Hobart Sill's independent testimony (r14) that the Hollow's cream consistently "ran heavy" at Larrow Green — more fat arrived than was ever credited to patrons, the opposite of what theft would produce.
3. The Association's own ledger, kept by bookkeeper Teague and reviewed by clerk Kyle (r23), which openly recorded a "station gain" every year — the missing fat was retained within the Association's own books, not vanishing to an outside thief.

These are independent because they come from three unrelated people and record-keeping systems — a family's firsthand recollection of the 1911 event, a buttermaker's personal memory from the far end of the supply chain, and the Association's own internal bookkeeping — none derived from or dependent on the others, and none dependent on the 1958 bulletin. Reasons 2 and 3 would still hold even if the glassware had never been measured in 1958, since neither required knowing *why* the fat wasn't disappearing, only that it wasn't.

Did the bookkeeper (apparently Teague, who urged the theft theory at the 1925 hearing) ever question the ledger entries that refute his own accusation? **No.** Per Kyle (r23), Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years" and let "as usual" stand as sufficient explanation for the station-gain line, year after year, even while apparently being the same officer who argued cream theft at the hearing.

**D3.** Manager Selby Vose knew the station's tests ran low in the sense that he publicly addressed it — circulating the 1919 water-blame circular and defending that theory again in 1926. But the sources indicate he did **not** know that the ledger evidence he personally signed off on every year contradicted his own explanation: per clerk Kyle's direct, firsthand testimony (r23), Vose "read the totals and he never read the lines above them... He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign," a thousand times over. So: he was aware of and publicly blamed patrons for the deficiency, but the only source that speaks to whether he read the "station gain" line itself — his own clerk, who watched him sign — says he did not, and that testimony stands uncontested by any other source.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. Single causal theory.** The station's own testing pipettes — bought new from Ammon & Sons and put into service 1 May 1897 — were blown to a nominal mark of 17.6 c.c. but, due to a manufacturing defect (the mold likely reground slightly undersized, per Ammon's own historian, r20), consistently delivered an average of only 16.27 c.c., a ~7.6% shortfall in volume. Because the fat percentage is read off a scale calibrated for a full 17.6 c.c. charge, every test made with that glass read proportionally low — regardless of the milk's true fat content, the herd, the season, or the tester's honesty. This explains:
- **the size of the error:** a fixed, mechanical proportional shortfall, translating to the repeatedly measured ~0.19-point gap;
- **why that specific size:** it was a direct function of one fixed manufacturing defect in one lot of glass, applied test after test;
- **why it began when it did:** the deficiency began once the station's own glass replaced the borrowed Larrow Green glass, 1 May 1897;
- **why it ended when it did:** it ended the moment the inspector condemned the lot, 12 June 1923, and new glass went into service — after which the average rose immediately and permanently to 3.85 (r22);
- **why nothing at the station or on the farms had to change:** the cause was entirely in the glassware; nothing in herd management, feeding, or honesty ever needed to change, and indeed nothing did — the readings changed exactly when, and only when, the glass changed (1911 and 1923 alike).

**The three competing theories, advocates, and defeating evidence:**
1. **Watered milk** — Selby Vose (1919 circular, 1926 address). Defeated by the lactometer readings at every inspection visit from 1908 on, never once finding added water (r22).
2. **Cream theft by the tester** — an Association officer (apparently bookkeeper Teague) at the 1925 hearing. Defeated by the 1911 interval (r22) and independently by Sill's "ran heavy" testimony (r14) and Kyle's ledger record of "station gain" (r23).
3. **Wet hay** — Grigg's 1908 committee (r15), reaffirmed by Kepp (r16), both citing Bulletin 214 but misquoting its finding. Defeated by the bulletin's actual verbatim text: a maximum 0.05-point winter effect and none measurable in summer — too small, and wrong in seasonal pattern, to explain a flat year-round 0.19-point gap (r22).

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?** No evidence that it did. Clerk Kyle lists exactly four exhibits placed before the board — none the physical glassware itself, which was already crated in the loft — and states plainly he could not say "that any man in that room laid [the inspector's notebook] beside [the test book] and looked" (r23). Oram independently corroborates the crate sat unexamined, twenty feet overhead, throughout the station sessions (r21).

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?** The sources conflict and this is not fully resolved. Duncan's firsthand account (r18) has Ansel saying at home in 1911, "it was something in the glass." Frick (r13) reports Keddie privately telling him he believed the patrons were at fault. I weight Duncan's account somewhat more heavily, since it is a direct family member's contemporaneous memory and is later vindicated by the 1958 evidence — but Frick's account has no obvious motive to be invented, and the two need not be fully incompatible over a fourteen-year span of unresolved suspicion. I do not consider this fully settled.

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

From 1897 to 1923, the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association's Ostrey Hollow station paid patrons on milk tests running a flat 0.19 points low, year-round. For a generation, three theories competed — watered milk, cream theft by tester Ansel Keddie, and thin wet-meadow hay — each with partisans, none with proof. A 1925 arbitration found no cause established, paid a token award, and dismissed Keddie anyway. The true cause, sitting forgotten in a crate in the station's own loft since a 1923 inspection, was a batch of pipettes manufactured slightly undersized, making every test read proportionally low no matter what the milk actually contained. Keddie's own granddaughter, Ivy, found and measured them in 1958, vindicating him 24 years after his death. It took eight separate, partial, sometimes contradictory records — ledgers, diaries, a circular, memoirs, a bulletin — because no single witness ever held both the cause and the proof at once.
