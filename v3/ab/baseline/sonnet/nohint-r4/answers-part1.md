## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1 — The Founding of the Hessel Bottom Books

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, organized by Gideon Vose, built a
skimming station at Ostrey Hollow in the winter of 1895–96, opening 4 May 1896, to serve
the six farms of Hessel Bottom (r22). For its first season the station borrowed testing
glass from the main creamery at Larrow Green (r23). Ansel Keddie was tester from the day
the station opened and kept the station's own test book in his hand from 1896 (r18). On
14 April 1897 the Association bought a full testing outfit from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet —
six dozen (72) milk-test pipettes gauged to deliver 17.6 c.c., twelve dozen test bottles,
an acid measure, and a four-bottle hand tester — for a total of $77.93, verified by summing
the invoice's own line items (r20). The outfit went into use on 1 May 1897, confirmed
independently by the invoice's own correspondence and by the diary of Ansel's wife Orra,
who wrote on that date that "the station's own glass came up" (r24). Bertram Kyle's later
recollection that the station's own glass began in 1898 (r23) is contradicted by these two
more direct, contemporaneous sources and is treated as a clerk's memory error decades on.
Separately, Emmet Brant, the largest of the six Hessel Bottom patrons, began his own
private weigh book on 1 January 1897 (r18), recording his own milk deliveries beside the
station's figures — not, as his granddaughter Hazel later recalled, in 1900 (r17); Hazel
herself opens her account by disclaiming her own reliability on years, so Duncan's more
careful, documentary family notes are preferred. Both books — the station's test book and
the Brant weigh book — would sit largely unconsulted for decades and later prove decisive
in clearing Ansel Keddie's name. **Quantities:** 6 farms; 72 pipettes at 17.6 c.c. each;
invoice total $77.93; outfit in service from 1 May 1897; weigh book begun 1 Jan 1897.
**Documents:** r20 (invoice, primary), r24 (diary, contemporaneous), r18 (family notes,
book custody), r17 (weigh book description, with a date error), r22 (station opening date),
r23 (first-season borrowing, with a date error).

### Story 2 — Fifty Years of a Flat Deficiency

From soon after it opened, the Ostrey Hollow station's butterfat tests ran consistently
lower than any other station or creamery in the county — a flat deficiency reported as
"nineteen hundredths" of a test point, present in every month of the year alike (r17, r22).
The first formal complaint was recorded in 1902 and renewed every decade after (r22). Three
explanations circulated in the valley: added water, tester dishonesty, and the wet pasture
of Hessel Bottom's bog meadows. An Association committee investigated the wet-hay theory in
1908, visiting the herds rather than the station, and reported that it was to blame (r24,
Jan–Mar 1908). By chance, an unplanned controlled test occurred: on 8 May 1911 the
station's own measuring glass broke, and a borrowed measure was used for nine weeks, until
10 July 1911 (r22, dated independently by Orra's diary entry of 8 May 1911, r24). During
that interval — same tester, same herds, same season — the tests rose markedly; the day
the station's own glass returned, they fell back to their old level (r22). Ansel Keddie
noted the fact but set only a question mark against it in the book, unwilling to accuse an
instrument he could not prove was at fault (r18). **Quantities:** 0.19-point deficiency;
first complaint 1902; broken-glass interval 8 May–10 July 1911 (nine weeks). **Documents:**
r22 (dates, theories, deficiency figure), r24 (diary corroboration of the break date and the
1908 committee visit), r17 (deficiency figure, valley folk theories), r18 (Ansel's private
suspicion and the question mark), r23 (long-run test/price context).

### Story 3 — The Water Letter

Manager Selby Vose circulated a warning letter in 1919 to the Ostrey Hollow patrons,
warning against added water in their milk (r17, r23, r24). Clerk Bertram Kyle typed it
(r23). Rather than mail it or leave it at the station, Ansel Keddie insisted on delivering
the letters himself, on foot, standing at each door while the recipient read it, and
returning home near midnight (r24, June 1919). Two days later, Emmet Brant — Ansel's own
brother-in-law and the station's largest patron — confronted Ansel in the yard, asking why
he had carried a letter he did not believe; Ansel answered that he had carried it himself
so that no other man would have to. Emmet did not enter the Keddie house again for the rest
of his life; he died in the spring of 1929, roughly a decade after the rift (r24). The
sources disagree on how many families were addressed: Hazel Brant recalls nine letters to
nine families on six farms, none of whom answered a word (r17); Orra's contemporaneous
diary states Ansel personally visited eleven families (r24). I lean toward the diary as the
more direct, contemporaneous witness, but the discrepancy is not fully resolved. The state
dairy inspector's own lactometer readings, taken at every visit from 1908 on, never once
found added water at the station (r22), which independently discredits the letter's
premise. **Quantities:** circular issued 1919; 9 or 11 families (sources conflict);
roughly a 10-year family estrangement (1919–1929). **Documents:** r24 (contemporaneous
diary, the rift), r23 (clerk, who typed the letter), r17 (Hazel's account and number), r22
(inspector's lactometer results disproving the watering theory).

### Story 4 — A Crate from Tarnet

Writing the firm's privately printed 1949 history, Junia Ammon set down the story of the
invoice that made Ammon & Sons briefly notorious. The house's works records burned in a
1931 fire, leaving only office correspondence; from that, Ammon reproduces the original
14 April 1897 invoice (6 dozen pipettes at 17.6 c.c., 35 cents apiece, total $77.93) and the
firm's own 2 July 1923 reply to the Association's complaint, admitting only that "our books
of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot" (r20). Ammon's own
theory, offered decades later with no documentary proof, is that a regauged mold produced a
lot that ran consistently short and passed every spot-check precisely because it was
consistently wrong (r20). Cleve Oram, who as a boy handled the actual crate in the station
loft, recalled it as holding five dozen (60) pipettes rather than the invoiced six dozen
(72) (r21) — a discrepancy I resolve in the invoice's favor, since it is the primary,
quoted business record against a childhood memory recalled some sixty years later.
**Quantities:** 72 pipettes ordered at 35 cents each; invoice total $77.93 (Ammon's own
narrative text and a 1958 newspaper account both separately restate this, in error, as
"$77.39" — see Section E). **Documents:** r20 (the firm's own history, invoice and letter
reproduced verbatim), r19 (newspaper's citation of the same letter and its own repetition of
the erroneous total), r21 (Cleve Oram's conflicting pipette count).
