# Answer Sheet — Part 3 of 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

**C1.**

*(a) Proportion.* The sources give three figures that do not fully reconcile:
- From the glassware itself (r19): pipettes marked 17.6 c.c., measured average delivery
  16.27 c.c. Shortfall = (17.6 − 16.27) / 17.6 = 1.33 / 17.6 ≈ **7.56%**.
- From the ledger's own recorded "station gain" line, entered every year by the bookkeeper
  (r23): credited butterfat ran **5.25%** below what was actually received at the main
  creamery — an actual recorded accounting figure, not a derived one.
- From the reported test-point deficiency (r17, r22): "nineteen hundredths" (0.19) of a
  point against a base average of roughly 3.78–3.85 ≈ **4.9–5.0%**.

  The latter two cluster near 5% and come from two independent kinds of evidence
  (bookkeeping entries and reported test averages, from different narrators). The 7.56%
  figure is an outlier, resting on a single newspaper's citation of the bulletin's raw
  numbers and unconfirmed anywhere else. I adopt **~5%** (using the ledger's 5.25% as the
  most concrete, actually-recorded figure) as best-supported, and flag the mismatch with
  7.56% as a discrepancy the sources do not let me settle (see E16).

*(b) True average and points short.* r22 states the post-1923 (1924-on) average was 3.85,
which I take as the closest approximation to the "true" average, since replacing the glass
should reveal real fat content and nothing indicates the herds or pasture changed at that
time. The same document gives the affected-years recorded average as 3.61 — a difference of
0.24 points, which does not match the "nineteen hundredths" (0.19) the same paragraph
attributes directly to the bulletin two sentences earlier. This is an internal
inconsistency in r22 itself (see E15). Because "nineteen hundredths" is independently
repeated by Hazel Brant (r17) as well as cited by Falke, I treat **0.19 points** as the
best-supported single deficiency figure, while flagging that Falke's own averages (3.61,
3.78, 3.85) do not cleanly support it.

*(c) Pounds not credited.* Using the total credited butterfat from C3(a) — 1,444,000 lbs —
and the 5.25% station-gain rate: 1,444,000 × 0.0525 ≈ **75,810 lbs** not credited over the
full affected years (this is consistent in order of magnitude with r21's rounder,
independently-stated "sixty-seven thousand pounds," though the two do not match exactly;
r21 gives no derivation, so I cannot reconcile them further).

*(d) Money value and fraction received.* r19 gives the bulletin's own later calculation of
what patrons were never paid: **$19,600**. Against that, the patrons actually received only
the 1925 arbitration award of **$3,800**, paid "without admission" and calculated on a
different basis (a negotiated settlement, not a reconstruction of the true shortfall).
Fraction received = 3,800 / 19,600 ≈ **19.4%**.

**C2.** (Emmet Brant, Hessel Bottom, largest patron, 1910 — figures from r17's directly
quoted weigh-book entry.)
- (a) Credited: 200,000 lbs milk × 3.42% test = **6,840 lbs** butterfat.
- (b) Owed and not credited: true test = 3.42 + 0.19 = 3.61%; true butterfat = 200,000 ×
  0.0361 = 7,220 lbs. Difference = **380 lbs** not credited.
- (c) Money value: r23 gives a long-run average butterfat price of 25 cents/lb, explicitly
  a multi-decade average and not verified for 1910 specifically. 380 × $0.25 = **$95.00**
  (approximate, with that caveat).

**C3.** (Full affected years; milk-volume figure from r23.)
- (a) Total credited: 40,000,000 lbs milk × 3.61% (recorded average, r22) = **1,444,000
  lbs** butterfat credited.
- (b) Total actually received at the creamery: credited × 1.0525 (station-gain rate, r23)
  ≈ **1,520,110 lbs**.
- (c) Difference as a percentage: **5.25%**, and — per r23's own wording — that percentage
  is explicitly of the amount *credited to the patrons* (the lower figure), not of the
  amount received at the creamery (the higher figure). Taking it as a percentage of the
  received figure instead would understate it.

**C4.**
- (a) Invoice total: **$77.93**, verified by summing the invoice's own line items in r20
  (25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93). Both r19's and r20's own narrative prose
  separately state "$77.39" instead — an internal arithmetic error, addressed in Section E.
- (b) **72 pipettes** (six dozen, per the invoice), each short by 17.6 − 16.27 = **1.33
  c.c.** (using r19's measured average).

**C5.**
- (a) The faulty glass was in service from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 — about **26 years, 1
  month**. The money/quantity calculations (the $19,600 figure, r21's 67,000-lb figure, and
  Kyle's 40,000,000-lb/1,600,000-per-year figure in r23) imply **25 full delivery years**.
  These differ because 26 years is the calendar span the glass was in use, while 25 years is
  the count of complete annual delivery cycles inside that span — the glass arrived partway
  through 1897 and was condemned partway through 1923, so those two years are only partial.
- (b) First season's milk: approximately **1,100,000 lbs** (r23) — a figure the clerk
  himself explicitly flags as an unverified recollection ("not in any book I ever ruled"),
  not a ledger figure, and should be treated with that caveat.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The station's own measuring glass broke on 8 May 1911 (r24, r22); a borrowed
measure was used for nine weeks, until 10 July 1911, when the station's own glass resumed
(r22). The variable was the testing instrument (borrowed vs. the station's own); held
constant were the tester, the herds, the pasture, and the season. Result: tests rose
markedly during the borrowed-glass interval and fell back the day the station's own glass
returned (r22). This establishes something a static calculation cannot: **causation**,
specifically that the deficiency traveled with the instrument and not with the tester,
the herds, or the season — ruling out, in that window at least, both the "wet hay" and the
"tester dishonesty" theories at a stroke. What it does **not** establish: the exact
mechanism or magnitude of the glass's fault (that required the 1958 measurement), whether
the *borrowed* glass was itself fully accurate, or anything about years outside that
nine-week window.

**D2.** Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream, each drawn
from a different narrator and each independent of the others in kind:
1. The 1911 broken-glass interval (r22, corroborated by r24's diary): same tester, same
   procedure, different instrument, different result — the deficiency tracked the glass,
   not the man.
2. The flatness of the deficiency itself (r17): "nineteen hundredths... every month, every
   season, wet years and dry... not a little in the winter and none in the summer" — a
   constant, unvarying shortfall is the signature of a mechanical fault, not of deliberate
   human skimming, which would be expected to vary with opportunity or risk.
3. Character/behavioral evidence independent of any measurement (r24, r18): Ansel's refusal
   to record a figure he could not prove (the 1911 question mark), his insistence on
   personally delivering the 1919 water-accusation letters he did not believe, and his
   decades of punctual, exacting habit.
Reasons 2 and 3 would still hold even if the glassware had never been measured in 1958;
reason 1 also predates 1958 (it occurred in 1911) and only its *explanation* was supplied
later. As for whether the Association's bookkeeper ever questioned the ledger entries that
refute the accusation: **no**. Bertram Kyle (r23) states plainly that the annual "station
gain... as usual" line "never once crossed my mind to wonder at it," and that of the four
documents entered at the 1925 hearing, he could not say that "any man in that room laid the
second beside the fourth and looked."

**D3.** The sources indicate the manager, Selby Vose, most likely did **not** know, before
1923, that the station's tests ran low — despite having documentary access to information
that would have revealed it. His own clerk describes his invariable habit of reading only
the bottom total of each ledger sheet and never the lines above it, including the "station
gain" line that recorded the very discrepancy at issue (r23). This is corroborated
indirectly by his having signed and issued the 1919 circular accusing patrons of watering
their milk — a genuine, if mistaken, belief in a different cause, which is hard to square
with a manager who was privately aware of an instrumental flaw and shifting blame elsewhere.

## Section F — Theory

**Causal theory.** The Ostrey Hollow station's testing pipettes, part of the original
outfit bought from Ammon & Sons in 1897, were manufactured to deliver less than their
marked 17.6 c.c. charge of milk — plausibly, per Ammon's own later speculation, because a
regauged mold produced a lot that ran consistently short and so passed every spot-check.
Because every butterfat test performed with that glass used a short charge of milk against
a scale ruled for a full one, every test read low by the same fixed proportion, regardless
of season, herd, or pasture — explaining both the *size* of the error (fixed by the glass's
manufacturing tolerance, not by any variable condition) and its *constancy* year-round
(r17). It began when the flawed outfit went into service (1 May 1897, r20, r24) and ended
when the state inspector condemned and replaced the glass (12 June 1923, r22), after which
the recorded average rose (r22). Nothing at the station or on the farms needed to change
because the defect was purely instrumental — confirmed by the 1911 natural experiment, in
which substituting a different instrument with the same herds, tester, and season changed
the result (r22, D1).

**Three competing explanations, and what defeats each:**
1. *Patrons watering their milk* — advanced by manager Selby Vose via his 1919 circular
   (r17, r23, r24). Defeated by the state inspector's own lactometer readings, taken at
   every visit from 1908 on, which never once found added water (r22).
2. *Tester skimming cream and falsifying low tests to conceal it* — urged by an Association
   officer at the 1925 hearing (r22). Defeated by the 1911 natural experiment: same tester,
   borrowed glass, tests rose (r22, corroborated by r24).
3. *Wet hay/pasture thinning the cream* — advanced by an Association committee (1908) and a
   veterinarian (1926) (r22). Defeated by the college's own 1911 bog-hay feeding trial
   (Bulletin No. 214), which found bog hay depresses butterfat by at most 0.05 points in
   winter and nothing measurable in summer — far short of the observed, all-season 0.19-point
   deficiency (r22's appended note).

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?** No evidence that it did. The crate
had been sitting in the loft since 1923, directly above the very room where two of the
three 1925 sessions were held (r17, r21, r24); Bertram Kyle (r23) lists only four documents
entered as evidence — none of them the glassware itself — and states that no one appears to
have even cross-referenced those four documents against each other.

**What did the tester himself believe?** Per his son Duncan (r18), after the 1911
broken-glass incident Ansel came home saying "it was something in the glass," and wrote a
question mark in the book rather than assert an unproven claim — he suspected the glass
specifically, from as early as 1911, but felt he could not prove it.

## Section G — Summary

For decades, the Ostrey Hollow milk-testing station paid its patrons on butterfat tests
that ran mysteriously, flatly low. The valley blamed watering, a dishonest tester, or wet
pasture; a 1925 arbitration board dismissed tester Ansel Keddie without proving any of it.
The real cause — short-delivering test pipettes, bought new in 1897 and condemned unnoticed
in 1923 — sat crated in a loft until his granddaughter Ivy measured the glass in 1958,
clearing his name after his death. No single account holds the whole story: the family kept
genealogy and dates, the clerk kept ledgers, the manufacturer kept correspondence, a diary
kept the human cost, and the college bulletin supplied the physical proof. Eight overlapping,
imperfect witnesses were needed because each held one thread, and several got dates, sums,
or relationships wrong — which is itself part of what this record shows.
