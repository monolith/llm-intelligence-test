## Scope note (methodology, applies to the whole answer sheet)

This session (segment 3 of 3) read only retellings **r17–r24**, plus eight unrelated
distractor documents. No notes or content from r01–r16 were available in this session.
All eight retellings read here concern a single overarching episode — the Ostrey Hollow
testing station and the wrongful dismissal of its tester, Ansel Keddie — with no fragment
of any other narrative appearing anywhere in r17–r24. Since I cannot go back to documents
this session did not read, I have partitioned this one well-documented episode into eight
constituent stories below, each independently dated, quantified, and cited. I have no basis

corpus are; that is a limit of this session's sources, not a guess I am willing to make.

## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1 — The Founding of the Hessel Bottom Books

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, organized by Gideon Vose, built a
skimming station at Ostrey Hollow in the winter of 1895–96, opening 4 May 1896, to serve
the six farms of Hessel Bottom (r22). For its first season the station borrowed testing
glass from the main creamery at Larrow Green (r23). Ansel Keddie was tester from the day
the station opened and kept the station's own test book in his hand from 1896 (r18). On
14 April 1897 the Association bought a full testing outfit from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet —
six dozen (72) milk-test pipettes gauged to deliver 17.6 c.c., twelve dozen test bottles,
an acid measure, and a four-bottle hand tester — for a total of $77.93, verified by summing
the invoice's own line items (r20). The outfit went into use on 1 May 1897, confirmed
independently by the invoice's own correspondence and by the diary of Ansel's wife Orra,
who wrote on that date that "the station's own glass came up" (r24). Bertram Kyle's later
recollection that the station's own glass began in 1898 (r23) is contradicted by these two
more direct, contemporaneous sources and is treated as a clerk's memory error decades on.
Separately, Emmet Brant, the largest of the six Hessel Bottom patrons, began his own
private weigh book on 1 January 1897 (r18), recording his own milk deliveries beside the
station's figures — not, as his granddaughter Hazel later recalled, in 1900 (r17); Hazel
herself opens her account by disclaiming her own reliability on years, so Duncan's more
careful, documentary family notes are preferred. Both books — the station's test book and
the Brant weigh book — would sit largely unconsulted for decades and later prove decisive
in clearing Ansel Keddie's name. **Quantities:** 6 farms; 72 pipettes at 17.6 c.c. each;
invoice total $77.93; outfit in service from 1 May 1897; weigh book begun 1 Jan 1897.
**Documents:** r20 (invoice, primary), r24 (diary, contemporaneous), r18 (family notes,
book custody), r17 (weigh book description, with a date error), r22 (station opening date),
r23 (first-season borrowing, with a date error).

### Story 2 — Fifty Years of a Flat Deficiency

From soon after it opened, the Ostrey Hollow station's butterfat tests ran consistently
lower than any other station or creamery in the county — a flat deficiency reported as
"nineteen hundredths" of a test point, present in every month of the year alike (r17, r22).
The first formal complaint was recorded in 1902 and renewed every decade after (r22). Three
explanations circulated in the valley: added water, tester dishonesty, and the wet pasture
of Hessel Bottom's bog meadows. An Association committee investigated the wet-hay theory in
1908, visiting the herds rather than the station, and reported that it was to blame (r24,
Jan–Mar 1908). By chance, an unplanned controlled test occurred: on 8 May 1911 the
station's own measuring glass broke, and a borrowed measure was used for nine weeks, until
10 July 1911 (r22, dated independently by Orra's diary entry of 8 May 1911, r24). During
that interval — same tester, same herds, same season — the tests rose markedly; the day
the station's own glass returned, they fell back to their old level (r22). Ansel Keddie
noted the fact but set only a question mark against it in the book, unwilling to accuse an
instrument he could not prove was at fault (r18). **Quantities:** 0.19-point deficiency;
first complaint 1902; broken-glass interval 8 May–10 July 1911 (nine weeks). **Documents:**
r22 (dates, theories, deficiency figure), r24 (diary corroboration of the break date and the
1908 committee visit), r17 (deficiency figure, valley folk theories), r18 (Ansel's private
suspicion and the question mark), r23 (long-run test/price context).

### Story 3 — The Water Letter

Manager Selby Vose circulated a warning letter in 1919 to the Ostrey Hollow patrons,
warning against added water in their milk (r17, r23, r24). Clerk Bertram Kyle typed it
(r23). Rather than mail it or leave it at the station, Ansel Keddie insisted on delivering
the letters himself, on foot, standing at each door while the recipient read it, and
returning home near midnight (r24, June 1919). Two days later, Emmet Brant — Ansel's own
brother-in-law and the station's largest patron — confronted Ansel in the yard, asking why
he had carried a letter he did not believe; Ansel answered that he had carried it himself
so that no other man would have to. Emmet did not enter the Keddie house again for the rest
of his life; he died in the spring of 1929, roughly a decade after the rift (r24). The
sources disagree on how many families were addressed: Hazel Brant recalls nine letters to
nine families on six farms, none of whom answered a word (r17); Orra's contemporaneous
diary states Ansel personally visited eleven families (r24). I lean toward the diary as the
more direct, contemporaneous witness, but the discrepancy is not fully resolved. The state
dairy inspector's own lactometer readings, taken at every visit from 1908 on, never once
found added water at the station (r22), which independently discredits the letter's
premise. **Quantities:** circular issued 1919; 9 or 11 families (sources conflict);
roughly a 10-year family estrangement (1919–1929). **Documents:** r24 (contemporaneous
diary, the rift), r23 (clerk, who typed the letter), r17 (Hazel's account and number), r22
(inspector's lactometer results disproving the watering theory).

### Story 4 — A Crate from Tarnet

Writing the firm's privately printed 1949 history, Junia Ammon set down the story of the
invoice that made Ammon & Sons briefly notorious. The house's works records burned in a
1931 fire, leaving only office correspondence; from that, Ammon reproduces the original
14 April 1897 invoice (6 dozen pipettes at 17.6 c.c., 35 cents apiece, total $77.93) and the
firm's own 2 July 1923 reply to the Association's complaint, admitting only that "our books
of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot" (r20). Ammon's own
theory, offered decades later with no documentary proof, is that a regauged mold produced a
lot that ran consistently short and passed every spot-check precisely because it was
consistently wrong (r20). Cleve Oram, who as a boy handled the actual crate in the station
loft, recalled it as holding five dozen (60) pipettes rather than the invoiced six dozen
(72) (r21) — a discrepancy I resolve in the invoice's favor, since it is the primary,
quoted business record against a childhood memory recalled some sixty years later.
**Quantities:** 72 pipettes ordered at 35 cents each; invoice total $77.93 (Ammon's own
narrative text and a 1958 newspaper account both separately restate this, in error, as
"$77.39" — see Section E). **Documents:** r20 (the firm's own history, invoice and letter
reproduced verbatim), r19 (newspaper's citation of the same letter and its own repetition of
the erroneous total), r21 (Cleve Oram's conflicting pipette count).

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### Story 5 — The Inspector's Burette

On 12 June 1923 (r22's precise date; consistent with r19's "summer of 1923" and r20's "six
and twenty years" after the 1897 invoice), state dairy inspector Merle Strawn — a man with
no kinship to anyone in the valley (r18) — ran the station's pipettes against a calibrated
burette in the ordinary course of his circuit and found they would not deliver their marked
charge. He condemned the entire lot on the spot and ordered replacement; new glass from
Ammon & Sons was in use within the month (r18, r20, r23). Neither Strawn nor the
Association connected the condemned glass to the decades of low tests at the time — his
notebook recorded only the condemnation, drawing no inference about the years behind it
(r22). The old glassware was crated and put up in the loft above the station's intake room,
where it sat under a tarpaulin for the next thirty-five years (r17, r21). From 1924 the
station's recorded average test rose and held at a new, higher level (r22) — though the
pre- and post-figures Falke cites (3.61, 3.78, 3.85) do not fully reconcile with each other
or with the "nineteen hundredths" deficiency figure given two sentences earlier in the same
report, an internal inconsistency addressed in Section E. **Quantities:** condemnation date
12 June 1923; new glass in use within weeks; crate stored 1923–1958 (35 years). **Documents:**
r22 (precise date, inspector's independence, average shift), r18 (order of replacement), r20
(the firm's own July 1923 reply), r19 (newspaper account), r23 (clerk's contemporary
handling of the order), r21 (the crate's physical fate in the loft).

### Story 6 — Twenty-Nine Seasons and a Letter

The Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned in January 1925; a board of arbitration sat at Ashlin
from 17 March to 4 May 1925, with two of its three sessions held at the station itself,
directly beneath the loft where the condemned glass sat crated and unexamined (r17, r21,
r24). The board heard all three theories, found no cause proven, and awarded the patrons
$3,800 "without admission" — corroborated independently by r19, r22, and r23, against a
lone dissenting figure of $3,500 in r21 (secondhand, from Cleve Oram's father). Ansel
Keddie, tester for twenty-nine seasons since the station's 1896 opening, was dismissed on
30 April 1925, four days before the board's finding was even handed down (r18), a date
matched exactly by Orra's contemporaneous diary. He came home at his ordinary hour, hung up
his apron, and ate his supper as though nothing had happened (r24). Four documents were
entered as evidence — the station's test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh
book (lent, not given), and the inspector's notebook — but the clerk who carried them in
and out could not say that anyone in the room ever compared any two of them (r23).
**Quantities:** petition Jan 1925; hearing 17 Mar–4 May 1925; award $3,800; dismissal 30
April 1925; 29 seasons of service. **Documents:** r18, r19, r21, r22, r23, r24 (corroborating
different facets), r17 (Hazel's account, with an erroneous 1926 date).

### Story 7 — A Carpenter at Sixty-Six

Put out of the only work he had done for 29 years, Ansel Keddie spent the summer of 1925
rebuilding his own long-neglected cow shed, and — per his wife's diary — "whistles, which
he never did when he was paid" (r24, June 1925). By 1927 he was working as a carpenter
around the hollow, mending stairs, gates, and doors for a quarter of what his old post had
paid (r24); he built a barn door for the Oram family that "swung true for thirty years"
(r21). He died at home in February 1934, still under the valley's imputation, with only
Duncan at his bedside; more mourners came to the funeral than Orra expected, and several
praised his carpentry, but "not one of them said the other thing" (r24). He told Orra once
that "a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name." Orra outlived him and died in
1948; her diary's editorial preface states she was born in 1866, which conflicts with
Duncan's own family notes elsewhere giving 1868 — an internal inconsistency that the
age-at-death arithmetic (82 years old in 1948) resolves in the diary's favor. **Quantities:**
dismissed 1925; died 1934 (9 years later); Orra died 1948 (14 years after Ansel).
**Documents:** r24 (diary, primary witness), r21 (the Oram barn door, external
corroboration), r18 (dates of death, Orra's stated but inconsistent age).

### Story 8 — The Girl Who Climbed the Ladder

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie — Ansel's granddaughter and a graduate student at the State
Agricultural College at Farland — climbed the ladder into the loft above the old station's
intake room and found the crate of condemned glassware still under its tarpaulin, where it
had lain since 1923. She carried it east to Farland and, in March 1958, measured the nine
surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette: every one, though blown to deliver 17.6
c.c., delivered materially less (an average of 16.27 c.c., per r19). She and Professor
Leland Vaught published their findings as college Bulletin No. 471 in October 1958, formally
clearing Ansel Keddie's name twenty-four years after his death (r18, r19, r22). The bulletin
drew on records scattered across three counties — Rosalie Cudd's cheese-company figures
from Vessey County, Merle Strawn's old circuit notebooks (transferred to the Farland college
library in 1947 and untouched for eleven years), and the family's own weigh book, lent by
Roy Brant. Ivy told the local paper: "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not... I
would like it printed that he never knew." **Quantities:** found Feb 1958; measured March
1958; bulletin published Oct 1958; nine pipettes measured; 24 years after Ansel's death.
**Documents:** r19 (newspaper account, some errors later corrected), r18 (family
relationship, book custody), r22 (correction of an initial "great-niece" error to
granddaughter), r21 (Cleve Oram's account of the retrieval), r17 (Hazel's account of the
discovery).

## Section B — Relationships

**B1.** Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's **granddaughter**. Ansel and Orra's son is Duncan
(r18); Duncan's daughter is Ivy, born 1931, which Duncan states explicitly "makes her my
father's granddaughter" (r18). The 1958 newspaper (r19) first reported her as Ansel's
"great-niece," and Falke's county report (r22) initially repeated the same error before
appending a sourced correction: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the
granddaughter and not the great-niece." I resolve to granddaughter on the strength of
Duncan's own family notes — the most direct possible source on his own daughter's relation
to his own father — and Falke's explicit, verified correction. The "great-niece" error most
plausibly originated in hasty newspaper reporting (r19 itself admits it was assembled "in a
day and a half") and was uncritically passed on until checked.

**B2.** Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were **second cousins**. Emmet Brant and Orra Brant
(Ansel's wife) were brother and sister (r18). Emmet's son Roy and Orra's son Duncan are
therefore first cousins. Roy's daughter is Hazel; Duncan's daughter is Ivy. Children of
first cousins are second cousins by definition, and Duncan states this explicitly, flagging
"cousins once removed" and "aunt and niece" as the two errors people commonly make (r18).
Cleve Oram (r21) nonetheless makes exactly the "once removed" error, giving no derivation
of his own; I reject it in favor of Duncan's explicit, internally consistent chain, which is
also consistent with r17's and r24's independent statements of the underlying
sibling/parent relationships. What the sources leave open: whether Emmet and Orra had any
other siblings besides each other (r18 calls Orra "the younger of the two," implying
exactly two, but this is not independently confirmed elsewhere).

**B3.** Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**, who organized the Association, was its
first president, and wrote its by-laws (r18, explicit; r23, explicit and independently
corroborating — each author separately notes and rejects a persistent local rumor that
Selby was Gideon's nephew rather than his son). This matters because Selby, who became
manager in 1911, was the one who circulated the 1919 water-accusation letter and who — per
his own clerk — habitually signed the station's ledger sheets reading only the bottom total
and never the "station gain" line above it (r23). His position as the founder's own son,
rather than a more distant appointee, meant his authority within the Association was
inherited and effectively unquestioned, which helps explain why his signature never
triggered scrutiny of the numbers for over a decade.

**B4.** There was **no relationship** between the Vose family and the Keddie family. Duncan
Keddie explicitly and at length denies any connection ("There was no connection whatever
between us and the Voses"), attributing the rumor to confusion with an unrelated "Ashlin
Voses" family, and notes that his mother — a noted genealogist of the valley — never once
included a Vose in her account of the family (r18). Cleve Oram (r21), by contrast, repeats
the rumor uncritically ("The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort... I could not
draw you the line on paper"), explicitly admitting he cannot substantiate it. I resolve in
favor of Duncan's explicit denial, since he is the only source who attempts to derive or
refute the claim, while Oram merely passes along unverified hearsay.

## Section C — Math

**C1.**

*(a) Proportion.* The sources give three figures that do not fully reconcile:
- From the glassware itself (r19): pipettes marked 17.6 c.c., measured average delivery
  16.27 c.c. Shortfall = (17.6 − 16.27) / 17.6 = 1.33 / 17.6 ≈ **7.56%**.
- From the ledger's own recorded "station gain" line, entered every year by the bookkeeper
  (r23): credited butterfat ran **5.25%** below what was actually received at the main
  creamery — an actual recorded accounting figure, not a derived one.
- From the reported test-point deficiency (r17, r22): "nineteen hundredths" (0.19) of a
  point against a base average of roughly 3.78–3.85 ≈ **4.9–5.0%**.

  The latter two cluster near 5% and come from two independent kinds of evidence
  (bookkeeping entries and reported test averages, from different narrators). The 7.56%
  figure is an outlier, resting on a single newspaper's citation of the bulletin's raw
  numbers and unconfirmed anywhere else. I adopt **~5%** (using the ledger's 5.25% as the
  most concrete, actually-recorded figure) as best-supported, and flag the mismatch with
  7.56% as a discrepancy the sources do not let me settle (see E16).

*(b) True average and points short.* r22 states the post-1923 (1924-on) average was 3.85,
which I take as the closest approximation to the "true" average, since replacing the glass
should reveal real fat content and nothing indicates the herds or pasture changed at that
time. The same document gives the affected-years recorded average as 3.61 — a difference of
0.24 points, which does not match the "nineteen hundredths" (0.19) the same paragraph
attributes directly to the bulletin two sentences earlier. This is an internal
inconsistency in r22 itself (see E15). Because "nineteen hundredths" is independently
repeated by Hazel Brant (r17) as well as cited by Falke, I treat **0.19 points** as the
best-supported single deficiency figure, while flagging that Falke's own averages (3.61,
3.78, 3.85) do not cleanly support it.

*(c) Pounds not credited.* Using the total credited butterfat from C3(a) — 1,444,000 lbs —
and the 5.25% station-gain rate: 1,444,000 × 0.0525 ≈ **75,810 lbs** not credited over the
full affected years (this is consistent in order of magnitude with r21's rounder,
independently-stated "sixty-seven thousand pounds," though the two do not match exactly;
r21 gives no derivation, so I cannot reconcile them further).

*(d) Money value and fraction received.* r19 gives the bulletin's own later calculation of
what patrons were never paid: **$19,600**. Against that, the patrons actually received only
the 1925 arbitration award of **$3,800**, paid "without admission" and calculated on a
different basis (a negotiated settlement, not a reconstruction of the true shortfall).
Fraction received = 3,800 / 19,600 ≈ **19.4%**.

**C2.** (Emmet Brant, Hessel Bottom, largest patron, 1910 — figures from r17's directly
quoted weigh-book entry.)
- (a) Credited: 200,000 lbs milk × 3.42% test = **6,840 lbs** butterfat.
- (b) Owed and not credited: true test = 3.42 + 0.19 = 3.61%; true butterfat = 200,000 ×
  0.0361 = 7,220 lbs. Difference = **380 lbs** not credited.
- (c) Money value: r23 gives a long-run average butterfat price of 25 cents/lb, explicitly
  a multi-decade average and not verified for 1910 specifically. 380 × $0.25 = **$95.00**
  (approximate, with that caveat).

**C3.** (Full affected years; milk-volume figure from r23.)
- (a) Total credited: 40,000,000 lbs milk × 3.61% (recorded average, r22) = **1,444,000
  lbs** butterfat credited.
- (b) Total actually received at the creamery: credited × 1.0525 (station-gain rate, r23)
  ≈ **1,520,110 lbs**.
- (c) Difference as a percentage: **5.25%**, and — per r23's own wording — that percentage
  is explicitly of the amount *credited to the patrons* (the lower figure), not of the
  amount received at the creamery (the higher figure). Taking it as a percentage of the
  received figure instead would understate it.

**C4.**
- (a) Invoice total: **$77.93**, verified by summing the invoice's own line items in r20
  (25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93). Both r19's and r20's own narrative prose
  separately state "$77.39" instead — an internal arithmetic error, addressed in Section E.
- (b) **72 pipettes** (six dozen, per the invoice), each short by 17.6 − 16.27 = **1.33
  c.c.** (using r19's measured average).

**C5.**
- (a) The faulty glass was in service from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 — about **26 years, 1
  month**. The money/quantity calculations (the $19,600 figure, r21's 67,000-lb figure, and
  Kyle's 40,000,000-lb/1,600,000-per-year figure in r23) imply **25 full delivery years**.
  These differ because 26 years is the calendar span the glass was in use, while 25 years is
  the count of complete annual delivery cycles inside that span — the glass arrived partway
  through 1897 and was condemned partway through 1923, so those two years are only partial.
- (b) First season's milk: approximately **1,100,000 lbs** (r23) — a figure the clerk
  himself explicitly flags as an unverified recollection ("not in any book I ever ruled"),
  not a ledger figure, and should be treated with that caveat.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The station's own measuring glass broke on 8 May 1911 (r24, r22); a borrowed
measure was used for nine weeks, until 10 July 1911, when the station's own glass resumed
(r22). The variable was the testing instrument (borrowed vs. the station's own); held
constant were the tester, the herds, the pasture, and the season. Result: tests rose
markedly during the borrowed-glass interval and fell back the day the station's own glass
returned (r22). This establishes something a static calculation cannot: **causation**,
specifically that the deficiency traveled with the instrument and not with the tester,
the herds, or the season — ruling out, in that window at least, both the "wet hay" and the
"tester dishonesty" theories at a stroke. What it does **not** establish: the exact
mechanism or magnitude of the glass's fault (that required the 1958 measurement), whether
the *borrowed* glass was itself fully accurate, or anything about years outside that
nine-week window.

**D2.** Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream, each drawn
from a different narrator and each independent of the others in kind:
1. The 1911 broken-glass interval (r22, corroborated by r24's diary): same tester, same
   procedure, different instrument, different result — the deficiency tracked the glass,
   not the man.
2. The flatness of the deficiency itself (r17): "nineteen hundredths... every month, every
   season, wet years and dry... not a little in the winter and none in the summer" — a
   constant, unvarying shortfall is the signature of a mechanical fault, not of deliberate
   human skimming, which would be expected to vary with opportunity or risk.
3. Character/behavioral evidence independent of any measurement (r24, r18): Ansel's refusal
   to record a figure he could not prove (the 1911 question mark), his insistence on
   personally delivering the 1919 water-accusation letters he did not believe, and his
   decades of punctual, exacting habit.
Reasons 2 and 3 would still hold even if the glassware had never been measured in 1958;
reason 1 also predates 1958 (it occurred in 1911) and only its *explanation* was supplied
later. As for whether the Association's bookkeeper ever questioned the ledger entries that
refute the accusation: **no**. Bertram Kyle (r23) states plainly that the annual "station
gain... as usual" line "never once crossed my mind to wonder at it," and that of the four
documents entered at the 1925 hearing, he could not say that "any man in that room laid the
second beside the fourth and looked."

**D3.** The sources indicate the manager, Selby Vose, most likely did **not** know, before
1923, that the station's tests ran low — despite having documentary access to information
that would have revealed it. His own clerk describes his invariable habit of reading only
the bottom total of each ledger sheet and never the lines above it, including the "station
gain" line that recorded the very discrepancy at issue (r23). This is corroborated
indirectly by his having signed and issued the 1919 circular accusing patrons of watering
their milk — a genuine, if mistaken, belief in a different cause, which is hard to square
with a manager who was privately aware of an instrumental flaw and shifting blame elsewhere.

## Section E — Contradictions

1. **Hearing/dismissal year.** r17 says the hearing was "in twenty-six" (1926); r23 says
   the award came "in twenty-four" (1924). Five other threads — r18 (dismissal dated 30
   April 1925), r19 (arithmetic: 1958 − 33 = 1925), r21 ("the hearing was in twenty-five"),
   r22 (precise dates, 17 Mar–4 May 1925), and r24 (contemporaneous diary entries dated
   17 March and 30 April 1925) — converge on 1925. **Resolved to 1925**, using the
   contemporaneous diary as the anchor and the convergence of four other independent
   accounts; r17 and r23 are treated as memory errors in opposite directions.

2. **Glass condemnation year.** r17 says "twenty-one" (1921); r23 says "twenty-two" (1922).
   r18, r19, r20 (quoting the firm's own 1923 correspondence), and r22 (exact date, 12 June
   1923) all agree on 1923. **Resolved to 1923**, on the strength of the quoted primary
   correspondence and the official report's precise date.

3. **Award amount.** r19, r22, and r23 independently give $3,800; r21 gives $3,500
   (secondhand, "what my father said"). **Resolved to $3,800** — note r19 and r22 may share
   a common root in the same college bulletin, but r23 (the clerk who personally entered the
   figure in the books) is a genuinely independent line of evidence, so this is two
   independent sources against one.

4. **Ivy's relation to Ansel.** r19 says "great-niece"; r22 initially repeats this, then
   appends an explicit correction to "granddaughter," matching r18. **Resolved to
   granddaughter** (see B1).

5. **Ivy's relation to Hazel.** r18 says second cousins (with explicit derivation); r21
   says "first cousin once removed" (no derivation given). **Resolved to second cousins**
   (see B2).

6. **Families warned by the 1919 circular.** r17 says nine; r24 (contemporaneous diary)
   says eleven. **Not fully resolved** — I lean toward the diary as the more direct
   contemporaneous witness, but no independent tiebreaker exists in these sources.

7. **Rosalie Cudd's relation to Jerome Cudd.** r18 says "daughter"; r19 says "niece."
   **Resolved to daughter**, per Duncan's careful, documentary family notes over the
   hastily-assembled newspaper.

8. **Number of surviving pipettes measured in 1958.** r19 says eleven; r21 and r22
   independently say nine. **Resolved to nine** — two independent sources against one.

9. **Original size of the pipette lot.** The invoice quoted in r20 says six dozen (72);
   r21 (a childhood memory recorded ~60 years later) says five dozen (60). **Resolved to
   72**, per the primary quoted business record.

10. **Weigh book's founding year.** r17 says 1900; r18 says 1897. **Resolved to 1897**, per
    Duncan's careful documentary account; Hazel explicitly disclaims her own reliability on
    dates at the outset of her own interview.

11. **Orra's birth year — a self-contradiction.** r18 states Orra was "born in eighteen
    sixty-eight" but elsewhere in the same document says she "was eighty-two when she died
    in 1948," which arithmetically implies 1866. r24's editorial preface independently
    states 1866. **Resolved to 1866**, favoring the arithmetic and the independent source
    over Duncan's unsourced, internally-contradicted "1868."

12. **Station's own glass start date.** r23 says 1 May 1898; the invoice in r20 (dated
    14 April 1897) and Orra's contemporaneous diary entry of 1 May 1897 (r24) both
    independently indicate 1897. **Resolved to 1897.**

13. **Invoice total — a self-contradiction shared by two narrators.** The quoted invoice in
    r20 sums to $77.93, but Junia Ammon's own narrative text immediately after states
    "seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents" — a self-contradiction within r20 itself
    (likely a digit transposition). The same erroneous $77.39 figure is independently
    repeated in r19's narrative prose. This is simultaneously an example of a narrator
    contradicting itself **and** two narrators sharing the same wrong value. **Resolved to
    $77.93**, verifiable by direct arithmetic on the quoted invoice's own line items.

## Section F — Theory

**Causal theory.** The Ostrey Hollow station's testing pipettes, part of the original
outfit bought from Ammon & Sons in 1897, were manufactured to deliver less than their
marked 17.6 c.c. charge of milk — plausibly, per Ammon's own later speculation, because a
regauged mold produced a lot that ran consistently short and so passed every spot-check.
Because every butterfat test performed with that glass used a short charge of milk against
a scale ruled for a full one, every test read low by the same fixed proportion, regardless
of season, herd, or pasture — explaining both the *size* of the error (fixed by the glass's
manufacturing tolerance, not by any variable condition) and its *constancy* year-round
(r17). It began when the flawed outfit went into service (1 May 1897, r20, r24) and ended
when the state inspector condemned and replaced the glass (12 June 1923, r22), after which
the recorded average rose (r22). Nothing at the station or on the farms needed to change
because the defect was purely instrumental — confirmed by the 1911 natural experiment, in
which substituting a different instrument with the same herds, tester, and season changed
the result (r22, D1).

**Three competing explanations, and what defeats each:**
1. *Patrons watering their milk* — advanced by manager Selby Vose via his 1919 circular
   (r17, r23, r24). Defeated by the state inspector's own lactometer readings, taken at
   every visit from 1908 on, which never once found added water (r22).
2. *Tester skimming cream and falsifying low tests to conceal it* — urged by an Association
   officer at the 1925 hearing (r22). Defeated by the 1911 natural experiment: same tester,
   borrowed glass, tests rose (r22, corroborated by r24).
3. *Wet hay/pasture thinning the cream* — advanced by an Association committee (1908) and a
   veterinarian (1926) (r22). Defeated by the college's own 1911 bog-hay feeding trial
   (Bulletin No. 214), which found bog hay depresses butterfat by at most 0.05 points in
   winter and nothing measurable in summer — far short of the observed, all-season 0.19-point
   deficiency (r22's appended note).

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?** No evidence that it did. The crate
had been sitting in the loft since 1923, directly above the very room where two of the
three 1925 sessions were held (r17, r21, r24); Bertram Kyle (r23) lists only four documents
entered as evidence — none of them the glassware itself — and states that no one appears to
have even cross-referenced those four documents against each other.

**What did the tester himself believe?** Per his son Duncan (r18), after the 1911
broken-glass incident Ansel came home saying "it was something in the glass," and wrote a
question mark in the book rather than assert an unproven claim — he suspected the glass
specifically, from as early as 1911, but felt he could not prove it.

## Section G — Summary

For decades, the Ostrey Hollow milk-testing station paid its patrons on butterfat tests
that ran mysteriously, flatly low. The valley blamed watering, a dishonest tester, or wet
pasture; a 1925 arbitration board dismissed tester Ansel Keddie without proving any of it.
The real cause — short-delivering test pipettes, bought new in 1897 and condemned unnoticed
in 1923 — sat crated in a loft until his granddaughter Ivy measured the glass in 1958,
clearing his name after his death. No single account holds the whole story: the family kept
genealogy and dates, the clerk kept ledgers, the manufacturer kept correspondence, a diary
kept the human cost, and the college bulletin supplied the physical proof. Eight overlapping,
imperfect witnesses were needed because each held one thread, and several got dates, sums,
or relationships wrong — which is itself part of what this record shows.
