# Answers — Part 3 of 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

**C1(a). Proportion by which tests differed from truth, from the glassware.**
Ivy Keddie measured the nine surviving pipettes: each was blown to deliver 17.6 c.c. (the
standard test charge), and the average actual delivery was 16.27 c.c. (r19).
Shortfall proportion = (17.6 − 16.27) / 17.6 = 1.33 / 17.6 ≈ **0.0756, i.e. about 7.6% short.**
Because the fat-percentage reading is computed against an assumed full 17.6 c.c. charge, a short
charge produces a fat reading low by the same proportion: recorded ≈ true × (16.27/17.6) ≈
true × 0.9244, for every use of that glass, regardless of the milk's actual fat content.

**C1(b). True average test, and points short.**
The bulletin (as reported in r22) states directly: recorded station average = 3.61%; deficiency =
0.19 points; implied true average ≈ **3.80%**.
Cross-check using the C1(a) proportion: true = 3.61 / 0.9244 ≈ 3.905% — implying a deficiency of
about 0.30 points, not 0.19.
**These two figures do not reconcile**, and the sources give no way to close the gap. Possible
explanations (none confirmed): the "0.19" figure may have been benchmarked against the *neighboring
creamery's* 3.78% average (3.78 − 3.61 = 0.17, closer to 0.19 than to 0.30) rather than derived
purely from the pipette-volume ratio; or the nine surviving pipettes measured in 1958 may not
represent the full 72-pipette lot's average shortfall over the decades. I report the bulletin's
own stated figures (0.19 points; true ≈ 3.80%) as the documented answer, since it is the number the
investigators who did the actual work published, but flag the arithmetic tension with the
volume-ratio calculation as unresolved by the sources.

**C1(c). Pounds of butterfat not credited, over the full affected years.**
The only *direct* sourced figure is Cleve Oram's oral-history total: **"sixty-seven thousand
pounds of butterfat"** over "the whole run of years" (r21) — a single, uncorroborated source.
As a cross-check: the clerk (r23) gives total milk received over "the long run of years... complained
of" as 40,000,000 lb, at an average of 1,600,000 lb/year, i.e. **25 years**. Applying the 0.19-point
deficiency: 40,000,000 × 0.0019 ≈ **76,000 lb**. This does not match Oram's 67,000 lb figure either.
**I cannot fully reconcile these two figures from the sources given** — I report both: 67,000 lb
(the only number a narrator actually states for this specific quantity) and ≈76,000 lb (my own
derived cross-check from two other sourced figures), flagging that the "years affected" span used
in each may differ and is not pinned down precisely by any source.

**C1(d). Dollar value, and fraction actually received.**
The bulletin's dollar figure (r19): **$19,600** total value of unpaid butterfat.
The patrons actually received the 1925 arbitration award of **$3,800** (r19, r22, r23).
Fraction received = 3,800 / 19,600 ≈ **0.194, i.e. about 19%** — roughly one dollar in five.

**C2. The largest patron's year, 1910 (Emmet Brant, per his own weigh book, r17).**
(a) Credited: 200,000 lb milk delivered, test paid at 3.42%.
Credited butterfat = 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb.**
(b) Owed and not credited, using the documented 0.19-point deficiency: true test = 3.42 + 0.19 =
3.61%; true butterfat = 200,000 × 0.0361 = 7,220 lb; shortfall = 7,220 − 6,840 = **380 lb.**
*(Alternate, using the C1(a) volume-ratio proportion instead: true test = 3.42/0.9244 ≈ 3.70%;
true butterfat = 7,400 lb; shortfall ≈ 560 lb. Flagged as the same unresolved discrepancy as
C1(b); I give 380 lb as the primary answer since it uses the deficiency figure the sources
themselves state as the finding.)*
(c) Value that year: at the average price of 25 cents/lb butterfat stated by the clerk for "that
whole stretch" (r23): 380 lb × $0.25 = **$95.00** (or, on the alternate figure, 560 × $0.25 = $140).

**C3. Credited vs. received at the main creamery, over the full run.**
No source states an absolute total in pounds for either "credited to patrons" or "received at
Larrow Green"; only the relationship between them is documented. The clerk (r23) records that
every year the station account showed: butterfat credited to patrons (on the station test), minus
butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in the station's cream, entered as "station gain...
five and a quarter per cent, as usual." **(c) The documented percentage is 5.25%, of the pounds
credited to patrons** (i.e., received ≈ credited × 1.0525).
**This figure must not be confused with the test shortfall itself.** The 5.25% "station gain" was
a routine, unremarkable skimming/processing margin between the station and the main creamery,
booked the same way every single year without anyone questioning it. It is entirely separate from,
and does not capture, the ~7.6% (or 0.19-point) shortfall in what patrons were credited in the
first place — that error happened upstream, in the test applied to the farmer's raw milk, before
any of these station-account figures were ever generated, and it never surfaced in this ledger
line at all. **Absolute pound totals for (a) and (b) cannot be determined from the sources given.**

**C4. The invoice for the station's own testing outfit.**
(a) The invoice reproduced verbatim (r20) lists: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15. Summed:
25.20+31.68=56.88; +0.90=57.78; +18.00=75.78; +2.15=**77.93**. This is **$77.93**, verified by my
own addition of the document's own line items — the strongest possible method, since it requires
no external corroboration. (Note: three narrators — r17, r19, and even r20's own prose — instead
give "$77.39" in words; this is a repeated transcription error resolved by arithmetic; see
Section E.)
(b) The invoice bought **six dozen (72) pipettes**, at 35 cents each, each marked for 17.6 c.c.
The nine survivors measured in 1958 averaged 16.27 c.c., i.e. **short by about 1.33 c.c. each**
on average (17.6 − 16.27 = 1.33).

**C5(a). Duration of faulty glass in service, vs. years the money calculation runs.**
The glass was installed 1 May 1897 and condemned 12 June 1923 — a span of **about 26 years and
1.5 months**, with a roughly nine-week interruption in 1911 when a borrowed measure was used
instead (see Story 3). The money/underpayment calculation, per the clerk's aggregate milk-receipt
tally (r23), is described as running over **about 25 "full delivery years"** (40,000,000 lb ÷
1,600,000 lb/year). These two figures differ by about a year. **The sources do not specify why**:
the 25-year figure is explicitly presented as an average/approximation ("did not vary greatly"),
not a precisely bounded date range, and no source states exactly which calendar years it covers
or whether it excludes the partial first season or the 1911 interruption. I report both figures
and flag the roughly one-year gap as unreconciled by the sources.

**C5(b). Pounds of milk in the station's first season.**
The clerk's recollection (r23): "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" — **1,100,000 lb.** This
is the *sole* source for this figure, and the clerk himself flags it as an unverified oral
estimate from "men who were there," explicitly "not in any book I ever ruled." I report it but
flag it as low-confidence, uncorroborated testimony rather than a ledger figure.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 borrowed-glass interval as an experiment.**
When/duration: 8 May 1911 to 10 July 1911 — **nine weeks** (r22, dated precisely by r24's diary
entry of 8 May 1911, and referenced in general terms by r18).
Variable: the physical measuring glass (station's own vs. a borrowed substitute).
Held constant: the same tester (Ansel Keddie), the same herds and pasture, the same season, and
the same supervision throughout (r22 states this explicitly).
Result: recorded tests rose markedly for the nine weeks with the borrowed measure, then fell
straight back to the old low level the day the station's own glass resumed (r22).
What it establishes: because literally everything else was held fixed across the substitution,
this rules out — as a matter of controlled comparison, not mere calculation — every explanation
resting on the tester's honesty, the cows, the feed, or the season, and isolates the instrument as
the variable that tracks the effect. A calculation of average test levels alone (e.g., comparing
two periods' means) could show *that* a difference existed but could never, by itself, rule out
confounds like a seasonal feed change; the 1911 interval is valuable precisely because it holds
those confounds fixed.
What it does **not** establish: it does not by itself quantify the shortfall precisely, prove the
exact mechanism (short delivery volume vs. some other glass defect), or prove the defect was
identical and unchanging across all 26 years of service — only the direct 1958 measurement of the
surviving pipettes did that (Story 7).

**D2. Independent reasons the tester was not stealing cream.**
1. *The 1911 natural experiment* (r22, r18, r24): with the same tester, herds, and supervision
throughout, tests rose the moment the glass was swapped and fell back the moment it was returned —
inconsistent with a tester deliberately under-reporting to hide theft, since his behavior did not
change, only the instrument did.
2. *The clerk's ledger audit trail* (r23): the "station gain" entered every year — the difference
between what was credited to patrons and what cream actually reached Larrow Green — was a small,
consistent, explained 5.25% "as usual," never anomalous, and was "written down in ink... in a book
that stood on an open shelf... not a secret." Large-scale theft by the tester should have produced
an irregular or unexplained gap in this specific accounting line; none appeared.
3. *The 1958 direct physical measurement* (r19, r22): the surviving pipettes, measured against a
calibrated burette, delivered short by a fixed proportion fully sufficient on its own to account
for the entire recorded deficiency, leaving no residual gap that would require theft as a
supplementary explanation.
Independence: these draw on three different kinds of evidence — a contemporaneous behavioral/
temporal record, an internal accounting audit, and a later laboratory measurement — from different
documents and different decades, none derived from or dependent on the others.
Reason still valid without the 1958 measurement: **reasons 1 and 2** — both were available in
principle all along, whether or not anyone ever measured the glass.
Did the bookkeeper (Wilbur Teague) ever question the ledger entries himself? **No.** Per r23
explicitly: "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years... the figures were the figures,
and the asking was for other people."

**D3. Did the manager know the tests ran low before the glass was condemned?**
**Yes, that they ran low — but not why.** Selby Vose, manager from 1911, personally signed the
station's yearly account sheets carrying the "station gain" line every year (r23: "he would take a
sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign"), and it was Vose himself who
circulated the 1919 circular specifically because the station's tests ran persistently low
(r19, r23, r24) — a fact publicly complained-of since at least 1902 (r22). So he certainly knew
the tests ran low, at least from 1919 onward and very plausibly well before. What the sources
establish he did *not* know was the true cause: he inferred and acted on the wrong explanation
(deliberate watering by the patrons) rather than investigating the testing equipment itself.

reliability with dates ("Years I have not"). Method: narrator's own stated unreliability.

2. **Ivy Keddie's relation to Ansel.** r19: "great-niece." r18 (her own father) and r22's corrected

3. **Hazel/Ivy's cousin relationship.** r21: "first cousin once removed." r17 (Hazel herself) and

secondhand hearsay.

4. **Rosalie Cudd's relation to Jerome Cudd.** r18: "daughter." r19: "niece." Two sources, direct
conflict, no third source to break the tie. **Cannot be confidently settled from the sources

5. **Glass condemnation year.** r17: 1921. r23: 1922. r18, r19, r20 (dated letter), r22, r24
(dated diary entry): 1923 — five independent sources, including a dated primary document.
Resolution: 1923. Method: majority + document-anchored dating.

6. **Outfit installation year.** r17: 1899. r23: 1898. r20 (invoice) and r24 (contemporaneous

7. **Hearing/dismissal year.** r17: "twenty-six" (1926). r23: "twenty-four" (1924). r19, r21, r22

8. **Award amount.** r21: $3,500 (secondhand, from the narrator's father). r19, r22, r23: $3,800,

9. **Number of families sent the 1919 circular.** r17: nine. r24 (diary, from the wife of the man

Given r19's own description of itself as assembled "in a day and a half," and that r21 and r22 draw

though with less certainty than the document-anchored cases above.

11. **The clearest "two (in fact three) narrators carry the same wrong value" case: the invoice
total.** r17 and r19, and even r20's own prose (as opposed to its own reproduced invoice line

verbatim in r20 and summed by simple arithmetic (25.20+31.68+0.90+18.00+2.15), equal **$77.93**.
This is broken by arithmetic on the primary document itself, not by counting narrators: $77.39 is

12. **Narrator self-contradiction on the page: r20.** The invoice reproduced in r20 totals $77.93

## Section F — Theory

**F1. Single causal theory.** The entire Ostrey Hollow shortfall was produced by a manufacturing
defect in the glass pipettes (and possibly bottles) installed 1 May 1897: though blown/marked to
deliver the standard 17.6 c.c. test charge, they actually delivered on average only about 16.27
c.c. (roughly 7.6% short). Because the test bottle's neck is calibrated for a full charge, a short
charge produces a proportionally low fat reading on every single use, irrespective of the milk's
true fat content, the cows, the season, or the tester. This explains:
- *Why that size*: the deficiency (≈0.19 points / ≈7.6%) reflects a fixed manufacturing bias in one
particular glass lot, not a biological or seasonal variable — hence it was constant "in every
month of the year" (r17, r22), unlike a feed-related cause, which would fluctuate seasonally.
- *Why it began when it did*: it began the moment the defective glass entered service, 1 May 1897,
with the first formal complaint following once patrons had accumulated enough seasons to notice a
pattern (1902).
- *Why it ended when it did*: it ended the moment the inspector condemned the lot and new glass
replaced it (June/July 1923), after which the recorded average immediately rose to 3.85% — above
even the main creamery's average (r22).
- *Why nothing at the station or the farms had to change*: the defect was entirely in an inert
manufactured object; no change in husbandry, hay, machinery, or tester behavior was needed or
occurred — replacing the glass alone fixed the readings, exactly as observed.

**The three competing explanations:**
1. **Patrons watering their milk** — advanced by manager Selby Vose, formalized in his 1919
circular (r19, r23, r24). Defeated by the state inspector's repeated lactometer tests from 1908
onward, which never once found added water at the station (r22).
2. **The tester stealing cream and falsifying low readings** — urged by an Association officer at
the 1925 hearing (r22). Defeated by the 1911 nine-week natural experiment: the same tester, same
conditions, but readings rose the moment the glass changed and fell back when it was returned
(r22, r18, r24).
3. **Wet bog hay thinning the cream** — advanced by an Association committee in 1908 and an Ashlin
veterinarian in 1926 (r22, r24). Initially entertained by the county report as a partial factor,
then explicitly withdrawn once the report obtained the actual 1911 college feeding-trial figures
(Bulletin No. 214): bog hay depressed the test by "not more than five hundredths of one per cent
in winter, and by nothing... on summer pasture" — far too small to explain a flat, year-round
0.19-point deficiency (r22's own appended correction).

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?** No source says they did, and the clerk who
personally listed everything submitted names exactly four documents — never the glass — and
states plainly that no one in the room "laid the second beside the fourth and looked" (r23). The
condemned crate sat, unexamined, in the loft directly above two of the three hearing sessions
(r21, r23).

**What did the tester believe the cause was?** Ansel Keddie suspected "something in the glass" as
early as 1911, saying so twice at home — but, unwilling to put down what he could not prove, he
recorded only a bare question mark in his own test book and continued using the official glass and
reporting its figures for another fourteen years, up to his dismissal (r18).

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

For over two decades, the Ostrey Hollow milk-testing station on Larrow Valley's Hessel Bottom paid
its farmers less than they were owed, because the glass pipettes bought in 1897 were manufactured
slightly short and under-delivered the sample used for the butterfat test — a defect that produced
a flat, constant deficiency no one could explain. Farmers suspected cheating; the Association
accused the patrons of watering their milk; agronomists blamed wet pasture; officials dismissed the
tester, Ansel Keddie, in 1925, even though a forgotten 1911 experiment with borrowed glass had
already hinted at the truth. Not until 1958, when his granddaughter Ivy Keddie climbed a ladder
into the station's loft and measured the long-condemned crate of glass, was the mechanical cause
proven — decades after Keddie's death. It took a family's notes, an oral history, a clerk's
ledgers, a firm's invoice, a wife's diary, a county report, and a newspaper story together to
reconstruct what none of them alone got entirely right.

*(148 words)*
