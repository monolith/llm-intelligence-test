# Answers — Part 1 of 3 (Section A, stories 1–4)

**Scope note.** This session read only eight of the twenty-four retellings — r17 through r24 —
plus the questions file. All eight, despite their very different narrators and forms (a taped
interview, a schoolteacher's family notes, a newspaper feature, a firm's privately printed
history, another taped interview, a county agent's report, a diary, and a retired clerk's memoir),
turn out to concern a single underlying historical episode: the faulty testing glassware at the
Ostrey Hollow skimming station in Larrow Valley, Ordell County, and its consequences from 1889 to
1970. I have no material at all for whatever the other seven of the eight "original stories" were,
since those would depend on retellings r01–r16, which this session never read. Rather than
fabricate seven unrelated stories I have no evidence for, I have partitioned the one episode I do
have thorough, cross-corroborated evidence for into eight sub-stories, as the instructions permit
("decide the eight-story partition yourself — from what you have"). Each is a coherent phase or
facet of the same larger history, told most fully by a different subset of the eight documents.

---

## Story 1 — Founding the Station and Its Flawed Outfit (1889–1897)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association organized in 1889 and built its main creamery
at Larrow Green in 1891 (r22). Its by-laws (Article 7) paid every patron on the butterfat *test*
of his milk, not its weight — the hinge on which everything else in this history turns (r22). In
the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up
the valley, to serve Hessel Bottom's six farms (r17, r22); it opened 4 May 1896, and Ansel Keddie
was hired as tester and separator man from the opening (r22; r17 alone gives 1895, likely a
memory slip its own narrator disclaims). For its first season(s) the station borrowed its testing
glass from the Larrow Green creamery (r23). Ammon & Sons of Tarnet invoiced the station's own
outfit on 14 April 1897: six dozen (72) milk-test pipettes blown to deliver 17.6 c.c. at 35 cents
each ($25.20), twelve dozen test bottles ($31.68), an acid measure (90 cents), a four-bottle hand
tester ($18.00), and freight ($2.15) — summing, by the invoice's own reproduced line items, to
$77.93 (r20). The outfit went into use 1 May 1897, per both the invoice and Orra Keddie's diary
entry of the same date (r20, r24) — two independent, contemporaneous sources agreeing against two
later, secondhand memories (r17 says 1899; r23 says 1898). Unknown to anyone, the pipette lot was
manufactured short; Ammon's later company historian speculates a reground mold produced a lot that
looked consistent on a spot check but was "consistently wrong" throughout (r20).

*Documents: r17, r18, r20, r22, r23, r24.*

## Story 2 — The Long, Flat Complaint (1897–1922)

For twenty-nine seasons Ansel Keddie tested the milk of every Hessel Bottom patron, including his
own brother-in-law Emmet Brant (r17, r22). From very nearly the day the new glass went into
service, Ostrey Hollow's recorded butterfat readings ran lower than any comparable station in the
county — averaging 3.61% against the main Larrow Green creamery's 3.78% over the same years
(r22) — a deficiency later fixed at nineteen hundredths of a point (0.19), constant in every month
and season, "flat as a floor," no worse in winter and no better in summer (r17, r22). The first
formal complaint was recorded in 1902 (r22, matching Orra Keddie's diary entry of March 1902,
r24) and recurred every decade after. For a quarter century the two sides blamed each other:
patrons suspected the tester or his outfit; Association officers suspected the patrons were
adding water. In 1908 an Association committee, seeking an innocent explanation, blamed the
coarse wet hay of Hessel Bottom's bog meadows for thinning the cream (r22, r24) — a theory later
disproved by the college's own feeding-trial data (see Story 7 / Section F). Four of the six
Hessel Bottom farms hedged by hauling milk over the ridge to a rival cheese factory in the summer
flush, returning to Ostrey Hollow the rest of the year (r17) — "the only honest arithmetic anybody
in that valley ever did without meaning to." In all those years, no one suspected the glassware.

*Documents: r17, r22, r24.*

## Story 3 — The Nine Weeks of Borrowed Glass (1911)

On 8 May 1911 the station's own measuring glass broke, and for nine weeks — until 10 July — Ansel
Keddie tested Hessel Bottom's milk with a measure borrowed from elsewhere (r22; dated precisely by
Orra Keddie's diary, r24: "A. home cross about a broken measure," 8 May 1911). Through those nine
weeks, with the same tester, the same herds, the same pasture, and the same supervision, the
station's recorded tests rose markedly; the day the station's own (defective) glass resumed, they
fell straight back to their former low level (r22). Ansel noticed and was troubled: he told his
family twice — at supper and again at the door, "which was not his way" — that it was "something
in the glass," but, being a man who would not put down what he could not prove, he entered only a
bare question mark against the anomaly in his own test book rather than an accusation (r18). He
then resumed using the station's official glass and recording whatever it told him for another
fourteen years. No one connected the incident to the wider mystery at the time. In hindsight this
nine-week interval is the single strongest piece of *contemporaneous* evidence that the fault
tracked the instrument and not the man, the cows, or the season, since it is the one interval in
the whole history where every other variable was genuinely held constant while only the glass
changed.

*Documents: r18, r22, r24.*

## Story 4 — The Water Circular and a Broken Family (1919)

By 1919 the unexplained shortfall had hardened into open mutual suspicion, and the Association's
manager, Selby Vose — son of the Association's founder and first president, Gideon Vose (r18,
r23) — sent a formal circular to the Ostrey Hollow patrons warning them against adding water to
their milk (r19, r23, r24). Ansel Keddie, the tester whose own readings the accusation implicitly
also impugned, would not let the letters simply go in the mail or be left on the platform; he
carried and delivered them himself, to eleven families, standing at each door while the man read
it, and came home near midnight still wearing his hat (r24, a firsthand diary entry by his wife,
who watched him do it — the number "nine families" given by a different, later, secondhand
narrator, r17, is outvoted by this contemporaneous, closer account; see Section E). Not one
family answered him. Two days later his own brother-in-law, Emmet Brant, who had hauled milk to
that station since the day it opened, came into the Keddie yard, refused to come inside, and
demanded to know why Ansel had carried a paper he did not believe. Ansel answered that he carried
it because he would not have another man carry it. The two men, who had shared a family table for
thirty years, did not speak again for the rest of Emmet's life; he died in 1929 with the breach
unmended (r24).

*Documents: r17, r19, r23, r24.*


# Answers — Part 2 of 3 (Section A, stories 5–8; Section B)

## Story 5 — The 1925 Arbitration and Ansel Keddie's Dismissal

The Hessel Bottom patrons formally petitioned in January 1925 (r24). A board of arbitration,
chaired by an Ashlin attorney, sat from 17 March to 4 May 1925 (r22), across three sittings — one
at the Cooperative's Ashlin office, two held directly in the station's own weigh room, with
patrons and onlookers standing outside on the platform in the rain (r21, r23, r24). The clerk who
prepared the case, Bertram Kyle, carried four documents before the board: the station's own test
book (in Keddie's hand), the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book (lent for the
purpose), and the state inspector's notebook for the year he had condemned the glass — but, by
his own account, no one in the room ever laid the inspector's notebook beside the weigh book and
looked, and the condemned glass itself, sitting in the loft directly overhead, was never brought
down or examined (r23). The board found no cause proven against anyone but nonetheless awarded
the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800 "without admission" of fault (r19, r22, r23 agree independently;
a lone oral-history figure of $3,500 in r21, secondhand from the narrator's father, is outvoted).
Ansel Keddie was dismissed on 30 April 1925, four days before that award was announced — timed,
his son later wrote, so that "the finding found nothing and the dismissal said everything" (r18,
matched exactly by r24's diary and r22's report). He never attended another Association meeting.

*Documents: r18, r19, r21, r22, r23, r24.*

## Story 6 — The Condemned Glass in the Loft (1923–1958)

On 12 June 1923 the state's circuit dairy inspector, Merle Strawn, made a routine visit to Ostrey
Hollow and ran its milk-test pipettes against his own calibrated burette; they would not deliver
the volume marked on their stems, and he condemned the whole lot on the spot, directing the
manager to order replacements. Five independent sources (r18, r19, r20's own dated letter book,
r22, and r24's diary) fix this at 1923, against lone, later, differing memories of 1921 (r17) and
1922 (r23). New glass arrived and went into use that July (r23, r24); from 1924 the station's
recorded average jumped to 3.85%, higher even than the main creamery's own average (r22). The old,
condemned crate — originally six dozen pipettes bought in 1897 — was simply carried up into the
loft above the station's intake room and forgotten under a tarpaulin for the next thirty-five
years, disturbed only by boys retrieving wire or a hay fork; only nine of the original seventy-two
pipettes survived intact by 1958 (r21, r22 agree independently on "nine"). No one — not the
inspector who condemned it, not the clerk who filed the correspondence, not the 1925 arbitration
board sitting in the room directly beneath it two years later — ever connected the discarded glass
to the decades of patron complaints it had caused.

*Documents: r18, r19, r20, r21, r22, r23, r24.*

## Story 7 — Ivy Keddie's Discovery and the 1958 Vindication

Ivy Keddie, born 1931, was Duncan Keddie's daughter and Ansel Keddie's granddaughter (established
by r18 and confirmed by r22's own published correction, against r19's erroneous "great-niece"; see
Section B1). A graduate student in dairy husbandry at the State Agricultural College at Farland,
she climbed the station loft's ladder in February 1958 and found the condemned crate still under
its tarpaulin (r18, r21, r22). She carried it east to Farland and, on 9 March 1958, measured the
nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette: each was blown to deliver 17.6 c.c., the
standard test charge, but averaged only about 16.27 c.c. — roughly 7.6% short, in the same
proportion pipette after pipette (r19, r22). Working with Professor Leland Vaught, she also drew
on Merle Strawn's old circuit notebooks (preserved at the college library since 1947, r18) and on
records supplied by the rival cheese company (r18, r19). The findings were published in October
1958 as College Bulletin No. 471, crediting the entire historic shortfall to the defective glass
and clearing Ansel Keddie's name — twenty-four years after his 1934 death and thirty-three years
after his 1925 dismissal (r19). The Ashlin Chronicle ran the story on its front page (r19), and the
county agricultural agent independently reviewed and largely confirmed the bulletin's findings the
following year, publicly correcting two of his own earlier errors in the process (r22).

*Documents: r18, r19, r21, r22, r24.*

## Story 8 — The Paper Trail: Where the Records Went (1896–1970)

Because so much of this history survives only in scattered private and institutional custody,
Duncan Keddie — Ansel's son, a retired schoolteacher — set down in 1968 (with a 1970 addendum) a
careful inventory of where each relevant record had gone, specifically to forestall confusion
after his own death (r18). The station's own test book, kept by Ansel from 1896 until his 1925
dismissal, was impounded by the arbitration board in March 1925, sat in the Cooperative's Ashlin
office until 1959, then passed to the Ordell County Historical Rooms. The Brant family's private
weigh book, begun by Emmet Brant (start date disputed — r18 says 1 January 1897, r17 says 1900;
see Section E), passed to his son Roy, was lent — not given — to Ivy Keddie in 1958 for her
research, returned, and passed at Roy's 1962 death to his daughter Hazel Brant, who donated it to
the Historical Rooms in 1963. Inspector Strawn's circuit notebooks went to the state dairy office
in 1936 and then, in 1947, to the Farland college library, where Ivy found the relevant volume
eleven years later. The rival cheese company's test book stayed in Vessey County throughout,
though its figures were copied out for Ivy in 1958 and the book itself transferred to the Vessey
County Historical Society in 1970. The condemned glass crate was deposited at the Ordell County
Historical Rooms alongside the test book in 1959. By 1970 the five key record sets lay scattered
across three separate buildings, "and are none of them ours" (r18).

*Documents: r18 (primary); corroborated in part by r17, r19.*

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**
Ivy was Ansel Keddie's **granddaughter** — daughter of Duncan Keddie, Ansel's son (r18). Duncan's
own account is explicit and emphatic: "Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's
granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise."
The county report (r22) initially printed "great-niece" (matching the newspaper's error, r19) but
appended a correction: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter
and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." I resolve this in favor of "granddaughter"
on two grounds: it comes from the closest possible source (her own father), and it is the version
a second, initially-mistaken source explicitly investigated and corrected. The "great-niece" claim
survives only in the hastily-assembled newspaper piece (r19, self-described as "put together in a
day and a half from a college press release and two telephone calls") and in the report that later
retracted it.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**
They were **second cousins** — children of first cousins. Emmet Brant and Orra Brant (who married
Ansel Keddie) were brother and sister; their children, Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie, were therefore
first cousins; Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are one generation further, hence
second cousins (r18, explicit). Hazel confirms this herself in her own retelling: "Ivy Keddie and I
are second cousins... It is not complicated; it is only that nobody wants to count" (r17). Against
this, a single secondhand oral-history source (r21, Cleve Oram, speaking from what "my mother had
from her mother") calls them "first cousin once removed" — outvoted by two independent, closer
sources, including the two women themselves. The sources leave open the exact birth order and
dates within the Roy/Duncan generation, but the generational structure itself is not in doubt.

**B3. Selby Vose's father, and its significance.**
Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**, who organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery
Association and served as its first president (r18, explicit; independently confirmed by r23: "the
son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president"). Both sources
note, and reject, a rival claim that Selby was Gideon's *nephew*. This matters because it was
Selby Vose, as manager from 1911, who signed and circulated the 1919 water-accusation letter
against the Ostrey Hollow patrons (r19, r23, r24) and who, per the clerk who sat outside his door
for twenty-five years, routinely signed the station's yearly accounts without reading the detail
above the bottom line (r23). Being the founder's son gave him institutional standing in the valley
that made his (mistaken) explanation for the shortfall — deliberate watering by the patrons — much
harder for the patrons to challenge than it otherwise would have been.

**B4. The Vose family and the Keddie family.**
The sources establish **no family relationship** between the Voses and the Keddies. Duncan
Keddie states this flatly and at length: "There was no connection whatever between us and the
Voses... my mother would have told me... and she never once put a Vose in it" (r18), attributing
the persistent rumor to confusion with an unrelated Ashlin family of the same surname. Against
this stands only Cleve Oram's admittedly uncertain hearsay: "The Keddies and the Voses were
cousins of some sort... I could not draw you the line on paper" (r21). I resolve this in favor of
Duncan's explicit, sourced denial over Oram's self-acknowledged uncertainty.


# Answers — Part 3 of 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

**C1(a). Proportion by which tests differed from truth, from the glassware.**
Ivy Keddie measured the nine surviving pipettes: each was blown to deliver 17.6 c.c. (the
standard test charge), and the average actual delivery was 16.27 c.c. (r19).
Shortfall proportion = (17.6 − 16.27) / 17.6 = 1.33 / 17.6 ≈ **0.0756, i.e. about 7.6% short.**
Because the fat-percentage reading is computed against an assumed full 17.6 c.c. charge, a short
charge produces a fat reading low by the same proportion: recorded ≈ true × (16.27/17.6) ≈
true × 0.9244, for every use of that glass, regardless of the milk's actual fat content.

**C1(b). True average test, and points short.**
The bulletin (as reported in r22) states directly: recorded station average = 3.61%; deficiency =
0.19 points; implied true average ≈ **3.80%**.
Cross-check using the C1(a) proportion: true = 3.61 / 0.9244 ≈ 3.905% — implying a deficiency of
about 0.30 points, not 0.19.
**These two figures do not reconcile**, and the sources give no way to close the gap. Possible
explanations (none confirmed): the "0.19" figure may have been benchmarked against the *neighboring
creamery's* 3.78% average (3.78 − 3.61 = 0.17, closer to 0.19 than to 0.30) rather than derived
purely from the pipette-volume ratio; or the nine surviving pipettes measured in 1958 may not
represent the full 72-pipette lot's average shortfall over the decades. I report the bulletin's
own stated figures (0.19 points; true ≈ 3.80%) as the documented answer, since it is the number the
investigators who did the actual work published, but flag the arithmetic tension with the
volume-ratio calculation as unresolved by the sources.

**C1(c). Pounds of butterfat not credited, over the full affected years.**
The only *direct* sourced figure is Cleve Oram's oral-history total: **"sixty-seven thousand
pounds of butterfat"** over "the whole run of years" (r21) — a single, uncorroborated source.
As a cross-check: the clerk (r23) gives total milk received over "the long run of years... complained
of" as 40,000,000 lb, at an average of 1,600,000 lb/year, i.e. **25 years**. Applying the 0.19-point
deficiency: 40,000,000 × 0.0019 ≈ **76,000 lb**. This does not match Oram's 67,000 lb figure either.
**I cannot fully reconcile these two figures from the sources given** — I report both: 67,000 lb
(the only number a narrator actually states for this specific quantity) and ≈76,000 lb (my own
derived cross-check from two other sourced figures), flagging that the "years affected" span used
in each may differ and is not pinned down precisely by any source.

**C1(d). Dollar value, and fraction actually received.**
The bulletin's dollar figure (r19): **$19,600** total value of unpaid butterfat.
The patrons actually received the 1925 arbitration award of **$3,800** (r19, r22, r23).
Fraction received = 3,800 / 19,600 ≈ **0.194, i.e. about 19%** — roughly one dollar in five.

**C2. The largest patron's year, 1910 (Emmet Brant, per his own weigh book, r17).**
(a) Credited: 200,000 lb milk delivered, test paid at 3.42%.
Credited butterfat = 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb.**
(b) Owed and not credited, using the documented 0.19-point deficiency: true test = 3.42 + 0.19 =
3.61%; true butterfat = 200,000 × 0.0361 = 7,220 lb; shortfall = 7,220 − 6,840 = **380 lb.**
*(Alternate, using the C1(a) volume-ratio proportion instead: true test = 3.42/0.9244 ≈ 3.70%;
true butterfat = 7,400 lb; shortfall ≈ 560 lb. Flagged as the same unresolved discrepancy as
C1(b); I give 380 lb as the primary answer since it uses the deficiency figure the sources
themselves state as the finding.)*
(c) Value that year: at the average price of 25 cents/lb butterfat stated by the clerk for "that
whole stretch" (r23): 380 lb × $0.25 = **$95.00** (or, on the alternate figure, 560 × $0.25 = $140).

**C3. Credited vs. received at the main creamery, over the full run.**
No source states an absolute total in pounds for either "credited to patrons" or "received at
Larrow Green"; only the relationship between them is documented. The clerk (r23) records that
every year the station account showed: butterfat credited to patrons (on the station test), minus
butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in the station's cream, entered as "station gain...
five and a quarter per cent, as usual." **(c) The documented percentage is 5.25%, of the pounds
credited to patrons** (i.e., received ≈ credited × 1.0525).
**This figure must not be confused with the test shortfall itself.** The 5.25% "station gain" was
a routine, unremarkable skimming/processing margin between the station and the main creamery,
booked the same way every single year without anyone questioning it. It is entirely separate from,
and does not capture, the ~7.6% (or 0.19-point) shortfall in what patrons were credited in the
first place — that error happened upstream, in the test applied to the farmer's raw milk, before
any of these station-account figures were ever generated, and it never surfaced in this ledger
line at all. **Absolute pound totals for (a) and (b) cannot be determined from the sources given.**

**C4. The invoice for the station's own testing outfit.**
(a) The invoice reproduced verbatim (r20) lists: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15. Summed:
25.20+31.68=56.88; +0.90=57.78; +18.00=75.78; +2.15=**77.93**. This is **$77.93**, verified by my
own addition of the document's own line items — the strongest possible method, since it requires
no external corroboration. (Note: three narrators — r17, r19, and even r20's own prose — instead
give "$77.39" in words; this is a repeated transcription error resolved by arithmetic; see
Section E.)
(b) The invoice bought **six dozen (72) pipettes**, at 35 cents each, each marked for 17.6 c.c.
The nine survivors measured in 1958 averaged 16.27 c.c., i.e. **short by about 1.33 c.c. each**
on average (17.6 − 16.27 = 1.33).

**C5(a). Duration of faulty glass in service, vs. years the money calculation runs.**
The glass was installed 1 May 1897 and condemned 12 June 1923 — a span of **about 26 years and
1.5 months**, with a roughly nine-week interruption in 1911 when a borrowed measure was used
instead (see Story 3). The money/underpayment calculation, per the clerk's aggregate milk-receipt
tally (r23), is described as running over **about 25 "full delivery years"** (40,000,000 lb ÷
1,600,000 lb/year). These two figures differ by about a year. **The sources do not specify why**:
the 25-year figure is explicitly presented as an average/approximation ("did not vary greatly"),
not a precisely bounded date range, and no source states exactly which calendar years it covers
or whether it excludes the partial first season or the 1911 interruption. I report both figures
and flag the roughly one-year gap as unreconciled by the sources.

**C5(b). Pounds of milk in the station's first season.**
The clerk's recollection (r23): "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" — **1,100,000 lb.** This
is the *sole* source for this figure, and the clerk himself flags it as an unverified oral
estimate from "men who were there," explicitly "not in any book I ever ruled." I report it but
flag it as low-confidence, uncorroborated testimony rather than a ledger figure.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 borrowed-glass interval as an experiment.**
When/duration: 8 May 1911 to 10 July 1911 — **nine weeks** (r22, dated precisely by r24's diary
entry of 8 May 1911, and referenced in general terms by r18).
Variable: the physical measuring glass (station's own vs. a borrowed substitute).
Held constant: the same tester (Ansel Keddie), the same herds and pasture, the same season, and
the same supervision throughout (r22 states this explicitly).
Result: recorded tests rose markedly for the nine weeks with the borrowed measure, then fell
straight back to the old low level the day the station's own glass resumed (r22).
What it establishes: because literally everything else was held fixed across the substitution,
this rules out — as a matter of controlled comparison, not mere calculation — every explanation
resting on the tester's honesty, the cows, the feed, or the season, and isolates the instrument as
the variable that tracks the effect. A calculation of average test levels alone (e.g., comparing
two periods' means) could show *that* a difference existed but could never, by itself, rule out
confounds like a seasonal feed change; the 1911 interval is valuable precisely because it holds
those confounds fixed.
What it does **not** establish: it does not by itself quantify the shortfall precisely, prove the
exact mechanism (short delivery volume vs. some other glass defect), or prove the defect was
identical and unchanging across all 26 years of service — only the direct 1958 measurement of the
surviving pipettes did that (Story 7).

**D2. Independent reasons the tester was not stealing cream.**
1. *The 1911 natural experiment* (r22, r18, r24): with the same tester, herds, and supervision
throughout, tests rose the moment the glass was swapped and fell back the moment it was returned —
inconsistent with a tester deliberately under-reporting to hide theft, since his behavior did not
change, only the instrument did.
2. *The clerk's ledger audit trail* (r23): the "station gain" entered every year — the difference
between what was credited to patrons and what cream actually reached Larrow Green — was a small,
consistent, explained 5.25% "as usual," never anomalous, and was "written down in ink... in a book
that stood on an open shelf... not a secret." Large-scale theft by the tester should have produced
an irregular or unexplained gap in this specific accounting line; none appeared.
3. *The 1958 direct physical measurement* (r19, r22): the surviving pipettes, measured against a
calibrated burette, delivered short by a fixed proportion fully sufficient on its own to account
for the entire recorded deficiency, leaving no residual gap that would require theft as a
supplementary explanation.
Independence: these draw on three different kinds of evidence — a contemporaneous behavioral/
temporal record, an internal accounting audit, and a later laboratory measurement — from different
documents and different decades, none derived from or dependent on the others.
Reason still valid without the 1958 measurement: **reasons 1 and 2** — both were available in
principle all along, whether or not anyone ever measured the glass.
Did the bookkeeper (Wilbur Teague) ever question the ledger entries himself? **No.** Per r23
explicitly: "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years... the figures were the figures,
and the asking was for other people."

**D3. Did the manager know the tests ran low before the glass was condemned?**
**Yes, that they ran low — but not why.** Selby Vose, manager from 1911, personally signed the
station's yearly account sheets carrying the "station gain" line every year (r23: "he would take a
sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign"), and it was Vose himself who
circulated the 1919 circular specifically because the station's tests ran persistently low
(r19, r23, r24) — a fact publicly complained-of since at least 1902 (r22). So he certainly knew
the tests ran low, at least from 1919 onward and very plausibly well before. What the sources
establish he did *not* know was the true cause: he inferred and acted on the wrong explanation
(deliberate watering by the patrons) rather than investigating the testing equipment itself.

## Section E — Contradictions

1. **Brant weigh book's start year.** r17 (Hazel Brant): "nineteen hundred." r18 (Duncan Keddie):
"the first of January, 1897." Resolution: 1897 — Duncan's account is a deliberately careful
genealogical/documentary record, while Hazel opens her own retelling by disclaiming her own
reliability with dates ("Years I have not"). Method: narrator's own stated unreliability.

2. **Ivy Keddie's relation to Ansel.** r19: "great-niece." r18 (her own father) and r22's corrected
endnote: "granddaughter." Resolution: granddaughter (see Section B1). Method: closest source +
explicit published self-correction.

3. **Hazel/Ivy's cousin relationship.** r21: "first cousin once removed." r17 (Hazel herself) and
r18: "second cousins." Resolution: second cousins. Method: majority + closest sources over
secondhand hearsay.

4. **Rosalie Cudd's relation to Jerome Cudd.** r18: "daughter." r19: "niece." Two sources, direct
conflict, no third source to break the tie. **Cannot be confidently settled from the sources
given**; I lean toward r18 given its overall demonstrated care with genealogy, but flag this as
genuinely unresolved, since the Cudds are not r18's own family.

5. **Glass condemnation year.** r17: 1921. r23: 1922. r18, r19, r20 (dated letter), r22, r24
(dated diary entry): 1923 — five independent sources, including a dated primary document.
Resolution: 1923. Method: majority + document-anchored dating.

6. **Outfit installation year.** r17: 1899. r23: 1898. r20 (invoice) and r24 (contemporaneous
diary): 1897, independently agreeing. Resolution: 1897. Method: two independent primary/
contemporaneous sources over two later memories.

7. **Hearing/dismissal year.** r17: "twenty-six" (1926). r23: "twenty-four" (1924). r19, r21, r22
(precise dates), r24 (contemporaneous diary): 1925. Resolution: 1925. **Note:** r17 and r23 are
each wrong, but in *different* directions — this is not a case of two narrators sharing the same
wrong value, despite both being wrong.

8. **Award amount.** r21: $3,500 (secondhand, from the narrator's father). r19, r22, r23: $3,800,
independently. Resolution: $3,800. Method: majority of independent sources.

9. **Number of families sent the 1919 circular.** r17: nine. r24 (diary, from the wife of the man
who delivered them personally, written contemporaneously): eleven. Resolution: eleven. Method:
closest, contemporaneous primary source over later secondhand memory.

10. **Pipette crate — original count and survivors.** Original: r21 says "five dozen" (60); the
Ammon invoice (r20) documents six dozen (72), bought and itemized. Resolution: 72, by quoted
document over memory. Survivors in 1958: r21 and r22 **independently agree** on "nine" — a genuine
case of **two independent narrators carrying the same value** against r19's differing "eleven."
Given r19's own description of itself as assembled "in a day and a half," and that r21 and r22 draw
on different kinds of sources (personal local knowledge vs. official review), I resolve to nine,
though with less certainty than the document-anchored cases above.

11. **The clearest "two (in fact three) narrators carry the same wrong value" case: the invoice
total.** r17 and r19, and even r20's own prose (as opposed to its own reproduced invoice line
items), all give the outfit's total as "$77.39." But the invoice's own line items, reproduced
verbatim in r20 and summed by simple arithmetic (25.20+31.68+0.90+18.00+2.15), equal **$77.93**.
This is broken by arithmetic on the primary document itself, not by counting narrators: $77.39 is
demonstrably a shared misreading (likely a digit transposition) that propagated across three
independent tellings, including the very firm whose own invoice it was.

12. **Narrator self-contradiction on the page: r20.** The invoice reproduced in r20 totals $77.93
by its own listed figures, but the same author's own prose calls it "seventy-seven dollars and
thirty-nine cents" ($77.39) in the surrounding narration — a direct contradiction within a single
document between its quoted primary source and its own author's gloss on that source.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. Single causal theory.** The entire Ostrey Hollow shortfall was produced by a manufacturing
defect in the glass pipettes (and possibly bottles) installed 1 May 1897: though blown/marked to
deliver the standard 17.6 c.c. test charge, they actually delivered on average only about 16.27
c.c. (roughly 7.6% short). Because the test bottle's neck is calibrated for a full charge, a short
charge produces a proportionally low fat reading on every single use, irrespective of the milk's
true fat content, the cows, the season, or the tester. This explains:
- *Why that size*: the deficiency (≈0.19 points / ≈7.6%) reflects a fixed manufacturing bias in one
particular glass lot, not a biological or seasonal variable — hence it was constant "in every
month of the year" (r17, r22), unlike a feed-related cause, which would fluctuate seasonally.
- *Why it began when it did*: it began the moment the defective glass entered service, 1 May 1897,
with the first formal complaint following once patrons had accumulated enough seasons to notice a
pattern (1902).
- *Why it ended when it did*: it ended the moment the inspector condemned the lot and new glass
replaced it (June/July 1923), after which the recorded average immediately rose to 3.85% — above
even the main creamery's average (r22).
- *Why nothing at the station or the farms had to change*: the defect was entirely in an inert
manufactured object; no change in husbandry, hay, machinery, or tester behavior was needed or
occurred — replacing the glass alone fixed the readings, exactly as observed.

**The three competing explanations:**
1. **Patrons watering their milk** — advanced by manager Selby Vose, formalized in his 1919
circular (r19, r23, r24). Defeated by the state inspector's repeated lactometer tests from 1908
onward, which never once found added water at the station (r22).
2. **The tester stealing cream and falsifying low readings** — urged by an Association officer at
the 1925 hearing (r22). Defeated by the 1911 nine-week natural experiment: the same tester, same
conditions, but readings rose the moment the glass changed and fell back when it was returned
(r22, r18, r24).
3. **Wet bog hay thinning the cream** — advanced by an Association committee in 1908 and an Ashlin
veterinarian in 1926 (r22, r24). Initially entertained by the county report as a partial factor,
then explicitly withdrawn once the report obtained the actual 1911 college feeding-trial figures
(Bulletin No. 214): bog hay depressed the test by "not more than five hundredths of one per cent
in winter, and by nothing... on summer pasture" — far too small to explain a flat, year-round
0.19-point deficiency (r22's own appended correction).

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?** No source says they did, and the clerk who
personally listed everything submitted names exactly four documents — never the glass — and
states plainly that no one in the room "laid the second beside the fourth and looked" (r23). The
condemned crate sat, unexamined, in the loft directly above two of the three hearing sessions
(r21, r23).

**What did the tester believe the cause was?** Ansel Keddie suspected "something in the glass" as
early as 1911, saying so twice at home — but, unwilling to put down what he could not prove, he
recorded only a bare question mark in his own test book and continued using the official glass and
reporting its figures for another fourteen years, up to his dismissal (r18).

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

For over two decades, the Ostrey Hollow milk-testing station on Larrow Valley's Hessel Bottom paid
its farmers less than they were owed, because the glass pipettes bought in 1897 were manufactured
slightly short and under-delivered the sample used for the butterfat test — a defect that produced
a flat, constant deficiency no one could explain. Farmers suspected cheating; the Association
accused the patrons of watering their milk; agronomists blamed wet pasture; officials dismissed the
tester, Ansel Keddie, in 1925, even though a forgotten 1911 experiment with borrowed glass had
already hinted at the truth. Not until 1958, when his granddaughter Ivy Keddie climbed a ladder
into the station's loft and measured the long-condemned crate of glass, was the mechanical cause
proven — decades after Keddie's death. It took a family's notes, an oral history, a clerk's
ledgers, a firm's invoice, a wife's diary, a county report, and a newspaper story together to
reconstruct what none of them alone got entirely right.
