# Answers — Part 2 of 3 (Section A, stories 5–8; Section B)

## Story 5 — The 1925 Arbitration and Ansel Keddie's Dismissal

The Hessel Bottom patrons formally petitioned in January 1925 (r24). A board of arbitration,
chaired by an Ashlin attorney, sat from 17 March to 4 May 1925 (r22), across three sittings — one
at the Cooperative's Ashlin office, two held directly in the station's own weigh room, with
patrons and onlookers standing outside on the platform in the rain (r21, r23, r24). The clerk who
prepared the case, Bertram Kyle, carried four documents before the board: the station's own test
book (in Keddie's hand), the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book (lent for the
purpose), and the state inspector's notebook for the year he had condemned the glass — but, by
his own account, no one in the room ever laid the inspector's notebook beside the weigh book and
looked, and the condemned glass itself, sitting in the loft directly overhead, was never brought
down or examined (r23). The board found no cause proven against anyone but nonetheless awarded
the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800 "without admission" of fault (r19, r22, r23 agree independently;
a lone oral-history figure of $3,500 in r21, secondhand from the narrator's father, is outvoted).
Ansel Keddie was dismissed on 30 April 1925, four days before that award was announced — timed,
his son later wrote, so that "the finding found nothing and the dismissal said everything" (r18,
matched exactly by r24's diary and r22's report). He never attended another Association meeting.

*Documents: r18, r19, r21, r22, r23, r24.*

## Story 6 — The Condemned Glass in the Loft (1923–1958)

On 12 June 1923 the state's circuit dairy inspector, Merle Strawn, made a routine visit to Ostrey
Hollow and ran its milk-test pipettes against his own calibrated burette; they would not deliver
the volume marked on their stems, and he condemned the whole lot on the spot, directing the
manager to order replacements. Five independent sources (r18, r19, r20's own dated letter book,
r22, and r24's diary) fix this at 1923, against lone, later, differing memories of 1921 (r17) and
1922 (r23). New glass arrived and went into use that July (r23, r24); from 1924 the station's
recorded average jumped to 3.85%, higher even than the main creamery's own average (r22). The old,
condemned crate — originally six dozen pipettes bought in 1897 — was simply carried up into the
loft above the station's intake room and forgotten under a tarpaulin for the next thirty-five
years, disturbed only by boys retrieving wire or a hay fork; only nine of the original seventy-two
pipettes survived intact by 1958 (r21, r22 agree independently on "nine"). No one — not the
inspector who condemned it, not the clerk who filed the correspondence, not the 1925 arbitration
board sitting in the room directly beneath it two years later — ever connected the discarded glass
to the decades of patron complaints it had caused.

*Documents: r18, r19, r20, r21, r22, r23, r24.*

## Story 7 — Ivy Keddie's Discovery and the 1958 Vindication

Ivy Keddie, born 1931, was Duncan Keddie's daughter and Ansel Keddie's granddaughter (established
by r18 and confirmed by r22's own published correction, against r19's erroneous "great-niece"; see
Section B1). A graduate student in dairy husbandry at the State Agricultural College at Farland,
she climbed the station loft's ladder in February 1958 and found the condemned crate still under
its tarpaulin (r18, r21, r22). She carried it east to Farland and, on 9 March 1958, measured the
nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette: each was blown to deliver 17.6 c.c., the
standard test charge, but averaged only about 16.27 c.c. — roughly 7.6% short, in the same
proportion pipette after pipette (r19, r22). Working with Professor Leland Vaught, she also drew
on Merle Strawn's old circuit notebooks (preserved at the college library since 1947, r18) and on
records supplied by the rival cheese company (r18, r19). The findings were published in October
1958 as College Bulletin No. 471, crediting the entire historic shortfall to the defective glass
and clearing Ansel Keddie's name — twenty-four years after his 1934 death and thirty-three years
after his 1925 dismissal (r19). The Ashlin Chronicle ran the story on its front page (r19), and the
county agricultural agent independently reviewed and largely confirmed the bulletin's findings the
following year, publicly correcting two of his own earlier errors in the process (r22).

*Documents: r18, r19, r21, r22, r24.*

## Story 8 — The Paper Trail: Where the Records Went (1896–1970)

Because so much of this history survives only in scattered private and institutional custody,
Duncan Keddie — Ansel's son, a retired schoolteacher — set down in 1968 (with a 1970 addendum) a
careful inventory of where each relevant record had gone, specifically to forestall confusion
after his own death (r18). The station's own test book, kept by Ansel from 1896 until his 1925
dismissal, was impounded by the arbitration board in March 1925, sat in the Cooperative's Ashlin
office until 1959, then passed to the Ordell County Historical Rooms. The Brant family's private
weigh book, begun by Emmet Brant (start date disputed — r18 says 1 January 1897, r17 says 1900;
see Section E), passed to his son Roy, was lent — not given — to Ivy Keddie in 1958 for her
research, returned, and passed at Roy's 1962 death to his daughter Hazel Brant, who donated it to
the Historical Rooms in 1963. Inspector Strawn's circuit notebooks went to the state dairy office
in 1936 and then, in 1947, to the Farland college library, where Ivy found the relevant volume
eleven years later. The rival cheese company's test book stayed in Vessey County throughout,
though its figures were copied out for Ivy in 1958 and the book itself transferred to the Vessey
County Historical Society in 1970. The condemned glass crate was deposited at the Ordell County
Historical Rooms alongside the test book in 1959. By 1970 the five key record sets lay scattered
across three separate buildings, "and are none of them ours" (r18).

*Documents: r18 (primary); corroborated in part by r17, r19.*

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**
Ivy was Ansel Keddie's **granddaughter** — daughter of Duncan Keddie, Ansel's son (r18). Duncan's
own account is explicit and emphatic: "Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's
granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise."
The county report (r22) initially printed "great-niece" (matching the newspaper's error, r19) but
appended a correction: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter
and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." I resolve this in favor of "granddaughter"
on two grounds: it comes from the closest possible source (her own father), and it is the version
a second, initially-mistaken source explicitly investigated and corrected. The "great-niece" claim
survives only in the hastily-assembled newspaper piece (r19, self-described as "put together in a
day and a half from a college press release and two telephone calls") and in the report that later
retracted it.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**
They were **second cousins** — children of first cousins. Emmet Brant and Orra Brant (who married
Ansel Keddie) were brother and sister; their children, Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie, were therefore
first cousins; Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are one generation further, hence
second cousins (r18, explicit). Hazel confirms this herself in her own retelling: "Ivy Keddie and I
are second cousins... It is not complicated; it is only that nobody wants to count" (r17). Against
this, a single secondhand oral-history source (r21, Cleve Oram, speaking from what "my mother had
from her mother") calls them "first cousin once removed" — outvoted by two independent, closer
sources, including the two women themselves. The sources leave open the exact birth order and
dates within the Roy/Duncan generation, but the generational structure itself is not in doubt.

**B3. Selby Vose's father, and its significance.**
Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**, who organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery
Association and served as its first president (r18, explicit; independently confirmed by r23: "the
son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president"). Both sources
note, and reject, a rival claim that Selby was Gideon's *nephew*. This matters because it was
Selby Vose, as manager from 1911, who signed and circulated the 1919 water-accusation letter
against the Ostrey Hollow patrons (r19, r23, r24) and who, per the clerk who sat outside his door
for twenty-five years, routinely signed the station's yearly accounts without reading the detail
above the bottom line (r23). Being the founder's son gave him institutional standing in the valley
that made his (mistaken) explanation for the shortfall — deliberate watering by the patrons — much
harder for the patrons to challenge than it otherwise would have been.

**B4. The Vose family and the Keddie family.**
The sources establish **no family relationship** between the Voses and the Keddies. Duncan
Keddie states this flatly and at length: "There was no connection whatever between us and the
Voses... my mother would have told me... and she never once put a Vose in it" (r18), attributing
the persistent rumor to confusion with an unrelated Ashlin family of the same surname. Against
this stands only Cleve Oram's admittedly uncertain hearsay: "The Keddies and the Voses were
cousins of some sort... I could not draw you the line on paper" (r21). I resolve this in favor of
Duncan's explicit, sourced denial over Oram's self-acknowledged uncertainty.
