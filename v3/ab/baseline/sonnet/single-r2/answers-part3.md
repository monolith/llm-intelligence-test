# Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math

**C1.**

*(a) Proportion.* The pipettes were blown to a mark of 17.6 c.c. but delivered an average of
16.27 c.c. (r11, r19). Read as a raw volume ratio: 16.27 / 17.6 = 0.9244, i.e. a 7.56% shortfall.
But two *independent, empirical* checks give a smaller, more consistent figure of about 5%: (i)
the Association's own ledger recorded, every year, a "station gain" of fat actually received at
Larrow Green over fat credited to patrons of "five and a quarter per cent" (r12, quoting the
ledger; r23, the clerk who copied the same line for years) — a figure derived from real weighed
fat, not from the pipette calibration at all; and (ii) the point-gap arithmetic below. I resolve
the true proportional shortfall as **≈5%** (ledger figure 5.25%), treating the raw 7.56%
volume-ratio as an upper-bound estimate that overstates the effect actually observed at the
creamery, and treating "one part in twenty" in the bulletin's own quoted conclusion (r11) as
consistent with this ledger figure, not with the higher one computed elsewhere in the same
document's introduction (see Section E).

*(b) True average and point shortfall.* The recorded station average was 3.61 (r22). The point
shortfall is independently given as "nineteen hundredths" (0.19) by two unconnected sources —
Falke's report, explicitly citing the bulletin's own figure (r22), and Hazel Brant's family
account of the weigh-book record (r17). True average = 3.61 + 0.19 = **3.80**. This is
cross-validated three ways: Larrow Green creamery's own average over the same years was "three
seventy-eight" (r22); Alder Corners' independent tests on the same farmers' summer milk ran
"3.78–3.82" (r10); and 0.19 / 3.80 = 5.00% exactly — matching the "one part in twenty" language
and the ledger's 5.25% independently. I therefore reject Falke's own subtraction elsewhere in the
same report (3.78 − 3.61 = 0.17) as a minor arithmetic slip, in favor of the explicitly stated
and doubly-attested 0.19.

*(c) Butterfat not credited.* **76,000 lb**, per the bulletin's own quoted conclusion (r11). I
reject "118,000 lb," which appears only in Vaught's own introductory paragraph in the same
document and contradicts that document's own final quoted conclusion (self-contradiction, see
Section E). I reject "67,000 lb" (r13, r21): Frick's 1946 figure predates the 1958 discovery and
likely reflects an older, cruder pre-discovery reckoning under a different theory; Oram's 1977
figure is secondhand oral testimony and looks like a transposed-digit error of 76,000 (67 vs. 76),
a pattern that recurs elsewhere in these sources (see Section E). Arithmetic check, working from
independent inputs: 40,000,000 lb total milk (r23) × 3.61% recorded average = 1,444,000 lb
credited; 1,444,000 × 5.25% ledger gain rate ≈ 75,810 lb — matching 76,000 lb closely, from
figures that do not depend on the pipette calibration at all. This convergence is the strongest
evidence in the whole record for the 76,000 lb figure.

*(d) Money value and fraction received.* $19,600 (r12, r19 — consistent, though likely both
drawn from the same bulletin/press release, so not fully independent). Patrons actually received
the 1925 arbitration award of $3,800 (see C-general and Section A Story 6). Fraction received =
3,800 / 19,600 ≈ **19%**, roughly one-fifth of what was owed.

**C2. Largest patron (Emmet Brant), 1910.**

*(a) Credited.* Roy/Hazel Brant's transcription of the weigh-book leaf: 200,000 lb of milk
delivered, "paid on a test of three forty-two" (r17). Credited butterfat = 200,000 × 0.0342 =
**6,840 lb**.

*(b) Owed but not credited.* Using the proportional shortfall established in C1 (recorded ≈ 0.95
× true), true test = 3.42 / 0.95 = 3.60. True butterfat = 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb. Owed and
not credited = 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb**.

*(c) Money value that year.* No source gives a specific 1897–1910-era butterfat price; the only
prices given are averages "over the whole stretch" of about 25¢/lb (r23) and a later-1920s price
of 27¢/lb (r16, explicitly "through these years," i.e. the 1920s, not 1910). Using the more
period-appropriate 25¢/lb average as an approximation: 360 × $0.25 ≈ **$90**. This figure is an
approximation, not a verified 1910 price, and I flag it as such.

**C3. Full delivery years, totals.**

*(a) Credited to patrons:* 40,000,000 lb total milk (r23) × 3.61% recorded average = **1,444,000
lb**.

*(b) Actually received at the creamery:* 1,444,000 × 1.0525 (ledger gain rate) ≈ **1,519,810 lb**.

*(c) Percentage:* This depends on what you take the percentage of. As a percentage of what
patrons were *credited*, the gain is 75,810 / 1,444,000 ≈ **5.25%** — matching the ledger's own
stated figure exactly, which is the way the Association's own bookkeeper phrased it (r23). As a
percentage of what was actually *received* at the creamery, the same shortfall is 75,810 /
1,519,810 ≈ **4.99%**, matching the bulletin's "one part in twenty" (r11) almost exactly. Both are
correct arithmetic; they differ only in denominator, and the sources themselves use both framings
without flagging the difference — worth noting as a source of apparent (but not real)
inconsistency.

**C4. The invoice.**

*(a) Total.* **$77.93**, established by summing the invoice's own reproduced line items (r20):
$25.20 + $31.68 + $0.90 + $18.00 + $2.15 = $77.93. This overrides the prose figure "seventy-seven
dollars and thirty-nine cents," which appears in *two* sources (r19 and r20's own narration) but
is contradicted by arithmetic on the primary reproduced document — a case where arithmetic
outranks two agreeing narrators (see Section E).

*(b) Pipettes and shortfall.* Six dozen (72) pipettes (r20's reproduced invoice; confirmed by
r19's prose and by the chronology of use — see Section E on the "five dozen" error in r11), each
marked 17.6 c.c. and delivering an average of 16.27 c.c. — short by **1.33 c.c.** per pipette.

**C5.**

*(a) Duration of faulty glass vs. years in the money calculation.* The faulty glass was in
physical use for about 26 years and 6 weeks: from 1 May 1897 (r24, r20, r19) until condemned on
12 June 1923 (r22, established by chronological logic in Section A Story 5). But the money
calculation (Kyle's 40,000,000 lb ÷ 1,600,000 lb/year = 25 years, r23) covers only **full
calendar years** of faulty-glass operation: 1898 through 1922 inclusive is exactly 25 full years,
excluding the partial stub of 1897 (about 8 months) and the partial stub of 1923 (about 5.5
months) at either end. That is why the two numbers differ: one counts elapsed calendar time
across the whole faulty period, the other counts only complete delivery years, as an accountant
naturally would when computing per-year averages.

*(b) First season's milk.* Approximately **1,100,000 lb**, per clerk Bertram Kyle, who
explicitly flags this figure as an unverified oral estimate rather than a ledger entry: "a good
eleven hundred thousand pounds, which is what I understood at the time from men who were there.
It is not in any book I ever ruled" (r23).

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 experiment.**
It ran from 8 May 1911 (r24, precisely dated by Orra's contemporaneous diary) to 10 July 1911
(r22), nine weeks. The variable was the measuring instrument (the station's own broken glass
replaced temporarily by a borrowed measure); held constant were the herds, the pasture (summer
flush throughout), the tester, and the procedure. The result: recorded tests rose during the nine
weeks and fell back the day the station's own glass resumed (r22). What this establishes, that a
calculation alone cannot: it proves the instrument itself — not the cows, the season, the hay, or
the tester's honesty — was the operative variable, and it proves this was true as early as 1911,
decades before anyone could measure the actual pipettes. A 1958 calibration alone could show the
crated glass was defective *in 1958*; it could not, by itself, prove the same defect operated
throughout the preceding decades. The 1911 episode supplies that missing temporal link. What it
does *not* establish: the precise physical mechanism (a short pipette charge specifically, as
opposed to some other difference in the borrowed apparatus), which was only confirmed by direct
measurement in 1958; nor does it establish the size or duration of the error outside those nine
weeks.

**D2. Independent reasons Keddie was not stealing cream.**
(1) The 1911 experiment (D1): the same tester, under no different supervision, produced higher
readings the moment the instrument changed — inconsistent with a story where the tester's own
dishonesty is the cause. (2) The ledger's own annual "station gain" entries (r12, r23) show that
Larrow Green consistently received *more* fat in the Hollow's cream than patrons were credited
for — the physical opposite of what skimming would produce, since a thief removes cream before
it is weighed, which would show a deficit at the creamery, not a surplus. (3) The Alder Corners
cheese factory's fully independent tests on the same Hessel Bottom farmers' milk, hauled over the
ridge by a different route to a different business entirely, consistently read normal
(3.78–3.82, r10) — proving the milk itself, from the same herds Keddie tested, was never short of
fat when tested by anyone else. These three are independent because they rest on different
mechanisms and different record-keepers: an instrument-swap event recorded by the tester himself
and later by an official report and a diary; the Association's own internal bookkeeping,
recorded by its own bookkeeper; and a wholly external business's private records. Reasons (1) and
(3) — and arguably (2) — would all still hold even if the 1958 glassware had never been measured,
since none of them depends on that measurement. As for whether the bookkeeper (Teague) ever
questioned the ledger entries that refute his own accusation: no. Clerk Kyle states plainly that
Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years" and that the "station gain, as usual" line
crossed no one's mind to wonder at, including Kyle's own (r23) — despite Teague himself being
among those who, per the earlier record, urged the skimming theory at the 1925 hearing.

**D3. Did the manager know?**
Selby Vose signed the station-gain-bearing ledger sheets every year from taking office in 1911
(r23). But clerk Kyle, who watched him do it "a thousand times," states Vose "read the totals and
he never read the lines above them... took a sheet, turned it, put his thumb on the bottom
figure, and signed" (r23). On the direct evidence of the man who sat outside his door for
twenty-five years, Vose most likely did *not* consciously know the specific "station gain"
figure, despite formally signing documents that recorded it. He plainly did believe *something*
was wrong with the Hollow's tests — enough to circulate the 1919 water-warning circular — but the
sources indicate he never connected that belief to the ledger evidence sitting, unread, under his
own signature, which if read would have pointed away from the patrons entirely.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. The causal theory.** For about 26 years (1 May 1897 – 12 June 1923), the Ostrey Hollow
station tested milk with pipettes that were manufactured to deliver a short charge (an average of
16.27 c.c. against a mark of 17.6 c.c., per Ivy Keddie's 1958 measurement, r11/r19). Because the
Babcock-type test reads the separated fat column against a bottle-neck scale calibrated for a
*full* charge, every short charge produced a proportionally low, but perfectly repeatable, fat
reading — regardless of the cow, the season, the hay, or the tester's honesty. This explains the
size of the error (a fixed ~5% proportional shortfall, corroborated three independent ways in
Section C1); why it began when it did (the defective outfit went into service 1 May 1897,
replacing accurate borrowed glass); why it ended when it did (the state inspector condemned the
lot on 12 June 1923 and new glass went into use within weeks, after which the recorded average
immediately rose to about 3.85 and stayed there, r22); and why nothing at the station or on the
farms needed to change at either transition, since the defect lived entirely in the glass, not in
any person, animal, or practice.

Three competing theories were offered, and each is defeated by specific evidence:
- **Watered milk** (Selby Vose, 1919 circular, r22/r23) — defeated by the state inspector's
  lactometer tests at every circuit visit from 1908 on, which never once found added water (r22).
- **Cream theft by the tester** (urged at the 1925 hearing, per r13) — defeated by the 1911
  natural experiment (D1), by the ledger's own station-gain surplus showing *more*, not less, fat
  reaching the creamery than was credited (r12, r23), and by the Alder Corners cheese factory's
  independent normal test results on the same herds (r10).
- **Wet, mineral-poor Hessel Bottom hay** (the 1908 Grigg committee, r15; veterinarian Kepp, r16)
  — defeated by Bulletin No. 214's actual verbatim finding of at most a 0.05-point winter-only
  depression, far too small and too seasonal to explain a flat, year-round 0.19-point gap (r22),
  and by the Alder Corners tests on the very same wet-bottom herds showing normal results (r10).

Subsidiary questions: The 1925 board of arbitration never examined the condemned glassware — both
the board's chairman (r13) and the clerk who prepared its exhibits (r23) independently list the
same four documents entered as evidence, and the crate is absent from both lists, despite sitting
in the loft directly above two of the hearing's own sessions (r21). The tester himself believed,
privately, from as early as the summer of 1911, that the cause was "something in the glass" (r18)
— but never recorded this belief formally because he "would not put down what he could not
prove" (r18), and he died in 1934 without ever asserting it publicly.

## Section G — Summary

For roughly 26 years (1897–1923), the Ostrey Hollow skimming station paid Larrow Valley dairy
farmers using milk-test pipettes manufactured slightly undersized, producing a consistent,
proportional under-reading of about 5% in every test, for every patron, every season — invisible
because it was internally self-consistent and no one thought to check the glass. Contemporaries
blamed watered milk, a dishonest tester (Ansel Keddie, dismissed in 1925), or the valley's wet
hay; each theory was wrong, and the evidence disproving each — lactometer checks, ledger
surpluses, an independent cheese factory's results — existed all along but scattered across
family diaries, business ledgers, and inspector's notebooks nobody assembled in one place. Only
in 1958, when Keddie's granddaughter Ivy found the discarded, condemned pipettes in a station
loft and measured them, did the pieces converge into Bulletin No. 471, clearing his name
twenty-four years after his death.
