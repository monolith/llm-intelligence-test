# Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

*Note on method: r01–r08 are known to me only through my own earlier notes taken while reading
them (I cannot re-open them), so citations to those eight are necessarily thinner and more
general than citations to r09–r24, which I can quote closely. Where a fact rests on a document I
can no longer quote precisely, I say so.*

## Story 1 — The Founding of the Association and the Station's Fateful Glass (1889–1897)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was organized in 1889 by farmers meeting at
Larrow Green (r22), who built their main creamery there in 1891 (r22) and adopted by-laws
(article seven, per r22) under which every patron was paid by butterfat test, not by milk
weight — the provision that made the coming discrepancy a matter of money rather than pride.
Duncan Keddie's family notes (r18) supply the marriage that would entangle tester and patron:
Ansel Keddie (b. 1861) married Orra Brant (b. 1868) in 1889, the same year as the Association's
founding; Orra's brother Emmet Brant farmed the largest place on Hessel Bottom and would hold the
greatest stake in the station's accuracy.

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles
up-valley, opening it on 4 May 1896 (r22) with Ansel Keddie as tester and separator man. For its
first season the station had no glassware of its own: buttermaker Hobart Sill personally lent
Larrow Green's own test glass to open it (r14), and when that glass was later returned, Sill
noted one piece had been chipped — a detail that may or may not connect to two chipped pipettes
later catalogued among the station's own crate (cannot be determined from the sources).

The station's permanent outfit was ordered from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet, per an invoice dated 14
April 1897 (reproduced in facsimile in r20): six dozen milk-test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. at 35
cents apiece ($25.20), twelve dozen test bottles ($31.68), one acid measure ($0.90), one
four-bottle hand tester ($18.00), and freight ($2.15) — summing, by arithmetic, to $77.93 (the
invoice's own prose elsewhere gives the mistaken figure "$77.39"; see Section E). This outfit
went into use on 1 May 1897, confirmed independently by Orra Keddie's diary entry of that exact
date (r24) and by Ammon's own firm history (r20). Nothing about the transaction seemed
remarkable; the pipettes that would cost the Hollow's patrons a quarter-century of pay had simply
been delivered like any other order.

## Story 2 — The Long Complaint and the 1908 Wet-Hay Committee

Almost from the station's early years, patrons at Ostrey Hollow noticed their milk testing lower
than milk delivered anywhere else in the valley. Orin Falke's 1959 county report (r22) dates the
first recorded complaint to 1902 and states it recurred in every decade thereafter. By January
1908 the grumbling had reached the Association's board, which appointed a three-man committee
chaired by farmer Nyle Grigg (r15) to investigate.

The committee visited the six Hessel Bottom farms and, per its own printed 1908 report (reprinted
by Grigg himself in r15), states it sampled the herds "through January, February and March" —
not the full year. It found the herds sound, the milk whole, and the scales honest, and concluded
instead that the bottom's wet, sour, sedge-hay pasture thinned the cream, "without reproach" to
the patrons.

Eleven years later, in a 1919 covering letter reprinting the same report (r15), Grigg claimed the
committee had sampled "the whole of 1908... every month of that year" — directly contradicting
his own reprinted report on the same page — and cited the State College's Bulletin No. 214 as
confirming a "two tenths" (0.2-point) winter depression from wet hay.

That same "two tenths" figure and the same wet-hay theory were advanced again in 1926 by
veterinarian Roland Kepp (r16), who dismissed the 1911 borrowed-measure episode (Story 3) as an
insignificant "six weeks" summer fluke and cited a field gap of "twenty-one hundredths" — a
number that echoes manager Selby Vose's own claim more than any independent measurement of
Kepp's.

Both the "two tenths" figure and the "six weeks" mischaracterization would prove to be errors,
exposed when county agent Falke actually obtained and quoted Bulletin No. 214 verbatim in 1959
(r22): the trial found a depression of at most "five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and...
nothing... on summer pasture" — far too small to explain the Hollow's year-round 0.19-point gap.

## Story 3 — The Nine Weeks of Borrowed Glass, 1911

On 8 May 1911 the Ostrey Hollow station's own measuring glass broke. Orra Keddie's diary records
the day itself ("A. home cross about a broken measure," r24); Orin Falke's 1959 report (r22)
supplies the resolution: a measure borrowed from elsewhere was used for nine weeks, until 10 July
1911, after which the station's own glass resumed.

During those nine weeks — same tester, same herds, same pasture (the flush of early summer), same
oversight — the station's recorded tests rose markedly; the day the station's own glass returned,
the figures fell back to their old level (r22). Ansel Keddie noted the anomaly in his own book
and set only a question mark against it (r22); his son Duncan later recorded that his father came
home that summer saying it was "something in the glass," but "never dared put it in the book"
without proof (r18) — Ansel privately intuited the true mechanism forty-seven years before it was
confirmed.

This episode is a natural experiment: it isolates the measuring instrument as the variable while
holding every other factor constant, and it is the one piece of evidence in the whole record that
neither the watered-milk theory nor the wet-hay theory can explain away — both later advocates
(Grigg, r15; Kepp, r16) tried to neutralize it by mischaracterizing its length as "six weeks," a
shared error contradicted by the precise dating in both Falke's report and Orra's contemporaneous
diary. The 1911 episode was not recognized as decisive until Ivy Keddie's 1958 discovery gave it
a mechanism to confirm.

## Story 4 — The Manager's Circular and the Rift in the Family (1919)

By 1919 the Association's manager, Selby Vose — son of the Association's founding president
Gideon Vose (r18, r23) — had concluded, without evidence beyond the persistent low tests, that
Ostrey Hollow patrons must be watering their milk. He circulated a written warning to that effect
(r22, r23), which clerk Bertram Kyle recalls typing himself, and thought "a hard thing to send to
men who were bringing their milk in every morning of their lives" (r23).

Sources disagree on the circular's reach: Orra Keddie's diary, written the same month, says Ansel
personally delivered it to eleven families rather than let it go by mail or sit on the station
platform (r24); Hazel Brant's 1981 recollection instead gives nine families, matching the six
Hessel Bottom farms, "a farm can hold more than one family" (r17). The contemporaneous diary
entry is the stronger source (see Section E).

The circular's most lasting effect was personal, not statistical: Ansel's insistence on
delivering it himself, standing at each man's door while he read it, provoked a public breach
with his own brother-in-law. Two days after the circular went out, Emmet Brant came to the
Keddie yard, refused to enter the house, and demanded to know why Ansel had carried a paper he
did not believe; Ansel answered only that he would not have another man carry it. Emmet did not
enter that house again for a decade (r24).

The watered-milk theory itself never had supporting evidence: the state dairy inspector tested
with a lactometer at every visit from 1908 onward and never once found added water at the station
(r22) — meaning Vose's accusation, and the family rupture it caused, rested on nothing that any
contemporary check ever substantiated.
