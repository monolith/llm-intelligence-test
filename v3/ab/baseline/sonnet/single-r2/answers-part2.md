# Answers — Part 2: Section A, Stories 5–8; Section B

## Story 5 — The Condemned Glass, 1923

In the summer of 1923 the state's dairy inspector for the Ordell circuit, Merle Strawn — a man
"kin to nobody in the valley" (r18) — made his ordinary rounds and tested the Ostrey Hollow
station's milk pipettes against a calibrated burette. He found they would not deliver their
marked charge and condemned the whole lot on the spot, directing the manager to order new glass.

The exact date is contested: Falke's 1959 report (r22), the most carefully documented account,
gives 12 June 1923; Duncan Keddie's notes (r18) give 1921; clerk Bertram Kyle's memoir (r23)
gives 1922. The correspondence settles it: Ammon & Sons' own letter book (reproduced in r20)
shows the Association's replacement order dated 28 June 1923 and Ammon's reply dated 2 July
1923 — both necessarily after the condemnation, which rules out 1921 or 1922, and also rules out
a "12 July" date sometimes given elsewhere, since an order already sent on 28 June cannot follow
a condemnation in July. Twelve June 1923 is the only date consistent with the surrounding
correspondence.

New glass went into use within the month (r20, r22), and the station's recorded average test rose
from about 3.61 to about 3.85 from 1924 onward (r22) — a rise no one at the time connected to the
change of instrument. Ammon's reply to the Association was brief and unhelpful: "Our books of the
nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name" (r20) — an answer the
firm's own later historian, Junia Ammon, called "a thin thing to send to a customer of
twenty-six years' standing" (r20), speculating that the original 1897 lot came from a mold that
had been reground and ran consistently, invisibly short.

The condemned pipettes themselves were carried up into the loft over the station's intake room
and forgotten there for thirty-five years (r18, r21).

## Story 6 — The 1925 Arbitration and Ansel Keddie's Dismissal

The patrons of Ostrey Hollow petitioned the Association in January 1925 (r22), and a board of
arbitration sat at Ashlin from 17 March to 4 May 1925 under an attorney chairman (r22) whom
Alonzo Frick's own recollections (r13) identify as himself — dates corroborated independently by
Orra Keddie's diary, which records Ansel leaving for the first sitting on 17 March and two later
sittings held at the station itself (r24), matching Cleve Oram's recollection of "three
sittings... two of them... at the station itself" (r21).

Three theories were argued before the board (r13): watered milk, deliberate skimming by Keddie,
and wet-hay depression. Only four exhibits were entered — the station's own test book, the
station's receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book (lent for the purpose), and the state
inspector's circuit notebook for 1923 — a list confirmed item-for-item by both the board's
chairman (r13) and the clerk who prepared it (r23). Neither list includes the crate of condemned
pipettes, which sat, unexamined, in the loft directly over two of the hearing's own sessions (r21,
r23).

The board found no cause established, awarded the patrons $3,800 "without admission" (r19, r22,
r23 agree; Oram's secondhand $3,500 in r21 is the outlier), and dismissed Ansel Keddie on 30
April 1925 — four days before the award (r18, r22, corroborated by Orra's diary entry of that
exact date, r24). After twenty-nine seasons (r22), Keddie came home at his ordinary hour, hung up
his apron, and asked what there was for supper (r24).

Frick's own 1946 recollections give an average test of 3.16 and a patron loss of 67,000 lb — both
figures at odds with the better-supported figures of roughly 3.61 and 76,000 lb (Section C),
suggesting Frick's memory, or the board's own working numbers, were already imprecise.

## Story 7 — Keddie's Last Years and Death, 1925–1934

Dismissed and unexonerated, Ansel Keddie spent his last nine years as a carpenter, beginning work
on the family's long-neglected cow shed the month after his dismissal and, per Orra's diary,
whistling at it "which he never did when he was paid" (r24). He mended stairs, gates, and doors
across the hollow into his late sixties — "better at it than he ever was at the other" (r24) —
and hung a barn door for the Oram family that "swung true for thirty years" (r21).

He never spoke publicly of the accusation again. He told Orra once, and only once, that "a man
cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name" (r24). He died at home in February 1934, nine
years after his dismissal (r18, r24); his funeral was "well attended and nobody said much" (r21),
and men who had not spoken to him in nine years praised his carpentry but never mentioned the
other matter (r24).

Orra outlived him by fourteen years, dying in 1948 (r18, r24) — a decade before her husband's
name was cleared. Their son Duncan, writing in 1968, preserved the single fact that made the
eventual vindication possible: his father's 1911 private suspicion that the fault lay "in the
glass" (r18). Their granddaughter Hazel Brant, reflecting in 1981, said she was glad for her
grandfather Emmet Brant's sake, and gladder still for Ansel Keddie's — "who did not live to see
it" (r17) — capturing the cost of the twenty-four years between Keddie's death and his
vindication.

## Story 8 — Ivy Keddie's Discovery and Bulletin No. 471 (1958–1959 and after)

In February 1958, Ansel Keddie's granddaughter Ivy — a graduate student at Farland working on a
project about the station — climbed a ladder into the loft over the old intake room and found the
condemned crate, still under its tarpaulin since 1923 (r18, r19, r21). She carried it east and,
on 9 March 1958, measured the nine surviving pipettes (the best-supported count — see Section E)
against a calibrated burette (r22).

Every pipette, blown to a mark of 17.6 c.c., delivered an average of only 16.27 c.c. (r11, r19) —
a short charge that, fed into a test bottle whose graduated neck assumed a full charge, produced a
proportionally low but perfectly internally consistent fat reading every time. Working with
Professor Leland Vaught, Ivy Keddie published the finding as Bulletin No. 471 in October 1958
(r11): no milk had been watered and no cream stolen — the fault was "a piece of glass" (r19).

The finding drew immediate local coverage (the Ashlin Chronicle's front page, r19) and, the
following year, a formal review by county agent Orin Falke (r22), who initially credited a
partial wet-hay contribution but retracted the concession after obtaining and reading Bulletin
No. 214's actual text, and separately corrected the press's error calling Ivy Ansel's
"great-niece" rather than his granddaughter.

In the years after, participants and descendants reflected on the case from their own vantage:
Duncan Keddie (r18) traced the family's genealogy and the scattered records' provenance; Cleve
Oram (r21), Hazel Brant (r17), and Bertram Kyle (r23) each gave retrospective accounts, none
entirely error-free, but together supplying the corroborating detail — the ledger's own "station
gain" entries, Emmet Brant's matching private weigh book, the Alder Corners cheese factory's
independent tests — that turned Ivy Keddie's single measurement into a fully reconstructed
history.

# Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**
Granddaughter. Duncan Keddie — Ansel's own son and Ivy's own father — states plainly "my daughter
Ivy... which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already
twice seen it printed otherwise" (r18). Orin Falke's 1959 report first wrote "great-niece" (r22,
matching the Ashlin Chronicle's identical error, r19) but appended a correction: "I am informed,
and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on
page two is mine" (r22). A parent's direct statement, plus a self-correcting official source,
outranks two press mentions that likely share one origin.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**
Second cousins. Duncan (Ansel and Orra's son) and Roy Brant (Emmet's son) are first cousins,
being the children of siblings Orra and Emmet (r18). Ivy is Duncan's daughter and Hazel is Roy's
daughter, so they are the children of first cousins — second cousins, not "cousins once removed"
and not aunt/niece (r17, r18, both working the generations through explicitly and agreeing). Cleve
Oram's "first cousin once removed" (r21) is unreasoned village hearsay and is rejected. What the
sources leave open: whether Orra and Emmet were full or half siblings (r09 says "full sister";
Hazel Brant says "half sister... by his father's second wife," r17) — this does not change the
cousin arithmetic, but it is not resolved by anything in the record.

**B3. Selby Vose's father.**
Gideon Vose, who organized the Association and was its first president and wrote its by-laws
(r18, r23) — not his uncle, a rumor both sources explicitly name and reject. This matters because
Selby inherited both an institutional stake in the payment-by-test system his father had built and,
per clerk Kyle's direct observation, a managerial habit of signing off on totals without reading
the lines above them (r23) — a style of institutional trust that let the low tests persist
unquestioned under his own signature for over a decade before the true cause was found.

**B4. The Vose and Keddie families.**
No genuine kinship. Duncan Keddie devotes a section of his notes to refuting the rumor directly:
his mother Orra, who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection" in the
valley, "never once put a Vose in it" (r18). Cleve Oram repeats the rumor but immediately admits
he "could not draw you the line on paper" (r21) — an unverifiable claim by his own account. The
detailed, reasoned family-insider source outranks the admittedly unsubstantiated one.
