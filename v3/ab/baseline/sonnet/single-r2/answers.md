# Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

*Note on method: r01–r08 are known to me only through my own earlier notes taken while reading
them (I cannot re-open them), so citations to those eight are necessarily thinner and more
general than citations to r09–r24, which I can quote closely. Where a fact rests on a document I
can no longer quote precisely, I say so.*

## Story 1 — The Founding of the Association and the Station's Fateful Glass (1889–1897)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was organized in 1889 by farmers meeting at
Larrow Green (r22), who built their main creamery there in 1891 (r22) and adopted by-laws
(article seven, per r22) under which every patron was paid by butterfat test, not by milk
weight — the provision that made the coming discrepancy a matter of money rather than pride.
Duncan Keddie's family notes (r18) supply the marriage that would entangle tester and patron:
Ansel Keddie (b. 1861) married Orra Brant (b. 1868) in 1889, the same year as the Association's
founding; Orra's brother Emmet Brant farmed the largest place on Hessel Bottom and would hold the
greatest stake in the station's accuracy.

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles
up-valley, opening it on 4 May 1896 (r22) with Ansel Keddie as tester and separator man. For its
first season the station had no glassware of its own: buttermaker Hobart Sill personally lent
Larrow Green's own test glass to open it (r14), and when that glass was later returned, Sill
noted one piece had been chipped — a detail that may or may not connect to two chipped pipettes
later catalogued among the station's own crate (cannot be determined from the sources).

The station's permanent outfit was ordered from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet, per an invoice dated 14
April 1897 (reproduced in facsimile in r20): six dozen milk-test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. at 35
cents apiece ($25.20), twelve dozen test bottles ($31.68), one acid measure ($0.90), one
four-bottle hand tester ($18.00), and freight ($2.15) — summing, by arithmetic, to $77.93 (the
invoice's own prose elsewhere gives the mistaken figure "$77.39"; see Section E). This outfit
went into use on 1 May 1897, confirmed independently by Orra Keddie's diary entry of that exact
date (r24) and by Ammon's own firm history (r20). Nothing about the transaction seemed
remarkable; the pipettes that would cost the Hollow's patrons a quarter-century of pay had simply
been delivered like any other order.

## Story 2 — The Long Complaint and the 1908 Wet-Hay Committee

Almost from the station's early years, patrons at Ostrey Hollow noticed their milk testing lower
than milk delivered anywhere else in the valley. Orin Falke's 1959 county report (r22) dates the
first recorded complaint to 1902 and states it recurred in every decade thereafter. By January
1908 the grumbling had reached the Association's board, which appointed a three-man committee
chaired by farmer Nyle Grigg (r15) to investigate.

The committee visited the six Hessel Bottom farms and, per its own printed 1908 report (reprinted
by Grigg himself in r15), states it sampled the herds "through January, February and March" —
not the full year. It found the herds sound, the milk whole, and the scales honest, and concluded
instead that the bottom's wet, sour, sedge-hay pasture thinned the cream, "without reproach" to
the patrons.

Eleven years later, in a 1919 covering letter reprinting the same report (r15), Grigg claimed the
committee had sampled "the whole of 1908... every month of that year" — directly contradicting
his own reprinted report on the same page — and cited the State College's Bulletin No. 214 as
confirming a "two tenths" (0.2-point) winter depression from wet hay.

That same "two tenths" figure and the same wet-hay theory were advanced again in 1926 by
veterinarian Roland Kepp (r16), who dismissed the 1911 borrowed-measure episode (Story 3) as an
insignificant "six weeks" summer fluke and cited a field gap of "twenty-one hundredths" — a
number that echoes manager Selby Vose's own claim more than any independent measurement of
Kepp's.

Both the "two tenths" figure and the "six weeks" mischaracterization would prove to be errors,
exposed when county agent Falke actually obtained and quoted Bulletin No. 214 verbatim in 1959
(r22): the trial found a depression of at most "five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and...
nothing... on summer pasture" — far too small to explain the Hollow's year-round 0.19-point gap.

## Story 3 — The Nine Weeks of Borrowed Glass, 1911

On 8 May 1911 the Ostrey Hollow station's own measuring glass broke. Orra Keddie's diary records
the day itself ("A. home cross about a broken measure," r24); Orin Falke's 1959 report (r22)
supplies the resolution: a measure borrowed from elsewhere was used for nine weeks, until 10 July
1911, after which the station's own glass resumed.

During those nine weeks — same tester, same herds, same pasture (the flush of early summer), same
oversight — the station's recorded tests rose markedly; the day the station's own glass returned,
the figures fell back to their old level (r22). Ansel Keddie noted the anomaly in his own book
and set only a question mark against it (r22); his son Duncan later recorded that his father came
home that summer saying it was "something in the glass," but "never dared put it in the book"
without proof (r18) — Ansel privately intuited the true mechanism forty-seven years before it was
confirmed.

This episode is a natural experiment: it isolates the measuring instrument as the variable while
holding every other factor constant, and it is the one piece of evidence in the whole record that
neither the watered-milk theory nor the wet-hay theory can explain away — both later advocates
(Grigg, r15; Kepp, r16) tried to neutralize it by mischaracterizing its length as "six weeks," a
shared error contradicted by the precise dating in both Falke's report and Orra's contemporaneous
diary. The 1911 episode was not recognized as decisive until Ivy Keddie's 1958 discovery gave it
a mechanism to confirm.

## Story 4 — The Manager's Circular and the Rift in the Family (1919)

By 1919 the Association's manager, Selby Vose — son of the Association's founding president
Gideon Vose (r18, r23) — had concluded, without evidence beyond the persistent low tests, that
Ostrey Hollow patrons must be watering their milk. He circulated a written warning to that effect
(r22, r23), which clerk Bertram Kyle recalls typing himself, and thought "a hard thing to send to
men who were bringing their milk in every morning of their lives" (r23).

Sources disagree on the circular's reach: Orra Keddie's diary, written the same month, says Ansel
personally delivered it to eleven families rather than let it go by mail or sit on the station
platform (r24); Hazel Brant's 1981 recollection instead gives nine families, matching the six
Hessel Bottom farms, "a farm can hold more than one family" (r17). The contemporaneous diary
entry is the stronger source (see Section E).

The circular's most lasting effect was personal, not statistical: Ansel's insistence on
delivering it himself, standing at each man's door while he read it, provoked a public breach
with his own brother-in-law. Two days after the circular went out, Emmet Brant came to the
Keddie yard, refused to enter the house, and demanded to know why Ansel had carried a paper he
did not believe; Ansel answered only that he would not have another man carry it. Emmet did not
enter that house again for a decade (r24).

The watered-milk theory itself never had supporting evidence: the state dairy inspector tested
with a lactometer at every visit from 1908 onward and never once found added water at the station
(r22) — meaning Vose's accusation, and the family rupture it caused, rested on nothing that any
contemporary check ever substantiated.


# Answers — Part 2: Section A, Stories 5–8; Section B

## Story 5 — The Condemned Glass, 1923

In the summer of 1923 the state's dairy inspector for the Ordell circuit, Merle Strawn — a man
"kin to nobody in the valley" (r18) — made his ordinary rounds and tested the Ostrey Hollow
station's milk pipettes against a calibrated burette. He found they would not deliver their
marked charge and condemned the whole lot on the spot, directing the manager to order new glass.

The exact date is contested: Falke's 1959 report (r22), the most carefully documented account,
gives 12 June 1923; Duncan Keddie's notes (r18) give 1921; clerk Bertram Kyle's memoir (r23)
gives 1922. The correspondence settles it: Ammon & Sons' own letter book (reproduced in r20)
shows the Association's replacement order dated 28 June 1923 and Ammon's reply dated 2 July
1923 — both necessarily after the condemnation, which rules out 1921 or 1922, and also rules out
a "12 July" date sometimes given elsewhere, since an order already sent on 28 June cannot follow
a condemnation in July. Twelve June 1923 is the only date consistent with the surrounding
correspondence.

New glass went into use within the month (r20, r22), and the station's recorded average test rose
from about 3.61 to about 3.85 from 1924 onward (r22) — a rise no one at the time connected to the
change of instrument. Ammon's reply to the Association was brief and unhelpful: "Our books of the
nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name" (r20) — an answer the
firm's own later historian, Junia Ammon, called "a thin thing to send to a customer of
twenty-six years' standing" (r20), speculating that the original 1897 lot came from a mold that
had been reground and ran consistently, invisibly short.

The condemned pipettes themselves were carried up into the loft over the station's intake room
and forgotten there for thirty-five years (r18, r21).

## Story 6 — The 1925 Arbitration and Ansel Keddie's Dismissal

The patrons of Ostrey Hollow petitioned the Association in January 1925 (r22), and a board of
arbitration sat at Ashlin from 17 March to 4 May 1925 under an attorney chairman (r22) whom
Alonzo Frick's own recollections (r13) identify as himself — dates corroborated independently by
Orra Keddie's diary, which records Ansel leaving for the first sitting on 17 March and two later
sittings held at the station itself (r24), matching Cleve Oram's recollection of "three
sittings... two of them... at the station itself" (r21).

Three theories were argued before the board (r13): watered milk, deliberate skimming by Keddie,
and wet-hay depression. Only four exhibits were entered — the station's own test book, the
station's receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book (lent for the purpose), and the state
inspector's circuit notebook for 1923 — a list confirmed item-for-item by both the board's
chairman (r13) and the clerk who prepared it (r23). Neither list includes the crate of condemned
pipettes, which sat, unexamined, in the loft directly over two of the hearing's own sessions (r21,
r23).

The board found no cause established, awarded the patrons $3,800 "without admission" (r19, r22,
r23 agree; Oram's secondhand $3,500 in r21 is the outlier), and dismissed Ansel Keddie on 30
April 1925 — four days before the award (r18, r22, corroborated by Orra's diary entry of that
exact date, r24). After twenty-nine seasons (r22), Keddie came home at his ordinary hour, hung up
his apron, and asked what there was for supper (r24).

Frick's own 1946 recollections give an average test of 3.16 and a patron loss of 67,000 lb — both
figures at odds with the better-supported figures of roughly 3.61 and 76,000 lb (Section C),
suggesting Frick's memory, or the board's own working numbers, were already imprecise.

## Story 7 — Keddie's Last Years and Death, 1925–1934

Dismissed and unexonerated, Ansel Keddie spent his last nine years as a carpenter, beginning work
on the family's long-neglected cow shed the month after his dismissal and, per Orra's diary,
whistling at it "which he never did when he was paid" (r24). He mended stairs, gates, and doors
across the hollow into his late sixties — "better at it than he ever was at the other" (r24) —
and hung a barn door for the Oram family that "swung true for thirty years" (r21).

He never spoke publicly of the accusation again. He told Orra once, and only once, that "a man
cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name" (r24). He died at home in February 1934, nine
years after his dismissal (r18, r24); his funeral was "well attended and nobody said much" (r21),
and men who had not spoken to him in nine years praised his carpentry but never mentioned the
other matter (r24).

Orra outlived him by fourteen years, dying in 1948 (r18, r24) — a decade before her husband's
name was cleared. Their son Duncan, writing in 1968, preserved the single fact that made the
eventual vindication possible: his father's 1911 private suspicion that the fault lay "in the
glass" (r18). Their granddaughter Hazel Brant, reflecting in 1981, said she was glad for her
grandfather Emmet Brant's sake, and gladder still for Ansel Keddie's — "who did not live to see
it" (r17) — capturing the cost of the twenty-four years between Keddie's death and his
vindication.

## Story 8 — Ivy Keddie's Discovery and Bulletin No. 471 (1958–1959 and after)

In February 1958, Ansel Keddie's granddaughter Ivy — a graduate student at Farland working on a
project about the station — climbed a ladder into the loft over the old intake room and found the
condemned crate, still under its tarpaulin since 1923 (r18, r19, r21). She carried it east and,
on 9 March 1958, measured the nine surviving pipettes (the best-supported count — see Section E)
against a calibrated burette (r22).

Every pipette, blown to a mark of 17.6 c.c., delivered an average of only 16.27 c.c. (r11, r19) —
a short charge that, fed into a test bottle whose graduated neck assumed a full charge, produced a
proportionally low but perfectly internally consistent fat reading every time. Working with
Professor Leland Vaught, Ivy Keddie published the finding as Bulletin No. 471 in October 1958
(r11): no milk had been watered and no cream stolen — the fault was "a piece of glass" (r19).

The finding drew immediate local coverage (the Ashlin Chronicle's front page, r19) and, the
following year, a formal review by county agent Orin Falke (r22), who initially credited a
partial wet-hay contribution but retracted the concession after obtaining and reading Bulletin
No. 214's actual text, and separately corrected the press's error calling Ivy Ansel's
"great-niece" rather than his granddaughter.

In the years after, participants and descendants reflected on the case from their own vantage:
Duncan Keddie (r18) traced the family's genealogy and the scattered records' provenance; Cleve
Oram (r21), Hazel Brant (r17), and Bertram Kyle (r23) each gave retrospective accounts, none
entirely error-free, but together supplying the corroborating detail — the ledger's own "station
gain" entries, Emmet Brant's matching private weigh book, the Alder Corners cheese factory's
independent tests — that turned Ivy Keddie's single measurement into a fully reconstructed
history.

# Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**
Granddaughter. Duncan Keddie — Ansel's own son and Ivy's own father — states plainly "my daughter
Ivy... which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already
twice seen it printed otherwise" (r18). Orin Falke's 1959 report first wrote "great-niece" (r22,
matching the Ashlin Chronicle's identical error, r19) but appended a correction: "I am informed,
and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on
page two is mine" (r22). A parent's direct statement, plus a self-correcting official source,
outranks two press mentions that likely share one origin.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**
Second cousins. Duncan (Ansel and Orra's son) and Roy Brant (Emmet's son) are first cousins,
being the children of siblings Orra and Emmet (r18). Ivy is Duncan's daughter and Hazel is Roy's
daughter, so they are the children of first cousins — second cousins, not "cousins once removed"
and not aunt/niece (r17, r18, both working the generations through explicitly and agreeing). Cleve
Oram's "first cousin once removed" (r21) is unreasoned village hearsay and is rejected. What the
sources leave open: whether Orra and Emmet were full or half siblings (r09 says "full sister";
Hazel Brant says "half sister... by his father's second wife," r17) — this does not change the
cousin arithmetic, but it is not resolved by anything in the record.

**B3. Selby Vose's father.**
Gideon Vose, who organized the Association and was its first president and wrote its by-laws
(r18, r23) — not his uncle, a rumor both sources explicitly name and reject. This matters because
Selby inherited both an institutional stake in the payment-by-test system his father had built and,
per clerk Kyle's direct observation, a managerial habit of signing off on totals without reading
the lines above them (r23) — a style of institutional trust that let the low tests persist
unquestioned under his own signature for over a decade before the true cause was found.

**B4. The Vose and Keddie families.**
No genuine kinship. Duncan Keddie devotes a section of his notes to refuting the rumor directly:
his mother Orra, who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection" in the
valley, "never once put a Vose in it" (r18). Cleve Oram repeats the rumor but immediately admits
he "could not draw you the line on paper" (r21) — an unverifiable claim by his own account. The
detailed, reasoned family-insider source outranks the admittedly unsubstantiated one.


# Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math

**C1.**

*(a) Proportion.* The pipettes were blown to a mark of 17.6 c.c. but delivered an average of
16.27 c.c. (r11, r19). Read as a raw volume ratio: 16.27 / 17.6 = 0.9244, i.e. a 7.56% shortfall.
But two *independent, empirical* checks give a smaller, more consistent figure of about 5%: (i)
the Association's own ledger recorded, every year, a "station gain" of fat actually received at
Larrow Green over fat credited to patrons of "five and a quarter per cent" (r12, quoting the
ledger; r23, the clerk who copied the same line for years) — a figure derived from real weighed
fat, not from the pipette calibration at all; and (ii) the point-gap arithmetic below. I resolve
the true proportional shortfall as **≈5%** (ledger figure 5.25%), treating the raw 7.56%
volume-ratio as an upper-bound estimate that overstates the effect actually observed at the
creamery, and treating "one part in twenty" in the bulletin's own quoted conclusion (r11) as
consistent with this ledger figure, not with the higher one computed elsewhere in the same
document's introduction (see Section E).

*(b) True average and point shortfall.* The recorded station average was 3.61 (r22). The point
shortfall is independently given as "nineteen hundredths" (0.19) by two unconnected sources —
Falke's report, explicitly citing the bulletin's own figure (r22), and Hazel Brant's family
account of the weigh-book record (r17). True average = 3.61 + 0.19 = **3.80**. This is
cross-validated three ways: Larrow Green creamery's own average over the same years was "three
seventy-eight" (r22); Alder Corners' independent tests on the same farmers' summer milk ran
"3.78–3.82" (r10); and 0.19 / 3.80 = 5.00% exactly — matching the "one part in twenty" language
and the ledger's 5.25% independently. I therefore reject Falke's own subtraction elsewhere in the
same report (3.78 − 3.61 = 0.17) as a minor arithmetic slip, in favor of the explicitly stated
and doubly-attested 0.19.

*(c) Butterfat not credited.* **76,000 lb**, per the bulletin's own quoted conclusion (r11). I
reject "118,000 lb," which appears only in Vaught's own introductory paragraph in the same
document and contradicts that document's own final quoted conclusion (self-contradiction, see
Section E). I reject "67,000 lb" (r13, r21): Frick's 1946 figure predates the 1958 discovery and
likely reflects an older, cruder pre-discovery reckoning under a different theory; Oram's 1977
figure is secondhand oral testimony and looks like a transposed-digit error of 76,000 (67 vs. 76),
a pattern that recurs elsewhere in these sources (see Section E). Arithmetic check, working from
independent inputs: 40,000,000 lb total milk (r23) × 3.61% recorded average = 1,444,000 lb
credited; 1,444,000 × 5.25% ledger gain rate ≈ 75,810 lb — matching 76,000 lb closely, from
figures that do not depend on the pipette calibration at all. This convergence is the strongest
evidence in the whole record for the 76,000 lb figure.

*(d) Money value and fraction received.* $19,600 (r12, r19 — consistent, though likely both
drawn from the same bulletin/press release, so not fully independent). Patrons actually received
the 1925 arbitration award of $3,800 (see C-general and Section A Story 6). Fraction received =
3,800 / 19,600 ≈ **19%**, roughly one-fifth of what was owed.

**C2. Largest patron (Emmet Brant), 1910.**

*(a) Credited.* Roy/Hazel Brant's transcription of the weigh-book leaf: 200,000 lb of milk
delivered, "paid on a test of three forty-two" (r17). Credited butterfat = 200,000 × 0.0342 =
**6,840 lb**.

*(b) Owed but not credited.* Using the proportional shortfall established in C1 (recorded ≈ 0.95
× true), true test = 3.42 / 0.95 = 3.60. True butterfat = 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb. Owed and
not credited = 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb**.

*(c) Money value that year.* No source gives a specific 1897–1910-era butterfat price; the only
prices given are averages "over the whole stretch" of about 25¢/lb (r23) and a later-1920s price
of 27¢/lb (r16, explicitly "through these years," i.e. the 1920s, not 1910). Using the more
period-appropriate 25¢/lb average as an approximation: 360 × $0.25 ≈ **$90**. This figure is an
approximation, not a verified 1910 price, and I flag it as such.

**C3. Full delivery years, totals.**

*(a) Credited to patrons:* 40,000,000 lb total milk (r23) × 3.61% recorded average = **1,444,000
lb**.

*(b) Actually received at the creamery:* 1,444,000 × 1.0525 (ledger gain rate) ≈ **1,519,810 lb**.

*(c) Percentage:* This depends on what you take the percentage of. As a percentage of what
patrons were *credited*, the gain is 75,810 / 1,444,000 ≈ **5.25%** — matching the ledger's own
stated figure exactly, which is the way the Association's own bookkeeper phrased it (r23). As a
percentage of what was actually *received* at the creamery, the same shortfall is 75,810 /
1,519,810 ≈ **4.99%**, matching the bulletin's "one part in twenty" (r11) almost exactly. Both are
correct arithmetic; they differ only in denominator, and the sources themselves use both framings
without flagging the difference — worth noting as a source of apparent (but not real)
inconsistency.

**C4. The invoice.**

*(a) Total.* **$77.93**, established by summing the invoice's own reproduced line items (r20):
$25.20 + $31.68 + $0.90 + $18.00 + $2.15 = $77.93. This overrides the prose figure "seventy-seven
dollars and thirty-nine cents," which appears in *two* sources (r19 and r20's own narration) but
is contradicted by arithmetic on the primary reproduced document — a case where arithmetic
outranks two agreeing narrators (see Section E).

*(b) Pipettes and shortfall.* Six dozen (72) pipettes (r20's reproduced invoice; confirmed by
r19's prose and by the chronology of use — see Section E on the "five dozen" error in r11), each
marked 17.6 c.c. and delivering an average of 16.27 c.c. — short by **1.33 c.c.** per pipette.

**C5.**

*(a) Duration of faulty glass vs. years in the money calculation.* The faulty glass was in
physical use for about 26 years and 6 weeks: from 1 May 1897 (r24, r20, r19) until condemned on
12 June 1923 (r22, established by chronological logic in Section A Story 5). But the money
calculation (Kyle's 40,000,000 lb ÷ 1,600,000 lb/year = 25 years, r23) covers only **full
calendar years** of faulty-glass operation: 1898 through 1922 inclusive is exactly 25 full years,
excluding the partial stub of 1897 (about 8 months) and the partial stub of 1923 (about 5.5
months) at either end. That is why the two numbers differ: one counts elapsed calendar time
across the whole faulty period, the other counts only complete delivery years, as an accountant
naturally would when computing per-year averages.

*(b) First season's milk.* Approximately **1,100,000 lb**, per clerk Bertram Kyle, who
explicitly flags this figure as an unverified oral estimate rather than a ledger entry: "a good
eleven hundred thousand pounds, which is what I understood at the time from men who were there.
It is not in any book I ever ruled" (r23).

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 experiment.**
It ran from 8 May 1911 (r24, precisely dated by Orra's contemporaneous diary) to 10 July 1911
(r22), nine weeks. The variable was the measuring instrument (the station's own broken glass
replaced temporarily by a borrowed measure); held constant were the herds, the pasture (summer
flush throughout), the tester, and the procedure. The result: recorded tests rose during the nine
weeks and fell back the day the station's own glass resumed (r22). What this establishes, that a
calculation alone cannot: it proves the instrument itself — not the cows, the season, the hay, or
the tester's honesty — was the operative variable, and it proves this was true as early as 1911,
decades before anyone could measure the actual pipettes. A 1958 calibration alone could show the
crated glass was defective *in 1958*; it could not, by itself, prove the same defect operated
throughout the preceding decades. The 1911 episode supplies that missing temporal link. What it
does *not* establish: the precise physical mechanism (a short pipette charge specifically, as
opposed to some other difference in the borrowed apparatus), which was only confirmed by direct
measurement in 1958; nor does it establish the size or duration of the error outside those nine
weeks.

**D2. Independent reasons Keddie was not stealing cream.**
(1) The 1911 experiment (D1): the same tester, under no different supervision, produced higher
readings the moment the instrument changed — inconsistent with a story where the tester's own
dishonesty is the cause. (2) The ledger's own annual "station gain" entries (r12, r23) show that
Larrow Green consistently received *more* fat in the Hollow's cream than patrons were credited
for — the physical opposite of what skimming would produce, since a thief removes cream before
it is weighed, which would show a deficit at the creamery, not a surplus. (3) The Alder Corners
cheese factory's fully independent tests on the same Hessel Bottom farmers' milk, hauled over the
ridge by a different route to a different business entirely, consistently read normal
(3.78–3.82, r10) — proving the milk itself, from the same herds Keddie tested, was never short of
fat when tested by anyone else. These three are independent because they rest on different
mechanisms and different record-keepers: an instrument-swap event recorded by the tester himself
and later by an official report and a diary; the Association's own internal bookkeeping,
recorded by its own bookkeeper; and a wholly external business's private records. Reasons (1) and
(3) — and arguably (2) — would all still hold even if the 1958 glassware had never been measured,
since none of them depends on that measurement. As for whether the bookkeeper (Teague) ever
questioned the ledger entries that refute his own accusation: no. Clerk Kyle states plainly that
Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years" and that the "station gain, as usual" line
crossed no one's mind to wonder at, including Kyle's own (r23) — despite Teague himself being
among those who, per the earlier record, urged the skimming theory at the 1925 hearing.

**D3. Did the manager know?**
Selby Vose signed the station-gain-bearing ledger sheets every year from taking office in 1911
(r23). But clerk Kyle, who watched him do it "a thousand times," states Vose "read the totals and
he never read the lines above them... took a sheet, turned it, put his thumb on the bottom
figure, and signed" (r23). On the direct evidence of the man who sat outside his door for
twenty-five years, Vose most likely did *not* consciously know the specific "station gain"
figure, despite formally signing documents that recorded it. He plainly did believe *something*
was wrong with the Hollow's tests — enough to circulate the 1919 water-warning circular — but the
sources indicate he never connected that belief to the ledger evidence sitting, unread, under his
own signature, which if read would have pointed away from the patrons entirely.

## Section E — Contradictions

Each entry gives the conflicting claims, the narrators, the resolution (or a statement that it
cannot be resolved), and the method used.

1. **Pipettes purchased:** "five dozen" (r11) vs. "six dozen" (r20's reproduced invoice, r19).
   Resolved to six dozen — a reproduced primary invoice outranks a secondary bulletin
   introduction that is independently shown to contain other errors.
2. **Pipettes surviving to 1958:** "nine" (r21, r22) vs. "eleven" (r19, r12). Resolved to nine —
   two accounts (Oram's direct physical memory, Falke's report) against two press/pamphlet
   accounts that likely share one press-release origin and are therefore not doubly independent.
3. **Mean pipette delivery:** 16.27 c.c. (r11, r19) vs. 16.72 c.c. (r07, per my earlier notes).
   Resolved to 16.27 — matches the bulletin's own primary figure; 16.72 looks like a digit
   transposition.
4. **Butterfat shortfall:** 76,000 lb vs. 118,000 lb vs. 67,000 lb. Resolved to 76,000 lb; see C1(c)
   for full reasoning, including an independent arithmetic check that lands almost exactly on
   76,000.
5. **Settlement award:** $3,800 (r19, r22, r23) vs. $3,500 (r21). Resolved to $3,800 by majority

## Section F — Theory

**F1. The causal theory.** For about 26 years (1 May 1897 – 12 June 1923), the Ostrey Hollow
station tested milk with pipettes that were manufactured to deliver a short charge (an average of
16.27 c.c. against a mark of 17.6 c.c., per Ivy Keddie's 1958 measurement, r11/r19). Because the
Babcock-type test reads the separated fat column against a bottle-neck scale calibrated for a
*full* charge, every short charge produced a proportionally low, but perfectly repeatable, fat
reading — regardless of the cow, the season, the hay, or the tester's honesty. This explains the
size of the error (a fixed ~5% proportional shortfall, corroborated three independent ways in
Section C1); why it began when it did (the defective outfit went into service 1 May 1897,
replacing accurate borrowed glass); why it ended when it did (the state inspector condemned the
lot on 12 June 1923 and new glass went into use within weeks, after which the recorded average
immediately rose to about 3.85 and stayed there, r22); and why nothing at the station or on the
farms needed to change at either transition, since the defect lived entirely in the glass, not in
any person, animal, or practice.

Three competing theories were offered, and each is defeated by specific evidence:
- **Watered milk** (Selby Vose, 1919 circular, r22/r23) — defeated by the state inspector's
  lactometer tests at every circuit visit from 1908 on, which never once found added water (r22).
- **Cream theft by the tester** (urged at the 1925 hearing, per r13) — defeated by the 1911
  natural experiment (D1), by the ledger's own station-gain surplus showing *more*, not less, fat
  reaching the creamery than was credited (r12, r23), and by the Alder Corners cheese factory's
  independent normal test results on the same herds (r10).
- **Wet, mineral-poor Hessel Bottom hay** (the 1908 Grigg committee, r15; veterinarian Kepp, r16)
  — defeated by Bulletin No. 214's actual verbatim finding of at most a 0.05-point winter-only
  depression, far too small and too seasonal to explain a flat, year-round 0.19-point gap (r22),
  and by the Alder Corners tests on the very same wet-bottom herds showing normal results (r10).

Subsidiary questions: The 1925 board of arbitration never examined the condemned glassware — both
the board's chairman (r13) and the clerk who prepared its exhibits (r23) independently list the
same four documents entered as evidence, and the crate is absent from both lists, despite sitting
in the loft directly above two of the hearing's own sessions (r21). The tester himself believed,
privately, from as early as the summer of 1911, that the cause was "something in the glass" (r18)
— but never recorded this belief formally because he "would not put down what he could not
prove" (r18), and he died in 1934 without ever asserting it publicly.

## Section G — Summary

For roughly 26 years (1897–1923), the Ostrey Hollow skimming station paid Larrow Valley dairy
farmers using milk-test pipettes manufactured slightly undersized, producing a consistent,
proportional under-reading of about 5% in every test, for every patron, every season — invisible
because it was internally self-consistent and no one thought to check the glass. Contemporaries
blamed watered milk, a dishonest tester (Ansel Keddie, dismissed in 1925), or the valley's wet
hay; each theory was wrong, and the evidence disproving each — lactometer checks, ledger
surpluses, an independent cheese factory's results — existed all along but scattered across
family diaries, business ledgers, and inspector's notebooks nobody assembled in one place. Only
in 1958, when Keddie's granddaughter Ivy found the discarded, condemned pipettes in a station
loft and measured them, did the pieces converge into Bulletin No. 471, clearing his name
twenty-four years after his death.
