## Story 1 — The Short Glass of Ostrey Hollow

**Who:** Ansel Keddie (1861–1934), tester and separator man at the Larrow
Valley Cooperative Creamery Association's Ostrey Hollow skimming station from
its opening (4 May 1896) until his dismissal in 1925; his wife Orra (née
Brant); their son Duncan; their granddaughter Ivy Keddie; the patrons of
Hessel Bottom, chiefly Emmet Brant (the largest hauler) and his son Roy and
granddaughter Hazel; the Association's manager Selby Vose; bookkeeper Wilbur
Teague and clerk Bertram Kyle; the glass supplier Ammon & Sons of Tarnet; and
state dairy inspector Merle Strawn.

**What happened:** From the station's opening its milk tested persistently
lower in butterfat than neighboring stations, which paid patrons on the test
rather than the weight of milk delivered. Three explanations circulated for
thirty years: added water, deliberate theft/false readings by the tester, and
thin cream from the wet bog hay of Hessel Bottom. All three were argued at a
1925 arbitration hearing, which awarded patrons $3,800 "without admission" and
dismissed Keddie four days later. The true cause, discovered only in 1958 when
his granddaughter Ivy found the station's own condemned glassware crated in
the station loft, was that the 1897 lot of milk-test pipettes (bought from
Ammon & Sons, blown to a 17.6 c.c. mark) had been manufactured short and
delivered on average only 16.27 c.c. — producing a constant, season-blind
low reading in every test made with that glass. State inspector Strawn had
condemned the glass in June 1923 for an unrelated reason (it failed a burette
check) without ever connecting it to the years of complaint; the crate then
sat forgotten for 35 years until Ivy climbed the loft ladder.

**When:** Glass in service 1 May 1897 – 12 June 1923; hearing and dismissal,
1925; vindication, 1958.

**Quantities that matter:** ~7.6% proportional shortfall in every test;
$3,800 awarded vs. an estimated $19,600 owed; 72 pipettes (6 dozen) bought for
$77.93 in 1897.

**Documents:** r17, r18, r19, r20, r21, r22, r23, r24 (all eight).

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**
Ivy is Ansel's **granddaughter**. Chain: Ansel and Orra Keddie's son is Duncan
(r18, r24); Duncan's daughter, born 1931, is Ivy (r18, explicit: "My daughter
is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write
that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise"). Two
sources call her "great-niece" instead: the *Ashlin Chronicle* (r19) and Orin
Falke's county report as first drafted (r22, ¶2). Falke's report then
self-corrects in an appended note: "I am informed, and have verified, that
Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page
two is mine." I resolve this in favor of "granddaughter": it is stated by
Ivy's own father (the strongest possible authority on his own daughter's
parentage), by Cleve Oram independently (r21, "the granddaughter came back"),
and by Falke's own verified correction. The "great-niece" figure looks like a
single error (probably originating in an imprecise press release) that the
Chronicle and Falke's draft both repeated — one source, not two independent
ones.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**
They are **second cousins** — children of first cousins. Chain: Emmet Brant
and Orra (Keddie) were brother and sister; Emmet's son Roy and Orra's son
Duncan are therefore first cousins; Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter
Ivy are therefore second cousins. This exact relationship, and the explicit
rejection of "cousins once removed" or "aunt and niece," is stated
independently by both principals: Hazel herself (r17: "Second cousins. It is
not complicated; it is only that nobody wants to count") and Duncan himself
(r18, Section II, with the same reasoning). Cleve Oram (r21) instead calls
Ivy "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed" — a single, secondhand,
valley-gossip source contradicted by both people it concerns; I discard it.
Nothing in the sources leaves the generational chain itself open — it is
fully specified — the only open question the sources leave is Orra's exact
birth year (see E1), which does not affect the cousin count.

**B3. Selby Vose's father, and why it matters.**
Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**, who organized the Larrow Valley
Cooperative Creamery Association and was its first president (r18, r23).
Selby became the Association's manager in 1911 (r23) and it was he who signed
and circulated the 1919 warning letter accusing Ostrey Hollow patrons of
watering their milk (r22, r23, r24). This matters because it shows the
accusation against the patrons came from the very top of the organization
that had bought and never audited the defective glass, and because a rumor
that Selby was Gideon's *nephew* rather than son was in circulation and is
explicitly and independently rejected by two sources with direct knowledge:
Duncan Keddie's family notes (r18) and Bertram Kyle, the clerk who worked
immediately outside Selby's office door for a quarter century (r23: "the
son, whatever is said at Ashlin... I have heard the nephew story twice in the
last year and it is nonsense"). Two independent, well-placed sources agreeing
settles this.

**B4. Relationship between the Vose and Keddie families.**
The sources establish **no genuine family relationship**. Duncan Keddie
addresses this directly and as a named section of his notes ("A thing that is
said and is not so," r18): he states flatly there was no connection, that
people confused the Voses with an unrelated Ashlin family of the same name,
and that his mother — who knew everyone's connections in the valley — never
once included a Vose. Against this stands only one source, Cleve Oram (r21),
reporting vague inherited hearsay ("The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of
some sort... I could not draw you the line on paper"). Duncan's testimony is
both a direct family source and explicitly rebuts the specific claim; Oram's
is unverifiable secondhand rumor with no genealogical detail. I resolve this
as: no relationship, and the belief that there was one is itself a known
folk-confusion (per Duncan) between two unrelated Vose families.

## Section C — Math

**C1.**
(a) The pipette proportion is the direct arithmetic base. The 1958 measurement
(r19, the *Ashlin Chronicle* quoting the college bulletin) found the pipettes
blown to a 17.6 c.c. mark delivered on average 16.27 c.c. Proportion of truth
recorded = 16.27/17.6 = 0.9244, i.e. tests read at **~92.4% of true**, a
relative shortfall of (17.6−16.27)/17.6 = **~7.56%**.

(b) This is where the sources genuinely conflict, and I cannot fully settle
it. Falke's report (r22 ¶5) gives the pre-1923 station average as 3.61 and the
Larrow Green creamery's average as 3.78 — a gap of 0.17 — but the very same
paragraph asserts "the deficiency, as the bulletin now fixes it, was nineteen
hundredths of a point" (0.19), which Hazel Brant (r17) also states
independently ("Nineteen hundredths... flat as a floor"). Falke separately
states (¶11) that after the glass was replaced in 1923, "the station's
average test stood at three eighty-five and remained there" — an *observed*
post-repair average, giving a gap from 3.61 of 0.24. Applying the 7.56%
proportional correction from C1(a) directly to 3.61 gives a theoretical true
value of ≈3.91, a gap of ≈0.30. Four figures (0.17, 0.19, 0.24, 0.30) are all
derivable from these documents and they do not agree. I weight 3.85 (an
actually observed post-repair average, not a derived approximation) as the
best available estimate of the true rate, giving a deficit of **~0.24
points**, while flagging that the "nineteen hundredths" figure recurs
independently in two sources and may reflect a comparison against a
different baseline (the neighboring creamery rather than the station's own
post-repair performance). I do not consider this fully resolved.

(c) **~67,000 lbs** of butterfat, per Cleve Oram (r21: "Sixty-seven thousand
pounds of butterfat... that the Hollow men were never paid for"), which he
attributes to "they" (the college bulletin's figure). Note: this and the
dollar figure in (d) both trace to the same 1958 bulletin — one source
relayed by two narrators, not two independent corroborations.

(d) **$19,600** (*Ashlin Chronicle*, r19, "the figure is nineteen thousand
six hundred dollars"). Patrons actually received the 1925 arbitration award
of **$3,800** (see E1 for how this figure is settled against a competing
$3,500). Fraction received = 3,800/19,600 ≈ **0.194**, roughly one-fifth.

**C2.** The largest patron was Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom (established in
r17, r18, r21, r23 — he "had the largest piece of it" and "hauled the most
milk into the station of any man on the creek"). For 1910, Hazel Brant's
weigh-book reading (r17) gives 200,000 lbs delivered, tested at 3.42%.
(a) Credited butterfat = 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lbs**.
(b) Applying the C1(a) proportional correction (an inference — the 1958
measurement was of glass in 1958, projected back onto a 1910 reading; the
sources do not independently verify the 1910 glass was the same lot, though
Ammon's account confirms the same lot was in continuous use 1897–1923, which
covers 1910): true test ≈ 3.42/0.9244 ≈ 3.70%; true butterfat ≈ 200,000 ×
0.0370 ≈ 7,400 lbs. Owed and not credited ≈ 7,400 − 6,840 = **~560 lbs**.
(c) Only a period-general butterfat price survives in the sources — Bertram
Kyle's clerk figure of "twenty-five cents the pound... taking the fat years
with the lean" averaged over 25 years (r23), not a 1910-specific price. Using
it as an approximation: 560 × $0.25 ≈ **$140**. This is a rough estimate, not
a documented 1910 figure.

**C3.**
(a)/(b) No source gives a direct total of pounds credited vs. pounds received
at the creamery over the full run of years. Bertram Kyle (r23) gives total
milk received as 40,000,000 lbs over 25 years (1,600,000/year) and an average
butterfat price of $0.25/lb, but not a fat percentage to convert milk-weight
to butterfat-weight for a station total. Combining Kyle's milk-weight figure
with Falke's recorded average test (3.61%, r22) as a cross-document estimate:
credited butterfat ≈ 40,000,000 × 0.0361 ≈ **1,444,000 lbs** (an estimate, not
a stated total). Applying Kyle's own recorded "station gain" rate of 5.25%
(r23) as the amount by which cream received at the creamery exceeded what was
credited to patrons: received ≈ 1,444,000 × 1.0525 ≈ **1,520,000 lbs**.
(c) Kyle's ledger consistently booked the gap as **"five and a quarter per
cent, as usual"** (r23) — the bookkeeping's own documented percentage. This
does *not* match the ~7.56% shortfall derived from the glassware measurement
in C1(a). I cannot determine from the sources whether 5.25% was itself a
rounded, unaudited traditional entry (Kyle: "it never once crossed my mind to
wonder at it") rather than a figure independently re-derived each year, so I
flag this 5.25%-vs-7.56% discrepancy rather than resolve it.

**C4.**
(a) The invoice itself (reproduced in full in r20) lists: 25.20 + 31.68 +
0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = **$77.93**. This is arithmetically the correct total. It
directly contradicts the same document's own prose, which states "Seventy-
seven dollars and thirty-nine cents," and Duncan Keddie's notes (r18), which
independently cite the same wrong figure, "$77.39" (see E1 for this as a
"same wrong value" case). I use the itemized invoice sum as authoritative
since it is arithmetic, not restated memory.
(b) The invoice bought **72 pipettes** (6 dozen). Each delivered on average
17.6 − 16.27 = **1.33 c.c. short** of its marked capacity.

**C5.**
(a) The faulty glass was in continuous use (with one 9-week interruption in
1911, see D1) from **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 — 26 years** (confirmed
arithmetically by Ammon's own account, r20: "six and twenty years
afterward... 1923"). The money/damages calculation, per Bertram Kyle's clerk
figures (r23: 40,000,000 lbs ÷ 1,600,000 lbs/year = 25 years), runs over
**25 full delivery years**. These differ because 1897 and 1923 were each only
*partial* delivery years — the new glass arrived partway through 1897 (from
1 May) and was condemned partway through 1923 (12 June) — so the damages
calculation reasonably excludes those two partial bookend years and counts
only the 25 full calendar years between them (1898–1922).
(b) **~1,100,000 lbs** of milk, per Bertram Kyle (r23: "a good eleven hundred
thousand pounds"), for the station's very first season, before it had its own
station account. Kyle himself flags this as uncertain, secondhand
recollection ("what I understood at the time from men who were there. It is
not in any book I ever ruled") — it is the only figure given and should be
treated as unverified.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The station's own measuring glass broke on 8 May 1911; a measure
borrowed from elsewhere was used until 10 July 1911 — **nine weeks** (r22
¶6). Variable: the measuring glass. Held constant: the same tester (Ansel
Keddie), the same herds and pasture, the same season (spring into midsummer),
the same supervision. Result: tests rose markedly for the nine weeks, then
fell back to the former low level the day the station's own glass resumed.
This establishes something a calculation alone cannot: that the low readings
tracked the *instrument*, not the man, the cows, or the season — a natural,
controlled swap that rules out the tester and the herd as causes in a way no
retrospective statistical argument could, because it was contemporaneous and
the only thing that changed was the glass. What it does *not* establish: the
mechanism of the glass's fault (that it was a short-delivery pipette
specifically, rather than some other defect), the magnitude of the error, or
that the same defect held throughout the full 26 years — it is one nine-week
snapshot in 1911, twelve years before the glass was condemned. Notably, Ansel
himself recognized this and still would not commit to it in writing: per
Duncan (r18), he said at the time "it was something in the glass" but "never
dared put it in the book," entering only a question mark, "because he would
not put down what he could not prove."

**D2.** Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream:
(1) The 1911 borrowed-glass interval (D1, r22): if Keddie had simply been
writing false low figures regardless of the true reading, changing the
physical instrument should have made no difference — instead the readings
rose and fell with the glass, which only makes sense if the glass, not the
man, governed the number.
(2) The deficit's flatness: Hazel Brant (r17) reports it was "not a little in
the winter and none in the summer, the way a feed thing goes... flat as a
floor," and Falke (r22 ¶5) confirms it "ran at the same nineteen hundredths
in every month." A constant, season-independent deficit is the signature of a
fixed instrument error, not of opportunistic theft, which would plausibly
vary with season, need, or opportunity.
(3) Orra Keddie's diary (r24, June 1919): Ansel personally carried the
water-accusation circular to all patrons himself and stood at each door while
it was read, rather than mailing it — an act that estranged him permanently
from his own brother-in-law Emmet Brant. A man secretly stealing cream from
his own wife's family would have little reason to expose himself personally
and at high family cost to a document he knew implicated him by association.
These three are independent: (1) is a contemporaneous physical experiment,
(2) is a statistical pattern noted by two separate later narrators, (3) is a
behavioral/biographical fact from a fourth, unrelated source (his wife).
Reason (2), the flat year-round pattern, would still hold even if the
glassware had never been measured in 1958 — it was observed and recorded
during the complaint years themselves, independent of the later discovery.
As for whether the Association's bookkeeper ever questioned the ledger
entries that refute the theft accusation: **no**. Bertram Kyle (r23) states
plainly that the "station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual" entry
"never once crossed my mind to wonder at," and that bookkeeper Wilbur Teague
"never asked anybody anything in forty years."

**D3.** Yes — the manager (Selby Vose) knew the station's tests ran low
*before* the glass was condemned, but not why. His own 1919 circular warning
patrons about "water in the milk" (r22, r23, r24) presupposes he was aware of
the persistently low readings; that is the entire reason for the warning. But
per Bertram Kyle (r23), Vose "read the totals and he never read the lines
above them... He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom
figure, and sign" — meaning he saw and endorsed the ledgers containing the
normalized "station gain" line every year without examining the component
figures that would have flagged the anomaly as structural rather than
behavioral. So: aware of the symptom (low tests), attributed it to the wrong
cause (farmer fraud), and never connected it to the accounting pattern he was
signing off on annually.

7. **Hearing/dismissal year:** 1925, strongly supported (Falke's dated

data: "I withdraw the concession made on page four." A clear self-
contradiction within one document, resolved by the author's own later,
better-evidenced correction.

## Section F — Theory

**F1.** The shortfall was produced by a manufacturing defect in the 1897 lot
of 72 milk-test pipettes bought from Ammon & Sons: each was blown with a
17.6 c.c. capacity mark but delivered, on average, only 16.27 c.c. — a fixed
proportional shortfall of about 7.6% (r19, r20). Because the butterfat
reading is directly proportional to the volume of milk actually charged into
the test bottle relative to its calibrated mark, every test made with that
glass read low by essentially the same proportion, regardless of season,
herd, or pasture — which explains why the deficit was constant year-round
("flat as a floor," r17) rather than varying the way a feed-related or
seasonal cause would. It began precisely in May 1897, when the new (defective)
outfit replaced the borrowed Larrow Green glass the station had used before
(r24, r20), and ended precisely in June 1923, when the state inspector
condemned the lot on an unrelated routine check and new glass was ordered
(r22); the recorded average then rose and stayed up (r22 ¶11) with nothing
else at the station or on the farms — the same herds, the same land, the same
tester until 1925 — having changed at all. This before/after symmetry, with no
other variable moving, is the strongest evidence the glass alone was
responsible.

Three competing explanations, and what defeats each:
1. **Added water**, advanced by manager Selby Vose (1919 circular, r22–24).
Defeated by the state inspector's lactometer testing "at every visit from
1908 forward," which never once found added water (r22 ¶7).
2. **Theft / falsified low readings by the tester**, urged by an Association
officer at the 1925 hearing (r22 ¶8). Defeated by the 1911 borrowed-glass
natural experiment (D1): the same tester, same supervision, produced higher
readings with a different instrument, then reverted the moment his own
(defective) glass returned — the number tracked the glass, not the man.
3. **Wet bog hay thinning the cream**, advanced by an Association committee
(1908) and a veterinarian (1926), and initially half-credited by Falke.
Defeated by the college's own 1911 feeding trial (Bulletin No. 214, cited in
Falke's appended note, r22): bog hay depresses the test by at most 0.05 points
in winter and nothing measurable in summer — far too small, and wrong in
season, to explain a deficit that ran flat through the summer flush.

Subsidiary questions: The 1925 board of arbitration **never examined the
condemned glassware itself**. It sat in the loft "over their heads the
entire time" of the hearing (Cleve Oram, r21), and Bertram Kyle (r23) lists
the four documents actually placed before the board — none of which was the
glassware, and nobody "laid [one] beside [another] and looked." The tester
himself believed, from as early as 1911, that the cause was **"something in
the glass"** (Duncan Keddie, r18) — he said so twice at home that summer —
but would not commit the suspicion to the record because he had no proof,
writing only a question mark in his book.

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

For twenty-six years, every butterfat test at the Ostrey Hollow skimming
station read low, because a 1897 lot of glass pipettes was manufactured to
deliver 16.27 c.c. instead of the 17.6 c.c. marked on their stems — a fixed,
invisible ~7.6% error. Patrons blamed watered milk or a cheating tester; the
Association blamed the patrons; a committee blamed wet pasture. A 1925
arbitration heard all three theories, paid patrons $3,800 against a true loss
near $19,600, and dismissed tester Ansel Keddie, who had privately suspected
the glass since 1911 but had no proof. The condemned glass itself sat crated
and forgotten in the station loft until his granddaughter, Ivy Keddie, found
and measured it in 1958 — clearing him twenty-four years after his death. It
took eight records — family notes, a diary, a clerk's ledger, a supplier's
history, oral memory, and an official report — because each held only one
piece, and no one, for decades, thought to hold them side by side.
