# Notes after Retellings 1–8 (Ostrey Hollow / Larrow Valley dairy saga)

## Overview / core subject
All eight retellings concern the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (Larrow Green, on Larrow Creek) and its skimming station seven miles up-valley at Ostrey Hollow, serving farms on "Hessel Bottom" (a wet meadow). The central controversy: for ~26 years (1897–1923) the Ostrey Hollow station's butterfat tests ran suspiciously low compared to other stations. Patrons believed they were being cheated (either by watered milk claims against them, or by cream theft by the tester). A 1925 board of arbitration found "no cause established" yet paid $3,800. Only in 1958–59 was it discovered (via a granddaughter's research) that the true cause was defective pipettes (glassware) that under-delivered milk by 5%, mechanically producing low readings — exonerating tester Ansel Keddie completely, who had died in the 1930s under a cloud of suspicion.

## Timeline of major events (as reconstructed across sources)
- **1889**: Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association organized at Larrow Green; Gideon Vose elected first president (served to 1911).
- **1891**: Main creamery built at Larrow Green, on Larrow Creek (same building still standing per Loomis, 1941).
- **1892**: By-laws adopted at annual meeting, including Article VII (quoted below) — drafted by Gideon Vose.
- **1895**: Ansel Keddie goes up to Ostrey Hollow station to prepare it for opening (per Vira Toland).
- **1896**: Ostrey Hollow skimming station opens; test book "Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie." First-season (1896) average test: 3.80.
- **1897**: Station's own glass/equipment arrives — per invoice: **Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, Tarnet, dated 14 April 1897**, itemized: 6 doz. milk test pipettes (17.6 c.c.) at $0.35 = $25.20; 12 doz. test bottles (8% neck) at $0.22 = $31.68; 1 acid measure = $0.90; 1 hand tester (4-bottle) = $18.00; freight = $2.15; **total $77.93** (Ansel Keddie's letter, Retelling 3, gives the rounded figure "$77.93 for the whole outfit" as well — consistent). In use from 1 May 1897. Farm weigh book (Hessel Bottom/Brant household) begun 1 January 1897.
- **1898**: First full year with new glass; recorded average test 3.61 (this becomes the "normal" baseline, "3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since" except the 1911 anomaly).
- **1902**: Complaints first begin at Larrow Green that Ostrey Hollow milk "did not test with the rest" (per Keddie's letter).
- **1905**: Wilbur Teague comes in as bookkeeper; invents the "station-gain" ledger line himself (not an old founders' device) — tracking the discrepancy between butterfat credited to patrons on the station's test vs. butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in the station's cream. Sample entry (1898, given as typical): credited 57,760 lb; received 60,800 lb; "station gain" 3,040 lb, "five and a quarter per cent, as usual."
- **1907**: Merle Strawn takes the Ordell dairy inspection circuit (fifty-odd plants in four counties). First round: Ostrey Hollow — "Scales true against my weights. Cold room fair... tester keeps his book in his own hand, and his hand is better than most."
- **1908**: A committee investigates Hessel Bottom herds through Jan–March 1908 and reports (signed, fair copy) that "the milk is whole and the scales are honest... the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and the remedy lies with the land and not with the station."
- **1909**: Strawn visits Ostrey Hollow in fall — scales true, boiler in order, one man cautioned about a rusted seam on a can.
- **1910**: Selby Vose's own Jersey herd (at the home place) tested 4.56 (per his 1926 address). Separately, the Brant/Hessel Bottom farm weigh book records for 1910: "Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42."
- **1911**: THE MEASURE ANOMALY — the station's own measure (pipette) broke 8 May 1911; a borrowed measure (from Larrow Green, came up with the cream wagon) was used until 10 July 1911 (nine/six weeks — Keddie's letter and Ivy Keddie's talk both say "nine weeks"; Teague's memoir says "six weeks"). During this period the average test jumped to **3.80** (vs. normal 3.61) and reverted the day the station's own measure returned. Keddie's book entries: "May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." / "July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." Strawn's circuit note for 1911 (visited June, mid-anomaly): "Scales true. Watched a test through. Nothing here to note" (he apparently didn't catch/flag the anomaly). Bulletin No. 214 (State Agricultural College, 1911) is cited later (by Ivy Keddie, 1958) as testing the "wet hay" theory directly: "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture" — far too small (a fourth the size) and wrong-shaped (present in all seasons, not just winter) to explain the station's ~0.19-point year-round gap.
- **1912**: Strawn visits — scales true, no added water, cream shipped down same day it's separated ("more than some do").
- **1913**: Selby Vose made manager (serves to 1938 per Loomis). Strawn visits Larrow Green creamery two days; new manager (Vose) comes out to the platform with him, "which the last one never did."
- **1915**: Strawn orders a wash-room floor graded (holding water at the drain).
- **1916**: Strawn visits — tester (Keddie) complains patrons blame him for their figures; Strawn tells him "that is the trade." SEPARATELY: Wilbur Teague says he raised the "station gain" discrepancy with Selby Vose in 1916 and "was told to let it alone" — he did, and didn't raise it again.
- **1917**: Gideon Vose dies. Strawn visits a cheese factory then Larrow Green; notes plants generally short-handed (bad year for help), two testers doing three men's work.
- **1919**: THE CIRCULAR — a printed letter over Selby Vose's signature goes to nine Ostrey Hollow families alleging "water in the cans." Vira Toland (age 7) recalls her father reading it at the table with his hat on, reading it twice, then filing it in a drawer where it stayed till he died (his son/her brother reportedly still has it); some families kept the letter for decades (one woman pinned hers inside her trunk lid). Strawn's circuit note for 1919: "The manager... has sent a circular round to the patrons of this station about water in the cans. I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so." Vose himself (1926 address) defends the circular as his duty, says it "named nobody, accused nobody," warned that "added water would be looked for and would be dealt with."
- **1920**: Strawn visits twice (spring and fall) — scales true, no added water; cold room short of ice in fall, corrected before he left the county.
- **1921**: State begins issuing graduated burettes (in wooden cases) to field inspectors for checking measures/pipettes at plants — Strawn receives his that spring.
- **1922**: Strawn does NOT run the station's glass against his new burette this year ("the day being short and the road home being what it was"). SEPARATELY: Ansel Keddie makes up a summary leaf in his test book with yearly averages (see "Article VII / test-book" section below); this is also the year the ledger's 25-year tally period (1898–1922) ends, used for later calculations (40,000,000 lb total milk intake at Ostrey Hollow over 1898–1922, averaging 1,600,000 lb/year; average butterfat price 25¢/lb over the period — per Effie Loomis, corroborated by curator Nym's accession notes and Ivy Keddie's talk).
- **1923**: 
  - Strawn finally runs the station's pipettes against the burette. His notebook entry, dated **12 June 1923**: "Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk pipettes against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them." (This 12 June 1923 date is corroborated by Ivy Keddie's Retelling 4 and by curator Nym's Item 5/Item 9 accession notes — Item 9 is literally a photostat of this same Strawn entry.) 
  - **CONFLICT**: Selby Vose's 1926 address instead gives the condemnation date as "the twelfth of July, 1923" — one month off from all other sources (Strawn's own notebook, Ivy Keddie's talk, and the curator's catalogue all agree on 12 June).
  - Letter, Ammon & Sons to the Association, dated 2 July 1923, acknowledges the Association's order (dated 28 June 1923) for replacement pipettes/bottles, promises shipment that month, and "declines to say anything of the earlier lot."
  - New glass in use from July 1923 (Strawn's Oct. 1923 visit note: "New glass in use since July. Ran the new pipettes against the burette. They deliver their mark. Scales true.")
  - The crate of nine surviving old (condemned) pipettes, stenciled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET," was set aside in the loft over the station's intake room under a tarpaulin, where it sat until found in Feb. 1958.
- **1924**: Tests improve. Strawn's note: "Scales true, no added water, tests improved. Their book gives the year at 3.80." Ansel Keddie's own letter states: "In 1924 the average of this station was 3.80." **CONFLICT**: Selby Vose's 1926 address claims the 1924 average was **3.90** ("tests at that station rose by twenty-one hundredths and the average for 1924 stood at 3.90") — disagreeing with both Keddie's own book and Strawn's independent inspection note, both of which say 3.80.
- **1925**: 
  - January: Ostrey Hollow patrons petition the Association, claiming their milk "had never tested as their neighbors' milk tested" and asking to be made whole.
  - Board of arbitration convened at Ashlin under Alonzo Frick, sat **17 March – 4 May 1925**.
  - 17 March 1925: Ansel Keddie gives his nine-sentence statement to the board (quoted in full in Retelling 3) — denies ever taking cream, describes his meticulous, unchanging method, notes no one ever came and watched him work except the state inspector.
  - Wilbur Teague also testified, telling the board (in substance, as he repeats in his 1948 memoir) that the station was a one-man operation for 29 seasons, vulnerable to skimming, though he explicitly refused to accuse anyone by name.
  - Award (recorded by Secretary Effie Loomis, and independently quoted identically in curator Nym's Item 7): "The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account." Loomis remarks she'd never taken down a more self-contradictory sentence pair in the minute book.
  - The crate of condemned pipettes/measures was never mentioned in any of the three hearing sessions — noted pointedly by Effie Loomis as the "interesting part... generally what none of them thought worth saying."
  - **30 April 1925**: Ansel Keddie is "put out" (dismissed) — the same day, per his own letter, as (implicitly) close to the end of the hearing period. He notes: "They found no cause established and they put me out on the last day of the month, and both of those things are true at once."
  - He did not test any milk in the 1925 season itself (a new, "quick" tester replaces him — noted disapprovingly by Strawn in his fall 1925 visit: "New man at the tester's bench this year. He is quick, and quick is not the thing I look for.")
  - Vira Toland's account (Retelling 1) offers a different, harsher popular narrative: that Keddie "was taking the cream," was "caught," and this — not defective glass — explains why "the numbers went right the summer after" (1926, in her account, though other sources date the rise to 1924, i.e., before his dismissal). This is a major, explicit CONFLICT with the true cause established in 1958 (defective pipettes) and with the actual chronology (tests had already risen in 1924, a year before Keddie was ever removed, and while the same equipment/tester was still testing — undermining the "he was caught and stopped" folk theory).
- **1926**: Selby Vose gives his annual manager's address (Retelling 8; printed in the Association's annual report), 13 years into his managership. He defends the 1919 circular, insists the true cause was patrons adding water to cans and that Association "scrutiny" made it stop (pointing to the 1924 rise), dismisses the pipette condemnation as routine equipment wear/replacement ("a piece of housekeeping that appears in our own order book with a date and a price against it"), refuses to name any suspect, and says "nobody has been or will be" prosecuted. He invokes his own herd's 1910 test of 4.56 to argue Larrow Valley milk/cattle are not inherently inferior.
- **1928**: Strawn visits — station "quieter than it was"; loft over intake room "full of odds and ends" (presumably including the crate); told it's "been full since before the war."
- **1929**: Brant/Hessel Bottom farm weigh book passes to Roy Brant (Emmet Brant's son) after his father's death.
- **1931**: Bad ice year across the four counties; two plants buying ice from the railroad.
- **1934**: Strawn's visit notes tester's book "kept up, though not in the hand it used to be kept in" (implying Ansel Keddie is gone/dead by now, consistent with Ivy Keddie's later statement he died in 1934; Vira Toland says "he died in the thirties").
- **1936**: State's burette program discontinued (inspectors keep their instruments but no more issued). March 1936: Strawn's last circuit round — "Nothing to note anywhere and nothing left to say about it." Strawn's 28 circuit books (one per year, oilcloth covers, pocket size), covering 1907(ish)–1936, go to the state dairy office on his retirement, per the rules.
- **1938**: Association office moved; the 1889–1892 minute book is lost in the move (never recovered) — noted by secretary Effie Loomis in 1941 as an unfillable gap in the record. Selby Vose's managership ends this year (per Loomis).
- **1940**: Effie Loomis retires as secretary (secretary 1901–1940).
- **1941**: Loomis writes "Notes toward a history of the Association" (Retelling 2), the year after laying down the minute book.
- **1945**: Wilbur Teague retires as bookkeeper (served 1905–1945).
- **1947**: Strawn's 28 circuit notebooks go to the library of the State Agricultural College at Farland (where the 1923 volume specifically will later be found and copied, 1958).
- **1948**: Wilbur Teague writes his memoir chapter ("The Books of the Association," Chapter Nine) at Ashlin, winter 1948.
- **1950s-ish**: (implied) Vira Toland marries and lives two years at Ashlin before returning to Ostrey Hollow permanently.
- **1958**: 
  - A young woman researcher (from "the college away east" per Toland — likely Ivy Keddie, though see discrepancy noted above about "east") visits Vira Toland's area, and separately visits/pokes about the old (by-then-shut) station building, used for storage of fence wire and salt.
  - February 1958: Ivy Keddie finds the crate of nine surviving condemned pipettes in the station building loft, under a tarpaulin, stenciled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET."
  - She gathers five sets of records that had "never been in one room before": (1) the station test book (in the Association office since 1925, came to the Historical Rooms in 1959); (2) the Brant/Hessel Bottom weigh book (lent by Roy Brant); (3) Alder Corners Cheese Company test figures (Vessey County, over Redlow Ridge — copied out for her by Rosalie Cudd, niece of founder Jerome Cudd; book itself never left Vessey County); (4) Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks (at Farland library since 1947); (5) the Association's own annual ledgers.
  - **9 March 1958**: Ivy Keddie measures the nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette at Farland. Measurement sheet (quoted twice, identically, in Retellings 4 and 7): "Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for." (Two of the nine pipettes are noted elsewhere, in curator Nym's Item 5, as "chipped at the tip.")

Closing paragraph (quoted identically in Retellings 4 and 7): "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."
- **1959**: Ivy Keddie delivers her talk "What the Crate Held" to the Ordell County Historical Society at Ashlin (Retelling 4). The station test book and (eventually) the pipette crate itself come to the Historical Rooms this year (per curator Nym).

## Article VII (by-laws) — quoted in full (appears identically in Retellings 2 and 7)
> "Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."
Drafted by Gideon Vose, adopted at the annual meeting of 1892 (per Loomis). Curator Nym notes the printed pamphlet copies carry no year on their face, only "adopted at the annual meeting." Loomis stresses the final clause — "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" — as "the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business": no appeal from the station's own test, no averaging against other stations.

## The quantitative core of the mystery (as finally explained, 1958)
- Pipettes ordered/installed 1897, blown to deliver 17.6 c.c., but the crate's actual glass (measured 1958) delivered only a mean of 16.72 c.c. — short by 0.88 c.c., i.e., 95% (19/20) of the intended charge.
- This produced test readings that were proportionally low by 1/20 (5%) of the true value, across every season, every month, rain or shine — NOT explained by "wet hay" (Bulletin 214, 1911, shows wet hay effect is far too small and wrong-shaped) and NOT by cream theft or water dilution.
- Recorded ("credited") average test: 3.61 (1898 on). True average: 3.80 (3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80). Gap = 0.19 points = 5% of the true 3.80, or 5.25% of the credited 3.61 — "two percentages have been quoted... and both are right, so long as you say what they are percentages of" (Ivy Keddie). This 5.25%-of-credited figure exactly matches Wilbur Teague's independently-kept ledger "station gain" line, which for decades recorded the gain "as usual" at "five and a quarter per cent" — strong cross-source corroboration between the bookkeeper's ledger and the later physical/scientific reconstruction.
- Over 1898–1922 (25 full years): station took in **40,000,000 lb** of milk (avg 1,600,000 lb/year — per Effie Loomis and confirmed in curator Nym's notes). Credited fat: 1,444,000 lb (at 3.61%). True fat: 1,520,000 lb (at 3.80%). Difference: **76,000 lb of butterfat**, worth **$19,000** at the 25¢/lb average price over the period (Loomis's figure). The 1925 arbitration award of $3,800 was thus about one-fifth of the true amount owed ("That is a fifth of it" — Ivy Keddie).
- Because the shortfall was proportional, it hit richer milk hardest: a herd recorded at 4.56 was short 0.24 points; a herd recorded at 3.42 was short 0.18 points.
- **CONFLICT — total milk intake and average test figures disagree between sources**: Wilbur Teague's own 1948 memoir states the station "took in forty-four million pounds of milk" (44,000,000 lb, not 40,000,000 lb) over "the years the account ran," and gives "the average test of all the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, taken over the whole period" as **3.16** — a markedly different (much lower) figure than the 3.61 recorded average used everywhere else (Keddie's own book, Loomis, curator Nym, Ivy Keddie's bulletin). It's unclear whether Teague's 44M/3.16 figures cover a longer or differently-bounded period than the 40M/3.61 figures used elsewhere (his account isn't explicit about the exact year range, saying only "over the years the account ran"), but as stated they are inconsistent with the other sources and should be flagged as a discrepancy rather than silently reconciled.
- Corroborating outside check: the Alder Corners Cheese Company (Vessey County) tested milk from four Hessel Bottom men during their summer flush; where the two books overlap (14 seasons), that same milk off that same wet ground ran about 0.19 points higher at Alder Corners than it was recorded at home — matching the calculated true gap almost exactly.
- Item 16 in curator Nym's catalogue (Larrow Green creamery's own receiving/churn books) shows that over the same years the station's recorded average was 3.61, the Larrow Green average ran "a little under two tenths of a point better" — i.e., consistent with (roughly corroborates) the ~0.19-point gap.

11. **Ansel Keddie's 17 March 1925 statement to the board** (Retelling 3, full 9-sentence text; also catalogued as Item 8 by curator Nym but "not transcribed" there — full text only appears in Retelling 3): affirms he kept the book in his own hand since opening, never wrote a figure over a figure, never varied his method, "I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself."
12. **Merle Strawn's 12 June 1923 circuit-book entry** (quoted identically in Retelling 6 and as Item 9/photostat in Retelling 7): "Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk pipettes against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them."
13. **Wilbur Teague's ledger entry** (typical/1898 example, quoted in Retellings 5 and 7 identically): "Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account."
14. **Farm weigh book 1910 leaf** (Item 4, curator Nym): "Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42."
15. **The 1919 circular** — text not directly quoted in any retelling (only characterized): per Vira Toland it said "there was water in the cans coming down from Ostrey Hollow," went to nine families, was signed by manager Selby Vose. Per Selby Vose's own 1926 description: "It warned the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, in temperate language, that added water would be looked for and would be dealt with. It named nobody. It accused nobody."

## Conflicts / oddities catalogued across sources (explicit — do not resolve, just track)
1. **Cause of the low tests**: Vira Toland's folk account (Retelling 1) says Ansel Keddie was "caught" skimming cream and was put out for it, and that this is why the numbers "went right" the following summer. This is directly contradicted by (a) Keddie's own sworn denial (Retelling 3), (b) Merle Strawn's independent inspection findings across decades never finding added water or discrepancy of that kind, (c) the 1925 board's own finding of "no cause established," and (d) the definitive 1958 scientific finding (Bulletin 471) that "no milk was watered... and no cream was stolen." Toland's account is essentially the debunked popular myth.
2. **Timing problem with the "caught him" theory**: tests had already risen (to 3.80, per Keddie's own book and Strawn's inspection) in the 1924 season — a full year before Keddie was dismissed (30 April 1925) — while Keddie was still the tester. This undermines the notion that his removal caused the improvement; instead, the timing lines up with the July 1923 replacement of the defective pipettes.
3. **Selby Vose's persistent alternative theory**: Even in his 1926 address — after the arbitration, and while acknowledging the pipette condemnation as a fact — Vose insists the true cause was patrons adding water to cans, that Association "scrutiny" (not new glass) caused the 1924 improvement, and frames the pipette replacement as mere routine wear ("a piece of housekeeping"). This is his own persistent narrative, seemingly never revised even by 1926, and is contradicted by the later 1958 finding — but note Vose's address (1926) predates that finding by decades, so there's no indication he ever learned of/accepted it.
4. **Date of pipette condemnation**: Strawn's own notebook (Retelling 6) and its photostat (curator Nym, Item 9), plus Ivy Keddie's talk (Retelling 4) and the crate's catalogue entry (Item 5), all agree on **12 June 1923**. Selby Vose's 1926 address instead states **"the twelfth of July, 1923."** This is an unresolved one-month discrepancy, with Vose as the outlier against three independent, more contemporaneous/documentary sources.
5. **1924 average test value**: Keddie's own book and Strawn's inspection both independently record **3.80** for 1924. Selby Vose's 1926 address instead claims **3.90** ("rose by twenty-one hundredths... stood at 3.90"). Vose is again the outlier.
6. **Total milk intake / overall average test figures**: Effie Loomis (1941), curator Nym's accession notes (1972), and Ivy Keddie's calculations (1958) all use **40,000,000 lb over 1898–1922** and a recorded average of **3.61**. Wilbur Teague's own memoir (1948) instead states **44,000,000 lb** total and an overall average of **3.16** — significantly different figures from the same underlying ledgers he himself kept. Not reconciled by any source; flagged as an internal discrepancy.
7. **Duration of the 1911 measure substitution**: Ansel Keddie's own letter and Ivy Keddie's 1959 talk both say the anomaly lasted "nine weeks" (8 May–10 July 1911). Wilbur Teague's 1948 memoir instead says "six weeks." Minor discrepancy in duration (nine vs. six weeks) though the substance — a temporary borrowed measure causing anomalously higher, i.e., more accurate, readings — agrees.
8. **Curatorial mislabeling of Items 3 and 4** (see Objects section above): Item 3 (station test book, actually Ansel Keddie's, signed "A. Keddie") is labeled by curator Nym as "kept by E. Brant"; Item 4 (Hessel Bottom farm weigh book, with clear Brant-family provenance/custody chain) is labeled "kept by A. Keddie." These attributions appear swapped — likely a cataloguing error by curator Nym (or a transcription slip), never flagged as such within Retelling 7 itself.
9. **"No cause established" vs. $3,800 award**: Multiple narrators (Loomis, Teague, Vose himself) separately register the built-in self-contradiction of the 1925 board's finding — clearing everyone of wrongdoing while nonetheless paying out $3,800 "without admission." Loomis explicitly flags this as illogical ("it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once"); Vose defends it in his address as a goodwill/peace-buying gesture rather than an admission.
10. **The crate/condemned measures were never mentioned at the 1925 hearing** — explicitly noted as an omission by Effie Loomis (the secretary who transcribed the entire hearing) in her 1941 notes, and independently confirmed by Ivy Keddie's 1959 talk ("The crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all"). This is presented as the central irony of the whole affair: the physical evidence that would have solved the case sat, already condemned and in storage, throughout the very hearing meant to investigate the problem.
11. **Identity/description of the 1958 visiting researcher**: Vira Toland (1979) describes a young woman visiting in 1958 from "the college away east," identified as "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed" — very likely Ivy Keddie (matches the year and general activity of poking around the shut station), but Toland does not name her, and the "away east" college description is not corroborated (Ivy Keddie's own account places her at Farland, direction unspecified). Treat as probable but not certain identification.
12. **Emmet Brant's family relationships**: Orra Keddie (Ansel's wife) is Emmet Brant's sister and was, per Vira Toland, also a first cousin of "old Mrs. Vose" — meaning the tester's wife and the accusing manager's family were linked by blood, a fact "everybody up here knew and nobody wrote down anywhere" (per Toland) — this specific claim (Orra/Mrs. Vose cousinship) appears only in Toland's oral account and is not corroborated or contradicted by any other retelling.
