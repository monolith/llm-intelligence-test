# Answers — Part 2: Section A (Stories 5–8) and Section B

## Story 5 — The Nine Weeks of 1911

On 8 May 1911 the station's own measuring glass broke — the date is fixed by Orra Keddie's own diary entry that week ("A. home cross about a broken measure"). A borrowed measure was substituted for nine weeks, until 10 July 1911: this duration is corroborated by five independent sources (Ansel Keddie's own test book, his granddaughter Ivy Keddie's 1959 talk, Roy Brant's farm-level weigh-book record, Vaught's Bulletin 471, and Orin Falke's 1959 county report), against a rival "six weeks" figure shared only by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague and veterinarian Dr. Kepp — two sources that may share a common (mistaken) origin rather than being independent.

During the nine weeks, with the herds, pasture, season, tester and procedures all held constant, the station's average test rose to 3.80; the day the station's own glass returned, it fell back to 3.61. Roy Brant's own weigh book independently recorded the same farm-level rise and fall, "and he could make nothing of it." Ansel Keddie entered only a question mark in his book, unwilling to accuse without proof, though he told his family, per son Duncan, that "it was something in the glass." This episode is best understood as an accidental controlled experiment: because only the measuring equipment changed, and every other variable was held constant, it demonstrates — independently of, and 47 years before, the 1958 pipette measurement — that the deficiency traveled with the station's own equipment, not with the tester's conduct, the patrons' milk, or the season. It does not by itself establish the mechanism (a proportional under-delivery from the pipette bore) or the precise magnitude of the error; that required the direct 1958 measurement.

## Story 6 — The Circular and the Silence (1919)

In June 1919, manager Selby Vose circulated a written warning to Ostrey Hollow patrons alleging added water in their milk. The number of families addressed is disputed: the majority figure, given by several later narrators (Roy Brant/Rundle, and explicitly by Hazel Brant, "nine families, six farms"), is nine; but Orra Keddie's own contemporaneous 1919 diary entry states her husband delivered the circulars personally "to eleven families." As a primary eyewitness account written at the time, against a figure repeated later by non-eyewitnesses, this conflict cannot be confidently resolved either way and is left open.

Ansel Keddie refused to mail the circulars or leave them on the collection platform; he delivered each by hand and stood at the door while the recipient read it — an act buttermaker Hobart Sill later praised as "the only plain thing that office ever did." Two days later, Emmet Brant confronted Ansel Keddie directly and personally in the farmyard (per Orra's diary, correcting an earlier account that framed the rift as being between Orra and her brother) — not over the water accusation itself, but over Keddie's having carried the paper himself without publicly repudiating it. Keddie replied he had done it so no other man would have to. Emmet did not enter the Keddie house again before his death in 1929. Per schoolteacher Dell Rundle, the two ends of the valley "did not speak civilly to one another for six years," an estrangement that extended to the schoolchildren "without a word of it being explained."

## Story 7 — The Board of Arbitration, 1925

The patrons petitioned in January 1925. A three-member board was formed, chaired by Ashlin attorney Alonzo Frick, who says he was chosen "because I was the one of the three who kept no cow." The first session sat at the Association's Ashlin office on 17 March 1925 — a date fixed independently by Frick's own memoir and Orra Keddie's diary. The second and third sessions were held at Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's own weigh room — confirmed by four independent sources (Frick, eyewitness Cleve Oram, who stood outside on the platform in the rain, Orra's diary, and clerk Bertram Kyle), overturning an earlier assumption that the entire hearing sat at Ashlin.

The exhibits before the board, per matching lists independently supplied by both chairman Frick and clerk Kyle, were only four: the station's own test book (1896–1924, in Keddie's hand), the station receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book (lent for the purpose), and the state dairy inspector's 1923 circuit notebook. The condemned glassware itself was not among them — it remained, unexamined, in the station loft. Three explanations were argued: added water (Selby Vose), cream theft (obliquely, by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague), and wet hay (via the 1908 report). Ansel Keddie was dismissed 30 April 1925, four days before the board reported. On 4 May 1925 it found "no cause established" and awarded the patrons $3,800 "without admission" — a figure confirmed by four independent sources (Loomis, Nym, Falke, Kyle) against Oram's single, secondhand "$3,500."

## Story 8 — The Crate in the Loft (1958)

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie — Ansel Keddie's granddaughter (not, as a 1958 newspaper mistakenly reported, his great-niece; the error was explicitly corrected the following year by county agent Orin Falke), then a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland — climbed into the loft over the Ostrey Hollow station's intake room and found the crate of glassware condemned by the state dairy inspector on 12 June 1923. That date is the best-supported of several offered (five independent sources: Strawn's notebook and its photostat, Ivy Keddie's own talk, curator Nym, and Bulletin 471's conclusion, against isolated single-source claims of July, 1921, or 1922 from other, later narrators). Nine pipettes had survived intact — confirmed by Ivy Keddie's own measurement sheet, Vaught's Bulletin 471, Falke's report, and independently by lifelong local witness Cleve Oram's recollection of the crate's contents, against a single outlying claim of "eleven" shared by a pamphlet footnote and a newspaper account that may share a common secondhand source.

On 9 March 1958 Ivy Keddie measured the nine pipettes against a calibrated burette at Farland: marked 17.6 c.c., they delivered on average 16.72 c.c. — a shortfall of 0.88 c.c., about one part in twenty (5%). Bulletin No. 471 (Vaught and Keddie, October 1958) concluded no milk had been watered and no cream stolen: the station's tests had run low by that same proportion for the entire span the defective glass was in service (1 May 1897 – 12 June 1923), crediting patrons an average of 3.61 against a true 3.80 — a 0.19-point gap — for 76,000 lb of unpaid butterfat, worth roughly $19,000 at the era's average price, against the $3,800 the patrons had actually received in 1925. Rosalie Cudd's independently kept Alder Corners test records for the same four Hessel Bottom haulers, across sixteen separate summers (1915–1930), averaged about 3.80 — corroborating the "true" figure from an entirely unrelated source. The finding reached the public via the Ashlin Chronicle (3 October 1958) and was formally reconciled, with corrections to the newspaper's errors, in Falke's 1959 county report.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**
Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter: her father, Duncan Keddie, was Ansel and Orra's son. This is stated explicitly and emphatically by Duncan Keddie himself ("my daughter is Ivy... which makes her my father's granddaughter... I have already twice seen it printed otherwise"), and confirmed independently when county agent Orin Falke corrected his own 1959 report after verifying the point ("I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece"). The contrary "great-niece" claim traces to a single source — the Ashlin Chronicle's 1958 news story, assembled in a day and a half from a press release and phone calls — and is an established error, not a genuine competing claim.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**
They were second cousins: Ivy's father Duncan Keddie and Hazel's father Roy Brant were themselves first cousins (their parents, Orra Keddie and Emmet Brant, being brother and sister), making Duncan's and Roy's children — Ivy and Hazel — second cousins, children of first cousins. This chain is stated explicitly by Duncan Keddie, corroborated independently by Roy Brant, and confirmed again by Hazel Brant herself, all of whom separately and pointedly reject the alternative labels ("aunt and niece," "cousins once removed") that circulated locally. Cleve Oram's claim that they were "first cousins once removed" is a single outlying error from a non-family observer, outweighed by four family-insider sources describing the same chain identically. The intervening generations are unusually fully documented here and leave nothing open.

**B3. Selby Vose's father.**
This cannot be fully resolved from the sources. Two independent insider sources call Selby the *son* of founder Gideon Vose: Ansel Keddie's son Duncan Keddie ("not, as I have heard it given, his nephew") and longtime Association clerk Bertram Kyle ("the son, whatever is said at Ashlin... I have heard the nephew story twice... and it is nonsense"). Two other independent sources call him the *nephew*: Association secretary Effie Loomis and schoolteacher Dell Rundle, the latter reporting that Selby himself "used to say that his uncle wrote by-laws" — a reported habitual remark, though not a verbatim quotation, and so suggestive rather than conclusive. With credible, explicit, insider testimony on both sides in a 2–2 split, this is left open rather than forced.

It matters because Selby Vose, on either account, inherited his managerial authority within a family line running back to the founder who wrote the by-laws establishing payment-by-test — the very mechanism that made the pipette defect costly. That inherited authority may help explain both his confidence in publicly accusing the patrons in 1919 and clerk Kyle's account of an incurious managerial style (signing ledger sheets without reading the lines above the totals) that allowed the station-gain evidence disproving that accusation to go unexamined for decades.

**B4. The Vose family and the Keddie family.**
The sources do not establish any real connection. Cleve Oram reports a vague local rumor ("The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort... I could not draw you the line on paper"), attributed to his mother's hearsay. Duncan Keddie addresses this exact rumor directly, in a section titled "A thing that is said and is not so," and denies it flatly, citing his mother Orra's own encyclopedic genealogical knowledge of the valley as the more authoritative source ("she never once put a Vose in it"). Given Duncan Keddie's specific, sourced denial against Oram's admittedly unverifiable hearsay, the rumor is treated as unfounded.
