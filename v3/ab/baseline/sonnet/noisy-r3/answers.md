# Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

*Method note: throughout, conflicts are resolved by counting independent sources (not repetitions of the same figure), preferring quoted primary documents and contemporaneous records (invoices, diary entries written at the time) over later memory, and using arithmetic cross-checks where the sources permit them. Where evidence is genuinely split among independent, credible sources, this is stated rather than guessed.*

## Story 1 — Founding of the Association and the Opening of Ostrey Hollow (1889–1899)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was organized in 1889 at a late-running meeting in the Larrow Green schoolhouse, with Gideon Vose elected first president and drafter of the by-laws (Rundle). The main creamery was built at Larrow Green in 1891, on the creek below the mill; the churn was set up the same year. Article VII of the by-laws established payment by butterfat test rather than by milk weight — the mechanism that made everything that follows possible. Its adoption year is disputed three ways (1891, Rundle; 1892, Loomis; 1893, Grigg), and the county's own 1959 investigation (Falke) found the Association's early minute book lost in an office move and explicitly declined to fix a date — this specific date cannot be determined from the sources.

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up-valley, to serve Hessel Bottom. It opened 4 May 1896. Buttermaker Hobart Sill personally lent the new station his own glassware from the Larrow Green bench, since its own outfit was not ready; his equipment was returned in spring 1897, one piece chipped. The station's own outfit was ordered from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet: invoice dated 14 April 1897, totaling $77.93 (verified by addition: $25.20 + $31.68 + $0.90 + $18.00 + $2.15), for six dozen (72) milk-test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. at $0.35 each, twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, a hand tester, and freight. It went into service 1 May 1897 — confirmed by the invoice itself, by Orra Keddie's diary entry of that date ("A. says the station's own glass came up"), and by Bulletin 471's own concluding paragraph; later claims of 1898 (Vaught's bulletin introduction, Kyle's clerk memoir) are outweighed and appear to be transposition errors. Ansel Keddie was tester from the opening. The first (partial) season's intake cannot be fixed precisely: Sill recalled "about nine hundred thousand pounds," Kyle recalled "a good eleven hundred thousand," and Kyle himself notes his figure is not from any ledger — this remains undetermined.

## Story 2 — The Weigh Book and the Brants of Hessel Bottom

Emmet Brant farmed the largest place on Hessel Bottom (six farms, ~160 wet acres) and hauled more milk to Ostrey Hollow than any other patron. He began a private weigh book on 1 January 1897 — the best-supported date, given independently by curator Nym's catalogue, Alonzo Frick's 1925 exhibit list, and his own grandson-in-law Duncan Keddie's notes (three independent sources), against his own son Roy Brant's later recollection of 1898 and granddaughter Hazel Brant's recollection of 1900 (both single, decades-later family memories). The book recorded two columns daily — his own barn-scale weight and the station's returned figure — which, per Roy Brant, "always agreed," establishing that weighing (as opposed to testing) was never at issue. Its best-documented leaf, quoted identically by multiple narrators, reads: "Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even... Paid on a test of 3.42."

Emmet Brant died in 1929; the book passed to his son Roy Brant, who kept it "exactly the same way" until lending it — not giving it, a distinction both Roy and Hazel insist on — to his niece's daughter Ivy Keddie in 1958 for her research at Farland. Roy died in 1962; the book passed to his daughter Hazel Brant, who donated it to the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1963, where it remains, available to "anybody who wants" to see it.

## Story 3 — Ansel and Orra Keddie: A Marriage Across the Complaint

Ansel Keddie (b. 1861, d. 1934) married Orra Brant (b. 1866 per her diary's editor, though Duncan Keddie's notes give 1868 — a minor, unresolved discrepancy) in 1889. Orra was Emmet Brant's full sister — resolved against Hazel Brant's lone claim of a "half sister, by his father's second wife," which is directly contradicted by three independent sources: Roy Brant's own explicit denial of "any story with a stepmother in it," Duncan Keddie's unqualified treatment of the siblingship, and Orra's own diary, which calls Emmet "my brother" throughout without qualification. Ansel served as Ostrey Hollow's tester for 29 seasons, from the 1896 opening until his dismissal 30 April 1925.

Orra's diary (kept 1897–1930, with a final leaf added 1934) is the fullest surviving firsthand record of the household: the couple's reaction to the first public complaints (March 1902 — "it is not your cows and not your hands... but it is my figures they will look at"); the 1908 committee's visit; a broken measure noted 8 May 1911, matching the documented start of that year's nine-week equipment anomaly; the 1919 circular, which Ansel refused to mail and instead delivered by hand; his dismissal ("Twenty-nine seasons and a letter... He ate [his supper]"); a post-dismissal career in carpentry; and his death, 21 February 1934, with the imputation of dishonesty still on him. His widow outlived him fourteen years, dying in 1948, never seeing him cleared. A final loose leaf records his one recorded remark on the subject: "a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."

## Story 4 — Three Theories for a Quarter Century (1902–1919)

Complaints about Ostrey Hollow's low tests began in 1902 and recurred every decade after. In January 1908 the Association appointed a three-man investigating committee chaired by Nyle Grigg, a non-patron with (he states) "no interest whatever in the answer." The committee sampled Hessel Bottom's six herds, drew and sealed its own milk samples, tested station scales against a known weight and against patrons' own scales, and inspected water supplies — over January, February and March 1908, per the report's own text (Grigg's 1919 covering letter instead claims sampling ran "through the whole of 1908," an internal self-contradiction within his own reprinted document). The committee's finding: "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter," with the shortfall attributed to Hessel Bottom's wet, sour bog hay.

The wet-hay theory was revived with a scientific gloss in 1926 by Dr. Roland Kepp, a veterinarian, who attributed it to acid, mineral-poor bog soil forcing cows to "rob their own frame," giving a gap figure of 0.21 point. Both Grigg (1919) and Kepp (1926) independently — but identically — misquote the college's actual 1911 feeding trial (Bulletin No. 214) as finding a "two tenths" depression in winter feeding; its real, verbatim finding was "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and... nothing... on summer pasture," a four-fold overstatement shared by both men, probably reflecting a common garbled secondhand source rather than independent errors. County agent Orin Falke's 1959 report does the arithmetic explicitly: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July," rejecting the wet-hay theory as insufficient to the observed, flat, all-season deficiency. A rival theory — that the manager suspected added water, and an officer obliquely suspected the tester of theft — is treated in Stories 6–8.


# Answers — Part 2: Section A (Stories 5–8) and Section B

## Story 5 — The Nine Weeks of 1911

On 8 May 1911 the station's own measuring glass broke — the date is fixed by Orra Keddie's own diary entry that week ("A. home cross about a broken measure"). A borrowed measure was substituted for nine weeks, until 10 July 1911: this duration is corroborated by five independent sources (Ansel Keddie's own test book, his granddaughter Ivy Keddie's 1959 talk, Roy Brant's farm-level weigh-book record, Vaught's Bulletin 471, and Orin Falke's 1959 county report), against a rival "six weeks" figure shared only by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague and veterinarian Dr. Kepp — two sources that may share a common (mistaken) origin rather than being independent.

During the nine weeks, with the herds, pasture, season, tester and procedures all held constant, the station's average test rose to 3.80; the day the station's own glass returned, it fell back to 3.61. Roy Brant's own weigh book independently recorded the same farm-level rise and fall, "and he could make nothing of it." Ansel Keddie entered only a question mark in his book, unwilling to accuse without proof, though he told his family, per son Duncan, that "it was something in the glass." This episode is best understood as an accidental controlled experiment: because only the measuring equipment changed, and every other variable was held constant, it demonstrates — independently of, and 47 years before, the 1958 pipette measurement — that the deficiency traveled with the station's own equipment, not with the tester's conduct, the patrons' milk, or the season. It does not by itself establish the mechanism (a proportional under-delivery from the pipette bore) or the precise magnitude of the error; that required the direct 1958 measurement.

## Story 6 — The Circular and the Silence (1919)

In June 1919, manager Selby Vose circulated a written warning to Ostrey Hollow patrons alleging added water in their milk. The number of families addressed is disputed: the majority figure, given by several later narrators (Roy Brant/Rundle, and explicitly by Hazel Brant, "nine families, six farms"), is nine; but Orra Keddie's own contemporaneous 1919 diary entry states her husband delivered the circulars personally "to eleven families." As a primary eyewitness account written at the time, against a figure repeated later by non-eyewitnesses, this conflict cannot be confidently resolved either way and is left open.

Ansel Keddie refused to mail the circulars or leave them on the collection platform; he delivered each by hand and stood at the door while the recipient read it — an act buttermaker Hobart Sill later praised as "the only plain thing that office ever did." Two days later, Emmet Brant confronted Ansel Keddie directly and personally in the farmyard (per Orra's diary, correcting an earlier account that framed the rift as being between Orra and her brother) — not over the water accusation itself, but over Keddie's having carried the paper himself without publicly repudiating it. Keddie replied he had done it so no other man would have to. Emmet did not enter the Keddie house again before his death in 1929. Per schoolteacher Dell Rundle, the two ends of the valley "did not speak civilly to one another for six years," an estrangement that extended to the schoolchildren "without a word of it being explained."

## Story 7 — The Board of Arbitration, 1925

The patrons petitioned in January 1925. A three-member board was formed, chaired by Ashlin attorney Alonzo Frick, who says he was chosen "because I was the one of the three who kept no cow." The first session sat at the Association's Ashlin office on 17 March 1925 — a date fixed independently by Frick's own memoir and Orra Keddie's diary. The second and third sessions were held at Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's own weigh room — confirmed by four independent sources (Frick, eyewitness Cleve Oram, who stood outside on the platform in the rain, Orra's diary, and clerk Bertram Kyle), overturning an earlier assumption that the entire hearing sat at Ashlin.

The exhibits before the board, per matching lists independently supplied by both chairman Frick and clerk Kyle, were only four: the station's own test book (1896–1924, in Keddie's hand), the station receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book (lent for the purpose), and the state dairy inspector's 1923 circuit notebook. The condemned glassware itself was not among them — it remained, unexamined, in the station loft. Three explanations were argued: added water (Selby Vose), cream theft (obliquely, by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague), and wet hay (via the 1908 report). Ansel Keddie was dismissed 30 April 1925, four days before the board reported. On 4 May 1925 it found "no cause established" and awarded the patrons $3,800 "without admission" — a figure confirmed by four independent sources (Loomis, Nym, Falke, Kyle) against Oram's single, secondhand "$3,500."

## Story 8 — The Crate in the Loft (1958)

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie — Ansel Keddie's granddaughter (not, as a 1958 newspaper mistakenly reported, his great-niece; the error was explicitly corrected the following year by county agent Orin Falke), then a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland — climbed into the loft over the Ostrey Hollow station's intake room and found the crate of glassware condemned by the state dairy inspector on 12 June 1923. That date is the best-supported of several offered (five independent sources: Strawn's notebook and its photostat, Ivy Keddie's own talk, curator Nym, and Bulletin 471's conclusion, against isolated single-source claims of July, 1921, or 1922 from other, later narrators). Nine pipettes had survived intact — confirmed by Ivy Keddie's own measurement sheet, Vaught's Bulletin 471, Falke's report, and independently by lifelong local witness Cleve Oram's recollection of the crate's contents, against a single outlying claim of "eleven" shared by a pamphlet footnote and a newspaper account that may share a common secondhand source.

On 9 March 1958 Ivy Keddie measured the nine pipettes against a calibrated burette at Farland: marked 17.6 c.c., they delivered on average 16.72 c.c. — a shortfall of 0.88 c.c., about one part in twenty (5%). Bulletin No. 471 (Vaught and Keddie, October 1958) concluded no milk had been watered and no cream stolen: the station's tests had run low by that same proportion for the entire span the defective glass was in service (1 May 1897 – 12 June 1923), crediting patrons an average of 3.61 against a true 3.80 — a 0.19-point gap — for 76,000 lb of unpaid butterfat, worth roughly $19,000 at the era's average price, against the $3,800 the patrons had actually received in 1925. Rosalie Cudd's independently kept Alder Corners test records for the same four Hessel Bottom haulers, across sixteen separate summers (1915–1930), averaged about 3.80 — corroborating the "true" figure from an entirely unrelated source. The finding reached the public via the Ashlin Chronicle (3 October 1958) and was formally reconciled, with corrections to the newspaper's errors, in Falke's 1959 county report.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**
Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter: her father, Duncan Keddie, was Ansel and Orra's son. This is stated explicitly and emphatically by Duncan Keddie himself ("my daughter is Ivy... which makes her my father's granddaughter... I have already twice seen it printed otherwise"), and confirmed independently when county agent Orin Falke corrected his own 1959 report after verifying the point ("I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece"). The contrary "great-niece" claim traces to a single source — the Ashlin Chronicle's 1958 news story, assembled in a day and a half from a press release and phone calls — and is an established error, not a genuine competing claim.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**
They were second cousins: Ivy's father Duncan Keddie and Hazel's father Roy Brant were themselves first cousins (their parents, Orra Keddie and Emmet Brant, being brother and sister), making Duncan's and Roy's children — Ivy and Hazel — second cousins, children of first cousins. This chain is stated explicitly by Duncan Keddie, corroborated independently by Roy Brant, and confirmed again by Hazel Brant herself, all of whom separately and pointedly reject the alternative labels ("aunt and niece," "cousins once removed") that circulated locally. Cleve Oram's claim that they were "first cousins once removed" is a single outlying error from a non-family observer, outweighed by four family-insider sources describing the same chain identically. The intervening generations are unusually fully documented here and leave nothing open.

**B3. Selby Vose's father.**
This cannot be fully resolved from the sources. Two independent insider sources call Selby the *son* of founder Gideon Vose: Ansel Keddie's son Duncan Keddie ("not, as I have heard it given, his nephew") and longtime Association clerk Bertram Kyle ("the son, whatever is said at Ashlin... I have heard the nephew story twice... and it is nonsense"). Two other independent sources call him the *nephew*: Association secretary Effie Loomis and schoolteacher Dell Rundle, the latter reporting that Selby himself "used to say that his uncle wrote by-laws" — a reported habitual remark, though not a verbatim quotation, and so suggestive rather than conclusive. With credible, explicit, insider testimony on both sides in a 2–2 split, this is left open rather than forced.

It matters because Selby Vose, on either account, inherited his managerial authority within a family line running back to the founder who wrote the by-laws establishing payment-by-test — the very mechanism that made the pipette defect costly. That inherited authority may help explain both his confidence in publicly accusing the patrons in 1919 and clerk Kyle's account of an incurious managerial style (signing ledger sheets without reading the lines above the totals) that allowed the station-gain evidence disproving that accusation to go unexamined for decades.

**B4. The Vose family and the Keddie family.**
The sources do not establish any real connection. Cleve Oram reports a vague local rumor ("The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort... I could not draw you the line on paper"), attributed to his mother's hearsay. Duncan Keddie addresses this exact rumor directly, in a section titled "A thing that is said and is not so," and denies it flatly, citing his mother Orra's own encyclopedic genealogical knowledge of the valley as the more authoritative source ("she never once put a Vose in it"). Given Duncan Keddie's specific, sourced denial against Oram's admittedly unverifiable hearsay, the rumor is treated as unfounded.


# Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math

**C1.**
(a) Shortfall proportion = (marked − delivered) / marked = (17.6 − 16.72) / 17.6 = 0.88 / 17.6 = **0.05, i.e. one part in twenty (5%)**. This uses the primary, twice-quoted 1958 measurement sheet figure of 16.72 c.c. (Ivy Keddie's own measurement, corroborated by curator Nym), which is self-consistent with Bulletin 471's own concluding paragraph ("low by one part in twenty"). Vaught's bulletin *introduction* instead uses 16.27 c.c., yielding a different, larger shortfall (0.295 point) — but this figure conflicts with the bulletin's own conclusion and is not used here; see E1.

(b) True average = recorded average ÷ 0.95 = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. Shortfall = 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 of a point**. This matches both Bulletin 471's conclusion and county agent Falke's independently stated "nineteen hundredths."

(c) **76,000 lb**, per Bulletin 471's concluding paragraph — and independently reproducible by direct calculation (see C3), which cross-validates it against the bulletin's own worked total of 118,000 lb (based on the internally conflicting 16.27 c.c. figure), which is not used here.

(d) At the era's average butterfat price of about 25 cents/lb (Effie Loomis; independently corroborated by Roy Brant's "about twenty-five cents... over the whole stretch"): 76,000 × $0.25 = **about $19,000** — which matches Loomis's own independently-stated dollar figure almost exactly, a strong cross-check. Against the $3,800 arbitration award actually paid, the patrons received **$3,800 / $19,000 = 20%, i.e. one-fifth**, of what they were later calculated to be owed.

**C2.** (Emmet Brant, largest patron, 1910: 200,000 lb delivered, credited on a test of 3.42.)
(a) Credited butterfat = 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb**.
(b) Applying the same proportional (ratio) correction established in C1 — true test = recorded ÷ 0.95 = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60 — true butterfat = 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb. Owed and not credited = 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb**.
*(Note: if instead a flat +0.19-point correction is assumed, as Hazel Brant's description of the gap as "flat as a floor" might suggest, the true test would be 3.61, true butterfat 7,220 lb, and the shortfall 380 lb. The sources do not allow a definitive choice between the proportional and flat models at the individual-patron level; the proportional model is preferred here because it matches the documented physical mechanism — a fixed-ratio measurement error — rather than a farmer's later impression of the pattern.)*
(c) At the 1910 price of 24 cents/lb (Roy Brant): 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40** (or, under the flat-gap alternative, 380 × $0.24 = $91.20).

**C3.** (Full delivery years: 40,000,000 lb of milk received, per Bulletin 471, independently corroborated by clerk Kyle's "forty million pounds... sixteen hundred thousand pounds a year.")
(a) Credited = 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**.
(b) Actually delivered (true average 3.80) = 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lb**.
(c) Difference = 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 lb, matching C1(c) exactly. As a percentage, the base matters: expressed as a percentage of the **true (actual) total delivered**, 76,000 / 1,520,000 = **5.0% exactly**, matching the bulletin's own "one part in twenty" language. Expressed instead as a percentage of the **credited total**, 76,000 / 1,444,000 ≈ **5.26%**. The bulletin's own phrasing indicates the first (of true delivered) is the intended basis.

**C4.**
(a) **$77.93** — the 1897 Ammon & Sons invoice, verified by addition of its own line items ($25.20 + $31.68 + $0.90 + $18.00 + $2.15), and reproduced identically in the firm's own 1949 history.
(b) **72 pipettes (six dozen)** at $0.35 each = $25.20, exactly matching the invoice's own line total — this arithmetic decisively confirms "six dozen" over Vaught's rival "five dozen" (60 pipettes), which would total only $21.00 and does not match the invoice. Shortfall per pipette = 17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88 c.c.**

**C5.**
(a) The faulty glassware was physically in service from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 — about **26 years and 6 weeks**. The money calculation in C1–C3, however, runs over **25 full delivery years**, because 1897 and 1923 were both partial years (the glass entered service partway through 1897 and was condemned partway through 1923), leaving only 1898–1922 inclusive as full calendar years of affected deliveries. The two numbers differ because one measures elapsed calendar time and the other counts only complete years of milk deliveries used as the basis for the shortfall calculation.
(b) **Cannot be determined precisely.** Two figures are offered for the station's first (partial) season intake — "about nine hundred thousand pounds" (buttermaker Hobart Sill, present at the time) and "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" (clerk Bertram Kyle, decades later) — and Kyle explicitly states his own figure is not drawn from any ledger. Neither is a documentary record; the true figure is not established.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran 8 May – 10 July 1911 (nine weeks), fixed by Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary entry and corroborated by four further independent sources (Ansel Keddie's own book, Ivy Keddie's talk, Roy Brant's weigh book, Vaught's bulletin, and Falke's report). The variable was the measuring glass itself — the station's own (defective) measure was temporarily replaced by a borrowed one; the herds, pasture, season, tester, and procedures were all held constant. The result: tests rose from 3.61 to 3.80 for the nine weeks, then fell back the moment the station's own glass resumed. This establishes, independently of any laboratory measurement, that the cause traveled with the specific physical equipment, not with the tester's conduct, the cows, the feed, or the season — because everything else was unchanged and only the instrument varied. It does **not** establish the mechanism (a proportional under-delivery from a mis-blown pipette bore) or its exact magnitude; that required the direct 1958 measurement of the actual pipettes against a calibrated burette. Nor does it, by itself, prove the borrowed glass was correctly calibrated — only that it produced different, and (with hindsight) more accurate, results.

**D2.** Four independent reasons, from different narrators, rule out cream theft by the tester:
1. The 1911 measure-substitution interval (Duncan Keddie, Orra Keddie's diary, Roy Brant, Falke's report): the same tester, under the same conditions, produced higher (correct) readings the moment the equipment changed — inconsistent with personal manipulation, which would not track a hardware swap.
2. Roy Brant's weigh book: his own farm-scale weight and the station's intake weight "always agreed," across decades — ruling out physical diversion of milk at intake.
3. The Association's own "station gain" ledger line (kept by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague, copied yearly by clerk Bertram Kyle, and belatedly noticed by Dell Rundle): cream arriving at the main creamery from Ostrey Hollow was consistently *heavier* than the amount credited to patrons, every year. Theft would produce the opposite pattern — cream arriving light, not heavy.
4. Rosalie Cudd's independently kept Alder Corners test book: the same Hessel Bottom farmers' milk, tested by a different person on different equipment at an unrelated cheese factory, consistently averaged the "true" figure (~3.80) — showing the deficiency was tied to the Ostrey Hollow station specifically, not to any person's handling of the milk.
These are independent because they rest on four different record-keepers and four different kinds of evidence (a real-time equipment swap, a private farm ledger, the Association's own accounting, and a wholly external business's records), none derived from another. Reason 3 (the station-gain ledger) would still hold even if the glassware had never been measured in 1958 — it was sitting in the Association's own open books the entire time. As to whether the bookkeeper who advanced the theft accusation ever questioned those same entries: **no**. Per clerk Kyle, who worked alongside him, Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years... the figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people" — he wrote the very "station gain" line that refuted his own oblique accusation and never revisited it.

**D3.** The manager (Selby Vose) certainly knew the *recorded numbers* ran persistently low — that is precisely why he circulated the 1919 circular accusing patrons of watering their milk. But the sources do not show he understood *why*. Clerk Bertram Kyle, who sat outside his door for 25 years, states plainly that Vose "read the totals and he never read the lines above them... He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign" — meaning Vose personally signed the very station-gain ledger entries that would have pointed away from the patrons and the tester and toward the equipment, every year, without engaging with their detail. So: he knew the fact of the low tests, but the sources indicate he never connected it to the evidence already in his own signed records, and there is no evidence he suspected the glassware before its 1923 condemnation.

## Section F — Theory

**Causal theory.** In April 1897 the Ostrey Hollow station received a lot of 72 pipettes from Ammon & Sons, each marked to deliver 17.6 c.c. of milk but — because of an undetected fault in reground blowing molds (per the manufacturer's own later conjecture) — actually delivering only about 16.72 c.c., a constant proportional shortfall of about 5%. Because payment was made strictly by test, and because a short charge of milk into a bottle graduated for a full charge yields a butterfat reading short in the same fixed proportion, every test made with that glass read low by the same ~5% (0.19 point at the station's ~3.6–3.8% average), regardless of season, herd, or handling — explaining why the error was flat across the year and why nothing about the cows, the feed, or the personnel needed to change for it to appear. It began precisely when the new glass entered service (1 May 1897) and ended precisely when that lot was condemned and replaced (12 June 1923) — a purely mechanical, on/off cause requiring no change on the farms or in the station's practices to explain either boundary.

Three competing explanations were current, each defeated by specific evidence:
1. **Water added by patrons** (advanced by manager Selby Vose, via the 1919 circular). Defeated by the state dairy inspector's lactometer tests, made at every visit from 1908 forward, which never once found added water.
2. **Cream theft by the tester** (advanced obliquely by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague at the 1925 hearing). Defeated by the 1911 measure-substitution interval (same tester, same conditions, different — correct — results when the equipment changed) and by the Association's own "station gain" ledger entries, which show cream arriving at the main creamery heavier, not lighter, than credited every year — the opposite of what theft would produce.
3. **Wet bog hay depressing butterfat** (advanced by the 1908 committee and later Dr. Kepp). Defeated by the actual, correctly quoted text of Bulletin 214 (a depression of at most five-hundredths of a point in winter and none in summer — far too small and too seasonal to explain a flat 0.19-point, year-round gap) and by Alder Corners' independent test records showing the same Hessel Bottom cows and hay testing normally (~3.80) when tested elsewhere.

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?** No. The board's own exhibit list (given independently and identically by chairman Frick and clerk Kyle) names only four items — the test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book, and the 1923 inspector's notebook — not the pipettes themselves, which sat unexamined in the station loft until 1958.

**What did the tester himself believe?** The sources present two different, only partly reconcilable pictures. Per his son Duncan, Ansel Keddie privately suspected the glass itself from 1911 onward, saying so at home but entering only a question mark in his book for want of proof. Per arbitration chairman Alonzo Frick, Keddie privately told him, around the time of the 1925 hearing, that he "had always thought the patrons were at fault." The sources do not allow these to be fully reconciled; it is possible he held both suspicions at different times, or simultaneously and unresolved, but he died in 1934 without a settled theory of his own.

## Section G — Summary

For 26 years, farmers on Hessel Bottom were paid on butterfat tests that ran persistently low at the Larrow Valley Creamery's Ostrey Hollow station. Patrons suspected fraud; the manager suspected watered milk; a 1908 committee blamed wet bog hay. A 1925 arbitration board, unable to decide, dismissed tester Ansel Keddie and paid the patrons $3,800 "without admission" — vindicating no one. Keddie died in 1934 still under suspicion. In 1958 his granddaughter Ivy Keddie found the station's long-condemned 1897 glassware in a forgotten loft crate: the pipettes delivered five percent less milk than marked, mechanically depressing every test for 26 years. No one had watered anything; no one had stolen anything. It took a farm ledger, a rival cheese factory's independent records, a chance nine-week equipment swap in 1911, and finally a burette in a college laboratory to prove what a piece of glass had been doing all along.
