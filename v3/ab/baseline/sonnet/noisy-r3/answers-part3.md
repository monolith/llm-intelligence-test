# Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math

**C1.**
(a) Shortfall proportion = (marked − delivered) / marked = (17.6 − 16.72) / 17.6 = 0.88 / 17.6 = **0.05, i.e. one part in twenty (5%)**. This uses the primary, twice-quoted 1958 measurement sheet figure of 16.72 c.c. (Ivy Keddie's own measurement, corroborated by curator Nym), which is self-consistent with Bulletin 471's own concluding paragraph ("low by one part in twenty"). Vaught's bulletin *introduction* instead uses 16.27 c.c., yielding a different, larger shortfall (0.295 point) — but this figure conflicts with the bulletin's own conclusion and is not used here; see E1.

(b) True average = recorded average ÷ 0.95 = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. Shortfall = 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 of a point**. This matches both Bulletin 471's conclusion and county agent Falke's independently stated "nineteen hundredths."

(c) **76,000 lb**, per Bulletin 471's concluding paragraph — and independently reproducible by direct calculation (see C3), which cross-validates it against the bulletin's own worked total of 118,000 lb (based on the internally conflicting 16.27 c.c. figure), which is not used here.

(d) At the era's average butterfat price of about 25 cents/lb (Effie Loomis; independently corroborated by Roy Brant's "about twenty-five cents... over the whole stretch"): 76,000 × $0.25 = **about $19,000** — which matches Loomis's own independently-stated dollar figure almost exactly, a strong cross-check. Against the $3,800 arbitration award actually paid, the patrons received **$3,800 / $19,000 = 20%, i.e. one-fifth**, of what they were later calculated to be owed.

**C2.** (Emmet Brant, largest patron, 1910: 200,000 lb delivered, credited on a test of 3.42.)
(a) Credited butterfat = 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb**.
(b) Applying the same proportional (ratio) correction established in C1 — true test = recorded ÷ 0.95 = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60 — true butterfat = 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb. Owed and not credited = 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb**.
*(Note: if instead a flat +0.19-point correction is assumed, as Hazel Brant's description of the gap as "flat as a floor" might suggest, the true test would be 3.61, true butterfat 7,220 lb, and the shortfall 380 lb. The sources do not allow a definitive choice between the proportional and flat models at the individual-patron level; the proportional model is preferred here because it matches the documented physical mechanism — a fixed-ratio measurement error — rather than a farmer's later impression of the pattern.)*
(c) At the 1910 price of 24 cents/lb (Roy Brant): 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40** (or, under the flat-gap alternative, 380 × $0.24 = $91.20).

**C3.** (Full delivery years: 40,000,000 lb of milk received, per Bulletin 471, independently corroborated by clerk Kyle's "forty million pounds... sixteen hundred thousand pounds a year.")
(a) Credited = 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**.
(b) Actually delivered (true average 3.80) = 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lb**.
(c) Difference = 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 lb, matching C1(c) exactly. As a percentage, the base matters: expressed as a percentage of the **true (actual) total delivered**, 76,000 / 1,520,000 = **5.0% exactly**, matching the bulletin's own "one part in twenty" language. Expressed instead as a percentage of the **credited total**, 76,000 / 1,444,000 ≈ **5.26%**. The bulletin's own phrasing indicates the first (of true delivered) is the intended basis.

**C4.**
(a) **$77.93** — the 1897 Ammon & Sons invoice, verified by addition of its own line items ($25.20 + $31.68 + $0.90 + $18.00 + $2.15), and reproduced identically in the firm's own 1949 history.
(b) **72 pipettes (six dozen)** at $0.35 each = $25.20, exactly matching the invoice's own line total — this arithmetic decisively confirms "six dozen" over Vaught's rival "five dozen" (60 pipettes), which would total only $21.00 and does not match the invoice. Shortfall per pipette = 17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88 c.c.**

**C5.**
(a) The faulty glassware was physically in service from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 — about **26 years and 6 weeks**. The money calculation in C1–C3, however, runs over **25 full delivery years**, because 1897 and 1923 were both partial years (the glass entered service partway through 1897 and was condemned partway through 1923), leaving only 1898–1922 inclusive as full calendar years of affected deliveries. The two numbers differ because one measures elapsed calendar time and the other counts only complete years of milk deliveries used as the basis for the shortfall calculation.
(b) **Cannot be determined precisely.** Two figures are offered for the station's first (partial) season intake — "about nine hundred thousand pounds" (buttermaker Hobart Sill, present at the time) and "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" (clerk Bertram Kyle, decades later) — and Kyle explicitly states his own figure is not drawn from any ledger. Neither is a documentary record; the true figure is not established.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran 8 May – 10 July 1911 (nine weeks), fixed by Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary entry and corroborated by four further independent sources (Ansel Keddie's own book, Ivy Keddie's talk, Roy Brant's weigh book, Vaught's bulletin, and Falke's report). The variable was the measuring glass itself — the station's own (defective) measure was temporarily replaced by a borrowed one; the herds, pasture, season, tester, and procedures were all held constant. The result: tests rose from 3.61 to 3.80 for the nine weeks, then fell back the moment the station's own glass resumed. This establishes, independently of any laboratory measurement, that the cause traveled with the specific physical equipment, not with the tester's conduct, the cows, the feed, or the season — because everything else was unchanged and only the instrument varied. It does **not** establish the mechanism (a proportional under-delivery from a mis-blown pipette bore) or its exact magnitude; that required the direct 1958 measurement of the actual pipettes against a calibrated burette. Nor does it, by itself, prove the borrowed glass was correctly calibrated — only that it produced different, and (with hindsight) more accurate, results.

**D2.** Four independent reasons, from different narrators, rule out cream theft by the tester:
1. The 1911 measure-substitution interval (Duncan Keddie, Orra Keddie's diary, Roy Brant, Falke's report): the same tester, under the same conditions, produced higher (correct) readings the moment the equipment changed — inconsistent with personal manipulation, which would not track a hardware swap.
2. Roy Brant's weigh book: his own farm-scale weight and the station's intake weight "always agreed," across decades — ruling out physical diversion of milk at intake.
3. The Association's own "station gain" ledger line (kept by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague, copied yearly by clerk Bertram Kyle, and belatedly noticed by Dell Rundle): cream arriving at the main creamery from Ostrey Hollow was consistently *heavier* than the amount credited to patrons, every year. Theft would produce the opposite pattern — cream arriving light, not heavy.
4. Rosalie Cudd's independently kept Alder Corners test book: the same Hessel Bottom farmers' milk, tested by a different person on different equipment at an unrelated cheese factory, consistently averaged the "true" figure (~3.80) — showing the deficiency was tied to the Ostrey Hollow station specifically, not to any person's handling of the milk.
These are independent because they rest on four different record-keepers and four different kinds of evidence (a real-time equipment swap, a private farm ledger, the Association's own accounting, and a wholly external business's records), none derived from another. Reason 3 (the station-gain ledger) would still hold even if the glassware had never been measured in 1958 — it was sitting in the Association's own open books the entire time. As to whether the bookkeeper who advanced the theft accusation ever questioned those same entries: **no**. Per clerk Kyle, who worked alongside him, Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years... the figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people" — he wrote the very "station gain" line that refuted his own oblique accusation and never revisited it.

**D3.** The manager (Selby Vose) certainly knew the *recorded numbers* ran persistently low — that is precisely why he circulated the 1919 circular accusing patrons of watering their milk. But the sources do not show he understood *why*. Clerk Bertram Kyle, who sat outside his door for 25 years, states plainly that Vose "read the totals and he never read the lines above them... He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign" — meaning Vose personally signed the very station-gain ledger entries that would have pointed away from the patrons and the tester and toward the equipment, every year, without engaging with their detail. So: he knew the fact of the low tests, but the sources indicate he never connected it to the evidence already in his own signed records, and there is no evidence he suspected the glassware before its 1923 condemnation.

## Section F — Theory

**Causal theory.** In April 1897 the Ostrey Hollow station received a lot of 72 pipettes from Ammon & Sons, each marked to deliver 17.6 c.c. of milk but — because of an undetected fault in reground blowing molds (per the manufacturer's own later conjecture) — actually delivering only about 16.72 c.c., a constant proportional shortfall of about 5%. Because payment was made strictly by test, and because a short charge of milk into a bottle graduated for a full charge yields a butterfat reading short in the same fixed proportion, every test made with that glass read low by the same ~5% (0.19 point at the station's ~3.6–3.8% average), regardless of season, herd, or handling — explaining why the error was flat across the year and why nothing about the cows, the feed, or the personnel needed to change for it to appear. It began precisely when the new glass entered service (1 May 1897) and ended precisely when that lot was condemned and replaced (12 June 1923) — a purely mechanical, on/off cause requiring no change on the farms or in the station's practices to explain either boundary.

Three competing explanations were current, each defeated by specific evidence:
1. **Water added by patrons** (advanced by manager Selby Vose, via the 1919 circular). Defeated by the state dairy inspector's lactometer tests, made at every visit from 1908 forward, which never once found added water.
2. **Cream theft by the tester** (advanced obliquely by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague at the 1925 hearing). Defeated by the 1911 measure-substitution interval (same tester, same conditions, different — correct — results when the equipment changed) and by the Association's own "station gain" ledger entries, which show cream arriving at the main creamery heavier, not lighter, than credited every year — the opposite of what theft would produce.
3. **Wet bog hay depressing butterfat** (advanced by the 1908 committee and later Dr. Kepp). Defeated by the actual, correctly quoted text of Bulletin 214 (a depression of at most five-hundredths of a point in winter and none in summer — far too small and too seasonal to explain a flat 0.19-point, year-round gap) and by Alder Corners' independent test records showing the same Hessel Bottom cows and hay testing normally (~3.80) when tested elsewhere.

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?** No. The board's own exhibit list (given independently and identically by chairman Frick and clerk Kyle) names only four items — the test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book, and the 1923 inspector's notebook — not the pipettes themselves, which sat unexamined in the station loft until 1958.

**What did the tester himself believe?** The sources present two different, only partly reconcilable pictures. Per his son Duncan, Ansel Keddie privately suspected the glass itself from 1911 onward, saying so at home but entering only a question mark in his book for want of proof. Per arbitration chairman Alonzo Frick, Keddie privately told him, around the time of the 1925 hearing, that he "had always thought the patrons were at fault." The sources do not allow these to be fully reconciled; it is possible he held both suspicions at different times, or simultaneously and unresolved, but he died in 1934 without a settled theory of his own.

## Section G — Summary

For 26 years, farmers on Hessel Bottom were paid on butterfat tests that ran persistently low at the Larrow Valley Creamery's Ostrey Hollow station. Patrons suspected fraud; the manager suspected watered milk; a 1908 committee blamed wet bog hay. A 1925 arbitration board, unable to decide, dismissed tester Ansel Keddie and paid the patrons $3,800 "without admission" — vindicating no one. Keddie died in 1934 still under suspicion. In 1958 his granddaughter Ivy Keddie found the station's long-condemned 1897 glassware in a forgotten loft crate: the pipettes delivered five percent less milk than marked, mechanically depressing every test for 26 years. No one had watered anything; no one had stolen anything. It took a farm ledger, a rival cheese factory's independent records, a chance nine-week equipment swap in 1911, and finally a burette in a college laboratory to prove what a piece of glass had been doing all along.
