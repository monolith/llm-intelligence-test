# Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

*Method note: throughout, conflicts are resolved by counting independent sources (not repetitions of the same figure), preferring quoted primary documents and contemporaneous records (invoices, diary entries written at the time) over later memory, and using arithmetic cross-checks where the sources permit them. Where evidence is genuinely split among independent, credible sources, this is stated rather than guessed.*

## Story 1 — Founding of the Association and the Opening of Ostrey Hollow (1889–1899)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was organized in 1889 at a late-running meeting in the Larrow Green schoolhouse, with Gideon Vose elected first president and drafter of the by-laws (Rundle). The main creamery was built at Larrow Green in 1891, on the creek below the mill; the churn was set up the same year. Article VII of the by-laws established payment by butterfat test rather than by milk weight — the mechanism that made everything that follows possible. Its adoption year is disputed three ways (1891, Rundle; 1892, Loomis; 1893, Grigg), and the county's own 1959 investigation (Falke) found the Association's early minute book lost in an office move and explicitly declined to fix a date — this specific date cannot be determined from the sources.

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up-valley, to serve Hessel Bottom. It opened 4 May 1896. Buttermaker Hobart Sill personally lent the new station his own glassware from the Larrow Green bench, since its own outfit was not ready; his equipment was returned in spring 1897, one piece chipped. The station's own outfit was ordered from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet: invoice dated 14 April 1897, totaling $77.93 (verified by addition: $25.20 + $31.68 + $0.90 + $18.00 + $2.15), for six dozen (72) milk-test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. at $0.35 each, twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, a hand tester, and freight. It went into service 1 May 1897 — confirmed by the invoice itself, by Orra Keddie's diary entry of that date ("A. says the station's own glass came up"), and by Bulletin 471's own concluding paragraph; later claims of 1898 (Vaught's bulletin introduction, Kyle's clerk memoir) are outweighed and appear to be transposition errors. Ansel Keddie was tester from the opening. The first (partial) season's intake cannot be fixed precisely: Sill recalled "about nine hundred thousand pounds," Kyle recalled "a good eleven hundred thousand," and Kyle himself notes his figure is not from any ledger — this remains undetermined.

## Story 2 — The Weigh Book and the Brants of Hessel Bottom

Emmet Brant farmed the largest place on Hessel Bottom (six farms, ~160 wet acres) and hauled more milk to Ostrey Hollow than any other patron. He began a private weigh book on 1 January 1897 — the best-supported date, given independently by curator Nym's catalogue, Alonzo Frick's 1925 exhibit list, and his own grandson-in-law Duncan Keddie's notes (three independent sources), against his own son Roy Brant's later recollection of 1898 and granddaughter Hazel Brant's recollection of 1900 (both single, decades-later family memories). The book recorded two columns daily — his own barn-scale weight and the station's returned figure — which, per Roy Brant, "always agreed," establishing that weighing (as opposed to testing) was never at issue. Its best-documented leaf, quoted identically by multiple narrators, reads: "Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even... Paid on a test of 3.42."

Emmet Brant died in 1929; the book passed to his son Roy Brant, who kept it "exactly the same way" until lending it — not giving it, a distinction both Roy and Hazel insist on — to his niece's daughter Ivy Keddie in 1958 for her research at Farland. Roy died in 1962; the book passed to his daughter Hazel Brant, who donated it to the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1963, where it remains, available to "anybody who wants" to see it.

## Story 3 — Ansel and Orra Keddie: A Marriage Across the Complaint

Ansel Keddie (b. 1861, d. 1934) married Orra Brant (b. 1866 per her diary's editor, though Duncan Keddie's notes give 1868 — a minor, unresolved discrepancy) in 1889. Orra was Emmet Brant's full sister — resolved against Hazel Brant's lone claim of a "half sister, by his father's second wife," which is directly contradicted by three independent sources: Roy Brant's own explicit denial of "any story with a stepmother in it," Duncan Keddie's unqualified treatment of the siblingship, and Orra's own diary, which calls Emmet "my brother" throughout without qualification. Ansel served as Ostrey Hollow's tester for 29 seasons, from the 1896 opening until his dismissal 30 April 1925.

Orra's diary (kept 1897–1930, with a final leaf added 1934) is the fullest surviving firsthand record of the household: the couple's reaction to the first public complaints (March 1902 — "it is not your cows and not your hands... but it is my figures they will look at"); the 1908 committee's visit; a broken measure noted 8 May 1911, matching the documented start of that year's nine-week equipment anomaly; the 1919 circular, which Ansel refused to mail and instead delivered by hand; his dismissal ("Twenty-nine seasons and a letter... He ate [his supper]"); a post-dismissal career in carpentry; and his death, 21 February 1934, with the imputation of dishonesty still on him. His widow outlived him fourteen years, dying in 1948, never seeing him cleared. A final loose leaf records his one recorded remark on the subject: "a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."

## Story 4 — Three Theories for a Quarter Century (1902–1919)

Complaints about Ostrey Hollow's low tests began in 1902 and recurred every decade after. In January 1908 the Association appointed a three-man investigating committee chaired by Nyle Grigg, a non-patron with (he states) "no interest whatever in the answer." The committee sampled Hessel Bottom's six herds, drew and sealed its own milk samples, tested station scales against a known weight and against patrons' own scales, and inspected water supplies — over January, February and March 1908, per the report's own text (Grigg's 1919 covering letter instead claims sampling ran "through the whole of 1908," an internal self-contradiction within his own reprinted document). The committee's finding: "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter," with the shortfall attributed to Hessel Bottom's wet, sour bog hay.

The wet-hay theory was revived with a scientific gloss in 1926 by Dr. Roland Kepp, a veterinarian, who attributed it to acid, mineral-poor bog soil forcing cows to "rob their own frame," giving a gap figure of 0.21 point. Both Grigg (1919) and Kepp (1926) independently — but identically — misquote the college's actual 1911 feeding trial (Bulletin No. 214) as finding a "two tenths" depression in winter feeding; its real, verbatim finding was "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and... nothing... on summer pasture," a four-fold overstatement shared by both men, probably reflecting a common garbled secondhand source rather than independent errors. County agent Orin Falke's 1959 report does the arithmetic explicitly: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July," rejecting the wet-hay theory as insufficient to the observed, flat, all-season deficiency. A rival theory — that the manager suspected added water, and an officer obliquely suspected the tester of theft — is treated in Stories 6–8.
