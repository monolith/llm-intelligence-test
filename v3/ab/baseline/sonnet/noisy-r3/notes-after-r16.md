# Notes after Retellings 1–16 (Ostrey Hollow / Larrow Valley dairy saga)

## Overview / core subject
All sixteen retellings concern the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (Larrow Green, on Larrow Creek) and its skimming station seven miles up-valley at Ostrey Hollow, serving farms on "Hessel Bottom" (a wet meadow). The central controversy: for ~26 years (1897–1923) the Ostrey Hollow station's butterfat tests ran suspiciously low compared to other stations. Patrons believed they were being cheated (either by watered milk claims against them, or by cream theft by the tester). A 1925 board of arbitration found "no cause established" yet paid $3,800. Only in 1958–59 was it discovered (via a granddaughter's research) that the true cause was defective pipettes (glassware) that under-delivered milk by ~5%, mechanically producing low readings — exonerating tester Ansel Keddie completely, who had died in the 1930s under a cloud of suspicion. Retellings 9–16 add corroborating documentary sources (a farmer's weigh book, a rival cheesemaker's letter, the actual Bulletin 471 text, a jubilee pamphlet, the arbitration chairman's memoir, the buttermaker's oral account, a committee chairman's covering letter, and a veterinarian's soil-science paper) — several of which introduce NEW numeric/date conflicts on top of those already catalogued after retellings 1–8.

## Key people (cumulative cast, retellings 1–16)
- **Vira Toland** — narrator of Retelling 1; b. 1912, Ostrey Hollow patron's daughter; interviewed 1979.
- **Ansel Keddie** — tester at Ostrey Hollow station from opening (1896) until put out 30 April 1925. Married to Orra (née Brant). Died in the 1930s (Toland: "in the thirties"; Ivy Keddie: 1934). Narrator of Retelling 3. In Retelling 13 (Frick's memoir), Keddie privately told Frick in the Ashlin corridor that he "had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the room" — i.e., though he never accused anyone in the room and went to his grave "accusing nobody," Frick states this does NOT mean he "suspected nobody." This is new and sits in tension with the 1958 scientific exoneration of everybody (patrons included).
- **Orra Keddie** (née Brant) — Ansel's wife; sister of Emmet Brant; first cousin of "old Mrs. Vose" (per Toland only). Per Roy Brant (Retelling 9): Orra and Emmet were "full brother and sister, same mother and same father, whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it" — Roy explicitly rebuts an alternate rumor of half-sibling/stepmother relation. After the 1919 circular, brother and sister (or, per Roy Brant, Emmet and brother-in-law Keddie) stopped speaking.
- **Emmet Brant** — biggest patron/farmer on Hessel Bottom; Orra Keddie's brother; father of Roy Brant. Per Retelling 9: born 1854, died 1929; kept the farm weigh book from 1 Jan 1898 (Roy's own account) — NOTE: this conflicts with the previously established start date of 1 January 1897 for the Brant/Hessel Bottom weigh book (per Retellings 1–8, curator Nym's Item 4, and Alonzo Frick's exhibit list in Retelling 13, which both say "begun 1 January 1897"). Roy Brant's own retelling gives 1898 as when his father began; this is an unresolved one-year discrepancy about the weigh book's start date.
- **Roy Brant** — Emmet Brant's son; b. presumably ~1888 (says he is 67 in winter 1955); narrator of Retelling 9 ("My Father's Book," 1955, farm bureau local-history collection, with a 1958 postscript); kept up the weigh book after his father's death (1929); lent it to Ivy Keddie in 1958 (per postscript, returned to him, then goes to his daughter Hazel afterward). First cousin of Duncan Keddie (Ansel & Orra's son). His daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are second cousins.
- **Duncan Keddie** — Ansel Keddie's son; taught school at Ashlin; Ivy Keddie's father; Roy Brant's first cousin.
- **Ivy Keddie** — granddaughter of Ansel Keddie; narrator of Retelling 4 (1959 talk). In Retelling 10 she is the addressee of Rosalie Cudd's 1958 letter, described as "a graduate student at Farland." In Retelling 9's postscript (1958) she borrows Roy Brant's weigh book "for a piece of work she is doing at the college at Farland." In Retelling 12's 1959 footnote she is called "second cousin to Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom" (consistent with Roy Brant's own account).
- **Selby Vose** — manager of the Association from 1913–1938. Nephew of Gideon Vose (explicitly confirmed again in Retelling 12: "His nephew Selby Vose, afterward manager of the Association, used to say that his uncle wrote by-laws..." — this corroborates the "nephew" relationship over the earlier tentative inference from Retelling 8 that Selby might be Gideon's son; the "father" inference from the notes-after-r08 file appears to have been a misreading, and is superseded/corrected by Retelling 12's explicit "nephew"/"uncle" wording). Signed the 1919 circular. Before the board in 1925 (per Frick, Retelling 13) he personally advanced the "water in the cans" theory as the first of three explanations offered.
- **Gideon Vose** — founding president 1889–1911; drafted by-laws himself. Per Retelling 12 (Rundle's pamphlet): "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the law and thought better of it," wrote by-laws "at his own kitchen table," in a hand "the Association's clerks were still complaining about thirty years later." Died 1917.
- **Effie Loomis** — Association secretary 1901–1940; narrator of Retelling 2.
- **Wilbur Teague** — Association bookkeeper 1905–1945; narrator of Retelling 5. Per Frick's Retelling 13, before the 1925 board Teague advanced the second explanation (cream theft by the tester) obliquely, "in the way an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it," without ever making a direct charge.
- **Hobart Sill** — buttermaker at Larrow Green, 1893–1934 (41 years); kept churn/receiving book; narrator of Retelling 14 (oral remarks recorded the day he retired, 1934). Personally supplied the Ostrey Hollow station's original borrowed glassware/outfit at its May 1896 opening, out of his own bench equipment at Larrow Green ("I said no, they can have mine... it went up the valley on the wagon and they opened on it. Their own came the spring after and they sent mine back to me, and one piece of it was chipped"). Holds the "water in the cans" folk theory himself; states Larrow Green's own patrons' milk tested 3.78 "one year with another," and asserts the Hollow's "true" test was "3.85 and no better" — a THIRD distinct "true test" figure, conflicting with the 3.80 figure established via the pipette-shortfall calculation (Bulletin 471) and Keddie's own book.
- **Merle Strawn** — state dairy inspector for the Ordell circuit, 1907–1936; narrator of Retelling 6.
- **Alonzo Frick** — attorney of Ashlin; chairman of the 1925 board of arbitration. Narrator of Retelling 13 (1946 bar-association yearbook article, "Recollections of the Ordell Arbitration"), written at age 74. States he was chosen as chairman "because I was the one of the three who kept no cow." Records that the board sat three times: first session at the Association's Ashlin office (17 March 1925); he requested the second and third sessions be held AT Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's own weigh room — this conflicts with the previously established understanding (from notes after r1–8) that the whole hearing "sat 17 March – 4 May 1925 at Ashlin." Frick names the "three explanations" put to the board: (1) water in the cans (Selby Vose), (2) cream theft by the tester (Wilbur Teague, obliquely), (3) the wet hay/bog meadow theory (via the reprinted 1908 committee report). Reprints his own exhibit list (see Documents section below) — only FOUR exhibits, far fewer than curator Nym's later 16-item catalogue. Gives the recorded average test as **3.16** (matching Teague's outlier figure from the earlier notes, NOT the 3.61 figure used by Loomis/Nym/Ivy Keddie) and the patrons' own reckoning of loss as **67,000 lb of butterfat** (yet another distinct figure from 76,000 lb [Bulletin 471], 118,000 lb [Vaught's bulletin body text], or Rundle's dollar figure).
- **Professor Leland Vaught** — author of Bulletin No. 471 (Oct. 1958); full name given for the first time in Retelling 11 ("L. VAUGHT"), professor of dairy husbandry, State Agricultural College at Farland. Narrator of Retelling 11 (the bulletin's own introduction and method sections).
- **Rosalie Cudd** — runs the Alder Corners Cheese Company (Vessey County); daughter (not just niece, per Retelling 12/Rundle) of founder Jerome Cudd. NOTE: notes-after-r08 described her as Jerome Cudd's "niece"; Retelling 10 (her own letter) and Retelling 12 (Rundle's pamphlet) both explicitly call her his **daughter**, which corrects/supersedes the earlier "niece" characterization. Narrator of Retelling 10 (a 1958 letter to Ivy Keddie). States she was "a girl of twelve when the first vat was set," took over the factory in 1941 "that same spring" her father died, and has run it "seventeen years now" as of writing (1958).
- **Jerome Cudd** — founded the Alder Corners Cheese Company. Per Rosalie Cudd's own letter (Retelling 10): built the factory in **1913**, "on the flat below the church," died March 1941 "between the setting and the cutting of a vat." Per Dell Rundle's pamphlet (Retelling 12) and Roy Brant's account (Retelling 9): the factory opened in **1914** — a direct one-year conflict between Rosalie Cudd's own first-hand account (1913) and two secondary retellings (1914).
- **Hazel Brant** — Roy Brant's daughter; later custodian of the farm weigh book (received 1962, per earlier notes); second cousin of Ivy Keddie.
- **Halbert Nym** — curator, Ordell County Historical Rooms; narrator of Retelling 7.
- **Dell Rundle** — schoolteacher at Larrow Green; narrator of Retelling 12, "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years" (jubilee pamphlet, printed 1939, reprinted 1959 with a footnote and a note added "while in press"). States he taught "the children of both ends" of the valley through the 1919–1925 estrangement years. Repeats the popular "cream was stolen"/"figures went right when the man who kept it did not" theory as "the settled opinion of the valley," but appends a "NOTE ADDED WHILE IN PRESS" in which, after being shown the Association's old ledgers by chance ("on another errand altogether"), he sees the station-gain entry (cream arriving heavier than credited) and explicitly retracts his own Chapter IV logic: "Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand. I leave it as written and correct it here." His 1959 footnote states: "Eleven pipettes out of the station's old condemned crate were found still to be in existence" — CONFLICTS with the firmly established figure of **nine** surviving pipettes (Ivy Keddie's own 1958 measurement sheet lists nine values; curator Nym's Item 5 says nine).
- **Nyle Grigg** — farmer of Larrow Green; chaired the 1908 investigating committee; narrator of Retelling 15 (a 1919 covering letter reprinting the 1908 report, occasioned by the complaint being "reopened" in 1919). States he is not a patron of Ostrey Hollow and had "no interest whatever in the answer." Notes payment-by-test "came in with the 1893 meeting" — a THIRD distinct date for the by-laws/payment-by-test adoption, conflicting with Loomis's 1892 and Rundle's 1891 (churn/by-laws same year). Misquotes Bulletin No. 214 in his covering letter as finding the bog hay depressed the test "by two tenths in the winter feeding" — this directly conflicts with the bulletin's actual wording (quoted verbatim elsewhere: "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing... on summer pasture") — a four-fold overstatement. Also, within his own document, an internal conflict: his covering letter claims the committee "sampled those herds through the whole of 1908... every month of that year," while the report he is reprinting (his own 1908 committee report, quoted in full) says only "through January, February and March."
- **Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S.** — veterinary surgeon of Ashlin, thirty years' practice; narrator of Retelling 16, a 1926 paper "The bog meadows and the milk" read before the Ordell County Farm Club (with floor discussion appended by the club secretary). Attributes the low tests to soil chemistry (acid, lime/phosphate-poor bog soil) forcing cows to "rob their own frame," depressing butterfat specifically (the "dearest" thing the cow makes). Gives yet another gap figure: **0.21 point (twenty-one hundredths)** between Hessel Bottom milk and valley milk at large — distinct from the 0.19-point gap (Bulletin 471 calculation) and the 0.295-point figure in Vaught's bulletin body text (Retelling 11). Also misquotes Bulletin 214 as finding depression of "two tenths in the winter feeding" (same misquotation as Grigg's, word for word — "two tenths"). Dismisses the 1911 measure-substitution anomaly as insignificant because it lasted only "six weeks" (siding with Teague's six-week figure, not Keddie's/Ivy Keddie's nine weeks) and calling it merely "the flush of the season." In floor discussion, a member asks whether the trouble might lie in the station itself; Kepp replies he has "no competence" in reading tests and prefers the soil explanation as it fits "every wet bottom in the county alike."

## Timeline of major events (as reconstructed across all sources, retellings 1–16; new/updated items marked)
- **1889**: Association organized at Larrow Green; Gideon Vose elected first president. Per Rundle (Retelling 12): met "in the schoolhouse, in cold weather"; the men "remembered afterward chiefly that it went on very late and that somebody's horse got loose in the middle of it."
- **1891**: Creamery built at Larrow Green "on the creek below the mill" (Rundle); per Rundle, the churn was set up this same year and by-laws (including Article VII) date from 1891 — CONFLICTS with Loomis's dating of by-laws adoption to the "annual meeting of 1892," and with Nyle Grigg's claim (Retelling 15) that "payment by test came in with the 1893 meeting." Three different years (1891/1892/1893) now given across sources for the by-laws/payment-by-test adoption.
- **1893**: Hobart Sill comes to Larrow Green as buttermaker (per Retelling 14); "no floor in this room but plank" then, cooled with creek ice.
- **1895**: Ansel Keddie prepares Ostrey Hollow station for opening (per Toland).
- **1896**: Ostrey Hollow station opens, 4 May ("in May," per Sill, "a crowd went up for it"). Per Sill (Retelling 14): the station had NO equipment of its own ready; there was talk of holding the season off; Sill personally supplied an outfit "out of my own glass" from his own Larrow Green bench, which went up by wagon; the station's own equipment arrived "the spring after" (i.e., 1897) and Sill's borrowed glass was returned to him with one piece chipped. First season (May–Dec 1896, partial year) took in "about nine hundred thousand pounds" of milk (Sill's figure — new quantity). First-season average test: 3.80 (established earlier).
- **1897**: Station's own glass arrives (Ammon & Sons invoice, 14 April 1897, total $77.93); in use from 1 May 1897 (per earlier retellings) — though Vaught's Bulletin 471 body text (Retelling 11) instead states the station's own glass "went into use on the first of May, 1898" (a one-year discrepancy against the invoice date and the earlier-established narrative, and also against the bulletin's own concluding paragraph, which says "From the first of May, 1897..."). Farm/Brant weigh book begun 1 January 1897 (per curator Nym, Frick's exhibit list) — but Roy Brant's own account (Retelling 9) states his father wrote in the book "from the first of January, 1898."
- **1898**: First full year with new glass; recorded average 3.61 established as baseline.
- **1902**: Complaints first begin. Per Roy Brant (Retelling 9): "The first men to say so out loud said it in 1902, and they were laughed at, and one of them was my father." Per Sill (Retelling 14): "Nineteen two is the first I remember of it, a man in here shouting that his milk tested under his brother-in-law's down the creek." Per Grigg (Retelling 15): grumbling "from about 1902."
- **1905**: Wilbur Teague becomes bookkeeper; invents "station-gain" ledger line.
- **1907–1936**: Merle Strawn's circuit tenure (as before).
- **1908**: Investigating committee, headed by **Nyle Grigg** (named for the first time as chairman in Retellings 9 and 15; earlier notes had him unnamed/only as "a committee"). Per Grigg's own covering letter (1919, Retelling 15): committee named in January 1908 (three members); sampled Hessel Bottom herds; drew and sealed samples themselves; watched tests made twice; tried station scales against a known weight and against patrons' own scales; inspected water supply at each of the six farms. Report (quoted in full in Retelling 15, matches the shorter quotation already known from Retelling 1's era) concludes wet hay is the cause, no dishonesty found. Grigg's covering letter claims sampling ran "through the whole of 1908... every month," directly contradicting the report's own text ("through January, February and March") — flagged as an internal conflict.
- **1910**: Selby Vose's Jersey herd tested 4.56. Brant/Hessel Bottom weigh book 1910 entry (quoted again, identically, in Roy Brant's Retelling 9 and previously in curator Nym's Item 4): "Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42." Roy Brant adds context: his father "was pleased about the evenness of it; he wrote 'even' and underlined it, which for him was carrying on." Butterfat price that year: 24 cents (per Roy Brant) — over the whole stretch of complaint years, "about twenty-five cents" (Roy Brant) — corroborates Loomis's 25¢ average figure.
- **1911**: THE MEASURE ANOMALY (8 May–10 July, i.e., ~9 weeks per Keddie/Ivy Keddie; ~6 weeks per Teague AND now also per Dr. Kepp, Retelling 16, who calls it "six weeks... of June and July"). During the anomaly, average test rose to 3.80 (established figure) — Vaught's Bulletin 471 introduction (Retelling 11) reframes this rise as "from 3.61 to 3.80," consistent with the established figures. Roy Brant (Retelling 9) independently confirms the same phenomenon at the farm level: "Through those nine weeks our tests came up, all of us, every farm on the Bottom; and then the station's own came back into use and the figures went down again... my father noticed the rise and he noticed the fall and he entered both, and he could make nothing of it... and there it sat for near on fifty years." Bulletin No. 214 (1911) — the wet-hay feeding trial — quoted verbatim again in Retelling 11: "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." This is misquoted by BOTH Nyle Grigg (Retelling 15, "two tenths in the winter feeding") and Dr. Kepp (Retelling 16, "two tenths in the winter feeding" — identical misquotation) — a shared, four-fold-inflated misreading of the actual finding, used by both to bolster the wet-hay theory.
- **1913**: Selby Vose made manager. Per Rosalie Cudd's own letter (Retelling 10): this is also the year her father Jerome Cudd built the Alder Corners Cheese Company factory (conflicts with the 1914 date given by Rundle and Roy Brant — see Conflicts).
- **1914**: Per Rundle (Retelling 12) and Roy Brant (Retelling 9): Alder Corners Cheese Company opens over Redlow Ridge, built by Jerome Cudd (see conflict above re: 1913 vs. 1914).
- **1915–1930**: Four Hessel Bottom men haul milk over Redlow Ridge to Alder Corners Cheese Company each summer flush (roughly June to mid-September), returning to Ostrey Hollow the rest of the year, for **sixteen seasons** (1915–1930 inclusive; last season 1930, per Rosalie Cudd's letter, Retelling 10). Roy Brant (Retelling 9) corroborates: "From 1915, four of the Bottom men hauled their milk over Redlow Ridge... they went on that way for sixteen seasons... it was talked about here as though they had gone over to the enemy." Roy Brant did not participate, having "the biggest herd on the meadow." Rosalie Cudd's letter gives full detail: the ridge road is impassable roughly November–April; her father Jerome Cudd tested this milk himself, on his own bench/glassware, never comparing figures with Ostrey Hollow's; he recorded (quoted verbatim, Retelling 10) for summer 1919: "The four Hessel Bottom men, summer of 1919: 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge." A full table of Alder Corners' summer-average test figures for these four men, 1915–1930, is given (see Documents/Quantities section below) — averaging ~3.80 across all sixteen seasons, strongly corroborating the "true" 3.80 figure and undermining the wet-hay theory (since Alder Corners' cheese-making process would reveal watered milk "within a day," and Jerome Cudd never found any issue in 16 years).
- **1916**: Teague raises "station gain" with Vose, told to let it alone.
- **1917**: Gideon Vose dies.
- **1919**: THE CIRCULAR (Selby Vose's signature, alleging water in the cans, sent to nine Ostrey Hollow families). Roy Brant (Retelling 9): his father Emmet Brant and Ansel Keddie (brothers-in-law) stopped speaking after this — "not from any quarrel over the water, but because my father held that the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not" (a nuance not previously recorded: the estrangement was about Keddie's silence, not the water accusation itself, per Roy Brant's account — though Vira Toland's earlier account attributed a broader/different family rift). Hobart Sill (Retelling 14) says he "thought well of" the manager for the circular, calling it "the only plain thing that office ever did." Nyle Grigg's covering letter (Retelling 15) is occasioned by the complaint being "reopened this season" (1919) by "men who were boys when we made it" (i.e., in 1908) — situates the 1919 circular/renewed complaint alongside Grigg's decision to reprint his 1908 report with an added justificatory letter. Dell Rundle (Retelling 12, as schoolteacher) notes the two ends of the valley "did not speak civilly to one another for six years" after 1919, and that this estrangement "came down to [the schoolchildren] entire, without a word of it being explained, the way a debt does."
- **1923**: Pipette condemnation. Merle Strawn's notebook entry and curator Nym's catalogue: **12 June 1923**. Selby Vose's 1926 address: "12 July 1923." Dell Rundle's pamphlet (Retelling 12) AGREES with Vose's July date: "The state inspector condemned the station's glassware on the twelfth of July, 1923" — this adds a second source for the July date, though still outnumbered by the June-dated sources (Strawn's own notebook, its photostat, Ivy Keddie's talk, curator Nym's catalogue, and Bulletin 471's own concluding paragraph, all giving 12 June).
- **1925**: Arbitration. Per Alonzo Frick's own memoir (Retelling 13): petition filed January 1925; board of three named, Frick as chairman ("I have always supposed because I was the one of the three who kept no cow"); FIRST session at the Association's Ashlin office, 17 March 1925; Frick specifically requested the SECOND and THIRD sessions be held at Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's own weigh room, "so that the board might see the plant." This directly conflicts with the earlier-established understanding that the whole hearing "sat 17 March – 4 May 1925 at Ashlin." At Ostrey Hollow, the board watched the scales tried and the test performed twice. Keddie's statement is again described (17 March 1925), and Frick adds his private-corridor admission (see People section, Keddie entry). Dismissal of Keddie: "the thirtieth of April, four days before we made our award" (consistent with 30 April dismissal, 4 May award). Award: 4 May 1925, $3,800, "no cause established," "without admission" — quoted again, matching prior sources. Frick states the patrons' own reckoning of loss was "sixty-seven thousand pounds of butter fat" — a new/conflicting figure (vs. 76,000 lb in Bulletin 471, 118,000 lb in Vaught's bulletin-body-text calculation). Frick's own exhibit list (quoted in full, see Documents section) lists only 4 items, far short of curator Nym's later 16-item catalogue — reflecting that many records (Strawn's notebooks at Farland, Alder Corners' book, etc.) were not before the 1925 board at all, only surfacing in 1958.
- **1926**: Selby Vose's manager's address (as before). Dr. Kepp's paper (Retelling 16) to the Ordell County Farm Club also dates to 1926, advancing the soil/mineral theory and citing Bulletin 214 (misquoted).
- **1934**: Hobart Sill retires after 41 years at the Larrow Green churn (narrator of Retelling 14, oral remarks "recorded... the day he left it").
- **1939**: Dell Rundle's jubilee pamphlet, "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years," first printed (Retelling 12).
- **1941**: Loomis writes her "Notes toward a history" (Retelling 2). Separately: Jerome Cudd dies in March 1941 "between the setting and the cutting of a vat"; Rosalie Cudd takes over the Alder Corners factory that same spring.
- **1946**: Alonzo Frick, age 74, writes "Recollections of the Ordell Arbitration" for the Ordell County Bar Association yearbook (Retelling 13).
- **1955**: Roy Brant, age 67, writes "My Father's Book" for the county farm bureau's local-history collection (Retelling 9), describing Hessel Bottom, the weigh book, and the family history.
- **1958**: 
  - Ivy Keddie's research trip. Roy Brant's 1958 postscript to Retelling 9: "Duncan's girl Ivy came out to the place this year and asked whether she might borrow the book for a piece of work she is doing at the college at Farland. I let her have it. She is careful with paper. It comes back to me when she is through, and after me it is Hazel's."
  - Rosalie Cudd's letter to Ivy Keddie (Retelling 10), replying to Ivy's written inquiry about the four Hessel Bottom men's test figures at Alder Corners; includes the full 1915–1930 summer-average table.
  - February 1958: crate of nine condemned pipettes found (as before). 9 March 1958: measurement against burette at Farland (quoted again identically).
- **October 1958**: Bulletin No. 471 issued (Vaught & Ivy Keddie). Retelling 11 gives the bulletin's actual introduction/method sections (Sections I–VII), written by Vaught for extension agents and county workers, including extended discussion of WHY a pipette error is proportional, precise, and invisible from inside the plant (see Documents section for extended quotations) — and containing several numeric figures that conflict with the bulletin's own previously-known concluding paragraph (see Conflicts section).
- **1959**: Rundle's pamphlet reprinted with a new footnote (citing Ivy Keddie's research and — incorrectly — "eleven" surviving pipettes) and reissued; also the year of Ivy Keddie's talk to the Historical Society (as before).
- **1962/1963**: weigh book passes to Hazel Brant, then to Historical Rooms (as before).
- **1970/1972/1979**: as before (Alder Corners book to Vessey County Historical Society; curator Nym's catalogue notes; Toland's oral history).

The recorded station average over the affected years is 3.61; the corresponding true average is 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.905, a shortfall of 0.295 of a point. Applied to the 40,000,000 lb of milk received at the station in the twenty-five full delivery years, this yields 118,000 lb of butterfat delivered and not credited." ## Conflicts / oddities catalogued across sources — NEW in retellings 9–16 (numbered continuing from the 12 already catalogued after retellings 1–8)
13. **Weigh book start date**: Roy Brant (Retelling 9) says his father Emmet Brant began the weigh book "from the first of January, 1898." Curator Nym's Item 4 catalogue entry and Alonzo Frick's 1925 exhibit list (Retelling 13) both say "begun 1 January 1897." One-year discrepancy, unresolved.
14. **Selby Vose's relationship to Gideon Vose**: Retelling 12 (Rundle) explicitly and repeatedly confirms Selby is Gideon's **nephew** ("His nephew Selby Vose... his uncle wrote by-laws..."), reinforcing Retelling 2's original "nephew" statement and superseding the tentative/uncertain inference in the notes-after-r08 file that Selby might instead be Gideon's son (based on an ambiguous line in Retelling 8). Treat "nephew" as now well-corroborated.
15. **Jerome Cudd factory founding date**: Rosalie Cudd's own letter (Retelling 10, presumably the most authoritative/first-hand source, being his daughter) says the factory was built in **1913**. Dell Rundle's pamphlet (Retelling 12) and Roy Brant's account (Retelling 9) both independently say **1914**. Two-against-one, but the "two" are secondhand/valley-side accounts and the "one" is the direct family account.
16. **Number of pipettes originally purchased in 1897**: The original Ammon & Sons invoice (14 April 1897, quoted in earlier retellings) specifies "6 doz. milk test pipettes" = 72. Vaught's Bulletin 471 introduction (Retelling 11) instead states "five dozen pipettes were purchased" = 60. Unresolved discrepancy between the primary invoice document and the bulletin's own summary of it.
17. **Mean measured delivery of the nine surviving pipettes**: Ivy Keddie's 9 March 1958 measurement sheet (quoted identically in Retellings 4 and 7) gives individual readings averaging to "16.72 c.c." Vaught's Bulletin 471 introduction (Retelling 11) instead states "the mean delivery of the nine is 16.27 c.c." — note this looks like a possible digit transposition of 16.72, but is presented as a distinct, deliberately used figure throughout Vaught's calculations (feeding into a different ratio, shortfall, and total-pounds figure than the bulletin's own concluding paragraph uses). This is the same document containing both figures without reconciling them — see item 19 below.
18. **Station glass in-service start date**: Multiple earlier sources (the 1897 Ammon & Sons invoice, Ansel Keddie's own letter, and Bulletin 471's OWN concluding paragraph, "From the first of May, 1897...") give **1 May 1897**. Vaught's bulletin introduction (Retelling 11), in its own body text, instead gives "the first of May, 1898" — an internal one-year conflict within the same document (Bulletin 471) between its introduction and its conclusion.
19. **Vaught's own bulletin (Retelling 11) is internally inconsistent in its magnitude section**: using the 16.27 c.c. figure, Vaught calculates a ratio of 0.9244, a "true" average of 3.905 (shortfall of 0.295 point), and a total shortfall of 118,000 lb of butterfat. This entire calculation conflicts with the bulletin's own famous concluding paragraph, which states the shortfall was "one part in twenty" (5%, i.e., ratio ~0.95, matching the 16.72 c.c./17.6 c.c. figures used elsewhere) and "seventy-six thousand pounds." Vaught's own text flags awareness of a version of this tension ("this figure is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the glassware") without resolving it — this is an explicit, acknowledged-but-unresolved internal contradiction within a single authored document.
20. **1925 arbitration hearing location**: Earlier sources (Loomis, curator Nym) imply/state the whole hearing "sat 17 March – 4 May 1925 at Ashlin." Alonzo Frick, the board's own chairman (Retelling 13), states the first session only was at Ashlin (17 March), and that he specifically arranged for the second and third sessions to be held at Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's weigh room. This is a location conflict; Frick, as chairman, is arguably the most authoritative source on this point.
21. **Patrons' claimed/reckoned loss in butterfat**: At least four different figures now exist across sources: 76,000 lb (Bulletin 471's concluding paragraph, tied to the 40,000,000 lb/25-year/3.61-vs-3.80 calculation); 118,000 lb (Vaught's own bulletin body text, Retelling 11, using the conflicting 16.27 c.c./3.905 figures); 67,000 lb (Alonzo Frick's memoir, Retelling 13, "the patrons' own reckoning of what they had lost," presented at the 1925 hearing, i.e., a contemporary 1925 patron estimate rather than the 1958 scientific calculation); and Dell Rundle's pamphlet (Retelling 12), which frames the patrons' 1925 petition as citing a dollar loss of "$19,600" (close to, but not identical with, Loomis's independently-calculated $19,000 figure).
22. **Recorded/credited average test over the affected period**: The dominant figure across most sources (Keddie's test book, Loomis, curator Nym, Ivy Keddie, Bulletin 471) is **3.61**. Wilbur Teague's own 1948 memoir instead gives **3.16** (previously flagged, notes-after-r08 conflict #6) — and this SAME 3.16 figure now recurs, independently, in Alonzo Frick's 1946 memoir (Retelling 13: "paid, year upon year, on an average test of 3.16"). This strengthens the "3.16" figure as possibly reflecting a genuine alternative accounting (shared by two independent narrators, Teague and Frick) rather than a one-off error by Teague alone — though it still directly conflicts with the majority "3.61" figure used by Loomis/Nym/Ivy Keddie/Bulletin 471.
23. **By-laws / payment-by-test adoption year**: Now THREE different years given across sources: **1891** (Rundle's pamphlet, tied to the year the churn was set up), **1892** (Effie Loomis, "annual meeting of 1892," per notes-after-r08), and **1893** (Nyle Grigg's covering letter: "Payment by test came in with the 1893 meeting, as I have always understood it").
24. **Pipette condemnation date**: Previously a two-way conflict (12 June 1923 per Strawn/Ivy Keddie/curator Nym vs. 12 July 1923 per Selby Vose). Dell Rundle's pamphlet (Retelling 12) now independently corroborates Vose's **July** date ("the twelfth of July, 1923"), making it a 3-vs-2 split (June: Strawn's notebook, its photostat, Ivy Keddie's talk, curator Nym, Bulletin 471's concluding paragraph — 5 sources; July: Selby Vose, Dell Rundle — 2 sources).
25. **Number of pipettes found surviving in the crate in 1958**: Established as **nine** across Ivy Keddie's own measurement sheet (Retellings 4 and 7) and Vaught's bulletin (Retelling 11, "nine were recovered"). Dell Rundle's 1959 pamphlet footnote (Retelling 12) instead states "eleven pipettes... were found still to be in existence" — a direct numeric conflict, with Rundle as the sole outlier (secondhand, added after the fact, vs. the primary researcher's own multiply-corroborated figure of nine).
26. **"True" test-value / gap figures multiply proliferate across retellings 11, 13, 14, 16**: In addition to the core, most-supported figures (recorded 3.61, true 3.80, gap 0.19 — Bulletin 471's conclusion; or recorded 3.61, "true" 3.905, gap 0.295 — Vaught's own conflicting body-text calculation), further distinct "true"/gap figures are offered by lay narrators with no access to the 1958 pipette measurement: Hobart Sill (Retelling 14) asserts the Hollow's true test was "3.85 and no better" against Larrow Green's own 3.78; Dr. Kepp (Retelling 16) gives a gap of "twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21" between bottom and valley-at-large milk, attributing it to soil chemistry rather than glassware. None of these lay estimates match each other or the two figures derived from the actual 1958 measurement, though they cluster in the same rough range (0.19–0.295 point gap; 3.80–3.905 "true" test).
27. **Grigg's and Kepp's shared misquotation of Bulletin No. 214**: Both Nyle Grigg (1919 letter, Retelling 15) and Dr. Kepp (1926 paper, Retelling 16) independently state that Bulletin 214 found the bog hay depressed the butterfat test "by two tenths [of a point] in the winter feeding." The bulletin's actual, verbatim-quoted text (appearing in earlier retellings and again in Vaught's Bulletin 471 introduction, Retelling 11) says "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing... on summer pasture" — i.e., roughly four times smaller than what Grigg and Kepp claim, and confined to winter only (both misquotations retain the "winter" qualifier, suggesting they may share a common secondhand source or garbled memory of the bulletin rather than having read it directly).
28. **Grigg's internal self-contradiction**: Within Retelling 15 itself, Grigg's 1919 covering letter states the 1908 committee "sampled those herds through the whole of 1908... every month of that year," while the 1908 report he is reprinting in the very same document states sampling was done only "through January, February and March." Grigg appears to misremember or overstate his own committee's actual (more limited) methodology eleven years after the fact.
29. **Ansel Keddie's private admission vs. public denial**: Established across many sources that Keddie denied ever taking cream and "went to his grave accusing nobody" (of wrongdoing). Alonzo Frick's memoir (Retelling 13) adds a previously unrecorded detail — a private, off-the-record remark by Keddie in the Ashlin courthouse corridor that he "had always thought the patrons were at fault," which Frick is careful to distinguish from public accusation: "it has been said since that he went to his grave accusing nobody, which is true, and that he suspected nobody, which is not." This complicates (without necessarily contradicting) the 1958 scientific finding that neither patrons nor tester were at fault — Keddie himself, per Frick, never learned or accepted that finding (he died in the 1930s, decades before Bulletin 471) and apparently held onto an incorrect suspicion of his own patrons until his death.
30. **Rosalie Cudd's title relative to Jerome Cudd**: notes-after-r08 described her as his "niece"; her own letter (Retelling 10) and Dell Rundle's pamphlet (Retelling 12) both clarify she is his **daughter**. This is a correction to the earlier notes rather than a true cross-source conflict (the "niece" characterization in the pre-r08 notes appears to have been an error/misreading, now corrected by two direct sources).
31. **Emmet Brant/Ansel Keddie estrangement — cause, per Roy Brant vs. Vira Toland**: Vira Toland's account (notes-after-r08) frames the post-1919 estrangement as being between Orra Keddie and her brother Emmet Brant over the circular itself. Roy Brant's own account (Retelling 9) instead frames it as a rift between his father Emmet Brant and Ansel Keddie specifically (not Orra), and attributes the cause not to the water accusation itself but to Keddie's failure to publicly defend the patrons against the circular ("the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not"). These are not necessarily mutually exclusive (multiple family estrangements could have occurred simultaneously) but represent differing emphases/details from family members on the same underlying event.
