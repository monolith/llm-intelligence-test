# Answers — Part 2: Section A (Stories 5–8) and Section B

## Story 5 — The 1925 Hearing and Dismissal

**Who:** A board of arbitration at Ashlin; Ansel Keddie; the Hessel Bottom patrons; the
Association's officers.
**What:** The patrons petitioned in January 1925. A board sat at Ashlin from 17 March to 4 May
1925 — three sittings, two of them held at the station itself, in the weigh room, because the
books and scales were there (r21, r22, r24). It heard two accusations against Keddie (watering by
patrons, and separately, theft by the tester) and found neither established, but also found no
cause to clear him: it awarded the patrons money "without admission." Keddie was dismissed 30
April 1925, four days before the award was announced, "so that the finding found nothing and the
dismissal said everything" (r18). He came home at his ordinary hour, hung his apron behind the
shed door, and ate his supper (r24, diary entry for that date). He never attended another
Association meeting, though he had been a man who liked meetings (r17, via his son-in-law's line
by way of Hazel). He lived nine more years, doing carpentry, and died in 1934 with the accusation
still unanswered; no one at his funeral would name it aloud (r18, r24).
**When:** January–May 1925 (hearing); 30 April 1925 (dismissal); 1934 (death).
**Quantities:** The award was $3,800, "without admission" (agreed by r18, r19, r22, and r23 the
clerk who personally charged it to the station account; contradicted only by r21's secondhand
"$3,500," see Section E).
**Documents:** r22 (most precise dates); r24 (contemporaneous, the day itself); r18 and r23
(institutional/family perspectives on the dismissal's meaning); r21 and r19 (outside/later
perspectives).

## Story 6 — Ammon & Sons and the Faulty Pipettes

**Who:** Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet; the state dairy inspector (Merle Strawn).
**What:** On 14 April 1897 Ammon & Sons invoiced the Association $77.93 for a complete testing
outfit for the new Ostrey Hollow station — six dozen milk test pipettes (marked 17.6 c.c.), twelve
dozen test bottles, an acid measure, and a hand tester — put into use 1 May 1897 (r20's reproduced
invoice, corroborated by Orra's diary entry of the same date, r24). Junia Ammon's 1949 company
history speculates that the pipette mold had been reground and the lot ran consistently and
undetectably short — "the one fault a check of that kind cannot find" (r20). In June 1923 the
state inspector ran the station's pipettes against a calibrated burette, found they would not
deliver their mark, and condemned the whole lot; new glass was ordered and in use within the month
(r22, r24). The firm's 1923 reply to the Association's order — "our books of the nineties are not
such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name" — is reproduced by both the firm's own
historian (r20) and paraphrased by the newspaper (r19), and both quote it as an inadequate answer
to a customer of twenty-six years' standing.
**When:** 14 April 1897 (invoice) – June/July 1923 (condemnation and replacement): roughly
twenty-six years in service.
**Quantities:** $77.93 total invoice; 72 pipettes at $0.35 each, each blown to deliver 17.6 c.c.
and actually delivering an average of 16.27 c.c. when later measured (see Section C).
**Documents:** r20 (the firm's own history and reproduced invoice/letter — primary); r22 and r24
(condemnation date); r19 (secondary, and the source of at least one transposition error, see
Section E); r23 (the office side of ordering the replacement).

## Story 7 — The Crate in the Loft and Ivy Keddie's Discovery

**Who:** Ivy Keddie (b. 1931), Ansel's granddaughter, a graduate student at the State Agricultural
College at Farland; Professor Leland Vaught.
**What:** The condemned glassware was put up in the loft over the station's intake room in 1923
and left there, forgotten, for thirty-five years — sitting, by more than one account, directly
above the room in which two of the 1925 hearing's three sittings were held, unexamined the whole
time (r18, r21). In February 1958 Ivy Keddie, doing graduate work on the station's history, found
the crate under a tarpaulin, still there because a boy could touch it or worse only so often over
three and a half decades; of six dozen original pipettes, nine survived intact (r21, r22 — see
Section E on a newspaper claim of eleven). She carried them to Farland and on 9 March 1958
measured them against a calibrated burette: each was blown to deliver 17.6 c.c., and the average
actually delivered was 16.27 c.c. She and Vaught published Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958),
clearing Ansel Keddie of both watering and theft and identifying the short glass as the sole
cause. A local paper, *The Ashlin Chronicle*, ran the story on its front page the same week (r19).
**When:** February–March 1958 (discovery and measurement); October 1958 (bulletin published).
**Quantities:** Nine surviving pipettes; 17.6 c.c. mark vs. 16.27 c.c. actual delivery — a
shortfall of about 7.6% (worked in Section C).
**Documents:** r19 (contemporary newspaper account, with some errors); r22 (the county agent's
later, corrected summary); r18 and r21 (family and community memory of the loft and the crate).

## Story 8 — The Family Record: Correcting the Valley's Genealogy

**Who:** Duncan Keddie; Hazel Brant; Bertram Kyle; the wider valley's rumor mill.
**What:** Because the case turned on family ties (Ansel married to Emmet's sister; the tester's
own granddaughter doing the exonerating research), the valley accumulated a thicket of confused
and sometimes invented kinships, and two of the people closest to the facts — Duncan Keddie in his
1968 family notes and, independently, Bertram Kyle in his 1953 office memoir — each took pains to
set the record straight. Duncan lays out precisely: Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie were siblings;
their children Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie were therefore first cousins; Roy's daughter Hazel and
Duncan's daughter Ivy are second cousins, not cousins-once-removed and not aunt-and-niece as he
had "twice... seen it printed otherwise." He separately denies any Vose connection to his family,
and clarifies that Selby Vose managed the Association as Gideon Vose's *son*, not nephew. Kyle,
independently, confirms the Selby-Vose-is-the-son point from the office side, having "heard the
nephew story twice in the last year" and calling it nonsense. Even Cleve Oram (r21), reporting the
same rumors as unresolved valley talk, signals their unreliability by admitting he "could not draw
you the line on paper."
**When:** Ongoing valley talk (unspecified decades) through 1953–68, when Kyle and Duncan Keddie
wrote to correct it.
**Quantities:** None specific; the "quantities" here are relationships, not sums.
**Documents:** r18 (the fullest genealogical correction, and its most authoritative source); r23
(independent corroboration on the Vose question); r21 (the uncorrected rumor as it circulated);
r17 (corroborates the Brant side of the second-cousin relationship).

# Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**
Ivy is Ansel's **granddaughter**. Duncan Keddie — her own father — states this plainly and
explicitly in his family notes (r18): "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my
father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed
otherwise." Two other sources say "great-niece" instead: the newspaper (r19) and, in its
uncorrected first paragraph, the county agent's own report (r22). But r22 explicitly retracts
itself in an appended note: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the
granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." I resolve this in favor of
granddaughter on two grounds: it is stated by the single most authoritative possible source (her
own father, giving explicit reasoning), and it is the version the erring source itself corrects
after verification.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**
They are **second cousins** — the children of first cousins. The chain: Orra Keddie (née Brant)
and Emmet Brant were brother and sister; their children, Duncan Keddie and Roy Brant, were
therefore first cousins; and Duncan's daughter Ivy and Roy's daughter Hazel are second cousins.
This is stated explicitly and carefully by both women's own close relatives: Duncan Keddie (r18),
who spells out the definition and explicitly rules out "cousins once removed" and "aunt and
niece" as errors he has heard; and Hazel Brant herself (r17), who independently gives the same
relationship and complains that people are "forever telling me she is my niece or my cousin once
removed... and she is not." Cleve Oram (r21) instead calls them "first cousin once removed" — an
error from a hearsay source, outweighed by two independent, well-positioned, explicit accounts.
What the sources leave open: Hazel's own birth year is never given (Ivy's is, 1931), so I cannot
independently verify the generational alignment beyond trusting the two family members' own
arithmetic, which I have no reason to doubt.

**B3. Selby Vose's father, and why it matters.**
Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**, who organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery
Association, was its first president, and wrote its by-laws. This is stated independently by
Duncan Keddie (r18) and by Bertram Kyle (r23), both of whom explicitly rebut a circulating rumor
that Selby was Gideon's nephew rather than his son. It matters because it means the Association's
founding leadership and the leadership presiding over the controversy were the same family in
direct succession: Selby Vose, who as manager signed the 1919 accusatory circular against the
Hessel Bottom patrons and who annually signed off on the very ledger lines that (per Kyle, r23)
contained the evidence refuting the theft theory, inherited both the institution and, apparently,
its habit of not looking closely at the station's figures.

**B4. The Vose–Keddie family connection.**
On the weight of the sources, there was **none**. Duncan Keddie (r18) addresses this directly and
at length: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses... it is talk, and it has
been talk since before I could walk," and he notes that his mother, who "could not pass a
headstone without giving you the connection" for everyone else in the valley, never once
attributed one to the Voses. Cleve Oram (r21), by contrast, repeats the rumor as something "my
mother had... from her mother," admits he "never heard it contradicted at the time" but also that
he "could not draw... the line on paper" — i.e., he offers no genealogical substance, only that
the rumor circulated. I resolve this in favor of no connection: a detailed, reasoned denial from
inside the family outweighs an admittedly unverifiable rumor from outside it.
