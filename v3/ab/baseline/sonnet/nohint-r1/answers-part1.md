# Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

**A note on scope.** This session read only retellings r17–r24 (eight documents), not r01–r16. All
eight of what I read turn out to concern a single interlocking affair — the Ostrey Hollow testing
station and Ansel Keddie — told from eight different angles (granddaughter's tape, son's notes,
newspaper, glass-firm history, cream hauler's son, county agent's report, office clerk's memoir,
wife's diary). I cannot reconstruct the other stories that presumably live in r01–r16; those
documents were not available to me this session. What follows is my partition of the *one* affair
I do have into eight sub-stories, drawn only from r17–r24, as the instructions direct ("decide the
partition yourself — from what you have"). Where the eight "original stories" of the test design
in fact correspond to different material entirely, that is outside what I can determine from this
session's reading, and I flag it here rather than guess.

## Story 1 — The Weigh Book and the Founding of Ostrey Hollow

**Who:** The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association; Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom.
**What:** Farmers of the Larrow Valley organized the Association in 1889 and built a main
creamery at Larrow Green in 1891. Its by-laws (article seven) paid every patron by the butterfat
his milk tested at, not by weight — the hinge on which the entire later affair turns, since a
weight-only scheme would have cost no one anything (r22). In the winter of 1895–96 the Association
built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up-valley, to serve Hessel Bottom's six
farms; it opened 4 May 1896 (r22). Emmet Brant, the largest of the six Hessel Bottom farmers,
began his own private weigh book on 1 January 1897, recording his own scale reading alongside the
station's, a parallel record that would matter decades later (r17, r18).
**When:** 1889 (Association founded) through 1896 (station opens) through 1897 (Brant's book
begun).
**Quantities:** Six farms on Hessel Bottom, nine families among them (r17). Emmet Brant's book
records, for 1910, 200,000 lbs of milk delivered, tested at 3.42, "his scale and theirs agreeing"
(r17) — the founding record's own internal check of accuracy.
**Documents:** r17 (Hazel Brant, keeper of the book after her father); r18 (Duncan Keddie, who
gives the by-law context and the book's later custody); r22 (Falke's report, precise on the
by-laws and opening date); r23 (Kyle, the Association clerk, who describes the general-ledger
side of the same station accounts).

## Story 2 — Ansel Keddie, Tester, and His Household

**Who:** Ansel Keddie (b. 1861), his wife Orra (née Brant, b. 1866, m. 1889), their son Duncan
(b. 1894).
**What:** Ansel Keddie was hired as tester and separator man at Ostrey Hollow from the station's
opening in 1896 and held the post for twenty-nine seasons, until his dismissal in April 1925
(r22, r24). He married Orra Brant in 1889 — she was the younger sister of Emmet Brant, the
station's largest patron, which meant the man later accused of shorting the valley was married
into the family of the man who stood to lose the most by it, and the two dined together on
Sundays for thirty-six years (r18). Orra's diary, kept from 1897, records the texture of the
household: Ansel up before dawn every working day of his life, a man who "never once said he was
tired" in her hearing (r24). Duncan grew up in the house, left in 1911 at seventeen to teach
school at Ashlin, and later wrote his own "family notes" (1968) specifically to correct errors he
had seen printed and heard spoken about his parents and their kin.
**When:** 1861–1934 (Ansel's life); 1889 (marriage); 1896–1925 (his tenure).
**Quantities:** Twenty-nine seasons of service; one marriage connecting two families whose names
would later be pitted against each other at a hearing.
**Documents:** r18 (Duncan's own account of his parents); r24 (Orra's diary, the household's
contemporaneous record); r17 (confirms the marriage and sibling relationship from the Brant side).

## Story 3 — The Flat Deficiency and the 1911 Experiment

**Who:** Ansel Keddie; the patrons of Hessel Bottom.
**What:** From very nearly the day the station opened, its recorded tests ran low compared to
every other station in the county, "flat as a floor" — the same shortfall in wet years and dry,
winter and summer alike (r17), which is itself a clue later readers seize on, since a feed or
pasture effect would be expected to vary with the season and this did not. On 8 May 1911 the
station's own measuring glass broke; a borrowed measure was used for nine weeks, until 10 July;
during that interval the tests rose markedly, then fell back the day the station's own glass
resumed (r22, corroborated by Orra's diary entry for 8 May 1911 and by Duncan's account that his
father came home that summer "saying it was something in the glass"). Ansel wrote only a question
mark in the station book — he would not record a suspicion he could not prove — and kept using the
glass, and recording its figures, for another fourteen years (r18).
**When:** Complaints from 1902 onward (first recorded complaint, r22); the controlled interval,
8 May–10 July 1911.
**Quantities:** A deficiency the 1958 bulletin later fixed at "nineteen hundredths" of a test
point (r17, r22) — a figure I could not reconcile arithmetically against the other averages the
sources give (see Section E); nine weeks' duration for the 1911 interval.
**Documents:** r22 (fullest account of the 1911 interval and its evidentiary weight); r18 (Ansel's
own private suspicion and the question mark); r24 (contemporaneous diary entry); r17 (the
flatness of the deficiency across seasons).

## Story 4 — The Water Letter and the Break with Emmet Brant

**Who:** The Association's manager (Selby Vose, per Stories 6–8); Ansel Keddie; Emmet Brant.
**What:** In 1919 the Association's manager circulated a warning letter to the Hessel Bottom
patrons about adding water to their milk. Accounts differ on the number of recipients — nine
families by Hazel Brant's account (r17) against eleven by Orra's contemporaneous diary (r24), the
latter being the stronger source since she describes her own husband delivering them by hand.
Ansel would not let the letters go out by mail or be left on the platform; he carried them round
himself, stood at each man's door while it was read, and came home at eleven at night with his
hat still on (r24). Two days later Emmet Brant came to the Keddie yard, would not come in, and
asked Ansel why he had carried a paper he did not believe; Ansel answered that he carried it
because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet did not enter that house again for ten
years, until his death in 1929 (r24). No patron ever answered the letter in writing, "which in
that valley is a shout" (r17).
**When:** June 1919 (circular and rupture); 1929 (reconciliation only at Emmet's death).
**Quantities:** Nine or eleven letters, depending on source (see Section E); a ten-year family
estrangement.
**Documents:** r24 (the only eyewitness, contemporaneous account of the delivery and the rupture);
r17 (the patrons' side and the letter count); r23 (the manager's authorship and signature on the
circular, from the office side).
