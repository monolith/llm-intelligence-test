# Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

**A note on scope.** This session read only retellings r17–r24 (eight documents), not r01–r16. All
eight of what I read turn out to concern a single interlocking affair — the Ostrey Hollow testing
station and Ansel Keddie — told from eight different angles (granddaughter's tape, son's notes,
newspaper, glass-firm history, cream hauler's son, county agent's report, office clerk's memoir,
wife's diary). I cannot reconstruct the other stories that presumably live in r01–r16; those
documents were not available to me this session. What follows is my partition of the *one* affair
I do have into eight sub-stories, drawn only from r17–r24, as the instructions direct ("decide the
partition yourself — from what you have"). Where the eight "original stories" of the test design
in fact correspond to different material entirely, that is outside what I can determine from this
session's reading, and I flag it here rather than guess.

---

## Story 1 — The Weigh Book and the Founding of Ostrey Hollow

**Who:** The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association; Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom.
**What:** Farmers of the Larrow Valley organized the Association in 1889 and built a main
creamery at Larrow Green in 1891. Its by-laws (article seven) paid every patron by the butterfat
his milk tested at, not by weight — the hinge on which the entire later affair turns, since a
weight-only scheme would have cost no one anything (r22). In the winter of 1895–96 the Association
built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up-valley, to serve Hessel Bottom's six
farms; it opened 4 May 1896 (r22). Emmet Brant, the largest of the six Hessel Bottom farmers,
began his own private weigh book on 1 January 1897, recording his own scale reading alongside the
station's, a parallel record that would matter decades later (r17, r18).
**When:** 1889 (Association founded) through 1896 (station opens) through 1897 (Brant's book
begun).
**Quantities:** Six farms on Hessel Bottom, nine families among them (r17). Emmet Brant's book
records, for 1910, 200,000 lbs of milk delivered, tested at 3.42, "his scale and theirs agreeing"
(r17) — the founding record's own internal check of accuracy.
**Documents:** r17 (Hazel Brant, keeper of the book after her father); r18 (Duncan Keddie, who
gives the by-law context and the book's later custody); r22 (Falke's report, precise on the
by-laws and opening date); r23 (Kyle, the Association clerk, who describes the general-ledger
side of the same station accounts).

---

## Story 2 — Ansel Keddie, Tester, and His Household

**Who:** Ansel Keddie (b. 1861), his wife Orra (née Brant, b. 1866, m. 1889), their son Duncan
(b. 1894).
**What:** Ansel Keddie was hired as tester and separator man at Ostrey Hollow from the station's
opening in 1896 and held the post for twenty-nine seasons, until his dismissal in April 1925
(r22, r24). He married Orra Brant in 1889 — she was the younger sister of Emmet Brant, the
station's largest patron, which meant the man later accused of shorting the valley was married
into the family of the man who stood to lose the most by it, and the two dined together on
Sundays for thirty-six years (r18). Orra's diary, kept from 1897, records the texture of the
household: Ansel up before dawn every working day of his life, a man who "never once said he was
tired" in her hearing (r24). Duncan grew up in the house, left in 1911 at seventeen to teach
school at Ashlin, and later wrote his own "family notes" (1968) specifically to correct errors he
had seen printed and heard spoken about his parents and their kin.
**When:** 1861–1934 (Ansel's life); 1889 (marriage); 1896–1925 (his tenure).
**Quantities:** Twenty-nine seasons of service; one marriage connecting two families whose names
would later be pitted against each other at a hearing.
**Documents:** r18 (Duncan's own account of his parents); r24 (Orra's diary, the household's
contemporaneous record); r17 (confirms the marriage and sibling relationship from the Brant side).

---

## Story 3 — The Flat Deficiency and the 1911 Experiment

**Who:** Ansel Keddie; the patrons of Hessel Bottom.
**What:** From very nearly the day the station opened, its recorded tests ran low compared to
every other station in the county, "flat as a floor" — the same shortfall in wet years and dry,
winter and summer alike (r17), which is itself a clue later readers seize on, since a feed or
pasture effect would be expected to vary with the season and this did not. On 8 May 1911 the
station's own measuring glass broke; a borrowed measure was used for nine weeks, until 10 July;
during that interval the tests rose markedly, then fell back the day the station's own glass
resumed (r22, corroborated by Orra's diary entry for 8 May 1911 and by Duncan's account that his
father came home that summer "saying it was something in the glass"). Ansel wrote only a question
mark in the station book — he would not record a suspicion he could not prove — and kept using the
glass, and recording its figures, for another fourteen years (r18).
**When:** Complaints from 1902 onward (first recorded complaint, r22); the controlled interval,
8 May–10 July 1911.
**Quantities:** A deficiency the 1958 bulletin later fixed at "nineteen hundredths" of a test
point (r17, r22) — a figure I could not reconcile arithmetically against the other averages the
sources give (see Section E); nine weeks' duration for the 1911 interval.
**Documents:** r22 (fullest account of the 1911 interval and its evidentiary weight); r18 (Ansel's
own private suspicion and the question mark); r24 (contemporaneous diary entry); r17 (the
flatness of the deficiency across seasons).

---

## Story 4 — The Water Letter and the Break with Emmet Brant

**Who:** The Association's manager (Selby Vose, per Stories 6–8); Ansel Keddie; Emmet Brant.
**What:** In 1919 the Association's manager circulated a warning letter to the Hessel Bottom
patrons about adding water to their milk. Accounts differ on the number of recipients — nine
families by Hazel Brant's account (r17) against eleven by Orra's contemporaneous diary (r24), the
latter being the stronger source since she describes her own husband delivering them by hand.
Ansel would not let the letters go out by mail or be left on the platform; he carried them round
himself, stood at each man's door while it was read, and came home at eleven at night with his
hat still on (r24). Two days later Emmet Brant came to the Keddie yard, would not come in, and
asked Ansel why he had carried a paper he did not believe; Ansel answered that he carried it
because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet did not enter that house again for ten
years, until his death in 1929 (r24). No patron ever answered the letter in writing, "which in
that valley is a shout" (r17).
**When:** June 1919 (circular and rupture); 1929 (reconciliation only at Emmet's death).
**Quantities:** Nine or eleven letters, depending on source (see Section E); a ten-year family
estrangement.
**Documents:** r24 (the only eyewitness, contemporaneous account of the delivery and the rupture);
r17 (the patrons' side and the letter count); r23 (the manager's authorship and signature on the
circular, from the office side).


# Answers — Part 2: Section A (Stories 5–8) and Section B

## Story 5 — The 1925 Hearing and Dismissal

**Who:** A board of arbitration at Ashlin; Ansel Keddie; the Hessel Bottom patrons; the
Association's officers.
**What:** The patrons petitioned in January 1925. A board sat at Ashlin from 17 March to 4 May
1925 — three sittings, two of them held at the station itself, in the weigh room, because the
books and scales were there (r21, r22, r24). It heard two accusations against Keddie (watering by
patrons, and separately, theft by the tester) and found neither established, but also found no
cause to clear him: it awarded the patrons money "without admission." Keddie was dismissed 30
April 1925, four days before the award was announced, "so that the finding found nothing and the
dismissal said everything" (r18). He came home at his ordinary hour, hung his apron behind the
shed door, and ate his supper (r24, diary entry for that date). He never attended another
Association meeting, though he had been a man who liked meetings (r17, via his son-in-law's line
by way of Hazel). He lived nine more years, doing carpentry, and died in 1934 with the accusation
still unanswered; no one at his funeral would name it aloud (r18, r24).
**When:** January–May 1925 (hearing); 30 April 1925 (dismissal); 1934 (death).
**Quantities:** The award was $3,800, "without admission" (agreed by r18, r19, r22, and r23 the
clerk who personally charged it to the station account; contradicted only by r21's secondhand
"$3,500," see Section E).
**Documents:** r22 (most precise dates); r24 (contemporaneous, the day itself); r18 and r23
(institutional/family perspectives on the dismissal's meaning); r21 and r19 (outside/later
perspectives).

## Story 6 — Ammon & Sons and the Faulty Pipettes

**Who:** Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet; the state dairy inspector (Merle Strawn).
**What:** On 14 April 1897 Ammon & Sons invoiced the Association $77.93 for a complete testing
outfit for the new Ostrey Hollow station — six dozen milk test pipettes (marked 17.6 c.c.), twelve
dozen test bottles, an acid measure, and a hand tester — put into use 1 May 1897 (r20's reproduced
invoice, corroborated by Orra's diary entry of the same date, r24). Junia Ammon's 1949 company
history speculates that the pipette mold had been reground and the lot ran consistently and
undetectably short — "the one fault a check of that kind cannot find" (r20). In June 1923 the
state inspector ran the station's pipettes against a calibrated burette, found they would not
deliver their mark, and condemned the whole lot; new glass was ordered and in use within the month
(r22, r24). The firm's 1923 reply to the Association's order — "our books of the nineties are not
such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name" — is reproduced by both the firm's own
historian (r20) and paraphrased by the newspaper (r19), and both quote it as an inadequate answer
to a customer of twenty-six years' standing.
**When:** 14 April 1897 (invoice) – June/July 1923 (condemnation and replacement): roughly
twenty-six years in service.
**Quantities:** $77.93 total invoice; 72 pipettes at $0.35 each, each blown to deliver 17.6 c.c.
and actually delivering an average of 16.27 c.c. when later measured (see Section C).
**Documents:** r20 (the firm's own history and reproduced invoice/letter — primary); r22 and r24
(condemnation date); r19 (secondary, and the source of at least one transposition error, see
Section E); r23 (the office side of ordering the replacement).

## Story 7 — The Crate in the Loft and Ivy Keddie's Discovery

**Who:** Ivy Keddie (b. 1931), Ansel's granddaughter, a graduate student at the State Agricultural
College at Farland; Professor Leland Vaught.
**What:** The condemned glassware was put up in the loft over the station's intake room in 1923
and left there, forgotten, for thirty-five years — sitting, by more than one account, directly
above the room in which two of the 1925 hearing's three sittings were held, unexamined the whole
time (r18, r21). In February 1958 Ivy Keddie, doing graduate work on the station's history, found
the crate under a tarpaulin, still there because a boy could touch it or worse only so often over
three and a half decades; of six dozen original pipettes, nine survived intact (r21, r22 — see
Section E on a newspaper claim of eleven). She carried them to Farland and on 9 March 1958
measured them against a calibrated burette: each was blown to deliver 17.6 c.c., and the average
actually delivered was 16.27 c.c. She and Vaught published Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958),
clearing Ansel Keddie of both watering and theft and identifying the short glass as the sole
cause. A local paper, *The Ashlin Chronicle*, ran the story on its front page the same week (r19).
**When:** February–March 1958 (discovery and measurement); October 1958 (bulletin published).
**Quantities:** Nine surviving pipettes; 17.6 c.c. mark vs. 16.27 c.c. actual delivery — a
shortfall of about 7.6% (worked in Section C).
**Documents:** r19 (contemporary newspaper account, with some errors); r22 (the county agent's
later, corrected summary); r18 and r21 (family and community memory of the loft and the crate).

## Story 8 — The Family Record: Correcting the Valley's Genealogy

**Who:** Duncan Keddie; Hazel Brant; Bertram Kyle; the wider valley's rumor mill.
**What:** Because the case turned on family ties (Ansel married to Emmet's sister; the tester's
own granddaughter doing the exonerating research), the valley accumulated a thicket of confused
and sometimes invented kinships, and two of the people closest to the facts — Duncan Keddie in his
1968 family notes and, independently, Bertram Kyle in his 1953 office memoir — each took pains to
set the record straight. Duncan lays out precisely: Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie were siblings;
their children Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie were therefore first cousins; Roy's daughter Hazel and
Duncan's daughter Ivy are second cousins, not cousins-once-removed and not aunt-and-niece as he
had "twice... seen it printed otherwise." He separately denies any Vose connection to his family,
and clarifies that Selby Vose managed the Association as Gideon Vose's *son*, not nephew. Kyle,
independently, confirms the Selby-Vose-is-the-son point from the office side, having "heard the
nephew story twice in the last year" and calling it nonsense. Even Cleve Oram (r21), reporting the
same rumors as unresolved valley talk, signals their unreliability by admitting he "could not draw
you the line on paper."
**When:** Ongoing valley talk (unspecified decades) through 1953–68, when Kyle and Duncan Keddie
wrote to correct it.
**Quantities:** None specific; the "quantities" here are relationships, not sums.
**Documents:** r18 (the fullest genealogical correction, and its most authoritative source); r23
(independent corroboration on the Vose question); r21 (the uncorrected rumor as it circulated);
r17 (corroborates the Brant side of the second-cousin relationship).

# Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**
Ivy is Ansel's **granddaughter**. Duncan Keddie — her own father — states this plainly and
explicitly in his family notes (r18): "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my
father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed
otherwise." Two other sources say "great-niece" instead: the newspaper (r19) and, in its
uncorrected first paragraph, the county agent's own report (r22). But r22 explicitly retracts
itself in an appended note: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the
granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." I resolve this in favor of
granddaughter on two grounds: it is stated by the single most authoritative possible source (her
own father, giving explicit reasoning), and it is the version the erring source itself corrects
after verification.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**
They are **second cousins** — the children of first cousins. The chain: Orra Keddie (née Brant)
and Emmet Brant were brother and sister; their children, Duncan Keddie and Roy Brant, were
therefore first cousins; and Duncan's daughter Ivy and Roy's daughter Hazel are second cousins.
This is stated explicitly and carefully by both women's own close relatives: Duncan Keddie (r18),
who spells out the definition and explicitly rules out "cousins once removed" and "aunt and
niece" as errors he has heard; and Hazel Brant herself (r17), who independently gives the same
relationship and complains that people are "forever telling me she is my niece or my cousin once
removed... and she is not." Cleve Oram (r21) instead calls them "first cousin once removed" — an
error from a hearsay source, outweighed by two independent, well-positioned, explicit accounts.
What the sources leave open: Hazel's own birth year is never given (Ivy's is, 1931), so I cannot
independently verify the generational alignment beyond trusting the two family members' own
arithmetic, which I have no reason to doubt.

**B3. Selby Vose's father, and why it matters.**
Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**, who organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery
Association, was its first president, and wrote its by-laws. This is stated independently by
Duncan Keddie (r18) and by Bertram Kyle (r23), both of whom explicitly rebut a circulating rumor
that Selby was Gideon's nephew rather than his son. It matters because it means the Association's
founding leadership and the leadership presiding over the controversy were the same family in
direct succession: Selby Vose, who as manager signed the 1919 accusatory circular against the
Hessel Bottom patrons and who annually signed off on the very ledger lines that (per Kyle, r23)
contained the evidence refuting the theft theory, inherited both the institution and, apparently,
its habit of not looking closely at the station's figures.

**B4. The Vose–Keddie family connection.**
On the weight of the sources, there was **none**. Duncan Keddie (r18) addresses this directly and
at length: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses... it is talk, and it has
been talk since before I could walk," and he notes that his mother, who "could not pass a
headstone without giving you the connection" for everyone else in the valley, never once
attributed one to the Voses. Cleve Oram (r21), by contrast, repeats the rumor as something "my
mother had... from her mother," admits he "never heard it contradicted at the time" but also that
he "could not draw... the line on paper" — i.e., he offers no genealogical substance, only that
the rumor circulated. I resolve this in favor of no connection: a detailed, reasoned denial from
inside the family outweighs an admittedly unverifiable rumor from outside it.


# Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math

**C1.**

**(a) Proportion the tests differed from the truth, from the glassware.**
Every surviving pipette was blown to deliver 17.6 c.c. (the standard test charge) but the nine
survivors, measured by Ivy Keddie against a calibrated burette in March 1958, averaged only
16.27 c.c. delivered (r19, r22). Shortfall = 17.6 − 16.27 = 1.33 c.c. As a proportion of the
intended charge: 1.33 / 17.6 = 0.0756, i.e., **about 7.6% low**. Since a butterfat test reads the
fat found in whatever charge of milk actually enters the bottle, and the neck is graduated for a
full 17.6 c.c. charge, a short charge yields a reading low by the same proportion regardless of
the milk's true richness — this is the mechanism, not just the number.

**(b) True average test and shortfall in points.**
r22 gives two directly stated averages: the station's long-run recorded average, 3.61, and the
average after the glass was replaced (from 1924 on), 3.85. Taking 3.85 as the best available
direct measurement of the true average (it is an actual post-repair average, not an estimate), the
recorded tests were short by 3.85 − 3.61 = **0.24 points**. I flag that this does not match the
figure the bulletin itself is twice quoted as using — "nineteen hundredths" (0.19), by r17 and by
r22 in its own body text. Nor does subtracting the *other* average r22 gives (Larrow Green's 3.78)
from 3.61 produce 0.19; it produces 0.17. I cannot reconcile 0.19 against either pairing of the
averages actually stated in the sources; it may cover a different span of years than "the long
period of complaint," but that is not stated. I use 0.24 below because it is derived from the two
directly-measured averages for the same station: **this is a case where I could not fully settle
the number and say so** (see also Section E).

**(c) Pounds of butterfat patrons were not credited with.**
Using Bertram Kyle's clerk figures (r23) — 40,000,000 lbs of milk received over the affected
years, recorded average test 3.61 — credited butterfat = 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lbs**.
Applying the glass shortfall of 7.56% to recover the true amount: true fat = 1,444,000 /
(1 − 0.0756) ≈ **1,562,300 lbs**. Difference (uncredited) ≈ **118,300 lbs**.
This does not match two other figures the sources offer: Cleve Oram's secondhand "sixty-seven
thousand pounds" (r21, admittedly something he "heard" and could not himself picture), and a
figure back-solved from the bulletin's own stated dollar value in (d) below, which gives roughly
78,400 lbs. All three are the same order of magnitude but none match exactly, and the sources
never show their respective methods or the exact span of years each used. **I cannot fully
reconcile this figure from the sources**; I present 118,300 lbs as my own working estimate because
it is the only one I can show the arithmetic for, but flag the other two as competing, unreconciled
figures.

**(d) Money value, and fraction actually received.**
The bulletin's own stated figure, per the newspaper (r19), is **$19,600**. At Kyle's stated average
price of $0.25/lb (r23) my own 118,300-lb estimate would be worth ≈$29,575 — again in the same
range as, but not equal to, $19,600; I cannot close this gap from the sources given. Taking $19,600
as the more authoritative figure (it is explicitly attributed to the bulletin itself, the one
document that did the full original calculation, rather than reconstructed by me from partial
figures), and the patrons' actual award as $3,800 (see Section E for why I prefer this over
Oram's $3,500): the patrons received 3,800 / 19,600 ≈ **19%**, roughly a fifth of what the
bulletin later calculated they were actually owed.

**C2. The largest patron's year, 1910 (Emmet Brant, per r17).**

(a) Credited: 200,000 lbs delivered, tested at 3.42 → 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lbs** butterfat
credited.

(b) Owed but not credited: applying the 7.56% shortfall, true test ≈ 3.42 / (1 − 0.0756) ≈ 3.70.
True butterfat ≈ 200,000 × 0.0370 ≈ 7,399 lbs. Difference ≈ 7,399 − 6,840 = **≈ 559 lbs** not
credited.

(c) Money value that year: no 1910-specific price is given anywhere in the sources; the only price
figure available is Kyle's stated overall average of $0.25/lb "taking the fat years with the lean"
(r23), which I apply here only as an approximation: 559 × $0.25 ≈ **$140**. This is explicitly an
estimate using an average price that may not reflect 1910 specifically — the sources do not give
me a better number.

---

**C3. Full delivery years, credited vs. received.**

(a) Credited to patrons: **1,444,000 lbs** (as in C1c, from Kyle's 40,000,000 lbs at 3.61%).

(b) Actually reaching the creamery in the station's cream: the sources do not give me this figure
directly as a total pounds number. Bertram Kyle (r23) instead reports the *annual ledger
mechanism*: each year the difference between credited and received fat was booked as "station
gain," and this gain ran at "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" — i.e., the cream reaching
Larrow Green consistently contained about 5.25% more fat than was credited to the patrons.

(c) As a percentage: this ledger-stated gain, **5.25%**, is the number actually recorded
year-over-year in the Association's own books (a primary figure, not derived). I note it does
**not** match the 7.56% shortfall computed from the glassware in C1(a) — if the glass defect were
the entire explanation, and "gain" is expressed as a fraction of what was credited, the expected
gain would be closer to 1/(1−0.0756) − 1 ≈ 8.2%; if expressed as a fraction of what was received,
closer to 7.6%. Neither matches 5.25% exactly. **This is a genuine unresolved gap**: either the
"station gain" percentage was a separate, older accounting convention only partly driven by the
glass error, or "as usual" means this margin existed at other stations too and was not unique to
Ostrey Hollow — but the sources give no comparison figures for other stations, so I cannot
determine which. I flag this rather than force a false reconciliation. What is clear and important
independent of the exact percentage: the ledger shows *more* fat arriving than was credited, the
opposite of what cream-theft by the tester would produce (see D2).

**C4. The invoice.**

(a) Total: **$77.93** — from the invoice itself, reproduced in full by Ammon & Sons' own company
history (r20): 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93. I use this over the newspaper's figure
of "$77.39" (r19), which is very likely a digit transposition of the same number; the reproduced
itemized invoice, which sums correctly on its own line items, is the more reliable, quoted primary
document.

(b) Pipettes bought: six dozen = **72 pipettes**, at $0.35 each (r20, corroborated by r19). Each
was blown to deliver 17.6 c.c. and (per the nine survivors measured in 1958) actually delivered an
average of 16.27 c.c. — short by **1.33 c.c.** each. I note this per-pipette shortfall is an
average over the nine survivors, not a figure for all 72 individually, since the other 63 were
broken or lost over the thirty-five years in the loft (r21) and never measured.

---

**C5.**

(a) The faulty glass was in service from **1 May 1897** (invoice r20, confirmed same-day by Orra's
diary, r24) until it was condemned on **12 June 1923** and replaced the following month (r22,
confirmed by Orra's diary entry for July 1923) — **about 26 years** of physical use. The money
calculations, however, run over "full delivery years," which by Kyle's own figures (40,000,000 lbs
at a stated 1,600,000 lbs/year, r23) implies **25 full years**. These two numbers differ because
the glass was in service for parts of two additional calendar years — a partial first season
(May–December 1897) and a partial final season (January–June 1923) — that do not count as complete
delivery years for the purposes of the annual milk-total figure.

(b) The station's first season's total is given only by Kyle (r23), and only as personal
recollection, not from any book: "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" — **≈ 1,100,000 lbs**. He
explicitly flags this himself as unverified: "It is not in any book I ever ruled." No other source
gives a first-season figure, so this cannot be independently checked from what I have.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 borrowed-measure interval as an experiment.**
It ran from **8 May 1911 to 10 July 1911** — about nine weeks (r22, confirmed by Orra's diary
entry of 8 May 1911 and Duncan's account that his father spoke of it "that summer"). The variable
that changed was the physical measuring glass in use (the station's own vs. a borrowed one); held
constant were the herds, the pasture, the season, the tester, and the rest of the machinery (r22
states this explicitly). The result: tests rose markedly for the nine weeks, then fell back to
their former level the day the station's own glass resumed. What this establishes that a
calculation alone (like the 1958 c.c. measurement) cannot: it is a real, in-situ demonstration that
*swapping the instrument alone* changed the outcome, with everything else — including the tester
himself — held fixed. A later laboratory measurement proves the glass was miscalibrated in the
abstract; only the 1911 interval proves that this specific miscalibration was actually producing
the specific pattern of low readings seen at that station, in real conditions, and not some other
simultaneously-present cause. What it does **not** establish: the magnitude of the effect (no
numeric test values from those nine weeks survive in these sources), whether the borrowed measure
itself was perfectly accurate (only that it was "more" accurate, relatively), or that the same
defect persisted unchanged for the full twenty-six years rather than only around 1911.

**D2. Three independent reasons the tester was not stealing cream.**
(1) The 1911 experiment itself (r22): the *same* tester, under no different supervision, produced
higher figures with a different glass — if he were personally skimming cream, changing the
measuring glass should not have changed the outcome, but it did.
(2) The flatness of the deficiency across seasons — "not a little in the winter and none in the
summer... nineteen hundredths, flat as a floor" (r17). A man varying his theft with opportunity or
need would be expected to produce a varying shortfall; a constant, unvarying one across wet years
and dry is the signature of a fixed mechanical cause, not variable human behavior.
(3) The Association's own ledger, per its clerk Bertram Kyle (r23): the annual "station gain" line
shows *more* butterfat arriving at Larrow Green in the station's cream than was ever credited to
the patrons — the opposite of the pattern theft would produce (theft would show a deficit at the
creamery end, not a surplus).
These three are independent in kind: (1) is a real-world controlled substitution, (2) is a
statistical/behavioral pattern argument, and (3) is a documentary accounting record — they draw on
different narrators (Falke/diary, Hazel Brant, Kyle) and different types of evidence. All three
predate and do not depend on the 1958 glassware measurement; I would specifically point to (3), the
ledger evidence, as one that would hold even if the crate had never been found, since it was
sitting in the Association's own books from the start.
Did the bookkeeper ever question these entries? **No.** Wilbur Teague, who wrote the "station
gain" line every year, "never asked anybody anything in forty years" — per his own successor
Bertram Kyle, who copied the same line himself for years and states plainly it "never once crossed
my mind to wonder at it" either (r23).

**D3. Did the manager know the tests ran low before the glass was condemned?**
Yes, as to the *fact* — no, evidently, as to the *cause*. Selby Vose became manager in 1911, by
which time complaints had already been on record since 1902 (r22); he personally signed and
circulated the 1919 accusatory circular about "added water," which could only have been sent by
someone who knew the tests were running low and had settled on an explanation for it (r23, r24).
But per Kyle (r23), Vose habitually signed the annual ledger sheets — including the "station gain"
lines that, properly read, contradicted his own theory — by reading only the bottom total: "he
would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign... he never read the
lines above them." So he knew the pattern existed and had a theory about it, but the sources
support that he never examined, or at least never acted on, the documentary evidence that would
have pointed away from his own accusation and toward the equipment.

## Section E — Contradictions

1. **Invoice total.** r20 (reproduced invoice, itemized, sums correctly): $77.93. r19 (newspaper):
"$77.39." Resolution: a quoted primary document with itemized figures that sum to $77.93 outranks
a paraphrased newspaper figure; likely a digit transposition in r19.

2. **Number of surviving pipettes.** r21 (Oram, independent personal memory, unconnected to the
college bulletin): nine. r22 (Falke, citing the bulletin): nine. r19 (newspaper): eleven.
Resolution: two independent sources agree on nine; the newspaper, explicitly assembled "in a day
and a half from a college press release and two telephone calls," is the outlier and the weaker
source. Nine is correct.

3. **Ivy Keddie: granddaughter or great-niece?** r19 and r22 (in its own initial text) both say
"great-niece" — **this is the case of two narrators carrying the same wrong value.** The tie is
broken by r22 itself, which appends a correction after verifying with the family: "the error on
page two is mine," and by Duncan Keddie (r18), her father, stating "granddaughter" directly. Note
this is also **a narrator contradicting itself on the page**: r22 states "great-niece" in its body
and "granddaughter" in its own appended note.

4. **Number of pipettes in the original outfit.** r20 and r19 (both citing/echoing the invoice):
six dozen (72). r21 (Oram, decades-later personal memory): "five dozen... that was the whole
outfit." Resolution: the invoice is a reproduced primary document; six dozen is correct.

5. **Number of families receiving the 1919 water circular.** r17 (Hazel, secondhand family
account, 1981): nine. r24 (Orra, contemporaneous diary, wife of the man who delivered them by
hand): eleven. Resolution: r24 is the strongest possible source for this specific fact — a
contemporaneous, firsthand account from inside the delivery itself — over a secondhand recollection
taped six decades later. I take eleven as correct and Hazel's nine as an inherited family error.

6. **Ivy and Hazel: second cousins, or first cousins once removed?** r17 and r18 (the two women's
own fathers/selves, both reasoning explicitly): second cousins. r21 (Oram, hearsay): "first cousin
once removed." Resolution: two independent, well-positioned, explicit sources outweigh one
admittedly uncertain hearsay source.

7. **Selby Vose: son or nephew of Gideon Vose?** r18 and r23, independently, both say son, both
explicitly rebutting a circulating "nephew" story. No source actually asserts "nephew" as fact —
both mentions of it are of the rumor, cited only to reject it. Son is correct and essentially
undisputed among sources that address it directly.

8. **Any Vose–Keddie family connection?** r18 (Duncan, detailed denial from inside the family):
none. r21 (Oram): reports it as valley talk he cannot substantiate. Resolution: Duncan's specific,
reasoned denial outweighs an admittedly unverifiable rumor. No connection.

9. **Year of the hearing/dismissal.** r18, r19, r21, r22, and r24 all agree on **1925** (r24 is a
contemporaneous diary; r22 gives precise matching dates: petition January 1925, sittings 17
March–4 May 1925, dismissal 30 April 1925; r21's narrator independently cross-checks the year
against his own age). r17 says "twenty-six" and r23 (Kyle) says "twenty-four" — **two different**
wrong values from two different narrators, not the same wrong value, so this is not the "two
narrators, same error" case; it is two separate, unrelated memory errors, both outweighed by the
contemporaneous diary and the detailed official-style report.

10. **Award amount.** r18, r19, r22, and r23 (the last being the clerk who personally processed the
payment) all state **$3,800**. r21 (Oram, citing his father secondhand) states "$3,500."
Resolution: firsthand/documentary sources (especially Kyle, an eyewitness participant in the
bookkeeping) outweigh a thirdhand oral figure.

11. **Date the station's own testing glass first went into use.** r20 (invoice) and r24
(contemporaneous diary) both give **1 May 1897**. r23 (Kyle, writing from memory decades later,
and admitting he "came in as a boy" after this period) gives 1 May **1898**. Resolution: two
primary/contemporaneous sources outweigh one later reconstruction; 1897 is correct.

12. **Year the glassware was condemned.** r19, r20 (implicitly), r22, and r24 (contemporaneous
diary, July 1923 entry) all place it in **1923**. r23 says "in twenty-two." Resolution: the
contemporaneous diary and the precise official-style report (which gives an exact date, 12 June
1923) outweigh Kyle's later, and by this point repeatedly imprecise, memory.

13. **Arithmetic inconsistency within r22 itself (self-contradiction).** r22 states the station's
recorded average (3.61) against Larrow Green's average (3.78) — a difference of 0.17 — and
separately gives the post-repair average (3.85) — a difference from 3.61 of 0.24 — yet states in
the same document that "the deficiency, as the bulletin now fixes it, was nineteen hundredths"
(0.19). None of r22's own stated pairs of numbers produces 0.19 by subtraction. **I cannot settle
this from the sources**; I flag it as an internal inconsistency rather than force an explanation
the text does not supply.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. Causal theory.** The station's entire testing outfit, ordered from Ammon & Sons and put into
service 1 May 1897, included pipettes blown to a 17.6 c.c. mark that in fact delivered materially
less (an average of 16.27 c.c. among the survivors later measured) — most plausibly, per the
manufacturer's own historian, because the mold had been reground and the resulting lot ran short
in a way no visual or spot check could catch, since the error was *consistent* rather than random
(r20). Every test performed with this glass introduced a short charge of milk into a bottle neck
graduated for a full charge, so every fat reading came out low by the same proportion, regardless
of the cows, the season, or the tester — which explains why the error had one particular size (set
purely by the physical dimensions of the miscast glass), why it was flat across seasons (a
mechanical cause, not a biological one), why it began the exact day the new outfit went into use
(1 May 1897), and why it ended the moment the state inspector condemned and replaced the glass
(June/July 1923) — with nothing whatsoever needing to change at the station or on the farms for
either the onset or the resolution, since the cause and its removal were both purely instrumental.

The sources offer three competing explanations, each explicitly defeated:
1. **Patrons watering their milk** — advanced by the Association's manager (the 1919 circular,
r23, r24). Defeated by the state dairy inspector's own lactometer tests, taken at every circuit
visit from 1908 forward, which never once found added water at the station (r22).
2. **The tester stealing cream** — urged by an Association officer at the 1925 hearing (r22).
Defeated by the 1911 borrowed-glass interval (same tester, different glass, higher readings) and
by the ledger's own "station gain" entries showing a surplus, not a deficit, of fat reaching the
creamery (r23; see D2).
3. **Wet bog hay thinning the cream** — raised by an Association committee in 1908 and repeated by
a veterinarian in 1926 (r22). Defeated by the college's own 1911 feeding trial, Bulletin No. 214,
which Falke tracked down after his initial report went to print: bog hay depresses the test "by
not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing... on summer pasture" — far
too small to explain a flat, year-round deficiency, prompting Falke to explicitly withdraw his
earlier concession to this theory (r22).

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?** No. Two of the three 1925 sittings were
held in the very room over which the crate sat in the loft, "twenty feet, if that," the entire
time (r21, r18); and Bertram Kyle, who personally carried the four exhibit books into that hearing,
states he cannot say that "any man in that room laid [the tester's book] beside [the inspector's
notebook] and looked" (r23) — no cross-referencing, let alone physical examination of the glass
itself, is recorded by any source.

**What did the tester himself believe?** Ansel Keddie suspected "something in the glass" as early
as the summer of 1911, following the borrowed-measure interval, but — "a man who would not put
down what he could not prove" — recorded only a question mark in the station book and continued
using the glass and recording its figures faithfully for another fourteen years (r18).

## Section G — Summary

For twenty-six years, a Larrow Valley creamery station tested its patrons' milk with pipettes that
looked correct but delivered short of their marked capacity — a manufacturing accident from 1897
that no one caught because the error was perfectly consistent. Every reading came out low by the
same fixed proportion, in every season, for every farmer, including the tester's own brother-in-law.
The valley blamed the patrons, then blamed the tester, Ansel Keddie, who was dismissed in 1925
without proof and died in 1934 still under suspicion. The condemned glass itself had been sitting,
unexamined, in a loft directly over the hearing room the whole time. It took his granddaughter,
climbing a ladder in 1958, to measure it and clear him. No single witness — patron, clerk,
manager, or tester's family — held every piece: the hearsay, the ledgers, the diary, the invoice,
and the glass itself were scattered across a family, an office, and a state college, which is why
it took eight separate records, compared against one another, to reconstruct what one look at the
crate in 1911 might have settled in an afternoon.
