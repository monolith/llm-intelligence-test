## Section C — Math (show working)

**C1.**
(a) The pipettes were blown to deliver 17.6 c.c. but actually delivered a measured average of **16.72 c.c.** (r04's measurement sheet; r07 Item 14; the bulletin's own reproduced conclusion in r07/r11). A competing figure of 16.27 c.c. appears in r11's body text and r19's Chronicle account, but is rejected: 16.72/17.6 = exactly **0.95** (a 5% shortfall), which is an unusually clean ratio for a real measurement to hit by chance, and it is the figure consistent with every other number in the record (see below), whereas 16.27/17.6 = 0.9244, an unclean ratio that (as shown in (b)) implies a true average outside the range independently corroborated by three other sources. r11 also self-contradicts: its body text uses 16.27/118,000 lb, but its own reproduced "conclusion" paragraph cites 76,000 lb, which only follows from 16.72. **Proportion: the glass delivered 95% of its marked volume, i.e., was 5% short.**

(b) Recorded average test = 3.61 (majority figure: r02, r03, r04, r07, r11, r22). Since recorded = true × 0.95, true = 3.61 / 0.95 = **3.80**. This is not an isolated number: it matches Larrow Green's own contemporaneous average of 3.78 (r22), the Alder Corners cheese-factory test records for the same Hessel Bottom herds of 3.78–3.82 (r10), and the Ostrey Hollow station's own post-1923 average with corrected glass of 3.85 (r22) — four independent figures converging on the 3.78–3.85 band, against which 3.80 fits and the alternative 3.905 (implied by 16.27 c.c.) does not. **True average: 3.80. Shortfall: 3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19 points** — matching "nineteen hundredths" given independently by Hazel Brant (r17) and Falke (r22), both citing the corrected bulletin figure.

(c) Total intake over the affected years = 40,000,000 lb (majority: r02, r04, r07, r11, r23; the 44,000,000 lb figure from Teague/r05 and Rundle/r12 is not preferred — see Section E). Shortfall = 40,000,000 × 0.0019 = **76,000 lb** of butterfat, which is exact and matches r04/r07's own stated figure directly.

(d) At 25 cents/lb (Kyle, r23 — the only figure that reconciles cleanly): 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000** (matching r04/r07; the $19,600 figure in r12/r19 is not preferred, see Section E). The patrons received $3,800 from the 1925 arbitration award. $3,800 / $19,000 = **exactly 1/5 (20%)** — a clean fraction that itself supports both $19,000 and $3,800 as the correct paired figures.

**C2.** Largest patron = Emmet Brant, per his weigh book as read by his granddaughter Hazel Brant (r17): in 1910 he delivered 200,000 lb of milk, credited at a test of 3.42.
(a) Credited butterfat = 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb**.
(b) Multiple independent narrators (Roy Brant, r09; Hazel Brant, r17; Falke, r22) describe the deficiency as a flat, constant *point* difference across seasons and years, not a constant proportion — so applying the corrected flat deficiency of 0.19 points: true test = 3.42 + 0.19 = 3.61; true credit = 200,000 × 0.0361 = 7,220 lb; owed and not credited = 7,220 − 6,840 = **380 lb**. *(Note: applying the proportional 0.95 ratio instead gives true = 3.42/0.95 = 3.60, true credit = 7,200 lb, shortfall = 360 lb. The two models differ by only 20 lb; the flat-point model is preferred because it is the one the sources themselves explicitly describe.)*
(c) No 1910-specific butterfat price is given anywhere in the sources; only the multi-year average of 25 cents/lb (Kyle, r23) is available. Applying it as an approximation: 380 × $0.25 = **≈$95.00**. This should be treated as an estimate using an out-of-year average price, not a verified 1910 figure — the sources do not allow a precise answer here.

**C3.**
(a) Total credited to patrons = 40,000,000 lb × 3.61% = **1,444,000 lb** butterfat.
(b) Total actually received at the creamery = 40,000,000 lb × 3.80% (true average) = **1,520,000 lb**. This is corroborated independently by Bertram Kyle's ledger figure (r23): the "station gain" line was entered "as usual" at **5.25%**, and 1,444,000 × 1.0525 ≈ 1,520,110 lb — matching the true-average method to within rounding.
(c) Difference = 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 lb (consistent with C1c). As a **percentage of the credited total** (the ledger's own convention, since "station gain" was booked as a percentage of what had already been credited): 76,000 / 1,444,000 = **5.26%**, matching Kyle's stated 5.25% almost exactly. As a percentage of the true/received total instead: 76,000 / 1,520,000 = 5.0%. **The two are not the same number; the historically-used base was the credited amount, giving ≈5.25–5.26%.**

**C4.**
(a) Invoice total = **$77.93** (itemized: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93, per r20's own reproduced invoice). The commonly repeated $77.39 (r19, and r20's own prose) is an arithmetic transcription error against the invoice's own line items.
(b) The invoice bought **six dozen (72) pipettes**, each short by 17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88 c.c.**

**C5.**
(a) The faulty glass was in continuous use for **26 years**: from 1 May 1897 (Orra's diary, r24) to 12 June 1923 (Strawn's contemporaneous circuit-book entry, r06/r22) — a span itself independently confirmed as "twenty-six years" in r19 and r20. The money calculation, however, runs over **25 full delivery years**: 40,000,000 lb ÷ 1,600,000 lb/year (Kyle's stated annual rate, r23) = exactly 25. The two numbers differ because the glass's first year (1897, May–December) and last year (1923, January–June) were only *partial* delivery years under the faulty glass; the headline arithmetic uses only the complete calendar years in between (1898–1922 = 25 full years) for a clean per-year rate, while the glass itself was physically installed and removed at points 26 years apart.
(b) The station's first-season intake is given as **approximately 1,100,000 lb** (Bertram Kyle, r23) — but this figure is explicitly sourced by Kyle to oral recollection ("from men who were there... not in any book I ever ruled"), not to any surviving written record, and is uncorroborated elsewhere. It should be treated as an approximate, unverified figure rather than an established one.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran from 8 May 1911 to 10 July 1911 (about nine weeks), when the station's own broken glass was replaced with a borrowed measure (Falke, r22; dated exactly by Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary entry, r24). The variable was the testing instrument alone; held constant were the herds, the pasture, the tester, the season (within those nine weeks), and the water supply. The result: recorded tests rose markedly for the duration of the borrowed glass and fell back to the old level the moment the station's own glass resumed. This establishes causation directly, by observation of a real-world swap of the single relevant variable, decades before anyone could measure the glass in a lab — something the 1958 arithmetic alone (comparing marked vs. delivered volume) could never establish on its own, since a calculation shows only that the glass *could* produce an error, not that it *did*, historically, drive the actual valley-wide deficiency. What the interval does **not** establish: the precise magnitude of the glass's error (only the later 1958 burette measurement quantified that); which specific mechanical flaw was responsible; or, strictly, that no other factor coincided with the nine weeks (Kepp's objection that this was the early-summer flush season is not fully answered by the interval alone, though the convergence with the ledger and 1958 evidence closes that gap).

**D2.** Three independent lines of evidence rule out theft by the tester. (1) The 1911 nine-week natural experiment (Ansel Keddie's own test book, r03; corroborated by Orra's diary, r24; synthesized by Falke, r22): the same tester's recorded figures rose and fell purely with the instrument, which is inconsistent with a theory requiring the tester's own conduct to explain the deficiency. (2) The Association's own "station gain" ledger entries (Bertram Kyle, the clerk who copied them for decades, r23): every year, *more* butterfat arrived at the creamery in the Hollow's cream than had been credited to the patrons — the opposite of what cream theft by the tester would produce. (3) Hobart Sill, the receiving buttermaker at Larrow Green with no institutional stake in Ostrey Hollow's defense, independently observed that cream from the Hollow consistently arrived "heavy" (r14) — a physically separate observation, from a different vantage point (the receiving end, not the station), corroborating the ledger pattern. These three are independent because they come from different observers (the tester's own contemporaneous record, the Association's bookkeeping clerk, and an outside buttermaker), using different methods (a natural instrument-swap experiment, ledger accounting, and direct handling of the received cream) at different times. Reason (2), the ledger pattern, would hold even if the glassware had never been measured in 1958, since it was recorded contemporaneously, year by year, decades before the 1958 discovery. As for whether the bookkeeper ever questioned these entries: **no** — Wilbur Teague, who was among those who pressed the theft theory at the 1925 hearing, wrote the "station gain" line into the ledger every year of his 40-year service and, per Kyle, "never asked anybody anything"; Kyle himself, who copied the same line for years, admits it "never once crossed my mind to wonder at it." The very entries that refuted the accusation sat in the accuser's own handwriting, unexamined, for decades.

**D3.** Selby Vose almost certainly **knew that the tests ran low**, since this was common valley knowledge from 1902 onward and was the explicit premise of his own 1919 circular accusing the patrons of watering their milk. But there is no evidence he understood **why**. Bertram Kyle's memoir (r23) states plainly that Vose, in signing the yearly station account sheets — the very sheets carrying the "station gain" entries that, properly read, pointed away from the patrons and toward the equipment — habitually read only the bottom-line total and never the itemized lines above it: "He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign." So the answer is two-part: yes to knowing the fact of the deficiency (it was the reason for his own circular), no to any evidence he grasped its cause, despite the disproving evidence having passed under his signature every year of his tenure.

## Section F — Theory

**What actually happened:** In 1897 the Ostrey Hollow station purchased, from Ammon & Sons, six dozen milk-test pipettes from a lot whose mold had apparently been reground, producing a defect uniform enough across the whole lot to pass ordinary inspection: every pipette delivered about 16.72 c.c. against its 17.6 c.c. mark — a constant 5% shortfall. Because the error was purely instrumental and identical on every use, it depressed every test made with that glass by the same proportion, in every season, on every farm, regardless of what the cows ate or drank — which explains both why the deficiency was flat across all months (ruling out any theory tied to seasonal feed) and why it began precisely when the new glass entered service (1 May 1897) and vanished precisely when it was replaced (June/July 1923, after which the average jumped from 3.61 to 3.85). Because the fault lay entirely in a single crate of glassware, nothing on the farms or at the station ever needed to change to produce or end the effect — only the glass itself needed replacing, which happened, unremarked, in 1923.

**The three defeated theories:**
1. *Water adulteration* (advanced by manager Selby Vose, 1919 circular) — defeated by the state inspector's repeated lactometer tests from 1908 onward, which never once found added water (Falke, r22, citing the inspector's own circuit record).
2. *Cream theft by the tester* (advanced by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague and pressed at the 1925 hearing) — defeated by the 1911 nine-week natural experiment (same tester, different instrument, figures moved with the instrument) and by the Association's own "station gain" ledger entries showing more, not less, fat arriving at the creamery than was credited (see D2).
3. *Wet/bog hay* (advanced by the 1908 committee under Nyle Grigg, revived by Dr. Kepp in 1926) — defeated by Bulletin No. 214's actual, verified finding (at most 0.05 points in winter, none in summer — far too small to explain a flat 0.19-point year-round gap), by the 1911 anomaly occurring during the summer flush when hay effects should be negligible, and by Rosalie Cudd's independent Alder Corners records showing the same Hessel Bottom herds testing normally (3.78–3.82) when hauled to a different station.

**Subsidiary questions:** The 1925 arbitration board never examined the condemned glassware — it was not among the four exhibits Kyle (r23) lists as entered into evidence, and it sat, per Cleve Oram (r21), in the loft roughly twenty feet above two of the hearing's own sessions, untouched. As for what the tester himself believed: per his son Duncan (r18), Ansel privately suspected "something in the glass" from 1911 onward, though he never committed this to his own book beyond a question mark, being unwilling to record what he could not prove. This sits uneasily beside Alonzo Frick's account (r13) that Keddie privately told him, in a corridor during the 1925 hearing, that he suspected the patrons — a claim the sources do not let us reconcile with Duncan's account; it may reflect an earlier suspicion later supplanted, a guarded remark not meant literally, or a genuine conflict between a family member's long-run memory and a single reported aside. **This point cannot be fully settled from the sources.**

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

For a quarter century, milk from Hessel Bottom's six farms tested markedly lower at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station than anywhere else in the valley, costing its patrons roughly $19,000 in unpaid butterfat. Farmers suspected the tester, Ansel Keddie, of theft; the manager suspected them of watering their milk; agricultural committees blamed the bottom's wet hay. All three theories were wrong. In 1897 the station had bought a crate of milk-test pipettes with a manufacturing defect: they delivered less liquid than marked, so every test read low by a constant margin, in every season, for 26 years. Keddie was dismissed in 1925 without proof of wrongdoing and died in 1934 still under suspicion. In 1958 his granddaughter Ivy found the discarded, uncalibrated glass in the station's loft, measured it, and proved the instrument — not any person — was at fault. It took eight separate records because no one witness ever held the ledgers, the diary, the invoice, and the lab result at once.
