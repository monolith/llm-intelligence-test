### Story 1: The Founding of Ostrey Hollow and the Purchase of Flawed Glass (1895–1898)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, organized by valley farmers in 1889 with its main creamery at Larrow Green (built 1891), built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up-valley, over the winter of 1895–96 to serve the six farms of Hessel Bottom. The station opened 4 May 1896 (Falke, r22) with Ansel Keddie as tester and separator man, a post he held 29 seasons. For its first year the station had no glassware of its own: Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green, lent his own testing glass to open it (r14), and one piece came back chipped. The station's own outfit — ordered from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet — was invoiced 14 April 1897: six dozen (72) milk-test pipettes at 17.6 c.c., twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, and a four-bottle hand tester, itemized total $77.93 (r20's own reproduced invoice; the $77.39 figure quoted in Ammon's own prose and independently in the Ashlin Chronicle, r19, is an arithmetic error — the line items sum to $77.93). The outfit went into use 1 May 1897, confirmed by Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary entry that day ("A. says the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green," r24) and by the invoice-to-use timeline in r19/r20. (r11 and r23 instead give 1 May 1898; the contemporaneous diary is preferred — see Section E.) Unknown to anyone, the pipette mold had apparently been reground and the lot ran consistently short: each pipette actually delivered about 16.72 c.c. against its 17.6 c.c. mark, a defect uniform enough across the whole lot to pass ordinary spot-checks (Ammon's own theory, r20). This crate of glass would remain in daily use, silently shorting every test made with it, for the next 26 years. Documents: r07 (Item 2/invoice reference), r14, r18, r19, r20, r22, r23, r24.

### Story 2: The Long Complaint and the Three Rival Theories (1902–1926)

From very nearly the station's opening, Ostrey Hollow's recorded milk tests ran below the rest of the valley: a station average of 3.61 against Larrow Green's own 3.78 (Falke, r22), a gap later fixed by the corrected bulletin arithmetic at 0.19 points, flat in every month and season (Hazel Brant, r17; Falke, r22). The first complaint of record came in March 1902 (Orra's diary, r24, matching Falke). Three explanations circulated for a quarter century. (1) Added water: the Association's manager, Selby Vose, believed the Hollow patrons were watering their milk and circulated a warning to that effect in 1919. (2) Theft: the bookkeeper Wilbur Teague and others suspected the tester, Ansel Keddie, of skimming cream for himself; this view was pressed at the 1925 hearing. (3) Wet hay: a committee headed by Nyle Grigg sampled the Hessel Bottom herds through 1908 and blamed the bottom's sour, sedge-fed hay for thinning the cream; the veterinarian Dr. Roland Kepp revived and elaborated this theory in a 1926 paper to the county farm club, citing an inflated "two tenths" reading from the college's Bulletin No. 214 (the bulletin's actual finding, verified later by Falke, was at most 0.05 points in winter and none in summer). No theory could be proven, and each had committed advocates: Vose for water, Teague for theft, Grigg and Kepp for hay. The dispute consumed annual meetings, crossroads talk, and eventually a formal hearing, without anyone thinking, as Falke later put it, that "the trouble might be furniture." Documents: r09, r13, r15, r16, r17, r22, r23, r24 (plus the earlier-summarized r02–r08 measurement/exhibit sources).

### Story 3: The Nine-Week Anomaly of 1911

On 8 May 1911 the station's own measuring glass broke, and a borrowed measure was used in its place for nine weeks, until 10 July 1911 — precise dates given by Falke (r22) and corroborated exactly by Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary entry of 8 May 1911 ("A. home cross about a broken measure," r24). Through those nine weeks, with the same herds, same pasture, same tester, and same season, the station's recorded tests rose markedly; the day the station's own (defective) glass resumed, the figures fell back to their old level. Ansel Keddie noted the fact in his own test book and set a question mark against it rather than draw a conclusion he could not prove (Duncan Keddie, r18). Privately, per Duncan, his father told the family at home — twice, once at supper and once at the door, "which was not his way" — that he suspected "something in the glass," fourteen years before anyone could prove it. This episode functioned as an unplanned natural experiment: by varying only the measuring instrument while holding the cows, land, season, and personnel constant, it isolated the instrument as the cause of the deficiency and stands as the single strongest piece of contemporaneous evidence against both the theft and the wet-hay theories, since neither the tester's behavior nor the hay changed during those nine weeks, yet the readings did. (Dr. Kepp, r16, later mischaracterized the interval as lasting only six weeks in order to dismiss it as a seasonal quirk — a distortion refuted by the primary test-book record and Orra's diary alike.) Documents: r03 (Keddie's own book, referenced), r09, r16, r17, r18, r22, r24.

### Story 4: The 1919 Water Circular and the Brant–Keddie Rift

In 1919, manager Selby Vose circulated a written warning to the Ostrey Hollow patrons accusing them, in substance, of adding water to their milk. Ansel Keddie — who by then privately suspected the glassware, not the patrons — refused to let the circulars go out by mail or be left on the station platform; he insisted on delivering them himself, on foot, door to door, "and stood at the door while each man read it" (Orra's diary, r24, which gives eleven families as the number visited; Hazel Brant's 1981 account, r17, instead gives nine families to six farms — the contemporaneous diary is preferred here). He came home the night of the delivery "at eleven at night with his hat still on." Two days later his own brother-in-law, Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom — the largest patron on the bottom, married to Ansel's sister Orra — came to the Keddie yard and, without coming inside, confronted Ansel: he had hauled whole milk to that station since it opened and Ansel knew it, so why had Ansel carried the accusatory paper himself if he did not believe it? Ansel answered that he had carried it himself because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet turned and left, and the two brothers-in-law — who had shared a Sunday table for thirty years — did not speak again until Emmet's death in the spring of 1929, ten years later (Orra's diary, r24; corroborated by Roy Brant's account of the same rift, r09). The episode shows the human cost of the unresolved mystery falling hardest on the two men least responsible for it: the tester who already suspected his own instrument, and the largest patron who trusted him. Documents: r09, r17, r18, r24.

### Story 5: The 1925 Arbitration Hearing and Keddie's Dismissal

The Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned in January 1925. A board of arbitration, chaired by the Ashlin attorney Alonzo Frick, sat from 17 March to 4 May 1925 (Orra's diary gives the opening date exactly; Falke's report, r22, gives the full span), partly at the Cooperative's Ashlin office and partly at the station itself, in the weigh room, with onlookers standing outside on the platform (Cleve Oram, r21, who stood there as a young man). The board heard all three rival theories argued — water, theft, and wet hay — reached no finding of cause, and awarded the patrons $3,800 "without admission" (Frick, r13, the board's own chairman; Falke, r22, independently; Oram's secondhand $3,500 figure, r21, is not preferred — see Section E). Four documents were put before the board: the station's own test book in Keddie's hand, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book (lent for the purpose), and the state inspector's notebook (Bertram Kyle, the Cooperative's clerk, r23, who physically carried them in and out and noted that no one in the room appears to have laid the station book beside the inspector's book and compared them). The condemned glassware itself — sitting in the station's loft only twenty feet above where two of the hearing's sessions were held — was never produced or examined (Oram, r21; Duncan Keddie, r18). Ansel Keddie was dismissed on 30 April 1925, four days before the award was announced, "so that the finding found nothing and the dismissal said everything" (Duncan, r18; exact date corroborated by Orra's diary, r24). Privately, per Frick (r13), Keddie told him in a hearing-room corridor that he suspected the patrons himself, though he would not say so publicly — a claim in some tension with Duncan's account that his father had privately blamed the glass since 1911 (see Section F). Documents: r13, r18, r19, r21, r22, r23, r24.

### Story 6: Ansel Keddie's Final Years and Death (1925–1934)

Put out after 29 seasons with only a dismissal letter, Ansel Keddie went home, hung his tester's apron behind the shed door, and asked what there was for supper (Orra's diary, r24). He turned immediately to carpentry, a trade he had not practiced for pay before, beginning with the family's own long-neglected cow shed and working outward to neighbors' doors and gates; Cleve Oram (r21) recalls him hanging a barn door that "swung true for thirty years." He worked, by his wife's account, "from light to dark and whistles, which he never did when he was paid" (r24). He never attended another Association meeting, "and he was a man who liked meetings" (his father, per Hazel Brant's telling of Roy Brant's account, r09/r17). He died at home in February 1934, without much pain, his son Duncan having come up in the night; more people attended the burial than expected, and several who had not spoken to him in nine years told his widow what a hand he was with a door — but, Orra recorded, "not one of them said the other thing" (r24). He told Orra once, and only once, that "a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name." Orra outlived him by fourteen years, dying in 1948 at 82, and never spoke of the matter except once a year, in April — the month of his dismissal (r24; corroborated by Duncan, r18). He did not live to see his name cleared, twenty-four years later, in 1958. Documents: r18, r21, r24.

### Story 7: The 1958 Discovery — Ivy Keddie and the Crate in the Loft

Ivy Keddie — Ansel's granddaughter (see Section B1), a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, and daughter of Duncan Keddie — began researching the Ostrey Hollow question in the mid-1950s. She borrowed Roy Brant's family weigh book in 1958 (Roy lent it, and was careful that it was a loan, not a gift, per his daughter Hazel, r17). She obtained Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks from the state college library, where they had sat unrequested since a 1947 transfer (Duncan, r18), and found the volume covering 1923, with its contemporaneous 12 June 1923 condemnation entry. She corresponded with Rosalie Cudd of Vessey County, who supplied independent cheese-factory test records for the same Hessel Bottom farms (1915–1930, testing normally at roughly 3.78–3.82). In February 1958, climbing a ladder into the loft over the old station's intake room, she found the condemned pipette crate under a tarpaulin, where it had sat since being ordered off the scale by inspector Strawn in June 1923. She carried it east to Farland and, on 9 March 1958, measured the nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette (Falke, r22, gives the exact date; the "eleven pipettes" figure in r12's 1959 footnote and r19's Chronicle account is a minority figure — see Section E). Every pipette was blown to the standard 17.6 c.c. mark; none delivered it. This measurement, combined with the station's own historical ledgers and Strawn's notebook, became the basis of a joint bulletin with Professor Leland Vaught. Documents: r10, r11, r17, r18, r19, r22.

---

### Story 8: Bulletin No. 471, Public Vindication, and the Fate of the Records

In October 1958 the State Agricultural College at Farland published Bulletin No. 471, jointly credited to Professor Leland Vaught and Ivy Keddie, laying out the pipette measurements and concluding that no milk was watered and no cream was stolen at Ostrey Hollow — the shortfall was entirely instrumental. The Ashlin Chronicle ran the story on its front page on 3 October 1958 (r19), reaching a wide local audience, though with two errors of its own (Ivy's relationship to Ansel; the pipette count) later corrected by Falke's 1959 county report (r22), which independently re-derived the key figures and, on obtaining Bulletin No. 214's actual text, retracted its own initial concession to the wet-hay theory. Dell Rundle's 1939 jubilee pamphlet was reprinted in 1959 with a postscript acknowledging the same finding via the ledger's "station gain" entries. Ammon & Sons' own 1949 company history (r20) had already, unknowingly, supplied the likely mechanism (a reground mold) nine years before the crate was even found. The physical and documentary record was gradually consolidated: the station's test book went from the Cooperative's Ashlin office (1925–1959) to the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1959; the Brant weigh book passed from Roy to his daughter Hazel in 1962 and was donated in 1963; Strawn's notebooks remained at the Farland college library; the Alder Corners test book stayed in Vessey County, transferring to that county's own historical society in 1970; and the condemned crate itself, after being measured, was deposited with the Historical Rooms in 1959 alongside the station's test book (Duncan Keddie's 1968/1970 provenance notes, r18). Ansel Keddie's name was cleared in 1958, twenty-four years after his death; his widow did not live to see it. Documents: r10, r11, r12, r18, r19, r20, r22.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1.** Ivy Keddie is Ansel Keddie's **granddaughter**: she is the daughter of Duncan Keddie, who is Ansel and Orra's son (Duncan's own 1968 genealogical notes, r18, state this plainly and note he has "already twice seen it printed otherwise"). Falke's 1959 county report (r22) independently made the same "great-niece" error in its main text and explicitly retracted it in a postscript after verification: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece." The Ashlin Chronicle (r19) still carries the uncorrected "great-niece" claim. Resolution: Duncan is the most authoritative possible source (Ansel's own son and Ivy's own father, writing specifically to correct the record), and Falke's independent verification agrees — "granddaughter" is correct.

**B2.** Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant are **second cousins**: their fathers, Duncan Keddie and Roy Brant, are first cousins (children of siblings Orra Brant Keddie and Emmet Brant), making Duncan's daughter Ivy and Roy's daughter Hazel second cousins — "the children of first cousins," as Duncan states it (r18), and Hazel independently gives the identical relationship and explicitly rejects being called Ivy's aunt or cousin-once-removed (r17). Cleve Oram's oral account (r21) instead calls them "first cousin once removed," which is not preferred: it is a single secondhand valley account against two precise, concordant statements from the family members themselves. The sources leave nothing material open in the intervening generations — both family-insider accounts agree on every link (Emmet/Orra siblings → Roy/Duncan first cousins → Hazel/Ivy second cousins).

**B3.** Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**, who organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, served as its first president, and wrote its by-laws (Duncan Keddie, r18; Bertram Kyle, r23, who independently rejects the competing "nephew" rumor as "nonsense"). This matters because it makes Selby not a neutral hired manager but the son of the Association's founding figure, inheriting institutional standing and, arguably, a stake in the Association's reputation; it is Selby, in this position, who issued the 1919 circular blaming the patrons for watering their milk rather than questioning the Association's own equipment — a choice more explicable if he saw himself as defending an institution his father had built rather than investigating it.

**B4.** The sources indicate **no established family relationship** between the Vose and Keddie families. Duncan Keddie addresses this directly (r18, Section III), stating there was "no connection whatever" and specifically debunking a persistent piece of valley gossip that his mother Orra and Gideon Vose's wife were cousins, noting his mother — who could "not pass a headstone without giving you the connection" — never once included a Vose in her account of who was related to whom. Cleve Oram's account (r21) repeats the rumor ("the Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort") but explicitly disclaims certainty ("I could not draw you the line on paper"). Resolution favors Duncan: he is a direct family member with a demonstrated, careful interest in genealogical accuracy, against admittedly uncertain hearsay.

## Section C — Math (show working)

**C1.**
(a) The pipettes were blown to deliver 17.6 c.c. but actually delivered a measured average of **16.72 c.c.** (r04's measurement sheet; r07 Item 14; the bulletin's own reproduced conclusion in r07/r11). A competing figure of 16.27 c.c. appears in r11's body text and r19's Chronicle account, but is rejected: 16.72/17.6 = exactly **0.95** (a 5% shortfall), which is an unusually clean ratio for a real measurement to hit by chance, and it is the figure consistent with every other number in the record (see below), whereas 16.27/17.6 = 0.9244, an unclean ratio that (as shown in (b)) implies a true average outside the range independently corroborated by three other sources. r11 also self-contradicts: its body text uses 16.27/118,000 lb, but its own reproduced "conclusion" paragraph cites 76,000 lb, which only follows from 16.72. **Proportion: the glass delivered 95% of its marked volume, i.e., was 5% short.**

(b) Recorded average test = 3.61 (majority figure: r02, r03, r04, r07, r11, r22). Since recorded = true × 0.95, true = 3.61 / 0.95 = **3.80**. This is not an isolated number: it matches Larrow Green's own contemporaneous average of 3.78 (r22), the Alder Corners cheese-factory test records for the same Hessel Bottom herds of 3.78–3.82 (r10), and the Ostrey Hollow station's own post-1923 average with corrected glass of 3.85 (r22) — four independent figures converging on the 3.78–3.85 band, against which 3.80 fits and the alternative 3.905 (implied by 16.27 c.c.) does not. **True average: 3.80. Shortfall: 3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19 points** — matching "nineteen hundredths" given independently by Hazel Brant (r17) and Falke (r22), both citing the corrected bulletin figure.

(c) Total intake over the affected years = 40,000,000 lb (majority: r02, r04, r07, r11, r23; the 44,000,000 lb figure from Teague/r05 and Rundle/r12 is not preferred — see Section E). Shortfall = 40,000,000 × 0.0019 = **76,000 lb** of butterfat, which is exact and matches r04/r07's own stated figure directly.

(d) At 25 cents/lb (Kyle, r23 — the only figure that reconciles cleanly): 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000** (matching r04/r07; the $19,600 figure in r12/r19 is not preferred, see Section E). The patrons received $3,800 from the 1925 arbitration award. $3,800 / $19,000 = **exactly 1/5 (20%)** — a clean fraction that itself supports both $19,000 and $3,800 as the correct paired figures.

**C2.** Largest patron = Emmet Brant, per his weigh book as read by his granddaughter Hazel Brant (r17): in 1910 he delivered 200,000 lb of milk, credited at a test of 3.42.
(a) Credited butterfat = 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb**.
(b) Multiple independent narrators (Roy Brant, r09; Hazel Brant, r17; Falke, r22) describe the deficiency as a flat, constant *point* difference across seasons and years, not a constant proportion — so applying the corrected flat deficiency of 0.19 points: true test = 3.42 + 0.19 = 3.61; true credit = 200,000 × 0.0361 = 7,220 lb; owed and not credited = 7,220 − 6,840 = **380 lb**. *(Note: applying the proportional 0.95 ratio instead gives true = 3.42/0.95 = 3.60, true credit = 7,200 lb, shortfall = 360 lb. The two models differ by only 20 lb; the flat-point model is preferred because it is the one the sources themselves explicitly describe.)*
(c) No 1910-specific butterfat price is given anywhere in the sources; only the multi-year average of 25 cents/lb (Kyle, r23) is available. Applying it as an approximation: 380 × $0.25 = **≈$95.00**. This should be treated as an estimate using an out-of-year average price, not a verified 1910 figure — the sources do not allow a precise answer here.

**C3.**
(a) Total credited to patrons = 40,000,000 lb × 3.61% = **1,444,000 lb** butterfat.
(b) Total actually received at the creamery = 40,000,000 lb × 3.80% (true average) = **1,520,000 lb**. This is corroborated independently by Bertram Kyle's ledger figure (r23): the "station gain" line was entered "as usual" at **5.25%**, and 1,444,000 × 1.0525 ≈ 1,520,110 lb — matching the true-average method to within rounding.
(c) Difference = 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 lb (consistent with C1c). As a **percentage of the credited total** (the ledger's own convention, since "station gain" was booked as a percentage of what had already been credited): 76,000 / 1,444,000 = **5.26%**, matching Kyle's stated 5.25% almost exactly. As a percentage of the true/received total instead: 76,000 / 1,520,000 = 5.0%. **The two are not the same number; the historically-used base was the credited amount, giving ≈5.25–5.26%.**

**C4.**
(a) Invoice total = **$77.93** (itemized: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93, per r20's own reproduced invoice). The commonly repeated $77.39 (r19, and r20's own prose) is an arithmetic transcription error against the invoice's own line items.
(b) The invoice bought **six dozen (72) pipettes**, each short by 17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88 c.c.**

**C5.**
(a) The faulty glass was in continuous use for **26 years**: from 1 May 1897 (Orra's diary, r24) to 12 June 1923 (Strawn's contemporaneous circuit-book entry, r06/r22) — a span itself independently confirmed as "twenty-six years" in r19 and r20. The money calculation, however, runs over **25 full delivery years**: 40,000,000 lb ÷ 1,600,000 lb/year (Kyle's stated annual rate, r23) = exactly 25. The two numbers differ because the glass's first year (1897, May–December) and last year (1923, January–June) were only *partial* delivery years under the faulty glass; the headline arithmetic uses only the complete calendar years in between (1898–1922 = 25 full years) for a clean per-year rate, while the glass itself was physically installed and removed at points 26 years apart.
(b) The station's first-season intake is given as **approximately 1,100,000 lb** (Bertram Kyle, r23) — but this figure is explicitly sourced by Kyle to oral recollection ("from men who were there... not in any book I ever ruled"), not to any surviving written record, and is uncorroborated elsewhere. It should be treated as an approximate, unverified figure rather than an established one.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran from 8 May 1911 to 10 July 1911 (about nine weeks), when the station's own broken glass was replaced with a borrowed measure (Falke, r22; dated exactly by Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary entry, r24). The variable was the testing instrument alone; held constant were the herds, the pasture, the tester, the season (within those nine weeks), and the water supply. The result: recorded tests rose markedly for the duration of the borrowed glass and fell back to the old level the moment the station's own glass resumed. This establishes causation directly, by observation of a real-world swap of the single relevant variable, decades before anyone could measure the glass in a lab — something the 1958 arithmetic alone (comparing marked vs. delivered volume) could never establish on its own, since a calculation shows only that the glass *could* produce an error, not that it *did*, historically, drive the actual valley-wide deficiency. What the interval does **not** establish: the precise magnitude of the glass's error (only the later 1958 burette measurement quantified that); which specific mechanical flaw was responsible; or, strictly, that no other factor coincided with the nine weeks (Kepp's objection that this was the early-summer flush season is not fully answered by the interval alone, though the convergence with the ledger and 1958 evidence closes that gap).

**D2.** Three independent lines of evidence rule out theft by the tester. (1) The 1911 nine-week natural experiment (Ansel Keddie's own test book, r03; corroborated by Orra's diary, r24; synthesized by Falke, r22): the same tester's recorded figures rose and fell purely with the instrument, which is inconsistent with a theory requiring the tester's own conduct to explain the deficiency. (2) The Association's own "station gain" ledger entries (Bertram Kyle, the clerk who copied them for decades, r23): every year, *more* butterfat arrived at the creamery in the Hollow's cream than had been credited to the patrons — the opposite of what cream theft by the tester would produce. (3) Hobart Sill, the receiving buttermaker at Larrow Green with no institutional stake in Ostrey Hollow's defense, independently observed that cream from the Hollow consistently arrived "heavy" (r14) — a physically separate observation, from a different vantage point (the receiving end, not the station), corroborating the ledger pattern. These three are independent because they come from different observers (the tester's own contemporaneous record, the Association's bookkeeping clerk, and an outside buttermaker), using different methods (a natural instrument-swap experiment, ledger accounting, and direct handling of the received cream) at different times. Reason (2), the ledger pattern, would hold even if the glassware had never been measured in 1958, since it was recorded contemporaneously, year by year, decades before the 1958 discovery. As for whether the bookkeeper ever questioned these entries: **no** — Wilbur Teague, who was among those who pressed the theft theory at the 1925 hearing, wrote the "station gain" line into the ledger every year of his 40-year service and, per Kyle, "never asked anybody anything"; Kyle himself, who copied the same line for years, admits it "never once crossed my mind to wonder at it." The very entries that refuted the accusation sat in the accuser's own handwriting, unexamined, for decades.

**D3.** Selby Vose almost certainly **knew that the tests ran low**, since this was common valley knowledge from 1902 onward and was the explicit premise of his own 1919 circular accusing the patrons of watering their milk. But there is no evidence he understood **why**. Bertram Kyle's memoir (r23) states plainly that Vose, in signing the yearly station account sheets — the very sheets carrying the "station gain" entries that, properly read, pointed away from the patrons and toward the equipment — habitually read only the bottom-line total and never the itemized lines above it: "He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign." So the answer is two-part: yes to knowing the fact of the deficiency (it was the reason for his own circular), no to any evidence he grasped its cause, despite the disproving evidence having passed under his signature every year of his tenure.

## Section E — Contradictions (selected; fuller list summarized)

Below, each entry gives the conflicting claims, the narrators, the resolution (or "cannot be settled"), and the method.

1. **Pipette delivery**: 16.72 c.c. (r04, r07) vs. 16.27 c.c. (r11 body, r19). *Resolved* for 16.72, by arithmetic clean-ness (0.95 ratio) and convergence with four independent "true average" figures (see C1b). Method: arithmetic + independent convergence.
2. **Butterfat shortfall**: 76,000 lb (r04, r07 — matches exact arithmetic) vs. 118,000 lb (r11 body — self-contradicts its own conclusion) vs. 67,000 lb (r13 Frick and r21 Oram — but these are **not independent**: both report the same "patrons' 1925 reckoning," so they count as one source). *Resolved* for 76,000 lb. Method: arithmetic + de-duplication of non-independent sources.
3. **Total intake**: 40,000,000 lb (majority, arithmetically clean) vs. 44,000,000 lb (Teague/r05 and Rundle/r12, likely not independent of each other). *Resolved* for 40,000,000. Method: arithmetic + majority + source dependency.
4. **Recorded average test**: 3.61 (majority) vs. 3.16 (Teague/r05, repeated once by Frick/r13 explicitly citing Teague — one source, not two). *Resolved* for 3.61.
5. **Condemnation date**: 12 June 1923 (Strawn's contemporaneous circuit-book, r06; Falke, r22) vs. 12 July 1923 (Vose/r08, Rundle/r12) vs. 1922 (Kyle/r23, a 30-years-later memoir). *Resolved* for 12 June 1923 — a contemporaneous primary official record outranks later recollections.
6. **Pipettes recovered**: nine (r04, r07, r11, r21, r22 — majority, including Falke's exact dated account and Oram's independent physical memory) vs. eleven (r12's 1959 footnote, r19 — likely sharing one origin). *Resolved* for nine.
7. **Dozen purchased**: six dozen (r07, r19, and r20's own primary invoice) vs. five dozen (r11, r21). *Resolved* for six dozen — the supplier's own invoice is the strongest possible source.
8. **Bulletin 214's finding**: "five hundredths... nothing in summer" (verbatim, quoted by r04, r07, r11, and directly re-verified by Falke's postscript in r22) vs. "two tenths" (r15 Grigg, r16 Kepp — **same wrong value, two narrators**). *Tie-break*: Falke's postscript quotes the bulletin itself after writing away for it — the only source that checked the primary document directly. Grigg and Kepp both had a stake in the wet-hay theory, and Kepp's paper (1926) postdates and may derive from Grigg's already-circulating 1919 misstatement (1908 report reissued in 1919) — not fully independent repetition of the same distortion.
9. **1911 anomaly duration**: nine weeks (r03, r04, r07, r11, r22 — primary source plus exact corroborating dates) vs. six weeks (r16 Kepp, unsourced, self-serving). *Resolved* for nine weeks.

## Section F — Theory

**What actually happened:** In 1897 the Ostrey Hollow station purchased, from Ammon & Sons, six dozen milk-test pipettes from a lot whose mold had apparently been reground, producing a defect uniform enough across the whole lot to pass ordinary inspection: every pipette delivered about 16.72 c.c. against its 17.6 c.c. mark — a constant 5% shortfall. Because the error was purely instrumental and identical on every use, it depressed every test made with that glass by the same proportion, in every season, on every farm, regardless of what the cows ate or drank — which explains both why the deficiency was flat across all months (ruling out any theory tied to seasonal feed) and why it began precisely when the new glass entered service (1 May 1897) and vanished precisely when it was replaced (June/July 1923, after which the average jumped from 3.61 to 3.85). Because the fault lay entirely in a single crate of glassware, nothing on the farms or at the station ever needed to change to produce or end the effect — only the glass itself needed replacing, which happened, unremarked, in 1923.

**The three defeated theories:**
1. *Water adulteration* (advanced by manager Selby Vose, 1919 circular) — defeated by the state inspector's repeated lactometer tests from 1908 onward, which never once found added water (Falke, r22, citing the inspector's own circuit record).
2. *Cream theft by the tester* (advanced by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague and pressed at the 1925 hearing) — defeated by the 1911 nine-week natural experiment (same tester, different instrument, figures moved with the instrument) and by the Association's own "station gain" ledger entries showing more, not less, fat arriving at the creamery than was credited (see D2).
3. *Wet/bog hay* (advanced by the 1908 committee under Nyle Grigg, revived by Dr. Kepp in 1926) — defeated by Bulletin No. 214's actual, verified finding (at most 0.05 points in winter, none in summer — far too small to explain a flat 0.19-point year-round gap), by the 1911 anomaly occurring during the summer flush when hay effects should be negligible, and by Rosalie Cudd's independent Alder Corners records showing the same Hessel Bottom herds testing normally (3.78–3.82) when hauled to a different station.

**Subsidiary questions:** The 1925 arbitration board never examined the condemned glassware — it was not among the four exhibits Kyle (r23) lists as entered into evidence, and it sat, per Cleve Oram (r21), in the loft roughly twenty feet above two of the hearing's own sessions, untouched. As for what the tester himself believed: per his son Duncan (r18), Ansel privately suspected "something in the glass" from 1911 onward, though he never committed this to his own book beyond a question mark, being unwilling to record what he could not prove. This sits uneasily beside Alonzo Frick's account (r13) that Keddie privately told him, in a corridor during the 1925 hearing, that he suspected the patrons — a claim the sources do not let us reconcile with Duncan's account; it may reflect an earlier suspicion later supplanted, a guarded remark not meant literally, or a genuine conflict between a family member's long-run memory and a single reported aside. **This point cannot be fully settled from the sources.**

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

For a quarter century, milk from Hessel Bottom's six farms tested markedly lower at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station than anywhere else in the valley, costing its patrons roughly $19,000 in unpaid butterfat. Farmers suspected the tester, Ansel Keddie, of theft; the manager suspected them of watering their milk; agricultural committees blamed the bottom's wet hay. All three theories were wrong. In 1897 the station had bought a crate of milk-test pipettes with a manufacturing defect: they delivered less liquid than marked, so every test read low by a constant margin, in every season, for 26 years. Keddie was dismissed in 1925 without proof of wrongdoing and died in 1934 still under suspicion. In 1958 his granddaughter Ivy found the discarded, uncalibrated glass in the station's loft, measured it, and proved the instrument — not any person — was at fault. It took eight separate records because no one witness ever held the ledgers, the diary, the invoice, and the lab result at once.
