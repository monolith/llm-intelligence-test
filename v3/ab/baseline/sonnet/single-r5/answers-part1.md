### Story 1: The Founding of Ostrey Hollow and the Purchase of Flawed Glass (1895–1898)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, organized by valley farmers in 1889 with its main creamery at Larrow Green (built 1891), built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up-valley, over the winter of 1895–96 to serve the six farms of Hessel Bottom. The station opened 4 May 1896 (Falke, r22) with Ansel Keddie as tester and separator man, a post he held 29 seasons. For its first year the station had no glassware of its own: Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green, lent his own testing glass to open it (r14), and one piece came back chipped. The station's own outfit — ordered from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet — was invoiced 14 April 1897: six dozen (72) milk-test pipettes at 17.6 c.c., twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, and a four-bottle hand tester, itemized total $77.93 (r20's own reproduced invoice; the $77.39 figure quoted in Ammon's own prose and independently in the Ashlin Chronicle, r19, is an arithmetic error — the line items sum to $77.93). The outfit went into use 1 May 1897, confirmed by Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary entry that day ("A. says the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green," r24) and by the invoice-to-use timeline in r19/r20. (r11 and r23 instead give 1 May 1898; the contemporaneous diary is preferred — see Section E.) Unknown to anyone, the pipette mold had apparently been reground and the lot ran consistently short: each pipette actually delivered about 16.72 c.c. against its 17.6 c.c. mark, a defect uniform enough across the whole lot to pass ordinary spot-checks (Ammon's own theory, r20). This crate of glass would remain in daily use, silently shorting every test made with it, for the next 26 years. Documents: r07 (Item 2/invoice reference), r14, r18, r19, r20, r22, r23, r24.

### Story 2: The Long Complaint and the Three Rival Theories (1902–1926)

From very nearly the station's opening, Ostrey Hollow's recorded milk tests ran below the rest of the valley: a station average of 3.61 against Larrow Green's own 3.78 (Falke, r22), a gap later fixed by the corrected bulletin arithmetic at 0.19 points, flat in every month and season (Hazel Brant, r17; Falke, r22). The first complaint of record came in March 1902 (Orra's diary, r24, matching Falke). Three explanations circulated for a quarter century. (1) Added water: the Association's manager, Selby Vose, believed the Hollow patrons were watering their milk and circulated a warning to that effect in 1919. (2) Theft: the bookkeeper Wilbur Teague and others suspected the tester, Ansel Keddie, of skimming cream for himself; this view was pressed at the 1925 hearing. (3) Wet hay: a committee headed by Nyle Grigg sampled the Hessel Bottom herds through 1908 and blamed the bottom's sour, sedge-fed hay for thinning the cream; the veterinarian Dr. Roland Kepp revived and elaborated this theory in a 1926 paper to the county farm club, citing an inflated "two tenths" reading from the college's Bulletin No. 214 (the bulletin's actual finding, verified later by Falke, was at most 0.05 points in winter and none in summer). No theory could be proven, and each had committed advocates: Vose for water, Teague for theft, Grigg and Kepp for hay. The dispute consumed annual meetings, crossroads talk, and eventually a formal hearing, without anyone thinking, as Falke later put it, that "the trouble might be furniture." Documents: r09, r13, r15, r16, r17, r22, r23, r24 (plus the earlier-summarized r02–r08 measurement/exhibit sources).

### Story 3: The Nine-Week Anomaly of 1911

On 8 May 1911 the station's own measuring glass broke, and a borrowed measure was used in its place for nine weeks, until 10 July 1911 — precise dates given by Falke (r22) and corroborated exactly by Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary entry of 8 May 1911 ("A. home cross about a broken measure," r24). Through those nine weeks, with the same herds, same pasture, same tester, and same season, the station's recorded tests rose markedly; the day the station's own (defective) glass resumed, the figures fell back to their old level. Ansel Keddie noted the fact in his own test book and set a question mark against it rather than draw a conclusion he could not prove (Duncan Keddie, r18). Privately, per Duncan, his father told the family at home — twice, once at supper and once at the door, "which was not his way" — that he suspected "something in the glass," fourteen years before anyone could prove it. This episode functioned as an unplanned natural experiment: by varying only the measuring instrument while holding the cows, land, season, and personnel constant, it isolated the instrument as the cause of the deficiency and stands as the single strongest piece of contemporaneous evidence against both the theft and the wet-hay theories, since neither the tester's behavior nor the hay changed during those nine weeks, yet the readings did. (Dr. Kepp, r16, later mischaracterized the interval as lasting only six weeks in order to dismiss it as a seasonal quirk — a distortion refuted by the primary test-book record and Orra's diary alike.) Documents: r03 (Keddie's own book, referenced), r09, r16, r17, r18, r22, r24.

### Story 4: The 1919 Water Circular and the Brant–Keddie Rift

In 1919, manager Selby Vose circulated a written warning to the Ostrey Hollow patrons accusing them, in substance, of adding water to their milk. Ansel Keddie — who by then privately suspected the glassware, not the patrons — refused to let the circulars go out by mail or be left on the station platform; he insisted on delivering them himself, on foot, door to door, "and stood at the door while each man read it" (Orra's diary, r24, which gives eleven families as the number visited; Hazel Brant's 1981 account, r17, instead gives nine families to six farms — the contemporaneous diary is preferred here). He came home the night of the delivery "at eleven at night with his hat still on." Two days later his own brother-in-law, Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom — the largest patron on the bottom, married to Ansel's sister Orra — came to the Keddie yard and, without coming inside, confronted Ansel: he had hauled whole milk to that station since it opened and Ansel knew it, so why had Ansel carried the accusatory paper himself if he did not believe it? Ansel answered that he had carried it himself because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet turned and left, and the two brothers-in-law — who had shared a Sunday table for thirty years — did not speak again until Emmet's death in the spring of 1929, ten years later (Orra's diary, r24; corroborated by Roy Brant's account of the same rift, r09). The episode shows the human cost of the unresolved mystery falling hardest on the two men least responsible for it: the tester who already suspected his own instrument, and the largest patron who trusted him. Documents: r09, r17, r18, r24.
