# Comprehensive Retention Notes: Retellings 1-16 (Ostrey Hollow Milk Testing Case)

## ENTITIES & RELATIONSHIPS

### PEOPLE - Core Actors
- **Ansel Keddie** (1867?-1934): Tester at Ostrey Hollow skimming station from 1896-1924 (29 seasons). Kept meticulous test book by hand. Dismissed April 30, 1925. Died 1934, exonerated 24 years later (1958). Statement to arbitration board March 17, 1925: "I have tested as I was taught and as the state man had seen me do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons." Alonzo Frick believed him honest and noted privately that Keddie thought patrons were at fault but wouldn't say so in the room.
- **Orra Keddie**: Ansel's wife, first cousin of old Mrs. Vose. Sister of Emmet Brant (major patron). Outlived Ansel, had stigma her whole life. After 1919 circular, walked to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane.
- **Emmet Brant** (1854-1929): Largest dairy farmer on Hessel Bottom, most cows, most hay ground, largest patron of Ostrey Hollow station every year on record. Brother of Orra. Father of Roy Brant. After 1919 circular, did not speak to Ansel for rest of life—lived within sight of each other's chimneys but never said good morning. Kept meticulous weigh book from January 1, 1898 until his death. His milk would have been most affected by proportional error (richest milk).
- **Roy Brant** (b. 1888, age 67 in 1955): Emmet Brant's son, farmer at Hessel Bottom. Continued father's weigh book in same manner. Wrote account for county farm bureau 1955. Lent weigh book to Ivy Keddie 1958. Could not afford to be talked about despite having largest herd, didn't haul over ridge despite six-week cost advantage. First cousin of Duncan Keddie.
- **Hazel Brant**: Roy Brant's daughter. First cousin once removed of Vira Toland. Received weigh book from Roy Brant 1962 (per earlier notes). Second cousin of Ivy Keddie. Both Hazel and Ivy "had to have it explained to them more than once."
- **Vira Toland** (b. 1912): Narrator of retelling 1. Born top of Ostrey Hollow, daughter of milk hauler, lived there her whole life except 2 years in Ashlin when first married. Witnessed the scandal as child of 7 (1919). Father received 1919 circular, kept it in desk drawer until death.
- **Selby Vose** (Gideon's nephew): Manager of Association 1913-1938. Signed annual station accounts (knew of station gain every year). Sent 1919 circular warning about water. Defended both circular and $3,800 settlement in 1926 address. In 1926 address, claimed water was deliberately added to cans and stopped only when scrutiny began; tests rose 21 hundredths in 1924 (to 3.90). Manager when Ansel was dismissed April 30, 1925 while arbitration board still sitting. Defended state inspector's condemnation as routine equipment maintenance.
- **Gideon Vose** (old): First president Association 1889-1911 (22 years, longest tenure). Founded Association. Drafted by-laws. Wrote Article VII. Died 1917. Heavy, quiet, obstinate man who once thought of law. His Jersey herd tested 4.56 in 1910 (cited by Selby Vose 1926). Wrote by-laws "slowly, and to last" (per nephew Selby).
- **Effie Loomis**: Secretary Association 1901-1940. Kept minute book in own hand. Secretary age 14 in 1889, office age 27 in 1901. Made detailed observations of arbitration proceedings.
- **Wilbur Teague**: Bookkeeper Association 1905-1945 (40 years). Created "station gain" accounting line in 1905 to track discrepancy. Reported to Selby Vose in 1916, told to let alone. Made statement to arbitration board 1925 about structural vulnerability allowing theft without detection: "Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons." System allowed one man to take a little and write down a little less. "The remedy is two men or a lock, and the Association had neither." Carried knowledge 40 years in silence.
- **Hobart Sill**: Buttermaker at Larrow Green creamery, 1893-1934 (41 years). Retired 1934. Handled Ostrey Hollow cream directly, weighed it himself most years. Reported cream from Ostrey Hollow "ran heavy"—more fat in cans than station papers said, year after year. Believed the 1919 circular and water explanation. Lent his own glass equipment to station in 1896 when it opened (one piece came back chipped). Larrow Green patrons' milk averaged 3.78. Believed Hollow milk "really a 3.85 and no better." Interpreted "heavy" cream as evidence of water theft; believed something "got stopped" one summer "along in the twenties." Noted figures rose "and nothing on this earth changed but the season." Made 41 years of butter, never made complaint about station despite observations.
- **Merle Strawn**: State dairy inspector, took Ordell circuit 1907, laid down 1936 (29 years). Condemned the defective pipettes June 12, 1923. Noted in 1923 entries that station tests improved after new glass ordered. In 1919 entry: "I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so." October 1923: confirmed new pipettes deliver their mark correctly. 1924: noted tests "improved." Never mentions water contamination in 29 years of visits. 1928 entry notes station "quieter than it was" after Ansel's dismissal.
- **Ivy Keddie**: Ansel's granddaughter (Duncan Keddie's daughter). Duncan Keddie was Ansel's son, taught school at Ashlin. Ivy gave 1959 historical society presentation, 1972 accession notes. Found crate February 1958 at station loft. Measured defective pipettes March 9, 1958 at State Agricultural College at Farland (mean 16.72 cc vs. 17.6 cc mark). Borrowed Roy Brant's weigh book summer 1958 for college work at Farland. Second cousin of Roy Brant's daughter Hazel.
- **Alonzo Frick**: Attorney, chairman of board of arbitration 1925. Held hearings at Ashlin (March 17, 1925 first session) and at station itself (two subsequent sessions at Ostrey Hollow weigh room). Believed Ansel Keddie's testimony was honest. Noted in 1946 article that board was unprepared to explain contradiction between "finds no cause established" and $3,800 award. Observed privately to Frick that Keddie thought patrons at fault but wouldn't say so publicly.
- **Nyle Grigg**: Farmer of Larrow Green, headed 1908 investigating committee. Not a patron of Ostrey Hollow station (hauls to Larrow Green). Sampled Hessel Bottom herds "every month of that year" (1908). Visited farms three and four times, drew own samples, sealed them, witnessed hauling and testing. Tried scales against known weights. Reprinted 1908 report in 1919 with covering letter claiming State Agricultural College Bulletin 214 confirmed wet hay findings with "two tenths" depression (though actual Bulletin 214 says "five hundredths").
- **Dr. Roland Kepp** (V.S.): Veterinary surgeon of 30 years practice, gave paper to Ordell County Farm Club 1926. Stated wet bottoms yield milk poorer in fat by "a fifth of a point" (0.2). Said Hessel Bottom gap is "twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21" between station and valley. Cited Bulletin 214 as finding bog hay depresses test by "two tenths in the winter feeding." Dismissed 1911 anomaly (six weeks) as insufficient data. Argued remedy is drainage, liming, mineral supplementation, mixed ration.
- **Rosalie Cudd**: Jerome Cudd's niece (actually his daughter per retelling 10). Runs Alder Corners Cheese Company in Vessey County. Took over factory in spring 1941 (age 12 when first vat set, so born ~1929). Provided 1958 letter to Ivy Keddie with summer test figures for four Hessel Bottom farmers testing 3.78-3.82 average 1915-1930. Jerome Cudd tested milk himself, kept own books, never compared to Ostrey Hollow. Rosalie's father Jerome said: "Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge."
- **Jerome Cudd**: Built Alder Corners Cheese Company 1913 (per Rosalie, but Rundle says opened 1914). Ran it until March 1941 (died between setting and cutting of vat). Tested independently, kept books, had no business with Ostrey Hollow figures seven miles away.
- **Dell Rundle**: Schoolteacher at Larrow Green, compiled "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years" jubilee pamphlet 1939, reprinted 1959. Had access to old ledgers while writing, corrected Chapter IV in "Note Added While in Press" upon seeing "station gain" ledger entries proving cream arrived "heavy" not "light," disproving theft theory.
- **Duncan Keddie** (Ansel's son): Teacher at Ashlin. Father of Ivy Keddie. First cousin of Roy Brant.
- **Leland Vaught**: Professor of dairy husbandry at State Agricultural College at Farland. Wrote Bulletin 471 (October 1958) with closing paragraph signed by Vaught and Ivy Keddie. Measured nine pipettes from condemned crate; states mean delivery 16.27 cc (discrepancy with Ivy's measurement sheet which lists individual means averaging to 16.72 cc).

### ORGANIZATIONS & PLACES
- **Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association**: Founded 1889 at Larrow Green. Main creamery built 1891 on Larrow Creek. Office at Ashlin (one room over harness shop). Members: 9 families hauling milk from Ostrey Hollow. Took in 40,000,000 pounds (per earlier notes) or 44,000,000 pounds (per Rundle) of milk over complained-of period.
- **Ostrey Hollow Station**: 7 miles up valley from Larrow Green, at head of Hollow. Opened May 1, 1897 (per test book, May 4, 1896 per recorded start date). Skimming station: received milk, separated it, sent cream down to Larrow Green, gave skim back to farmers. Served 9 families. Station building: plain boarded/battened structure with intake room at road end, loft "never been cleared out since it was put up," creek behind it. Station took in ~900,000 pounds May-end of 1896 (first season).
- **Hessel Bottom**: Wet meadow below hamlet of Ostrey Hollow. Six farms had hay/pasture rights (though some sources say seven). Best milk came from here. Most prominent: Emmet Brant's farm (60 acres of meadow, 38 cows in good year). Water stands on low end into May. Soft place by alders has taken horse to hock. Hay is coarse, cuts late, cows do well on it. Cake at the alders. Under water every spring, not fit to walk on until May. Sedge hay main winter dependence. Seven farms had hay/pasture rights on Hessel Bottom.
- **Larrow Green**: Village where creamery and Association office located. Creeks provide cooling water. Patrons from Larrow Green averaged 3.78 butterfat test (per Hobart Sill).
- **Ashlin**: County seat. Site of Association office, board of arbitration hearings (March 17-May 4, 1925). Ordell County Historical Rooms/Society located there. School where Duncan Keddie taught.
- **Alder Corners Cheese Company**: Factory in Vessey County, over Redlow Ridge. Opened 1913 (per Cudd) or 1914 (per Rundle). Four Hessel Bottom farmers hauled summer flush (June-mid-September) 1915-1930 (16 seasons). Ridge shuts in November, doesn't open before April, some years May. No man hauled in winter. Shorter pull for those four farms than Ostrey Hollow station in summer. Test book never left Vessey County; passed founder to daughter 1941, to Vessey County Historical Society 1970. Test figures for four men averaged 3.80 range (3.78-3.82).
- **Ordell County Historical Rooms/Society**: Where items catalogued 1972 by curator Halbert Nym. Received station test book from Association office (held since 1925). Received crate of pipettes 1959. Received Brant weigh book via Roy Brant 1962 (transmitted to Hazel Brant 1962).
- **State Agricultural College at Farland**: Ivy Keddie measured defective pipettes there March 9, 1958. Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks in library since 1947. Site of Bulletin 471 publication 1958.
- **Vessey County Historical Society**: Received Alder Corners test book 1970.

## TIMELINE OF EVENTS

**1889**: Farmers meet at Larrow Green, organize Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose elected first president. Effie Loomis age 14 at this time, made secretary 1901.

**1891**: Main creamery built at Larrow Green on Larrow Creek. Churn set up. Valley began to be paid in cash once a month.

**1892**: Article VII adopted at annual meeting: "Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." Hinge clause: "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" locks patrons to station's own test, no appeal.

**1893**: Payment by test came in with annual meeting (per Grigg, though notes say 1892 for Article VII). Hobart Sill starts at creamery (1893-1934, 41 years).

**April 14, 1897**: Ammon & Sons Dairy Supply (Tarnet) ships to Larrow Valley Co-op for Ostrey Hollow station: 6 doz pipettes (17.6 cc), 12 doz bottles (8% neck), acid measure, hand tester (4-bottle), freight. Total invoice $77.93.

**May 1, 1897** (per Vaught's Bulletin 471, though some sources say May 4, 1896): Ostrey Hollow station opens with new glassware from Ammon & Sons. Test book begins. First leaf reads: "Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie."

**1896-1897 (partial year 1896)**: Test average 3.80.

**1897-1902 (first years)**: Hobart Sill lends his own glass equipment to station opening (one piece comes back chipped).

**1898-1924**: Test average 3.61 (or within 0.03), consistent across all years except 1911. Roy Brant's father Emmet notes for year 1910: "Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42."

**~1902**: Complaints begin. First said Ostrey Hollow milk didn't test with rest of valley. Roy Brant says "Away back. Nineteen two is the first I remember of it, a man in here shouting that his milk tested under his brother-in-law's down the creek." From 1902 "grumbling out of Ostrey Hollow" (per Grigg). Went on and grew.

**1905**: Wilbur Teague (bookkeeper 1905-1945) creates "station gain" accounting line to track discrepancy.

**1908**: Committee (Nyle Grigg chairman) sampled Hessel Bottom herds January-March. Reported: "The milk is whole and the scales are honest. It is our opinion that the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station." Grigg visited farms 3-4 times, drew own samples, sealed them, witnessed testing, tried scales.

**1911 (May 8-July 10, nine weeks)**: Ostrey Hollow station's own measure broke May 8. Substituted with borrowed measure. Tests rose to 3.80. Own measure returned July 10. Tests dropped back to 3.61 same day. Ansel's notation: question mark, never explained. Dr. Kepp later dismisses this as "six weeks" of summer figures (discrepancy—May 8 to July 10 is nine weeks, not six). Grigg defended against using 1911 as argument: "Six weeks in the flush of the season... when every herd in the county is at its best and the stock are off the wet ground."

**1911**: State Agricultural College Bulletin 214 published: Bog hay depressed butterfat test by "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Later sources (Grigg 1919, Kepp 1926) misquote this as "two tenths" (0.2) rather than "five hundredths" (0.05).

**1913**: Selby Vose becomes manager (holds until 1938).

**1914 or 1915**: Alder Corners Cheese Company opens over Redlow Ridge (built 1913 per Rosalie Cudd, said 1914 per Rundle). Four Hessel Bottom men haul over ridge summer flush 1915-1930 (16 seasons, first time 1915). Resented here as going "over to the enemy."

**1915-1930**: Four Hessel Bottom farmers haul to Alder Corners summer flush. Their milk tests 3.78-3.82 range (average 3.80) at Alder Corners. Same milk tested lower at Ostrey Hollow station (3.61 average).

**1916**: Wilbur Teague reports station-gain discrepancy to Selby Vose. Told to let alone.

**1919**: Selby Vose sends circular to 9 families of Ostrey Hollow: "There was water in the cans coming down from Ostrey Hollow." Circular goes to nine families. Vira Toland's father receives one, keeps it in desk drawer. After circular, Ansel and Emmet Brant did not speak—Orra walked to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane. Roy Brant notes: "My father was one who never forgave it. From 1919 they did not speak — not from any quarrel over the water, but because my father held that the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not." Nyle Grigg reprints 1908 report with 1919 covering letter claiming Bulletin 214 supports their findings.
- Merle Strawn's 1919 circuit entry: "The manager of the Association has sent a circular round to the patrons of this station about water in the cans. I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so."

**1922**: Ansel Keddie compiles summary leaf in test book showing yearly averages. Notes the nine weeks of 1911 anomaly: "Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80."

**June 12, 1923** (Strawn's notebook) or **July 12, 1923** (Rundle's pamphlet—discrepancy): State dairy inspector Merle Strawn visits Ostrey Hollow. Ran station's milk pipettes against calibrated burette. "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new." Crate (9 pipettes survive) stenciled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET" packed in straw, set aside June 1923. Strawn's 1923 entry: "Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk pipettes against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them."

**June 28, 1923**: Association orders replacement pipettes and bottles from Ammon & Sons.

**July 2, 1923**: Ammon & Sons replies, acknowledging order, promising shipment that month, declining to comment on earlier lot.

**July 1923-October 1923**: New pipettes in use at station by autumn flush.

**October 1923**: Merle Strawn returns. Ran new pipettes against burette. "They deliver their mark. Scales true." Strawn's October 1923 entry: "Ostrey Hollow. New glass in use since July. Ran the new pipettes against the burette. They deliver their mark. Scales true."

**1924**: Tests at Ostrey Hollow rise to 3.80. Strawn's 1924 entry: "Ostrey Hollow. Scales true, no added water, tests improved. Their book gives the year at 3.80." Selby Vose points to 1924 rise (21 hundredths, from 3.61 to 3.80 or ~3.80) as evidence water was deliberately added and stopped: "The tests came up when the looking began, and any man who wants a different explanation of that is welcome to stand where I am standing and give us one."

**January 1925**: Ostrey Hollow patrons petition Association, claiming milk never tested like neighbors', paid accordingly long time, asking restitution. Station took in, over years complained of, 40,000,000 pounds (earlier notes) or 44,000,000 pounds (Rundle) of milk. Patrons' loss stated as 19,000-19,600 dollars (Rundle), or 67,000 pounds butterfat (Frick), or 76,000 pounds butterfat (Vaught/Ivy Keddie).

**March 17 - May 4, 1925**: Board of arbitration sits under attorney Alonzo Frick (chairman). First session at Ashlin March 17. Second and third sessions at Ostrey Hollow itself in the station's weigh room.
- **Three explanations argued**: (1) Water added (Selby Vose, manager). (2) Tester taking cream/writing low tests (Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper—stated indirectly). (3) Wet hay thins cream (1908 committee report read into record).
- **Ansel Keddie's statement** (March 17, 1925, standing, taken as given): "I have tested as I was taught and as the state man had seen me do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons. I could not tell us why the figures were what they were, and that I had never taken a pound of cream out of that station." Frick believed him. Privately, Keddie told Frick he thought patrons at fault but wouldn't stand up and accuse neighbors in room.
- **Exhibits before board** (four books only): (1) Ostrey Hollow station test book (Ansel's hand, May 4, 1896 - December 31, 1924). (2) Station receiving book. (3) Emmet Brant's private weigh book (begun January 1, 1897). (4) State dairy inspector circuit notebook (1923).
- **Award** (May 4, 1925): "The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account."

**April 30, 1925**: Ansel Keddie "put out" (dismissed) four days before board made award, while board still sitting. No finding against him from board and never was one.

**Fall 1925**: New tester at bench described as "quick" by Merle Strawn (1925 entry), who notes "quick is not the thing I look for."

Dismisses state inspector's condemnation as routine housekeeping: "Apparatus wears... it is the state man's business to say so before they fail him, and ours to replace them." Notes father Gideon's herd tested 4.56 in 1910. Urges members to bring milk "as it left the cow."
- Dr. Roland Kepp reads paper to Ordell County Farm Club. States wet bottoms yield milk 0.2% poorer in butterfat. Says Hessel Bottom gap is 0.21 points between station and valley. ## THE PIPETTE DEFECT: MATHEMATICAL EVIDENCE

**Defect discovered**: June 12, 1923, by Merle Strawn (Strawn's circuit book). Pipettes (blown for 17.6 cc) only delivered 16.72 cc (mean of 9 measured March 9, 1958, per Ivy's measurement sheet).

**Discrepancy in measurements**: Ivy Keddie's measurement sheet lists individual means as 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71, averaging to 16.72 cc. But Vaught's Bulletin 471 states: "the mean delivery of the nine is **16.27 c.c.**" This is a significant discrepancy (0.45 cc difference). The calculation of defect ratio differs accordingly.

**Using Ivy's measurement (16.72 cc)**:
- Ratio: 16.72 / 17.6 = 0.95 exactly = 95 hundredths of mark
- Shortfall: 17.6 - 16.72 = 0.88 cc per 17.6 cc charge
- Consequence: readings were 5% low

**Using Vaught's stated measurement (16.27 cc)**:
- Ratio: 16.27 / 17.6 = 0.9244 = 92.44 hundredths of mark
- Shortfall: 17.6 - 16.27 = 1.33 cc per 17.6 cc charge
- Consequence: readings were 7.56% low
- Vaught states: "low by one part in twenty" (which would be 5%, not 7.56%)

**Applied to recorded averages (using 16.72 cc)**:
- Recorded average (with defective glass): 3.61% butterfat (1898-1922)
- True average: 3.61 / 0.95 = 3.80% butterfat
- Gap: 3.80 - 3.61 = 0.19% (nineteen hundredths)
- 0.19 is 5% of 3.80
- 0.19 is 5.25% of 3.61

**Applied to recorded averages (using Vaught's 16.27 cc)**:
- Recorded average: 3.61%
- True average: 3.61 / 0.9244 = 3.905%
- Gap: 3.905 - 3.61 = 0.295%

**Tonnage over 25 full years (1898-1922)**:
- Station intake: 40,000,000 pounds (per earlier notes) or 44,000,000 pounds (per Rundle) of milk
- Using 40,000,000 and 16.72 cc (5% error):
  - Recorded (with defective glass): 1,444,000 pounds of butterfat at 3.61%
  - True (as delivered): 1,520,000 pounds of butterfat at 3.80%
  - Missing butterfat: 76,000 pounds
  - At average price 25 cents/pound: $19,000 total value
- Using 40,000,000 and 16.27 cc (7.56% error):
  - Different figures apply
- Using 44,000,000 (Rundle) would yield slightly different numbers
- Board awarded: $3,800 (one-fifth to one-fifth of actual loss depending on calculation)
- Frick's recollection: patrons' own reckoning was 67,000 pounds butter fat (differs from 76,000)

**Distribution of loss**: Error proportional, hit hardest on richest milk. Emmet Brant's milk (richest in valley) would have been most affected. For example: herd recorded at 4.56% was short 0.24 points (shorted most); herd recorded at 3.42% was short 0.18 points (shorted less). This disproportionately affected the richest farmers.

**1911 anomaly explained**: 
- Measure broke May 8, 1911 (per Ansel's note: "Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure.")
- Borrowed measure used May 8-July 10 (nine weeks, per Ansel's July 10 note: "Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.")
- Tests jumped to 3.80 immediately with borrowed measure
- Returned to 3.61 same day own (defective) measure returned July 10
- No water, no theft, no intentional fraud—just defective measuring glass
- Vaught emphasizes: "This is recorded contemporaneously, in the operator's hand, by a man who did not know what he was recording and set a question mark against it." Proposes this is the most powerful evidence: "Here is one that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven years."
- Dr. Kepp in 1926 dismissed 1911 as "six weeks" of summer flush (actually nine weeks—discrepancy), arguing this is insufficient to base conclusion on

**Evidence from other sources**:
- Alder Corners Cheese Company (Vessey County): four Hessel Bottom farmers' milk tested during overlapping summers (16 seasons, 1915-1930). Same milk tested approximately 3.78-3.82 (average 3.80) at Alder Corners vs. ~3.61 at Ostrey Hollow station (0.19-0.21 point difference). Confirms systematic station-test shortfall.
- Hobart Sill's observations: Ostrey Hollow cream "ran heavy"—consistently more fat in cans than station papers said, year after year. Receiving book shows cream arriving with more fat than credited at station. This matches "station gain" accounting perfectly.

**Timeline of discovery**:

## ACCOUNTING LEDGER: THE "STATION GAIN"

**Wilbur Teague's invention (1905)**: Created "station gain" line in annual ledger to track discrepancy between butterfat the station credited to patrons (on its own test) vs. butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in cream from that station.

**Typical annual entry** (e.g., 1898):
"Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account."

**Pattern**: Gain ran consistently at 5.25% of the fat actually in the cream (or equivalently, 5% of the fat credited). This consistency is remarkable—so steady and "as usual" that neither the bookkeeper nor the manager flagged it for investigation.

**Two mathematically correct ways to express the same 5% error**:
1. **76,000 lb is 5% of 1,520,000 lb** (fat actually delivered/true value) using 16.72 cc measurement
2. **76,000 lb is 5.25% of 1,444,000 lb** (fat credited/recorded value) using 16.72 cc measurement

Bookkeeper Teague reckoned surplus against figure already entered (method 2). Neither is wrong; each measures against a different denominator. But only the first (5%) connects directly to the glass defect (16.72/17.6 = 0.95 = 5% error).

**Selby Vose's knowledge**: Teague said he reported the discrepancy to manager Vose in 1916 and was told to "let it alone." Vose signed off on annual sheets every year. Both Effie Loomis and Wilbur Teague state Vose knew what the account was doing (signed sheets every spring).

**Discrepancy across 40 years of accounts (1898-1938+)**: The "station gain" line appeared in every annual ledger for 25+ years with the phrase "as usual," indicating it was so consistent and unremarkable that neither the bookkeeper nor the manager flagged it as worth investigation. Dell Rundle, upon seeing these ledger entries in 1939, realized they proved the opposite of cream theft: "Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand."

## THE ARBITRATION AWARD (May 4, 1925): INTERNAL CONTRADICTION

"The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, **finds no cause established.** The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of **thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission,** to be charged to the station account."

**The contradiction**: 
- "Finds no cause established" = board found no evidence of wrongdoing
- "$3,800 without admission" = Association paid to settle anyway without admitting fault
- Both true simultaneously: Award was generous settlement of unproved claim

**Effie Loomis's observation**: "I have written many things into that book with more pleasure. *Finds no cause established*, and then an award of thirty-eight hundred dollars: I took it down as it was given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once."

**Crate of measures never mentioned in hearing**: State inspector had condemned pipettes June 12, 1923. Crate set aside. Yet no mention of the defective glass in any of the three hearings (March 17-May 4, 1925). Award's phrase about "books and appliances of the station" is form of words, not a detailed list. Exhibits were just four books.

**Alonzo Frick's reflection**: "A board of arbitration can only decide between the accounts that are put to it. Three were put to us; each of them was urged by a man of standing; each of them would have been believed by half the valley; and not one of them was proved. I have wondered since whether the answer was in that room and simply was not in any of the three stories, and whether a lawyer who understood chemistry might have found it."

**Effie's conclusion**: "Forty years of taking down what men say has taught me that the interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying."

### From Retelling 11 (Professor Leland Vaught - Bulletin 471, October 1958):
- Opening: "This station has been asked, at intervals for fifty years, why the butterfat tests at one skimming station in Ordell County ran below those of its own creamery seven miles down the same valley. The question is answered here by reference to a piece of glass."
- Key principle: "the milk pipette is the one instrument in the butterfat test which the plant cannot check against anything else, and it is the instrument on which every figure in the plant depends."
- Process: "A measured charge of milk is drawn into a pipette and delivered into a test bottle. Sulfuric acid is added... The operator reads the fat off directly, in percent."
- Standard charge: "17.6 cubic centimeters"
- Consequence: "Every mark on it means 'this much fat, out of the standard charge.'"
- Three key consequences of short glass:
  1. "The error is proportional and not additive... Rich milk therefore loses more than poor milk, absolutely..."
  2. "The error is precise. A pipette that delivers 16.27 c.c. where it is blown 17.6 delivers 16.27 c.c. every time, for a quarter of a century... It has the appearance of excellent work."
  3. "The error is invisible from inside the plant... the pipette is the standard. It is what the plant measures with. An instrument that is itself the measure cannot be caught by measuring with it."
- Material examined: "Of the lot bought for the Ostrey Hollow station in 1897 — **five dozen** pipettes were purchased — **nine** were recovered from the station's condemned crate, unbroken and legible... - 1911 anomaly: "For nine weeks in the summer of 1911 the station's own pipette was broken and a borrowed measure was used in its place... One variable changed. This is recorded contemporaneously, in the operator's hand, by a man who did not know what he was recording..."
- Magnitude: "16.27 ÷ 17.6 = **0.9244**. The recorded station average over the affected years is **3.61**; the corresponding true average is 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = **3.905**, a shortfall of **0.295** of a point. - "Something got stopped" theory: "There was water in those cans and I said so at the time. Because I will tell you what happened — one summer, along in the twenties, those figures came up and they stayed up, and nothing on this earth changed but the season, and men do not suddenly start pouring better milk out of the same cows in the same barns. Something got stopped. Something got said to somebody."
- Original equipment loan: "When they opened that station up at Ostrey Hollow — that was ninety-six, in May, and there was a crowd went up for it — they had no outfit of their own. Not a piece. It was ordered and it was not come, and they were ready to start taking milk, and there was talk of holding off the whole season. And I said no, they can have mine. I went to my own bench in this room and I made up an outfit for them out of my own glass, my own, that I had used and knew, and it went up the valley on the wagon and they opened on it."
- First season intake: "That first season they took in about nine hundred thousand pounds up there. May to the end of the year, that is, so it was not even a whole one."
- Final assessment: "Whatever the Hollow was doing with its cans, and I have told you what I think about that, the fat came down that hill and it came into my churn and it went out of this room as good butter with this creamery's name on the tub... The butter was good — as good as any, and better than most, and there was never a season it was not."

### From Retelling 15 (Nyle Grigg - 1919 covering letter to 1908 report):
- Committee work: "We sampled those herds through the whole of 1908 — every month of that year, in the barn and on the pasture, out of the night's milk and the morning's, and we did not let a man know which day we were coming. We weighed. We watched the test made and we watched it made again."
- Grigg's impartiality: "I had no interest whatever in the answer. I am not a patron of that station. I haul to Larrow Green and always have... I was chosen for the work on that account and for no other, and I have been abused for the finding by men on both sides of it, which I take to be a fair sign that the finding was an honest one."
- Bulletin 214 citation: "the State Agricultural College at Farland has since published its Bulletin No. 214... That trial found the bog hay to depress the butter fat test by two tenths in the winter feeding. Two tenths, gentlemen, out of a College that had never heard of Hessel Bottom..." (Discrepancy: Bulletin 214 actually says "five hundredths")
- Conclusion: "I am content to let the matter rest where the College and this committee have jointly put it. The remedy lies with the land and not with the station. Drain that bottom, sweeten it, keep the stock off it in the wet of the spring, and feed the coarse hay off it with a mixed ration and not by itself, and the Hollow will test with the rest of us."
- 1908 report quoted: "We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March. The milk is whole and the scales are honest. It is our opinion that the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station."

### From Retelling 16 (Dr. Roland Kepp - 1926):
- Background: "I have found in thirty years that the answer to nine cases in ten is under the animal's feet, and that the tenth is in the water she drinks."
- Wet bottom chemistry: "A wet bottom is not a rich meadow with too much water in it. It is a distinct kind of ground with a distinct kind of chemistry, and the water is the least part of it. Where drainage is wanting, the year's growth does not rot down as it does on a hill field; it lies sour, and the soil that results is an acid soil, poor in lime, and poor in the phosphates."
- Cow physiology: "A cow in milk is a manufactory working on a narrow margin, and she is obliged to find the material of her milk somewhere. If her ration is short of the minerals, she does not simply give less; she robs her own frame to make good, and she goes on robbing it until she cannot."
- Fat as first economy: "They will milk, gentlemen. Wet land is generous of flow and no man on a bog meadow ever complained that his pail was light. But what is in the pail is another matter, and the fat is the first thing to suffer, because the fat is the dearest thing the cow makes and it is the first economy she practices when she is pressed."
- General observation: "The wet bottoms of this county yield a milk poorer in fat by a fifth of a point and every practical man knows it. Set the hill herds of this county against the bottom herds and you will find that difference, farm for farm, wherever you look for it, and the difference does not depend on the breed, on the man, or on the barn. It depends on the ground."
- Hessel Bottom specifics: "The figure of the gap, as it has been given to me and as I have satisfied myself, is twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21 — between the milk of that station and the milk of this valley at large." (Discrepancy: generally cited as 0.19)
- Bulletin 214: "The College fed the bog hay under control and measured what it did, and it found the butter fat test depressed by two tenths in the winter feeding. That is the whole case. Two tenths from the College, laid alongside the fifth of a point I have found in the field and the twenty-one hundredths that station has been complaining of, and the three agree so nearly that I do not know what more a reasonable man requires." (Actual Bulletin 214 says "five hundredths," not "two tenths")
- 1911 anomaly dismissal: "It has been urged upon me... that in the summer of 1911 the testing at that station was broken off for six weeks, their own apparatus having failed them and a borrowed one being used, and that the figures stood higher during those six weeks than before or after. Six weeks in the flush of the season, gentlemen. Six weeks of June and July, when every herd in the county is at its best and the stock are off the wet ground and up on the aftermath. I am astonished that anybody should found an argument on six weeks of summer figures." (Actually nine weeks, not six)
- Remedy: "It is not a mystery and it is not cheap. Drain the bottom; open the old ditches and cut new; lime the ground and lime it again; sow it down when it will carry a seeding; keep the stock off it in the wet of the spring... Fat has been worth twenty-seven cents the pound through these years..."
- Discussion response to station criticism: "He had no interest in that quarrel and no competence in it; that he had never in his life read a test and would not know how; but that when a cause is found in the soil, in the herbage, in the condition of the stock, in the College's trial, and in every wet bottom in the county alike, it is not good sense to go looking for a second cause in a building."

## ODDITIES & CONFLICTS

1. **Arbitration board contradiction**: Finds "no cause established" yet awards $3,800 "without admission"

2. **1919 circular interpretation divergence**: Selby Vose claims deliberate water addition; state inspector Merle Strawn never detected water in 29 years of visits; Ansel's unsent letter makes no mention of water; Ivy Keddie's science proves glass defect, not water; Hobart Sill believed water was added and "something got stopped" in summer of twenties

3. **1911 anomaly timing discrepancy**: Measure breaks May 8, tests jump to 3.80 immediately; measure returns July 10, tests drop to 3.61 same day. This is nine weeks (May 8-July 10), but Dr. Kepp dismisses it as "six weeks" of summer flush. Ansel marks with question mark, gives no explanation, "let it stand 14 years." Later scientific work shows this is the smoking gun for glass defect (borrowed measure was correct; own measure was defective). Grigg also says "six weeks" when dismissing the anomaly.

4. **Station gain consistency**: Gain runs at exactly 5.25% year after year for 25+ years, so consistent Wilbur Teague writes "as usual" in the entry. This perfect consistency supports glass defect (proportional, mathematical), not random theft or seasonal variations. Selby Vose signs off every year without intervention.

5. **Wilbur Teague's report 1916**: Teague reported discrepancy to manager Vose in 1916, was told to let alone. No action taken until 1925 arbitration. But ledger entries show cream arrived "heavy" (more fat than credited), which later proves theft impossible.

6. **Crate not mentioned in arbitration**: State inspector condemned defective glassware June 12, 1923. Hearing March-May 1925, almost 2 years later. Award mentions "books and appliances" but no testimony about the condemned crate. Effie Loomis notes: "The interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying."

7. **Article VII wording**: "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern"—no appeal clause. Hinge of Ostrey Hollow problem because it locked stations into their own tests, preventing re-testing or averaging

8. **Loss distribution**: Error proportional, so richest milk hurt most. Farmers with best cows (higher butterfat content) were cheated more than farmers with poor cows. Emmet Brant's milk would have been most affected. Roy Brant's 1910 note shows his father delivered 200,000 pounds and was paid on 3.42 test—with 5% error, true value would be 3.60.

9. **Post-exoneration**: Ansel dead 24 years (1934-1958) before Bulletin 471 published. Orra outlived him and carried stigma her whole life. Both died long before vindication.

10. **Dismissal timing**: Ansel dismissed April 30, 1925, four days before arbitration board made award on May 4, while board still sitting. No finding against him yet. Frick never could "think well of the Association for it."

11. **Hobart Sill's observations vs. interpretation**: Sill observed cream arrived "heavy" (more fat than credited at station), which actually proves glass defect (station underestimated fat content). But Sill interpreted "heavy" as evidence of water theft, not recognizing the proportional error pattern.

12. **Alonzo Frick's test average discrepancy**: Frick's retelling says patrons paid "on an average test of 3.16," but every other source cites 3.61 as the recorded average. This is major discrepancy—0.45 points difference. Roy Brant's 1910 leaf shows 3.42 test that year. Was 3.16 a different metric or error?

13. **Measurement mean discrepancy**: Ivy's March 9, 1958 measurement sheet lists nine individual pipette means (16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71) averaging to 16.72 cc. But Vaught's Bulletin 471 states "the mean delivery of the nine is **16.27 c.c.**" This is 0.45 cc difference, substantially affecting all downstream calculations of error magnitude.

14. **Butterfat loss discrepancy**: Frick says patrons' reckoning was 67,000 pounds; Bulletin 471 says 76,000 pounds; Vaught's calculations using 16.27 cc would yield 118,000 lb. These three figures do not align.

15. **Condemnation date discrepancy**: Rundle's 1939 pamphlet says "twelfth of July, 1923," but Merle Strawn's own circuit notebook (the primary source) clearly states June 12, 1923. Rundle's date is wrong.

16. **Station milk intake discrepancy**: Earlier notes cite 40,000,000 pounds; Rundle says 44,000,000 pounds over the complained-of years.

17. **Financial loss discrepancy**: Rundle says patrons' loss "nineteen thousand six hundred dollars"; earlier notes round to $19,000.

18. **Pipette count discrepancy**: Rundle's 1959 footnote says "Eleven pipettes out of the station's old condemned crate were found still to be in existence," but Vaught's Bulletin 471 and Ivy's measurement sheet clearly document only 9 pipettes (six dozen=72 purchased in 1897, nine recovered, two chipped at tips).

19. **Bulletin 214 misquotation**: Grigg (1919) and Kepp (1926) both cite Bulletin 214 as finding bog hay "two tenths" depression. The actual text says "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter." This is a systematic error in multiple retellings, likely due to misremembering or oral transmission.

20. **Gap magnitude discrepancy**: Kepp says Hessel Bottom gap is "0.21" (twenty-one hundredths); Roy Brant says "seventeen hundredths"; typical calculation from recorded 3.61 to true 3.80 gives 0.19 (nineteen hundredths). Multiple inconsistent figures for the same phenomenon.

21. **Payment by test date discrepancy**: Grigg says "Payment by test came in with the 1893 meeting"; earlier notes say Article VII adopted 1892. (Both could be true if article was 1892 but implementation was 1893.)

22. **Alder Corners opening date**: Rosalie Cudd says Jerome Cudd "built this factory in nineteen thirteen"; Rundle says "opened... in 1914." Jerome "built" in 1913, opened 1914?

23. **Rosalie Cudd's age and timeline**: She says "I was a girl of twelve when the first vat was set" (1913 per her own statement), making her born ~1901. But she also says she took over factory "that same spring" Jerome died (1941), having run it "seventeen years now" (from 1958 perspective, so ~1941), giving her age as ~57 in 1958. The math roughly checks (57 in 1958 means born 1901, age 12 in 1913). But her letter says she took over "spring 1941" and "I have had it since — seventeen years now," which from 1958 would be 17 years (1941-1958 is 17 years). But her wording "seventeen years" at time of writing in 1958 suggests she's been running it for 17 years, counting from 1941. This is consistent but her phrasing is ambiguous.

24. **Roy Brant's family relationships clarity**: "Duncan Keddie, who taught school at Ashlin, was Ansel and Orra's boy, and he and I are first cousins." Roy's father Emmet was brother of Orra, so Orra's son Duncan is Roy's first cousin—correct. "My daughter Hazel and his daughter Ivy are second cousins, and I mention it because both of them have had to have it explained to them by me more than once." If Roy is first cousin to Duncan, then Roy's daughter Hazel is second cousin to Duncan's daughter Ivy—correct. This checks out.

25. **Effie Loomis's timeline**: "Secretary age 14 in 1889, office age 27 in 1901." If she was age 14 in 1889 and 27 in 1901, that's 12 years apart, but 1901-1889 is also 12 years. So she would have been born ~1875, age 14 in 1889, age 26 in 1901... wait, that's only 26 at the later date, not 27. Or born 1874, age 15 in 1889, age 27 in 1901. The wording says "Secretary age 14" and "office age 27" which is a difference of 13 years, while 1901-1889 is 12 years. Close but potential discrepancy.

26. **Jerome Cudd's death timing**: Rosalie says he "died in 1941, in March, between the setting and the cutting of a vat." Cheese is "set" (coagulated) and then "cut" (curd is cut). This happens on same day or next day in cheesemaking. So he died between setting the vat (beginning) and cutting the curd (same day or morning after). The poetic phrasing makes this ambiguous whether it's literally same-day or just the night between?

## SUMMARY OF RESOLUTION

**The case is solved by intersecting five historical documents that were never in one room before 1958**:

1. **Station test book** (kept by Ansel Keddie 1896-1924): Shows anomalous 1911 (measure break), 25-year 3.61% average
2. **Roy Brant's weigh book** (kept 1898 onward): Shows farm-side weights always matched station weights, despite lower testing
3. **Wilbur Teague's ledgers** (1905-1945): Shows "station gain" line at 5.25% for 25+ years
4. **Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks** (1907-1936): June 12, 1923 condemnation, October 1923 confirmation, 1919 note of no water found, 1924 note of improved tests
5. **The crate itself** (found February 1958, measured March 9, 1958): 9 surviving pipettes measured at mean 16.72 cc (short 0.88 cc, 95 hundredths of mark)
6. **Alder Corners test book** (Rosalie Cudd 1958): Same four farmers' milk tested 3.78-3.82 (avg 3.80) summer 1915-1930, higher than Ostrey Hollow

**The evidence chain**:
- Glass ordered 1897 at 17.6 cc specification
- Glass in use May 1, 1897 - June 12, 1923 (26 years, 1 month, 12 days)
- Systematic 5% underreading of all tests during this period (proportional, hit richest milk hardest)
- May 8-July 10, 1911: substitution of borrowed measure (correct spec) causes tests to jump to true value 3.80; return to defective measure causes tests to drop back to false 3.61
- June 12, 1923: condemned as not delivering their mark
- June 28, 1923: new glass ordered
- July 1923: new glass in use
- October 1923: new glass confirmed correct
- 1924: tests rise to 3.80 (consistent with true value)
- 1925: Arbitration award $3,800 (one-fifth of actual loss of $19,000 over 25 years)
- February 1958: Defective crate found
- March 9, 1958: Defective pipettes measured at mean 16.72 cc
- October 1958: Bulletin 471 published, Ansel exonerated

**The human cost**:
- Ansel Keddie: Dismissed April 30, 1925 (age ~58). Died 1934, still stigmatized. Exonerated 33 years after dismissal (1925-1958), 24 years after death (1934-1958).
- Orra Keddie: Never spoke to her brother Emmet from 1919 until both dead. Outlived Ansel. Entire community looked at her "a certain way in church" for rest of her life.
- Emmet Brant: Did not speak to Ansel/Orra for rest of life after 1919. Lived within sight of each other's chimneys but "never said good morning." Died 1929, still estranged.
- Roy Brant: Couldn't afford to be talked about despite having richest herd. Didn't haul over ridge to Alder Corners despite 6-week cost advantage, fearing gossip. Carried family knowledge 60+ years.
- Nine families of Ostrey Hollow: Paid $3,800 out of $19,000 actual loss ($15,200 shortfall remains uncompensated if using 40M pounds intake and 5% error, or different amount with other assumptions)
- Farmers with richest milk (like Emmet Brant's herd): Lost proportionally more
- Selby Vose: Defended and doubled down on interpretation of deliberate water fraud in 1926 address. Never acknowledged possibility of instrument error.
- Wilbur Teague: Reported issue 1916, told to let alone. Carried the knowledge 40 years in silence.
- Association management: Signed off on "station gain" line every year without intervention or investigation.
- Hobart Sill: Made excellent butter 41 years, observed "heavy" cream every year, believed water explanation, carried observations silently.

**The exoneration**:
- Ansel Keddie officially exonerated 33 years after dismissal (1925-1958)
- Exoneration published 24 years after his death (1934-1958)
- Ivy Keddie's scientific and documentary work restored his memory and vindicated his lifetime of careful work
- Bulletin 471 conclusively proves: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty..."

## CRITICAL DISCREPANCIES REQUIRING RESOLUTION

The following discrepancies between sources remain unresolved and should be investigated in further research:

1. **Mean pipette delivery measurement**: Ivy's sheet shows individual means averaging 16.72 cc; Vaught states 16.27 cc. (0.45 cc difference)
2. **Recorded average test**: Frick states 3.16; all other sources state 3.61. (0.45% difference)
3. **Butterfat loss tonnage**: Frick says 67,000 lb; others say 76,000 lb; Vaught calculates 118,000 lb.
4. **Gap between stations**: Roy Brant says 0.17; Kepp says 0.21; typical calculation gives 0.19.
5. **1911 anomaly duration**: Ansel's dates May 8-July 10 = 9 weeks; Kepp and Grigg dismiss as "six weeks."
6. **Condemnation date**: Strawn's notebook June 12, 1923; Rundle says July 12, 1923.
7. **Bulletin 214 findings**: Actual text says "five hundredths"; Grigg (1919) and Kepp (1926) both misquote as "two tenths."
8. **Alonzo Frick's test average**: Says 3.16; needs clarification whether this is correct or a misremembering.

These discrepancies may represent transcription errors, genuine measurement variations, misremembering, or honest differences in how to express the same facts. Complete resolution requires access to original source documents.
