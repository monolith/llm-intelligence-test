# Answer Sheet — Section A: Stories 1–4

## Story 1: The Foundation — Article VII and the Opening (1889–1897)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was organized in 1889 by farmers meeting at Larrow Green, incorporating principles that would prove fateful. In 1891, the main creamery opened, introducing payment by weight of butter fat rather than milk weight—a principle enshrined in Article VII of the by-laws, which stated that patrons would be paid "according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." This hinge clause locked each station's test as the authoritative measure, with no appeal or cross-check.

Seven miles up the valley at Ostrey Hollow, a hamlet on Hessel Bottom (a wet meadow where six farms held hay and pasture rights), a skimming station was built in winter 1895-96 to serve nine families. The station opened on May 1, 1897 (Duncan Keddie and Orin Falke cite this; Orra Keddie's diary says May 4). Ansel Keddie, a careful man with meticulous habits, began as tester and separator operator that day and would hold the post for 29 seasons. The station's original testing outfit—six dozen milk pipettes blown to 17.6 cc, test bottles, acid measure, hand tester, and freight—arrived from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet on April 14, 1897, costing $77.93 (Junia Ammon's invoice and Bertram Kyle confirm this price). The first season (May–December 1897) brought approximately 900,000 pounds of milk according to Hobart Sill's recollection; the creamery's own records were not kept with station accounts that first year (Kyle notes the receiving book sheets "were never put with the station papers"). Larrow Green creamery patrons' milk tested at 3.78 average; the Hollow's average would be 3.61 for the next 26 years. The difference—0.17 to 0.19 points—would not be noticed until around 1902, when "grumbling out of Ostrey Hollow" began (Grigg).

**Documents**: Ansel Keddie's test book (first leaf: "Begun this 4th day of May, 1896"; Ordell County Historical Rooms); Ammon & Sons invoice (April 14, 1897); Junia Ammon's firm history (1949); Larrow Valley Association by-laws, Article VII; Bertram Kyle's office ledgers and retelling; Hobart Sill's observations; Orra Keddie's diary (May 1, 1897: "station's own glass came up").

**Quantities**: $77.93 invoice; 72 pipettes (6 dozen) at 17.6 cc specification; 900,000 pounds first-season intake; 3.61% recorded average vs. 3.78% valley average for 26 years.

## Story 2: The First Complaint and the 1908 Investigation (1902–1908)

By 1902, farmers of Ostrey Hollow noticed their milk tested lower than the same class of milk elsewhere in the valley, and they began to complain. Orra Keddie's diary records March 1902: "Talk at the store that our milk up here runs poorer than theirs down the valley. A. came home and would not eat." The complaint resurged annually at Association meetings, becoming what Roy Brant called "grumbling" that "went on and grew" (Grigg says from 1902 it was "the first I remember of it, a man in here shouting that his milk tested under his brother-in-law's down the creek").

In 1908, after six years of complaints, the Association appointed an investigating committee headed by Nyle Grigg (a farmer from Larrow Green who hauled to that creamery, not to Ostrey Hollow, thus having no partisan interest). From January through March 1908, Grigg and others sampled Hessel Bottom herds "every month of that year, in the barn and on the pasture, out of the night's milk and the morning's" without advance notice, weighed milk, sealed samples, witnessed hauling and testing, and tried scales against known weights (Grigg's detailed 1919 letter). Orra Keddie's diary notes: "Four men up the road today in Grigg's sleigh, come to look into the herds. They went to my brother Emmet's first, being the biggest, and he gave them their dinner." The committee's report concluded: "We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March. The milk is whole and the scales are honest. It is our opinion that the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station." This explanation—that Hessel Bottom's acidic bog hay and poor drainage naturally reduced butterfat content—would dominate discussion for the next 17 years, endorsed later by State Agricultural College Bulletin 214 (1911), which stated bog hay depressed butter fat test by "not more than five hundredths" in winter (0.05%), insufficient to account for the observed 0.19% gap.

**Documents**: Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee report; Grigg's 1919 covering letter reprinting it; Orra Keddie's diary (January 1908, March 1908); Ansel Keddie's test book and summary leaf noting the consistent 3.61 average; Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks (1919 entry, though Strawn never found water); State Agricultural College Bulletin 214 (1911); Roy Brant's recounting (1955); Orin Falke's 1959 analysis.

**Quantities**: Six farms on Hessel Bottom; recorded average 3.61% at station vs. 3.78% at Larrow Green (0.17–0.19% gap); Bulletin 214 effect (0.05%) insufficient to explain gap.

## Story 3: The Water Circular and the Family Rupture (1919)

In 1919, as complaints persisted, Selby Vose, the Association's manager (who took office in 1913), made a fateful decision. He circulated a written warning to the nine Ostrey Hollow patron families cautioning them against adding water to their milk. Bertram Kyle, the office clerk, recalled: "In nineteen nineteen there was the circular. I typed it and I remember typing it... It went up to the Ostrey Hollow patrons and it warned them about water in their milk, in a civil form of words but with no doubt at all in it about what was meant... It went out over his [Vose's] name and no one else's. I thought at the time it was a hard thing to send to men who were bringing their milk in every morning of their lives." The circular's exact wording survives in Vira Toland's retelling: "There was water in the cans coming down from Ostrey Hollow" (found in her father's desk drawer).

The circular devastated the valley's social fabric. Orra Keddie's diary records June 1919: "A circular came to the station for the patrons, over the manager's name, about water in the milk. A. would not put them in the mail nor leave them on the platform. He took them round himself, to eleven families, and stood at the door while each man read it, and came home at eleven at night with his hat still on." This choice—Ansel's decision to personally deliver the circular rather than mail it—became critical. Two days later: "My brother Emmet came up. He did not come in. He stood in the yard and said to A. that he had hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and A. knew it better than any man living, and then he asked him why he carried the paper himself if he did not believe it. A. said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. He went home. He has not been in this house since, and he is my brother."

The rift never healed. Roy Brant (Emmet's son) explained: "My father was one who never forgave it. He and Ansel Keddie were brothers-in-law, and from 1919 they did not speak — not from any quarrel over the water, but because my father held that the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not." Orra walked to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane; she did not see him again until his deathbed in 1929, when Roy "was kind" and she sat one hour in the kitchen where she was born. Merle Strawn's 1919 circuit notebook entry, however, contradicted Vose: "The manager of the Association has sent a circular round to the patrons of this station about water in the cans. I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so."

**Documents**: Vira Toland's circular text ("There was water in the cans coming down from Ostrey Hollow"); Bertram Kyle's retelling (typing, delivery); Orra Keddie's diary (June 1919 entries, two days apart); Roy Brant's family account (1955); Merle Strawn's circuit notebook 1919 entry; Duncan Keddie's genealogical notes on family rupture; Nyle Grigg's 1919 covering letter (reprinting 1908 report).

**Quantities**: Nine patron families received the circular; Ansel personally delivered to eleven families; after 1919, Emmet and Ansel did not speak for rest of their lives (Emmet died 1929, 10 years of estrangement).

## Story 4: The Silent Ledger — The "Station Gain" Account (1905–1925)

In 1905, Wilbur Teague, the Association's bookkeeper (serving 1905–1945, 40 years), invented an accounting line he called "station gain" to track a persistent discrepancy. Every year, the butterfat credited to patrons on the station's own test fell short of the butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in the station's cream. Teague's annual ledger entry (reproduced in his retelling) read: "Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account." This 5.25% gain ran with such consistency—"as usual"—that for two decades it accumulated unquestioned.

In 1916, Teague reported the discrepancy to manager Selby Vose and was told to "let it alone" (Teague's 1948 statement). Vose, who had become manager in 1913, signed off on these sheets every spring without examining the line items. Bertram Kyle, the office clerk who copied the ledger sheets in fair hand for the annual meeting, observed Vose at close hand for 25 years: "He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign. I watched him do it a thousand times and thought nothing of it, because that is what managers were." Kyle himself copied the "station gain" line dozens of times: "I copied that line, or the like of it, more times than I can count, and I will tell the Society plainly that it never once crossed my mind to wonder at it... It had a name. That is the thing about a name. A figure with a name on it has been explained, and you rule under it and go on to the next."

Effie Loomis, the Association's secretary (1901–1940), kept the minute book and witnessed the arbitration. She said later: "I have written many things into that book with more pleasure... But that entry I had to set down as it was given to me: 'Finds no cause established, and then an award of thirty-eight hundred dollars.' I took it down as it was given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once." This ledger line ran unexamined for 25 years, read out annually to 50 farmers at the Association's meetings (Kyle: "read out at the annual meeting to fifty farmers with their hats on their knees"), yet no one connected it to wrongdoing or questioned why Teague did not investigate. Dell Rundle, who compiled the Association's 50-year jubilee pamphlet in 1939 and accessed the old ledgers while researching, noted his shock upon finding the entries: he had blamed cream theft in his draft but realized "Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand." The ledger proved the opposite of theft: cream arrived over-weight, supporting a glass-defect theory decades before it was scientifically confirmed.

**Documents**: Wilbur Teague's annual ledger entries (reproduced in his 1948 retelling and catalogued by Halbert Nym); Bertram Kyle's office account and ledger copies; Effie Loomis's minute books and arbitration observations; Dell Rundle's 1939 jubilee pamphlet with 1939 "Note Added While in Press" (correcting Chapter IV). Teague reported to Vose in 1916 (Teague's testimony to arbitration board 1925).

**Quantities**: 5.25% consistent gain year after year for 25+ years (3,040 lb out of 60,800 lb received = 5%); 76,000 pounds total butterfat uncompensated over full delivery years (per Bulletin 471, though Frick recalled 67,000 lb); 40,000,000 to 44,000,000 pounds total milk received at station (discrepancy between sources).

## Story 5: The 1911 Measure Anomaly — The Unintended Experiment (May 8 – July 10, 1911)

In spring 1911, the station's measuring pipette broke. Ansel Keddie's test book records in his hand: "Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." He dated this entry May 8, 1911. For nine weeks (May 8 to July 10, 1911), while the station's own defective pipette was out of use, a borrowed measure—presumed correct—replaced it. During this nine-week interval, tests jumped dramatically. The station's average rose from the usual 3.61% to 3.80%, matching the true value and the Larrow Green average. When Ansel returned to the station's own pipette on July 10, tests immediately dropped back to 3.61%. Ansel noted in the book: "Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." The question mark he appended indicates he recognized the connection but could not, or would not, assert it.

This interval is the most powerful evidence the sources offer, because it constitutes a controlled experiment conducted unknowingly. One variable changed (the measuring glass); everything else remained constant—the cows, the herds, the season, the farms, the milk composition, the methodology, Ansel's hands, the operator's integrity. The recorded test figures moved proportionally with the pipette change and returned when it reversed, on the same day, suggesting no seasonal effect. Duncan Keddie recalled his father coming home that summer "saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book. I was seventeen and I remember the kitchen and I remember that he said it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not his way." But Ansel put only a question mark. Duncan reflected later: "He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove, and I have thought since that this was the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had."

The nine-week interval does not establish whether the shortfall was intentional, malicious, or accidental. It does not establish the specific mechanism of the glass defect (proportional delivery shortfall). It does not directly measure the defect. But it isolates glass as the variable, eliminates feed, weather, management, and operator integrity as explanations, and points unambiguously toward an instrument fault. Dr. Kepp's 1926 dismissal of the anomaly as "six weeks" of summer (actually nine weeks) shows how easily the key evidence could be minimized. Vaught noted in 1958: "This is recorded contemporaneously, in the operator's hand, by a man who did not know what he was recording and set a question mark against it...the most powerful evidence: one that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven years."

**Documents**: Ansel Keddie's test book (May 8 and July 10, 1911 entries); Ansel's summary leaf (1922: "nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80"); Duncan Keddie's written account (1968) of hearing his father's suspicion; Dr. Roland Kepp's 1926 dismissal of "six weeks"; Professor Leland Vaught's 1958 Bulletin 471 analysis; Roy Brant's 1955 account misremembering as "six weeks."

**Quantities**: Nine weeks duration (May 8–July 10, 1911, not six); tests 3.80 with borrowed measure, 3.61 with station's measure; gap of 0.19% demonstrating proportional change; no change in herds, season, management, or operator.

## Story 6: The Condemnation, Arbitration, and Dismissal (1923–1925)

On June 12, 1923, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn visited Ostrey Hollow station on his circuit. Strawn was a man of 29 years on the dairy inspector circuit (1907–1936) who tested at Ostrey Hollow regularly and kept detailed circuit notebooks recording every observation. In 1919, he had written: "I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so." On June 12, 1923, his notebook entry read: "Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk pipettes against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them." The crate—containing the six dozen pipettes from Ammon & Sons' 1897 shipment—was set aside in the loft of the station building on that day, June 12, 1923, where it lay for 35 years.

The Association ordered replacement glass from Ammon & Sons on June 28, 1923. Ammon's reply of July 2, 1923 survives: "Your order of the 28th ultimo for six dozen milk pipettes and six dozen test bottles is entered and will go forward this month. We regret the trouble. Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name." New glass arrived July 1923 and was in use by autumn. Strawn returned in October 1923: "Ostrey Hollow. New glass in use since July. Ran the new pipettes against the burette. They deliver their mark. Scales true." In 1924, with correct glass in use, station tests rose to 3.80 and stayed there. Strawn's 1924 entry: "Ostrey Hollow. Scales true, no added water, tests improved. Their book gives the year at 3.80."

Meanwhile, in January 1925, Ostrey Hollow patrons, emboldened by the poor test history and recent water accusations, petitioned the Association for restitution, claiming they had been underpaid over two decades. The Association, rather than resist, invoked arbitration provisions of its by-laws. A board of arbitration was formed, chaired by Alonzo Frick, a county attorney. The board held three sittings: March 17, 1925 at the Association office in Ashlin, and two sessions at the Ostrey Hollow station itself in the weigh room (the same room where Ansel tested milk, with the condemned crate lying in the loft directly overhead, only 20 feet away per Cleve Oram's recollection). Exhibits presented were four books only: (1) Ansel Keddie's station test book; (2) the station's receiving book; (3) Emmet Brant's private weigh book; (4) Merle Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook. The three competing explanations were argued: (1) Selby Vose claimed water had been added to cans and was stopped when scrutiny began; (2) Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper, indirectly suggested the tester had been taking cream and writing low figures to hide it (his statement gestured to the structural vulnerability: "Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons... The remedy is two men or a lock, and the Association had neither"); (3) the 1908 committee report was read into the record, blaming wet hay. 

On March 17, Ansel gave his statement standing: "I have tested as I was taught and as the state man had seen me do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons. I could not tell us why the figures were what they were, and that I had never taken a pound of cream out of that station." Alonzo Frick believed him. Privately, Ansel told Frick he had "always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the room. He was not a bitter man about it. He had lived among them thirty years and would not stand up and accuse his neighbors."

The board, on May 4, 1925, issued its award: "The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account." The $3,800 award settled the dispute without finding fault—Frick called it "a generous settlement of an unproved claim." The patrons' own reckoning was 67,000 to 76,000 pounds of butterfat shortfall (equaling $19,000–$19,600 at 25 cents per pound); the award represented one-fifth of the actual loss.

But four days before the award, on April 30, 1925, Ansel Keddie was dismissed. Orra Keddie's diary: "They have put him out. Twenty-nine seasons and a letter. He came up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm and hung it behind the shed door and asked what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it." Frick noted: "On the thirtieth of April, four days before we made our award, the Association dismissed him. There was no finding against him from us and there never was one; the board had not concluded and did not conclude that he had done anything whatever. He was simply let go while the board was still sitting, and I have never been able to think well of the Association for it."

**Documents**: Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks (June 12, 1923; October 1923; 1924 entries); Ammon & Sons letter July 2, 1923; Larrow Valley Association arbitration award May 4, 1925; Alonzo Frick's 1946 Bar Association yearbook article ("Recollections of the Ordell Arbitration"); Orra Keddie's diary (March 17, April 30, May 1925); Ansel Keddie's statement to board March 17, 1925; Effie Loomis's minute books and observations; Cleve Oram's 1977 recollection (crate "twenty feet over their heads").

**Quantities**: June 12, 1923 condemnation date; April 30, 1925 dismissal date; May 4, 1925 award date; 29 seasons of service; $3,800 award to patrons; one-fifth of actual loss ($19,000–$19,600 total); four days between dismissal and award.

## Story 7: Ansel's After-Life — Carpenter, Isolation, Dignity, Death (1925–1934)

After his dismissal in April 1925, Ansel Keddie—now 58 years old and carrying the stigma of the valley's suspicion—returned to the farm at Ostrey Hollow. He had kept a few cows throughout his testing career, and he turned to work he had learned young: carpentry. Orra Keddie's diary traces his transformation: "June 1925. He has begun on the cow shed, which has wanted doing since his father's time. He works at it from light to dark and whistles, which he never did when he was paid." Cleve Oram, the cream hauler's son, remembered Ansel offering to hang a barn door: "He hung a barn door for us and it swung true for thirty years." Oram observed that "Keddie went home after twenty-five and stayed home. He kept four or five cows on a piece of ground that was not big enough for them, and he did carpentry—good carpentry... and he died in the thirties."

Orin Falke noted that Ansel's carpentry "pays a quarter of what the other did" (around 1927, when butterfat payment would have been roughly 25 cents per pound, Ansel's carpentry wages were perhaps 5–6 cents, or $1–2 per day for skilled work). Yet he worked steadily and well. Orra wrote: "He is better at it than he ever was at the other and it pays a quarter of what the other did." Falke's account adds another detail: "A carpenter at sixty-six. He is better at it than he ever was at the other."

The isolation was complete. Roy Brant recalled: "from 1919 they did not speak—not from any quarrel over the water, but because my father held that the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not." Orra's diary records with devastating brevity the loss of her brother: "Spring 1929. Word this morning that my brother Emmet is dead. I went down. Roy met me at the gate and was kind. I sat an hour in that kitchen where I was a girl and came home. Ten years and neither of them would go up the road first, and now it is settled the way those things get settled." Emmet had been the largest patron, the one most affected by the error, and the one Ansel had married into. Neither would break the silence even to reconcile.

Duncan Keddie noted: "He lived nine years after it and died in 1934 with the imputation on him, in this county, among men he had weighed milk for since before their fathers died." Orra's diary for February 1934 captures his death with quiet grace: "A. died this morning, at home, in the forenoon, without much pain at the end of it. Duncan came up in the night. There were more at the burying than I looked for, and men shook my hand who had not spoken to him in nine years, and every one of them said what a hand he was with a door. Not one of them said the other thing. Nine years they have had to say it and they had not said it and they did not say it then. He never asked them to. He said to me once and only once, and not lately, that a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."

Orra herself carried the stigma. Roy Brant's daughter Hazel recalled: "I was glad about it for my grandfather and gladder about it for Ansel Keddie, who did not live to see it, and I will tell you the truth, I was a little sour too... a thing you could have settled in an afternoon with a burette and a straight face, and it took fifty years and a girl from the college who thought to climb a ladder." Orra outlived Ansel by 14 years, dying in 1948 at age 82. Duncan noted that Orra "had kept every letter and she left them to me, and there is nothing in them that anybody would want, only the weather and the price of things and her brother's health." She never spoke of the scandal except once a year, in April.

**Documents**: Orra Keddie's diary (June 1925, October 1927, Spring 1929, August 1930, February 1934); Cleve Oram's 1977 recollection; Orin Falke's 1959 county agent report; Duncan Keddie's family notes (1968); Roy Brant's 1955 account; Hazel Brant's 1981 taped interview. Death: Duncan Keddie (1968) "died in 1934"; Orra's diary confirms February 1934.

**Quantities**: April 30, 1925 dismissal to February 1934 death = 8 years 10 months of after-life; Orra outlived him 14 years (1934–1948); dismissed at age ~58, died at age ~67; carpentry paid one-quarter of testing wages.

## Story 8: The Discovery and Vindication (1958)

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie, Ansel's granddaughter (daughter of Duncan), visited the abandoned Ostrey Hollow station building and climbed the ladder to the loft over the intake room. There, under tarpaulin and chaff, she found the wooden crate that Merle Strawn had condemned and the Association had set aside 35 years earlier. The crate contained defective glassware—the pipettes that had measured every test Ansel Keddie made for 26 years.

Ivy, then a graduate student at State Agricultural College at Farland studying dairy husbandry, carried the crate to the college. In March 1958, she measured nine surviving pipettes (two were chipped, two broken, and several lost; six dozen = 72, leaving 9 whole) against a calibrated burette. Her measurement sheet, signed March 9, 1958, recorded individual delivery measurements for each pipette. The mean of her measurements was 16.72 cc per pipette (note: Bulletin 471 later stated 16.27 cc, a discrepancy of 0.45 cc suggesting either measurement variation or a transcription error; the measurement sheet is the primary source and lists individual means averaging 16.72). The specification blow on the stem read 17.6 cc. The deficiency was 0.88 cc per pipette, or 5% of the specified volume.

Using Ivy's measurement (16.72 cc), the math is exact: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 = 95 hundredths of the mark. Every test was 5% low. Recorded average 3.61% × 95% = 3.431%, so the true average should have been 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80%, a shortfall of 0.19% (nineteen hundredths). The 1911 anomaly was explained: when a borrowed measure (correct spec) replaced the defective pipette, tests jumped from 3.61 to 3.80—proving the glass was the variable. When Ansel returned to the defective glass, tests dropped back to 3.61. No theft, no water, no intentional fraud—just defective measuring glass that had never been caught until the day it was condemned.

In October 1958, State Agricultural College published Bulletin 471, authored by Professor Leland Vaught and Ivy Keddie, concluding: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

Ansel Keddie had been dead for 24 years. Orra for 10 years. The Ashlin Chronicle (October 3, 1958) announced the discovery under the headline "Old Ordell Wrong Righted." The county agent (Orin Falke) prepared a historical report for the board of supervisors. Ivy's achievement was profound: she had established, scientifically and from primary evidence, that an honest man had been ruined by a piece of glass, and that neither he nor anyone in the county had known it for half a century.

**Documents**: Crate of pipettes (found February 1958, Ordell County Historical Rooms); Ivy Keddie's March 9, 1958 measurement sheet; State Agricultural College Bulletin 471 (October 1958, co-authored by Leland Vaught and Ivy Keddie); Ashlin Chronicle article (October 3, 1958); Orin Falke's 1959 county agent report; Merle Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook (primary evidence of condemnation); Roy Brant's weigh book (lent to Ivy summer 1958 for college work); Alder Corners test book (Rosalie Cudd provided copies 1958 showing four farmers tested 3.78–3.82 at cheese factory vs. lower at Ostrey Hollow). Ivy's historical presentation to Ordell County Historical Society (1959).

**Quantities**: Nine surviving pipettes out of original 72 (6 dozen); 16.72 cc delivery vs. 17.6 cc specification = 0.88 cc short = 5% error; recorded 3.61% average; true 3.80% average; gap 0.19%; 76,000 pounds butterfat uncompensated; 35-year gap between condemnation (June 12, 1923) and discovery (February 1958); 24 years between Ansel's death (February 1934) and exoneration (October 1958).

# Section B: Relationships

## B1: What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Ansel (b. 1861, d. 1934) was Ivy's paternal grandfather. Ivy's father was Duncan Keddie (b. 1894), who was Ansel's son. Duncan married and had Ivy (b. 1931). The sources are consistent on this relationship: Orin Falke's 1959 report notes Ivy is "the great-niece of the tester" but then appends a correction: "Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." Duncan's 1968 family notes confirm: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931." The Ashlin Chronicle initially called her "great-niece" but this was a press error. She was granddaughter.

## B2: What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant? State anything the sources leave open.

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. Ivy's father Duncan (b. 1894) and Hazel's father Roy Brant (b. 1888) were first cousins—their parents were Ansel/Orra Keddie's son Duncan and Emmet Brant (Orra's brother), making them children of a sister and her brother. Therefore Ivy and Hazel, as the children of first cousins, are second cousins. Duncan's 1968 notes spell this out: "Roy Brant and I are therefore first cousins—the children of a brother and a sister, which is the whole definition and there is nothing more to it. Roy's daughter is Hazel Brant. My daughter is Ivy. Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece, which people say because it is shorter."

However, Cleve Oram (in 1977) misidentified this relationship as "first cousin once removed," and some transcription or recollection errors in earlier narratives conflated the relationship. The genealogical reality is definitive: second cousins. What the sources leave open is whether Ivy and Hazel knew each other well or saw much of each other; both "had to have it explained to them more than once" per Duncan and Hazel themselves, suggesting the relationship was distant enough to be confusing even to the family.

## B3: Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter?

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose, the founder and first president of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (1889–1911, 22 years). Duncan Keddie's 1968 notes correct a widespread misunderstanding: "Selby Vose, who managed the Association, was Gideon Vose's son. Gideon organized the Association and was its first president and wrote its by-laws; Selby was his son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew." Bertram Kyle confirms: "Mr. Selby Vose became manager in nineteen eleven. He was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president — the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense."

This matters because Gideon drafted the by-laws "slowly, and to last" (per Dell Rundle's description of his method), and those by-laws—particularly Article VII—are the hinge of the entire scandal. Article VII locked each station's test as the authoritative measure with no appeal. Selby inherited not just a managerial position but the system his father had designed. When tests ran low at Ostrey Hollow, Selby interpreted the discrepancy through his inherited framework: either the farmers were cheating (water in milk) or the tester was corrupt. The by-law he inherited gave him no tools to question the tests themselves, and it gave Ansel Keddie (locked to Ostrey Hollow's own test) no recourse. Gideon's by-laws, well-intentioned for fair payment by butterfat, created a structure vulnerable to exactly the kind of systemic measurement error that occurred.

## B4: What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?

Duncan Keddie flatly denied any family connection between the Voses and the Keddies. His 1968 notes state: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either. I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her sit down. It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me. She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it."

However, Cleve Oram (in 1977, age 77) reported: "And I will say this while I am on families, because you will hear it anyway and you may as well hear it from somebody who is not repeating it out of malice. The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley. My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper."

The sources cannot definitively settle this. Duncan's testimony is from someone with direct knowledge who claims he would have been told; Oram's is third-hand rumor. No documented genealogy connects them. The sources suggest the rumor may have confused the Ashlin Voses (a different family) with Gideon's line. For the purposes of this reconstruction, the most likely answer is: no direct family relationship between the Voses and Keddies, though valley gossip conflated them with the Ashlin Voses and created a false rumor of cousinship.


# Answer Sheet — Sections C–G: Mathematics, Logic, Contradictions, Theory, Summary

# Section C: Mathematics

## C1: Defect proportion and true average

**(a) Proportion of error:**

The Ostrey Hollow station's pipettes, blown for 17.6 cc, delivered a mean of 16.72 cc (Ivy Keddie's measurement sheet, March 9, 1958; note: Bulletin 471 cites 16.27 cc, a discrepancy of 0.45 cc; the measurement sheet is primary).

Using Ivy's measurement: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly.

The tests were 5% low (since every measurement was 95 hundredths of its true value).

Proportional error: −5% or −0.05 or 1/20.

**(b) True average test and shortfall:**

Recorded average at Ostrey Hollow: 3.61% butterfat (Ansel's summary leaf 1922; confirmed by all sources).

True average: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80% butterfat.

Shortfall: 3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19 points (nineteen hundredths).

**(c) Pounds of butterfat uncompensated:**

Station intake over 25 full delivery years (1898–1922): 40,000,000 pounds milk (per earlier notes; Rundle says 44,000,000; sources conflict; using 40M as notes cite it first).

Butterfat credited at recorded 3.61%: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb.

True butterfat at 3.80%: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb.

Uncompensated shortfall: 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 lb (matching Bulletin 471).

(Alternative: using Vaught's 16.27 cc measurement and 7.56% error yields ~118,000 lb, and using 44M milk intake shifts figures. Frick recalled patrons' reckoning as 67,000 lb. The 76,000 lb figure from Bulletin 471 and the 5% measurement are most internally consistent.)

**(d) Money value and recovery:**

At 25 cents per pound average price (Roy Brant's statement; Orin Falke confirms): 76,000 lb × $0.25 = $19,000.

Award to patrons: $3,800 (May 4, 1925, arbitration award; Cleve Oram says $3,500, a discrepancy of $300—using $3,800 as primary source from multiple independent narrators: Frick, Kyle, Falke).

Fraction recovered: $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20 = 20%, or one-fifth of actual loss.

Uncompensated loss: $19,000 − $3,800 = $15,200 (about 80% of the loss).

## C2: Largest patron's year of 1910

Emmet Brant, holder of largest plot on Hessel Bottom and largest patron "every year on record" (notes), had a recorded year in Roy Brant's weigh book (begun Jan 1, 1897, continued by Roy after Emmet's death).

Roy Brant's 1955 account: "Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of three forty-two."

**(a) Butterfat credited:**

200,000 lb × 0.0342 = 6,840 lb butterfat.

**(b) Butterfat owed and not credited:**

True test at Ostrey Hollow should have been: 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60%.

True butterfat: 200,000 lb × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb.

Short: 7,200 − 6,840 = 360 lb.

Alternatively, if he was truly at 3.60%, the 5% loss would be 360 lb; if higher (the richest milk, as notes suggest), loss would be larger. Using his recorded 3.42 test: loss is 360 lb.

**(c) Dollar value that year:**

360 lb × $0.25 = $90.00 loss on his milk alone in 1910 (worth $90 in 1910 dollars, equivalent to roughly $3,000 in 2024; a significant sum for a farmer).

## C3: Station gain calculation—total pounds and percentage

**(a) Pounds credited to patrons on station test (1898–1922, 25 full years):**

Using recorded average 3.61% and 40M pounds milk: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb.

**(b) Pounds actually received at Larrow Green in station cream:**

Using true average 3.80%: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb.

(Or using the "station gain" ledger entries: Teague reported ~3,040 lb gain per year × 25 years = 76,000 lb total; this is the difference: 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 lb. The ledger gains ran at "five and a quarter per cent, as usual": 1,444,000 × 0.0525 = 75,810 lb ≈ 76,000 lb.)

**(c) Expressed as a percentage—clarifying the denominator:**

The "station gain" of 76,000 lb can be expressed two ways, both mathematically correct:

- **Method 1 (Vaught's method):** 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 (true butterfat actually in cream) = 0.05 = **5% of the fat actually delivered**
- **Method 2 (Teague's method):** 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 (fat credited to patrons) = 0.0526 = **5.25% of the fat credited** (matching Teague's ledger phrase "five and a quarter per cent, as usual")

The "station gain" ledger line used Method 2 because it subtracted from the credited figure. But the defect itself is a proportional error in measuring: every pipette delivered 5% less than marked, so every true charge was 5% overstated as a percentage of truth. Vaught emphasizes this: "low by one part in twenty" = 5%.

# Section D: Logic

## D1: The 1911 measure anomaly as experiment

**When and duration:**

May 8 to July 10, 1911 (nine weeks, not "six weeks" as sometimes misremembered). Ansel's notation: "Pipette broke this morning" (May 8); "Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." (July 10).

**Variables:**
- Changed: the measuring glass (station's defective pipette → borrowed correct measure → back to station's defective pipette)
- Held constant: the herds, the individual cows, the season, the pasture, the farms, the farmers, Ansel's hands and methodology, supervision, acid, bottles, testing protocol, milk composition

**Result:**

Tests rose from 3.61% (station's defective glass) to 3.80% (borrowed correct glass), then dropped back to 3.61% on the same day the defective glass was reintroduced. The change was immediate and proportional.

**What it establishes:**

The 1911 anomaly proves that measuring glass is the causal variable for the recorded shortfall. It isolates glass from all other possible causes (feed, weather, management, operator integrity, herds, methodology). No other hypothesis can explain why tests jump by 0.19 points when one variable changes, remain elevated for nine weeks while that variable is changed, and drop back immediately when it reverts. The experiment was conducted unwittingly by Ansel, recorded contemporaneously, and lay uninterpreted for 47 years.

**What it does not establish:**

The 1911 interval does not establish the specific mechanism of the glass defect (e.g., that the pipettes delivered consistently 5% less). It does not measure the defect directly. It does not establish whether the defect was intentional manufacturing error or accidental. It does not prove that no water was ever added or no cream taken (those claims require other evidence: Strawn's lactometer tests, the "station gain" ledger showing heavy cream arrival, and Roy Brant's scales matching the station's). It does not prove Ansel Keddie's moral innocence—though his contemporaneous notation (a question mark, not a confession or excuse) and the fact that the test rise occurred "under no different supervision" (per Alonzo Frick) support his integrity. Lastly, it does not explain why the defect was not caught for 26 years: Ansel's question mark shows he suspected glass but could not prove it, and in 1911 testing procedures offered no routine way to verify pipettes against a calibrated measure (that required state inspector Strawn's 1923 burette test).

---

## D2: Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream—three independent reasons

**Reason 1 (from the 1911 measure anomaly, detailed above):**

If Ansel had been taking cream and writing low tests to hide the loss, the nine-week interval in 1911 when a different measure was used would not explain why his figures rose. The theft itself (removing cream) was not interrupted; only the measuring glass changed. Yet figures rose immediately with the new measure and fell immediately when the old one returned. This proves the discrepancy was in measurement, not in cream removal or test falsification. (Independent of 1958 measurement; stands on 1911 data alone.)

**Reason 2 (from Wilbur Teague's own ledger—the "station gain"):**

Teague, the bookkeeper, indirectly accused Ansel of cream theft in the 1925 arbitration, stating the structural vulnerability: "Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons... The remedy is two men or a lock, and the Association had neither." Yet Teague's own ledger line—which he created in 1905 and maintained for 40 years—shows "station gain," not "station loss." Cream arrived at Larrow Green heavier than the station papers said it weighed. If Ansel had stolen cream, less cream would have arrived; instead, the "station gain" entries prove more cream arrived than the station recorded. Teague's accusation contradicts his own books. (This is doubly independent: it does not rely on 1911 or 1958 measurement, and it comes from Teague's own contemporaneous ledgers.)

**Reason 3 (from Hobart Sill's observations):**

Hobart Sill, the buttermaker at Larrow Green creamery (1893–1934, 41 years), received and weighed Ostrey Hollow cream directly: "I weighed it myself, most years. The platform man weighed it and I weighed it over again when it came into the cold room, because I have never in my life taken another man's figure for what was going into my churn." Sill's testimony: "That cream ran heavy. Every year it ran heavy. There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there." Sill believed this proved water had been added (a rival theory), but the evidence itself proves the opposite of theft: stolen cream would have reduced what arrived at Larrow Green; instead, the cream arrived "heavy"—over what was credited at Ostrey Hollow station. This matches the "station gain" ledger exactly. Sill's 41 years of personal observation prove cream was never short at reception.

**Why these are independent:**

- Reason 1 derives from Ansel's 1911 measure change—an experiment unrelated to theft and unrelated to the 1958 measurement.
- Reason 2 derives from Teague's own ledger—created before any suspicion was lodged and contradicting Teague's own later accusation.
- Reason 3 derives from an independent observer (Sill) who had no stake in the outcome and whose job was to verify cream weight—he would have caught theft immediately.

None of these three would crumble even if the 1958 measurement had never occurred. All three converge on one finding: the discrepancy was in measurement, not in cream removal.

**Did Teague question the ledger entries?**

No. Teague reported the "station gain" discrepancy to manager Selby Vose in 1916 and was told to "let it alone" (Teague's 1925 arbitration testimony). After that, Teague continued the line without investigation for 9 more years until the 1925 arbitration. At the arbitration itself, Teague obliquely suggested Ansel was stealing cream—advancing the theft theory rather than questioning the ledger gains that contradicted it. Teague had the primary evidence (the "station gain" running consistently high) but interpreted it as theft rather than as proof of his own misunderstanding. Duncan Keddie noted later: "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years. That is not a criticism. It was his notion of the position: the figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people, and if the other people did not ask it was not his affair."

## D3: Did Selby Vose know the station's tests ran low?

**Answer: Almost certainly yes, Vose knew or had reason to know.**

**Justification:**

1. **Signed the station gain line every year (1913–1925).** Vose became manager in 1913 and signed off on the annual station accounts every spring until the 1925 hearing. Each sheet carried the "station gain" line: "Station gain, [X] lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account." Bertram Kyle, who sat at the outer desk with his "face to the manager's door," testified: "He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign. I watched him do it a thousand times and thought nothing of it, because that is what managers were." Vose did not read the detail—but the gain line was there every year for 12 consecutive years under his watch (1913–1925), and it was explicitly entered into the annual meeting minutes read to 50 farmers.

2. **Sent the 1919 water circular.** In 1919, Vose circulated a warning letter to Ostrey Hollow patrons about water in milk. This warning presupposes he had noticed the low tests and formed a hypothesis about their cause. Selby Vose's own 1926 address confirms he was aware of the discrepancy: "Water was going into cans at the head of that valley and it stopped when the Association began to look. The rise of 1924 is the measure of it... The tests came up when the looking began, and any man who wants a different explanation of that is welcome to stand where I am standing and give us one." Vose had clearly noticed both the low tests and the 1924 rise (when new glass was installed).

3. **Defended the $3,800 award and equipment replacement as routine.**  In his 1926 manager's address, Vose defended the 1923 condemnation and 1925 award without distinguishing between them: "Apparatus wears... it is the state man's business to say so before they fail him, and ours to replace them." He acknowledged equipment failure had occurred and needed replacement—a candid statement suggesting he accepted (or feigned acceptance) that apparatus issues, not wrongdoing, explained events.

4. **The "station gain" itself was named, making invisibility plausible—but not excusable.** Bertram Kyle's observation is key: "It had a name. That is the thing about a name. A figure with a name on it has been explained, and you rule under it and go on to the next." Vose may have rationalized the "station gain" as a routine feature of accounting (cream separation loss, rounding, unavoidable variance). But naming alone does not excuse management: a 5% loss every year for 12 years, from the same station, under a manager's signature, should have prompted investigation or explanation.

**Conclusion:** Vose knew or reasonably should have known that Ostrey Hollow station's tests ran low—the facts are in the record he signed, the warning he sent, and the defect he later defended. Whether he understood the cause (water, theft, glass, or hay) is unclear; but ignorance of the low tests themselves is not credible.

# Section F: Theory

## F1: Causal theory and competing explanations

**Actual cause (unified theory):**

The Ostrey Hollow milk-testing shortfall was caused by a defective piece of measuring glass—a set of pipettes blown for 17.6 cubic centimeters but consistently delivering 16.72 cc (or within 5% of specification). This defect operated in pure proportion: every test was 5% low, and the error was proportionally distributed across all milk, hitting richest milk hardest in absolute pounds lost. The glass was ordered by Ammon & Sons of Tarnet in April 1897, went into use May 1, 1897, and remained in use until June 12, 1923 when state inspector Merle Strawn condemned it after running it against a calibrated burette. The station's tests recorded 3.61% butterfat for 26 years; the true average was 3.80%. No milk was watered. No cream was stolen. No intentional fraud occurred. The error was mechanical, invisible from inside the plant (because the pipette itself was the standard against which all samples were measured), consistent enough to seem "normal," and explained away by blame directed at either the farmers (water in milk), the tester (cream theft), or the land (wet hay). It took 61 years (1897–1958) of unexamined assumption and 35 years of a condemned crate gathering dust in a loft before the true cause was measured and proved.

**The three competing explanations—who advanced and what defeats each:**

1. **Water in milk (Selby Vose, manager)**

Vose circulated the 1919 warning and defended it in his 1926 address: "Water was going into cans at the head of that valley and it stopped when the Association began to look. The rise of 1924 is the measure of it."

**Evidence defeating it:**
- Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks: tested for water with lactometer at every visit from 1908 forward ("I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any"); 1923 entry ("Lactometer, no added water, as always here") immediately before condemning glass.
- The 1911 measure anomaly: if water had been in cans, it would have remained through the nine-week interval; the measure change alone would not have raised tests by 0.19 points. But tests rose precisely when the measure changed and fell when it reverted—proving the measure, not water, was the variable.
- Ansel's unsent letter (spring 1925): describes his testing methodology in detail; makes no mention of water observed or suspected.

Water was ruled out conclusively by Strawn's testing method (lactometer detects free water) and by the 1911 experiment (water would not correlate with measure change).

---

2. **Cream theft by the tester (Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper, in 1925 arbitration)**

Teague indirectly argued Ansel removed cream and wrote low tests to hide the loss. "Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons."

**Evidence defeating it:**
- Teague's own ledger (the "station gain"): recorded more butterfat arriving at Larrow Green than was credited at Ostrey Hollow. Stolen cream would reduce arrival totals; instead, cream "ran heavy" (Hobart Sill's phrase). The ledger entries prove the opposite of theft: the recorded discrepancy is one of measurement underestimation at the source, not loss in transit.
- Hobart Sill's 41 years of personal observation: "I weighed it myself, most years...There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there." The buttermaker, who received and processed the cream, found it over-weight every year—consistent with underestimation of tests, not theft.
- The 1911 measure anomaly: if Ansel had been stealing cream, he would not have immediately raised his test figures when the measure changed. He would either have continued the low figures (to hide the theft) or gradually adjusted them. Instead, tests rose immediately—proving he was recording what the measure gave him, not what he wished it to give. Ansel's question mark suggests he recognized the glass issue but did not investigate further, an honest response.

Theft is disproven by Teague's own ledger and by Sill's independent verification.

---

3. **Wet hay of Hessel Bottom thins cream (1908 investigating committee, Nyle Grigg; supported by Dr. Roland Kepp, 1926)**

Grigg's 1908 report: "The milk is whole and the scales are honest. It is our opinion that the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream." Kepp's 1926 paper: "Wet bottoms yield milk poorer in fat...The Hessel Bottom gap is twenty-one hundredths."

**Evidence defeating it:**
- State Agricultural College Bulletin 214 (1911): bog hay "depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." This finding supports Grigg's theory but is quantitatively insufficient. The observed gap at Ostrey Hollow was 0.19% (nineteen hundredths) year-round, wet and dry, summer and winter. Bulletin 214 accounts for at most 0.05% in winter, zero in summer. It explains only one-fourth of the gap at best.
- The 1911 measure anomaly: if wet hay caused low tests, the tests should not have risen when the measure changed (assuming the same cows, same hay, same season). But tests rose 0.19 points immediately. Hay does not change in nine weeks—glass does. This proves hay is not the cause.
- Alder Corners Cheese Company data (Jerome Cudd, 1915–1930): the same four Hessel Bottom farmers hauled their milk over Redlow Ridge to Alder Corners in the summer flush (June–September), where it tested 3.78–3.82 average (≈3.80). When these same farmers hauled to Ostrey Hollow station in winter, their milk tested ≈3.61 (same milk, same farmers, but lower test). This contradicts the hay hypothesis: if Hessel Bottom hay were thinning the milk, summer milk should test low too (the farmers were on Hessel Bottom hay year-round). Instead, summer milk at Alder Corners tested high, and winter milk at Ostrey Hollow tested low—proving the station's test, not the hay, was the variable.

The hay hypothesis is contradicted by Bulletin 214's own figures and by the summer-winter discrepancy in the Alder Corners data.

**Subsidiary questions:**

**Whether the 1925 board examined the condemned glassware:**

No. The arbitration award states the board "had before it the books and appliances of the station"—but the four exhibits named are only books (test book, receiving book, weigh book, circuit notebook). The condemned crate sat in the loft, 20 feet above the weigh room where two of the three hearings were held (per Cleve Oram), unmentioned in any testimony or board proceeding. Alonzo Frick later reflected: "I have wondered since whether the answer was in that room and simply was not in any of the three stories, and whether a lawyer who understood chemistry might have found it." The board never examined the glass and never knew to test it.

**What Ansel Keddie believed the cause was:**

Ansel suspected the glass. Duncan Keddie recalled his father coming home summer 1911 "saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book...He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove." Ansel noted a question mark in his test book but offered no assertion. He could suspect it but could not prove it in 1911, and by 1925 his suspicion had never been validated or investigated. Ansel never publicly named glass as the cause, and the arbitration board never asked him to speculate. His statement to the board was: "I could not tell us why the figures were what they were"—an honest admission of powerlessness. He tested milk as taught and recorded what the glass gave him.

# Section G: Summary

## G1: Summary of the eight-story history (≤150 words)

Between 1897 and 1923, the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association's Ostrey Hollow skimming station's milk tests consistently ran 5% low due to defective pipettes supplied in 1897. The station's recorded average was 3.61%; the true average was 3.80%. This proportional error accumulated 76,000 pounds of uncompensated butterfat over 25 years, worth ~$19,000. Three wrong explanations circulated: water in milk (ruled out by state inspector Merle Strawn's testing), cream theft (disproven by the "station gain" ledger showing heavy cream arrival), and wet hay (insufficient to account for the gap). A 1911 measure anomaly—when a borrowed correct pipette replaced the defective one for nine weeks, tests rose to 3.80 then dropped back to 3.61—proved glass was the cause. Despite this evidence lying dormant, Ansel Keddie was dismissed in 1925 and died stigmatized in 1934. His granddaughter Ivy Keddie discovered the condemned crate in 1958, measured the glass, and published Bulletin 471, exonerating him 24 years after death. Eight separate narratives (founding, first complaint, water scare, silent ledger, measure anomaly, condemnation, dismissal, discovery) were needed to reconstruct the whole.
