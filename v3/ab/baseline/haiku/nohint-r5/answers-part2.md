# Section A: The Eight Stories (Part 2 — Stories 5–8) and Section B: Relationships

## Story 5: The Water Warning and the Estrangement (1919–1925)

In June 1919, manager Selby Vose circulated a formal warning to the Ostrey Hollow patrons—nine letters to nine families, though only six farms—cautioning them against adding water to their milk. The tone was civil but the implication was direct: someone was cheating. Bertram Kyle, the clerk at Larrow Green office, typed the letter himself and remembered it. Orra Keddie, Ansel's wife, received hers, read it twice, and put it in the stove. The patrons answered with silence, which in a valley is louder than argument. Ansel Keddie took the matter into his own hands: he carried the letters around himself rather than leave them for another to deliver, a deliberate gesture of solidarity with the farmers he worked among. He stood at each door while the man read it. He came home at eleven at night with his hat still on—a signal Orra immediately recognized. Days later, Orra's brother Emmet came up to the station. He was the largest farmer on the bottom, the one Ansel's figures most affected, the man whose milk and reputation were most at stake. Emmet did not come into the house. He stood in the yard and said to Ansel: you have hauled whole milk to this station since the day it opened, you know what's happening better than any man living, why did you carry the paper yourself if you don't believe it? Ansel's answer was bare: I carried it because I would not have another man carry it. Emmet went home. He did not return to that house for ten years. The accusation—whether of fraud by the farmers or of wrongful accusation by the manager—had rent the family. The issue was not settled; it was abandoned in silence. Orra wrote in her diary: "I do not blame him and I will not say so to A." That refusal to name the breach even privately marked how deep it went. A man who had spent his life in one small valley, measuring the milk of his wife's brother, keeping records no one questioned, suddenly found himself accused of either stupidity or dishonesty by the very people he worked to serve.

## Story 6: The 1925 Arbitration, Dismissal, and Award (March–April 1925)

In January 1925, after years of complaint, the Ostrey Hollow patrons formally petitioned for arbitration. A board convened under the chairmanship of an Ashlin attorney. Three sittings occurred: the first at the Cooperative's office at Ashlin on March 17; two more at the station itself, in the weigh room with witnesses and materials at hand. Cleve Oram, then twenty-five, stood outside in the rain on one afternoon with about a dozen others, hearing only voices. What he did not know was that the crate of condemned glass sat twenty feet overhead in the loft, completely invisible and unthought of. The board heard three explanations: that the patrons had added water; that Ansel Keddie had stolen cream; that the wet pasture of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream. The state inspector's notebooks showed no added water. The 1911 glass interval had refuted cream theft—the same man produced different figures with different glass. The committee reports on pasture were real but insufficient to account for nineteen hundredths year-round and in every season. On May 1, 1925, the board awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800 "without admission of any cause," a legal formula meaning: we are paying you and we are not saying why. On April 30—four days *before* the award, one day before the final sitting—Ansel Keddie was dismissed by letter. He walked up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm, hung it behind the shed door, and asked what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it. He was sixty-four years old. He had worked at that station for twenty-nine seasons. He had never been late. Nine years later, in 1934, he died at home. Men shook Orra's hand at the burying who had not spoken to him in nine years. Not one said the thing they now had thirty-three years' chance to say. He had asked for nothing except what he was owed, and they had given him nothing at all.

## Story 7: The 1923 Glass Condemnation, Unheeded (June 1923)

In June 1923, the state dairy inspector Merle Strawn rode his circuit and came to the Ostrey Hollow station. He was a methodical man, kin to nobody in the valley, and he carried a little notebook with a rubber band around it. He had begun bringing a burette on his circuit visits—a precise measuring instrument used in chemistry and dairy testing. On June 12, 1923, he ran the station's pipettes against his burette and found immediately that they would not deliver their marked capacity. He condemned the whole lot on the spot and directed the manager to order new. Selby Vose entered the order that same week to Ammon & Sons at Tarnet, dated June 28. Junia Ammon, on July 2, replied with her careful, minimal letter: the order was entered and would ship this month, the firm regretted the trouble, but their books of the nineties would not tell them anything about the original lot. New glass arrived within a month and went into use. From 1924 onward, the station's average test stood at 3.85—the accurate level for that milk. The glass fault had been identified, the glass replaced, and the station cleaned. But nothing in Strawn's notebooks suggested to him or anyone else that the condemned glass had been in continuous use since 1897, that twenty-six years of tests were wrong, or that the whole history of complaint and accusation rested on a material defect sitting on a bench. Strawn wrote in his notebook what he had done, filed his report, and moved on. The crate went to the loft. No connection was made. No memory persisted. Eight months later, the arbitration board met to settle the dispute. They had four books to work from: the station test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book, and Merle Strawn's circuit notebook for 1923. No one laid Strawn's notebook beside the trial books and asked whether the two told the same story. The condemned glass sat overhead, silent, invisible, unremembered.

## Story 8: Ivy's Discovery and Exoneration (February–October 1958)

In February 1958, thirty-five years after the glass was condemned, Ivy Keddie—Ansel's granddaughter, Duncan's daughter, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland studying dairy husbandry—went up a ladder into the loft over the old station's intake room and found a wooden crate sitting under a tarpaulin with about an inch of chaff. It was the station's original glassware, condemned in 1923, put up out of the way, left undisturbed. Nobody had thought about it for decades. The crate held nine pipettes in condition to be measured. In March 1958, in a college laboratory, Ivy measured each against a calibrated burette. Every single one delivered materially less than 17.6 cubic centimeters. The average was 16.27 cc—short by 0.73 cc per measure, the same systematic shortfall, one to another. She was twenty-seven years old, great-niece of the dismissed tester, granddaughter of the man who kept the weigh book, second cousin to the clerk who sat outside the manager's door. She had read the record, understood the mathematics, and climbed a ladder that no one else in her family had climbed. In October 1958, the State Agricultural College issued Bulletin No. 471 under the names of Professor Leland Vaught and Miss Ivy Keddie. The bulletin presented the measurements, the history, the mathematics, and the conclusion: a short charge into a bottle marked for a full charge yields a butterfat test short by the same proportion, in perpetuity. No milk was watered. No cream was stolen. No hay thinned the tests. A piece of glass blown in 1897 ran consistently short, and every test from May 1897 to June 1923 was false by that constant margin. The county agricultural agent prepared a formal report. The newspaper printed the story. Ansel Keddie had died in 1934 without vindication, his reputation clouded by the whole valley. His widow Orra lived until 1948 and did not see the clearing. The patrons who suffered the loss were also dead. What had been settled with $3,800 and "without admission" could now be stated plainly: the tester was innocent, and the accusation had never been fair.

# Section B: Relationships

## B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Duncan Keddie, Ansel's son, was her father. Duncan Keddie's Notes state plainly: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter." The Ashlin Chronicle initially erred, calling her his "great-niece," but Orin Falke's report on receipt of the college bulletin corrected this: "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece." This is the only genealogical error in the sources concerning her, and Falke immediately corrected it himself.

## B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant?

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. Duncan Keddie's genealogy is explicit: Roy Brant (Hazel's father) and Duncan Keddie are first cousins—children of siblings Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie. Therefore their children, Hazel and Ivy, are second cousins. Duncan's note emphasizes this precisely because it was often gotten wrong: "Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece, which people say because it is shorter." Hazel herself confirms this understanding in her retelling, referring to "my cousin Ivy Keddie" and noting the family connection explicitly. The intervening generation is clear: Emmet Brant → Roy Brant → Hazel; Orra Keddie (Emmet's sister) → Duncan Keddie → Ivy.

## B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter?

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose. Duncan Keddie sets this out carefully because it was constantly confused: "Selby Vose, who managed the Association, was Gideon Vose's son... And I say this plainly because it has been got wrong, as I have heard it given as his nephew." Bertram Kyle, the clerk who sat in the outer office for twenty-five years, confirms: "Mr. Selby Vose became manager in nineteen eleven. He was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president—the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense." This matters because it establishes the authority and family weight behind Selby Vose's position. As the founder's son, inheriting the management, he had institutional authority. When he signed the 1919 water-warning and circulated it to the Ostrey Hollow patrons, or when he entered the order for replacement glass in 1923, or when he managed the Association's defense during the 1925 arbitration, he did so as the organization's principal officer and as the heir to its founding authority.

## B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?

No relationship existed between the Vose family and the Keddie family. Duncan Keddie states this explicitly in a section titled "A thing that is said and is not so": "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either. I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her sit down. It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me. She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it." However, Cleve Oram (the cream hauler's son) offers contradictory gossip: "And I will say this while I am on families, because you will hear it anyway and you may as well hear it from somebody who is not repeating it out of malice. The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley. My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper." Duncan Keddie's testimony is more reliable because he was explicitly told family genealogy by his mother Orra and kept meticulous notes. Cleve Oram admits he cannot trace the connection and heard it only as unverified gossip. The resolution: no documented family connection between Keddies and Voses exists in any retelling that provides verifiable genealogy. The rumor persisted in the valley, but Duncan Keddie's direct denial, supported by his mother's meticulous knowledge, outweighs Oram's hearsay. The confusion may stem from the small size of the valley and the intermingling of names in general.
