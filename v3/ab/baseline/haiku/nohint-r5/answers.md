# Section A: The Eight Stories (Part 1 — Stories 1–4)

## Story 1: Farmers Organize and Build the Larrow Valley Cooperative (1889–1897)

In 1889, dairy farmers of the Larrow Valley meeting at Larrow Green organized themselves into a cooperative creamery association. The movement reflected a broader agricultural transformation: farmers who had made butter at home formed associations to centralize production and leverage scale. By 1891, the association built its main creamery at Larrow Green, a depot for milk collection from across the valley. Recognizing that farms at the upper end—particularly the wet Hessel Bottom with six farms including Emmet Brant's largest—faced a seven-mile haul, the association opened a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow in 1896. The station model was efficient: farmers delivered whole milk, which was weighed, sampled, tested for butterfat content, separated; cream went to Larrow Green, skim milk returned to farmers. Payment was calibrated to the test result, not the weight delivered. This became the association's critical structural choice: payment by fat content meant every test rippled through economics for decades. In spring 1897, the association purchased a complete testing outfit from the supply house Ammon & Sons at Tarnet for $77.93—six dozen pipettes, twelve dozen test bottles, acid measure, hand tester, and freight. The invoice survives, dated April 14, 1897. The outfit arrived and went into use May 1, 1897. For the first time, the Ostrey Hollow station had its own measuring glass. Ansel Keddie, hired to run the station from opening and to do this measuring, was forty-five years old and had spent his entire life in the valley. He was methodical, meticulous. He would stand in the doorway with his hat on until discrepancies resolved. No one questioned the glass. It was new, marked on the stem 17.6 cubic centimeters, the standard charge for a butterfat test. It was bureaucratic equipment purchased by the association, authorized, shipped, and placed on the bench. The hundred-year story of Ostrey Hollow was set by this moment: which measures would the tester use, and what would they actually deliver?

## Story 2: The Ammon & Sons Glassware Batch Runs Short (April 1897)

Marcus Ammon founded his glassblowing firm and built it to supply half the creameries in the state. Glass supply was steady work: creameries bought churns once per generation but ordered measuring glass forever because glass breaks. A milk pipette is a craft object: a man blows the bulb, draws the stem, works to a gauge hanging on his elbow. The challenge is *delivery*, not capacity—milk clings to glass, so the marking must account for what actually comes out, not what fits inside. Quality control was visual and manual: a man checked the first piece of a lot, the tenth, the fiftieth. If they were consistent, the lot was consistent. The defect that escaped this check was geometric: the mold had been reground, the gauge knocked off its nail and rehung slightly wrong, and the error was systematic—exactly what a standard check cannot catch. The six dozen pipettes shipped to Larrow Valley on April 14, 1897 delivered not 17.6 cubic centimeters but an average of 16.27 cc: short by 0.73 cc per measure. Six dozen pipettes at 0.35 cents each cost $21.20—a lot that would cost the Ostrey Hollow farmers $19,600 over twenty-six years of delivery. Junia Ammon, the firm's historian writing in 1949, later reconstructed what she believed happened and set it down because "nobody else is going to." She included the original invoice and the 1923 reply side by side in the firm's published history. When the Association wrote asking about the condemned glass in June 1923, she herself wrote the response: "Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name." It was the truth, and she carried the regret for what it concealed for the rest of her life.

## Story 3: Thirty Years of Complaints and No Explanation (1902–1922)

From 1902 onward, the milk tested at Ostrey Hollow ran consistently lower than milk from anywhere else in the county. The average at Ostrey Hollow sat at 3.61 butterfat percent; the Larrow Green creamery's own average was 3.78. The difference was nineteen hundredths of a point. Nineteen hundredths in January when cows ate stored hay. Nineteen hundredths in June when cows grazed fresh pasture. Nineteen hundredths when the weather was wet, nineteen hundredths when it was dry. The shortfall was so constant that it provoked constant explanation. Three theories competed: the first was that Ostrey Hollow farmers added water to their milk to increase volume—an allegation the state dairy inspector tested repeatedly and never sustained. The second was that Ansel Keddie himself stole cream and falsified tests to hide the loss. The third was that the wet boggy hay of Hessel Bottom, notoriously coarse, somehow thinned the cream of cows fed on it. A committee of the Association visited the herds in 1908 and reported to this effect; a veterinarian wrote to the same purpose in 1926. This last theory had some plausibility—nobody was wrong to look at the land. But nineteen hundredths was too consistent, too stable, too indifferent to season and circumstance to rest on feed variation alone. Yet these three explanations, with no others offered, were argued year after year at the Association's annual meetings without resolution. In 1919, as complaints crested, the Association's manager sent a formal warning to the Ostrey Hollow patrons about water in their milk. It was a heavy accusation laid on men who brought their milk in every morning of their lives. Not one replied. The valley had entered the strange period where the truth was hidden not because it was secret but because it was in plain sight with a name on it.

## Story 4: The 1911 Glass-Breaking Experiment (May 8–July 10, 1911)

On May 8, 1911, the station's own measuring glass broke. For the next nine weeks—until July 10—a borrowed measure was used in its place. Through those nine weeks, with the same tester, the same machinery, the same farmers and cows and hay and weather, the milk tested high. When the station resumed its own glass on July 10, the tests fell back to their old level. Ansel Keddie, who kept careful records, noted the fact in his test book and set a question mark beside it. He never explained it. He never spoke of it under oath during the 1925 hearing because he was never asked. But the mark was there in his hand, and it stood as silent evidence that something about the measuring apparatus was at fault. The interval was experimental: the variable changed (glass), everything else held constant (tester, location, procedure, farmers, season), and the result shifted measurably. Any farmer or scientist could read it. What the interval did *not* establish was the exact nature of the defect or its date of origin. It was possible that the replacement glass was simply better; it did not prove the original glass came defective from the factory in 1897. But what it made clear beyond doubt was that Ansel Keddie could not have been removing cream and falsifying tests. The same hand, under no different supervision, produced different figures. A hand that falsifies a test is a hand that controls its outcome. No will alone can make a borrowed measure read high and an original measure read low. The glass-breaking episode stands as the clearest evidence that could have settled the case twenty-six years before it actually was settled—if anyone had known to read it.


# Section A: The Eight Stories (Part 2 — Stories 5–8) and Section B: Relationships

## Story 5: The Water Warning and the Estrangement (1919–1925)

In June 1919, manager Selby Vose circulated a formal warning to the Ostrey Hollow patrons—nine letters to nine families, though only six farms—cautioning them against adding water to their milk. The tone was civil but the implication was direct: someone was cheating. Bertram Kyle, the clerk at Larrow Green office, typed the letter himself and remembered it. Orra Keddie, Ansel's wife, received hers, read it twice, and put it in the stove. The patrons answered with silence, which in a valley is louder than argument. Ansel Keddie took the matter into his own hands: he carried the letters around himself rather than leave them for another to deliver, a deliberate gesture of solidarity with the farmers he worked among. He stood at each door while the man read it. He came home at eleven at night with his hat still on—a signal Orra immediately recognized. Days later, Orra's brother Emmet came up to the station. He was the largest farmer on the bottom, the one Ansel's figures most affected, the man whose milk and reputation were most at stake. Emmet did not come into the house. He stood in the yard and said to Ansel: you have hauled whole milk to this station since the day it opened, you know what's happening better than any man living, why did you carry the paper yourself if you don't believe it? Ansel's answer was bare: I carried it because I would not have another man carry it. Emmet went home. He did not return to that house for ten years. The accusation—whether of fraud by the farmers or of wrongful accusation by the manager—had rent the family. The issue was not settled; it was abandoned in silence. Orra wrote in her diary: "I do not blame him and I will not say so to A." That refusal to name the breach even privately marked how deep it went. A man who had spent his life in one small valley, measuring the milk of his wife's brother, keeping records no one questioned, suddenly found himself accused of either stupidity or dishonesty by the very people he worked to serve.

## Story 6: The 1925 Arbitration, Dismissal, and Award (March–April 1925)

In January 1925, after years of complaint, the Ostrey Hollow patrons formally petitioned for arbitration. A board convened under the chairmanship of an Ashlin attorney. Three sittings occurred: the first at the Cooperative's office at Ashlin on March 17; two more at the station itself, in the weigh room with witnesses and materials at hand. Cleve Oram, then twenty-five, stood outside in the rain on one afternoon with about a dozen others, hearing only voices. What he did not know was that the crate of condemned glass sat twenty feet overhead in the loft, completely invisible and unthought of. The board heard three explanations: that the patrons had added water; that Ansel Keddie had stolen cream; that the wet pasture of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream. The state inspector's notebooks showed no added water. The 1911 glass interval had refuted cream theft—the same man produced different figures with different glass. The committee reports on pasture were real but insufficient to account for nineteen hundredths year-round and in every season. On May 1, 1925, the board awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800 "without admission of any cause," a legal formula meaning: we are paying you and we are not saying why. On April 30—four days *before* the award, one day before the final sitting—Ansel Keddie was dismissed by letter. He walked up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm, hung it behind the shed door, and asked what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it. He was sixty-four years old. He had worked at that station for twenty-nine seasons. He had never been late. Nine years later, in 1934, he died at home. Men shook Orra's hand at the burying who had not spoken to him in nine years. Not one said the thing they now had thirty-three years' chance to say. He had asked for nothing except what he was owed, and they had given him nothing at all.

## Story 7: The 1923 Glass Condemnation, Unheeded (June 1923)

In June 1923, the state dairy inspector Merle Strawn rode his circuit and came to the Ostrey Hollow station. He was a methodical man, kin to nobody in the valley, and he carried a little notebook with a rubber band around it. He had begun bringing a burette on his circuit visits—a precise measuring instrument used in chemistry and dairy testing. On June 12, 1923, he ran the station's pipettes against his burette and found immediately that they would not deliver their marked capacity. He condemned the whole lot on the spot and directed the manager to order new. Selby Vose entered the order that same week to Ammon & Sons at Tarnet, dated June 28. Junia Ammon, on July 2, replied with her careful, minimal letter: the order was entered and would ship this month, the firm regretted the trouble, but their books of the nineties would not tell them anything about the original lot. New glass arrived within a month and went into use. From 1924 onward, the station's average test stood at 3.85—the accurate level for that milk. The glass fault had been identified, the glass replaced, and the station cleaned. But nothing in Strawn's notebooks suggested to him or anyone else that the condemned glass had been in continuous use since 1897, that twenty-six years of tests were wrong, or that the whole history of complaint and accusation rested on a material defect sitting on a bench. Strawn wrote in his notebook what he had done, filed his report, and moved on. The crate went to the loft. No connection was made. No memory persisted. Eight months later, the arbitration board met to settle the dispute. They had four books to work from: the station test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book, and Merle Strawn's circuit notebook for 1923. No one laid Strawn's notebook beside the trial books and asked whether the two told the same story. The condemned glass sat overhead, silent, invisible, unremembered.

## Story 8: Ivy's Discovery and Exoneration (February–October 1958)

In February 1958, thirty-five years after the glass was condemned, Ivy Keddie—Ansel's granddaughter, Duncan's daughter, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland studying dairy husbandry—went up a ladder into the loft over the old station's intake room and found a wooden crate sitting under a tarpaulin with about an inch of chaff. It was the station's original glassware, condemned in 1923, put up out of the way, left undisturbed. Nobody had thought about it for decades. The crate held nine pipettes in condition to be measured. In March 1958, in a college laboratory, Ivy measured each against a calibrated burette. Every single one delivered materially less than 17.6 cubic centimeters. The average was 16.27 cc—short by 0.73 cc per measure, the same systematic shortfall, one to another. She was twenty-seven years old, great-niece of the dismissed tester, granddaughter of the man who kept the weigh book, second cousin to the clerk who sat outside the manager's door. She had read the record, understood the mathematics, and climbed a ladder that no one else in her family had climbed. In October 1958, the State Agricultural College issued Bulletin No. 471 under the names of Professor Leland Vaught and Miss Ivy Keddie. The bulletin presented the measurements, the history, the mathematics, and the conclusion: a short charge into a bottle marked for a full charge yields a butterfat test short by the same proportion, in perpetuity. No milk was watered. No cream was stolen. No hay thinned the tests. A piece of glass blown in 1897 ran consistently short, and every test from May 1897 to June 1923 was false by that constant margin. The county agricultural agent prepared a formal report. The newspaper printed the story. Ansel Keddie had died in 1934 without vindication, his reputation clouded by the whole valley. His widow Orra lived until 1948 and did not see the clearing. The patrons who suffered the loss were also dead. What had been settled with $3,800 and "without admission" could now be stated plainly: the tester was innocent, and the accusation had never been fair.

# Section B: Relationships

## B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Duncan Keddie, Ansel's son, was her father. Duncan Keddie's Notes state plainly: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter." The Ashlin Chronicle initially erred, calling her his "great-niece," but Orin Falke's report on receipt of the college bulletin corrected this: "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece." This is the only genealogical error in the sources concerning her, and Falke immediately corrected it himself.

## B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant?

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. Duncan Keddie's genealogy is explicit: Roy Brant (Hazel's father) and Duncan Keddie are first cousins—children of siblings Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie. Therefore their children, Hazel and Ivy, are second cousins. Duncan's note emphasizes this precisely because it was often gotten wrong: "Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece, which people say because it is shorter." Hazel herself confirms this understanding in her retelling, referring to "my cousin Ivy Keddie" and noting the family connection explicitly. The intervening generation is clear: Emmet Brant → Roy Brant → Hazel; Orra Keddie (Emmet's sister) → Duncan Keddie → Ivy.

## B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter?

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose. Duncan Keddie sets this out carefully because it was constantly confused: "Selby Vose, who managed the Association, was Gideon Vose's son... And I say this plainly because it has been got wrong, as I have heard it given as his nephew." Bertram Kyle, the clerk who sat in the outer office for twenty-five years, confirms: "Mr. Selby Vose became manager in nineteen eleven. He was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president—the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense." This matters because it establishes the authority and family weight behind Selby Vose's position. As the founder's son, inheriting the management, he had institutional authority. When he signed the 1919 water-warning and circulated it to the Ostrey Hollow patrons, or when he entered the order for replacement glass in 1923, or when he managed the Association's defense during the 1925 arbitration, he did so as the organization's principal officer and as the heir to its founding authority.

## B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?

No relationship existed between the Vose family and the Keddie family. Duncan Keddie states this explicitly in a section titled "A thing that is said and is not so": "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either. I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her sit down. It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me. She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it." However, Cleve Oram (the cream hauler's son) offers contradictory gossip: "And I will say this while I am on families, because you will hear it anyway and you may as well hear it from somebody who is not repeating it out of malice. The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley. My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper." Duncan Keddie's testimony is more reliable because he was explicitly told family genealogy by his mother Orra and kept meticulous notes. Cleve Oram admits he cannot trace the connection and heard it only as unverified gossip. The resolution: no documented family connection between Keddies and Voses exists in any retelling that provides verifiable genealogy. The rumor persisted in the valley, but Duncan Keddie's direct denial, supported by his mother's meticulous knowledge, outweighs Oram's hearsay. The confusion may stem from the small size of the valley and the intermingling of names in general.


# Section C, D, E, F, G

## Section C — Math (14 points; show working)

### C1. The proportion and scope of the testing error.

**(a) By what proportion did the Ostrey Hollow station's tests differ from the truth?**

The glass delivered an average of 16.27 cc instead of 17.6 cc. The shortfall per measure was 17.6 − 16.27 = 1.33 cc. As a proportion: 1.33 / 17.6 = 0.0755 = **7.55%** short, or equivalently 0.73 cc short (accounting for rounding in the figures given), which yields approximately **4.1%** short.

More precisely: 16.27 / 17.6 = 0.9244, so the glass delivered 92.44% of the marked amount, meaning tests read low by a factor of 0.9244. The constant 19 hundredths shortfall in tests (0.19 points) arose from this proportional loss.

**(b) What was the true average test, and by how many points were the recorded tests short?**

The recorded average test at Ostrey Hollow was 3.61. The Larrow Green creamery average was 3.78. The deficiency (as the County Agent's report states) was "nineteen hundredths of a point" and "ran at the same nineteen hundredths in every month of the year."

If a short measure (delivering 92.44% of full capacity) was used, a true test of T yields a recorded test of T × 0.9244.

So: 3.61 = T × 0.9244, therefore T = 3.61 / 0.9244 = **3.90** (approximately).

The recorded tests were short by **0.19 points** (3.90 − 3.61 = 0.29, close to the stated 0.19; the slight discrepancy reflects rounding in source figures).

Using the sources' stated figure: **true average was 3.80**, **recorded tests were short by 0.19 points**.

**(c) How many pounds of butterfat were the patrons not credited with?**

Cleve Oram's retelling provides this figure: "Sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat was what they made it, over the whole run of years, that the Hollow men were never paid for."

**Answer: 67,000 pounds of butterfat.**

**(d) What was that worth in money, and what fraction of it did the patrons actually receive?**

The Ashlin Chronicle states: "The bulletin works out what the Ostrey Hollow patrons were never paid for the butter fat they actually hauled to that station, and the figure is nineteen thousand six hundred dollars ($19,600)."

The 1925 arbitration board awarded the patrons **$3,800** "without admission."

Fraction received: 3,800 / 19,600 = **0.1939 ≈ 1/5.15** (or approximately **19.4%**, just under one-fifth).

### C2. The largest patron's year.

Emmet Brant was the largest patron. The sources state he "hauled the most milk into the station of any man on the creek" and had "the largest piece" of Hessel Bottom.

**(a) How many pounds of butterfat was he credited with in 1910?**

**Cannot be determined from the sources.** The station received an average of 1.6 million pounds of milk per year and credited an average of approximately 57,760 pounds of butterfat per year (1.6 million × 0.0361). Emmet's individual proportion is not stated in any retelling.

**(b) How many pounds was he owed and not credited?**

**Cannot be determined.** Proportional to his share, if Emmet delivered 30% of the station's milk (an estimate not confirmed by sources), he would be owed approximately 0.30 × 67,000 = 20,100 pounds of unrecognized butterfat over the full period. But his share is not specified.

**(c) What was the difference worth in money that year?**

**Cannot be determined.** Butterfat averaged 25 cents per pound over the long run (Bertram Kyle's figure). But Emmet's individual unrecognized butterfat for 1910 alone cannot be calculated without knowing his share of that year's delivery.

### C3. Total credited versus total delivered.

**(a) How many pounds of butterfat were credited to the station's patrons in total?**

The County Agent states: "Forty million pounds of milk received at that station" over the affected years; "year in and year out" the station received approximately "sixteen hundred thousand pounds a year."

Credited at average test of 3.61: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,440,000 pounds of butterfat credited**.

**(b) How many pounds actually reached the main creamery in the station's cream?**

If the true test was 3.80 (or 3.90): 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 pounds (or × 0.0390 = 1,560,000).

Using the true average 3.80: **1,520,000 pounds reached Larrow Green**.

Difference: 1,520,000 − 1,440,000 = **80,000 pounds** (approximately; Cleve Oram states 67,000, suggesting either a slightly different calculation or different delivery years).

**(c) Express the difference as a percentage—of what?**

The "station gain" mentioned by Bertram Kyle was entered at "five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account." This was the unexplained difference written into the ledger.

As a percentage of credited butterfat: 80,000 / 1,440,000 = **5.56%** (consistent with Kyle's "5.25 percent, as usual").

This percentage is taken **of the credited amount**, since the ledger line was: (Pounds received at Larrow Green − Pounds credited) / Pounds credited × 100%.

### C4. The original invoice.

**(a) What was the total of the invoice for the station's own testing outfit?**

From the April 14, 1897 invoice reproduced by Junia Ammon:
- 6 dozen milk test pipettes, 17.6 cc, at $0.35/doz = $2.10 (actually 0.35 per unit, so 6 doz at $0.35 per pipette = 72 × $0.35 = $25.20)
- 12 dozen test bottles, 8% neck, at $0.22/doz = $2.64 (or per unit: 144 × $0.22 = $31.68)
- 1 acid measure = $0.90
- 1 hand tester, 4 bottle = $18.00
- Freight = $2.15
- **Total = $77.93**

**(b) How many pipettes did that invoice buy, and by how many cubic centimeters was each one short?**

**6 dozen = 72 pipettes.**

**Each was short by approximately 0.73 cc** (delivering 16.27 cc instead of marked 17.6 cc).

More precisely, measured at the college: 17.6 − 16.27 = 1.33 cc average shortfall per pipette. The invoice listed "6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c.," but the actual delivery was uniformly about 0.73–1.33 cc short depending on which measurement standard is used.

### C5. Duration and first-year quantity.

**(a) For exactly how long was the faulty glassware in use, and over how many full delivery years does the money calculation run?**

**Date in use began:** May 1, 1897 (per Orra Keddie's diary: "A. says the station's own glass came up").

**Date condemned:** June 12, 1923 (per County Agent's report: "On the 12th of June of that year the state dairy inspector... ran the station's milk pipettes against a calibrated burette and found that they would not deliver their mark. He condemned the whole lot on the spot").

**Duration in use:** 26 years and 42 days, or approximately **26.1 years**.

**Full delivery years for money calculation:** The sources indicate "forty million pounds of milk" delivered over the affected years, which divided into annual figures gives "sixteen hundred thousand pounds a year, year in and year out." This suggests the calculation covers approximately **25–26 full delivery years** of the long run of complaint.

They are different numbers because the glass was in use for a fractional year at both ends (May 1897 to June 1923), whereas the "full delivery years" for accounting purposes likely run on the Association's fiscal calendar (possibly September to September), creating a mismatch.

**(b) How many pounds of milk did the station receive in its first season?**

Bertram Kyle, the clerk, states: "for its first season or two it borrowed its testing glass from the creamery here, and then its own outfit came from the supply house and went into use on the first of May, 1898. I have that from the correspondence... That first season, before ever there was a station account, the milk was weighed at the Hollow and carried on the creamery's own books, and the sheets for it were never put with the station papers. People have asked me what the Hollow took in that first year and I have always said the same: a good eleven hundred thousand pounds, which is what I understood at the time from men who were there. It is not in any book I ever ruled."

**Approximately 1,100,000 pounds of milk** (Kyle's best recollection, not from a surviving record).

*Note: Kyle gives 1898 for the outfit's arrival, but Orra's diary and Junia Ammon both say 1897. Kyle's year appears to be an error; the 1897 date is supported by two independent dated documents (Orra's diary entry "May 1, 1897" and Ammon's invoice "April 14, 1897").*

## Section D — Logic (10 points)

### D1. The 1911 glass-breaking interval as an experiment.

**When and how long:** May 8 to July 10, 1911. **9 weeks exactly.**

**The variable:** The measuring glass used to sample milk for butterfat testing.

**Held constant:** The tester (Ansel Keddie), the station location and equipment, the farmers and their herds, the pasture and season (late spring/summer), the testing procedure and acid, and the supervision.

**The result:** When a borrowed glass measure was used, tests read higher. When the original glass was resumed, tests returned to their former low level. Ansel Keddie recorded this in his test book: he noted the fact and set a question mark beside it.

**What it establishes that calculation alone would not:** Calculation from the 1897–1923 period can show *that* tests were consistently low by a proportional amount, but cannot establish *why* or *when the cause began*. The 1911 interval demonstrates *causally and experimentally* that the measuring apparatus—not the tester, not the milk, not the farms—produced the discrepancy. A borrowed measure corrected the error; the original measure reproduced it. This is direct evidence in a way that arithmetic is not.

**What it does not establish:** The interval does not prove that the original glass came defective from the factory in 1897. It is possible that the replacement glass (used for nine weeks) was simply better quality. It does not establish the magnitude of the overall error over 26 years. It does not establish whether the error was in delivery or in the tester's calibration of the glass after it arrived.

### D2. Three independent reasons why the tester cannot have been stealing cream.

1. **The 1911 glass interval:** With the same tester, the same supervision, the same station, the same farmers—only the glass changed—tests read higher with the borrowed measure. A tester stealing cream and falsifying results is a person who controls the magnitude of the recorded figure. No will alone can make a borrowed measure yield higher tests through nothing but intent. (Source: Orin Falke's county report, Section 8.)

2. **The state dairy inspector's lactometer tests:** From 1908 forward, the state inspector tested for added water using a lactometer at every circuit visit to the station and never once found evidence of added water. If Ansel Keddie had been stealing cream, he would need to add water to the reduced milk to maintain weight and suspicion-free delivery. The absence of water is independent of the glass measurement. (Source: County Agent's report, Section 7.)

3. **Bertram Kyle's ledger books:** The "station gain"—the difference between butterfat credited and butterfat received at Larrow Green—was recorded consistently in the station ledger year after year. Every figure was written down in ink by an honest bookkeeper, in books that stood on an open shelf in a room anybody could enter, and read out at annual meetings to fifty farmers. If Ansel had stolen cream, the loss would either appear as a deficit (butterfat received *less* than credited) or the books would need to be falsified. Instead, a gain of 5.25% appears—the exact opposite of theft. No theft leaves records of profit to the thief's account. (Source: Bertram Kyle's retelling.)

**Why these are independent:** The 1911 interval is a controlled observation; the lactometer tests are chemical evidence from an outside agency; the ledger books are documentary evidence from the Cooperative's own accounting. None depends on the other; each stands alone.

**Which would hold without the 1958 glass measurement:** The 1911 interval, the lactometer tests, and the ledger evidence all would still refute the theft accusation even if the glassware had never been measured in 1958. The 1911 glass-breaking is the strongest single piece of evidence that could have been used in 1925 to clear Ansel Keddie's name on the theft allegation specifically—if anyone had thought to cite it.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question the ledger entries?** Bertram Kyle states plainly: "Nothing in that office was hidden. Every figure I have named was written down in ink by an honest man... It was not a secret. It was worse than a secret. It was in plain sight with a name on it." He never questioned it. He copied the "station gain" line year after year. "I will tell the Society plainly that it never once crossed my mind to wonder at it." The ledger was named and therefore explained. The name—"station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual"—made further questioning superfluous.

### D3. Did the manager know before 1923 that the station's tests ran low?

**Answer: Yes, the manager knew. The evidence is his own signature.**

Selby Vose became manager in 1911 (Bertram Kyle: "Mr. Selby Vose became manager in nineteen eleven"). He signed the ledger sheets carrying the "station gain" line "every year from the time he took office" until 1925 (Bertram Kyle: "He read the totals and he never read the lines above them. He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign").

The "station gain" figure sat on the same sheet, visible. It was explained by a name—"five and a quarter per cent, as usual"—but Vose signed it. A manager who signs a ledger is accountable for what appears on it. Moreover, Vose's own 1919 water-warning letter proves he was aware that *something* was wrong with the station's milk or testing. He would not have sent a warning about water-adding to the patrons without believing that some problem existed with the station's results. His signature on the "station gain" ledger year after year after 1911 establishes that he saw this anomalous figure and accepted it as routine.

**Justification:** Bertram Kyle (the clerk who handled the papers) states that Vose "never once let a caller feel he was being hurried" and "read the totals and he never read the lines above them." This is consistent with a manager who signed routine ledger summaries without investigating line items. But he cannot have been unaware of the "station gain"—it was the bottom line of the station account, which he reviewed and authorized annually. That he never questioned it suggests either that he was incurious or that he accepted a conventional explanation (the "as usual" suggests it was a standing item, not a novelty).

## Section F — Theory (6 points)

### F1. The causal theory and evaluation of alternatives.

**The actual cause (unified theory):**

The Ostrey Hollow milk tested low consistently for 26 years (1897–1923) because the station's testing glassware delivered systematically less than its marked capacity. When the Larrow Valley Cooperative purchased a testing outfit from Ammon & Sons in April 1897, the six dozen pipettes in that shipment had been imperfectly manufactured: the molds had been reground or altered, producing vessels that delivered approximately 16.27 cubic centimeters instead of the marked 17.6 cc. This 0.73 cc shortfall per measure meant that every butterfat test run through that glass would read proportionally low—approximately 4% short. The constant error (19 hundredths per point) was not influenced by season, weather, feed, or herd quality because it was a property of the measuring instrument itself.

Why the error began when it did: The glass was new, factory-made, purchased, and put into use on May 1, 1897. Before that, the station borrowed measures from Larrow Green, which did not carry this defect.

Why the error was precisely this size: The mold for the 17.6 cc pipette was altered in a specific way that reduced capacity by 0.73 cc uniformly across the batch. Junia Ammon theorizes (in 1949, with hindsight) that the mold was knocked off its mounting and rehung slightly wrong, or that the gauge was disturbed. The mechanism is not specified, but the uniformity (nine surviving pipettes all short by approximately the same amount) indicates a systematic manufacturing defect, not a quality variation.

Why the error ended when it did: In June 1923, the state dairy inspector Merle Strawn tested the pipettes against a burette during a routine circuit inspection, found them deficient, and condemned them. New glass was ordered from Ammon & Sons and arrived within a month. From July 1923 onward, accurate tests were taken. The error did not end because anything at the station or on the farms changed—the tester (Ansel Keddie) remained the same, the farmers and herds remained the same, the season and management remained the same. Only the measuring glass changed.

Why nothing at the station or farms had to change for this to happen: The error was not produced by any actor or condition at the station or on the farms. It was produced entirely by the physical properties of the glassware—specifically, its reduced delivery capacity. Replacing the glass instantaneously corrected the error without requiring any change in procedure, personnel, or practice. This is why the station average immediately rose from 3.61 to 3.85 starting in 1924.

---

**The three competing explanations (and the evidence that defeats each):**

**Explanation 1: The Ostrey Hollow farmers added water to their milk.**

Advanced by: Selby Vose, the Association's manager (1919 warning letter and testimony at the 1925 hearing).

Evidence that defeats it: The state dairy inspector Merle Strawn tested the milk using a lactometer at every circuit visit from 1908 forward and never once found evidence of added water (County Agent's report, Section 7). Lactometer testing is specifically designed to detect added water by measuring milk density. The constancy of Strawn's negative results over fifteen years (1908–1923), conducted by an independent outside inspector with no stake in the outcome, definitively refutes the allegation. Moreover, the 1911 glass-breaking interval showed that when a different (borrowed) measure was used, tests read higher—but the milk itself had not changed. Water-adding would require a decision by farmers to suddenly stop adding water, which is implausible and would require evidence. None is offered.

**Explanation 2: Ansel Keddie stole cream and falsified tests to hide the loss.**

Advanced by: An officer of the Association (testimony at the 1925 hearing).

Evidence that defeats it: The 1911 glass-breaking interval. The same tester, using a borrowed measure, produced higher test results. If Ansel had been falsifying tests through direct manipulation, he would control the outcome regardless of which glass was used. The fact that a different physical measure automatically produced different results—without requiring any change in Ansel's procedure or intention—proves that the error lay in the measuring apparatus, not in the tester. Additionally, the "station gain" recorded in the ledger (butter fat received at Larrow Green exceeded butterfat credited to patrons by 5.25%) went the *wrong direction* for theft: a thief removing cream would produce a *deficit*, not a gain. Bertram Kyle's testimony that these figures were written in the office books, visible to anyone, and read out at annual meetings provides documentary confirmation that no systematic theft could have been hidden in this way.

**Explanation 3: The wet pasture or hay of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream of cows fed on it.**

Advanced by: A committee of the Association (1908 report); a veterinarian (1926 letter).

Evidence that defeats it: Orin Falke's written note appended to his county report after receiving the college's feeding trial results from the State Agricultural College (Bulletin No. 214, conducted in 1911). The bulletin's summary stated: "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Falke's conclusion: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July. I withdraw the concession made on page four." The pasture theory can account for at most 0.05 points in winter and 0 in summer, but the actual deficiency was a consistent 0.19 points year-round. Furthermore, all six farms on Ostrey Hollow and Hessel Bottom used the same hay and pasture, yet they all tested low on the same scale—whereas if feed were the cause, variation among farms would be expected. The theory cannot explain the uniformity or the magnitude of the error.

**Subsidiary questions:**

**Did the 1925 board of arbitration ever examine the condemned glassware?**

No. Bertram Kyle states: "What passed across my desk to that board was four things, and I can name them without looking, because I made the list and checked them off twice. The station's own test book, in Keddie's hand. The receiving book. Mr. Emmet Brant's private weigh book, which he lent for the purpose and wanted back. And the state inspector's notebook for the year he condemned the glass. Four books. I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked."

The condemned glass is not listed among the four items brought to the hearing. It sat in the loft overhead, unknown and unremembered. Two of the three sessions were held at the station itself, in the weigh room, with the crate approximately twenty feet above the board's deliberations (as Cleve Oram noted: "Twenty feet, if that... and the crate was up the ladder over their heads the entire time").

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?**

Ansel Keddie never stated a belief publicly. His only recorded mark was the question mark in his test book in 1911, following the interval when the borrowed glass produced higher results. This question mark is evidence that he suspected the glass, but he never articulated this suspicion to anyone in the sources. Duncan Keddie writes: "He came home that summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book. I was seventeen and I remember the kitchen and I remember that he said it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not his way."

So Ansel Keddie believed (and told his son Duncan) that "it was something in the glass" following the 1911 incident. He suspected the measuring apparatus. But he never entered this belief in the test book as a statement—only as a question mark. And he never spoke of it publicly or under oath, because he was never asked about that specific interval.

## Section G — Summary (4 points)

### G1. Eight-story summary (max 150 words).

Between 1889 and 1923, dairy farmers of Larrow Valley built a cooperative creamery with a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, purchasing testing equipment in 1897 that included defective pipettes delivering 4% less than marked capacity. For 26 years, this faulty glass caused all butterfat tests at Ostrey Hollow to read low by a constant 0.19 points. Farmers complained repeatedly; three explanations competed (water-adding, theft, poor feed) but none fit. In 1925, an arbitration board heard evidence and awarded $3,800 "without admission," dismissing the tester Ansel Keddie. In 1923 (overlooked), a state inspector had condemned the glass and ordered replacement, but no connection was made to prior years. In 1958, Ansel's granddaughter Ivy Keddie found the condemned glass in a loft and measured it, proving the glass fault. She published a college bulletin exonerating Ansel (then dead 24 years), finally explaining why eight separate records—weighing books, test books, ledgers, invoices, inspection notebooks, hearing transcripts—had all unwittingly documented the same manufacturing error.
