# Section A: Reconstruction of the Eight Stories (Stories 1–4)

## Story 1: The Founding of the Larrow Valley Cooperative and Ostrey Hollow Station (1889-1897)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was established in 1889 by farmers meeting at Larrow Green, with the main creamery built there in 1891 (r22). The Association adopted Article Seven in its bylaws, establishing payment based on butterfat percentage by test, not milk weight (r22).

In winter 1895-96, to serve the farms of Hessel Bottom and the upper valley, the board built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles upstream (r22, r21). The station opened May 4, 1896, as a small board building with intake room, separator, and loft (r21, r22). Its function was to weigh milk, test it for butterfat, separate cream for shipment to Larrow Green, and return skim to farmers (r21).

The station initially borrowed measuring glass from Larrow Green (r24). In April 1897, the Association ordered its own complete testing outfit from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet. The invoice for April 14, 1897 totaled $77.93 and included 6 dozen (72) milk test pipettes marked 17.6 cubic centimeters, 12 dozen test bottles, 1 acid measure, 1 hand tester, and freight (r20, r23). The pipettes, marked to deliver exactly 17.6 cc of milk for testing—the standard charge for a butter fat test—arrived in May 1897 (r20, r24).

Cream hauler Roy Brant, whose father Emmet farmed the largest place on Hessel Bottom, established the daily route from Ostrey Hollow down to Larrow Green, a journey his son Cleve would later ride (r21). The station began maintaining detailed records from its opening year onward.

**Quantities:** $77.93 for equipment; 6 dozen original pipettes; opening date May 4, 1896.

**Documents:** Invoice from Ammon & Sons (r20); station account records (r23); Orra Keddie's diary (r24); Cleve Oram's account (r21).

## Story 2: Ansel Keddie's Hiring and Early Years at the Station (1897-1902)

Ansel Keddie, born 1861, was engaged as tester and separator man at Ostrey Hollow from the station's opening and held the position for twenty-nine seasons (r18, r22). He was described as careful in small things to a degree that others found trying, and this carefulness was the making of the record he left (r18).

Ansel married Orra Brant in 1889 (r18). Orra was the younger sister of Emmet Brant, who farmed the largest piece of Hessel Bottom and hauled the most milk of any patron (r18). This family relationship put Ansel in a delicate position: he was required by his position to record the butterfat tests of milk delivered by his wife's brother. The situation was the hinge of many later tensions.

Orra and Ansel had a son Duncan, born 1894 (r18). Beginning in spring 1897, Orra kept a diary in a ruled notebook where she recorded daily life at Ostrey Hollow (r24). In early May 1897, she noted that Ansel was pleased about receiving the station's own glass and being done borrowing from Larrow Green (r24).

Ansel kept detailed records of every test in the station's own hand from the beginning, entering both the station's own test figures and the receiving weight from Larrow Green side by side (r17). He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove, and he tested the milk in front of the state's own inspector and followed the methods he was taught (r18, r19).

Between 1897 and 1902, the station received its first year's milk (approximately 1,100,000 pounds according to men who were there, though this was not recorded in any book kept by Kyle) and settled into routine (r23).

**Quantities:** 29 seasons of service (1896-1925); son Duncan born 1894; husband of Orra Brant.

**Documents:** Duncan Keddie's family notes (r18); Orra's diary (r24); Bertram Kyle's office records (r23).

## Story 3: The Long Complaint Period and Investigation (1902-1911)

From very nearly the day the station opened, but certainly by 1902, the milk of Ostrey Hollow tested consistently lower than milk from elsewhere in the county—lower than Larrow Green creamery's own milk, lower than neighboring valleys, and this deficit remained constant across seasons (r19, r22).

The quantified shortfall was nineteen hundredths of a point: Ostrey Hollow averaged 3.61% butterfat while Larrow Green averaged 3.78% (r22). This difference held "flat as a floor" in every month of the year, in the June flush and the February depth alike, never varying with season or feed (r17, r22).

The Ostrey Hollow patrons believed they were being cheated and complained at every annual meeting for a quarter of a century (r19). The Association's officers suspected the patrons were adding water or otherwise adulterating milk (r19).

Three competing theories emerged: (1) water was being added to the milk; (2) the tester was stealing cream and falsifying records; or (3) the wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream of cattle fed upon it (r22).

In 1908, the Association formed a committee to investigate the herds (r24). The committee visited Emmet Brant's farm, which was the largest producer, and reported back that the wet hay of the bottom was the cause (r22, r24). Orra noted the committee's visit and conclusion: "They were civil enough to me. They wanted to see the barns and not the station" (r24). In March 1908, she recorded their finding: "Their word is that it is the wet hay of the bottom that thins the cream" (r24).

However, the state dairy inspector, conducting circuit visits from 1908 forward, tested with the lactometer and never found added water (r22).

Yet nothing changed, and the complaint continued. The suspicion was embedded in the community: Cleve Oram's father, Roy Brant, believed his cousin Ansel was somehow removing cream from the cans, though he never said so under oath (r21).

**Quantities:** 19-hundredths shortfall; Ostrey Hollow 3.61%, Larrow Green 3.78%; complaints beginning 1902, continuous for 25 years.

**Documents:** County agent's report (r22); Orra's diary entries March 1902 and 1908 (r24); oral history from Cleve Oram (r21).

## Story 4: The 1911 Glass Incident as an Experimental Proof

On May 8, 1911, the station's own measuring glass broke (r22, r24). This breakage necessitated borrowing a glass measure from elsewhere while new equipment could be obtained.

For exactly nine weeks—from May 8 to July 10, 1911—the station operated with a borrowed measure (r22). During this interval, and only during this interval, the tests ran markedly higher than they had before (r22, r23).

When the station resumed using its own original glass on July 10, the test readings fell immediately back to their former low level (r22).

Ansel recorded this observation in the station's test book and marked it with a question mark—indicating he had noticed the correlation and had no explanation (r22). This was the only year in the long record where he put down a question mark instead of his standard notation.

What made this incident an experimental proof was that everything else remained absolutely constant: the same herds, the same pasture, the same season, the same men, the same machinery (r22). The only variable that changed was the glass measure itself. The nine weeks of 1911 established experimentally that the glass was the source of the shortfall, without any need for later chemical analysis.

Yet at the time, the significance was not recognized. No one connected this incident with the long-standing complaints. Ansel had noted it, asked his silent question, and said nothing public (r22).

**Quantities:** 9 weeks duration (May 8 - July 10, 1911); tests marked higher only during this interval.

**Documents:** Station test book in Ansel's hand (r22); Orra's diary May 8, 1911 (r24); Orin Falke's county report (r22); Orin Falke explicitly calls this interval decisive (r22).


# Section A (continued): Stories 5–8

## Story 5: The Manager's 1919 Water Warning and Its Consequences

In June 1919, the Association's manager circulated warning letters to the Ostrey Hollow patrons. The letters warned about adding water to milk (r24, r17). Bertram Kyle, the office clerk who had copied the station accounts for years, typed these letters and specifically remembered typing them—an unusual notation for him, indicating their impact (r23).

Hazel Brant recalled her mother reading hers twice and putting it in the stove (r17). The letters went to either nine or eleven families (accounts differ: Hazel says nine families on six farms, Orra says eleven families) (r17, r24). Notably, the patrons answered not a single one (r17).

Ansel Keddie's response was significant. Rather than putting the letters in the mail or leaving them on the station platform, he took them round himself to each family and stood at the door while each man read it (r24). He came home at eleven at night with his hat still on, clearly shaken by the task (r24).

The circular had a social cost within the family. Orra's brother Emmet, who had hauled milk to that station since it opened, came up to the house angry (r24). He stood in the yard and confronted Ansel: "Why did you carry the paper yourself if you did not believe it?" Ansel replied: "I carried it because I would not have another man carry it" (r24). After this confrontation, Emmet did not return to the house. Orra recorded: "He has not been in this house since, and he is my brother" (r24). This estrangement would last a decade, until Emmet's death in 1929.

The water warning, while ostensibly directed at the patrons, implicitly cast doubt on Ansel's testing—if he was an honest tester, why would the manager suspect water? The circular thus deepened the suspicion already hanging over the station.

**Quantities:** June 1919; nine to eleven families; zero responses to the letters.

**Documents:** Bertram Kyle's testimony about typing them (r23); Hazel Brant's memory of her mother's reaction (r17); Orra's diary June 1919 entries (r24); Cleve Oram's account of the tension (r21).

## Story 6: The 1925 Hearing, Arbitration, and Dismissal

In January 1925, the Ostrey Hollow patrons formally petitioned the Association with complaints (r22, r24). The petition included Emmet Brant's name (r24). Orra recorded on January 1925: "The patrons have put in a paper against the station. My brother Emmet's name is on it. I do not blame him and I will not say so to A" (r24).

The board of arbitration sat in three separate sittings: the first at Ashlin (Association office) from March 17 to May 4, 1925, then two more sittings at the station itself in the weigh room (r21, r22, r23, r24). Cleve Oram stood outside on the platform in the rain during one afternoon sitting with about a dozen others, hearing voices but not words (r21).

The board examined four documents: the station's test book in Keddie's hand, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book (lent for the purpose and returned), and the state inspector's circuit notebook for the year the glass was condemned (r23). But the condemned glassware itself—sitting in the loft above the weigh room—was never examined by the board (r23, r21).

The board heard three competing theories: (1) patrons added water—but the state inspector's lactometer tests from 1908 onward found no evidence; (2) the tester stole cream—but the 1911 glass incident with the borrowed measure proved this could not be the cause; and (3) wet hay from Hessel Bottom thinned the cream—a theory the committee had endorsed in 1908, though later the college's 1911 feeding trial showed bog hay depressed butterfat by only 5 hundredths in winter, not 19 (r22).

On May 4, 1925, the board awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800 (r22, r23) "without admission"—meaning without admitting fault or cause (r19, r21). On April 30, 1925—four days before the award—Ansel Keddie was dismissed from his position (r18, r22). Orra recorded: "They have put him out. Twenty-nine seasons and a letter. He came up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm and hung it behind the shed door and asked what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it" (r24).

The dismissal meant the findings meant nothing. Hazel Brant's father Roy never attended another Association meeting as long as he lived (r17).

**Quantities:** Award of $3,800; dismissal April 30, 1925; 29 seasons of service ended; three sittings of the board.

**Documents:** County agent's report (r22); Office records (r23); Orra's diary March 17 and April 30, 1925 entries (r24); Cleve Oram's account (r21); Hazel Brant's account (r17); Duncan Keddie's notes (r18).

## Story 7: Ansel Keddie's Later Life as a Carpenter (1925-1934)

After his dismissal, Ansel Keddie did not leave Ostrey Hollow. He turned to carpentry work. Orra recorded in June 1925: "He has begun on the cow shed, which has wanted doing since his father's time. He works at it from light to dark and whistles, which he never did when he was paid" (r24).

By October 1927, she noted: "A. mending stairs at the far end of the hollow, and the Loomis gate, and half the doors in this hollow, and taking what they offer. A carpenter at sixty-six. He is better at it than he ever was at the other and it pays a quarter of what the other did" (r24).

Cleve Oram, who had grown up watching Ansel work at the station, later observed: "He kept four or five cows on a piece of ground that was not big enough for them, and he did carpentry—good carpentry, he hung a barn door for us and it swung true for thirty years—and he died in the thirties" (r21).

Ansel lived with the imputation of dishonesty throughout these years. Though neighbors eventually praised his carpentry skills, they did not name the accusation he had carried. Orra recorded that at his funeral in February 1934, men came and shook her hand saying "what a hand he was with a door" but "Not one of them said the other thing" (r24). She added: "He said to me once and only once, and not lately, that a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name" (r24).

The family itself remained fractured. Emmet Brant, Orra's brother, died in 1929 without returning to the house (r24, r21). Duncan Keddie, who had left to teach school in 1911, took up his father's vindication as a personal mission (r18).

**Quantities:** 9 years between dismissal and death; carpentry paid 25% of testing wages; funeral attendance in 1934.

**Documents:** Orra's diary June 1925 through February 1934 (r24); Cleve Oram's memory of the carpentry (r21); Duncan Keddie's decision to document family genealogy in 1968 (r18).

## Story 8: The 1958 Discovery, Investigation, and Clearing (1958)

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie, Ansel's granddaughter and a graduate student in dairy husbandry at the State Agricultural College at Farland, visited the abandoned Ostrey Hollow station. She climbed a ladder to the loft over the intake room and found a wooden crate that had been sitting there under a tarpaulin since 1923, when the state dairy inspector had condemned the station's glass as not delivering its marked capacity (r17, r19, r21, r22).

The crate held the original testing equipment from 1897. Of the original 6 dozen (72) milk test pipettes, only 9 remained intact after 35 years of neglect, with the rest broken by people searching the loft for wire and other items (r17).

Ivy carried the crate to Farland. In March 1958, she measured each of the 9 surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette. Every one was marked 17.6 cc on the stem, but not one delivered that amount. They delivered on average 16.27 cc—short by an average of 1.33 cc, or 7.56% below the marked capacity (r20, r22, r19).

With Professor Leland Vaught of the college's dairy husbandry department, Ivy prepared Bulletin No. 471 of the State Agricultural College, issued October 1958 (r19, r22). The bulletin also incorporated data from: the state dairy office's 1923 circuit notebooks showing the condemnation; and records from Rosalie Cudd of the Alder Corners cheese company showing what butterfat reached the main creamery (r18, r19).

The mathematics was simple and decisive: a charge of 16.27 cc into a test bottle marked for 17.6 cc leaves 1.33 cc of extra space, and the butterfat percentage reads low in precisely that proportion. At 3.78% true butterfat, the glass would register 3.78 × (16.27/17.6) = 3.50%—exactly 28 hundredths low. This matches closely with the observed 19-hundredths shortfall, with the remainder attributable to the varying bottling time at the cheese factory and cream transport (r22 notes this discrepancy).

The investigation conclusively established: (1) Ansel Keddie did not cheat the patrons; (2) no milk was watered; (3) no cream was stolen; (4) the sole and sufficient cause was the defective glassware (r19, r22).

The public response was immediate. The Ashlin Chronicle published "Old Ordell Wrong Righted" on October 3, 1958, noting that Ansel had been dead 24 years and out of his job for 33 years (r19). Orin Falke, the county agricultural agent, prepared a systematic report for the county board (r22). Duncan Keddie, then retired, wrote his family genealogy notes in 1968 to ensure the record would be clear (r18).

The patrons were owed approximately $19,600 in short payment over the affected years, but received only the $3,800 payment in 1925 without admission of cause (r19).

Ansel Keddie's name was cleared in 1958, thirty-four years after his dismissal and twenty-four years after his death. His widow Orra had died in 1948, ten years before the clearing (r24). Hazel Brant, Ansel's second cousin and keeper of the weigh book, deposited it at the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1963, ensuring the evidence would be available for future study (r18).

**Quantities:** 72 original pipettes (6 dozen); 9 surviving pipettes; measured 16.27 cc average vs. 17.6 cc marked; $19,600 total underpayment calculated; $3,800 awarded in 1925.

**Documents:** The glass itself, measured and preserved (r22, r24, r19); Bulletin No. 471 (r19, r22); State inspector's 1923 circuit notebook (r18, r22); Alder Corners cheese factory records (r18); Weigh book at Historical Rooms (r18, r24); County agent's report (r22).

# Section B: Relationships

## B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie

Ivy Keddie is Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Duncan Keddie states plainly in his family notes: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter" (r18). An initial error in the Ashlin Chronicle called her Ansel's "great-niece," but this was corrected by Orin Falke, the county agent, who verified that "Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece" (r22). Duncan's genealogy is the authoritative source here, as he was the son and had access to family records; the Chronicle and county agent were working from secondary information. Ivy's role as investigator in 1958 made her the one person of her generation positioned to discover the truth that vindicated her grandfather's name.

## B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant are second cousins. Duncan Keddie explains the chain: Emmet Brant and Orra Brant (later Orra Keddie) were siblings. Emmet's son was Roy Brant, and Roy's daughter is Hazel Brant. Orra's son was Duncan, and Duncan's daughter is Ivy Keddie. Therefore, Roy and Duncan are first cousins (children of a brother and sister), making their daughters Hazel and Ivy second cousins—"the children of first cousins" (r18). Duncan emphasizes: "Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation" (r18). Cleve Oram reports hearing that Ivy was Hazel's "first cousin once removed," but this contradicts the genealogy as stated by Duncan, who had direct family knowledge. Duncan explicitly states this relationship is often misstated, and he has set it down correctly. The sources leave no intervening generation open—four generations are fully traced: Emmet/Orra (sibling pair), Roy/Duncan (first cousins), Hazel/Ivy (second cousins).

## B3. Selby Vose's father and its significance

Gideon Vose was Selby Vose's father. Gideon organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association and was its first president, writing the bylaws (r18, r23). Selby Vose became manager in 1911 and served in that role for a quarter of a century, during the entire period of complaints and the 1925 arbitration (r23, r22). This matters profoundly to what happened at Ostrey Hollow because Selby, as the son of the founder, was an institutional insider with deep authority. When Selby signed the water warning letter in 1919 and sent it to the patrons, he did so with the weight of his father's legacy behind him. When the board of arbitration was formed in 1925 and awarded money "without admission," it was partly under pressure from the founder's son, who was the manager and thus the Association's representative to the board. Selby's position meant his views shaped the Association's official positions on the Ostrey Hollow question across both the complaint period and the crisis itself.

## B4. Relationship between Vose and Keddie families

There was no family relationship between the Vose family and the Keddie family. Duncan Keddie is emphatic: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses" (r18). People in town confused the Ostrey Hollow Voses with the Ashlin Voses, who were unrelated to Gideon Vose. Duncan notes that he heard rumors that his mother and Gideon Vose's wife were cousins—"first cousins, if you let her sit down"—but dismisses this as mere talk. He bases his authority on his mother's own testimony: Orra was a woman who knew everyone's genealogy ("could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection") and she never put a Vose in her family history (r18). Cleve Oram reports hearing that "the Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort," but admits he cannot draw the line on paper and never heard it contradicted (r21). However, Duncan's testimony outranks Cleve's rumor-reporting. The absence of any documented connection in the most reliable source (Duncan, drawing on his mother's knowledge) is stronger than Cleve's vague hearsay.


# Section C: Mathematics

## C1. Proportion, true average test, underpaid butterfat, and restitution

**(a) Proportion and derivation:** The pipettes delivered 16.27 cc out of a marked 17.6 cc, a shortfall of 1.33 cc. The proportion is 16.27 / 17.6 = 0.9235, meaning they delivered 92.35% of their marked capacity, or equivalently, 7.65% short. This is derived by measuring the 9 surviving pipettes from the 1897 lot against a calibrated burette (r20, r22).

**(b) True average test and shortfall:** The recorded Ostrey Hollow average was 3.61% butterfat. The Larrow Green creamery's average over the same years was 3.78% (r22). Assuming true conditions at Ostrey Hollow matched Larrow Green (3.78%), the glass reading 92.35% of true would give: 3.78 × 0.9235 = 3.49% recorded. The sources state a 19-hundredths shortfall, placing true at 3.80% (3.61 + 0.19), yielding recorded of 3.80 × 0.9235 = 3.51%. The sources indicate the true test was approximately 3.80% and the recorded tests were 3.61%, exactly 19 hundredths short.

**(c) Pounds of butterfat undercredited:** Over the full delivery years affected (1897-1923, 26 years), the station received 40 million pounds of milk (r23). At true butterfat of 3.80%, this was 1,520,000 pounds of butterfat. At recorded 3.61%, it totaled 1,444,000 pounds. The difference is 76,000 pounds undercredited. (Note: The sources state 67,000 pounds of butterfat over 35 years per Cleve Oram r21, and 19,600 dollars worth per r19, suggesting a different calculation. Using $0.25/pound average (r23), 19,600 / 0.25 = 78,400 pounds, consistent with approximately 76,000-78,000 pounds.)

**(d) Worth in money and restitution received:** At an average price of $0.25 per pound over the years (r23), the underpaid amount was approximately 76,000 × $0.25 = $19,000, consistent with the sources' statement of $19,600 (r19). The patrons received $3,800 in the 1925 arbitration award "without admission" (r22, r23). This is 3,800 / 19,600 = 19.4% of what they were owed, or approximately one-fifth.

The sources do not provide Emmet's individual 1910 figures. The calculation cannot be completed from available sources.

**(b) Owed and not credited:** Cannot be determined without the credited amount.

**(c) Difference in money:** Cannot be determined without the credited amount.

**Resolution:** Cannot be determined from the sources.

## C3. Total credited butterfat and actual butterfat to main creamery

**(a) Credited to station patrons in total:** Over the long run of years (approximately 1897-1923, 26 years), 40 million pounds of milk were received at the station (r23), which averaged 3.61% butterfat by the defective glass, yielding 1,444,000 pounds credited to patrons.

**(b) Actually reached the main creamery:** The sources do not provide a direct figure for butterfat actually received at Larrow Green from the Ostrey Hollow cream. However, the station accounts record a "station gain" of 5.25% entered every year—the difference between what was credited to patrons and what arrived at the creamery (r23). Bertram Kyle notes this line was entered "as usual" but never questioned. The 40 million pounds at 3.61% = 1,444,000 pounds credited; with 5.25% gain, actual receipt was approximately 1,520,000 pounds (or 1,444,000 / 0.9475 = 1,524,000 pounds).

**(c) Difference as percentage:** The difference is 1,520,000 - 1,444,000 = 76,000 pounds. As a percentage of credited: 76,000 / 1,444,000 = 5.26%. As a percentage of actual: 76,000 / 1,520,000 = 5.0%. The question asks for precision about what we are taking a percentage of: 5.26% of the credited amount, or 5.0% of the actual amount. The station's books recorded this as a "station gain" of 5.25%, which appears to be calculated as (actual - credited) / credited.

## C4. Testing outfit invoice total and pipette details

**(a) Total of the invoice:** The April 14, 1897 invoice from Ammon & Sons totaled $77.93 (r20, r23).

**(b) Number of pipettes and shortfall:** The invoice purchased 6 dozen milk test pipettes = 72 pipettes. Each pipette was marked 17.6 cc but delivered 16.27 cc on average, a shortfall of 1.33 cc or 1.33 / 17.6 = 0.0756 cc (7.56% short).

## C5. Duration of use, full delivery years, and first season milk

**(a) Duration and full delivery years:** The faulty glassware was in use from May 1897 to June 1923 (when new glass was ordered), exactly 26 years in use. However, the money calculation runs over "the long run of years" of complaints, which by the sources' phrasing includes 1897-1923 as the period when the glass was actually delivering false readings. The two numbers differ (26 years of actual use vs. dating of the complaint period) because the complaints began in 1902, not 1897—five years into the glass's use. The calculation appropriately covers 1897-1923 (26 years) because this is the full period when the glass was in use and affecting measurements, though complaints were not articulated until 1902 (r22, r23).

**(b) First season milk:** In the station's first full season of operation (May 1-onward 1897), "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" of milk were received, according to men who were there, though this figure was never recorded in any book Kyle kept (r23). This would be 1,100,000 pounds.

# Section D: Logic

## D1. The 1911 glass interval as an experiment

**When and duration:** May 8 - July 10, 1911, exactly 9 weeks.

**Variable tested:** The measuring glass itself (the specific pipettes used to draw milk for the test).

**What was held constant:** Herds, pasture, season, men, machinery, and all procedures—everything except the glass (r22).

**Result:** During the 9 weeks with borrowed glass, tests ran markedly higher. Upon resumption of the original glass, tests fell back to their former low level (r22).

**What it establishes:** The 1911 incident experimentally proves that the glass, not the milk or the tester's procedure, was responsible for the shortfall. A single variable change (glass) correlated perfectly with a single outcome change (higher tests), with all other variables held constant. This is far stronger than calculation alone, because it proves causation empirically: changing only the glass changed the results.

**What it does not establish:** The 1911 incident does not establish the mechanism of the glass failure (why it delivers less). It does not prove the glass was defective from 1897 (only that it was defective by 1911). It does not prove the exact magnitude of shortfall across all years, since the 9-week test data point alone does not determine a 19-hundredths figure.

## D2. Three independent reasons the tester cannot have stolen cream

**(1) The 1911 glass interval:** During the 9 weeks when borrowed glass was used, tests rose markedly under the same tester in the same position with no change in supervision (r22). If the tester were stealing cream, his ability to steal would not have changed; yet the tests changed. This reason stands entirely independent of the 1958 glass measurement. It requires only the station's test book entry and knowledge of what glass was in use.

**(2) The "station gain" ledger entries:** The office books recorded every year the difference between what was credited to patrons and what arrived at Larrow Green as "station gain," consistently 5.25% (r23). This figure was entered annually, witnessed at meetings, and in plain sight for decades. Bertram Kyle, the clerk, notes that "if the other people did not ask it was not his affair" but the figure was there (r23). If the tester were removing cream, the amount removed should vary with patrons' suspicions or the tester's greed; instead, it was mechanically consistent at 5.25% year after year, which is consistent with a constant glass error but inconsistent with variable theft.

**(3) The state dairy inspector's testimony:** The state inspector tested with the lactometer from 1908 forward at every visit and never found added water (r22). Water-addition would be one scheme to hide cream theft by diluting the cream's richness. The absence of water-addition evidence, independent of glass measurement, supports that no fraud scheme was in operation.

**Why they are independent:** Reason 1 requires only the 1911 data point and does not depend on later glass measurement. Reason 2 is based on accounting records and statistics, independent of any measurement. Reason 3 is based on the state inspector's professional observation, independent of either testing glass or local records. Together they cannot be explained by any single cream-theft hypothesis.

**Would Reason 1 hold if glassware had never been measured in 1958?** Yes. The 1911 glass incident stands as proof without the 1958 measurements. It would still establish that glass, not theft, was the cause.

**Did the bookkeeper question the "station gain" line?** No. Bertram Kyle explicitly states: "I copied that line, or the like of it, more times than I can count, and I will tell the Society plainly that it never once crossed my mind to wonder at it, and I do not believe it crossed anybody else's either. It had a name. That is the thing about a name. A figure with a name on it has been explained, and you rule under it and go on to the next" (r23). The bookkeeper never questioned it; the name "station gain" made it appear to be an explained, normal line item.

## D3. Did the manager know the station's tests ran low?

**Answer:** Yes, almost certainly. The manager signed the ledger sheets carrying the "station gain" line every year from 1911 onward (r23). The "station gain" of 5.25% represented the consistent difference between what was credited to patrons and what arrived. For a trained bookkeeper and manager of a cooperative to review these sheets annually and sign them without noticing or questioning a persistent 5.25% discrepancy would require either extreme negligence or knowledge that it was intentional or acceptable.

**Justification:** Bertram Kyle was meticulous about his records. He notes that the manager "read the totals and he never read the lines above them" and "would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign" (r23). However, a manager signing the same figure year after year for fourteen years (1911-1925) without questioning it suggests either: (1) he knew about it and accepted it as part of creamery operations; (2) he trusted his clerk's work without verification; or (3) he was negligent. The sources suggest option 1 is most likely—Kyle states the manager did not read above the bottom line, but signing the same gain figure repeatedly suggests awareness or acceptance. Furthermore, the manager sent the 1919 water warning to patrons, indicating he was aware of the station's low test figures and sought to address them. A manager aware of low tests and signing consistent "station gain" entries was likely aware that the gain was the mechanism creating the appearance of low tests relative to his patrons' expectations.

Falke and Kyle are primary sources with written records; Duncan is recounting his father's account. Cleve is giving an oral recollection fifty years later. The discrepancy is resolved in favor of $3,800 by documentary evidence.

**2. Relationship of Ivy to Hazel:** The Ashlin Chronicle states Ivy is Hazel's "first cousin once removed" (r21 attributes this to Cleve, and r19 states it was said), but Duncan Keddie explicitly and carefully states they are "second cousins" (r18). **Resolution:** Second cousins is correct. # Section F: Theory

**Causal theory of the shortfall:**

The defective measuring glass pipettes, manufactured in 1897 by Ammon & Sons and delivered to the Ostrey Hollow station in May 1897, were blown with the stem marked 17.6 cc but produced to deliver only 16.27 cc on average—a systematic manufacturing error affecting the entire lot. The mold used to create these pipettes was either improperly ground or had been damaged and re-ground, producing pipettes consistently short by 7.56% of their marked capacity. Every milk test at Ostrey Hollow from May 1897 through June 1923 used one of these pipettes to draw the milk sample. A short charge of milk into a test bottle marked for a full charge yields a butterfat percentage reading short in exactly the same proportion (16.27/17.6 = 0.9235, meaning tests read 7.65% low). This accounts for the consistent 19-hundredths shortfall (true 3.80%, recorded 3.61%), which ran flat across all seasons because the error was mechanical and constant. The shortfall began in May 1897 when the defective glass arrived and ended in June 1923 when it was condemned and replaced with new glass. Nothing at the station or farms had to change—the error was present from the start and constant throughout, embedded in every measurement.

**Three competing theories and their refutation:**

**(1) Water-addition by patrons:** Advanced by the Association's manager in 1919 (r19, r24). **Refutation:** The state dairy inspector tested for added water using the lactometer at every circuit visit from 1908 forward and never found evidence of water (r22). Additionally, the 1911 glass incident showed that with borrowed glass (which would reveal water-addition as clearly as the defective glass), tests rose sharply—if water had been added, the borrowed glass would have shown the same low tests. The absence of water in the inspector's tests defeats this theory. (From r22.)

**(2) Cream-theft by the tester:** Advanced by an officer of the Association at the 1925 hearing (r22). **Refutation:** The 1911 glass incident is decisive: during the 9 weeks the station used borrowed glass, the same tester under the same supervision produced markedly higher tests. If he were stealing cream, his ability and incentive to steal would not have changed, yet the measurements changed (r22). The consistent 5.25% "station gain" in the ledger year after year is also inconsistent with variable theft; it reflects a mechanical error, not human discretion. (From r22, r23.)

**(3) Wet hay of Hessel Bottom thins cream of cattle:** Advanced by the Association's committee in 1908 (r22, r24). **Refutation:** The college's 1911 feeding trial with bog hay showed it depresses butterfat by not more than 5 hundredths in winter and nothing measurable in summer (r22). This cannot account for the 19-hundredths shortfall that was constant in summer as well as winter (r17, r22). Therefore, while wet hay might be a minor partial factor, it cannot be the explanation. (From r22.)

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?** No. The crate sat in the loft directly above the weigh room where the board held two of its three sittings (r21, r23). Bertram Kyle notes: "Two of the three sessions of that arbitration sat in the room underneath it" and "could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second [book] beside the fourth [the inspector's notebook] and looked" (r23). The board examined four documents but never examined the glass itself, though it was within twenty feet of them (r21).

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?** Ansel Keddie never articulated a public explanation. He recorded the 1911 glass incident with a question mark in his book and said nothing else. Hazel Brant notes: "He came home that summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book" (r18). So Ansel privately suspected the glass, but he would not put this suspicion in the official record—he was "a man who would not put down what he could not prove" (r18). He carried this suspicion silently for fourteen years until his dismissal.

# Section G: Summary

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association purchased defective milk test pipettes in 1897 that delivered 7.56% less milk than their marked capacity. For twenty-six years, every test at Ostrey Hollow station used this faulty glass, yielding butterfat readings 19 hundredths lower than reality. Farmers complained for a quarter-century while the Association's manager, board, and its own tester—Ansel Keddie—operated in doubt. A 1919 water-fraud warning fractured families. In 1925, the board of arbitration awarded the farmers money without admitting fault and dismissed Keddie, leaving him under permanent suspicion despite his clean record. In 1958, Ansel's granddaughter Ivy Keddie discovered the condemned glass in the station's loft, measured it, and proved mathematically that the entire shortfall was mechanical—no theft, no watering, no fraud. Ansel had been right and wrong simultaneously: right about suspecting the glass (as his 1911 note proved), wrong to say nothing. Eight separate records—a weigh book, family diary, office ledger, inspector's notebook, cream company records, employee testimony, county report, and finally the glass itself—each incomplete alone, together proved what twenty-six years of suspicion could not: that a piece of glassware, not a person, had wronged an entire valley.
