# Section A: Reconstruction of the Eight Stories (Stories 1–4)

## Story 1: The Founding of the Larrow Valley Cooperative and Ostrey Hollow Station (1889-1897)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was established in 1889 by farmers meeting at Larrow Green, with the main creamery built there in 1891 (r22). The Association adopted Article Seven in its bylaws, establishing payment based on butterfat percentage by test, not milk weight (r22).

In winter 1895-96, to serve the farms of Hessel Bottom and the upper valley, the board built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles upstream (r22, r21). The station opened May 4, 1896, as a small board building with intake room, separator, and loft (r21, r22). Its function was to weigh milk, test it for butterfat, separate cream for shipment to Larrow Green, and return skim to farmers (r21).

The station initially borrowed measuring glass from Larrow Green (r24). In April 1897, the Association ordered its own complete testing outfit from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet. The invoice for April 14, 1897 totaled $77.93 and included 6 dozen (72) milk test pipettes marked 17.6 cubic centimeters, 12 dozen test bottles, 1 acid measure, 1 hand tester, and freight (r20, r23). The pipettes, marked to deliver exactly 17.6 cc of milk for testing—the standard charge for a butter fat test—arrived in May 1897 (r20, r24).

Cream hauler Roy Brant, whose father Emmet farmed the largest place on Hessel Bottom, established the daily route from Ostrey Hollow down to Larrow Green, a journey his son Cleve would later ride (r21). The station began maintaining detailed records from its opening year onward.

**Quantities:** $77.93 for equipment; 6 dozen original pipettes; opening date May 4, 1896.

**Documents:** Invoice from Ammon & Sons (r20); station account records (r23); Orra Keddie's diary (r24); Cleve Oram's account (r21).

## Story 2: Ansel Keddie's Hiring and Early Years at the Station (1897-1902)

Ansel Keddie, born 1861, was engaged as tester and separator man at Ostrey Hollow from the station's opening and held the position for twenty-nine seasons (r18, r22). He was described as careful in small things to a degree that others found trying, and this carefulness was the making of the record he left (r18).

Ansel married Orra Brant in 1889 (r18). Orra was the younger sister of Emmet Brant, who farmed the largest piece of Hessel Bottom and hauled the most milk of any patron (r18). This family relationship put Ansel in a delicate position: he was required by his position to record the butterfat tests of milk delivered by his wife's brother. The situation was the hinge of many later tensions.

Orra and Ansel had a son Duncan, born 1894 (r18). Beginning in spring 1897, Orra kept a diary in a ruled notebook where she recorded daily life at Ostrey Hollow (r24). In early May 1897, she noted that Ansel was pleased about receiving the station's own glass and being done borrowing from Larrow Green (r24).

Ansel kept detailed records of every test in the station's own hand from the beginning, entering both the station's own test figures and the receiving weight from Larrow Green side by side (r17). He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove, and he tested the milk in front of the state's own inspector and followed the methods he was taught (r18, r19).

Between 1897 and 1902, the station received its first year's milk (approximately 1,100,000 pounds according to men who were there, though this was not recorded in any book kept by Kyle) and settled into routine (r23).

**Quantities:** 29 seasons of service (1896-1925); son Duncan born 1894; husband of Orra Brant.

**Documents:** Duncan Keddie's family notes (r18); Orra's diary (r24); Bertram Kyle's office records (r23).

## Story 3: The Long Complaint Period and Investigation (1902-1911)

From very nearly the day the station opened, but certainly by 1902, the milk of Ostrey Hollow tested consistently lower than milk from elsewhere in the county—lower than Larrow Green creamery's own milk, lower than neighboring valleys, and this deficit remained constant across seasons (r19, r22).

The quantified shortfall was nineteen hundredths of a point: Ostrey Hollow averaged 3.61% butterfat while Larrow Green averaged 3.78% (r22). This difference held "flat as a floor" in every month of the year, in the June flush and the February depth alike, never varying with season or feed (r17, r22).

The Ostrey Hollow patrons believed they were being cheated and complained at every annual meeting for a quarter of a century (r19). The Association's officers suspected the patrons were adding water or otherwise adulterating milk (r19).

Three competing theories emerged: (1) water was being added to the milk; (2) the tester was stealing cream and falsifying records; or (3) the wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream of cattle fed upon it (r22).

In 1908, the Association formed a committee to investigate the herds (r24). The committee visited Emmet Brant's farm, which was the largest producer, and reported back that the wet hay of the bottom was the cause (r22, r24). Orra noted the committee's visit and conclusion: "They were civil enough to me. They wanted to see the barns and not the station" (r24). In March 1908, she recorded their finding: "Their word is that it is the wet hay of the bottom that thins the cream" (r24).

However, the state dairy inspector, conducting circuit visits from 1908 forward, tested with the lactometer and never found added water (r22).

Yet nothing changed, and the complaint continued. The suspicion was embedded in the community: Cleve Oram's father, Roy Brant, believed his cousin Ansel was somehow removing cream from the cans, though he never said so under oath (r21).

**Quantities:** 19-hundredths shortfall; Ostrey Hollow 3.61%, Larrow Green 3.78%; complaints beginning 1902, continuous for 25 years.

**Documents:** County agent's report (r22); Orra's diary entries March 1902 and 1908 (r24); oral history from Cleve Oram (r21).

## Story 4: The 1911 Glass Incident as an Experimental Proof

On May 8, 1911, the station's own measuring glass broke (r22, r24). This breakage necessitated borrowing a glass measure from elsewhere while new equipment could be obtained.

For exactly nine weeks—from May 8 to July 10, 1911—the station operated with a borrowed measure (r22). During this interval, and only during this interval, the tests ran markedly higher than they had before (r22, r23).

When the station resumed using its own original glass on July 10, the test readings fell immediately back to their former low level (r22).

Ansel recorded this observation in the station's test book and marked it with a question mark—indicating he had noticed the correlation and had no explanation (r22). This was the only year in the long record where he put down a question mark instead of his standard notation.

What made this incident an experimental proof was that everything else remained absolutely constant: the same herds, the same pasture, the same season, the same men, the same machinery (r22). The only variable that changed was the glass measure itself. The nine weeks of 1911 established experimentally that the glass was the source of the shortfall, without any need for later chemical analysis.

Yet at the time, the significance was not recognized. No one connected this incident with the long-standing complaints. Ansel had noted it, asked his silent question, and said nothing public (r22).

**Quantities:** 9 weeks duration (May 8 - July 10, 1911); tests marked higher only during this interval.

**Documents:** Station test book in Ansel's hand (r22); Orra's diary May 8, 1911 (r24); Orin Falke's county report (r22); Orin Falke explicitly calls this interval decisive (r22).
