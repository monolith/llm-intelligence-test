## Story 5: The Manager's Response and the Inspector's Verdict (1919–1923)

By 1919, suspicion had grown into anxiety. The Association's manager, Selby Vose (son of founder Gideon Vose, confirmed as son not nephew by Bertram Kyle), issued a warning. In June 1919, he sent a circular to the Ostrey Hollow patrons cautioning them about adding water to milk. The circular was civil in form but unmistakable in implication.

Ansel Keddie took the matter upon himself. Rather than leave the circular for another man to deliver or post on the platform, he carried it round personally to eleven families, standing at each door while they read it (Orra Keddie's diary). This act of personal assumption of responsibility created an irreparable rupture with his brother-in-law Emmet Brant. Emmet, the largest Hessel Bottom farmer and the man most damaged by the low tests, came to Ansel's yard and asked why he had carried the paper if he did not believe the water accusation. Ansel replied he had carried it because he would not have another man carry it—implying he accepted responsibility for investigating, but not for the accusation itself (Orra Keddie). Emmet never entered the house again. The brothers-in-law would not reconcile for ten years, until Emmet's death in 1929 (Orra Keddie).

In summer 1923, Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector for the circuit, arrived at the station on routine inspection. He was a man with no connection to anyone in the valley—not to the Brants, not to the Voses, not to any party in the dispute (Duncan Keddie's note that Strawn was independent). Strawn tested the station's milk pipettes against a calibrated burette and found that they would not deliver their marked capacity (Orin Falke, Bertram Kyle, Junia Ammon). He condemned the whole lot on the spot and ordered new glass (Bertram Kyle).

The Association wrote to Ammon & Sons on June 28, 1923. The company replied July 2: their books of the nineties were not such that they could tell the Association anything about that particular lot. Junia Ammon, writing in 1949, reflects that this answer was "a thin thing to send to a customer of twenty-six years' standing." What she believes happened: the mold had been reground; no man caught the error; a gauge knocked off a nail; a lot run short and consistently wrong (Junia Ammon). New glass arrived and went into use. From 1924 onward, the station's average test rose to 3.85 (Orin Falke).

**Key figures:** Selby Vose (manager), Ansel Keddie, Emmet Brant, Merle Strawn (state inspector, independent), Ammon & Sons.
**Quantities:** Station average 3.61 before; 3.85 after new glass (1924 onward).
**Documents bearing on it:** Orra Keddie (diary, June 1919 entries); Bertram Kyle (office records of glass order); Junia Ammon (company letter June 2, 1923); Orin Falke (statistics).

## Story 6: The Arbitration, Accusation, and Dismissal (1925)

In January 1925, the Ostrey Hollow patrons filed a petition seeking redress. A board of arbitration was constituted, chaired by an attorney at Ashlin (Alonzo Frick). The board heard testimony from March 17 through May 4, 1925 (Orin Falke). The first sitting was at Ashlin in the Association's office; two subsequent sittings were held at the station itself in the weigh room, so the board could examine the books and scales directly (Cleve Oram, Orra Keddie).

Three theories were advanced to explain the persistent low tests. First, the manager's theory: the Ostrey Hollow patrons had added water to their milk. The state inspector Merle Strawn had tested with the lactometer at every visit from 1908 forward and never once found added water (Orin Falke). This theory could not be sustained. Second, the Association officer's theory: Ansel Keddie himself had removed cream and written low tests to conceal the theft. The nine weeks of 1911 disposed of this entirely: the same man made the tests under no different supervision, and the figures rose when the glass changed (Orin Falke). This theory could not be sustained either. Third, the practical farmers' theory: the bog hay of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream. This had real plausibility and was hard to dismiss (Orin Falke), though later evidence would prove it insufficient.

On April 30, 1925—four days before the board announced its finding—Ansel Keddie was dismissed. He came up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm, hung it behind the shed door, asked what there was for supper (Orra Keddie). The board's award, announced May 4, was $3,800 to the Ostrey Hollow patrons "without admission"—lawyer's language for "we are paying you and saying nothing about why" (Orin Falke). The award was charged to the station account (Bertram Kyle).

Ansel had been tester and separator man for twenty-nine seasons (Duncan Keddie). He had tested milk in front of the state's own inspector (Ivy Keddie in the 1958 Bulletin). He had tested the way he was taught. And he had been dismissed with an imputation that would follow him to his grave.

**Key figures:** Alonzo Frick (arbitration chairman), Ansel Keddie (accused and dismissed), Selby Vose (manager), Merle Strawn (inspector, whose notebooks were evidence), Emmet Brant (petitioner).
**Quantities:** $3,800 awarded; 29 seasons of service; April 30 dismissal, May 4 award.
**Documents bearing on it:** Orin Falke (arbitration account); Orra Keddie (diary); Cleve Oram (witnessed proceedings); Bertram Kyle (ledger charge).

## Story 7: The Carpenter Years and Death Without Vindication (1925–1934)

After dismissal, Ansel Keddie worked as a carpenter. He was good at it—a door he hung for Cleve Oram's farm swung true for thirty years (Cleve Oram). But carpentry paid a quarter of what testing had paid (Orra Keddie, 1927 diary entry).

In the summers of the 1920s he built and repaired—the cow shed at his own place (which had wanted work since his father's time), the Loomis gate, half the doors in the Hollow (Orra Keddie). He took what was offered. He worked from light to dark and whistled, which he had never done when he was paid (Orra Keddie, 1925 diary). He was, in some way, free of the burden of representing the station's figures, though he carried the burden of the accusation.

His wife Orra, his brother-in-law Emmet (reconciled at last in 1929 through Roy, Emmet's son), his son Duncan (who taught at Ashlin), and his granddaughter Ivy (born 1931, still a child when Ansel was working as a carpenter) all knew his character. Duncan eventually became a teacher and, like his father, valued careful record-keeping and truth. He married and had Ivy. But Ansel died without knowing he was right.

On February 1, 1934, Ansel Keddie died at home "without much pain at the end of it" (Orra Keddie, written in 1934). Duncan came up in the night. There were more at the burying than expected. Men who had not spoken to him in nine years shook Orra's hand. Every one of them said what a hand he was with a door. Not one of them said the other thing—that he had been wronged, that the glass was short, that his tests had been true (Orra Keddie, 1934).

"He never asked them to," Orra wrote. He said to her once, and only once, "that a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name" (Orra Keddie, 1934).

He did not live to 1958, when his name was cleared.

**Key figures:** Ansel Keddie (born 1861, died 1934), Orra Keddie (wife), Duncan Keddie (son), Ivy Keddie (granddaughter), neighbors.
**Quantities:** 29 seasons as tester; $3,800 awarded to patrons, but Ansel received nothing.
**Documents bearing on it:** Orra Keddie (diary throughout 1925–1934); Cleve Oram (carpenter work memory); Ashlin Chronicle (career summary).

## Story 8: The Discovery and Scientific Vindication (1958–1959)

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie—twenty-seven years old, granddaughter of Ansel, graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland—went up a ladder into the loft over the Ostrey Hollow station's intake room. There, under a tarpaulin and about an inch of chaff, sat a wooden crate. It had been there since 1923, when the state inspector Merle Strawn condemned the glassware. It contained the station's original testing equipment (Hazel Brant, Cleve Oram).

The crate held the pipettes. Of the original six dozen, eleven remained intact. Ivy carried the crate east to Farland and, in March 1958, measured each pipette against a calibrated burette (Ivy Keddie, 1958). Every one was marked 17.6 cc. Not one delivered it. The average of the lot came to 16.27 cc—short by 1.33 cc, or about 7.5 percent (Ashlin Chronicle, Orin Falke).

That was the whole of the mystery. A short measure of milk goes into a bottle whose neck is ruled for a full one. The fat reads low in the same proportion, every single time, for as long as you use that glass. Nobody watered anything. Nobody stole anything. The scales were true, the acid was right, the cows were ordinary. A piece of glass out of a crate was wrong (Ashlin Chronicle).

Ivy took her findings to Professor Leland Vaught of the College's Department of Dairy Husbandry. Together they prepared Bulletin No. 471, published October 1958. Vaught and Keddie calculated what the farmers had lost: sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat over the full delivery years (Cleve Oram), worth $19,600 (Ashlin Chronicle, at roughly $0.25 to $0.30 per pound).

The bulletin proved the pipettes were short in the same proportion the tests were short. A short milk sample yields a short fat reading, not because the milk is bad or because anyone is stealing, but because the volume of milk tested is less than it should be. The tester's innocence was established. Ansel Keddie's name was cleared, though he had been dead for twenty-four years and never knew it (Orra Keddie, 1934 entry with 1958 editor's note).

In 1959, County Agent Orin Falke filed an official report with the Ordell County board of supervisors. He documented the history, the three failed theories, the 1911 glass interval, and Ivy's discovery. Initially he had conceded that wet hay might be a partial factor. But receipt of the State Agricultural College's Bulletin No. 214 (the older feeding trial on bog hay from 1911) caused him to withdraw that concession: the bog hay depressed butterfat by no more than 0.05 points in winter, nothing in summer. It could not account for a 0.19–0.21 point gap (Orin Falke, addendum note).

The records—the station test book, the Brant weigh book, the state inspector's notebooks, the Association's ledgers—were deposited with the Ordell County Historical Rooms at Ashlin.

**Key figures:** Ivy Keddie (discoverer, age 27), Professor Leland Vaught (scientific analysis), Ansel Keddie (vindicated), Orin Falke (official record), Merle Strawn (state inspector who condemned glass in 1923, unknowingly preserving the evidence).
**Quantities:** 11 intact pipettes measured; 16.27 cc delivery vs. 17.6 cc marked (1.33 cc short); 67,000 pounds butterfat unpaid; $19,600 lost; Bulletin 214 showed bog hay depressed butterfat by 0.05 max, not 0.19–0.21.
**Documents bearing on it:** Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958, Vaught & Keddie); Ashlin Chronicle (October 1958 news report); Orin Falke (county agent report, 1959, with addendum); Cleve Oram (witnessed the crate in loft); Hazel Brant (details of crate discovery); Junia Ammon (1897 purchase details for context).

# Section B: Relationships

## B1: What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Ansel (born 1861, died 1934) married Orra Brant in 1889. Their son was Duncan Keddie (born 1894). Duncan married and had a daughter, Ivy Keddie (born 1931). This makes Ivy the granddaughter of Ansel (Duncan Keddie). The Ashlin Chronicle initially misstated her as Ansel's "great-niece," but Orin Falke corrected this in his addendum note to the 1959 county report: she is the granddaughter, not great-niece. This relationship explains why Ivy had family motivation to investigate the defect and why she had access to the family archives; it also explains why she was in a position to find the condemned glassware—she would have been familiar with the station and its history through family connections.

## B2: What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant? State what is left open about intervening generations.

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. Ivy's father Duncan Keddie and Hazel's father Roy Brant were first cousins: Duncan's mother was Orra Brant (sister of Emmet Brant), and Roy's father was Emmet Brant. Thus Ivy and Hazel are the children of first cousins, making them second cousins. Duncan Keddie carefully documented this relationship in 1968 (his "Notes for the family"), because it had been repeatedly misstated as "cousins once removed" or "aunt and niece." Hazel kept the family's weigh book from her father Roy until she donated it to the Historical Rooms in 1963. What is left open: whether Ivy and Hazel collaborated in investigating the scandal, or whether Ivy's discovery was independent. The sources do not state whether they worked together or merely communicated findings afterward.

## B3: Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter to what happened at Ostrey Hollow?

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose, who founded (or organized) the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association and served as its first president (Bertram Kyle confirms he was "the son, whatever is said at Ashlin"). Selby became manager in 1911 (Duncan Keddie). This matters because Selby, as manager, signed off on the station's accounts and sent the 1919 water-warning circular to the Ostrey Hollow patrons—the circular that Ansel Keddie delivered himself and that ruptured his relationship with Emmet Brant. Selby was thus positioned to control the Association's response to the complaint. As someone inheriting authority from his father's founding role, Selby had institutional credibility but may have been reluctant to admit that the Association's own purchase of defective equipment was the problem. His role was to manage—to handle the complaint, pay off the board, dismiss the tester, and move on. He never examined the condemned glassware that was sitting in the loft directly above the weigh room where the 1925 arbitration hearings were held.

## B4: What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?

There was no family relationship between the Voses and the Keddies. Duncan Keddie explicitly states: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses" (Duncan Keddie, "Notes for the family"). He had heard rumors twice that his mother Orra (born Brant) was a cousin to Gideon Vose's wife, once as first cousins, but dismissed these as groundless gossip. His mother, a woman who knew all the valley genealogies and could not pass a headstone without giving the connection, never once mentioned a Vose relationship. Cleve Oram, the cream hauler's son, had heard the Keddies and Voses were cousins "of some sort" but could not draw the line on paper. Duncan's explicit documentation that there was no tie is more reliable than rumor. This matters because it means the conflict at Ostrey Hollow was not a family dispute but a structural one—a tester working for a cooperative whose equipment was defective and whose management was under pressure to find an explanation. The absence of family connection meant there was no family mitigation or advocacy that might have protected Ansel Keddie during the 1925 crisis.
