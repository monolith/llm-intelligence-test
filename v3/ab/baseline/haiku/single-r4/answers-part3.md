# Section C: Math

## C1: The Measurement Error and Its Consequences

**(a) Proportion of error in the glassware:**

The eleven surviving pipettes were marked 17.6 cc but delivered an average of 16.27 cc (Ashlin Chronicle, Ivy Keddie's measurement).

Shortfall per pipette: 17.6 − 16.27 = 1.33 cc
Proportion short: 1.33 / 17.6 = 0.0756 = **7.56%** or approximately 1 in 13

When a milk sample is 7.56% shorter than it should be, the butterfat test reads low by the same proportion. A milk sample containing true 3.80% butterfat, when only 92.44% of the volume is measured, appears to contain 3.80% × 0.9244 = 3.51% butterfat instead.

**(b) True average test and shortfall:**

Ostrey Hollow reported average test over the complaint years: 3.61 points (Orin Falke)
Valley average (Larrow Green creamery): 3.78 points (Orin Falke)

If the Ostrey Hollow average of 3.61 resulted from measuring only 92.44% of the milk, the true average was:
True average = 3.61 / 0.9244 = **3.905 ≈ 3.91 points**

This represents a measured shortfall of: 3.91 − 3.61 = **0.30 points**

However, the observed gap between Ostrey Hollow and the valley was 0.19–0.21 points. The discrepancy (0.30 − 0.21 = 0.09 points) reflects the partially confounding effect of wet hay, which Bulletin 214 established depressed butterfat by approximately 0.05 points in winter, nothing in summer (Orin Falke's addendum; Bulletin 214 as revised). The coincidental combination of both measurement error and real hay quality made it plausible that the complaint could be dismissed as a land issue.

**(c) Total butterfat not credited over the full complaint years:**

Period: 1902–1923 (21 full delivery seasons, before the glassware was condemned and replaced)
Total milk received at Ostrey Hollow station: 40 million pounds (Bertram Kyle)

Using the observed gap approach (conservative):
- Gap: 0.19–0.21 points between Ostrey Hollow and valley
- 40 million pounds × 0.20 (average gap) / 100 = **80,000 pounds of butterfat not credited**

Using the calculated correction approach (maximum):
- True butterfat: 40 million × 3.91 / 100 = 1,564,000 pounds
- Credited butterfat: 40 million × 3.61 / 100 = 1,444,000 pounds
- Not credited: **120,000 pounds**

Sources cite Cleve Oram's recollection of "sixty-seven thousand pounds" and the Ashlin Chronicle's calculation of the loss. Reconciling these: **67,000 pounds** represents the portion attributable to the defective glass alone (not including any overlapping wet hay effect).

**(d) Money value and what patrons received:**

At an average butterfat price of $0.25 per pound (Orin Falke notes "Fat has been worth twenty-seven cents the pound through these years"):
- Total loss: 67,000 pounds × $0.25 = **$16,750** (conservative, 67,000 lbs)
- Or using Ashlin Chronicle's $19,600 figure → $19,600 / $0.25 = 78,400 pounds butterfat
- Average price implied: $19,600 / 67,000 = **$0.293 per pound**

**What patrons received:**
- 1925 arbitration award: $3,800 "without admission" (Orin Falke)
- Fraction of total loss: $3,800 / $19,600 = **19.4%** or approximately 1/5 of actual loss
- This represents recovery of about **0.05 pound value per 67,000 pounds**, or less than $0.01 per pound of lost butterfat

# Section D: Logic

## D1: The 1911 Glass Incident as an Experiment

**When:** May 8 through July 10, 1911 (nine weeks)
**How long:** Exactly 9 weeks

**The variable:** Measuring glass (or "measure" — the pipette or measuring vessel used to draw the milk sample)

**Held constant:** 
- Ansel Keddie (same tester)
- Same herds (Ostrey Hollow farmers)
- Same procedure (testing method)
- Same machinery (separator, acid, hand tester)
- Same season (late spring through early summer, June and July, flush season)
- Same weather conditions
- Same pasture (stock up on aftermath off the wet ground)
- Same labor and care

**Result:** During the nine weeks with borrowed measure, the station's tests "stood markedly higher" (Orin Falke). When returned to the original measure, tests "fell back to their former level" (Orin Falke). The difference was material—large enough to notice and record. Ansel noted the fact with a question mark in the station book.

**What this establishes:**
1. The tester was not cheating: if cream were being stolen, the borrowing of a different measure would not cause tests to rise—they would remain low or fall further.
2. The herds were not bad: the same cows tested higher with different equipment.
3. The procedure was sound: the way of testing was correct.
4. The problem was in the glass: something about the original measuring vessel delivered an incorrect volume, causing systematically lower tests.

**What it does NOT establish:**
1. The exact nature of the glass defect (short delivery vs. marking error vs. material contraction, etc.)
2. Whether the defect was intentional or accidental
3. Whether anyone else at the station or in the Association knew about or suspected the measure
4. Whether the Association could have investigated and found the cause in 1911 (they apparently did not)
5. The proportion of the gap attributable to the glass vs. other causes (wet hay, testing variation, etc.)

The interval proves the tester's innocence more conclusively than any calculation would have. A calculation alone shows averages over years; the experiment shows causation by isolating a single variable and observing immediate response.

## D2: Why the Tester Cannot Have Been Stealing Cream

**Three independent reasons from different narrators:**

**Reason 1 (The 1911 glass experiment—Orin Falke):**
If Ansel Keddie had been secretly removing cream and falsifying records, the borrowed measure would not have changed the results. The same cream amount would still be missing. Yet the tests rose materially when the measure changed, then fell back when the original returned. This proves the variation was in the measure, not the cream or the record-keeping. This reason stands independently of whether the glass was ever measured in 1958; it stands on the observed behavior in 1911 alone.

**Reason 2 (The state inspector's independent verification—Orin Falke, citing Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks):**
The state dairy inspector Merle Strawn, who had no stake in the Association's internal disputes and was independent of all parties (Duncan Keddie emphasizes Strawn was "kin to nobody in the valley whatever"), tested the milk at every visit from 1908 onward using his own instruments (the lactometer). He never once found evidence of added water or tampering at that station. If cream were being stolen and milk falsified, the lactometer would have detected the tampering (diluted or altered milk has detectably different properties). Strawn's independent testing exonerates the tester based on a different method entirely.

**Reason 3 (The arithmetic of the ledger and the farmer-tester relationship—Bertram Kyle, Duncan Keddie):**
Ansel Keddie was married to Orra Brant, sister of Emmet Brant, the largest and most-affected Hollow farmer. Emmet was Ansel's wife's brother and came to the table every Sunday for decades. If Ansel had been stealing cream, he would have been stealing from his own wife's family—the family that most suffered from low tests and most suspected foul play. The family tension visible in 1919 (when Emmet severed relations over the water-warning circular) and again in 1925 (when Emmet signed the petition against the station) would have been even more acute if Ansel had been theft. Yet there is no accusation of theft between them until the formal arbitration. The family relationship and the fact that Emmet continued to haul to the station for decades after the low tests began make personal theft by Ansel implausible on structural grounds.

**Independence of the three reasons:**
- Reason 1 is based on experimental observation (1911 incident).
- Reason 2 is based on external independent verification (state inspector).
- Reason 3 is based on family and business structure (no motive, continued dealing).

These are genuinely independent: they address different aspects (equipment malfunction, independent verification, motive/relationship). A reader could doubt any one and still be convinced by the others.

**Which would hold even if the 1958 glass discovery had never happened?**
Reason 1 (the 1911 experiment) and Reason 2 (the state inspector) both stood before 1958 and still stand even if no glass had ever been found in the loft. They prove the tester innocent on behavioral and external evidence, not on the measurement of the defective equipment itself. Reason 3 also predates 1958 and survives it.

**Did the Association's bookkeeper question the ledger entries?**
No. Bertram Kyle explicitly states that he "never once crossed [his] mind to wonder" at the consistent "5.25% as usual station gain" line. He copied it year after year, and it "had a name on it." Once something has a name, "you rule under it and go on to the next." The bookkeeper did not question it. Moreover, Kyle notes that Selby Vose "read the totals and he never read the lines above them. He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign." The manager signed the bottom line (the total) without examining the ledger entries that justified it. So the ledger entries—which should have flagged the issue—went unquestioned by both the clerk and the manager.

## D3: Did the Manager Know the Tests Ran Low?

**Answer:** Yes, Selby Vose knew that the Ostrey Hollow station's tests ran low before the glassware was condemned in 1923. He had access to station accounts and the averaging data. However, there is no evidence he investigated the cause or suspected the equipment.

**Justification:**

Selby Vose became manager in 1911 (Duncan Keddie). He would have received the annual station accounts prepared by Bertram Kyle, which showed the consistent gap. He "read the totals" of these accounts and signed them every year (Bertram Kyle).

More directly, Vose received the 1908 committee report (Nyle Grigg), which documented the complaint and attributed it to wet hay. From 1908 through 1919, the complaint was "renewed, in one form or another, in every decade" (Orin Falke) and came up "at every annual meeting" (Grigg's 1908 report). The manager cannot have been unaware.

In 1919, Vose himself drafted and signed the water-warning circular to the Ostrey Hollow patrons (Orra Keddie), indicating he believed something was amiss with the milk or the farmers' practices.

**However:** There is no evidence Vose suspected the measuring glass. The 1919 circular points to water, not equipment. The fact that he never examined the condemned glassware (even though it sat 20 feet directly above the arbitration hearing room in 1925) suggests he did not have equipment defect on his mind. He was looking for farmer misconduct or land problems, not manufacturing failure.

# Section F: Theory

## F1: Causal Theory of the Shortfall

**The core fact:** From 1897 onward, a testing station's milk tested at 3.61 average butterfat while the same farms' milk tested at 3.78–3.80 elsewhere. The gap persisted at 0.19–0.21 points consistently across seasons for 26 years.

**The cause:** The measuring pipettes installed at the station in May 1897 were defectively manufactured. They were marked 17.6 cc but delivered only an average of 16.27 cc. When a milk sample 7.56% too small is tested for butterfat, the test reads 7.56% too low. A milk truly containing 3.91% butterfat, when only 92.44% of the correct volume is measured, is recorded as 3.61%.

**Why that particular size:** The discrepancy of 1.33 cc on a 17.6 cc pipette (7.56% short) directly produced a butterfat test gap of approximately 0.28–0.30 points at the working butterfat levels of 3.6–3.9. The gap happened to stabilize at 0.19–0.21 because of partial coincidental mitigation: the wet hay of Hessel Bottom truly did depress butterfat by approximately 0.05–0.10 points (per State Bulletin 214's revision), offsetting the full manifestation of the 0.28–0.30 point measurement error.

**Why it began when it did:** The defective pipettes were manufactured in 1897 and installed May 1, 1897. The gap began immediately but was not recognized as abnormal for 5 years, until 1902. Early years may have appeared as normal variation; by 1902, the consistent pattern was obvious.

**Why it ended when it did:** In June 1923, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn tested the pipettes and found them defective. He condemned them and ordered replacements. New pipettes arrived and were in use by July 1923. Thereafter, the station's average test rose to 3.85, eliminating the gap. The state inspector's ordinary circuit inspection, using an independent calibrated instrument (a burette), happened to occur at the moment 26 years of low tests would finally be resolved.

**Why nothing had to change for either beginning or end:** The measurement error was introduced by faulty equipment, not by farmer behavior, herd quality, pasture condition, testing procedure, or tester competence. The equipment was not damaged or worn out over time; it was wrong from manufacture. Thus no change in farm practice, no change in the land, no change in procedure, and no change in personnel was required to cause the discrepancy to appear or disappear. It appeared when defective glass was installed. It disappeared when correct glass replaced it.

## Three competing explanations advanced and defeated:

**Explanation 1: Water Added by Farmers (Manager Selby Vose)**
- **Claim:** Ostrey Hollow farmers were adding water to their milk to increase volume while reducing test, defrauding the creamery of butterfat.
- **Evidence advanced:** None; the manager simply asserted it and issued a warning circular in 1919.
- **Specific evidence that defeats it:** State dairy inspector Merle Strawn tested the milk with a lactometer at every circuit visit from 1908 onward and never detected added water. Water-added milk has a characteristic lower lactometer reading. Strawn's independent method, using his own instrument and having no stake in the dispute, found no evidence (Orin Falke citing Strawn's circuit notebooks).

**Explanation 2: Cream Theft by the Tester (Association Officer)**
- **Claim:** Ansel Keddie removed cream from the milk and falsified the test records to hide the theft.
- **Evidence advanced:** Consistent low tests; Keddie had access and opportunity; someone must be doing it on purpose.
- **Specific evidence that defeats it:** The 1911 glass interval. When a broken measure was replaced with borrowed equipment for nine weeks, the same tester under no different supervision produced markedly higher tests. The tests fell back when the original measure was resumed. If cream were being stolen, the borrowing of a different measure would have made no difference to the theft or the false recording. Yet it made a material difference to the test. This proves the variation was in the measure, not the cream or the record-keeping. The tester cannot have been stealing; the measure was the variable (Orin Falke's definitive analysis).

**Explanation 3: Wet Hay of Hessel Bottom (Committee of 1908; Veterinarian Kepp, 1926)**
- **Claim:** The poor-quality hay from the wet meadow of Hessel Bottom, lacking lime and phosphorus, reduced butterfat production in cows fed upon it, explaining the low tests.
- **Evidence advanced:** Observation of stock condition (thin in flank, slow to flesh, rough coat); knowledge of bog hay quality; consistency with field experience; citation of State Bulletin 214.
- **Specific evidence that defeats it (partial):** State Agricultural College Bulletin 214, the feeding trial that Kepp and others cited, found bog hay depressed butterfat by "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture" (Orin Falke's reading of the College document). Five hundredths (0.05 points) cannot account for a 0.19–0.21 point gap. The Committee of 1908 and Kepp in 1926 overstated the hay's effect. The wet hay theory is **partially correct but insufficient**. It contributes about 0.05 points of the observed 0.21-point gap; the remaining 0.16 points comes from the defective glassware.

## Two subsidiary questions:

**Did the 1925 arbitration board ever examine the condemned glassware?**

No. Bertram Kyle (who carried the books to the arbitration) notes: "I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked."

The four books were: (1) the station's test book in Keddie's hand, (2) the receiving book, (3) Emmet Brant's private weigh book, (4) the state inspector's notebook (recording the 1923 condemnation).

The crate of condemned glassware was sitting in the loft 20 feet directly above the weigh room where two of the three arbitration hearings were held (Orra Keddie, Cleve Oram). The board examined the books but apparently never asked about the physical equipment—the very pipettes whose defect would have proven their case. The glassware was in the building, unseen and unconsidered, while the board deliberated 20 feet below.

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?**

Ansel Keddie suspected it was the glass. After the 1911 incident, when the borrowed measure produced higher tests, he noted the phenomenon with a question mark in the station book. Duncan Keddie records: "He came home that summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book. I was seventeen and I remember the kitchen and I remember that he said it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not his way."

Ansel could not prove it. He had nothing but suspicion. Against it stood institutional trust in the equipment, the practices of other stations, and the consensus of the industry. So he "kept on. He kept on for another fourteen years, and wrote down every figure the glass gave him, and the figures were what convicted him" at arbitration (Duncan Keddie).

Ansel died in 1934 believing his suspicion was correct but without proof and without vindication. He was proven right in 1958 by his granddaughter's discovery and measurement. He never knew it.

# Section G: Summary

From 1889 onward, the Larrow Valley farmers organized a cooperative creamery at Larrow Green and built a milk-testing station at Ostrey Hollow to serve the Hessel Bottom farms. In 1897, they purchased measuring pipettes from Ammon & Sons, not knowing the glassware was manufactured short—marked 17.6 cc but delivering only 16.27 cc. For 26 years, milk tested at Ostrey Hollow appeared to contain 0.19–0.21 points less butterfat than milk from the rest of the valley, costing farmers approximately $19,600 in unpaid butterfat. Three false explanations were offered: farmers were adding water (unproven), the tester was stealing cream (disproven by a 1911 incident where borrowed measuring equipment raised the tests), or the wet hay of the bottom thinned cream (partially true but insufficient to explain the gap). In 1925, facing mounting pressure, the Association dismissed the tester, Ansel Keddie, and awarded $3,800 to the farmers without admitting fault. Keddie died in 1934, unvindicated. In 1958, his granddaughter Ivy Keddie found the condemned glassware in the station loft and measured it against a calibrated burette at the State Agricultural College. The measurements proved the pipettes had been defective from the start, exonerating Keddie completely and explaining the entire 27-year saga through one manufacturing error, never investigated, preserved by chance in a loft, and finally discovered and measured 35 years after being condemned. The eight separate records—farm weigh books, station test book, inspector's notebooks, Association ledgers, arbitration documents, office files, the crate itself—had to be brought together to understand what no single narrator or record could show alone.
