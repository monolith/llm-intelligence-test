# Section A: Reconstruction of Eight Stories

## Story 1: The Cooperative Movement and Station Construction (1889–1896)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association formed in 1889 as farmers organized for shared butter production. In 1891, they built their main creamery at Larrow Green. Gideon Vose organized the Association and served as its first president (Bertram Kyle). The Association's by-laws established payment by butterfat test, not milk weight, with the station test determining each farmer's pay—a crucial decision that made the defects that followed financially catastrophic (Orin Falke).

In winter 1895–1896, the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up-valley from Larrow Green, to serve the Hessel Bottom farms. The station opened May 4, 1896 (Orin Falke), with a board building on a flat by the creek. The platform allowed farmers to roll milk cans down level into the intake room. Here Ansel Keddie, hired from the opening, would weigh the milk, take a sample, test it for butterfat, separate the cream, return skim to farmers, and send cream down to Larrow Green (Orin Falke, Cleve Oram).

Larrow Green's main creamery and the Ostrey Hollow station represented the practical embodiment of the cooperative ideal: pooling resources across valleys to achieve economies of scale. The system was sound in principle. The tragedy lay in what neither the founders nor the farmers could have anticipated: the testing equipment itself was defective from the start.

**Key figures:** Gideon Vose (founder/president), Ansel Keddie (tester), farmers of Hessel Bottom (six farms).
**Quantities:** Station 7 miles from main creamery; 6 farms served.
**Documents bearing on it:** Orin Falke (county agent report on Association structure); Bertram Kyle (office clerk on Vose/Association history); Oram (cream hauler on station layout).

## Story 2: The Fatal Purchase—Defective Glassware (1897–1902)

In April 1897, the Larrow Valley Association purchased its testing outfit from Ammon & Sons at Tarnet for $77.93. The invoice, reproduced by Junia Ammon (company historian) shows: 6 dozen milk test pipettes marked 17.6 cc at $25.20; 12 dozen test bottles at $31.68; an acid measure; a hand tester; and freight. The pipettes arrived and went into use May 1, 1897 (Junia Ammon, Orin Falke).

From the very beginning, milk tested at Ostrey Hollow came in lower than milk from the rest of the valley. The persistent gap was approximately 0.19–0.21 points of butterfat test—tiny enough to be plausible as natural variation, large enough to cost farmers real money month after month (Orin Falke).

Junia Ammon reflects, in 1949, on what she believes happened: the mold for the pipettes had been reground slightly; the gauges get knocked off nails; a boy checks the first, tenth, and fiftieth pieces in a lot and finds them consistent—consistently wrong (Junia Ammon). No man on the floor was thanked for what went to scrap.

By 1902, patrons of Ostrey Hollow were beginning to complain. Their complaint was perfectly just: they delivered whole, honest milk but were paid as if it were inferior (Orin Falke). What no one yet knew was that their milk was sound; the glass measuring it was not.

**Key figures:** Ammon & Sons (supplier), Ansel Keddie (first user), Larrow Valley Association (purchaser).
**Quantities:** 6 dozen pipettes (72 total), marked 17.6 cc; cost $25.20; outfi total $77.93.
**Delivered:** 16.27 cc per pipette (discovered 1958).
**Gap:** 0.19–0.21 points between Ostrey Hollow and valley average tests.
**Documents bearing on it:** Junia Ammon (1897 invoice, 1923 letter from company); Orin Falke (statistics).

## Story 3: The Complaint and Grigg's Investigation (1902–1908)

From 1902 onward, Ostrey Hollow farmers knew something was wrong. Their milk tested consistently lower than their neighbors' milk, despite identical herds, care, and pasture. The complaint "was renewed, in one form or another, in every decade thereafter" (Orin Falke). Orra Keddie's diary (March 1902) captures the moment: her husband came home from testing and "would not eat," saying "it is my figures they will look at" (Orra Keddie).

By 1908, the Association could not ignore the pattern. A committee was appointed, chaired by Nyle Grigg, a farmer of standing. The committee—composed of practical farmers—visited each of the six Hessel Bottom farms. They sampled herds in every season, sealed samples themselves, watched the test made, inspected scales, checked water supplies. They observed the stock's condition and found it "thin in the flank at the end of the winter feeding" and "slow to come to their flesh in the spring" (Nyle Grigg).

The committee found no dishonesty. They reported it "in the same type as the rest" (Nyle Grigg). The scales were honest; the milk was whole. But the committee concluded, from their ground observation, that "wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station."

Hessel Bottom—six farms, lying along the creek under Ostrey Hollow—was wet ground. Its herbage was sedge, rush, and coarse tussock grasses. "There is no better winter keep to be had there," Grigg wrote, "and they took it as their fathers left it to them" (Nyle Grigg). The bog hay theory was plausible. It was also wrong, but it satisfied the Association. The question was declared closed for eleven years.

**Key figures:** Nyle Grigg (committee chairman), Emmet Brant (largest Hollow farmer), six Hessel Bottom farmers, committee members (unnamed in some sources).
**Quantities:** Six farms; wet meadow; sedge and rush hay.
**Documents bearing on it:** Nyle Grigg (1908 committee report, reprinted 1919 with covering letter); Orra Keddie (diary 1908); Orin Falke (historical summary).

## Story 4: The Glass Breaks—And Proves the Truth (1911)

On May 8, 1911, the station's own measuring glass broke. For nine weeks—May 8 to July 10, 1911—the station used a borrowed glass measure (source of loan not specified) (Orin Falke). During this interval, the station's tests stood "markedly higher" (Orin Falke). When the original glass was returned to use, the tests "fell back to their former level" (Orin Falke). Ansel Keddie noted the fact in his station book and "set a question mark against it" (Orin Falke).

What made this interval decisive: "The herds, the pasture, the season, the men and the machinery were the same throughout" (Orin Falke). June and July were the flush season; the stock were off wet ground, up on aftermath; the weather identical; the tester the same; the procedure identical. Only the glass differed. Yet the tests differed materially.

This destroyed the theft theory (the tester could not have been stealing cream; the same man made higher tests with different glass). It proved the tester's procedure was sound. It proved the herds were not bad. It could only mean something was wrong with the original measuring glass.

Ansel Keddie understood this. He suspected it was something in the glass. But suspicion is not proof. He had nothing to hold in his hand but the question mark and a nine-week anomaly. Against it stood the entirety of testing practice—the equipment the station had been taught to trust, the equipment every other station in the county used, the consensus of dairy science (Orin Falke). He could not prove it. So he kept on testing with the faulty glass and writing down every figure it gave him. The figures were what would eventually convict him in the 1925 arbitration.

**When:** May 8–July 10, 1911.
**The variable:** Measuring glass (borrowed vs. original).
**Held constant:** Herds, pasture, tester, procedure, machinery, season.
**Result:** Tests rose during borrowed-glass period; fell when original returned.
**What it proves:** The tester was not stealing; the herds were sound; the procedure was correct; the glass was the problem.
**What it does not prove:** The exact nature of the glass defect; whether anyone else knew; whether the Association could have investigated and found the cause.
**Documents bearing on it:** Orin Falke (definitive account); Ansel's question mark in station book (noted by Orin Falke).
