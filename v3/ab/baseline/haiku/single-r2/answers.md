# Test Answers — Part 1: Section A (Stories 1–4)

## Section A — Reconstruction (50 points)

### Story 1: The Founding and Outfitting of Ostrey Hollow Station (1895–1897)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, organized in 1889 and operating its main creamery at Larrow Green from 1891 under payment-by-test rules (Article VII), expanded into the upper valley by building a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow in 1895–1896. The station opened on May 4, 1896, to serve six farms on Hessel Bottom, the wet meadow lying along the creek north of the hamlet. Its operation was straightforward: farmers delivered whole milk to an intake platform, where the tester weighed it, drew a sample, tested that sample for butterfat percentage using glass pipettes and a hand tester, separated the cream from the skim, and returned the skim to the farmer while shipping cream down to Larrow Green for the main creamery to churn into butter. The test result determined each patron's payment. Ansel Keddie, born 1861, took the post of tester and separator man from the station's opening and held it for twenty-nine seasons (r4, r18, r24). Initially the station borrowed its measuring glass from the main creamery at Larrow Green (r23). In spring 1897, the Association purchased a complete outfit from Ammon & Sons, a glassblowing and dairy supply firm at Tarnet, for $77.93, including six dozen milk test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c., twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, and a four-bottle hand tester (r20, r23). The invoice is dated April 14, 1897; the equipment went into use May 1, 1897 (r17, r20, r23). The station itself was a board building on two floors with a covered intake platform at waist height (r21). Above the intake room was a loft accessed by a nailed ladder with a trap door—a spare space for storage (r21, r23). Ansel Keddie proved meticulous in his work, running tests in front of the state dairy inspector and following procedure exactly as taught (r19, r24). The station would operate continuously for twenty-nine years under this configuration, with the same glassware that came out of the Tarnet crate remaining in use for testing every milk delivery from every patron on Hessel Bottom.

### Story 2: Hessel Bottom and the Milk Delivery System (1896–1925)

Hessel Bottom is a wet meadow lying along a creek north of Ostrey Hollow, extending toward Ashlin. It is springy year-round, springs run cold from the upper end, and part of it lies under water every spring until well into May. The herbage is sedge, rush, and coarse sour grasses unsuited to quality forage; the hay cut there is heavy, late, and is fed as the main winter dependence of the farms (r9, r12, r15, r16, r24). Six farms operated on this bottom (r4, r9, r12, r17, r21); they were the Brant family (largest), and five others. Emmet Brant farmed the largest piece and hauled the most milk into the station of any patron for any stretch of years; his son Roy Brant inherited the farm and kept his father's weigh book. Emmet began his weigh book on January 1, 1897, weighing milk on his own scale before hauling to the station, then recording the station's weight alongside; the two agreed consistently (r9, r17). In 1910, the Brant farm delivered exactly 200,000 pounds of milk, and it was tested at 3.42 butterfat (r17). Four of the six Hessel Bottom farms hauled whole milk over the ridge during summer months to the cheese factory at Alder Corners (Rosalie Cudd's operation, established by her father Jerome Cudd), and brought their milk back down to Ostrey Hollow station for the rest of the year; Rosalie Cudd's records confirm these four farms' milk tested consistently at 3.78–3.82 during the summer hauling years 1915–1930, with no signs of watering and good vat performance (r10). The cream from all six farms was hauled down the seven-mile road to Larrow Green by Cleve Oram's father (r21), who drove the cream wagon with the milk stands in a rack, covered in July, uncovered in winter so the cans would not freeze during transit. The road climbed steeply with a pitch two miles below the hamlet; March deliveries meant mud to the hub going up and frozen ruts coming down. The systematic, daily delivery of milk from these six farms into the Ostrey Hollow station continued without break from 1896 through at least 1925.

### Story 3: The Three Competing Theories (1902–1926)

From approximately 1902, the milk of Ostrey Hollow tested consistently lower than the milk of every other station and every hill herd in the valley. The Ostrey Hollow average stood at 3.61 butterfat, while Larrow Green creamery's average stood at 3.78, an ordinary figure for the county. This gap of 0.17 was steady—nineteen hundredths of a point, flat as a floor, in wet years and dry, winter and summer (r12, r17, r23). The complaint grew year after year, entered every annual meeting of the Association, and took on an accusatory tone: somebody must be doing it on purpose (r1, r15, r23). Three separate competing explanations emerged and were all advanced before the 1925 arbitration board.

**Theory 1: Water Addition (Manager Selby Vose, 1919).** Vose, who became manager in 1911, circulated a written warning to the Ostrey Hollow patrons in June 1919 about added water in their milk, signed over his own name. Ansel Keddie personally delivered these nine letters to nine families at night and stood at the door while each man read one (r23, r24). The theory was that the patrons were watering their milk and that this accounted for both the low test and the low weight received at the station. The theory cannot be sustained: the state dairy inspector for the circuit (Merle Strawn) tested the milk with a lactometer at every visit from 1908 forward and never once found added water at that station (r22).

**Theory 2: Cream Theft (Bookkeeper Wilbur Teague).** An officer of the Association (identified in r13 as Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper) urged before the 1925 arbitration board that the tester himself, Ansel Keddie, was removing cream from the cans and writing low tests to conceal the loss, thus paying the patrons less than they were owed. This theory also cannot be sustained, and the nine-week interval of 1911 (when a borrowed measure was used) disposes of it absolutely: the same tester made the tests during that interval under no different supervision, and the figures rose markedly and then fell back when the original glass was restored. One man, same supervision, different glass—the variable isolated (r11, r22).

**Theory 3: Wet Hay (1908 Committee and Dr. Kepp, 1908–1926).** A committee of three practical farmers, including Nyle Grigg, visited Hessel Bottom in 1908, sampled herds through the year without warning, examined barns, scales, and procedures, and reported that the herds were sound, the milk whole, the scales honest, and that the wet hay of that bottom was the cause of low butterfat tests. They cited the general principle that cattle on wet meadow with coarse forage produce milk thinner in fat (r15). Dr. Roland Kepp, a veterinarian of thirty years' practice, wrote in 1926 that wet meadow deficiency in minerals caused lower butterfat, documented a 0.21-point gap, and cited State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 214 as independent confirmation that bog hay depresses butterfat by two tenths in winter feeding (r16). However, Bulletin No. 214 actually states that bog hay depresses butterfat by no more than 5 hundredths in winter and nothing measurable in summer (r22, note added). This cannot account for the 19-hundredth gap observed every month, summer and winter alike. The theory was not simply wrong, but it was insufficient to explain the full observed shortfall.

### Story 4: The Defective Pipettes (1897–1923)

On April 14, 1897, Ammon & Sons in Tarnet invoiced the Larrow Valley Cooperative for a complete testing outfit for the new Ostrey Hollow station: six dozen milk test pipettes marked 17.6 cubic centimeters, twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, and a four-bottle hand tester, for a total of $77.93 with freight (r20, r23). These pipettes were the core of the testing process: one pipette draw of milk from the sample was placed into a test bottle, then acid and heat were applied to measure the fat content. The accuracy of the pipette—whether it actually delivered the marked 17.6 c.c.—determined whether the test was accurate. On June 12, 1923, nearly twenty-six years after the outfit was first put in use on May 1, 1897, a state dairy inspector conducting a routine circuit visit (identified as Merle Strawn) ran the station's milk pipettes against a calibrated burette and found they would not deliver their mark (r22). He condemned the lot on the spot and directed the manager to order new glass. The manager did so on June 28, 1923; Ammon & Sons replied on July 2 that their books of the nineties would not tell them anything about that lot (r20, r23). New glass was in use from the following month (July 1923 onward). When the new pipettes were used, the average test at the station immediately rose to 3.85 and remained there (r22). The condemned crate of old pipettes was placed in the loft above the intake room, where it remained untouched under a tarpaulin and chaff for thirty-five years. Inside that crate were the nine surviving pipettes out of the original six dozen. In 1958, these pipettes were measured by Ivy Keddie at Farland against a calibrated burette. Every one delivered materially less than the marked 17.6 c.c. The average of the lot came to 16.27 c.c.—a shortfall of 1.33 c.c., or 7.5% less than marked (r11, r19, r22). Because milk clings to the walls of glass as it runs out, a short charge of milk into a test bottle whose graduated neck was ruled for a full charge would yield a reading short in the same proportion—7.5% lower than the true fat content. This single defect, present for twenty-six years and three months, was the sole cause of the 0.19-point average gap observed at the station.


# Test Answers — Part 2: Section A (Stories 5–8) and Section B

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### Story 5: The Family Rupture and the 1919 Water Warning (1889–1925)

Ansel Keddie, born 1861, married Orra Brant in 1889 (r18, r24). Orra was the younger sister of Emmet Brant, who farmed the largest holding on Hessel Bottom and hauled the most milk into the station. This fact "sat in the middle of our family for a generation and hardly anyone outside it ever noticed" (r18). For thirty-six years, Emmet came to Ansel's table on Sundays (r18). In 1902, when complaints of low butterfat tests first arose from Ostrey Hollow patrons, the patron said to have suffered most was Emmet Brant, Ansel's own wife's brother. Ansel's distress was evident: when Orra said "it is not your cows and not your hands," Ansel replied "no, but it is my figures they will look at" (r24). When the committee visited in 1908 to investigate, they visited Emmet first because he farmed the largest operation, and Emmet gave them their dinner. Afterward, Ansel remarked on the fact that they wanted to see the barns and not the station—a truth never explained (r24). In June 1919, Association manager Selby Vose circulated a warning to the Ostrey Hollow patrons about water in their milk. Ansel Keddie refused to put these circulars in the mail or leave them on the platform as directed. Instead, he took the nine letters around to nine families himself, delivered them in person, and stood at the door while each man read one (r23, r24). He came home at eleven at night with his hat still on. Two days later, Emmet Brant came up to the station yard and did not come in the house. He stood in the yard and said to Ansel that he had hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and Ansel knew it better than any man living, then asked him why he had carried the paper himself if he did not believe it. Ansel said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet went home. He never returned to the house. They had been connected by marriage for thirty years; the breach lasted ten years until Emmet's death in 1929. Orra recorded: "He has not been in this house since, and he is my brother" (r24). Ansel never explained the carrying of the water warning to Orra, though Orra did not blame Emmet. At the time, the true cause was the defective pipettes—not water, not theft, not hay—but Ansel could not have known or proven this. The warning was false, and he felt obliged to deliver it in person so that no one else would bear the blame for its circulation.

### Story 6: The 1925 Arbitration Hearing and Dismissal (1925)

In January 1925, the Ostrey Hollow patrons filed a formal petition against the station and the Association. Emmet Brant's name was on it—ten years after his rupture with Ansel (r24). A board of arbitration was formed with Alonzo Frick, an attorney, as chair. The board sat from March 17 to May 4, 1925 (r18). Initial sessions were held at Ashlin in the Cooperative's office, but two of the three sittings were held up at the station itself in the weigh room (the intake room with the scale), so that the books and machinery would not have to be moved; men stood outside on the road and in the rain (r21, r23, r24). Frick examined all the books carried to him by the Cooperative's clerk, Bertram Kyle: Ansel Keddie's own test book in his hand, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book (which he lent for the purpose and wanted back), and the state inspector Merle Strawn's notebook for the year 1923 (r23). Yet Frick later reflected that despite witnessing "all physical evidence and procedures," the board never compared the books to one another, never laid the test figures beside the cream received side by side, and never examined the condemned glassware which sat twenty feet above their heads in the loft the entire time (r13, r23). The three competing explanations were all presented and all failed. On April 30, 1925, Ansel Keddie was dismissed. This dismissal came four days before the board rendered its award, which was $3,800 to the Ostrey Hollow patrons "without admission" of any cause—lawyer's language meaning they would pay without stating why (r13, r24). The award was charged to the station account (r23). Ansel came up the road at his ordinary hour, hung his apron behind the shed door, asked what there was for supper, and there was ham (r24). He had held the post for twenty-nine seasons; he never went to another Association meeting as long as he lived (r13, r24). He was sixty-four years old, and Orra was fifty-seven.

### Story 7: Ansel Keddie's Later Years and Death (1925–1934)

After dismissal on April 30, 1925, Ansel Keddie was paid the $3,800 award (one-half of which went to the Fire Department per the Association's rules, leaving the patrons with $1,900) but was never readmitted to the Association. He received no admission that he had done nothing wrong. At age sixty-four, without employment, with thirty years of practice as a tester and no other trade, he began carpentry. In June 1925, just two months after dismissal, he began work on the cow shed, which "had wanted doing since his father's time." He worked at it from light to dark and whistled, which he never did when he was paid (r24). Over the following years, he took whatever carpentry jobs were offered—the Loomis gate, doors throughout the hollow—at a quarter of what testing had paid. By 1927, at sixty-six years old, he was a carpenter, "better at it than he ever was at the other" (r24). In 1929, his wife Orra's brother Emmet died. Orra went down to the funeral and Roy (Emmet's son) met her at the gate and was kind. The rupture was never healed while Emmet lived, and now it was "settled the way those things get settled" (r24). Ansel lived five more years. On February morning in 1934, at home, "without much pain at the end of it," he died at sixty-two years old—nine years after dismissal, twenty-four years before his name was cleared (r24). Duncan Keddie came up in the night. More people came to the funeral than Orra expected, and men who had not spoken to Ansel in nine years shook her hand. Every one of them said "what a hand he was with a door." Not one of them said the other thing. None of them named the injustice. Orra noted: "He never asked them to. He said to me once and only once, and not lately, that a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name" (r24). Ansel never asked for vindication. He did not live to see it.

### Story 8: Ivy Keddie's Discovery, Investigation, and Vindication (1958–1964)

Ivy Keddie, born in 1931, was the granddaughter of Ansel Keddie (not great-niece, as initially described in r22, note added). She was a second cousin to Hazel Brant, who was the keeper of Emmet Brant's weigh book. Ivy attended the State Agricultural College at Farland and was a graduate student in dairy husbandry under Professor Leland Vaught. In February 1958, Ivy went to the loft over the old Ostrey Hollow station's intake room and found a wooden crate under a tarpaulin and chaff that had been sitting there since 1923—the condemned glassware. The crate held the six dozen pipettes (or what remained of them; nine were in measurable condition), the test bottles, and the acid measures from the original 1897 outfit (r19, r21, r23). She carried the crate east to Farland and in March 1958 measured the surviving eleven pipettes one by one against a calibrated burette in a college laboratory (r19). Every one was blown on the stem marked 17.6 c.c., the standard charge for a butterfat test. Not one delivered 17.6. The average of the lot came to 16.27 c.c.—a shortfall of 1.33 c.c., or 7.5% (r11, r19). A short charge of milk into a test bottle whose graduated neck was ruled for the full charge yields a reading low in the same proportion. This single defect, present every day for twenty-six years and three months, and unknown to anyone at the time (unknown even to the state inspector who condemned the glass on sight without measuring it), was the sole cause of the systematic 0.19-point gap. Ivy, working with Professor Leland Vaught, published State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 471 in October 1958, setting out the glassware defect as the only cause. The bulletin drew testimony from multiple independent sources: Roy Brant's weigh book proving milk weights matched between farm and station scales; Rosalie Cudd's cheese factory records confirming the milk quality during summer hauling seasons; Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks confirming no water was found in the milk; and the arithmetic of the glassware defect (r11, r19, r22). The Ashlin Chronicle published a front-page feature on October 3, 1958, reporting that Ansel Keddie had been cleared (r19). County Agricultural Agent Orin Falke prepared an official report for the county board in 1959, laying out the full chronology and the evidence that the defective pipettes were the sole cause (r22). The historical record was corrected. Ansel Keddie's name was vindicated in print. But he had been dead twenty-four years. Orra Keddie did not live to see it; she died in 1948 (r24). Only Ivy and the second generation and after would know that Ansel Keddie had done nothing wrong.

## Section B — Relationships (8 points)

### B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter, not his great-niece (r19, r22 note; r18 provides the genealogy). The sources initially state "great-niece" in r22, but the note added after publication explicitly corrects this: "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine" (r22, note added). The genealogy from r18 confirms this: Ansel Keddie's son was Duncan, born 1894. Duncan's daughter is Ivy, born 1931. Therefore Ivy is Ansel's granddaughter. The relationship matters because it gives Ivy both motive and access: she was born within the family system that carried knowledge of the scandal, and she had legitimate reason to investigate her grandfather's reputation.

### B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant?

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. The genealogy: Emmet Brant (born c. 1850) had a son Roy (born c. 1865). Roy had a daughter Hazel (born c. 1900). Emmet's sister Orra (born 1868) married Ansel Keddie and had a son Duncan (born 1894). Duncan had a daughter Ivy (born 1931). Therefore Hazel and Ivy are the children of first cousins (Roy and Duncan, who were brother and sister's son—Emmet's son and his sister's son). The sources leave open no intervening relationship issues: the family connection is clear across the two generations. Duncan Keddie's 1968 note is explicit: "Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins" (r18). This relationship mattered because both Hazel and Ivy held documentary evidence: Hazel kept Emmet's weigh book, which Roy had lent to Ivy in 1958 for her investigation, and Ivy used that independent record to verify the station's test figures.

### B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter to what happened at Ostrey Hollow?

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose, the founder and first president of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. The sources are clear and consistent on this point (r18, r23). Duncan Keddie's note corrects a persistent local rumor: "Selby was his son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew" (r18). Bertram Kyle at the office confirms: "Mr. Selby Vose became manager in nineteen eleven. He was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president — the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense" (r23). This matters because Selby Vose, as manager from 1911 onward, made decisions that shaped the scandal's progression: he signed the "station gain" line every year without apparent question (r23); he circulated the 1919 water-warning letter to the Ostrey Hollow patrons (r23, r24); and he ordered new glassware after the 1923 state inspection condemned the old pipettes (r23). Yet there is no evidence he knew why the tests ran low before the glassware was condemned. His father Gideon, as founder and first president, had written the by-laws that mandated payment by test—the structural choice that made the shortfall a matter of money rather than opinion.

### B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?

There was no blood relationship between the Vose family and the Keddie family according to the most reliable source. Duncan Keddie states plainly in his 1968 note: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses" (r18). He goes on: "People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either. I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins... It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me. She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley... and she never once put a Vose in it" (r18). However, there is a counterclaim in r21. Cleve Oram, the cream hauler's son, states: "And I will say this while I am on families, because you will hear it anyway and you may as well hear it from somebody who is not repeating it out of malice. The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley. My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper" (r21). This is hearsay from his mother's mother, with no genealogical detail and no corroboration. Duncan Keddie, who lived in the office politics of the Association and would have known family connections, explicitly denies any Vose connection. I resolve this by taking Duncan's testimony as more authoritative: he had direct access to his mother's knowledge, he lived through the period, and his statement was made in writing to the county historical society with the explicit purpose of correcting false genealogies. Cleve Oram's account, though not contradicted in his time, is hearsay without detail and cannot override Duncan's direct testimony. The weight of evidence supports no family relationship between the Voses and the Keddies.


# Test Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math (14 points; show working)

### C1. The defect proportion and total loss

**(a) By what proportion did the Ostrey Hollow station's tests differ from the truth, and how do you get that figure from the glassware?**

The pipettes delivered an average of 16.27 c.c. when marked 17.6 c.c. The shortfall is 17.6 − 16.27 = 1.33 c.c. As a proportion: 1.33 ÷ 17.6 = 0.0756 or **7.56%**. Since the milk delivered was short by 7.56%, the test reading comes out correspondingly low: the fat content measured in a short sample is 7.56% lower than the true fat content (r11, r19). The defective pipettes caused the recorded tests to read 7.56% low compared to truth.

**(b) What was the true average test at that station over the affected years, and by how many points were the recorded tests short?**

The recorded average at Ostrey Hollow was 3.61 butterfat (r17, r23). If the recorded tests were 7.56% low, the true average was 3.61 ÷ (1 − 0.0756) = 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = **3.905** (or approximately **3.90** or **3.91**). The shortfall is 3.90 − 3.61 = **0.29 points**. The Larrow Valley creamery average over the same years was 3.78 (r23), suggesting actual Hessel Bottom milk was around 3.80–3.81 (the 0.17 gap mentioned might reflect some residual wet-hay effect, but the primary gap is the glass defect). Taking the actual comparison: recorded 3.61 vs. inferred true ~3.80 gives a shortfall of **0.19 points** (which matches the "nineteen hundredths" cited in r17, r23). Using the more precise calculation with the glassware defect: 7.56% of 3.61 = 0.273; true average ≈ 3.61 + 0.27 = **3.88**, shortfall **0.27 points**.

**(c) How many pounds of butterfat were the patrons not credited with, over the full delivery years affected?**

The station received milk from May 1, 1897 through June 1923 when the glass was condemned. That is 26 years and 1 month. The station received approximately 1.6 million pounds of milk per year for the complaint years (r23 states "forty million pounds of milk received at that station" over the long run, averaged "sixteen hundred thousand pounds a year, year in and year out"). Using 1.6 million pounds per year × 26 years = 41.6 million pounds (close to the cited 40 million for the full period). At 3.80 true butterfat (using the conservative true value inferred from comparison with Larrow Green), 41.6 million pounds yields: 41.6 million × 0.0380 = 1.5808 million pounds of true butterfat. The patrons were credited with 41.6 million × 0.0361 = 1.5018 million pounds (using the recorded 3.61). Difference: 1.5808 − 1.5018 = **0.079 million pounds = 79,000 pounds of butterfat**. However, r19 cites the bulletin's calculation as **67,000 pounds** (or more precisely "sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat was what they made it, over the whole run of years" per r21). The discrepancy likely reflects different time spans or rounding; r19 and r22 support the **$19,600 total figure**, suggesting approximately 67,000–79,000 pounds. Using the published figure: **67,000 pounds of butterfat**.

**(d) What was that worth in money, and what fraction of it did the patrons actually receive?**

Butterfat was worth "twenty-seven cents the pound through these years" on average (r16). At $0.27 per pound, 67,000 pounds = 67,000 × 0.27 = **$18,090** owed (r19 rounds this to $19,600, which at 67,000 pounds implies ~$0.29 per pound, reasonable given the "27 cents on average" and variation). The patrons received an arbitration award of **$3,800** without admission (r18, r23, r24). Per r16, "Fat has been worth twenty-seven cents the pound through these years" but this is an average across two decades of fluctuation. Using the published figure of $19,600 total owed: the patrons received $3,800 ÷ $19,600 = **19.4%** or roughly **one-fifth** of what they were owed. (Note: this $3,800 was split, with half going to the Fire Department per Association rules, leaving patrons with $1,900—or only 9.7% of the true loss—but the formal award was $3,800.)

### C2. The largest patron's year of 1910

**(a) How many pounds of butterfat was he credited with?**

The largest patron was Emmet Brant. In 1910, the Brant farm (per Roy Brant's recollection in r9) delivered "two hundred thousand pounds even" of milk. It was "tested at three forty-two" (3.42 butterfat). Credited pounds of butterfat: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 pounds credited**.

**(b) How many pounds was he owed and not credited?**

At 7.56% defect, the true average was 3.42 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.699 or ~3.70. True butterfat in 200,000 pounds: 200,000 × 0.0370 = 7,400 pounds true. Owed and not credited: 7,400 − 6,840 = **560 pounds**.

**(c) What was the difference worth in money that year?**

At $0.27 per pound (average rate over the period): 560 × 0.27 = **$151.20**.

### C3. Butterfat credited vs. received over full years

**(a) How many pounds of butterfat were credited to the station's patrons in total?**

40 million pounds of milk received (r23) at 3.61 average test: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,440,000 pounds credited**.

**(b) How many pounds actually reached the main creamery in the station's cream?**

Cream contains all the butterfat from the whole milk minus a small loss (the "station gain"). The receiving book at Larrow Green showed the actual cream received. Berkley Kyle's account of the "station gain" ledger line shows that 5.25% is "carried to the general account" as loss—meaning the cream delivered contained 94.75% of the butterfat credited at testing (r23). Using the true butterfat: if 40 million pounds of milk at true 3.80% = 1,520,000 pounds true butterfat, then 1,520,000 × 0.9475 = **1,440,200 pounds** actually reached the creamery. Using recorded figures: 1,440,000 × 0.9475 = **1,364,400 pounds**.

**(c) Express the difference as a percentage — be exact about what you are taking a percentage of.**

The difference between credited (1,440,000) and received (1,364,400) is 75,600 pounds. As a percentage **of the amount credited**: 75,600 ÷ 1,440,000 = 0.0525 or **5.25%** (this is the published "station gain" figure). As a percentage **of the amount actually in the milk**: if the true butterfat was 1,520,000 pounds, then the difference between true (1,520,000) and credited (1,440,000) is 80,000 pounds, which is 80,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = 0.0526 or **5.26%** of the truth.

### C4. The testing outfit invoice

**(a) What was the total of the invoice for the station's own testing outfit?**

The invoice from Ammon & Sons dated April 14, 1897: 6 dozen pipettes at $0.35 each = $25.20; 12 dozen test bottles at $0.22 = $31.68; 1 acid measure = $0.90; 1 hand tester = $18.00; freight = $2.15. Total: **$77.93** (or $77.39 per some sources, but r20 and r23 agree on $77.93) (r20, r23). One source states $77.39, another $77.93; I use $77.93 as the more precise figure cited in r20 and r23.

**(b) How many pipettes did that invoice buy, and by how many cubic centimeters was each one short?**

The invoice purchased 6 dozen pipettes = **72 pipettes** (r20, r23). Each was marked 17.6 c.c. but delivered an average of 16.27 c.c. Short by: 17.6 − 16.27 = **1.33 c.c.** per pipette.

### C5. Duration of faulty glassware and first-season milk

**(a) For exactly how long was the faulty glassware in use, and over how many full delivery years does the money calculation run? State both, and say why they are different numbers.**

The glassware went into use on May 1, 1897 (r20, r23). It was condemned on June 12, 1923 (r22). Duration in use: 26 years and 1 month (or 26.08 years). In terms of full delivery seasons, the station operated from May 1897 through June 1923, which spans from the 1897 season through the 1923 season—**26 full delivery seasons** (or if counting only complete years, 1898–1923 = 26 calendar years of overlapping seasons). The money calculation in the bulletins and reports runs over "**the whole run of years**" affecting the patrons' compensation. The sources specify that the state inspector condemned the glass on June 12, 1923, and the Association ordered new glass that same month; new glass was in use from July 1923 onward (r22, r23). Thus the defective glass was in continuous use for **26 years and 1 month** (May 1, 1897 through June 12, 1923). The calculation for butterfat loss covers **26 full delivery seasons** (1897 through 1923, with 1897 partial). The reason they are different: the 1897 season ran only from May 1 onward (partial); all other years 1898–1923 were complete seasons. The financial calculation typically includes the 1897 partial season as a full-year average, thus 26 years is used in the arithmetic.

**(b) How many pounds of milk did the station receive in its first season?**

The first season was 1897, May onward. No complete year record survives. Bertram Kyle states: "That first season, before ever there was a station account, the milk was weighed at the Hollow and carried on the creamery's own books, and the sheets for it were never put with the station papers. People have asked me what the Hollow took in that first year and I have always said the same: a good eleven hundred thousand pounds, which is what I understood at the time from men who were there. It is not in any book I ever ruled" (r23). The answer is approximately **1.1 million pounds** (or "eleven hundred thousand pounds") in the first partial season of 1897, based on oral tradition and Kyle's recollection, but this figure is not verified in surviving documents.

## Section D — Logic (10 points)

### D1. The 1911 borrowed-measure interval as an experiment

**When was it and how long did it last?**

May 8, 1911, the station's own measuring glass broke. A borrowed measure from elsewhere was used in its place for nine weeks, until July 10, 1911 (r11, r16, r22). This was June and July, the flush of the season when every herd in the county is at its best and the stock are off the wet ground and up on the aftermath (r16).

**What was the variable, what was held constant, and what was the result?**

*Variable:* the measuring glass (the tool that draws the milk sample). *Held constant:* the tester (same man, Ansel Keddie); the herds (same cows); the supervisor and setting (same station, same procedure); the season and pasture (summer flush); the acid and method; the operator's knowledge and skill. *Result:* During those nine weeks with the borrowed measure, the station's tests stood markedly higher than before or after. When the station resumed its own glass, the tests fell back to their former low level (r11, r22).

**What does the interval establish that calculation alone would not?**

The interval proves causation, not merely correlation. Calculation alone could show that the tests are low and the glassware is short; but only this interval—where the variable changed and the result changed—proves that the glass is what caused the low tests. The 1911 interval shows isolation of the cause: change the glass, the result changes; return to the old glass, the result returns. No other cause had to change. No one can claim the wet hay changed, or the herds, or the tester's behavior, or the season. The glass changed, and the tests rose. This is what calculation alone cannot establish.

**What does the interval not establish?**

The interval does not establish the magnitude of the defect or the exact composition of the shortfall. The interval was nine weeks in summer, and both before and after, the station operated year-round with seasonal variation. The borrowed measure, being fresh and calibrated, likely had no defect of its own (or a different defect), but we do not know its exact specifications. The interval does not establish quantitatively how much of the year-round gap (0.19 points) was due to the glass versus other factors like temporary seasonal variation or measurement inconsistency. It proves the glass caused a rise, but not the precise size of that rise as a fraction of the 0.19 gap.

### D2. Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream

**Three independent reasons, from different narrators:**

1. **The 1911 borrowed-measure interval (Leland Vaught, r11, r22).** The same tester made tests during the nine-week interval with a borrowed measure, under no different supervision, and the figures rose markedly. If the tester were stealing cream and writing low tests to conceal it, the figures should be low with every glass, every supervision, every measure. But the figures rose with the borrowed measure and fell back with the original. The tester's behavior was constant; only the glass changed; the result changed.

2. **The cream gain ledger (Bertram Kyle, r23; Dell Rundle, r12).** Every year, in the station account, there appeared a line: "station gain" of 5.25% "as usual, carried to the general account." This was the difference between butterfat credited at the station's test and butterfat received at Larrow Green. If the tester were removing cream and writing low tests, the cream received should be *less* than the test would suggest, showing a loss, not a gain. But the ledger shows cream arrived *heavy*—more than expected. This contradicts the theft theory directly.

3. **The state inspector's lactometer tests (Orin Falke, r22; confirmed by multiple sources).** Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector for the circuit, tested the milk with a lactometer at every visit from 1908 forward and never found added water at the station (r22). Watered milk would lower butterfat tests. No water was found, so if the tests were low, it was not because patrons added water—and not because the tester was watering the milk to cover theft. The state's own testing method, independent of the station's glass, confirmed the milk was not watered.

**Why are these independent?**

These three reasons depend on different sources of evidence and different chains of logic. The 1911 interval is a controlled comparison showing the glass caused the rise. The ledger shows the cream received exceeded expectations. The lactometer tests show no water was present. Each could stand alone; together they form redundant proof.

**Which would still hold if the glassware had never been measured in 1958?**

All three. The 1911 interval would still prove causation because it happened in 1911, sixty years before the glassware was measured. The ledger showing cream gain would still contradict theft because it is written proof in the books. The state inspector's water tests from 1908 onward would still refute watering because those tests were made and recorded independently. None of these three depends on the 1958 measurement of the pipettes.

**Did the Association's bookkeeper ever question the ledger entries that refute the accusation he made?**

No. Wilbur Teague was the bookkeeper who wrote the "station gain" line every year and who, at the 1925 arbitration hearing, accused Ansel Keddie of stealing cream and writing low tests to conceal it (r13). Yet Teague had written the very ledger lines that refute this accusation—the cream gain figures showing that cream arrived heavier than the tests would suggest. The sources do not record that Teague ever reconciled his accusation with his own ledger entries. Bertram Kyle, the clerk who copied those ledger lines, notes that the figures "never once crossed my mind to wonder at it" (r23), suggesting no one questioned the standing of the station gain line. There is no evidence Teague examined the receiving book alongside the test book, or that he noticed the contradiction between his accusation and the data he himself recorded.

### D3. Did the manager know the tests ran low before the glassware was condemned?

**Answer: Cannot be definitively determined from the sources.**

**Justification:**

Selby Vose became manager in 1911 (r23). From 1902 onward, complaints arose about low tests (r24). Vose signed the "station gain" line every year from 1911 onward without apparent objection (r23). However, the sources do not record any statement by Vose acknowledging that he knew the tests were low—only that he signed off on figures showing they were low. Bertram Kyle observes: "He was courteous to every man who came into that office... And he read the totals and he never read the lines above them. He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign" (r23). This suggests Vose might not have carefully examined the test figures or understood their significance. In June 1919, Vose circulated a warning about water in the milk (r23, r24), which implies he believed the low tests were caused by watering, not by a problem at the station itself. Yet he did not investigate or question the station's equipment or procedures. This suggests either he did not suspect the glass was faulty, or he did not examine the matter closely enough to discover a fault. When the state inspector condemned the glassware in June 1923, Vose ordered new glass without comment or investigation. There is no record of Vose stating before or after the 1923 condemnation whether he believed the low tests were caused by the glassware. The sources leave his knowledge state unclear.

## Section E — Contradictions (8 points)

### E1. Direct factual contradictions

**Contradiction 1: Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie**

*Conflicting claims:* r22 states Ivy Keddie is Ansel Keddie's "great-niece" (page 2). However, r22 later adds a note: "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece" (note added after publication).

*Which narrators made them:* Orin Falke (county agent) in the first publication; Orin Falke (same author) in the correction note.

*Resolution:* Ivy Keddie was the **granddaughter** of Ansel Keddie, not great-niece. The genealogy from r18 confirms this: Ansel had son Duncan (b. 1894); Duncan had daughter Ivy (b. 1931). The correction note is authoritative because it comes from the same author after verification, superseding the original error.

**Contradiction 2: Total loss in butterfat over the affected years**

*Conflicting claims:* r21 states "Sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat" were unpaid over the whole run of years. r19 states the bulletin calculates the loss as "nineteen thousand six hundred dollars ($19,600)." r16 states "Fat has been worth twenty-seven cents the pound." If 67,000 pounds × $0.27 = $18,090, not $19,600.

*Which narrators made them:* Cleve Oram (r21) cites 67,000 pounds; Ashlin Chronicle (r19) cites $19,600; Dr. Kepp (r16) cites the 27-cent price.

*Resolution:* The $19,600 figure likely uses a slightly higher per-pound price or more precise calculation than the "27 cents on average" stated by Kepp. Using $19,600 ÷ 67,000 ≈ $0.29 per pound, which is within the range of prices over the 26-year period. I take the **$19,600 total as the official published figure** from Bulletin No. 471 and the 67,000 pounds as a working estimate, both cited in r19 and r21. The method: defer to the published bulletin's calculation over the arithmetic from individual narrators.

**Contradiction 3: Date the station opened**

*Conflicting claims:* r4 (r18) states Ansel Keddie was "tester and separator man at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station... from the year it opened." r23 states the station opened "on the 4th of May, 1896." r20 references the invoice of April 14, 1897, with equipment "went into use on the first of May, 1897."

*Which narrators made them:* Duncan Keddie (r18) says "from the year it opened"; Orin Falke (r22) says "4th of May, 1896"; Junia Ammon (r20) says May 1, 1897; Bertram Kyle (r23) says "4th of May, 1896."

*Resolution:* The station **opened on May 4, 1896** (r22, r23 agree; Orin Falke was an official county agent compiling the record). The equipment invoice is dated April 14, 1897, with delivery May 1, 1897. The station operated with **borrowed glassware from the main creamery** from May 1896 to April 1897 (r23: "its own outfit came from the supply house and went into use on the first of May, 1898"—note the apparent typo, should be 1897 given the April 14, 1897 invoice). The opening date is 1896; the glassware defect began with the 1897 equipment. Method: defer to contemporary official record (Falke's county compilation citing multiple sources).

**Two narrators carrying the same wrong value:**

Dr. Roland Kepp (r16) and Nyle Grigg (r15) both cite Bulletin No. 214's finding that bog hay depresses butterfat by "two tenths" (0.20 points). However, Orin Falke's 1959 note (r22) states: "The College fed the bog hay under control and... it found the butter fat test depressed by two tenths in the winter feeding." But then the note adds Bulletin No. 214's actual summary: "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Falke concludes: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July. I withdraw the concession made on page four."

*Conflicting claims:* Kepp and Grigg both state two tenths (0.20); Bulletin No. 214 itself states five hundredths (0.05).

*Resolution:* Kepp and Grigg either misremembered or the bulletin's title/summary was misread by them. The **actual Bulletin No. 214 states 0.05, not 0.20**. This breaks the tie: the primary source (the bulletin itself, quoted by Falke) outranks the secondary sources' (Kepp's and Grigg's) recollection of it. Method: a quoted document outranks a narrator's memory.

**Narrator contradicting itself on the page:**

None found. Each narrator is internally consistent within their own retelling. Where retellings conflict with each other, it is between narrators, not within a single narrator's testimony.

## Section F — Theory (6 points)

### F1. Causal theory and competing explanations

**Single coherent causal theory:**

The Ostrey Hollow station systematically undercounted the butterfat in milk delivered by its patrons due to a single defect: the measuring pipettes supplied by Ammon & Sons in the 1897 equipment crate were manufactured with a mold error that caused them to deliver an average of 16.27 cubic centimeters instead of the marked 17.6 c.c.—a shortfall of 7.56%. Because milk adheres to glass, a short charge of milk into a test bottle whose graduated neck is ruled for a full charge yields a reading proportionally low. This defect was present continuously from May 1, 1897 through June 12, 1923—26 years and 1 month—during which time approximately 40 million pounds of milk was delivered, containing an estimated 67,000 pounds of butterfat that was never credited to the patrons due to the low test readings. The defect began immediately upon the installation of the 1897 equipment and persisted unchanged until the state dairy inspector ran the same pipettes against a burette on June 12, 1923, found them short, and condemned the lot. New, accurate glassware was installed by July 1923, at which point the station's average test immediately rose to 3.85 and remained there. The defect ended because the equipment was replaced, not because conditions on the farms changed. No person at the station or in the Association knew the glass was defective during its entire 26 years of use. The glass was not measured until Ivy Keddie recovered it from the loft in February 1958, 35 years after its condemnation, and measured it against a calibrated burette.

**Three competing explanations and the evidence that defeats each:**

1. **Water Addition (Selby Vose, manager, 1919).** Vose circulated a written warning to Ostrey Hollow patrons in June 1919 claiming they were adding water to their milk, which would lower both the weight and fat content of the milk. *Evidence that defeats it:* Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, tested the milk with a lactometer at every circuit visit from 1908 forward and never found added water at Ostrey Hollow (r22). The state's independent water-detection method contradicts the theory.

2. **Cream Theft (Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper, 1925).** Teague accused Ansel Keddie at the 1925 arbitration hearing of removing cream from the cans and writing low tests to conceal the theft. *Evidence that defeats it:* The 1911 borrowed-measure interval shows that when a different measure was used for nine weeks, the tests rose markedly and fell back when the original measure was restored. The same tester, same supervision, different glass—the glass was the variable (r22). Additionally, the station's own ledger shows "station gain" of 5.25% yearly, meaning cream arrived at Larrow Green heavier than the station's tests would suggest, not lighter (r23). If the tester were stealing cream, the received cream should show a loss, not a gain.

3. **Wet Hay (Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee, and Dr. Roland Kepp, 1926).** The committee reported that the wet hay of Hessel Bottom thins the cream due to nutritional deficiency, and Kepp cited State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 214 as independent confirmation that bog hay depresses butterfat. *Evidence that defeats it:* Bulletin No. 214 actually states that bog hay depresses butterfat by "not more than five hundredths" in winter and "nothing that could be measured" in summer (r22, note added). Five hundredths cannot account for the 19-hundredth gap observed every month, summer and winter alike (r22). Additionally, the 1911 interval shows the same herds with the same hay produced higher tests with a different measure, ruling out a permanent feed-based deficiency as the sole cause.

**Subsidiary questions:**

- **Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?** No. Alonzo Frick, the arbitration board's chair, later reflected that the crate of condemned glassware "sat up in that loft in the summer... twenty feet of ladder, thirty-five years, and two of the three sessions of that arbitration sat in the room underneath it" (r13). The board sat in the intake room (the weigh room) where the tests were made, directly beneath the loft where the crate lay. Yet there is no evidence the board examined the crate or even knew it was there (r23: "I could not have told you... that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked"). The board had the condemned crate two rooms away and never looked at it.

- **What did the tester himself believe the cause was?** In 1911, Ansel Keddie told his wife Orra it was "something in the glass" (r18). He noted only a question mark in his book at that entry and never dared put this suspicion in writing, believing he could not prove it against the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county (r18). He held this suspicion for fourteen more years but never voiced it publicly. He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove (r18). He died in 1934 without ever seeing his suspicion validated.

## Section G — Summary (4 points)

### G1. Summary in at most 150 words

For twenty-six years, the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association's Ostrey Hollow skimming station systematically underpaid six Hessel Bottom farms for their butterfat. Tests recorded 3.61% butterfat; the true content was approximately 3.80%, a gap of 0.19 points. Three competing theories emerged: that farmers were watering milk (disproven by state lactometer tests), that the tester was stealing cream (disproven by an independent experiment using a borrowed measure in 1911), and that wet hay depressed butterfat (insufficient to explain the consistent 0.19-point gap). The actual cause was discovered only in 1958: the measuring pipettes from the original 1897 equipment were manufactured short by 7.56%, delivering 16.27 c.c. instead of marked 17.6 c.c. A state inspector condemned them in 1923 without measuring precisely how short. They lay in the station loft for thirty-five years until Ivy Keddie recovered them, measured them, and proved they—not water, theft, or hay—were responsible. The tester, Ansel Keddie, was dismissed in 1925 without vindication and died in 1934. His name was cleared publicly in 1958, twenty-four years after his death.
