# Section A: Eight Stories

## Story 1: The Station Established

In the winter of 1895–96, the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, organized in 1889, built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley from its main creamery at Larrow Green. The station opened on May 4, 1896, to serve the farms of the upper valley, principally Hessel Bottom, which had six farms. Ansel Keddie, appointed from the opening, took the position of tester and separator man.

On April 14, 1897, the Association ordered a complete testing outfit from Ammon & Sons, a glassblowing firm at Tarnet. The order included six dozen milk-test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c., twelve dozen test bottles (8 percent neck), one acid measure, one four-bottle hand tester, and freight, totaling $77.93. This equipment arrived and went into use on May 1, 1897.

Ansel Keddie, married to Orra Brant (sister of Emmet Brant, the largest patron), kept meticulous records. He weighed the milk delivered to the station and recorded both his own test results and the results when the milk reached the main creamery at Larrow Green. For his first full year of 1910, the station received forty million pounds of milk across the full delivery years—approximately 1,600,000 pounds annually. The butterfat averaged twenty-five cents per pound over the long run.

The station's basic operation was established: farmers brought milk to the covered platform; the milk was weighed, sampled, and tested; the tester recorded results; the cream was hauled to Larrow Green; skim was returned to farmers. Keddie kept his own private weigh book alongside the station's test book. The procedure was identical to other cooperative creamery associations across the state, and nothing suggested anything was amiss. The documents that would later matter—the ledgers, test books, and equipment itself—were already in place.

## Story 2: The First Warning Sign—The Broken Glass Experiment (May 1911)

On May 8, 1911, Ansel Keddie came home cross about a broken measure—his own milk-test pipette had broken. The station immediately switched to a borrowed measuring glass from elsewhere, probably from the main creamery at Larrow Green. This makeshift solution lasted nine weeks, from May 8 until July 10, 1911.

During those nine weeks, the milk tests at Ostrey Hollow rose markedly. When the station returned to Keddie's own glassware on July 10, the tests fell back immediately to their former depressed level. Keddie noted the fact in his test book and marked it with a question mark—the only symbol he permitted himself when he could not prove something with certainty.

The variables in this accidental experiment were controlled: the same tester (Keddie), the same men, the same machinery, the same season, the same pasture. The herds of cows had not changed. Yet the recorded butterfat content jumped by nineteen hundredths of a point during the nine-week interval with the borrowed glass, then dropped back when his own glass was restored. This was the single piece of evidence that his own glass was delivering less than it was marked to deliver—that something physical, not something about the milk or the cows or the tester's skill, was causing the systematic shortfall.

Keddie kept this knowledge private. He never reported the anomaly to the Association. He wrote only the question mark. This act of careful silence would later be seen as his virtue and his doom: he would not record what he could not prove, though the evidence of his own eyes and the station's books had already provided proof.

## Story 3: The Persistent Discrepancy and Competing Theories (1902–1925)

From an early date—the first complaint of record was made in 1902—milk from Ostrey Hollow tested lower than milk from any other station in the county. The Ostrey Hollow average stood at 3.61 percent butterfat, while the Larrow Green creamery's average was 3.78 percent. The difference was constant: nineteen hundredths of a point, flat as a floor, in every month of the year, in June's flush and February's depth, regardless of season or weather or feed.

Over two decades, three competing explanations emerged. First: the patrons of Ostrey Hollow were adulterating their milk with water. The Association's manager, Selby Vose (son of Gideon Vose, the Association's founder and first president), circulated a written warning to the Ostrey Hollow patrons in June 1919, sent to nine families on nine letters, about adding water to milk. The patrons answered by silence—which in that valley was a shout. The manager's own investigation found no evidence of water; the state dairy inspector, Merle Strawn, tested with the lactometer at every circuit visit from 1908 onward and never found added water.

Second: the tester himself, Ansel Keddie, was removing cream and writing low tests to conceal the loss. This theory was urged at the 1925 arbitration hearing by an officer of the Association. But the nine weeks of 1911 disposed of it: the same man had made the tests under no different supervision, and the figures rose.

Third: the hay and pasture of Hessel Bottom, being wet ground, thinned the cream of cows fed upon them. A committee of the Association sampled the Hessel Bottom herds in 1908 and reported to this effect. A veterinarian at Ashlin wrote similarly in 1926. This theory had real merit—the bog meadows of Hessel Bottom were known for coarse hay. But a 1911 feeding trial by the State Agricultural College showed that bog hay depressed butter-fat tests by not more than five hundredths of a point in winter, and nothing measurable in summer. Five hundredths could not account for nineteen hundredths in July.

Three theories. None could stand. Yet the shortfall persisted, year after year, and nobody looked at a crate of glass.

## Story 4: The State Inspector's Condemnation (June 1922)

In June 1922, Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector for the Ordell County circuit, made a routine circuit visit to Ostrey Hollow station. He carried a calibrated burette, the standard instrument for measuring the accuracy of dairy glassware. He ran the station's milk pipettes—the very pipettes that had been ordered from Ammon & Sons in 1897 and placed in use on May 1, 1897—against the burette.

The pipettes would not deliver their marked capacity. Each one was blown on the stem 17.6 c.c., but when tested against the burette, they delivered materially less. Strawn condemned the entire lot on the spot. He directed the manager to order new glassware.

The Association wrote to Ammon & Sons on June 28, 1922, ordering replacement pipettes and test bottles. On July 2, Ammon & Sons replied that the order was entered and would ship that month, and wrote: "We regret the trouble. Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name."

This reply was technically truthful and practically evasive. The works records of Ammon & Sons had burned in a 1931 fire on the third floor, leaving only office documents—invoices, letter books, and day books. But no inquiry was made about what had gone wrong. The new glassware was installed. From 1924 onward, the station's average test stood at 3.85 and remained there. No one in the office connected the condemnation with the shortfall of the previous twenty-seven years. Strawn himself drew no inference about the years behind the defective glass. The crate containing the condemned pipettes was carried up into the loft over the intake room and left there, where it would sit for thirty-five years, during two sessions of arbitration held in the room below it.


# Section A: Eight Stories (continued)

## Story 5: The Arbitration and Dismissal (1925)

In January 1925, the patrons of Ostrey Hollow petitioned for relief, their patience exhausted. The Association appointed a board of arbitration, chaired by an attorney at Ashlin. The board sat from March 17 to May 4, 1925. Two of the three sittings were held at the station itself, in the weigh room, with the men standing outside in the road, the documents and machinery present for examination.

What passed across the desk of the office clerk, Bertram Kyle, to the board was four things: the station's test book in Ansel Keddie's hand; the receiving book showing what had come in; Emmet Brant's private weigh book, lent for the purpose; and the state inspector's notebook for 1922, when the glass was condemned. Four books lay in that room, twenty feet below where the condemned crate sat in the loft. No man on the board, by Kyle's certain knowledge, laid the second book beside the fourth and looked.

The board heard charges that Ansel Keddie had been cheating the patrons, that the tester had removed cream or manipulated tests. Keddie, present, answered the charges. The state inspector's lactometer tests—showing no added water—were introduced. The evidence that nothing was amiss was entered.

On May 4, 1925, the board handed down its finding: the patrons were awarded three thousand eight hundred dollars, "without admission," which is the language of payment without confession. But on April 30, 1925—four days before the finding—Ansel Keddie was dismissed from his position, a post he had held for twenty-nine seasons. He came up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm, hung it behind the shed door, and asked his wife what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it.

The dismissal said everything. The finding said nothing. Ansel Keddie lived nine more years, never asked back, with the imputation on him in that county. He died in 1934. The patrons received their money. Ansel Keddie received nothing, and nobody in the valley spoke the other thing aloud—not for nine years, not for thirty-three years.

## Story 6: The Discovery in the Loft (February 1958)

Ivy Keddie, Ansel Keddie's granddaughter, had taken her first degree at the State Agricultural College at Farland and stayed on for graduate work in dairy husbandry under Professor Leland Vaught. She was twenty-seven years old, grew up in Ashlin where her father Duncan taught school, and had spent years on this matter in her own time and largely at her own expense.

In February 1958, she went up a ladder into the loft over the station's intake room at Ostrey Hollow—the same loft that had been sealed above the arbitration hearing in 1925. Under dust and chaff and a tarpaulin, she found a wooden crate. Inside the crate was the station's original glassware, condemned in 1922. The crate contained the complete testing outfit: pipettes, test bottles, acid measures.

Six dozen pipettes had gone into that crate in 1897. Thirty-five years later, nine of them remained in a condition to be measured. Ivy Keddie carried the crate east to Farland. In March 1958, she measured the nine surviving pipettes one by one against a calibrated burette in a college laboratory.

Every one of them was blown on the stem 17.6 c.c.—the standard charge. Not one delivered it. The average of the lot came to 16.27 c.c., consistently short by 1.33 c.c., the same proportion in every pipette. The glass itself was the thing. It had always been the glass. Every man on that bottom had been paid short for butterfat they never received payment for. Sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat, over the whole run of years, was the total never paid for.

## Story 7: The Scientific Proof and the Bulletin (March–October 1958)

The measurements Ivy Keddie made in March 1958 established the physical fact: the glassware was short by approximately 7.55 percent (1.33 c.c. short on a 17.6 c.c. charge). When a short charge of milk goes into a test bottle whose neck is ruled for the full charge, the fat reads low in the same proportion—approximately 7.55 percent—every single time, for as long as the glass is used.

Ivy Keddie compiled these measurements with her advisor Professor Leland Vaught and additional sources: the state dairy office provided Merle Strawn's old circuit notebooks; Mrs. Rosalie Cudd of Vessey County, who managed the cheese company over the ridge (and was Jerome Cudd's daughter, keeper of her family's test book), provided access to production records. The college's Department of Dairy Husbandry prepared Bulletin No. 471, issued in October 1958, under the names of Professor Vaught and Miss Ivy Keddie.

The bulletin was temperate and did not overstate its case. It presented the physical measurements, the timeline, the cost to the patrons ($19,600—nearly two hundred times the cost of the original equipment in 1897, accounting for the money of the 1890s and early 1900s), and the conclusion: nobody watered anything, nobody stole anything, the scales were true, the acid was right, the cows were ordinary, and a piece of glass out of a crate was wrong.

The charge of the glass was short by approximately 1.33 c.c. per sample. Over twenty-six years of use (1897–1923), with two deliveries daily from multiple farms, the accumulated effect was sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat never credited and never paid for.

## Story 8: The Vindication and Its Limits (October 1958–1964)

On October 3, 1958, the Ashlin Chronicle ran the story: "Old Ordell Wrong Righted—College Bulletin Clears Station Tester Thirty-Three Years After He Was Turned Out." The article quoted Ivy Keddie: "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not. He tested the milk in front of the state's own man and he tested it the way he was taught, and the reason his figures would not come right was in a crate over his head the entire time. I would like it printed that he never knew. It would have been a kindness if somebody in this county had thought to climb a ladder."

Ansel Keddie had been dead for twenty-four years. His widow, Orra, who had kept all the letters from those years and lived with the imputation on him and on his name, died in 1948—ten years before the vindication. She did not live to see it.

The county agricultural agent, Orin Falke, prepared a formal report for the board of supervisors, documenting the matter for the county's permanent file. The county board took no action, all parties being dead and the Association's account long since closed. The surviving records were left with the Historical Rooms at Ashlin.

The vindication was complete but posthumous. Ansel Keddie's name was cleared. The community acknowledged what he had been doing all along: testing the milk correctly, the way he was taught, keeping his careful records, refusing to claim knowledge he did not possess. But he had paid for this virtue with his job, his reputation, and nine years of his life. The men who had worked alongside him said, at his funeral, what a hand he was with a door—the carpentry he took up after his dismissal—but not one of them said the other thing, not for nine years, not until after he was dead and could not hear it.

The crate was deposited with the Historical Rooms. Ivy Keddie's graduate work became a college bulletin and a permanent record. The eight stories—the station's opening, the broken glass experiment, the persistent discrepancy, the inspector's condemnation, the arbitration, the discovery, the measurement, and the vindication—had taken sixty-two years to fully unfold.

# Section B: Relationships

## B1: What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Duncan Keddie (r18) identifies Ivy as "my daughter," and Duncan was the son of Ansel Keddie. Therefore Ivy is Ansel's granddaughter. The Ashlin Chronicle (r19) mistakenly calls her his "great-niece" in the original article, but Orin Falke's 1959 report (r22) corrects this in an added note: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece." Duncan Keddie himself (r18) had already stated plainly: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter," and notes that he had "already twice seen it printed otherwise." The corrected fact: granddaughter.

## B2: What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant?

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. Hazel Brant (r17) establishes that Ivy is "Duncan Keddie's girl" (his daughter), and Hazel is "Roy's" daughter—Roy Brant, who farmed Hessel Bottom after his father Emmet. Duncan Keddie (r18) confirms the genealogy: Emmet Brant and Orra (Ansel Keddie's wife) were brother and sister; Roy was Emmet's son; Hazel was Roy's daughter. Duncan and Roy are therefore first cousins (children of brother and sister). Since Ivy is Duncan's daughter and Hazel is Roy's daughter, Ivy and Hazel are second cousins—the children of first cousins.

The intervening generations: Emmet Brant and Orra Brant were born to the same father and different mothers (half-siblings through their father's second marriage). Ansel Keddie married Orra in 1889, making him brother-in-law to Emmet. This placed the Keddie and Brant families in close relation, though they were not related by blood—only by marriage through Orra.

## B3: Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter to what happened at Ostrey Hollow?

Selby Vose was the son of Gideon Vose. Gideon Vose organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association in 1889 and was its first president; he wrote its by-laws. Selby Vose became manager of the Association in 1911 (r24) and held that position for a quarter of a century (r23), sitting behind the manager's door while Bertram Kyle sat outside at the outer desk.

Why this matters: Selby Vose, in his capacity as manager, signed the circular warning letter of June 1919 accusing the Ostrey Hollow patrons of adding water to their milk. He signed the boards' decisions to award arbitration money without admission. He sat above the ledgers that carried year after year the "station gain" line—5.25 percent, as usual—which Kyle copied dozens of times. Vose was described as "courteous to every man who came into that office" but as a manager who "read the totals and never read the lines above them." He would take a sheet, turn it, place his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign—a thousand times—without examining the detail. Duncan Keddie (r18) notes that Duncan's father Ansel was "the making of the record he left and, I have sometimes thought, the unmaking of him"—Ansel's meticulous record-keeping exposed what should have been noticed by those in authority.

## B4: What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?

No blood relationship existed between the Voses and the Keddies. Bertram Kyle (r23) states flatly: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses." Duncan Keddie (r18) confirms: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either."

However, Cleve Oram (r21) adds a note: "The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley. My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper." This rumor existed in the valley but was unsubstantiated. Duncan Keddie, who had his mother's careful attention to genealogy, would have known if such a connection existed; his categorical denial and his mother's silence on the matter carry weight against the rumor. The Vose-Keddie relationship was one of institutional authority (Vose as manager, Keddie as employee) and family connection only through marriage (Ansel Keddie to Orra Brant, whose brother Emmet interacted with Vose as a major patron)—but no direct Vose-Keddie tie.

# Section C: Mathematics

## C1: Proportion, true average, total butterfat loss, and value

**(a) By what proportion did tests differ from truth, and how derived from glassware?**

The defective pipettes delivered 16.27 c.c. when marked for 17.6 c.c. Short by: 17.6 - 16.27 = 1.33 c.c.
Proportion short: 1.33 ÷ 17.6 = 0.0755 or approximately 7.55% (short measure).
This is derived from Ivy Keddie's March 1958 measurements (r19, r22): "Every one of them was blown on the stem 17.6 c.c... The average of the lot came to 16.27 c.c."

When a short measure of milk enters a test bottle ruled for the full measure, the fat percentage reads low by the same proportion. The tester cannot see that the glass itself is the variable.

**(b) True average test at the station and points short**

Ostrey Hollow recorded average: 3.61% butterfat (r22, r23)
Larrow Green creamery average over same years: 3.78% (r22)
Difference recorded: 0.19 percentage points (19 hundredths)
The records show this 19-hundredth discrepancy "flat as a floor" month to month (r22, r17).

The recorded deficiency was 0.19 percentage points. The actual deficiency caused by the short glass was approximately 7.55% of the measured value. If the true butterfat was 3.78%, and the glass made it read 7.55% low, the recorded value would be 3.78 × (1 - 0.0755) = 3.78 × 0.9245 = 3.495%, or approximately 3.50%.

Wait—let me reconsider. The shortfall shown was consistently 0.19 percentage points. A 7.55% short measure on a true 3.78% test would reduce it by 0.286 percentage points (3.78 × 0.0755 = 0.286). This is slightly higher than the 0.19 recorded.

However, I should use what the sources establish. The sources state the discrepancy was 19 hundredths (0.19 percentage points) consistently. Therefore:
True average test = 3.61 + 0.19 = 3.80% (approximately, matching 3.78% at Larrow Green)
Recorded tests were short by 0.19 percentage points.

**(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited**

From r22 and r21: "Sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat was what they made it, over the whole run of years, that the Hollow men were never paid for."

The sources establish: 67,000 pounds of butterfat over the full delivery years.

**(d) Value in money and fraction received**

From r19 and r22: "The bulletin works out what the Ostrey Hollow patrons were never paid for the butter fat they actually hauled to that station, and the figure is nineteen thousand six hundred dollars ($19,600)."

From r23: The patrons received $3,800 in the 1925 arbitration award (r22: "thirty-eight hundred dollars, without admission").

Fraction received: $3,800 ÷ $19,600 = 0.1939 or approximately 19.4% (or roughly one-fifth).

The patrons received only about 19% of what they were owed.

## C3: Crediting and receiving over full delivery years

**(a) Total butterfat credited to patrons**

From r23: Milk received at station: 40 million pounds over the full delivery years (approximately 25 years of complaints, 1900–1925).

At recorded average of 3.61%: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 pounds butterfat credited.

**(b) Butterfat actually received at main creamery**

From r23: "So many pounds of butterfat credited to the patrons on the station test. So many pounds of butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station cream. And then the difference of the two, entered as station gain, so many pounds—and after it, always...five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account."

The "station gain" (loss) was consistently 5.25% of credited butterfat, entered as "usual" and carried to the general account (meaning it was treated as normal shrinkage, not investigated).

Received at creamery: 1,444,000 - (1,444,000 × 0.0525) = 1,444,000 × 0.9475 = 1,369,370 pounds.

**(c) Express difference as percentage**

Difference: 1,444,000 - 1,369,370 = 74,630 pounds.

As a percentage of what was credited: 74,630 ÷ 1,444,000 = 0.0517 or 5.17% (the station gain).

As a percentage of what was actually received: 74,630 ÷ 1,369,370 = 0.0545 or 5.45%.

The sources are examining the "as usual, five and a quarter per cent" line, which represents loss—the difference between what the station test said was there and what the main creamery received. This was treated as normal evaporation and handling loss, but it was actually partly artifice: the station's glass had already reduced the credited amount by 7.55%, so the "station gain" appeared to shrink the already-reduced figure by 5.25%, compounding the loss.

# Section D: Logic

## D1: The broken glass experiment of 1911—interval, variables, result, and what is not established

**When and how long:** May 8–July 10, 1911. Nine weeks.

**Variable:** The measuring glass (pipette). Ansel Keddie's own glass broke on May 8, 1911. A borrowed glass was immediately substituted—likely from the main creamery at Larrow Green. On July 10, when Keddie's own glass had presumably been repaired or replaced with his original, the station switched back.

**Held constant:** Same tester (Ansel Keddie), same patrons and herds, same machinery, same season (May through July—the flush season), same pasture, same feed, same testing procedure, same recording method. No personnel changes, no equipment changes except the pipette.

**Result:** During the nine weeks with the borrowed glass, the milk tests rose markedly. When the station returned to Keddie's own glass, the tests fell back immediately to their former depressed level. This is documented in Orin Falke's county report (r22): "On the 8th of May the station's own measuring glass was broken, and a measure borrowed from elsewhere was used in its place for nine weeks, until the 10th of July. Through those nine weeks the station's tests stood markedly higher. On the day the station resumed its own glass they fell back to their former level."

**What the interval establishes:** That something physical about Keddie's glass—not something about his skill, not something about the milk, not something about the cows—was causing the systematic shortfall. The borrowed glass delivered more fat-percentage readings; his own glass delivered less. This is the only direct experimental evidence in the record that the glass, not the tester or the milk, was responsible.

**What it does NOT establish:** Whether the borrowed glass was itself accurate, or merely less wrong. Whether all glassware at that time was similarly defective. Whether the defect was in the pipette, the test bottle, or both. The specific reason for the defect (mold reground, gauge misaligned, etc.). It establishes that Keddie's glass was problematic; it does not establish why or by how much.

Critically: Keddie noted the fact in his book and marked it with a question mark. He did not report it to the Association or to the manager. He kept silence. He was not asked to testify about this interval in the 1925 arbitration. This evidence—perhaps the clearest evidence—was never formally examined.

## D2: Three independent reasons why Ansel Keddie cannot have been stealing cream

**Reason 1 (The 1911 interval experiment):** If Keddie were removing cream and writing low tests to conceal the loss, his tests would have been artificially low because he had already removed the cream. When the borrowed glass was used—a glass that actually delivered what it was marked to deliver—tests should still have been artificially low due to cream removal. Instead, they rose. This is independent of any 1958 measurement. Orin Falke (r22) states: "It cannot be sustained either, and the nine weeks of 1911 dispose of it: the same man made the tests in that interval, under no different supervision, and the figures rose."

**Reason 2 (The state inspector's lactometer tests):** Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, tested with the lactometer at every circuit visit from 1908 onward. The lactometer detects added water (dilution). He never found added water at Ostrey Hollow (r22). If Keddie were stealing cream (removing butterfat), he would likely need to add water to restore volume and consistency, or the removal would be obvious. The absence of water detection argues against systematic cream theft.

**Reason 3 (The station gain line in the ledgers):** Bertram Kyle (r23) copied a ledger line every year: pounds of butterfat credited at the station test, pounds of butterfat received at Larrow Green, and the difference entered as "station gain"—about 5.25%, described as "as usual." If Keddie were stealing and the cream reaching Larrow Green were less than credited at the station, the "gain" line would show it. Instead, the line shows the expected loss (evaporation, handling), consistent with nothing missing between the two locations. This figure was copied, entered, and carried to the annual meeting for fifty farmers to hear—it was public and repeated. Kyle himself (r23) notes: "A figure with a name on it has been explained, and you rule under it and go on to the next." The ledger line accounting for what reached Larrow Green matches what was credited—no disappearance, no theft.

**Independence of these reasons:** Reason 1 is based on the timing of test results and the change of glass. Reason 2 is based on chemical composition (water detection). Reason 3 is based on the ledger accounting between two locations. They appeal to different evidence. Reason 1 would still hold if the glass had never been measured in 1958—the experiment of 1911 is self-contained. Reasons 2 and 3 are independent of each other (one tests the milk, one tests the accounting).

**Whether the bookkeeper questioned the ledger entries:** Wilbur Teague was the bookkeeper who wrote the station gain lines every year (r23). "A dry man, very correct...Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years. That is not a criticism. It was his notion of the position: the figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people." Teague did not question the entries. Bertram Kyle (r23) worked at the outer desk and copied Teague's work, but Kyle also did not question it: "It never once crossed my mind to wonder at it, and I do not believe it crossed anybody else's either."

## D3: Did the manager know the station's tests ran low before the glass was condemned in 1923?

**Answer:** The sources cannot determine whether Selby Vose consciously knew that the tests were systematically low. However, the evidence suggests he had access to information that should have prompted inquiry, but did not pursue it.

**Justification:** Selby Vose became manager in 1911 (r24). From 1911 onward, complaints about low tests at Ostrey Hollow were ongoing (the first complaint was in 1902, renewed in every decade). Vose sat behind the office door while all correspondence passed by Bertram Kyle's desk at the outer room. Kyle (r23) states: "everything which went in to him and everything which came out passed within a foot of my elbow."

Vose signed the ledger sheets every year (r23). These sheets carried the "station gain" line—the discrepancy between what the station test said was there and what Larrow Green received. Kyle (r23) notes: "He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign. I watched him do it a thousand times and thought nothing of it, because that is what managers were."

However, Kyle also notes (r23): "He read the totals and he never read the lines above them. He was not a man who read."

In 1919, Vose sent the water-warning circular to the Ostrey Hollow patrons, asserting their milk might be adulterated. This accusation addressed a proposed explanation for low tests. Vose's action suggests he was aware that tests ran low (otherwise, why warn about water?).

By 1922, when Inspector Strawn condemned the glass, Vose was manager and should have been informed. But no record suggests Vose connected the condemnation of glass in 1922 with the low tests of the previous 25 years. Kyle (r23): "There was no more said of it in the office than there would have been of a broken window. I entered the order and I filed his word for it and that was the end of the matter as far as the outer room was concerned."

Conclusion: Vose's awareness of low tests cannot be determined. He had access to information (complaints, the warning letter he sent, the ledgers he signed). But whether he understood the systematic nature of the problem, or investigated it, is not established. His passivity—his signature on totals without reading detail—may indicate lack of curiosity rather than lack of knowledge. The sources do not settle whether he knew; they indicate he did not investigate.

# Section F: Theory

## F1: Causal theory of the shortfall

**The Theory:**

The Ostrey Hollow testing station received defective milk-test pipettes from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet in 1897. These pipettes were blown (manufactured) with a systematic error: each was marked 17.6 c.c. (the standard charge for butter-fat testing) but delivered approximately 16.27 c.c.—roughly 1.33 c.c. short, or about 7.55% under specification. This defect was intrinsic to the glassware and unchanging. When less milk enters a test bottle (whose graduated neck is marked for a full 17.6 c.c. charge) than should be there, the fat percentage reads low by the same proportion. The error was not in the tester, not in the milk, not in the cows, not in the feed or pasture, and not in the machinery. It was in a piece of glass sitting on a shelf.

**Why this size of error:** Junia Ammon (r20), the company historian, offers her theory: "The mold had been reground and the lot ran short. It was an accident and nobody in the works caught it. A gauge sits on a nail and gets knocked off the nail and hung back on it; a mold comes back from the shop a hair different and looks exactly as it looked before; a boy checks the first of a lot and the tenth and the fiftieth and finds them consistent, because they are consistent—they are consistently wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind cannot find." The size of the error (1.33 c.c. short per 17.6 c.c. charge) represents the tolerance in the glassblowing process and the inconsistency in quality control—a systematic error introduced in manufacture that remained undetected through the 1897–1923 period.

**Why it began when it did:** The glass was ordered April 14, 1897, shipped from Tarnet, and put into use May 1, 1897. This was the beginning of the Ostrey Hollow station's second full season of operation (the first season, May 4, 1896 onward, had used borrowed equipment from the main creamery). The systematic testing records begin with the new glass, which is why the 25-year record of error runs 1897–1922.

**Why it ended when it did:** On June 12, 1922 (or June 1922), Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, conducted a routine circuit visit and tested the station's pipettes against a calibrated burette. He found they did not deliver their marked capacity and condemned the entire lot. New glass was ordered and installed by July 1922. From 1924 onward (the first full year on record with the new glass), the station's average test stood at 3.85 percent—aligned with other stations. The error ended because the physical cause (defective glass) was removed.

**Why nothing at the station or farms had to change for either beginning or ending:** The error was entirely a property of the glassware, not of management, testing procedure, animal husbandry, feed, pasture, or the tester's technique. Ansel Keddie tested consistently, followed protocol, kept careful records. The farmers delivered milk consistently. No change in practice occurred between 1897 (when the new glass arrived) and 1922 (when it was condemned), yet the error persisted. Conversely, no change in practice occurred between 1922 and 1924 other than the replacement of the glass, yet the error ended. This proves that the error was intrinsic to the equipment, not extrinsic to it.

**Three competing explanations and the evidence against each:**

1. **Explanation: Water-adultering by patrons.** Advanced by Selby Vose (manager), first articulated in his 1919 circular warning to the Ostrey Hollow patrons. The evidence that defeats it: Merle Strawn tested with the lactometer at every circuit visit from 1908 onward and never found added water (r22). The state inspector's lactometer tests are independent observations by a trained state official, with no interest in exonerating either the patrons or Keddie. This defeats the theory completely.

2. **Explanation: Cream removal and embezzlement by the tester, Ansel Keddie.** Advanced at the 1925 arbitration hearing by an officer of the Association. The evidence that defeats it: (a) The 1911 broken-glass experiment—when a borrowed glass was used for nine weeks, tests rose immediately, establishing that the glass, not the tester's practice, drove the low results. (b) Strawn's lactometer tests found no evidence of water addition (which would be necessary to conceal cream removal). (c) The station ledger's "station gain" line (r23) accounts for what reached the main creamery and matches what was credited at the station; no unaccounted-for loss of cream is recorded. If cream were being removed, the accounting would show it. These three independent lines of evidence eliminate the theory.

3. **Explanation: The hay and pasture of Hessel Bottom thin the cream.** Articulated by a 1908 committee of the Association and by a veterinarian in 1926. The evidence that defeats it: The State Agricultural College conducted a feeding trial with bog hay (cited in their 1911 Bulletin No. 214) and found that bog hay depressed butter-fat test by "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture" (r22). Five hundredths (0.05 percentage points) cannot account for nineteen hundredths (0.19 percentage points), particularly not in July when pasture is abundant. The 1911 broken-glass experiment also defeats this: the borrowed glass used in May–July 1911 delivered higher tests on the same pasture and the same herds, proving that feed is not the variable. Orin Falke (r22), after receiving the college's feeding trial results, withdrew this concession: "I withdraw the concession made on page four."

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?**

No. The crate of condemned glass sat in the loft above the weigh room where two of the three arbitration sessions were held, in March, April, and May 1925. Bertram Kyle (r23): "Four books lay in that room, twenty feet below where the condemned crate sat in the loft. No man on the board, by Kyle's certain knowledge, laid the second book beside the fourth and looked." The glass was never brought before the board. Cleve Oram (r21) stood outside on the platform during one afternoon session in the rain: "And the crate was up the ladder over their heads the entire time. Twenty feet, if that. I have thought about that since, I will not pretend I thought about it then." The board had access to the physical evidence but did not examine it.

**What the tester himself believed the cause was:**

Ansel Keddie believed something was wrong with the glass. Duncan Keddie (r18) states: "In the summer of 1911 [Ansel] came home saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book. I was seventeen and I remember the kitchen and I remember that he said it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not his way. What he wrote in the book was a question mark."

Keddie told his son. He did not tell the Association, did not formally accuse the equipment, did not insist on investigation. He was "a man who would not put down what he could not prove." Thus, he kept the suspicion private. He noted the question mark—the only symbol he permitted himself when he could not prove something. This was his virtue: scrupulous honesty about what he knew versus what he suspected. And this was his doom: he refused to cry out until he could prove, and by the time proof came (in 1958), he was thirty-four years dead.

# Section G: Summary

The eight stories chronicle a systematic measurement error at a small dairy testing station that persisted for twenty-six years and cost six families on a wet valley bottom sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat and nineteen thousand six hundred dollars—nearly all of which they never received payment for. In 1897, a glass company in Tarnet sold the station defective pipettes. No one detected the defect. For a quarter-century, the tester, Ansel Keddie, recorded numbers honestly; the farmers brought their milk faithfully; the creamery kept its books correctly. No one stole anything. The machinery worked. The science was sound. And nothing was amiss. Then, in 1922, an inspector condemned the glass. In 1925, Keddie was dismissed for cheating no one had found evidence of. In 1958, his granddaughter climbed a ladder, found the condemned crate in the loft, measured the glass, and proved he had been right all along. By then he had been dead for twenty-four years. The story required eight separate witnesses—a farmer's daughter with the weigh book, a schoolteacher son reconstructing genealogy, a newspaper editor reporting in a day and a half, a company historian remembering the firm's shame, a cream hauler's son recalling the wagon road, a county agent writing a report, an office clerk who had copied the ledgers, and the keeper's wife noting the dismissal—because no single person had seen the whole of it until long after it was over.
