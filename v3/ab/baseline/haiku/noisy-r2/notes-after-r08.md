# Retention Notes: Retellings 1–8

## Core Narrative Overview
The Ostrey Hollow station scandal: a milk testing station in a valley that served nine farming families from 1896 onward. In 1919, the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association sent a letter to these nine families claiming water was found in their milk cans. The station tester, Ansel Keddie, was eventually dismissed after a 1925 arbitration hearing despite the board finding "no cause established." The true cause, not discovered until 1958, was defective glass pipettes that systematically read 5% low (95% of the mark), causing patrons to lose approximately 76,000 pounds of butterfat worth roughly $19,000, yet receive only a $3,800 settlement (one-fifth the actual loss).

## People and Relationships

### Central Figures
- **Ansel Keddie** (d. 1934): Station tester 1896–1924/1925. Kept the test book himself in five columns (date, patron, pounds, test, fat). Married to Orra Keddie. Tested 31 times per week for 29 seasons. Claims he never took cream. Built a leaf of averages in 1922. Made the first entry 4 May 1896. The mysterious 1911 anomaly when a borrowed pipette showed 3.80 tests instead of the usual 3.61. Gave testimony 17 March 1925 to the arbitration board. Was dismissed on 30 April (presumably 1925) despite "no cause established." Died in the 1930s under the shadow of the accusations.

- **Orra Keddie**: Ansel's wife. First cousin of old Mrs. Vose (Gideon Vose's wife). Sister of Emmet Brant (the largest patron at Ostrey Hollow). Cut off from her brother Emmet after 1919; they lived within sight of each other's chimney smoke but did not speak for years. Outlived Ansel and continued to bear the community's judgment.

- **Emmet Brant**: Largest farmer on Hessel Bottom, the wet meadow below the hamlet. The station's largest patron in every recorded year. Had the most cows and most hay ground in the Bottom. Suffered most from the 1919 water letter since he brought the most milk. Became estranged from his sister Orra after 1919 when Orra's husband was implicated.

- **Gideon Vose** (d. 1917): First president of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association from 1889 to 1911 (22 years). Drafted the by-laws himself (not a lawyer, they "read as a farmer wrote them"). Wrote Article VII in its final form. Dead by 1917, so could not be asked about the meaning of Article VII's language.

- **Selby Vose**: Gideon Vose's nephew. Manager of the Association from 1913 to 1938. Nephew of old Gideon Vose (first president). Signed the letter to patrons in 1919 warning about water in cans. In his 1926 annual address, he argues that water was actually being added to cans and stopped when the Association began investigating. Sends the 1919 circular and defends it. States his theory that tests rose 21 hundredths in 1924 because the "looking" stopped the water-adding.

- **Effie Loomis**: Secretary of the Association 1901–1940. Wrote out the minute book in her own hand. Lost the 1889–1892 minute book when the office moved in 1938. Mentions in her notes that the crate of condemned measures was never mentioned in the 1925 arbitration hearing even though it was condemned in June 1923.

- **Wilbur Teague**: Bookkeeper 1905–1945. Invented the "station gain" line in 1905 as ordinary bookkeeping to account for the difference between butterfat credited to patrons on the station's test and butterfat actually received at Larrow Green. The station gain ran at 5.25% of the fat actually in the cream, year after year. Raised concerns with Selby Vose in 1916 about the station account but was told to let it alone. Laid the annual sheet before Vose each spring and Vose signed it; thus Vose knew what the account was doing.

- **Ivy Keddie**: Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Gave a talk to the Ordell County Historical Society in 1959. Found the crate of condemned pipettes in the station building loft in February of last year (1958). Measured 9 unbroken pipettes at Farland on 9 March 1958 using a calibrated burette. Found they delivered 16.72 c.c. instead of 17.6 c.c. (95% of the mark, or short 0.88 c.c.). This proved every test read 95% of the truth, a 5% error. Calculated that over 25 years patrons lost 76,000 pounds of butterfat worth $19,000 while receiving only $3,800. Her father was Duncan Keddie, who taught school at Ashlin.

- **Merle Strawn**: State dairy inspector on the Ordell circuit from 1907 to 1936. Made routine inspections of Ostrey Hollow station and recorded findings in pocket circuit books. On 12 June 1923, ran the station's milk pipettes against a burette (newly issued to field men in 1921) and found "These measures will not deliver their mark." Condemned the whole crate and told the manager to order new glass. Tested for added water at every visit to the station and never found any. Noted no added water repeatedly (1919, 1920, 1922, 1924, 1925).

- **Vira Toland** (b. 1912): Born at the top end of Ostrey Hollow in her father's father's house. Her father hauled milk to the station. Nine families hauled to the station together. Witnessed the 1919 letter arriving, remembered it raining that day. Her father kept the letter in his desk drawer until he died. Recalls that a young woman from the college came round in 1958 asking questions and was related to the Brants (Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed).

- **Hobart Sill**: Buttermaker at Larrow Green creamery. Had the churn book and cream receiving book. Never went up to the Ostrey Hollow station.

- **Alonzo Frick**: Attorney of Ashlin. Chairman of the board of arbitration 1925. Described by Wilbur Teague as "as fair a chairman as ever a hearing had."

## The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association

### Founding and Structure
- Organized in 1889 at Larrow Green by farmers of the Larrow Valley.
- Gideon Vose elected first president at the 1889 meeting, served until 1911 (22 years).
- Main creamery built at Larrow Green in 1891 on Larrow Creek (same building standing in subsequent years).
- Before the creamery, farmers sold milk where they could get a price with no common test and no common price.
- Three main facilities: creamery at Larrow Green, office at Ashlin (one room over a harness shop), skimming station at Ostrey Hollow (seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green).

### Article VII (adopted annual meeting 1892)
Quoted in full in multiple sources:
> *Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*

Effie Loomis notes this was a just rule but the last clause is "the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business." The critical phrase is "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" — not the test of the creamery, not the average of the valley, not a test made over again anywhere else. No appeal from the station's test.

### Ostrey Hollow Station Operations
- Opened 1896 (Ansel Keddie took it to 1895 to prepare it; first season partially recorded from 4 May 1896).
- Located at the head of Ostrey Hollow where the road bends and goes to nothing but the ridge.
- Low building, whitewashed, cold as a spring house even in August. Smelled of milk, steam, and hot iron. Engine audible before arriving.
- Seven farms had rights on Hessel Bottom (the wet meadow below the hamlet). Cut hay and pastured there.
- Emmet Brant's farm was the largest of the seven. He was the station's largest patron in every recorded year.
- Station operations: took in milk, separated it, sent cream down to Larrow Green, gave skim back to farmers for hogs and calves.
- One man held multiple operations spring through fall: weighed milk, drew samples, made tests, wrote figures for pay slip, ran separator. No second man in that building spring to fall. Wilbur Teague notes this concentration of duties in a single hand was a structural vulnerability.
- Cream shipped down daily and sweet (by 1912). Cold room quality noted by inspectors.

## Testing Procedures (from Ansel Keddie's unsent letter)

### Process (described in detail)
Work began at 5:30 a.m. Wagons came up in darkness most of the year. Intake room open, lamps lit, scale swinging free before first wagon.

1. **Weighing**: Every can weighed on the scale (tested by state man, true), weight called and written before milk went elsewhere.
2. **Sampling**: Sample taken from each patron's milk as it was weighed. Samples went into patron's own jar, numbered to patron. Jar made up over 10 days before testing.
3. **Preparation**: On testing morning, jar set in warm water until blood-warm throughout. Poured from vessel to vessel, poured again, until cream carried down through whole mixture. A sample not warmed and mixed is worth nothing.
4. **Charge**: Milk drawn up in measure to mark on stem, tip wiped, charge blown into bottle with patron's number.
5. **Acid**: Acid poured down the side, bottle turned until milk dark.
6. **Testing**: Four bottles in hand tester, weighted against each other, cover down, crank turned at learned speed (no man made Ansel hurry it). Fat rises in clear column in bottle neck.
7. **Reading**: Fat read off in percent against rulings with dividers. Figure written down.

This procedure was the same every time. Ansel claims he "tested as I was taught and as the state man saw me do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons."

## Test Book and Figures

### Ansel's Test Book
- Half-bound, marbled boards, back mended with cloth.
- Columns: date, patron, pounds, test (percentage), fat (pounds).
- Kept by one hand (Ansel's) for 29 seasons (1896–1924).
- Ansel's note on first leaf: "Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie."
- Entry discipline: when Ansel made an error, he crossed it and put his initials against the crossing. Never wrote figure over figure.
- Made a summary leaf in 1922 with yearly averages:
  - 1896: 3.80
  - 1897: part year
  - 1898: 3.61
  - 1898–1910: 3.61 or within three hundredths
  - 1911 (nine weeks): 3.80 (anomaly with borrowed measure)
  - 1912–1922 (or 1924?): 3.61 or within three hundredths

### 1911 Anomaly (Merle Strawn's and Ansel's explanations)
- Station's own measure broke 8 May 1911.
- Used borrowed measure from 8 May to 10 July 1911 (approximately nine weeks).
- Tests stood at 3.80 during this period. When station's own measure returned 10 July, tests went back down to 3.61 the same day.
- Ansel noted in his book: "May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." and "July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."
- Ansel carried a question mark for 14 years (since 1911, writing in 1925). Could not answer it then, could not answer it in his 1925 letter.
- Merle Strawn noted in 1911: "Watched a test through. Nothing here to note."
- Later explanation: the borrowed measure from Larrow Green was accurate (delivered its mark). The station's own glass was defective (delivered only 95% of mark). This explains why tests were correct (3.80, true value) with the good glass and incorrect (3.61, 5% low) with the bad glass.

### Wilbur Teague's Station Gain Column
- Created when he arrived as bookkeeper in 1905 (not "some old dodge of the founders").
- Ordinary bookkeeping to account for a consistent discrepancy: butterfat credited to patrons on station test vs. butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in station cream.
- The second figure (actual received) was always larger.
- Difference carried to general account as "station gain."
- Year-by-year entry (example 1898): "butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account."
- "As usual" phrase stopped the bookkeeper looking at it. A figure that came in at 5.25% every year became "furniture."
- Over the years, the station account ran: 44 million pounds of milk intake (Wilbur says) or 40 million pounds (Effie says) across approximately 25 full years 1898–1922.
- Average test of all Ostrey Hollow patrons taken over the whole period: 3.16 (Wilbur's figure). Over the same period, Larrow Green did better. Ostrey Hollow men "not a season in twenty-five years when the Ostrey Hollow men were not sore about it."

## The 1919 Water Letter

- January 1919: printed letter from the Association over manager Selby Vose's signature.
- Stated: "There is water in the cans coming down from Ostrey Hollow. That is what it said. Water in the cans."
- Sent to nine families, including Vira's father.
- Vira's father read it at table with his hat still on (unusual for him), twice. Put it in desk drawer. It was still there when he died. "Some of them kept theirs till they died. One woman I knew had hers pinned inside the lid of her trunk."
- One year later, Merle Strawn (state inspector) noted in 1919: "The manager of the Association has sent a circular round to the patrons of this station about water in the cans. I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so."
- Again in 1920, 1922, 1924, 1925, Merle Strawn recorded "no added water" at Ostrey Hollow.

### Selby Vose's 1926 Defense of the 1919 Letter
- Defends the 1919 circular as his duty to write.
- Argues: "A little water in a can is not a felony in a man's own mind. It is a way of making a hard year come out even, and the man who does it tells himself he will stop when the price improves."
- Says he wrote it "without naming a name, because I do not know a name and I would not use it if I did."
- Claims it "warned the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, in temperate language, that added water would be looked for and would be dealt with."
- Asserts: "A manager who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing to the men who make them is not being kind to them; he is being idle at their expense."

## The 1925 Arbitration Hearing

### Proceedings
- Date: 17 March to 4 May 1925 at Ashlin.
- Chairman: Alonzo Frick, attorney of Ashlin.
- Petition: In January 1925, patrons of Ostrey Hollow petitioned the Association claiming their milk never tested as neighbors' milk tested and they had been paid accordingly for a very long time.
- Participants: patrons, officers, board members. Wilbur Teague testified (as bookkeeper). Ansel Keddie gave statement on 17 March.
- Books, appliances, and records of the station were examined.

### The Finding and Award
- Finding: "The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established."
- Award: "The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account."
- Effie Loomis: "Finds no cause established, and then an award of thirty-eight hundred dollars: I took it down as it was given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once."
- Wilbur Teague's 1925 statement to the board (repeated in his 1948 memoir): "Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons... a business which is arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken from it, and that no set of books ever made can prevent it. The remedy is two men or a lock, and the Association had neither."
- Selby Vose's 1926 response: He supported the payment and still supported it in 1926. Describes it as "a generous settlement of an unproved claim." States: "This Association paid thirty-eight hundred dollars rather than let a body of its own members go on believing they had been used badly by their neighbors. There is not a business house in Ordell County that would have done it. We did it because we are not a business house; we are the farmers of this valley in company together."

## The Glass Defect (discovered 1958, explained 1923)

### Equipment Purchase
- Invoice from Ammon & Sons Dairy Supply, Tarnet, dated 14 April 1897:
  - 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — $25.20
  - 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at .22 — $31.68
  - 1 acid measure — $.90
  - 1 hand tester, 4 bottle — $18.00
  - Freight — $2.15
  - Total — $77.93
- Invoice office file shows in use at station from 1 May 1897 onward.
- The glass: six dozen pipettes, blown at 17.6 c.c. (the mark the bottles' necks were ruled for).

### June 1923 Inspection and Condemnation
- Merle Strawn, state inspector, visited 12 June 1923 with a burette (newly issued to field men in 1921 for checking delivery of measures and pipettes).
- Field book entry: "Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk pipettes against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them."
- New glass ordered by 28 June 1923, arrived by October 1923. Merle Strawn returned in October: "New glass in use since July. Ran the new pipettes against the burette. They deliver their mark. Scales true."
- Note: Effie Loomis observed that "the crate" was never mentioned in the 1925 arbitration hearing, though it was condemned in June 1923. "I took every word down and it is not there."

### The Crate Discovery (1958)
- February 1958: Ivy Keddie found a wooden crate stenciled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET" in the loft of the station building (which had been used for storage since closing).
- Crate carried up in June 1923, found still there in 1958 (35 years later).
- Inside: condemned milk-test pipettes, packed in straw.
- Nine pipettes unbroken in the crate.

### Measurement of Defective Pipettes (9 March 1958)
- Ivy Keddie, at Farland using a calibrated burette, measured the nine pipettes on 9 March 1958.
- Her measurement sheet (Item 14 in Halbert Nym's accession notes):
  - Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c.
  - Delivered against burette, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71.
  - Mean delivery: 16.72 c.c.
  - Short: 0.88 c.c.
  - That is 95 hundredths of the mark.
  - The mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for.

### Mathematical Analysis (Ivy Keddie's 1959 presentation)
- A butterfat test is made by drawing a measured charge into a bottle whose neck is ruled to read percent for that charge and no other.
- Put in a short charge and you get a short reading, in the same proportion.
- Pipettes delivered 16.72 where blown for 17.6.
- 16.72 / 17.6 = 0.95 (exactly).
- Every test made at that station with that glass read 95% of the truth, or 5% low.
- Recorded average over period: 3.61 butterfat.
- True average: 3.61 / 0.95 = 3.80 butterfat.
- The gap is 0.19 (nineteen hundredths). This is 5% of 3.80.
- The error was proportional (5% loss), so it fell hardest on the best milk:
  - Herd recorded at 4.56: short 0.24 of a point.
  - Herd recorded at 3.42: short 0.18.
  - The richer the milk, the more was taken from it.

### Total Loss Calculation
- Over 25 full delivery years, 1898 to 1922:
  - Station took in 40 million pounds of milk (per Effie; Wilbur says 44 million, curator notes say 40,000,000 lb for these years).
  - Average of 1.6 million pounds per year.
  - At recorded 3.61%, patrons credited with 1,444,000 lb butterfat.
  - At true 3.80%, they delivered 1,520,000 lb butterfat.
  - Difference: 76,000 lb butterfat.
  - Butterfat averaged 25 cents per pound over the period.
  - Unpaid fat worth: 76,000 × $0.25 = $19,000.
  - Settlement: $3,800 (one-fifth of actual loss).

### Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958)
- State Agricultural College, issued after Ivy's measurement sheet.
- Closing paragraph: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

### Timeline
- 1897: Defective glass put into service 1 May 1897.
- 1911: Anomaly shows (with good borrowed glass).
- 1919: Water warning letter sent (actually measuring defect being called water).
- 1923: 12 June — glass condemned by Merle Strawn. New glass ordered, in use by July.
- 1924: Tests rise to 3.80 (improved tests, attributed by Selby Vose to water-adding stopping).
- 1925: Arbitration board awards $3,800 despite finding no cause.
- 1934: Ansel Keddie dies, still under the shadow.
- 1958: Crate found with condemned glass. Ivy measures it (9 March). State bulletin (October) exonerates Ansel.
- Ansel was "dismissed in 1925: thirty-three years. He died in 1934 and had been dead twenty-four years when the sheet was read that clears him." (Ivy's chronology, 1959)

## Competing Narratives and Explanations

### Official 1908 Committee Explanation
- Committee visited, sampled herds in January, February, March 1908.
- Finding: "The milk is whole and the scales are honest. It is our opinion that the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station."
- State Bulletin No. 214 (1911) tested this theory:
  - "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."
  - Result: 5 hundredths maximum in winter, nothing in summer.
  - Gap at Ostrey Hollow: 19 hundredths, flat every month of every year, July and February alike.
  - Scientific conclusion: four times too small, wrong shape.
  - Additional evidence: over the ridge, the Alder Corners Cheese Company tested milk of four Hessel Bottom men in summer flush; where the two books overlap (14 seasons), that same milk off the same wet ground ran about 19 hundredths higher at Alder Corners than at home (Ostrey Hollow).

### Selby Vose's 1926 Explanation
- In his 1926 annual address, Selby proposes his own theory: water was being added by one or more farmers at Ostrey Hollow.
- Evidence he cites:
  1. The rise of tests in 1924 (from 3.61 to 3.90, or rise of 21 hundredths).
  2. "For a generation the milk brought to that station tested below the milk brought to Larrow Green... Then the Association's attention came to rest on the station, and there was a petition, and a hearing, and men were required to answer questions in front of their neighbors. And in the very next season the tests at that station rose by twenty-one hundredths..."
  3. "Nothing was done to those cows. Nothing was done to that bottom land. Nobody changed their feed and nobody changed their pasture. The tests came up when the looking began, and any man who wants a different explanation of that is welcome to stand where I am standing and give us one."
- Dismisses the inspector's role: "The state man's visit of that period explains the whole thing. It does not. He came through the county as he came through it every year or two, he tried our scales against his weights and found them true, and he condemned the station's testing apparatus on the twelfth of July, 1923, and told us to order new... Apparatus wears... I decline to build a mystery on a piece of housekeeping that appears in our own order book with a date and a price against it."
  - Note: Merle Strawn condemned the glass 12 June 1923 (not July), and ordered new glass which arrived by October 1923.
- Selby's character defense: "I am aware that this is not a comfortable thing to say to a room with Ostrey Hollow men in it, and I say it without naming a name, because I do not know a name and I would not use it if I did. A little water in a can is not a felony in a man's own mind. It is a way of making a hard year come out even, and the man who does it tells himself he will stop when the price improves. I have no wish to see anybody prosecuted, and nobody has been or will be."
- His Jersey herd: "My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law under which every patron in this Association is paid on the test of the station where he delivers, kept a herd of Jerseys at the home place... In 1910 that herd tested 4.56."

### Ansel Keddie's 1925 Defense
- His statement 17 March 1925 (nine sentences, as given):
  - "I have kept the test book of this station in my own hand since the station opened, and there is not a figure in it that I did not put there."
  - Details his error-correction method (line through, initials).
  - "I have made the tests in the same order and at the same hour of every working day of every season."
  - "I have heard what the patrons say of me and I have heard what the officers say of me. Neither of them has come up that road on a working morning and stood at my elbow and watched the work done."
  - "I am asked to account for figures, and the figures are what I read and what I wrote and nothing besides."
  - "I am an old man and I have no other trade. I have tested as I was taught and as the state man saw me do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, and I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are."
  - "I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself." (An appeal to the men's private beliefs.)

- His unsent letter (spring 1925):
  - Detailed technical description of the testing process (see "Testing Procedures" above).
  - His complaint: "I set this down because I could not say it in that room, where a man is allowed to answer and not allowed to explain..."
  - On the 1911 anomaly: "Those nine weeks in 1911 are the strangest thing in my book and I have never had an account of them to give... I could not answer it then and I cannot answer it now, and I will not put anything in its place that I do not know."
  - On the gap: "They did not come and go with the season. The gap was there in July as it was there in February, in the flush of the grass and in the dead of the winter on dry hay, and it was the same in a wet year as in a dry one."
  - Chronology of complaints: "The complaints began in 1902... In 1908 a committee came up and sampled the herds through the winter and reported that the wet hay of the Bottom thinned the cream... In 1919 a circular went out from the Association to nine families up here saying there was water in their cans. I carry no charge against any man who put his name to that paper. I only say that I stood in that room every morning of those years and I know what came into it, and that nine kitchens in this Hollow have had that letter in a drawer ever since."
  - On his estrangement: "My wife Orra is Emmet Brant's sister. Emmet brought more milk to my door than any other man on the Bottom, and every pound of it I weighed myself and every test of it I made myself and wrote in the book. He and I have not spoken since the circular. That is the plainest fact in this letter and it is the one I find I have the least to say about."
  - On the dismissal: "I was put out on the thirtieth of April. They found no cause established and they put me out on the last day of the month, and both of those things are true at once, and I have given up trying to hold them in my head together. I have tested as I was taught. I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are."

## Merle Strawn's Inspection Notes (1907–1936)

Full chronology of Ostrey Hollow entries:
- **1907 (first round)**: Scales true, cold room fair, tester keeps book in his hand (hand better than most), watched two tests (slow, clean, reads own figure without coaxing).
- **1909 (fall)**: Scales true, boiler in order, safety tried, milk sweet, cans clean, one man cautioned about rusted seam.
- **1911 (June)**: Scales true, watched test, nothing to note.
- **1912**: Scales true, lactometer no added water, cream shipped same day separated.
- **1913**: (No Ostrey Hollow entry, but notes new manager at Association, comes out with Merle, which last one never did.)
- **1915**: Scales true, wash room floor holding water at drain (ordered grading).
- **1916**: Scales true, lactometer no water, tester complains patrons blame him for figures. Merle tells him "that is the trade."
- **1917**: (No specific Ostrey Hollow entry, but notes plants short-handed.)
- **1919**: Scales true, lactometer no water, manager sent circular about water. Merle says he tested for water every visit and found none.
- **1920** (spring and fall): Scales true both times, lactometer no water, cold room short of ice in fall (reported, remedied).
- **1921**: Notes state began issuing burettes to field men this year.
- **1922**: Scales true, lactometer no water, boiler in order, but "I did not run their glass against the burette this year, the day being short and the road home being what it was, and I have put that down because it is what happened."
- **1923 June 12**: "Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk pipettes against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them."
- **1923 October**: New glass in use since July. Ran new pipettes against burette. They deliver mark. Scales true.
- **1924**: Scales true, no water, tests improved. Their book gives year at 3.80.
- **1925** (fall): Scales true, no water, tests improved. New man at tester's bench, quick (and quick not what Merle looks for).
- **1926** (note on burette program): Word from state office that program discontinued. Instruments kept by men but no more issued or returns wanted.
- **1928**: Scales true, lactometer no water, station quieter than it was. Loft over intake room full of odds and ends (told them a loft over milk room is place for nothing but air; told in return it had been full since before the war).
- **1931** (same week as Larrow Green): Both in order. Bad ice year, every plant short.
- **1934**: Scales true, lactometer no water, separator bearing run hot, shut down two days (repaired). Tester's book kept up, though not in hand it used to be.
- **1936 March** (last round): Scales true. Nothing to note.

## Documentary Evidence (Catalogued by Halbert Nym, 1972)

The curator organized 16 items comprising the complete documentary record:

1. **By-laws** (printed, no year): Article VII in full.
2. **Invoice, Ammon & Sons, 14 April 1897**: Equipment totaling $77.93 (six dozen pipettes at 17.6 c.c., 12 dozen bottles, acid measure, hand tester, freight).
3. **Station test book** (Ansel Keddie, one hand, 1896–1924): First leaf entry and summary leaf with averages. Note in catalog: "kept by E. Brant" (presumably meaning provenance or who it belonged to).
4. **Farm weigh book** (attributed to Ansel Keddie in header, but provenance shows Emmet Brant ownership from 1897): weights delivered and test paid on.
5. **Crate with nine pipettes**: Condemned 12 June 1923. Nine pipettes survive, measured at 16.72 c.c. mean delivery. Marked much stained.
6. **Annual ledgers, station account**: Example entry for 1898 (given by Wilbur Teague). Shows station gain of 5.25% accumulating to 76,000 lb butterfat over 1898–1922 from 40 million lb intake.
7. **Minute book of board of arbitration** (17 March–4 May 1925): Award entry in hand of Association secretary (Effie Loomis).
8. **Statement of A. Keddie** (17 March 1925): One leaf, ruled, both sides, in hand of test book, signed. Not transcribed in catalog.
9. **Photostat of Merle Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook**: Entry of 12 June with condemnation. Original kept by state (to state dairy office 1936, to State Ag. College library 1947).
10. **Letter, Ammon & Sons to Association** (2 July 1923): Acknowledging order of 28 June for replacement pipettes, promising shipment, declining to say anything of earlier lot.
11. **Report of 1908 investigating committee** (fair copy, signed by chairman): Sampled herds January–March 1908. Opinion: wet hay thinned cream, remedy lies with land.
12. **State Bulletin No. 214** (1911): About bog hay effect on butterfat (five hundredths max in winter, nothing in summer).
13. **Correspondence file, Vessey County**: Figures from Alder Corners Cheese Company test book, 1919 season (never transcribed; original in Vessey County Historical Society since 1970).
14. **Measurement sheet** (9 March 1958, Ivy Keddie): Nine pipettes measured against burette, mean 16.72 c.c., short 0.88 c.c. (95% of mark).
15. **State Bulletin No. 471** (October 1958): Official exoneration: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there... the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty..."
16. **Receiving and churn books of Larrow Green creamery** (water-damaged): Cream in, butter out, creamery's own tests. Over same years as station's 3.61, Larrow Green average runs under two tenths better.

## Unresolved or Disputed Elements

1. **Exact figures for total milk intake**: Effie Loomis says 40 million pounds over 25 years. Wilbur Teague says 44 million. Curator's notes cite 40,000,000 lb for 1898–1922 period.

2. **Average test figures**: Ansel's summary shows 3.61 average (or within three hundredths) from 1898 onward. Wilbur Teague cites 3.16 as the overall average test of Ostrey Hollow patrons over the whole period. The difference may reflect methodology (Ansel's per-year average vs. Wilbur's aggregate).

3. **Rise in 1924 tests**: Selby Vose claims tests rose 21 hundredths in 1924 from previous 3.61 to reach 3.90. But Merle Strawn's 1924 note says "tests improved. Their book gives the year at 3.80." The true value would be 3.80 (correct with new good glass), and Ansel's recorded figure would have been lower (3.61) with the old bad glass. The discrepancy in Selby's 3.90 figure is unexplained.

4. **Exact dismissal date of Ansel**: Ansel says "put out on the thirtieth of April" in his unsent letter (April 1925 presumed). Whether this was 1925 or another year is not explicitly stated in his letter, but context (1925 hearing was spring, letter written spring 1925) suggests April 30, 1925.

5. **Why the crate was never mentioned in 1925**: Effie Loomis notes it explicitly: the condemned pipettes found in June 1923 were never brought up in the arbitration hearing. The award's phrase "books and appliances of the station" is vague. The crate was stored in the loft. It's possible the board didn't know of it, or the patrons' attorney didn't think to mention it, or it was deliberately omitted.

## Key Relationships and Conflicts

- **Ansel and Emmet Brant**: Before 1919, families "ate Sunday dinners together." After 1919, never spoke. Emmet suffered most from the water letter since he had the most milk.
- **Vose family**: Gideon (founder) and nephew Selby both presidents/managers. Both signed documents related to the affair (Gideon wrote Article VII; Selby signed 1919 letter). Both likely knew more than they said.
- **Wilbur Teague and Selby Vose**: Wilbur raised concerns in 1916, was told to drop it. Yet Wilbur kept meticulous books and Vose signed the sheets every year, so Vose was aware.
- **Three explanations for the gap**:
  1. Water being added (1919 letter, Selby Vose's theory).
  2. Defective glass (true explanation, discovered 1958).
  3. Wet hay of Hessel Bottom (1908 committee, disproved by science).

## Chronology of Key Events

- **1889**: Association organized at Larrow Green. Gideon Vose elected first president.
- **1892**: Annual meeting adopts Article VII (test of the station governs).
- **1896**: Ansel Keddie prepares station. First entry 4 May 1896.
- **1897**: Ammon & Sons equipment invoice (14 April). Station operational 1 May with borrowed glass from Larrow Green. Station's own glass installed spring 1897 (defective glass, 17.6 c.c. blown mark but delivering 16.72 c.c., 95% of mark).
- **1902**: Complaints begin that Ostrey Hollow milk doesn't test like the rest.
- **1908**: Investigating committee samples herds winter, reports wet hay as cause.
- **1911**: Station's measure breaks (8 May). Borrowed good measure from Larrow Green used 8 May–10 July. Tests read 3.80 (correct). Own measure returned 10 July, tests back to 3.61 (5% low).
- **1913**: Selby Vose becomes manager.
- **1916**: Wilbur Teague raises concerns with Vose; told to let it alone.
- **1919**: Selby Vose issues water warning circular to nine families.
- **1921**: Merle Strawn begins receiving burettes for checking glass.
- **1922**: Ansel makes summary leaf of averages.
- **1923**:
  - 12 June: Merle Strawn condemns pipettes against burette. Orders new glass.
  - October: New glass installed, runs true against burette.
- **1924**: Tests rise to 3.80 (correct with good glass).
- **1925**:
  - January: Petition filed.
  - 17 March–4 May: Arbitration hearing.
  - Award of $3,800 "without admission."
  - 30 April: Ansel dismissed.
  - Selby Vose disputes water theory, supports settlement.
- **1926**: Selby's annual address explains his water-adding theory.
- **1934**: Ansel Keddie dies.
- **1936**: Merle Strawn retires from dairy inspection.
- **1941**: Effie Loomis writes "Notes toward a history."
- **1948**: Wilbur Teague writes memoir chapter on station account.
- **1958**:
  - February: Ivy Keddie finds crate.
  - 9 March: Ivy measures pipettes.
  - October: State Bulletin 471 exonerates Ansel.
- **1959**: Ivy Keddie speaks to Ordell County Historical Society.
- **1972**: Halbert Nym catalogues 16 items of evidence.

## Quotes of Particular Importance

From Effie Loomis on the crate: "The crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all... Forty years of taking down what men say has taught me that the interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying."

From Ivy Keddie on the truth: "He was dismissed in 1925: thirty-three years. He died in 1934 and had been dead twenty-four years when the sheet was read that clears him. I have no remedy to propose. I have only the crate, and it is on the table, and you may look into it on your way out."

From Selby Vose (1926): "I will not have peace bought a second time..."

From Wilbur Teague on the temptation of the office structure: "Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons... a business which is arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken from it..."

From Ansel's unsent letter on the gap: "Those nine weeks in 1911 are the strangest thing in my book and I have never had an account of them to give... I could not answer it then and I cannot answer it now, and I will not put anything in its place that I do not know."

From Bulletin 471: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

From Vira Toland on the lingering stigma: "That is the part that sits badly with me now that I am old. Whatever he did or did not do, he had all those years of this valley looking at him a certain way in church, and Orra had it too, and she outlived him a good while and had it the whole time."
