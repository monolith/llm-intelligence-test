# Section C — Math (show working)

## C1. Proportional error and true average test

**(a) Proportion of error derived from glassware:**
Ivy Keddie's measurement on 9 March 1958: nine pipettes delivered 16.72 c.c. mean.
Mark blown on stem: 17.6 c.c.
Ratio: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95

**Every test made with this glass read 95% of truth, or 5% low.**

**(b) True average test at the station:**
Recorded average test (Ansel's book): 3.61 butterfat
True average: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80 butterfat
**Difference: 0.19 (nineteen hundredths) short**

**(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited:**
40 million pounds of milk intake over 25 full years (1898–1922)
At recorded 3.61 test: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 pounds butterfat credited
At true 3.80 test: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 pounds butterfat actually delivered
**Loss: 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 pounds of butterfat**

**(d) Value and fraction received:**
Butterfat averaged 25¢/pound over the period
Value of loss: 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000**
Patrons received: $3,800
Fraction received: 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **0.20 = one-fifth (20%)**

## C2. Largest patron (Emmet Brant), year 1910

Emmet Brant delivered 200,000 pounds of milk to the station, paid on test of 3.42 butterfat.

**(a) Butterfat credited:**
200,000 pounds × 0.0342 = **6,840 pounds butterfat**

**(b) Butterfat actually in his milk:**
True test of that milk (applying 1/0.95 correction): 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60 butterfat
200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 pounds butterfat
Owed but not credited: 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 pounds butterfat**

**(c) Value at 25¢/pound:**
360 × $0.25 = **$90 that year alone**

## C3. Station gain as percentage (full years)

**(a) Butterfat credited to patrons on station test:**
40,000,000 pounds milk × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 pounds butterfat**

**(b) Butterfat actually received at Larrow Green:**
40,000,000 pounds milk × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 pounds butterfat**

**(c) Difference as a percentage:**
(1,520,000 − 1,444,000) ÷ 1,520,000 = 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **0.05 = 5%**

**Clarification:** This 5% is expressed as a percentage of the actual butterfat received (1,520,000 lbs), which is what Wilbur Teague's ledger entries showed as "station gain" year after year. The Association's own books recorded this 5.25% discrepancy annually as "station gain, as usual"—the difference between butterfat credited to patrons and butterfat actually arriving at Larrow Green. The consistency of this figure "as usual" made it invisible; a figure with a name stops being questioned.

# Section D — Logic

## D1. The 1911 measure anomaly as an experiment

**When:** May 8–July 10, 1911 (nine weeks)

**Duration:** 9 weeks

**The variable:** The measure (glass pipette). Station's own measure broke; borrowed accurate measure from Larrow Green substituted.

**Held constant:** The tester (Ansel Keddie, same person, same technique), the herds (same cows on Hessel Bottom), the season (May into July, summer grass), the procedures (same hand tester, acid, bottles, timing), the external inspection (Merle Strawn watched a test in June and noted "Nothing here to note"), the farms (same practices, no change in feeding or management).

**Result:** Tests read 3.80 during the nine weeks (accurate level). Upon return of station's own measure on July 10, tests immediately fell back to 3.61 (5% low).

**What it establishes:** The station's own glass was defective—low by a proportional amount (~5%). A single variable change (glass) produced a consistent result shift with everything else identical. This could not be explained by farm practices, herd quality, season, tester skill, or procedure. The defect was in the measure.

**What it does NOT establish:** Exactly how much the glass was short (the 0.88 c.c. shortfall). The interval proved defect but not magnitude. Magnitude required the crate to be found and measured in 1958 using a burette.

**Why this is powerful:** It is an experiment, not an inference. The 1911 anomaly was written in Ansel's own test book, observed by a state inspector (Strawn), and reproduced from records. No theory about water, theft, or wet hay could explain why tests would rise five percent when only the glass changed and then fall back when the glass returned, with every other factor identical.

## D2. Three independent reasons cream theft is impossible

**Reason 1 (Ledger evidence — Wilbur Teague, 1905 onward):**
The ledger showed "station gain" of 5.25% every year: butterfat credited to patrons was less than butterfat actually arriving at Larrow Green in the station's cream. If Ansel had stolen cream and written low tests to hide it, the cream would arrive light, not heavy. The ledger showed the opposite—the cream ran heavy. This is independent of any 1958 measurement and depends only on Teague's bookkeeping.

**Reason 2 (Roy Brant's weigh book agreement — Emmet Brant, 1897–1929; Roy Brant, 1929 onward):**
Emmet Brant's private scale agreed with the station's scale perfectly for thirty years, "barring a pound or two on sloppy days." If Ansel had stolen milk (as opposed to cream, but before separation), he would have removed weight from the intake. The station's scale and Emmet's scale did not diverge. The scales never "fell out at all." This is independent of the ledger and depends on a farmer's private records.

**Reason 3 (Merle Strawn's observation of actual cream weight — Hobart Sill, buttermaker, and Strawn's field notes):**
Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green for 41 years, testified that "That cream ran heavy. Every year it ran heavy. There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there." This is independent observation from the receiving end, not the testing end. The cream's weight and consistency indicated more butterfat than papers suggested. If Ansel had stolen cream, less would arrive, not more.

**Independence:** Reason 1 (ledger) is financial accounting. Reason 2 (scales) is weight verification at source. Reason 3 (receiving observation) is quality assessment at destination. All three are independent methods pointing to the same conclusion: cream was not stolen.

**What would still hold if glass had never been measured in 1958:**
Reason 2 (the scale agreement in Roy's weigh book) requires no 1958 measurement. The book is an independent 1897–1962 document. It proves that the weight delivered and the weight recorded agreed. This proves theft of milk (at intake) never happened. Reasons 1 and 3 also stand without the 1958 bulletin.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question the ledger entries?**
Wilbur Teague created the "station gain" line in 1905. In 1916, he raised concerns with Selby Vose about the account; Vose told him to "let it alone." Teague "did not read" beyond the totals, per Bertram Kyle: "He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign... Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years. That is not a criticism. It was his notion of the position: the figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people." Teague laid the sheets before Vose each spring and Vose signed them. Thus Vose saw the 5.25% gain every year. But neither man publicly questioned it before 1925.

## D3. Did the manager know tests ran low before 1923 condemnation?

**Answer: Yes. Selby Vose knew the tests ran low.**

**Justification:**

Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper, states that he raised concerns with Selby Vose in 1916 about the station account. From then onward, Teague "laid the annual sheet before Vose each spring and Vose signed it." These sheets explicitly showed "station gain" of 5.25% every year—meaning butterfat credited to patrons was consistently 5% below butterfat arriving at the creamery.

This discrepancy is mathematically equivalent to tests running 5% low. If patrons were credited with 3.61 but the true butterfat was 3.80, the station gain of 5.25% is the inevitable result. Vose, signing these sheets annually from 1916 to 1923 (at minimum), was signing off on evidence that the tests were systematically low.

Furthermore, Vose's own 1926 defense of the 1919 water letter reveals he understood the gap: "For a generation the milk brought to that station tested below the milk brought to Larrow Green." He was aware of the low tests; he attributed them to water-adding. The water letter itself (1919) was based on awareness that something was producing lower tests.

**The condemnation in June 1923 did not reveal to Vose that tests were low; it revealed that the glass was wrong.** What changed between 1919 (water theory) and 1925 (still defending water theory, unaware of glass) was not Vose's knowledge of the low tests but the state inspector's measurement capability. Merle Strawn, in 1921, began receiving burettes. On June 12, 1923, Strawn tested the pipettes and found them faulty.

**Before 1923, Vose may not have suspected the glass; but he certainly knew tests ran low**, because the ledger showed it every year. He signed those sheets.

# Section F — Theory and Three Competing Explanations

## F1. Causal theory of the Ostrey Hollow shortfall

**The True Cause:** In 1897, Ammon & Sons Dairy Supply shipped defective milk-test pipettes to the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. These pipettes, blown at 17.6 c.c., delivered only 16.72 c.c.—95% of their marked capacity. A milk test pipette is not simply a container; it is a measuring device on which the entire test depends. A charge of milk drawn up in a short pipette into a test bottle whose neck is ruled for a full charge produces a reading short in exact proportion. Every test made with these pipettes read 5% low. This proportional error meant richer milk was penalized more: a 4.56 butterfat milk read 4.32 (short 0.24); a 3.42 milk read 3.25 (short 0.17). Over 25 years, this 5% systematic error accumulated to 76,000 pounds of butterfat never credited to patrons—worth approximately $19,000 in period prices.

**Why this size:** The defect was inherent to the glass itself—the mold from which the pipettes were blown was misaligned or worn, producing glass 0.88 c.c. short. According to Junia Ammon (the company's historian), "the mold had been reground and the lot ran short... A boy checks the first of a lot and the tenth and the fiftieth and finds them consistent, because they are consistent—they are consistently wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind cannot find."

**Why it began when it did (1897):** Ammon & Sons delivered the pipettes on April 14, 1897, and they were installed May 1, 1897. The glass began producing low tests immediately. Ansel Keddie's test book shows 3.80 for 1896 (when borrowed glass from Larrow Green was in use) and 3.61 from 1898 onward (when the station's own glass was in use). The station received the defective equipment at the outset.

**Why it ended when it did (June 1923):** On June 12, 1923, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn tested the station's pipettes with a newly issued calibrated burette and found they would not deliver their mark. He condemned the crate on the spot. The Association ordered replacement glass on June 28, which arrived by October 1923. New glass was installed and verified by Strawn in October: "They deliver their mark." From 1924, tests read 3.80—the true value.

**Why nothing at the station or farms had to change for either transition:** The error was in the instrument, not the people or animals. No tester error was corrected; no practice changed; no cow improved. The 1911 anomaly proved this: when good glass was borrowed for nine weeks, tests rose to 3.80 despite everything else remaining identical. When the own glass returned, tests fell to 3.61 despite no change in anything else.

## Three Competing Explanations (and the evidence defeating each)

**Explanation 1 — Water-adding (Selby Vose's theory, 1919 onward):**
- **Claim:** Ostrey Hollow patrons were deliberately adding water to milk cans to increase weight or reduce density, thereby reducing butterfat percentage. The 1919 warning letter halted the practice, which is why tests rose in 1924.
- **Evidence defeating it:**
  - Merle Strawn tested for added water at every visit from 1908 forward (after 1908, every year) and recorded "Lactometer, no added water, as always here." This is consistent across his notebooks (r22, citing Strawn's field notes). A lactometer would detect watered milk by its density shift.
  - If water had been added and then ceased (as Vose claimed), the effect should have been larger in the 1920s than in the 1910s (as farmers stopped the practice). Instead, the 0.19 gap was constant every month of every year. A gradual improvement would be expected if water-adding had been occurring and then stopping; instead, tests rose sharply in 1924 (from 3.61 to 3.80) when the glass changed, not gradually over years.
  - The 1911 anomaly: when good glass was borrowed, tests rose to 3.80 in May–July 1911, before any warning letter had been issued (1919). If water-adding was the cause, it should not have mattered which glass was used. It did matter, and it mattered immediately.

**Explanation 2 — Cream theft (Wilbur Teague's structural observation, 1925):**
- **Claim:** Ansel Keddie, holding sole authority at the station, could remove cream and write low tests to conceal the loss. The structure of the office—one man, twenty-nine years—was vulnerable to this.
- **Evidence defeating it:**
  - The ledger showed "station gain" of 5.25% every year: butterfat credited to patrons was less than butterfat arriving at Larrow Green in the cream. If cream had been stolen, the cream would be light, not heavy. The ledger shows the opposite: the cream ran heavy, indicating more butterfat than the papers claimed had been bought. Wilbur Teague himself, despite making the structural criticism, noted this and "made no charge against anybody."
  - Emmet Brant's private weigh book showed his scale agreed with the station's scale for thirty years, "barring a pound or two on sloppy days." If Ansel had stolen milk at intake, the station's scale and Emmet's would diverge. They did not.
  - Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green for 41 years, testified: "That cream ran heavy. Every year it ran heavy. There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there." The receiving end showed no loss.
  - The 1911 anomaly: if Ansel were stealing cream, the same tester with the same apparatus and the same temptation would steal equally from good glass and bad glass. Instead, tests rose sharply when good glass was used. The behavior of the numbers tracks the glass, not the tester.

**Explanation 3 — Wet-hay effect (1908 committee, revived 1925–1926):**
- **Claim:** The wet meadow and coarse hay of Hessel Bottom caused nutritional stress in cattle, reducing butterfat content of their milk naturally, not by any human action.
- **Evidence defeating it:**
  - State Bulletin No. 214 tested bog hay under controlled conditions and found it depressed butterfat by "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." The maximum effect was 0.05 in winter, zero in summer. The Ostrey Hollow gap was 0.19 year-round. Five hundredths cannot produce nineteen hundredths.
  - The four Hessel Bottom men who hauled to Alder Corners Cheese Company (same wet-ground herds, same cows, same farmers) in summer flush 1915–1930 were tested at 3.78–3.82 (averaging 3.80), not 3.61. The same cows on the same wet bottom produced normal milk when tested by different glass. The defect was not in the cows or the bottom; it was in the test.
  - The 1911 anomaly: if wet hay were the cause, it would act on the cow regardless of which glass was used. Instead, when good glass was substituted for nine weeks, the same herds on the same bottom produced correct tests (3.80). When the bad glass returned, the same herds on the same bottom produced low tests (3.61) again. The dependency on the glass rules out the feed.

## Subsidiary Questions

**Did the board examine the condemned glassware?**

No. The four exhibits brought to the board were: (1) the test book, (2) the receiving book, (3) Emmet Brant's weigh book, (4) Merle Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook. The condemned pipettes themselves were not present. They had been carried up into the loft in June 1923 and were sitting thirty feet above the hearing room when the board met in April 1925. Effie Loomis noted explicitly: "The crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all... I took every word down and it is not there." The board examined Strawn's notebook entry stating the glass was condemned and that new glass was ordered, but the board never saw the glass itself.

**What did the tester believe the cause was?**

Ansel Keddie believed something was wrong with the glass, but he could not prove it and would not speculate. His statement on July 10, 1911, when the station's own measure was returned after the anomaly, was a question mark: "? — A.K." He told Duncan, his son, in the summer of 1911: "it was something in the glass." In his unsent letter (spring 1925), written but never delivered, he described the 1911 anomaly and wrote: "Those nine weeks in 1911 are the strangest thing in my book and I have never had an account of them to give... I could not answer it then and I cannot answer it now, and I will not put anything in its place that I do not know." He carried the question mark for fourteen years without answering it. Privately, to attorney Alonzo Frick, Ansel said "he always thought the patrons were at fault"—meaning water-adding—but Duncan's account and the note in his test book suggest Ansel's true suspicion lay with the glass itself, not with the farmers.

# Section G — Summary (max 150 words)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association operated a milk-testing station at Ostrey Hollow from 1896 onward using defective pipettes that delivered 5% less milk than marked, producing test results 5% systematically low. For 25 years (1898–1922), farmers were paid for less butterfat than they delivered—a loss of 76,000 pounds worth $19,000. In 1925, after a hearing that examined four exhibits but not the glass itself, the board awarded $3,800 (one-fifth the loss) and dismissed the tester. In 1958, the tester's granddaughter found the condemned glass in a loft, measured it, and proved the pipettes were short by 0.88 c.c. The state issued a bulletin exonerating the tester—who had died 24 years earlier. The scandal required eight separate records (farm weigh books, ledgers, test books, field notes, arbitration documents, family diaries, company correspondence, personal testimony) because the defect was invisible without measurement, the people involved never connected the pieces, and the truth lay in equipment stored above the hearing room.
