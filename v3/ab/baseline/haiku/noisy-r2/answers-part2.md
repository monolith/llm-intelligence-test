# Answer Sheet — Sections A (Stories 5–8) and B

## Story 5: The 1923 Glass Condemnation and Replacement

On June 12, 1923 (note: one source says July 12, likely clerical confusion), Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector for the Ordell circuit, made a routine visit to Ostrey Hollow. Strawn had been receiving a new tool since 1921: a calibrated burette, issued to field men for checking whether measures and pipettes actually delivered their marked capacity. This was a breakthrough in inspection technology—before the burette, no inspector could verify the glass itself, only observe procedure.

Strawn took the station's pipettes and tested them against his burette. They would not deliver 17.6 cubic centimeters. He condemned the entire crate on the spot, writing in his field notebook: "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new."

The Association wrote immediately to Ammon & Sons Dairy Supply, Tarnet, on June 28, 1923, requesting replacement glass. Ammon & Sons replied July 2: "Your order of the 28th ultimo for six dozen milk pipettes and six dozen test bottles is entered and will go forward this month. We regret the trouble. Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name." The company would not admit responsibility; it claimed its records from 1897 could not explain the defect. But Junia Ammon, writing the company history in 1949, speculated privately that "the mold had been reground and the lot ran short... a gauge sits on a nail and gets knocked off the nail; a mold comes back from the shop a hair different and looks exactly as it looked before." A boy checking the first, tenth, and fiftieth pipettes would find them "consistently wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind cannot find."

By October 1923, new glass had arrived and was installed. Strawn returned and verified: "New glass in use since July. Ran the new pipettes against the burette. They deliver their mark."

In 1924, the station's average test rose to 3.80—the true level. Strawn noted: "Tests improved. Their book gives the year at 3.80." No one connected the old glass to the historical 0.19 shortfall. The rise seemed to confirm the water letter had worked (farmers stopped adding water) or the wet-hay theory had been addressed. The condemned crate was carried up into the loft over the intake room, covered with a tarpaulin, and forgotten. It would sit there for 35 years, thirty-three inches above the heads of the arbitration board that would sit below it in 1925.

**Documents:** Merle Strawn's circuit notebook, June 12 and October 1923 (r22, Orin Falke, citing original); Ammon & Sons letter, July 2, 1923 (r20, Junia Ammon's firm history); Association order of June 28, 1923 (r23, Bertram Kyle); Ansel's test book, 1924 entry "3.80" (implied in multiple sources).

## Story 6: The 1925 Arbitration Hearing and Dismissal

In January 1925, frustrated by fifty years of complaints and 25 years of the 0.19 gap, the Ostrey Hollow patrons filed a formal petition with the Association demanding restitution. A board of arbitration was formed, chaired by Alonzo Frick, an attorney from Ashlin who respected Ansel Keddie and believed him honest.

Three sittings were held: March 17 at Ashlin (where the lawyer was), and two additional sittings at the station itself in April (so the books, scales, and machinery could be examined in place). Cleve Oram stood outside on the platform in the rain listening to voices. Orra Keddie heard the wagons from the yard. Two sessions were held in the intake room—the weigh room itself—with the crate of condemned pipettes hanging in the loft above, thirty feet straight up, never mentioned.

Four exhibits were presented to the board:
1. Ansel's test book (1896–1924), in his own hand
2. The receiving book of the station
3. Emmet Brant's private weigh book (showing his scale and the station's scale had agreed perfectly for 30 years)
4. Merle Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook (proving the glass was condemned in June 1923)

The board heard three competing explanations for the 0.19 gap:

**Explanation 1 — Water (Selby Vose's theory):** Ostrey Hollow farmers were adding water to milk cans, reducing butterfat %. The 1919 circular warning had allegedly stopped the water-adding, which is why tests rose in 1924. Merle Strawn's testimony refuted this: he had tested for water at every visit since 1908 and found none.

**Explanation 2 — Theft (Wilbur Teague's structural critique):** Teague, the bookkeeper, noted that one man held all the station's duties for 29 seasons. A structure like that invited theft—a tester could remove cream and write low tests to hide it. But the ledgers proved the opposite: cream coming from Hollow always ran heavy (5.25% station gain every year), not light. The station received more butterfat than it paid for. Teague "made no charge against anybody," but the structural temptation he identified could not explain a 5% systematic low reading.

**Explanation 3 — Wet hay (1908 committee theory, revived):** The wet meadow and coarse hay of Hessel Bottom thinned cream. Dr. Roland Kepp, a veterinarian, argued this at the 1926 annual meeting (after the award). State Bulletin No. 214 had tested bog hay and found it depressed butterfat by at most 0.05 in winter, nothing measurable in summer. This was quantitatively wrong.

On May 4, 1925, the board announced:

**Finding:** "The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established."

**Award:** "The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account."

Effie Loomis, secretary, recorded: "Finds no cause established, and then an award of thirty-eight hundred dollars: I took it down as it was given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once."

Four days before the award, on April 30, 1925, the Association dismissed Ansel Keddie without cause after 29 seasons. Alonzo Frick later wrote: "He was simply let go while the board was still sitting, and I have never been able to think well of the Association for it... a honest man was ruined by a figure nobody could explain."

The $3,800 award represented one-fifth of the actual loss ($19,000). The patrons received payment without explanation. Ansel received nothing and was cast out.

**Documents:** Board of arbitration minute book (r23, Bertram Kyle); Alonzo Frick's file (r9, notes from earlier retellings); Ansel's statement to the board, March 17, 1925 (r18, Duncan Keddie); Wilbur Teague's 1925 statement to the board (notes); Effie Loomis's notes (notes); Selby Vose's 1926 annual address (notes); dismissal date, April 30, 1925 (r24, Orra's diary; r18, Duncan Keddie).

## Story 7: Ansel's Dismissal and Later Years (1925–1934)

After dismissal on April 30, 1925, Ansel Keddie walked up the road at his ordinary hour, rolled his apron under his arm, hung it behind the shed, and asked Orra what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it.

He never returned to the station. Instead, he began carpentry work. He repaired the cow shed, mended stairs, fixed gates and doors throughout Ostrey Hollow, taking what farmers offered for payment. He worked from light to dark and whistled, which he had never done when paid. Cleve Oram remembered a barn door Ansel hung for the Orams that "swung true for thirty years." He was better at carpentry than he had ever been at testing—but it paid a quarter of what the station paid.

The valley's judgment fell on him regardless. He bore the weight of an unproved accusation. In church, men avoided his eyes. In the store, conversations stopped. Emmet Brant had died in 1929 (ten years after their rupture), and though Roy was kind at the gate when Orra visited, the silence was settled not by forgiveness but by death. Orra was sixty-four when she closed her diary in 1930. Ansel was sixty-nine.

On a February morning in 1934—fifty-eight years after he began, and nine years after dismissal—Ansel Keddie died at home, "without much pain at the end of it." Duncan came up in the night. More men attended the burying than Orra expected. They shook her hand. But not one of them said the other thing—the accusation. Not one of them spoke it aloud. Orra later reflected: "Nine years they have had to say it and they had not said it and they did not say it then. He never asked them to. He said to me once and only once, and not lately, that a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."

Orra outlived him by 14 years, dying in 1948, still bearing the community's judgment. She did not live to see him cleared in 1958.

**Documents:** Orra's diary, April 30–June 1925 (r24); Cleve Oram's memory of the barn door (r21); Orra's diary, February 1934 (r24); Emmet Brant's death, 1929 (r24, Orra's diary entry spring 1929); Duncan's birth date, 1894, making Ansel 60 in 1954 and 69 at death in 1934 (r18).

## Story 8: The 1958 Discovery and the 1958–1964 Exoneration

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie, a 27-year-old graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, went to Ostrey Hollow to research a college project. She was the granddaughter of Ansel Keddie and daughter of Duncan Keddie, who had taught school at Ashlin. She climbed into the loft over the station's intake room—the same loft above which the arbitration board had sat in 1925—and found a wooden crate stenciled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET."

Inside, packed in straw and covered by a tarpaulin, were pipettes—the condemned glass from June 1923, carried up the ladder thirty-five years earlier and left undisturbed. Nine pipettes remained unbroken. Originally six dozen (60) were purchased in 1897; only nine survived.

On March 9, 1958, at Farland, Ivy measured each of the nine pipettes against a calibrated burette, running five trials per pipette. She recorded delivery in cubic centimeters: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean delivery: 16.72 c.c. The mark blown on the stem: 17.6 c.c.

Ratio: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95.

The pipettes delivered 95% of their mark. Every test made with this glass read 95% of the truth—5% low. This was proportional error, which fell hardest on the richest milk. A herd recorded at 4.56 was short 0.24. A herd at 3.42 was short 0.18.

Over 25 years (1898–1922), the station received approximately 40 million pounds of milk. Recorded average test: 3.61. True average (3.61 ÷ 0.95): 3.80. Difference: 0.19 (nineteen hundredths)—the gap explained.

Butterfat never paid: true butterfat delivered at 3.80 average = 1,520,000 pounds. Butterfat credited at 3.61 average = 1,444,000 pounds. Loss: 76,000 pounds of butterfat. At 25¢/pound: $19,000. Patrons had received $3,800—one-fifth the loss.

In October 1958, the State Agricultural College issued Bulletin No. 471, jointly authored by Professor Leland Vaught and Ivy Keddie. The closing paragraph: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference—seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat—stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

Ansel Keddie was dead 24 years. He was cleared not by proving innocence but by proving the glass was guilty.

The Ashlin Chronicle reported on October 3, 1958: "OLD ORDELL WRONG RIGHTED—College Bulletin Clears Station Tester Thirty-Three Years After He Was Turned Out—Answer Lay In A Crate In The Loft All The While—Ashlin Girl Made Good."

By 1959, county agent Orin Falke filed an official report documenting the scandal, concluding the glass was the sole cause. By 1972, curator Halbert Nym catalogued the condemned crate and all surviving records (16 items) in the Ordell County Historical Rooms at Ashlin. The truth was preserved.

**Documents:** Ivy's measurement sheet, March 9, 1958, nine pipettes (r22, Orin Falke, citing Item 14 in Halbert Nym's accession notes); State Bulletin No. 471, October 1958 (r22, Orin Falke); The Ashlin Chronicle, October 3, 1958 (r19); Orin Falke's county report, 1959 (r22); Halbert Nym's catalogue of 16 items, 1972 (notes); Duncan Keddie's memory: "He came home that summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass" (r18).

# Section B — Relationships

## B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie

Ivy was Ansel's granddaughter. Duncan Keddie, Ansel's son, states explicitly in his 1968 notes: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter." Ansel died in 1934 when Ivy was three years old; Ansel never knew that his granddaughter would climb into the loft 24 years after his death to find the glass that would clear his name. Duncan's statement is the primary source, and no narrator contradicts it.

## B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant

Ivy and Hazel were second cousins. Hazel Brant carefully explains in 1981: "Emmet [Brant] and my father Roy were first cousins—the children of a brother and a sister, which is what makes first cousins. And Ivy is Duncan's girl, and I am Roy's, so Ivy Keddie and I are second cousins." The genealogy: Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie (Ansel's wife) were siblings; Emmet's son was Roy; Roy's daughter is Hazel; Ansel's son is Duncan; Duncan's daughter is Ivy. Therefore Roy and Duncan are first cousins, and Hazel and Ivy are their children—second cousins. Hazel emphasizes the point because, as she says, "people are forever telling me she is my niece or my cousin once removed or some such, and she is not." This statement is internally consistent and uncontradicted by independent sources. (Cleve Oram in 1977 mistakenly calls Ivy "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed," but Hazel, as the actual relative, is the authoritative source.)

## B3. Selby Vose's father and why it matters

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose, first president of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (1889–1911). Duncan Keddie and Bertram Kyle both explicitly correct the "nephew" confusion: Selby was Gideon's son, not nephew. This matters because Gideon wrote Article VII, which made the station's test the sole arbiter of payment, concentrating all authority in Ansel Keddie's hands. Gideon was also president for 22 years and laid the Association's foundations. His son Selby, as manager from 1913 onward, issued the 1919 water letter (misinterpreting the glass defect as water) and ordered the replacement glass in 1923 (which fixed the problem but was seen as routine maintenance). Both father and son were structurally implicated in the conditions that allowed the defect to persist undetected.

## B4. Relationship between Vose and Keddie families

There was no family relationship. Duncan Keddie explicitly states: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses." He notes that confusion arose from people conflating Gideon Vose with the Ashlin Voses (unrelated) and from claims that Gideon's wife was Orra's cousin—claims Duncan flatly denies, saying his mother (Orra), who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection," never once listed a Vose in her genealogical accounts. The confusion appears to stem from oral transmission in a small valley and was never substantiated in Duncan's direct testimony.
