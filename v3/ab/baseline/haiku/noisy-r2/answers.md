# Answer Sheet — Section A: Eight Stories (Stories 1–4)

## Story 1: Founding and Opening of the Larrow Valley Station (1889–1898)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was organized in 1889 at a meeting of local farmers at Larrow Green, with Gideon Vose elected first president. They built the main creamery at Larrow Green in 1891 on Larrow Creek. At the 1892 annual meeting, Article VII was adopted, establishing that every patron would be paid for butterfat content "ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." This last clause—the test of the station where delivered—became the foundation of all disputes that followed.

In 1895–1896, the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley, to serve Hessel Bottom (the wet meadow below the hamlet) and nine farming families of that end. The station opened May 4, 1896, with Ansel Keddie, age 35, hired as tester and separator man. Ansel's role was absolute: he weighed every milk delivery on a true scale (certified by state inspection), took samples into patron-numbered jars, tested them daily using glass pipettes to measure charge, recorded results in a ledger he kept in his own hand (five columns: date, patron, pounds, test percentage, fat), separated the cream from skim, and sent cream daily downhill to Larrow Green and skim back to farmers.

On April 14, 1897, equipment arrived from Ammon & Sons Dairy Supply, Tarnet, for $77.93: six dozen pipettes (17.6 c.c. blown mark), 12 dozen test bottles (8% neck), acid measure, hand tester (4-bottle), and freight. This equipment was in use from May 1, 1897. The pipettes were the critical tool: they measured the exact volume of milk to be tested, and that volume determined the calibration of the test bottle's neck. Ansel tested thirty-one times per week for twenty-nine seasons without missing the procedure once.

From the outset, Ostrey Hollow milk tested lower: averaging 3.61 butterfat where milk from Larrow Green averaged 3.78. The gap was 0.19 (nineteen hundredths), constant across seasons. In 1902, twelve years into operation, the first complaints of record emerged. For the next fifty-six years, patrons would wonder why their milk tested so much lower than neighbors' milk, and increasingly blame either the tester or their own cows.

**Documents:** Station test book (r24 excerpts); invoice Ammon & Sons 1897 (r20, Junia Ammon); Article VII (r22, Orin Falke); Ansel's note in test book "Begun this 4th day of May, 1896... Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes."

## Story 2: The 1908 Committee and the Wet-Hay Theory

In response to years of complaints (beginning 1902) that Ostrey Hollow milk tested lower, the Association formed an investigating committee chaired by Nyle Grigg, a farmer at Larrow Green (not a Hollow patron, ensuring impartiality). In January, February, and March 1908, the committee visited Hessel Bottom farms, sampled each herd, examined the cows, hay, pasture, and farming practices. Their conclusion: "The milk is whole and the scales are honest. It is our opinion that the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station."

The theory was scientifically plausible. Hessel Bottom was a wet meadow prone to flooding in spring (the creek rose to the sill of the station and twice came over it). The hay harvested from such ground was coarse sedge, rich in mineral-poor vegetation. Cattle fed on wet-meadow hay could suffer mineral deficiency, forcing them to mobilize body fat for milk production—a metabolic stress that slightly reduced butterfat content. The committee had walked the ground. They were practical men. The finding was published and later reprinted in 1919 by Nyle Grigg with a covering letter citing State Bulletin No. 214 from the State Agricultural College, claiming the College had found bog hay depresses butterfat by two tenths in winter.

However, the numbers proved insufficient. The gap at Ostrey Hollow was 0.19 (nineteen hundredths), constant year-round—July as well as February, in wet years and dry. State Bulletin No. 214, when later examined by county agent Orin Falke in 1959, stated only that bog hay "depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Five hundredths maximum could not produce nineteen hundredths in summer. The wet-hay theory was quantitatively too small by a factor of four, and it could not explain why the July gap equaled the February gap.

Crucially, four Hessel Bottom men hauled their milk to Alder Corners Cheese Company (over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County) in summer flush, 1915–1930, where the same milk tested 3.78–3.82 (averaging 3.80), not 3.61. Their cows on the same wet bottom produced normal milk when tested elsewhere. The remedy had to lie with the station, not the land.

**Documents:** 1908 committee report (r22, Orin Falke); Nyle Grigg's 1919 covering letter citing Bulletin No. 214 (r22); Alder Corners test records, 1919 season (r22, notes from Vessey County Historical Society); Orin Falke's 1959 note on Bulletin No. 214 stating actual depression was "not more than five hundredths."

## Story 3: The 1911 Measure Anomaly—The Experiment in Plain Sight

On May 8, 1911, the station's own milk-test pipette broke. Ansel Keddie noted this in the test book and noted it again when the broken pipette was taken for repair. Unable to proceed without the measure, he borrowed one from Larrow Green for the next nine weeks until July 10, 1911, when his own glass was returned. This interval became an accidental controlled experiment with a single variable.

During those nine weeks (May 8–July 10), Ostrey Hollow tests stood at 3.80 butterfat—the correct level, the level at which milk from those same wet-ground herds tested at Alder Corners. On July 10, the moment the station's own glass returned, tests fell back to 3.61, where they had been for the previous 14 years. The shift was immediate and complete.

Everything else was held constant: the same tester (Ansel), the same herds, the same season (summer grass), the same procedures, the same hand-tester apparatus, the same acid, the same bottles. Ansel made no change in technique—state inspector Merle Strawn had watched him test in June 1911 and noted "Nothing here to note." The farms made no change: the same cows were milked by the same men using the same practices. The weather changed from May to July as it always does, but the test reading changed only when the glass changed.

The 1911 interval proved that the glass was wrong. Ansel recognized something was amiss. He wrote a question mark in his book—"? — A.K."—and carried it for fourteen years, never able to explain it. "Those nine weeks in 1911 are the strangest thing in my book and I have never had an account of them to give... I could not answer it then and I cannot answer it now, and I will not put anything in its place that I do not know."

What the interval proved: when accurate glass was used, tests were correct (3.80). When the station's own glass was used, tests were 5% low. What it did not prove (without measurement): exactly how short the station's glass was. That required the crate to be found and measured in 1958.

**Documents:** Ansel's test book, 1911 entries (r18, Duncan Keddie); Ansel's unsent letter, spring 1925 (notes from r9, earlier sources); Merle Strawn's circuit notebook, June 1911: "Watched a test through. Nothing here to note" (r22, Orin Falke, citing original); Roy Brant's later statement that his father "kept his father's weigh book... in the same way... Two scales... agreed perfectly for 30 years, barring a pound or two on sloppy days" (notes).

## Story 4: The 1919 Water Letter and the Rupture of Ansel and Emmet

In January 1919, Selby Vose, manager of the Association (office 1913–1938), issued a printed circular letter to the nine families of Ostrey Hollow. The letter, civil in tone but unmistakable in implication, warned that water had been found in milk cans coming from the station and that the Association would be investigating. The language was, per Vose's own later defense, designed to warn patrons that water-adding "would be looked for and would be dealt with."

The letter fell like an accusation. Vira Toland, born in the hollow in 1912, remembered the day it arrived—it rained that day. Her father read it twice at the table, then filed it in his desk drawer, where it remained until he died. Other families kept theirs pinned in trunks. The letter implied that Hollow farmers were deliberately adding water to their milk to increase weight or reduce density, a felony of dishonesty.

Ansel Keddie took the letter himself, delivering it personally to each family, standing at their doors while they read it, returning home at eleven at night with his hat still on—unusual for him, noted by Orra in her diary. He did this because he would not let another man carry it. But the letter divided him from Emmet Brant, his brother-in-law and the largest patron.

Two days after the letter, in June 1919, Emmet came up to Ansel's house. He did not enter. He stood in the yard and confronted Ansel: he had hauled whole milk to the station since the day it opened, and Ansel knew it better than any man living. Why had Ansel carried the paper himself if he did not believe it? Ansel replied: he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet went home. He did not return to Ansel's house for ten years—they lived "within sight of each other's chimney smoke but did not speak."

Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector, tested for water at every subsequent visit (1919, 1920, 1922, 1924, 1925) and recorded: "Lactometer, no added water, as always here." No water was ever found. The 1919 letter was based on the 5% shortfall in tests, which Vose interpreted as water, but which was actually the defective glass. The letter poisoned the valley's relationships based on a misunderstanding of a physical defect that no one had measured and no one suspected.

**Documents:** The 1919 circular, typed by Bertram Kyle (r23); Orra's diary, June 1919 (r24); Vira Toland's recollection (r9, notes from earlier retellings); Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks for 1919–1925 (r22, Orin Falke, citing original); Selby Vose's 1926 defense (r10, notes from earlier retellings); Roy Brant's recollection: "He and I have not spoken since the circular" (r17, Hazel Brant).

## Story 5: The 1923 Glass Condemnation and Replacement

On June 12, 1923 (note: one source says July 12, likely clerical confusion), Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector for the Ordell circuit, made a routine visit to Ostrey Hollow. Strawn had been receiving a new tool since 1921: a calibrated burette, issued to field men for checking whether measures and pipettes actually delivered their marked capacity. This was a breakthrough in inspection technology—before the burette, no inspector could verify the glass itself, only observe procedure.

Strawn took the station's pipettes and tested them against his burette. They would not deliver 17.6 cubic centimeters. He condemned the entire crate on the spot, writing in his field notebook: "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new."

The Association wrote immediately to Ammon & Sons Dairy Supply, Tarnet, on June 28, 1923, requesting replacement glass. Ammon & Sons replied July 2: "Your order of the 28th ultimo for six dozen milk pipettes and six dozen test bottles is entered and will go forward this month. We regret the trouble. Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name." The company would not admit responsibility; it claimed its records from 1897 could not explain the defect. But Junia Ammon, writing the company history in 1949, speculated privately that "the mold had been reground and the lot ran short... a gauge sits on a nail and gets knocked off the nail; a mold comes back from the shop a hair different and looks exactly as it looked before." A boy checking the first, tenth, and fiftieth pipettes would find them "consistently wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind cannot find."

By October 1923, new glass had arrived and was installed. Strawn returned and verified: "New glass in use since July. Ran the new pipettes against the burette. They deliver their mark."

In 1924, the station's average test rose to 3.80—the true level. Strawn noted: "Tests improved. Their book gives the year at 3.80." No one connected the old glass to the historical 0.19 shortfall. The rise seemed to confirm the water letter had worked (farmers stopped adding water) or the wet-hay theory had been addressed. The condemned crate was carried up into the loft over the intake room, covered with a tarpaulin, and forgotten. It would sit there for 35 years, thirty-three inches above the heads of the arbitration board that would sit below it in 1925.

**Documents:** Merle Strawn's circuit notebook, June 12 and October 1923 (r22, Orin Falke, citing original); Ammon & Sons letter, July 2, 1923 (r20, Junia Ammon's firm history); Association order of June 28, 1923 (r23, Bertram Kyle); Ansel's test book, 1924 entry "3.80" (implied in multiple sources).

## Story 6: The 1925 Arbitration Hearing and Dismissal

In January 1925, frustrated by fifty years of complaints and 25 years of the 0.19 gap, the Ostrey Hollow patrons filed a formal petition with the Association demanding restitution. A board of arbitration was formed, chaired by Alonzo Frick, an attorney from Ashlin who respected Ansel Keddie and believed him honest.

Three sittings were held: March 17 at Ashlin (where the lawyer was), and two additional sittings at the station itself in April (so the books, scales, and machinery could be examined in place). Cleve Oram stood outside on the platform in the rain listening to voices. Orra Keddie heard the wagons from the yard. Two sessions were held in the intake room—the weigh room itself—with the crate of condemned pipettes hanging in the loft above, thirty feet straight up, never mentioned.

Four exhibits were presented to the board:
1. Ansel's test book (1896–1924), in his own hand
2. The receiving book of the station
3. Emmet Brant's private weigh book (showing his scale and the station's scale had agreed perfectly for 30 years)
4. Merle Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook (proving the glass was condemned in June 1923)

The board heard three competing explanations for the 0.19 gap:

**Explanation 1 — Water (Selby Vose's theory):** Ostrey Hollow farmers were adding water to milk cans, reducing butterfat %. The 1919 circular warning had allegedly stopped the water-adding, which is why tests rose in 1924. Merle Strawn's testimony refuted this: he had tested for water at every visit since 1908 and found none.

**Explanation 2 — Theft (Wilbur Teague's structural critique):** Teague, the bookkeeper, noted that one man held all the station's duties for 29 seasons. A structure like that invited theft—a tester could remove cream and write low tests to hide it. But the ledgers proved the opposite: cream coming from Hollow always ran heavy (5.25% station gain every year), not light. The station received more butterfat than it paid for. Teague "made no charge against anybody," but the structural temptation he identified could not explain a 5% systematic low reading.

**Explanation 3 — Wet hay (1908 committee theory, revived):** The wet meadow and coarse hay of Hessel Bottom thinned cream. Dr. Roland Kepp, a veterinarian, argued this at the 1926 annual meeting (after the award). State Bulletin No. 214 had tested bog hay and found it depressed butterfat by at most 0.05 in winter, nothing measurable in summer. This was quantitatively wrong.

On May 4, 1925, the board announced:

**Finding:** "The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established."

**Award:** "The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account."

Effie Loomis, secretary, recorded: "Finds no cause established, and then an award of thirty-eight hundred dollars: I took it down as it was given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once."

Four days before the award, on April 30, 1925, the Association dismissed Ansel Keddie without cause after 29 seasons. Alonzo Frick later wrote: "He was simply let go while the board was still sitting, and I have never been able to think well of the Association for it... a honest man was ruined by a figure nobody could explain."

The $3,800 award represented one-fifth of the actual loss ($19,000). The patrons received payment without explanation. Ansel received nothing and was cast out.

**Documents:** Board of arbitration minute book (r23, Bertram Kyle); Alonzo Frick's file (r9, notes from earlier retellings); Ansel's statement to the board, March 17, 1925 (r18, Duncan Keddie); Wilbur Teague's 1925 statement to the board (notes); Effie Loomis's notes (notes); Selby Vose's 1926 annual address (notes); dismissal date, April 30, 1925 (r24, Orra's diary; r18, Duncan Keddie).

## Story 7: Ansel's Dismissal and Later Years (1925–1934)

After dismissal on April 30, 1925, Ansel Keddie walked up the road at his ordinary hour, rolled his apron under his arm, hung it behind the shed, and asked Orra what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it.

He never returned to the station. Instead, he began carpentry work. He repaired the cow shed, mended stairs, fixed gates and doors throughout Ostrey Hollow, taking what farmers offered for payment. He worked from light to dark and whistled, which he had never done when paid. Cleve Oram remembered a barn door Ansel hung for the Orams that "swung true for thirty years." He was better at carpentry than he had ever been at testing—but it paid a quarter of what the station paid.

The valley's judgment fell on him regardless. He bore the weight of an unproved accusation. In church, men avoided his eyes. In the store, conversations stopped. Emmet Brant had died in 1929 (ten years after their rupture), and though Roy was kind at the gate when Orra visited, the silence was settled not by forgiveness but by death. Orra was sixty-four when she closed her diary in 1930. Ansel was sixty-nine.

On a February morning in 1934—fifty-eight years after he began, and nine years after dismissal—Ansel Keddie died at home, "without much pain at the end of it." Duncan came up in the night. More men attended the burying than Orra expected. They shook her hand. But not one of them said the other thing—the accusation. Not one of them spoke it aloud. Orra later reflected: "Nine years they have had to say it and they had not said it and they did not say it then. He never asked them to. He said to me once and only once, and not lately, that a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."

Orra outlived him by 14 years, dying in 1948, still bearing the community's judgment. She did not live to see him cleared in 1958.

**Documents:** Orra's diary, April 30–June 1925 (r24); Cleve Oram's memory of the barn door (r21); Orra's diary, February 1934 (r24); Emmet Brant's death, 1929 (r24, Orra's diary entry spring 1929); Duncan's birth date, 1894, making Ansel 60 in 1954 and 69 at death in 1934 (r18).

## Story 8: The 1958 Discovery and the 1958–1964 Exoneration

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie, a 27-year-old graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, went to Ostrey Hollow to research a college project. She was the granddaughter of Ansel Keddie and daughter of Duncan Keddie, who had taught school at Ashlin. She climbed into the loft over the station's intake room—the same loft above which the arbitration board had sat in 1925—and found a wooden crate stenciled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET."

Inside, packed in straw and covered by a tarpaulin, were pipettes—the condemned glass from June 1923, carried up the ladder thirty-five years earlier and left undisturbed. Nine pipettes remained unbroken. Originally six dozen (60) were purchased in 1897; only nine survived.

On March 9, 1958, at Farland, Ivy measured each of the nine pipettes against a calibrated burette, running five trials per pipette. She recorded delivery in cubic centimeters: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean delivery: 16.72 c.c. The mark blown on the stem: 17.6 c.c.

Ratio: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95.

The pipettes delivered 95% of their mark. Every test made with this glass read 95% of the truth—5% low. This was proportional error, which fell hardest on the richest milk. A herd recorded at 4.56 was short 0.24. A herd at 3.42 was short 0.18.

Over 25 years (1898–1922), the station received approximately 40 million pounds of milk. Recorded average test: 3.61. True average (3.61 ÷ 0.95): 3.80. Difference: 0.19 (nineteen hundredths)—the gap explained.

Butterfat never paid: true butterfat delivered at 3.80 average = 1,520,000 pounds. Butterfat credited at 3.61 average = 1,444,000 pounds. Loss: 76,000 pounds of butterfat. At 25¢/pound: $19,000. Patrons had received $3,800—one-fifth the loss.

In October 1958, the State Agricultural College issued Bulletin No. 471, jointly authored by Professor Leland Vaught and Ivy Keddie. The closing paragraph: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference—seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat—stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

Ansel Keddie was dead 24 years. He was cleared not by proving innocence but by proving the glass was guilty.

The Ashlin Chronicle reported on October 3, 1958: "OLD ORDELL WRONG RIGHTED—College Bulletin Clears Station Tester Thirty-Three Years After He Was Turned Out—Answer Lay In A Crate In The Loft All The While—Ashlin Girl Made Good."

By 1959, county agent Orin Falke filed an official report documenting the scandal, concluding the glass was the sole cause. By 1972, curator Halbert Nym catalogued the condemned crate and all surviving records (16 items) in the Ordell County Historical Rooms at Ashlin. The truth was preserved.

**Documents:** Ivy's measurement sheet, March 9, 1958, nine pipettes (r22, Orin Falke, citing Item 14 in Halbert Nym's accession notes); State Bulletin No. 471, October 1958 (r22, Orin Falke); The Ashlin Chronicle, October 3, 1958 (r19); Orin Falke's county report, 1959 (r22); Halbert Nym's catalogue of 16 items, 1972 (notes); Duncan Keddie's memory: "He came home that summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass" (r18).

# Section B — Relationships

## B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie

Ivy was Ansel's granddaughter. Duncan Keddie, Ansel's son, states explicitly in his 1968 notes: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter." Ansel died in 1934 when Ivy was three years old; Ansel never knew that his granddaughter would climb into the loft 24 years after his death to find the glass that would clear his name. Duncan's statement is the primary source, and no narrator contradicts it.

## B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant

Ivy and Hazel were second cousins. Hazel Brant carefully explains in 1981: "Emmet [Brant] and my father Roy were first cousins—the children of a brother and a sister, which is what makes first cousins. And Ivy is Duncan's girl, and I am Roy's, so Ivy Keddie and I are second cousins." The genealogy: Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie (Ansel's wife) were siblings; Emmet's son was Roy; Roy's daughter is Hazel; Ansel's son is Duncan; Duncan's daughter is Ivy. Therefore Roy and Duncan are first cousins, and Hazel and Ivy are their children—second cousins. Hazel emphasizes the point because, as she says, "people are forever telling me she is my niece or my cousin once removed or some such, and she is not." This statement is internally consistent and uncontradicted by independent sources. (Cleve Oram in 1977 mistakenly calls Ivy "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed," but Hazel, as the actual relative, is the authoritative source.)

## B3. Selby Vose's father and why it matters

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose, first president of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (1889–1911). Duncan Keddie and Bertram Kyle both explicitly correct the "nephew" confusion: Selby was Gideon's son, not nephew. This matters because Gideon wrote Article VII, which made the station's test the sole arbiter of payment, concentrating all authority in Ansel Keddie's hands. Gideon was also president for 22 years and laid the Association's foundations. His son Selby, as manager from 1913 onward, issued the 1919 water letter (misinterpreting the glass defect as water) and ordered the replacement glass in 1923 (which fixed the problem but was seen as routine maintenance). Both father and son were structurally implicated in the conditions that allowed the defect to persist undetected.

## B4. Relationship between Vose and Keddie families

There was no family relationship. Duncan Keddie explicitly states: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses." He notes that confusion arose from people conflating Gideon Vose with the Ashlin Voses (unrelated) and from claims that Gideon's wife was Orra's cousin—claims Duncan flatly denies, saying his mother (Orra), who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection," never once listed a Vose in her genealogical accounts. The confusion appears to stem from oral transmission in a small valley and was never substantiated in Duncan's direct testimony.

## C3. Station gain as percentage (full years)

**(a) Butterfat credited to patrons on station test:**
40,000,000 pounds milk × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 pounds butterfat**

**(b) Butterfat actually received at Larrow Green:**
40,000,000 pounds milk × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 pounds butterfat**

**(c) Difference as a percentage:**
(1,520,000 − 1,444,000) ÷ 1,520,000 = 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **0.05 = 5%**

**Clarification:** This 5% is expressed as a percentage of the actual butterfat received (1,520,000 lbs), which is what Wilbur Teague's ledger entries showed as "station gain" year after year. The Association's own books recorded this 5.25% discrepancy annually as "station gain, as usual"—the difference between butterfat credited to patrons and butterfat actually arriving at Larrow Green. The consistency of this figure "as usual" made it invisible; a figure with a name stops being questioned.

# Section D — Logic

## D1. The 1911 measure anomaly as an experiment

**When:** May 8–July 10, 1911 (nine weeks)

**Duration:** 9 weeks

**The variable:** The measure (glass pipette). Station's own measure broke; borrowed accurate measure from Larrow Green substituted.

**Held constant:** The tester (Ansel Keddie, same person, same technique), the herds (same cows on Hessel Bottom), the season (May into July, summer grass), the procedures (same hand tester, acid, bottles, timing), the external inspection (Merle Strawn watched a test in June and noted "Nothing here to note"), the farms (same practices, no change in feeding or management).

**Result:** Tests read 3.80 during the nine weeks (accurate level). Upon return of station's own measure on July 10, tests immediately fell back to 3.61 (5% low).

**What it establishes:** The station's own glass was defective—low by a proportional amount (~5%). A single variable change (glass) produced a consistent result shift with everything else identical. This could not be explained by farm practices, herd quality, season, tester skill, or procedure. The defect was in the measure.

**What it does NOT establish:** Exactly how much the glass was short (the 0.88 c.c. shortfall). The interval proved defect but not magnitude. Magnitude required the crate to be found and measured in 1958 using a burette.

**Why this is powerful:** It is an experiment, not an inference. The 1911 anomaly was written in Ansel's own test book, observed by a state inspector (Strawn), and reproduced from records. No theory about water, theft, or wet hay could explain why tests would rise five percent when only the glass changed and then fall back when the glass returned, with every other factor identical.

---

## D2. Three independent reasons cream theft is impossible

**Reason 1 (Ledger evidence — Wilbur Teague, 1905 onward):**
The ledger showed "station gain" of 5.25% every year: butterfat credited to patrons was less than butterfat actually arriving at Larrow Green in the station's cream. If Ansel had stolen cream and written low tests to hide it, the cream would arrive light, not heavy. The ledger showed the opposite—the cream ran heavy. This is independent of any 1958 measurement and depends only on Teague's bookkeeping.

**Reason 2 (Roy Brant's weigh book agreement — Emmet Brant, 1897–1929; Roy Brant, 1929 onward):**
Emmet Brant's private scale agreed with the station's scale perfectly for thirty years, "barring a pound or two on sloppy days." If Ansel had stolen milk (as opposed to cream, but before separation), he would have removed weight from the intake. The station's scale and Emmet's scale did not diverge. The scales never "fell out at all." This is independent of the ledger and depends on a farmer's private records.

**Reason 3 (Merle Strawn's observation of actual cream weight — Hobart Sill, buttermaker, and Strawn's field notes):**
Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green for 41 years, testified that "That cream ran heavy. Every year it ran heavy. There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there." This is independent observation from the receiving end, not the testing end. The cream's weight and consistency indicated more butterfat than papers suggested. If Ansel had stolen cream, less would arrive, not more.

**Independence:** Reason 1 (ledger) is financial accounting. Reason 2 (scales) is weight verification at source. Reason 3 (receiving observation) is quality assessment at destination. All three are independent methods pointing to the same conclusion: cream was not stolen.

**What would still hold if glass had never been measured in 1958:**
Reason 2 (the scale agreement in Roy's weigh book) requires no 1958 measurement. The book is an independent 1897–1962 document. It proves that the weight delivered and the weight recorded agreed. This proves theft of milk (at intake) never happened. Reasons 1 and 3 also stand without the 1958 bulletin.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question the ledger entries?**
Wilbur Teague created the "station gain" line in 1905. In 1916, he raised concerns with Selby Vose about the account; Vose told him to "let it alone." Teague "did not read" beyond the totals, per Bertram Kyle: "He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign... Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years. That is not a criticism. It was his notion of the position: the figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people." Teague laid the sheets before Vose each spring and Vose signed them. Thus Vose saw the 5.25% gain every year. But neither man publicly questioned it before 1925.

## D3. Did the manager know tests ran low before 1923 condemnation?

**Answer: Yes. Selby Vose knew the tests ran low.**

**Justification:**

Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper, states that he raised concerns with Selby Vose in 1916 about the station account. From then onward, Teague "laid the annual sheet before Vose each spring and Vose signed it." These sheets explicitly showed "station gain" of 5.25% every year—meaning butterfat credited to patrons was consistently 5% below butterfat arriving at the creamery.

This discrepancy is mathematically equivalent to tests running 5% low. If patrons were credited with 3.61 but the true butterfat was 3.80, the station gain of 5.25% is the inevitable result. Vose, signing these sheets annually from 1916 to 1923 (at minimum), was signing off on evidence that the tests were systematically low.

Furthermore, Vose's own 1926 defense of the 1919 water letter reveals he understood the gap: "For a generation the milk brought to that station tested below the milk brought to Larrow Green." He was aware of the low tests; he attributed them to water-adding. The water letter itself (1919) was based on awareness that something was producing lower tests.

**The condemnation in June 1923 did not reveal to Vose that tests were low; it revealed that the glass was wrong.** What changed between 1919 (water theory) and 1925 (still defending water theory, unaware of glass) was not Vose's knowledge of the low tests but the state inspector's measurement capability. Merle Strawn, in 1921, began receiving burettes. On June 12, 1923, Strawn tested the pipettes and found them faulty.

**Before 1923, Vose may not have suspected the glass; but he certainly knew tests ran low**, because the ledger showed it every year. He signed those sheets.

# Section F — Theory and Three Competing Explanations

## F1. Causal theory of the Ostrey Hollow shortfall

**The True Cause:** In 1897, Ammon & Sons Dairy Supply shipped defective milk-test pipettes to the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. These pipettes, blown at 17.6 c.c., delivered only 16.72 c.c.—95% of their marked capacity. A milk test pipette is not simply a container; it is a measuring device on which the entire test depends. A charge of milk drawn up in a short pipette into a test bottle whose neck is ruled for a full charge produces a reading short in exact proportion. Every test made with these pipettes read 5% low. This proportional error meant richer milk was penalized more: a 4.56 butterfat milk read 4.32 (short 0.24); a 3.42 milk read 3.25 (short 0.17). Over 25 years, this 5% systematic error accumulated to 76,000 pounds of butterfat never credited to patrons—worth approximately $19,000 in period prices.

**Why this size:** The defect was inherent to the glass itself—the mold from which the pipettes were blown was misaligned or worn, producing glass 0.88 c.c. short. According to Junia Ammon (the company's historian), "the mold had been reground and the lot ran short... A boy checks the first of a lot and the tenth and the fiftieth and finds them consistent, because they are consistent—they are consistently wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind cannot find."

**Why it began when it did (1897):** Ammon & Sons delivered the pipettes on April 14, 1897, and they were installed May 1, 1897. The glass began producing low tests immediately. Ansel Keddie's test book shows 3.80 for 1896 (when borrowed glass from Larrow Green was in use) and 3.61 from 1898 onward (when the station's own glass was in use). The station received the defective equipment at the outset.

**Why it ended when it did (June 1923):** On June 12, 1923, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn tested the station's pipettes with a newly issued calibrated burette and found they would not deliver their mark. He condemned the crate on the spot. The Association ordered replacement glass on June 28, which arrived by October 1923. New glass was installed and verified by Strawn in October: "They deliver their mark." From 1924, tests read 3.80—the true value.

**Why nothing at the station or farms had to change for either transition:** The error was in the instrument, not the people or animals. No tester error was corrected; no practice changed; no cow improved. The 1911 anomaly proved this: when good glass was borrowed for nine weeks, tests rose to 3.80 despite everything else remaining identical. When the own glass returned, tests fell to 3.61 despite no change in anything else.

---

## Three Competing Explanations (and the evidence defeating each)

**Explanation 1 — Water-adding (Selby Vose's theory, 1919 onward):**
- **Claim:** Ostrey Hollow patrons were deliberately adding water to milk cans to increase weight or reduce density, thereby reducing butterfat percentage. The 1919 warning letter halted the practice, which is why tests rose in 1924.
- **Evidence defeating it:**
  - Merle Strawn tested for added water at every visit from 1908 forward (after 1908, every year) and recorded "Lactometer, no added water, as always here." This is consistent across his notebooks (r22, citing Strawn's field notes). A lactometer would detect watered milk by its density shift.
  - If water had been added and then ceased (as Vose claimed), the effect should have been larger in the 1920s than in the 1910s (as farmers stopped the practice). Instead, the 0.19 gap was constant every month of every year. A gradual improvement would be expected if water-adding had been occurring and then stopping; instead, tests rose sharply in 1924 (from 3.61 to 3.80) when the glass changed, not gradually over years.
  - The 1911 anomaly: when good glass was borrowed, tests rose to 3.80 in May–July 1911, before any warning letter had been issued (1919). If water-adding was the cause, it should not have mattered which glass was used. It did matter, and it mattered immediately.

**Explanation 2 — Cream theft (Wilbur Teague's structural observation, 1925):**
- **Claim:** Ansel Keddie, holding sole authority at the station, could remove cream and write low tests to conceal the loss. The structure of the office—one man, twenty-nine years—was vulnerable to this.
- **Evidence defeating it:**
  - The ledger showed "station gain" of 5.25% every year: butterfat credited to patrons was less than butterfat arriving at Larrow Green in the cream. If cream had been stolen, the cream would be light, not heavy. The ledger shows the opposite: the cream ran heavy, indicating more butterfat than the papers claimed had been bought. Wilbur Teague himself, despite making the structural criticism, noted this and "made no charge against anybody."
  - Emmet Brant's private weigh book showed his scale agreed with the station's scale for thirty years, "barring a pound or two on sloppy days." If Ansel had stolen milk at intake, the station's scale and Emmet's would diverge. They did not.
  - Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green for 41 years, testified: "That cream ran heavy. Every year it ran heavy. There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there." The receiving end showed no loss.
  - The 1911 anomaly: if Ansel were stealing cream, the same tester with the same apparatus and the same temptation would steal equally from good glass and bad glass. Instead, tests rose sharply when good glass was used. The behavior of the numbers tracks the glass, not the tester.

**Explanation 3 — Wet-hay effect (1908 committee, revived 1925–1926):**
- **Claim:** The wet meadow and coarse hay of Hessel Bottom caused nutritional stress in cattle, reducing butterfat content of their milk naturally, not by any human action.
- **Evidence defeating it:**
  - State Bulletin No. 214 tested bog hay under controlled conditions and found it depressed butterfat by "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." The maximum effect was 0.05 in winter, zero in summer. The Ostrey Hollow gap was 0.19 year-round. Five hundredths cannot produce nineteen hundredths.
  - The four Hessel Bottom men who hauled to Alder Corners Cheese Company (same wet-ground herds, same cows, same farmers) in summer flush 1915–1930 were tested at 3.78–3.82 (averaging 3.80), not 3.61. The same cows on the same wet bottom produced normal milk when tested by different glass. The defect was not in the cows or the bottom; it was in the test.
  - The 1911 anomaly: if wet hay were the cause, it would act on the cow regardless of which glass was used. Instead, when good glass was substituted for nine weeks, the same herds on the same bottom produced correct tests (3.80). When the bad glass returned, the same herds on the same bottom produced low tests (3.61) again. The dependency on the glass rules out the feed.

## Subsidiary Questions

**Did the board examine the condemned glassware?**

No. The four exhibits brought to the board were: (1) the test book, (2) the receiving book, (3) Emmet Brant's weigh book, (4) Merle Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook. The condemned pipettes themselves were not present. They had been carried up into the loft in June 1923 and were sitting thirty feet above the hearing room when the board met in April 1925. Effie Loomis noted explicitly: "The crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all... I took every word down and it is not there." The board examined Strawn's notebook entry stating the glass was condemned and that new glass was ordered, but the board never saw the glass itself.

**What did the tester believe the cause was?**

Ansel Keddie believed something was wrong with the glass, but he could not prove it and would not speculate. His statement on July 10, 1911, when the station's own measure was returned after the anomaly, was a question mark: "? — A.K." He told Duncan, his son, in the summer of 1911: "it was something in the glass." In his unsent letter (spring 1925), written but never delivered, he described the 1911 anomaly and wrote: "Those nine weeks in 1911 are the strangest thing in my book and I have never had an account of them to give... I could not answer it then and I cannot answer it now, and I will not put anything in its place that I do not know." He carried the question mark for fourteen years without answering it. Privately, to attorney Alonzo Frick, Ansel said "he always thought the patrons were at fault"—meaning water-adding—but Duncan's account and the note in his test book suggest Ansel's true suspicion lay with the glass itself, not with the farmers.

# Section G — Summary (max 150 words)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association operated a milk-testing station at Ostrey Hollow from 1896 onward using defective pipettes that delivered 5% less milk than marked, producing test results 5% systematically low. For 25 years (1898–1922), farmers were paid for less butterfat than they delivered—a loss of 76,000 pounds worth $19,000. In 1925, after a hearing that examined four exhibits but not the glass itself, the board awarded $3,800 (one-fifth the loss) and dismissed the tester. In 1958, the tester's granddaughter found the condemned glass in a loft, measured it, and proved the pipettes were short by 0.88 c.c. The state issued a bulletin exonerating the tester—who had died 24 years earlier. The scandal required eight separate records (farm weigh books, ledgers, test books, field notes, arbitration documents, family diaries, company correspondence, personal testimony) because the defect was invisible without measurement, the people involved never connected the pieces, and the truth lay in equipment stored above the hearing room.
