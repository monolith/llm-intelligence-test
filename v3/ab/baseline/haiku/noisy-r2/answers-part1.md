# Answer Sheet — Section A: Eight Stories (Stories 1–4)

## Story 1: Founding and Opening of the Larrow Valley Station (1889–1898)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was organized in 1889 at a meeting of local farmers at Larrow Green, with Gideon Vose elected first president. They built the main creamery at Larrow Green in 1891 on Larrow Creek. At the 1892 annual meeting, Article VII was adopted, establishing that every patron would be paid for butterfat content "ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." This last clause—the test of the station where delivered—became the foundation of all disputes that followed.

In 1895–1896, the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley, to serve Hessel Bottom (the wet meadow below the hamlet) and nine farming families of that end. The station opened May 4, 1896, with Ansel Keddie, age 35, hired as tester and separator man. Ansel's role was absolute: he weighed every milk delivery on a true scale (certified by state inspection), took samples into patron-numbered jars, tested them daily using glass pipettes to measure charge, recorded results in a ledger he kept in his own hand (five columns: date, patron, pounds, test percentage, fat), separated the cream from skim, and sent cream daily downhill to Larrow Green and skim back to farmers.

On April 14, 1897, equipment arrived from Ammon & Sons Dairy Supply, Tarnet, for $77.93: six dozen pipettes (17.6 c.c. blown mark), 12 dozen test bottles (8% neck), acid measure, hand tester (4-bottle), and freight. This equipment was in use from May 1, 1897. The pipettes were the critical tool: they measured the exact volume of milk to be tested, and that volume determined the calibration of the test bottle's neck. Ansel tested thirty-one times per week for twenty-nine seasons without missing the procedure once.

From the outset, Ostrey Hollow milk tested lower: averaging 3.61 butterfat where milk from Larrow Green averaged 3.78. The gap was 0.19 (nineteen hundredths), constant across seasons. In 1902, twelve years into operation, the first complaints of record emerged. For the next fifty-six years, patrons would wonder why their milk tested so much lower than neighbors' milk, and increasingly blame either the tester or their own cows.

**Documents:** Station test book (r24 excerpts); invoice Ammon & Sons 1897 (r20, Junia Ammon); Article VII (r22, Orin Falke); Ansel's note in test book "Begun this 4th day of May, 1896... Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes."

## Story 2: The 1908 Committee and the Wet-Hay Theory

In response to years of complaints (beginning 1902) that Ostrey Hollow milk tested lower, the Association formed an investigating committee chaired by Nyle Grigg, a farmer at Larrow Green (not a Hollow patron, ensuring impartiality). In January, February, and March 1908, the committee visited Hessel Bottom farms, sampled each herd, examined the cows, hay, pasture, and farming practices. Their conclusion: "The milk is whole and the scales are honest. It is our opinion that the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station."

The theory was scientifically plausible. Hessel Bottom was a wet meadow prone to flooding in spring (the creek rose to the sill of the station and twice came over it). The hay harvested from such ground was coarse sedge, rich in mineral-poor vegetation. Cattle fed on wet-meadow hay could suffer mineral deficiency, forcing them to mobilize body fat for milk production—a metabolic stress that slightly reduced butterfat content. The committee had walked the ground. They were practical men. The finding was published and later reprinted in 1919 by Nyle Grigg with a covering letter citing State Bulletin No. 214 from the State Agricultural College, claiming the College had found bog hay depresses butterfat by two tenths in winter.

However, the numbers proved insufficient. The gap at Ostrey Hollow was 0.19 (nineteen hundredths), constant year-round—July as well as February, in wet years and dry. State Bulletin No. 214, when later examined by county agent Orin Falke in 1959, stated only that bog hay "depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Five hundredths maximum could not produce nineteen hundredths in summer. The wet-hay theory was quantitatively too small by a factor of four, and it could not explain why the July gap equaled the February gap.

Crucially, four Hessel Bottom men hauled their milk to Alder Corners Cheese Company (over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County) in summer flush, 1915–1930, where the same milk tested 3.78–3.82 (averaging 3.80), not 3.61. Their cows on the same wet bottom produced normal milk when tested elsewhere. The remedy had to lie with the station, not the land.

**Documents:** 1908 committee report (r22, Orin Falke); Nyle Grigg's 1919 covering letter citing Bulletin No. 214 (r22); Alder Corners test records, 1919 season (r22, notes from Vessey County Historical Society); Orin Falke's 1959 note on Bulletin No. 214 stating actual depression was "not more than five hundredths."

## Story 3: The 1911 Measure Anomaly—The Experiment in Plain Sight

On May 8, 1911, the station's own milk-test pipette broke. Ansel Keddie noted this in the test book and noted it again when the broken pipette was taken for repair. Unable to proceed without the measure, he borrowed one from Larrow Green for the next nine weeks until July 10, 1911, when his own glass was returned. This interval became an accidental controlled experiment with a single variable.

During those nine weeks (May 8–July 10), Ostrey Hollow tests stood at 3.80 butterfat—the correct level, the level at which milk from those same wet-ground herds tested at Alder Corners. On July 10, the moment the station's own glass returned, tests fell back to 3.61, where they had been for the previous 14 years. The shift was immediate and complete.

Everything else was held constant: the same tester (Ansel), the same herds, the same season (summer grass), the same procedures, the same hand-tester apparatus, the same acid, the same bottles. Ansel made no change in technique—state inspector Merle Strawn had watched him test in June 1911 and noted "Nothing here to note." The farms made no change: the same cows were milked by the same men using the same practices. The weather changed from May to July as it always does, but the test reading changed only when the glass changed.

The 1911 interval proved that the glass was wrong. Ansel recognized something was amiss. He wrote a question mark in his book—"? — A.K."—and carried it for fourteen years, never able to explain it. "Those nine weeks in 1911 are the strangest thing in my book and I have never had an account of them to give... I could not answer it then and I cannot answer it now, and I will not put anything in its place that I do not know."

What the interval proved: when accurate glass was used, tests were correct (3.80). When the station's own glass was used, tests were 5% low. What it did not prove (without measurement): exactly how short the station's glass was. That required the crate to be found and measured in 1958.

**Documents:** Ansel's test book, 1911 entries (r18, Duncan Keddie); Ansel's unsent letter, spring 1925 (notes from r9, earlier sources); Merle Strawn's circuit notebook, June 1911: "Watched a test through. Nothing here to note" (r22, Orin Falke, citing original); Roy Brant's later statement that his father "kept his father's weigh book... in the same way... Two scales... agreed perfectly for 30 years, barring a pound or two on sloppy days" (notes).

## Story 4: The 1919 Water Letter and the Rupture of Ansel and Emmet

In January 1919, Selby Vose, manager of the Association (office 1913–1938), issued a printed circular letter to the nine families of Ostrey Hollow. The letter, civil in tone but unmistakable in implication, warned that water had been found in milk cans coming from the station and that the Association would be investigating. The language was, per Vose's own later defense, designed to warn patrons that water-adding "would be looked for and would be dealt with."

The letter fell like an accusation. Vira Toland, born in the hollow in 1912, remembered the day it arrived—it rained that day. Her father read it twice at the table, then filed it in his desk drawer, where it remained until he died. Other families kept theirs pinned in trunks. The letter implied that Hollow farmers were deliberately adding water to their milk to increase weight or reduce density, a felony of dishonesty.

Ansel Keddie took the letter himself, delivering it personally to each family, standing at their doors while they read it, returning home at eleven at night with his hat still on—unusual for him, noted by Orra in her diary. He did this because he would not let another man carry it. But the letter divided him from Emmet Brant, his brother-in-law and the largest patron.

Two days after the letter, in June 1919, Emmet came up to Ansel's house. He did not enter. He stood in the yard and confronted Ansel: he had hauled whole milk to the station since the day it opened, and Ansel knew it better than any man living. Why had Ansel carried the paper himself if he did not believe it? Ansel replied: he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet went home. He did not return to Ansel's house for ten years—they lived "within sight of each other's chimney smoke but did not speak."

Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector, tested for water at every subsequent visit (1919, 1920, 1922, 1924, 1925) and recorded: "Lactometer, no added water, as always here." No water was ever found. The 1919 letter was based on the 5% shortfall in tests, which Vose interpreted as water, but which was actually the defective glass. The letter poisoned the valley's relationships based on a misunderstanding of a physical defect that no one had measured and no one suspected.

**Documents:** The 1919 circular, typed by Bertram Kyle (r23); Orra's diary, June 1919 (r24); Vira Toland's recollection (r9, notes from earlier retellings); Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks for 1919–1925 (r22, Orin Falke, citing original); Selby Vose's 1926 defense (r10, notes from earlier retellings); Roy Brant's recollection: "He and I have not spoken since the circular" (r17, Hazel Brant).
