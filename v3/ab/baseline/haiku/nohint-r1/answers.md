# Answer Sheet — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

## Story 1: The Founding and Development of the Larrow Valley Cooperative and Its Ostrey Hollow Station (1889–1897)

Farmers of the Larrow Valley organized themselves into a cooperative creamery association in 1889 at a meeting in Larrow Green. They built the main creamery at Larrow Green in 1891, the same structure where milk would be received and cream processed. By the mid-1890s, growth and geography demanded a second facility. In the winter of 1895–1896, the association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green, to serve the farms of that end of the valley—Hessel Bottom in particular, a wet bottom of six farms. The station opened on May 4, 1896 (per Orin Falke's report). It was a simple board structure with two floors, a shingle roof, and a covered loading platform on the road side where farmers could roll milk cans level rather than lifting them. Inside was the intake room with the scale, testing apparatus, and separator. The station borrowed its testing glass from Larrow Green at first, then ordered its own outfit from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet. That outfit arrived in April 1897 and went into use on May 1, 1897. The association appointed Ansel Keddie as tester and separator man from the station's opening and he would hold the position for twenty-nine seasons. By-law Seven of the association (date of adoption not recorded) established that every patron would be paid according to pounds of butterfat as determined by test, not by weight of milk; and the test made at the delivery station would be the controlling test for payment. This legal structure became the hinge upon which the entire subsequent history turned.

## Story 2: Ammon & Sons and the Faulty Pipettes (1897–1923)

Marcus Ammon founded the glassware firm on a rented floor with two men and a secondhand oven. By the time Junia Ammon, the firm's historian at age sixty, wrote her account in 1949, Ammon & Sons occupied four stories and a basement at Tarnet, supplying half the state's creameries with glass: pipettes, test bottles, acid measures, thermometers. A milk test pipette is a precision tool—a glassblower gathers glass, blows the bulb, draws the stem down, and works to a gauge, creating a vessel that must deliver (not merely hold) a stated quantity of milk. The mark goes on the stem while the glass cools, then the piece goes into the lehr overnight and is gauged. The firm kept detailed works books recording what glass, what gauge, what man, what day. On April 14, 1897, Ammon & Sons sold to the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, for the Ostrey Hollow station, six dozen milk test pipettes at 17.6 c.c., twelve dozen test bottles, one acid measure, one hand tester, and freight—total $77.93. The pipettes were marked for 17.6 c.c., the standard charge for a butter fat test. The firm's historian later believed a mold had been reground, the lot ran short, and no one in the works caught it; such accidents happen when a gauge is knocked off its nail and replaced, or a mold comes back slightly different. On June 12, 1923, the state dairy inspector Merle Strawn visited the station, ran the pipettes against a calibrated burette, and found they would not deliver their mark. He condemned the entire lot and directed new glass be ordered. The association wrote to Ammon & Sons on June 28, 1923. The firm's reply of July 2, 1923 (in the letter book) stated they regretted the trouble but their books of the nineties could tell them nothing about that particular lot. The condemned glass—the crate with pipettes, test bottles, acid measures—was put into the loft over the intake room, where it sat undisturbed for thirty-five years.

## Story 3: Ansel Keddie's Twenty-Nine Seasons: The Persistent Discrepancy (1897–1911)

Ansel Keddie, born in 1861, married Orra Brant in 1889; Orra was the sister of Emmet Brant, the largest patron on Hessel Bottom. When the Ostrey Hollow station opened in 1896, Ansel was appointed tester and separator man—the man who would weigh incoming milk, take a sample, test the sample for butter fat by volume on the pipette, separate the milk, send the cream down to Larrow Green, and return the skim to the farmer. He would record his test figures in the station's own test book. From the earliest days of the station, the milk tested at Ostrey Hollow tested lower than milk tested anywhere else in the county. Over the long period of complaint (from 1902 onward), the station's average test stood at 3.61 percent butter fat, where the Larrow Green creamery's average over the same years was 3.78—a deficiency of nineteen hundredths of a percent. This nineteen-hundredth shortfall appeared in every month, the flush of June and the depth of February alike, in wet years and dry, with the flatness of a floor. Ansel was meticulous; he kept his books in his own careful hand, recording both his test and the Larrow Green retest, checking for agreement. He was the kind of man who would stand in the doorway of the weigh room with his hat on until discrepancies were explained. In May 1911, an event occurred that would later prove the solution. On May 8, 1911, the station's own measuring pipette broke. For nine weeks, from May 8 to July 10, 1911, the station used a measure borrowed from elsewhere. During those nine weeks, the tests stood markedly higher. On the day the station resumed its own glass, the results fell back to their former level. Ansel noted the fact in his test book and set a question mark against it—his way of recording uncertainty without making an accusation he could not prove. The herds had not changed, the pasture had not changed, the season was the same, the men were the same, the machinery was the same. Only the glass had changed. The answer lay in plain view but would not be seen for fifty years.

## Story 4: The Manager's Response and the Community's Doubt (1902–1924)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Association was managed by Gideon Vose from its founding through his tenure as first president. By 1911 his son Selby Vose had become manager, a position he held for a quarter century through 1936. Bertram Kyle, the clerk at the outer desk in the Larrow Green office for twenty-five years, observed Selby Vose daily: a courteous man who took a sheet, turned it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and signed—a manager who read the totals and never read the lines above them. The bookkeeper was Wilbur Teague, a dry man who wrote the station account lines every year. Each year, the station account for Ostrey Hollow contained the same three figures and the same sentence: pounds of butterfat credited to the patrons on the station test; pounds received at Larrow Green in the station's cream; and the difference, entered as "station gain," typically five and a quarter percent, marked in the ledger "as usual, carried to the general account." Kyle copied this line more times than he could count; it had a name, and a figure with a name on it has been explained, so he ruled under it and moved on. No one questioned it. From 1902 onward, patrons complained that Ostrey Hollow's milk tested lower than anywhere else. The Association's officers had theories: the Ostrey Hollow farmers were adding water; Ansel Keddie was stealing cream and writing low tests to conceal it; or the wet hay and poor pasture of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream. In 1908, a committee sampled the Hessel Bottom herds and reported that the hay might be the cause. In 1919, however, rather than investigate further, manager Selby Vose circulated a warning circular to nine families on the bottom, written in civil language but leaving no doubt, warning the patrons about adding water to their milk. Orra Keddie read hers twice and put it in the stove. Ansel took the nine letters round himself and stood at the door while each man read it, coming home at eleven at night with his hat still on. Her brother Emmet never returned to Ansel's house. The complaint persisted through the 1920s until 1925, when matters came to a head.


# Answer Sheet — Part 2: Section A, Stories 5–8 and Section B

## Story 5: The 1925 Arbitration Hearing and Dismissal

In January 1925, the patrons of Ostrey Hollow filed a formal petition against the station. A board of arbitration was appointed to sit under the chairmanship of an attorney at Ashlin. There were three sittings: the first at Ashlin from March 17 to May 4, 1925, and two more held at the station itself in the weigh room where the scales and machinery stood. Bertram Kyle carried the critical documents: the station's test book in Ansel Keddie's hand, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book (lent for the purpose), and the state inspector's notebook for 1923 when he condemned the glass. Kyle carried them in and carried them out and could never have told anyone, nor could tell now, whether any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked. The board heard testimony on three competing theories: that the patrons had added water (refuted by state dairy inspector records showing no added water found at any visit since 1908); that Ansel Keddie had stolen cream and written low tests to conceal it (refuted by the nine weeks in 1911 when borrowed glass was used and tests rose, while Ansel remained in post); and that the wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream (supported by a committee report from 1908 and a veterinarian's letter from 1926, though a 1911 State Agricultural College feeding trial on bog hay showed it depressed butter fat by at most five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer—insufficient to carry nineteen hundredths). The board handed down its award on May 4, 1925: three thousand eight hundred dollars to the Ostrey Hollow patrons, "without admission" of any cause—lawyer's language for "we pay you and we say nothing." But on April 30, 1925, four days before the award, Ansel Keddie was dismissed. He came up the road at his ordinary hour, with his canvas apron rolled under his arm, hung it behind the shed door, and asked what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it. He had been tester and separator man at that station for twenty-nine seasons.

## Story 6: Ansel Keddie After Dismissal (1925–1934)

After his dismissal in 1925, Ansel Keddie, at age sixty-four, took up carpentry. He kept four or five cows on a small piece of ground and hung barn doors for neighbors, good work that swung true for thirty years. Orra noted in her diary that in June 1925, soon after dismissal, he began on the cow shed that had wanted doing since his father's time, and worked at it from light to dark and whistled—which he never did when he was paid. By October 1927 he was mending stairs at the far end of the hollow and the Loomis gate and half the doors in the valley, taking what people offered. A carpenter at sixty-six, he was better at it than he ever was at testing, and it paid a quarter of what the testing had done. He lived nine years with the imputation on him, in the county, among men he had weighed milk for since before their fathers died. Orra outlived him by fourteen years and kept every letter, though there was nothing in them that anybody would want—only the weather, the price of things, her brother Emmet's health. She spoke of the business only once a year in April, in grief and silence. Ansel died at home on a February morning in 1934, without much pain at the end. Duncan came up in the night. More attended the funeral than Orra looked for—men who had not spoken to him in nine years shook her hand and told her what a hand he was with a door. Not one of them said the other thing. Not one named what they had accused him of. He had never asked them to. He had said to Orra once, and only once, that a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name.

## Story 7: The Orra Keddie Household: Family and Farm (1897–1934)

Orra Brant was born in 1868, the younger sister of Emmet Brant who farmed the largest place on Hessel Bottom. She married Ansel Keddie in 1889. Their son Duncan was born in 1894 and grew up to teach school at Ashlin, beginning at seventeen. Orra kept a diary in ruled notebooks from 1897 to 1930 (she was sixty-four in August 1930 and her hand was not what it was), plus a loose leaf written in 1934 after Ansel's death. The diary records daily life: ground still frozen in April, setting eggs, Ansel walking to the station in all weather before light and returning after dark, wild strawberries picked on the way, hay work, threshing, apple trees planted that she would not live to see bear well. It records the seasons—March mud to the hub of the wagons, February freezes, the low piece under water in April rains. She notes her brother Emmet's visits and his dignity as the largest patron. In June 1919, the circular warning about water in the milk came, and Ansel took the letters round himself. In June 1919, two days after, Emmet came up to the house and stood in the yard and asked Ansel why he had carried the paper himself if he did not believe it. Ansel said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet went home. He had not been in that house since, though he was her brother. In January 1925, the patrons filed their petition; Emmet's name was on it. Orra wrote: "I do not blame him and I will not say so to A." On March 17, 1925, Ansel went down to Ashlin in his good coat (the one he'd had since Duncan married) in his good coat, which he had worn since Duncan was married. He was up at three and did not sleep before that. Orra ironed the coat twice. In April 1925, two sittings were held up at the station itself; she did not go down but heard the wagons from the yard all that afternoon. On April 30, he came up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm. In June 1925, he began the carpentry that would sustain them. Spring 1929, word came that Emmet had died. Roy, Emmet's son, met her at the gate and was kind. She sat an hour in the kitchen where she was a girl and came home. Ten years, and neither of them would go up the road first. Now it was settled the way those things get settled. July 1929, hollyhocks bloomed the best she had had. Ansel sat out with her an evening and named the ones her mother had. February 1934, Ansel died at home in the forenoon. Duncan came up in the night. More attended the burying than she looked for.

## Story 8: The Discovery and Clearing (1958)

In February 1958, thirty-three years after Ansel Keddie was dismissed and twenty-four years after his death, his great-niece Ivy Keddie, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, climbed the ladder into the loft over the old Ostrey Hollow station's intake room. There, under a tarpaulin and about an inch of chaff, lay the wooden crate that had been condemned by state dairy inspector Merle Strawn in June 1923. She carried the crate east to Farland. In March 1958, in a college laboratory, she measured the nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette. Every one was blown on the stem 17.6 c.c., the standard mark. Not one delivered it. The average of the lot came to 16.27 c.c.—a shortfall of 1.33 c.c. per pipette, which is exactly 7.6 percent. A short charge of milk goes into a bottle whose neck is ruled for the full charge, and the fat reads low in the same proportion, in every test, forever. Nobody watered anything. Nobody stole anything. The scales were true. The acid was right. The cows were ordinary. A piece of glass out of a crate was wrong. In October 1958, Professor Leland Vaught of the college's department of dairy husbandry and Ivy Keddie published Bulletin No. 471 from the State Agricultural College at Farland. The bulletin calculated that over the full delivery years affected, the Ostrey Hollow patrons were unpaid for sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat, worth $19,600 in the money of those years. It documented the nineteen-hundredth shortfall with mathematical exactness and traced it to the faulty pipettes. The Ashlin Chronicle ran a front-page feature, "Old Ordell Wrong Righted," and asked why Ammon & Sons had not said what became of the lot in 1923. Orin Falke, county agricultural agent, prepared a report for the county board. In his closing note, after checking the college's 1911 feeding trial on bog hay, he withdrew his earlier concession that hay could account for the shortfall. Ansel Keddie's name was cleared twenty-four years after his death. His widow Orra had died in 1948 and did not live to see it.

# Section B — Relationships

## B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. The sources show: Ansel married Orra Brant in 1889; their son was Duncan Keddie (born 1894); Duncan's daughter was Ivy Keddie (born 1931). Therefore Ansel was Ivy's grandfather. The 1958 Ashlin Chronicle article incorrectly identified her as Ansel's "great-niece," but Orin Falke's county report corrected this in a note added in 1959, stating: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." This correction is authoritative because Falke was writing for the county record and verified the fact. Duncan Keddie's own family notes confirm the lineage: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise."

## B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant?

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. The derivation: Ansel Keddie married Orra Brant; Orra's brother was Emmet Brant; Emmet's son was Roy Brant; Roy's daughter was Hazel Brant. Therefore Emmet and Roy were uncle and nephew (brother and nephew, actually—Emmet was Roy's father), making Roy and Ansel's wife Orra siblings. Since Roy and Ansel's son Duncan are first cousins (children of siblings), Duncan's daughter Ivy and Roy's daughter Hazel are second cousins (children of first cousins). Duncan Keddie's notes are explicit: "Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece, which people say because it is shorter." Cleve Oram incorrectly called Ivy "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed," which is wrong; the intervening generation is clear—Emmet/Ansel's generation, then Roy/Duncan's generation, then Hazel/Ivy's generation.

## B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter?

Selby Vose was the son of Gideon Vose, the man who organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Association and was its first president. Duncan Keddie's family notes make this explicit: "Selby Vose, who managed the Association, was Gideon Vose's son. Gideon organized the Association and was its first president and wrote its by-laws; Selby was his son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew." Bertram Kyle (the clerk) also confirms: "Mr. Selby Vose became manager in nineteen eleven. He was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president—the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense." This matters because Selby Vose, as manager, was the officer who signed the ledger sheets every year that included the "station gain" line showing the five and a quarter percent loss at Ostrey Hollow. If he truly never read the lines above the totals (as Kyle observed), then the loss was visible in the books but unexamined by management for a quarter century.

## B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?

There was no family relationship between the Vose family and the Keddie family. Duncan Keddie's family notes directly address rumors to the contrary: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either. I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her sit down. It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me. She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it." Cleve Oram mentions rumor: "And I will say this while I am on families, because you will hear it anyway and you may as well hear it from somebody who is not repeating it out of malice. The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort." But Duncan Keddie's explicit statement (made by someone who knew the family records intimately) carries more weight: Orra never told him of any such connection, and she was meticulous about family relationships. The relationship, if any, is purely through institutional positions at the Association—Gideon Vose founded and presided over it; Ansel Keddie worked as tester and separator man within it. There was no blood tie.

# Section C — Math (show working)

## C1. Pipette shortfall and calculations

**(a) Proportion by which tests differed from the truth, derived from glassware:**

From Junia Ammon and the 1958 measurements: The pipettes were marked 17.6 c.c. (the standard charge for a butter fat test). When Ivy Keddie measured the surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette in March 1958, they delivered an average of 16.27 c.c.

Shortfall per pipette: 17.6 − 16.27 = 1.33 c.c.
Proportion shortfall: 1.33 ÷ 17.6 = 0.0756 or **7.56% short**

This means every test reading would be 7.56% lower than the actual milk's fat content. Stated as a multiplicative factor, a recorded test of X% represented milk that was actually X ÷ 0.9244 = 1.0816X (approximately).

**(b) True average test and shortfall:**

Station's recorded average over the complaint period: 3.61% (Orin Falke)
Larrow Green reference average over same years: 3.78% (Orin Falke)
Recorded shortfall: 3.78 − 3.61 = 0.17 percentage points

If the glass was uniformly 7.56% short, and Larrow Green tested at 3.78%, the true Ostrey Hollow average should have been approximately 3.78% (same milk, tested honestly). The recorded tests were short by approximately **0.17 percentage points** (sources round this to "nineteen hundredths," which may include rounding of intermediate figures).

More precisely: if recorded 3.61% represents milk that was actually 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.90%, then the shortfall would be 3.90 − 3.61 = 0.29 percentage points. The figure of 0.19 percentage points in Falke's report may reflect a different baseline or rounding.

**(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited:**

Cleve Oram states directly: "Sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat was what they made it, over the whole run of years, that the Hollow men were never paid for."

The 1958 bulletin confirms this figure: **67,000 pounds of butterfat** over the full affected years.

**(d) Value and fraction received:**

From the Ashlin Chronicle: "The bulletin works out what the Ostrey Hollow patrons were never paid for the butter fat they actually hauled to that station, and the figure is nineteen thousand six hundred dollars ($19,600) — in a valley of six farms on a wet bottom, in the money of the eighteen-nineties and the years after."

Total value not paid: **$19,600**

Value actually received: In 1925, the arbitration board awarded the patrons $3,800 "without admission." This was partial compensation, so the fraction received was 3,800 ÷ 19,600 = **0.1939 or approximately 19.4%** (less than one-fifth of what was actually owed).

## C2. The largest patron (Emmet Brant) in 1910

**(a) Pounds of butterfat credited:**

The sources do not provide Emmet Brant's individual delivery figures for 1910. Bertram Kyle states that Emmet "hauled the most milk into the station of any man on the creek" and that the station received "forty million pounds of milk... sixteen hundred thousand pounds a year." If Emmet supplied approximately 25-30% (reasonable for the largest patron of six farms), this would be 400,000-480,000 pounds per year. At a 3.61% test, this would yield roughly 14,400-17,300 pounds of butterfat credited. However, this is an inference, not a stated figure.

**Cannot be determined precisely from the sources.**

**(b) Pounds owed and not credited:**

If Emmet supplied 25-30% of the station's milk and was tested at 3.61% when it should have tested at approximately 3.78-3.90%, he was owed the 7.56% shortfall on his annual butterfat. Applying this to an inferred 15,000 pounds credited would yield approximately 1,134 pounds owed. However, **the exact figure for Emmet's 1910 delivery cannot be determined from the sources.**

**(c) Value difference that year:**

Bertram Kyle states butterfat averaged "twenty-five cents the pound" over the full stretch. If 1,100-1,200 pounds were owed, at $0.25/pound, this would be **approximately $275-300 worth** in 1910 money. **But again, Emmet's specific 1910 figures are not given.**

## C3. Total delivery years: accounting and percentages

**(a) Pounds credited to patrons in total:**

Bertram Kyle: "For the long run of years that the patrons afterward complained of... Forty million pounds of milk received at that station... at an average test of 3.61%..."

Total butterfat credited: 40,000,000 pounds × 0.0361 = **1,440,000 pounds**

**(b) Pounds that actually reached the main creamery:**

The station's "station gain" was consistently recorded as "five and a quarter percent, as usual." This is the difference between butterfat credited and butterfat received at Larrow Green.

If 1,440,000 pounds of butterfat was credited, and 5.25% was lost/retained, then:
Butterfat actually received = 1,440,000 × (1 − 0.0525) = 1,440,000 × 0.9475 = **1,364,400 pounds**

**(c) Express the difference as a percentage:**

Difference: 1,440,000 − 1,364,400 = 75,600 pounds

This 75,600 pounds represents the "station gain." **What percentage is it of?**

If expressed as a percentage of butterfat credited: 75,600 ÷ 1,440,000 = **5.25%** (confirming the "five and a quarter percent" figure)

If expressed as a percentage of butterfat actually received: 75,600 ÷ 1,364,400 = **5.54%**

The standard in the books was 5.25% of credited butterfat, reflecting separation efficiency and normal cream recovery.

## C5. Duration of faulty glassware and delivery years affected

**(a) How long was glassware in use; why duration differs from money calculation period:**

*Faulty glassware in use:* May 1, 1897 (when "the station's own outfit... went into use," per Bertram Kyle) to June 12, 1923 (when state inspector Merle Strawn "condemned the whole lot," per Orin Falke).

Duration: **26 years and 1.5 months** (or approximately **26–27 delivery seasons**)

*Money calculation period:* The patrons began complaining in 1902 (first recorded complaint, per Orin Falke). The arbitration board awarded compensation in 1925 (May 4). The money calculation thus covers **approximately 1902–1925, or about 23–24 full delivery years** (depending on where the boundaries are drawn—May to May for dairy seasons).

**Why they differ:** The glass was faulty from installation (May 1897) but patrons did not notice or lodge formal complaints until 1902. Five years of losses (1897–1902) were never formally recognized. Additionally, the arbitration board's award covered the complaint period (1902–1925) rather than the full period the glass was in use. The true loss occurred over 26–27 years, but compensation was calculated (and inadequately provided) for only 23–24 years.

**(b) First season's milk delivery:**

Bertram Kyle (the clerk who handled the records): "People have asked me what the Hollow took in that first year and I have always said the same: a good eleven hundred thousand pounds, which is what I understood at the time from men who were there. It is not in any book I ever ruled."

**First season (May 1896–May 1897): approximately 1,100,000 pounds** (estimate from oral report, not recorded in surviving books)

# Section D — Logic

## D1. The 1911 measure-swap interval as an experiment

**When and duration:**
May 8, 1911: The station's own pipette broke.
May 8 – July 10, 1911: A borrowed pipette from elsewhere was used.
July 10, 1911 onward: The station resumed its own pipette.
**Duration: 9 weeks (Ansel Keddie's account; Orin Falke's report)**

**The experimental design:**
- **Variable tested:** The measuring glass (pipette)
- **Held constant:** The herds, the pasture, the season (spring to summer 1911), the tester (Ansel Keddie), the machinery, all other conditions
- **Result:** During the 9 weeks with borrowed glass, tests stood markedly higher. When the original glass resumed, tests fell back to the original lower level.
- **Establishes:** The disparity in test results was attributable to the glass, not to herd quality, pasture, season, operator error, or fraud. This eliminates three of the four theories: (1) the water-addition theory (the cows and milk didn't change), (2) the cream-stealing theory (the tester didn't change and his methodology was the same), and potentially (3) the pasture-quality theory (seasonal pasture didn't change in May-July).

**What the interval does NOT establish:**
- It does not prove that the station's glass was permanently faulty; only that there was a functional difference on that particular date.
- It does not quantify the magnitude of the error or how long the station's glass had been faulty before May 1911.
- It does not prove the glass was defective by manufacture rather than damaged through use before 1911.
- A skeptic could argue the borrowed glass was superior or maintained to higher standards than the station's usual practice.

However, combined with the 1923 condemned glass measurement and the consistent 0.19-point shortfall over decades, this 1911 interval provides the most direct physical evidence that glass was the cause.

## D2. Three independent reasons why Ansel Keddie cannot have stolen cream

1. **The 1911 glass-swap interval (Ansel Keddie's own account and Orin Falke's analysis):** During the 9 weeks when a borrowed pipette was used, test results rose significantly while the same tester (Ansel) remained in post under no different supervision. If Ansel were stealing cream and concealing it by false testing, the stolen cream would have been absent whether the glass was his or borrowed. The fact that tests rose with the borrowed glass indicates the glass, not Ansel's manipulation, caused the low tests. **This reason holds even if the glassware had never been measured in 1958.**

2. **The 1923 condemned glass measurement (Ivy Keddie, State Agricultural College 1958):** The glass was physically measured and found to deliver 16.27 c.c. instead of 17.6 c.c. This deficiency produces a mechanical 7.56% undercount in every test, regardless of operator or intent. No human theft scheme could produce such uniform, perfectly consistent error across 26 years. **This is independent of reason 1 and was discovered in 1958.**

3. **The station account ledger, kept by Wilbur Teague and signed off by manager Selby Vose (Bertram Kyle's testimony):** Every year, the station account showed three figures: butterfat credited to patrons on the station test, butterfat received at Larrow Green, and the difference as "station gain," consistently "five and a quarter percent, as usual." If Ansel were stealing cream, this gain would vary—more cream stolen in flush season (June), less in lean season (February). Instead, the 5.25% was constant. Moreover, Teague (the bookkeeper) entered these figures reliably and never questioned the ledger line. Kyle (the clerk) observed that Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years"—not because he was complicit, but because the figures were the figures and the asking was for other people. This independent documentary evidence contradicts the theft theory.

**Independence:** Reasons 1 and 2 are independent because (1) relies on experimental logic from a single interval and (2) relies on physical measurement. Reason 3 is independent of both because it is documentary and administrative, not experimental or physical. Reason 1 would hold true even without the 1958 measurement because it proves the glass caused variation simply by showing tests rose when glass changed—without needing to know by how much the glass was faulty.

**Question: Did the Association's bookkeeper ever question the ledger entries that refute the accusation against Ansel?**

No. Wilbur Teague was "a dry man, very correct" who kept meticulous station records. But according to Bertram Kyle, Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years. That is not a criticism. It was his notion of the position: the figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people." Teague recorded the "station gain" line year after year as instructed, but there is no evidence he ever questioned why the gain was constant, or compared it to patterns that might indicate theft versus mechanical error. The ledger entries themselves refute the theft accusation, but the man who wrote them appears never to have examined what they meant.

## D3. Did the manager know, before the glassware was condemned, that tests ran low?

**Answer: Yes, almost certainly, but he chose not to act on the information.**

**Justification:**

Manager Selby Vose took office in 1911 (Bertram Kyle). From that point onward, he signed the ledger sheets every year showing the station account with its persistent "5.25% station gain" and the recorded tests at approximately 3.61%. 

Vose would have seen (or had access to see):
1. The written complaint of 1902 (first recorded complaint, per Orin Falke)
2. His father Gideon's founding documents and the by-laws establishing that tests control payment
3. The circular warning about water that **he himself signed and sent in 1919**—an action that only makes sense if he was aware tests ran low
4. The ledger sheets showing the Ostrey Hollow station consistently at 3.61% while Larrow Green ran at 3.78%

Bertram Kyle's observation is conclusive: Vose was "courteous to every man who came into that office... and he never once let a caller feel he was being hurried. And he read the totals and he never read the lines above them. He was not a man who read."

Vose knew the tests ran low because:
- He signed the 1919 circular alleging water (implying low tests = suspected cheating)
- He saw the numbers in the ledgers he signed
- Every patron complained

He did not investigate because it was easier to pay out $3,800 in 1925 "without admission" than to examine the glass, read his own books closely, or entertain the possibility that an expensive piece of machinery installed at his own father's association in 1897 might be faulty.

# Section E — Contradictions

After careful reading of all 24 retellings, here are the factual contradictions found:

### E1. Ivy Keddie's exact relationship to Ansel Keddie

**Conflicting claims:**
- 1958 Ashlin Chronicle: "Miss Ivy Keddie... Her great-niece" (describing her as Ansel's great-niece)
- 1959 Orin Falke county report (added note): "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine."
- Duncan Keddie family notes (1968): "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise and once heard it said aloud in this town by a man who ought to have known."

**Resolution:** Ivy was Ansel's granddaughter (great-niece was an error). **Resolution method:** Primary documentary evidence (Duncan Keddie's own genealogical notes tracing descent) outranks a newspaper article. Falke's correction corroborates this. The Chronicle made an error which was publicly corrected.

### E2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant relationship

**Conflicting claims:**
- Cleve Oram (1977): "Ivy Keddie was Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed, that is the way of it"
- Duncan Keddie (1968): "Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation"

**Resolution:** Second cousins is correct; first cousin once removed is incorrect. Oram's genealogy was wrong. **Resolution method:** Duncan Keddie's genealogical notes, written from primary family knowledge, outrank Oram's inference. Duncan explicitly states he is correcting this confusion: "I have already twice seen it printed otherwise." Deriving: Emmet Brant (Roy's father) and Orra Brant/Keddie (Duncan's mother) are siblings → Roy and Duncan are first cousins → Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are second cousins.

### E3. Selby Vose's relationship to Gideon Vose

**Conflicting claims:**
- Cleve Oram (1977): "The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort" (implies blood relation)
- Bertram Kyle (1953): "I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense" (contradicting the notion that Selby was Gideon's nephew)
- Duncan Keddie (1968): "Selby Vose, who managed the Association, was Gideon Vose's son...Selby was his son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew"

**Contradiction within narrative:** Bertram Kyle says the "nephew" story is nonsense but doesn't explicitly state Selby was the son. However, he does refer to "Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president—the son, whatever is said at Ashlin" (meaning Selby was the son, not nephew, regardless of what Ashlin gossips said).

**Resolution:** Selby Vose was Gideon's son, not nephew. **Method:** Duncan Keddie's explicit genealogical note and Kyle's strong denial of the nephew theory.

### E4. When the station's glass was condemned

**Conflicting claims:**
- Orin Falke (1959): "On the 12th of June of that year [1923] the state dairy inspector...ran the station's milk pipettes against a calibrated burette and found that they would not deliver their mark. He condemned the whole lot on the spot"
- Bertram Kyle (1953): "Then in twenty-two the state man came into the office...he had condemned the whole lot of them, and he came in to say so"

**Apparent contradiction:** "Twenty-two" (1922) vs. "the 12th of June...1923"

**Resolution:** The Falke source (1959) is more precise and authoritative (county record with specific date: June 12, 1923). Kyle's "twenty-two" is an off-by-one error in his recollection nearly 25 years later. **Method:** The documentary source (Falke's report citing the state inspector's records) outranks memory; the exact date June 12, 1923 is too specific to be accidental.

### E5. When milk testing glassware arrived at the station

**Conflicting claims:**
- Bertram Kyle (1953): "its own outfit came from the supply house and went into use on the first of May, 1898"
- Junia Ammon (1949) invoice: "dated the fourteenth of April, 1897" for sale to Larrow Valley Creamery for Ostrey Hollow station
- Hazel Brant (1981): "My grandfather Emmet began it [the weigh book] in nineteen hundred, on the first day of January, because he was a man who wanted a clean date under a new habit"

**Apparent contradiction:** May 1, 1898 vs. April 14, 1897 invoice

**Resolution:** The invoice is dated April 14, 1897 (manufacturing date), but the station opened May 4, 1896 (per Orin Falke). Kyle says the outfit "went into use on the first of May, 1898"—but Orin Falke says the station borrowed glass from Larrow Green initially, then "The Larrow Valley Association put that outfit into use at its Ostrey Hollow station on the first of May, 1897." **Method:** Orin Falke's official county report, written from records, against Kyle's 25-year-old recollection. The invoice date (April 14, 1897) and Falke's account (May 1, 1897) align; Kyle was wrong about the year. **Corrected date: May 1, 1897** (not 1898).

### E6. Ansel Keddie's dismissal date

**Conflicting claims:**
- Orra Keddie diary (1925): "April 30, 1925. They have put him out."
- Duncan Keddie family notes (1968): "He was dismissed on the thirtieth of April, 1925, four days before the board handed down what it called its finding"
- Orin Falke (1959): "The tester was dismissed on the 30th of April, 1925, four days before the award"

**No contradiction found:** All three sources agree on April 30, 1925.

### E7. The amount awarded to patrons

**Conflicting claims:**
- Cleve Oram (1977): "What they gave the patrons at the end of it was thirty-five hundred dollars"
- Duncan Keddie (1968): "the board handed down...without admission of anything by anybody" (no figure given)
- Orin Falke (1959): "awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons three thousand eight hundred dollars, without admission"
- Orra Keddie diary (1925): "[The award of the board is not mentioned in the diary. — Ed.]"

**Conflicting claims:** Oram says $3,500; Falke says $3,800.

**Resolution:** Falke's county official report (1959), written from board records, states $3,800. Oram's recollection is approximate ("What they gave...was") and off by $300. Cleve Oram was 25 years removed from the 1925 event (recalling in 1977). **Correct figure: $3,800.** **Method:** Official documented record outranks oral recollection.

### Summary of contradiction resolution methods used:
1. **Documentary sources (invoices, ledgers, official reports) > Recollected oral accounts** (E4, E7)
2. **Explicit genealogical notes > Inference or rumor** (E1, E2, E3)
3. **Exact dates from records > Rounded or off-by-one recollection** (E4, E5)

No contradictions appear to reflect actual disagreement about events—all reflect differences between documentary precision and human memory.

# Section F — Theory

## F1. Causal theory of the Ostrey Hollow shortfall

**The Theory:**

The Ostrey Hollow milk testing shortfall was caused by a single, mechanical defect in the station's testing glassware, specifically the milk test pipettes supplied by Ammon & Sons of Tarnet in April 1897. These pipettes were manufactured to deliver 17.6 cubic centimeters of milk (the standard charge for a butter fat test), but due to an error in the mold or gauge (likely a reground mold with no catch for the error), they delivered an average of 16.27 c.c.—1.33 c.c. short, or 7.56% undercharge.

This mechanical undercharge produced a proportional undercount in every test: when milk containing X% butter fat was measured in a short pipette and tested, the result would be approximately 0.9244X (7.56% lower). This error was **constant across all seasons and all years**: the 19 hundredths of a percentage point shortfall appeared in June and February alike, in wet years and dry, with the "flatness of a floor" because it was a mechanical constant, not a biological or seasonal variable.

**Why the error began when it did:** The faulty pipettes went into use May 1, 1897, when the station's own testing outfit (purchased from Ammon & Sons) was first employed. No one in the works at Ammon & Sons had detected the defect at manufacture; the mold had been reground, and a gauge placed on a nail gets knocked off and replaced; a mold comes back from the shop a hair different but looks the same. The check caught some pipettes but not all, or none.

**Why the error ended when it did:** On June 12, 1923, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn visited the station with his calibrated burette (an independent standard), ran the station's pipettes against it, found they would not deliver their mark, and condemned the entire lot. The Association ordered new glass within days. From July 1923 onward, when new glass arrived, the station's average test stood at 3.85%, consistent with Larrow Green's 3.78%—the error ceased.

**Why nothing at the station or on the farms had to change:** The milk from the Hessel Bottom farms was of normal quality. The herds had not changed. The pasture was the same wet bottom. The operator (Ansel Keddie) was meticulous and careful, unchanged for 29 years. The machinery worked correctly. The separator worked correctly. What had changed was the single thing no one was looking for: the capacity of a piece of glass.

**Three competing explanations and the evidence that defeats each:**

1. **The water-addition theory** (advanced by the Association's manager, Selby Vose, and embodied in the 1919 circular warning patrons about added water):
   - **Specific evidence that defeats it:** State dairy inspector Merle Strawn tested for added water at every circuit visit from 1908 forward, using the lactometer, and never once found added water at the Ostrey Hollow station (Orin Falke). If water had been systematically added for 26 years, it would have been detected in those tests. Furthermore, tests rose during the 9-week interval in 1911 when a borrowed pipette was used—the milk hadn't changed, the farmers hadn't changed their practices, and there would be no reason for water-adding to suddenly stop for 9 weeks.

2. **The cream-stealing theory** (advanced by an Association officer at the 1925 hearing: Ansel Keddie was stealing cream and writing low tests to conceal the loss):
   - **Specific evidence that defeats it:** The 1911 glass-swap experiment. For 9 weeks, the same tester (Ansel) using a borrowed pipette produced higher tests. If Ansel were stealing cream, the cream would be gone whether the glass was his or borrowed; tests should have remained low. Instead, they rose. This proves Ansel's methodology was honest and the glass was the variable. Additionally, Bertram Kyle's ledger records show "station gain" (the cream recovered at Larrow Green as a percentage of credited butterfat) was consistently 5.25% year after year—a steady and legitimate separation rate, not a variable theft rate that would be larger in some years and smaller in others.

3. **The pasture-quality theory** (advanced by a 1908 Association committee visiting Hessel Bottom, and supported by a veterinarian's 1926 letter: the wet hay and poor pasture of the bottom thinned the cream):
   - **Specific evidence that defeats it:** A State Agricultural College feeding trial published in Bulletin No. 214 (1911), citing bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage, showed depression of butter fat test by not more than 5 hundredths in winter and nothing in summer. Orin Falke, noting this trial outcome, explicitly withdrew his earlier concession that hay could account for the shortfall: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July. I withdraw the concession made on page four." The 0.19 percentage point shortfall (more precisely, 0.17–0.29 percentage points depending on baseline) cannot be explained by pasture alone. Moreover, the Hessel Bottom herds did not change between 1897 and 1923; if poor pasture was the cause, the shortfall should have been present from the first day, discovered immediately, and corrected. Instead, complaints did not arise until 1902, and no one suspected the glass until 1923.

**Subsidiary questions:**

**(1) Did the 1925 board of arbitration ever examine the condemned glassware?**

No. The condemned crate sat in the loft over the intake room from June 1923 to February 1958—35 years. Bertram Kyle carried four documents to the arbitration board in 1925: the station's test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book, and the state inspector's notebook for 1923. He carried them in, carried them out, and "could never have told anyone...whether any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked." The inspector's notebook documented that the glass had been condemned in June 1923, just months before the arbitration began. However, no one in that room examined the actual glass, measured it, or tested it. The crate itself "was twenty feet, if that" above the weigh room where two of the three sittings were held—visible, accessible, but ignored.

**(2) What did the tester himself believe the cause was?**

Ansel Keddie believed (or at least came to suspect) that the glass was at fault. In May 1911, when the station's pipette broke and was replaced with a borrowed one, he noted in his test book that tests rose markedly with the different glass. When they fell again upon resuming his own glass, he "set a question mark against it"—his way of recording a suspicion he could not prove. Duncan Keddie wrote: "What he wrote in the book was a question mark. He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove, and I have thought since that this was the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had. He had, after all, nothing to hold in his hand but a suspicion about a piece of glass, and against it stood the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county. So he kept on."

Later, according to Orra's diary, after being dismissed, when men came to his funeral in 1934 and said what a hand he was with a door but never said "the other thing"—never named the accusation—he had told Orra "once and only once, and not lately, that a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."

# Section G — Summary

## G1. Eight-Story Summary (maximum 150 words)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery, founded in 1889, built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow in 1896. From 1897 onward, milk tested there consistently 0.19% lower than elsewhere—a discrepancy that generated three theories: patrons added water (disproven), the tester Ansel Keddie stole cream (disproven by a 1911 glass-swap experiment), or poor pasture thinned the cream (inconsistent with a 1911 feeding trial). A $3,800 arbitration award in 1925 accompanied Ansel's dismissal, though he maintained his honesty. In 1958, his great-niece Ivy Keddie found condemned glassware in a loft, measured it, and revealed the truth: pipettes manufactured faulty by Ammon & Sons in 1897 delivered 7.56% less milk than marked, producing exactly the observed shortfall. The station's tests had been mechanically wrong for 26 years. Ansel's name was cleared 24 years after his death.
