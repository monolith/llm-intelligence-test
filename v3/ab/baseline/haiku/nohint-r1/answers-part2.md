# Answer Sheet — Part 2: Section A, Stories 5–8 and Section B

## Story 5: The 1925 Arbitration Hearing and Dismissal

In January 1925, the patrons of Ostrey Hollow filed a formal petition against the station. A board of arbitration was appointed to sit under the chairmanship of an attorney at Ashlin. There were three sittings: the first at Ashlin from March 17 to May 4, 1925, and two more held at the station itself in the weigh room where the scales and machinery stood. Bertram Kyle carried the critical documents: the station's test book in Ansel Keddie's hand, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book (lent for the purpose), and the state inspector's notebook for 1923 when he condemned the glass. Kyle carried them in and carried them out and could never have told anyone, nor could tell now, whether any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked. The board heard testimony on three competing theories: that the patrons had added water (refuted by state dairy inspector records showing no added water found at any visit since 1908); that Ansel Keddie had stolen cream and written low tests to conceal it (refuted by the nine weeks in 1911 when borrowed glass was used and tests rose, while Ansel remained in post); and that the wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream (supported by a committee report from 1908 and a veterinarian's letter from 1926, though a 1911 State Agricultural College feeding trial on bog hay showed it depressed butter fat by at most five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer—insufficient to carry nineteen hundredths). The board handed down its award on May 4, 1925: three thousand eight hundred dollars to the Ostrey Hollow patrons, "without admission" of any cause—lawyer's language for "we pay you and we say nothing." But on April 30, 1925, four days before the award, Ansel Keddie was dismissed. He came up the road at his ordinary hour, with his canvas apron rolled under his arm, hung it behind the shed door, and asked what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it. He had been tester and separator man at that station for twenty-nine seasons.

## Story 6: Ansel Keddie After Dismissal (1925–1934)

After his dismissal in 1925, Ansel Keddie, at age sixty-four, took up carpentry. He kept four or five cows on a small piece of ground and hung barn doors for neighbors, good work that swung true for thirty years. Orra noted in her diary that in June 1925, soon after dismissal, he began on the cow shed that had wanted doing since his father's time, and worked at it from light to dark and whistled—which he never did when he was paid. By October 1927 he was mending stairs at the far end of the hollow and the Loomis gate and half the doors in the valley, taking what people offered. A carpenter at sixty-six, he was better at it than he ever was at testing, and it paid a quarter of what the testing had done. He lived nine years with the imputation on him, in the county, among men he had weighed milk for since before their fathers died. Orra outlived him by fourteen years and kept every letter, though there was nothing in them that anybody would want—only the weather, the price of things, her brother Emmet's health. She spoke of the business only once a year in April, in grief and silence. Ansel died at home on a February morning in 1934, without much pain at the end. Duncan came up in the night. More attended the funeral than Orra looked for—men who had not spoken to him in nine years shook her hand and told her what a hand he was with a door. Not one of them said the other thing. Not one named what they had accused him of. He had never asked them to. He had said to Orra once, and only once, that a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name.

## Story 7: The Orra Keddie Household: Family and Farm (1897–1934)

Orra Brant was born in 1868, the younger sister of Emmet Brant who farmed the largest place on Hessel Bottom. She married Ansel Keddie in 1889. Their son Duncan was born in 1894 and grew up to teach school at Ashlin, beginning at seventeen. Orra kept a diary in ruled notebooks from 1897 to 1930 (she was sixty-four in August 1930 and her hand was not what it was), plus a loose leaf written in 1934 after Ansel's death. The diary records daily life: ground still frozen in April, setting eggs, Ansel walking to the station in all weather before light and returning after dark, wild strawberries picked on the way, hay work, threshing, apple trees planted that she would not live to see bear well. It records the seasons—March mud to the hub of the wagons, February freezes, the low piece under water in April rains. She notes her brother Emmet's visits and his dignity as the largest patron. In June 1919, the circular warning about water in the milk came, and Ansel took the letters round himself. In June 1919, two days after, Emmet came up to the house and stood in the yard and asked Ansel why he had carried the paper himself if he did not believe it. Ansel said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet went home. He had not been in that house since, though he was her brother. In January 1925, the patrons filed their petition; Emmet's name was on it. Orra wrote: "I do not blame him and I will not say so to A." On March 17, 1925, Ansel went down to Ashlin in his good coat (the one he'd had since Duncan married) in his good coat, which he had worn since Duncan was married. He was up at three and did not sleep before that. Orra ironed the coat twice. In April 1925, two sittings were held up at the station itself; she did not go down but heard the wagons from the yard all that afternoon. On April 30, he came up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm. In June 1925, he began the carpentry that would sustain them. Spring 1929, word came that Emmet had died. Roy, Emmet's son, met her at the gate and was kind. She sat an hour in the kitchen where she was a girl and came home. Ten years, and neither of them would go up the road first. Now it was settled the way those things get settled. July 1929, hollyhocks bloomed the best she had had. Ansel sat out with her an evening and named the ones her mother had. February 1934, Ansel died at home in the forenoon. Duncan came up in the night. More attended the burying than she looked for.

## Story 8: The Discovery and Clearing (1958)

In February 1958, thirty-three years after Ansel Keddie was dismissed and twenty-four years after his death, his great-niece Ivy Keddie, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, climbed the ladder into the loft over the old Ostrey Hollow station's intake room. There, under a tarpaulin and about an inch of chaff, lay the wooden crate that had been condemned by state dairy inspector Merle Strawn in June 1923. She carried the crate east to Farland. In March 1958, in a college laboratory, she measured the nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette. Every one was blown on the stem 17.6 c.c., the standard mark. Not one delivered it. The average of the lot came to 16.27 c.c.—a shortfall of 1.33 c.c. per pipette, which is exactly 7.6 percent. A short charge of milk goes into a bottle whose neck is ruled for the full charge, and the fat reads low in the same proportion, in every test, forever. Nobody watered anything. Nobody stole anything. The scales were true. The acid was right. The cows were ordinary. A piece of glass out of a crate was wrong. In October 1958, Professor Leland Vaught of the college's department of dairy husbandry and Ivy Keddie published Bulletin No. 471 from the State Agricultural College at Farland. The bulletin calculated that over the full delivery years affected, the Ostrey Hollow patrons were unpaid for sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat, worth $19,600 in the money of those years. It documented the nineteen-hundredth shortfall with mathematical exactness and traced it to the faulty pipettes. The Ashlin Chronicle ran a front-page feature, "Old Ordell Wrong Righted," and asked why Ammon & Sons had not said what became of the lot in 1923. Orin Falke, county agricultural agent, prepared a report for the county board. In his closing note, after checking the college's 1911 feeding trial on bog hay, he withdrew his earlier concession that hay could account for the shortfall. Ansel Keddie's name was cleared twenty-four years after his death. His widow Orra had died in 1948 and did not live to see it.

# Section B — Relationships

## B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. The sources show: Ansel married Orra Brant in 1889; their son was Duncan Keddie (born 1894); Duncan's daughter was Ivy Keddie (born 1931). Therefore Ansel was Ivy's grandfather. The 1958 Ashlin Chronicle article incorrectly identified her as Ansel's "great-niece," but Orin Falke's county report corrected this in a note added in 1959, stating: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." This correction is authoritative because Falke was writing for the county record and verified the fact. Duncan Keddie's own family notes confirm the lineage: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise."

## B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant?

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. The derivation: Ansel Keddie married Orra Brant; Orra's brother was Emmet Brant; Emmet's son was Roy Brant; Roy's daughter was Hazel Brant. Therefore Emmet and Roy were uncle and nephew (brother and nephew, actually—Emmet was Roy's father), making Roy and Ansel's wife Orra siblings. Since Roy and Ansel's son Duncan are first cousins (children of siblings), Duncan's daughter Ivy and Roy's daughter Hazel are second cousins (children of first cousins). Duncan Keddie's notes are explicit: "Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece, which people say because it is shorter." Cleve Oram incorrectly called Ivy "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed," which is wrong; the intervening generation is clear—Emmet/Ansel's generation, then Roy/Duncan's generation, then Hazel/Ivy's generation.

## B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter?

Selby Vose was the son of Gideon Vose, the man who organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Association and was its first president. Duncan Keddie's family notes make this explicit: "Selby Vose, who managed the Association, was Gideon Vose's son. Gideon organized the Association and was its first president and wrote its by-laws; Selby was his son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew." Bertram Kyle (the clerk) also confirms: "Mr. Selby Vose became manager in nineteen eleven. He was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president—the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense." This matters because Selby Vose, as manager, was the officer who signed the ledger sheets every year that included the "station gain" line showing the five and a quarter percent loss at Ostrey Hollow. If he truly never read the lines above the totals (as Kyle observed), then the loss was visible in the books but unexamined by management for a quarter century.

## B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?

There was no family relationship between the Vose family and the Keddie family. Duncan Keddie's family notes directly address rumors to the contrary: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either. I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her sit down. It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me. She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it." Cleve Oram mentions rumor: "And I will say this while I am on families, because you will hear it anyway and you may as well hear it from somebody who is not repeating it out of malice. The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort." But Duncan Keddie's explicit statement (made by someone who knew the family records intimately) carries more weight: Orra never told him of any such connection, and she was meticulous about family relationships. The relationship, if any, is purely through institutional positions at the Association—Gideon Vose founded and presided over it; Ansel Keddie worked as tester and separator man within it. There was no blood tie.
