## Section C: Mathematics (14 points)

### C1. Glassware proportion and calculations

**(a) Proportion by which tests differ from truth:**

Nine intact pipettes from the condemned crate measured 16.27 cc average against calibrated burette. Marked capacity: 17.6 cc. Shortfall: 17.6 − 16.27 = 1.33 cc. Proportion short: 1.33 ÷ 17.6 = 0.0756 or **7.56% short of marked volume**, or delivering only **92.44% of marked capacity**. The milk sample volume input was systematically undersized by this proportion; the test bottle's graduated neck (calibrated for 17.6 cc) received only 16.27 cc; therefore the fat percentage read low by the same proportion.

**(b) True average test and deficit:**

Station recorded average: 3.61 points. If recorded reading is 92.44% of true reading (due to proportionally short measure), then:
True average = recorded average ÷ proportion delivered = 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = **3.90 points** (rounded).
Recorded average across affected years: 3.61 points.
**Deficit: 0.29 points per milk sample.**

(This matches the 1911 nine-week borrowed-measure observation: tests rose to 3.80, close to the valley average of 3.78–3.80, and the consistent 0.19-point Ostrey Hollow deficit reported by multiple narrators.)

**(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited:**

Leland Vaught (Bulletin 471 via Ashlin Chronicle): **76,000 pounds of butterfat** over the full delivery years affected (1897–1923, or effective complaint period 1902–1923). This represents the cumulative station gain (credited to patrons minus actually delivered to Larrow Green) attributable to the short-measure error.

**(d) Money value and fraction received:**

Total butterfat value at 25 cents per pound (per Bertram Kyle's notation of contemporary pricing): 76,000 lbs × $0.25/lb = **$19,000** (Falke's 1959 figure; Chronicle cites $19,600, likely with freight or rounding adjustment).

Patrons received by arbitration award: **$3,800 without admission of cause.**

Fraction of owed amount received: $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = **0.20 or 20%** (approximately one-fifth of actual underpayment).

Shortfall: 7,800 − 6,840 = **960 lbs butterfat owed and not credited.**

(Note: Using 3.42 recorded test rather than 3.61 station average suggests Emmet's milk tested slightly better than average that year, or Hazel's recollection is approximate.)

**(c) Money value:**

960 lbs × $0.25/lb = **$240 that year.**

### C3. Station-gain accounting over full delivery years

**(a) Total butterfat credited to station's patrons:**

Bertram Kyle: "Forty million pounds of milk received at that station" over the long complaint period, averaging 1,600,000 lbs/year for roughly 25 years. At recorded test of 3.61%:
40,000,000 lbs × 0.0361 = **1,440,000 lbs butterfat credited** to patrons in station account.

**(b) Butterfat actually received at Larrow Green:**

Station cream reaching Larrow Green: 1,440,000 − 76,000 = **1,364,000 lbs butterfat received** (or, by direct calculation at true 3.90 test: 40,000,000 × 0.0390 − small seasonal variation ≈ 1,560,000, with the gap of 76,000 lbs representing the undelivered portion).

Using the yield-difference approach: **Difference: 76,000 lbs butterfat** (credited but not delivered).

**(c) Expressed as percentage:**

Of butterfat **credited** (denominator): (76,000 ÷ 1,440,000) × 100 = **5.28%** "station gain" (matching the "5.25 per cent, as usual" Kyle observed annually).

Of butterfat **actually produced** (true yield at 3.90): (76,000 ÷ 1,520,000) × 100 ≈ **5.0%** undelivered due to short measure.

## Section D: Logic (10 points)

### D1. The 1911 borrowed-measure interval as experiment

**When and duration:** May 8, 1911 to July 10, 1911 = **9 weeks (or 9 weeks and 2 days, ~64 days).**

**Variable:** The measuring glass (the station's own faulty pipettes vs. a borrowed measure of known accuracy).

**Held constant:** (1) The tester: Ansel Keddie, same man both before and after; (2) The herds: identical milk-producing cattle; (3) The pasture: the Hessel Bottom stock, all off wet ground on summer pasture; (4) The season: June–July flush, same year; (5) The machinery: separator, scale, all other equipment unchanged; (6) The supervision: no different management or oversight; (7) The methodology: same testing procedure, same hands performing it.

**Result:** Tests rose from the station's chronic 3.61–3.65 average to 3.80, the normal valley figure (per Orin Falke's 1959 summary and Nyle Grigg's discussion of the interval). On the day the station resumed its own glass, tests fell back to their former low level. The change was immediate and total; no gradual drift.

**What it establishes:** That the measuring glass itself was the causal factor producing low tests. No other variable changed; only the glass changed; only the tests changed in response. This proves causation by elimination: it cannot be the milk (same source), the cows (same herds), the land (same Hessel Bottom pasture), the men (same tester), the machinery (unchanged). The cause is physical—something in or about the glass.

**What it does NOT establish:** (1) It does not measure the exact proportion of error (that required 1958 measurement). (2) It does not rule out a combination of causes (e.g., glass contributing 90% and some other factor 10%), though the sharpness of the interval (tests exactly at 3.80 when borrowed glass was used, exactly at 3.61 when own glass resumed) suggests the glass accounts for nearly all the deficit. (3) It does not establish that the glass began faulty in 1897 vs. becoming faulty at some later date (though the consistent nature of the deficit from 1902 onward suggests it was faulty from the start).

### D2. Three independent reasons cream was not stolen

**Reason 1 — The 1911 interval (historical experiment, independent of 1958 measurement):** The same tester, using borrowed glass, produced normal tests (3.80) for nine weeks. If Keddie were stealing cream, tests would have remained low during this interval (same man, same position to steal, same opportunity). The rise in tests proves Keddie was not the bottleneck; the glass was. This reason survives even if the glass had never been measured in 1958—it is documentary and mathematical proof from 1911 itself.

**Reason 2 — Station-gain accounting consistency (independent of glass measurement):** Bertram Kyle's ledger showed the station gain at 5.25% "as usual" year after year, "as uniform as a floor" per Hazel Brant. Cream theft would show irregular patterns: higher gain in years when Keddie was hungry or desperate, lower in other years, or theft accelerating over time. The mechanical consistency of 5.25% every year points to a systematic physical cause (faulty glass), not human agency subject to variation. This holds regardless of 1958 measurement.

**Reason 3 — Independent observation by third parties (direct witnesses, unrelated to glass theory):** Cleve Oram's father drove the cream wagon every day and observed that the cream arriving at Larrow Green appeared heavier/richer than the station's recorded tests indicated—a direct sensory observation requiring no specialized knowledge. Hobart Sill, the buttermaker at Larrow Green, made churns from the cream for 41 years and was emphatic that Ostrey Hollow cream consistently arrived "heavier than recorded tests indicated." Both men were independent of the glass theory, had no motive to support it, and were describing physical facts from their own experience.

**Independence of these reasons:** (1) is historical and mathematical; (2) is accounting and pattern analysis; (3) is sensory observation and third-party testimony. None depends on the others' truth to stand.

**Regarding the ledger refutation:** Wilbur Teague, the Association's bookkeeper, did advance the theft theory in his 1948 memoir, after the fact. However, Bertram Kyle's account (who worked alongside Teague in the office) states: "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years. That is not a criticism. It was his notion of the position: the figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people." Kyle found no evidence that Teague, who annually copied and certified the "station gain" figures, ever questioned or even consulted about them. Teague accepted and carried forward numbers that directly contradicted his later theft allegation. **He never questioned the ledger entries in real time.**

### D3. Did the manager know tests ran low?

**Answer: Yes, definitively.**

**Justification:** Selby Vose became manager in 1911, the exact year of the nine-week interval. As manager, he signed every annual ledger sheet showing "station gain" of 5.25% "as usual." For a manager to sign financial statements showing consistent underpayment to one station while other stations paid normally, he must have known (a) that the tests were low, and (b) that either something was wrong with the station or with the patrons.

Further, in June 1919, **Selby Vose personally signed and circulated the water-warning letter** to eleven Hollow families cautioning against milk adulteration. This act proves he believed the deficiency was in the milk, not the equipment. Had Vose suspected the glass, he would have ordered new equipment or investigated; instead, he accused the patrons. His signature on the water-warning circular is direct evidence that he knew tests ran low and had formed a theory about why (added water).

His decision to blame patrons rather than investigate the equipment—despite signing statements for eight years (1911–1919) showing low tests and persistent station gain—indicates either (a) he did not suspect the glass, or (b) he suspected it but chose to blame patrons as more politically manageable than admitting the Association's equipment was faulty. Either way, **he knew the tests were low.**

## Section E: Contradictions and Inconsistencies

### E1. Direct factual contradictions

**(1) Date of Ammon & Sons outfit delivery and use:**

**Conflicting claims:**
- Junia Ammon (1949, r20): Invoice April 14, 1897; outfit "went into use on the first of May, 1897"
- Bertram Kyle (1953, r23): Outfit "came from the supply house and went into use on the first of May, 1898"
- Hobart Sill (1934, r14): Claims he lent his own testing equipment to start the Ostrey Hollow station in "May 1896 when their outfit hadn't arrived"

**Narrators:** Ammon (company historian), Kyle (office clerk), Sill (buttermaker).

**Resolution:** The station opened May 4, 1896. Sill's claim that he lent equipment in May 1896 suggests the ordered outfit had not yet arrived. The invoice from Ammon is dated April 14, 1897, and Ammon states use began May 1, 1897. Kyle's 1898 date is likely a clerical or memory error; the documented invoice points to 1897. **Resolved: Sill lent equipment May 1896; Ammon outfit arrived May 1, 1897 (Ammon's date carries more weight as a contemporary business record; Kyle's is a later memory).**

**(2) Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie:**

**Conflicting claims:**
- Ashlin Chronicle (October 1958, r19): Ivy is Ansel's "great-niece"
- Orin Falke (1959, r22): Ivy is Ansel's "great-niece"
- Duncan Keddie (1968, r18): Ivy is Ansel's "granddaughter" (Duncan is Ansel's son; Ivy is Duncan's daughter)

**Narrators:** Chronicle and Falke (both relying on college press release), Keddie (drawing from family knowledge).

**Resolution:** Duncan Keddie's account is authoritative; he is the direct participant. Falke even appends a correction: "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece." **Resolved: Ivy is Ansel's granddaughter. Chronicle and Falke mistakenly relied on faulty press release information; family genealogy corrects it.**

**(3) Butterfat loss estimate (76,000 vs. 67,000 lbs):**

**Conflicting claims:**
- Leland Vaught / Bulletin 471 (1958, inferred from r11 and r19): "seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat"
- Cleve Oram (1977, r21): "Sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat"

**Narrators:** Vaught (contemporary scientist), Oram (recalling in 1977, ~20 years after publication).

**Resolution:** Oram is recalling from memory two decades after the bulletin's publication. His figure is approximate; the 9,000-lb difference is consistent with rounding or inexact recall. Vaught's figure (76,000) is from the contemporary technical bulletin and is more precise. **Resolved: 76,000 lbs is correct; Oram's recollection of 67,000 is approximate/unreliable.**

**(4) Bog hay depression amount (0.2 points vs. 0.05 points):**

**Conflicting claims:**
- Nyle Grigg (1908 report and 1919 reprint, r15): "two tenths" (0.2 points) depression from bog hay
- Dr. Roland Kepp (1926, r16): States bog hay depressed test "by two tenths in the winter feeding" and "the three agree so nearly" (referring to 0.2, 0.21, and Grigg's 0.2)
- State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 214: "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter" (0.05 points)
- Orin Falke (1959, r22, in appended note after receiving actual bulletin): Corrects himself, citing the actual bulletin: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July. I withdraw the concession made on page four."

**Narrators:** Grigg (farmer, 1908), Kepp (veterinarian, 1926), State College (1911 feeding trial published), Falke (county official).

**Resolution:** Grigg and Kepp were writing about what they believed Bulletin No. 214 showed (or what they recalled it showing). When Falke obtained the actual bulletin in 1959, he discovered it stated only 0.05 points depression, not 0.2. He explicitly withdrew his earlier acceptance of the wet-hay theory. **Resolved: Bulletin No. 214 actually says 0.05 points (documentary evidence). Grigg and Kepp either misremembered, misread, or were paraphrasing imprecisely. The wet-hay explanation is thereby defeated.**

### Case where two narrators carry the same wrong value

**Ivy Keddie's relationship:** Both the Ashlin Chronicle (October 1958) and Orin Falke (1959 report, later corrected) initially stated "great-niece." Both were drawing from the State Agricultural College press release accompanying Bulletin 471, making them a single source repeated twice, not independent confirmation. Falke later corrected himself upon "further verification," indicating he discovered the source was wrong.

### Case where a narrator contradicts itself

**Orin Falke (1959, r22):** In the body of his report (section 9), Falke writes: "This office is not prepared to dismiss it [the wet-hay explanation]. The bog meadows of that bottom are known to every farmer in the county for the coarseness of their hay...Whatever else was at work at Ostrey Hollow, the wet hay of that bottom must be allowed as a real if partial factor..."

**Later in the same document (appended note):** Upon receiving Bulletin No. 214, Falke reverses: "I withdraw the concession made on page four." He cites the actual bulletin showing only 0.05-point depression, which cannot account for the 0.19-point deficit at Ostrey Hollow.

**Resolution:** Documentary evidence (actual bulletin) overturns Falke's initial deduction based on secondary sources and general knowledge. His self-correction indicates intellectual honesty and proper use of evidence hierarchy.

## Section F: Causal Theory and Analysis (6 points)

### F1. Coherent causal theory

**The Theory:**

The Ostrey Hollow milk-testing shortfall was caused by **faulty glassware—specifically, milk-test pipettes that did not deliver their marked capacity—from the station's opening in 1897 through their condemnation in 1923.**

The equipment was purchased from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet and invoiced April 14, 1897. Six dozen pipettes, marked 17.6 cubic centimeters, were included. The pipettes were manufactured with a systematic calibration error: they delivered approximately 16.27 cc on average (measured in 1958) or 16.72 cc (depending on source), representing 7–5% undershoot from marked value. This likely resulted from a mold being reground at some point in manufacturing without recalibration, as Junia Ammon speculated in her 1949 company history.

When milk was tested, the sample size was undersized: 16.27 cc was drawn instead of 17.6 cc. The test bottle's graduated neck was calibrated for the full 17.6 cc charge. A proportionally shorter sample reads proportionally lower in fat content. Thus every test at Ostrey Hollow read systematically low by approximately 7%, producing recorded tests of 3.61 when true tests were 3.90–3.80.

**Why this size of error:** The error was mechanically determined by how much short each pipette was blown. 5–7% short was the margin of manufacturing error in the faulty mold.

**Why it began when it did:** The glass was faulty from May 1, 1897, when the equipment was first used. The systematic error began immediately but was not noticed until after 1902, when enough accumulated data made the pattern visible to alert observers like Emmet Brant.

**Why it ended when it did:** In June 1923, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn, conducting a routine circuit visit, ran the station's pipettes against a calibrated burette and found them short. He condemned the lot on the spot and directed replacement. New glass was in use by the following month. The error ended not because anything changed in milk production or land management, but because the faulty glass was physically removed and replaced.

**Why nothing at the station or farms had to change:** The error was purely physical—a property of the glass itself. The tester (Ansel Keddie) was competent and faithful. The cows, pasture, hay, milk quality, and herd genetics were unchanged. The error reflected neither dishonesty nor incompetence; it reflected faulty equipment beyond the tester's control or knowledge.

### Three competing explanations—names, proponents, defeating evidence

**Explanation 1: Added Water**
- **Proponent:** Selby Vose, manager of the Association, in his June 1919 warning circular sent to Ostrey Hollow patrons.
- **Specific evidence that defeats it:** State dairy inspector Merle Strawn conducted lactometer tests at every visit from 1908 forward and found no evidence of added water. The lactometer would detect water addition immediately. Zero detections over 15 years of testing defeat the theory completely.

**Explanation 2: Cream Theft**
- **Proponents:** Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper (1948 memoir); Hobart Sill, buttermaker (1934 recollection).
- **Specific evidence that defeats it:** (a) The 1911 nine-week interval: when borrowed glass replaced the station's own, tests rose to normal while the same tester worked under identical conditions. If Keddie were stealing cream, tests would have remained low; they rose, proving Keddie was not the cause. (b) Station-gain accounting was precisely consistent at 5.25% every year, showing a mechanical pattern, not human variation. Theft would be irregular. (c) Independent observers (Cleve Oram's father, the cream hauler; Hobart Sill himself, the buttermaker) noted that cream arriving at Larrow Green was heavier than tests indicated—directly contradicting the theft theory and corroborating heavy cream received, not light.

**Explanation 3: Wet Hay from Hessel Bottom**
- **Proponents:** Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee; Dr. Roland Kepp in 1926, citing State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 214.
- **Specific evidence that defeats it:** (a) The 1911 interval occurred in June–July when stock were off wet ground and on summer pasture. If wet hay caused the deficit, tests would not improve in summer; they improved sharply, to 3.80. (b) Orin Falke's 1959 acquisition of the actual Bulletin No. 214 revealed it showed only 0.05-point depression in winter feeding—insufficient to explain a 0.19-point deficit at any season. (c) Rosalie Cudd documented that the same Hessel Bottom farmers' milk, when tested at Alder Corners Cheese Company over four summer seasons (1915–1930), consistently tested 3.78–3.82, proving the milk itself was of normal quality regardless of land. The wet-hay theory is defeated by seasonal contradiction (summer improvement despite wet-hay causation being constant), by actual College data (0.05 point vs. 0.19 needed), and by evidence that the milk itself was good quality when tested elsewhere.

### Did the 1925 arbitration board examine the condemned glassware?

**Answer: No.**

**Evidence:** Cleve Oram reported that two of the three sittings of the arbitration were held at the Ostrey Hollow station itself, "in the weigh room—that is the intake room, the same room, with the scale in it." The condemned crate "was up the ladder over their heads the entire time. Twenty feet, if that." Oram stood outside on the platform during one afternoon and could hear voices but not words. He later thought about how "the crate was up the ladder over their heads the entire time" during the hearings, but admitted "I will not pretend I thought about it then."

Bertram Kyle, who carried records to the arbitration board, stated: "I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second [receiving book] beside the fourth [state inspector's notebook] and looked."

No record indicates the board climbed the ladder to examine the condemned glass. The crate remained undiscovered (in its original sealed location) until February 1958, thirty-five years after the arbitration, when Ivy Keddie found it.

### What did the tester himself believe?

**Answer: Ansel Keddie privately suspected the glass but never publicly claimed it.**

**Evidence:** In summer 1911, during the nine-week interval, Duncan Keddie (his son, age 17) recalled: "[My father] came home that summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book...I was seventeen and I remember the kitchen and I remember that he said it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not his way."

Duncan continues: "What he wrote in the book was a question mark. He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove, and I have thought since that this was the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had. He had, after all, nothing to hold in his hand but a suspicion about a piece of glass, and against it stood the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county. So he kept on."

In Ansel's 1925 statement, defending himself before the arbitration board, he maintained only that he had tested faithfully and found nothing to suspect—he made no public allegation about the glass. Orra's diary shows that after dismissal, he never spoke of the business except once a year in April, reflecting silently on it.

**Conclusion:** Ansel Keddie suspected the glass in 1911 (based on the sudden improvement with borrowed glass), but as a practical man who "would not put down what he could not prove," and facing the institutional authority of the equipment itself and the established practice of all other stations, he kept silence and continued testing with faulty glass for fourteen more years. He defended his work rather than accuse the equipment. He died in 1934 without ever publicly stating what he had privately suspected.

## Section G: Summary (4 points, ≤150 words)

A six-farm community in Ordell County sent milk through a cooperative creamery since 1896. The Ostrey Hollow station consistently tested butterfat 0.19 points below the valley average, costing farmers thousands over 26 years. Three theories blamed the patrons: water, theft, or wet-hay feed. A 1911 accident proved otherwise: when the station's glass measure broke and was replaced with borrowed equipment for nine weeks, tests rose to normal; when the station's own glass resumed, tests fell again. In 1925, the board dismissed the station's tester without admitting fault and awarded partial compensation. He lived nine years under suspicion, never publicly cleared. In 1958, a college student found the condemned pipettes in a loft and measured them: they delivered 7% less milk than marked—the sole cause. She published findings; twenty-four years after his death, the tester's name was vindicated by measurement, not memory.
