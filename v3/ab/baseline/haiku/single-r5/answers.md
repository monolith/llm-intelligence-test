# Test Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

## Section A: Reconstruction of Eight Stories

### Story 1: Building the Station (1895–1898)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, organized in 1889 and building its main creamery at Larrow Green in 1891, decided in the winter of 1895–1896 to establish a skimming station upstream at Ostrey Hollow to serve the six farms of Hessel Bottom. The station opened on May 4, 1896, in a board building on the flat by the creek. Ansel Keddie came to the post of tester and separator man at or near the opening—Hobart Sill claims he lent Keddie his own testing equipment to start the station in May 1896, suggesting the ordered outfit had not yet arrived. The official equipment—a hand tester, pipettes marked 17.6 cc, test bottles, and an acid measure—was ordered from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet on April 14, 1897, for $77.93, and went into use on May 1, 1897 (or May 1, 1898 by Kyle's account). The equipment came new from the glassblowing firm as a complete outfit. The station's role was to weigh milk delivered by Hollow patrons, sample and test it for butterfat, separate the cream for shipment down to Larrow Green, and return skim to farmers. Emmet Brant, the largest patron on Hessel Bottom, kept his own weigh book from January 1, 1897 (or 1900 by Hazel's account), recording both his farm weight and the station's test weight side-by-side. The station became the sole measure of what each patron was paid, under Article VII of the Association's bylaws: "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." This single sentence made the accuracy of that glass equipment the foundation of every farmer's income at the Hollow.

### Story 2: The Problem Emerges (1902–1911)

From its earliest years, the station's milk tested low. The first complaint of record came in 1902, when Emmet Brant noticed his milk, tested at 3.42 in 1910, consistently ran about 0.19 to 0.21 points below the valley average of 3.78–3.80. Talk at stores blamed the farmers themselves—the Hollow patrons must be adding water or their cows were inferior. By 1908, the grumbling had grown enough that the Association appointed a committee of three farmers, Nyle Grigg among them, to investigate. That committee, sampling the herds monthly through 1908, found the cattle sound, the milk whole, the scales honest, and the patrons' equipment accurate. They concluded the hay of Hessel Bottom—the wet, acidic, sedge-filled bottom—was the culprit. The State Agricultural College's Bulletin No. 214 had earlier published a feeding trial showing bog hay depressed butterfat by 0.2 points in winter, and the committee pointed to this as vindication. The matter seemed settled. Then in May 1911, Ansel Keddie's glass measure broke. For nine weeks—May 8 through July 10—he used a borrowed measure, and the station's tests rose visibly to 3.80, the normal valley figure. On the day Keddie resumed his own glass, the tests fell back to 3.61 as before. The herds, pasture, season, men, and machinery were identical throughout. Keddie wrote a question mark in his book and said nothing else. The 1911 interval proved mathematically that something about the station's own equipment was wrong, not the land, not the cows, not the men—yet no one drew the inference, and the low tests continued for another twelve years.

### Story 3: Three Theories Tested (1911–1923)

In the years following the 1911 nine-week anomaly, three competing explanations crystallized in valley conversation and would later be argued before the 1925 arbitration board. The first theory was added water. In June 1919, Association manager Selby Vose circulated a warning letter to eleven families on Hessel Bottom cautioning them against adulterating milk. Orra Keddie remembered receiving it and putting it in the stove. Ansel Keddie—the tester himself, the manager's target, and Emmet Brant's brother-in-law—carried the letters himself to each farm door and stood while each patron read it. According to Orra, Emmet confronted Ansel at the house and asked why he carried the paper if he didn't believe it; Ansel replied he carried it so no other man would have to, and Emmet left and did not return for a decade. The state dairy inspector, testing with the lactometer at every visit from 1908 forward, never found added water at that station. The second theory was cream theft: Ansel Keddie, the tester, removed cream and wrote low tests to conceal the loss. Hobart Sill, the buttermaker at Larrow Green, stood in front of the churn for 41 years and insisted the cream from Ostrey Hollow arrived heavier than the station's recorded tests indicated. Wilbur Teague, the Association's bookkeeper, later suggested (in his 1948 memoir) that the station's "gain"—the butterfat credited to patrons minus the butterfat received at Larrow Green—was the result of Keddie shorting the tests. Yet Dell Rundle's 1939 history, republished in 1959 with a new footnote, noted that ledger entries showed heavy cream arriving, contradicting theft. The third theory remained wet hay from Hessel Bottom. Dr. Roland Kepp, a veterinary surgeon with thirty years' practice, delivered a paper to the Ordell County Farm Club in 1926 arguing that acidic, phosphorus-poor bog soil forced cows to economize butterfat production, as "fat is the dearest thing the cow makes." He cited Bulletin No. 214, which showed 0.2-point depression in winter, and dismissed the 1911 six-week anomaly as a summer outlier. In 1923, during a routine circuit visit, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn ran the station's milk pipettes against a burette and found they would not deliver their marked capacity. He condemned the lot on the spot and directed the manager to order new glass. Strawn left no inference about the years behind the short measure; he simply entered the condemnation in his notebook and moved to the next station.

### Story 4: The Arbitration & Dismissal (1925)

In January 1925, the Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned the Association's board for redress, naming Ansel Keddie as the cause of their losses. The board formed an arbitration committee chaired by attorney Alonzo Frick. Three sittings were held: one at the Cooperative's office in Ashlin, and two at the Ostrey Hollow station itself, in the weigh room where the scale and the machinery stood. The condemned crate of glass—now empty of the broken pipettes, now condemned, now useless—sat in the loft above the weighroom, invisible to every man in the room. The board heard the manager's allegation of added water, the bookkeeper's suggestion of cream theft, the committee's 1908 findings on wet hay, and Ansel Keddie's meticulous defense of his testing. Keddie, by his own statement written in 1925, maintained that he had tested faithfully before the state man himself and that he had done the work exactly as taught. He had found nothing in his practice to suspect. The board, after deliberation, issued a finding: "No cause established," and awarded the patrons $3,800 without admission of any fault by anyone. On April 30, 1925, four days before the award was announced, Keddie was dismissed. He came home at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm, hung it behind the shed door, and asked what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it. He had held the post for twenty-nine seasons and left with a letter. In the eyes of Ordell County, he remained the man who had cheated the farmers of Hessel Bottom, and neither the board's finding nor his own integrity could clear him.


# Test Answers — Part 2: Section A Stories 5–8, Section B

## Story 5: The Aftermath & Estrangement (1925–1934)

After his dismissal on April 30, 1925, Ansel Keddie never returned to the Ostrey Hollow station. He was sixty-four years old. He went home and began work on projects deferred for decades: the cow shed his father had wanted repaired, carpentry jobs around the hollow. He worked from light to dark, and according to Orra's diary, he whistled—something he never did when he was paid. His carpentry was excellent; Cleve Oram's father remarked that Keddie "was a hand with a door," and Oram himself saw a barn door Keddie hung thirty years later still swinging true. He took what work came, paid at a quarter of what the testing had paid. Orra noted in 1926 that several diary leaves had been torn out, leaving a gap in the record. The estrangement from her brother Emmet Brant, which had begun in 1919 over the water-warning circular, persisted. In spring 1929, Orra learned that Emmet had died. She went down to Hessel Bottom and sat in the kitchen where she had been a girl, and Roy (Emmet's son and first cousin to Ivy Keddie) met her at the gate and was kind. Ten years had passed with neither brother willing to go up the road first. In July 1929, Ansel and Orra sat out together and he named the hollyhocks his mother had grown. On February 12, 1934, Ansel Keddie died at home in the forenoon without much pain. Duncan, his son and Ivy's father, came up in the night. More people attended the funeral than Orra had expected. Men shook her hand who had not spoken to Ansel in nine years. Every one of them said what a hand he was with a door. Not one of them said the other thing—the accusation that had followed him since 1925. Orra reflected that he never asked them to, and that he had said to her once and only once that a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name. He lived nine years after dismissal, never knowing that measurement would one day vindicate him.

## Story 6: The Discovery in the Loft (February–March 1958)

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie, granddaughter of Ansel and student of dairy husbandry at the State Agricultural College at Farland, climbed the ladder to the loft over the Ostrey Hollow station's intake room. Under a tarpaulin and about an inch of chaff lay the condemned crate that Merle Strawn had ordered sealed in 1923. It had sat there for thirty-five years, through two sittings of the 1925 arbitration, while men argued in the weighroom beneath. The glassware inside was damaged—years in the loft had broken many of the pipettes—but nine survived intact. On March 9, 1958, Ivy carried the crate back to Farland and spent a day measuring each surviving pipette against a calibrated burette in a college laboratory. Every pipette was blown on the stem with the mark "17.6 c.c." but not one delivered that volume. The average of the nine measured to 16.27 cc. They were consistently short by 0.88 cc, or one part in twenty, or about 5 percent. A short charge of milk into a test bottle marked for the full charge yields a butterfat reading short in the same proportion. This and nothing else explained the entire discrepancy. No milk had been watered. No cream had been stolen. No wet hay alone had dimmed the fat. The glass itself had been wrong from the beginning, from May 1, 1897 (or 1898), and had remained wrong until the state inspector finally measured it in June 1923 and condemned it. For twenty-six years, the glass had delivered a systematic error affecting every patron on Hessel Bottom.

## Story 7: Scientific Vindication (October 1958–1959)

In October 1958, the State Agricultural College at Farland published Bulletin No. 471 under the names of Professor Leland Vaught of the dairy husbandry department and graduate student Ivy Keddie. The bulletin presented the measurement data, explained the physics of the proportional error, analyzed the 1911 nine-week interval as proof of causation (when borrowed measure showed 3.80 tests, the same man and same herds), and concluded: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there...seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat...stands credited...as station gain." The Ashlin Chronicle published the story the following week, noting that it was Ivy Keddie, Ansel's great-niece (later corrected to granddaughter), who had solved a mystery thirty-three years after his dismissal and twenty-four years after his death. The bulletin vindicated Ansel Keddie completely. It also indicted Ammon & Sons, the glassblowing firm. Junia Ammon, historian of the company, acknowledged in her firm's own 1949 history that they had sold short pipettes; she speculated the mold had been reground and the error went undetected in checking. She reprinted the 1897 invoice and the evasive 1923 reply to the Association's request for replacement glass: "Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name." Ammon wrote that she regretted the letter and what it signified about the firm's response to a customer of twenty-six years. In 1959, county agent Orin Falke filed an official summary for the Ordell County Board, and was prompted to look up Bulletin No. 214 to verify the bad-hay theory. He found it: bog hay depressed butterfat by only 0.05 points in winter, not enough to carry the 0.19-point deficit. He withdrew his earlier concession to the wet-hay theory and noted that the vindication was complete.

## Story 8: Institutional Memory & Documentary Preservation (1896–1963)

From 1896 onward, the Ostrey Hollow station generated written records: Ansel Keddie's test book, the station's receiving book, the income and disbursement accounts. In 1897, the Association's office began copying station figures into ledgers, and by 1898, Bertram Kyle, clerk at Larrow Green, was ruling pages to carry the annual summaries to the membership. Every year for a quarter-century, the ledger showed three figures and a sentence under "Ostrey Hollow Station": butterfat credited to patrons, butterfat received in cream at Larrow Green, and the difference—the "station gain"—always about 5.25 percent, always labeled "as usual." No one questioned it. The name explained it; explanation stopped inquiry. Emmet Brant kept his own weigh book from 1897 (or 1900), recording his farm weights and the station's tests. Roy Brant inherited it and in 1958 lent it to Ivy Keddie for her college work; she returned it, and at Roy's death in 1962 it went to his daughter Hazel, who donated it to the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1963. Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks, kept from 1896 onward with one for each year, went to the state dairy office in 1936 and were transferred to the State Agricultural College library in 1947. Ivy found the 1923 volume eleven years later when she was researching. Rosalie Cudd, who ran the Alder Corners Cheese Company across the ridge and whose father Jerome Cudd had kept the company's test book, had the figures copied and sent to Ivy in 1958. In 1959, Ivy's findings went to the Historical Rooms along with the condemned crate and pipettes. Duncan Keddie, Ansel's son, wrote a 1968 account documenting where every book had gone so that future seekers would know the records had existed. The accidental survival of that glass in a loft, the institutional decision to preserve ledgers and dairy records, and Ivy's recognition that a question mark in an 1911 ledger entry was worth investigating—these made vindication possible twenty-four years after Ansel Keddie's death.

## Section B: Relationships

### B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's **granddaughter**. Ansel had a son, Duncan Keddie (born 1894), who became a schoolteacher at Ashlin. Duncan married and had a daughter, Ivy (born 1931). Therefore Ivy was the child of Duncan, the son of Ansel. The Ashlin Chronicle initially misidentified her as "great-niece" in October 1958; Orin Falke's 1959 county report made the same error. Duncan Keddie corrected this in his 1968 family notes, and the county agent later issued a correction. The error likely arose from Duncan being a generation removed and not immediately recognizable as Ansel's direct child in casual conversation, but the genealogy is clear and documented.

### B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were **second cousins**. Hazel Brant was the daughter of Roy Brant. Roy Brant was the son of Emmet Brant. Emmet Brant was the brother of Orra Brant, who married Ansel Keddie and had a son, Duncan Keddie (Ivy's father). Therefore: Emmet and Orra were siblings; Roy was Emmet's son; Duncan was Orra's son; Ivy was Duncan's daughter; Hazel was Roy's daughter. Ivy and Hazel are therefore children of first cousins (Duncan and Roy), making them second cousins. Duncan Keddie's 1968 account carefully distinguishes this from "cousins once removed" (which is a different generation relation) and from "aunt and niece" (which people incorrectly said). The two were indeed closely related through both the Brant and Keddie lines, with both sides centered on Hessel Bottom. The sources leave open no ambiguity about intervening generations—the chain is direct and documented in family records, the weigh book, and multiple accounts.

### B3. Selby Vose's father and its significance

Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**, the founder and first president of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon organized the Association and wrote its bylaws, including Article VII, which stated that the test at the station where milk was delivered would govern payment. Selby Vose became manager of the Association in 1911 and served for a quarter-century. This matters enormously to what happened at Ostrey Hollow because Selby was responsible for managing the Association during the critical 1911–1925 period when the problem should have been discovered. Gideon had written the rule that made the station's test absolute; Selby administered that rule and signed the annual ledger summaries showing the persistent station gain. Bertram Kyle, the office clerk, noted that Selby "never read the lines above" the totals—he would put his thumb on the bottom figure and sign. The significance is that Selby Vose, inheriting leadership from Gideon Vose, inherited the framework that gave the low tests their power. Had there been any mechanism to question the station's test, it would have required Selby's action. Instead, in 1919, Selby signed the water-warning circular accusing the patrons themselves. Bertram Kyle typed it and remembered typing it, remembering also that it was a hard thing to send. Selby's management, passive rather than investigative, let the problem persist until 1925, when he was required to convene the arbitration.

### B4. Relationship between the Vose and Keddie families

The sources are contradictory and confused on this point. Duncan Keddie explicitly stated in his 1968 account: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either." He reported that he had twice been told (by women in Ashlin) that his mother Orra and Gideon Vose's wife were cousins, and one woman claimed first cousins; Duncan dismissed this as talk that had circulated since before he could walk. He noted that his mother, who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection," never once placed a Vose in the genealogy she taught him. Duncan's testimony is that there was **no family relationship**. However, Cleve Oram, the cream hauler's son, stated in his 1977 oral history: "The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort...My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper." Oram's account is vaguer, from family hearsay, and he admits he cannot document it. Duncan Keddie's direct statement, made with explicit attention to the confusion, carries more weight, and the resolution is that **no documented familial relationship existed**, though valley rumor persisted in suggesting one. The sources establish that Duncan knew the rumor and explicitly rejected it; Oram reported it as hearsay but unverified.


# Test Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C: Mathematics (14 points)

### C1. Glassware proportion and calculations

**(a) Proportion by which tests differ from truth:**

Nine intact pipettes from the condemned crate measured 16.27 cc average against calibrated burette. Marked capacity: 17.6 cc. Shortfall: 17.6 − 16.27 = 1.33 cc. Proportion short: 1.33 ÷ 17.6 = 0.0756 or **7.56% short of marked volume**, or delivering only **92.44% of marked capacity**. The milk sample volume input was systematically undersized by this proportion; the test bottle's graduated neck (calibrated for 17.6 cc) received only 16.27 cc; therefore the fat percentage read low by the same proportion.

**(b) True average test and deficit:**

Station recorded average: 3.61 points. If recorded reading is 92.44% of true reading (due to proportionally short measure), then:
True average = recorded average ÷ proportion delivered = 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = **3.90 points** (rounded).
Recorded average across affected years: 3.61 points.
**Deficit: 0.29 points per milk sample.**

(This matches the 1911 nine-week borrowed-measure observation: tests rose to 3.80, close to the valley average of 3.78–3.80, and the consistent 0.19-point Ostrey Hollow deficit reported by multiple narrators.)

**(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited:**

Leland Vaught (Bulletin 471 via Ashlin Chronicle): **76,000 pounds of butterfat** over the full delivery years affected (1897–1923, or effective complaint period 1902–1923). This represents the cumulative station gain (credited to patrons minus actually delivered to Larrow Green) attributable to the short-measure error.

**(d) Money value and fraction received:**

Total butterfat value at 25 cents per pound (per Bertram Kyle's notation of contemporary pricing): 76,000 lbs × $0.25/lb = **$19,000** (Falke's 1959 figure; Chronicle cites $19,600, likely with freight or rounding adjustment).

Patrons received by arbitration award: **$3,800 without admission of cause.**

Fraction of owed amount received: $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = **0.20 or 20%** (approximately one-fifth of actual underpayment).

### C2. Emmet Brant's largest patron year (1910)

**(a) Pounds of butterfat credited:**

Hazel Brant (weigh-book keeper) recalled Emmet delivered "Two hundred thousand pounds even" in 1910, and it was paid "on a test of three forty-two" (3.42 points). Butterfat credited: 200,000 lbs × 0.0342 = **6,840 lbs butterfat credited.**

**(b) Pounds owed and not credited:**

True test (as established above): 3.90 points.
Butterfat actually produced: 200,000 lbs × 0.0390 = **7,800 lbs butterfat actual.**
Shortfall: 7,800 − 6,840 = **960 lbs butterfat owed and not credited.**

(Note: Using 3.42 recorded test rather than 3.61 station average suggests Emmet's milk tested slightly better than average that year, or Hazel's recollection is approximate.)

**(c) Money value:**

960 lbs × $0.25/lb = **$240 that year.**

### C3. Station-gain accounting over full delivery years

**(a) Total butterfat credited to station's patrons:**

Bertram Kyle: "Forty million pounds of milk received at that station" over the long complaint period, averaging 1,600,000 lbs/year for roughly 25 years. At recorded test of 3.61%:
40,000,000 lbs × 0.0361 = **1,440,000 lbs butterfat credited** to patrons in station account.

**(b) Butterfat actually received at Larrow Green:**

Station cream reaching Larrow Green: 1,440,000 − 76,000 = **1,364,000 lbs butterfat received** (or, by direct calculation at true 3.90 test: 40,000,000 × 0.0390 − small seasonal variation ≈ 1,560,000, with the gap of 76,000 lbs representing the undelivered portion).

Using the yield-difference approach: **Difference: 76,000 lbs butterfat** (credited but not delivered).

**(c) Expressed as percentage:**

Of butterfat **credited** (denominator): (76,000 ÷ 1,440,000) × 100 = **5.28%** "station gain" (matching the "5.25 per cent, as usual" Kyle observed annually).

Of butterfat **actually produced** (true yield at 3.90): (76,000 ÷ 1,520,000) × 100 ≈ **5.0%** undelivered due to short measure.

- Freight: $2.15
- **Total: $77.93** (or $77.39; sources vary slightly on cents).

**(b) Number of pipettes and shortfall per pipette:**

Pipettes: 6 dozen = **72 pipettes** at 17.6 cc mark.
Shortfall per pipette: 17.6 − 16.27 = **1.33 cc short per pipette** (or 0.88 cc if using alternative measurement of 16.72 cc average; sources show some variance; using measured average of 16.27 cc).

### C5. Duration of faulty glassware and first-season milk

**(a) Length of use vs. delivery-year calculation:**

**Length faulty glass was in use:** May 1, 1897 to June 1923 (when condemned) = **26 years continuous**.

**Calculation runs over:** Complaints began 1902; money calculation (40 million lbs received) covers roughly 1902–1923 or the "long complaint period" = **21–26 years of affected deliveries**, likely 21–22 years of deliberate billing period.

**Why they differ:** The glass was faulty from 1897, but patrons did not complain until 1902 (possibly due to lag in noticing the pattern, or lack of comparative data from other stations). The arbitration and compensation calculation focused on years of documented complaint and proven damage, not the first 5 years when the defect existed but was unrecognized. Thus the legal calculation runs shorter than the actual duration of the defect.

**(b) First season milk:**

Bertram Kyle, office clerk, stated he could not verify the first season (before station accounts were formalized): "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" (approximately 1.1 million lbs), per general recollection of men present at the time. Kyle explicitly notes: "It is not in any book I ever ruled." This figure is **unverified in any document** and should be treated as approximate or unreliable.

## Section D: Logic (10 points)

### D1. The 1911 borrowed-measure interval as experiment

**When and duration:** May 8, 1911 to July 10, 1911 = **9 weeks (or 9 weeks and 2 days, ~64 days).**

**Variable:** The measuring glass (the station's own faulty pipettes vs. a borrowed measure of known accuracy).

**Held constant:** (1) The tester: Ansel Keddie, same man both before and after; (2) The herds: identical milk-producing cattle; (3) The pasture: the Hessel Bottom stock, all off wet ground on summer pasture; (4) The season: June–July flush, same year; (5) The machinery: separator, scale, all other equipment unchanged; (6) The supervision: no different management or oversight; (7) The methodology: same testing procedure, same hands performing it.

**Result:** Tests rose from the station's chronic 3.61–3.65 average to 3.80, the normal valley figure (per Orin Falke's 1959 summary and Nyle Grigg's discussion of the interval). On the day the station resumed its own glass, tests fell back to their former low level. The change was immediate and total; no gradual drift.

**What it establishes:** That the measuring glass itself was the causal factor producing low tests. No other variable changed; only the glass changed; only the tests changed in response. This proves causation by elimination: it cannot be the milk (same source), the cows (same herds), the land (same Hessel Bottom pasture), the men (same tester), the machinery (unchanged). The cause is physical—something in or about the glass.

**What it does NOT establish:** (1) It does not measure the exact proportion of error (that required 1958 measurement). (2) It does not rule out a combination of causes (e.g., glass contributing 90% and some other factor 10%), though the sharpness of the interval (tests exactly at 3.80 when borrowed glass was used, exactly at 3.61 when own glass resumed) suggests the glass accounts for nearly all the deficit. (3) It does not establish that the glass began faulty in 1897 vs. becoming faulty at some later date (though the consistent nature of the deficit from 1902 onward suggests it was faulty from the start).

### D2. Three independent reasons cream was not stolen

**Reason 1 — The 1911 interval (historical experiment, independent of 1958 measurement):** The same tester, using borrowed glass, produced normal tests (3.80) for nine weeks. If Keddie were stealing cream, tests would have remained low during this interval (same man, same position to steal, same opportunity). The rise in tests proves Keddie was not the bottleneck; the glass was. This reason survives even if the glass had never been measured in 1958—it is documentary and mathematical proof from 1911 itself.

**Reason 2 — Station-gain accounting consistency (independent of glass measurement):** Bertram Kyle's ledger showed the station gain at 5.25% "as usual" year after year, "as uniform as a floor" per Hazel Brant. Cream theft would show irregular patterns: higher gain in years when Keddie was hungry or desperate, lower in other years, or theft accelerating over time. The mechanical consistency of 5.25% every year points to a systematic physical cause (faulty glass), not human agency subject to variation. This holds regardless of 1958 measurement.

**Reason 3 — Independent observation by third parties (direct witnesses, unrelated to glass theory):** Cleve Oram's father drove the cream wagon every day and observed that the cream arriving at Larrow Green appeared heavier/richer than the station's recorded tests indicated—a direct sensory observation requiring no specialized knowledge. Hobart Sill, the buttermaker at Larrow Green, made churns from the cream for 41 years and was emphatic that Ostrey Hollow cream consistently arrived "heavier than recorded tests indicated." Both men were independent of the glass theory, had no motive to support it, and were describing physical facts from their own experience.

**Independence of these reasons:** (1) is historical and mathematical; (2) is accounting and pattern analysis; (3) is sensory observation and third-party testimony. None depends on the others' truth to stand.

**Regarding the ledger refutation:** Wilbur Teague, the Association's bookkeeper, did advance the theft theory in his 1948 memoir, after the fact. However, Bertram Kyle's account (who worked alongside Teague in the office) states: "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years. That is not a criticism. It was his notion of the position: the figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people." Kyle found no evidence that Teague, who annually copied and certified the "station gain" figures, ever questioned or even consulted about them. Teague accepted and carried forward numbers that directly contradicted his later theft allegation. **He never questioned the ledger entries in real time.**

### D3. Did the manager know tests ran low?

**Answer: Yes, definitively.**

**Justification:** Selby Vose became manager in 1911, the exact year of the nine-week interval. As manager, he signed every annual ledger sheet showing "station gain" of 5.25% "as usual." For a manager to sign financial statements showing consistent underpayment to one station while other stations paid normally, he must have known (a) that the tests were low, and (b) that either something was wrong with the station or with the patrons.

Further, in June 1919, **Selby Vose personally signed and circulated the water-warning letter** to eleven Hollow families cautioning against milk adulteration. This act proves he believed the deficiency was in the milk, not the equipment. Had Vose suspected the glass, he would have ordered new equipment or investigated; instead, he accused the patrons. His signature on the water-warning circular is direct evidence that he knew tests ran low and had formed a theory about why (added water).

His decision to blame patrons rather than investigate the equipment—despite signing statements for eight years (1911–1919) showing low tests and persistent station gain—indicates either (a) he did not suspect the glass, or (b) he suspected it but chose to blame patrons as more politically manageable than admitting the Association's equipment was faulty. Either way, **he knew the tests were low.**

## Section E: Contradictions and Inconsistencies

### E1. Direct factual contradictions

**(1) Date of Ammon & Sons outfit delivery and use:**

**Conflicting claims:**
- Junia Ammon (1949, r20): Invoice April 14, 1897; outfit "went into use on the first of May, 1897"
- Bertram Kyle (1953, r23): Outfit "came from the supply house and went into use on the first of May, 1898"
- Hobart Sill (1934, r14): Claims he lent his own testing equipment to start the Ostrey Hollow station in "May 1896 when their outfit hadn't arrived"

**Narrators:** Ammon (company historian), Kyle (office clerk), Sill (buttermaker).

**Resolution:** The station opened May 4, 1896. Sill's claim that he lent equipment in May 1896 suggests the ordered outfit had not yet arrived. The invoice from Ammon is dated April 14, 1897, and Ammon states use began May 1, 1897. Kyle's 1898 date is likely a clerical or memory error; the documented invoice points to 1897. **Resolved: Sill lent equipment May 1896; Ammon outfit arrived May 1, 1897 (Ammon's date carries more weight as a contemporary business record; Kyle's is a later memory).**

**(2) Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie:**

**Conflicting claims:**
- Ashlin Chronicle (October 1958, r19): Ivy is Ansel's "great-niece"

## Section F: Causal Theory and Analysis (6 points)

### F1. Coherent causal theory

**The Theory:**

The Ostrey Hollow milk-testing shortfall was caused by **faulty glassware—specifically, milk-test pipettes that did not deliver their marked capacity—from the station's opening in 1897 through their condemnation in 1923.**

The equipment was purchased from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet and invoiced April 14, 1897. Six dozen pipettes, marked 17.6 cubic centimeters, were included. The pipettes were manufactured with a systematic calibration error: they delivered approximately 16.27 cc on average (measured in 1958) or 16.72 cc (depending on source), representing 7–5% undershoot from marked value. This likely resulted from a mold being reground at some point in manufacturing without recalibration, as Junia Ammon speculated in her 1949 company history.

When milk was tested, the sample size was undersized: 16.27 cc was drawn instead of 17.6 cc. The test bottle's graduated neck was calibrated for the full 17.6 cc charge. A proportionally shorter sample reads proportionally lower in fat content. Thus every test at Ostrey Hollow read systematically low by approximately 7%, producing recorded tests of 3.61 when true tests were 3.90–3.80.

**Why this size of error:** The error was mechanically determined by how much short each pipette was blown. 5–7% short was the margin of manufacturing error in the faulty mold.

**Why it began when it did:** The glass was faulty from May 1, 1897, when the equipment was first used. The systematic error began immediately but was not noticed until after 1902, when enough accumulated data made the pattern visible to alert observers like Emmet Brant.

**Why it ended when it did:** In June 1923, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn, conducting a routine circuit visit, ran the station's pipettes against a calibrated burette and found them short. He condemned the lot on the spot and directed replacement. New glass was in use by the following month. The error ended not because anything changed in milk production or land management, but because the faulty glass was physically removed and replaced.

**Why nothing at the station or farms had to change:** The error was purely physical—a property of the glass itself. The tester (Ansel Keddie) was competent and faithful. The cows, pasture, hay, milk quality, and herd genetics were unchanged. The error reflected neither dishonesty nor incompetence; it reflected faulty equipment beyond the tester's control or knowledge.

### Three competing explanations—names, proponents, defeating evidence

**Explanation 1: Added Water**
- **Proponent:** Selby Vose, manager of the Association, in his June 1919 warning circular sent to Ostrey Hollow patrons.
- **Specific evidence that defeats it:** State dairy inspector Merle Strawn conducted lactometer tests at every visit from 1908 forward and found no evidence of added water. The lactometer would detect water addition immediately. Zero detections over 15 years of testing defeat the theory completely.

**Explanation 2: Cream Theft**
- **Proponents:** Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper (1948 memoir); Hobart Sill, buttermaker (1934 recollection).
- **Specific evidence that defeats it:** (a) The 1911 nine-week interval: when borrowed glass replaced the station's own, tests rose to normal while the same tester worked under identical conditions. If Keddie were stealing cream, tests would have remained low; they rose, proving Keddie was not the cause. (b) Station-gain accounting was precisely consistent at 5.25% every year, showing a mechanical pattern, not human variation. Theft would be irregular. (c) Independent observers (Cleve Oram's father, the cream hauler; Hobart Sill himself, the buttermaker) noted that cream arriving at Larrow Green was heavier than tests indicated—directly contradicting the theft theory and corroborating heavy cream received, not light.

**Explanation 3: Wet Hay from Hessel Bottom**
- **Proponents:** Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee; Dr. Roland Kepp in 1926, citing State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 214.
- **Specific evidence that defeats it:** (a) The 1911 interval occurred in June–July when stock were off wet ground and on summer pasture. If wet hay caused the deficit, tests would not improve in summer; they improved sharply, to 3.80. (b) Orin Falke's 1959 acquisition of the actual Bulletin No. 214 revealed it showed only 0.05-point depression in winter feeding—insufficient to explain a 0.19-point deficit at any season. (c) Rosalie Cudd documented that the same Hessel Bottom farmers' milk, when tested at Alder Corners Cheese Company over four summer seasons (1915–1930), consistently tested 3.78–3.82, proving the milk itself was of normal quality regardless of land. The wet-hay theory is defeated by seasonal contradiction (summer improvement despite wet-hay causation being constant), by actual College data (0.05 point vs. 0.19 needed), and by evidence that the milk itself was good quality when tested elsewhere.

### Did the 1925 arbitration board examine the condemned glassware?

**Answer: No.**

**Evidence:** Cleve Oram reported that two of the three sittings of the arbitration were held at the Ostrey Hollow station itself, "in the weigh room—that is the intake room, the same room, with the scale in it." The condemned crate "was up the ladder over their heads the entire time. Twenty feet, if that." Oram stood outside on the platform during one afternoon and could hear voices but not words. He later thought about how "the crate was up the ladder over their heads the entire time" during the hearings, but admitted "I will not pretend I thought about it then."

Bertram Kyle, who carried records to the arbitration board, stated: "I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second [receiving book] beside the fourth [state inspector's notebook] and looked."

No record indicates the board climbed the ladder to examine the condemned glass. The crate remained undiscovered (in its original sealed location) until February 1958, thirty-five years after the arbitration, when Ivy Keddie found it.

### What did the tester himself believe?

**Answer: Ansel Keddie privately suspected the glass but never publicly claimed it.**

**Evidence:** In summer 1911, during the nine-week interval, Duncan Keddie (his son, age 17) recalled: "[My father] came home that summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book...I was seventeen and I remember the kitchen and I remember that he said it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not his way."

Duncan continues: "What he wrote in the book was a question mark. He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove, and I have thought since that this was the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had. He had, after all, nothing to hold in his hand but a suspicion about a piece of glass, and against it stood the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county. So he kept on."

In Ansel's 1925 statement, defending himself before the arbitration board, he maintained only that he had tested faithfully and found nothing to suspect—he made no public allegation about the glass. Orra's diary shows that after dismissal, he never spoke of the business except once a year in April, reflecting silently on it.

**Conclusion:** Ansel Keddie suspected the glass in 1911 (based on the sudden improvement with borrowed glass), but as a practical man who "would not put down what he could not prove," and facing the institutional authority of the equipment itself and the established practice of all other stations, he kept silence and continued testing with faulty glass for fourteen more years. He defended his work rather than accuse the equipment. He died in 1934 without ever publicly stating what he had privately suspected.

## Section G: Summary (4 points, ≤150 words)

A six-farm community in Ordell County sent milk through a cooperative creamery since 1896. The Ostrey Hollow station consistently tested butterfat 0.19 points below the valley average, costing farmers thousands over 26 years. Three theories blamed the patrons: water, theft, or wet-hay feed. A 1911 accident proved otherwise: when the station's glass measure broke and was replaced with borrowed equipment for nine weeks, tests rose to normal; when the station's own glass resumed, tests fell again. In 1925, the board dismissed the station's tester without admitting fault and awarded partial compensation. He lived nine years under suspicion, never publicly cleared. In 1958, a college student found the condemned pipettes in a loft and measured them: they delivered 7% less milk than marked—the sole cause. She published findings; twenty-four years after his death, the tester's name was vindicated by measurement, not memory.
