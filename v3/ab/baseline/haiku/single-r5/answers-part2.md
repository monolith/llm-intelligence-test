# Test Answers — Part 2: Section A Stories 5–8, Section B

## Story 5: The Aftermath & Estrangement (1925–1934)

After his dismissal on April 30, 1925, Ansel Keddie never returned to the Ostrey Hollow station. He was sixty-four years old. He went home and began work on projects deferred for decades: the cow shed his father had wanted repaired, carpentry jobs around the hollow. He worked from light to dark, and according to Orra's diary, he whistled—something he never did when he was paid. His carpentry was excellent; Cleve Oram's father remarked that Keddie "was a hand with a door," and Oram himself saw a barn door Keddie hung thirty years later still swinging true. He took what work came, paid at a quarter of what the testing had paid. Orra noted in 1926 that several diary leaves had been torn out, leaving a gap in the record. The estrangement from her brother Emmet Brant, which had begun in 1919 over the water-warning circular, persisted. In spring 1929, Orra learned that Emmet had died. She went down to Hessel Bottom and sat in the kitchen where she had been a girl, and Roy (Emmet's son and first cousin to Ivy Keddie) met her at the gate and was kind. Ten years had passed with neither brother willing to go up the road first. In July 1929, Ansel and Orra sat out together and he named the hollyhocks his mother had grown. On February 12, 1934, Ansel Keddie died at home in the forenoon without much pain. Duncan, his son and Ivy's father, came up in the night. More people attended the funeral than Orra had expected. Men shook her hand who had not spoken to Ansel in nine years. Every one of them said what a hand he was with a door. Not one of them said the other thing—the accusation that had followed him since 1925. Orra reflected that he never asked them to, and that he had said to her once and only once that a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name. He lived nine years after dismissal, never knowing that measurement would one day vindicate him.

## Story 6: The Discovery in the Loft (February–March 1958)

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie, granddaughter of Ansel and student of dairy husbandry at the State Agricultural College at Farland, climbed the ladder to the loft over the Ostrey Hollow station's intake room. Under a tarpaulin and about an inch of chaff lay the condemned crate that Merle Strawn had ordered sealed in 1923. It had sat there for thirty-five years, through two sittings of the 1925 arbitration, while men argued in the weighroom beneath. The glassware inside was damaged—years in the loft had broken many of the pipettes—but nine survived intact. On March 9, 1958, Ivy carried the crate back to Farland and spent a day measuring each surviving pipette against a calibrated burette in a college laboratory. Every pipette was blown on the stem with the mark "17.6 c.c." but not one delivered that volume. The average of the nine measured to 16.27 cc. They were consistently short by 0.88 cc, or one part in twenty, or about 5 percent. A short charge of milk into a test bottle marked for the full charge yields a butterfat reading short in the same proportion. This and nothing else explained the entire discrepancy. No milk had been watered. No cream had been stolen. No wet hay alone had dimmed the fat. The glass itself had been wrong from the beginning, from May 1, 1897 (or 1898), and had remained wrong until the state inspector finally measured it in June 1923 and condemned it. For twenty-six years, the glass had delivered a systematic error affecting every patron on Hessel Bottom.

## Story 7: Scientific Vindication (October 1958–1959)

In October 1958, the State Agricultural College at Farland published Bulletin No. 471 under the names of Professor Leland Vaught of the dairy husbandry department and graduate student Ivy Keddie. The bulletin presented the measurement data, explained the physics of the proportional error, analyzed the 1911 nine-week interval as proof of causation (when borrowed measure showed 3.80 tests, the same man and same herds), and concluded: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there...seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat...stands credited...as station gain." The Ashlin Chronicle published the story the following week, noting that it was Ivy Keddie, Ansel's great-niece (later corrected to granddaughter), who had solved a mystery thirty-three years after his dismissal and twenty-four years after his death. The bulletin vindicated Ansel Keddie completely. It also indicted Ammon & Sons, the glassblowing firm. Junia Ammon, historian of the company, acknowledged in her firm's own 1949 history that they had sold short pipettes; she speculated the mold had been reground and the error went undetected in checking. She reprinted the 1897 invoice and the evasive 1923 reply to the Association's request for replacement glass: "Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name." Ammon wrote that she regretted the letter and what it signified about the firm's response to a customer of twenty-six years. In 1959, county agent Orin Falke filed an official summary for the Ordell County Board, and was prompted to look up Bulletin No. 214 to verify the bad-hay theory. He found it: bog hay depressed butterfat by only 0.05 points in winter, not enough to carry the 0.19-point deficit. He withdrew his earlier concession to the wet-hay theory and noted that the vindication was complete.

## Story 8: Institutional Memory & Documentary Preservation (1896–1963)

From 1896 onward, the Ostrey Hollow station generated written records: Ansel Keddie's test book, the station's receiving book, the income and disbursement accounts. In 1897, the Association's office began copying station figures into ledgers, and by 1898, Bertram Kyle, clerk at Larrow Green, was ruling pages to carry the annual summaries to the membership. Every year for a quarter-century, the ledger showed three figures and a sentence under "Ostrey Hollow Station": butterfat credited to patrons, butterfat received in cream at Larrow Green, and the difference—the "station gain"—always about 5.25 percent, always labeled "as usual." No one questioned it. The name explained it; explanation stopped inquiry. Emmet Brant kept his own weigh book from 1897 (or 1900), recording his farm weights and the station's tests. Roy Brant inherited it and in 1958 lent it to Ivy Keddie for her college work; she returned it, and at Roy's death in 1962 it went to his daughter Hazel, who donated it to the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1963. Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks, kept from 1896 onward with one for each year, went to the state dairy office in 1936 and were transferred to the State Agricultural College library in 1947. Ivy found the 1923 volume eleven years later when she was researching. Rosalie Cudd, who ran the Alder Corners Cheese Company across the ridge and whose father Jerome Cudd had kept the company's test book, had the figures copied and sent to Ivy in 1958. In 1959, Ivy's findings went to the Historical Rooms along with the condemned crate and pipettes. Duncan Keddie, Ansel's son, wrote a 1968 account documenting where every book had gone so that future seekers would know the records had existed. The accidental survival of that glass in a loft, the institutional decision to preserve ledgers and dairy records, and Ivy's recognition that a question mark in an 1911 ledger entry was worth investigating—these made vindication possible twenty-four years after Ansel Keddie's death.

## Section B: Relationships

### B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's **granddaughter**. Ansel had a son, Duncan Keddie (born 1894), who became a schoolteacher at Ashlin. Duncan married and had a daughter, Ivy (born 1931). Therefore Ivy was the child of Duncan, the son of Ansel. The Ashlin Chronicle initially misidentified her as "great-niece" in October 1958; Orin Falke's 1959 county report made the same error. Duncan Keddie corrected this in his 1968 family notes, and the county agent later issued a correction. The error likely arose from Duncan being a generation removed and not immediately recognizable as Ansel's direct child in casual conversation, but the genealogy is clear and documented.

### B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were **second cousins**. Hazel Brant was the daughter of Roy Brant. Roy Brant was the son of Emmet Brant. Emmet Brant was the brother of Orra Brant, who married Ansel Keddie and had a son, Duncan Keddie (Ivy's father). Therefore: Emmet and Orra were siblings; Roy was Emmet's son; Duncan was Orra's son; Ivy was Duncan's daughter; Hazel was Roy's daughter. Ivy and Hazel are therefore children of first cousins (Duncan and Roy), making them second cousins. Duncan Keddie's 1968 account carefully distinguishes this from "cousins once removed" (which is a different generation relation) and from "aunt and niece" (which people incorrectly said). The two were indeed closely related through both the Brant and Keddie lines, with both sides centered on Hessel Bottom. The sources leave open no ambiguity about intervening generations—the chain is direct and documented in family records, the weigh book, and multiple accounts.

### B3. Selby Vose's father and its significance

Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**, the founder and first president of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon organized the Association and wrote its bylaws, including Article VII, which stated that the test at the station where milk was delivered would govern payment. Selby Vose became manager of the Association in 1911 and served for a quarter-century. This matters enormously to what happened at Ostrey Hollow because Selby was responsible for managing the Association during the critical 1911–1925 period when the problem should have been discovered. Gideon had written the rule that made the station's test absolute; Selby administered that rule and signed the annual ledger summaries showing the persistent station gain. Bertram Kyle, the office clerk, noted that Selby "never read the lines above" the totals—he would put his thumb on the bottom figure and sign. The significance is that Selby Vose, inheriting leadership from Gideon Vose, inherited the framework that gave the low tests their power. Had there been any mechanism to question the station's test, it would have required Selby's action. Instead, in 1919, Selby signed the water-warning circular accusing the patrons themselves. Bertram Kyle typed it and remembered typing it, remembering also that it was a hard thing to send. Selby's management, passive rather than investigative, let the problem persist until 1925, when he was required to convene the arbitration.

### B4. Relationship between the Vose and Keddie families

The sources are contradictory and confused on this point. Duncan Keddie explicitly stated in his 1968 account: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either." He reported that he had twice been told (by women in Ashlin) that his mother Orra and Gideon Vose's wife were cousins, and one woman claimed first cousins; Duncan dismissed this as talk that had circulated since before he could walk. He noted that his mother, who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection," never once placed a Vose in the genealogy she taught him. Duncan's testimony is that there was **no family relationship**. However, Cleve Oram, the cream hauler's son, stated in his 1977 oral history: "The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort...My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper." Oram's account is vaguer, from family hearsay, and he admits he cannot document it. Duncan Keddie's direct statement, made with explicit attention to the confusion, carries more weight, and the resolution is that **no documented familial relationship existed**, though valley rumor persisted in suggesting one. The sources establish that Duncan knew the rumor and explicitly rejected it; Oram reported it as hearsay but unverified.
