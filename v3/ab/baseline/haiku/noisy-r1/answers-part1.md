# Answers — Section A (Stories 1–4)

## Story 1: The Founding of the Larrow Valley Cooperative (1889–1892)

In 1889, farmers of the Larrow Valley, responding to the cooperative creamery movement sweeping the state, met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose was elected the first president. These farmers had been making butter individually in their kitchens but saw the efficiency of pooling their milk through a central creamery.

In 1891, the Association built its main creamery at Larrow Green on Larrow Creek with a churn operation. The Association required formal bylaws. In 1892, Article VII was adopted at an annual meeting, establishing that patrons would be paid for their milk not by weight but by the percentage of butterfat as determined by test at the station where they deliver. This article, written by Gideon Vose (who was not a lawyer; Bertram Kyle notes the bylaws "read as a farmer wrote them"), became the foundation of the entire system: "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."

This single article set the structure that would govern all future disputes. Under it, patrons had no recourse if they believed a station's test was wrong—they could not appeal to Larrow Green's standards or request a re-test. The test was the whole bargain. This proved to be the hinge on which the entire Ostrey Hollow business turned.

Gideon Vose kept Jerseys on his home place, tested at 4.56 in 1910—a point of comparison for later inquiries. He died in 1917, before questions about Article VII could be asked of him.

The Association began with modest means and grew through the 1890s as the cooperative movement succeeded. The bylaws were printed and adopted at an annual meeting. The minute book recording the founding and first four annual meetings (1889–1892) was lost in an office removal in 1938, leaving no first-hand record of what was discussed or decided at the founding. Effie Loomis, who became secretary in 1901, explicitly states she has no records for 1889–1892; the minute book was lost in the moving in 1938.

**Documents:** By-laws pamphlet with Article VII; Effie Loomis's "Notes toward a History" (1941) on the lost minute book; Orin Falke's 1959 county report; Duncan Keddie's family notes (1968); Bertram Kyle's office account (1953).

## Story 2: The Opening of the Ostrey Hollow Station (1896–1897)

Responding to the needs of farmers at the head of the valley, the Association decided in 1895–1896 to build a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green, in the hamlet of Ostrey Hollow on the road toward Hessel Bottom. This location served the six farms of Hessel Bottom, which had hay and pasture rights on the wet meadow below the station, as well as other farms in that end of the valley.

The station opened on May 4, 1896. It was a board building with two floors. The intake room on the ground floor contained a scale for weighing milk, a vat, and a separator. Above the intake room was a loft accessed by a ladder nailed to the wall with a rope-pulled trap at the top, where equipment and materials were stored. A covered platform at waist height on the road side allowed farmers to back wagons and roll cans level rather than lifting them. Skim milk came back out the same platform for farmers to take home to their hogs. Cream went into cans for daily hauling down to Larrow Green.

Ansel Keddie came to the station as tester and separator man from the opening. He would remain for twenty-nine seasons until 1925. Cleve Oram's father hauled the cream down the road daily, a seven-mile climb in all weather.

Initially, the station borrowed its testing glass from Larrow Green. On April 14, 1897, the Association ordered a complete outfit from Ammon & Sons Dairy Supply of Tarnet: six dozen milk test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. ($25.20), twelve dozen test bottles with 8 per cent neck ($31.68), one acid measure ($0.90), one hand tester for four bottles ($18.00), and freight ($2.15), for a total of $77.93. This glass arrived around May 1, 1897, and was put into use, ending the need to borrow. Ansel Keddie's own test book, begun May 4, 1896, records: "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie."

The first season saw approximately 900,000 to 1,100,000 pounds of milk delivered. The first full season of station accounts began in 1898.

**Documents:** Station test book (Ansel Keddie's hand, 1896–1924); Ammon & Sons invoice dated April 14, 1897; Orra Keddie's diary entries (May 1, 1897); Cleve Oram's recorded account (1977); Hazel Brant's recorded account (1981); Bertram Kyle's office account (1953).

## Story 3: The Emergence of the Test Discrepancy and Early Complaints (1902–1908)

From very nearly the day the station opened, the milk of Ostrey Hollow tested lower than the milk of anywhere else in the valley. By the early 1900s, this discrepancy became a topic of conversation.

In 1902, the first complaint of record was made. Orra Keddie's diary for March 1902 notes: "Talk at the store that our milk up here runs poorer than theirs down the valley. A. came home and would not eat. I said it is not your cows and not your hands, and he said no, but it is my figures they will look at." Ansel was clearly troubled but said no more.

The station's average test settled at 3.61 butterfat percentage, while the Larrow Green creamery's own average over the same years was approximately 3.78. This was an ordinary figure for the county and neighboring counties, but the Ostrey Hollow figure was consistently 0.17 to 0.19 points lower—a gap that should not exist if the herds and pastures were similar.

The Ostrey Hollow patrons, led by complaints at annual meetings, argued they were being cheated. The Association's officers, in turn, suspected the patrons of adding water to their milk to increase volume. No one appears to have questioned the apparatus itself—that the problem might be in the glassware.

This discrepancy persisted for years without resolution. Orra's diary and statements from multiple narrators confirm the pattern held steady "in every month of the year, in the flush of June and in the depth of February alike." The consistency was actually a crucial clue that the error was not due to seasonal factors (hay quality, cow condition) but to something constant—the measuring apparatus.

By 1908, the complaints had been renewed "in one form or another, in every decade" since 1902, according to Orin Falke's summary. The issue dominated valley discussion but remained unresolved.

**Documents:** Station test book (showing consistent 3.61 from 1898 onward); Orra Keddie's diary (March 1902); Orin Falke's 1959 county report; Hazel Brant's 1981 recorded account; Cleve Oram's 1977 recorded account; earlier notes on 1902 complaints.

## Story 4: The 1908 Investigating Committee and Hay Theory

In January 1908, the Association appointed a committee to investigate the low test figures at Ostrey Hollow. Nyle Grigg, a farmer from Larrow Green (not a patron of the Hollow station), chaired the committee. Grigg and his committee members sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February, and March 1908—the dead of winter, when the hay was being fed.

The committee walked the farms, looked into the mangers, examined the stock. They found no dishonesty of any kind. They did observe that the cattle were thin in the flank at the end of winter, slow to come to flesh, with rough coats—consistent with marginal feed quality. Their written report concluded: "We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March. The milk is whole and the scales are honest. It is our opinion that the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station."

This report proposed the "hay theory"—that the wet meadow soil of Hessel Bottom, being boggy and poor in minerals, produced hay that when fed to cows, depressed the butterfat test. State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 214 (published 1911) tested this theory under controlled conditions and found that bog hay depressed butterfat test by "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."

The committee's report was received and accepted at the time. The question was considered closed for eleven years (1908–1919). Orra Keddie's diary for March 1908 records the committee's departure and their conclusion. Ansel Keddie "said nothing at all, which is his way."

However, the hay theory proved inadequate to explain the 0.19–0.21 point gap observed at Ostrey Hollow. A deficiency of only 0.05 points maximum (per Bulletin 214) could not account for the consistent 0.19-point shortfall. The actual cause—defective glassware—had not been considered.

**Documents:** 1908 committee report (fair copy); State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 214 (1911); Nyle Grigg's 1919 covering letter republishing the report; Orra Keddie's diary (March 1908); Orin Falke's 1959 county report (noting the Bulletin 214 discrepancy).
