# Comprehensive Notes: Retellings 1–16 (Ostrey Hollow Dairy Station Case)

## Central Narrative

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, organized in 1889 and incorporated as a dairy marketing cooperative in the Larrow Valley, operated three facilities: a main creamery at Larrow Green (built 1891), an office at Ashlin (county seat), and a skimming station at the head of Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green.

From 1896/1897 to 1923, the Ostrey Hollow station was operated by Ansel Keddie, a careful tester who maintained a handwritten test book recording milk delivered by farmers, pounds, butterfat test percentage, and calculated butterfat. The cream tested at the station was separated and sent daily down to Larrow Green for further processing.

In 1925, patrons of Ostrey Hollow mounted a petition claiming they had been systematically underpaid for their milk over many years. A board of arbitration sat at Ashlin from March 17 to May 4, 1925, hearing all parties. The board declared "no cause established" but awarded $3,800 "without admission" to the Ostrey Hollow patrons. Ansel Keddie was dismissed on April 30, 1925. He died in the 1930s (Vira Toland says "he died in the thirties," Ivy Keddie says 1934) without vindication.

In 1958, Ivy Keddie (his granddaughter, then attending State Agricultural College at Farland) discovered a wooden crate in the abandoned station loft containing the condemned test pipettes. Measuring them against a calibrated burette on March 9, 1958, she found they delivered only 16.72 c.c. instead of their stated 17.6 c.c.—95 hundredths of the mark (or 16.27 c.c. per Bulletin 471, a discrepancy). This systematic shortfall of 5% explains all the recorded discrepancies. Bulletin No. 471, issued by the College in October 1958, declared Keddie innocent: no water was added, no cream was stolen; the tests were simply low because the apparatus was short.

## Key Persons and Relationships

### The Keddie/Brant Complex
- **Ansel Keddie** (tester, Ostrey Hollow station, 1896–1924)
  - Kept test book in his own hand, 1896 to 1924 (29 seasons; some sources say 1897-1923, 26+ years)
  - Noted for meticulous record-keeping, straight lines, no blots
  - Married to Orra Keddie (q.v.)
  - Died 1934 (or "the thirties" per Vira Toland)
  - Dismissed April 30, 1925 (while board still sitting, four days before award)
  - His son was Duncan Keddie (schoolteacher at Ashlin)
  - Duncan's daughter was Ivy Keddie (born post-1896)
  - Made statement to board March 17, 1925: tested as taught, state man saw him do it 31 times/week for 29 seasons, never took pound of cream out of station
  - Told Alonzo Frick privately that he always thought patrons at fault but wouldn't accuse neighbors in the room

- **Orra Keddie** (wife of Ansel)
  - Sister of Emmet Brant (largest patron at Ostrey Hollow station)
  - First cousin of Mrs. Vose (wife of Gideon Vose, first president of Association)
  - After 1919 circular, did not speak to her brother Emmet for decades
  - Outlived Ansel; "had it [the shame] the whole time"
  - "Years and years those two lived within sight of each other's chimney smoke and never said good morning"

- **Emmet Brant** (largest patron, Hessel Bottom)
  - Born 1854, died 1929
  - Operated the largest of six/seven farms with hay/pasture rights on Hessel Bottom
  - Had more cows and more hay ground than any other patron; delivered more milk than any patron
  - Kept weigh book from January 1, 1897 to week before he died
  - Kept handwritten ledger (plain, ruled by hand) with two columns: weight off his own scale and station's weight—they agreed consistently
  - Did not speak to his sister Orra Keddie after 1919
  - "The letter cost the most" to Emmet Brant
  - Son: Roy Brant (kept the weigh book after father's death, lent it to Ivy Keddie in 1958)

- **Roy Brant** (Emmet's son)
  - Age 67 in 1955; still milking at Hessel Bottom
  - Kept father's weigh book and continued in same manner after father's death
  - Emphasis on fact that two scales (his and station's) agreed, despite men not speaking after 1919
  - Observed that milk tested 17 hundredths lower year-round, not seasonal
  - Did not haul to cheese factory over Redlow Ridge (unlike four other Bottom men in summers 1915-1930)
  - Related to Hazel Brant and Ivy Keddie (daughter of Duncan)
  - Lent weigh book to Ivy in 1958 for college work

- **Ivy Keddie** (Ansel's granddaughter, Duncan Keddie's daughter)
  - Attending State Agricultural College at Farland
  - Measured the condemned pipettes on March 9, 1958, finding them short 0.88 c.c. (5% error) [or 16.72 c.c. vs 17.6 c.c. per measurement; Bulletin 471 says mean 16.27 c.c.—discrepancy]
  - Gave talk to Ordell County Historical Society in 1959
  - Collected and cross-referenced five sets of records: station test book, Brant weigh book, Alder Corners test figures, Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks, Association annual ledgers
  - Found the crate in the station loft in February 1958
  - Borrowed Roy Brant's weigh book in 1958 for college work
  - Second cousin to Hazel Brant
  - Related to Hazel Brant (connection as granddaughter to Ansel)

- **Hazel Brant** (Roy's daughter, or possibly Emmet's daughter)
  - Had a first cousin once removed (a young college woman) who visited Vira Toland in 1958 to research the case
  - Was later involved in receiving historical documents
  - Second cousin to Ivy Keddie

### Association Officers and Staff
- **Gideon Vose** (first president, 1889–1911, served 22 years)
  - Drafted the by-laws himself (not a lawyer; "they read as a farmer wrote them")
  - Wrote Article VII in 1892 (though some sources say 1891)
  - Kept Jerseys on home place; herd tested at 4.56 in 1910
  - Died 1917 (before questions about Article VII could be asked of him)

- **Selby Vose** (manager, 1913–1938; son of Gideon)
  - Became manager in 1913
  - Gave annual address in 1926 (13 years as manager)
  - Signed annual station account sheets each year (1898 onward)
  - Sent the 1919 circular warning patrons about water in cans
  - His family and the Keddie/Brant families were intertwined: Mrs. Vose (Selby's mother, wife of Gideon) was first cousin of Orra Keddie
  - In 1926 address: defended the $3,800 settlement and claimed water was being added to cans; said "Water was going into cans at the head of that valley and it stopped when the Association began to look"
  - Never found evidence of water addition per Merle Strawn's repeated field visits

- **Effie Loomis** (secretary, 1901–1940)
  - Kept minute book throughout her tenure, in her own hand
  - Was 14 years old in 1889; came into office in 1901
  - Did not witness the Association's founding
  - Wrote "Notes toward a History" in 1941, three years after laying down the minute book
  - Explicitly states she has no records for 1889–1892 (minute book lost in 1938 office move)
  - Notes: "Forty years of taking down what men say has taught me that the interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying"
  - Recorded the arbitration award: "no cause established" and $3,800 "without admission"
  - Remarked: "I took it down as it was given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once"

- **Wilbur Teague** (bookkeeper, 1905–1945)
  - Came in as bookkeeper in 1905
  - Kept ledgers for 40 years (through 1945)
  - Created the "station gain" account line in 1905 (it was his own device, not a pre-existing dodge)
  - Raised the issue with Vose in 1916, was told to let it alone; complied, believing a bookkeeper is not the company's conscience
  - Gave testimony to the board of arbitration in 1925
  - Wrote chapter 9 of memoir "The Books of the Association" in 1948, three years after retiring
  - Notes: "a bookkeeper who wants admiring has generally got something behind him that he would rather you did not turn over"
  - At arbitration: put forward implication that tester had been taking cream and writing low tests (without direct accusation)

- **Hobart Sill** (buttermaker at Larrow Green, ret. 1934)
  - Kept churn book and cream receiving book
  - Started 1893, worked 41 years
  - Key observation: Cream from Ostrey Hollow always ran HEAVY—more fat in cans than test figures said
  - Weighed cream himself most years in addition to platform man
  - Believed Hollow milk should have tested 3.85, not 3.61
  - Thought water was being added; said so at the time
  - Observed around 1920s: figures came up and stayed up, nothing else changed but season
  - Larrow Green's own milk tested 3.78 taking one year with another
  - Made premium butter that was always good, never had word against quality of cream
  - Lent his own glass equipment to Ostrey Hollow in 1896 when station opened (no equipment arrived yet)
  - Likely overseen by Teague and Loomis

### External Figures
- **Alonzo Frick** (attorney, Ashlin; chairman of arbitration board)
  - Age 74 in 1946
  - Presided over the March 17 – May 4, 1925 hearings
  - Described by Wilbur Teague as "as fair a chairman as ever a hearing had"
  - Emphasized: "A board of arbitration can only decide between the accounts that are put to it"
  - Never found chemistry straightforward, understood that Ansel Keddie was honest but honest man ruined by figure nobody could explain
  - Wished answer was in the room but was not in any of three stories (water, theft, hay)
  - Noted: Keddie dismissed while board still sitting, four days before award

- **Merle Strawn** (state dairy inspector)
  - Took Ordell circuit in 1907; retired 1936
  - Inspected 50+ plants across four counties
  - Carried burettes from 1921 onward for checking pipettes and measures
  - Made circuit visits to Ostrey Hollow in: 1907, 1909, 1911, 1912, 1913, 1915, 1916, 1917, 1919, 1920 (twice), 1921, 1922, 1923 (twice), 1924, 1925, 1926, 1928, 1931, 1934, 1936
  - Consistently found scales true, no added water
  - On June 12, 1923, condemned the entire crate of pipettes as "will not deliver their mark"
  - On October 1923, confirmed new pipettes delivered their mark
  - Notebook entry routine: measure weights, lactometer, boiler/water/cold room, and watch a test performed
  - Reported in 1922: did not run glass against burette due to short day
  - Reported in 1925: new tester was "quick" (which Strawn disliked; he preferred "slow and clean")
  - Field notes quote: "I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any"
  - Entry June 12, 1923: "Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk pipettes against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them."

- **Leland Vaught** (professor of dairy husbandry, State Agricultural College)
  - Wrote introduction to Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958)
  - Emphasized that milk pipette is the one instrument that cannot be checked against anything else and is what all figures depend on
  - Noted three key properties of the error: proportional (not additive), precise (consistent), and invisible from inside plant
  - Material examined: 9 pipettes (unbroken, legible) from the 5 dozen (60) lot purchased in 1897
  - Stems blown for 17.6 c.c., measured at mean 16.27 c.c. [discrepancy with 16.72 c.c. from Ivy's measurement]
  - Station's glass in use from May 1, 1898 (not 1897 as previously stated—another discrepancy) to June 12, 1923
  - The 1911 interval proved apparatus error: 9 weeks with borrowed measure showed average test 3.80; returned to 3.61 when station's own measure came back
  - Calculation: ratio 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244; recorded average 3.61; true average 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.905; shortfall 0.295 of a point
  - Applied to 40,000,000 lb milk over 25 full delivery years = 118,000 lb of butterfat delivered and not credited
  - Ledgers show "station gain" of 5.25% of butterfat actually delivered
  - Bulletin No. 214 (1911) showed bog hay effect: maximum 0.05% in winter, nothing in summer
  - Conclusion: from May 1, 1897 to June 12, 1923, tests were low by 1/20 because pipettes were short by 1/20; difference = 76,000 lb of butterfat in ledgers as station gain

- **Halbert Nym** (curator, Ordell County Historical Rooms)
  - Catalogued 16 items for the dairy collection in 1972
  - Working notes checked twice

- **Nyle Grigg** (farmer, Larrow Green; chairman of 1908 investigating committee)
  - Not a patron of Ostrey Hollow; hauled to Larrow Green
  - Appointed to committee in January 1908
  - Sampled herds through January, February, March 1908 [note: his 1919 letter says "whole of 1908 — every month" but actual report says Jan-Feb-March only; discrepancy]
  - Found: no dishonesty; milk whole; scales honest; wet hay thins cream; remedy lies with land, not station
  - 1919 covering letter: cited State Bulletin No. 214 as validating committee's findings, claimed it showed bog hay depresses test by "two tenths" in winter [but bulletin said 0.05 max—discrepancy]
  - Report received and accepted; question closed for 11 years (1908-1919)

- **Dr. Roland Kepp** (veterinary surgeon, Ashlin, 30 years practice)
  - Presented paper "The bog meadows and the milk" to Ordell County Farm Club in 1926
  - Argued: wet bottom is distinct soil chemistry; sour soil poor in lime and phosphates
  - Cow in milk on poor ration robs her own frame; fat is first thing to suffer because it's the dearest thing cow makes
  - Observation: wet bottoms yield milk poorer in fat by "a fifth of a point" (0.20)
  - Gap between Ostrey Hollow and rest of valley: "twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21"
  - Cited Bulletin No. 214: bog hay depresses test by "two tenths in the winter feeding" [discrepancy with bulletin's 0.05]
  - Claimed three figures agree: College's 0.20, his 0.20, station's 0.21—close enough
  - At discussion: rejected idea that difference was in cows, defended that farm land condition was determinant
  - Dismissed notion that station apparatus was cause; said when cause found in soil, herbage, stock, College trial, and every wet bottom alike, not good sense to look for second cause in building

- **Rosalie Cudd** (cheesemaker at Alder Corners Cheese Company, Vessey County)
  - Runs factory inherited from father Jerome Cudd (built 1913, died March 1941)
  - Took over spring 1941 (17 years before 1958)
  - Jerome's test book shows: summer of 1919, four Hessel Bottom men tested 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82
  - Jerome Cudd's note: "Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge"
  - Four Hessel Bottom men hauled to her factory summers 1915-1930 (16 seasons)
  - Season averages (1915-1930): range 3.78 to 3.82, average about 3.80
  - Strong emphasis: in 16 summers, no complaints about water or quality; cheese tells truth (watered milk won't knit, yields down)
  - Road (Redlow Ridge) shuts November, opens April-May (4 miles switchback)
  - No man ever hauled in winter

- **Dell Rundle** (schoolteacher at Larrow Green)
  - Compiled "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years" jubilee pamphlet in 1939 for 50th year
  - Reprinted 1959 with footnote noting Ivy Keddie's work on the matter
  - In note added while in press: examined Association's old ledgers, found station account shows cream reached Larrow Green HEAVY every year, not light as would be expected if stolen
  - Footnote (1959 reprint): notes Ivy Keddie (second cousin to Hazel Brant) took up Hollow matter at college; found "11 pipettes out of station's old condemned crate" [discrepancy: other sources say 9 pipettes]

- **Vira Toland** (patron's daughter, recalls events)
  - Father was one of nine families who received the 1919 circular about water
  - Her father kept the letter in his desk drawer
  - Visited by young college woman in 1958 asking about case
  - Recalls: before 1919 families ate Sunday dinners together; after 1919 Ansel and Emmet didn't speak
  - Says Ansel Keddie died "in the thirties"

## Timeline of Key Events

**1889:** Farmers of Larrow Valley meet at Larrow Green and organize the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose elected first president.

**1891:** Main creamery built at Larrow Green on Larrow Creek. Churn set up.

**1892:** Article VII adopted at annual meeting, establishing payment by butterfat test rather than milk weight. Clause: "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."

**1893:** Payment by test came in (per Nyle Grigg).

**1896–1897 (spring):** Ostrey Hollow skimming station opens. Test book begun May 4, 1896. Glass borrowed from Larrow Green until May 1, 1897, when Ammon & Sons equipment (6 doz pipettes at 17.6 c.c., etc., $77.93) arrives. First season (May-end of year): about 900,000 pounds.

**1898:** Station account ledgers begin. Average test recorded as 3.61.

**1901:** Effie Loomis becomes secretary of Association.

**1902:** Complaints begin that Ostrey Hollow milk does not test as high as milk from other stations. First man to say so out loud was Emmet Brant; complaints grew.

**1905:** Wilbur Teague becomes bookkeeper. Creates "station gain" account line.

**1908:** Committee (Nyle Grigg, chairman) samples Hessel Bottom herds through January, February, March. Reports: wet hay thins cream; remedy lies with land, not station. Report received and accepted; question closed.

**1911 May 8:** Pipette (measure) breaks at Ostrey Hollow station. Station switches to borrowed measure from Larrow Green.

**1911 July 10:** Station's own measure returned and put back in use. Tests immediately return to normal 3.61 level. During the nine weeks of borrowed measure, tests stood at 3.80.

**1911:** State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 214 published, testing bog hay effect: maximum 0.05% in winter, nothing in summer. [Note: multiple later sources cite this as "two tenths"—discrepancy]

**1913:** Selby Vose becomes manager of Association.

**1914:** Alder Corners Cheese Company opened over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County by Jerome Cudd.

**1915-1930:** Four Hessel Bottom men (out of six farm families) haul milk to Alder Corners in summer flush (June 1 to Sept 15). Last summer 1930. Summer averages test around 3.80. Handled by Jerome Cudd until his death in 1941; then Rosalie Cudd.

**1916:** Wilbur Teague raises the "station gain" issue with Vose; is told to let it alone. Complies. Vose signs the annual sheets each year without raising issue.

**1919:** Circular from Association (over Selby Vose's signature, as manager) sent to nine families at Ostrey Hollow saying "there was water in the cans." The circular goes to Ansel Keddie's patrons, implicating him. Emmet Brant and Ansel Keddie stop speaking. Nyle Grigg reprints 1908 report with covering letter claiming Bulletin No. 214 validates the "two tenths" finding (discrepancy with actual bulletin). Merle Strawn's field notes continue to show no added water in repeated visits (1919 visits onward).

**1922:** Ansel Keddie makes up summary leaf in test book showing yearly averages.

**1923 June 12:** Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector, visits Ostrey Hollow and runs the station's milk test pipettes against a calibrated burette. Finds they "will not deliver their mark" and condemns the entire crate, telling the manager to order new. [Later note: not mentioned in 1925 arbitration hearing.]

**1923 June 28:** Association orders replacement pipettes and bottles from Ammon & Sons.

**1923 July–October:** New pipettes in use by July. Strawn confirms in October that new pipettes deliver their mark.

**1924:** Tests at Ostrey Hollow improve; average for year recorded as 3.80 or 3.90 (sources vary). Rise of 21 hundredths from prior norm.

**1925 January:** Patrons of Ostrey Hollow petition the Association, claiming they have been underpaid. Claim: milk delivered to station over years complained of (roughly 1898-1923) was 40,000,000 lb (or 44,000,000 lb per some sources) or took in "forty-four million pounds"; patrons' loss $19,600 or $19,000 (or $67,000 per Frick's account of patrons' reckoning—discrepancies).

**1925 March 17:** Board of arbitration sits at Ashlin under attorney Alonzo Frick. First session at Association's office. Ansel Keddie makes statement to the board.

**1925 March 17 – May 4:** Board holds sessions; second and third at Ostrey Hollow in station's weigh room. Hears all parties (Vose, Teague, Frick, Keddie), reviews books and appliances. Three explanations heard: water (Vose), cream theft (Teague's implication), hay (1908 committee report).

**1925 April 30:** Ansel Keddie is dismissed from the station (while board still sitting, four days before award).

**1925 May 4:** Board finds "no cause established," awards $3,800 "without admission," charged to station account.

**1925 spring/fall:** Ansel Keddie writes (but does not send) a letter explaining the test procedures, the 1911 mystery, and the history of complaints. "Those nine weeks in 1911 are the strangest thing in my book and I have never had an account of them to give... I could not answer it then and I cannot answer it now."

**1925 fall:** New tester takes over at Ostrey Hollow; Strawn notes he is "quick" (not ideal).

**1926:** Selby Vose gives annual address to membership, defending the $3,800 settlement and explaining his belief that water was being added to cans. ## Key Conflicts and Oddities

### 1. The "No Cause Established" Paradox
The board of arbitration found "no cause established" yet awarded $3,800 "without admission" to the Ostrey Hollow patrons. Effie Loomis, who recorded the award in the minute book, explicitly notes: "I took it down as it was given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once."

Alonzo Frick later reflected: "A board of arbitration can only decide between the accounts that are put to it. Three were put to us; each of them was urged by a man of standing; each of them would have been believed by half the valley; and not one of them was proved."

This formulation suggests the board either:
- Found the cause but would not name it, or
- Made a settlement to keep peace despite lack of proof

### 2. Dismissed Without Cause
Ansel Keddie was dismissed on April 30, 1925—four days before the board made its award, while the board was still sitting. He had worked for 29 seasons (or 26+ years) without documented complaint of dishonesty. Alonzo Frick noted: "On the thirtieth of April, four days before we made our award, the Association dismissed him. There was no finding against him from us and there never was one; the board had not concluded and did not conclude that he had done anything whatever. He was simply let go while the board was still sitting, and I have never been able to think well of the Association for it."

He died in 1934 (or "the thirties") without learning he was vindicated in 1958—24 years after his death.

### 3. The 1911 Mystery, Unresolved for 47 Years
When the station's measure broke on May 8, 1911, a borrowed one was used until July 10, 1911. During those nine weeks, tests rose from 3.61 to 3.80. When the station's own measure was reinstalled, tests dropped back to 3.61 immediately.

Ansel Keddie marked this in his test book with a question mark (July 10 entry: "Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."). In his unsent letter of spring 1925, he wrote: "Those nine weeks in 1911 are the strangest thing in my book and I have never had an account of them to give... That question mark is mine and I have let it stand for fourteen years. I could not answer it then and I cannot answer it now, and I will not put anything in its place that I do not know."

Dr. Kepp, in 1926, dismissed this interval as irrelevant: "Six weeks in the flush of the season, gentlemen. Six weeks of June and July, when every herd in the county is at its best and the stock are off the wet ground and up on the aftermath. I am astonished that anybody should found an argument on six weeks of summer figures." [Note: Kepp said "six weeks" but it was nine weeks, May 8 to July 10.]

The answer was only provided in Bulletin No. 471 (1958): the borrowed measure was true; the station's own pipettes were short by 5%. This had been the case since 1897 (or 1898), making 1911 a brief window into the true test values. This is described as an "experiment" that "ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven years."

### 4. Missing Official Records
The 1889–1892 minute book (covering the Association's founding and first four annual meetings) was lost in the 1938 office move. It "was not thrown out; it was carried out with the rest and it did not arrive" (Effie Loomis). Thus, no first-hand record exists of what was discussed or resolved at the founding. Loomis notes: "it is not there" (in the building) but does not say whether a search was ever made elsewhere.

Further, the crate of condemned pipettes was never mentioned in the 1925 arbitration hearing, despite being impounded by Merle Strawn on June 12, 1923 (four months before the petition was filed, two years before the hearing, and being in the Association's office at Ashlin). Effie Loomis notes: "The second is the crate. The crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all... Not a word of it was spoken in any of the three sessions, by the patrons, by the officers, or by the board."

### 5. Article VII's Unappealable Clause
Article VII (adopted 1892 or 1891) states: "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." This clause, written by Gideon Vose and adopted before most living people were born, meant that patrons had no recourse if they believed the station's test was wrong. They could not request a re-test or appeal to Larrow Green's standards.

Alonzo Frick observes: "It makes the test the whole of the bargain, and it leaves a dissatisfied man with no remedy on earth except to quarrel with a figure."

Effie Loomis observes: "That clause is the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business and it was written in 1892 by a man who died in 1917 and could not be asked about it afterward."

### 6. Ansel's Careful Procedure Did Not Prevent Systematic Error
Ansel Keddie's unsent letter provides a detailed account of his testing procedure:
- Samples warmed to blood temperature
- Cream mixed down through the sample by pouring vessel to vessel
- Milk drawn to the mark on the pipette stem
- Tip wiped
- Charge blown into numbered bottle
- Acid poured down the side
- Milk turned dark with acid
- Four bottles weighted against each other in hand tester
- Crank turned at steady speed
- Fat read off in neck with dividers
- Figure written down

Merle Strawn watched him perform tests and reported: "He works slow and clean and reads his own figure without being coaxed to it." Yet a systematic 5% error persisted for 26+ years. The error was in the apparatus (short pipettes), not the procedure.

### 7. The Estrangement of Ansel and Emmet Through Orra
Ansel Keddie married Orra Keddie, who was the sister of Emmet Brant, the largest patron at Ostrey Hollow station.

Before 1919: "those two families ate their Sunday dinners together, I have seen them at it, the two houses in one kitchen and the men out at the fence after."

After 1919 (the circular): "they did not speak. Not one word, not at the store, not at a funeral. Orra would walk down to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane."

Vira Toland sums up the tragedy: "Years and years those two lived within sight of each other's chimney smoke and never said good morning, and both of them long in the ground now, and for what."

The 1919 circular, sent by manager Selby Vose to warn patrons about water in cans, implicitly accused the station (Ansel Keddie) and by extension his wife's family.

Roy Brant notes: "My father held that the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not."

Alonzo Frick notes: "In the corridor at Ashlin, Keddie told me privately that he always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the room."

### 8. Vose's Contradictory Positions
In his 1926 address, Selby Vose claims that someone at Ostrey Hollow was adding water to the cans and stopped only under Association scrutiny in 1924. He says: "Water was going into cans at the head of that valley and it stopped when the Association began to look. The rise of 1924 is the measure of it."

However, Merle Strawn's field notes state clearly and repeatedly: No added water found on any visit. Strawn's 1919 note: "I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any." And in subsequent visits (1920, 1923, 1924, 1925), Strawn continued to find "no added water."

Vose defends his 1919 circular at the 1925 board: "I wrote it because it was my duty to write it... A manager who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing to the men who make them is not being kind to them; he is being idle at their expense."

But the actual cause (short pipettes) had nothing to do with added water. The apparent improvement in 1924 (tests rising 21 hundredths from the prior 3.61 norm) was due to new pipettes being installed in July 1923, not behavioral change.

Dr. Kepp, in 1926, claimed to support Vose by alleging wetland hay was the culprit. But at the arbitration hearing (implicitly), Kepp had no interest in the quarrel and said: "he had never in his life read a test and would not know how; but that when a cause is found in the soil, in the herbage, in the condition of the stock, in the College's trial, and in every wet bottom in the county alike, it is not good sense to go looking for a second cause in a building."

### 9. The "Station Gain" Account
Wilbur Teague created the "station gain" line in his ledger in 1905 as a simple accounting device: the difference between fat credited to patrons (on the station's own test) and fat actually received at Larrow Green.

The typical entry reads:
> "Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual."

This 5.25% gain appeared year after year with remarkable consistency. Teague notes: "A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture" (i.e., invisible to the eye because of its regularity).

Teague raised this with Vose in 1916. Vose told him to let it alone. Teague obeyed. But Vose signed the annual sheets each year, meaning he saw the pattern and chose not to investigate.

Hobart Sill, observing the cream arriving at Larrow Green, noted it always ran heavy: "Every year it ran heavy. There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there."

The "station gain" of 76,000 lb of fat over 25 years represents exactly the 5% shortfall from the defective pipettes.

### 10. The Hazel Brant Connection and the College Girl
A young college woman (described as Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed) visited Vira Toland in 1958 asking about the case. Vira Toland told her about the 1919 letter in her father's desk drawer.

The exact relationship between this girl and the various Brants is unclear. Roy Brant's daughter Hazel is mentioned; she may be Emmet Brant's granddaughter. The young woman appears to have been part of a later effort to research and understand the case—possibly connected to Ivy Keddie's work or conducted in parallel. Ivy Keddie is noted as second cousin to Hazel Brant.

### 11. Merle Strawn's Thoroughness and Powerlessness
Merle Strawn made 20+ visits to Ostrey Hollow over 28+ years (1907–1936), consistently testing for water and finding none. He was thorough: "I put down what I found. I did not put down what I thought about it... The state pays a field man to weigh and to look... I have been asked once or twice... to say in a general way how a certain plant stood in my estimation... I do not keep an estimation, I keep a book."

Yet he had no authority to order a second set of measurements or a re-audit. He could only condemn faulty apparatus (which he did on June 12, 1923) and recommend replacement. The condemned crate was never mentioned in the 1925 arbitration hearing.

### 12. The Alder Corners Parallel
The Alder Corners Cheese Company, located over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County, tested milk from some Hessel Bottom patrons (four men, summer seasons, 1915-1930). Their test book showed that the same milk tested approximately 0.19 higher at Alder Corners than at Ostrey Hollow.

This 19-hundredth gap is the exact shortfall predicted by the 5% error in the Ostrey Hollow pipettes (3.61 × 1.05 ≈ 3.80, a gap of 0.19).

Rosalie Cudd emphasizes: "Cheese is a witness. In sixteen summers my father never made one complaint of those four men, never docked one of them a cent, and never — not once that I heard, and I was in the room — said a word about their milk except that he wished he had more of it in July."

### 13. Bulletin No. 214 Discrepancy
Multiple sources (Nyle Grigg 1919, Dr. Kepp 1926) cite Bulletin No. 214 as showing bog hay depresses butterfat test by "two tenths" in winter. However, the actual Bulletin No. 214 (1911) states: "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter." 

This is a major discrepancy: 0.20 (two tenths) vs. 0.05 (five hundredths). The actual bulletin figure (0.05) does not account for the 0.19-0.21 gap observed at Ostrey Hollow, suggesting wet hay alone was not the explanation.

### 14. Apparatus Measurement Discrepancy
Ivy Keddie's 1958 measurement of the pipettes shows mean delivery of 16.72 c.c. (short 0.88 c.c., or 95 hundredths of the mark). However, Bulletin No. 471 (written by Leland Vaught, presumably with access to the same pipettes) states the mean delivery as 16.27 c.c. This is a significant discrepancy (16.72 vs. 16.27), though both agree on the approximate 5% error and the ratio calculation (both arrive at figures between 16.2 and 16.7 out of 17.6).

### 15. Hobart Sill's Observation vs. Teague's "Station Gain"
Hobart Sill, the buttermaker, observed that cream from Ostrey Hollow always arrived at Larrow Green heavier than the station's test figures indicated. This is consistent with Teague's "station gain" ledger entries (cream received exceeded fat credited to patrons). However, Sill interpreted this as evidence of added water in the cans, whereas the actual cause was the short pipettes at the station causing all tests to read low.

### 16. The Discrepancy on Equipment Date
The station's glass came "into use from May 1, 1897" (earlier notes), but Bulletin No. 471 states "May 1, 1898." The test book's first leaf says "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie" and the date is May 4, 1896. The equipment invoice from Ammon & Sons is dated April 14, 1897, suggesting the glass arrived around that date (say, May 1, 1897). Yet Bulletin 471 says the glass was in use from May 1, 1898. This is a discrepancy worth noting.

### 17. Hobart Sill's Dismissal of the 1911 Mystery
Hobart Sill, the buttermaker at Larrow Green, noted that Ostrey Hollow cream always ran heavy. When asked about the 1911 anomaly (nine weeks of higher tests), he might have been expected to confirm it, but this is not documented. Dr. Kepp dismissed it as "six weeks in the flush of the season" when cows were at their best. Leland Vaught, however, recognized it as "an experiment that ran itself."

## Unanswered Questions Across Sources

1. **Exactly when did Ansel Keddie die?** Vira Toland says "in the thirties." Ivy Keddie says "1934." Bulletin No. 471 would have been written with knowledge of his death; it is published in October 1958.

2. **Did the board of arbitration know about the condemned crate?** The crate was condemned by Merle Strawn on June 12, 1923, and was in the Association's office (presumably known to the office staff) when the board sat in March–May 1925. Yet no mention appears in the minute book of the hearing. Why was this evidence not presented?

3. **What happened to the 1889–1892 minute book after it left the office?** It was "carried out" during the 1938 move and "did not arrive." Effie Loomis says "it is not there" (in the building) but does not say whether a search was ever made elsewhere or whether it might exist in an archive.

4. **Why did Vose sign the annual sheets each year if he believed there was fraud or negligence?** His 1926 address claims he suspected water was being added, yet he re-signed the "station gain" accounts each year without raising the issue until pressed by the petitioners in 1925.

5. **What was the relationship between Rosalie Cudd and Emmet/Hazel Brant?** She is described as Jerome Cudd's niece and as running the Alder Corners Cheese Company (after Jerome's death in 1941). Did she have family ties to Hessel Bottom? The connection is not explained in sources.

6. **What became of the young college woman who visited Vira Toland in 1958?** Vira notes she "was Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed, so she was of the family both sides in a manner of speaking." Was she involved in the later research or publication efforts? Did she contribute to the college work or other documentation?

7. **Did anyone formally apologize to Ansel Keddie's descendants or clear his name publicly during his lifetime or shortly after his death?** The 1958 bulletin exonerates him completely, but he had been dead for at least 24 years. No record suggests a formal apology or public vindication before his death.

8. **How did the apparatus shortfall go undetected for 26+ years before Merle Strawn used a burette in 1921–1923?** The burette program was new (started 1921); before that, field inspectors checked apparatus only visually or by standard procedures, not by precision measurement. The 1911 nine-week anomaly should have prompted investigation, but did not.

9. **Which retellings describe the apparatus delivery date as May 1, 1897 vs. May 1, 1898?** Discrepancy needs clarification.

10. **Which sources say 9 pipettes vs. 11 pipettes vs. other counts?** Dell Rundle's 1959 footnote says "11 pipettes out of the station's old condemned crate." Halbert Nym's curatorial notes say "Nine pipettes survive (unbroken)." Bulletin No. 471 says "nine were recovered... and these nine constitute the material of this bulletin." This is a material discrepancy.

11. **Why did Bulletin No. 214 cite "two tenths" depression when the actual bulletin said "not more than five hundredths"?** Multiple sources misquote or misremember this figure. Were the retellings working from a different edition of the bulletin, or did misreporting compound over time?

12. **Why were the three explanations (water, theft, hay) presented at arbitration without any mention of the defective apparatus, which had been condemned nine months earlier?** Was this a failure of evidence presentation, a deliberate omission, or a misunderstanding of the significance of the condemned crate?

> "I have kept the test book of this station in my own hand since the station opened, and there is not a figure in it that I did not put there. I am an old man and I have no other trade. I have tested as I was taught and as the state man saw me do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, and I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are. ### Merle Strawn on Field Method (Covering Note, 1936)

### Ivy Keddie's Measurement and Interpretation (Talk, 1959)
> "Everything else follows from that line, and I will go through it slowly, because it is arithmetic and not conjuring. A butterfat test is made by drawing a measured charge of milk into a bottle whose neck is ruled to read percent for that charge and no other. Put in a short charge and you get a short reading, in the same proportion. These pipettes delivered 16.72 where they were blown for 17.6. Sixteen point seven two divided by seventeen point six is ninety-five hundredths, exactly. Every test made at that station with that glass read ninety-five percent of the truth."

(On the 1911 mystery solved:)
> "For nine weeks in 1911 there was an experiment at that station, and nobody knew it was one. The station's own measure broke on the eighth of May and a substitute was used until the tenth of July. Same patrons, same herds, same pasture, same season, same acid, same bottles, same man at the crank — one object changed — and the average went to 3.80 and came back to 3.61 the day the station's own measure returned. No explanation of this valley's twenty-six-year anomaly survives those nine weeks except the one I have given you."

(On Ansel Keddie's exoneration:)
> "I have no remedy to propose. I have only the crate, and it is on the table, and you may look into it on your way out."

(On Ansel and Emmet's estrangement:)
> "Before nineteen nineteen those two families ate their Sunday dinners together, I have seen them at it, the two houses in one kitchen and the men out at the fence after. After nineteen nineteen they did not speak. Not one word, not at the store, not at a funeral. Orra would walk down to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane. Years and years those two lived within sight of each other's chimney smoke and never said good morning, and both of them long in the ground now, and for what."

### Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958)
> "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

### Hobart Sill on Cream from Ostrey Hollow (1934)
> "That cream ran heavy. Every year it ran heavy. There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there. The office would send down its sheet and the sheet would say so many pounds of fat bought at the station, and the fat that came into this room off that wagon was more than the sheet said. Always more. Never less."

### Roy Brant on the Weigh Book (1955)
> "The two columns agreed. That is the thing about the book that people do not take in when I tell them, so I will say it slowly. Day after day, month after month, for the whole run of years my father kept it and for the years I have kept it, his weight and the station's weight came out the same, barring a pound or two here and there, and those were days when a can got slopped on the hill. Two scales, his and the station's, kept by two men who by the end were not speaking to one another, and the scales never fell out at all. Whatever was wrong at that station — and something was wrong at that station for a very long time — it was not the weighing."

### Alonzo Frick on the Arbitration (1946)
> "A board of arbitration can only decide between the accounts that are put to it. Three were put to us; each of them was urged by a man of standing; each of them would have been believed by half the valley; and not one of them was proved. I have wondered since whether the answer was in that room and simply was not in any of the three stories, and whether a lawyer who understood chemistry might have found it. I did not understand chemistry. I understood that an honest man was ruined by a figure nobody could explain, and that we paid his neighbors money without being able to tell them what they were being paid for."

### Rosalie Cudd on Jerome Cudd's Test Book (1958)
> "You would have liked the look of it. They started before daylight to have the cans on my platform by seven, because milk that has been four miles down a switchback in a wagon on a July morning is not improving while it stands, and they came in with the horses steaming and the tarps wet with the cloud that sits on the top of the ridge till the sun is well up. One of them brought his boy, who used to sit on my scale platform and be weighed for a joke. That boy is a man in the state road department now and does not remember me."

(On milk quality and cheese:)
> "A cheesemaker knows watered milk inside a day. He does not need a test to know it, though the test will tell him too. The curd comes soft and it will not knit; you get a slack, weak body and your yield is down, and you can stand at the vat and see your money going out with the whey. It cannot be hidden from the man who makes the cheese, because the cheese tells him, and it tells him the next morning, not next year. Butter is more forgiving that way. Cheese is a witness."

### Leland Vaught on the Milk Pipette (1958)
> "The milk pipette is the one instrument in the butterfat test which the plant cannot check against anything else, and it is the instrument on which every figure in the plant depends."

(On error properties:)
> "First, the error is proportional and not additive... Second, the error is precise... Third — and this is the reason the thing ran so long — the error is invisible from inside the plant."

### Nyle Grigg's 1919 Covering Letter
> "I will add, since a man who prints a thing twice ought to say where he stands in it, that I had no interest whatever in the answer. I am not a patron of that station. I haul to Larrow Green and always have, and whatever the Hollow was paid or not paid never put a cent into my pocket nor took one out of it."

(On the college finding:)
> "The State Agricultural College at Farland has since published its Bulletin No. 214, which was a feeding trial upon bog hay carried out under the College's own eye, with weighed rations and no interest in the outcome. That trial found the bog hay to depress the butter fat test by two tenths in the winter feeding. Two tenths, gentlemen, out of a College that had never heard of Hessel Bottom and had no reason on earth to flatter us."

### Dr. Roland Kepp on Wet Ground and Milk (1926)
> "A wet bottom is not a rich meadow with too much water in it. It is a distinct kind of ground with a distinct kind of chemistry, and the water is the least part of it."

(On the cow and minerals:)
> "A cow in milk is a manufactory working on a narrow margin, and she is obliged to find the material of her milk somewhere. If her ration is short of the minerals, she does not simply give less; she robs her own frame to make good."

(On the fat:)
> "They will milk, gentlemen. Wet land is generous of flow and no man on a bog meadow ever complained that his pail was light. But what is in the pail is another matter, and the fat is the first thing to suffer, because the fat is the dearest thing the cow makes and it is the first economy she practices when she is pressed."

## Summary of the Case

The Ostrey Hollow scandal was ultimately not a scandal at all, but a 26-year-long (or 1897-1923, approximately 26 years) measurement error caused by defective glass apparatus, compounded by a social tragedy created by misattribution of blame.

**The Facts:**
- Test pipettes were short by 5% (16.72 c.c. or 16.27 c.c. vs. 17.6 c.c., depending on source).
- This caused all tests at Ostrey Hollow to read 5% low, from May 1, 1897 (or 1898) to June 12, 1923.
- Patrons were systematically underpaid by approximately $19,000 over 25 years (about $760/year for nine patrons, or higher if 67,000 lb figure is used).
- The award of $3,800 (1925) was roughly one-fifth of the actual loss (or 6% if actual loss was $67,000).

**The Social Tragedy:**
- The Association and its manager (Selby Vose) blamed the patrons for adding water to cans (no evidence found by Merle Strawn in repeated visits over 28+ years).
- The station operator (Ansel Keddie) was dismissed despite the board finding "no cause established."
- His wife Orra, sister of the largest patron (Emmet Brant), suffered estrangement from her brother for decades.
- Ansel Keddie died in 1934 (or "the thirties") without vindication.
- Vindication came in October 1958—24+ years after his death—when Bulletin No. 471 exonerated him completely.

**The Archive and Evidence:**
- The condemned crate of pipettes, found in 1958 (in abandoned station loft or at Association office), provided physical evidence.
- Measurement in March 1958 by Ivy Keddie (Ansel's granddaughter) proved the apparatus was the cause.
- The 1911 anomaly (nine weeks when a borrowed, true measure was used; tests rose from 3.61 to 3.80) provided the strongest historical evidence.
- Merle Strawn's field notes confirmed no water contamination in repeated visits over 28+ years.
- The Alder Corners parallel showed Hessel Bottom milk testing true (~3.80) at an independent location over Redlow Ridge.
- Hobart Sill's observation that cream from Ostrey Hollow always arrived at Larrow Green heavier than credited supported the apparatus error theory.
- The "station gain" account line in Wilbur Teague's ledgers (5.25% consistent year after year) aligned with the 5% pipette error.

**Key Insight:**
Effie Loomis, the secretary, observed: "Forty years of taking down what men say has taught me that the interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying." The condemned crate was never mentioned in the 1925 hearing, the 1889–1892 minute book was lost, and the 1911 mystery went unexplained for 47 years. Yet the physical evidence and the contemporaneous field notes—carefully recorded by Merle Strawn and preserved in a library, measured by Ivy Keddie against a burette—told the true story.

Alonzo Frick reflected: "It is not complicated. Nobody in that valley lacked the intelligence to find it. What they lacked was any occasion to suspect the one article in the plant that nobody in the trade is in the habit of suspecting."

The milk pipette is the one instrument that cannot be checked against anything else. It is the standard by which all other tests are judged. An instrument that is itself the measure cannot be caught by measuring with it. It could only be caught by a burette brought in from outside—and burettes reached the state's field men very late in this history.

## Critical Discrepancies and Notes for Future Readers

1. **Pipette delivery measurement:** Ivy Keddie measured mean of 16.72 c.c.; Bulletin No. 471 states 16.27 c.c. Both support ~5% error.

2. **Pipette count:** Most sources say 9 pipettes unbroken; Dell Rundle's 1959 footnote says 11.

3. **Equipment date:** Test book begun May 4, 1896; invoice April 14, 1897; in use from May 1, 1897 (per earlier notes) or May 1, 1898 (per Bulletin 471).

4. **Butter fat loss:** Calculated 76,000 lb (earlier notes); Bulletin 471 says 76,000 lb; Vaught's calculation suggests 118,000 lb; Frick notes patrons claimed 67,000 lb.

5. **Financial loss:** $19,000 (earlier notes), $19,600 (Rundle), $67,000 (per Frick's account of patrons' claim).

6. **Test average at Ostrey Hollow:** 3.61 (most sources), 3.16 (Alonzo Frick's account), 3.42 (1910 year per Roy Brant).

7. **Bulletin No. 214 effect:** Actual text says "not more than five hundredths" (0.05); Grigg and Kepp both cite "two tenths" (0.20)—significant discrepancy.

8. **Nyle Grigg's sampling:** His letter says "whole of 1908 — every month"; actual report says "January, February and March"—contradiction.

9. **Gap between Ostrey Hollow and rest of valley:** 0.17 (per Roy Brant), 0.19 (Alder Corners comparison), 0.20 (Kepp's "fifth of a point"), 0.21 (Kepp's direct figure).
