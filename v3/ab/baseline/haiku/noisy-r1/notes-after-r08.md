# Comprehensive Notes: Retellings 1–8 (Ostrey Hollow Dairy Station Case)

## Central Narrative

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, organized in 1889 and incorporated as a dairy marketing cooperative in the Larrow Valley, operated three facilities: a main creamery at Larrow Green (built 1891), an office at Ashlin (county seat), and a skimming station at the head of Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green.

From 1897 to 1923, the Ostrey Hollow station was operated by Ansel Keddie, a careful tester who maintained a handwritten test book recording milk delivered by farmers, pounds, butterfat test percentage, and calculated butterfat. The cream tested at the station was separated and sent daily down to Larrow Green for further processing.

In 1925, patrons of Ostrey Hollow mounted a petition claiming they had been systematically underpaid for their milk over many years. A board of arbitration sat at Ashlin from March 17 to May 4, 1925, hearing all parties. The board declared "no cause established" but awarded $3,800 "without admission" to the Ostrey Hollow patrons. Ansel Keddie was dismissed on April 30, 1925. He died in 1934 without vindication.

In 1958, Ivy Keddie (his granddaughter, then attending State Agricultural College at Farland) discovered a wooden crate in the abandoned station loft containing the condemned test pipettes. Measuring them against a calibrated burette on March 9, 1958, she found they delivered only 16.72 c.c. instead of their stated 17.6 c.c.—95 hundredths of the mark. This systematic shortfall of 5% explains all the recorded discrepancies. Bulletin No. 471, issued by the College in October 1958, declared Keddie innocent: no water was added, no cream was stolen; the tests were simply low because the apparatus was short.

### The Shortfall Calculation
- **Period:** May 1, 1897 to June 12, 1923 (26+ years)
- **Total milk intake (1898–1922, 25 full years):** 40,000,000 pounds
- **Annual average milk intake:** 1,600,000 pounds/year
- **Recorded butterfat percentage:** 3.61
- **True butterfat percentage (calculated):** 3.80
- **Recorded butterfat credited to patrons:** 1,444,000 pounds (40M × 0.0361)

- **Difference (station gain):** 76,000 pounds of butterfat
- **Value at average price of 25¢/pound:** $19,000
- **Arbitration award:** $3,800 (approximately 20% of actual loss)
- **Percentage of fat lost:** 5% (consistent with pipette shortfall of 16.72/17.6 = 95 hundredths, meaning 5% low)

## Key Conflicts and Oddities

### 1. The "No Cause Established" Paradox
The board of arbitration found "no cause established" yet awarded $3,800 "without admission" to the Ostrey Hollow patrons. Effie Loomis, who recorded the award in the minute book, explicitly notes: "I took it down as it was given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once."

This formulation suggests the board either:
- Found the cause but would not name it, or
- Made a settlement to keep peace despite lack of proof

### 2. Dismissed Without Cause
Ansel Keddie was dismissed on April 30, 1925—the same day or days after the board found "no cause established." He had worked for 29 seasons without documented complaint of dishonesty. He died in 1934 without learning he was vindicated in 1958.

### 3. The 1911 Mystery, Unresolved for 47 Years
When the station's measure broke on May 8, 1911, a borrowed one was used until July 10, 1911. During those nine weeks, tests rose from 3.61 to 3.80. When the station's own measure was reinstalled, tests dropped back to 3.61 immediately.

Ansel Keddie marked this in his test book with a question mark (July 10 entry: "Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."). In his unsent letter of spring 1925, he wrote: "Those nine weeks in 1911 are the strangest thing in my book and I have never had an account of them to give... That question mark is mine and I have let it stand for fourteen years. I could not answer it then and I cannot answer it now."

The answer was only provided in Bulletin No. 471 (1958): the borrowed measure was true; the station's own pipettes were short by 5%. This had been the case since 1897, making 1911 a brief window into the true test values.

### 4. Missing Official Records
The 1889–1892 minute book (covering the Association's founding and first four annual meetings) was lost in the 1938 office move. It "was not thrown out; it was carried out with the rest and it did not arrive" (Effie Loomis). Thus, no first-hand record exists of what was discussed or resolved at the founding.

Further, the crate of condemned pipettes was never mentioned in the 1925 arbitration hearing, despite being impounded by Merle Strawn on June 12, 1923 (four months before the petition was filed, two years before the hearing). Effie Loomis notes: "The second is the crate. The crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all... Not a word of it was spoken in any of the three sessions, by the patrons, by the officers, or by the board."

### 5. Article VII's Unappealable Clause
Article VII (adopted 1892) states: "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." This clause, written by Gideon Vose and adopted before most living people were born, meant that patrons had no recourse if they believed the station's test was wrong. They could not request a re-test or appeal to Larrow Green's standards.

Effie Loomis observes: "That clause is the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business and it was written in 1892 by a man who died in 1917 and could not be asked about it afterward."

### 6. Ansel's Careful Procedure Did Not Prevent Systematic Error
Ansel Keddie's unsent letter provides a detailed account of his testing procedure:
- Samples warmed to blood temperature
- Cream mixed down through the sample by pouring vessel to vessel
- Milk drawn to the mark on the pipette stem
- Tip wiped
- Charge blown into numbered bottle
- Acid poured down the side
- Milk turned dark with acid
- Four bottles weighted against each other in hand tester
- Crank turned at steady speed
- Fat read off in neck with dividers
- Figure written down

Merle Strawn watched him perform tests and reported: "He works slow and clean and reads his own figure without being coaxed to it." Yet a systematic 5% error persisted for 26 years. The error was in the apparatus (short pipettes), not the procedure.

### 7. The Estrangement of Ansel and Emmet Through Orra
Ansel Keddie married Orra Keddie, who was the sister of Emmet Brant, the largest patron at Ostrey Hollow station.

Before 1919: "those two families ate their Sunday dinners together, I have seen them at it, the two houses in one kitchen and the men out at the fence after."

After 1919 (the circular): "they did not speak. Not one word, not at the store, not at a funeral. Orra would walk down to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane."

Vira Toland sums up the tragedy: "Years and years those two lived within sight of each other's chimney smoke and never said good morning, and both of them long in the ground now, and for what."

The 1919 circular, sent by manager Selby Vose to warn patrons about water in cans, implicitly accused the station (Ansel Keddie) and by extension his wife's family.

### 8. Vose's Contradictory Positions
In his 1926 address, Selby Vose claims that someone at Ostrey Hollow was adding water to the cans and stopped only under Association scrutiny in 1924. He says: "Water was going into cans at the head of that valley and it stopped when the Association began to look."

However, Merle Strawn's field notes state clearly: "I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any" (1919 note). And in subsequent visits (1920, 1923, 1924, 1925), Strawn continued to find "no added water."

Vose defends his 1919 circular: "I wrote it because it was my duty to write it... I wish only to have it understood, once, in the hearing of the whole membership, why that station's figures were what they were and why they are what they are now."

But the actual cause (short pipettes) had nothing to do with added water. The apparent improvement in 1924 (tests rising 21 hundredths) was due to new pipettes being installed in July 1923, not behavioral change.

### 9. The "Station Gain" Account
Wilbur Teague created the "station gain" line in his ledger in 1905 as a simple accounting device: the difference between fat credited to patrons (on the station's own test) and fat actually received at Larrow Green.

The typical entry reads:
> "Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual."

This 5.25% gain appeared year after year with remarkable consistency. Teague notes: "A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture" (i.e., invisible to the eye because of its regularity).

Teague raised this with Vose in 1916. Vose told him to let it alone. Teague obeyed. But Vose signed the annual sheets each year, meaning he saw the pattern and chose not to investigate.

The "station gain" of 76,000 lb of fat over 25 years represents exactly the 5% shortfall from the defective pipettes.

### 10. The Hazel Brant Connection and the College Girl
A young college woman (described as Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed) visited Vira Toland in 1958 asking about the case. Vira Toland told her about the 1919 letter in her father's desk drawer.

The exact relationship between this girl and the various Brants is unclear, but it appears she was part of a later effort to research and understand the case—possibly connected to Ivy Keddie's work or conducted in parallel.

### 11. Merle Strawn's Thoroughness and Powerlessness
Merle Strawn made 20+ visits to Ostrey Hollow over 28 years (1907–1936), consistently testing for water and finding none. He was thorough: "I did not put down what I thought about it... The state pays a field man to weigh and to look... I have been asked once or twice... to say in a general way how a certain plant stood in my estimation... I do not keep an estimation, I keep a book."

Yet he had no authority to order a second set of measurements or a re-audit. He could only condemn faulty apparatus (which he did on June 12, 1923) and recommend replacement.

### 12. The Alder Corners Parallel
The Alder Corners Cheese Company, located over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County, tested milk from some Hessel Bottom patrons. Their test book (never left Vessey County; copies obtained 1958) showed that the same milk tested approximately 0.19 higher at Alder Corners than at Ostrey Hollow.

This 19-hundredth gap is the exact shortfall predicted by the 5% error in the Ostrey Hollow pipettes (3.61 × 1.05 ≈ 3.80, a gap of 0.19).

## Unanswered Questions Across Sources

1. **Exactly when did Ansel Keddie die?** Vira Toland says "in the thirties." Ivy Keddie says "1934." Bulletin No. 471 would have been written with knowledge of his death; it is published in October 1958.

2. **Did the board of arbitration know about the condemned crate?** The crate was condemned by Merle Strawn on June 12, 1923, and was in the Association's office (presumably known to the office staff) when the board sat in March–May 1925. Yet no mention appears in the minute book of the hearing.

3. **What happened to the 1889–1892 minute book after it left the office?** It was "carried out" during the 1938 move and "did not arrive." Effie Loomis says "it is not there" (in the building) but does not say whether a search was ever made elsewhere.

4. **Why did Vose sign the annual sheets each year if he believed there was fraud or negligence?** His 1926 address claims he suspected water was being added, yet he re-signed the "station gain" accounts each year without raising the issue until pressed by the petitioners in 1925.

5. **What was the relationship between Rosalie Cudd and Emmet/Hazel Brant?** She is described as Jerome Cudd's niece and as running the Alder Corners Cheese Company. Did she have family ties to Hessel Bottom?

6. **What became of the young college woman who visited Vira Toland in 1958?** Vira notes she "was Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed, so she was of the family both sides in a manner of speaking." Was she involved in the later research or publication efforts?

7. **Did anyone formally apologize to Ansel Keddie's descendants or clear his name publicly during his lifetime or shortly after his death?** The 1958 bulletin exonerates him, but he had been dead for at least 24 years.

8. **How did the apparatus shortfall go undetected for 26 years before Merle Strawn used a burette in 1921–1923?** The burette program was new (started 1921); before that, field inspectors checked apparatus only visually or by standard procedures, not by precision measurement.

### Ivy Keddie's Measurement and Interpretation (Talk, 1959)
> "Everything else follows from that line, and I will go through it slowly, because it is arithmetic and not conjuring. A butterfat test is made by drawing a measured charge of milk into a bottle whose neck is ruled to read percent for that charge and no other. Put in a short charge and you get a short reading, in the same proportion. These pipettes delivered 16.72 where they were blown for 17.6. Sixteen point seven two divided by seventeen point six is ninety-five hundredths, exactly. Every test made at that station with that glass read ninety-five percent of the truth."

(On the 1911 mystery solved:)
> "For nine weeks in 1911 there was an experiment at that station, and nobody knew it was one. The station's own measure broke on the eighth of May and a substitute was used until the tenth of July. Same patrons, same herds, same pasture, same season, same acid, same bottles, same man at the crank — one object changed — and the average went to 3.80 and came back to 3.61 the day the station's own measure returned. No explanation of this valley's twenty-six-year anomaly survives those nine weeks except the one I have given you."

(On Ansel Keddie's exoneration:)
> "I have no remedy to propose. I have only the crate, and it is on the table, and you may look into it on your way out."

### Vira Toland on the 1919 Letter (1979)
> "A letter came from the Association, printed, over the manager's signature, and it said there was water in the cans coming down from Ostrey Hollow. That is what it said. Water in the cans. It went to nine families and my father was one of the nine and he read it at the table with his hat still on, which he never did... Some of them kept theirs till they died. ### Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958)
> "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

## Summary of the Case

The Ostrey Hollow scandal was ultimately not a scandal at all, but a 26-year-long measurement error caused by defective glass apparatus, compounded by a social tragedy created by misattribution of blame.

**The Facts:**
- Test pipettes were short by 5% (16.72 c.c. vs. 17.6 c.c.).
- This caused all tests at Ostrey Hollow to read 5% low, from May 1, 1897 to June 12, 1923.
- Patrons were systematically underpaid by approximately $19,000 over 25 years (about $760/year for nine patrons).
- The award of $3,800 (1925) was roughly one-fifth of the actual loss.

**The Social Tragedy:**
- The Association and its manager (Selby Vose) blamed the patrons for adding water to cans (no evidence found by Merle Strawn in repeated visits).
- The station operator (Ansel Keddie) was dismissed despite the board finding "no cause established."
- His wife Orra, sister of the largest patron (Emmet Brant), suffered estrangement from her brother for decades.
- Ansel Keddie died in 1934 without vindication.
- Vindication came in October 1958—24 years after his death—when Bulletin No. 471 exonerated him completely.

**The Archive:**
- The condemned crate of pipettes, found in 1958, provided physical evidence.
- Measurement in March 1958 by Ivy Keddie (Ansel's granddaughter) proved the apparatus was the cause.
- The 1911 anomaly (nine weeks at 3.80 when a borrowed, true measure was used) provided the strongest historical evidence.
- Merle Strawn's field notes confirmed no water contamination in repeated visits.
- The Alder Corners parallel showed Hessel Bottom milk testing true at an independent location.

**Key Insight:**
Effie Loomis, the secretary, observed: "Forty years of taking down what men say has taught me that the interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying." The condemned crate was never mentioned in the 1925 hearing, the 1889–1892 minute book was lost, and the 1911 mystery went unexplained for 47 years. Yet the physical evidence and the contemporaneous field notes—carefully recorded by Merle Strawn and preserved in a library—told the true story.
