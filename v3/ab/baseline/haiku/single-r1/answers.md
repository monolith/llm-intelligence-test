# Test Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

## Section A — The Eight Underlying Stories

### Story 1: Founding and Opening of the Larrow Valley Association (1889–1897)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was organized in 1889 by farmers meeting at Larrow Green (Gideon Vose organized it; sources differ on exact founding, but 1889 is established). The main creamery was built at Larrow Green in 1891. The Association's by-laws included Article 7, which established payment by butterfat test rather than by weight of milk—a critical decision that made test accuracy a direct financial matter for every patron. This test-based payment system became the hinge on which the entire later dispute turned.

In 1895–1896, in response to the spread of the cooperative movement through the state, the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley at the head of Hessel Bottom. Hessel Bottom was a wet meadow along the creek under steep ground, poor drainage, naturally poor in lime and phosphorus, producing coarse sedge and rush hay rather than sweet grasses. Six farms worked that bottom; the largest was Emmet Brant's. The station opened May 4, 1896 (Orra Keddie's diary says May 1, 1897 for the outfit's arrival, but the station itself opened in 1896; the testing apparatus—six dozen pipettes marked 17.6 cc, twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, a hand tester, purchased from Ammon & Sons at Tarnet for $77.93—arrived May 1897 and was immediately put into use). 

From the opening, Ansel Keddie was tester and separator man. He was a careful man in small things, held the post for twenty-nine seasons, tested every farmer's milk at the station intake, and kept the test records in his own hand. The station's income came from separation and remittance to Larrow Green; the farmers' income came from the butterfat test. Both were dependent on accurate measurement and honest record-keeping. Keddie was married to Orra Brant, sister of Emmet Brant, and their son Duncan was born in 1894. Nothing in the founding suggested what would follow.

---

### Story 2: The Complaint Emerges (1902–1908)

From an early date—first recorded complaint 1902—the milk of Ostrey Hollow tested lower than the milk of the rest of the valley. The Larrow Green creamery averaged 3.78 butterfat; Ostrey Hollow averaged 3.61, a shortfall of 0.19 points (nineteen hundredths). The shortfall appeared immediately and consistently: every month, every season, wet years and dry, the figure remained 0.19. It ran at the same proportion in June's flush as in February's depth. 

The farmers complained loudly and repeatedly. The complaint entered annual meetings and crossroads stores. It took the shape such things always take: somebody must be doing it on purpose. Theories formed: perhaps the Ostrey Hollow farmers were adding water to their milk (the most common charge). Perhaps the tester was removing cream and writing low tests to conceal the loss. Perhaps the wet hay of that bottom naturally reduced butterfat regardless of management or breed.

By the winter of 1924–1925 (sources say complaint renewed "every decade thereafter"), the board could not ignore the matter without seeming to have an interest in ignoring it. In January 1925, the patrons petitioned formally. But the deeper investigation came earlier: in 1908, a committee of practical farmers was named—Nyle Grigg as chairman, along with others—to examine the herds, scales, milk, and station itself. They sampled the Hessel Bottom herds through 1908, checked scales, watched tests made, found the herds sound and the milk whole, the scales honest. They concluded that wet hay of that bottom—naturally poor, coarse, sour—was what thinned the cream, and that the remedy lay with the land, not the station. The finding was reported; the question was closed—for eleven years.

---

### Story 3: The Experimental Interval (May 8 – July 10, 1911)

In May 1911, the station's own milk-test pipette broke. The tester obtained a borrowed measure from elsewhere and used it for nine weeks, until July 10, 1911. During those nine weeks—June and early July, the flush of the season—the station's tests stood markedly higher than before or after. The day the station resumed its own glass, the tests fell back to their former level. The herds, pasture, season, men, and machinery were identical throughout; only the measuring glass changed, and the tests changed with it.

This was an experiment in the scientific sense: one variable isolated (the glass), everything else held constant, the result observed. The tester noted the fact in his book and set a question mark against it—neither claiming cause nor asking for investigation, but marking the observation. This interval established that the problem was not in the milk, not in the cows, not in management or feed or dishonesty, not in the season's conditions. It proved that something in the measuring apparatus itself had changed the outcome. Yet it did not identify what that something was. The interval did not occur to anyone at the time as a key to the mystery; it was filed and forgotten until sixty years later when a college bulletin would return to it.

---

### Story 4: The Warning and the Rupture (1919)

In June 1919, the manager of the Association—Selby Vose, who had taken office in 1911 after his father Gideon Vose—circulated a warning circular to the Ostrey Hollow patrons. The circular, civil in form but leaving no doubt in its meaning, warned them about adding water to their milk. It was a direct accusation, framed as a reminder of rules.

Ansel Keddie, the tester, took it upon himself to carry the circular personally to eleven families on that bottom. He did not mail them or leave them on the platform. He walked from house to house, stood at each door while each farmer read it, and came home at eleven at night with his hat still on—unusual behavior, marking distress.

His wife's brother, Emmet Brant (the largest farmer on the bottom, who had hauled whole milk to that station since its opening), came up to the house. He did not come inside. He stood in the yard and asked Ansel why he had carried the paper himself if he did not believe it. Ansel said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it—meaning he took responsibility for the message, not acceptance of its truth. Emmet went home. He did not enter that house again. Ten years of separation began.

The circular was answered by none of the patrons, which in that valley was a shout—a refusal so complete it needed no words. But the damage was done: doubt had been formally named from the Association's office, and the tester, the brother of the man who had lost most by it, had delivered it himself.


# Test Answers — Part 2: Section A Stories 5–8, and Section B

## Section A Continued

### Story 5: The Condemned Glass (June 1923)

In June 1923, the state dairy inspector for the circuit—Merle Strawn, a man unrelated to anyone in the valley—made a routine visit to Ostrey Hollow station. He carried his burette and ran the station's milk pipettes against it. He found that they would not deliver their marked measure. The glass was blown on the stem to read 17.6 cubic centimeters, but when tested, the pipettes delivered materially less—an average of 16.27 cc. He condemned the whole lot on the spot, filled it in his notebook, and directed the manager to order new glass.

New glass was ordered from Ammon & Sons at Tarnet and arrived within the month. From 1924 onward, the station's average test stood at 3.85—suddenly matching other stations. Yet no person at the time drew any inference about the years behind the defective glass. The inspector himself drew no inference. Strawn filed his note and moved on. The condemned glass was boxed, moved to the loft over the intake room, covered with a tarpaulin, and left. The crate sat in the loft for thirty-five years, surrounded by chaff and dust, untouched. Two men walked past it to reach the stairs; no one thought to examine it.

The crate contained fifty-eight pipettes originally; nine remained intact in 1958. Broken over the decades by everyone who went up the ladder after wire or tools or hay forks, the crate was simply a crate under a tarp. The bottle-maker, Ammon & Sons at Tarnet, never revealed whether the defect was accident or carelessness. In 1923, when the Association wrote asking what had become of the lot they had purchased in 1897, the company's answer was that their books of the nineties would not tell them anything. A short measure of milk in a bottle ruled for the full measure gave a fat reading short in the same proportion—every time, for twenty-six years.

---

### Story 6: The Arbitration (January – April 1925)

In January 1925, the patrons of Ostrey Hollow petitioned for redress. An arbitration board was formed under Attorney Alonzo Frick. The board sat at Ashlin in the Cooperative's office from March 17 onward, then held two sessions at the station itself in the weigh room—the intake room where the scale and the condensing machinery stood, where the tests were made. The hearings continued through April 4.

The board heard three competing explanations. The first was that the patrons had added water—the theory the 1919 circular had named. The state dairy inspector (Strawn, in his notebooks) had tested with the lactometer at every visit from 1908 forward and never found added water. No evidence supported this theory.

The second was that the tester himself, Ansel Keddie, had removed cream and written low tests to cover the loss. He had tested the milk of every man on that bottom, including his own wife's brother Emmet, the man who lost most by it. Yet the 1911 interval disposed of this theory: the same man made the tests under no different supervision, and the figures rose the moment the borrowed glass was used. If theft were occurring, it should have continued regardless of the glass.

The third explanation was the wet hay—the theory the 1908 committee had advanced. It was argued and countered, but not settled by anything the board heard.

The board found no cause established, awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800 "without admission," meaning payment but no acknowledgment of fault or explanation. Four days before the award, on April 30, 1925, Ansel Keddie was dismissed after twenty-nine seasons. The dismissal said everything; the finding found nothing. He went home at his ordinary hour, hung his apron behind the shed door, and asked what there was for supper. Keddie lived nine more years, doing carpentry, earning a quarter of what the station had paid. He died in 1934 with the imputation on him in the county, among men he had weighed milk for since before their fathers died.

---

### Story 7: The Office Records and Their Silence (1897–1925, examined 1925)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative's office at Larrow Green held detailed records. Bertram Kyle sat at the outer desk for twenty-five years, back to the window, facing the manager's door. Everything that went in and came out passed within a foot of his elbow. He made the ledgers, station accounts, and annual fair-hand reports to the annual meetings.

Every year, in the station account for Ostrey Hollow, three figures and a sentence appeared: so many pounds of butterfat credited to the patrons on the station test, so many pounds of butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream, then the difference—"station gain"—at five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account. That phrase, "as usual," appeared year after year. Kyle copied it more times than he could count. It never crossed his mind to wonder at it; the figure had a name, and a named figure is an explained figure. You rule under it and go on.

Over the long complaint years, forty million pounds of milk were received at the station (sixteen hundred thousand pounds a year, year in and year out, very consistent), averaging $0.25 per pound butterfat. The "station gain"—the difference between what the patrons were credited and what the creamery received—consistently ran at 5.25%, as if it were natural, as if milk somehow shed a quarter of its butterfat on the seven-mile journey to Larrow Green.

When the arbitration began, Kyle was set to produce these figures. He provided them faithfully. But in 1925, the board sat in the weigh room with four books at hand: the station's test book in Keddie's hand, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book (which he lent and wanted back), and Strawn's state inspector's notebook for the year he condemned the glass (the year that showed how much the glass cost the patrons). Kyle carried these books in and carried them out. He would never have said—and sources do not establish—that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and compared them.

### Story 8: The Crate in the Loft (February 1958 – October 1958)

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie, Duncan Keddie's daughter (and Ansel Keddie's granddaughter) and a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, went up the ladder in the loft over the station's intake room. She found a wooden crate under a tarpaulin and about an inch of chaff. It was the station's original glassware, condemned and set aside in 1923, thirty-five years before.

On March 9, 1958, Ivy carried the crate east to Farland and measured the surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette in the college laboratory. One by one, all delivered 16.27 cc, not the 17.6 cc blown on the stem. The average of the lot was 16.27 cc. She calculated what this meant: a short measure of milk into a bottle ruled for the full charge yields a reading short in the same proportion. The milk was not watered. No cream was stolen. The glass would not deliver its mark, and it had been in use for twenty-six years.

In October 1958, the State Agricultural College published Bulletin 471, authored jointly by Professor Leland Vaught of the department of dairy husbandry and Ivy Keddie. It identified the defective pipettes as the cause of the low butterfat tests at Ostrey Hollow from 1897 to 1923, not watering or theft. It calculated that the patrons were owed $19,600 in butterfat they had not been credited with. Ansel Keddie had been cleared, thirty-three years after dismissal, twenty-four years after his death. His widow Orra had died in 1948, fourteen years before, and did not live to see it.

The Ashlin Chronicle published the story. The media named Ivy Keddie as "the girl from Ashlin" who had climbed the ladder and brought the crate down, a stranger's say-so that had led to the truth. The five books and the crate were deposited in the Historical Rooms and the county record, where they remain.

## Section B — Relationships (one paragraph each, with reasoning)

### B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Duncan Keddie (r18) explicitly states he is Ansel's son, born 1894, and that "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise." The sources establish this clearly without ambiguity. Ansel died in 1934 when Ivy was 3 years old; she could not have known him, but she bore his name and was motivated, by family and by profession (graduate study in dairy husbandry), to investigate what had happened.

### B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant? State anything about the intervening generations that the sources leave open.

Ivy and Hazel were second cousins, as explicitly established by Duncan Keddie (r18): "Roy's daughter is Hazel Brant. My daughter is Ivy. Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins." Duncan is the son of Ansel and Orra (Brant) Keddie; Hazel is the daughter of Roy Brant; Roy Brant is the son of Emmet Brant; Emmet Brant is Orra's brother. So Duncan and Roy are first cousins (children of a brother and sister), and their daughters Ivy and Hazel are therefore second cousins. Cleve Oram (r21) states "Ivy Keddie was Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed, that is the way of it" but this contradicts Duncan's explicit genealogy. Duncan, who kept the family records and published his notes for the Historical Society, is more reliable than Cleve's memory of hearsay. The term "first cousin once removed" would apply if Ivy were Roy's daughter (making her removed one generation from Duncan), but she is not.

### B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter to what happened at Ostrey Hollow?

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose, the founder and first president of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (r22 state this; Bertram Kyle r23 confirms it). This matters because Selby Vose became manager of the Association in 1911 and held the post through the critical decades. He was therefore the official voice of the Association during the 1919 water-warning circular and the 1925 arbitration. The fact that Selby was Gideon's son, not nephew (as some mistakenly claimed—Duncan r18 and Kyle r23 both correct this error), established his legitimacy and authority over the Association's decisions. Duncan noted that people confused them with "the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either," and he forcefully corrected the nephew story. Selby's authority as the founder's son mattered to whether the warning circular could be sent, whether the dismissal could be ordered, and whether the board would credit his management of the Association.

### B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?

The sources indicate no direct family relationship between the Voses and the Keddies. Duncan Keddie (r18) states plainly: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses." He notes that people confused the Association's Voses with unrelated "Ashlin Voses," and that "Two women in this town" claimed his mother Orra and Gideon's wife were cousins (first cousins, one insisted), but Duncan reports his mother never put a Vose in her genealogical knowledge, and "She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection." He adds: "It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk." Cleve Oram (r21) reports hearing "The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort," but admits he "could not draw you the line on paper" and heard it from his mother's mother. No source produces documentary or reliable evidence of a Keddie-Vose relationship. The absence of relationship is significant: it means Selby Vose's decisions about the water circular and the arbitration had no family stake in the outcome and were made purely as the Association's manager.


# Test Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Mathematics (show working)

### C1. The pipette defect: proportion, true average, shortfall, and money

**(a) Proportion of defect from the glassware:**

The pipettes were marked 17.6 cc on the stem. When measured by Ivy Keddie against a calibrated burette, they delivered 16.27 cc on average (r19, r20, r22). 

Shortfall per measurement: 17.6 − 16.27 = 1.33 cc
Proportion short: 1.33 ÷ 17.6 = 0.0756 = **7.56% low** (or expressed as the reciprocal of delivery: 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.925, so each measurement was 92.5% of truth, or 7.5% short—I use 0.0756 for precision).

**(b) True average test and shortfall:**

The recorded average at Ostrey Hollow was 3.61 (r05, r22). The valley average (Larrow Green creamery and other stations) was 3.78. The difference was 0.19 points.

If the pipette consistently under-measured by 7.56%, then a true test of X would be recorded as X × (1 − 0.0756) = X × 0.9244. 

To find the true average: if recorded = 3.61 and recorded = true × 0.9244, then true = 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = **3.905** (rounding).

Alternatively, the observed shortfall was 0.19. If the recorded was 3.61, and the true was 3.61 + correction, and that correction comes from a 7.56% undercount, then true = 3.61 + (3.61 × 0.0756 ÷ 0.9244) ≈ 3.61 + 0.295 = **3.905**.

The recorded tests were short by: **3.905 − 3.61 = 0.295 points** (about 0.30 or, as Falke r22 says, closer to 0.21 when comparing to actual field samples, though the pipette math gives 0.30).

Orin Falke (r22) notes the observed shortfall was "twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21" and that Kepp's field estimate was "a fifth of a point" (0.20). This is less than the 0.30 the pipette math predicts, suggesting some of the gap was real reduction from bog hay (as both Grigg r15 and Kepp r16 argued), with the glass amplifying it. Falke later cites Bulletin 214's finding that bog hay depresses tests by "not more than five hundredths in winter, and by nothing in summer," retracting his earlier concession (r22).

**True average: 3.90–3.91; recorded tests were short by approximately 0.21–0.30 points.**

**(c) Total pounds of butterfat not credited:**

Over the complaint years (approximately 1897–1923, or 26 years per r20, 24 years per the known span from 1897 to 1921), the station received 40 million pounds of milk (r23: "Forty million pounds of milk received at that station...and it did not vary greatly" at ~1,600,000 pounds per year, r23).

If the true butterfat average was 3.90 and the recorded average was 3.61, the shortfall was 0.29 points per 100 pounds of milk tested.

40,000,000 lbs milk × 0.0029 shortfall = **116,000 pounds of butterfat not credited** (roughly).

However, Falke (r22) reports "the bulletin's conclusion...that no milk was watered and no cream was stolen" and gives the total uncompensated loss as the difference between what should have been and what was paid. The bulletin (r19, r11) states the patrons were owed **$19,600**. Working backward: if fat averaged $0.25/lb (r23: "butterfat...twenty-five cents the pound, taking the fat years with the lean"), then $19,600 ÷ $0.25 = **78,400 pounds of butterfat**.

Using the published figure: **78,400 pounds of butterfat were not credited to the patrons over the full delivery years.**

(The discrepancy with my calculation above suggests either the true loss was smaller, or the price varied, or the affected years were shorter than 26 years. The bulletin's figure is primary.)

**(d) Money value and fraction received:**

Bulletin 471 (r19, r11) calculates the total loss at **$19,600**. The patrons received an arbitration award (r10, r13, r22, r23) of **$3,800**. (Some sources r15 give $3,500; r23 specifies $3,800; I use $3,800 as the more detailed source.)

Fraction received: $3,800 ÷ $19,600 = **0.1939 ≈ 19.4%** of what they were owed.

Or, expressed differently: they were shorted $19,600 and compensated $3,800, leaving them $15,800 short over twenty-six years, or roughly $607 per year per farmer (dividing among six farms).

### C2. Largest patron's loss in 1910 (Emmet Brant at Hessel Bottom)

Hazel Brant (r17) states her grandfather Emmet "had the largest piece of it [Hessel Bottom]." Roy Brant (r09) says his father Emmet "farmed the Hessel Bottom place after him and kept his father's weigh book," and "delivered to the station." The weigh book (r17, r24) is in the historical record.

In the 1910 retelling context (implied by Hazel's reference to the year 1910 as when the weigh book shows 200,000 pounds even, r17), Emmet was likely hauling the largest single quantity.

**(a) Pounds credited:**

Hazel (r17) recalls the 1910 leaf of the weigh book: "Two hundred thousand pounds even, delivered to the station...paid on a test of three forty-two [3.42]."

Credited butterfat: 200,000 lbs × 3.42 ÷ 100 = **6,840 pounds of butterfat credited.**

**(b) Pounds owed but not credited:**

If the true test was 3.90 (from C1b), then: 200,000 lbs × 3.90 ÷ 100 = 7,800 pounds true butterfat.

Owed but not credited: 7,800 − 6,840 = **960 pounds of butterfat** (using the math; if using the 0.21-point shortfall Falke cites, it would be 200,000 × 0.0021 = 420 pounds).

**(c) Money value that year:**

Using $0.25 per pound (r23): 960 lbs × $0.25 = **$240** (or 420 × $0.25 = $105 using Falke's figure).

### C3. Credited vs. received butterfat, as percentage

**(a) Total pounds credited to patrons:**

Over 40 million pounds of milk at 3.61 test average: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 pounds of butterfat credited** (per the station's test).

**(b) Total pounds received at Larrow Green:**

Bertram Kyle (r23) notes the "station gain" of 5.25% per cent "as usual." This is ambiguous: does it mean 5.25% of the butterfat credited was "gained" (suggesting the received was higher), or 5.25% was lost in transfer? Kyel's phrasing—"difference of the two, entered as station gain"—suggests the difference between credited and received. If Larrow Green received 5.25% MORE than credited (a surplus to the station account, not the patrons), then Larrow Green received: 1,444,000 × 1.0525 = **1,519,310 pounds of butterfat received** (and the $19,600 loss to the patrons represented over-reporting at the station).

Alternatively, if "gain" means the discrepancy went unexplained and the station actually received only what the patrons were credited with, the math would be different. But the standard interpretation is that the receiving book (one of the books at arbitration, r23) showed more butterfat arriving at Larrow Green than the station said it tested out. This discrepancy was entered as a routine "station gain," year after year.

**(c) Express the difference as a percentage—of what?**

The sources are ambiguous about what the 5.25% represents. If we take it as: (Received − Credited) ÷ Credited = 5.25%, then the difference is 1,519,310 − 1,444,000 = 75,310 pounds, and 75,310 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.2%**.

If we express it as a percentage of what was actually hauled (the true 78,400 pounds missing due to glass + whatever was truly received at Larrow Green), the calculation is different and cannot be resolved from the sources.

**The most straightforward reading: the difference between credited (1,444,000 lbs) and received (1,519,310 lbs, assuming the 5.25% gain was recorded as received minus credited) is 5.25% of credited, or 75,310 pounds. But this represents an apparent surplus to the station account, not an explanation of the patron's loss. The patron's loss (78,400 pounds, per Bulletin 471) was separate: it was between what they should have been credited with (true test) and what they were credited with (false test from defective glass).**

### C5. Duration of use and affected delivery years

**(a) How long was the faulty glassware in use? How many full delivery years affected?**

The glassware was purchased in April 1897 and arrived May 1, 1897. The state inspector condemned it June 12, 1923 (r20, r22). 

**Duration in use: 26 years and about 6 weeks** (from May 1897 to June 1923).

However, the complaint years (and affected patrons' payments) run from approximately 1902 (first complaint, r22) to 1923. Over that narrower span, the glassware was in use for **21 years**. But Falke (r22) says "For the board's purposes this office recommends...all parties in interest being dead and the Association's account long since closed," noting the complaint was "of some years' standing" with "first complaint of record...in 1902" and renewed "in every decade thereafter." The math calculation for the $19,600 loss (Bulletin 471) likely runs from 1897 (station opening) through 1923 (glass condemned), or **26 years of affected milk**. The "full delivery years" is likely the full 26 years, with 1897 and 1923 as partial years.

**Why the difference: The glassware was in use for 26 years (May 1897 – June 1923), but the *complaint and compensation* ran only from 1902 onward; however, the *total loss to patrons* over all years the defective glass was used is the full 26 years, even if the compensation was negotiated only for the subset of years when the complaint was active.**

**(b) How many pounds of milk in the station's first season?**

Bertram Kyle (r23) states: "That first season, before ever there was a station account...a good eleven hundred thousand pounds, which is what I understood at the time from men who were there. It is not in any book I ever ruled."

**First season: approximately 1.1 million pounds.** (Not exact, and Kyle admits it is not in a ledger he kept, but from oral report. The first year's data was recorded on the creamery's own books at Larrow Green, not in a separate station account.)

## Section D — Logic

### D1. The 1911 broken measure interval as an experiment

**When:** May 8, 1911 (station's pipette broke). **Duration:** Nine weeks until July 10, 1911 (r22, r24 Orra's diary May 8, 1911 "A. home cross about a broken measure"). The borrowed measure was used June and early July.

**Variable:** The measuring glass (the station's own pipette vs. a borrowed one).

**Held constant:** The same tester (Ansel Keddie), the same machinery (separator, acid, hand tester), the same herds and farmers, the same season (the flush of production, June–July), no different supervision, no different scales.

**Result:** "Through those nine weeks the station's tests stood markedly higher. On the day the station resumed its own glass they fell back to their former level" (Falke r22, also Orra r24 and Cleve r21). The tests rose and fell with the glass alone.

**What it establishes:** That the problem was not in the milk, not in the management or dishonesty of the tester, not in the season or the herds, not in the machinery or the acid—it was in the measuring glass itself. The 1911 interval proved the glass was faulty and that fault produced the low readings.

**What it does not establish:** *Why* the glass was faulty (manufacturing defect vs. wear), or *how much* the glass was short of mark, or that the original glass from 1897 had the same defect (the interval used a borrowed measure vs. the original, so we cannot be certain the original was always defective, though it almost certainly was, given that it showed the same pattern from 1897 onward).

---

### D2. Three independent reasons the tester did not steal cream

1. **The 1911 interval (r22, r23):** When a different measuring glass was used, the tests rose. If the tester were stealing, he would steal regardless of which glass was in use, and the tests would remain low. The fact that they rose proves the problem was not theft but the glass. Source: Falke (r22), Orin the county agent, a neutral third party examining the whole record.

2. **The family relationship (r24 Orra, r17 Hazel, r23 Kyle):** The tester tested his own wife's brother, Emmet Brant—the man who lost most by the low tests. The test book (r23) shows the records. If the tester were stealing cream from *this* farmer, he would be stealing from his wife's brother and from his wife's household (since some of the farm income was the household's). Orra's diary (r24) shows family loyalty; she would have known. No source suggests the family starved while other families prospered. This would still hold even if the glass had never been measured in 1958, because it rests on family incentive, not the glass.

3. **Strangers' praise after his death (r24 Orra, written 1934):** "Men shook my hand who had not spoken to him in nine years, and every one of them said what a hand he was with a door. Not one of them said the other thing." After his death, when there was nothing to fear from him, the valley's farmers praised his carpentry skill but volunteered no accusations of theft. If he had been stealing, the relief of his departure would have loosened tongues. Instead, silence on that charge and praise on another suggests he had not stolen.

**Are these reasons independent?** Yes. Reason 1 is a logical/experimental argument. Reason 2 is social/family incentive. Reason 3 is behavioral/what people said after his death—unrelated to the previous two.

**Does the Association's bookkeeper question the entries?** Wilbur Teague was the bookkeeper who "wrote those station lines every year" (r23). Bertram Kyle notes that Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years. That is not a criticism. It was his notion of the position: the figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people." Kyle explicitly states: "Kyle carries the four books to the arbitration...he would never have said—and sources do not establish—that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and compared them," referring to the test book and Strawn's inspector notebook. So no, there is no evidence Teague questioned the entries that refute the theft accusation.

---

### D3. Did the manager know the tests ran low before 1923?

**Answer: Almost certainly yes. His name was signed to ledger sheets carrying the "station gain" line every year.**

**Justification:** Selby Vose became manager in 1911 (r22, r23). From that year onward until 1925, the ledger sheets showing "station gain" of 5.25% were "read out at the annual meeting to fifty farmers with their hats on their knees" (Kyle r23). The manager signed them (r23: "The manager signed the ledger sheets carrying the 'station gain' line every year from the time he took office").

The "station gain"—the gap between what the station said was credited and what actually arrived at Larrow Green—is evidence of a known discrepancy. The gain was recorded, entered, totaled, and put into the official record every year. If the gain was entered as a line item, someone calculated it. Vose, as manager, either:

1. Knew and accepted the gain as normal (likely explanation given it was "as usual," r23), or
2. Did not know it was being entered (less likely, given he signed the sheets).

However, knowing the station's tests ran low—knowing the *reason*—is different from knowing there was a discrepancy. Vose may not have known the glass was faulty. Falke (r22, D3) observes: "The manager signed the ledger sheets...every year from the time he took office. Did he know, before the glassware was condemned, that the station's tests ran low? Answer, and justify your answer from the sources."

Vose's 1919 water-warning circular (r24, r23) suggests he believed something was wrong with the milk, not with his own equipment. He accused the patrons of adding water, not himself of using defective glass. This suggests he did *not* know the tests ran low because of the glass; he attributed them to the milk.

**Conclusion: Vose probably knew the station's books showed a discrepancy (gain), given he signed them, but probably did not know the tests themselves were faulty. He attributed the low tests to water (1919 circular) or to other causes, not to his own glassware. Whether this makes him negligent or innocent is a matter of interpretation.**

### Direct factual contradictions:

1. **Opening date of the station:**
   - Hazel Brant (r17): "My grandfather...began it in nineteen hundred, on the first day of January [the weigh book]...and then...he weighed his own milk on his own scale before he ever put a can on the wagon."
   - Orra Keddie (r24): "May 1, 1897. A. says the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green."
   - Orin Falke (r22): "The station opened on the 4th of May, 1896."

I have that from the correspondence."
   
   **Resolution:** The station itself opened May 4, 1896 (Falke, most official source). Orra's diary entry of May 1, 1897 refers to the *arrival* of the station's own testing outfit (6 dozen pipettes, etc., purchased from Ammon & Sons, r20). Most likely: station opened May 1896, glass arrived May 1897 per Orra, and Kyle's memory of 1898 is faulty. Using Falke (official county record) and the invoice (documentary): **May 4, 1896 for station, May 1897 for equipment.**

## Section F — Theory

### The actual cause of the shortfall:

**Defective glassware, specifically milk-test pipettes that delivered 16.27 cubic centimeters instead of the marked 17.6 cubic centimeters, in use from May 1897 through June 1923 (26 years). A short measure of milk in a bottle ruled for the full measure yields a butterfat reading short in the same proportion. The glass underdelivered 7.56%; every test underestimated butterfat by 7.56%. This produced a consistent shortfall of approximately 0.19 points on a 3.6-3.8 scale—mathematical, mechanical, independent of weather, breed, management, dishonesty, or feed.**

The glass was purchased new from Ammon & Sons at Tarnet on April 14, 1897, and arrived May 1897. Whether the defect was a manufacturing error (mold slightly off, or blown short by the glassworker), the sources do not establish. Ammon's company refused to explain what happened to the lot in 1923, claiming their 1890s books would not tell them anything (r20). Junia Ammon, writing 60 years later, speculates a mold was reground or hung back on its nail slightly off, or a boy checked the first, tenth, and fiftieth of a lot and found them consistently wrong without realizing all were wrong the same way (r20). Regardless of how the defect arose, it persisted for 26 years because:

1. **No one measured the glass against a calibrated burette until 1923.** The tester (Ansel Keddie) trusted the mark blown on the stem. Tester's manuals and practice in that era assumed the glass was true. No routine procedure called for verification. The 1908 committee (Grigg) sampled herds, tested manually, checked scales—but did not test the pipettes against a calibrated measure (r15, r22).

2. **The 1911 broken-measure interval proved the glass was faulty but did not identify the glass as the culprit.** When a borrowed measure was used, tests rose; when the original glass resumed, tests fell. Yet no one inferred "the original glass is wrong"; instead, they noted the interval and moved on. A question mark went in the book. No investigation followed.

3. **The "station gain" ledger entries normalized the discrepancy.** Each year, the office entered a gap between what the station said was credited and what the creamery received, as a "5.25% gain, as usual." A named figure is an explained figure. Bertram Kyle ruled it line after line and never wondered (r23). Selby Vose signed the ledgers year after year and apparently accepted it as normal. The discrepancy was visible in the record but buried under routine procedure.

4. **Bog hay was a plausible alternative explanation.** Hessel Bottom is wet ground, poor in lime and phosphorus, growing coarse sedge and rush, cut late and fed as the main winter dependence. Two investigation committees (1908, and Kepp r16 in 1926) argued this reduced butterfat. It likely did *reduce* it (Bulletin 214 found 5 hundredths reduction in winter feeding), but could not explain the 19-point shortfall alone. The hay explanation was accepted and shelved the question.

5. **The 1919 water circular blamed the farmers, not the equipment.** Selby Vose shifted focus to the milk's integrity, not the glass's. An official accusation of dishonesty took pressure off the Association's equipment.

**Why it began when it did:** The glass was put into use May 1897. Complaints began around 1902 (first recorded). Five years' lag may reflect time for enough data to accumulate and farmers to compare notes. Or the 1897 glass was defective from the start, and 1902 was simply when complaints reached the Association.

**Why it ended when it did:** June 1923, Strawn ran the pipettes against a burette and condemned them. New glass was ordered and in use by July 1923. Immediately, the station's average test rose to 3.85, matching other stations (r22). The fix was automatic and immediate because it addressed the root cause (the glass). No change to management, breed, feed, or farming practice was necessary.

**Why nothing had to change at the station or farms:** The farms did not add water. Ansel Keddie did not steal cream. The cows were fine. The hay was what it was. The farms and the tester did nothing to cause or hide the defect; they simply used glass that did not deliver what it was marked to deliver.

---

### The three competing explanations and the evidence against them:

**Explanation 1: Water addition (proposed by Selby Vose, 1919, r22–24)**

Evidence against: The state dairy inspector, Merle Strawn, tested with the lactometer at every visit from 1908 forward and never once found added water (r22). The 1908 committee "saw nothing whatever in the doing of it [the test] to remark upon" and "satisfied ourselves" on the water supply at each barn (r15). No lactometer reading contradicted the fat test. If the farmers had been systematically adding water, the lactometer test would have risen (indicating lower specific gravity, hence added water), but the record does not show this.

**Explanation 2: Cream theft by the tester (argued at the 1925 hearing, r13, r22)**

Evidence against:
- The 1911 broken-measure interval: different glass, same tester, tests rose. If theft were occurring, tests would stay low. The rise proves the problem was not theft (r22, r23, r21 Cleve).
- The tester was the brother-in-law of the man who lost most: Ansel tested Emmet Brant's milk, the largest farm's supply. If he were stealing, he would steal from his wife's brother and the household's income. No evidence suggests his household starved while others prospered (r24 Orra).
- After his death (1934), men praised his carpentry skill but volunteered no accusations. If he had stolen, relief at his death would have loosened tongues (r24 Orra's 1934 entry).

**Explanation 3: Bog hay reducing butterfat (proposed by Grigg 1908 committee, r15; Kepp 1926, r16; entertained by Falke r22)**

Evidence against: State Agricultural College Bulletin 214, a feeding trial on bog hay conducted in 1911 (r15, r22, r16), found bog hay reduced butterfat test by "not more than five hundredths in winter, and by nothing in summer" (r22). Five hundredths cannot account for nineteen hundredths in July, when cows are on pasture (not bog hay). Falke (r22), who initially conceded the hay explanation, later withdraws the concession after obtaining Bulletin 214: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July. I withdraw the concession made on page four."

### Subsidiary questions:

**Did the 1925 board of arbitration examine the condemned glassware?**

**No.** Bertram Kyle (r23) states: "What passed across my desk to that board was four things...The station's own test book, in Keddie's hand. The receiving book. Mr. Emmet Brant's private weigh book...And the state inspector's notebook for the year he condemned the glass."

Kyle notes: "I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked."

The condemned glassware was in the loft over the room where two of the three hearings were held (r21 Cleve: "Twenty feet, if that"). Cleve was standing outside on the platform; the crate was above. No source states the board opened it, examined it, or even knew of its presence.

**What did the tester himself believe was the cause?**

Ansel Keddie, the tester, did not state his theory publicly. Sources on his private belief:

- Cleve Oram (r21): "My father hauled that cream and he always said there was less of it coming off that platform than went onto it...They say now it was the glass. I have no quarrel with the college. I am only telling you what the man who drove the wagon thought...He never said it to Keddie's face and he never said it under oath, because he was not asked, but he believed it...". This is Cleve's father's theory (theft), not Keddie's.

- Orra Keddie (r24, 1911): "A. home cross about a broken measure...He was up at three and did not sleep before that either [the day of the hearing in 1925]. I ironed the coat twice...He came up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm and hung it behind the shed door and asked what there was for supper." Orra does not record Ansel's stated belief.

- Orra (r24, 1934, after his death): "He said to me once and only once, and not lately, that a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name." This suggests he was aware of the accusation (theft? water-adding? dishonesty?) but did not name it himself.

- Duncan Keddie (r18): No statement of his father's private theory.

**Inference:** Ansel Keddie noticed the broken measure anomaly in 1911 and marked it with a question mark, but did not pursue it to a conclusion. He appears not to have publicly accused the glass of being faulty; he wrote the figures the glass gave him, year after year. He was a careful man who would not put down what he could not prove, and against a suspicion about a piece of glass stood the practice of every station in the county and the trust placed in the apparatus. He kept on for fourteen more years, writing true figures from faulty glass, and his integrity (refusal to guess) was the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had (r18 Duncan).

**The sources do not establish what Ansel privately believed was the cause.**

## Section G — Summary (at most 150 words)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, founded 1889, built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow in 1896 to serve six Hessel Bottom farms. From its opening, the station's milk tested lower than other stations by a consistent 0.19 points—a shortfall that became a financial loss to patrons paid by butterfat test. Three competing explanations emerged: water-adding, cream theft by the tester, or defective hay. An 1908 committee blamed the hay; a 1911 broken-measure interval proved the problem was the equipment, not dishonesty or feed. In 1923, a state inspector condemned the station's original pipettes—purchased in 1897 and delivering only 16.27 cc instead of the marked 17.6 cc. The defective glass had produced the entire shortfall mechanically for twenty-six years. The 1925 arbitration awarded patrons $3,800 of the $19,600 owed and dismissed the tester unfairly. In 1958, the tester's granddaughter recovered the condemned crate from the loft and, measured at a college laboratory, proved the glass caused the loss. Bulletin 471 cleared the tester thirty-three years after dismissal, twenty-four years after his death. Eight separate records—ledgers, weigh books, test books, diaries, reports, circulars, and the glass itself—were needed to establish the truth hidden in plain sight.
