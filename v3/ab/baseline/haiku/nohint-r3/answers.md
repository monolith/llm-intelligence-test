# Test Answers – Section A (Stories 1–4)

## Story 1: The Cooperative is Built (1889–1896)

The farmers of the Larrow Valley organized a cooperative creamery association in 1889 to collectively sell their milk and cream. Gideon Vose, a visionary organizer, founded the Association and served as its first president, drafting the by-laws that would govern how farmers were paid. He built the main creamery at Larrow Green in 1891, establishing a sophisticated system: farmers would bring their milk to a central intake station, where it would be weighed, sampled, and tested for butterfat. The crucial decision was to pay farmers not by the weight of milk delivered but by the butterfat content as measured by a standardized test. This represented the cutting edge of 1890s dairy technology.

By 1895-1896, as the cooperative grew, the Association built a skimming station seven miles up the valley at Ostrey Hollow to serve the farms at the head of the valley, particularly Hessel Bottom. This remote location meant farmers no longer had to haul fresh milk all the way to Larrow Green; instead, cream would be separated and hauled down, with skim milk returned to the farmers for their hogs. The Ostrey Hollow station opened on May 4, 1896, equipped with a complete testing outfit purchased from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet for $77.93: six dozen milk test pipettes marked at 17.6 cubic centimeters, test bottles, acid measures, and a hand tester. This invoice would later become the most consequential piece of paper in Ordell County history.

The station's infrastructure reflected the cooperative's values: a two-story board building with a covered platform for loading cans level with the wagon bed, an intake room on the first floor with the scale and vat, and a small loft above where supplies were stored. The system was designed with integrity as its core principle—farmers would be paid based on precise measurement, with every figure recorded in books that would be checked at the annual meeting. The cooperative's success depended on trust in the accuracy of these tests.

**Key documents:** Invoice from Ammon & Sons dated April 14, 1897; Association by-laws; records of station opening date.

---

## Story 2: Ansel Keddie Takes the Station (1896–1902)

On May 4, 1896, Ansel Keddie began work as the tester and separator man at Ostrey Hollow, a position he would hold for twenty-nine seasons. Keddie was born in 1861, married Orra Brant (sister of Emmet Brant, the largest farmer on Hessel Bottom) in 1889, and brought to his work an almost religious commitment to precision and honesty. Every morning before dawn, he would walk to the station—sometimes breaking the path himself in winter when the mare was lame—to weigh the farmers' milk, take samples, test them for butterfat using the station's equipment, and record every figure in the test book in his own hand.

Keddie's method was meticulous. Following the lead of his father-in-law Emmet Brant, who had kept his own weigh book recording both his own measurements and the station's, Keddie documented everything. His careful approach to the work established a pattern: complete and honest recording of every measurement, trust in the accuracy of the testing apparatus, and a commitment that every farmer would be paid fairly. He understood that his figures would determine how much money every farmer in the valley earned. He attended Association meetings, stood in doorways asking questions when figures didn't align, and insisted on accuracy down to the pound and the hundredth of a point in butterfat testing.

This was the foundation of what would later be called "the whole of the business" by observers: a single man, methodical and honest, recording every transaction in bound ledgers that lived on open shelves in a public room, visible to anyone who cared to look, read aloud at annual meetings to fifty farmers with their hats on their knees. Keddie had no thought he was documenting a crime. He thought he was documenting arithmetic. By 1902, the first complaints about low tests at Ostrey Hollow began to surface, but Keddie's reputation for accuracy was unquestionable among the farmers.

**Key documents:** Station test book kept in Ansel Keddie's hand from 1896; Emmet Brant's private weigh book; Association records and annual meeting minutes.

---

## Story 3: A Mystery Appears (1902–1911)

By 1902, farmers at Ostrey Hollow began to notice something troubling. Their milk, tested at the station, consistently showed lower butterfat content than milk from other stations in the valley and neighboring counties. Year after year, in drought and flood, in the flush of June and the depth of February, the Ostrey Hollow tests ran exactly 0.19 points (nineteen hundredths) lower than the Larrow Green creamery's average and lower than farms elsewhere. The deficiency was mechanical, invariant, impossible to dismiss as seasonal variation.

The farmers complained at every annual meeting for a quarter-century. The Association's officers grew skeptical of the Ostrey Hollow patrons, suggesting perhaps they were adding water to their milk to increase volume while being paid on butterfat—a contradiction that made no economic sense, but the accusation took hold. Theories multiplied: perhaps the wet hay of Hessel Bottom, known throughout the county for its coarseness, thinned the cream of cows fed upon it. A 1908 committee of practical men walked the bottom and reported this as likely. A state veterinarian in 1926 said the same. These were not fools; the hypothesis was not groundless. It simply wasn't complete.

For decades, the question persisted: why were Ostrey Hollow tests consistently low? Theories blamed the farmers (water-addition), blamed the environment (poor hay), even blamed the tester (theft), yet no explanation accounted for the mechanical precision of the deficiency. The state dairy inspector began visiting every year from 1908 forward, testing with a lactometer for added water and finding none. Ansel Keddie kept his books faithfully, recording 200,000 pounds of milk delivered in 1910, year after year the same pattern. The station became famous for its low tests, a mystery that confounded everyone.

**Key documents:** Station test book showing the pattern; committee reports from 1908; state dairy inspector's lactometer tests; Association meeting minutes recording complaints from 1902 forward.

## Story 4: The 1911 Experiment (May 8 – July 10, 1911)

On May 8, 1911, the station's milk pipette—the very instrument used to measure out exactly 17.6 cubic centimeters of milk for every butterfat test—broke. A borrowed pipette from elsewhere was pressed into use. For nine weeks, until July 10, 1911, the station's tests climbed noticeably higher. On the day the station resumed its own original pipette, the tests fell back to their chronic low level—precisely, mechanically, with no change in the herd, pasture, season, men, or machinery. Ansel Keddie noted the fact in his test book. He set a question mark beside it. He asked nothing more aloud. But he knew something was wrong with the glass.

This nine-week interval functioned as a controlled experiment, the kind that cannot be repeated and therefore could never prove its point in a court of law. When the station's own measure broke on May 8, 1911, after fifteen years of continuous use, the borrowed pipette was used for nine weeks. The variable was the measuring glass. Everything else was held constant: the same tester (Ansel Keddie), the same herds, the same pasture and season, the same machinery, the same procedure. The result was unambiguous. With the borrowed glass, tests rose. With the station's own glass restored, they fell.

This established definitively that something about the station's glass was causing artificially low readings. It could not be the hay, could not be the cows, could not be watered milk, could not be theft. The glass itself was the variable that produced the measured effect. What this interval did not establish was why the glass was wrong, how long it had been wrong, or the magnitude of error over years. It proved the glass was a culprit but gave no numbers for damages. Ansel Keddie never spoke about his suspicion aloud except to his wife. He kept on for fourteen more years, recording every figure the glass gave him—figures that would later be called evidence against him.

**Key documents:** Station test book showing the broken measure incident in May 1911 and the recovery in July 1911; Orra Keddie's diary entry for May 8, 1911; Duncan Keddie's recollection of his father's statement in summer 1911.

## Story 5: The Water Accusation (1919–1924)

By 1919, frustration at the Association's offices had reached a breaking point. For seventeen years, the Ostrey Hollow tests had run low. The manager, Selby Vose (son of the Association's founder Gideon Vose, who had organized the Association in 1889 and was its first president), and the Association's board had reached a conclusion: the Ostrey Hollow farmers must be adding water to their milk to increase volume, watering down the butterfat and therefore collecting less payment while selling more weight. This theory solved nothing—it made no economic sense—but it provided someone to blame.

In June 1919, without evidence and without asking, Vose circulated a written warning to nine families at Ostrey Hollow, cautioning them against the practice of adding water to their milk. The warning was civil in form but unambiguous in its accusation. Ansel Keddie, to the horror of his wife Orra, took the nine letters himself to each farm's door and stood there while each man read it. He came home at eleven that night with his hat still on. Orra wrote in her diary: "He took them round himself, to eleven families... and came home at eleven at night with his hat still on."

Emmet Brant, Orra's brother and the largest farmer on the bottom, confronted Ansel in the yard two days later. He asked: if Ansel truly believed the accusation, why had he carried the paper himself instead of letting another man carry it? Ansel replied: "I carried it because I would not have another man carry it." Emmet never entered the house again. The accusation fractured a family—a brother and sister divided by a suspicion everyone knew was false but no one could disprove while the truth sat invisible in a crate in the loft above their heads. The accusation was answered with silence. In that valley, silence was a shout. The state dairy inspector tested with a lactometer at every visit from 1908 forward and never once found added water at that station.

**Key documents:** The water-warning circular dated June 1919, signed by Selby Vose; Orra Keddie's diary entries for June 1919; Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks showing no evidence of water.

## Story 6: Crisis and Dismissal (March–April 1925)

In January 1925, the Ostrey Hollow patrons filed a formal petition against the Association, claiming they had been systematically underpaid for decades. A board of arbitration was formed to hear the case. Sittings were held at Ashlin in March and then at the station itself in April, with approximately a dozen spectators standing in the rain outside the weigh room where two of the hearings proceeded. Three explanations were explicitly put forward before the board: that the farmers added water (already tested and refuted by the state dairy inspector from 1908 onward); that Ansel Keddie stole cream and wrote low tests to conceal it (impossible, as the 1911 experiment proved with no change in procedure); or that the hay of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream (plausible but later shown insufficient).

The board examined the books, the scale, the machinery. They heard testimony from Ansel Keddie and from officers of the Association. They examined four crucial documents: the station's own test book in Keddie's hand, Roy Brant's private weigh book, Merle Strawn's circuit notebook for 1923, and the receiving book from Larrow Green. Yet no one laid the state inspector's notebook beside the condemned glassware, or connected the 1911 experiment to the persistent deficiency. Bertram Kyle, the clerk, carried these documents in and out of the room, made a list, checked it twice, and could not tell afterward whether any board member compared them.

On April 30, 1925, four days before the board announced its findings, Ansel Keddie was dismissed from his position of twenty-nine seasons. He came up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm, hung it behind the shed, and asked Orra what there was for supper. There was ham. The board's "finding" arrived days later: $3,800 to the patrons, "without admission"—meaning the Association was paying money without admitting fault or named cause. Ansel Keddie was guilty of nothing that the Association would name, but dismissed anyway. He had been at that station since before any of the farmers' children were born.

**Key documents:** Station test book in Ansel Keddie's hand, 1896-1925; Roy Brant's weigh book; Merle Strawn's circuit notebook; receiving book from Larrow Green; Orra Keddie's diary entry April 30, 1925; minutes of the 1925 arbitration; the board's award of $3,800 "without admission."

## Story 7: Afterward (1925–1934)

After dismissal, Ansel Keddie lived as a carpenter. He was sixty-four years old and skilled with his hands. He worked on the cow shed that had needed work since his father's time, and he worked at it from light to dark, whistling—a thing Orra noted he had never done when he was paid. He hung a barn door for Cleve Oram that swung true for thirty years. He moved from farm to farm throughout the hollow, doing carpentry work, taking what farmers offered, earning a quarter of what he had earned at the station. He was one man who saw both ends of the cream every day of his life, who testified honestly, who recorded figures in good ink in books on open shelves—and was destroyed by an error he could not prove and could not name.

He carried the imputation with him for nine years. In Ordell County, among the men he had weighed milk for since before their fathers died, he lived under an accusation that had never been named and that no one could or would remove. A man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name. That is what he said to Orra once, and only once, and not in his later years. Her brother Emmet, from whom both had been estranged for six years after the water-warning incident of 1919, died in 1929. Orra went down for the funeral; Roy Brant met her at the gate and was kind. They stood in the yard where once Emmet had asked Ansel why he had carried the water-warning paper himself.

He died on a February morning in 1934, at home, in the forenoon, without much pain. Duncan came up in the night. More men attended his funeral than Orra expected. Every one of them said what a hand he was with a door. Not one of them said the other thing. They had nine years to say it. They did not say it then.

**Key documents:** Orra Keddie's diary entries 1925-1934; Cleve Oram's recollection of Ansel's carpentry work; town records of his death; funeral records.

## Story 8: Truth and Vindication (1958–1964)

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie, Duncan's daughter and Ansel Keddie's granddaughter, climbed a ladder into the loft above the old Ostrey Hollow station's intake room. She was twenty-seven years old and a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, pursuing dairy science under Professor Leland Vaught. Thirty-five years earlier, when the state dairy inspector Merle Strawn had condemned the station's glassware as defective in June 1923, the crate containing the old testing outfit had been placed in that loft under a tarpaulin. No one had thought about it since. She brought the crate down herself.

The crate still contained nine surviving pipettes out of the original six dozen purchased from Ammon & Sons in 1897. Ivy carried them back to Farland and measured each one against a calibrated burette in a college laboratory. Every single pipette was blown on the stem to deliver 17.6 cubic centimeters—the exact measure marked upon it—but not one delivered what it was marked for. The average of the lot was 16.27 cubic centimeters. The short measure was consistent, systematic, and had persisted for sixty-one years.

A short charge of milk into a bottle whose neck is ruled for the full charge yields a reading short in the same proportion. If milk is supposed to be 17.6 cc but only 16.27 cc enters the bottle, and the fat reads proportionally low, then year after year the station's tests would run short by approximately 0.19 points—matching exactly the observed deficiency. The mystery was resolved. Nobody watered anything. Nobody stole anything. Nobody was lying. The glass was wrong—not from the beginning (it came from the factory this way), but from the moment it left Ammon & Sons in April 1897.

Working with Professor Leland Vaught, Ivy published findings in Bulletin No. 471 of the State Agricultural College in October 1958. The bulletin calculated that the Ostrey Hollow patrons had been underpaid for approximately 67,000 pounds of butterfat over the full period—worth approximately $19,600 at the average price of 25 cents per pound during those years (from 1897 to 1923). The 1925 board of arbitration had awarded $3,800 without admitting cause. The true debt was more than five times larger, and no liability could be assigned because all parties were dead. Ansel Keddie's name was cleared in print in 1958, twenty-four years after his death. He never knew.

**Key documents:** State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958); the condemned glass crate and surviving pipettes; measurements of pipette capacity; Ammon & Sons invoice dated April 14, 1897; the 1958 newspaper article in The Ashlin Chronicle; Ivy Keddie's graduate research notes.

# Section B — Relationships

## B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie? Derive it, and resolve anything in the sources that points elsewhere.

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Ansel was born 1861, married Orra Brant in 1889, and had a son Duncan, born 1894. Duncan married and had a daughter Ivy, born 1931. This makes Ivy Duncan's daughter and Ansel's granddaughter. One source (The Ashlin Chronicle, r19) initially states she is his "great-niece," but Duncan Keddie's own writing (r18) establishes the correct genealogy: Duncan is Ivy's father, and Duncan is Ansel's son. Orin Falke (r22) later notes a correction to The Ashlin Chronicle, stating "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." The corrected relationship is granddaughter.

## B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant? Derive it, and state anything about the intervening generations that the sources leave open.

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. The derivation: Emmet Brant and Orra Brant were brother and sister. Emmet's son was Roy Brant. Orra married Ansel Keddie and had a son Duncan. Duncan had a daughter Ivy. Roy had a daughter Hazel. Therefore: Emmet and Orra were siblings (one generation), so Roy and Duncan are first cousins (second generation), so Hazel and Ivy are second cousins (third generation). Duncan Keddie (r18) states this explicitly: "Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece, which people say because it is shorter." Cleve Oram (r21) incorrectly states "Ivy Keddie was Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed," but the sources confirm the relationship is second cousins. The intervening generations are clear from the documentation, leaving nothing significantly open.

## B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter to what happened at Ostrey Hollow?

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose. Gideon Vose organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association in 1889, was its first president, and drafted its by-laws. Selby became the Association's manager in 1911 and held the post for approximately twenty-five years. It matters because: (1) Selby inherited the authority and reputation of his father's founding work, and (2) the sources are careful to distinguish between Gideon (the founder) and Selby (the manager), with no family relation to the Keddies or Brants. Bertram Kyle (r23) emphasizes this distinction: "Mr. Selby Vose became manager in nineteen eleven. He was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president — the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense." This distinction matters because if Selby had been Gideon's nephew rather than his son, it would suggest a weaker claim to inherited authority, and because the story emphasizes that the Voses had no family connection to the people whose milk was being tested and whose livelihood depended on accurate measurement.

## B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?

There was no family relationship between the Vose family and the Keddie family. Duncan Keddie (r18) emphasizes: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses." He explains that people frequently confused the Larrow Valley Voses with the Ashlin Voses, or falsely claimed that his mother and Gideon Vose's wife were cousins. His mother (Orra) told him extensively about family connections in the valley, but "she never once put a Vose in it." Merle Strawn (r22), the state dairy inspector, "was kin to nobody in the valley whatever—not to us, not to the Brants, not to the Voses, not to anybody on the creek." The absence of family relationship mattered because Selby Vose, as a manager with no kinship ties to the farming families, could make decisions about testing and payment without conflicting family loyalties. However, Cleve Oram (r21) notes: "And I will say this while I am on families, because you will hear it anyway and you may as well hear it from somebody who is not repeating it out of malice. The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley." Oram heard this from his mother who heard it from hers, but he admits he could not draw the line on paper. This is an unverified claim in a oral tradition against the explicit denial by Duncan Keddie and no documentary evidence. The most reliable conclusion is no established family connection between Voses and Keddies.

## C1. Butterfat Deficiency Proportion and Monetary Loss

**(a) By what proportion did the tests differ from the truth?**

The pipettes were marked to deliver 17.6 cc but delivered an average of 16.27 cc.
- Short measure: 17.6 - 16.27 = 1.33 cc
- Proportion short: 1.33 ÷ 17.6 = 0.0756 = 7.56%

All tests at Ostrey Hollow ran systematically low by approximately 7.56% because samples were consistently under-volume. This translates to a consistent shortfall in the butterfat percentage reading relative to truth.

**(b) True average test and points short:**

- Ostrey Hollow recorded average: 3.61 (from r22)
- Larrow Green/elsewhere average: 3.78–3.80
- Consistent deficiency observed: 0.19 points (from r22, r17)
- True average at Ostrey Hollow: 3.61 + 0.19 = 3.80
- Recorded tests short by: 0.19 points

**(c) Total pounds of butterfat not credited:**

Approximately 67,000 pounds of butterfat (from r19 and r22 "sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat was what they made it, over the whole run of years")

**(d) Monetary value and fraction received:**

- Total worth at average price of 25¢/pound: 67,000 × $0.25 = $16,750
- Amount actually paid in 1925 award: $3,800
- Fraction actually received: $3,800 ÷ $16,750 = 0.227 or approximately 23%
- (Note: The bulletin calculated $19,600 total loss, differing from this figure; the variance may reflect different period selections or price averages)

## C2. Largest Patron's Loss in 1910 (Emmet Brant)

Emmet Brant farmed the largest piece on Hessel Bottom and hauled the most milk into the station of any man on the creek.

**(a) Pounds of butterfat credited:**

From r17, Hazel Brant states: "Two hundred thousand pounds even, delivered to the station. Even — that is what fixes it... And paid on a test of three forty-two."
- 200,000 pounds of milk × 3.42% test = 6,840 pounds of butterfat credited in 1910

**(b) Pounds owed but not credited:**

If true test was 3.61 points higher (using the consistent 0.19 deficiency), true butterfat would have been:
- 200,000 pounds × (3.42 + 0.19) = 200,000 × 3.61 = 7,220 pounds
- Not credited: 7,220 - 6,840 = 380 pounds

**(c) Monetary difference in 1910:**

Using average price of 25¢/pound (from r22):
- 380 pounds × $0.25 = $95 unpaid in 1910

Over the full delivery years (1897–1923):

# Section D — Logic

## D1. The 1911 Broken Measure Interval as Experiment

**When and how long:** May 8, 1911 to July 10, 1911 = exactly 9 weeks (from r22 and r18: Duncan Keddie notes the broken measure incident; Orra Keddie's diary confirms the date)

**Variable, constants, result:**
- **Variable:** The measuring glass (pipette)
- **Constants held:** Same tester (Ansel Keddie), same herds, same pasture and season, same machinery, same procedure, same manager (Selby Vose in position from 1911 onward)
- **Result:** With borrowed pipette, tests were noticeably higher. With the station's own pipette restored, tests fell back to their chronic low level immediately

**What it establishes:** That something physically wrong with the station's glass caused artificially low test readings. It proved the deficiency was not caused by: watered milk, theft of cream, poor hay, or procedural error by the tester. The glass itself was the variable producing the measured effect.

**What it does NOT establish:**
- The magnitude of error (how many cc short)
- How long the glass had been defective before 1911
- The precise proportion of deficiency
- Fault or liability
- Why the glass was wrong (manufacturing defect)
- Whether anyone should have discovered it sooner

The interval proves the CULPRIT but provides no quantitative basis for damages, and therefore could not settle a liability case or calculate payment.

## D2. Three Independent Reasons Tester Cannot Have Been Stealing Cream

**Reason 1 (Ledger evidence):** Bertram Kyle (r23) notes that every year the station account showed three figures: butterfat credited on test, butterfat received at Larrow Green, and station gain (the difference). This difference was always approximately 5.25% "as usual, carried to the general account." If Ansel were stealing cream, the "gain" would have been erratic, not consistent year-to-year. This line would have been named and explained only because a consistent pattern (not theft) had produced it.

**Reason 2 (1911 experiment):** When the pipette broke and was replaced, tests rose. The same man (Ansel Keddie) was tester throughout, under no different supervision, with no change in his opportunity to steal. If he were stealing cream, his theft rate would not change when a borrowed glass was used—but the tests rose. Therefore, the change in tests was caused by the glass, not the tester's behavior. Duncan Keddie (r18) notes his father "wrote down every figure the glass gave him, and the figures were what convicted him"—meaning he recorded accurately.

**Reason 3 (State inspector evidence):** Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, tested at every visit from 1908 forward and "never once found added water at that station" (r22). More broadly, there is no evidence of cream going missing or any shortage in accounts—only tests running low. If cream were being stolen, there would be evidence of loss in volume, not merely in test percentage.

**Why these are independent:** Reason 1 is documentary (ledger), Reason 2 is experimental (the 1911 interval), and Reason 3 is official inspection. They do not depend on one another. Reason 2 (the 1911 experiment) would hold even if the glassware were never measured in 1958, because it proved the deficiency was in the measuring instrument, not human behavior.

**Bookkeeper's question:** Bertram Kyle (r23) explicitly states: "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years" regarding the "station gain" figure. "The figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people." Kyle also states he "could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second [book] beside the fourth and looked" during the arbitration. The bookkeeper never questioned the entries.

## D3. Did the Manager Know the Tests Ran Low Before Condemnation?

**Answer:** There is evidence suggesting Selby Vose may have known or suspected the problem but did not connect it to the glass.

**Justification:**
- Selby Vose took office in 1911 (r23), the exact year of the broken measure incident and Ansel Keddie's notation of the problem
- The persistent low tests had been complained about at every annual meeting from 1902 onward (r22) for twenty-three years before Vose took office—he inherited a known problem
- In 1919, Vose personally signed and circulated the water-warning circular to Ostrey Hollow families (r23, r24), indicating he believed he understood the cause of low tests
- However, there is no evidence that Vose read the figures beyond the bottom line: "He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign" (r23)
- The 1908 committee report on hay, which Vose would have known about, offered an alternative explanation
- Vose never connected the water-warning (1919) to the 1911 incident
- The state inspector condemned the glass in June 1923 "and came in to say so... and he came in to say so and to tell the manager to order new. Which we did, that same week... There was no more said of it in the office than there would have been of a broken window" (r23)

**Conclusion:** Vose knew the tests ran low, but treated it as a procedural or farmer-behavior problem. He did not know—and had no reason from the sources available to him to conclude—that the glass was defective.

# Section F — Theory

## F1. Causal Theory of the Shortfall

**Coherent causal theory:** Defective glassware—specifically, milk test pipettes manufactured to mark 17.6 cubic centimeters but actually delivering only 16.27 cubic centimeters on average—was placed in use at the Ostrey Hollow station on May 1, 1897. Because all butterfat tests depended on measuring exactly 17.6 cc of milk into a standard test bottle with a graduated neck, the consistent under-delivery of milk by 7.56% caused every butterfat percentage to read proportionally low. This produced a deficiency of approximately 0.19 percentage points in all tests at that station. The error began immediately in May 1897 because the defective pipettes came from the factory wrong; Ammon & Sons either made an error in the mold or the lot was misgauged. The error persisted for 26 years until June 1923 when the state inspector Merle Strawn ran the pipettes against a calibrated burette, found them short, and condemned the entire lot. The error ended that month when new glassware was ordered and put in use. Nothing about the Ostrey Hollow station, the farms, the herds, or the tester had to change for the error to appear or disappear—only the testing instrument changed.

**Three competing explanations and their refutation:**

1. **Water-addition theory** (advanced by manager Selby Vose, 1919): The Ostrey Hollow farmers were adding water to milk to increase volume while being paid on butterfat percentage.
   - **Refutation:** The state dairy inspector Merle Strawn tested for added water with a lactometer at every visit from 1908 forward and never found any added water at Ostrey Hollow (r22). Additionally, adding water would increase volume but not change the fat-to-volume ratio observed in the tests consistently, and farmers would gain no economic advantage by doing so (r22).

2. **Theft theory** (urged at the 1925 hearing by an Association officer): The tester Ansel Keddie removed cream from the milk and wrote low tests to conceal his theft.
   - **Refutation:** The 1911 interval proves this impossible. When the station's pipette broke and a borrowed one was used for nine weeks (May 8–July 10, 1911), the tests rose markedly, then fell back when the original glass was restored. The same tester (Ansel) was present throughout with no change in supervision or opportunity. If he were stealing, his theft rate would not change when a different measuring glass was used—but the tests did change. Therefore, the variable causing the change was the glass, not the tester (r18, r22). Additionally, the station accounts show a consistent "station gain" of approximately 5.25% "as usual" every year, not an erratic pattern that would indicate theft (r23).

3. **Poor forage theory** (advanced by a 1908 committee and veterinarian): The wet hay and poor pasture of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream of cows fed upon it.
   - **Refutation:** The State Agricultural College conducted a feeding trial in 1911 (Bulletin No. 214, cited in r22) showing that bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed butterfat by "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." This is insufficient to account for a consistent 0.19-point deficiency. Orin Falke (r22) explicitly states: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July. I withdraw the concession made on page four"—meaning he accepted this theory as a partial factor but definitively rejects it as sufficient.

**Subsidiary questions:**

- **Did the 1925 board of arbitration ever examine the condemned glassware?** No. The glassware was condemned in June 1923 and placed in the station loft. The 1925 hearing examined the test book, receiving book, weigh book, and inspector's notebook, but Bertram Kyle (r23) explicitly states: "I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked." No one connected the pieces. The crate sat in the loft for thirty-five years until Ivy Keddie found it in February 1958.

- **What did the tester himself believe the cause was?** Ansel Keddie believed, based on the 1911 experiment, that something was wrong with the glass. He noted the fact in his test book and set a question mark beside it. He told Orra (his wife), who recorded it. Duncan (r18) notes: "He had, after all, nothing but a suspicion about a piece of glass, and against it stood the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county. So he kept on. He kept on for another fourteen years, and wrote down every figure the glass gave him, and the figures were what convicted him." He did not speak the suspicion aloud except to his wife.

# Section G — Summary (150 words maximum)

## G1. Eight-Story History Summary

A farmers' cooperative in the Larrow Valley (founded 1889) built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow in 1896, paying farmers based on butterfat test percentage. Ansel Keddie, the station's meticulous tester for twenty-nine years, kept precise records. By 1902, farmers noticed their milk tested consistently 0.19 points lower than milk elsewhere. Theories blamed them (watered milk), blamed Keddie (theft), or blamed local hay—all false. In 1911, when Keddie's measuring pipette broke and was temporarily replaced, tests rose, then fell when his original glass was restored. This proved the glass was defective but raised no alarm. In 1919, the manager circulated a water-warning letter, fracturing families and reputations. The 1925 arbitration dismissed Keddie despite his perfect record. He worked as a carpenter until his 1934 death, under unfounded suspicion. In 1958, his granddaughter Ivy Keddie found the condemned glassware in the station loft, proved it delivered 7.56% less than marked, and cleared his name—thirty-three years after dismissal, twenty-four years after his death. Eight separate records were needed because each narrator only saw part of the truth.
