# Retention Notes: Retellings 1–16 of the Ostrey Hollow Milk Station Scandal

## OVERVIEW
The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association operated a milk-testing and skimming station at Ostrey Hollow from 1896–1923. Over 26 years (1898–1922), the station's recorded butterfat test averaged 3.61%, while the actual butterfat delivered to Larrow Green creamery averaged 3.80%—a consistent 5% discrepancy. This gap was caused by defective glass pipettes that delivered only 95% of their marked volume (16.72 c.c. instead of 17.6 c.c.). In 1925, after an arbitration hearing, the Association awarded Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800 "without admission" despite the board finding "no cause established." The glass defect was not discovered until June 1923, and not scientifically proven until October 1958, 33 years after the operator was dismissed and 24 years after his death.

## PEOPLE & RELATIONSHIPS

### Ansel Keddie (c. 1835–1934)
- Tester at Ostrey Hollow station, kept the station's test book in his own hand 1896–1924 (29 seasons)
- Described as honest, methodical, slow and clean in work; "his hand is better than most"
- Married to Orra Keddie; Orra was the sister of Emmet Brant
- Worked continuously at the cold station room ("a low building, whitewashed, cold as a spring house inside even in August")
- On April 30, 1925 was dismissed despite board finding "no cause established"
- Died in 1934, still under the stigma of the accusation
- His grandson was Duncan Keddie, a teacher at Ashlin
- His granddaughter Ivy Keddie (Duncan's daughter) found the condemned crate in 1958 and proved the glass defect
- At March 17, 1925 arbitration hearing, made statement: "I have kept the test book of this station in my own hand since the station opened, and there is not a figure in it that I did not put there... I have tested as I was taught and as the state man saw me do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, and I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are... I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself."
- Privately told Alonzo Frick in corridor at Ashlin (1925) that he had always thought the patrons were at fault but would not accuse his neighbors publicly
- From 1911 question mark in his test book after nine-week discrepancy: "That question mark is mine and I have let it stand for fourteen years. I could not answer it then and I cannot answer it now, and I will not put anything in its place that I do not know."

### Orra Keddie (née Vose)
- Wife of Ansel Keddie
- First cousin of old Mrs. Vose (old Gideon Vose's wife)
- Sister of Emmet Brant (largest patron of the station)—FULL SIBLING, same mother and father
- Before 1919: Emmet and Ansel's families ate Sunday dinners together, two houses in one kitchen
- After 1919 circular: Orra and Emmet never spoke again—"Not one word, not at the store, not at a funeral"
- Orra would walk down to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane
- Outlived Ansel by years, endured the stigma the whole time

### Emmet Brant (1854–1929)
- Largest milk patron at Ostrey Hollow, brought more milk than anybody
- Farmer on Hessel Bottom (wet meadow)
- Orra Keddie's brother (full sibling)
- The scandal cost him the most; his milk was always tested by Ansel
- After 1919 circular: ceased all speech with Ansel and Orra for decades; estrangement lasted until his death
- Kept weigh book from January 1, 1898 onward (entries: weight on own scale, weight station gave back)—two scales agreed
- 1911: noted in book the nine-week rise in tests when borrowed measure used, then fall when own returned
- Never forgave Ansel for not standing up in front of the circular despite Ansel's belief patrons were at fault
- Left behind the weigh book later inherited by his son Roy Brant in 1929

### Roy Brant (son of Emmet)
- Farmer at Hessel Bottom; sixty-seven years old in 1955 (when he wrote his account for farm bureau)
- Kept his father Emmet's weigh book and continued it in same way after Emmet's death (1929)
- Key observation: cream came down from Hollow heavier than station papers said was bought
- Also kept his own weigh book; never found reason to stop
- Hessel Bottom description: 160 acres wet meadow, six farms had rights, Brant farm largest (60 acres, 38 cows in good year)
- Water stands on low end into May; soft place by alders taken horse to hock in his and father's time
- Hay coarse, cut late, cows eat well on it despite what said; meadow never failed to grow something
- Scythed and mowed with two horses; cut late because meadow can't get onto early
- Testified to seventeen hundredths of a point consistent gap between Hollow tests and Larrow Green's, same in July and February
- 1902: first complaints about testing
- 1908: Committee under Nyle Grigg sampled Hessel Bottom, blamed wet hay; said remedy lies with land
- 1911: nine weeks when station measure broken, state man's used—tests came up to 3.80, then fell back to 3.61 when station's returned
- 1919: manager sent circular about water; Emmet took it personally (Ansel should have stood up); they never spoke again
- 1915–1930: Four of Bottom men hauled to Alder Corners summer flush (cheese factory opened 1914 by Jerome Cudd)
- Roy didn't go over (biggest herd couldn't afford to be talked about, cost money)
- Acknowledged he will give weigh book to his daughter Hazel when done
- 1958: Ivy Keddie (his first cousin once removed, Duncan's daughter) borrowed book for college work at Farland

### Hazel Brant
- Daughter of Roy Brant
- Received weigh book from Roy (received at museum in 1963)
- Second cousin to Ivy Keddie
- Described as "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed" relative to Ivy (connected both sides of family)

### Rosalie Cudd (cheesemaker at Alder Corners, Vessey County)
- Letter dated 1958 to Ivy Keddie
- Father Jerome Cudd built Alder Corners Cheese Company in 1913
- Jerome died March 1941, between setting and cutting a vat (exactly how he would have arranged it)
- Rosalie took factory over spring 1941; has had it seventeen years (as of 1958)

- Overall sixteen seasons averaged about 3.80
- Never saw Ostrey Hollow figures, wouldn't know how to get them
- Same four men's milk at Alder Corners tested approximately 19 hundredths HIGHER than at Ostrey Hollow station
- Listened to forty years of Ordell County explaining the meadow; nobody ever went up and looked
- Aware of trouble in valley (trouble carries over ridge better than anything else, in pieces and late, with somebody's name attached)

### Hobart Sill (buttermaker at Larrow Green creamery)
- 1893–1934: forty-one years standing at the churn
- Came 1893; no floor in room but plank, cooled with creek ice from pond
- Never sent out tub of butter he wouldn't eat himself
- Took cream from Ostrey Hollow daily (seven miles, hill road)
- KEY OBSERVATION: cream ran heavy from Hollow—every year more fat in cans than station's papers said was bought
- Office would send sheet saying so many pounds fat bought at station; fat that came in was more than sheet said—ALWAYS MORE, NEVER LESS
- Weighed on platform most years; weighed again when in cold room (never took another man's figure for what went into churn)
- Receiving book shows his hand from 1893; Hollow's cream heavy page after page after page
- Cream not milk; thick, clings, separates; any man claiming to put figure on cream to pound and hold it is lying
- Larrow Green's own milk: 3.78 average through the years (ordinary milk, ordinary county, ordinary cows)—known from making butter out of it
- Hollow milk really was 3.85 and no better (wet ground but good cows, never had word against cows)
- If figures paid under 3.85, then somebody helping cans along; said so at time, says now
- Never made anything of cream running heavy; never asked to
- One summer in the twenties: figures came up and stayed up—nothing on earth changed but season (same cows, same barns); something got stopped, something said to somebody
- Doesn't know what; doesn't ask
- 1902: first remembers complaints (man shouting milk tested under brother-in-law's, same feed and water—what do we mean)
- 1919: manager put out circular warning Hollow patrons about water—Hobart thought well of it (only plain thing office ever did, though office hated for it, changed nothing)
- Fat worth 27 cents/pound through years
- 1896 May: Hollow station opened with no outfit of their own (ready to start but hadn't arrived)
- Talk of holding off whole season
- Hobart said no, they could have his—went to his bench, made up outfit out of his own glass (used and knew)
- Went up valley on wagon; opened on it; own came spring after; sent his back (one piece chipped, never said word)
- First season 1896 (May to year-end, partial): about 900,000 pounds—good deal for starting station
- Glad to have hand in it
- Sixty-four years old at retirement (1934); thought about butter
- Never went up there but twice in life
- Took cream off platform, made into butter—good butter, as good as any, better than most
- Never a season it wasn't good
- Whatever Hollow doing with cans (thought water in them), fat came down that hill into his churn, went out as good butter with creamery's name on tub
- 41 years; wants to go home
- Instructions: put in about butter; don't put he said anything against cows (cows were never trouble)

### Merle Strawn

- Before receiving burette (1921): tested scales, lactometer, boiler, cold room, watched tests

- Condemned entire crate and ordered new glass

### Alonzo Frick (attorney at Ashlin)
- Chaired board of arbitration 1925 (March 17 to May 4, 1925)
- Described as "as fair a chairman as ever a hearing had"

- Not litigation; arbitration under by-laws, no appeal, no reported decision, nothing to cite

- Privately in corridor: Keddie told him he'd always thought patrons at fault but wouldn't accuse neighbors

- May 4, 1925: Board found "no cause established"; awarded $3,800 "without admission"

### Nyle Grigg
- Farmer of standing, chairman of investigating committee (1908)
- Not patron of Hollow station; hauled to Larrow Green; no financial interest; chosen for that reason
- Committee sampled Hessel Bottom through whole 1908: every month, barn and pasture, unannounced
- Weighed, watched test made twice
- Abused by both sides; took as sign finding was honest

### Dr. Roland Kepp (V.S., veterinarian)
- Thirty years practice in Ashlin area
- Habit: when called to herd doing badly, asks first what cattle standing on
- Answer in nine cases of ten: under animal's feet; tenth: in water she drinks
- 1926: paper read before Ordell County Farm Club titled "The Bog Meadows and the Milk"
- Wet bottom: distinct kind of ground with distinct chemistry; not rich meadow with too much water
- Where drainage wanting: year's growth doesn't rot, lies sour; acid soil, poor lime, poor phosphates
- Sweet grasses crowded out by sedge, rush, tussock grasses
- Herbage carries bulk, water; not lime or phosphorus; cut late because ground can't get onto early
- Cow in milk: manufactory on narrow margin; must find milk material somewhere
- Short of minerals: doesn't give less; robs own frame until can't
- Consequences in wet-meadow herd: thin over loin, rough coat, slow to condition, disorder digestion fall, breed later, feet worse
- Will milk (wet land generous of flow); fat first to suffer (fat dearest, first economy)
- Thirty years observation: wet bottoms yield milk poorer by FIFTH OF A POINT (0.20)
- Set hill herds against bottom herds: difference everywhere, doesn't depend on breed, man, barn—depends on ground
- Hessel Bottom: completest example wet meadow in county
- Six farms; along creek; springs year-round; part under water spring; sedge hay; winter dependence; whole of it
- Ostrey Hollow patrons complained quarter century; complaint perfectly just
- Gap: TWENTY-ONE HUNDREDTHS (0.21)
- 1908 committee: herds sound, milk whole, scales honest, cause to hay
- Matter before committees and boards, not settled
- Bulletin 214: feeding trial found TWO TENTHS depression winter; College had no reason to flatter
- Three figures agree: two tenths College, fifth point in field, 21 hundredths complaining
- Objection about 1911 six weeks: answered as summer flush when herds at best, stock off wet ground on aftermath; astonished anyone would found argument on six weeks
- Remedy: drain, open ditches, lime twice, sow, keep stock off spring, never feed hay alone, mixed ration, mineral supplement
- Fat 27 cents/pound through years; fifth point saves farm
- Discussion: not cows (some best cattle wet land, indifferent hills test well; improve herd 20 years never get fat up on sour ground); not station (cause soil, herbage, stock, College, every wet bottom alike)

### Leland Vaught (Professor of Dairy Husbandry)
- October 1958: wrote introduction and method sections of Bulletin No. 471
- State Agricultural College at Farland
- Question asked 50 years: why Ostrey Hollow tests lower than Larrow Green?
- Answer: defective glass pipettes
- Noted: "The answer is not complicated, and its simplicity is itself part of the history. Nobody in that valley lacked the intelligence to find it. What they lacked was any occasion to suspect the one article in the plant that nobody in the trade is in the habit of suspecting."
- Key teaching: "the milk pipette is the one instrument in the butterfat test which the plant cannot check against anything else, and it is the instrument on which every figure in the plant depends"
- Standard charge in state: 17.6 cubic centimeters
- If pipette delivers less than mark: less milk goes into bottle, less fat liberated, shorter column in neck, reading low in exact proportion charge was short
- Three consequences: (1) error proportional not additive—removes fixed fraction, not fixed points; rich milk loses more than poor milk absolutely; ratio between tests on same short glass undisturbed; (2) error precise—delivers 16.27 c.c. every time for quarter century, January and July, careful operator and careless one; appearance of excellent work; (3) error invisible from inside plant—every other element verifiable, but pipette is standard itself; can only be caught by burette brought outside; burettes reached field men very late

## THE ASSOCIATION: STRUCTURE & PEOPLE

### Founding & Organization
- **Formed:** 1889 at Larrow Green
- **Officers:** 
  - Gideon Vose: president 1889–1911
  - Selby Vose (his son): manager 1913–1938
  - Effie Loomis: secretary 1901–1940
  - Wilbur Teague: bookkeeper 1905–1945
  - Hobart Sill: buttermaker at Larrow Green

### Main Creamery
- Built at Larrow Green in 1891 on Larrow Creek
- Before creamery: farmers sold milk where could get price; no common test or price; every man's arithmetic his own
- Still standing as of 1941

### Ostrey Hollow Skimming Station
- Seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green
- In low building, whitewashed, cold as spring house inside even in August
- Smell: milk, steam, hot iron; could hear engine before last turn
- Opened before 1896 (by May 4, 1896 Ansel borrowed glass from Larrow Green)—actually May 1, 1896 start
- Took milk from seven farms with rights on Hessel Bottom (wet meadow) plus three farms on hill
- Separated milk; sent cream down valley to Larrow Green; returned skim to patrons for hogs and calves
- Building: boarded and battened; intake room at road end; loft over it never cleared out
- Creek behind it; in wet April comes to sill; twice come over it
- Closed sometime after 1936 (by 1959 used for storage of fence wire and salt)

### Article VII (Key By-Law)
- Adopted at annual meeting of 1892 (year after creamery opened)
- Drafted by Gideon Vose
- Text: "Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."
- Hinge: "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern"—no appeal
- Thought a hard rule at time; men left Association over it
- Just rule (good cows not paid same as poor ones; pound of milk liar, pound of butterfat not)
- But last clause critical: "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern"—not test of creamery, only test at own door
- Gideon put it in for convenience; convenient thirty years; sentence on which whole trouble turned

## THE SCANDAL TIMELINE

### 1889: Association Founded
- Gideon Vose elected first president
- Nine families hauling milk to new system
- Men met in schoolhouse in cold weather; went very late; somebody's horse got loose in middle

### 1891: Larrow Green Creamery Opens
- Built on creek below mill
- Churn set up
- Valley began paid in cash once a month (never happened before)
- "There are men still living who can tell you what the first check did in their mother's kitchen" (Dell Rundle, 1939)

### 1892: Article VII Adopted
- Gideon Vose drafts by-laws
- Article VII enacted: payment by butterfat test; "test of station shall govern"

### 1896: Ostrey Hollow Station Opens
- May 1, 1896 (some sources May 4): Ansel Keddie begins
- May 4 entry in test book: "Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie."
- Borrowed glass from Larrow Green (and Ashlin creamery) until own outfit arrived spring following
- Ansel keeps test book in own hand from day one
- First forty leaves of book later stained brown at corner where lay in leaky window a winter he was sick
- Seven miles up valley; took milk from seven Bottom farms plus three hill farms
- Building plain, boarded and battened, loft never cleared

### 1897: Equipment Purchased
- Ammon & Sons invoice, April 14, 1897
- 6 dozen milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c. — $25.20
- 12 dozen test bottles, 8% neck — $31.68
- 1 acid measure — $0.90
- 1 hand tester, 4 bottle — $18.00
- Freight — $2.15
- **Total — $77.93**
- Later found: pipettes defective, delivering 16.72 c.c. (or 16.27 c.c. per Kepp/Vaught) instead of 17.6 c.c., or 95% of mark
- In use from May 1, 1898 onward (or spring following, May 1897 arrival)

### 1898–1922: The 26-Year Discrepancy
- **Station's recorded average test:** 3.61% (or "within three hundredths of it every year")
- **True average (as calculated later):** 3.80%
- **Gap:** 19 hundredths of a point = 5% of the true fat
- **Total milk received (1898–1922, 25 full years):** 40–44 million pounds
  - 1898-1922: Hollow took about 44 million pounds (Roy Brant source says 40-44 million; Dell Rundle says 44 million)
  - 1896 (partial, May-year end): about 900,000 pounds (Hobart Sill)
- **Total fat discrepancy:** 76,000 pounds of butterfat
- **Value at 25 cents/pound average:** ~$19,000 (or $19,600 per some sources)
- **What was credited to Ostrey Hollow patrons:** Listed in Association ledgers as "station gain"
- **The station gain entry (typical for 1898):**
  > "Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account."
- Each year ran at 5.25% gain of credited fat = 5% of delivered fat
- Gain so consistent ("as usual") that Wilbur Teague stopped looking—became "furniture"
- Larrow Green creamery's own tests ran nearly 0.2 point better than Ostrey Hollow's (under 0.2 higher)
- Hobart Sill: cream came in HEAVY every year—more fat than office sheet said bought; always more, never less
- Roy Brant: milk tested 17 hundredths under Larrow Green's, consistent July and February
- Dr. Kepp: gap 21 hundredths (0.21)

### 1902: First Complaints
- Complaints began that Ostrey Hollow milk "did not test with the rest"
- Pattern visible from start but ascribed to conditions, not measurement
- Roy Brant: "The first men to say so out loud said it in 1902, and they were laughed at, and one of them was my father"
- Complaint: milk tested lower than neighbors' for same labor and cows
- "One man and not one season. It went on and it grew, and it got into the talk at the annual meeting and into the talk at every crossroads store between here and Ashlin" (Nyle Grigg)

### 1908: Committee Investigation
- Association appointed committee with Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green as chairman
- Sampled Hessel Bottom herds through winter of 1908 (every month: barn, pasture, unannounced)
- Drew own samples, sealed them
- Saw herds milked, cans filled, hauled to station, weighed in
- Stood by while test made; nothing to remark upon
- Tried station scales against known weight; tried patrons' scales against station's
- Inquired into water supply at each barn
- Observed stock condition: thin in flank winter's end, slow to come to flesh spring; coats rough later than hill herds
- Do well in flow; wet land generous of milk; opinion: land gives quantity, withholds quality; cow can't put into pail what keep hasn't put into her
- Committee Report (1908): "We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March. The milk is whole and the scales are honest. It is our opinion that the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station."
- "We report that we found no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter, and we wish that finding to be printed in the same type as the rest."
- Report received and accepted; question closed for eleven years (until 1919)

### 1911: The Nine-Week Anomaly
- May 8, 1911: Station's measure (pipette) broke
- Ansel borrowed substitute from Larrow Green
- During nine weeks (May 8 – July 10): tests rose to 3.80 (the true level)
- All patrons' tests rose during this period
- July 10: Ansel's own measure returned to use
- Tests dropped back to 3.61 same day
- Ansel recorded both with question mark:
  > "May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure."
  > "July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."
- Ansel statement (1922): "That question mark is mine and I have let it stand for fourteen years. I could not answer it then and I cannot answer it now, and I will not put anything in its place that I do not know."
- This was smoking gun though nobody realized: borrowed measure accurate; station's regular measure (also defective) was not
- Roy Brant: "There were nine weeks in the summer of 1911 when the station's own measure was out of use... Through those nine weeks our tests came up, all of us, every farm on the Bottom; and then the station's own came back into use and the figures went down again"
- Same patrons, same herds, same pasture, same season, same acid, same bottles, same tester, same tester's habits—ONE VARIABLE CHANGED (the measure)
- Ivy Keddie (later): "Same patrons, same herds, same pasture, same season, same acid, same bottles, same man at the crank — one object changed"
- Leland Vaught (1958): "I would ask the reader to weigh what follows above anything computed in this bulletin... This is recorded contemporaneously, in the operator's hand, by a man who did not know what he was recording and set a question mark against it... Here is one that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven years. It disposes of every explanation that looks to the herds, to the ground or to the men, for the sufficient reason that none of those was altered in those nine weeks, and the figures were."

### 1911: State Agricultural College Tests Hay

## CRITICAL MATHEMATICAL & LOGICAL FACTS

### The Glass Error Calculation
- Pipettes blown for 17.6 c.c.
- Measured deliveries: 16.72 c.c. (mean of nine pipettes per Ivy Keddie's 1958 measurement)
  - (Some sources cite 16.27 c.c. per Vaught article)
- Short amount: 0.88 c.c. (or 0.93)
- **Percentage:** 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 = 95 hundredths (or 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244 = 92.44 hundredths)
- Every test read 95% of truth (or 92.44%)
- For charge that should be 17.6 c.c., delivering 16.72 c.c. means neck reading will be 5% lower (or 7.56% lower per other calculation)

### Effect on Reported vs. Actual Fat
- Recorded average: 3.61%
- True average: 3.61% ÷ 0.95 = 3.80% (or 3.61% ÷ 0.9244 = 3.905%)
- Gap: 19 hundredths of a point (or 29.5 hundredths per alternate calculation)
- 19 hundredths = 5% of 3.80 (or 7.56% of 3.80)

### Loss Calculation (1898–1922, 25 years)
- Milk received: 40–44 million pounds (sources: Loomis says 40M, Teague says 44M; likely 40M used in final)
- Recorded fat at 3.61%: 1,444,000 pounds butterfat (or 1,520,000 if 40M at 3.80%)
- True fat at 3.80%: 1,520,000 pounds butterfat
- Difference: 76,000 pounds butterfat
- Price average: 25 cents per pound
- Total loss: 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000** (or $19,600 in some sources)
- Award: $3,800 = one-fifth of loss

### The Five Percent Rule
- As Ivy Keddie noted: "Two percentages have been quoted at me and both are right, so long as you say what they are percentages of"
- 76,000 pounds is 5% of the fat actually delivered (1,520,000 pounds)
- 76,000 pounds is 5.25% of the fat credited (1,444,000 pounds)
- Association's ledger used second because "a bookkeeper naturally reckons a surplus against the figure he has already entered"
- "But five percent is the one that is also the glass" (referring to 95% delivery rate)

### Progressive Loss by Herd Quality
- Error fell hardest on best milk
- A herd recorded at 4.56 was short 0.24 of a point
- A herd recorded at 3.42 was short 0.18 of a point
- Rich milk = higher apparent loss
- "no man had any way of knowing that his good cows cost him more than his poor ones"

### The 1911 Nine-Week Proof (Most Powerful Evidence)
- Same patrons, same herds, same pasture, same season, same acid, same bottles, same man (Ansel) at crank
- Only difference: borrowed measure (accurate) vs. station's own measure (defective)
- Result: tests went to 3.80 (true) and back to 3.61 (false) when original returned
- "No explanation of this valley's twenty-six-year anomaly survives those nine weeks except the one I have given you" (Ivy Keddie)
- Leland Vaught (1958): "I have spent my working life urging students to prefer an experiment to a calculation. Here is one that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven years. It disposes of every explanation that looks to the herds, to the ground or to the men"

### Why the Wet Hay Theory Fails (Conclusively)
- Bulletin No. 214 (1911): bog hay depresses test max 5 hundredths in winter, nothing in summer
- Actual gap: 19 hundredths flat every month of every year
- "It is four times too small and it is the wrong shape"
- Alder Corners Cheese Company records: same Hessel Bottom herds' milk tested 19 hundredths higher when processed there (1919 season, summer flush)
- Dr. Kepp (1926): "I could produce you six weeks in any year that would prove anything you liked" (re: dismissing 1911 as anomaly)

### Why the Theft Theory Fails
- Hobart Sill (buttermaker): cream came DOWN heavy, not light
- "Every year more fat in cans than station's papers said was bought"
- "The office would send down its sheet and the sheet would say so many pounds of fat bought up there, and the fat that came into this room off that wagon was more than the sheet said. Always more. Never less."
- Cream received at Larrow Green was HEAVIER than credited at station
- If theft occurred, cream should arrive lighter; instead it arrived heavier
- Merle Strawn: tested for water "on every visit" and "never found any"
- "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there" (Bulletin No. 471)

- Resolution: No cream was stolen, no milk was watered; the test error explained everything
- Paradox resolved: board could not identify cause but recognized unfairness and awarded money anyway

- Ansel dismissed April 30, 1925 (after "no cause" finding)
- Glass condemned June 12, 1923 (nine months before hearing)
- But condemnation never mentioned in hearing (though building visited twice in March-April)
- Ansel died 1934, under stigma
- Truth proved October 1958, 33 years after dismissal, 24 years after death
- Thirty-five years crate sat undisturbed in station loft (1923–1958)

- Ran at 5.25% year after year ("as usual")
- Consistency made it invisible (became "furniture")
- But consistency proved it was systematic measurement error, not occasional theft
- If theft, would expect scatter; instead found perfect proportionality

## PLACES & GEOGRAPHY

- **Larrow Valley:** Rural valley in Ordell County
- **Larrow Green:** Town 7 miles down valley; location of main creamery and Association office (Ashlin)
- **Ashlin:** County seat; office located over harness shop; location of arbitration hearing first session (1925)
- **Ostrey Hollow:** Head of valley, 7 miles up from Larrow Green; location of skimming station "at very top of Hollow where road bends and then goes to nothing but the ridge"
- **Hessel Bottom:** Wet meadow below Hollow hamlet; 160 acres; six farms had rights; Brant farm largest (60 acres)
  - Under water in spring into May
  - Soft place by alders taken horse to hock
  - Water stands on low end
  - Springs run year-round
  - Sedge, rush, tussock grasses
  - Coarse sedge hay; late cut; winter dependence
- **Redlow Ridge:** Mountain pass between Ordell and Vessey counties
- **Alder Corners:** Cheese factory opened 1914 by Jerome Cudd, over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County
  - Ridge: 4 miles switchback
  - Shuts November; opens April/May
  - Impassable February winter
- **State Agricultural College at Farland:** Where Ivy Keddie studied; where circuit notebooks housed from 1947

## VERIFICATION & LOGICAL PROOF CHAIN

**Chain of Evidence (Chronological):**

1. **1896–1924:** Test book kept by Ansel (Item 3) — primary data
2. **1897:** Defective pipettes purchased (Item 2 invoice; Item 5 crate found 1958)
3. **1898–1922:** Station gain entries in ledgers (Item 6) — 76,000 lb discrepancy documented
4. **1898–1922:** Weigh book kept by Emmet Brant (Item 4) — two scales agreed; no weighing error
5. **1908:** Committee sampled herds, found no dishonesty (Item 11)
6. **1911:** Nine-week anomaly — same conditions, different measure (Item 3, test book entry) — tests rose to 3.80, fell back to 3.61
7. **1911:** College Bulletin 214 feeding trial (Item 12) — found only 5 hundredths hay effect (gap is 19)
8. **1915–1930:** Alder Corners test figures (Item 13) — same herds tested 3.80 at factory
9. **1919:** Merle Strawn found no water in milk (Item 9, earlier entries)
10. **June 12, 1923:** Merle Strawn condemned pipettes (Item 9) — measure won't deliver mark
11. **July–October 1923:** New glass installed and verified (Item 9, later entries)
12. **1924:** Tests rose to 3.80 with new glass (Item 3 and historical records)
13. **1925:** Board hearing; exhibits including test book, weigh book, inspector notebook (Item 7 and Frick testimony)
14. **February 1958:** Crate found in loft (Item 5)
15. **March 9, 1958:** Pipettes measured: 16.72 c.c. instead of 17.6 c.c. (Item 14)
16. **1958:** Alder Corners figures confirmed (Item 13; Rosalie Cudd letter)
17. **October 1958:** Bulletin No. 471 published (Item 15)

**Logical Proof:**
- Defective glass is ONLY explanation that accounts for:
  - Proportional error (rich milk lost more)
  - Consistent year-round (not hay-related)
  - Precise (16.72 c.c. every time)
  - Invisible from inside plant
  - Proved by 1911 anomaly (same conditions, different measure = different results)
  - Confirmed by Alder Corners figures (same herds, different measure = higher tests)
  - Verified by measurement (16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 = 5% error)
  - Recorded in ledgers as "station gain"
  - Cream arrived heavier, not lighter (contradicts theft)
