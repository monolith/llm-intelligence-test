# Section A: Eight Stories (Stories 5–8) & Section B: Relationships

## Story 5: The Family Rift (1919–1929)

In June 1919, Selby Vose, the Association manager, sent a circular letter to nine families in the Ostrey Hollow valley warning them about adding water to milk. Orra Keddie (Ansel's wife and Emmet Brant's sister) received one, read it twice, and burned it. The letter had a civil form but left no doubt about its meaning: the Hollow men were watering. Ansel was forced to deliver the letters himself—he would not let another man carry an accusation against his neighbors. He took the papers door-to-door, stood at each threshold while the man read, and came home at 11 p.m. with his hat still on. Two days later, Emmet Brant came up to the house. He did not come in. He stood in the yard and asked Ansel directly: "You have hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and you know it better than any man living—why did you carry the paper if you don't believe it?" Ansel replied: "I carried it because I will not have another man carry it." Emmet left. He did not enter the house again for ten years. Orra and Emmet had been siblings sharing Sunday dinners; now they were estranged. Orra would walk down to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane. The 1919 circular split the family. Emmet believed (or convinced himself) that Ansel should have publicly refused to carry the letter; Ansel believed the patrons themselves were at fault (as he privately told lawyer Alonzo Frick) but would not accuse his neighbors publicly. Neither forgave the other for their version of conscience. In 1929, Emmet died. Orra went down, sat in the kitchen where she had been a girl, and came home. The ten-year silence ended in death, not reconciliation. Ansel followed five years later.

**Documents and narrators:** Orra Keddie's diary (r24), Roy Brant (r9), Alonzo Frick (r13), Hazel Brant (r17), Emmet Brant's character (r8, r9 notes).

**Key event:** June 1919 circular; Emmet's estrangement; ten-year silence (1919–1929); Emmet's death 1929; Ansel's death 1934.

## Story 6: The Glass Condemned and Ignored (June 12, 1923)

On June 12, 1923, Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector on the Ordell circuit, made a routine visit to Ostrey Hollow station. He ran the station's milk pipettes against a calibrated burette—a procedure he had not been able to complete in 1922 due to road conditions. The pipettes "would not deliver their mark." He condemned the entire crate on the spot and ordered the manager to get new glass. His entry in his circuit notebook: "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them." The Association ordered replacement glass on June 28, 1923. Ammon & Sons responded July 2, 1923: "Your order of the 28th ultimo for six dozen milk pipettes and six dozen test bottles is entered and will go forward this month. We regret the trouble. Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name." The firm could not (or would not) explain what had gone wrong. The new glass arrived and was in use by late summer 1923. No person at the time connected the condemnation of the glass to the 26-year discrepancy. The condemned crate was carried up into the loft over the intake room and left there under a tarpaulin. For 35 years it sat in darkness, its existence unknown or forgotten, while Ansel was dismissed, paid nothing, and died under a cloud. Cleve Oram, a cream hauler's son, saw that crate a hundred times and put his hand on it but gave it no thought. Bertram Kyle, the office clerk, entered the order and filed Strawn's word for it and moved on—"no more said of it in the office than there would have been of a broken window."

**Documents and narrators:** Merle Strawn's circuit notebook (r7), Ammon & Sons letter (r20), Junia Ammon (r20), Bertram Kyle (r23), Cleve Oram (r21).

**Key measurements:** June 12, 1923 condemnation; July 2 response; new glass in use by July/August; test rise in 1924 to 3.85.

## Story 7: False Accusation and Unjust Dismissal (1925)

In January 1925, the Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned the Association, asking to be made whole for 25 years of underpayment. They calculated the loss as 67,000 pounds of butterfat at a value of ~$19,000. The Association formed a board of arbitration chaired by attorney Alonzo Frick, described as fair and impartial. The board sat three times: first at the Association office in Ashlin (March 17), then twice at the station itself in the weigh room (Frick wanted to see the equipment). Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper, testified that a system designed for a single man (one tester, one separator man) was inherently vulnerable to embezzlement and theft. Selby Vose blamed watering by the patrons, claimed they added water to "make a hard year come out even," and pointed to 1924's higher tests as proof that water had stopped when "the Association's attention came to rest on the station." (The real cause—new glass—was never mentioned.) Ansel Keddie testified March 17, a single sheet in his own hand: "I have kept the test book of this station in my own hand since the station opened... I have tested as I was taught and as the state man saw me do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, and I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are... I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself." On April 30, 1925—four days before the board handed down its decision—Ansel was dismissed. The board found "no cause established" but awarded $3,800 to the patrons "without admission." Ansel got nothing, kept no job, and carried the stigma for nine years until his death in 1934. Alonzo Frick later wrote that he had believed Ansel honest, but "I understood that an honest man was ruined by a figure nobody could explain." The board never examined the condemned glass, which sat in the loft 20 feet above their heads.

**Documents and narrators:** Alonzo Frick (r13), Ansel Keddie (r5), Wilbur Teague (r6), Selby Vose, Orra Keddie's diary (r24), Cleve Oram (r21), Bertram Kyle (r23).

**Key dates:** January 1925 petition; March 17–May 4, 1925 hearings; April 30 dismissal; May 4 award of $3,800.

## Story 8: Scientific Truth Discovered Too Late (1958)

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, went up a ladder into the loft over the old station building. She found a crate under a tarpaulin, stenciled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET," the condemned glass from June 1923. On March 9, 1958, she measured nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette at the college. Each was blown on the stem at 17.6 c.c. (the standard charge). None delivered the full amount. Mean delivery: 16.72 c.c. (some sources: 16.27 c.c.). Short by 0.88 c.c. (or 0.93), which is 95% of the mark (or 92.4% per alternate calculation). A short charge into a bottle ruled for a full charge yields a reading short in the same proportion—5% error in measure, 5% error in reading, every single time for 26 years. By October 1958, Bulletin No. 471, written by Professor Leland Vaught and Ivy Keddie, stated: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference—seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat—stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain." The answer had been in the room the whole time: the glass. Ansel Keddie had died in 1934. His widow, Orra, died in 1948. Neither lived to see their names cleared. The scientific proof came 24 years after Ansel's death, 33 years after his dismissal. The valley's opinion, which had crushed him for nine years after 1925, was finally proven wrong—but the man it had wronged was 24 years in his grave.

**Documents and narrators:** Ivy Keddie (r4), Duncan Keddie (r18), The Ashlin Chronicle (r19), Bulletin No. 471 (r11), Orin Falke (r22), Orra Keddie's diary (r24).

**Key measurements:** 16.72 c.c. mean delivery (95% of 17.6 c.c. mark); 76,000 pounds butterfat loss; published October 1958; Ansel dead 1934, Orra 1948.

# Section B: Relationships

**B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie? Derive it, and resolve anything in the sources that points elsewhere.**

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Duncan Keddie (born 1894, Ansel's son) fathered Ivy (born 1931). The Ashlin Chronicle initially called her Ansel's "great-niece," but county agent Orin Falke corrected this in his 1959 report, stating "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." Duncan Keddie himself clarified: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise." The sources establish unambiguously that Ivy is Ansel's granddaughter, not great-niece.

**B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant? Derive it, and state anything about the intervening generations that the sources leave open.**

Ivy and Hazel were second cousins. Hazel Brant (daughter of Roy Brant) and Ivy Keddie (daughter of Duncan Keddie) are children of first cousins: Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie. Roy's father was Emmet Brant; Duncan's father was Ansel Keddie; Ansel's wife was Orra Brant, sister of Emmet. Thus Roy and Duncan are first cousins (their parents were siblings), making their children second cousins. Duncan Keddie emphasized this at length in his 1968 family notes: "Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece, which people say because it is shorter." Cleve Oram (r21) mistakenly called Ivy "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed," but the family genealogy establishes second cousins as correct.

**B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter to what happened at Ostrey Hollow?**

Selby Vose (manager 1913–1938) was the son of Gideon Vose (founder and first president 1889–1911). Duncan Keddie explicitly stated: "Selby Vose, who managed the Association, was Gideon Vose's son. Gideon organized the Association and was its first president and wrote its by-laws; Selby was his son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew." This matters because Gideon Vose drafted Article VII, the by-law that locked payment to the station test alone with no appeal. Selby inherited both the Association's management and the institutional blindness to the station gain. Bertram Kyle observed that Selby "read the totals and he never read the lines above them... I watched him do it a thousand times and thought nothing of it, because that is what managers were." Selby signed the ledger sheets carrying the station gain line every year from 1913 onward, and when Wilbur Teague raised concerns in 1916, Selby told him to "let it alone." Selby also signed the 1919 water circular. His managerial passivity—not investigating despite signing the very ledgers that contradicted his own theories—enabled the silence.

**B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?**

Duncan Keddie explicitly denied any family relationship: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses... My mother would have told me. She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it." Local gossip claimed Orra and Gideon's wife were cousins, but Duncan, who heard this story from "two women in this town" (one claiming "first cousins"), rejected it firmly. He emphasized that his mother Orra, who was intimately familiar with valley genealogy, never once mentioned a Vose connection. The persistent rumor (which Cleve Oram also repeats in r21) is unsupported by any documentary evidence or the testimony of those in position to know. The Keddie and Vose families were unrelated.
