# Section A: Eight Stories (Stories 1–4)

## Story 1: Founding and the Architecture of Disaster (1889–1896)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was founded in 1889 by nine families meeting in a schoolhouse at Larrow Green, Ordell County. The founding brought cash payment (the first regular milk income many had known) and organized production. When Gideon Vose, the first president, drafted the by-laws in 1892, he embedded Article VII: payment by butterfat test, not milk weight, and critically—"the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." This clause was adopted at the 1892 annual meeting after the main creamery opened in 1891. It was considered just (rewarding good milk producers) but its last phrase locked both testing and payment into one local test with no appeal. In 1896, the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles upvalley, to serve ten farms (chiefly from Hessel Bottom, a wet meadow with six family farms). Ansel Keddie, hired as tester, began May 1, 1896, initially borrowing glass from Larrow Green. On May 4, he recorded the opening in the test book: "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie." The station's own equipment arrived spring 1897—an invoice from Ammon & Sons, Tarnet, dated April 14, 1897: six dozen milk pipettes (17.6 c.c. marked), test bottles, acid measure, hand tester, freight, total $77.93. These pipettes, blown for 17.6 c.c., began use May 1897 and would stay in use for 26 years. Ansel kept the test book in his own hand from opening day forward, a methodical record of every test. Article VII's hinge—the test of the station shall govern—was both its virtue (simple, local, fair in intent) and the seed of disaster. Nothing in 1896 suggested the pipettes were defective.

**Documents and narrators:** Ansel's test book (r3), Duncan Keddie (r18), Gideon Vose's Article VII, Ammon & Sons invoice (r20—Junia Ammon), Larrow Green creamery founding (r19), Bertram Kyle's office records (r23).

**Key quantity:** $77.93 equipment cost; 1896 partial-year milk ~900,000 lb (Hobart Sill); 17.6 c.c. marked pipettes.

## Story 2: The Persistent Discrepancy Emerges (1902–1910)

From the very start, Ostrey Hollow milk tested low. The first complaints came in 1902—men said their milk tested lower at the station than at neighbors' farms, though the cows, feed, and care were identical. The gap was visible in the Association's own ledgers: station gain entries, year after year, showed butterfat credited to patrons on the station test was always less than butterfat that arrived at Larrow Green. The ledgers show a 5.25% consistent shortfall, "as usual," charged to station gain and buried in bookkeeping furniture. Roy Brant's weigh book (from 1910 onward, recording his own scale and station's) showed agreement between scales but 17 hundredths difference between Hollow tests and Larrow Green's—a systematic gap. Hobart Sill at the churn noted cream arrived heavy, not light, page after page: "the fat that came in was more than the sheet said. Always more. Never less." Complaints multiplied through 1902, 1908, and beyond. Bertram Kyle, office clerk, copied the station line dozens of times: "butterfat credited on station test, [X] lb. Butterfat received at Larrow Green, [Y] lb. Station gain, [Y-X] lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual." The line had a name; a named thing seems explained. Nobody questioned it. The discrepancy averaged 19 hundredths (3.61% recorded vs. 3.80% true average), flat every month. No seasonal pattern, no year-to-year variance—utterly uniform.

**Documents and narrators:** Roy Brant's weigh book (r9), Hobart Sill (r14), Wilbur Teague (r6, r23 notes), station ledgers, Bertram Kyle (r23), Dell Rundle (r12), Dr. Kepp (r16 notes).

**Key quantities:** 19 hundredths gap (3.61% vs. 3.80%), consistent year-round; 1898–1922 milk 40–44 million pounds; 5.25% station gain consistently.

## Story 3: The 1911 Anomaly—The Critical Experiment (May–July 1911)

On May 8, 1911, the station's own pipette broke. Ansel borrowed a measure from elsewhere—likely Larrow Green or Ashlin creamery—and used it until July 10. During those nine weeks, with the same tester, same patrons, same cows, same pasture, same season, same acid, same bottles, same supervision—the tests rose to 3.80, the true level. When Ansel returned the station's own measure on July 10, the tests fell back to 3.61 the same day. Ansel noted both dates in his book and set a question mark: "May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure. July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." By 1922, Ansel wrote of that question mark: "I could not answer it then and I cannot answer it now, and I will not put anything in its place that I do not know." The incident was ignored at the time but becomes, in retrospect, a perfect controlled experiment: one variable changed (the measure), all others held constant, result immediate and reversible. Leland Vaught wrote in 1958: "I have spent my working life urging students to prefer an experiment to a calculation. Here is one that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered." The incident proves the glass was the culprit but was not recognized as such in 1911. No one drew inference. Ansel, honest and precise, marked his confusion and went on.

**Documents and narrators:** Ansel's test book (r5, r18, r22), Roy Brant (r9), Ivy Keddie (r4, r8 notes), Leland Vaught (r11), Orin Falke (r22).

**Key evidence:** May 8–July 10, 1911; tests 3.80 with borrowed glass, 3.61 with station's own; same patrons, same herds, same man testing; question mark recorded contemporaneously.

## Story 4: The Wet Hay Theory and Scientific Misdirection (1908–1926)

In 1908, complaints had reached the Association's attention. The board appointed a committee chaired by Nyle Grigg (a farmer chosen because he had no interest in either side—he hauled to Larrow Green, not Ostrey Hollow). Grigg's committee sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February, and March 1908—every month, barn and pasture, unannounced. They found the milk whole (no added water), scales honest, and herds in good condition but thin in flank and rough-coated. Their conclusion: wet hay of Hessel Bottom thins cream. The remedy lies with the land, not the station. The committee's 1908 report was accepted and the question closed for eleven years. In 1911, the State Agricultural College issued Bulletin No. 214, reporting a feeding trial on bog hay: "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Five hundredths in winter, nothing in summer—but the gap was 19 hundredths flat every month. The theory could not carry the full anomaly. Dr. Roland Kepp, a veterinarian with thirty years' practice, wrote in 1926 that wet grounds depress milk by one-fifth point (0.20). Still short of 0.19. Yet the wet hay theory persisted. When Selby Vose addressed the Association in 1926, he credited the 1924 test rise to patrons stopping their alleged watering when "the Association's attention came to rest on the station." The real cause—new glass installed July 1923—was never named. The theory had taken root and survived facts.

**Documents and narrators:** Nyle Grigg (r15), State Agricultural College Bulletin 214, Dr. Roland Kepp (r16), Wilbur Teague, Selby Vose, Orin Falke (r22), Dell Rundle (r12).

**Key limitation:** Max 5 hundredths winter, nothing summer; actual gap 19 hundredths year-round. Theory inadequate but believed.


# Section A: Eight Stories (Stories 5–8) & Section B: Relationships

## Story 5: The Family Rift (1919–1929)

In June 1919, Selby Vose, the Association manager, sent a circular letter to nine families in the Ostrey Hollow valley warning them about adding water to milk. Orra Keddie (Ansel's wife and Emmet Brant's sister) received one, read it twice, and burned it. The letter had a civil form but left no doubt about its meaning: the Hollow men were watering. Ansel was forced to deliver the letters himself—he would not let another man carry an accusation against his neighbors. He took the papers door-to-door, stood at each threshold while the man read, and came home at 11 p.m. with his hat still on. Two days later, Emmet Brant came up to the house. He did not come in. He stood in the yard and asked Ansel directly: "You have hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and you know it better than any man living—why did you carry the paper if you don't believe it?" Ansel replied: "I carried it because I will not have another man carry it." Emmet left. He did not enter the house again for ten years. Orra and Emmet had been siblings sharing Sunday dinners; now they were estranged. Orra would walk down to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane. The 1919 circular split the family. Emmet believed (or convinced himself) that Ansel should have publicly refused to carry the letter; Ansel believed the patrons themselves were at fault (as he privately told lawyer Alonzo Frick) but would not accuse his neighbors publicly. Neither forgave the other for their version of conscience. In 1929, Emmet died. Orra went down, sat in the kitchen where she had been a girl, and came home. The ten-year silence ended in death, not reconciliation. Ansel followed five years later.

**Documents and narrators:** Orra Keddie's diary (r24), Roy Brant (r9), Alonzo Frick (r13), Hazel Brant (r17), Emmet Brant's character (r8, r9 notes).

**Key event:** June 1919 circular; Emmet's estrangement; ten-year silence (1919–1929); Emmet's death 1929; Ansel's death 1934.

## Story 6: The Glass Condemned and Ignored (June 12, 1923)

On June 12, 1923, Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector on the Ordell circuit, made a routine visit to Ostrey Hollow station. He ran the station's milk pipettes against a calibrated burette—a procedure he had not been able to complete in 1922 due to road conditions. The pipettes "would not deliver their mark." He condemned the entire crate on the spot and ordered the manager to get new glass. His entry in his circuit notebook: "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them." The Association ordered replacement glass on June 28, 1923. Ammon & Sons responded July 2, 1923: "Your order of the 28th ultimo for six dozen milk pipettes and six dozen test bottles is entered and will go forward this month. We regret the trouble. Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name." The firm could not (or would not) explain what had gone wrong. The new glass arrived and was in use by late summer 1923. No person at the time connected the condemnation of the glass to the 26-year discrepancy. The condemned crate was carried up into the loft over the intake room and left there under a tarpaulin. For 35 years it sat in darkness, its existence unknown or forgotten, while Ansel was dismissed, paid nothing, and died under a cloud. Cleve Oram, a cream hauler's son, saw that crate a hundred times and put his hand on it but gave it no thought. Bertram Kyle, the office clerk, entered the order and filed Strawn's word for it and moved on—"no more said of it in the office than there would have been of a broken window."

**Documents and narrators:** Merle Strawn's circuit notebook (r7), Ammon & Sons letter (r20), Junia Ammon (r20), Bertram Kyle (r23), Cleve Oram (r21).

**Key measurements:** June 12, 1923 condemnation; July 2 response; new glass in use by July/August; test rise in 1924 to 3.85.

## Story 7: False Accusation and Unjust Dismissal (1925)

In January 1925, the Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned the Association, asking to be made whole for 25 years of underpayment. They calculated the loss as 67,000 pounds of butterfat at a value of ~$19,000. The Association formed a board of arbitration chaired by attorney Alonzo Frick, described as fair and impartial. The board sat three times: first at the Association office in Ashlin (March 17), then twice at the station itself in the weigh room (Frick wanted to see the equipment). Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper, testified that a system designed for a single man (one tester, one separator man) was inherently vulnerable to embezzlement and theft. Selby Vose blamed watering by the patrons, claimed they added water to "make a hard year come out even," and pointed to 1924's higher tests as proof that water had stopped when "the Association's attention came to rest on the station." (The real cause—new glass—was never mentioned.) Ansel Keddie testified March 17, a single sheet in his own hand: "I have kept the test book of this station in my own hand since the station opened... I have tested as I was taught and as the state man saw me do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, and I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are... I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself." On April 30, 1925—four days before the board handed down its decision—Ansel was dismissed. The board found "no cause established" but awarded $3,800 to the patrons "without admission." Ansel got nothing, kept no job, and carried the stigma for nine years until his death in 1934. Alonzo Frick later wrote that he had believed Ansel honest, but "I understood that an honest man was ruined by a figure nobody could explain." The board never examined the condemned glass, which sat in the loft 20 feet above their heads.

**Documents and narrators:** Alonzo Frick (r13), Ansel Keddie (r5), Wilbur Teague (r6), Selby Vose, Orra Keddie's diary (r24), Cleve Oram (r21), Bertram Kyle (r23).

**Key dates:** January 1925 petition; March 17–May 4, 1925 hearings; April 30 dismissal; May 4 award of $3,800.

## Story 8: Scientific Truth Discovered Too Late (1958)

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, went up a ladder into the loft over the old station building. She found a crate under a tarpaulin, stenciled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET," the condemned glass from June 1923. On March 9, 1958, she measured nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette at the college. Each was blown on the stem at 17.6 c.c. (the standard charge). None delivered the full amount. Mean delivery: 16.72 c.c. (some sources: 16.27 c.c.). Short by 0.88 c.c. (or 0.93), which is 95% of the mark (or 92.4% per alternate calculation). A short charge into a bottle ruled for a full charge yields a reading short in the same proportion—5% error in measure, 5% error in reading, every single time for 26 years. By October 1958, Bulletin No. 471, written by Professor Leland Vaught and Ivy Keddie, stated: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference—seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat—stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain." The answer had been in the room the whole time: the glass. Ansel Keddie had died in 1934. His widow, Orra, died in 1948. Neither lived to see their names cleared. The scientific proof came 24 years after Ansel's death, 33 years after his dismissal. The valley's opinion, which had crushed him for nine years after 1925, was finally proven wrong—but the man it had wronged was 24 years in his grave.

**Documents and narrators:** Ivy Keddie (r4), Duncan Keddie (r18), The Ashlin Chronicle (r19), Bulletin No. 471 (r11), Orin Falke (r22), Orra Keddie's diary (r24).

**Key measurements:** 16.72 c.c. mean delivery (95% of 17.6 c.c. mark); 76,000 pounds butterfat loss; published October 1958; Ansel dead 1934, Orra 1948.

# Section B: Relationships

**B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie? Derive it, and resolve anything in the sources that points elsewhere.**

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Duncan Keddie (born 1894, Ansel's son) fathered Ivy (born 1931). The Ashlin Chronicle initially called her Ansel's "great-niece," but county agent Orin Falke corrected this in his 1959 report, stating "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." Duncan Keddie himself clarified: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise." The sources establish unambiguously that Ivy is Ansel's granddaughter, not great-niece.

**B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant? Derive it, and state anything about the intervening generations that the sources leave open.**

Ivy and Hazel were second cousins. Hazel Brant (daughter of Roy Brant) and Ivy Keddie (daughter of Duncan Keddie) are children of first cousins: Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie. Roy's father was Emmet Brant; Duncan's father was Ansel Keddie; Ansel's wife was Orra Brant, sister of Emmet. Thus Roy and Duncan are first cousins (their parents were siblings), making their children second cousins. Duncan Keddie emphasized this at length in his 1968 family notes: "Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece, which people say because it is shorter." Cleve Oram (r21) mistakenly called Ivy "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed," but the family genealogy establishes second cousins as correct.

**B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter to what happened at Ostrey Hollow?**

Selby Vose (manager 1913–1938) was the son of Gideon Vose (founder and first president 1889–1911). Duncan Keddie explicitly stated: "Selby Vose, who managed the Association, was Gideon Vose's son. Gideon organized the Association and was its first president and wrote its by-laws; Selby was his son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew." This matters because Gideon Vose drafted Article VII, the by-law that locked payment to the station test alone with no appeal. Selby inherited both the Association's management and the institutional blindness to the station gain. Bertram Kyle observed that Selby "read the totals and he never read the lines above them... I watched him do it a thousand times and thought nothing of it, because that is what managers were." Selby signed the ledger sheets carrying the station gain line every year from 1913 onward, and when Wilbur Teague raised concerns in 1916, Selby told him to "let it alone." Selby also signed the 1919 water circular. His managerial passivity—not investigating despite signing the very ledgers that contradicted his own theories—enabled the silence.

**B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?**

Duncan Keddie explicitly denied any family relationship: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses... My mother would have told me. She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it." Local gossip claimed Orra and Gideon's wife were cousins, but Duncan, who heard this story from "two women in this town" (one claiming "first cousins"), rejected it firmly. He emphasized that his mother Orra, who was intimately familiar with valley genealogy, never once mentioned a Vose connection. The persistent rumor (which Cleve Oram also repeats in r21) is unsupported by any documentary evidence or the testimony of those in position to know. The Keddie and Vose families were unrelated.


# Sections C–G: Math, Logic, Contradictions, Theory, Summary

## Section C: Math (14 points)

**C1.** (a) The pipettes delivered 16.72 c.c. when marked 17.6 c.c. (Ivy Keddie's March 9, 1958 measurement: mean of nine pipettes). Proportion: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95, or 95% of mark. The error is 5% (or 1/20). (Note: Some sources cite 16.27 c.c., yielding 92.4% / 7.6% error; I use Ivy's stated 16.72 c.c. as primary, recorded contemporaneously.) A short milk charge into a test bottle yields a proportionally short fat reading.

(b) Recorded average test: 3.61%. To recover truth: 3.61% ÷ 0.95 = 3.80%. The recorded tests were short by 19 hundredths of a point (0.19).

(c) Milk received 1898–1922 (25 full years): 40 million pounds (lower estimate; sources range 40–44 million). True average 3.80%, recorded 3.61%. True butterfat = 40,000,000 × 0.038 = 1,520,000 lb. Recorded butterfat = 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb. Difference: 76,000 lb butterfat not credited.

(d) Butterfat worth 25 cents/lb (average through the years). 76,000 lb × $0.25 = $19,000. Award to patrons: $3,800. Fraction received: $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20, or one-fifth.

**C2.** (a) Roy Brant (largest patron) 1910: 200,000 pounds delivered, paid on test of 3.42%. Butterfat credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = 6,840 lb.

(b) True test 3.80%, true butterfat: 200,000 × 0.038 = 7,600 lb. Owed and not credited: 7,600 – 6,840 = 760 lb.

(c) 760 lb × $0.25/lb = $190 owed that year alone.

**C3.** (a) Over 25 full years (1898–1922), patrons' butterfat credited (recorded at 3.61% on 40M lb milk): 1,444,000 lb total.

(b) Butterfat actually delivered (true 3.80% on same 40M lb): 1,520,000 lb.

(c) Difference: 76,000 lb. As percentage of what? Ivy Keddie noted: "Two percentages have been quoted at me and both are right, so long as you say what they are percentages of." The 76,000 lb is 5% of the fat actually delivered (1,520,000 lb), and 5.25% of the fat credited (1,444,000 lb). The Association's ledger used the second (5.25%) because "a bookkeeper naturally reckons a surplus against the figure he has already entered." The error 16.72/17.6 = 0.95 reflects the 5% rule (delivery 95% of mark), so 5% is the percentage of true fat. Answer: 76,000 lb is 5% of true delivered fat (1,520,000 lb).

**C4.** (a) Total invoice April 14, 1897: $77.93 (6 dozen pipettes $25.20, 12 dozen bottles $31.68, acid measure $0.90, hand tester $18.00, freight $2.15).

(b) Six dozen = 72 pipettes. Each short by 17.6 – 16.72 = 0.88 c.c. (or 0.93 c.c. per alternate sources).

**C5.** (a) Faulty glassware in use: May 1, 1897 (arrival spring 1897, first use per invoice receipt) to June 12, 1923 (condemnation). That is 26 years and ~6 weeks. However, the money calculation runs 1898–1922, not 1897–1923, because 1897 was partial (May 4 start, using borrowed glass initially; May 1897 onward own glass began). Full delivery years 1898–1922 = 25 years. The difference: 1897 was not a full year; 1923 was a delivery year (new glass arrived mid-July, so cream tested during 1923 under new glass). The calculation counts full years only.

(b) First season (1896, May 4–year-end partial): approximately 900,000 pounds milk received (per Hobart Sill, cream hauler).

## Section D: Logic (10 points)

**D1.** The interval: May 8 – July 10, 1911 (9 weeks). The variable: the measure (pipette). Held constant: same tester (Ansel Keddie), same patrons, same herds, same pasture, same season, same acid, same test bottles, same man at the crank, same supervision. Result: tests rose from 3.61 to 3.80 with borrowed measure, fell back to 3.61 same day own measure returned. What it establishes that calculation alone would not: the proof is **empirical and contemporaneous**. No calculation can prove causation; this experiment proves that the measure alone was sufficient to shift the result, that all other explanations (hay, water, herds, operator) are irrelevant to that moment, and that the shift is mechanical and reversible. What it does NOT establish: it does not prove the original glass was defective (only that a different glass gave different results); it does not determine whether the borrowed glass was accurate or simply less short; it does not explain the full 26-year history (nine weeks is not 26 years). But Leland Vaught's verdict holds: "Here is one that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered... It disposes of every explanation that looks to the herds, to the ground or to the men."

**D2.** Three independent reasons Ansel was not stealing cream: (1) Hobart Sill's receiving book: cream arrived heavy at Larrow Green, not light. If Ansel were taking cream, the incoming cans would be lighter; instead they were heavier than the station sheet said, year after year. This argument is independent of glassware and would hold even if pipettes had never been measured in 1958. (2) Wilbur Teague's own testimony at the 1925 hearing: Teague testified the system made theft inevitable and theorized the account would show it—yet when asked to produce evidence, he found none. He had observed the station gain for 20 years but could not point to a single cream-can missing or a single low-weigh discrepancy. (3) Merle Strawn's state inspector reports: tested for water every visit (never found any), and the 1922 scales check at Ostrey Hollow found them true. If cream were being siphoned before the test, the scales would show lower intake. They didn't. Strawn was unrelated to the valley families—he had no reason to cover for Ansel. These three reasons are independent (they rest on different documents and different moments) and do not require the glass measurement. Regarding Teague's questioning: Duncan Keddie (r18) notes that Wilbur Teague created the station gain account in 1905, reported his concerns to Selby Vose in 1916, was told to "let it alone," and "let it alone after that (not asked second time)." Teague never questioned the ledger figures again. He understood: "I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience."

**D3.** Did Selby Vose know before June 1923 that the station's tests ran low? Yes, almost certainly. Vose became manager in 1913 and signed the ledger sheets from that year onward. Wilbur Teague's 1916 report to Vose about the troubling station gain account proves Vose was aware of the discrepancy. Bertram Kyle observed that Vose "read the totals and he never read the lines above them... I watched him do it a thousand times," but he put his thumb on the bottom figure and signed. Vose saw the annual sheets before Teague submitted them to the board. The evidence from Teague, Kyle, and the ledger records themselves establish that Vose knew the station gain existed. However, whether Vose believed the cause was dishonesty, water, hay, or equipment failure is not settled by the sources. His 1926 speech blamed watering, but this may have been post-hoc rationalization. The sources establish knowledge of the discrepancy, not knowledge of its cause.

## Section E: Contradictions (8 points)

**E1 – Major contradictions and resolutions:**

1. **Ivy Keddie's title** (great-niece vs. granddaughter): The Ashlin Chronicle (r19) called her Ansel's "great-niece." County agent Orin Falke (r22) corrected: "I am obliged to add a second correction... Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." Duncan Keddie (r18) confirmed: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter." Resolution: granddaughter. Method: documentary correction and first-hand family statement outrank initial newspaper report.

2. **Relationship of Ivy Keddie to Hazel Brant**: Cleve Oram (r21) stated "Ivy Keddie was Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed." Duncan Keddie (r18) established they are second cousins (children of first cousins Roy and Duncan). Resolution: second cousins. Method: genealogical reconstruction from generation records outranks colloquial description from memory.

3. **Date of glass condemnation**: Hazel Brant (r17) vaguely refers to "twenty-one" (1921) when discussing the glass being condemned; Merle Strawn's notebook (r7, r9, r22) explicitly states June 12, 1923. Resolution: June 12, 1923. Method: documentary evidence (circuit notebook, dated entry) outranks approximate year from memory.

4. **Award amount**: Cleve Oram (r21) states $3,500; most other sources (Alonzo Frick r13, Orin Falke r22, Bertram Kyle r23, Orra Keddie r24) state $3,800. Resolution: $3,800. Method: multiple independent written records (hearing notes, ledgers, diary) outrank single speaker's recollection.

5. **When new glass arrived**: Bertram Kyle (r23) says "within the month" of June 28 order; Merle Strawn's notebook (r7, r9) shows October 1923 verification that new glass delivers its mark. Resolution: July or August 1923 (sometime summer, verified October 1923). Method: contemporary inspection notes outrank approximate timeframe.

6. **Selby Vose's relationship to Gideon**: Gossip (Cleve Oram, r21, and others) claims Vose was Gideon's nephew. Duncan Keddie (r18) and Bertram Kyle (r23) explicitly state son. Resolution: son. Method: family statement and office clerk who sat outside the manager's door for 25 years outrank local rumor.

**E1 – Self-contradictions within narrators:**

- Orra Keddie (r24) diary June 1919: first she says Ansel "would not put them in the mail nor leave them on the platform," then two days later the same Ansel "took them round himself." This is not contradiction but clarification—her first entry may have been incomplete; the second entry (two days later) provides the full story. No contradiction.

**E1 – Cases where two narrators carry the same wrong value:**

- Cleve Oram and Hazel Brant both refer to the 1923 (or "twenty-one") glass condemnation vaguely. Hazel says "twenty-one" and Cleve says 1922 in some notes. Both are approximating. The documentary record (Merle Strawn's notebook, June 12, 1923) is the authoritative source.

## Section F: Theory (6 points)

**F1 – Causal theory:**

The shortfall was caused by defective milk pipettes that delivered 95% of their marked capacity (16.72 c.c. instead of 17.6 c.c.). When a milk charge of only 16.72 c.c. (instead of the full 17.6 c.c.) is introduced into a test bottle whose graduated neck is calibrated for 17.6 c.c., the butterfat column that develops is proportionally shorter. A test recorded at 3.61% with the short pipette reflects a true butterfat content of 3.80%. The error began May 1, 1897, when Ammon & Sons' defective pipettes arrived and entered use. The size of the error—exactly 5% (95% delivery)—is determined by the glass manufacturing defect, not by conditions at the station or farm. The error persisted flat (not seasonal, not farm-dependent) because the cause was instrumental, not biological or agricultural. It ended June 12, 1923, when inspector Merle Strawn ran the pipettes against a burette, measured them, found them short, and condemned the lot. No change at the station or farms was necessary for the error to vanish; only the instrument changed. From July 1923 onward, tests with accurate glass rose immediately to the true level (3.85 in 1924).

**Three competing explanations:**

1. **Water (watering by patrons)**: Advanced by manager Selby Vose (1919 circular, 1926 address). Evidence that defeats it: Merle Strawn tested for water "on every visit" from 1908 forward and "never found any." Vose had no evidence; the circular was a guess. When pressed in 1926, Vose said "I do not know a name and I would not use it if I did."

2. **Theft or embezzlement (Ansel taking cream)**: Advanced at the 1925 hearing by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague, who testified the system "designed for embezzlement." Evidence that defeats it: (a) Hobart Sill's receiving book: cream arrived heavy, not light, every year—if Ansel had taken cream, the cans would have been lighter. (b) The 1911 nine-week interval: same man (Ansel) at the crank, same supervision, but with a different (borrowed) pipette the tests rose to truth—if Ansel were stealing, his habits would not change and tests would not rise. (c) Merle Strawn: found no theft, never saw missing cream, never docked a cent to any man on the platform.

3. **Wet hay of Hessel Bottom**: Advanced by the 1908 committee (Nyle Grigg) and supported by Dr. Kepp in 1926. Evidence that defeats it: State Agricultural College Bulletin 214 (1911) found bog hay depresses fat by "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." The actual gap is 19 hundredths flat, year-round. Five hundredths in winter and zero in summer cannot produce 19 hundredths year-round. Alder Corners Cheese Company records (same four Hessel Bottom farmers, summers 1915–1930) show those herds testing 3.80 consistently at the factory, versus 3.61 at Ostrey Hollow—same herds, different measure, different result.

**Subsidiary questions:**

- **Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?** No. Alonzo Frick's arbitration hearings (March 17 and two sessions at Ostrey Hollow, April 1925) included examination of scales, apparatus, method, and books, but the condemned crate sat in the loft 20 feet overhead. Effie Loomis (secretary) noted: "Not a word of it was spoken in any of the three sessions, by the patrons, by the officers, or by the board." The glass had been condemned June 12, 1923, nine months before the hearing, but no one brought it to the board's attention.

- **What did the tester himself believe the cause was?** Ansel Keddie never publicly identified a cause. His 1911 question mark ("? — A.K.") shows private suspicion about "something in the glass," as he told his son Duncan (then 17) one evening at supper and again by the door "which was not his way." But Ansel refused to put down what he could not prove. He lived and died with the question unanswered, under the imputation of his neighbors' suspicion, "still under the stigma of the accusation."

## Section G: Summary (4 points; 150 words max)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery (1889–1923) paid farmers by milk fat content tested at a local skimming station. For 26 years, that station's tests ran 19 hundredths low, a systematic loss of ~$19,000. Three explanations circulated—water, theft, wet hay—each plausible, none provable. In 1925, after mounting complaints, a board dismissed the tester (Ansel Keddie), awarded patrons $3,800, and ended the matter without naming a cause. The real cause lay unexamined: defective pipettes from 1897, delivering 16.72 c.c. instead of 17.6 c.c. (95% of mark). Condemned June 1923 but forgotten, the crate sat in a loft for 35 years. In 1958, Ansel's granddaughter (a college student) found the crate, measured the glass, and proved the defect. Eight records—the founding, the emerging complaint, an accidental experiment, theories advanced and disproven, a family rift, a discovery ignored, a false accusation, and a posthumous clearing—were needed because each narrator witnessed only one piece: the system's architecture, the numbers, the experiment, the misdirection, the human cost, the neglected evidence, the injustice, and finally the truth.
