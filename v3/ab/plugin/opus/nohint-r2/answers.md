> Working note, stated plainly: this session began at segment 3. No notes on retellings r01–r16 were available to it, so these answers rest on r17–r24, read in full, plus arithmetic. Where r01–r16 might settle something I cannot see, I say so rather than guess.

## Section A — Reconstruction

### 1. A valley paid by the test (1889–1897)

**Who:** the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association and its founder Gideon Vose; its tester Ansel Keddie; the Hessel Bottom farmers, chiefly Emmet Brant.

**What happened.** Valley farmers meeting at Larrow Green organized the Association in 1889. Gideon Vose organized it, served as its first president and wrote its by-laws. The main creamery went up at Larrow Green in 1891. Article seven of the by-laws is the hinge of everything later. Each patron was paid on the pounds of butterfat his milk was found by test to contain, not on its weight, and the test at the station where he delivered set his pay. The by-laws are undated and the early minute book was lost when the office moved, so the year of adoption cannot be fixed.

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up a road that climbs the whole way. It served Hessel Bottom, six farms on wet ground, and opened on **4 May 1896**. Milk was weighed at the covered platform, then sampled, tested and separated in the intake (weigh) room. The Oram wagon hauled the cream down to Larrow Green, and the skim went back to the farms. A loft over the intake room was reached by a ladder nailed to the wall.

The tester and separator man from the first day was **Ansel Keddie (1861–1934)**. He held the post for **twenty-nine seasons** (1896–1924). In 1889 he married **Orra Brant (b. 1866)**, the sister of **Emmet Brant**, who farmed the largest place on the Bottom and hauled the most milk. For a generation the tester tested his own brother-in-law's milk. Their son **Duncan** was born in 1894. In its first season the station borrowed its testing glass from Larrow Green; its own outfit came the next spring (story 2).

**Quantities:** 6 farms; 7 miles; 29 seasons. The first season's milk cannot be determined. The only figure, "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds", is hearsay that the clerk says appears in no book he ruled.

**Resolved:** Orra was born in 1866. That comes from r24's preface, her "sixty-four" in August 1930, and r18's own "eighty-two in 1948"; r18's "1868" is rejected. Hazel's "ninety-five" for Ansel's start refers to the winter the station was built, and she says herself that she has no head for years. Whether Orra was Emmet's full sister (r18) or a half-sister by their father's second wife (r17) cannot be settled, because Orra's pages from before 1889 do not survive.

**Documents:** r22 ¶3–4; r18 §I–II; r17; r21; r23; r24.

### 2. Seventy-seven dollars and ninety-three cents of glass (1897–1923)

**Who:** Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet (founded by Marcus Ammon; its history written in 1949 by Junia Ammon), and the Association.

**What happened.** On **14 April 1897** Ammon & Sons invoiced the Association for the Ostrey Hollow station:

- 6 dozen milk-test pipettes marked **17.6 c.c.** at 35¢: $25.20
- 12 dozen test bottles (8 per cent neck) at 22¢: $31.68
- one acid measure: $0.90
- one four-bottle hand tester: $18.00
- freight: $2.15
- **total: $77.93**

The outfit went into use on **1 May 1897**. Orra's diary for that day says the station's own glass had come and "they are done borrowing of Larrow Green."

The **72 pipettes were short**. They were marked for 17.6 c.c. but delivered on average **16.72 c.c.**, which is **0.88 c.c. or exactly 5 per cent short**, and all of them were short in the same proportion. A short charge of milk in a bottle ruled for a full charge reads low by the same proportion every time, so the recorded test was 0.95 × the true test. The lot stayed in use until the state inspector condemned it on 12 June 1923, about **twenty-six years and six weeks**.

How the lot came to be short cannot be determined. The firm's works books (glass, gauge, man, day) burned in 1931 in the fire that took the old third floor. The historian believes a reground mould made the lot run consistently short, and notes that spot checks cannot catch an error that is consistent. That is her opinion, not a record. The *Chronicle*'s charge that the lot was sold knowingly has no support.

On 28 June 1923 the Association reordered six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles. Ammon's reply of 2 July entered the order, "regret[ted] the trouble" and said its books of the nineties could tell nothing about the lot. The new glass shipped that month.

**Resolved:**
- **Total $77.93:** the line items add to it. The prose of r20 and r19 says "$77.39", a transposition.
- **16.72 c.c. is my correction of r19's printed 16.27** (an inference, flagged). 16.72/17.6 = 0.95, which matches a recorded 3.61 against a true 3.80, Emmet Brant's 1910 figures, and the ledger's 5¼% gain. 16.27 would mean a 7.6% error and a shortfall of 0.29 points, which no other source shows.
- **Six dozen**, per the invoice and r19, not Oram's "five dozen".
- **In use from 1897**, per the invoice and the diary, not r23's 1898 or r17's 1899.
- r20 says the house sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900, which its own invoice contradicts.

**Documents:** r20; r19; r24; r22; r21.

### 3. Nineteen hundredths, flat as a floor: the low test and the hay theory (1897–1926)

**Who:** the Ostrey Hollow patrons, especially Emmet Brant; the Association's 1908 herd committee; the Cudd cheese factory at Alder Corners over the ridge.

**What happened.** Once the station used its own glass, its tests ran low. The long-run average was **3.61**, against **3.78** at the Larrow Green creamery, an ordinary figure for the county. The true station average was **3.80**, so recorded tests were **0.19 points** short. They were short by that amount in every month, in the June flush and in February alike. That steadiness is the mark of a proportional error (5% of about 3.8 is 0.19). A feed effect would change with the season.

The first complaint on record is from **1902**. Orra's diary for March 1902 reports talk at the store that "our milk up here runs poorer", and Ansel saying "it is my figures they will look at". Patrons complained at every annual meeting for a quarter-century.

Emmet Brant's weigh book, begun 1 January 1897, recorded his own scale and the station's scale side by side. The two agreed, so the weighing was never at fault. His 1910 page shows **200,000 lb** delivered and paid on a test of **3.42**. That credited him with **6,840 lb** of butterfat. The true test of 3.60 would have given **7,200 lb**, so he was **360 lb** short, worth about **$90** at the long-run price of 25¢.

In January 1908 an Association committee of four came up in Grigg's sleigh. They ate at Emmet's, looked at barns and mangers but not the station, and reported in March 1908 that the wet bog hay of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream. An Ashlin veterinarian said the same in 1926. The theory fails on two counts:

- The college's 1911 feeding trial (Bulletin No. 214) found that bog hay lowered the test by **at most 0.05 in winter and by nothing measurable on summer pasture**. It cannot account for 0.19 in July.
- **Four of the six Bottom farms** hauled their summer-flush milk over the ridge to Jerome Cudd's cheese factory (later run by his daughter Rosalie). There the same milk tested 0.19 higher: same cows and hay, different glass.

**Resolved:**
- The weigh book was begun in 1897, per Duncan's documented list, not Hazel's 1900; she disclaims years.
- Hazel's 3.42 is Emmet's own test, not the station average. Its gap of 0.18 is exactly 5%.

**Documents:** r22 ¶5, ¶9 and the added note; r17; r18 §V; r24; r19.

### 4. Nine weeks in 1911: the broken measure

**Who:** Ansel Keddie and his household.

**What happened.** On **8 May 1911** the station's own measuring glass broke. Orra's diary: "A. home cross about a broken measure." A measure borrowed from elsewhere was used for **nine weeks, from 8 May to 10 July 1911**. Throughout that time the station's tests stood markedly higher. The day the station went back to its own glass, they fell to their old level. Nothing else changed: the same tester, herds, pasture, season, separator, scales and acid, under the same supervision.

Ansel saw it. He noted the fact in the test book and put **a question mark** against it. That summer he came home saying "it was something in the glass". He said it twice, at supper and again by the door, and Duncan, then seventeen, remembered it because that was not his way. But he would not write down what he could not prove. Against a suspicion stood the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county. He kept testing for **fourteen more years** and recorded every figure the glass gave him. Those figures later convicted him.

The same year Selby Vose became manager, the Farland college ran the bog-hay trial published as Bulletin No. 214, and in October Duncan left to teach at Ashlin.

**Why it matters.** This was an experiment with one variable, the measure, and it clears both the man and the milk: the same man's tests rose with different glass. No one acted on it at the time. The 1958 bulletin treats it as decisive, and the 1959 county report says it disposes of the theft charge. It does not show:
- the size of the error, since my sources give no figure for the higher tests;
- which piece of glass was at fault;
- whether the borrowed measure was itself exact;
- why the station's glass was short.

**Quantities:** nine weeks (8 May – 10 July 1911); tests "markedly higher"; fourteen more years of testing (1911–1925).

**Documents:** r22 ¶6, ¶8; r24 (8 May and October 1911); r18 §IV.


# Answer sheet — Part 2 (Section A, stories 5–8; Section B)

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### 5. "Station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual": the office and the water circular (1898–1925)

**Who:** the Association's office at Larrow Green: manager **Selby Vose** (from 1911), bookkeeper **Wilbur Teague** and clerk **Bertram Kyle**.

**What happened.** Every year the Ostrey Hollow station account carried three figures and a sentence:
- the butterfat credited to patrons on the station test;
- the butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream;
- the difference, entered as "station gain … five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account".

Teague wrote that line every year he served and never asked why. Kyle copied it, and it was read out at every annual meeting. More fat reached the creamery than the patrons were paid for. That line is the missing butterfat, recorded in ink.

For the arbitration the clerk totalled the full delivery years: **40,000,000 lb** of milk, or **1,600,000 lb a year for 25 years**. Butterfat averaged **25¢ a pound** over the period.
- The patrons were credited with **1,444,000 lb** (at 3.61%).
- The cream carried about **1,520,000 lb** (at 3.80%).
- The **76,000 lb** gain is **5.26% of what was credited** (the ledger's "5¼ per cent") and **5% of what was received**.

Selby Vose, the founder's son, became manager in 1911 and sat behind that door for a quarter-century. He was courteous to every caller, and he put his thumb on the bottom figure and signed without reading the lines above it.

In **June 1919** the office sent the Ostrey Hollow patrons a circular warning against watering milk. Kyle typed it and Vose alone signed it. Ansel Keddie would not mail it or leave it on the platform. He carried it himself to **eleven families** and stood at each door while it was read. Hazel Brant's mother read hers twice and put it in the stove. Two days later Emmet Brant stood in Ansel's yard and asked why he had carried a paper he did not believe. Ansel said he would not have another man carry it. Emmet never entered the house again, and the rift lasted until his death in 1929.

The water theory was false. Inspector Strawn used the lactometer at every visit from 1908 and never found added water. In any case, water cannot increase the fat reaching Larrow Green.

**Resolved:**
- **Eleven families**, from Orra's diary written that week, not Hazel's nine: she was a small child and remembers the letter chiefly for the stove.
- Kyle's dates are loose (a clerk "lives by what is on the desk"), but his figures are careful.

**Documents:** r23; r24; r17; r22 ¶7; r21.

### 6. The inspector's burette and the crate in the loft (1923–1925)

**Who:** **Merle Strawn**, the state dairy inspector on the Ordell circuit, who was related to no one in the valley.

**What happened.** On **12 June 1923**, on an ordinary visit, Strawn ran the Ostrey Hollow pipettes against a calibrated burette and found they would not deliver their mark. He condemned the whole lot on the spot and told the manager to order new ones. He came into the Larrow Green office to say so, and Kyle entered the order and filed Strawn's word as if it were "a broken window". The Association wrote to Ammon & Sons on 28 June for six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles, and Ammon replied on 2 July. The new glass was in use by **July 1923**; Orra's diary notes "new glass at the station and new pails, and the roof is still bad". Strawn recorded that he had condemned the glass, but nothing about what it had been doing for the twenty-six years before.

The condemned glass was crated and carried up the ladder into the loft over the intake room. It sat under a tarpaulin among sacks, a broken churn and wire, and stayed there for **thirty-five years**.

From 1924 the station's average test stood at **3.85** and stayed there. Ansel Keddie was still the tester, so the same man's figures jumped once the glass was new. No one connected the two facts.

Strawn kept a notebook for every year of the circuit, a little book with a rubber band round it. When he retired in 1936 the notebooks went to the state dairy office, and in 1947 to the library of the State Agricultural College at Farland. The 1923 volume sat there, unrequested, until 1958.

**Quantities:** condemned 12 June 1923; reorder of 6 dozen pipettes and 6 dozen bottles; average test 3.85 after 1923 against 3.61 before; crate in the loft 1923–1958.

**Resolved:**
- **The year is 1923.** Strawn's dated visit (r22), Ammon's dated letter (r20), Orra's July 1923 entry, r18 and r19 all agree. Kyle's "twenty-two" and Hazel's "twenty-one" are rejected.
- **Where the notebooks were in 1958:** Duncan's custody list (Farland library from 1947) is preferred to the *Chronicle*'s "loan … by the state dairy office", which may just echo the bulletin's acknowledgments.

**Documents:** r22 ¶11; r20; r23; r24; r18 §V; r19; r21.

### 7. The arbitration of 1925 and a tester turned out (1925–1948)

**Who:** Ansel Keddie; the Ostrey Hollow patrons, with Emmet Brant's name on their petition; the Association; a board of arbitration.

**What happened.** The patrons petitioned in **January 1925**, and the station test book was impounded in March. A board chaired by an Ashlin attorney sat from **17 March to 4 May 1925** in three sittings. The first was in the Cooperative's office at Ashlin. The other two were held in April in the station's weigh room, with farmers standing in the rain outside, directly under the loft where the condemned crate lay twenty feet up a ladder.

Four books went before the board:
- the station test book, in Keddie's hand;
- the receiving book;
- Emmet Brant's weigh book, which he lent and wanted back;
- Strawn's notebook for the year he condemned the glass.

No one set the second beside the fourth, and no one climbed the ladder. Three theories were argued: watered milk, a tester skimming cream (urged by an officer of the Association), and wet hay.

Ansel was dismissed on **30 April 1925**, "twenty-nine seasons and a letter", four days before the finding. He came home at his usual hour, hung his apron behind the shed door and ate ham. On **4 May** the board found no cause established and awarded the patrons **$3,800 "without admission"**, which was charged to the station account. Roy Brant never went to another Association meeting.

Ansel rebuilt the cow shed, whistling. At sixty-six he was working as a carpenter (the Loomis gate, stairs, a barn door that swung true for thirty years) for a quarter of his old pay. Emmet died in spring 1929 with the quarrel unmended. Ansel died at home in **February 1934**, after nine years under the charge. The men at his burial praised his hand with a door, and "not one of them said the other thing". He had once told Orra that "a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name". Orra died in 1948, aged 82.

**Quantities:** the $3,800 award was **one-fifth** of the $19,000 owed (story 8).

**Resolved:**
- **$3,800:** three independent witnesses agree, namely r22 (the county report), r23 (the clerk who charged it) and r19. Oram's $3,500 is secondhand from his father.
- **1925**, not Kyle's "twenty-four" or Hazel's "twenty-six".
- r22's "sat at Ashlin" is shorthand. Three sources (r18, r21, r24) put two sittings at the station.
- Duncan says Emmet sat at their Sunday table for thirty-six years up to 1925. Orra's diary, written at the time, records no visits after June 1919, and it is preferred.

**Documents:** r22 ¶10; r18 §IV–V; r21; r23; r24; r17; r19.

### 8. The granddaughter and the ladder: the case reopened (1958–1970)

**Who:** **Ivy Keddie** (b. 1931), Duncan's daughter and Ansel's granddaughter, a graduate student in dairy husbandry at the State Agricultural College at Farland; **Professor Leland Vaught**.

**What happened.** In 1958 Ivy gathered records that no one had put together:
- she borrowed Emmet Brant's weigh book from Roy Brant (a loan, and returned);
- she found Strawn's 1923 notebook in the Farland library;
- Rosalie Cudd, Jerome Cudd's daughter, copied the Alder Corners test figures for her on a stranger's word.

In **February 1958** she climbed into the station loft, found the crate under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff, and took it east. On **9 March 1958** she measured the surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette. Every one was marked 17.6 c.c., and every one delivered less by the same proportion, averaging **16.72 c.c. (95%)**.

**Bulletin No. 471** (October 1958, by Vaught and Keddie) concluded that the short glass alone explained the low tests: no milk was watered and no cream was stolen. Its arithmetic:
- true test 3.80 against a recorded 3.61, a gap of 0.19 points or 5%;
- over 25 full years, 40,000,000 lb of milk × 0.19% = **76,000 lb** of butterfat not credited;
- at 25¢ a pound that is **$19,000**, of which the 1925 award returned $3,800, **one-fifth**.

The *Ashlin Chronicle* ran "Old Ordell Wrong Righted" on 3 October 1958, thirty-three years after the dismissal and twenty-four after Ansel's death. Ivy told it: "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not." In 1959 county agent Orin Falke reported to the county board. After reading Bulletin 214 he withdrew his concession on hay, and he corrected "great-niece" to granddaughter.

**Where the records went:**
- The test book (in the Cooperative office 1925–59) and the crate went to the Ordell County Historical Rooms at Ashlin in 1959.
- The weigh book passed from Roy (d. 1962) to Hazel, who gave it to the Historical Rooms in 1963.
- Strawn's notebooks stayed at Farland.
- The Cudd book went to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970.
- The station had closed in 1946 and was then used to store hay tools.

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant are second cousins.

**Resolved:**
- **Granddaughter**, per r18, r17, r21 and r22's own correction, not the "great-niece" of r19 and r22.
- **$19,000** by arithmetic, not the *Chronicle*'s $19,600, which would spoil the exact one-fifth.
- **76,000 lb**, not Oram's 67,000 (transposed digits).
- **Pipettes measured:** nine (r21, r22) or eleven (r19). This cannot be settled, and it changes no figure.

**Documents:** r19; r22; r18 §V; r17; r21.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** Ansel (b. 1861) and Orra had a son, Duncan (b. 1894; r18 §I). Ivy (b. 1931) is Duncan's daughter: r18 says "which makes her my father's granddaughter", r17 says "Ivy is Duncan's girl", and r21 calls her "the granddaughter". Two sources say otherwise. r19 and r22 (¶2) call her his great-niece, but both come from the 1958 college press release and bulletin coverage, so they count as one source. r22's note says the error was checked and withdrawn, and r19 contradicts itself by saying her father taught school at Ashlin, which Duncan did. For great-niece to be true, Ansel would need a sibling whose grandchild Ivy is, and no source names any sibling of Ansel. So the answer is granddaughter.

**B2. Ivy and Hazel are second cousins.** Emmet Brant and Orra were siblings. Their sons Roy (Emmet's) and Duncan (Orra's) were therefore first cousins, and Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are second cousins. Two independent sources say so explicitly, Hazel's tape (r17) and Duncan's written notes (r18). Oram's "first cousin once removed" (r21) needs a generation gap that does not exist: both women are grandchildren of the sibling pair. **What the sources leave open:** whether Emmet and Orra were full siblings (r18: "his sister, the younger of the two") or half-siblings by their father's second wife (r17). If they were half-siblings, Roy and Duncan were strictly half first cousins, and Hazel and Ivy half second cousins. Orra's diary says only "my brother Emmet", and her pages from before 1889 were not kept, so this cannot be settled. The mothers of Hazel and Ivy are not named, and Hazel's birth year is not given, but neither affects the relationship.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose,** who organized the Association, was its first president and wrote its by-laws. r18 and r23 say independently that Selby was his son and each dismisses the "nephew" story as talk. This matters because the father's by-law article seven paid every patron on the butterfat test at his own station (r22). That rule turned a 5% error in the glass into money: under payment by weight, nobody would have lost a dollar. The son then ran the father's system. From 1911 he signed the station accounts with the "station gain" line every year, without reading the lines above the total, and in 1919 he signed the circular that blamed the patrons for water. It is my inference, not a sourced fact, that a founder's son had little reason to doubt the test his father's rule depended on.

**B4. No relationship is established, and the better evidence is that there was none.** Only r21 (Oram) says the Keddies and Voses were "cousins of some sort", and that is third-hand, from his mother's mother; he says he "could not draw you the line". r18 (Duncan, Ansel's son) denies any connection and explains where the talk came from: people confuse the family with the Ashlin Voses, who are not related to Gideon either. He adds that his mother, who could give the connection for every headstone in the valley, never once mentioned a Vose. r18's specific denial from inside the family, with an explanation for the rumour, outranks untraceable hearsay. A negative cannot be proved absolutely, but no source traces any actual line.


# Answer sheet — Part 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

**C1.**
- **(a) Recorded tests were 95% of the truth, i.e. 5% low.** Equivalently, the truth was 5.26% above the recorded figure. The pipettes were marked 17.6 c.c. and delivered on average 16.72 c.c., and 16.72 / 17.6 = 0.95. The bottle neck is ruled for a 17.6 c.c. charge, so a 5% short charge reads 5% low. *Resolution:* r19 prints the average as 16.27. That would give 0.9244 (7.56% low), a true average of 3.905 and a shortfall of 0.295 points, which contradicts the 0.19 given by r22 and r17. 16.72 fits every independent figure (the station average, Emmet's 1910 page, the ledger's 5¼%), so I treat 16.27 as a transposed print. This is inference, flagged.
- **(b) The true average test was 3.80**, from 3.61 / 0.95. **Recorded tests were 0.19 points short** (3.80 − 3.61). As a check, Larrow Green averaged 3.78, and the station averaged 3.85 once the glass was new.
- **(c) 76,000 lb.** Over 25 full delivery years the milk totalled 40,000,000 lb (25 × 1,600,000; r23). The true fat was 40,000,000 × 3.80% = 1,520,000 lb and the credited fat 40,000,000 × 3.61% = 1,444,000 lb, a difference of **76,000 lb**. r21's 67,000 is a digit transposition.
- **(d) $19,000**, from 76,000 lb × $0.25 (the long-run average price, r23). The patrons received $3,800, and 3,800 / 19,000 = **one-fifth (20%)**. r19's $19,600 does not follow from any figure in the sources. It would need 78,400 lb, and it would spoil the exact one-fifth, so it is rejected.

**C2. Emmet Brant in 1910** (200,000 lb of milk, paid on a test of 3.42; r17):
- **(a) Credited:** 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb**.
- **(b) Owed and not credited:** the true fat was 6,840 / 0.95 = 7,200 lb (a true test of 3.60), so **360 lb** was owed. His shortfall was 0.18 points, not 0.19, because the error is proportional: 5% of his own test.
- **(c) Value: $90** (360 × $0.25). This uses the long-run average price; the actual 1910 price is not in the sources I have.

**C3.** Over the same 25 full years:
- **(a) Credited:** **1,444,000 lb**.
- **(b) Reached Larrow Green:** about **1,520,000 lb**. That is 1,444,000 plus the ledger's gain of 5¼%: 1,444,000 × 1.0526 = 1,520,000.
- **(c) The difference is 76,000 lb.** That is **5.26% of the fat credited to patrons**, the base the ledger used for its "five and a quarter per cent" (1/19). It is **5.00% of the fat actually received**. The ledger took the smaller credited figure as its base, so its percentage is larger than the true loss rate.

**C4.**
- **(a) $77.93.** The line items add up to it: $25.20 + $31.68 + $0.90 + $18.00 + $2.15. The "$77.39" in r20's prose and in r19 is a transposition.
- **(b) 72 pipettes** (6 dozen at 35¢ = $25.20). **Each was about 0.88 c.c. short** (17.6 − 16.72). They were all short in the same proportion (r22), so each was short by about the average. r19's 16.27 would make it 1.33 c.c., which is rejected as above.

**C5.**
- **(a) In use from 1 May 1897 until it was condemned on 12 June 1923: about 26 years, 1 month and 11 days.** New glass took over in July 1923. **The money calculation covers 25 full delivery years, 1898–1922.** The two figures differ because the clerk's total of 40,000,000 lb (1,600,000 lb × 25) counts only full years. The partial seasons of 1897 (from May) and 1923 (to June) are left out, as are the nine weeks of 1911 when borrowed glass was used. So the $19,000 is a round figure that slightly understates the part-seasons and ignores the 1911 gap.
- **(b) Cannot be determined from the sources.** That first season (1896) was carried on the creamery's books, and those sheets were never filed with the station papers (r23). The only figure is Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds", which he got from men who were there and says is in no book he ruled. The Brant weigh book begins in 1897. In any case, that season used borrowed Larrow Green glass, so it lies outside the loss.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 experiment.** The station's own measure broke on **8 May 1911**, and borrowed glass was used until **10 July 1911**, **nine weeks** (r22; r24 dates the break).
- **Variable:** the measuring glass.
- **Held constant:** the tester, herds, pasture, season, separator, scales, acid and supervision.
- **Result:** tests rose markedly while the borrowed glass was used and fell back to their old level the day the station's own glass returned.

A calculation can only show that a 5% glass error would *fit* the numbers. The interval shows it *happened*. The low reading depended on the glass and not on the man, the cows or the hay, and it cuts against the theft theory, because the same man's tests rose.

It does **not** establish:
- how large the error was, since no figure for the higher tests is given in my sources;
- which item was faulty (pipette or bottle);
- that the borrowed glass was itself accurate;
- how the lot came to be short;
- that anyone with authority learned of it (Ansel only wrote a question mark).

**D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream.**
1. **The 1911 interval** (r22, from the station test book; the break is confirmed by Orra's diary, r24). The same man tested with different glass and the figures rose.
2. **The office ledger** (r23, Kyle, from the Association's own books). Every year Larrow Green *received* 5¼% more butterfat than the patrons were credited with. A tester skimming cream and writing low tests to hide it would make the creamery receive *less* fat than the tests implied, not more. The fat that was "missing" arrived at the creamery.
3. **After the new glass** (r22 ¶11, the county agent's figures). With new glass from July 1923, Keddie tested until April 1925, and the average rose to 3.85 and stayed there. The man was the same and only the glass had changed.
4. Supporting: the same Hessel Bottom milk tested 0.19 higher at Alder Corners (r17), and the shortfall was a flat 0.19 in every month (r17, r22). That fits an instrument error, not a thief's opportunities.

These reasons are independent because they come from separate records, kept by different people for different purposes: Keddie's test book and Orra's diary; Teague's office ledger; the post-1923 station averages; and a rival factory's test book in another county. Reasons 1–3 all hold even if the glass had never been measured in 1958. The ledger (2) is the strongest: it refutes theft on its own.

**Did the bookkeeper question the entries?** No. Wilbur Teague wrote the "station gain … as usual" line every year he served and "never asked anybody anything in forty years" (r23). The entry had a name, so it counted as explained. One caveat: r22 says only that the theft charge was "urged … by an officer of the Association". Naming Teague as the person who made it rests on the question's premise or on r01–r16, which I could not see.

**D3. Did the manager know, before 1923, that the tests ran low?** Yes, he knew the tests were low, though not from the ledger, and he did not know why.
- The June 1919 water circular went out over **Selby Vose's name alone** (r23). A warning to the Ostrey Hollow patrons about watering milk only makes sense if he knew their tests ran low.
- Patrons had complained at every annual meeting since 1902, and he had been manager since 1911 (r19, r22, r23).

Knowing the tests were low is not the same as understanding them. The ledger line he signed every year showed more fat arriving than was credited, which contradicts his own water theory. But Kyle watched him put his thumb on the bottom figure and sign without ever reading the lines above. So he knew the tests were low and blamed the patrons. There is no evidence he ever saw what his own books showed, and none that he suspected the glass. When Strawn condemned it, the office treated it "like a broken window".

## Section F — Theory

**F1. What caused the shortfall.**

**The cause.** The station's testing charge was measured with one lot of 72 Ammon & Sons pipettes, delivered in April 1897. They were marked 17.6 c.c. and each delivered about **16.72 c.c., 5% short**, probably from a reground mould, though that cannot be proved. The test bottle's neck is ruled for a full 17.6 c.c. charge, so every reading was **0.95 × the true fat**.

**Why that size.** On a true station average of 3.80, 5% is **0.19 points**, which is why 3.61 was recorded. On Emmet's 3.60 herd it was 0.18. The shortfall looked flat, "nineteen hundredths every month", because 5% of any test between about 3.5 and 4.0 is roughly 0.18–0.20. A proportional instrument error does not follow the seasons.

**Why it began when it did.** It began on **1 May 1897**, when the station stopped borrowing Larrow Green's glass and started using its own. The first complaint on record is from 1902.

**Why it ended when it did.** It ended in **June–July 1923**, when Strawn condemned the lot and new glass arrived. From 1924 the average was 3.85. It paused for **nine weeks in 1911**, when a borrowed measure was in use and the tests rose.

**Why nothing else had to change.** The cows, hay, scales (Emmet's scale agreed with the station's), acid, separator and tester were the same throughout. The butterfat itself was never lost: it went down in the cream and turned up at Larrow Green as "station gain, 5¼ per cent" of the credited fat. Only the *reading* was short. Because by-law article seven paid on the reading, the patrons lost about 76,000 lb, or $19,000, and got back $3,800.

**The three competing explanations**
1. **Added water.** Advanced by manager **Selby Vose** (the 1919 circular). *Defeated by:* Strawn used the lactometer at every visit from 1908 and never found water (r22). The ledger shows more fat reaching the creamery than was credited, which water cannot produce (r23). The weights on the Brant farm and at the station agreed (r17).
2. **The tester skimming cream and writing low tests.** Urged at the 1925 hearing by **an officer of the Association** (r22), and believed by the cream hauler (r21). *Defeated by:* the 1911 nine weeks (same man, higher tests); the 3.85 average under the same man once the glass was new in 1923; and the ledger gain, since a thief would have made the creamery receive *less*.
3. **Wet bog hay.** Advanced by the **1908 Association committee**, repeated by an **Ashlin veterinarian (1926)**, and partly conceded by county agent **Falke** (r22 ¶9). *Defeated by:* Bulletin No. 214, which found at most 0.05 in winter and nothing in summer, so it cannot explain 0.19 in July. The same milk tested 0.19 higher at Alder Corners, and the tests jumped in 1911 and 1923 with the same hay.

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glass?** **No.** The crate was in the loft over the weigh room, twenty feet above two of the sittings, from 1923 until Ivy found it in February 1958 under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff (r18, r21, r17, r22). Only Strawn's notebook recording the condemnation was before the board, and no one set it beside the test book (r23). The board "found no cause established".

**What did the tester believe?** From 1911, **the glass**. He told his family "it was something in the glass" and put a question mark in the test book. He never dared write down what he could not prove (r18, r22). He kept faith with his figures, and at the end he said only that "a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name" (r24).

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** From 1897 to 1923 the Ostrey Hollow station in the Larrow Valley measured every milk test with 72 Tarnet pipettes that delivered 5% less than marked. The Association paid farmers by butterfat test, so the station read 3.61 instead of 3.80. Patrons were underpaid about 76,000 lb of butterfat, roughly $19,000, while the fat quietly reached the creamery as a "station gain". Hay, watered milk and finally tester Ansel Keddie were blamed. In 1925 he was dismissed, and the patrons got $3,800 without admission. The answer lay in separate hands: a glass-house invoice, a farmer's weigh book, an unread ledger line, a wife's diary of the 1911 borrowed measure, an inspector's notebook, a rival factory's tests, and a condemned crate in the loft. In 1958 his granddaughter Ivy Keddie brought them together, measured the glass and cleared him.
