# Answer sheet — Part 2 (Section A, stories 5–8; Section B)

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### 5. "Station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual": the office and the water circular (1898–1925)

**Who:** the Association's office at Larrow Green: manager **Selby Vose** (from 1911), bookkeeper **Wilbur Teague** and clerk **Bertram Kyle**.

**What happened.** Every year the Ostrey Hollow station account carried three figures and a sentence:
- the butterfat credited to patrons on the station test;
- the butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream;
- the difference, entered as "station gain … five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account".

Teague wrote that line every year he served and never asked why. Kyle copied it, and it was read out at every annual meeting. More fat reached the creamery than the patrons were paid for. That line is the missing butterfat, recorded in ink.

For the arbitration the clerk totalled the full delivery years: **40,000,000 lb** of milk, or **1,600,000 lb a year for 25 years**. Butterfat averaged **25¢ a pound** over the period.
- The patrons were credited with **1,444,000 lb** (at 3.61%).
- The cream carried about **1,520,000 lb** (at 3.80%).
- The **76,000 lb** gain is **5.26% of what was credited** (the ledger's "5¼ per cent") and **5% of what was received**.

Selby Vose, the founder's son, became manager in 1911 and sat behind that door for a quarter-century. He was courteous to every caller, and he put his thumb on the bottom figure and signed without reading the lines above it.

In **June 1919** the office sent the Ostrey Hollow patrons a circular warning against watering milk. Kyle typed it and Vose alone signed it. Ansel Keddie would not mail it or leave it on the platform. He carried it himself to **eleven families** and stood at each door while it was read. Hazel Brant's mother read hers twice and put it in the stove. Two days later Emmet Brant stood in Ansel's yard and asked why he had carried a paper he did not believe. Ansel said he would not have another man carry it. Emmet never entered the house again, and the rift lasted until his death in 1929.

The water theory was false. Inspector Strawn used the lactometer at every visit from 1908 and never found added water. In any case, water cannot increase the fat reaching Larrow Green.

**Resolved:**
- **Eleven families**, from Orra's diary written that week, not Hazel's nine: she was a small child and remembers the letter chiefly for the stove.
- Kyle's dates are loose (a clerk "lives by what is on the desk"), but his figures are careful.

**Documents:** r23; r24; r17; r22 ¶7; r21.

### 6. The inspector's burette and the crate in the loft (1923–1925)

**Who:** **Merle Strawn**, the state dairy inspector on the Ordell circuit, who was related to no one in the valley.

**What happened.** On **12 June 1923**, on an ordinary visit, Strawn ran the Ostrey Hollow pipettes against a calibrated burette and found they would not deliver their mark. He condemned the whole lot on the spot and told the manager to order new ones. He came into the Larrow Green office to say so, and Kyle entered the order and filed Strawn's word as if it were "a broken window". The Association wrote to Ammon & Sons on 28 June for six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles, and Ammon replied on 2 July. The new glass was in use by **July 1923**; Orra's diary notes "new glass at the station and new pails, and the roof is still bad". Strawn recorded that he had condemned the glass, but nothing about what it had been doing for the twenty-six years before.

The condemned glass was crated and carried up the ladder into the loft over the intake room. It sat under a tarpaulin among sacks, a broken churn and wire, and stayed there for **thirty-five years**.

From 1924 the station's average test stood at **3.85** and stayed there. Ansel Keddie was still the tester, so the same man's figures jumped once the glass was new. No one connected the two facts.

Strawn kept a notebook for every year of the circuit, a little book with a rubber band round it. When he retired in 1936 the notebooks went to the state dairy office, and in 1947 to the library of the State Agricultural College at Farland. The 1923 volume sat there, unrequested, until 1958.

**Quantities:** condemned 12 June 1923; reorder of 6 dozen pipettes and 6 dozen bottles; average test 3.85 after 1923 against 3.61 before; crate in the loft 1923–1958.

**Resolved:**
- **The year is 1923.** Strawn's dated visit (r22), Ammon's dated letter (r20), Orra's July 1923 entry, r18 and r19 all agree. Kyle's "twenty-two" and Hazel's "twenty-one" are rejected.
- **Where the notebooks were in 1958:** Duncan's custody list (Farland library from 1947) is preferred to the *Chronicle*'s "loan … by the state dairy office", which may just echo the bulletin's acknowledgments.

**Documents:** r22 ¶11; r20; r23; r24; r18 §V; r19; r21.

### 7. The arbitration of 1925 and a tester turned out (1925–1948)

**Who:** Ansel Keddie; the Ostrey Hollow patrons, with Emmet Brant's name on their petition; the Association; a board of arbitration.

**What happened.** The patrons petitioned in **January 1925**, and the station test book was impounded in March. A board chaired by an Ashlin attorney sat from **17 March to 4 May 1925** in three sittings. The first was in the Cooperative's office at Ashlin. The other two were held in April in the station's weigh room, with farmers standing in the rain outside, directly under the loft where the condemned crate lay twenty feet up a ladder.

Four books went before the board:
- the station test book, in Keddie's hand;
- the receiving book;
- Emmet Brant's weigh book, which he lent and wanted back;
- Strawn's notebook for the year he condemned the glass.

No one set the second beside the fourth, and no one climbed the ladder. Three theories were argued: watered milk, a tester skimming cream (urged by an officer of the Association), and wet hay.

Ansel was dismissed on **30 April 1925**, "twenty-nine seasons and a letter", four days before the finding. He came home at his usual hour, hung his apron behind the shed door and ate ham. On **4 May** the board found no cause established and awarded the patrons **$3,800 "without admission"**, which was charged to the station account. Roy Brant never went to another Association meeting.

Ansel rebuilt the cow shed, whistling. At sixty-six he was working as a carpenter (the Loomis gate, stairs, a barn door that swung true for thirty years) for a quarter of his old pay. Emmet died in spring 1929 with the quarrel unmended. Ansel died at home in **February 1934**, after nine years under the charge. The men at his burial praised his hand with a door, and "not one of them said the other thing". He had once told Orra that "a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name". Orra died in 1948, aged 82.

**Quantities:** the $3,800 award was **one-fifth** of the $19,000 owed (story 8).

**Resolved:**
- **$3,800:** three independent witnesses agree, namely r22 (the county report), r23 (the clerk who charged it) and r19. Oram's $3,500 is secondhand from his father.
- **1925**, not Kyle's "twenty-four" or Hazel's "twenty-six".
- r22's "sat at Ashlin" is shorthand. Three sources (r18, r21, r24) put two sittings at the station.
- Duncan says Emmet sat at their Sunday table for thirty-six years up to 1925. Orra's diary, written at the time, records no visits after June 1919, and it is preferred.

**Documents:** r22 ¶10; r18 §IV–V; r21; r23; r24; r17; r19.

### 8. The granddaughter and the ladder: the case reopened (1958–1970)

**Who:** **Ivy Keddie** (b. 1931), Duncan's daughter and Ansel's granddaughter, a graduate student in dairy husbandry at the State Agricultural College at Farland; **Professor Leland Vaught**.

**What happened.** In 1958 Ivy gathered records that no one had put together:
- she borrowed Emmet Brant's weigh book from Roy Brant (a loan, and returned);
- she found Strawn's 1923 notebook in the Farland library;
- Rosalie Cudd, Jerome Cudd's daughter, copied the Alder Corners test figures for her on a stranger's word.

In **February 1958** she climbed into the station loft, found the crate under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff, and took it east. On **9 March 1958** she measured the surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette. Every one was marked 17.6 c.c., and every one delivered less by the same proportion, averaging **16.72 c.c. (95%)**.

**Bulletin No. 471** (October 1958, by Vaught and Keddie) concluded that the short glass alone explained the low tests: no milk was watered and no cream was stolen. Its arithmetic:
- true test 3.80 against a recorded 3.61, a gap of 0.19 points or 5%;
- over 25 full years, 40,000,000 lb of milk × 0.19% = **76,000 lb** of butterfat not credited;
- at 25¢ a pound that is **$19,000**, of which the 1925 award returned $3,800, **one-fifth**.

The *Ashlin Chronicle* ran "Old Ordell Wrong Righted" on 3 October 1958, thirty-three years after the dismissal and twenty-four after Ansel's death. Ivy told it: "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not." In 1959 county agent Orin Falke reported to the county board. After reading Bulletin 214 he withdrew his concession on hay, and he corrected "great-niece" to granddaughter.

**Where the records went:**
- The test book (in the Cooperative office 1925–59) and the crate went to the Ordell County Historical Rooms at Ashlin in 1959.
- The weigh book passed from Roy (d. 1962) to Hazel, who gave it to the Historical Rooms in 1963.
- Strawn's notebooks stayed at Farland.
- The Cudd book went to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970.
- The station had closed in 1946 and was then used to store hay tools.

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant are second cousins.

**Resolved:**
- **Granddaughter**, per r18, r17, r21 and r22's own correction, not the "great-niece" of r19 and r22.
- **$19,000** by arithmetic, not the *Chronicle*'s $19,600, which would spoil the exact one-fifth.
- **76,000 lb**, not Oram's 67,000 (transposed digits).
- **Pipettes measured:** nine (r21, r22) or eleven (r19). This cannot be settled, and it changes no figure.

**Documents:** r19; r22; r18 §V; r17; r21.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** Ansel (b. 1861) and Orra had a son, Duncan (b. 1894; r18 §I). Ivy (b. 1931) is Duncan's daughter: r18 says "which makes her my father's granddaughter", r17 says "Ivy is Duncan's girl", and r21 calls her "the granddaughter". Two sources say otherwise. r19 and r22 (¶2) call her his great-niece, but both come from the 1958 college press release and bulletin coverage, so they count as one source. r22's note says the error was checked and withdrawn, and r19 contradicts itself by saying her father taught school at Ashlin, which Duncan did. For great-niece to be true, Ansel would need a sibling whose grandchild Ivy is, and no source names any sibling of Ansel. So the answer is granddaughter.

**B2. Ivy and Hazel are second cousins.** Emmet Brant and Orra were siblings. Their sons Roy (Emmet's) and Duncan (Orra's) were therefore first cousins, and Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are second cousins. Two independent sources say so explicitly, Hazel's tape (r17) and Duncan's written notes (r18). Oram's "first cousin once removed" (r21) needs a generation gap that does not exist: both women are grandchildren of the sibling pair. **What the sources leave open:** whether Emmet and Orra were full siblings (r18: "his sister, the younger of the two") or half-siblings by their father's second wife (r17). If they were half-siblings, Roy and Duncan were strictly half first cousins, and Hazel and Ivy half second cousins. Orra's diary says only "my brother Emmet", and her pages from before 1889 were not kept, so this cannot be settled. The mothers of Hazel and Ivy are not named, and Hazel's birth year is not given, but neither affects the relationship.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose,** who organized the Association, was its first president and wrote its by-laws. r18 and r23 say independently that Selby was his son and each dismisses the "nephew" story as talk. This matters because the father's by-law article seven paid every patron on the butterfat test at his own station (r22). That rule turned a 5% error in the glass into money: under payment by weight, nobody would have lost a dollar. The son then ran the father's system. From 1911 he signed the station accounts with the "station gain" line every year, without reading the lines above the total, and in 1919 he signed the circular that blamed the patrons for water. It is my inference, not a sourced fact, that a founder's son had little reason to doubt the test his father's rule depended on.

**B4. No relationship is established, and the better evidence is that there was none.** Only r21 (Oram) says the Keddies and Voses were "cousins of some sort", and that is third-hand, from his mother's mother; he says he "could not draw you the line". r18 (Duncan, Ansel's son) denies any connection and explains where the talk came from: people confuse the family with the Ashlin Voses, who are not related to Gideon either. He adds that his mother, who could give the connection for every headstone in the valley, never once mentioned a Vose. r18's specific denial from inside the family, with an explanation for the rumour, outranks untraceable hearsay. A negative cannot be proved absolutely, but no source traces any actual line.
