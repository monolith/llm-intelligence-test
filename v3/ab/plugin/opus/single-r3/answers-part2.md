# Answer Sheet — Part 2 (Section A, stories 5–8; Section B)

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### 5. The factory over the ridge: Alder Corners (1913–1930)

**The factory.** Jerome Cudd built a cheese factory at Alder Corners in Vessey County, on the far side of Redlow Ridge. His daughter says he built it in 1913; Roy and Rundle say it opened in 1914. The two can both be true: built one year and opened the next (my inference). The ridge road is four miles of switchback and is closed from November to April or May.

**The four men.** From **1915** four of the six Hessel Bottom farmers hauled over the ridge in the summer flush, from about 1 June to mid-September, and took their milk to the station for the rest of the year. They did this for **sixteen seasons**, the last in **1930**. Roy Brant did not go. Hazel called it "the only honest arithmetic anybody in that valley ever did without meaning to."

**Cudd's tests.**
- He tested with his own new glassware and paid on it.
- He never compared his figures with the station's.
- He never docked the men, and cheese exposes watered milk at once.
- In 1919, the year of the water circular, he wrote: "3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground."

His yearly averages all fall between 3.78 and 3.82, about **3.80** overall:

| Year | Test | Year | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1915 | 3.79 | 1923 | 3.81 |
| 1916 | 3.81 | 1924 | 3.80 |
| 1917 | 3.78 | 1925 | 3.79 |
| 1918 | 3.80 | 1926 | 3.82 |
| 1919 | 3.80 | 1927 | 3.78 |
| 1920 | 3.82 | 1928 | 3.80 |
| 1921 | 3.79 | 1929 | 3.81 |
| 1922 | 3.78 | 1930 | 3.79 |

**Why it matters.** The same cows, on the same wet ground, in the same summers, tested 3.80 at a factory where Keddie had no hand. Meanwhile the station's figures for those men ran about 0.19 lower until the glass was replaced. That removes both the land and the tester as causes, and it matches the true figure of 3.80.

**The record.** Jerome died in March 1941. His daughter **Rosalie**, who was twelve at the first vat, ran the factory for seventeen years. In 1958 she copied the figures for Ivy Keddie on a stranger's request. [She is Jerome's daughter by her own letter, Rundle and Duncan; Ivy's talk and the Chronicle wrongly call her his niece.] The book itself never left Vessey County and went to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970.

**An unresolved figure.** Ivy's "fourteen overlapping seasons" does not fit the table. The overlap with the short-glass years is 1915–1922, eight full seasons, plus part of 1923.

### 6. "Station gain … as usual": the ledger and the water circular (1898–1922)

**The ledger line.** Every year the Association's station account for Ostrey Hollow carried three figures and a phrase:
1. butterfat credited to the patrons on the station test;
2. butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream;
3. the difference, entered as **"station gain — five and a quarter per cent, as usual"** and carried to the general account.

**The figures.** The 1898 entry reads **57,760 lb** credited, **60,800 lb** received and **3,040 lb** gain. That is exactly 1,600,000 lb of milk at 3.61 and at 3.80. The gain is 5¼% (5.26%) of the fat *credited*, which is the same thing as **5%** of the fat actually delivered. Over 1898–1922, on 40,000,000 lb of milk, the totals are:
- credited: 1,444,000 lb;
- received: 1,520,000 lb;
- gain: 76,000 lb.

**Everyone handled it:**
- **Wilbur Teague**, bookkeeper 1905–1945, wrote the line every year. He later claimed it as his own 1905 device, which is impossible because the 1898 entry already says "as usual." He also misread it as 5¼% of the actual fat.
- **Bertram Kyle**, the clerk, copied it out in a fair hand for the annual meeting.
- **Hobart Sill**, the buttermaker, found the Hollow cream always ran heavy against the station sheets. He weighed it again himself and entered it in the receiving book, then put it down to "cream's nature."
- **Selby Vose**, manager 1913–1938, signed the sheets with his thumb on the total. [1913 comes from Effie and from Vose's own "thirteen years" in 1926; Kyle's 1911 is rejected.]

**What the line meant.** It was the error sitting in plain sight: fat the patrons had delivered but not been credited with turned up in the cream at Larrow Green. It also clears the tester, because cream stolen at the station would have arrived *light*.

**The Association blamed the patrons instead.** In June 1919 Vose's circular, typed by Kyle and signed by Vose alone, warned the Hollow patrons about water ("cannot pay for creek water").
- The same year the state inspector Strawn found no water and told the manager so.
- Keddie carried the circulars round himself.
- Sill praised the circular.
- Teague had already raised the matter with Vose in 1916 and was told he was "bookkeeper and not its conscience."
- Dell Rundle's jubilee pamphlet (1939) called cream theft the "settled opinion." Her 1959 reprint added a note in press retracting that chapter, because the ledger's station gain proves the cream arrived heavy.

Documents: r02, r03, r05, r06, r07, r08, r12, r14, r23, r24.

### 7. The glass condemned and the tester dismissed (1923–1925)

**The condemnation.** Merle Strawn was the state dairy inspector on the Ordell circuit from 1907 to 1936 and was kin to nobody in the valley. He was issued burettes in 1921 and did not run the station's glass in 1922.
- On **12 June 1923** he ran the Ostrey Hollow pipettes against his burette, found they would not deliver their mark, and condemned the whole crate: "Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them."
- The Association reordered from Ammon & Sons on **28 June**: six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles.
- Ammon replied on **2 July** that its "books of the nineties" could tell nothing about the lot.
- New glass was in use from **July 1923**, and the old crate went up into the loft over the intake room.

[Vose's and Rundle's "12 July" is impossible, since it would fall after the 28 June order. Hazel's 1921 and Kyle's 1922 are wrong.]

**The rise.** With the same tester, the same herds and the same undrained land, the station's **1924 average was 3.80** (Strawn's notebook, Keddie). [Vose's 3.90 and Falke's 3.85 are rejected.]

**The arbitration.** The patrons petitioned in January 1925. A board of three sat from **17 March to 4 May 1925** under **Alonzo Frick**, an Ashlin attorney who chaired as "the one who kept no cow."
- The first session was in the Association office at Ashlin. The second and third were in the station's weigh room, directly under the crate.
- The exhibits were **four books**: the test book (4 May 1896–31 December 1924), the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book, and Strawn's 1923 notebook.
- The crate was never mentioned.
- Three explanations were argued, and none was accepted: water (Vose); cream-taking (Teague: "Cream is portable… The remedy is two men or a lock"); and the 1908 hay report.

**The outcome.** Keddie's statement of 17 March ran nine sentences: thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, he had never taken a pound of cream, and he could not say why.
- He was **dismissed on 30 April 1925**, four days before the award, and wrote a letter he never sent.
- On **4 May** the board awarded the patrons **$3,800 "without admission,"** charged to the station account. [Vira's and Cleve's $3,500 is rejected against the secretary, the chairman and the clerk.]
- Keddie took up carpentry and died in **February 1934**.

Documents: r02, r03, r05, r06, r07, r08, r13, r18, r20, r21, r23, r24.

### 8. The crate in the loft: Bulletin 471 (1958–1972)

**Ivy's five records.** **Ivy Keddie** (born 1931), Ansel's granddaughter and a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, put five records side by side:
- the station test book;
- the Brant weigh book, lent by Roy;
- the Alder Corners figures, copied by Rosalie Cudd;
- Merle Strawn's twenty-eight circuit notebooks, which went to the state dairy office in 1936 and the Farland library in 1947, where she found the 1923 volume;
- the Association ledgers.

**The crate.** In **February 1958** she climbed the ladder into the loft over the old station's intake room. Under a tarpaulin and chaff she found the crate, stenciled AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET. **Nine pipettes** survived, two of them chipped.

**The measurement.** On **9 March 1958** at Farland she measured them against a calibrated burette. The mean was **16.72 c.c.** against the 17.6 c.c. mark: 0.88 c.c. short, or **0.95** of the charge, one part in twenty. A short charge in a bottle ruled for the full charge reads low in proportion:
- milk of 3.80 read as 3.61, 0.19 short;
- a herd recorded at 3.42 was 0.18 short;
- a herd recorded at 4.56 was 0.24 short.

**Bulletin No. 471** (October 1958, by Leland Vaught and Ivy Keddie) set out the case:
- the pipette is the one instrument a tester cannot check at the bench;
- the 1911 interval, unread for 47 years, is decisive;
- over 1898–1922, 40,000,000 lb × 0.0019 = **76,000 lb of fat × 25¢ = $19,000**, of which the $3,800 award was **one fifth**.

Vaught's own body text carries wrong figures (five dozen pipettes, 16.27 c.c., 1898, 118,000 lb), which his conclusion corrects.

**The aftermath:**
- The *Ashlin Chronicle* (3 October 1958) repeated several errors and accused Ammon & Sons of knowingly selling a short lot.
- The firm's historian, Junia Ammon, had already written in 1949 that a reground mold probably produced a short lot by accident. Checking pieces against each other cannot catch an error that is the same in every piece, and the works books burned in 1931, so **the cause of the short lot cannot be determined**.
- In 1959 Ivy told the Ordell County Historical Society that it was 35 years from condemnation to cause and 33 since the dismissal; Ansel had died in 1934 without knowing.
- Falke's 1959 report to the county found that no action lay.
- The crate and the test book went to the Historical Rooms at Ashlin in 1959 and the weigh book in 1963. Halbert Nym catalogued the collection in 1972.

Documents: r04, r07, r11, r12, r17, r18, r19, r20, r21, r22.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** Duncan Keddie states that he is Ansel's son and that Ivy (born 1931) is his daughter, and complains that he has seen it printed otherwise. Hazel ("Ivy is Duncan's girl"), Cleve Oram ("the granddaughter"), Ivy's own 1959 talk and Orra's diary editor all agree. The *Ashlin Chronicle* (1958) called her his "great-niece." That was a rushed piece written from a press release, and Falke's 1959 report copied the error and then corrected it in a note ("I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter"). The great-niece version is therefore a single error with one derivative, and it is outweighed by two family members with first-hand knowledge.

**B2. Ivy and Hazel were second cousins.** The derivation runs:
1. Emmet Brant and Orra (Brant) Keddie were siblings.
2. Their children, Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie, were first cousins.
3. Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy were therefore second cousins.

Duncan, Hazel and Roy all derive it this way. Vira Toland and Cleve Oram say "first cousin once removed," which would require the two women to be a generation apart; they were not. **What the sources leave open** is whether Emmet and Orra were full or half siblings:
- Roy calls Orra Emmet's full sister.
- Hazel says she was a half-sister "by his father's second wife."
- Duncan says only "sister, the younger of the two."
- Orra's own papers from before 1889 do not survive.

If they were half siblings, Roy and Duncan were half first cousins and Hazel and Ivy were half second cousins. The age gap (Emmet born 1854, Orra 1866) is consistent with a second wife but does not prove one. This cannot be determined.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose**, who organized the Association in 1889, wrote its by-laws and was its first president (1889–1911). Effie calls Selby the "son of the late president," Selby himself speaks of "my father's Jerseys," and Duncan and Kyle both insist on son. Vira ("old Gideon Vose's nephew") and Rundle say nephew; that is rejected against Selby's own words and the secretary's.

Why it matters: the manager was the son of the man who wrote the rule that made the station's test final (Article VII).
- He treated the shortfall as the patrons' dishonesty, not as a fault in the Association's own measure: the 1919 water circular ("cannot pay for creek water"; "Bring it as it left the cow").
- He rebuffed his own bookkeeper in 1916.
- He signed the ledger sheets without reading the line that showed the error.
- In 1926 he still called the award "a generous settlement of an unproved claim."

That he was defending his father's institution is my inference; the sources do not say so outright.

**B4. The Vose–Keddie relationship: none is established, and it cannot be proved either way.** Vira says Orra Keddie and "old Mrs. Vose" were first cousins, and Cleve says the families were "cousins of some sort," from his mother's mother. Duncan answers that there was no connection whatever:
- The Keddies were confused with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Gideon either.
- The story had been town talk since before he could walk; he had heard it from two women in town.
- His mother, who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection," never put a Vose in it.

The two affirmative accounts are not independent. Both are valley talk, the same stream Duncan describes, and neither names a line of descent. Duncan's denial is first-hand family knowledge but rests on negative evidence, and Orra's papers from before 1889 were not preserved. The weight of evidence is against any kinship. Separately, the "nephew" version of Selby, which travels with the same rumour, is wrong.
