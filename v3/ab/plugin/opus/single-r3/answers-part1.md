### 1. The Association, Article VII and the station at Ostrey Hollow (1889–1897)

**The Association.** Larrow Valley farmers organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association in 1889, meeting in the Larrow Green schoolhouse. Gideon Vose was president from 1889 to 1911 and wrote the by-laws; he died in 1917. The creamery was built on Larrow Creek at Larrow Green in 1891. The office was at Ashlin, over a harness shop, and Effie Loomis was secretary from 1901 to 1940.

**Article VII.** Every patron is paid on the pounds of butterfat his milk shows by test, and "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." Under payment by weight, nothing that happened later would have cost anyone a dollar; under Article VII the station test was final. The year it was adopted **cannot be determined**:
- Rundle says 1891, Effie 1892 and Grigg 1893 ("as I have always understood").
- The printed by-laws carry no date.
- The minute book for 1889–92 was lost in the 1938 office move.

**The station.** The skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley, was built in the winter of 1895–96 to serve Hessel Bottom. It opened on 4 May 1896, the day Ansel Keddie (born 1861) began the test book. He was tester and separator man for twenty-nine seasons, 1896–1924. Milk was weighed in the intake (weigh) room, sampled, given the Babcock test and separated. The cream went down to Larrow Green daily by wagon, and the skim went back to the farmer. A loft over the intake room was reached by a ladder.

**The first season's glass.** For the first season the station borrowed glass from Larrow Green: in May 1896 the buttermaker Hobart Sill made up an outfit from his own glass. It came back the next spring with one piece chipped. That season tested **3.80**. [Keddie's letter says the glass came "off the Ashlin creamery"; rejected against his own first leaf, Sill, Orra's diary and Nym, all of which say Larrow Green.]

**The station's own outfit.** Ammon & Sons Dairy Supply of Tarnet invoiced it on 14 April 1897:

| Item | Cost |
|---|---|
| 6 dozen milk-test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c., at 35¢ | $25.20 |
| 12 dozen test bottles | $31.68 |
| Acid measure | $0.90 |
| Four-bottle hand tester | $18.00 |
| Freight | $2.15 |
| **Total** | **$77.93** |

The outfit went into use on **1 May 1897**, when Orra wrote that they were "done borrowing of Larrow Green." [Vaught's and Kyle's "1 May 1898" is rejected against the dated invoice and the diary.]

**First-season milk.** How much milk the station took in its first season is not recorded anywhere. Sill's 900,000 lb and Kyle's 1,100,000 lb are both hearsay, and Nym found no figure.

### 2. Hessel Bottom, the Brant weigh book and a family divided (1897–1929)

**The Bottom.** Hessel Bottom is a wet meadow of about 160 acres below the station. It is spring-fed, partly under water every spring, and cut for sedge hay. **Six farms** worked it (Roy, Hazel, Grigg's report, Kepp, Falke) [Effie's "seven" is outnumbered], and a farm might hold more than one family. The largest patron was **Emmet Brant** (1854–1929), with sixty-odd acres and 38 cows.

**The weigh book.** Emmet weighed his milk on his own scale before it went on the wagon, and wrote the station's intake weight beside it. The book was begun on **1 January 1897**, the date it bore as an exhibit to the 1925 board (Frick) and as later catalogued (Nym, Duncan, Ivy). [Roy's 1898 and Hazel's 1900 are rejected; Hazel herself says she does not keep years.] The two columns always agreed, so the weights were never in question.

**The 1910 leaf.** It reads **200,000 lb "even,"** underlined, paid on a test of **3.42**, with fat at **24¢** that year.
- Credited: 6,840 lb of fat.
- True content: 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60, or 7,200 lb.
- Short: 360 lb, about $86.40.

**Where the book went.** Emmet's son Roy had it from 1929. Roy *lent* it, not gave it, to Ivy Keddie in 1958. When Roy died in 1962 it went to his daughter Hazel, who gave it to the Historical Rooms at Ashlin in 1963.

**The tester was family.** Orra Brant (born 1866, died 1948), Emmet's younger sister, married Ansel Keddie in 1889. Their son Duncan (born 1894) taught school at Ashlin, and Duncan's daughter was Ivy (born 1931). So the man later accused of shorting the patrons was married to the sister of the patron who lost the most.

**The break in 1919.** It came in June 1919 with the manager's water circular.
- Ansel would not mail the circulars or leave them on the platform; he carried them to the patrons himself.
- Two days later Emmet stood in Orra's yard, asked why Ansel had carried a paper he did not believe, and never came into the house again. Roy puts the cause as Ansel not standing up against the circular.
- In 1925 Keddie wrote that he had not spoken with Emmet since 1919. [Duncan's "at our table… thirty-six years" is rejected against Orra's contemporaneous diary, Keddie, Roy and Vira.]
- In January 1925 Emmet's name was on the patrons' petition.
- Emmet died in the spring of 1929, unreconciled. Orra went down to his kitchen, "where I was a girl."

Documents: r01, r02, r03, r07, r09, r13, r17, r18, r24.

### 3. The complaint and the hay theory (1902–1926)

**The complaint.** From **1902** the Ostrey Hollow patrons complained that their milk tested below the valley's. Emmet Brant was among the first, and Orra heard the talk at the store that March. Over the long period the station averaged **3.61** against Larrow Green's **3.78**. The shortfall was **0.19** of a point and flat: the same in February as in July (Roy, Hazel, Falke).

**The 1908 committee.** In January 1908 the board named a committee of three under **Nyle Grigg** of Larrow Green, who hauled to the creamery and was not a patron of the station. Orra saw four men arrive in Grigg's sleigh; the fourth was presumably a driver (my inference).
- They visited all six farms, drew and sealed their own samples, and watched the tests made.
- They tried the scales against a known weight and asked about the water at each barn.
- The printed report says they sampled **January, February and March**, and Orra's diary has them gone back down in March. [Grigg's 1919 claim of "the whole of 1908, every month" contradicts his own printed report.]
- They found no dishonesty ("the milk is whole and the scales are honest"), but judged that "what such land gives in quantity it withholds in quality." Their verdict was that the wet hay thins the cream, and their remedy was to drain the Bottom.
- Emmet answered that the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not been low.
- The question stayed closed for eleven years.

**Bulletin No. 214.** The State Agricultural College at Farland ran a feeding trial on bog hay (in 1911) and published it as Bulletin No. 214. Its summary, as printed by Falke: bog hay as the whole roughage depressed the test "by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."
- Grigg's 1919 reprint cited it as "two tenths" in winter.
- Dr. Roland Kepp, the Ashlin veterinarian, repeated "two tenths" in his 1926 Farm Club paper. He added his own "fifth of a point" and a gap of "0.21," and waved off the 1911 interval as "six weeks" of summer. He had never read a test himself.

**Why the hay fails.** At most 0.05, and only in winter, cannot account for 0.19 that was full-size in July; it is about a quarter of the size and the wrong shape. The county agent Orin Falke conceded hay as a partial cause in 1959, then withdrew the concession once he had read Bulletin 214.

### 4. The borrowed measure, 8 May–10 July 1911

**The break.** On **8 May 1911** a pipette broke at the station. Keddie's book reads "May 8 pipette broke… borrowed measure," and that night Orra wrote that A. was home "cross about a broken measure." For **nine weeks**, until **10 July**, the tests were made with a borrowed measure.

**The result.** Throughout those weeks the Hollow tested **3.80**, the Larrow Green figure, instead of 3.61. On 10 July Keddie went back to his own measure and the figures dropped at once: "July 10 own measure… Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."

**What stayed the same.** The tester, bottles, acid, hand tester, scales, herds, pasture and hay were all unchanged. The switch back came in the middle of the summer flush, so the season did not change either. The only variable was the measuring glass, and the error followed it.

**Keddie's reaction.** Duncan, then seventeen, remembers his father coming home and saying twice, at supper and again at the door, that it was "something in the glass." He wrote only a question mark, because he would not put down what he could not prove. Strawn's notebook for June 1911 says "Nothing here to note."

**Where the borrowed measure came from cannot be determined:**
- Ivy says it came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon.
- Teague says it came "from town."
- Roy says "the state man left his own measure that June." That fits neither the 8 May start nor Strawn's silence.

[Teague's and Kepp's "six weeks" are rejected against the dated entries, which give nine. Kepp's "summer figures" objection fails because the gap was the same in July as in February, and the drop came in mid-July.]

**Why it matters.** The entries lay unread for **47 years** until Bulletin 471 (1958) treated them as decisive: a step of 0.19 on 3.80 milk, a factor of 0.95, switched on and off by a change of glass alone. The interval proves that the station's own measure caused the shortfall. It does not measure the pipettes, show that the whole lot was short, or explain why they were short.

Documents: r03, r04, r05, r06, r09, r11, r16, r17, r18, r22, r24.
