# Answer Sheet — Part 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

**C1.**
- **(a) Tests read 5% low, a factor of 0.95 (one part in twenty).** The Babcock bottle neck is ruled for a 17.6 c.c. charge. The nine surviving pipettes delivered a mean of 16.72 c.c. (Ivy's measurement, 9 March 1958). The reading is proportional to the charge, so reading = true × 16.72 / 17.6 = true × **0.95**.
  - Check: 3.61 / 3.80 = 0.95.
  - [Vaught's 16.27 gives 0.9244 and a true test of 3.905, which is inconsistent with the 3.80 recorded in 1924 on new glass; rejected.]
- **(b) The true average was 3.80.** 3.61 / 0.95 = **3.80**. The recorded tests were short by 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19** of a point.
- **(c) 76,000 lb of fat were not credited.** The station received 40,000,000 lb of milk in the full delivery years 1898–1922 (Kyle, who compiled the figure; 1.6 million lb a year × 25 years).
  - True fat: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb.
  - Credited: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb.
  - Not credited: **76,000 lb**.
- **(d) $19,000, of which the patrons received one fifth.** 76,000 lb × $0.25 (the average fat price, which Kyle was set to find) = **$19,000**. The award was $3,800, and $3,800 / $19,000 = **1/5 (20%)**.

**C2.** Emmet Brant, 1910: 200,000 lb of milk, recorded test 3.42.
- **(a)** Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of fat.
- **(b)** True test: 3.42 / 0.95 = 3.60, so true fat = 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb. Owed and not credited: **360 lb** (a shortfall of 0.18 of a point, matching Ivy's "3.42 herd short 0.18").
- **(c)** At the 1910 price of 24¢ (Roy): 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40**. At the 25¢ long-run average it would be $90.00.

**C3.** Over 1898–1922:
- **(a)** Credited to the patrons: **1,444,000 lb**.
- **(b)** Received at Larrow Green in the station cream: **1,520,000 lb** (the true fat).
- **(c)** The difference is 76,000 lb:
  - as a percentage **of the fat credited**: 76,000 / 1,444,000 = **5.26% (≈ 5¼%)**. This is the ledger's "five and a quarter per cent, as usual."
  - as a percentage **of the fat actually delivered and received**: 76,000 / 1,520,000 = **5.00%**.
  - Check with the 1898 entry: 3,040 / 57,760 = 5.26%, and 3,040 / 60,800 = 5.00%.
  - Teague and Vaught wrongly call 5¼% a percentage of the actual fat.

**C4.**
- **(a)** **$77.93**. The line items sum to that: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93. [Junia Ammon's prose and the Chronicle give $77.39, which is a transposition.]
- **(b)** **72 pipettes** (6 dozen; $25.20 / $0.35 = 72). Each was short by about **0.88 c.c.** (17.60 − 16.72). This is the mean of the nine survivors; applying it to all 72 assumes the lot was uniform, which the proportional, flat error supports.

**C5.**
- **(a) Two different spans.**
  - **How long the glass was in use:** from **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923**, when it was condemned, which is **26 years, 1 month and 11 days** (about 26.1 years). Replacements arrived in July, so it may have stayed in use a few more weeks. It was out of use for the nine weeks of 1911.
  - **The money calculation:** **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922**.
  - **Why they differ:** 1897 (from 1 May) and 1923 (to June/July) were part years. Keddie's summary marks 1897 as "part year," and the 40,000,000 lb series covers whole years only. 1896 is excluded because it was tested on Larrow Green's accurate glass (3.80). The $19,000 therefore slightly understates the true loss by leaving out the part years, and it slightly overstates it by including the nine weeks of 1911 (my inference).
- **(b) Cannot be determined from the sources.** Sill says about 900,000 lb and Kyle says "a good eleven hundred thousand." Both are hearsay. Kyle himself says the first-season sheets were kept on the creamery's books and "it is not in any book I ever ruled," and Nym records that no 1896 intake figure exists.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment.**
- **When and how long:** 8 May to 10 July 1911, nine weeks (Keddie's dated entries; Orra's diary for 8 May). "Six weeks" (Teague, Kepp) is wrong.
- **The variable:** the measuring glass. The station's own pipette broke and a borrowed measure replaced it.
- **Held constant:** the same tester, bottles, acid, hand tester, scales, herds, pasture, hay and method. The season also stayed the same, because the switch back came in mid-July in the flush.
- **The result:** the tests rose from 3.61 to 3.80, the Larrow Green level, and fell back to 3.61 the day the station's own glass returned.
- **What it establishes that a calculation cannot:** causation. A calculation shows only that a 5% short charge *would* produce the gap. The interval shows that changing the glass alone turned the gap off and on. That rules out the tester, the hay, water and the season as the cause of that 0.19.
- **What it does not establish:**
  - how short the pipettes were, or whether every piece in the lot was short (only the 1958 measurement shows that);
  - why the lot was short;
  - the total money lost;
  - that the borrowed measure was itself exact (that 3.80 is true is inferred from Larrow Green, Alder Corners and 1924);
  - where the borrowed measure came from (cannot be determined).

**D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream.**
1. **The ledger's station gain** (Teague's entries, Kyle's copies, Sill's reweighing at Larrow Green). The Hollow cream arrived about 5% *heavier* in fat than the station credited, every year. Theft would have made it arrive light.
2. **The 1911 interval** (Keddie's book, Orra's diary, Duncan's memory). The same man was at the same bench, yet the tests rose to 3.80 for exactly the nine weeks the station's glass was out.
3. **Alder Corners** (Rosalie Cudd). The same Hessel Bottom milk tested 3.80 at a factory where Keddie had no hand, so the loss lay in the station's measurement, not in missing cream.
4. **1924** (Strawn's notebook, Keddie). After new glass arrived in July 1923, Keddie was still testing and the average was 3.80.
5. **The proportional error** (Ivy). Every herd was short by the same 5% of its own test (3.42 → 0.18 short, 4.56 → 0.24 short). Cream skimmed by hand would not scale that way.

**Why they are independent:** each rests on a different keeper, instrument and place. The Larrow Green buttermaker and ledger; Keddie's own dated entries; another county's factory with its own glass; the state inspector's notebook; and the 1958 laboratory measurement. None of them borrows from another.

**Reasons that hold even if the glass had never been measured in 1958:** 1, 2, 3 and 4 all do. The station gain alone refutes theft; Rundle retracted her theft chapter on that ground.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question those entries?** No. Wilbur Teague wrote the "station gain … five and a quarter per cent, as usual" line every year. He claimed it as his own device, misread it as 5¼% of the actual fat, and in 1925 told the board "Cream is portable… The remedy is two men or a lock," an accusation his own entries refute. Kyle says Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years." Teague's memoir records one approach to Vose in 1916, rebuffed ("bookkeeper and not its conscience"), but nothing shows he ever saw that the gain line disproves theft.

**D3. Did the manager know, before the condemnation, that the tests ran low?**
- **Yes, that they ran low compared with the valley.** Selby Vose was manager from 1913. The complaints dated from 1902 and the 1908 report existed. Teague raised the matter with him in 1916. His own 1919 water circular only makes sense if he knew the Hollow tested low, and Strawn told him in 1919 that there was no water.
- **No evidence that he knew they ran low against the truth**, that is, that the station's test itself was wrong.
  - The proof was the station-gain line on every sheet he signed, but Kyle watched him "put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign"; he "read the totals and never read the lines above them."
  - His 1926 address still blamed water, called the award "a generous settlement of an unproved claim," dated the rise to after the petition, and misdated the condemnation to 12 July. That suggests he never connected the glass with the gap even afterwards.
- **Conclusion (inference):** he had the information in his hands every year, but the sources do not show he understood it; they show he interpreted the low tests as the patrons' water.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. The cause: a short-delivering lot of pipettes.**

The Association bought 72 Ammon & Sons pipettes (invoice of 14 April 1897, $77.93). They were marked 17.6 c.c. but delivered on average **16.72 c.c., 0.95 of the charge**. They went into use at Ostrey Hollow on **1 May 1897**. The Babcock bottle neck is ruled for a 17.6 c.c. charge, so every test read 95% of the truth, and under Article VII the station's test fixed every patron's pay.
- **Why that size:** milk of 3.80 read 3.61, 0.19 short. The error scaled with each herd (3.60 read 3.42; 4.80 read 4.56), which is why the gap was the same in February and July and did not depend on breed, feed or farm.
- **Why it began when it did:** the first season (1896) was tested on glass lent by Sill from Larrow Green and read **3.80**. The shortfall starts with the Ammon glass in 1897, and the complaints followed from 1902.
- **Why it ended when it did:** Strawn condemned the crate on 12 June 1923. New glass was in use from July, and 1924 read **3.80**.
- **Why nothing else had to change:** the same tester (Keddie, through 1924), the same undrained Bottom (Kepp was still urging drainage in 1926) and the same herds gave 3.61 before and 3.80 after. The 1911 borrowed measure switched the gap off and on within one summer.
- **Why it stayed hidden:** an error that is identical in every piece passes any check that compares pieces with each other. The pipette cannot be checked at the bench, and Strawn had no burette until 1921. The one figure that showed the error, the 5¼% "station gain," had a name and was carried "as usual."

**The three competing explanations, and what defeats each:**
1. **Added water** (Selby Vose, the 1919 circular and his 1926 address; echoed by Sill).
   - Strawn's lactometer found no water and he told the manager so in 1919; Falke confirms it was never found.
   - Cudd's cheese would expose watered milk at once, and the same men's milk tested 3.80 there.
   - Watering adds no fat, so it cannot produce a station gain, fat arriving at Larrow Green beyond what was credited.
   - The tests rose in 1923–24, before the petition and with no change on the farms.
2. **Cream theft by the tester** (Teague, before the board; Rundle's "settled opinion"; the beliefs of Vira and of Cleve's father).
   - The station gain shows the cream arrived heavy, not light.
   - In 1911 the same man's tests rose with the borrowed measure.
   - In 1924 the same man's tests read 3.80.
   - Alder Corners read the same milk at 3.80.
3. **Wet bog hay** (Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee; Dr. Kepp in 1926; Falke's initial concession).
   - Bulletin 214 actually found at most 0.05 in winter and nothing in summer: about a quarter of the size and the wrong seasonal shape. The gap was a flat 0.19 in July.
   - The same land tested 3.80 in 1896, 1911 (nine weeks) and 1924, and 3.80 at Alder Corners in summer.
   - Emmet: the land had been wet since the flood "and the tests had not."

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware? No.** Its exhibits were four books only: the test book, the receiving book, the Brant weigh book and Strawn's 1923 notebook (Frick, Kyle). The crate was never mentioned (Effie). Two of the three sessions sat in the weigh room directly beneath it (Duncan, Cleve, Ivy). Strawn's notebook recording the condemnation was before them, and nobody connected it with the tests.

**What the tester believed: that it was the glass, though he could never prove it.**
- In 1911 he came home saying it was "something in the glass," and wrote only "?" (Duncan, Keddie's book).
- His unsent 1925 letter sets 1896 at 3.80, 1911 at 3.80 and 1924 at 3.80 against 3.61, and notes the gap was not seasonal.
- To the board he would say only that he had never taken a pound of cream and "cannot say why."

Frick's corridor report that he blamed the patrons is uncorroborated and inconsistent with this record.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** In 1897 the Larrow Valley creamery's Ostrey Hollow station began using a crate of Ammon & Sons pipettes that delivered 16.72 c.c. instead of 17.6. Because the station test fixed every patron's pay, the Hessel Bottom farmers were credited 3.61 for milk that was really 3.80 for twenty-six years. That came to 76,000 lb of butterfat, or $19,000. The Association blamed wet hay (1908), then water (1919), then the tester, Ansel Keddie. He was dismissed in 1925 as a board paid $3,800 without admission. The proof was scattered:
- a nine-week borrowed measure in 1911;
- a ledger "station gain" entered "as usual";
- the Brant weigh book;
- the Alder Corners cheese tests;
- an inspector's 1923 condemnation;
- the crate itself, found in the station loft in 1958 by Keddie's granddaughter Ivy.

No single record showed the cause. Together they cleared him, twenty-four years after his death.
