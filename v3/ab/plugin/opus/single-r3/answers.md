# Answer Sheet — Part 1 (Section A, stories 1–4)

## Section A — Reconstruction

How I divided the eight stories:
1. The founding and the station (1889–1897).
2. Hessel Bottom and the Brant family.
3. The complaint and the hay theory.
4. The 1911 borrowed measure.
5. Alder Corners.
6. The ledger's "station gain" and the water circular.
7. The condemnation and the arbitration (1923–25).
8. The 1958 rediscovery.

Where sources disagree, the rejected value is named in brackets with the reason.

### 1. The Association, Article VII and the station at Ostrey Hollow (1889–1897)

**The Association.** Larrow Valley farmers organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association in 1889, meeting in the Larrow Green schoolhouse. Gideon Vose was president from 1889 to 1911 and wrote the by-laws; he died in 1917. The creamery was built on Larrow Creek at Larrow Green in 1891. The office was at Ashlin, over a harness shop, and Effie Loomis was secretary from 1901 to 1940.

**Article VII.** Every patron is paid on the pounds of butterfat his milk shows by test, and "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." Under payment by weight, nothing that happened later would have cost anyone a dollar; under Article VII the station test was final. The year it was adopted **cannot be determined**:
- Rundle says 1891, Effie 1892 and Grigg 1893 ("as I have always understood").
- The printed by-laws carry no date.
- The minute book for 1889–92 was lost in the 1938 office move.

**The station.** The skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley, was built in the winter of 1895–96 to serve Hessel Bottom. It opened on 4 May 1896, the day Ansel Keddie (born 1861) began the test book. He was tester and separator man for twenty-nine seasons, 1896–1924. Milk was weighed in the intake (weigh) room, sampled, given the Babcock test and separated. The cream went down to Larrow Green daily by wagon, and the skim went back to the farmer. A loft over the intake room was reached by a ladder.

**The first season's glass.** For the first season the station borrowed glass from Larrow Green: in May 1896 the buttermaker Hobart Sill made up an outfit from his own glass. It came back the next spring with one piece chipped. That season tested **3.80**. [Keddie's letter says the glass came "off the Ashlin creamery"; rejected against his own first leaf, Sill, Orra's diary and Nym, all of which say Larrow Green.]

**The station's own outfit.** Ammon & Sons Dairy Supply of Tarnet invoiced it on 14 April 1897:

| Item | Cost |
|---|---|
| 6 dozen milk-test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c., at 35¢ | $25.20 |
| 12 dozen test bottles | $31.68 |
| Acid measure | $0.90 |
| Four-bottle hand tester | $18.00 |
| Freight | $2.15 |
| **Total** | **$77.93** |

The outfit went into use on **1 May 1897**, when Orra wrote that they were "done borrowing of Larrow Green." [Vaught's and Kyle's "1 May 1898" is rejected against the dated invoice and the diary.]

**First-season milk.** How much milk the station took in its first season is not recorded anywhere. Sill's 900,000 lb and Kyle's 1,100,000 lb are both hearsay, and Nym found no figure.

Documents: r02, r03, r07, r11, r12, r14, r20, r21, r22, r23, r24.

### 2. Hessel Bottom, the Brant weigh book and a family divided (1897–1929)

**The Bottom.** Hessel Bottom is a wet meadow of about 160 acres below the station. It is spring-fed, partly under water every spring, and cut for sedge hay. **Six farms** worked it (Roy, Hazel, Grigg's report, Kepp, Falke) [Effie's "seven" is outnumbered], and a farm might hold more than one family. The largest patron was **Emmet Brant** (1854–1929), with sixty-odd acres and 38 cows.

**The weigh book.** Emmet weighed his milk on his own scale before it went on the wagon, and wrote the station's intake weight beside it. The book was begun on **1 January 1897**, the date it bore as an exhibit to the 1925 board (Frick) and as later catalogued (Nym, Duncan, Ivy). [Roy's 1898 and Hazel's 1900 are rejected; Hazel herself says she does not keep years.] The two columns always agreed, so the weights were never in question.

**The 1910 leaf.** It reads **200,000 lb "even,"** underlined, paid on a test of **3.42**, with fat at **24¢** that year.
- Credited: 6,840 lb of fat.
- True content: 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60, or 7,200 lb.
- Short: 360 lb, about $86.40.

**Where the book went.** Emmet's son Roy had it from 1929. Roy *lent* it, not gave it, to Ivy Keddie in 1958. When Roy died in 1962 it went to his daughter Hazel, who gave it to the Historical Rooms at Ashlin in 1963.

**The tester was family.** Orra Brant (born 1866, died 1948), Emmet's younger sister, married Ansel Keddie in 1889. Their son Duncan (born 1894) taught school at Ashlin, and Duncan's daughter was Ivy (born 1931). So the man later accused of shorting the patrons was married to the sister of the patron who lost the most.

**The break in 1919.** It came in June 1919 with the manager's water circular.
- Ansel would not mail the circulars or leave them on the platform; he carried them to the patrons himself.
- Two days later Emmet stood in Orra's yard, asked why Ansel had carried a paper he did not believe, and never came into the house again. Roy puts the cause as Ansel not standing up against the circular.
- In 1925 Keddie wrote that he had not spoken with Emmet since 1919. [Duncan's "at our table… thirty-six years" is rejected against Orra's contemporaneous diary, Keddie, Roy and Vira.]
- In January 1925 Emmet's name was on the patrons' petition.
- Emmet died in the spring of 1929, unreconciled. Orra went down to his kitchen, "where I was a girl."

Documents: r01, r02, r03, r07, r09, r13, r17, r18, r24.

### 3. The complaint and the hay theory (1902–1926)

**The complaint.** From **1902** the Ostrey Hollow patrons complained that their milk tested below the valley's. Emmet Brant was among the first, and Orra heard the talk at the store that March. Over the long period the station averaged **3.61** against Larrow Green's **3.78**. The shortfall was **0.19** of a point and flat: the same in February as in July (Roy, Hazel, Falke).

**The 1908 committee.** In January 1908 the board named a committee of three under **Nyle Grigg** of Larrow Green, who hauled to the creamery and was not a patron of the station. Orra saw four men arrive in Grigg's sleigh; the fourth was presumably a driver (my inference).
- They visited all six farms, drew and sealed their own samples, and watched the tests made.
- They tried the scales against a known weight and asked about the water at each barn.
- The printed report says they sampled **January, February and March**, and Orra's diary has them gone back down in March. [Grigg's 1919 claim of "the whole of 1908, every month" contradicts his own printed report.]
- They found no dishonesty ("the milk is whole and the scales are honest"), but judged that "what such land gives in quantity it withholds in quality." Their verdict was that the wet hay thins the cream, and their remedy was to drain the Bottom.
- Emmet answered that the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not been low.
- The question stayed closed for eleven years.

**Bulletin No. 214.** The State Agricultural College at Farland ran a feeding trial on bog hay (in 1911) and published it as Bulletin No. 214. Its summary, as printed by Falke: bog hay as the whole roughage depressed the test "by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."
- Grigg's 1919 reprint cited it as "two tenths" in winter.
- Dr. Roland Kepp, the Ashlin veterinarian, repeated "two tenths" in his 1926 Farm Club paper. He added his own "fifth of a point" and a gap of "0.21," and waved off the 1911 interval as "six weeks" of summer. He had never read a test himself.

**Why the hay fails.** At most 0.05, and only in winter, cannot account for 0.19 that was full-size in July; it is about a quarter of the size and the wrong shape. The county agent Orin Falke conceded hay as a partial cause in 1959, then withdrew the concession once he had read Bulletin 214.

Documents: r04, r09, r11, r13, r15, r16, r17, r22, r24.

### 4. The borrowed measure, 8 May–10 July 1911

**The break.** On **8 May 1911** a pipette broke at the station. Keddie's book reads "May 8 pipette broke… borrowed measure," and that night Orra wrote that A. was home "cross about a broken measure." For **nine weeks**, until **10 July**, the tests were made with a borrowed measure.

**The result.** Throughout those weeks the Hollow tested **3.80**, the Larrow Green figure, instead of 3.61. On 10 July Keddie went back to his own measure and the figures dropped at once: "July 10 own measure… Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."

**What stayed the same.** The tester, bottles, acid, hand tester, scales, herds, pasture and hay were all unchanged. The switch back came in the middle of the summer flush, so the season did not change either. The only variable was the measuring glass, and the error followed it.

**Keddie's reaction.** Duncan, then seventeen, remembers his father coming home and saying twice, at supper and again at the door, that it was "something in the glass." He wrote only a question mark, because he would not put down what he could not prove. Strawn's notebook for June 1911 says "Nothing here to note."

**Where the borrowed measure came from cannot be determined:**
- Ivy says it came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon.
- Teague says it came "from town."
- Roy says "the state man left his own measure that June." That fits neither the 8 May start nor Strawn's silence.

[Teague's and Kepp's "six weeks" are rejected against the dated entries, which give nine. Kepp's "summer figures" objection fails because the gap was the same in July as in February, and the drop came in mid-July.]

**Why it matters.** The entries lay unread for **47 years** until Bulletin 471 (1958) treated them as decisive: a step of 0.19 on 3.80 milk, a factor of 0.95, switched on and off by a change of glass alone. The interval proves that the station's own measure caused the shortfall. It does not measure the pipettes, show that the whole lot was short, or explain why they were short.

Documents: r03, r04, r05, r06, r09, r11, r16, r17, r18, r22, r24.

### 5. The factory over the ridge: Alder Corners (1913–1930)

**The factory.** Jerome Cudd built a cheese factory at Alder Corners in Vessey County, on the far side of Redlow Ridge. His daughter says he built it in 1913; Roy and Rundle say it opened in 1914. The two can both be true: built one year and opened the next (my inference). The ridge road is four miles of switchback and is closed from November to April or May.

**The four men.** From **1915** four of the six Hessel Bottom farmers hauled over the ridge in the summer flush, from about 1 June to mid-September, and took their milk to the station for the rest of the year. They did this for **sixteen seasons**, the last in **1930**. Roy Brant did not go. Hazel called it "the only honest arithmetic anybody in that valley ever did without meaning to."

**Cudd's tests.**
- He tested with his own new glassware and paid on it.
- He never compared his figures with the station's.
- He never docked the men, and cheese exposes watered milk at once.
- In 1919, the year of the water circular, he wrote: "3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground."

His yearly averages all fall between 3.78 and 3.82, about **3.80** overall:

| Year | Test | Year | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1915 | 3.79 | 1923 | 3.81 |
| 1916 | 3.81 | 1924 | 3.80 |
| 1917 | 3.78 | 1925 | 3.79 |
| 1918 | 3.80 | 1926 | 3.82 |
| 1919 | 3.80 | 1927 | 3.78 |
| 1920 | 3.82 | 1928 | 3.80 |
| 1921 | 3.79 | 1929 | 3.81 |
| 1922 | 3.78 | 1930 | 3.79 |

**Why it matters.** The same cows, on the same wet ground, in the same summers, tested 3.80 at a factory where Keddie had no hand. Meanwhile the station's figures for those men ran about 0.19 lower until the glass was replaced. That removes both the land and the tester as causes, and it matches the true figure of 3.80.

**The record.** Jerome died in March 1941. His daughter **Rosalie**, who was twelve at the first vat, ran the factory for seventeen years. In 1958 she copied the figures for Ivy Keddie on a stranger's request. [She is Jerome's daughter by her own letter, Rundle and Duncan; Ivy's talk and the Chronicle wrongly call her his niece.] The book itself never left Vessey County and went to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970.

**An unresolved figure.** Ivy's "fourteen overlapping seasons" does not fit the table. The overlap with the short-glass years is 1915–1922, eight full seasons, plus part of 1923.

Documents: r04, r07, r09, r10, r12, r17, r18, r19.

### 6. "Station gain … as usual": the ledger and the water circular (1898–1922)

**The ledger line.** Every year the Association's station account for Ostrey Hollow carried three figures and a phrase:
1. butterfat credited to the patrons on the station test;
2. butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream;
3. the difference, entered as **"station gain — five and a quarter per cent, as usual"** and carried to the general account.

**The figures.** The 1898 entry reads **57,760 lb** credited, **60,800 lb** received and **3,040 lb** gain. That is exactly 1,600,000 lb of milk at 3.61 and at 3.80. The gain is 5¼% (5.26%) of the fat *credited*, which is the same thing as **5%** of the fat actually delivered. Over 1898–1922, on 40,000,000 lb of milk, the totals are:
- credited: 1,444,000 lb;
- received: 1,520,000 lb;
- gain: 76,000 lb.

**Everyone handled it:**
- **Wilbur Teague**, bookkeeper 1905–1945, wrote the line every year. He later claimed it as his own 1905 device, which is impossible because the 1898 entry already says "as usual." He also misread it as 5¼% of the actual fat.
- **Bertram Kyle**, the clerk, copied it out in a fair hand for the annual meeting.
- **Hobart Sill**, the buttermaker, found the Hollow cream always ran heavy against the station sheets. He weighed it again himself and entered it in the receiving book, then put it down to "cream's nature."
- **Selby Vose**, manager 1913–1938, signed the sheets with his thumb on the total. [1913 comes from Effie and from Vose's own "thirteen years" in 1926; Kyle's 1911 is rejected.]

**What the line meant.** It was the error sitting in plain sight: fat the patrons had delivered but not been credited with turned up in the cream at Larrow Green. It also clears the tester, because cream stolen at the station would have arrived *light*.

**The Association blamed the patrons instead.** In June 1919 Vose's circular, typed by Kyle and signed by Vose alone, warned the Hollow patrons about water ("cannot pay for creek water").
- The same year the state inspector Strawn found no water and told the manager so.
- Keddie carried the circulars round himself.
- Sill praised the circular.
- Teague had already raised the matter with Vose in 1916 and was told he was "bookkeeper and not its conscience."
- Dell Rundle's jubilee pamphlet (1939) called cream theft the "settled opinion." Her 1959 reprint added a note in press retracting that chapter, because the ledger's station gain proves the cream arrived heavy.

Documents: r02, r03, r05, r06, r07, r08, r12, r14, r23, r24.

### 7. The glass condemned and the tester dismissed (1923–1925)

**The condemnation.** Merle Strawn was the state dairy inspector on the Ordell circuit from 1907 to 1936 and was kin to nobody in the valley. He was issued burettes in 1921 and did not run the station's glass in 1922.
- On **12 June 1923** he ran the Ostrey Hollow pipettes against his burette, found they would not deliver their mark, and condemned the whole crate: "Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them."
- The Association reordered from Ammon & Sons on **28 June**: six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles.
- Ammon replied on **2 July** that its "books of the nineties" could tell nothing about the lot.
- New glass was in use from **July 1923**, and the old crate went up into the loft over the intake room.

[Vose's and Rundle's "12 July" is impossible, since it would fall after the 28 June order. Hazel's 1921 and Kyle's 1922 are wrong.]

**The rise.** With the same tester, the same herds and the same undrained land, the station's **1924 average was 3.80** (Strawn's notebook, Keddie). [Vose's 3.90 and Falke's 3.85 are rejected.]

**The arbitration.** The patrons petitioned in January 1925. A board of three sat from **17 March to 4 May 1925** under **Alonzo Frick**, an Ashlin attorney who chaired as "the one who kept no cow."
- The first session was in the Association office at Ashlin. The second and third were in the station's weigh room, directly under the crate.
- The exhibits were **four books**: the test book (4 May 1896–31 December 1924), the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book, and Strawn's 1923 notebook.
- The crate was never mentioned.
- Three explanations were argued, and none was accepted: water (Vose); cream-taking (Teague: "Cream is portable… The remedy is two men or a lock"); and the 1908 hay report.

**The outcome.** Keddie's statement of 17 March ran nine sentences: thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, he had never taken a pound of cream, and he could not say why.
- He was **dismissed on 30 April 1925**, four days before the award, and wrote a letter he never sent.
- On **4 May** the board awarded the patrons **$3,800 "without admission,"** charged to the station account. [Vira's and Cleve's $3,500 is rejected against the secretary, the chairman and the clerk.]
- Keddie took up carpentry and died in **February 1934**.

Documents: r02, r03, r05, r06, r07, r08, r13, r18, r20, r21, r23, r24.

### 8. The crate in the loft: Bulletin 471 (1958–1972)

**Ivy's five records.** **Ivy Keddie** (born 1931), Ansel's granddaughter and a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, put five records side by side:
- the station test book;
- the Brant weigh book, lent by Roy;
- the Alder Corners figures, copied by Rosalie Cudd;
- Merle Strawn's twenty-eight circuit notebooks, which went to the state dairy office in 1936 and the Farland library in 1947, where she found the 1923 volume;
- the Association ledgers.

**The crate.** In **February 1958** she climbed the ladder into the loft over the old station's intake room. Under a tarpaulin and chaff she found the crate, stenciled AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET. **Nine pipettes** survived, two of them chipped.

**The measurement.** On **9 March 1958** at Farland she measured them against a calibrated burette. The mean was **16.72 c.c.** against the 17.6 c.c. mark: 0.88 c.c. short, or **0.95** of the charge, one part in twenty. A short charge in a bottle ruled for the full charge reads low in proportion:
- milk of 3.80 read as 3.61, 0.19 short;
- a herd recorded at 3.42 was 0.18 short;
- a herd recorded at 4.56 was 0.24 short.

**Bulletin No. 471** (October 1958, by Leland Vaught and Ivy Keddie) set out the case:
- the pipette is the one instrument a tester cannot check at the bench;
- the 1911 interval, unread for 47 years, is decisive;
- over 1898–1922, 40,000,000 lb × 0.0019 = **76,000 lb of fat × 25¢ = $19,000**, of which the $3,800 award was **one fifth**.

Vaught's own body text carries wrong figures (five dozen pipettes, 16.27 c.c., 1898, 118,000 lb), which his conclusion corrects.

**The aftermath:**
- The *Ashlin Chronicle* (3 October 1958) repeated several errors and accused Ammon & Sons of knowingly selling a short lot.
- The firm's historian, Junia Ammon, had already written in 1949 that a reground mold probably produced a short lot by accident. Checking pieces against each other cannot catch an error that is the same in every piece, and the works books burned in 1931, so **the cause of the short lot cannot be determined**.
- In 1959 Ivy told the Ordell County Historical Society that it was 35 years from condemnation to cause and 33 since the dismissal; Ansel had died in 1934 without knowing.
- Falke's 1959 report to the county found that no action lay.
- The crate and the test book went to the Historical Rooms at Ashlin in 1959 and the weigh book in 1963. Halbert Nym catalogued the collection in 1972.

Documents: r04, r07, r11, r12, r17, r18, r19, r20, r21, r22.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** Duncan Keddie states that he is Ansel's son and that Ivy (born 1931) is his daughter, and complains that he has seen it printed otherwise. Hazel ("Ivy is Duncan's girl"), Cleve Oram ("the granddaughter"), Ivy's own 1959 talk and Orra's diary editor all agree. The *Ashlin Chronicle* (1958) called her his "great-niece." That was a rushed piece written from a press release, and Falke's 1959 report copied the error and then corrected it in a note ("I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter"). The great-niece version is therefore a single error with one derivative, and it is outweighed by two family members with first-hand knowledge.

**B2. Ivy and Hazel were second cousins.** The derivation runs:
1. Emmet Brant and Orra (Brant) Keddie were siblings.
2. Their children, Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie, were first cousins.
3. Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy were therefore second cousins.

Duncan, Hazel and Roy all derive it this way. Vira Toland and Cleve Oram say "first cousin once removed," which would require the two women to be a generation apart; they were not. **What the sources leave open** is whether Emmet and Orra were full or half siblings:
- Roy calls Orra Emmet's full sister.
- Hazel says she was a half-sister "by his father's second wife."
- Duncan says only "sister, the younger of the two."
- Orra's own papers from before 1889 do not survive.

If they were half siblings, Roy and Duncan were half first cousins and Hazel and Ivy were half second cousins. The age gap (Emmet born 1854, Orra 1866) is consistent with a second wife but does not prove one. This cannot be determined.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose**, who organized the Association in 1889, wrote its by-laws and was its first president (1889–1911). Effie calls Selby the "son of the late president," Selby himself speaks of "my father's Jerseys," and Duncan and Kyle both insist on son. Vira ("old Gideon Vose's nephew") and Rundle say nephew; that is rejected against Selby's own words and the secretary's.

Why it matters: the manager was the son of the man who wrote the rule that made the station's test final (Article VII).
- He treated the shortfall as the patrons' dishonesty, not as a fault in the Association's own measure: the 1919 water circular ("cannot pay for creek water"; "Bring it as it left the cow").
- He rebuffed his own bookkeeper in 1916.
- He signed the ledger sheets without reading the line that showed the error.
- In 1926 he still called the award "a generous settlement of an unproved claim."

That he was defending his father's institution is my inference; the sources do not say so outright.

**B4. The Vose–Keddie relationship: none is established, and it cannot be proved either way.** Vira says Orra Keddie and "old Mrs. Vose" were first cousins, and Cleve says the families were "cousins of some sort," from his mother's mother. Duncan answers that there was no connection whatever:
- The Keddies were confused with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Gideon either.
- The story had been town talk since before he could walk; he had heard it from two women in town.
- His mother, who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection," never put a Vose in it.

The two affirmative accounts are not independent. Both are valley talk, the same stream Duncan describes, and neither names a line of descent. Duncan's denial is first-hand family knowledge but rests on negative evidence, and Orra's papers from before 1889 were not preserved. The weight of evidence is against any kinship. Separately, the "nephew" version of Selby, which travels with the same rumour, is wrong.


# Answer Sheet — Part 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

**C1.**
- **(a) Tests read 5% low, a factor of 0.95 (one part in twenty).** The Babcock bottle neck is ruled for a 17.6 c.c. charge. The nine surviving pipettes delivered a mean of 16.72 c.c. (Ivy's measurement, 9 March 1958). The reading is proportional to the charge, so reading = true × 16.72 / 17.6 = true × **0.95**.
  - Check: 3.61 / 3.80 = 0.95.
  - [Vaught's 16.27 gives 0.9244 and a true test of 3.905, which is inconsistent with the 3.80 recorded in 1924 on new glass; rejected.]
- **(b) The true average was 3.80.** 3.61 / 0.95 = **3.80**. The recorded tests were short by 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19** of a point.
- **(c) 76,000 lb of fat were not credited.** The station received 40,000,000 lb of milk in the full delivery years 1898–1922 (Kyle, who compiled the figure; 1.6 million lb a year × 25 years).
  - True fat: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb.
  - Credited: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb.
  - Not credited: **76,000 lb**.
- **(d) $19,000, of which the patrons received one fifth.** 76,000 lb × $0.25 (the average fat price, which Kyle was set to find) = **$19,000**. The award was $3,800, and $3,800 / $19,000 = **1/5 (20%)**.

**C2.** Emmet Brant, 1910: 200,000 lb of milk, recorded test 3.42.
- **(a)** Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of fat.
- **(b)** True test: 3.42 / 0.95 = 3.60, so true fat = 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb. Owed and not credited: **360 lb** (a shortfall of 0.18 of a point, matching Ivy's "3.42 herd short 0.18").
- **(c)** At the 1910 price of 24¢ (Roy): 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40**. At the 25¢ long-run average it would be $90.00.

**C3.** Over 1898–1922:
- **(a)** Credited to the patrons: **1,444,000 lb**.
- **(b)** Received at Larrow Green in the station cream: **1,520,000 lb** (the true fat).
- **(c)** The difference is 76,000 lb:
  - as a percentage **of the fat credited**: 76,000 / 1,444,000 = **5.26% (≈ 5¼%)**. This is the ledger's "five and a quarter per cent, as usual."
  - as a percentage **of the fat actually delivered and received**: 76,000 / 1,520,000 = **5.00%**.
  - Check with the 1898 entry: 3,040 / 57,760 = 5.26%, and 3,040 / 60,800 = 5.00%.
  - Teague and Vaught wrongly call 5¼% a percentage of the actual fat.

**C4.**
- **(a)** **$77.93**. The line items sum to that: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93. [Junia Ammon's prose and the Chronicle give $77.39, which is a transposition.]
- **(b)** **72 pipettes** (6 dozen; $25.20 / $0.35 = 72). Each was short by about **0.88 c.c.** (17.60 − 16.72). This is the mean of the nine survivors; applying it to all 72 assumes the lot was uniform, which the proportional, flat error supports.

**C5.**
- **(a) Two different spans.**
  - **How long the glass was in use:** from **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923**, when it was condemned, which is **26 years, 1 month and 11 days** (about 26.1 years). Replacements arrived in July, so it may have stayed in use a few more weeks. It was out of use for the nine weeks of 1911.
  - **The money calculation:** **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922**.
  - **Why they differ:** 1897 (from 1 May) and 1923 (to June/July) were part years. Keddie's summary marks 1897 as "part year," and the 40,000,000 lb series covers whole years only. 1896 is excluded because it was tested on Larrow Green's accurate glass (3.80). The $19,000 therefore slightly understates the true loss by leaving out the part years, and it slightly overstates it by including the nine weeks of 1911 (my inference).
- **(b) Cannot be determined from the sources.** Sill says about 900,000 lb and Kyle says "a good eleven hundred thousand." Both are hearsay. Kyle himself says the first-season sheets were kept on the creamery's books and "it is not in any book I ever ruled," and Nym records that no 1896 intake figure exists.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment.**
- **When and how long:** 8 May to 10 July 1911, nine weeks (Keddie's dated entries; Orra's diary for 8 May). "Six weeks" (Teague, Kepp) is wrong.
- **The variable:** the measuring glass. The station's own pipette broke and a borrowed measure replaced it.
- **Held constant:** the same tester, bottles, acid, hand tester, scales, herds, pasture, hay and method. The season also stayed the same, because the switch back came in mid-July in the flush.
- **The result:** the tests rose from 3.61 to 3.80, the Larrow Green level, and fell back to 3.61 the day the station's own glass returned.
- **What it establishes that a calculation cannot:** causation. A calculation shows only that a 5% short charge *would* produce the gap. The interval shows that changing the glass alone turned the gap off and on. That rules out the tester, the hay, water and the season as the cause of that 0.19.
- **What it does not establish:**
  - how short the pipettes were, or whether every piece in the lot was short (only the 1958 measurement shows that);
  - why the lot was short;
  - the total money lost;
  - that the borrowed measure was itself exact (that 3.80 is true is inferred from Larrow Green, Alder Corners and 1924);
  - where the borrowed measure came from (cannot be determined).

**D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream.**
1. **The ledger's station gain** (Teague's entries, Kyle's copies, Sill's reweighing at Larrow Green). The Hollow cream arrived about 5% *heavier* in fat than the station credited, every year. Theft would have made it arrive light.
2. **The 1911 interval** (Keddie's book, Orra's diary, Duncan's memory). The same man was at the same bench, yet the tests rose to 3.80 for exactly the nine weeks the station's glass was out.
3. **Alder Corners** (Rosalie Cudd). The same Hessel Bottom milk tested 3.80 at a factory where Keddie had no hand, so the loss lay in the station's measurement, not in missing cream.
4. **1924** (Strawn's notebook, Keddie). After new glass arrived in July 1923, Keddie was still testing and the average was 3.80.
5. **The proportional error** (Ivy). Every herd was short by the same 5% of its own test (3.42 → 0.18 short, 4.56 → 0.24 short). Cream skimmed by hand would not scale that way.

**Why they are independent:** each rests on a different keeper, instrument and place. The Larrow Green buttermaker and ledger; Keddie's own dated entries; another county's factory with its own glass; the state inspector's notebook; and the 1958 laboratory measurement. None of them borrows from another.

**Reasons that hold even if the glass had never been measured in 1958:** 1, 2, 3 and 4 all do. The station gain alone refutes theft; Rundle retracted her theft chapter on that ground.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question those entries?** No. Wilbur Teague wrote the "station gain … five and a quarter per cent, as usual" line every year. He claimed it as his own device, misread it as 5¼% of the actual fat, and in 1925 told the board "Cream is portable… The remedy is two men or a lock," an accusation his own entries refute. Kyle says Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years." Teague's memoir records one approach to Vose in 1916, rebuffed ("bookkeeper and not its conscience"), but nothing shows he ever saw that the gain line disproves theft.

**D3. Did the manager know, before the condemnation, that the tests ran low?**
- **Yes, that they ran low compared with the valley.** Selby Vose was manager from 1913. The complaints dated from 1902 and the 1908 report existed. Teague raised the matter with him in 1916. His own 1919 water circular only makes sense if he knew the Hollow tested low, and Strawn told him in 1919 that there was no water.
- **No evidence that he knew they ran low against the truth**, that is, that the station's test itself was wrong.
  - The proof was the station-gain line on every sheet he signed, but Kyle watched him "put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign"; he "read the totals and never read the lines above them."
  - His 1926 address still blamed water, called the award "a generous settlement of an unproved claim," dated the rise to after the petition, and misdated the condemnation to 12 July. That suggests he never connected the glass with the gap even afterwards.
- **Conclusion (inference):** he had the information in his hands every year, but the sources do not show he understood it; they show he interpreted the low tests as the patrons' water.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. The cause: a short-delivering lot of pipettes.**

The Association bought 72 Ammon & Sons pipettes (invoice of 14 April 1897, $77.93). They were marked 17.6 c.c. but delivered on average **16.72 c.c., 0.95 of the charge**. They went into use at Ostrey Hollow on **1 May 1897**. The Babcock bottle neck is ruled for a 17.6 c.c. charge, so every test read 95% of the truth, and under Article VII the station's test fixed every patron's pay.
- **Why that size:** milk of 3.80 read 3.61, 0.19 short. The error scaled with each herd (3.60 read 3.42; 4.80 read 4.56), which is why the gap was the same in February and July and did not depend on breed, feed or farm.
- **Why it began when it did:** the first season (1896) was tested on glass lent by Sill from Larrow Green and read **3.80**. The shortfall starts with the Ammon glass in 1897, and the complaints followed from 1902.
- **Why it ended when it did:** Strawn condemned the crate on 12 June 1923. New glass was in use from July, and 1924 read **3.80**.
- **Why nothing else had to change:** the same tester (Keddie, through 1924), the same undrained Bottom (Kepp was still urging drainage in 1926) and the same herds gave 3.61 before and 3.80 after. The 1911 borrowed measure switched the gap off and on within one summer.
- **Why it stayed hidden:** an error that is identical in every piece passes any check that compares pieces with each other. The pipette cannot be checked at the bench, and Strawn had no burette until 1921. The one figure that showed the error, the 5¼% "station gain," had a name and was carried "as usual."

**The three competing explanations, and what defeats each:**
1. **Added water** (Selby Vose, the 1919 circular and his 1926 address; echoed by Sill).
   - Strawn's lactometer found no water and he told the manager so in 1919; Falke confirms it was never found.
   - Cudd's cheese would expose watered milk at once, and the same men's milk tested 3.80 there.
   - Watering adds no fat, so it cannot produce a station gain, fat arriving at Larrow Green beyond what was credited.
   - The tests rose in 1923–24, before the petition and with no change on the farms.
2. **Cream theft by the tester** (Teague, before the board; Rundle's "settled opinion"; the beliefs of Vira and of Cleve's father).
   - The station gain shows the cream arrived heavy, not light.
   - In 1911 the same man's tests rose with the borrowed measure.
   - In 1924 the same man's tests read 3.80.
   - Alder Corners read the same milk at 3.80.
3. **Wet bog hay** (Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee; Dr. Kepp in 1926; Falke's initial concession).
   - Bulletin 214 actually found at most 0.05 in winter and nothing in summer: about a quarter of the size and the wrong seasonal shape. The gap was a flat 0.19 in July.
   - The same land tested 3.80 in 1896, 1911 (nine weeks) and 1924, and 3.80 at Alder Corners in summer.
   - Emmet: the land had been wet since the flood "and the tests had not."

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware? No.** Its exhibits were four books only: the test book, the receiving book, the Brant weigh book and Strawn's 1923 notebook (Frick, Kyle). The crate was never mentioned (Effie). Two of the three sessions sat in the weigh room directly beneath it (Duncan, Cleve, Ivy). Strawn's notebook recording the condemnation was before them, and nobody connected it with the tests.

**What the tester believed: that it was the glass, though he could never prove it.**
- In 1911 he came home saying it was "something in the glass," and wrote only "?" (Duncan, Keddie's book).
- His unsent 1925 letter sets 1896 at 3.80, 1911 at 3.80 and 1924 at 3.80 against 3.61, and notes the gap was not seasonal.
- To the board he would say only that he had never taken a pound of cream and "cannot say why."

Frick's corridor report that he blamed the patrons is uncorroborated and inconsistent with this record.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** In 1897 the Larrow Valley creamery's Ostrey Hollow station began using a crate of Ammon & Sons pipettes that delivered 16.72 c.c. instead of 17.6. Because the station test fixed every patron's pay, the Hessel Bottom farmers were credited 3.61 for milk that was really 3.80 for twenty-six years. That came to 76,000 lb of butterfat, or $19,000. The Association blamed wet hay (1908), then water (1919), then the tester, Ansel Keddie. He was dismissed in 1925 as a board paid $3,800 without admission. The proof was scattered:
- a nine-week borrowed measure in 1911;
- a ledger "station gain" entered "as usual";
- the Brant weigh book;
- the Alder Corners cheese tests;
- an inspector's 1923 condemnation;
- the crate itself, found in the station loft in 1958 by Keddie's granddaughter Ivy.

No single record showed the cause. Together they cleared him, twenty-four years after his death.
