### Story 1. The Rule That Made the Loss: the Association and Article VII, 1889–1946

**Who:** the farmers of the Larrow Valley, Ordell County; Gideon Vose, organizer and first
president 1889–1911; Effie Loomis, secretary 1901–1940; Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945;
Bertram Kyle, office clerk; Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green 1893–1934; Selby Vose,
manager 1913–1938.

**What happened:** farmers who had been making butter in their kitchens organized the Larrow
Valley Cooperative Creamery Association at a meeting at Larrow Green in 1889, built the main
creamery there in 1891, and then put up skimming stations at the heads of the valleys — Ostrey
Hollow, seven miles up, among them. Gideon Vose wrote the by-laws. Their seventh article is the
hinge of everything that follows: a patron is paid not on the weight of his milk but on the pounds
of butterfat it is *found by test* to contain, "and the test of the station where he delivers
shall govern." Orin Falke, the county agent, put the consequence exactly in 1959: under payment
by weight, nothing in this history would have cost any man a dollar. Article VII made a
laboratory reading into money, and made one man's glassware the paymaster of a valley.

**When:** organized 1889; creamery 1891; Article VII adopted 1892 on the secretary's authority
(Dell Rundle's pamphlet dates it 1891). The year cannot be settled: the printed by-laws carry no
date on their face — only "adopted at the annual meeting" — and the minute book for 1889–92 was
lost in the 1938 office removal. The *text* is certain; three sources quote it identically.

**Quantities:** the Ostrey Hollow station received about 40,000,000 lb of milk over the twenty-five
full delivery years 1898–1922, roughly 1,600,000 lb a year; butterfat averaged 25¢ a pound over
that stretch (Kyle got both figures out himself for the arbitration and Effie has them from
Teague; Teague's own 44,000,000 and Sill's and Kepp's 27¢ are outliers). The station closed in
1946; the milk went down the valley by truck.

**Documents:** Effie Loomis's "Notes toward a history"; Rundle's jubilee pamphlet; Halbert Nym's
accession note on Item 1 (the undated printed by-laws); Falke's county report; Kyle's clerk's
memoir.

### Story 2. The Station and the Man Who Kept Its Book: Ostrey Hollow, 1896–1925

**Who:** Ansel Keddie, born 1861, tester and separator man; his wife Orra (Emmet Brant's sister);
the six farms of Hessel Bottom, of which Emmet Brant's was the largest — 60-odd acres of a
160-acre bottom, 38 cows, the most milk of any man on the creek.

**What happened:** the Association built the station in the winter of 1895–96 and opened it on
4 May 1896. A board building by the creek with a covered platform at wagon height, an intake room
holding the scale, the vat and the separator, and above the intake room a loft reached by a ladder
nailed to the wall — not a stair, a ladder. Milk was weighed in, sampled, tested, separated; cream
went down to Larrow Green on Oram's wagon, skim went back out the same platform for the hogs.
Keddie went up in 1895 and made every test for twenty-nine seasons: at the station by half past
five, every can weighed, a ten-day composite in each patron's numbered jar, the charge drawn to
the mark on the pipette stem, four bottles at a time in the hand tester, the fat column read
against the rulings on the bottle neck. "Thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons."

**When and what the book showed:** the first leaf reads "*Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass
borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes.*" The 1896 part-season averaged **3.80**. From 1898
the station stood at **3.61**, or within three hundredths of it, every year for a quarter of a
century — except the nine weeks of 1911. The Larrow Green creamery's own patrons ran 3.78 over the
same years. Complaints began in 1902 and were renewed every decade. The deficiency was
**nineteen hundredths of a point**, and — the fact that decides the case — it was the same
nineteen hundredths in the flush of June as in the depth of February.

**End:** dismissed 30 April 1925 after twenty-nine seasons "and a letter"; kept four or five cows,
did carpentry, and died in February 1934 with the imputation on him.

**Documents:** Keddie's statement of 17 March 1925 and his unsent letter; the station test book
and its 1922 summary leaf; Orra Keddie's diary; Duncan Keddie's family notes; Cleve Oram's tape
(the building itself); Falke's report.

### Story 3. One Lot of Pipettes: Ammon & Sons of Tarnet, 1897 and 1923

**Who:** Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet — Marcus Ammon, founder; Junia Ammon, the firm's
own historian, writing in 1949.

**What happened:** a milk-test pipette must *deliver* a stated quantity, not hold it, which is the
harder measurement, because milk clings to the glass. A man blows the bulb, draws the stem, and
marks it warm against a gauge hanging on a nail at his elbow; the piece is annealed overnight and
gauged the next morning. On 14 April 1897 the house billed the Larrow Valley Association for the
Ostrey Hollow station's whole outfit:

> 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20 · 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck,
> at .22 — 31.68 · 1 acid measure — .90 · 1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00 · Freight — 2.15 ·
> **Total — 77.93**

The outfit went into use at the station on **1 May 1897** — the date Orra Keddie also enters in
her diary that week, pleased for her husband. The lot ran short. Measured in 1958, the survivors
delivered a mean of **16.72 c.c.** against a blown mark of 17.6: short 0.88 c.c., **95 hundredths
of the mark**, one part in twenty, and short *consistently*.

**Why:** Junia Ammon's own explanation, offered because nobody else would: the mold had been
reground and the lot ran short, and no one in the works caught it, because "a boy checks the
first of a lot and the tenth and the fiftieth and finds them consistent — they are consistently
wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind cannot find." The works books that would have
settled it burned in 1931. The *Ashlin Chronicle*'s charge in 1958 that a cheap short lot was
knowingly put off on the Association is unsupported by anything but the firm's silence, and the
firm's own historian, printing her house's "bad morning," attributes it to accident.

**The end of it:** 12 June 1923 the state inspector ran the pipettes against a burette and
condemned the lot. The Association ordered on 28 June; Ammon's letter of 2 July entered the
order, regretted the trouble, and said "our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell
you anything about the lot you name." Junia Ammon calls that letter, not the accident, the thing
she regrets.

**Documents:** the invoice (in Nym's accession list and in facsimile in the firm's history); the
2 July 1923 letter-book copy; the measurement sheet of 9 March 1958.

### Story 4. The Experiment That Ran Itself: honest glass in 1896, 1911 and 1924

**Who:** Ansel Keddie; Hobart Sill, who made up the first outfit; Prof. Leland Vaught, who
recognised what the interval was, forty-seven years late.

**What happened:** the station's tests were governed by a single object, and three times in
twenty-eight years that object was changed while everything else stood still. Each time the
figures moved, and moved by exactly the amount the glass was wrong.

*1896.* The station opened with no outfit of its own. Sill made one up out of his own glass at his
own bench at Larrow Green and sent it up the valley on the wagon; the station's own came the
spring after and Sill's went back, one piece chipped. That borrowed season averaged **3.80**.

*1911.* "*May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure.*" Orra Keddie's diary for
8 May 1911 records her husband coming home cross about a broken measure — an independent date.
For **nine weeks**, to 10 July, the tests stood at 3.80. Then: "*July 10. Our own measure in use
again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.*" The herds, the pasture, the feed,
the season, the men, the method, the bottles, the acid and the scales were unchanged; one object
changed. Merle Strawn stood at the tester's elbow through a test that June and wrote "nothing
here to note." Ansel came home that summer saying it was something in the glass and said it
twice, at supper and again by the door — but he would not write down what he could not prove, and
what went into the book was a question mark, left standing fourteen years.

*1923–24.* The condemned glass was replaced from July; the 1924 average was **3.80**, in Keddie's
book and independently in Strawn's circuit book.

**What it proves:** the deficit tracked the instrument, not the milk and not the men. Vaught's
Bulletin §IV: an experiment that "ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have
bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven years."

**Unsettled:** where the 1911 measure came from — Ivy Keddie says up from Larrow Green with the
cream wagon, Roy Brant says the state man left his own that June. The state did not begin issuing
burettes to its field men until 1921, and Strawn's own June 1911 entry mentions no loan, which
leans against Roy; but the sources do not settle it.

**Documents:** the test book's 1911 entries and 1922 summary leaf; Orra's diary; Sill's last-day
remarks; Strawn's circuit books; Bulletin No. 471 §IV.
