### Story 5. Station Gain: the money that was never missing, 1897–1938

**Who:** Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945, who wrote the line; Bertram Kyle, the clerk who
copied it for twenty-five years; Hobart Sill, who weighed the cream at the other end; Selby Vose,
who signed the sheet; Dell Rundle, who saw it once and withdrew a chapter.

**What happened:** every year the Ostrey Hollow station account carried three figures and a
sentence — pounds of butterfat credited to the patrons on the station test; pounds of butterfat
received at Larrow Green in the station's cream; and the difference, entered as *station gain*.
The 1898 entry stands as the type of all of them: credited **57,760 lb**, received **60,800 lb**,
"*station gain 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account.*"
The Association's own books recorded, every year for a quarter of a century, that more fat
arrived at the creamery than the station had paid for. The missing butterfat was never missing.
It was entered, named, totalled, carried forward, and read out at the annual meeting to fifty
farmers with their hats on their knees.

**Why nobody saw it:** Kyle's explanation is the best thing in the record — "*It had a name. That
is the thing about a name. A figure with a name on it has been explained, and you rule under it
and go on to the next.*" Teague believed the line was his own device from 1905 (it cannot be:
the 1898 entry predates him and the column runs from 1897), and later made its very evenness his
argument — "a figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture." Sill, at the
churn, knew the physical fact first-hand and dismissed it as ordinary: "*That cream ran heavy.
Every year it ran heavy... Always more. Never less.*" Nobody ever asked him. Selby Vose signed the
annual sheet every year by putting his thumb on the bottom figure and never reading the lines
above. Teague put it to him once, in 1916, and was told to let it alone.

**Quantities:** over 1898–1922, 1,444,000 lb credited against 1,520,000 lb delivered — **76,000
lb** of butterfat, about **$19,000**. The gain is 5.00% of fat delivered and 5.25% of fat
credited; both percentages are right of their own denominator, and the 5% is the one that is also
the glass.

**Documents:** the ledgers (Nym's Item 6); Teague's memoir Ch. 9; Kyle's "What I saw of the
office"; Sill's remarks; Rundle's note added while in press — "*Cream stolen at the Hollow would
have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my
paragraph stand.*"

### Story 6. Three Wrong Answers: water, theft, and the wet hay, 1902–1926

**Who:** Selby Vose (water); Teague, Vira Toland, Oram's father, and the valley at large (theft);
the 1908 Grigg committee, Nyle Grigg, and Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S. (bog hay).

**Water.** Vose's theory, held without proof: nine families were adding water, and stopped when
the Association began to look. In June 1919 a circular went to the Ostrey Hollow patrons over his
signature and no one else's — Kyle typed it — warning them about water in their milk. Keddie
would not post them or leave them on the platform; he carried them round himself and stood at
each door while the man read it, and came home at eleven at night with his hat still on. Two days
later Emmet Brant stood in the yard, would not come in, and asked his brother-in-law why he had
carried the paper if he did not believe it. They did not speak again for ten years. *Defeated
by:* Merle Strawn's lactometer at every visit on the circuit from 1907 — he never once found
water at that station and told the manager so in 1919; Rosalie Cudd's sixteen summers at the vat
("a cheesemaker knows watered milk inside a day... cheese is a witness"); and the nine weeks of
1911, since nine families do not all get honest on 8 May and relapse on 10 July.

**Theft.** That the tester drew off cream and wrote low tests to cover it. Insinuated by Teague at
the 1925 hearing "in the way an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it," believed
by the hauler who saw both ends of the load every day, and settled opinion at the store.
*Defeated by:* Sill's weighing — stolen cream arrives light, and this cream arrived heavy every
year for forty years; the ledger, which accounts for every pound; and 1911 again, the same man
under no different supervision.

**Bog hay.** The most respectable error. The Association's 1908 committee sampled the six Hessel
Bottom herds and reported that "the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream... the remedy
lies with the land and not with the station," adding, to its credit, "we found no dishonesty of
any kind, in any quarter." Kepp gave it a chemistry in 1926 — sour ground, sedge and rush, a cow
short of minerals "robs her own frame, and the fat is the first economy she practices."
*Defeated by:* Bulletin No. 214, which found bog hay depressed the test "by not more than five
hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture" —
wrong magnitude and wrong seasonal shape against a flat 0.19 in every month; by Alder Corners,
where the same cows off the same wet ground tested 3.78–3.82 for sixteen seasons; and by the fact
that the theory's two chief advocates both quoted the bulletin as saying "two tenths in the
winter feeding," which it does not say. Grigg's 1919 covering letter also inflates his own
method — "every month of that year" — where the report he reprints says January, February and
March. Kepp conceded in discussion that he had "never in his life read a test and would not know
how." Falke conceded the hay a partial role in 1959 and withdrew the concession in a note when he
finally read Bulletin 214 entire.

**Documents:** the 1919 circular; Strawn's circuit books; Rosalie Cudd's letter and table; the
1908 report with Grigg's letter; Kepp's paper; Falke's report and its note.

### Story 7. The Arbitration of 1925 and the Ruin of Ansel Keddie

**Who:** Alonzo Frick, attorney of Ashlin, chairman of the board; the Ostrey Hollow patrons;
Keddie; Vose; Teague.

**What happened:** the patrons petitioned in January 1925 — Emmet Brant's name on it, his sister
not blaming him and not saying so to her husband — reckoning their loss at 67,000 lb of
butterfat. A board of arbitration sat under Frick from **17 March to 4 May 1925**: three sittings,
the first at the Association's office at Ashlin because that is where the lawyer was, and the
second and third at Frick's insistence in the station's own weigh room, with a dozen men standing
outside on the platform in the rain. The board saw the scales tried and a test made — on the new
glass, which delivered its mark. Frick: "I did not understand the test then and do not understand
it now."

Three explanations were argued: water, put by Selby Vose "without heat and without proof"; theft
by the tester, insinuated by Teague, who then observed that he made no charge against anybody; and
the hay, by the 1908 report read into the record, which "had the advantage of blaming nobody in
the room." Keddie gave a nine-sentence statement, described his method, and ended "I cannot tell
you why the figures are what they are" — which condemned him, because "a man who cannot account
for a figure is presumed by everybody in the room to be the figure's author."

**The exhibits — four books:** the station test book, 4 May 1896–31 Dec 1924, in Keddie's hand;
the station receiving book; Emmet Brant's private weigh book, lent and wanted back; and **the
state dairy inspector's circuit notebook for 1923** — the book containing the entry "*These
measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot.*" It lay on the table. Kyle carried the
four in and out and could not say that any man in that room ever laid the second beside the
fourth. The condemned crate itself was twenty feet overhead, in the loft, through two of the
three sittings.

**Outcome:** Keddie was dismissed on **30 April 1925**, four days before the award, with no
finding against him ever made. The award of **4 May 1925** "*finds no cause established...
thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station
account*" — one fifth of what had been lost. Vose called it in 1926 "a generous settlement of an
unproved claim."

**Documents:** Frick's yearbook article; Effie's notes (award quoted verbatim); Keddie's statement;
Orra's diary; Oram's and Hazel Brant's tapes; Kyle's memoir; Vose's 1926 address.

### Story 8. The Ladder: Ivy Keddie, 1958, and what came after

**Who:** Ivy Keddie, born 1931, Duncan's daughter and Ansel's granddaughter, a graduate student at
the State Agricultural College at Farland; Prof. Leland Vaught; Rosalie Cudd; Roy and Hazel Brant.

**What happened:** in **February 1958** Ivy Keddie went up the nailed ladder into the loft over
the station's intake room and found, under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff, the crate that had
gone up there the day the glass was condemned in 1923. Six dozen pipettes had been bought; **nine**
survived unbroken, the rest broken over thirty-five years by everybody who went up after wire. On
**9 March 1958** she measured all nine against a calibrated burette at Farland — 16.70, 16.71,
16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 — mean **16.72 c.c.** against a blown 17.6:
"**95 hundredths of the mark.**"

She then did what nobody had done in sixty years: put five separate record sets in one room — the
station test book, the Association's ledgers, Strawn's circuit notebooks (at Farland since 1947,
unasked for in the interval), Emmet Brant's weigh book (lent by Roy — lent, and both his daughter
and Ivy's father insist on the word), and Rosalie Cudd's Alder Corners figures, copied off the
leaves at a stranger's request. Every set agreed on 3.80.

**The result:** Bulletin No. 471, Farland, **October 1958**: "*No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow
and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the
station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in
twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the
Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain.*"

**The arithmetic of delay:** thirty-five years from the condemnation, thirty-three from the
dismissal, and Ansel Keddie twenty-four years dead. "I have no remedy." The *Ashlin Chronicle*
put it on the front page on 3 October 1958, with the substance right and most of the figures
wrong. Falke reported to the county board in 1959 and recommended no action, all parties in
interest being dead. The books went to three buildings; the crate and the test book to the Ordell
County Historical Rooms in 1959, the weigh book in 1963. Hazel Brant, who was glad and a little
sour: "A thing you could have settled in an afternoon with a burette and a straight face, and it
took fifty years and a girl from the college who thought to climb a ladder."

**Documents:** Ivy Keddie's 1959 talk; the measurement sheet; Bulletin 471; Rosalie Cudd's letter;
the *Chronicle*; Falke's report; Duncan Keddie's notes on where the books went; Hazel Brant's and
Cleve Oram's tapes.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter** — the daughter of Ansel and Orra's son Duncan.
The chain is stated by the one person best placed to know and hardest to mistake: Duncan himself,
writing family notes in 1968 precisely because he had "already twice seen it printed otherwise" —
"My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter." Ivy's own 1959
talk, Hazel Brant, and Roy Brant all agree. Two sources say "great-niece": the *Ashlin Chronicle*
of 3 October 1958 and page two of Falke's county report. These are not two independent witnesses —
both worked from the college press release, and the *Chronicle* built its story from that release
and two telephone calls in a day and a half. Falke then verified the point and retracted it in a
note at the end of his own report ("the error on page two is mine"), which leaves the granddaughter
reading uncontradicted by anyone who checked.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins** — the children of first cousins. Derivation:
Orra (Ansel's wife) and Emmet Brant were sister and brother; their children, Duncan Keddie and Roy
Brant, are therefore first cousins; Duncan's daughter Ivy and Roy's daughter Hazel are therefore
second cousins. All three of the people closest to it — Roy, Duncan and Hazel — state it
independently and with visible irritation at being told otherwise, and Rundle's 1959 footnote
agrees. Cleve Oram's "first cousin once removed" is a bystander's version of a relation he was not
in; that relation would require the two women to stand a generation apart, and they do not.
*What the sources leave open in the intervening generation:* whether Orra and Emmet were full or
half siblings. Roy Brant calls Orra his father's full sister; Hazel says she was a half sister, by
Emmet's father's second wife; Duncan says only "his sister, the younger of the two," and Orra's
own diary says "my brother" and puts her girlhood in the same kitchen. On balance I prefer Roy,
one generation nearer than Hazel, but the point is not settled — and it does not disturb the
cousin arithmetic either way.

**B3. Selby Vose was the son of Gideon Vose**, the Association's organizer, first president
(1889–1911), and author of the by-laws. Effie Loomis, the secretary, says son; Selby himself, in
his 1926 address, calls Gideon "my father"; Duncan Keddie and Kyle both write it down expressly
because they have heard the nephew story locally. Vira Toland and Rundle say nephew and are wrong.
*Why it matters:* the one office that could have noticed the anomaly was held, without a break
from the founding to 1938, by a father and then his son. Gideon wrote the rule that made the test
the paymaster and presided while the complaints began in 1902; Selby managed from 1913, signed
every annual sheet carrying the "station gain" line without reading the lines above the total, was
told by his own bookkeeper in 1916 that something was wrong and said to let it alone, sent the
1919 water circular over his own name, and in 1926 defended the settlement and the theory of
watering alike. The Association never got an outside eye on its own ledger because the ledger was
always signed by a Vose.

**B4. There was no established relationship between the Voses and the Keddies.** Cleve Oram
repeats that "the Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort," has it from his mother, who
had it from hers, and concedes he "could not draw you the line on paper." Against that, Duncan
Keddie denies it flatly, gives the source of the confusion — the Ashlin Voses, who were no
relation to Gideon either — notes that the rumor has run since before he could walk, and rests it
on his mother, "the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the
connection," who never once put a Vose in it. A named, traced denial from inside the family
outranks an untraceable village report from outside it. Treat the two families as unrelated;
strictly, the sources establish no connection rather than proving a negative.
