### Story 1. The Rule That Made the Loss: the Association and Article VII, 1889–1946

**Who:** the farmers of the Larrow Valley, Ordell County; Gideon Vose, organizer and first
president 1889–1911; Effie Loomis, secretary 1901–1940; Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945;
Bertram Kyle, office clerk; Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green 1893–1934; Selby Vose,
manager 1913–1938.

**What happened:** farmers who had been making butter in their kitchens organized the Larrow
Valley Cooperative Creamery Association at a meeting at Larrow Green in 1889, built the main
creamery there in 1891, and then put up skimming stations at the heads of the valleys — Ostrey
Hollow, seven miles up, among them. Gideon Vose wrote the by-laws. Their seventh article is the
hinge of everything that follows: a patron is paid not on the weight of his milk but on the pounds
of butterfat it is *found by test* to contain, "and the test of the station where he delivers
shall govern." Orin Falke, the county agent, put the consequence exactly in 1959: under payment
by weight, nothing in this history would have cost any man a dollar. Article VII made a
laboratory reading into money, and made one man's glassware the paymaster of a valley.

**When:** organized 1889; creamery 1891; Article VII adopted 1892 on the secretary's authority
(Dell Rundle's pamphlet dates it 1891). The year cannot be settled: the printed by-laws carry no
date on their face — only "adopted at the annual meeting" — and the minute book for 1889–92 was
lost in the 1938 office removal. The *text* is certain; three sources quote it identically.

**Quantities:** the Ostrey Hollow station received about 40,000,000 lb of milk over the twenty-five
full delivery years 1898–1922, roughly 1,600,000 lb a year; butterfat averaged 25¢ a pound over
that stretch (Kyle got both figures out himself for the arbitration and Effie has them from
Teague; Teague's own 44,000,000 and Sill's and Kepp's 27¢ are outliers). The station closed in
1946; the milk went down the valley by truck.

**Documents:** Effie Loomis's "Notes toward a history"; Rundle's jubilee pamphlet; Halbert Nym's
accession note on Item 1 (the undated printed by-laws); Falke's county report; Kyle's clerk's
memoir.

### Story 2. The Station and the Man Who Kept Its Book: Ostrey Hollow, 1896–1925

**Who:** Ansel Keddie, born 1861, tester and separator man; his wife Orra (Emmet Brant's sister);
the six farms of Hessel Bottom, of which Emmet Brant's was the largest — 60-odd acres of a
160-acre bottom, 38 cows, the most milk of any man on the creek.

**What happened:** the Association built the station in the winter of 1895–96 and opened it on
4 May 1896. A board building by the creek with a covered platform at wagon height, an intake room
holding the scale, the vat and the separator, and above the intake room a loft reached by a ladder
nailed to the wall — not a stair, a ladder. Milk was weighed in, sampled, tested, separated; cream
went down to Larrow Green on Oram's wagon, skim went back out the same platform for the hogs.
Keddie went up in 1895 and made every test for twenty-nine seasons: at the station by half past
five, every can weighed, a ten-day composite in each patron's numbered jar, the charge drawn to
the mark on the pipette stem, four bottles at a time in the hand tester, the fat column read
against the rulings on the bottle neck. "Thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons."

**When and what the book showed:** the first leaf reads "*Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass
borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes.*" The 1896 part-season averaged **3.80**. From 1898
the station stood at **3.61**, or within three hundredths of it, every year for a quarter of a
century — except the nine weeks of 1911. The Larrow Green creamery's own patrons ran 3.78 over the
same years. Complaints began in 1902 and were renewed every decade. The deficiency was
**nineteen hundredths of a point**, and — the fact that decides the case — it was the same
nineteen hundredths in the flush of June as in the depth of February.

**End:** dismissed 30 April 1925 after twenty-nine seasons "and a letter"; kept four or five cows,
did carpentry, and died in February 1934 with the imputation on him.

**Documents:** Keddie's statement of 17 March 1925 and his unsent letter; the station test book
and its 1922 summary leaf; Orra Keddie's diary; Duncan Keddie's family notes; Cleve Oram's tape
(the building itself); Falke's report.

### Story 3. One Lot of Pipettes: Ammon & Sons of Tarnet, 1897 and 1923

**Who:** Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet — Marcus Ammon, founder; Junia Ammon, the firm's
own historian, writing in 1949.

**What happened:** a milk-test pipette must *deliver* a stated quantity, not hold it, which is the
harder measurement, because milk clings to the glass. A man blows the bulb, draws the stem, and
marks it warm against a gauge hanging on a nail at his elbow; the piece is annealed overnight and
gauged the next morning. On 14 April 1897 the house billed the Larrow Valley Association for the
Ostrey Hollow station's whole outfit:

> 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20 · 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck,
> at .22 — 31.68 · 1 acid measure — .90 · 1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00 · Freight — 2.15 ·
> **Total — 77.93**

The outfit went into use at the station on **1 May 1897** — the date Orra Keddie also enters in
her diary that week, pleased for her husband. The lot ran short. Measured in 1958, the survivors
delivered a mean of **16.72 c.c.** against a blown mark of 17.6: short 0.88 c.c., **95 hundredths
of the mark**, one part in twenty, and short *consistently*.

**Why:** Junia Ammon's own explanation, offered because nobody else would: the mold had been
reground and the lot ran short, and no one in the works caught it, because "a boy checks the
first of a lot and the tenth and the fiftieth and finds them consistent — they are consistently
wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind cannot find." The works books that would have
settled it burned in 1931. The *Ashlin Chronicle*'s charge in 1958 that a cheap short lot was
knowingly put off on the Association is unsupported by anything but the firm's silence, and the
firm's own historian, printing her house's "bad morning," attributes it to accident.

**The end of it:** 12 June 1923 the state inspector ran the pipettes against a burette and
condemned the lot. The Association ordered on 28 June; Ammon's letter of 2 July entered the
order, regretted the trouble, and said "our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell
you anything about the lot you name." Junia Ammon calls that letter, not the accident, the thing
she regrets.

**Documents:** the invoice (in Nym's accession list and in facsimile in the firm's history); the
2 July 1923 letter-book copy; the measurement sheet of 9 March 1958.

### Story 4. The Experiment That Ran Itself: honest glass in 1896, 1911 and 1924

**Who:** Ansel Keddie; Hobart Sill, who made up the first outfit; Prof. Leland Vaught, who
recognised what the interval was, forty-seven years late.

**What happened:** the station's tests were governed by a single object, and three times in
twenty-eight years that object was changed while everything else stood still. Each time the
figures moved, and moved by exactly the amount the glass was wrong.

*1896.* The station opened with no outfit of its own. Sill made one up out of his own glass at his
own bench at Larrow Green and sent it up the valley on the wagon; the station's own came the
spring after and Sill's went back, one piece chipped. That borrowed season averaged **3.80**.

*1911.* "*May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure.*" Orra Keddie's diary for
8 May 1911 records her husband coming home cross about a broken measure — an independent date.
For **nine weeks**, to 10 July, the tests stood at 3.80. Then: "*July 10. Our own measure in use
again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.*" The herds, the pasture, the feed,
the season, the men, the method, the bottles, the acid and the scales were unchanged; one object
changed. Merle Strawn stood at the tester's elbow through a test that June and wrote "nothing
here to note." Ansel came home that summer saying it was something in the glass and said it
twice, at supper and again by the door — but he would not write down what he could not prove, and
what went into the book was a question mark, left standing fourteen years.

*1923–24.* The condemned glass was replaced from July; the 1924 average was **3.80**, in Keddie's
book and independently in Strawn's circuit book.

**What it proves:** the deficit tracked the instrument, not the milk and not the men. Vaught's
Bulletin §IV: an experiment that "ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have
bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven years."

**Unsettled:** where the 1911 measure came from — Ivy Keddie says up from Larrow Green with the
cream wagon, Roy Brant says the state man left his own that June. The state did not begin issuing
burettes to its field men until 1921, and Strawn's own June 1911 entry mentions no loan, which
leans against Roy; but the sources do not settle it.

**Documents:** the test book's 1911 entries and 1922 summary leaf; Orra's diary; Sill's last-day
remarks; Strawn's circuit books; Bulletin No. 471 §IV.

### Story 5. Station Gain: the money that was never missing, 1897–1938

**Who:** Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945, who wrote the line; Bertram Kyle, the clerk who
copied it for twenty-five years; Hobart Sill, who weighed the cream at the other end; Selby Vose,
who signed the sheet; Dell Rundle, who saw it once and withdrew a chapter.

**What happened:** every year the Ostrey Hollow station account carried three figures and a
sentence — pounds of butterfat credited to the patrons on the station test; pounds of butterfat
received at Larrow Green in the station's cream; and the difference, entered as *station gain*.
The 1898 entry stands as the type of all of them: credited **57,760 lb**, received **60,800 lb**,
"*station gain 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account.*"
The Association's own books recorded, every year for a quarter of a century, that more fat
arrived at the creamery than the station had paid for. The missing butterfat was never missing.
It was entered, named, totalled, carried forward, and read out at the annual meeting to fifty
farmers with their hats on their knees.

**Why nobody saw it:** Kyle's explanation is the best thing in the record — "*It had a name. That
is the thing about a name. A figure with a name on it has been explained, and you rule under it
and go on to the next.*" Teague believed the line was his own device from 1905 (it cannot be:
the 1898 entry predates him and the column runs from 1897), and later made its very evenness his
argument — "a figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture." Sill, at the
churn, knew the physical fact first-hand and dismissed it as ordinary: "*That cream ran heavy.
Every year it ran heavy... Always more. Never less.*" Nobody ever asked him. Selby Vose signed the
annual sheet every year by putting his thumb on the bottom figure and never reading the lines
above. Teague put it to him once, in 1916, and was told to let it alone.

**Quantities:** over 1898–1922, 1,444,000 lb credited against 1,520,000 lb delivered — **76,000
lb** of butterfat, about **$19,000**. The gain is 5.00% of fat delivered and 5.25% of fat
credited; both percentages are right of their own denominator, and the 5% is the one that is also
the glass.

**Documents:** the ledgers (Nym's Item 6); Teague's memoir Ch. 9; Kyle's "What I saw of the
office"; Sill's remarks; Rundle's note added while in press — "*Cream stolen at the Hollow would
have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my
paragraph stand.*"

### Story 6. Three Wrong Answers: water, theft, and the wet hay, 1902–1926

**Who:** Selby Vose (water); Teague, Vira Toland, Oram's father, and the valley at large (theft);
the 1908 Grigg committee, Nyle Grigg, and Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S. (bog hay).

**Water.** Vose's theory, held without proof: nine families were adding water, and stopped when
the Association began to look. In June 1919 a circular went to the Ostrey Hollow patrons over his
signature and no one else's — Kyle typed it — warning them about water in their milk. Keddie
would not post them or leave them on the platform; he carried them round himself and stood at
each door while the man read it, and came home at eleven at night with his hat still on. Two days
later Emmet Brant stood in the yard, would not come in, and asked his brother-in-law why he had
carried the paper if he did not believe it. They did not speak again for ten years. *Defeated
by:* Merle Strawn's lactometer at every visit on the circuit from 1907 — he never once found
water at that station and told the manager so in 1919; Rosalie Cudd's sixteen summers at the vat
("a cheesemaker knows watered milk inside a day... cheese is a witness"); and the nine weeks of
1911, since nine families do not all get honest on 8 May and relapse on 10 July.

**Theft.** That the tester drew off cream and wrote low tests to cover it. Insinuated by Teague at
the 1925 hearing "in the way an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it," believed
by the hauler who saw both ends of the load every day, and settled opinion at the store.
*Defeated by:* Sill's weighing — stolen cream arrives light, and this cream arrived heavy every
year for forty years; the ledger, which accounts for every pound; and 1911 again, the same man
under no different supervision.

**Bog hay.** The most respectable error. The Association's 1908 committee sampled the six Hessel
Bottom herds and reported that "the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream... the remedy
lies with the land and not with the station," adding, to its credit, "we found no dishonesty of
any kind, in any quarter." Kepp gave it a chemistry in 1926 — sour ground, sedge and rush, a cow
short of minerals "robs her own frame, and the fat is the first economy she practices."
*Defeated by:* Bulletin No. 214, which found bog hay depressed the test "by not more than five
hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture" —
wrong magnitude and wrong seasonal shape against a flat 0.19 in every month; by Alder Corners,
where the same cows off the same wet ground tested 3.78–3.82 for sixteen seasons; and by the fact
that the theory's two chief advocates both quoted the bulletin as saying "two tenths in the
winter feeding," which it does not say. Grigg's 1919 covering letter also inflates his own
method — "every month of that year" — where the report he reprints says January, February and
March. Kepp conceded in discussion that he had "never in his life read a test and would not know
how." Falke conceded the hay a partial role in 1959 and withdrew the concession in a note when he
finally read Bulletin 214 entire.

**Documents:** the 1919 circular; Strawn's circuit books; Rosalie Cudd's letter and table; the
1908 report with Grigg's letter; Kepp's paper; Falke's report and its note.

### Story 7. The Arbitration of 1925 and the Ruin of Ansel Keddie

**Who:** Alonzo Frick, attorney of Ashlin, chairman of the board; the Ostrey Hollow patrons;
Keddie; Vose; Teague.

**What happened:** the patrons petitioned in January 1925 — Emmet Brant's name on it, his sister
not blaming him and not saying so to her husband — reckoning their loss at 67,000 lb of
butterfat. A board of arbitration sat under Frick from **17 March to 4 May 1925**: three sittings,
the first at the Association's office at Ashlin because that is where the lawyer was, and the
second and third at Frick's insistence in the station's own weigh room, with a dozen men standing
outside on the platform in the rain. The board saw the scales tried and a test made — on the new
glass, which delivered its mark. Frick: "I did not understand the test then and do not understand
it now."

Three explanations were argued: water, put by Selby Vose "without heat and without proof"; theft
by the tester, insinuated by Teague, who then observed that he made no charge against anybody; and
the hay, by the 1908 report read into the record, which "had the advantage of blaming nobody in
the room." Keddie gave a nine-sentence statement, described his method, and ended "I cannot tell
you why the figures are what they are" — which condemned him, because "a man who cannot account
for a figure is presumed by everybody in the room to be the figure's author."

**The exhibits — four books:** the station test book, 4 May 1896–31 Dec 1924, in Keddie's hand;
the station receiving book; Emmet Brant's private weigh book, lent and wanted back; and **the
state dairy inspector's circuit notebook for 1923** — the book containing the entry "*These
measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot.*" It lay on the table. Kyle carried the
four in and out and could not say that any man in that room ever laid the second beside the
fourth. The condemned crate itself was twenty feet overhead, in the loft, through two of the
three sittings.

**Outcome:** Keddie was dismissed on **30 April 1925**, four days before the award, with no
finding against him ever made. The award of **4 May 1925** "*finds no cause established...
thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station
account*" — one fifth of what had been lost. Vose called it in 1926 "a generous settlement of an
unproved claim."

**Documents:** Frick's yearbook article; Effie's notes (award quoted verbatim); Keddie's statement;
Orra's diary; Oram's and Hazel Brant's tapes; Kyle's memoir; Vose's 1926 address.

### Story 8. The Ladder: Ivy Keddie, 1958, and what came after

**Who:** Ivy Keddie, born 1931, Duncan's daughter and Ansel's granddaughter, a graduate student at
the State Agricultural College at Farland; Prof. Leland Vaught; Rosalie Cudd; Roy and Hazel Brant.

**What happened:** in **February 1958** Ivy Keddie went up the nailed ladder into the loft over
the station's intake room and found, under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff, the crate that had
gone up there the day the glass was condemned in 1923. Six dozen pipettes had been bought; **nine**
survived unbroken, the rest broken over thirty-five years by everybody who went up after wire. On
**9 March 1958** she measured all nine against a calibrated burette at Farland — 16.70, 16.71,
16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 — mean **16.72 c.c.** against a blown 17.6:
"**95 hundredths of the mark.**"

She then did what nobody had done in sixty years: put five separate record sets in one room — the
station test book, the Association's ledgers, Strawn's circuit notebooks (at Farland since 1947,
unasked for in the interval), Emmet Brant's weigh book (lent by Roy — lent, and both his daughter
and Ivy's father insist on the word), and Rosalie Cudd's Alder Corners figures, copied off the
leaves at a stranger's request. Every set agreed on 3.80.

**The result:** Bulletin No. 471, Farland, **October 1958**: "*No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow
and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the
station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in
twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the
Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain.*"

**The arithmetic of delay:** thirty-five years from the condemnation, thirty-three from the
dismissal, and Ansel Keddie twenty-four years dead. "I have no remedy." The *Ashlin Chronicle*
put it on the front page on 3 October 1958, with the substance right and most of the figures
wrong. Falke reported to the county board in 1959 and recommended no action, all parties in
interest being dead. The books went to three buildings; the crate and the test book to the Ordell
County Historical Rooms in 1959, the weigh book in 1963. Hazel Brant, who was glad and a little
sour: "A thing you could have settled in an afternoon with a burette and a straight face, and it
took fifty years and a girl from the college who thought to climb a ladder."

**Documents:** Ivy Keddie's 1959 talk; the measurement sheet; Bulletin 471; Rosalie Cudd's letter;
the *Chronicle*; Falke's report; Duncan Keddie's notes on where the books went; Hazel Brant's and
Cleve Oram's tapes.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter** — the daughter of Ansel and Orra's son Duncan.
The chain is stated by the one person best placed to know and hardest to mistake: Duncan himself,
writing family notes in 1968 precisely because he had "already twice seen it printed otherwise" —
"My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter." Ivy's own 1959
talk, Hazel Brant, and Roy Brant all agree. Two sources say "great-niece": the *Ashlin Chronicle*
of 3 October 1958 and page two of Falke's county report. These are not two independent witnesses —
both worked from the college press release, and the *Chronicle* built its story from that release
and two telephone calls in a day and a half. Falke then verified the point and retracted it in a
note at the end of his own report ("the error on page two is mine"), which leaves the granddaughter
reading uncontradicted by anyone who checked.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins** — the children of first cousins. Derivation:
Orra (Ansel's wife) and Emmet Brant were sister and brother; their children, Duncan Keddie and Roy
Brant, are therefore first cousins; Duncan's daughter Ivy and Roy's daughter Hazel are therefore
second cousins. All three of the people closest to it — Roy, Duncan and Hazel — state it
independently and with visible irritation at being told otherwise, and Rundle's 1959 footnote
agrees. Cleve Oram's "first cousin once removed" is a bystander's version of a relation he was not
in; that relation would require the two women to stand a generation apart, and they do not.
*What the sources leave open in the intervening generation:* whether Orra and Emmet were full or
half siblings. Roy Brant calls Orra his father's full sister; Hazel says she was a half sister, by
Emmet's father's second wife; Duncan says only "his sister, the younger of the two," and Orra's
own diary says "my brother" and puts her girlhood in the same kitchen. On balance I prefer Roy,
one generation nearer than Hazel, but the point is not settled — and it does not disturb the
cousin arithmetic either way.

**B3. Selby Vose was the son of Gideon Vose**, the Association's organizer, first president
(1889–1911), and author of the by-laws. Effie Loomis, the secretary, says son; Selby himself, in
his 1926 address, calls Gideon "my father"; Duncan Keddie and Kyle both write it down expressly
because they have heard the nephew story locally. Vira Toland and Rundle say nephew and are wrong.
*Why it matters:* the one office that could have noticed the anomaly was held, without a break
from the founding to 1938, by a father and then his son. Gideon wrote the rule that made the test
the paymaster and presided while the complaints began in 1902; Selby managed from 1913, signed
every annual sheet carrying the "station gain" line without reading the lines above the total, was
told by his own bookkeeper in 1916 that something was wrong and said to let it alone, sent the
1919 water circular over his own name, and in 1926 defended the settlement and the theory of
watering alike. The Association never got an outside eye on its own ledger because the ledger was
always signed by a Vose.

**B4. There was no established relationship between the Voses and the Keddies.** Cleve Oram
repeats that "the Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort," has it from his mother, who
had it from hers, and concedes he "could not draw you the line on paper." Against that, Duncan
Keddie denies it flatly, gives the source of the confusion — the Ashlin Voses, who were no
relation to Gideon either — notes that the rumor has run since before he could walk, and rests it
on his mother, "the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the
connection," who never once put a Vose in it. A named, traced denial from inside the family
outranks an untraceable village report from outside it. Treat the two families as unrelated;
strictly, the sources establish no connection rather than proving a negative.

## Section C — Math

### C1.

**(a) The tests read 95% of the truth — short by one part in twenty (a 5% proportional deficit).**
From the glassware: the pipettes were blown on the stem to deliver 17.6 c.c., the standard charge
of milk, and the test bottle's neck is ruled for that one charge and no other. The nine survivors
measured on 9 March 1958 against a calibrated burette gave means of five runs each of 16.70,
16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 c.c.; mean **16.72**, short **0.88**.
16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95** exactly. A charge 5% short into a neck ruled for the full charge yields a
fat column 5% short. (Bulletin 471 §III's "16.27 c.c." and the *Chronicle*'s repetition of it are
corrupt: they cannot be the mean of the nine figures listed on the sheet, and 16.27 ÷ 17.6 =
0.9244, which yields a true 1924 average of 3.905 that §V itself admits "is not readily reconciled
with the station's own average of 3.80.")

**(b) True average 3.80; the recorded tests were short 0.19 of a point.**
3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19**, which is 5% of 3.80 — a proportion, not a
constant, which is why it was flat in every month and heaviest on the best milk. Four independent
confirmations of 3.80: the 1896 season on Larrow Green's borrowed glass; the nine weeks of 1911;
1924 on new glass (Keddie's book *and* Strawn's); and Alder Corners' sixteen seasons at 3.78–3.82
on the same men's milk.

**(c) 76,000 lb of butterfat**, over the 25 full delivery years 1898–1922.
40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb credited. 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb delivered.
1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = **76,000 lb**. (Equivalently 40,000,000 × 0.0019.)

**(d) $19,000; the patrons received exactly one fifth of it.**
76,000 lb × $0.25 = **$19,000**. The award was $3,800; 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **0.20**, one fifth.
(The petition's own claim, 67,000 lb and "$19,600 set out to the dollar," is the patrons' rougher
reckoning, not the true figure.)

### C2. Emmet Brant, Hessel Bottom, 1910 — 200,000 lb of milk paid on a test of 3.42.

**(a) Credited:** 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of butterfat.
**(b) Owed and not credited:** true test 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60; 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb owed;
7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb** (= 5% of 7,200, and 200,000 × 0.0018).
**(c) Worth:** at the 24¢ Roy Brant gives for 1910, 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40**; at the 25¢ long-run
average, $90.00. One year, one farm.

### C3. Over 1898–1922.

**(a) Credited to the patrons on the station test: 1,444,000 lb** (40,000,000 × 3.61%).
**(b) Actually reaching Larrow Green in the station's cream: 1,520,000 lb** (40,000,000 × 3.80%).
**(c) Difference 76,000 lb.** Being exact about the denominator:
- 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5.00% of the butterfat actually delivered** — this is the figure that
  equals the glass (one part in twenty), because the glass short-changed what was really there.
- 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.26% of the butterfat credited** — this is the ledger's "five and a
  quarter per cent, as usual," because a bookkeeper reckons a gain against the figure already
  entered.
Both are right of their own denominator; neither is "the" percentage without saying of what.
Check against the 1898 entry: 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.00%; 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26%; and
60,800 × (3.61/3.80) = 57,760. Bulletin 471 §V is wrong to call the gain "five and a quarter
per cent of the butterfat actually delivered" — that is the credited denominator wearing the
delivered label.

### C4.

**(a) $77.93.** Working: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = **77.93**. The invoice's own total
reads 77.93; the firm's historian writes "seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents" in her
prose two lines under the facsimile, and the *Chronicle* prints $77.39. The columns settle it —
a transposition, repeated because it was copied.
**(b) 6 doz. = 72 pipettes**, at 35¢ apiece (72 × 0.35 = $25.20, which is the invoice line, so the
"five dozen" of Bulletin §III and of Oram's memory cannot be right; the 1923 replacement order was
also for six dozen). Each was short on average **0.88 c.c.** (17.6 − 16.72); the nine measured ran
individually 0.86 to 0.90 c.c. short.

### C5.

**(a) In use 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 — twenty-six years and about six weeks (less the nine
weeks of 1911, when a borrowed measure was in the man's hand). The money runs over twenty-five
full delivery years, 1898–1922.** They differ because the two end years are part years and cannot
carry a full year's intake: 1897 began on 1 May, its first four months having been tested on the
honest borrowed glass, and 1923 was cut off on 12 June. The 40,000,000 lb / 1,600,000 lb-a-year
basis is built of whole years only, so the loss is reckoned across the 25 complete ones — which
is also why Bulletin 471's conclusion states the *exposure* (1 May 1897 – 12 June 1923) and its
arithmetic states the *whole years*. (§V's "first of May, 1898" is a further corruption; the
invoice and the diary both put the glass into use in 1897.)

**(b) Cannot be determined from the sources.** No intake figure for the 1896 part-season exists in
any book — the curator says so expressly, and Kyle says the milk of that first season was carried
on the creamery's own books and the sheets were never put with the station papers. Two secondhand
estimates survive and disagree by more than 20%: Sill's "about 900,000 pounds, May to the end of
the year," and Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds," which he had "from men who were
there" and which was before his time in the office. I would lean to Sill, who was at the receiving
end that season and made up the very glass the station used, but the figure is an estimate either
way. It does not enter the loss in any case: 1896 ran on Larrow Green's honest glass and tested
3.80.

## Section D — Logic

### D1. The borrowed-measure interval as an experiment.

**8 May to 10 July 1911 — nine weeks.** Dated at both ends by the test book ("*May 8. Pipette
broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure*" / "*July 10. Our own measure in use again from
today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.*") and independently at the front end by Orra
Keddie's diary for 8 May 1911. **The variable was the measuring pipette, and nothing else.** Held
constant: the same herds, the same pasture in the same flush season, the same feed, the same nine
families' milk, the same tester, the same method, the same bottles, acid, scales, separator and
building, and the same supervision — Strawn stood at the tester's elbow through a test that June
and wrote "nothing here to note." **Result:** the station average moved from 3.61 to **3.80** and
returned to 3.61 on the day the old glass came back.

**What it establishes that arithmetic alone could not:** the 1958 measurement proves the glass was
short; it does not by itself prove the shortness was the *whole* cause, since a critic could
always claim some further, unmeasured depression from feed or handling. The 1911 window proves
causation by intervention: change that one object and the entire deficit appears and disappears,
on the day, in both directions. It also exonerates every human being at the station — the same man
made the tests inside the window as outside it.

**What it does not establish:** not where the borrowed measure came from (Ivy says the cream wagon
from Larrow Green, Roy Brant says the state man left his own; unresolved); not that the borrowed
measure was itself accurate — only that it differed, though its agreement with 1896, 1924, Alder
Corners and Larrow Green shows it was; not the size of the total loss, which needs the ledger; not
the *reason* the glass was short (that comes from Ammon's account of a reground mold); and,
strictly, nothing at all about winter feeding, since the whole window falls in the flush season.
Kepp's jeer that "I could produce you six weeks in any year that would prove anything you liked"
was wrong on the count and on the facts, but that seasonal limitation is real — it is closed not
by 1911 but by the deficit's flatness in every month of the year.

### D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream — three independent reasons.

1. **Sill's own scale at the other end.** The buttermaker at Larrow Green weighed every delivery of
   station cream himself into the cold room for forty years — "I have never in my life taken
   another man's figure for what was going into my churn" — and it "ran heavy. Every year it ran
   heavy... Always more. Never less." Cream stolen at the Hollow arrives light. This one arrived
   heavy.
2. **The Association's own ledgers.** Every pound is accounted for on the books: 57,760 lb
   credited, 60,800 lb received, 3,040 lb entered as station gain and carried to the general
   account, year after year. The "missing" fat was not missing; it went to the Association. Rundle,
   the valley's own chronicler of the theft story, saw the ledger on another errand and killed his
   own chapter in press.
3. **The nine weeks of 1911.** The same man, the same room, no new supervision — and the tests rose
   nineteen hundredths and fell back the day his own glass returned. No thief suspends and resumes
   his theft to the day in step with a piece of glass he did not choose to break.
4. *(A fourth, for the weighing limb of the charge:* Emmet Brant's weigh book ran two columns, his
   own barn scale and the station's, and they agreed for thirty years barring a pound or two.)

**Why these are independent:** they come from different men, different places, different kinds of
evidence, and different ends of the process — a buttermaker's scale seven miles down the valley; a
bookkeeper's ledger in the Larrow Green office; a dated pair of entries in the station's own test
book; a farmer's private weigh book in his barn. No one of them was derived from any other, and
none was collected to answer the charge. **Reasons 1, 2 and 4 would all still hold if the glass
had never been measured in 1958** — Sill's is the strongest, since it needs no theory at all,
only a scale.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question the entries that refute his own accusation?** No. Teague wrote
that station-gain line himself every year of his service, claimed it as his own device, and made
its evenness the centrepiece of his memoir — and still insinuated theft by the tester at the 1925
hearing, before observing that he made no charge against anybody. He questioned the *situation*
once, putting it to Vose in 1916 and being told to let it alone; he never questioned his own
figures. Kyle's verdict on him is exact: "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years...
the figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people."

### D3. Did the manager know, before 1923, that the station's tests ran low?

**On the plain question, yes — certainly, and there was nothing secret about it.** The Ostrey
Hollow tests ran below every other book in the county from 1898; the patrons complained from 1902
and renewed it at every annual meeting for a quarter of a century; the Association's own 1908
committee was appointed because of it; and in 1919 Vose himself circulated a warning to the Ostrey
Hollow patrons about watered milk — a letter that makes no sense unless he knew their tests were
low and had a theory about why. In 1926 he was still explaining the low figures from the platform.
He knew.

**On the sharper question — whether he knew his own ledgers contradicted his theory — the sources
do not settle it, and I decline to say he did.** Teague states that he put it to Vose in 1916 and
was told to let it alone, and that Vose signed the annual sheet carrying the station-gain line
every year. If that is right, Vose was told once and chose not to look. But Teague is the only
source for the 1916 conversation, he is not disinterested (he is explaining in a memoir why the
man who wrote the line for forty years did nothing about it), and Kyle — who sat a foot from that
door and had no stake — gives an innocent mechanism that fits everything else: "He read the totals
and he never read the lines above them... He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the
bottom figure, and sign. I watched him do it a thousand times." A signature on twenty-five sheets
is not knowledge of what is above the total. The likeliest reading, and as far as the evidence
carries: Vose knew the tests ran low, was told at least once that the ledger was strange, and was
incurious rather than complicit.

## Section F — Theory

### F1. What actually produced the shortfall.

**The theory in one causal chain.** In April 1897 Ammon & Sons of Tarnet supplied the Ostrey Hollow
station with six dozen milk pipettes blown to deliver 17.6 c.c. Because a mold had been reground
and no one in the works caught it, the whole lot ran short and ran short *uniformly*: they
delivered a mean of 16.72 c.c., exactly 95 hundredths of their mark. The Babcock test measures fat
as a column read against rulings on a bottle neck that are calibrated for one charge — 17.6 c.c. —
and no other. Put in 5% less milk and the fat column stands 5% lower, every time. So from **1 May
1897**, when that outfit went into use, every test at that station read 95% of the truth: a milk
that was really 3.80 was recorded 3.61.

- *Why that size:* because the error is the ratio 16.72 ÷ 17.6 and nothing else. It is
  **proportional, not additive** — 0.19 on 3.80, 0.24 on Gideon Vose's 4.56 Jerseys, 0.18 on a
  3.60 — which is why it was flat in every month and fell heaviest on the best milk, and why no
  feeding theory can imitate it.
- *Why it began when it did:* because the station's first season, from 4 May 1896, ran on Hobart
  Sill's own glass borrowed from Larrow Green, and that season tested 3.80. The deficit begins with
  the crate, not with the herds, the hay, the tester or the patrons — none of which changed on
  1 May 1897.
- *Why it ended when it did:* because on 12 June 1923 Merle Strawn ran the pipettes against a
  calibrated burette — an instrument the state had only begun issuing to its field men in 1921 —
  and condemned the lot; new glass was in use within the month, and from 1924 the station's average
  stood at 3.80. Again nothing on the farms changed.
- *Why nothing had to change at either end:* the milk pipette is the one instrument in the plant
  that cannot be checked against anything else in the plant, and every figure depends on it.
  "An instrument that is itself the measure cannot be caught by measuring with it." The error was
  precise rather than scattered — it had "the appearance of excellent work" — and it preserved the
  ranking of herds among themselves, so it was invisible from inside and wrong only against the
  outside world. Meanwhile the difference did not vanish: it arrived at Larrow Green as extra fat
  in the cream and was written down in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as *station
  gain*, five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account — **76,000 lb of
  butterfat, about $19,000, over 1898–1922.**

**The three competing explanations, and what defeats each:**

1. **Watered milk** — advanced by Selby Vose, the manager (circular of 1919; his 1926 address,
   where he claims the rise of 1924 measures the watering that stopped), and believed by Sill
   ("you cannot tell me nine families all got honest in one summer"). *Defeated by:* Strawn's
   lactometer at every visit on the circuit, which never once found added water at that station,
   and his telling the manager so in 1919; Rosalie Cudd's sixteen summers making cheese of the same
   men's milk — watered milk cannot be hidden from a vat, the curd will not knit and the yield is
   down, and she never docked a cent; and 1911, since watering cannot start and stop with the
   breaking and mending of the tester's pipette.
2. **Cream stolen by the tester** — insinuated by Wilbur Teague at the hearing, asserted by Vira
   Toland, believed by the cream hauler, and settled opinion at the store (Rundle Ch. IV).
   *Defeated by:* Sill's weighing, cream heavy every year and never light; the ledger, in which the
   whole difference is present and accounted for; Rundle's own retraction on seeing it; Emmet
   Brant's two agreeing weight columns; and 1911.
3. **The wet hay of Hessel Bottom** — the 1908 Association committee under Nyle Grigg, revived by
   Dr. Kepp in 1926 and briefly conceded by Falke in 1959. *Defeated by:* Bulletin No. 214, which
   found bog hay depressed the test by not more than 0.05 in winter and nothing measurable on
   summer pasture — a quarter of the magnitude and the wrong seasonal shape against a deficit flat
   in every month; by the Alder Corners table, sixteen consecutive seasons of the same cows off the
   same wet ground at 3.78–3.82; by the misquotation ("two tenths in the winter feeding") on which
   both its advocates relied; by the 1908 sampling having covered only January to March; and by
   Kepp's own admission that he had never read a test in his life.

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?** **No.** It never saw it. The crate had
been in the loft over the intake room since June 1923 and stayed there until 1958 — twenty feet
above the board's heads through the second and third sittings, which were held in the weigh room
underneath. What the board did see was a test made on the *new* glass, which delivered its mark
and told them nothing; and, among its four exhibits, the state inspector's circuit notebook for
1923, containing the sentence "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot."
The book that answered the question was physically on the table and nobody connected it — as the
clerk who carried the exhibits in and out put it, no man in that room ever laid the second book
beside the fourth.

**What did the tester himself believe?** Two different things at two different times, and the
sources do not reconcile them. In the summer of 1911 he came home and said it was something in the
glass — his son heard him say it twice, at supper and again by the door — but he would not write
down what he could not prove, and what went into the book was "? — A.K." By March 1925, on Frick's
account, he told the chairman privately in the corridor at Ashlin that he had always thought the
patrons themselves were at fault and would not say so in the room. So: he accused nobody, which is
true, but "he suspected nobody" is false. What he said publicly, and all he would say, was "I
cannot tell you why the figures are what they are."

## Section G — Summary

### G1. (150 words)

A creamery association paid its farmers by butterfat test, and the test at its Ostrey Hollow
station governed. From 1897 that station's pipettes, accidentally blown short by one part in
twenty, made every test read 95% of the truth — 3.61 where the milk was 3.80. For twenty-six years
the valley blamed watered milk, a thieving tester, and wet hay; an arbitration in 1925 paid $3,800
of a $19,000 loss, found no cause, and let the tester be dismissed four days early. The missing
76,000 lb was never missing: it reached the creamery as cream and was booked as "station gain."
In 1958 the tester's granddaughter climbed a ladder, measured nine condemned pipettes, and proved
it. Eight records were needed because no single one held both ends: the glass, the ledger, the
weigh book, the cheese vat, the inspector's notebook, the diary, the hearing, the loft.
