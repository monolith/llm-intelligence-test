## The solved case, in one paragraph (established; do not relitigate)
The station's pipettes, bought 1897, delivered **16.72 c.c.** where they were blown **17.6 c.c.**
— 95% of the mark. The bottle neck is ruled for one charge and no other, so every test read 95%
of truth: **recorded 3.61 vs true 3.80**, a gap of **0.19** that is a *proportion* (5%), not a
constant, and therefore flat in every month and heaviest on the best milk. Over **25 full
delivery years (1898–1922), 40,000,000 lb** of milk, the shortfall is **76,000 lb** of butterfat
≈ **$19,000** at 25¢. It stands in the Association's own ledgers as the annual "station gain."
Glass condemned **12 June 1923**; cause found **1958**; Bulletin 471, **Oct 1958**.

**r11 — Prof. Leland Vaught**, introduction and method to **Bulletin No. 471**, Farland, Oct 1958.
The conceptual heart: **the milk pipette is the one instrument in the plant that cannot be
checked against anything else, and every figure depends on it** — "an instrument that is itself
the measure cannot be caught by measuring with it." Three properties: the error is
**proportional, not additive** (herds still rank correctly among themselves; only against the
outside world is the station wrong); it is **precise, not scattered** — "it has the appearance of
excellent work"; and it is **invisible from inside the plant**, catchable only by a burette
brought in from outside, and burettes reached the field men very late. Standard charge **17.6
c.c.** §IV treats the 1911 nine weeks as an experiment that "ran itself, under conditions no
designed trial could have bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven years": one variable changed,
3.61 → 3.80 and back. §VI quotes Bulletin 214 correctly (0.05 in winter, nothing in summer). §VII
reproduces the conclusion verbatim (1 May 1897 – 12 June 1923, one part in twenty, **76,000 lb**).
**But §III and §V are internally corrupt** — see contradictions below.

**r13 — Alonzo Frick**, chairman of the arbitration board, Ordell County Bar Association yearbook,

statement of 17 March believed: "a man who cannot account for a figure is presumed by everybody

30 April, four days before the award, with no finding against him ever made** — "I have never

**The patrons' own reckoning was 67,000 lb** of butterfat, never disturbed. **Exhibit list, four

**r14 — Hobart Sill**, buttermaker at Larrow Green 1893–1934, remarks on his last day, aged 64.
First-hand and decisive: **"That cream ran heavy. Every year it ran heavy. There was more fat in
the cans than the papers said had been bought up there … Always more. Never less."** He weighed
it himself over again into the cold room — "I have never in my life taken another man's figure
for what was going into my churn" — and his hand is on every page of the receiving book from
1893. He dismissed it as ordinary ("cream is not milk and it will not stand still and be
counted") and **nobody ever asked him about it**. **Larrow Green's own patrons ran 3.78, one year
with another.** Says the Hollow milk "was really a 3.85 and no better." Believes **water**: "you
cannot tell me nine families all got honest in one summer," placing the rise vaguely "one summer,
along in the twenties." 1902 the first he remembers. Approved the 1919 circular. Puts fat at
**27¢** through those years. **Resolves two open questions:** the station had no outfit of its
own when it opened in **May 1896**, so **Sill made up an outfit out of his own glass at his own
bench at Larrow Green** and it went up the valley on the wagon — "their own came the spring
after and they sent mine back, and one piece of it was chipped." And **the 1896 part-season
intake was about 900,000 pounds**, May to the end of the year. He went up to the Hollow twice in
his life.

**r15 — Nyle Grigg**, 1919 covering letter to his reprinted **1908 committee report**. A Larrow
Green farmer, **not a patron of that station** — chosen for that reason, and "abused for the
finding by men on both sides." **His letter and his own report contradict each other on the
central methodological point:** the letter claims "we sampled those herds through the whole of
1908 — every month of that year, in the barn and on the pasture … and we did not let a man know
which day we were coming," while the report he reprints ends "**We have sampled the herds of
Hessel Bottom through January, February and March.**" The letter also **misquotes Bulletin 214 as
finding a depression of "two tenths in the winter feeding"** and treats the College as having
independently confirmed him. Dates payment by test to "the 1893 meeting." The report itself:
Hessel Bottom, **six farms**; each visited three or four times; samples drawn and sealed by the
committee; station scales tried against a known weight and patrons' scales against the station's;
water supply at each barn inquired into; stock thin in the flank, rough in the coat; "what such
land gives in quantity it withholds in quality"; **"we found no dishonesty of any kind, in any
quarter, and we wish that finding to be printed in the same type as the rest"**; conclusion — the
wet hay thins the cream, the remedy lies with the land and not with the station. Received and
accepted; "the question was closed by it for eleven years."

**r16 — Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S.**, of Ashlin, paper to the Ordell County Farm Club, **1926**, with
discussion appended. The fullest and most respectable statement of the **bog-hay theory**: a wet
bottom is a distinct chemistry — sour, acid, poor in lime and phosphates; sweet grasses crowded
out by sedge, rush and tussock; late cutting takes out what little was there; a cow short of
minerals "robs her own frame," and **the fat is the first economy she practices**. Claims the
county's wet bottoms yield milk poorer by **a fifth of a point** and puts the Ostrey Hollow gap
at **0.21**. Hessel Bottom: **six farms**, springs running the year round, under water into May,
sedge hay very nearly the whole winter dependence. **Repeats Grigg's misquotation — Bulletin 214
"found the butter fat test depressed by two tenths in the winter feeding"** — and says his field
figure, the College's, and the complaint "agree so nearly that I do not know what more a
reasonable man requires." **Dismisses 1911 as "six weeks"** of flush-season figures ("I could
produce you six weeks in any year that would prove anything you liked") — wrong on the count and
on the point, since the gap was flat in every month. Fat at **27¢**. Remedy: drain, lime, seed,
keep stock off in the wet, never feed that hay alone. **In discussion he concedes he "had never
in his life read a test and would not know how,"** and argues it is not good sense to look for a
second cause in a building.

## The arithmetic (all self-consistent; use these to adjudicate)
- 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95** exactly → every test read 95% of truth; short by one part in twenty.
- 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**; gap **0.19**, which is **5% of 3.80** (a proportion, not a constant).
- 1898–1922 = **25 full delivery years**, **40,000,000 lb** milk (1.6M/yr).
  At 3.61% → **1,444,000 lb** credited. At 3.80% → **1,520,000 lb** delivered.
  Difference **76,000 lb**; at **25¢** = **$19,000**. Award $3,800 = **one fifth**.
- 76,000 is **5%** of 1,520,000 (delivered) and **5.25%** of 1,444,000 (credited). Both right of
  their own denominator; the ledger uses 5.25% because a bookkeeper reckons against the figure
  already entered; **5% is the one that is also the glass.**
- 1898 entry checks: 60,800 × (3.61/3.80) = 57,760; gain 3,040 = 5% of received, 5.26% of credited.
- Proportional damage: 4.56 recorded → 4.80 true (short 0.24); 3.42 recorded → 3.60 true (0.18).
- Brant weigh book 1910: 200,000 lb at a paid test of 3.42 → 6,840 lb credited vs 7,200 lb true;
  **360 lb short ≈ $90** at 25¢ (≈$86 at the 24¢ Roy gives for 1910).
- Independent corroborations of the true 3.80: Alder Corners **sixteen seasons at 3.78–3.82**
  (r10); Larrow Green's own books "a little under two tenths better" than 3.61 (r07 Item 16);
  Sill's Larrow Green patrons at **3.78** (r14); Strawn's and Keddie's **1924 = 3.80**.

## Contradicted claims (proven false — do not relearn)
- **No cream was stolen.** Refuted three ways: the glass (Bulletin 471); **Sill's own weighing —
  the cream came in heavy, never light, every year**; and Rundle's own retraction on seeing the
  ledger ("stolen cream would have reached the Green light"). Asserted by Vira (r01), insinuated
  by Teague (r05, and at the hearing per r13), and the settled valley opinion (r12 ch. IV).
- **No milk was watered.** Refuted by Strawn's lactometer on every visit (r06), by Rosalie Cudd's
  sixteen summers at the vat — watered milk cannot be hidden from a cheesemaker (r10) — and by
  Bulletin 471. Asserted by Vose (r08, r13) and Sill (r14).
- **The bog-hay explanation is dead** — Bulletin 214: ≤0.05 in winter, nothing in summer, against
  a flat 0.19 in every month; wrong magnitude *and* wrong seasonal shape. Its survival rests on a
  **misquotation ("two tenths in the winter feeding") shared by Grigg (r15) and Kepp (r16)**;
  and it is independently killed by the Alder Corners figures — the same cows off the same wet
  ground tested 3.80 over the ridge.
- **It was not the weighing** — Emmet Brant's two columns agreed for thirty years (r09); Strawn
  tried both scales against sealed weights every visit; the 1908 committee tried them too.
- **Award was $3,800, not $3,500** (Vira wrong).
- **Condemnation was 12 June 1923, not 12 July** (Vose r08 and Rundle r12 both wrong).
- **1924 averaged 3.80, not 3.90** (Vose wrong; Keddie's book and Strawn's independent entry agree).
- **Selby Vose was Gideon's son, not his nephew** (Effie and Vose himself; Vira r01 and Rundle
  r12 both say nephew).
- **The 1911 substitution lasted nine weeks (8 May – 10 July), not six** — dated book entries,
  and Roy Brant; Teague (r05) and Kepp (r16) say six.
- **Station intake was 40M lb and the recorded average 3.61** — the **44M lb** (Teague, Rundle)
  and **3.16** (Teague, Frick) figures are outliers.

## Reading strategy for the questions
- Narrators are reliable about **what they personally handled** and unreliable about **what they
  inferred**. Sill on the weight of cream, Roy Brant on the two columns, Rosalie on her vat,
  Strawn on his lactometer, Frick on who said what — all first-hand and trustworthy. Their
  theories are not.
- Four wrong explanations were offered in good faith (theft, watering, bog hay, and "apparatus
  wears"); one mechanical cause is proven. Questions may test whether each claim is attributed to
  the right narrator — **water = Vose (and Sill); theft = Teague/Vira/the valley; hay = the 1908
  committee, Grigg and Kepp.**
- Two documents *retract or undercut themselves*: Rundle's note in press (kills his own theft
  chapter) and Grigg's letter (contradicts his own report's sampling months). Bulletin 471's §III
  and §V contradict its own §VII.
