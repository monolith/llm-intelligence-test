# Answer sheet — Part 3 of 3

## Section C — Math

### C1.

**(a) The tests read 95% of the truth — short by one part in twenty (a 5% proportional deficit).**
From the glassware: the pipettes were blown on the stem to deliver 17.6 c.c., the standard charge
of milk, and the test bottle's neck is ruled for that one charge and no other. The nine survivors
measured on 9 March 1958 against a calibrated burette gave means of five runs each of 16.70,
16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 c.c.; mean **16.72**, short **0.88**.
16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95** exactly. A charge 5% short into a neck ruled for the full charge yields a
fat column 5% short. (Bulletin 471 §III's "16.27 c.c." and the *Chronicle*'s repetition of it are
corrupt: they cannot be the mean of the nine figures listed on the sheet, and 16.27 ÷ 17.6 =
0.9244, which yields a true 1924 average of 3.905 that §V itself admits "is not readily reconciled
with the station's own average of 3.80.")

**(b) True average 3.80; the recorded tests were short 0.19 of a point.**
3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19**, which is 5% of 3.80 — a proportion, not a
constant, which is why it was flat in every month and heaviest on the best milk. Four independent
confirmations of 3.80: the 1896 season on Larrow Green's borrowed glass; the nine weeks of 1911;
1924 on new glass (Keddie's book *and* Strawn's); and Alder Corners' sixteen seasons at 3.78–3.82
on the same men's milk.

**(c) 76,000 lb of butterfat**, over the 25 full delivery years 1898–1922.
40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb credited. 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb delivered.
1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = **76,000 lb**. (Equivalently 40,000,000 × 0.0019.)

**(d) $19,000; the patrons received exactly one fifth of it.**
76,000 lb × $0.25 = **$19,000**. The award was $3,800; 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **0.20**, one fifth.
(The petition's own claim, 67,000 lb and "$19,600 set out to the dollar," is the patrons' rougher
reckoning, not the true figure.)

### C2. Emmet Brant, Hessel Bottom, 1910 — 200,000 lb of milk paid on a test of 3.42.

**(a) Credited:** 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of butterfat.
**(b) Owed and not credited:** true test 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60; 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb owed;
7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb** (= 5% of 7,200, and 200,000 × 0.0018).
**(c) Worth:** at the 24¢ Roy Brant gives for 1910, 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40**; at the 25¢ long-run
average, $90.00. One year, one farm.

### C3. Over 1898–1922.

**(a) Credited to the patrons on the station test: 1,444,000 lb** (40,000,000 × 3.61%).
**(b) Actually reaching Larrow Green in the station's cream: 1,520,000 lb** (40,000,000 × 3.80%).
**(c) Difference 76,000 lb.** Being exact about the denominator:
- 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5.00% of the butterfat actually delivered** — this is the figure that
  equals the glass (one part in twenty), because the glass short-changed what was really there.
- 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.26% of the butterfat credited** — this is the ledger's "five and a
  quarter per cent, as usual," because a bookkeeper reckons a gain against the figure already
  entered.
Both are right of their own denominator; neither is "the" percentage without saying of what.
Check against the 1898 entry: 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.00%; 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26%; and
60,800 × (3.61/3.80) = 57,760. Bulletin 471 §V is wrong to call the gain "five and a quarter
per cent of the butterfat actually delivered" — that is the credited denominator wearing the
delivered label.

### C4.

**(a) $77.93.** Working: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = **77.93**. The invoice's own total
reads 77.93; the firm's historian writes "seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents" in her
prose two lines under the facsimile, and the *Chronicle* prints $77.39. The columns settle it —
a transposition, repeated because it was copied.
**(b) 6 doz. = 72 pipettes**, at 35¢ apiece (72 × 0.35 = $25.20, which is the invoice line, so the
"five dozen" of Bulletin §III and of Oram's memory cannot be right; the 1923 replacement order was
also for six dozen). Each was short on average **0.88 c.c.** (17.6 − 16.72); the nine measured ran
individually 0.86 to 0.90 c.c. short.

### C5.

**(a) In use 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 — twenty-six years and about six weeks (less the nine
weeks of 1911, when a borrowed measure was in the man's hand). The money runs over twenty-five
full delivery years, 1898–1922.** They differ because the two end years are part years and cannot
carry a full year's intake: 1897 began on 1 May, its first four months having been tested on the
honest borrowed glass, and 1923 was cut off on 12 June. The 40,000,000 lb / 1,600,000 lb-a-year
basis is built of whole years only, so the loss is reckoned across the 25 complete ones — which
is also why Bulletin 471's conclusion states the *exposure* (1 May 1897 – 12 June 1923) and its
arithmetic states the *whole years*. (§V's "first of May, 1898" is a further corruption; the
invoice and the diary both put the glass into use in 1897.)

**(b) Cannot be determined from the sources.** No intake figure for the 1896 part-season exists in
any book — the curator says so expressly, and Kyle says the milk of that first season was carried
on the creamery's own books and the sheets were never put with the station papers. Two secondhand
estimates survive and disagree by more than 20%: Sill's "about 900,000 pounds, May to the end of
the year," and Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds," which he had "from men who were
there" and which was before his time in the office. I would lean to Sill, who was at the receiving
end that season and made up the very glass the station used, but the figure is an estimate either
way. It does not enter the loss in any case: 1896 ran on Larrow Green's honest glass and tested
3.80.

## Section D — Logic

### D1. The borrowed-measure interval as an experiment.

**8 May to 10 July 1911 — nine weeks.** Dated at both ends by the test book ("*May 8. Pipette
broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure*" / "*July 10. Our own measure in use again from
today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.*") and independently at the front end by Orra
Keddie's diary for 8 May 1911. **The variable was the measuring pipette, and nothing else.** Held
constant: the same herds, the same pasture in the same flush season, the same feed, the same nine
families' milk, the same tester, the same method, the same bottles, acid, scales, separator and
building, and the same supervision — Strawn stood at the tester's elbow through a test that June
and wrote "nothing here to note." **Result:** the station average moved from 3.61 to **3.80** and
returned to 3.61 on the day the old glass came back.

**What it establishes that arithmetic alone could not:** the 1958 measurement proves the glass was
short; it does not by itself prove the shortness was the *whole* cause, since a critic could
always claim some further, unmeasured depression from feed or handling. The 1911 window proves
causation by intervention: change that one object and the entire deficit appears and disappears,
on the day, in both directions. It also exonerates every human being at the station — the same man
made the tests inside the window as outside it.

**What it does not establish:** not where the borrowed measure came from (Ivy says the cream wagon
from Larrow Green, Roy Brant says the state man left his own; unresolved); not that the borrowed
measure was itself accurate — only that it differed, though its agreement with 1896, 1924, Alder
Corners and Larrow Green shows it was; not the size of the total loss, which needs the ledger; not
the *reason* the glass was short (that comes from Ammon's account of a reground mold); and,
strictly, nothing at all about winter feeding, since the whole window falls in the flush season.
Kepp's jeer that "I could produce you six weeks in any year that would prove anything you liked"
was wrong on the count and on the facts, but that seasonal limitation is real — it is closed not
by 1911 but by the deficit's flatness in every month of the year.

### D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream — three independent reasons.

1. **Sill's own scale at the other end.** The buttermaker at Larrow Green weighed every delivery of
   station cream himself into the cold room for forty years — "I have never in my life taken
   another man's figure for what was going into my churn" — and it "ran heavy. Every year it ran
   heavy... Always more. Never less." Cream stolen at the Hollow arrives light. This one arrived
   heavy.
2. **The Association's own ledgers.** Every pound is accounted for on the books: 57,760 lb
   credited, 60,800 lb received, 3,040 lb entered as station gain and carried to the general
   account, year after year. The "missing" fat was not missing; it went to the Association. Rundle,
   the valley's own chronicler of the theft story, saw the ledger on another errand and killed his
   own chapter in press.
3. **The nine weeks of 1911.** The same man, the same room, no new supervision — and the tests rose
   nineteen hundredths and fell back the day his own glass returned. No thief suspends and resumes
   his theft to the day in step with a piece of glass he did not choose to break.
4. *(A fourth, for the weighing limb of the charge:* Emmet Brant's weigh book ran two columns, his
   own barn scale and the station's, and they agreed for thirty years barring a pound or two.)

**Why these are independent:** they come from different men, different places, different kinds of
evidence, and different ends of the process — a buttermaker's scale seven miles down the valley; a
bookkeeper's ledger in the Larrow Green office; a dated pair of entries in the station's own test
book; a farmer's private weigh book in his barn. No one of them was derived from any other, and
none was collected to answer the charge. **Reasons 1, 2 and 4 would all still hold if the glass
had never been measured in 1958** — Sill's is the strongest, since it needs no theory at all,
only a scale.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question the entries that refute his own accusation?** No. Teague wrote
that station-gain line himself every year of his service, claimed it as his own device, and made
its evenness the centrepiece of his memoir — and still insinuated theft by the tester at the 1925
hearing, before observing that he made no charge against anybody. He questioned the *situation*
once, putting it to Vose in 1916 and being told to let it alone; he never questioned his own
figures. Kyle's verdict on him is exact: "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years...
the figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people."

### D3. Did the manager know, before 1923, that the station's tests ran low?

**On the plain question, yes — certainly, and there was nothing secret about it.** The Ostrey
Hollow tests ran below every other book in the county from 1898; the patrons complained from 1902
and renewed it at every annual meeting for a quarter of a century; the Association's own 1908
committee was appointed because of it; and in 1919 Vose himself circulated a warning to the Ostrey
Hollow patrons about watered milk — a letter that makes no sense unless he knew their tests were
low and had a theory about why. In 1926 he was still explaining the low figures from the platform.
He knew.

**On the sharper question — whether he knew his own ledgers contradicted his theory — the sources
do not settle it, and I decline to say he did.** Teague states that he put it to Vose in 1916 and
was told to let it alone, and that Vose signed the annual sheet carrying the station-gain line
every year. If that is right, Vose was told once and chose not to look. But Teague is the only
source for the 1916 conversation, he is not disinterested (he is explaining in a memoir why the
man who wrote the line for forty years did nothing about it), and Kyle — who sat a foot from that
door and had no stake — gives an innocent mechanism that fits everything else: "He read the totals
and he never read the lines above them... He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the
bottom figure, and sign. I watched him do it a thousand times." A signature on twenty-five sheets
is not knowledge of what is above the total. The likeliest reading, and as far as the evidence
carries: Vose knew the tests ran low, was told at least once that the ledger was strange, and was
incurious rather than complicit.

## Section F — Theory

### F1. What actually produced the shortfall.

**The theory in one causal chain.** In April 1897 Ammon & Sons of Tarnet supplied the Ostrey Hollow
station with six dozen milk pipettes blown to deliver 17.6 c.c. Because a mold had been reground
and no one in the works caught it, the whole lot ran short and ran short *uniformly*: they
delivered a mean of 16.72 c.c., exactly 95 hundredths of their mark. The Babcock test measures fat
as a column read against rulings on a bottle neck that are calibrated for one charge — 17.6 c.c. —
and no other. Put in 5% less milk and the fat column stands 5% lower, every time. So from **1 May
1897**, when that outfit went into use, every test at that station read 95% of the truth: a milk
that was really 3.80 was recorded 3.61.

- *Why that size:* because the error is the ratio 16.72 ÷ 17.6 and nothing else. It is
  **proportional, not additive** — 0.19 on 3.80, 0.24 on Gideon Vose's 4.56 Jerseys, 0.18 on a
  3.60 — which is why it was flat in every month and fell heaviest on the best milk, and why no
  feeding theory can imitate it.
- *Why it began when it did:* because the station's first season, from 4 May 1896, ran on Hobart
  Sill's own glass borrowed from Larrow Green, and that season tested 3.80. The deficit begins with
  the crate, not with the herds, the hay, the tester or the patrons — none of which changed on
  1 May 1897.
- *Why it ended when it did:* because on 12 June 1923 Merle Strawn ran the pipettes against a
  calibrated burette — an instrument the state had only begun issuing to its field men in 1921 —
  and condemned the lot; new glass was in use within the month, and from 1924 the station's average
  stood at 3.80. Again nothing on the farms changed.
- *Why nothing had to change at either end:* the milk pipette is the one instrument in the plant
  that cannot be checked against anything else in the plant, and every figure depends on it.
  "An instrument that is itself the measure cannot be caught by measuring with it." The error was
  precise rather than scattered — it had "the appearance of excellent work" — and it preserved the
  ranking of herds among themselves, so it was invisible from inside and wrong only against the
  outside world. Meanwhile the difference did not vanish: it arrived at Larrow Green as extra fat
  in the cream and was written down in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as *station
  gain*, five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account — **76,000 lb of
  butterfat, about $19,000, over 1898–1922.**

**The three competing explanations, and what defeats each:**

1. **Watered milk** — advanced by Selby Vose, the manager (circular of 1919; his 1926 address,
   where he claims the rise of 1924 measures the watering that stopped), and believed by Sill
   ("you cannot tell me nine families all got honest in one summer"). *Defeated by:* Strawn's
   lactometer at every visit on the circuit, which never once found added water at that station,
   and his telling the manager so in 1919; Rosalie Cudd's sixteen summers making cheese of the same
   men's milk — watered milk cannot be hidden from a vat, the curd will not knit and the yield is
   down, and she never docked a cent; and 1911, since watering cannot start and stop with the
   breaking and mending of the tester's pipette.
2. **Cream stolen by the tester** — insinuated by Wilbur Teague at the hearing, asserted by Vira
   Toland, believed by the cream hauler, and settled opinion at the store (Rundle Ch. IV).
   *Defeated by:* Sill's weighing, cream heavy every year and never light; the ledger, in which the
   whole difference is present and accounted for; Rundle's own retraction on seeing it; Emmet
   Brant's two agreeing weight columns; and 1911.
3. **The wet hay of Hessel Bottom** — the 1908 Association committee under Nyle Grigg, revived by
   Dr. Kepp in 1926 and briefly conceded by Falke in 1959. *Defeated by:* Bulletin No. 214, which
   found bog hay depressed the test by not more than 0.05 in winter and nothing measurable on
   summer pasture — a quarter of the magnitude and the wrong seasonal shape against a deficit flat
   in every month; by the Alder Corners table, sixteen consecutive seasons of the same cows off the
   same wet ground at 3.78–3.82; by the misquotation ("two tenths in the winter feeding") on which
   both its advocates relied; by the 1908 sampling having covered only January to March; and by
   Kepp's own admission that he had never read a test in his life.

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?** **No.** It never saw it. The crate had
been in the loft over the intake room since June 1923 and stayed there until 1958 — twenty feet
above the board's heads through the second and third sittings, which were held in the weigh room
underneath. What the board did see was a test made on the *new* glass, which delivered its mark
and told them nothing; and, among its four exhibits, the state inspector's circuit notebook for
1923, containing the sentence "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot."
The book that answered the question was physically on the table and nobody connected it — as the
clerk who carried the exhibits in and out put it, no man in that room ever laid the second book
beside the fourth.

**What did the tester himself believe?** Two different things at two different times, and the
sources do not reconcile them. In the summer of 1911 he came home and said it was something in the
glass — his son heard him say it twice, at supper and again by the door — but he would not write
down what he could not prove, and what went into the book was "? — A.K." By March 1925, on Frick's
account, he told the chairman privately in the corridor at Ashlin that he had always thought the
patrons themselves were at fault and would not say so in the room. So: he accused nobody, which is
true, but "he suspected nobody" is false. What he said publicly, and all he would say, was "I
cannot tell you why the figures are what they are."

## Section G — Summary

### G1. (150 words)

A creamery association paid its farmers by butterfat test, and the test at its Ostrey Hollow
station governed. From 1897 that station's pipettes, accidentally blown short by one part in
twenty, made every test read 95% of the truth — 3.61 where the milk was 3.80. For twenty-six years
the valley blamed watered milk, a thieving tester, and wet hay; an arbitration in 1925 paid $3,800
of a $19,000 loss, found no cause, and let the tester be dismissed four days early. The missing
76,000 lb was never missing: it reached the creamery as cream and was booked as "station gain."
In 1958 the tester's granddaughter climbed a ladder, measured nine condemned pipettes, and proved
it. Eight records were needed because no single one held both ends: the glass, the ledger, the
weigh book, the cheese vat, the inspector's notebook, the diary, the hearing, the loft.
