# Answer sheet — Part 1 (Section A, stories 1–4)

## Section A — Reconstruction

How I resolved conflicts in general:
- A quoted contemporary document outranks a later memory. The documents are the test-book entries, the invoice, Strawn's dated entries, the ledger entry and the 1908 report text.
- Arithmetic that closes outranks arithmetic that does not.
- Independent witnesses outrank repeated ones.
- Where nothing decides, I say so.

---

### 1. Article VII and the Voses: the rule that made the station's test final (1889–1938)

**Who:** Gideon Vose, founder and first president, and his son Selby Vose, manager; also the Association's officers.

**What happened.** Farmers of the valley organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association at Larrow Green in 1889, meeting in the schoolhouse. Gideon Vose was president from 1889 to 1911 and died in 1917. The main creamery was built on the creek below the mill at Larrow Green in 1891. Gideon drafted the by-laws. Article VII stopped payment by weight of milk. Each patron was paid by the pounds of butterfat the Babcock test found in his milk, and **"the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."** That clause is the hinge of the whole history. Under payment by weight a short pipette would have cost nobody anything. Under Article VII one station test fixed every patron's pay, and nothing checked it.

The year of adoption **cannot be determined**:
- Rundle says 1891.
- Effie Loomis says the 1892 annual meeting.
- Grigg says payment by test came in "with the 1893 meeting, as I have always understood it."
- The printed by-laws carry no date (Nym item 1; Falke).
- The 1889–92 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move.

All that is certain is that the article was in force before the Ostrey Hollow station opened in 1896.

Selby Vose was Gideon's **son**. He says so himself (1926), and Effie, Duncan Keddie and the clerk Kyle agree. Vira's and Rundle's "nephew" is valley talk. He was **manager from 1913 to 1938**: his own "thirteen years" in 1926 and Strawn's 1913 entry "a new manager" both give 1913, so Kyle's 1911 is wrong. His father's Jerseys at Larrow Green tested **4.56 in 1910**. As manager Selby:
- signed the 1919 water circular;
- told Teague in 1916 to let the Hollow figures alone ("bookkeeper and not its conscience");
- called Strawn's 1923 condemnation "housekeeping";
- in 1926 called the $3,800 award, which was charged to the station account, "a generous settlement of an unproved claim."

Kyle, at the desk outside his door, says Vose signed every sheet with his thumb on the bottom figure and never read the lines above it. Those lines included the yearly Ostrey Hollow "station gain."

The officers were Effie Loomis (secretary 1901–1940), Wilbur Teague (bookkeeper 1905–1945), Hobart Sill (buttermaker 1893–1934) and Bertram Kyle (clerk). According to Effie, none of them ever stood in the station on a working morning.

**Quantities:** the station stood 7 miles up the valley; it took about 1.6 million lb of milk a year; butterfat averaged about 25¢/lb.

**Documents:** r02, r08, r12, r15, r18, r22, r23 (main); r01, r05, r06, r07.

### 2. Ansel Keddie and the Ostrey Hollow test book (1895–1934)

**Who:** Ansel Keddie (1861–1934), tester and separator man; his wife Orra (née Brant, married 1889); their son Duncan (born 1894).

**What happened.** Keddie went up in 1895 to make the new skimming station ready. The Association built it over the winter of 1895–96 at Ostrey Hollow to serve Hessel Bottom, and it opened on **4 May 1896**, the date on the test book's first leaf. He was its tester for **twenty-nine seasons, 1896–1924**.

His routine: the day began at 5:30. Milk was weighed in and sampled into numbered composite jars made up over ten days. Each sample was warmed and poured vessel to vessel. A **17.6 c.c.** pipette charge was blown into a test bottle, acid added, the bottles spun in a four-bottle hand tester, and the fat column read with dividers. He kept the book in his own hand, ruled in five columns, and never wrote over a figure: errors were crossed out and initialled.

In the 1896 season he tested on glass **borrowed from Larrow Green**. This is stated by the book's first leaf ("Glass borrowed of Larrow Green"), Orra's diary for 1 May 1897, Nym's invoice note and Kyle. Sill's "outfit from my own bench" is the same Larrow Green glass. The "Ashlin creamery" in Keddie's unsent 1925 letter is a slip that his own first leaf contradicts.

- **1896:** **3.80** on the borrowed glass.
- **1 May 1897:** the station's own outfit went into use.
- **1898 onward:** **3.61 ±0.03**, season after season, wet years and dry.
- **8 May 1911:** "Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure."
- **10 July 1911:** "Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." In those nine weeks the tests stood at 3.80.
- **1924:** new glass had arrived in July 1923, and the year averaged **3.80** under the same man.

Duncan says his father came home in 1911 saying it was "something in the glass," but he would not write down what he could not prove. The question mark stood for fourteen years.

On **17 March 1925** he told the arbitration that he could not say why the figures were what they were, and that he had never taken a pound of cream. He was **dismissed on 30 April 1925**, four days before the award, with no finding against him. After that he kept a few cows and did carpentry; he was a carpenter at sixty-six in 1927. He died at home in **February 1934** without being cleared. Bulletin 471 cleared him in 1958, twenty-four years after his death and thirty-three after his dismissal.

The book was impounded in March 1925, lay in the Association office at Ashlin from 1925 to 1959, and has been at the Ordell County Historical Rooms since 1959. (Nym's label "kept by E. Brant" is a curatorial error.)

**Quantities:**
- charge of 17.6 c.c.;
- 3.80 in 1896, 3.61 in 1898–1922, 3.80 in the 1911 weeks and in 1924;
- 29 seasons; dismissed 30 April 1925; died 1934.

**Documents:** r03, r24, r18, r22, r13 (main); r01, r04, r06, r07, r11, r17, r21.

### 3. The Ammon & Sons pipettes: sold 1897, condemned 1923, measured 1958

**Who:**
- Ammon & Sons Dairy Supply, Tarnet: Marcus Ammon founded it, and Junia Ammon wrote its history.
- The state inspector Merle Strawn.
- Ivy Keddie and Professor Leland Vaught of the State Agricultural College at Farland.

**What happened.** On **14 April 1897** Ammon invoiced the Association for the Ostrey Hollow outfit:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| 6 doz. milk-test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c., at $0.35 | $25.20 |
| 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at $0.22 | $31.68 |
| Acid measure | $0.90 |
| Four-bottle hand tester | $18.00 |
| Freight | $2.15 |
| **Total** | **$77.93** |

That is **72 pipettes**. The outfit went into use on **1 May 1897**, ending the borrowing from Larrow Green. The pipettes delivered on average **16.72 c.c.** instead of 17.6, which is **0.88 c.c. short, or 95%** of the mark. The bottle neck is graduated for the full charge, so every reading was **one part in twenty (5%) low**, in proportion to the milk's richness.

On **12 June 1923** Strawn, who had carried a burette since 1921, ran the measures against it and wrote: "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate." Then:
- **28 June:** the Association ordered six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles.
- **2 July:** Ammon acknowledged the order and replied that "our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot."
- **July:** new glass was in use.

The old glass went into its stencilled crate (AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET), under a tarpaulin in the loft over the intake room, reached by a ladder. The cream-hauler's son put his hand on it there. Two of the three 1925 arbitration sittings met in the room underneath it.

In **February 1958** Ivy Keddie, a graduate student at Farland, found the crate. **Nine** pipettes survived, two of them chipped. On **9 March 1958** she measured them against a calibrated burette. The mean was 16.72 c.c., and every one was short in the same proportion. **Bulletin 471** (Vaught and Keddie, October 1958) concluded: no water, no theft; the glass read one part in twenty low from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923. The crate went to the Historical Rooms at Ashlin in 1959.

Why the lot ran short **cannot be determined**. Junia Ammon (1949) infers an accidental reground mould that made the pipettes consistently wrong, which spot checks cannot catch. But the works books burned in 1931. The *Chronicle*'s claim that the firm acted "knowingly" has no support.

**Quantities:**
- 72 pipettes; $77.93;
- 16.72/17.6 = 0.95;
- in use about 26 years and 6 weeks;
- condemned 1923, cause found 1958 (35 years later).

**Documents:** r20, r07, r04, r06, r11 (main); r17, r18, r19, r21, r22, r24.

### 4. The Brant weigh book and the family across the intake (1897–1963)

**Who:** Emmet Brant (1854–1929), the station's largest patron; his sister Orra Keddie; his son Roy Brant (about 1888–1962); Roy's daughter Hazel; Orra's granddaughter Ivy.

**What happened.** Hessel Bottom is about 160 acres of wet meadow under Ostrey Hollow, in **six farms**, holding nine families. The six is confirmed by the 1908 committee, which visited each farm, and by Roy, Hazel and Kepp; Effie's "seven" is wrong. Emmet had the largest place: sixty-odd acres and up to 38 cows.

From **1 January 1897** he weighed his milk on his own scale before it went to the station and wrote his figure beside the station's intake weight. The start date comes from Nym's accession record and Frick's exhibit list, which are independent; Roy's 1898 and Hazel's 1900 are misremembered, and Hazel says herself that she keeps no years. The book is hand-ruled in two columns, its front cover gone and its back held with harness leather. The weights always agreed: "it was not the weighing."

The **1910 leaf** reads **200,000 lb even**, paid on a test of **3.42**, with fat at 24¢.

| 1910 | Result |
|---|---|
| Fat credited: 200,000 × 3.42% | 6,840 lb |
| True test: 3.42/0.95 | 3.60 |
| Fat actually delivered: 200,000 × 3.60% | 7,200 lb |
| Not credited | 360 lb |
| Value at 24¢ | $86.40 |

Roy put his milk 0.17 under Larrow Green's in July and in February alike. That fits Larrow Green's 3.78 against the station's 3.61.

**The family.** Orra was Emmet's younger sister and married Ansel Keddie in 1889. So the tester who was said to be shorting the patrons was married to the sister of the patron who lost most. Their son Duncan and Emmet's son Roy were first cousins, which makes Duncan's daughter Ivy (born 1931) and Roy's daughter Hazel second cousins.

The two households shared Sunday dinners until **June 1919**. Two days after Ansel carried Vose's water circular round himself, Emmet stood in the Keddie yard and asked why he had carried it if he did not believe it. He never entered the house again. Orra's contemporary diary settles this against Duncan's "at our table thirty-six years." Emmet signed the January 1925 petition and died in spring 1929 without making it up. Orra went down to the house and sat an hour in the kitchen.

**The book's custody.** It was exhibit 3 at the 1925 arbitration. Roy kept it from 1929 and **lent** it (not left it) to Ivy in 1958 for the Farland work, and had it back. When he died in 1962 it went to Hazel, who gave it to the Historical Rooms at Ashlin in 1963. (Nym's label "kept by A. Keddie" is wrong.)

**Documents:** r09, r17, r18, r24 (main); r01, r03, r04, r07, r13.

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### 5. The water charge and the inspector's notebooks (1907–1936)

**Who:** Selby Vose, the manager who made the charge; Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector; the Ostrey Hollow patron families.

**Strawn's record.** Strawn rode the Ordell circuit from **1907 until his last round in March 1936** and was kin to nobody in the valley. At each plant he tried the scales with weights, put a lactometer in the milk and watched a test made, and he kept a notebook for every year. His Ostrey Hollow entries:
- 1907: scales true, the tester's hand good.
- June 1911, while the borrowed measure was in use: "Nothing here to note."
- 1912: no water.
- 1913: a new manager.
- 1916: the tester says the patrons blame him.
- 1919: he had never found water and told the manager so.
- 1921: burettes issued.
- 1922: no check (a short day).
- **12 June 1923**: glass condemned.
- October 1923: the new glass, in use since July, is good.
- 1924: tests improved, **3.80**.
- Fall 1925: a new man, "quick, and quick is not the thing I look for."
- 1928: the loft "full since before the war."

"I do not keep an estimation, I keep a book." The 1923 volume was exhibit 4 before the 1925 board. The notebooks went to the state dairy office in 1936 and to the Farland college library in 1947, where Ivy found the 1923 volume in 1958.

**The circular.** In **June 1919** Vose sent the station's patron families a printed circular warning against water in the milk. Kyle typed it and Vose alone signed it: "cannot pay for creek water"; "bring it as it left the cow." Vira Toland and the Brants, who are independent of each other, say nine families received it; Orra's diary says eleven. Ansel would not post the copies and carried them round himself. Vira's father read his twice at the table with his hat on; Hazel's mother read hers twice and put it in the stove. Emmet Brant broke with Ansel over it, and the families never spoke civilly again. Sill, the buttermaker, sided with Vose: "nine families all got honest in one summer."

**Why the charge fails:**
- Strawn's lactometer never found water in some sixteen years of visits, and he told the manager so in 1919.
- The 1908 committee checked the water at every barn.
- Watered milk shows in cheese curd, and Alder Corners never had a complaint about the same men's milk.
- Watering cannot explain the 1911 jump to 3.80 from the same patrons.
- Vose's 1926 address claimed the tests rose "in the very next season" after the petition and hearing, by 0.21 to 3.90 for 1924. The figure and the order are both wrong. The rise was to **3.80 in 1924**, after the glass was changed in July 1923 and before the January 1925 petition, with nothing changed on the farms.

**Documents:** r06, r08, r23, r24, r01 (main); r07, r09, r13, r14, r17, r18, r22.

### 6. The hay theory: the Grigg committee, Bulletin 214 and Dr Kepp (1908–1959)

**Who:** Nyle Grigg and his committee; Dr Roland Kepp, V.S.; the State Agricultural College at Farland; the county agent Orin Falke.

**The 1908 committee.** Complaints began about 1902. In **January 1908** the board appointed a committee of **three** under Grigg, a Larrow Green farmer who was not a patron of the station but had bought hay from two Bottom men. The committee:
- visited each of the **six** Hessel Bottom farms, some three or four times;
- drew and sealed its own samples;
- watched the milking, the hauling, the intake and the test;
- tried the station scales and the patrons' scales;
- inquired into the water.

It found **"no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter,"** and blamed the late-cut sedge and rush hay: "what such land gives in quantity it withholds in quality."

The report text says they sampled **January, February and March**. Nym's copy covers only those months, and Orra's diary has the committee arriving in January and gone by March. Grigg's 1919 claim of sampling "through the whole of 1908" is therefore later inflation. The report closed the question for eleven years. Emmet Brant answered that the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not changed.

**Bulletin 214.** This was the College's 1911 bog-hay feeding trial. Its printed summary says that bog hay fed as the whole roughage depressed the test by **no more than 0.05 in winter, and by nothing measurable on summer pasture**. Grigg misquoted it in his 1919 reprint as "two tenths in the winter feeding," and Kepp repeated the misquote in his **1926** Farm Club paper. Kepp argued that sour, lime- and phosphate-poor wet ground makes milk "a fifth of a point" poorer, and that the gap was 0.21. He dismissed the 1911 interval as "six weeks" of flush figures and refused to look for "a second cause in a building."

**Why the theory fails.** The gap was a flat **0.19**, the same in July as in February (Brant book, Keddie, Hazel, Falke). The bulletin allows 0.05 in winter and nothing in summer, so hay is **about four times too small** and has the wrong seasonal pattern. Also:
- The same Bottom men's summer milk tested 3.80 at Alder Corners.
- The tests went to 3.80 in 1911 and again in 1924 on the same hay.

In 1959 Falke first allowed hay as "a real if partial factor." After reading the Bulletin 214 summary, Falke withdrew the concession: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July."

**Documents:** r15, r16, r22 (main); r04, r07, r09, r11, r13, r17, r24.

### 7. The cheese over the ridge: Alder Corners, 1914–1930

**Who:** Jerome Cudd (died March 1941), his daughter Rosalie Cudd, and four Hessel Bottom patrons.

**What happened.** Jerome Cudd built a cheese factory at Alder Corners, in Vessey County over Redlow Ridge, in **1913**; it opened for its first season in **1914**. Rosalie was twelve when the first vat was set. The ridge road is four miles of switchback and is closed from November until April or May, so there was no winter hauling.

From **1915**, four of the six Bottom men hauled their summer milk over the ridge, from about 1 June to mid-September, for **sixteen seasons, the last in 1930**. The rest of the year they delivered to the station. The Brants never went over the ridge, which Roy thought cost them money.

Jerome tested on his own new glass, compared his figures with no one's and never saw the station's. His season averages ran 3.78–3.82:

| Years | Averages |
|---|---|
| 1915–1919 | 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.80, 3.80 |
| 1920–1924 | 3.82, 3.79, 3.78, 3.81, 3.80 |
| 1925–1930 | 3.79, 3.82, 3.78, 3.80, 3.81, 3.79 |

That is about **3.80** overall. His 1919 entry reads: "3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge." Rosalie adds that "cheese is a witness": watered milk shows in the curd, and there was never a complaint or a docking.

The short glass was in use at the station for only **eight** of these seasons in full (1915–1922), plus part of 1923. Ivy's "fourteen overlapping seasons" is wrong, and the museum's copy (Nym item 13) holds only the 1919 season. In every overlap season the same cows' summer milk tested **3.80** over the ridge and **3.61** at the station, a gap of **0.19** in midsummer, when hay has no effect. After 1923 the station also read about 3.80 while Alder Corners stayed the same. This is an independent control on different glass, kept by a man with no stake, and it defeats both the hay and the water theories.

Rosalie took over the factory and the book in 1941 and ran it for seventeen years. In 1958 she copied the figures for Ivy on a stranger's say-so; Ivy never saw the book itself. The book went to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970.

**Quantities:** four men; sixteen summer seasons; eight full seasons of overlap with the short glass; 3.80 against 3.61.

**Documents:** r10, r09, r17, r04 (main); r07, r12, r18, r19.

### 8. The "station gain" line, the theft charge and the arbitration of 1925

**Who:** Wilbur Teague (bookkeeper), Bertram Kyle (clerk), Hobart Sill (buttermaker), Alonzo Frick (chairman of the arbitration board), the patrons, and Ansel Keddie.

**The ledger line.** Every year the Ostrey Hollow station account carried three figures:
- the fat credited to the patrons on the station test;
- the fat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream;
- the difference, entered as **"station gain … five and a quarter per cent, as usual"** and carried to the general account.

In **1898** the entry read **57,760 credited / 60,800 received / 3,040 gain**. The gain is 5¼% of the fat credited, which is exactly **5% of the fat received**: the pipette's one part in twenty. The line was already "as usual" in 1898, so Teague's claim to have invented it in 1905 is false. Sill reweighed the Hollow cream at the Green, found that it always "ran heavy," and entered it in his receiving book, but brushed it aside ("cream is not milk"; "nobody ever asked me"). Kyle copied the line for twenty-five years without wondering: "a figure with a name on it has been explained."

**The shortfall**, over the full delivery years **1898–1922**:

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| Milk: 25 years × 1.6M lb | **40,000,000 lb** |
| Fat credited at 3.61% | **1,444,000 lb** |
| Fat delivered at 3.80% | **1,520,000 lb** |
| Not credited | **76,000 lb** |
| Value at 25¢ (the average Kyle worked out for the arbitration) | **$19,000** |

**The arbitration.** The patrons petitioned in **January 1925**, and Emmet Brant signed. A three-man board chaired by Alonzo Frick, an Ashlin attorney chosen because he kept no cow, sat from **17 March to 4 May 1925**. The first sitting was at the Association office in Ashlin. The other two were in the station weigh room, under the crate, where the board saw the scales tried and a test made. It had "four books and nothing else": the test book, the receiving book, the Brant weigh book and Strawn's 1923 notebook. Nobody laid those books side by side. Three explanations were argued, and none was established:
- water (Vose);
- theft (Teague's insinuation: "two men or a lock");
- hay (the 1908 report).

Keddie was dismissed on **30 April 1925**. On **4 May** the board awarded **$3,800 "without admission,"** charged to the station account and not open to appeal. That is exactly **one-fifth** of the $19,000.

**Afterlife of the theft charge.** It survived in Rundle's 1939 jubilee pamphlet, which a 1959 note withdrew on seeing the ledger entry, and in the cream hauler's belief that less cream came off the platform than went onto it. The bookkeeper's own ledger refutes it: more fat reached Larrow Green than the station credited. The patrons' own 1925 claim cannot be recovered: Frick says 67,000 lb and Rundle says $19,600.

**Documents:** r05, r23, r14, r13, r02 (main); r03, r04, r07, r08, r12, r21, r24.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.**
- Duncan Keddie says he is the son of Ansel and Orra, born 1894. Orra's diary agrees ("Duncan five years old", September 1899).
- Duncan says Ivy, born 1931, is his daughter.
- Ivy's own essay, Hazel, Roy and Oram ("the granddaughter") agree.

The *Ashlin Chronicle* and Falke's report call her Ansel's "great-niece," which would make her the grandchild of one of Ansel's siblings. No source supports that. Falke wrote "I am informed, and have verified" and corrected it to granddaughter. The Chronicle piece was put together in a day and a half from a press release, and Falke probably copied it from there, so the two are one source. Her age (27 in October 1958) also fits a 1931 birth.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins.** The derivation:
1. Emmet Brant and Orra were siblings.
2. Roy (Emmet's son) and Duncan (Orra's son) were therefore first cousins.
3. Hazel (Roy's daughter) and Ivy (Duncan's daughter) are therefore second cousins.

Duncan, Hazel, Roy and Rundle all agree. Vira and Oram say "first cousin once removed," which is wrong: that is the relation between Ivy and Roy, or Hazel and Duncan.

What the sources leave open:
- **Whether Orra was Emmet's full or half sister.** Roy (Emmet's son: full sister, no stepmother) and Duncan (Orra's son: "brother and sister," "the younger of the two") say full. Hazel says half-sister, by Emmet's father's second wife. Orra's own pages from before 1889 were not kept, so her record cannot decide it. The two nearest witnesses say full, so I take full, but it is not proven. If she was a half-sister, Ivy and Hazel are half second cousins.
- **The other parents.** The spouses of Emmet, Roy and Duncan are never named, so any kinship through those lines is unknown.
- **Dates.** Roy's birth year (about 1888) is inferred from his being 67 in 1955. Orra's birth year is 1866 (diary preface) or 1868 (Duncan).

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose.** Gideon organized the Association, was its first president (1889–1911), and drafted the by-laws, including Article VII. The sources:
- For "son": Selby himself in 1926 ("son of the first president"), Effie Loomis ("son of the late president"), Duncan Keddie (who specifically rejects "nephew") and Kyle ("the son … the nephew story … is nonsense").
- For "nephew": Vira and Rundle, both passing on valley talk.

Why it matters: the rule that made the station's own test final, and paid on it, was his father's. As manager from 1913 to 1938, Selby administered that rule and defended it:
- He blamed the patrons' water in 1919 rather than the test.
- He signed off the "station gain" line every year without reading it.
- He told Teague in 1916 to let the figures alone.
- He called the 1923 condemnation "housekeeping."
- He called the $3,800 "a generous settlement of an unproved claim."

Questioning the test meant questioning his father's by-law, and his father's own milk tested high (4.56) under the same system at the Green. Reading this as a family stake behind his resistance is my inference; no source says it outright.

**B4. The Vose and Keddie families: no relationship is established, and the evidence goes against one.**
- Vira says Orra Keddie and "old Mrs. Vose" were first cousins.
- Oram says the Keddies and Voses were "cousins of some sort," which he had from his mother's mother and could not trace.
- Duncan Keddie, Orra's son, says there was no connection. Two Ashlin women spread the talk, which he puts down to confusion with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation of Gideon either. His mother, who gave the connection for every headstone, never named a Vose.

Vira and Oram are hearsay, and both give a wrong kinship elsewhere (Ivy and Hazel as "first cousin once removed"). The nearest witness denies it. Orra's pre-1889 family pages are lost, so a distant link cannot be ruled out absolutely. But nothing supports one, and I answer: none.


# Answer sheet — Part 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

### C1

**(a) The proportion.** The surviving pipettes delivered a mean of **16.72 c.c.** against the 17.6 c.c. marked on them (Ivy's sheet; Nym item 5).
- 16.72 / 17.6 = **0.95**.
- The Babcock bottle's neck is graduated for a full 17.6 c.c. charge. A 16.72 c.c. charge therefore carries only 95% of the fat the scale assumes, so the reading is 0.95 × the true test.
- The tests were **5% low, one part in twenty**. Each charge was 0.88 c.c. short.
- The error is proportional: richer milk lost more points (4.80 read as 4.56; 3.60 read as 3.42).

Vaught's "16.27" gives 0.9244 and a true test of 3.905, which he admits does not match 1924's 3.80. It is a transposition, and I reject it.

**(b) The true test.** 3.61 / 0.95 = **3.80**. The recorded tests were **0.19 points** short (3.80 − 3.61).

The 3.80 is confirmed independently five times:

| Check | Test |
|---|---|
| 1896, on borrowed glass | 3.80 |
| The nine weeks of 1911 | 3.80 |
| 1924, on new glass | 3.80 |
| Alder Corners, the same men's milk | about 3.80 |
| Ledger: 60,800 / 1,600,000 lb | 3.80 |

**(c) Fat not credited.** The full delivery years are 1898–1922: 25 years × 1,600,000 lb = **40,000,000 lb** of milk.

| | Calculation | Fat |
|---|---|---|
| True | 40,000,000 × 3.80% | 1,520,000 lb |
| Credited | 40,000,000 × 3.61% | 1,444,000 lb |
| **Not credited** | 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 (check: 40,000,000 × 0.19%) | **76,000 lb** |

**(d) Value and fraction received.** 76,000 lb × $0.25 = **$19,000**. The patrons received $3,800 (award of 4 May 1925). 3,800 / 19,000 = **one-fifth (20%)**.

I used 25¢, the average Kyle compiled for the arbitration, which Effie, Ivy and Roy also give. Sill's and Kepp's 27¢ is rejected.

### C2 — Emmet Brant, 1910

The 1910 leaf gives 200,000 lb, paid on a test of 3.42.

**(a) Credited:** 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of butterfat.

**(b) Owed and not credited:**
- True test: 3.42 / 0.95 = 3.60.
- True fat: 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb.
- Not credited: 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb**. That is 5% of the fat delivered, or 5¼% of the fat credited.

**(c) Value that year:** 360 × $0.24, the 1910 price on Roy's leaf, = **$86.40**. At the 25¢ long-run average it would be $90.00, but the leaf gives 24¢ for that year.

### C3 — 1898–1922

**(a) Credited to the station's patrons:** **1,444,000 lb**.

**(b) Reached Larrow Green in the station's cream:** **1,520,000 lb**. The "station gain" line records received fat equal to the true fat delivered. In 1898 it was 60,800 lb, which is 3.80% of 1.6M lb.

**(c) The difference is 76,000 lb:**
- As a share of the fat received at Larrow Green (the fat actually delivered): 76,000 / 1,520,000 = **5.00%**.
- As a share of the fat credited: 76,000 / 1,444,000 = **5.26%, which is the ledger's "5¼ per cent."**
- Single-year check, 1898: 3,040 / 60,800 = 5.00%, and 3,040 / 57,760 = 5.26%.

The ledger's 5¼% is a percentage of the **credited** fat. Teague ("5¼% of the fat actually in the cream") and Vaught ("of the butterfat actually delivered") put it on the wrong base.

### C4

**(a) Invoice total: $77.93.** Check: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93. The "$77.39" in Junia Ammon's text and in the *Chronicle* is a transposition that the reproduced invoice itself refutes.

**(b) Pipettes and shortfall:**
- The invoice bought **72 pipettes** (six dozen). Check: 6 × 12 × $0.35 = $25.20; five dozen would have cost $21.00.
- Each was short by **0.88 c.c.** on average (17.6 − 16.72). That average comes from the nine survivors measured on 9 March 1958. Falke reports they were short "in the same proportion, one to another." How short each of the other 63 was cannot be measured, because they no longer exist.

### C5

**(a) Two different spans.**
- **Time in use:** from **1 May 1897** (invoice; Orra's diary) to **12 June 1923** (Strawn's condemnation). That is **26 years, 1 month and 11 days** (about 9,537 days, roughly 26 years and 6 weeks). Strictly, the nine weeks of 1911 (8 May–10 July) on the borrowed measure should come out of it.
- **Money calculation:** **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922.**

They differ because 1897 (from 1 May) and 1923 (to 12 June) are part years. The ledger and arbitration figures (40M lb, 1.6M lb a year) were made up on whole years, and there is no part-year milk figure for either end. So the $19,000 leaves out roughly eight months of 1897 and five and a half of 1923. It slightly overstates 1911, whose nine weeks were tested correctly. It is a whole-years figure, not an exact total.

**(b) Milk received in the first season:** **cannot be determined.**
- Sill says "about 900,000 lb." That is a memory of 1934, and he went up to the station only twice.
- Kyle says "a good eleven hundred thousand," hearsay from "men who were there," and adds that "it is not in any book I ever ruled."
- Kyle also explains that the 1896 sheets were carried on the creamery's books and never filed with the station papers.
- Nym, the curator holding the ledgers, records that **no 1896 intake figure exists**.

Two memories that disagree, with no document behind either, cannot settle it. The 1.6M lb yearly average applies to full years from 1898, not to the season that began on 4 May 1896.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment.**

**When and how long:** from **8 May to 10 July 1911**, 63 days, exactly **nine weeks**. The source is Keddie's two dated entries, and Roy, Falke and Vaught agree. The "six weeks" of Teague and Kepp is wrong by the dates.

**Variable:** the measuring pipette. The station's own Ammon glass was replaced by a borrowed measure, then restored.

**Held constant:**
- the tester, his hand and his method;
- the bottles, acid, hand tester and scale;
- the same patrons, herds, land and hay;
- no change in supervision.

**Result:** every farm's test rose to about **3.80** and fell back to **3.61** on the very day the station's own glass returned ("Tests are back where they were. ?").

**What it establishes that a calculation cannot:**
- **Cause, shown in operation at the station.** The 1958 measurement of nine survivors shows only that the glass *could* read 5% low. The 1911 interval shows that switching the glass switched the result, both ways, with nothing else changed.
- **The milk's true level (3.80) was fixed without reference to the pipette measurements.**
- **Water, theft and hay are ruled out** as the cause of the gap. The fall came on 10 July, in midsummer, so Kepp's "flush of the season" objection fails: the flush did not end on 10 July.

**What it does not establish:**
- how short each pipette was, or why;
- that the borrowed measure was itself exact, only that it read as other good glass did;
- where the measure came from (Larrow Green by the cream wagon per Ivy, or the state man's per Roy);
- by itself, that the gap held in winter. That comes from the Brant book and Keddie's statement that it did not vary by season.
- the money owed.

**D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream.**

1. **The ledger and receiving book at Larrow Green (Teague, Kyle; Sill).** Every year more fat arrived at the Green in the station's cream than the station credited: 60,800 lb received against 57,760 lb credited in 1898, the "station gain" at 5% of receipts. Sill reweighed the Hollow cream and found it "ran heavy." A thief skimming cream would make the Green receive *less* than credited. (I count the ledger and Sill as one strand, since the ledger's receipts probably came from the receiving book.)
2. **The 1911 interval (Keddie's own book; Falke).** The same man, under the same supervision, tested 3.80 for nine weeks with a borrowed measure and 3.61 either side of it. A thief does not go honest when a pipette breaks.
3. **The state inspector's record (Strawn).** Strawn watched the tests on his visits from 1907: the tester's hand good, and "Nothing here to note" in June 1911. From July 1923, with new glass and **the same tester** still in post, the tests stood at 3.80 for 1924.
4. **Alder Corners (Rosalie Cudd).** The same men's summer milk read 3.80 on another man's glass. The low figure belonged to the station's instrument, not to anything removed after testing.

These are independent because different people kept them, in different places, with different instruments and no reason to agree: the Association's office clerks at Larrow Green, the tester at the station, the state inspector, and a cheesemaker in another county.

Reasons 1 and 2 (and 3) would stand even if the glass had never been measured in 1958. The ledger in particular was in plain sight every year and refutes theft on its own.

**Did the bookkeeper question the ledger entries that refute his own accusation?** Not in any way that led him to withdraw it.
- Teague says he raised the matter once, with Vose in **1916**, and was told to let it alone. Kyle, who sat outside Vose's door, says Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years." Whether that 1916 conversation happened **cannot be settled**.
- What is certain: at the 1925 hearing Teague hinted at theft ("two men or a lock") while his own line showed the cream arriving heavy.
- He never put the line to the board as evidence.
- He misdescribed its base as 5¼% "of the fat actually in the cream."
- He claimed to have invented it in 1905, though the 1898 entry he quotes already reads "as usual."

**D3. Did the manager know, before the glass was condemned, that the tests ran low?** **Yes, that the tests ran low.** That he understood why, or read the line that showed it, is not established.
- The **June 1919 water circular**, signed by Vose alone, exists only because the Hollow's tests ran low. Warning those patrons about water presupposes that he knew their milk tested below the valley's.
- Complaints had been made at annual meetings since 1902, and there was the 1908 committee.
- Strawn records telling "the manager" in 1919 that he had never found water.
- Teague says he raised it with Vose in 1916.
- Vose himself contrasted his father's Jerseys (4.56) with the Hollow.

On the "station gain" line: Kyle says Vose read only the totals and signed with his thumb on the bottom figure. No source shows he connected the 5¼% line with a short test. So he knew the tests ran low and attributed it to water. Whether he understood that his own ledger proved the milk was sound **cannot be determined**, and Kyle's testimony suggests he did not.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. The cause: one faulty instrument.** The station's own Ammon pipettes, 72 of them, invoiced on 14 April 1897 and marked 17.6 c.c., delivered on average **16.72 c.c.** The Babcock bottle neck is graduated for 17.6 c.c., so every reading was **16.72/17.6 = 0.95** of the truth.

**The size of the error.** Every reading was one part in twenty low. On the Hollow's true 3.80 milk that is **0.19 points**, so the book read 3.61.
- It is proportional: 4.80 reads 4.56; 3.60 reads 3.42.
- It is constant across seasons, weather and years, because the glass does not change: 3.61 ±0.03, and 0.19 in July as in February. That is the signature of an instrument, not of feed or fraud.
- Why that particular size: the lot was 0.88 c.c. short. How it came to be short (Junia's reground mould) cannot be verified.

**Why it began when it did.** On **1 May 1897** the station changed from borrowed Larrow Green glass to its own. The 1896 season on Larrow Green glass read 3.80. From 1898 the book reads 3.61.

**Why it ended when it did.** Strawn had carried a burette since 1921 and first ran the station's measures against it on **12 June 1923**. He condemned the lot, and new glass was in use from July. 1924 read **3.80**.

**Why nothing else had to change.** Throughout, the cows, land, hay, men, scales (the Brant weights always agreed), acid, bottles and tester stayed the same. Only the pipette changed, and the 1911 interval shows the effect switching on and off with nothing else changed. The error went unnoticed because:
- the pipette is the one instrument a plant cannot check (scales are checked with weights, bottles with mercury);
- a consistently wrong lot passes spot checks;
- the ledger turned the 5% into a named "station gain" that everyone copied.

**The three competing explanations:**
1. **Water**, advanced by Selby Vose (the 1919 circular and his 1926 address), echoed by Sill. Defeated by:
   - Strawn's lactometer, which never found water, as he told the manager;
   - the 1908 committee's check of every barn's water;
   - Alder Corners cheese, where watering would have shown in the curd;
   - the ledger showing more fat, not less, reaching the Green;
   - the 1924 rise coming with the new glass, before the petition.
2. **Theft by the tester**, insinuated by Wilbur Teague at the 1925 hearing ("two men or a lock"), told in Rundle's 1939 chapter IV, and believed by Oram's father. Defeated by:
   - the station-gain line: 60,800 lb received against 57,760 credited in 1898, 5% more reaching Larrow Green than credited;
   - Sill's heavy cream in the receiving book;
   - the same tester reading 3.80 in the 1911 weeks and in 1924.
3. **Wet hay**, advanced by Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee (reprinted in 1919) and Dr Roland Kepp (1926), and provisionally allowed by Falke. Defeated by:
   - Bulletin 214, which found at most 0.05 in winter and nothing in summer, about a quarter of the 0.19 and the wrong seasonal pattern;
   - the gap being flat all year;
   - the same men's summer milk reading 3.80 at Alder Corners;
   - the jumps in 1911 and 1924 on unchanged hay.

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glass?** **No.** It had "four books and nothing else" (Frick, confirmed by Kyle's list): the test book, the receiving book, the Brant weigh book and Strawn's 1923 notebook. The crate lay in the loft directly over the weigh room where two of the three sittings met. Strawn's condemnation entry was in the room as an exhibit, but by Kyle's account no one set it beside the test book, and Effie says the crate was never mentioned.

**What did the tester himself believe?** **This cannot be determined.**
- His own record is the question mark of 10 July 1911 against "Tests are back where they were."
- His sworn 1925 statement says he could not say why the figures were what they were.
- Duncan says his father came home in 1911 saying it was "something in the glass."
- Frick says Keddie privately told him in the Ashlin corridor that he had always thought the patrons were at fault.

The question mark shows he noticed the connection with the glass and would not commit to it. What he finally believed, the sources do not decide.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** From 1897 to 1923 the Ostrey Hollow station tested every patron's milk with a lot of Ammon pipettes that delivered 16.72 c.c. instead of 17.6. Because Article VII paid patrons on the station's test alone, every reading came out 5% low: 3.61 instead of 3.80. Over 1898–1922 the patrons lost 76,000 lb of butterfat, worth $19,000. The valley blamed water (the manager's 1919 circular), theft (the bookkeeper) and wet hay (the 1908 committee). A 1925 arbitration paid $3,800 and dismissed the tester, Ansel Keddie, who died uncleared in 1934. Each record held one piece of the answer: the tester's 1911 question mark, the Brant weigh book, the inspector's 1923 condemnation, the ledger's "station gain," the Alder Corners cheese tests and the invoice. No one put them together until his granddaughter Ivy found the crate in the loft in 1958 and measured the glass.
