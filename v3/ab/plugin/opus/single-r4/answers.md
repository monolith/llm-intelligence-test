# Answer Sheet — Part 1

## Section A — Reconstruction (Stories 1–4)

How I split the stories: I divided the history by the strands of record that each carried part of it. They are (1) the Association and its by-law; (2) the station and its tester; (3) the Brant farm and weigh book; (4) the hay theory; (5) the Alder Corners cheese factory; (6) the ledger's station-gain line; (7) the accusation and the arbitration; (8) the condemned crate and its discovery in 1958. Where sources conflict I use the corrected value and give the reason briefly in brackets.

---

### Story 1 — The by-law: the Larrow Valley Association and Article VII

Farmers of the valley organized the **Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association** in **1889**, at a cold-weather meeting in the Larrow Green schoolhouse. **Gideon Vose** was its first president from 1889 to **1911** (22 years). He was heavy, quiet and obstinate, had once thought of the law, and died in **1917**. In **1891** the Association built its creamery on **Larrow Creek at Larrow Green**. It was the same building still in use in 1941.

Gideon drafted the by-laws himself, at his kitchen table. **Article VII** is the hinge of the whole history. It pays each patron by the pounds of **butterfat** his milk shows by test, not by the weight of the milk, and "**the test of the station where he delivers shall govern**", with no appeal. Under payment by weight, nothing that followed would have cost anyone a dollar (Falke). **The year Article VII was adopted cannot be determined.** Loomis says 1892, Rundle 1891 and Grigg 1893. The printed pamphlet carries no date, and the minute book for 1889–1892 was lost in the **1938** office move.

Gideon's **son** (not nephew) **Selby Vose** was manager from **1913 to 1938**. [Son: his own words "my father… wrote the by-law", backed by Loomis, Duncan Keddie and Kyle. 1913: Loomis, Vose's own "thirteen years" in 1926, and Strawn's 1913 note, against Kyle's 1911.] The office was one room over a harness shop at **Ashlin**. The staff were **Effie Loomis**, secretary 1901–1940; **Wilbur Teague**, bookkeeper 1905–1945; and **Hobart Sill**, buttermaker at Larrow Green 1893–1934. Loomis admits that none of them, "I include myself first", ever stood in the station on a working morning. Butterfat averaged **25¢ a pound** over the years in dispute.

The by-law made one instrument's reading final and beyond appeal. As a result, twenty-five years of argument were conducted about men: the patrons, the tester and the land. The instrument itself was never questioned. In his 1926 annual address Selby defended the rule in his father's name ("Bring it as it left the cow"). He cited his father's Jersey herd, which tested **4.56** in 1910, and blamed water. He called the $3,800 award "a generous settlement of an unproved claim." The clerk Kyle says Selby signed every sheet with his thumb on the total, never reading the lines above it.

**Documents:** r02 (Loomis), r12 (Rundle), r08 (Vose), r15 (Grigg), r22 (Falke), r23 (Kyle), r18 (Duncan Keddie), r05 (Teague), r14 (Sill), r07 (Nym, item 1), r01 (Vira Toland).

### Story 2 — The station, its tester, and his book (1896–1925)

The **Ostrey Hollow skimming station** stood **7 miles** up the valley from Larrow Green, on the flat by the creek where the road bends. It was a two-floor board building with a covered platform. Its **intake (weigh) room** held the scale, vat and separator. Above that was a loft, reached by a ladder nailed to the wall. It was built in the winter of 1895–96; **Ansel Keddie** (1861–1934) went up in 1895 to get it ready, and it **opened on 4 May 1896**. Milk was weighed in, sampled, tested and separated. The cream went down to Larrow Green daily except Sunday on the Oram wagon, and the skim went back to the patrons. The patrons were **nine families**, on the six Hessel Bottom farms and three hill places.

Keddie was tester and separator man for **29 seasons (1896–1924)**. He had married **Orra Brant** in 1889, and their son **Duncan** was born in 1894. He kept the **test book** in his own hand, in five columns: date, patron, pounds, test, fat. His errors were struck through and initialed. His day began at 5:30. Every can was weighed and the weight called. Ten-day composite samples were kept in numbered jars and warmed blood-warm. The **17.6 c.c.** charge was drawn to the mark and blown into the bottle, acid was run down the side, four bottles went into the hand tester, and the fat column was read with dividers. Strawn found his work "slow and clean."

The first leaf reads: "Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes." It was Sill's own glass, lent from his bench. [The first leaf, Sill, Orra and Nym outweigh the "Ashlin creamery" in Keddie's letter.] On that borrowed glass the **1896 average was 3.80**. The station's own outfit came from **Ammon & Sons, Tarnet**, on an invoice dated **14 April 1897**:

- six dozen 17.6 c.c. pipettes at 35¢: $25.20
- twelve dozen 8% bottles: $31.68
- one acid measure: $0.90
- one four-bottle hand tester: $18.00
- freight: $2.15
- **total: $77.93**

It went into use on **1 May 1897**. From **1898** the average was **3.61**, within 0.03 every year.

The exception was **1911**. The pipette broke on **8 May** and a borrowed measure was used, and tests stood at **3.80**. On **10 July**, nine weeks later, the station's own measure was back and the tests fell that same day. Keddie wrote a "?" in the book. At home he said it was "something in the glass" (Duncan). After new glass arrived in **July 1923**, his **1924 average was 3.80**. He was dismissed on **30 April 1925**, turned carpenter, and died in **February 1934**.

**Documents:** r03 (Keddie), r01, r07 (items 2–3), r14 (Sill), r20 (Junia Ammon), r21 (Oram), r24 (Orra), r18, r06, r22, r11, r04.

### Story 3 — Hessel Bottom and the Brant weigh book

**Hessel Bottom** is about 160 acres of springy wet meadow along the creek under Ostrey Hollow. Water stands on it into May, and its hay is coarse sedge, cut late. **Six farms** had rights there (Roy Brant, Hazel Brant, Grigg's report and Kepp, against Loomis's "seven"), and nine families lived on them. The largest was **Emmet Brant's** (1854–1929): 60-odd acres and **38 cows** in a good year. Emmet was the station's largest patron in every recorded year.

From **1 January 1897** (Frick's exhibit list, Nym and Duncan; Roy says 1898 and Hazel 1900) Emmet weighed every can on his own scale. He wrote his weight beside the station's weight and the test he was paid on, after the second milking, by lamplight, with his hat on. The two scales always agreed to within a pound or two of slopped milk, so the weighing was never the fault. The book is tall and hand-ruled. Its front cover is gone, the back is held on with harness leather, and the first 40 leaves are stained. Roy kept it up after 1929. He **lent** it, not gave it, to Ivy in 1958. At Roy's death in **1962** it passed to Hazel, who gave it to the Historical Rooms in **1963**. It was one of the four exhibits before the 1925 board.

**The 1910 leaf:** **200,000 lb "even"**, the scales agreeing, **paid on 3.42**. That is **6,840 lb** of fat credited. On the corrected ratio the true test was 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = **3.60**, so he was owed **7,200 lb** and was **360 lb** short, worth **$86.40** at the 1910 price of 24¢.

The Bottom's milk read **0.19** below its true test, "flat as a floor", the same in July and February. Roy's "seventeen hundredths" is the gap against Larrow Green's own average of 3.78, not the gap against the truth. Complaints began in **1902**; Emmet was among the first and was laughed at.

The families were tied together. Emmet's **younger sister Orra** married **Ansel Keddie** in 1889. The tester later accused of shorting the patrons was therefore the brother-in-law of the patron who lost the most, and the two families shared Sunday dinners until the **1919** circular broke them apart. Emmet's name was on the January 1925 petition, and he died in spring 1929 still unreconciled. Roy and Duncan were first cousins, and their daughters Hazel and Ivy were second cousins. Four of the six Bottom men hauled their summer milk over the ridge to Alder Corners (Story 5). Roy did not; he could not afford to be talked about.

**Documents:** r09 (Roy Brant), r17 (Hazel Brant), r07 (item 4), r13, r18, r24, r01, r02, r15, r16, r22.

### Story 4 — The hay theory: the Grigg committee, Bulletin 214, and Dr. Kepp

In **January 1908**, with the Hollow still complaining, the board named a committee of three under **Nyle Grigg** of Larrow Green. Grigg hauled to Larrow Green, not to the station, though he had bought hay off two Bottom men. The committee visited each of the **six farms** three or four times in **January, February and March 1908**. Emmet, whose farm was the biggest, gave them their dinner; Orra noted that they looked at the barns and not the station. They drew and sealed their own samples and watched the test being made. They tried the station scales against a known weight and the patrons' scales against the station's, and they looked into the water at each barn. Their finding: the milk was whole, the scales were honest, and there was "**no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter**." They blamed the wet sedge hay fed from November to April ("what such land gives in quantity it withholds in quality"). They put the remedy "with the land and not with the station." The question was closed for **eleven years**.

In **1919**, with the complaint reopened, Grigg had the report reprinted with a covering letter. The letter claims the committee sampled "through the whole of 1908 — every month," which his own report ("January, February and March") contradicts. It also claims that **Bulletin No. 214** had since found a depression of "two tenths." Bulletin 214 was the State Agricultural College's **1911** feeding trial on bog hay at Farland. Its summary, quoted word for word by Falke, says bog hay depressed the test "**by not more than five hundredths**… in winter, and **by nothing** that could be measured on summer pasture."

**Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S.**, of Ashlin, gave the theory its fullest form in a **1926** paper to the Ordell County Farm Club. He argued that acid wet ground, poor in lime and phosphate, makes cows rob their frames, and that fat suffers first: a "fifth of a point." He put the station gap at 0.21 and repeated the false "two tenths." He dismissed the 1911 interval as "six weeks" of summer flush. He admitted he had never read a test, and he refused to look for "a second cause in a building."

The theory fails on the evidence:

- The real gap was **0.19 in every month**; a feed effect would show in winter only.
- Bulletin 214's maximum is about a quarter of it (0.19 ÷ 0.05 ≈ 3.8, Ivy's "four times"; Vaught's "six" does not follow).
- The 1911 tests fell on 10 July, still in summer.
- The same cows tested 3.80 at Alder Corners in summer, and at the station in 1924 on the same land and hay.

County agent Orin Falke conceded the hay "a real if partial factor" in **1959**, then withdrew the concession once he had read Bulletin 214.

**Documents:** r15 (Grigg), r16 (Kepp), r22 (Falke), r04, r11, r24, r09, r17, r02, r07 (items 11–12), r13.

### Story 5 — Over Redlow Ridge: the Alder Corners test book

**Jerome Cudd** built a cheese factory at **Alder Corners, Vessey County**, on the flat below the church, with spring water and a hard road east. His daughter says he built it in **1913**; Roy Brant and Rundle say it opened in 1914. The two fit if 1913 was the building and 1914 the first vat. Between the factory and Ostrey Hollow lies **Redlow Ridge**: four miles of lumber-road switchback, shut from November to April or May, so there was no winter hauling.

From **1915**, for **sixteen seasons ending in 1930**, **four of the six Hessel Bottom men** hauled their summer flush over the ridge, from about 1 June to mid-September. Cheese paid better in the flush, Cudd was buying hard, and the haul was shorter. The rest of the year they delivered to the station. Roy Brant, with the biggest herd, stayed home because he "cannot afford to be talked about." He called the arrangement "the only honest arithmetic anybody in that valley ever did without meaning to."

Cudd tested the milk on **his own new glassware**. He never compared figures with the station, which was "seven miles off over a mountain." For the summer of 1919 he wrote: "**3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82**. Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge." The season averages for 1915–1930 run from 3.78 to 3.82 and centre on **3.80**. Cheese is a second witness: watered milk shows within a day as a soft curd that will not knit. Cudd never docked the four men, so watering is ruled out independently of any lactometer.

The value of this record is that it is an **independent instrument measuring the same cows' milk**. It read **3.80** while the station's glass read about **3.61**, a gap of 0.19, exactly the pipette error. Bulletin 214 found **no** feed effect at all in summer, so a summer gap cannot be hay. The overlap with the short glass runs from **1915 to 1922**, eight full seasons, plus the first days of the 1923 flush before 12 June. Ivy's "fourteen overlapping seasons" is too many, but her 0.19 is right.

Jerome died in **March 1941**, between setting a vat and cutting it. His **daughter Rosalie** took over that spring and had run the factory 17 years by 1958. She is his daughter by her own letter, Rundle, Duncan Keddie and Nym, not his niece as Ivy and the *Chronicle* have it. She had been 12 when the first vat was set. In **1958** she copied the figures out for Ivy and invited her to see the book. The book itself never left Vessey County; it went to the **Vessey County Historical Society in 1970**.

**Documents:** r10 (Rosalie Cudd), r09, r17, r04, r07 (item 13), r12, r18, r19, r11.

### Story 6 — The station-gain line: "five and a quarter per cent, as usual"

The fat the patrons were never credited with never vanished. It rode down to Larrow Green in the station's cream every day. **Wilbur Teague**, the Association's bookkeeper at Ashlin from 1905 to 1945, entered it every year. In the station account he set the fat credited to patrons on the station test against the fat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream. The difference went down as "**station gain… five and a quarter per cent, as usual**," carried to the general account.

**The 1898 entry:** **57,760 lb credited, 60,800 lb received, gain 3,040 lb.** That is **5.26% of the fat credited**, and **exactly 5.00% of the fat received**. Teague (and Vaught) describe it as a percentage of the fat actually delivered, but that is the wrong base. On 1.6 million lb of milk a year, 60,800 lb is a 3.80% test and 57,760 lb is 3.61%, exactly the pipette ratio. Over **1898–1922**, on **40,000,000 lb of milk**, the ledgers give **1,444,000 lb credited, 1,520,000 lb received, and 76,000 lb of gain**. Teague's "44 million" and "3.16" are wrong. The clerk Kyle worked out the 40 million himself for the arbitration.

**Hobart Sill**, buttermaker at Larrow Green from 1893, saw the same thing at the churn. The Hollow cream "always ran heavy," with more fat than the sheets said, and he recorded it in his receiving book in his own hand. His own creamery's milk ran 3.78, and he judged the Hollow's "really a 3.85." He put it down to the nature of cream, and nobody asked him. He went up to the station only twice in his life.

**Bertram Kyle**, the clerk at the outer desk, copied the line in a fair hand for 25 years, and it was read to fifty farmers at every annual meeting. It had a name, so no one wondered at it, and Selby Vose signed only the totals. Teague called the line "furniture." Yet at the 1925 hearing he hinted that the tester was taking cream ("Cream is portable"; "the remedy is two men or a lock"). His own ledger showed **more** fat arriving than was credited, which is the opposite of theft. He never questioned the line; as Kyle put it, "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years."

**Dell Rundle** had written in her 1939 jubilee pamphlet that cream left by the back door. In a note added in press she withdrew that chapter after seeing the ledger entry: stolen cream would reach the Green light, and this came in heavy. In Kyle's words, "It was not a secret. It was worse than a secret. It was in plain sight with a name on it."

**Documents:** r05 (Teague), r14 (Sill), r23 (Kyle), r12 (Rundle), r04, r07 (item 6), r11, r02.

### Story 7 — The accusation: from the 1919 circular to the 1925 award

In **June 1919** the Association sent the Ostrey Hollow patrons a printed circular warning of **water in their milk**. It went out over **Selby Vose's** signature alone; Kyle typed it. It went to **nine families**: Vira Toland, Keddie, Roy and Hazel all say nine, against Orra's "eleven." Keddie would not mail it or leave it on the platform. He carried it round himself and stood at each door while the man read it. Two days later Emmet Brant stood in Keddie's yard and asked why he had carried it himself if he did not believe it. Emmet never entered the house again, and the families never spoke afterwards. Vira's father kept his copy in the desk drawer for forty years; Hazel's mother burned hers. The state inspector, **Strawn**, never found added water with his lactometer, and told the manager so. The same year Grigg reprinted his hay report.

In **January 1925** the patrons petitioned, with Emmet's name among them. A **board of arbitration of three** sat from **17 March to 4 May 1925**, chaired by the Ashlin attorney **Alonzo Frick**, chosen "because… kept no cow." It held **three sittings**. The first was at the Association office at Ashlin on **17 March**, where Keddie read his statement: "I have never taken a pound of cream… not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself." The second and third were held in April in the **station's own weigh room**, where the board saw the scales tried and a test made, with men standing outside in the rain.

The exhibits were "**four books and nothing else**":

- the test book, 4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924;
- the station receiving book;
- Emmet's weigh book;
- Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook.

Three explanations were argued: water (Vose), theft by the tester (Teague, by insinuation) and hay (the 1908 report). None was accepted. The crate of condemned glass lay in the loft directly over two of the sittings, and no one mentioned it.

The Association **dismissed Keddie on 30 April 1925**, four days before the award. On **4 May** the board "finds no cause established" and awarded **$3,800**, "without admission, to be charged to the station account." The award text and the arbitration minute book outweigh Vira's and Oram's $3,500. Split nine ways, it came to about $422 a family, less than the price of a hay rake for Vira's father. The patrons' own claim cannot be fixed: Frick gives 67,000 lb and Rundle $19,600. Vose called the award "a generous settlement of an unproved claim."

Keddie turned carpenter and whistled at the cow shed. He died in **February 1934** with the imputation still on him. At his funeral, men who had not spoken to him in nine years praised his hand with a door. He had once said that "a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name." Orra died in 1948.

**Documents:** r13 (Frick), r03 (Keddie), r24 (Orra), r01, r02, r08, r23, r09, r17, r21, r06, r05, r18.

### Story 8 — The crate: condemned in 1923, found in 1958

**Merle Strawn**, state dairy inspector on the Ordell circuit from **1907 to 1936**, kept 28 circuit books and was related to nobody in the valley. At every visit he checked the scales, the lactometer, the boiler, water and cold room, and watched a test at the tester's elbow. He was issued burettes in 1921; in 1922 he did not run the station's glass against his. On **12 June 1923** he did: "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate… Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them." [The 12 June date comes from the photostat and is confirmed by the reorder dated 28 June. It outweighs "12 July" (Vose, Rundle), "1921" (Hazel) and "1922" (Kyle).]

On **2 July 1923** Ammon & Sons acknowledged the order of 28 June, for six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles, adding: "Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot." The firm's works books burned in 1931. Junia Ammon, the firm's historian, guessed in 1949 that a reground mould had made the lot run consistently short by accident. The *Chronicle*'s charge that the lot was sold knowingly is unproven. The new glass was in use from **July 1923**, and the **1924** average was **3.80**, with Keddie still testing. Nobody connected the two facts.

The crate went up the ladder into the **loft over the intake room**, under a tarpaulin, stencilled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET." [Ivy, Duncan, Oram and Hazel put it there, against Nym's catalogue entry "found in the Association office at Ashlin."] In **February 1958** **Ivy Keddie** found it. She was Ansel's granddaughter, born in 1931, and a graduate student at Farland. Of the **72** pipettes bought, **nine** survived unbroken, two of them chipped at the tip. On **9 March 1958** at Farland she measured them against a calibrated burette. They delivered between 16.70 and 16.74 c.c., a **mean of 16.72 c.c.** against the 17.6 mark: **0.88 c.c. short, 95/100 of the mark**.

A 95% charge in a bottle ruled for 100% reads **95% of the truth**. So a true 3.80 reads **3.61**, a gap of **0.19**, and richer milk loses more: a recorded 4.56 was 0.24 short. Over 1898–1922 the shortfall comes to **76,000 lb of fat**, worth **$19,000** at 25¢; the award was one fifth of that. Ivy also found Strawn's 1923 notebook, untouched in the Farland library since 1947. **Bulletin 471** (October 1958, Vaught and Ivy Keddie) fixed the error at one part in twenty, running from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923. The *Ashlin Chronicle* ran "Old Ordell Wrong Righted" on 3 October 1958. By then the glass had been condemned 35 years, Keddie dismissed 33 and dead 24. In 1959 the crate and the test book went to the Historical Rooms.

**Documents:** r06 (Strawn), r04 (Ivy), r07, r11 (Vaught), r19, r20, r21, r22, r18, r17, r12.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** Ansel married Orra Brant in 1889. Their son **Duncan** was born in 1894, and Duncan's daughter **Ivy** was born in 1931. The sources for this are Ivy herself ("his granddaughter"), Duncan ("my daughter is Ivy… my father's granddaughter"), Hazel ("Ivy is Duncan's girl"), Oram ("the granddaughter"), Roy Brant, and Falke's correction. The only contrary claim is "**great-niece**": it appears in the *Chronicle* (3 October 1958) and in Falke's first draft, which evidently copied the newspaper. Those two are one source, not two, and Falke himself withdrew the claim ("the error on page two is mine"). Duncan says he had seen it printed wrong twice.

**B2. Ivy and Hazel were second cousins.** Emmet Brant and Orra Brant were siblings. Emmet's son **Roy** and Orra's son **Duncan** were therefore first cousins. Hazel is Roy's daughter and Ivy is Duncan's, so they are children of first cousins: **second cousins**. Roy, Duncan, Hazel and Rundle's 1959 footnote all agree. The "first cousin once removed" of Vira and Oram would need the two to stand in different generations, and they do not; it is valley talk, and the two of them are one strand. **What the sources leave open:**
- Whether Emmet and Orra were full or half siblings. Hazel says Orra was a half-sister "by his father's second wife." Roy says there was no stepmother, and Duncan says plainly "brother and sister." I accept full siblings, but if Hazel were right, the answer would be **half** second cousins.
- The mothers of Roy, Duncan, Hazel and Ivy, none of whom is named. So a second line of kinship cannot be excluded; Vira's "of the family both sides" hints at one, but nothing documents it.
- The ages of the intervening generations are only partly given: Roy was born about 1888, Duncan in 1894, Ivy in 1931, and Hazel was small in 1919.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose.** Gideon organized the Association in 1889, was its first president until 1911, and wrote the by-laws himself, including **Article VII**: pay by butterfat test, with the station's test final. It matters because the son spent a quarter-century as manager (1913–1938) administering his father's rule and defending it as beyond question. In 1926 he still invoked "my father… wrote the by-law" and his father's Jersey herd at 4.56. A manager with a filial stake in the rule's soundness looked for the fault in the patrons (the 1919 water circular) and never in the instrument the rule made final. He signed the annual station-gain line without reading it, and he let the award be booked "without admission." The "**nephew**" version (Vira, Rundle) is outweighed by Selby's own words and by the official record (Loomis, "son of the late president"), with Duncan and Kyle agreeing; Kyle calls the nephew story "nonsense."

**B4. No relationship between the Vose and Keddie families is established, and the best evidence says there was none.** Two claims point the other way:
- Vira says Orra Keddie and old Mrs Vose were first cousins, "known to all and written down nowhere."
- Oram says the families were "cousins of some sort," from his mother's mother, though he "could not draw you the line."

Both are unsourced valley talk of the same kind, so they count as one strand. Against them stands **Duncan Keddie**, Orra's son. He says it is talk that confuses the family with the unrelated Ashlin Voses, and that his mother, who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection," never once put a Vose in it. No document records any link. A negative cannot be strictly proven, but the claim is unsupported and the direct family evidence denies it.


# Answer Sheet — Part 3

## Section C — Math

**C1.**

**(a)** The pipettes delivered a mean of **16.72 c.c.** against a mark of **17.6 c.c.**, and 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95**. The bottle neck is ruled for a 17.6 c.c. charge, so the fat column read in the neck is proportional to the milk actually charged. Every recorded test was therefore **95% of the true test**: 5% of the truth low, or 1/0.95 − 1 = 5.26% below what should have been recorded.

**(b)** The true average was 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. The recorded tests were short by 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 point**. Four independent readings confirm 3.80:
- the borrowed glass in 1896;
- the borrowed measure in 1911;
- the new glass in 1924;
- Alder Corners, 1915–1930.

**(c)** The affected years are 1898–1922, with **40,000,000 lb** of milk.
- True fat: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb.
- Credited fat: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb.
- **Not credited: 76,000 lb** of butterfat.

**(d)** 76,000 lb × $0.25 = **$19,000**. The patrons received **$3,800**, which is 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **one fifth (20%)**. The 25¢ comes from the ledgers (Loomis, and Kyle, who worked it out for the arbitration), and $19,000 ÷ 76,000 = 25¢ confirms it. The 27¢ of Sill and Kepp is rejected.

**C2.** Emmet Brant, 1910: **200,000 lb** of milk, paid on a test of **3.42**.
- **(a)** Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of butterfat.
- **(b)** The true test was 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60, so the true fat was 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb. He was owed but not credited 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb**.
- **(c)** At Roy's 1910 price of **24¢**: 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40**. At the long-run 25¢ it would be $90.00, but the year-specific price is the one to use.

**C3.** Years 1898–1922.
- **(a)** Credited to the patrons: **1,444,000 lb**.
- **(b)** Reached Larrow Green in the station's cream: **1,520,000 lb**, all the fat actually delivered, because none was lost or stolen (the ledger's received side).
- **(c)** The difference is **76,000 lb**:
  - **5.26% (5¼%) of the fat credited**: 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 0.0526;
  - **5.00% of the fat received**, which is the fat actually delivered: 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000.

  The ledger's "five and a quarter per cent" is a percentage of the **credited** figure. Teague and Vaught's description of it as a percentage of "fat actually in the cream / delivered" uses the wrong base. Teague's 1898 entry checks the same way: 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26%, and 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.00%.

**C4.**

**(a)** The invoice total is **$77.93**. The line items add up to it: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93. The "$77.39" in the *Chronicle* and in Junia Ammon's prose is a transposition.

**(b)** The invoice bought **six dozen = 72 pipettes**, and 72 × $0.35 = $25.20. Five dozen would be $21.00, which does not match the invoice. Each pipette was **0.88 c.c. short** (17.6 − 16.72).

**C5.**

**(a)** **Time the faulty glass was in use:** from **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923**, which is **26 years and 42 days** (about 26 years 6 weeks).
- The **nine weeks** of 8 May to 10 July 1911 (63 days), when a borrowed measure was used, come out of that, leaving about 25 years 44 weeks of actual use.
- Keddie's 1897 figures are a part year, and 1896 was on borrowed glass.

**The money calculation** runs over **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922**, the 40,000,000 lb the ledgers cover. The two numbers differ because:
- 1897 (May to December) and 1923 (January to 12 June) are **partial years**, and the ledger and summary figures are kept by whole years.
- So the 76,000 lb omits the short-glass months of 1897 and 1923, and to that extent **understates** the loss. There are no intake figures for those months in the sources, so the omitted amount cannot be computed.
- The 1911 interval falls inside the 25 years. Since 76,000 lb is the actual ledger difference, those weeks are already reflected in it.

**(b) This cannot be determined from the sources.**
- Sill recalls "about 900,000 lb, May to year end."
- Kyle says "a good eleven hundred thousand," but only as hearsay from "men who were there," adding that "it is not in any book I ever ruled."
- Nym, cataloguing the records, notes that **no 1896 intake figure exists**, and Kyle explains why: that season was carried on the creamery's books and never filed with the station papers.

That leaves two unsupported memories that disagree by 200,000 lb and no document, so neither figure can be accepted.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment.**

**When and how long:** from **8 May to 10 July 1911, nine weeks**. The dates are Keddie's own dated entries: "Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure" and "Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ?" Nine weeks is the 63 days between them. The "six weeks" of Teague and Kepp is refuted by those dates.

**Variable:** only the pipette, the station's own short measure against a borrowed one. Ivy says the borrowed one came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon. Roy says the state man left his own; Strawn's June 1911 "Nothing here to note" weighs against that, and the source of the borrowed measure cannot be finally settled.

**Held constant:** everything else.
- the same tester and method;
- the same scales, bottles, acid and hand tester;
- the same patrons, herds, land and feed;
- the same stretch of season, since the return on 10 July was still in the summer flush.

**Result:** tests rose from 3.61 to **3.80** and fell back to 3.61 **on the day** the station's own measure returned.

**What it establishes that a calculation cannot:** the calculation shows only that a 95% pipette *would* produce the gap. The interval shows **causally** that the gap followed the measure and nothing else. That rules out water, theft (the same man made the tests) and seasonal feed (the drop happened in mid-summer). It also shows the true test was 3.80, and that the fault lay inside the station's own apparatus.

**What it does not establish:**
- By how much the station's glass was short; the interval gives the ratio of readings, not c.c.
- Whether the whole lot was short, rather than the one measure then in use.
- That the error ran from 1897 onward; it samples 1911 only.
- Why the glass was short: a mould error at Ammon's is a guess.
- Whether a small winter feed effect also existed; the interval covered summer only, so for that one needs Bulletin 214 and the flat year-round gap.
- Whether Keddie understood it: his "?" records a doubt, not a conclusion.

**D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream.**

1. **The cream arrived heavy.** Sill's receiving book at Larrow Green recorded more fat arriving than the sheets credited, every year from 1897, and Teague's ledger shows a **station gain** of 5¼% on the credited fat (1,520,000 lb received against 1,444,000 lb credited). Stolen cream would arrive light. (Sill's and Teague's figures probably share the same receiving data, so I count them as **one** reason. Rundle retracted her theft chapter on exactly this ground.)
2. **The 1911 interval** (Keddie's test book, with the pay confirmed in Roy's weigh book). The same man, under no new supervision, produced 3.80 tests for nine weeks on a borrowed measure. A thief does not raise his own tests.
3. **The 1924 recovery** (Strawn's circuit book, "book gives 3.80", and Keddie's 1924 average). With new glass from July 1923 and Keddie still testing until his dismissal in April 1925, the tests came right. They did not come right "when the man left," as Vira and Rundle believed.
4. **Alder Corners** (Rosalie Cudd). Over the ridge, on different glass in a different county, the same cows' milk tested about 3.80 across sixteen seasons. The milk was simply that good, and the station's lower reading belonged to the instrument, not the man.
5. **Watched tests.** Strawn watched a test at the tester's elbow at every visit ("slow and clean"); the 1908 committee watched it repeatedly; Frick's board watched it made in the weigh room. None saw anything amiss.

A further point of logic: taking cream after the sample is drawn does not lower a sample's test. A thief would have to write false tests *and* take exactly the difference, and then the ledger would show no gain.

**Why these are independent:** they rest on different records kept by different people for different purposes. They are the creamery's receiving side at Larrow Green; the station's own dated test book; the state inspector's notebook; and a cheese factory's book in another county whose owner never compared figures with the station. No one of them was derived from another.

**Reasons that hold without the 1958 measurement:** all of them. Most plainly the ledger's station gain, which was on the books every year from 1898, and the 1911 interval; both were in the room in 1925.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question the entries?** **No.** Teague wrote "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" every year and called it "furniture." At the hearing he nonetheless insinuated theft ("Cream is portable"; "two men or a lock"), though his own gain line refuted it. He says he raised the matter with Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone, but what he describes is suspicion of the station, not a query of the gain line. He called himself "the bookkeeper and not its conscience." Kyle, who sat outside the manager's door, says "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years." There is no record that he ever reconciled the gain with the accusation.

**D3. Did the manager know, before 1923, that the tests ran low?**

**Yes, he knew the tests ran low. There is no evidence he knew they were wrong.** Selby Vose took office in **1913**. By then the Hollow had been complaining since 1902, and the 1908 committee had reported on exactly that gap. His own **1919 circular** presupposes the low tests; it blames them on water. Strawn told him in 1919 that there was no added water. Strawn's 1913 entry notes the new manager coming out to the platform. Teague says he raised the matter with Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone.

What Vose did not grasp is that the tests were **false** rather than the milk poor. The proof was the station-gain line on the sheets he signed each year: more fat reached his own creamery than he paid for. But Kyle testifies that Vose "read the totals and he never read the lines above them," signing with his thumb on the bottom figure. Even in **1926**, with the condemnation behind him and the 1924 tests up, he still attributed the rise to water stopping "when the Association began to look." He called the condemnation "a piece of housekeeping," and he misdated it to 12 July.

So his knowledge of the lowness is certain. That he knew or suspected the instrument is unsupported, and his conduct argues against it. Whether his not reading the lines was negligence or wilful blindness cannot be determined from the sources.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. What produced the shortfall.** The testing outfit Ammon & Sons shipped to the station in April 1897 contained pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. that delivered only **16.72 c.c., 95% of the mark**. The fault was consistent across the lot; Junia Ammon's reground mould is a plausible guess, not proven. The Babcock bottle neck is ruled for a 17.6 c.c. charge. So every test made with that glass read **exactly 95% of the true fat**: true milk of 3.80 recorded as 3.61, and richer milk losing proportionally more (4.56 recorded was 0.24 short).

- **The size of the error** (0.19 point, 5%) is the pipette's shortfall: 0.88 c.c. out of 17.6.
- **Why it was flat all year:** it was proportional to the milk, not to the season or the feed.
- **Why it began when it did:** the error began when the outfit replaced the borrowed Larrow Green glass on **1 May 1897**. On borrowed glass, 1896 had averaged 3.80.
- **Why it ended when it did:** it ended when Strawn condemned the lot on **12 June 1923** and new glass arrived in July. The 1924 average was 3.80, with the same tester, farms and cows.
- **Why nothing else had to change:** the pipette is the one instrument a station cannot check against anything else it owns. The scales, acid, bottles, method and man were all correct, and the error was precise and invisible from inside the station. Only an outside burette could catch it.
- **Where the fat went:** it was never lost. It went down in the cream, and it appears in the ledger as the **station gain**: 5¼% of the fat credited, which is 5% of the fat received.
- **The one break:** the nine-week borrowed measure of 1911 is the experiment that confirms the whole account.

**The three competing explanations**
1. **Water in the milk.** Advanced by **Selby Vose**: the 1919 circular, the 1926 address and his case at the hearing; Sill agreed with it. Defeated because:
   - Strawn's lactometer never found added water ("no added water, as always here"), and he told Vose so in 1919;
   - Cudd's cheese would have shown watered milk within a day as soft curd, and he never docked the four men;
   - Emmet's own scale always agreed with the station's;
   - water cannot create the extra fat that reached Larrow Green;
   - the tests rose in 1911 and in July 1923 with no change on the farms.
2. **The tester stealing cream.** Advanced by **Wilbur Teague**, by insinuation at the 1925 hearing ("Cream is portable"). It is also the valley's "settled opinion" in Rundle's 1939 pamphlet, and the belief of Vira Toland and Oram's father. Defeated because:
   - the cream arrived **heavy**: Sill's receiving book and the ledger's 5¼% station gain;
   - the same man's tests rose on borrowed glass in 1911;
   - they rose again on new glass in 1924, before he was dismissed;
   - taking cream cannot lower the test of a sample.
3. **Wet bog hay.** Advanced by **Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee** (reprinted 1919) and **Dr. Roland Kepp** (1926); Falke conceded it and then withdrew. Defeated because:
   - Bulletin 214 found at most **0.05 in winter and nothing in summer**, about a quarter of the gap;
   - the gap was 0.19 in July as in February;
   - Alder Corners tested the same cows' summer milk at 3.80;
   - the 1911 drop came in mid-summer;
   - 1924 tested 3.80 on the same hay.

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware? No.** Its exhibits were "four books and nothing else" (Frick, Kyle): the test book, the receiving book, the Brant weigh book and Strawn's 1923 notebook. The crate lay in the loft over the room where two of the three sittings were held, and it was never mentioned (Loomis). "Books and appliances of the station" was a form of words, not a list. The board had the written record of the condemnation in front of it, in Strawn's notebook, but nobody "laid the second beside the fourth" (Kyle). It never looked at the glass.

**What did the tester believe? The sources do not settle it.**
- **Duncan**, his son, says that in summer 1911 Ansel came home saying it was "something in the glass," twice, but "never dared put it in the book." The contemporaneous "?" after "Tests are back where they were" (10 July 1911) supports a suspicion.
- **His own statement to the board** (17 March 1925): "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are." His unsent letter carefully notes the borrowed glass of 1896 and the 1911 episode without drawing a conclusion.
- **Frick** reports that Keddie told him privately in the corridor that he had always thought the patrons were at fault, and suspected his neighbours.

The best reading is that he suspected the glass in 1911 but could not prove it, never recorded more than a question mark, and by 1925 professed publicly not to know. Which belief he finally held cannot be determined; the one document in his own hand says only "?".

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** In 1897 the Larrow Valley creamery's Ostrey Hollow skimming station began using a $77.93 Ammon & Sons outfit whose pipettes delivered 16.72 c.c. instead of 17.6, so every butterfat test read 95% of the truth: 3.61 for 3.80. Paid by test under Article VII, the patrons were under-credited 76,000 lb of fat, about $19,000, over 1898–1922. Everyone blamed people: the manager blamed water, the bookkeeper hinted at theft, a committee blamed wet hay. A 1925 arbitration paid $3,800 without admission, and tester Ansel Keddie was dismissed. The proof lay scattered: Keddie's test book and its 1911 borrowed-measure weeks; the Brant weigh book; the Alder Corners cheese book, testing the same cows at 3.80; a ledger line showing 5¼% more fat arriving than credited; the inspector's 1923 condemnation; and the crate in the loft. In 1958 his granddaughter Ivy Keddie put them together.
