## The story these retellings share
The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (organized 1889, Larrow Green) pays patrons by
**butterfat test**, and by Article VII "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."
Its skimming station at **Ostrey Hollow**, seven miles up the valley, returned tests below the rest
of the valley for ~25 years. The retellings are eight witnesses with incompatible explanations;
Bulletin 471 (r11) supplies the answer: the station's **milk pipettes were short**.

## r09 — Roy Brant, Hessel Bottom farmer, farm-bureau account, 1955 (+ postscript 1958)
- [verified] Roy Brant, 67, still milking. Father **Emmet Brant**, b. 1854, d. 1929; kept the ledger
  from **1 January 1898** until the week before he died; Roy has continued it.
- Hessel Bottom: **160 acres** wet meadow under the hill at Ostrey Hollow; **six farms** had rights;
  Brant's was largest — sixty-odd acres of meadow, **38 cows** in a good year. Water stands into May.
- The ledger: two columns per delivery day — his own scale weight in his own barn, and the station's
  returned figure. **The two columns agreed** (barring a pound or two when a can slopped). So the
  weighing was never the problem. Physical book: front cover gone, back held by a strip of harness
  leather, first forty leaves stained brown from a leaky window.
- 1910 leaf, in Emmet's hand: *"Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own
  scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42."* Butterfat **24 cents** in 1910; about **25
  cents** over the whole stretch of the complaint.
- The gap: Hollow milk ran **seventeen hundredths (0.17)** under the same class of milk at Larrow
  Green — **the same in July as in February**, i.e. not seasonal. First said aloud in **1902** (Emmet
  one of them); they were laughed at.
- **1908**: Association committee under **Nyle Grigg** of Larrow Green blamed the wet hay / the land.
- **Nine weeks, summer 1911**: station's own measure broken; the **state man left his own measure that
  June**; tests rose for **every farm on the Bottom**, then fell back when the station's own returned.
  Emmet recorded both and could make nothing of it.
- **1919**: manager's circular to Hollow patrons warning against **watering** the milk; nine families
  received it. Emmet never forgave it; he and **Ansel Keddie** (his brother-in-law, who kept the
  station's test book) did not speak from 1919 — not over the water but because Keddie did not stand
  up against the circular.
- **From 1915** four Bottom men hauled over Redlow Ridge to the cheese factory (opened 1914) in the
  summer flush, sixteen seasons. Roy did not go: "the biggest herd is the one that cannot afford to be
  talked about" — he thinks that was a poor reason and cost money.
- Family: **Orra Keddie** was Emmet's full sister; her husband **Ansel** kept the test book;
  their son **Duncan Keddie** taught school at Ashlin (Roy's first cousin); Roy's daughter **Hazel**
  and Duncan's daughter **Ivy** are **second cousins**. Book goes to Hazel. 1958 postscript: **Ivy
  borrowed the book** for work at the college at Farland.

## r10 — Rosalie Cudd, Alder Corners Cheese Company, Vessey County, letter to Ivy Keddie, 1958
- [verified] Her father **Jerome Cudd** built the factory in **1913** (r12 says the company opened
  **1914** — conflict) on the flat below the church; died **March 1941** between setting and cutting a
  vat; she took over that spring, 17 years ago; she was 12 when the first vat was set.
- **Redlow Ridge**: four miles of switchback lumber road; shuts in November, does not open before
  April, some years May — so no Ordell milk ever came in winter. **Not possible**, whatever the price.
- The four Hessel Bottom men came **from 1915**, about **1 June to mid-September**, sixteen seasons,
  **last summer 1930**; rest of the year they went to Ostrey Hollow. Reasons: cheese paid better in
  flush, she bought hard in flush, shorter pull while the road was open. Nothing mysterious.
- Her father **tested himself**, own bench, own glassware, own book, and **never compared his figures
  with anyone else's** — "it never entered his head."
- Verbatim, end of summer **1919**: *"The four Hessel Bottom men, summer of 1919: 3.79, 3.81, 3.78,
  3.82. Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge."*
- **Table of 16 season averages (four men taken together), 1915–1930, all between 3.78 and 3.82**:
  1915 3.79, 1916 3.81, 1917 3.78, 1918 3.80, 1919 3.80, 1920 3.82, 1921 3.79, 1922 3.78, 1923 3.81,
  1924 3.80, 1925 3.79, 1926 3.82, 1927 3.78, 1928 3.80, 1929 3.81, 1930 3.79. Average ≈ **3.80**.
  Wet years and dry, nothing smoothed, no season thrown out.
- On watering: **"Cheese is a witness."** Watered milk shows within a day — curd comes soft, won't
  knit, slack body, yield down. In sixteen summers her father made **no complaint, docked no one a
  cent**, and only wished he had more of that milk in July.
- She knows nothing of the station's own figures and never saw them.

## r11 — Prof. Leland Vaught, Bulletin No. 471, State Agricultural College at Farland, Oct 1958
**The solution document.**
- The butterfat test: a measured charge of milk drawn by **pipette** into a test bottle, acid, spin,
  read the fat column **directly in percent**. The neck is ruled for one fixed charge — in this state
  **17.6 c.c.** If the pipette delivers short, the reading is low **in the exact proportion**.
- Three properties, the heart of the bulletin: the error is (1) **proportional, not additive** — herds
  still rank correctly against one another, so the station's books look perfectly orderly; (2)
  **precise** — the same short delivery every time, January and July, careful operator or careless, so
  it looks like excellent work; (3) **invisible from inside the plant** — scales, tester speed, acid
  and bottle graduations can all be checked, but *the pipette is the standard the plant measures with*,
  so it can only be caught by an outside **burette**, which reached field men very late.
- Material: of the lot bought for Ostrey Hollow in **1897** — **five dozen** pipettes purchased —
  **nine** were recovered from the condemned crate. Stems blown **17.6 c.c.**; five runs each against a
  calibrated burette; **mean delivery 16.27 c.c.**
- Station's own glass in use **1 May 1898** until the state inspector condemned the lot in **June 1923**.
- **1911 interval (§IV, which Vaught rates above all his arithmetic):** for **nine weeks** the station's
  own pipette was broken and a borrowed measure used. Same patrons, herds, pasture, season, acid,
  bottles, tester. **One variable changed.** Station average rose **3.61 → 3.80**, held nine weeks, and
  returned to **3.61** the day the station's own measure came back. Recorded contemporaneously by an
  operator who set a question mark against it; lay unread 47 years.
- Magnitude (§V): 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = **0.9244**; recorded average **3.61**; true 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = **3.905**;
  shortfall **0.295**; applied to **40,000,000 lb** of milk in **25 full delivery years** = **118,000 lb**
  of butterfat delivered and not credited. Vaught himself notes this "is not readily reconciled" with
  the station's own average of 3.80 the year after the glass was replaced.
- The same quantity sits on the Association's books as **"station gain," 5¼ percent** of the fat
  actually delivered.
- Bulletin No. 214 quoted: *"Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by
  not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on
  summer pasture."* The real discrepancy is present every month and ~six times the largest effect.
- **Conclusion, verbatim:** *"No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From
  the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in
  twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six
  thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as
  station gain."*

## r12 — Dell Rundle, schoolteacher, "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years," 1939 (repr. 1959)
- 1889: farmers meet at Larrow Green schoolhouse, organize the Association; somebody's horse got loose.
  **Gideon Vose** first president, wrote the by-laws himself; his nephew **Selby Vose** later manager.
  **1891**: creamery built at Larrow Green on the creek below the mill; valley paid cash monthly.
- **Article VII** (dates from **1891**), printed whole: paid by pounds of butterfat **ascertained by
  test**, not by weight of milk, *"and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."*
  Rundle: put in for convenience, and it is "the sentence on which the whole trouble turned."
- Ostrey Hollow station **opened 1896**, seven miles up; milk weighed, tested and separated on the
  spot; cream down to the Green, skim home the same morning. Plain boarded-and-battened building,
  intake room at the road end, an uncleared loft, creek behind that has twice come over the sill.
- Chapter IV: complaints from **1902**; 1908 Grigg committee (wet hay / the land); **1919** circular;
  the two ends of the valley did not speak civilly **for six years**. Valley's settled opinion: *cream
  left that station by the back door.*
- State inspector condemned the glassware **12 July 1923** "for reasons of his own." (r11 says **June**
  1923 / "twelfth of June" — conflict.) New glass put in.
- **January 1925** petition; station took in **44,000,000 lb** of milk over the years complained of;
  patrons' claimed loss **$19,600**, set out to the dollar. Board of arbitration at Ashlin that spring
  under **Alonzo Frick**, attorney; found nothing established, made an award without admitting anything.
- 1914: **Alder Corners Cheese Company** opened over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County by **Jerome Cudd**;
  resented in the valley. His daughter **Rosalie Cudd** makes the cheese there now.
- **1959 footnote:** the matter taken up at Farland by **Miss Ivy Keddie**, "second cousin to Hazel
  Brant"; **eleven** pipettes from the condemned crate found still in existence (r11 says **nine**
  recovered — conflict).
- **NOTE ADDED WHILE IN PRESS — the retraction.** From the Association's ledgers, the yearly station
  account entry: *"Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, **57,760 lb**. Butter
  fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, **60,800 lb**. Station gain, **3,040 lb, five and a
  quarter per cent, as usual.** Carried to the general account."* Rundle: "Cream stolen at the Hollow
  would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my
  paragraph stand." He leaves Chapter IV as written and corrects it here.

## r13 — Alonzo Frick, Ordell County Bar Association yearbook, 1946
- Frick, 74, chairman of the 1925 board — named, he supposes, because he was the one of the three who
  **kept no cow**. Forty years of titles, boundaries and two homicides; this stayed with him most.
- States the patrons had been paid on an average test of **3.16** (r11 says **3.61** — one of these is
  a transposition; r11's 3.61 is the one that fits the 1911 rise to 3.80 and the 5% station gain).
- **January 1925** petition; by-laws provided for arbitration; officers did not resist it. Board sat
  **three times**: first at the Association's office at **Ashlin, 17 March 1925**; the second and third
  Frick moved to **the station's own weigh room** so the board could see the plant. They saw the scales
  tried and the test made; Frick says plainly he did not understand the test then and does not now —
  "I am a lawyer and the thing is chemistry."
- **The three explanations, and who put each:** (1) **Water** — the manager, **Selby Vose**, who had
  circulated the 1919 warning; put without heat and without proof. (2) **The tester stealing cream and
  writing low tests to cover it** — insinuated by the bookkeeper, **Wilbur Teague**, who answered at
  length about how such an account would look and then said he made no charge against anybody.
  (3) **The hay** — the 1908 report read into the record; "it had the advantage of blaming nobody in
  the room."
- **Ansel Keddie** had tested at the station since it opened in **1896**, kept the book in his own hand
  every season; statement 17 March: he had tested as he was taught and as the state man had seen him
  do it, **thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons**, could not say why the figures were what
  they were, and had never taken a pound of cream. Frick believed him: "a man who cannot account for a
  figure is presumed by everybody in the room to be the figure's author."
- **Not in the minutes:** in the corridor at Ashlin, Keddie told Frick privately that **he had always
  thought the patrons were at fault** and would not say so in the room. So it is true he accused
  nobody publicly, false that he suspected nobody.
- **30 April 1925**: the Association **dismissed Keddie**, four days before the award, with no finding
  against him from the board. Frick has never been able to think well of them for it.
- **4 May 1925**: no cause established; award of **$3,800** to the Hollow patrons, without admission,
  charged to the station account. The patrons' own reckoning of the loss was **67,000 lb of butterfat**,
  never disturbed.
- **Exhibits (four books, reprinted from his file):** 1. the station's test book, **4 May 1896 to 31
  December 1924**, in Keddie's hand; 2. the station's receiving book; 3. **the private weigh book of
  Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom, begun 1 January 1897** (r09 says 1 January **1898** — conflict);
  4. the circuit notebook of the state dairy inspector for **1923**.
- Reflection: three accounts, each urged by a man of standing, none proved; he wonders whether the
  answer was in the room and in none of the three stories, and whether "a lawyer who understood
  chemistry might have found it."

## r14 — Hobart Sill, buttermaker (ret.), remarks at Larrow Green, 1934
**The most under-used witness besides r10.**
- Came in **1893**, 41 years at the churn, aged **64**; when he came there was no floor but plank and
  they cooled with creek ice.
- The cream wagon came down daily but Sunday. **"That cream ran heavy. Every year it ran heavy. There
  was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there... Always more. Never less."**
  He weighed it himself again into the cold room and his hand is on every page of the receiving book
  from '93. He explained it away to himself as "cream is not milk and it will not stand still and be
  counted," and **nobody ever asked him about it**.
- **Larrow Green's own patrons ran 3.78**, one year with another, through those years.
- His verdict on the Hollow: **"The Hollow milk was really a 3.85 and no better"** — good cows, wet
  ground — so if they were paid under that, "somebody up that hill road was helping the cans along."
  He believed there was **water** in the cans and said so at the time.
- Recalls that "one summer along in the twenties those figures came up and they stayed up, and nothing
  on this earth changed but the season" — he read it as somebody being stopped or spoken to. (Fits the
  **1923 replacement of the condemned glassware**, not a confession.)
- 1902 first he remembers of the hollering (a man shouting his milk tested under his brother-in-law's);
  1919 circular, which he thought well of — "a paper never emptied a can."
- Butterfat **27 cents** the pound through those years, one year with another.
- **The crucial fact nobody else supplies:** when the station opened — **May 1896** — **it had no outfit
  of its own**; the order had not come and there was talk of holding off the season. Sill **made up an
  outfit out of his own glass** from his own bench and sent it up the valley; **the station opened on
  Sill's glass. Their own came the spring after**, and his was sent back with one piece chipped.
- First season (May to end of year) the station took in about **900,000 lb** of milk.
- He went up there twice in his life. Closing request: "Put in that Hobart Sill made the butter and it
  was good butter... Do not put in that I said anything against the cows up there."

## r15 — Nyle Grigg, 1919 covering letter to the reprinted 1908 committee report
- Grigg reissues the report because the question was reopened "by men who were boys when we made it,"
  and has "nothing in it to take back."
- Says payment by test came in with **the 1893 meeting** "as I have always understood it" (r12: Article
  VII dates from **1891** — conflict).
- Declares his own disinterest: **not a patron of that station**, hauls to Larrow Green, chosen for
  that reason; abused by both sides, which he takes as a sign of honesty.
- **His letter claims** the committee sampled "the whole of 1908 — every month of that year, in the
  barn and on the pasture, out of the night's milk and the morning's," unannounced. **The report he
  reprints below it says: "We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and
  March."** The letter inflates a winter-only sample into a year-round one.
- **He cites Bulletin No. 214 as finding bog hay depressed the test "by two tenths in the winter
  feeding"** — r11 quotes the bulletin as **not more than five hundredths in winter and nothing
  measurable in summer**. Grigg treats the College as independent confirmation "arrived at by another
  road and by better men."
- The 1908 report itself: Hessel Bottom described as springy, part under water each spring, herbage
  sedge/rush/coarse sour grasses, hay cut late and the winter dependence Nov–April. They visited each
  of the six farms (some three or four times), drew and sealed their own samples, watched the milking,
  the hauling, the weighing and the test, **tried the station scales against a known weight and the
  patrons' scales against the station's**, and inquired into each barn's water supply. Herds thin in
  the flank at the end of winter, rough coats, "do well enough in flow."
  **"We report that we found no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter"** — asked to be printed in the
  same type. Finding: *the milk is whole and the scales are honest; the wet hay of that bottom is what
  thins the cream, and the remedy lies with the land and not with the station.*
- Grigg's remedy: drain, sweeten, keep stock off in spring, feed the coarse hay in a mixed ration.

## r16 — Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S., of Ashlin, paper to the Ordell County Farm Club, 1926
- Thirty years' practice; "the answer to nine cases in ten is under the animal's feet."
- Theory: a wet bottom is a distinct **acid soil, poor in lime and phosphates**; sweet grasses crowded
  out by sedge, rush and tussock; herbage carries bulk and water, not minerals; cut late, which takes
  out what little was there. The cow **robs her own frame** for minerals: thin over the loin, rough
  coat, slow to spring condition, autumn digestive trouble, breeds later, worse feet. Wet land is
  generous of flow, but **fat is the first economy the cow practices**.
- His field figure: wet bottoms yield milk poorer in fat **by a fifth of a point (0.20)**, farm for
  farm, independent of breed, man or barn.
- **He gives the Ostrey Hollow gap as twenty-one hundredths, 0.21** (r09 says 0.17). He says the
  patrons' complaint "is perfectly just and I have never doubted it."
- **Cites Bulletin No. 214 as finding the test depressed "by two tenths in the winter feeding"** — same
  misquotation as Grigg — and says his 0.20 field figure, the 0.21 gap and the College's "two tenths"
  agree so nearly that he does not know what more a reasonable man requires.
- **His handling of 1911 (the key self-refutation):** he calls it **six weeks** (r09/r11: **nine
  weeks**) and dismisses it as "six weeks of June and July, when every herd in the county is at its
  best and the stock are off the wet ground." But by his own theory and by Bulletin 214 the bog-hay
  effect is a **winter-feeding** effect — so summer is precisely when his explanation predicts **no**
  gap, and yet the gap was there every summer except the nine weeks of borrowed glass.
- Remedy: drain, open old ditches and cut new, lime twice, sow down, keep stock off in wet spring,
  never feed that hay alone — mixed ration plus mineral supplement. Fat has been worth **27 cents**.
- **Discussion:** asked whether the cows rather than the land were at fault, he rejected it (well-bred
  cattle stand on wet land; indifferent cattle on hills test well). Asked whether the trouble might lie
  **in the station**, he replied he had no interest and no competence in that quarrel, **"that he had
  never in his life read a test and would not know how,"** and that once a cause is found in the soil
  it is not good sense to look for a second cause "in a building."

## Contradicted claims (proven false — do not relearn)
- **False: the Ostrey Hollow milk was watered.** Ruled out three ways — Rosalie Cudd's cheese vat would
  have shown it within a day for sixteen summers (it never did); the 1908 committee found the milk
  whole; Vaught's conclusion states it outright.
- **False: cream was stolen at the station.** Rundle's own note in press kills it: the cream reached
  Larrow Green **heavy**, not light — 60,800 lb received against 57,760 lb credited, every year.
  Sill independently confirms it ran heavy every year. Theft makes cream arrive light.
- **False: the wet hay / the land thinned the cream.** Bulletin 214 (as quoted by Vaught) found ≤0.05
  in winter and nothing in summer; the gap was constant in July and February; and the 1911 nine weeks
  changed the figures with the land, herds, season and feed all unaltered. Grigg (1919) and Kepp (1926)
  both cite Bulletin 214 as "two tenths" — that quotation is wrong.
- **False: the weighing or the scales were at fault.** Emmet Brant's two columns agreed for thirty
  years; the 1908 committee tried both sets of scales; the arbitration board saw the scales tried.
- **False: Ansel Keddie was at fault.** He was dismissed 30 April 1925 with no finding against him,
  four days before an award that established no cause. His error was that he could not explain a figure.

## Useful inference for the next session (flagged as inference, not stated in any document)
- The evidence converges on a shortfall of **one part in twenty (5%)**: 3.61 → 3.80 in 1911; Alder
  Corners' independent 3.80 for the same cows; Larrow Green's own 3.78; the ledger's 57,760 / 60,800 =
  exactly 0.95. **Vaught's own §V arithmetic does not fit this**: 16.27 / 17.6 = 0.9244 (~7.6% short)
  gives a true test of 3.905 and 118,000 lb, whereas his conclusion says one part in twenty and
  **76,000 lb**. His §V and §VII contradict each other, and §V is the one at odds with every
  independent measurement. His start date is likewise given twice — **1 May 1898** in §III and
  **1 May 1897** in the conclusion; Sill (r14) independently fixes the station's own glass as arriving
  "the spring after" its May 1896 opening, and the first season ran on **Sill's borrowed glass**, which
  is why the shortfall cannot have begun in 1896.
