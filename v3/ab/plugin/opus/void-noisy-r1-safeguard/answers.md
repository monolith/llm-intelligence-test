### Story 1 — Article VII, and the Building of a Station Seven Miles Up (1889–1896)

In 1889 the farmers of the Larrow Valley met at the Larrow Green schoolhouse and organized the
Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. **Gideon Vose** was its first president and wrote
the by-laws himself. In **1891** the creamery was built at Larrow Green, on the creek below the
mill, and the valley was paid cash monthly.

The by-laws contained the sentence on which everything afterward turned. **Article VII**: a patron
is paid according to the pounds of butterfat his milk is found **by test** to contain, and not
according to the weight of his milk — *"and the test of the station where he delivers shall
govern."* Dell Rundle says it dates from 1891 and that it was put in for convenience; Nyle Grigg
says payment by test came in with an 1893 meeting; Orin Falke, who tried to check, found the
printed by-laws undated on their face and the Association's first minute book lost in an office
removal, and declined to state a year. 1891 is the best-supported date and is not settled. What is
settled is Falke's point: **under payment by weight, nothing that follows would have cost any man a
dollar.**

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a **skimming station at Ostrey Hollow**, seven miles
up the valley, chiefly to serve the six farms of Hessel Bottom. It was a plain boarded-and-battened
building of two floors: a covered platform at the road end about waist high, so cans could be
rolled off level; inside it the intake or weigh room, with the scale, the vat and the separator;
over that an uncleared **loft**, reached not by a stair but by a **ladder nailed to the wall**, with
a plank trap and a rope on it; a creek behind that has twice come over the sill. Milk was weighed,
sampled, tested and separated on the spot; the cream went down to Larrow Green, the skim back out
the same platform for the farmer's hogs.

It **opened on 4 May 1896** — the date the station test book begins. **Ansel Keddie** was tester and
separator man from the opening and held the post twenty-nine seasons.

One fact only Hobart Sill supplies: the station's ordered outfit **had not come**, and there was
talk of holding off the season. Sill, the buttermaker at Larrow Green, made up an outfit out of his
own glass from his own bench and sent it up the valley. **The station opened on Sill's glass**; his
own came back the spring after with one piece chipped. That first season the Hollow took in
roughly 900,000 lb of milk, carried on the creamery's own books; the sheets were never put with the
station papers. **Selby Vose**, Gideon's son, became manager in 1911.

*Bears on it:* r12 (Rundle), r22 (Falke), r14 (Sill), r13 (Frick), r15 (Grigg), r23 (Kyle),
r21 (Oram), r18 (Duncan Keddie).

### Story 2 — The Lot of Glass from Tarnet (1897–1923)

Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet, sold half the glass in the state's creameries. Their own
historian reproduces the invoice entire, dated **14 April 1897**, sold to the Larrow Valley Co-op.
Creamery Assn. for the Ostrey Hollow station:

> 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20 · 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at
> .22 — 31.68 · 1 acid measure — .90 · 1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00 · Freight — 2.15 ·
> **Total — 77.93**

The outfit went into use **1 May 1897**. Orra Keddie's diary for that day: *"A. says the station's
own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green. He is pleased about it."*

A butterfat test draws a measured charge of milk by **pipette** into a bottle whose graduated neck
is ruled for one fixed charge — in this state **17.6 c.c.** — and is read directly in percent. If
the pipette delivers short, the reading is low **in exactly that proportion**. This lot delivered
short by **one part in twenty**, about **16.72 c.c.** (see C1: the bulletin's printed 16.27 cannot be
reconciled with its own conclusion or with any independent measurement).

Junia Ammon's account of the cause is the only one offered and is credible: the mold had been
reground and the lot ran short; a gauge hangs on a nail and gets knocked off it; a boy checks the
first of a lot, the tenth and the fiftieth and finds them consistent — *"they are consistently
wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind cannot find."* The works books that would have
settled it **burned in 1931**.

On **12 June 1923**, Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector on that circuit, ran the station's
pipettes against a calibrated **burette**, found they would not deliver their mark, condemned the
whole lot on the spot and told the manager to order new. The Association wrote to Tarnet on 28
June; the firm answered 2 July, entered the order, regretted the trouble, and said its books of the
nineties would tell nobody anything about that lot. New glass was in use the following month (Orra,
July 1923: *"new glass at the station and new pails, and the roof is still bad"*), and **from 1924
the station's average test stood at 3.85.**

Nobody connected the one thing with the other. Strawn drew no inference as to the years behind it;
Kyle entered the reorder as he would a broken window. The condemned crate went up the ladder into
the loft and stayed there thirty-five years — seventy-two pipettes bought, breakage leaving about
five dozen crated in 1923 and **nine** by 1958.

*Bears on it:* r20 (Ammon), r24 (Orra), r11 (Vaught), r22 (Falke), r23 (Kyle), r19 (Chronicle),
r21 (Oram), r17 (Hazel).

### Story 3 — Hessel Bottom, and the Book with Two Columns (1897–1963)

Hessel Bottom is 160 acres of wet meadow under the hill at Ostrey Hollow; six farms had rights on
it and no more; the water stands into May, you could cut hay off it in a dry August and lose a
horse in a wet one, and a spring at the upper end ran cold enough in July to ache your wrist —
which is where the cans stood. **Emmet Brant** (b. 1854, d. spring 1929) had the largest piece,
sixty-odd acres of meadow and thirty-eight cows in a good year, and hauled the most milk into the
station of any man on the creek.

On **1 January 1897** he began a weigh book: a tall narrow account book, canvas over board, the
spine gone soft on the left where he carried it under his arm, the front cover afterward lost, the
back held by a strip of harness leather, the first forty leaves stained brown from a leaky window.
He kept it in one way for thirty-two years: **two columns for every delivery day — his own scale
weight, taken in his own barn before a can went on the wagon, and the station's returned figure.**
If the two disagreed he would stand in the doorway of the weigh room with his hat on until somebody
found out why. **They agreed**, barring a pound or two when a can slopped.

That agreement is the most useful negative fact in the whole history: **the weighing was never the
problem**, and every investigation that spent its time on scales — the 1908 committee, which tried
the station scales against a known weight and the patrons' scales against the station's, and the
1925 board, which watched the scales tried — was checking the one thing that was right.

The 1910 leaf, in Emmet's hand and the one page his granddaughter can recite: *"Delivered to the
station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of
3.42."* Butterfat was 24 cents that year. He was credited with 6,840 lb of fat; the milk actually
carried 7,200; the difference is 360 lb and $86.40, in one year, on one farm, on a page nobody set
against anything for fifty years.

From 1915, four of the six Bottom men hauled over Redlow Ridge in the summer flush. Roy Brant did
not: *"the biggest herd is the one that cannot afford to be talked about"* — which he later called
a poor reason that cost him money. Emmet kept the book until the week before he died; Roy continued
it identically; Roy **lent** it to Ivy Keddie in 1958 and had it back — his daughter Hazel is
emphatic that he lent it and did not leave it, and Duncan Keddie says the same word. At Roy's death
in 1962 it went to Hazel, who gave it to the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1963, *"where anybody
may look at it who wants to, which is nobody."*

*Bears on it:* r09 (Roy), r17 (Hazel), r18 (Duncan), r13 (Frick), r10 (Cudd), r22 (Falke).

### Story 4 — Twenty-Five Years of Complaint, and Three Explanations (1902–1926)

The first complaint of record is **1902**: talk at the store that the milk up the Hollow ran poorer
than the milk down the valley. Emmet Brant was one of the men who said it aloud and they were
laughed at; Ansel Keddie came home and would not eat, and said, *"it is not my cows and not my
hands, but it is my figures they will look at."* The complaint was renewed in one form or another
in every decade for twenty-five years.

The gap was **nineteen hundredths of a point** — the station averaged **3.61** where Larrow Green's
own patrons averaged 3.78 and the same Hessel Bottom herds averaged 3.80 over the ridge — and it ran
at the same nineteen hundredths **in every month of the year, in the flush of June and the depth of
February alike**. (Roy Brant remembered 0.17 and Dr. Kepp asserted 0.21; the arithmetic gives 0.19.)

**Explanation one: water in the milk.** The manager, Selby Vose, circulated a warning to the
Ostrey Hollow patrons in **June 1919** over his own name; Kyle typed it. Ansel Keddie would not put
them in the mail nor leave them on the platform: he took them round himself, **to eleven families**,
and stood at the door while each man read it, and came home at eleven at night with his hat still
on. Two days later Emmet Brant stood in his yard and asked why he had carried the paper himself if
he did not believe it; Ansel said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it.
Emmet never entered that house again, and he was Orra's brother.

**Explanation two: the wet hay of the bottom.** In January 1908 four men came up in Nyle Grigg's
sleigh, went to Emmet's first, being the biggest, and *"wanted to see the barns and not the
station."* They visited each of the six farms, drew and sealed their own samples, watched the
milking, hauling, weighing and the test, tried both sets of scales, and inquired into each barn's
water supply. They reported *"no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter"* and asked that the
sentence be printed in the same type — and found that the milk is whole, the scales honest, and the
wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, the remedy lying with the land and not the station.
Grigg reissued the report in 1919 with a covering letter claiming they had sampled every month of
1908; the report beneath his letter says January, February and March. **Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S.,**
gave the theory its most systematic form in 1926 — acid soil poor in lime and phosphate, sedge and
rush crowding the sweet grasses, the cow robbing her own frame, *"fat is the first economy the cow
practices,"* wet bottoms poorer by a fifth of a point farm for farm. Both Grigg and Kepp cite
Bulletin No. 214 as finding the test depressed *"by two tenths in the winter feeding."* It says
nothing of the kind.

**Explanation three: cream stolen at the station.** This was the valley's settled opinion, never
put in a document until it was insinuated at the 1925 hearing by the Association's own bookkeeper.

*Bears on it:* r24 (Orra), r15 (Grigg), r16 (Kepp), r09, r17, r12, r22, r23, r13.

## Section A — Reconstruction (stories 5–8)

### Story 5 — The Nine Weeks of 1911

On **8 May 1911** the station's own measuring glass broke. Orra Keddie's diary that day, with no
idea what she was recording: *"A. home cross about a broken measure. Rain all day, the low piece
under water again."* A measure was borrowed — the state man left his own that June — and the
station tested on it until **10 July**. Nine weeks.

Through those nine weeks the station's average test **rose from 3.61 to 3.80**, and the tests rose
for **every farm on the Bottom**, not for some. They held there nine weeks. On the day the
station's own measure came back they **fell to 3.61 again**. Roy Brant's father recorded both
columns through the whole interval and could make nothing of it. Ansel Keddie, who made every one
of those tests, set a **question mark** against the figures in the station's test book. It lay
unread for forty-seven years, and Professor Vaught, when he found it, rated the interval above all
his own arithmetic.

The interval is an accidental controlled experiment. **The variable was the measuring glass and
nothing else.** Held constant: the same patrons, the same herds, the same pasture, the same feed,
the same season, the same acid, the same test bottles, the same hand tester, the same scales, the
same building, the same tester working by the same method under no different supervision. What it
establishes that no calculation could is **location**: the cause lay inside the station's own
measuring glass, and this can be known without anyone measuring the glass, without any theory of
pipettes, and without trusting Vaught's arithmetic. It also falls in June and July — the months in
which the wet-hay theory predicts no gap at all.

Duncan Keddie, then seventeen, supplies what the book does not. His father **came home that summer
saying it was something in the glass** — said it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was
not his way. He would not write down what he could not prove; against a suspicion about a piece of
glass stood the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county.
So he kept on for another fourteen years and wrote down every figure the glass gave him, and the
figures were what convicted him. Selby Vose became manager that same year.

*Bears on it:* r24, r18, r11, r09, r22, r16 (Kepp, who calls it six weeks and dismisses it).

### Story 6 — The Cream That Ran Heavy, and the Cheese Over the Ridge (1893–1939)

Three bodies of evidence answered the question years before it was asked, and each sat unconsulted.

**Hobart Sill** stood at the churn at Larrow Green from 1893 for forty-one years and took in the
Hollow cream off the wagon daily but Sunday. *"That cream ran heavy. Every year it ran heavy. There
was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there... Always more. Never less."*
He weighed it himself into the cold room; his hand is on every page of the receiving book from '93.
He explained it away to himself — *"cream is not milk and it will not stand still and be counted"* —
and **nobody ever asked him about it** in forty-one years. He believed to the end that there was
water in the cans.

**The ledger line.** Every year the Ostrey Hollow station account carried three figures and a
sentence: fat credited to patrons on the station test, **57,760 lb**; fat received at Larrow Green
in the station cream, **60,800 lb**; **station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as
usual, carried to the general account.** Wilbur Teague wrote it; Bertram Kyle copied it in a fair
hand for a quarter of a century; it was read out at the annual meeting to fifty farmers with their
hats on their knees, and stood on an open shelf in a room anybody might walk into. Kyle's verdict is
the best sentence in the sources: *"It was not a secret. It was worse than a secret. It was in plain
sight with a name on it."* A figure with a name on it has been explained, and you rule under it and
go on. **Dell Rundle**, whose 1939 history had set down the valley's opinion that cream left that
station by the back door, found the entry while his book was in press and printed the retraction
rather than the paragraph: cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green **light**; it
reached the Green **heavy**, every year.

**The cheese factory.** Jerome Cudd built the Alder Corners Cheese Company in Vessey County in 1913
(opened 1914). From 1915 four Hessel Bottom men hauled to it from about 1 June to mid-September for
**sixteen seasons**, ending in 1930, and went back to Ostrey Hollow the rest of the year — Redlow
Ridge is four miles of switchback that shuts in November and does not open before April, so the
"secret winter market" the valley imagined was physically impossible. Cudd tested himself, on his
own bench and his own glassware, in his own book, and **never once compared his figures with
anybody's**. His sixteen season averages for the four men together run 3.78 to 3.82 and average
**3.80** — wet years and dry, no season thrown out. And *"cheese is a witness"*: watered milk shows
in the vat within a day, the curd soft, the body slack, the yield down. In sixteen summers he made
no complaint, docked no one a cent, and only wished he had more of that milk in July.

*Bears on it:* r14 (Sill), r23 (Kyle), r12 (Rundle), r10 (Cudd).

### Story 7 — The Arbitration, and the Ruin of Ansel Keddie (1925–1934)

In **January 1925** the Hollow patrons petitioned. Emmet Brant's name was on it; his sister did not
blame him and did not say so to her husband. They claimed **67,000 lb** of butterfat and **$19,600**,
set out to the dollar. The by-laws provided for arbitration and the officers did not resist it.

The board sat under **Alonzo Frick**, attorney of Ashlin, chairman — he supposed — because he was
the one of the three who kept no cow. It sat **three times**: first at the Association's office at
Ashlin on **17 March 1925**, and then twice, at Frick's direction, **in the station's own weigh
room**, so the board could see the plant, with men standing outside in the road in the rain. They
watched the scales tried and a test made. Frick says plainly he did not understand the test then and
does not now: *"I am a lawyer and the thing is chemistry."*

Four books came before it, carried in and out by Kyle, who made the list and checked it twice: the
station test book, 4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924, in Keddie's hand; the station receiving book;
Emmet Brant's private weigh book, lent and wanted back; and **the state dairy inspector's circuit
notebook for 1923 — the year he condemned the glass.** No man in that room laid the second beside
the fourth.

All three explanations were argued. Water, by the manager **Selby Vose**, without heat and without
proof. Theft by the tester, insinuated by the bookkeeper **Wilbur Teague**, who answered at length
about how such an account would look and then said he made no charge against anybody. The hay, by
reading the 1908 report into the record — which, as Frick observed, had the advantage of blaming
nobody in the room.

Keddie's statement of 17 March: he had tested as he was taught and as the state man had seen him do
it, **thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons**, could not say why the figures were what
they were, and had never taken a pound of cream. Frick believed him, and drew the lesson that ruined
him: *"a man who cannot account for a figure is presumed by everybody in the room to be the
figure's author."* Not in the minutes: in the corridor at Ashlin, Keddie told Frick privately that
he had always thought the patrons at fault and would not say so in the room.

On **30 April 1925** the Association dismissed him — twenty-nine seasons and a letter. He came up
the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm, hung it behind the shed door, and
asked what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it. **Four days later**, on 4 May, the board
found no cause established and awarded the patrons **$3,800 without admission**, charged to the
station account. Frick has never been able to think well of them for the order of those two dates.

Afterward he kept four or five cows on ground too small for them and did carpentry, from light to
dark, whistling, which he never did when he was paid — a carpenter at sixty-six at a quarter of the
money. He died in February 1934. More came to the burying than his widow looked for, and men shook
her hand who had not spoken to him in nine years, and every one of them said what a hand he was with
a door, and **not one of them said the other thing**. He had told her once: *a man cannot be cleared
of a thing nobody will name.*

*Bears on it:* r13 (Frick), r24 (Orra), r18 (Duncan), r21 (Oram), r22, r23, r12.

### Story 8 — The Ladder, the Crate, and Bulletin 471 (1958–1970)

In **February 1958** **Ivy Keddie** — twenty-seven, Ansel's granddaughter, a graduate student in
dairy husbandry at the State Agricultural College at Farland, Duncan's girl, raised in Ashlin —
went up the nailed ladder into the loft over the station's intake room and found the crate under a
tarpaulin and an inch of chaff, where it had lain since the day the glass was condemned. Twenty
feet of ladder, thirty-five years, and two of the three sittings of the arbitration held in the
room underneath it.

She carried it east. On **9 March 1958** she measured the **nine surviving pipettes** against a
calibrated burette at Farland. Every one was blown on the stem **17.6 c.c.**; not one delivered it;
and they were short **in the same proportion, one to another**.

She built the rest of the case out of records nobody had asked for. Roy Brant lent her his father's
weigh book — she sat in the kitchen in her coat and asked for the loan of it. Rosalie Cudd, a
stranger, had her father's sixteen seasons copied out and sent them. Merle Strawn's circuit
notebooks, transferred to the college library in 1947 and untouched since, gave her the volume for
1923. The station test book gave her Ansel's question mark of 1911.

**Bulletin No. 471**, October 1958, under Professor Leland Vaught's name and hers, states the three
properties that hid the fault for a generation: the error is **proportional, not additive**, so
herds still rank correctly and the books look orderly; it is **precise**, the same short delivery in
January and July, careful operator or careless, so it looks like excellent work; and it is
**invisible from inside the plant**, because the pipette *is* the standard the plant measures
everything else with, and only an outside burette can catch it. Its conclusion: *"No milk was
watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there... the station's tests were low by one part
in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six
thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as
station gain."*

The county read it through two filters. The **Ashlin Chronicle** of 3 October 1958, made in a day
and a half from a press release and two telephone calls, got the substance right and much else
wrong. **Orin Falke's** 1959 report to the county board conceded the wet hay as a partial factor,
then wrote to Farland for Bulletin 214, printed its summary entire, and struck the concession out:
*"Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July. I
withdraw the concession made on page four."* Rundle's 1959 reprint carried his own retraction.

The papers dispersed: the test book and the crate to the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1959, the
weigh book in 1963, Mrs. Cudd's test book to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970. Duncan's
last line: the five books are now in three buildings and are none of them ours. Ivy's: *"The glass
was short and Ansel Keddie was not... I would like it printed that he never knew. It would have been
a kindness if somebody in this county had thought to climb a ladder."*

*Bears on it:* r11, r19, r22, r17, r18, r21, r12, r10.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.**
The derivation is direct and comes from the intervening generation itself: Duncan Keddie writes,
"My father was Ansel Keddie... I am Duncan, born in 1894, their son... My daughter is Ivy, born in
1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter" — and he says he writes it plainly *because he has
twice seen it printed otherwise*. Ansel → Duncan → Ivy. Two sources point elsewhere and both say
"great-niece": the *Ashlin Chronicle* (r19) and page two of Falke's report (r22). They are **not
independent** — Falke was working from the same October 1958 bulletin notice the paper worked from —
and Falke destroys his own version in a note at the end: "I am informed, and have verified, that
Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." Hazel
Brant and Cleve Oram independently call her the granddaughter. A man stating his own daughter's
descent outranks a weekly paper's press-release gloss; and the one source that checked, corrected
itself.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins.**
Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie were brother and sister. Their children — Emmet's son Roy Brant and
Orra's son Duncan Keddie — are therefore **first cousins** (the children of a brother and a sister,
which is the whole definition). Roy's daughter is Hazel; Duncan's daughter is Ivy; the children of
first cousins are **second cousins**. Three narrators derive it the same way and two of them are the
fathers concerned (Roy in r09, Duncan in r18, Hazel in r17: "People are forever telling me she is my
niece or my cousin once removed... It is not complicated; it is only that nobody wants to count").
Cleve Oram's "first cousin once removed" (r21) is village talk about a family not his own, and
once-removed is a relation across generations, which these two are not. **What the sources leave
open in the intervening generation:** whether Orra was Emmet's *full* sister or his *half* sister.
Roy called her his father's full sister; Duncan calls her "Emmet Brant's sister, the younger of the
two"; Hazel says she was a half sister, "by his father's second wife." A fourteen-year age gap
(Emmet 1854, Orra 1866) is consistent with a second marriage, and Orra's own diary preserves nothing
from before 1889 — the pages of her girlhood were not kept — so there is no document to check it
against. It is unsettled, and it does not change the degree: half or whole, Roy and Duncan are first
cousins and their daughters are second cousins.

**B3. Selby Vose was the son of Gideon Vose**, the Association's organizer, first president, and the
author of its by-laws. Two independent sources say son: Duncan Keddie ("Selby was his son and not,
as I have heard it given, his nephew") and Bertram Kyle, who sat outside Selby Vose's door for
twenty-five years ("the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in
the last year and it is nonsense"). Rundle's history says nephew and is the source of the story. A
clerk in the outer office and the manager's contemporary in the same town outrank a schoolteacher's
compiled history on a point of parentage. **Why it matters:** the man who wrote Article VII — under
which the test of the delivering station governs a patron's pay, with no provision for checking that
test against anything — and the manager who for a quarter of a century administered the consequences
of it against the Hollow patrons, circulated the 1919 water warning over his own name, argued the
water theory before the 1925 board, and signed the annual "station gain" sheets without ever reading
above the totals, were father and son. Article VII never had a disinterested eye on it, and the
office that might have questioned the arrangement was held by the family that made it.

**B4. No relationship between the Voses and the Keddies is established by the sources, and the
evidence is against one.** The affirmative claim comes from Cleve Oram alone: "The Keddies and the
Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley. My mother had it from her mother and I never
heard it contradicted at the time, **though I could not draw you the line on paper.**" That is
hearsay at two removes about another family, with no line drawable — and Oram offers it as talk, in
good faith, not as knowledge. Against it stands Duncan Keddie, inside the family: there was no
connection whatever; the confusion is with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Gideon either;
two women in the town will tell you his mother and Gideon's wife were cousins, and one will say
first cousins if you let her sit down; and his mother "could not pass a headstone without giving you
the connection" and never once put a Vose in it. Method: a family's own genealogist, reported by her
son, against undocumented village rumor that its own carrier cannot trace. The honest form of the
answer is that the sources establish no kinship and give a positive reason to think there was none,
while noting that Orra's pre-1889 papers do not survive, so no documentary proof either way exists.

## Section C — Math

### C1

**(a) The tests were short by one part in twenty — 5 per cent — because the pipettes delivered
about 16.72 c.c. where their stems were blown 17.6 c.c.**

Working. The test draws a fixed charge of milk into a bottle whose neck is graduated to read fat
directly in percent *for that charge*. The reading is therefore short in the ratio
(delivered volume) ÷ (17.6). The bulletin prints a measured mean delivery of **16.27 c.c.**, giving
16.27 ÷ 17.6 = **0.9244**, i.e. 7.56 per cent short. **I reject 16.27.** Five independent
measurements of the proportion all give 0.95:

| source | figures | ratio |
|---|---|---|
| 1911 borrowed measure (r11, r09) | 3.61 ÷ 3.80 | 0.9500 |
| Association ledger (r12, r23) | 57,760 ÷ 60,800 | 0.9500 exactly |
| Alder Corners, same herds (r10) | 3.61 ÷ 3.80 | 0.9500 |
| Emmet Brant's 1910 leaf (r09, r17) | 3.42 ÷ 3.60 | 0.9500 exactly |
| Vaught's **own conclusion** (r11) | "low by one part in twenty" | 0.9500 |

17.6 × 0.95 = **16.72 c.c.** — and 16.72 → 16.27 is a digit transposition, the same class of slip
that produced $77.93 → "$77.39" in r20 and 3.61 → "3.16" in r13. Method: arithmetic over a printed
figure, plus the author's self-contradiction (§V against §VII of his own bulletin), plus four
independent confirmations. The Chronicle's 16.27 is not a sixth vote; it came off the same press
release.

**(b) True average 3.80; the recorded tests were short by 0.19 of a point.**
Recorded station average **3.61** ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. Shortfall 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19**. Confirmed
three ways that do not depend on the glass at all: the nine weeks of 1911 rose to exactly 3.80;
Alder Corners' sixteen-season average for four of those herds was 3.80; Larrow Green's own patrons
ran 3.78. (Roy Brant's 0.17 and Kepp's 0.21 are rejected by this arithmetic; Falke and Hazel say
0.19. Vaught's §V figure of 3.905 follows from the rejected 16.27 and is rejected with it.)

**(c) 76,000 lb of butterfat.**
40,000,000 lb of milk received over the affected years × 0.19 per cent = **76,000 lb**.
Check, independent of that multiplication: the ledger's annual station gain of 3,040 lb × 25 full
delivery years = **76,000 lb**. The two agree exactly, which is the strongest single result in the
whole record — a figure derived from the glassware and a figure written down annually by the
Association's own bookkeeper meet at the same number. Rejected: Vaught's §V figure of 118,000 lb
(built on 16.27) and the patrons' own 1925 reckoning of 67,000 lb (r13; Oram repeats it in r21 as
though it were the bulletin's, which it is not).

**(d) About $19,000, of which the patrons received $3,800 — one fifth, 20 per cent.**
76,000 lb × **$0.25** = **$19,000**. The 25-cent average is Roy Brant's recollection and, more to
the point, Kyle's figure, which he was set to find in the books for the arbitration and did find,
"taking the fat years with the lean." (Sill's and Kepp's 27 cents is a 1920s figure, not the
long average.) Received: the 1925 award of **$3,800**. 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **0.20 exactly**. Note that
the patrons' own petition claimed **$19,600** — nearly the right money by a wrong route (they
claimed 67,000 lb) — and that the *Chronicle* mistakenly printed $19,600 as the bulletin's figure.

### C2 — Emmet Brant, 1910 (200,000 lb delivered, paid on a test of 3.42)

- **(a) Credited:** 200,000 × 3.42 % = **6,840 lb of butterfat.**
- **(b) Owed and not credited:** true test 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = **3.60** exactly; true fat 200,000 × 3.60 %
  = 7,200 lb; **7,200 − 6,840 = 360 lb.** (Equivalently 200,000 × 0.18 % = 360 lb — note the
  per-farm shortfall in points is 0.18 here, not 0.19, because the shortfall is *proportional*:
  5 per cent of a 3.60 herd is 0.18, 5 per cent of the 3.80 station average is 0.19. That is the
  whole signature of the fault.)
- **(c)** Butterfat was **24 cents** in 1910 (Roy Brant, from the ledger year itself):
  360 × $0.24 = **$86.40** in that one year, on that one farm.

### C3 — Over the 25 full delivery years

- **(a) Credited to patrons:** 57,760 lb/yr × 25 = **1,444,000 lb.**
  (Check: 1,600,000 lb of milk a year × 3.61 % = 57,760 lb exactly.)
- **(b) Received at Larrow Green in the station cream:** 60,800 lb/yr × 25 = **1,520,000 lb.**
  (Check: 1,600,000 × 3.80 % = 60,800 exactly.)
- **(c) Difference 76,000 lb.** Being exact about the base:
  - **5.00 per cent of the fat actually delivered** (76,000 ÷ 1,520,000) — this is the real
    shortfall, one part in twenty;
  - **5.263 per cent of the fat credited to the patrons** (76,000 ÷ 1,444,000) — and *this* is the
    "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" of the Association's books, because the bookkeeper
    naturally divided the gain by the figure above it in his own column.
  So Vaught's phrase "station gain, 5¼ percent **of the fat actually delivered**" is loose: 5¼ per
  cent is of the credited fat, 5 per cent is of the delivered fat. The two numbers are the same
  event seen from two ends, and the difference between them is not evidence of anything.

### C4 — The invoice (Ammon & Sons, Tarnet, 14 April 1897)

- **(a) $77.93.** Working: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = **77.93**, which is the total
  printed on the invoice itself. Junia Ammon's own prose two lines later says "seventy-seven dollars
  and thirty-nine cents," and the *Chronicle* says $77.39. Addition settles it; and the Chronicle is
  not a second vote, since a transposed total is exactly the sort of thing a reporter copies.
- **(b) Six dozen = 72 pipettes**, confirmed by the line's own arithmetic (72 × $0.35 = $25.20
  exactly) and by the 1923 replacement order, which was for six dozen pipettes like for like. Each
  was short by **17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c.** (exactly one twentieth of 17.6). This is derived, not
  measured: the measured figure in the record is 1.33 c.c. short (17.6 − 16.27), which I reject with
  16.27 under C1(a). Vaught's "five dozen purchased" and Oram's "five dozen went into that crate"
  are reconcilable with 72 bought only in Oram's form — 72 bought in 1897, breakage over twenty-six
  years of daily use leaving about five dozen to be crated in 1923, and nine by 1958.

### C5

**(a) In use 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 — twenty-six years and about six weeks. The money runs over
twenty-five full delivery years.**
They differ for three reasons. First, both ends are partial: the glass went in a month into the 1897
season and was condemned in June of 1923, so only twenty-five *complete* delivery years lie inside
the period. Second, the station's own first season (from its opening on 4 May 1896) ran on Hobart
Sill's borrowed glass from the Larrow Green bench and is *outside* the fault altogether — which is
also why the shortfall cannot have begun with the station. Third, Kyle's 40,000,000 lb, the figure
he personally got out of the books for the arbitration and the one the money calculation uses, is
25 × 1,600,000 lb by construction. (Rundle's 44,000,000 lb is rejected: Kyle computed his; and
40,000,000 is what makes the ledger's 57,760 and 60,800 come out exactly.)

**(b) About 900,000 lb in the first season (May to the end of 1896) — but this cannot be firmly
settled.** Sill says a good 900,000; Kyle says "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds." Neither is
documentary, and Kyle says so himself: the milk of that season was carried on the creamery's own
books, the sheets were never put with the station papers, "It is not in any book I ever ruled," and
he had it from men who were there — he was not. Sill *was* there, in post from 1893, receiving it.
I therefore prefer 900,000 lb as the better-sourced recollection while noting that the record which
would decide it does not exist.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 interval.**
**8 May to 10 July 1911 — nine weeks.** (Orra Keddie's diary fixes the start: "A. home cross about a
broken measure"; Falke gives both dates; Vaught and Roy Brant both say nine weeks. Kepp's "six
weeks" is a single narrator with a motive, and is rejected.) The station's own measuring glass broke
and a borrowed measure was used. **The variable was the measuring glass and nothing else. Held
constant:** the same patrons, herds, pasture, feed, water, season, weather, acid, test bottles, hand
tester, scales, building, and the same tester working by the same method under no different
supervision. **Result:** the station average rose 3.61 → 3.80, rose for *every* farm on the Bottom,
held nine weeks, and fell back to 3.61 the day the station's own measure returned. **What it
establishes that calculation cannot:** it locates the cause. An arithmetical demonstration depends
on accepting Vaught's measurements, his sums and his assumptions — and his §V arithmetic is in fact
wrong. The 1911 interval needs none of that: it is an intervention with a control, recorded
contemporaneously by a man who did not understand what he was recording, and it proves the cause was
inside the station's own measuring glass rather than in the milk, the land, the cows, the men, the
scales or the books. It also lands in June and July, when the wet-hay theory predicts no gap at all,
so it refutes that theory in its own best season. **What it does not establish:** the *size* of the
shortfall (the borrowed measure was itself never verified — it is assumed true, and if it ran
generous the true figure would differ); *which* piece of glass was at fault (pipette rather than
bottle) or *why*; *when* the error began or whether it was constant before and after; that the
short glass had been short since 1897; and nothing whatever about anyone's honesty. It shows where
to look. It does not measure what is there.

**D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream.**

1. **The cream arrived heavy at the other end.** Hobart Sill weighed the Hollow cream into the cold
   room himself, daily but Sunday, for forty-one years: "There was more fat in the cans than the
   papers said had been bought up there... Always more. Never less." Theft makes cream arrive
   *light*.
2. **The Association's own ledgers say so in writing, every year.** 60,800 lb of fat received at
   Larrow Green against 57,760 lb credited at the Hollow — "station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a
   quarter per cent, as usual." Rundle found this while his book was in press and killed his own
   paragraph with it.
3. **The nine weeks of 1911.** The same man made every test in that interval, under no different
   supervision, and the figures rose and then fell back with a piece of borrowed glass. A thief's
   arithmetic does not obey the glassware.
4. (A fourth, if wanted: Frick, who chaired the hearing, believed Keddie's statement, and the board
   found no cause established.)

**Why these are independent.** They are different people, in different places, in different kinds of
evidence: (1) is one man's physical weighing at the *receiving* end, at Larrow Green; (2) is a
written entry made annually by a different man, Teague, in the *office*, from the creamery's returns;
(3) is an event at the *station* recorded by a third man, Keddie, in the test book. No one of them
was derived from another; Sill says explicitly that nobody ever asked him about it, and Rundle came
on the ledger entry by himself in 1939. **Reasons (1), (2) and (3) all stand whether or not anyone
ever measured the glassware in 1958** — reason (2) most cleanly of all, since it is the Association's
own bookkeeping, made contemporaneously, requiring no science and no interpretation.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question those entries? No.** Wilbur Teague *wrote* the station line every
year of his service, in his own words, "as usual," and Kyle says he "never asked anybody anything in
forty years" — the figures were the figures and the asking was for other people. Yet Teague is the
man who, at the 1925 hearing, insinuated at length that the tester was removing cream and writing
low tests to cover it, before adding that he made no charge against anybody. The accusation and its
refutation were in the same hand: he had been writing the sentence that disproved his own theory,
once a year, for a quarter of a century, and never once read it as evidence of anything.

**D3. Did Selby Vose know the tests ran low before the glass was condemned?**
**Yes — the fact was unavoidable, and he acted on it. What he did not know was the reason, and the
sources show why he did not.** He cannot have been ignorant of the low tests: the Hollow patrons
said so at annual meetings from 1902 and renewed it every decade; the 1908 committee was appointed
over it; he became manager in 1911; in June 1919 he himself circulated a warning to the Hollow
patrons about adding water, over his own name and no one else's — a letter which is unintelligible
unless he knew their tests ran below the valley's and had formed a theory about it; and he argued
that theory before the 1925 board. He knew the *symptom* and had an explanation for it that blamed
the farmers. What he did not know was that his own ledger sheets contained the refutation, and here
the sources give a mechanism rather than a guess: Kyle, who sat a foot from his door for
twenty-five years, watched him "read the totals and never the lines above them" — take a sheet, turn
it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign — "a thousand times." The station-gain line stood
on every one of those sheets from 1911. So the honest answer is layered: that he knew the tests ran
low is certain and documented; that he did not know the cream arrived heavy is an inference from
Kyle's testimony about his habits, and it is the best-supported reading, but it rests on one witness
describing another man's practice. Nothing in the sources suggests he suspected the glass, and
nothing suggests concealment: the whole record was in plain sight with a name on it.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. What actually produced the shortfall.**

The Association paid by butterfat, and by Article VII the test of the station where a patron
delivered governed his pay, with nothing to check that test against. The butterfat test works by
drawing a fixed charge of milk — 17.6 c.c. in this state — by pipette into a bottle whose graduated
neck is ruled to read fat directly in percent *for that charge*. The pipette is therefore not one
instrument among several; it is the standard by which the plant measures everything else.

On 14 April 1897 the Association bought the Ostrey Hollow station's outfit from Ammon & Sons of
Tarnet: six dozen pipettes blown 17.6 c.c., twelve dozen bottles, an acid measure, a hand tester
and freight, $77.93. That lot had been blown to a reground mold and ran short: each pipette
delivered about 16.72 c.c., **short by one part in twenty**. It went into use on 1 May 1897, and
from that day every test made at Ostrey Hollow read **5 per cent low** — a true 3.80 recorded as
3.61, a true 3.60 herd recorded as 3.42.

*Why that size:* the error is nothing but the ratio of what the glass delivered to what the neck was
ruled for. 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95. There is no other quantity in the story and no room for a second
cause; the size of the fault is the size of the glass's shortfall and nothing else.

*Why it began when it did:* on the day that lot was first used. The station had opened on 4 May 1896
on glass Hobart Sill made up out of his own bench at Larrow Green, which was sound; the borrowing
ended and the Tarnet lot went in on 1 May 1897, the day Orra Keddie recorded her husband's pleasure
at having their own glass at last.

*Why it ended when it did:* on 12 June 1923, when Merle Strawn, on an ordinary circuit visit, ran
the pipettes against a **burette** — an outside standard, the only kind of instrument that can catch
this fault — condemned the lot, and told the manager to order new. New glass was in use within the
month, and from 1924 the station's average stood at 3.85.

*Why nothing at the station or on the farms had to change at either end:* because the error was
**proportional, not additive** — every herd was reduced by the same 5 per cent, so herds still
ranked correctly against one another and every internal comparison the plant could make came out
consistent; because it was **precise** — the same short delivery in January and in July, careful
operator or careless, which made it look not like a fault but like unusually good work; and because
it was **invisible from inside the plant** — the scales, the tester's speed, the acid, the bottle
graduations could all be checked, but they could only be checked against the pipette. Nobody had to
change anything for the fault to start or stop. The glass changed, and everything else followed it.

**The three competing explanations, and what defeats each.**

1. **Water in the milk.** Advanced by **Selby Vose**, the manager, in his circular of June 1919 and
   again before the 1925 board; Hobart Sill believed it too. *Defeated by:* Rosalie Cudd's sixteen
   summers — "cheese is a witness," watered milk shows in the vat within a day, curd soft, body
   slack, yield down, and in sixteen seasons Jerome Cudd made no complaint, docked no one a cent,
   and only wished he had more of that milk in July, while his own book puts those same four herds
   at 3.78–3.82 every year; the state inspector's lactometer at every visit from 1908 forward, which
   never once found added water at that station; the 1908 committee, which sealed its own samples
   and reported the milk whole and "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter"; and the 1911
   interval, in which the same milk from the same cans tested 0.19 higher on a borrowed measure.
2. **The tester stealing cream and writing low tests to cover it.** Insinuated at the 1925 hearing by
   **Wilbur Teague**, the Association's bookkeeper; it was also the valley's settled opinion and
   Rundle printed it in 1939. *Defeated by:* the direction of the discrepancy. Theft makes cream
   arrive light; this cream arrived **heavy**, every year — 60,800 lb of fat received at Larrow
   Green against 57,760 lb credited at the Hollow, entered as "station gain, five and a quarter per
   cent, as usual" in Teague's own hand; Sill weighed it in himself and said the same thing
   independently for forty-one years; and Rundle, finding the ledger entry while his history was in
   press, wrote that he could not make his paragraph stand. The 1911 interval kills it a second
   time: the same man, the same supervision, and the figures obeyed the glass.
3. **The wet hay and sour land of Hessel Bottom.** Advanced by **Nyle Grigg's** committee in 1908,
   reissued by him in 1919, and given its fullest form by **Dr. Roland Kepp** in 1926. *Defeated by:*
   Bulletin No. 214, which both men misquote — it found bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage
   depressed the test by **not more than five hundredths in winter and by nothing measurable on
   summer pasture**, where the observed gap was 0.19 and present every month; by the constancy of
   the gap, the same in July as in February, which no feed effect can produce; by the 1911 interval
   falling in June and July, when the theory predicts no gap at all; by the same herds on the same
   hay testing 3.80 over the ridge at Alder Corners; and by Grigg's own committee having sampled
   only January, February and March. Falke conceded this explanation as a partial factor in 1959 and
   withdrew the concession the moment he read the bulletin: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing
   in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July."

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware? No — and this is the sharpest fact in the
record.** The board sat three times, and **two of the three sittings were held in the station's own
weigh room**, directly beneath the loft in which the condemned crate had been lying since 1923 —
twenty feet of ladder, as Duncan Keddie measures it, with Cleve Oram standing outside on the
platform in the rain. The board watched the scales tried and a test made, on the *new* glass, and
its chairman says he did not understand the test then and does not now. Worse, the record of the
condemnation was on the table: the fourth of the four exhibits was the state dairy inspector's
circuit notebook **for 1923**, laid beside the station's own receiving book, and, as Kyle puts it,
he could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second
beside the fourth and looked. The answer was in the building, in the exhibits, and in the ledger,
and the board found "no cause established."

**What did the tester himself believe?** **Ansel Keddie believed it was the glass — from the summer
of 1911.** His son, then seventeen, remembers him coming home saying "it was something in the
glass," saying it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not his way. He never dared put
it in the book: what he wrote against those nine weeks was **a question mark**, which lay unread for
forty-seven years and became Vaught's §IV. He would not put down what he could not prove — against
a suspicion about a piece of glass stood the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of
every station in the county — so he kept on for another fourteen years, writing down every figure
the glass gave him, and those figures were what convicted him. Publicly he said only that he had
tested as he was taught and as the state man had watched him do it, thirty-one times a week for
twenty-nine seasons, that he could not say why the figures were what they were, and that he had
never taken a pound of cream. One qualification, from Frick and not from the minutes: in the
corridor at Ashlin he said privately that he had always thought the patrons were at fault and would
not say so in the room. So he accused nobody publicly, and he was not without suspicions — but the
thing he had actually suspected, fourteen years earlier and correctly, was the glass.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** (149 words)

For twenty-six years the Larrow Valley creamery's skimming station at Ostrey Hollow tested its
patrons' milk 5 per cent low, because a lot of pipettes bought from Tarnet in 1897 delivered 16.72
c.c. where their stems were blown 17.6. Under Article VII the station's test governed the pay, so
six farms lost 76,000 lb of butterfat — about $19,000 — while the Association's own ledgers credited
the same amount, yearly, as "station gain." Nobody looked at the glass. They blamed the farmers'
water, the tester's honesty, and the wet hay, and in 1925 dismissed a blameless man four days before
awarding $3,800 for no established cause. It took eight records because no witness could see it
alone: the answer lay in the space between a farmer's weigh book, a cheesemaker's vat, a clerk's
ledger line, a broken measure in 1911, an invoice, and a crate in a loft nobody climbed to for
thirty-five years.
