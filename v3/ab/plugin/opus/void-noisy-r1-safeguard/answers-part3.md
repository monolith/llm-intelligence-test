# Answer sheet — Part 3 of 3

## Section C — Math

### C1

**(a) The tests were short by one part in twenty — 5 per cent — because the pipettes delivered
about 16.72 c.c. where their stems were blown 17.6 c.c.**

Working. The test draws a fixed charge of milk into a bottle whose neck is graduated to read fat
directly in percent *for that charge*. The reading is therefore short in the ratio
(delivered volume) ÷ (17.6). The bulletin prints a measured mean delivery of **16.27 c.c.**, giving
16.27 ÷ 17.6 = **0.9244**, i.e. 7.56 per cent short. **I reject 16.27.** Five independent
measurements of the proportion all give 0.95:

| source | figures | ratio |
|---|---|---|
| 1911 borrowed measure (r11, r09) | 3.61 ÷ 3.80 | 0.9500 |
| Association ledger (r12, r23) | 57,760 ÷ 60,800 | 0.9500 exactly |
| Alder Corners, same herds (r10) | 3.61 ÷ 3.80 | 0.9500 |
| Emmet Brant's 1910 leaf (r09, r17) | 3.42 ÷ 3.60 | 0.9500 exactly |
| Vaught's **own conclusion** (r11) | "low by one part in twenty" | 0.9500 |

17.6 × 0.95 = **16.72 c.c.** — and 16.72 → 16.27 is a digit transposition, the same class of slip
that produced $77.93 → "$77.39" in r20 and 3.61 → "3.16" in r13. Method: arithmetic over a printed
figure, plus the author's self-contradiction (§V against §VII of his own bulletin), plus four
independent confirmations. The Chronicle's 16.27 is not a sixth vote; it came off the same press
release.

**(b) True average 3.80; the recorded tests were short by 0.19 of a point.**
Recorded station average **3.61** ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. Shortfall 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19**. Confirmed
three ways that do not depend on the glass at all: the nine weeks of 1911 rose to exactly 3.80;
Alder Corners' sixteen-season average for four of those herds was 3.80; Larrow Green's own patrons
ran 3.78. (Roy Brant's 0.17 and Kepp's 0.21 are rejected by this arithmetic; Falke and Hazel say
0.19. Vaught's §V figure of 3.905 follows from the rejected 16.27 and is rejected with it.)

**(c) 76,000 lb of butterfat.**
40,000,000 lb of milk received over the affected years × 0.19 per cent = **76,000 lb**.
Check, independent of that multiplication: the ledger's annual station gain of 3,040 lb × 25 full
delivery years = **76,000 lb**. The two agree exactly, which is the strongest single result in the
whole record — a figure derived from the glassware and a figure written down annually by the
Association's own bookkeeper meet at the same number. Rejected: Vaught's §V figure of 118,000 lb
(built on 16.27) and the patrons' own 1925 reckoning of 67,000 lb (r13; Oram repeats it in r21 as
though it were the bulletin's, which it is not).

**(d) About $19,000, of which the patrons received $3,800 — one fifth, 20 per cent.**
76,000 lb × **$0.25** = **$19,000**. The 25-cent average is Roy Brant's recollection and, more to
the point, Kyle's figure, which he was set to find in the books for the arbitration and did find,
"taking the fat years with the lean." (Sill's and Kepp's 27 cents is a 1920s figure, not the
long average.) Received: the 1925 award of **$3,800**. 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **0.20 exactly**. Note that
the patrons' own petition claimed **$19,600** — nearly the right money by a wrong route (they
claimed 67,000 lb) — and that the *Chronicle* mistakenly printed $19,600 as the bulletin's figure.

### C2 — Emmet Brant, 1910 (200,000 lb delivered, paid on a test of 3.42)

- **(a) Credited:** 200,000 × 3.42 % = **6,840 lb of butterfat.**
- **(b) Owed and not credited:** true test 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = **3.60** exactly; true fat 200,000 × 3.60 %
  = 7,200 lb; **7,200 − 6,840 = 360 lb.** (Equivalently 200,000 × 0.18 % = 360 lb — note the
  per-farm shortfall in points is 0.18 here, not 0.19, because the shortfall is *proportional*:
  5 per cent of a 3.60 herd is 0.18, 5 per cent of the 3.80 station average is 0.19. That is the
  whole signature of the fault.)
- **(c)** Butterfat was **24 cents** in 1910 (Roy Brant, from the ledger year itself):
  360 × $0.24 = **$86.40** in that one year, on that one farm.

### C3 — Over the 25 full delivery years

- **(a) Credited to patrons:** 57,760 lb/yr × 25 = **1,444,000 lb.**
  (Check: 1,600,000 lb of milk a year × 3.61 % = 57,760 lb exactly.)
- **(b) Received at Larrow Green in the station cream:** 60,800 lb/yr × 25 = **1,520,000 lb.**
  (Check: 1,600,000 × 3.80 % = 60,800 exactly.)
- **(c) Difference 76,000 lb.** Being exact about the base:
  - **5.00 per cent of the fat actually delivered** (76,000 ÷ 1,520,000) — this is the real
    shortfall, one part in twenty;
  - **5.263 per cent of the fat credited to the patrons** (76,000 ÷ 1,444,000) — and *this* is the
    "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" of the Association's books, because the bookkeeper
    naturally divided the gain by the figure above it in his own column.
  So Vaught's phrase "station gain, 5¼ percent **of the fat actually delivered**" is loose: 5¼ per
  cent is of the credited fat, 5 per cent is of the delivered fat. The two numbers are the same
  event seen from two ends, and the difference between them is not evidence of anything.

### C4 — The invoice (Ammon & Sons, Tarnet, 14 April 1897)

- **(a) $77.93.** Working: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = **77.93**, which is the total
  printed on the invoice itself. Junia Ammon's own prose two lines later says "seventy-seven dollars
  and thirty-nine cents," and the *Chronicle* says $77.39. Addition settles it; and the Chronicle is
  not a second vote, since a transposed total is exactly the sort of thing a reporter copies.
- **(b) Six dozen = 72 pipettes**, confirmed by the line's own arithmetic (72 × $0.35 = $25.20
  exactly) and by the 1923 replacement order, which was for six dozen pipettes like for like. Each
  was short by **17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c.** (exactly one twentieth of 17.6). This is derived, not
  measured: the measured figure in the record is 1.33 c.c. short (17.6 − 16.27), which I reject with
  16.27 under C1(a). Vaught's "five dozen purchased" and Oram's "five dozen went into that crate"
  are reconcilable with 72 bought only in Oram's form — 72 bought in 1897, breakage over twenty-six
  years of daily use leaving about five dozen to be crated in 1923, and nine by 1958.

### C5

**(a) In use 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 — twenty-six years and about six weeks. The money runs over
twenty-five full delivery years.**
They differ for three reasons. First, both ends are partial: the glass went in a month into the 1897
season and was condemned in June of 1923, so only twenty-five *complete* delivery years lie inside
the period. Second, the station's own first season (from its opening on 4 May 1896) ran on Hobart
Sill's borrowed glass from the Larrow Green bench and is *outside* the fault altogether — which is
also why the shortfall cannot have begun with the station. Third, Kyle's 40,000,000 lb, the figure
he personally got out of the books for the arbitration and the one the money calculation uses, is
25 × 1,600,000 lb by construction. (Rundle's 44,000,000 lb is rejected: Kyle computed his; and
40,000,000 is what makes the ledger's 57,760 and 60,800 come out exactly.)

**(b) About 900,000 lb in the first season (May to the end of 1896) — but this cannot be firmly
settled.** Sill says a good 900,000; Kyle says "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds." Neither is
documentary, and Kyle says so himself: the milk of that season was carried on the creamery's own
books, the sheets were never put with the station papers, "It is not in any book I ever ruled," and
he had it from men who were there — he was not. Sill *was* there, in post from 1893, receiving it.
I therefore prefer 900,000 lb as the better-sourced recollection while noting that the record which
would decide it does not exist.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 interval.**
**8 May to 10 July 1911 — nine weeks.** (Orra Keddie's diary fixes the start: "A. home cross about a
broken measure"; Falke gives both dates; Vaught and Roy Brant both say nine weeks. Kepp's "six
weeks" is a single narrator with a motive, and is rejected.) The station's own measuring glass broke
and a borrowed measure was used. **The variable was the measuring glass and nothing else. Held
constant:** the same patrons, herds, pasture, feed, water, season, weather, acid, test bottles, hand
tester, scales, building, and the same tester working by the same method under no different
supervision. **Result:** the station average rose 3.61 → 3.80, rose for *every* farm on the Bottom,
held nine weeks, and fell back to 3.61 the day the station's own measure returned. **What it
establishes that calculation cannot:** it locates the cause. An arithmetical demonstration depends
on accepting Vaught's measurements, his sums and his assumptions — and his §V arithmetic is in fact
wrong. The 1911 interval needs none of that: it is an intervention with a control, recorded
contemporaneously by a man who did not understand what he was recording, and it proves the cause was
inside the station's own measuring glass rather than in the milk, the land, the cows, the men, the
scales or the books. It also lands in June and July, when the wet-hay theory predicts no gap at all,
so it refutes that theory in its own best season. **What it does not establish:** the *size* of the
shortfall (the borrowed measure was itself never verified — it is assumed true, and if it ran
generous the true figure would differ); *which* piece of glass was at fault (pipette rather than
bottle) or *why*; *when* the error began or whether it was constant before and after; that the
short glass had been short since 1897; and nothing whatever about anyone's honesty. It shows where
to look. It does not measure what is there.

**D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream.**

1. **The cream arrived heavy at the other end.** Hobart Sill weighed the Hollow cream into the cold
   room himself, daily but Sunday, for forty-one years: "There was more fat in the cans than the
   papers said had been bought up there... Always more. Never less." Theft makes cream arrive
   *light*.
2. **The Association's own ledgers say so in writing, every year.** 60,800 lb of fat received at
   Larrow Green against 57,760 lb credited at the Hollow — "station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a
   quarter per cent, as usual." Rundle found this while his book was in press and killed his own
   paragraph with it.
3. **The nine weeks of 1911.** The same man made every test in that interval, under no different
   supervision, and the figures rose and then fell back with a piece of borrowed glass. A thief's
   arithmetic does not obey the glassware.
4. (A fourth, if wanted: Frick, who chaired the hearing, believed Keddie's statement, and the board
   found no cause established.)

**Why these are independent.** They are different people, in different places, in different kinds of
evidence: (1) is one man's physical weighing at the *receiving* end, at Larrow Green; (2) is a
written entry made annually by a different man, Teague, in the *office*, from the creamery's returns;
(3) is an event at the *station* recorded by a third man, Keddie, in the test book. No one of them
was derived from another; Sill says explicitly that nobody ever asked him about it, and Rundle came
on the ledger entry by himself in 1939. **Reasons (1), (2) and (3) all stand whether or not anyone
ever measured the glassware in 1958** — reason (2) most cleanly of all, since it is the Association's
own bookkeeping, made contemporaneously, requiring no science and no interpretation.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question those entries? No.** Wilbur Teague *wrote* the station line every
year of his service, in his own words, "as usual," and Kyle says he "never asked anybody anything in
forty years" — the figures were the figures and the asking was for other people. Yet Teague is the
man who, at the 1925 hearing, insinuated at length that the tester was removing cream and writing
low tests to cover it, before adding that he made no charge against anybody. The accusation and its
refutation were in the same hand: he had been writing the sentence that disproved his own theory,
once a year, for a quarter of a century, and never once read it as evidence of anything.

**D3. Did Selby Vose know the tests ran low before the glass was condemned?**
**Yes — the fact was unavoidable, and he acted on it. What he did not know was the reason, and the
sources show why he did not.** He cannot have been ignorant of the low tests: the Hollow patrons
said so at annual meetings from 1902 and renewed it every decade; the 1908 committee was appointed
over it; he became manager in 1911; in June 1919 he himself circulated a warning to the Hollow
patrons about adding water, over his own name and no one else's — a letter which is unintelligible
unless he knew their tests ran below the valley's and had formed a theory about it; and he argued
that theory before the 1925 board. He knew the *symptom* and had an explanation for it that blamed
the farmers. What he did not know was that his own ledger sheets contained the refutation, and here
the sources give a mechanism rather than a guess: Kyle, who sat a foot from his door for
twenty-five years, watched him "read the totals and never the lines above them" — take a sheet, turn
it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign — "a thousand times." The station-gain line stood
on every one of those sheets from 1911. So the honest answer is layered: that he knew the tests ran
low is certain and documented; that he did not know the cream arrived heavy is an inference from
Kyle's testimony about his habits, and it is the best-supported reading, but it rests on one witness
describing another man's practice. Nothing in the sources suggests he suspected the glass, and
nothing suggests concealment: the whole record was in plain sight with a name on it.

### E1(ii) — Cases where **two narrators carry the same wrong value**

- **"Two tenths" from Bulletin 214 — Grigg (1919) and Kepp (1926).** They look like two authorities
  agreeing; Kepp even says his field figure, the observed gap and the College's finding "agree so
  nearly that he does not know what more a reasonable man requires." **What breaks the tie:** the
  bulletin's own text, quoted identically by Vaught in 1958 and independently obtained from the
  Farland library and printed entire by Falke in 1959 — five hundredths in winter, nothing
  measurable in summer. Kepp is almost certainly reading Grigg's widely reprinted 1919 letter, not
  Bulletin 214; two narrators, one source, and a misquotation that has been doing work for
  seventeen years.
- **"Nine families" circularized — Roy Brant and Hazel Brant.** Father and daughter, one household,
  one memory: not two sources. **Broken by** Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary entry of June 1919,
  written by the wife of the man who carried the letters to eleven doors.
- **"16.27 c.c." — Vaught and the Chronicle.** The paper says it worked from the college press
  release; one source. **Broken by** arithmetic and by Vaught's own conclusion in the same document.
- **"$77.39" — Junia Ammon's prose and the Chronicle.** **Broken by** adding the invoice column to
  $77.93 — and note that the Chronicle claims to have seen the invoice, which shows that having the
  document is not the same as adding it up.
- **"Great-niece" — the Chronicle and Falke's page two.** Falke was working from the same bulletin
  notice. **Broken by** Duncan Keddie's statement of his own daughter's descent, and by Falke's own
  verification and printed correction.
- **"Eleven pipettes" — Rundle's 1959 footnote and the Chronicle.** Both date from the 1958–59
  publicity around Ivy Keddie's work; not independent of one another. **Broken (imperfectly) by**
  Vaught, who had the pipettes, Falke, who dates the measurement to 9 March 1958, and Oram, who
  handled the crate and counted nine.
- **"67,000 lb" — Frick and Oram.** Frick records it correctly as *the patrons' own reckoning*;
  Oram, in 1977, repeats it as though it were the bulletin's finding. One figure, one origin, and a
  category error in the second telling.

### E1(iii) — Narrators who **contradict themselves on the page**

- **Vaught (r11), three times.** §V's arithmetic (16.27 c.c., ratio 0.9244, true test 3.905,
  118,000 lb) cannot stand with his own conclusion (§VII: "low by one part in twenty... seventy-six
  thousand pounds"). He half-sees it himself, noting that §V "is not readily reconciled" with the
  station's own 3.80. He also dates the glass into use at 1 May **1898** in §III and 1 May **1897**
  in the conclusion. And he calls the station gain "5¼ percent of the fat actually delivered," when
  5¼ per cent is of the fat *credited* and 5 per cent is of the fat delivered. Resolution in every
  case: the conclusion, because it is what the ledger, the 1911 interval and the invoice all
  independently support.
- **Grigg (r15).** His covering letter says the committee sampled "the whole of 1908 — every month
  of that year"; the report printed immediately beneath says "We have sampled the herds of Hessel
  Bottom through January, February and March." A winter-only sample inflated into a year-round one —
  which matters because the whole hay theory is a winter-feeding theory and the gap was a
  twelve-month gap.
- **Junia Ammon (r20), twice.** "The house sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900" — on the
  same page as an invoice of 14 April 1897 to an Ordell County association. And the invoice totals
  77.93 while her own next sentence says seventy-seven and thirty-nine.
- **Duncan Keddie (r18).** Gives his mother's birth year as 1868 and also says she was eighty-two
  when she died in 1948, which gives 1866 — as does her own diary ("I am sixty-four," August 1930).
  1866; the error is in the stated year, not the arithmetic.
- **Kepp (r16).** His theory and his cited authority both make the bog-hay effect a *winter-feeding*
  effect; he then disposes of the 1911 interval as "six weeks of June and July, when every herd in
  the county is at its best and the stock are off the wet ground" — i.e. precisely the months in
  which his own explanation predicts no gap, and in which the gap nevertheless existed except when
  the glass was borrowed. He also treats his 0.20 field figure, the 0.21 gap and the College's "two
  tenths" as three agreeing measurements when one is his own and one is a misquotation.
- **Hazel Brant (r17)** is a special case worth stating as a rule rather than an error list. She
  opens by declaring "Quantities I have. Years I have not," and the tape bears her out exactly:
  every year she gives is wrong (the crate from Tarnet in '99, condemned in '21, the hearing in '26,
  Keddie up in '95, the book begun in 1900) and every quantity is right (six farms, four haulers,
  200,000 lb even, a test of 3.42, nineteen hundredths) — **except one, "nine families," which she
  did not get from her own memory but from her father.** A narrator's self-assessment, when it is
  testable, is worth testing.
- **Oram (r21).** Says his father "was the one man in the valley who saw both ends of that cream
  every day of his life" while reporting his father's belief that less came off the platform than
  went on — a belief the receiving end's own weigher and the receiving end's own ledger both
  contradict.
- **Frick (r13).** Says Keddie "accused nobody," then reports that Keddie told him privately in the
  corridor that he had always thought the patrons at fault. Both are true and Frick knows it: no
  public accusation, but not no suspicion.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. What actually produced the shortfall.**

The Association paid by butterfat, and by Article VII the test of the station where a patron
delivered governed his pay, with nothing to check that test against. The butterfat test works by
drawing a fixed charge of milk — 17.6 c.c. in this state — by pipette into a bottle whose graduated
neck is ruled to read fat directly in percent *for that charge*. The pipette is therefore not one
instrument among several; it is the standard by which the plant measures everything else.

On 14 April 1897 the Association bought the Ostrey Hollow station's outfit from Ammon & Sons of
Tarnet: six dozen pipettes blown 17.6 c.c., twelve dozen bottles, an acid measure, a hand tester
and freight, $77.93. That lot had been blown to a reground mold and ran short: each pipette
delivered about 16.72 c.c., **short by one part in twenty**. It went into use on 1 May 1897, and
from that day every test made at Ostrey Hollow read **5 per cent low** — a true 3.80 recorded as
3.61, a true 3.60 herd recorded as 3.42.

*Why that size:* the error is nothing but the ratio of what the glass delivered to what the neck was
ruled for. 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95. There is no other quantity in the story and no room for a second
cause; the size of the fault is the size of the glass's shortfall and nothing else.

*Why it began when it did:* on the day that lot was first used. The station had opened on 4 May 1896
on glass Hobart Sill made up out of his own bench at Larrow Green, which was sound; the borrowing
ended and the Tarnet lot went in on 1 May 1897, the day Orra Keddie recorded her husband's pleasure
at having their own glass at last.

*Why it ended when it did:* on 12 June 1923, when Merle Strawn, on an ordinary circuit visit, ran
the pipettes against a **burette** — an outside standard, the only kind of instrument that can catch
this fault — condemned the lot, and told the manager to order new. New glass was in use within the
month, and from 1924 the station's average stood at 3.85.

*Why nothing at the station or on the farms had to change at either end:* because the error was
**proportional, not additive** — every herd was reduced by the same 5 per cent, so herds still
ranked correctly against one another and every internal comparison the plant could make came out
consistent; because it was **precise** — the same short delivery in January and in July, careful
operator or careless, which made it look not like a fault but like unusually good work; and because
it was **invisible from inside the plant** — the scales, the tester's speed, the acid, the bottle
graduations could all be checked, but they could only be checked against the pipette. Nobody had to
change anything for the fault to start or stop. The glass changed, and everything else followed it.

**The three competing explanations, and what defeats each.**

1. **Water in the milk.** Advanced by **Selby Vose**, the manager, in his circular of June 1919 and
   again before the 1925 board; Hobart Sill believed it too. *Defeated by:* Rosalie Cudd's sixteen
   summers — "cheese is a witness," watered milk shows in the vat within a day, curd soft, body
   slack, yield down, and in sixteen seasons Jerome Cudd made no complaint, docked no one a cent,
   and only wished he had more of that milk in July, while his own book puts those same four herds
   at 3.78–3.82 every year; the state inspector's lactometer at every visit from 1908 forward, which
   never once found added water at that station; the 1908 committee, which sealed its own samples
   and reported the milk whole and "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter"; and the 1911
   interval, in which the same milk from the same cans tested 0.19 higher on a borrowed measure.
2. **The tester stealing cream and writing low tests to cover it.** Insinuated at the 1925 hearing by
   **Wilbur Teague**, the Association's bookkeeper; it was also the valley's settled opinion and
   Rundle printed it in 1939. *Defeated by:* the direction of the discrepancy. Theft makes cream
   arrive light; this cream arrived **heavy**, every year — 60,800 lb of fat received at Larrow
   Green against 57,760 lb credited at the Hollow, entered as "station gain, five and a quarter per
   cent, as usual" in Teague's own hand; Sill weighed it in himself and said the same thing
   independently for forty-one years; and Rundle, finding the ledger entry while his history was in
   press, wrote that he could not make his paragraph stand. The 1911 interval kills it a second
   time: the same man, the same supervision, and the figures obeyed the glass.
3. **The wet hay and sour land of Hessel Bottom.** Advanced by **Nyle Grigg's** committee in 1908,
   reissued by him in 1919, and given its fullest form by **Dr. Roland Kepp** in 1926. *Defeated by:*
   Bulletin No. 214, which both men misquote — it found bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage
   depressed the test by **not more than five hundredths in winter and by nothing measurable on
   summer pasture**, where the observed gap was 0.19 and present every month; by the constancy of
   the gap, the same in July as in February, which no feed effect can produce; by the 1911 interval
   falling in June and July, when the theory predicts no gap at all; by the same herds on the same
   hay testing 3.80 over the ridge at Alder Corners; and by Grigg's own committee having sampled
   only January, February and March. Falke conceded this explanation as a partial factor in 1959 and
   withdrew the concession the moment he read the bulletin: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing
   in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July."

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware? No — and this is the sharpest fact in the
record.** The board sat three times, and **two of the three sittings were held in the station's own
weigh room**, directly beneath the loft in which the condemned crate had been lying since 1923 —
twenty feet of ladder, as Duncan Keddie measures it, with Cleve Oram standing outside on the
platform in the rain. The board watched the scales tried and a test made, on the *new* glass, and
its chairman says he did not understand the test then and does not now. Worse, the record of the
condemnation was on the table: the fourth of the four exhibits was the state dairy inspector's
circuit notebook **for 1923**, laid beside the station's own receiving book, and, as Kyle puts it,
he could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second
beside the fourth and looked. The answer was in the building, in the exhibits, and in the ledger,
and the board found "no cause established."

**What did the tester himself believe?** **Ansel Keddie believed it was the glass — from the summer
of 1911.** His son, then seventeen, remembers him coming home saying "it was something in the
glass," saying it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not his way. He never dared put
it in the book: what he wrote against those nine weeks was **a question mark**, which lay unread for
forty-seven years and became Vaught's §IV. He would not put down what he could not prove — against
a suspicion about a piece of glass stood the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of
every station in the county — so he kept on for another fourteen years, writing down every figure
the glass gave him, and those figures were what convicted him. Publicly he said only that he had
tested as he was taught and as the state man had watched him do it, thirty-one times a week for
twenty-nine seasons, that he could not say why the figures were what they were, and that he had
never taken a pound of cream. One qualification, from Frick and not from the minutes: in the
corridor at Ashlin he said privately that he had always thought the patrons were at fault and would
not say so in the room. So he accused nobody publicly, and he was not without suspicions — but the
thing he had actually suspected, fourteen years earlier and correctly, was the glass.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** (149 words)

For twenty-six years the Larrow Valley creamery's skimming station at Ostrey Hollow tested its
patrons' milk 5 per cent low, because a lot of pipettes bought from Tarnet in 1897 delivered 16.72
c.c. where their stems were blown 17.6. Under Article VII the station's test governed the pay, so
six farms lost 76,000 lb of butterfat — about $19,000 — while the Association's own ledgers credited
the same amount, yearly, as "station gain." Nobody looked at the glass. They blamed the farmers'
water, the tester's honesty, and the wet hay, and in 1925 dismissed a blameless man four days before
awarding $3,800 for no established cause. It took eight records because no witness could see it
alone: the answer lay in the space between a farmer's weigh book, a cheesemaker's vat, a clerk's
ledger line, a broken measure in 1911, an invoice, and a crate in a loft nobody climbed to for
thirty-five years.
