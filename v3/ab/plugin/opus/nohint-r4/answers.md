## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1 — The pay rule and the station at Ostrey Hollow (1889–1897)

Farmers meeting at Larrow Green organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association in 1889. Gideon Vose organized it, served as its first president and wrote its by-laws. The main creamery was built at Larrow Green in 1891. Article seven of the by-laws is the hinge of the whole history: each patron was paid on the pounds of **butterfat** his milk contained, as found by the test **at the station where he delivered**, and not on the weight of the milk. If patrons had been paid by weight, nothing that followed would have cost anyone a dollar. The article's date of adoption cannot be determined: the printed by-laws are undated and the early minute book was lost in an office move (r22).

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green. It served mainly Hessel Bottom, six farms on wet ground. The station opened on **4 May 1896** (r22). Hazel's "ninety-five" is the building winter, and she admits she has no memory for years. The station was a two-storey board building on the flat by the creek. It had a covered, waist-high platform on the road side, an intake (weigh) room holding the scale, vat and separator, and a loft above that was reached by a ladder nailed to the wall by the door (r21). Milk was weighed in, sampled, tested for fat and separated. Cleve Oram's father hauled the cream down to Larrow Green by wagon, and the skim went back to the farmers.

**Ansel Keddie** (born 1861) was tester and separator man from the day the station opened. He held the post for **twenty-nine seasons (1896–1924)** and kept the station test book in his own hand from 1896 (r18, r22, r24).

For its first season the station **borrowed its testing glass from the Larrow Green creamery**. Its own outfit went into use on **1 May 1897**, a date given by Junia Ammon's account of the invoice (r20) and by Orra's diary entry that same day: "done borrowing of Larrow Green" (r24). I reject Kyle's date of 1 May 1898 because two dated sources outweigh one memory. The first season's milk was carried on the creamery's own books and never filed with the station papers. The only figure for it, Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds", is admitted hearsay that appears in no book. In its regular years the station received about 1,600,000 lb of milk a year. It closed in 1946 and was later used to store hay tools (r21).

*Documents: r22, r18, r20, r24, r21, r23, r17.*

### Story 2 — The Tarnet invoice: seventy-two short pipettes (1897, 1923, 1931, 1949)

Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet, founded by Marcus Ammon, supplied about half the creamery **glass** in the state: test bottles, pipettes, acid measures and burettes. On **14 April 1897** it invoiced the Larrow Valley Association for the Ostrey Hollow station's outfit:

- 6 doz. milk test pipettes, marked 17.6 c.c., at $0.35: **$25.20**
- 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at $0.22: **$31.68**
- 1 acid measure: **$0.90**
- 1 four-bottle hand tester: **$18.00**
- Freight: **$2.15**
- Total: **$77.93**

The line items add up to $77.93. Junia's prose and the Chronicle both say "$77.39". That is a transposition, and the facsimile's own arithmetic breaks the tie. The invoice bought **72 pipettes**, which went into use on 1 May 1897. Hazel's "crate came out in ninety-nine" and Oram's "five dozen" are both wrong.

Each pipette was blown to deliver 17.6 c.c., the standard charge for a butterfat test and the charge the bottle neck is ruled for. In fact the lot delivered about **16.72 c.c.**, which is **0.88 c.c. or 5% short** (see Story 7 for how I derived this). A lot that is uniformly short passes a spot check of the first, tenth and fiftieth piece, because every piece is wrong in the same way.

In summer 1923 the state inspector condemned the lot. The Association wrote on **28 June 1923** ordering six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles. Ammon's reply of **2 July** said the order was entered and went on: "We regret the trouble. Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name." The new glass was in use in July 1923.

Junia Ammon, the firm's historian, wrote in 1949 at the age of sixty. She believes a reground mould, or a gauge knocked off its nail, made the lot run short **by accident**. The works books, which recorded glass, gauge, workman and day, **burned in the 1931 fire** that took the old third floor, so her theory cannot be proved. In 1958 the Chronicle claimed the lot was "put off knowingly" and that the 1923 silence was "proof". That is an inference, not evidence. **Whether Ammon knew cannot be determined.** Junia's regret is for the thin letter, not the accident. She also writes that the house sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900, which is contradicted by the 1897 invoice she reprints.

*Documents: r20 (invoice and letter in facsimile), r19, r22, r21, r17, r18.*

### Story 3 — Hessel Bottom, the low test and Emmet Brant's weigh book (1896–1925)

Almost from the day the station opened, its milk tested low. Over the long period of complaint the station averaged **3.61**, while Larrow Green averaged **3.78**, an ordinary figure for the county. The true Ostrey Hollow average was **3.80**. The shortfall was **0.19 points**, and it held **flat** in June and February and in wet years and dry. A feed effect would rise and fall with the season. A flat shortfall is what a proportional instrument error looks like (r17, r22).

Hessel Bottom was **six farms** on wet ground known for coarse bog hay. **Emmet Brant** farmed the largest piece and hauled the most milk. Four of the six farmers hauled over the ridge in the summer flush to Jerome Cudd's cheese factory at Alder Corners in Vessey County. That factory's test book gives a natural control: **the same milk tested 0.19 higher there** (r17).

Emmet weighed every haul on his own scale before it left the farm and wrote the station's intake weight beside his own. I take the start of the weigh book as **1 January 1897**, from Duncan. Hazel says 1900, but she disclaims any memory for years, so I prefer Duncan without being certain. On the **1910** leaf Emmet recorded **200,000 lb** of milk delivered, "his scale and theirs agreeing", and paid on a test of **3.42**. The agreement of the scales rules out short weighing. A reading of 3.42 taken with glass 5% short means the true figure was **3.60**. Emmet was credited with **6,840 lb** of butterfat and was owed **7,200 lb**, so **360 lb** went uncredited, about **$90** at the period's average of 25¢/lb.

The first complaint on record dates from **1902** (r22). Orra's diary for March 1902 notes store talk that "our milk up here runs poorer". Complaints were renewed at every annual meeting for about twenty-five years. In **January 1908** a four-man Association committee came up by sleigh, dined at Emmet's, and inspected herds and barns but not the station. In **March 1908** it reported that the wet hay thinned the cream. Emmet answered that "the bottom fed his father and it will feed him" (r24).

The weigh book passed from Emmet to Roy on Emmet's death in **1929**. Roy **lent** it (not bequeathed it) to Ivy in 1958 and got it back. On Roy's death in **1962** it went to Hazel, who gave it to the Ordell County Historical Rooms at Ashlin in **1963**. Emmet also lent it to the **1925** arbitration.

*Documents: r17, r18, r22, r23, r24, r19.*

### Story 4 — Nine weeks in 1911: the borrowed measure

On **8 May 1911** the station's own measuring glass broke. Orra's diary for that day reads: "A. home cross about a broken measure." A measure borrowed from elsewhere was used until **10 July 1911**, exactly **nine weeks** (63 days). Throughout those weeks the station's tests stood **markedly higher**. On the day the station went back to its own glass they **fell back to the old level** (r22). Nothing else changed: the herds, pasture, season, machinery and tester were the same. The return to low readings came in midsummer, on full pasture, with no change of feed.

Ansel wrote the fact in his test book and put a **question mark** against it. His son Duncan, then seventeen, remembered him coming home that summer and saying "it was something in the glass". He said it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not like him. He **never dared put it in the book**. He would not write down what he could not prove, and all he had was a suspicion about a piece of glass, set against an outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county. So he went on for fourteen more years, faithfully recording every figure the short glass gave him, and those figures were what convicted him (r18). In October 1911 Duncan left to teach at Ashlin.

In the same year the State Agricultural College ran a feeding trial on bog hay, published as **Bulletin No. 214**. It found that bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage lowered the test by **no more than 0.05 in winter and by nothing measurable on summer pasture**. At the time nobody set that result beside the nine weeks at Ostrey Hollow. **Selby Vose** also became manager of the Association in 1911 (r23).

The 1958 bulletin treats the interval as decisive. Taken as an experiment, the glass was the only variable and the result was clear: the fault lay in the station's own measuring glass. That rules out both the hay theory and the charge of theft in one stroke, because the same man tested under the same supervision and his figures rose. The episode could not show how short the glass was, since the borrowed measure was never calibrated, and it could not show why the glass was short. Answering those questions took the 1923 condemnation and the 1958 measurements.

*Documents: r22, r24, r18, r23.*

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### Story 5 — "Station gain, as usual": the office, the ledger and the water circular (1911–1923)

**Bertram Kyle** sat at the outer desk of the Cooperative's office at Larrow Green for twenty-five years, facing the manager's door. Every year the Ostrey Hollow station account held three figures and a sentence:

1. The pounds of butterfat **credited** to patrons on the station test.
2. The pounds of butterfat **received** at Larrow Green in the station's cream.
3. The difference, entered as **"station gain … five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account."**

The bookkeeper, **Wilbur Teague**, wrote that line every year he served. Kyle copied it fair, and it was read aloud at the annual meeting.

For the arbitration Kyle compiled the long-run totals: **40,000,000 lb** of milk (1,600,000 lb a year over **25** years) and an average butterfat price of **25¢/lb**. At 3.61 the patrons were credited with **1,444,000 lb** of fat. At the true 3.80, about **1,520,000 lb** reached the creamery. The "gain" of **76,000 lb** is **5.26% of the fat credited**, which is Teague's "five and a quarter". It is **5% of the fat received**, the exact size of the glass error. The error sat in plain sight with a name on it.

**Teague** never asked anybody anything in forty years. **Selby Vose**, Gideon's son (not his nephew), was manager from 1911 for about a quarter century. He was courteous to every caller, but he read only totals: he put his thumb on the bottom figure and signed.

In **June 1919** Kyle typed a circular, signed by Vose alone, warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons about water in their milk. Ansel refused to mail it or leave it on the platform. He carried it himself to **eleven families** and stood at each door while the man read it (Orra's diary). Hazel remembers nine. I prefer the diary, written that week, over a small child's memory sixty years on. Hazel's mother burned hers, and no one answered.

Two days later Emmet stood in the Keddie yard and asked Ansel why he had carried the paper if he did not believe it. Ansel said he would not have another man carry it. Emmet never entered the house again before he died in spring 1929. That contradicts Duncan's "at our table on a Sunday … for thirty-six years", and I prefer the contemporaneous diary.

Two things ruled out water. Strawn's lactometer never found added water at the station from 1908 on. And under payment by butterfat, water adds weight but no fat, so it could not raise a patron's pay.

*Documents: r23, r24, r17, r22, r18.*

### Story 6 — The condemned glass and the arbitration (1923–1934)

On **12 June 1923**, on an ordinary circuit visit, **Merle Strawn** ran the station's pipettes against his burette. Strawn was the state dairy inspector for the Ordell circuit and was related to no one in the valley. The pipettes would not deliver their mark. He **condemned the whole lot** on the spot, told the manager to order new glass, and noted it in his 1923 notebook. He drew no inference about the years that had gone before.

The date is fixed by several sources: Falke's exact date, Ammon's letters of 28 June and 2 July 1923, Orra's "new glass" entry of July 1923, and Duncan's reference to the 1923 notebook. I reject Kyle's "twenty-two" and Hazel's "twenty-one". The new glass was in use from July 1923. From **1924** the station averaged **3.85**, and **the same tester** was still making the tests (r22). The condemned crate went up into the loft over the intake room, and nobody connected the two things.

In **January 1925** the patrons petitioned, and Emmet's name was on the paper. In March the test book was impounded. A board of arbitration, chaired by an Ashlin attorney, sat from **17 March to 4 May 1925**. It held three sittings: one at Ashlin, and **two at the station, in the weigh room, directly under the crate** and about twenty feet of ladder away (r21, r24, r18). Kyle carried four books in and out: the station test book, the receiving book, Emmet's weigh book, and Strawn's 1923 notebook. No one laid them side by side.

Three theories were argued before the board: water, theft (urged by an officer of the Association), and hay. **Nobody examined the glassware.**

Ansel was **dismissed on 30 April 1925**, which Orra summed up as "twenty-nine seasons and a letter". Four days later the board found no cause established and awarded the patrons **$3,800, without admission**, charged to the station account. Oram's figure of $3,500, Kyle's date of 1924 and Hazel's 1926 are wrong. Roy Brant never went to another Association meeting.

After his dismissal Ansel rebuilt the cow shed, whistling as he worked, and at sixty-six he was doing carpentry for a quarter of his old pay. He died at home in **February 1934**. The funeral was well attended, and nobody named the charge. As Ansel himself had once said, "a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name." His widow Orra (1866–1948) did not live to see him cleared.

*Documents: r22, r23, r24, r21, r18, r17, r20.*

### Story 7 — The crate in the loft: Ivy Keddie's proof (1958)

**Ivy Keddie** was born in 1931, the daughter of Duncan and **granddaughter of Ansel**. She was a graduate student in dairy husbandry at the State Agricultural College at Farland. She assembled evidence from several places:

- **Emmet's weigh book**, borrowed from Roy Brant.
- **Strawn's 1923 notebook**, which had been transferred to the college library in 1947.
- **Alder Corners test figures**, copied out and sent by Mrs. Rosalie Cudd, Jerome's daughter.

In **February 1958** she climbed the ladder at the old station and found the crate under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff, where it had lain for **thirty-five years**. She took it east to Farland. On **9 March 1958** she measured the **nine** surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette. Falke and Oram's independent memory both give nine, and I reject the Chronicle's eleven. All nine were marked 17.6 c.c., and all delivered short **in the same proportion**.

I take the average delivery as **16.72 c.c.**, which is **0.88 c.c. or 5% short**. The Chronicle prints 16.27. I read that as a transposition, because 16.27 would mean a 7.6% error, and that fits neither the 0.19 shortfall on a true 3.80 nor the 5¼%-of-credited ledger gain. Both of those independently give a factor of 0.95. A short charge of milk in a bottle ruled for a full charge reads low in the same proportion, so a true 3.80 read as 3.61.

**Bulletin No. 471** (October 1958), by Professor **Leland Vaught** and Miss Keddie, covered **25 full delivery years**. It counted **76,000 lb** of butterfat never credited. Oram's "67,000" is a transposition of this. At 25¢/lb that comes to **$19,000**. I derive that figure and reject the Chronicle's $19,600. The patrons had received $3,800, **exactly one fifth**. No milk was watered and no cream was stolen.

On 3 October 1958 the Ashlin Chronicle ran the story on its front page as "Old Ordell Wrong Righted". It quoted Ivy: "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not." The bulletin came twenty-four years after his death and thirty-three after his dismissal. In 1959 the crate and the test book were deposited with the Historical Rooms at Ashlin.

*Documents: r19, r22, r18, r21, r17, r23.*

**The family.** **Ansel Keddie** (1861–1934) married **Orra Brant** in 1889. Orra was born in **1866**: her diary preface gives that year, her August 1930 entry says she was 64, and Duncan says she died in 1948 aged 82. Duncan's own "1868" is wrong. Orra was **Emmet Brant's** younger sister. Hazel says she was a **half**-sister, by their father's second wife. The sources cannot settle whether she was a half or a full sister, because no pages of Orra's diary survive from before her marriage.

The next generations:

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy and Ansel.** Ivy is Ansel's **granddaughter**. Duncan was Ansel and Orra's son (r18), and Ivy is Duncan's daughter (r18, r17). Duncan wrote this "plainly" because he had seen it misprinted. Oram also calls her the granddaughter. The Chronicle (r19) and page two of Falke's report (r22) call her a "great-niece". Falke withdrew that in his note ("I am informed, and have verified… the error on page two is mine"), and the Chronicle's own account (her father taught school in Ashlin, and she is a Keddie by name) fits a son's daughter. Both "great-niece" versions probably come from one source, the press release or bulletin coverage, so they count as a single source. The family's direct account and Falke's verified correction settle it.

**B2. Ivy and Hazel.** Ivy and Hazel are **second cousins**. Orra and Emmet were siblings, so their children Duncan and Roy were first cousins, and the daughters of first cousins are second cousins. Duncan (r18) and Hazel (r17) derive this independently, and both reject "cousin once removed" and "aunt/niece". Oram's "first cousin once removed" is wrong because Ivy and Hazel are the same generation. What the sources leave open is whether **Orra was a full or a half sister** of Emmet. Hazel says half, by their father's second wife. Duncan says only "brother and sister, the younger of the two", which does not exclude a half-sibling. Orra's early pages were not preserved. If she was a half sister, Duncan and Roy were half first cousins and Ivy and Hazel are half second cousins. The degree of cousinship is the same either way, but the sources cannot settle whether it is full or half.

**B3. Selby Vose's father.** His father was **Gideon Vose**, who organized the Association, served as its first president and **wrote its by-laws**, including article seven, which paid every patron on the fat found by his own station's test (r18, r23; the "nephew" story is gossip). This matters because Selby, as manager from 1911, inherited his father's rule and was its guardian. The rule turned a 5% short pipette into a 5% cut in every Ostrey Hollow patron's pay. Selby also signed the "station gain, 5¼%" ledger sheets every year without reading them, and he signed the 1919 water circular. The rule was his father's, the ledger that showed the error went across his desk, and he accused the patrons instead of questioning the test.

**B4. The Voses and the Keddies.** They were **not related**. The only claim of kinship is Oram's (r21), and it is admitted thirdhand gossip ("my mother had it from her mother… I could not draw you the line"). Duncan (r18) denies it outright. He attributes the story to confusion with the unrelated Ashlin Voses and to town gossip, and notes that his mother, a meticulous genealogist of the valley, never mentioned any Vose connection. I prefer the family insider's direct statement to gossip. The only link was through the Association: Gideon's rule, Selby's circular, and Ansel as the Association's employee.

## Section C — Math

### C1

**(a) How far the tests were off.** The tests read **5% low**, a factor of **0.95**. The pipettes were marked 17.6 c.c. but delivered about 16.72 c.c.:

- 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95, so each charge was 0.88 c.c. (5%) short.
- The bottle neck is ruled for a full 17.6 c.c. charge, so the fat reading falls in the same proportion.

The Chronicle prints "16.27", which would give a factor of 0.924. I treat that as a transposition, because two independent figures both give 0.95:

- the 0.19 shortfall on a true 3.80 (3.61 ÷ 3.80 = 0.95, from r22 and r17);
- Kyle's ledger gain of 5¼% of the fat credited (1 ÷ 0.95 − 1 = 5.26%).

**(b) True average and shortfall.** The true average test was **3.80**. The recorded average was 3.61, so the tests were **0.19 points** short (3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80). This fits Larrow Green's ordinary 3.78. Falke's 1924 figure of 3.85 is a single source and I did not use it.

**(c) Butterfat not credited.**

| | Calculation | Pounds of fat |
|---|---|---|
| Milk received | 40,000,000 lb (Kyle: 1,600,000 × 25 years) | — |
| True fat | 40,000,000 × 0.0380 | 1,520,000 |
| Fat credited | 40,000,000 × 0.0361 | 1,444,000 |
| **Not credited** | 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 | **76,000** |

Oram's "67,000" is a transposition.

**(d) Value and fraction received.** 76,000 lb × $0.25 = **$19,000**. The Chronicle's $19,600 is rejected. The patrons received $3,800, and 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **1/5 (20%)**. That the award comes out to exactly one fifth supports the $19,000 figure.

### C2 — The largest patron (Emmet Brant) in 1910

Milk delivered: 200,000 lb, paid on a test of 3.42 (Hazel's 1910 leaf).

**(a)** He was credited with 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of butterfat.

**(b)** The true test was 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60, so he was owed 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb. He was owed and not credited **360 lb**.

The station-average 0.19 applies to the station's 3.61 average. For a herd testing 3.42, the proportional shortfall is 0.18 points. Applying a flat 0.19 instead would give 380 lb and $95.

**(c)** 360 × $0.25 = **$90**. This uses Kyle's long-run average price. The actual 1910 price cannot be determined from the sources I read.

### C3 — Over the 25 full delivery years

**(a)** Fat credited to patrons: **1,444,000 lb**.

**(b)** Fat reaching Larrow Green in the station cream: about **1,520,000 lb**. This assumes the cream carried the true fat, with no separation loss.

**(c)** The difference is 76,000 lb. Which base you take the percentage of matters:

- As a percentage **of the fat credited**: 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.26%**. This is the ledger's "station gain, five and a quarter per cent".
- As a percentage **of the fat received**: 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5.00%**. This is the true size of the glass error.

The 5.26% is computed on the wrong base. The patrons' true fat was 5% larger than the figure they were paid on.

### C4

**(a)** The invoice total was **$77.93**:

25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93

The line items check: 72 × 0.35 = 25.20 and 144 × 0.22 = 31.68. The "$77.39" in r20's prose and in r19 is a transposition.

**(b)** The invoice bought **72 pipettes** (6 dozen). Oram's "five dozen" is wrong. Each was on average **0.88 c.c.** short (17.6 − 16.72).

### C5

**(a)** The faulty glass was in use from **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923**, about **26 years and 6 weeks** (26 years, 1 month, 11 days). The money calculation runs over **25 full delivery years**, from Kyle's 40,000,000 ÷ 1,600,000.

The numbers differ because the first and last seasons were only partly on the short glass. The glass arrived at the start of the 1897 season, which was not a full year of the station's accounts, and it was withdrawn in mid-June 1923. Only complete years were counted. Identifying those 25 years as 1898–1922 is my own inference; the sources I read do not name them.

**(b)** The first season was **1896**, when the station opened on 4 May on borrowed glass. The only figure is Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" (**1,100,000 lb**). He admits it is hearsay "not in any book I ever ruled", because the first season's sheets were kept with the creamery's own books. On r17–r24 alone, this **cannot be confirmed from a document**. 1,100,000 lb is the only figure offered and should be read as unverified.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment.** It ran from **8 May to 10 July 1911**, nine weeks (r22; the break is confirmed the same day in Orra's diary, r24).

- **Variable:** the measuring glass, the station's own versus a borrowed measure.
- **Held constant:** herds, pasture and hay, season, tester, supervision, machinery, scales and acid.
- **Result:** the tests rose markedly for the nine weeks and dropped back to the old level the day the station's own glass returned.
- **What it shows that a calculation cannot:** a calculation can only show that the numbers *could* be explained by short glass. The interval shows that the glass *caused* the low readings. Changing it alone moved the tests up and then down again, at a fixed point in midsummer with no change of feed. That rules out hay, water and theft, since the same man was testing throughout.
- **What it does not establish:** how short the station's glass was, because the borrowed measure's accuracy was unknown and "markedly higher" is not a measurement. It also does not show which pipettes were faulty or why, whether the error ran back to 1897, or anything about Ammon's knowledge. The 1923 condemnation and the 1958 measurements were needed for those.

**D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream.** There are four reasons, drawn from different narrators and different underlying records:

1. **Kyle's ledger (r23):** every year **more** fat reached Larrow Green than was credited, a "station gain" of 5¼%. Skimming cream would leave the creamery with **less** than was credited, not more.
2. **The 1911 interval (r22, r24, r18):** the same man, under the same supervision, produced higher tests with borrowed glass.
3. **The 1924 recovery (r22):** with new glass from July 1923, the station averaged **3.85 in 1924 with Ansel still testing** until April 1925.
4. **The Alder Corners and Hessel Bottom evidence (r17):** the same milk tested 0.19 higher at another factory, and the shortfall ran flat through the seasons. That is consistent with a proportional instrument error, not with discretionary skimming.

These are independent because they rest on separate records kept by different people: the Association's office books, the station test book and a wife's diary, the station's post-1923 figures, and a rival company's test book. None depends on another.

Reasons 1, 2 and 3 would **all still hold if the glass had never been measured in 1958**. Reason 1 was in the Association's own ledger all along.

Did the bookkeeper ever question the entries? **No.** Wilbur Teague wrote "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" every year and "never asked anybody anything in forty years" (r23). My sources say the theft charge was "urged by an officer of the Association" (r22) and do not name Teague as its author. Whoever made it, the bookkeeper's own ledger line refuted it, and he never questioned it.

**D3. Did the manager know the tests ran low?** **Yes**, he knew the station's tests ran low. He did **not** know, or never looked into, why. The evidence:

- In June 1919, four years before the condemnation, Selby Vose signed the water circular (r23, r24). A warning about watering only makes sense as an explanation for low tests, so he knew they were low and chose a cause.
- Complaints had been made at every annual meeting since 1902.
- Every year the ledger he signed showed the fat credited to be about 5¼% below the fat received.

Kyle says he read only the bottom figure and "never read the lines above them". So he probably never connected the "station gain" line to the complaints. His knowledge was of the low tests, not of their cause. Nothing suggests he suspected the glass before Strawn condemned it.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. What caused the shortfall.** One lot of 72 Ammon pipettes, sold on 14 April 1897 and marked 17.6 c.c., delivered only about 16.72 c.c., **5% short**. Every butterfat test at Ostrey Hollow put 5% too little milk into a bottle whose neck was ruled for 17.6 c.c., so every reading came out 5% low. At the station's true level of 3.80, that meant a flat **0.19** points (3.80 → 3.61) in every month. Under article seven, the patrons were paid 5% short on every pound of fat.

- **Why this size:** the reading falls in the same proportion as the charge, 0.88 ÷ 17.6 = 5%. The same 5% shows up in the office ledger as the "station gain" of 5¼% of the fat credited.
- **Why it began when it did:** the glass went into use on 1 May 1897, replacing the borrowed Larrow Green glass.
- **Why it ended when it did:** Strawn condemned the lot on 12 June 1923 and new glass was in use from July. From 1924 the tests were normal, with the same tester still at work.
- **Why nothing else had to change:** the error was entirely inside the measuring vessel. The cows, hay, scales, acid, the tester's honesty and his method were all constant and all correct. That is why the 1911 swap moved the tests up and down while everything else stayed the same.

**The three competing explanations, and what defeats each:**

1. **Added water**, advanced by the manager, Selby Vose, in his 1919 circular. Strawn's lactometer never found water at the station from 1908 on. Under payment by fat, water adds no fat and cannot raise pay. The Alder Corners test of the same milk was normal.
2. **The tester stealing cream**, urged at the 1925 hearing by an officer of the Association, and believed by Oram's father. The ledger shows *more* fat reaching the creamery than was credited. The 1911 swap raised the tests with the same man testing. The 1924 tests were normal with Ansel still at the station.
3. **Wet bog hay thinning the cream**, advanced by the 1908 Association committee and a 1926 Ashlin veterinarian, and conceded by Falke before he withdrew it. Bulletin 214 found at most 0.05 lower in winter and nothing on summer pasture, so hay cannot produce 0.19 in July. The shortfall was flat through the seasons. The 1911 swap changed the tests in midsummer with no change of feed. The same Hessel Bottom milk tested 0.19 higher at Alder Corners.

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glass?** **No.** The glass lay crated in the loft over the weigh room where two of the board's three sittings were held, but it was never brought down. Kyle's list of what went before the board has four books and no glassware. The board did have Strawn's 1923 notebook recording the condemnation, and nobody connected it to the tests.

**What the tester believed.** Ansel believed **the cause was in the glass**. In 1911 he came home saying "it was something in the glass" and marked the interval with a question mark in his book (r18, r22). He never wrote it down as a finding because he could not prove it. That is the sense of Ivy's "he never knew" (r19): he suspected, but he never had proof.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** In 1897 the Ostrey Hollow skimming station began testing its patrons' milk with 72 pipettes from Tarnet, costing $77.93 for the whole outfit. The pipettes delivered 5% short. Because the Association paid on butterfat by station test, the Hessel Bottom farmers were underpaid by 0.19 points (3.61 against a true 3.80) for twenty-six years, about 76,000 lb of fat worth $19,000. The valley blamed water, hay and the tester, Ansel Keddie. An inspector condemned the glass in 1923 without drawing a conclusion. In 1925 an arbitration board sat beneath the crate, paid the patrons $3,800 and dismissed Keddie, who died uncleared in 1934. In 1958 his granddaughter Ivy measured the crated glass and proved the cause. The proof was scattered through eight separate records: an invoice, a weigh book, a diary, a ledger's "station gain", a rival company's test book, an inspector's notebook, a feeding bulletin and the crate itself. None was enough alone.
