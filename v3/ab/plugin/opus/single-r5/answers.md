# Answer Sheet — Part 1

## Section A — Reconstruction (stories 1–4)

### Story 1 — The Association, Article VII and the Ostrey Hollow station (1889–1925)

Farmers of the Larrow Valley organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association in 1889, meeting in the schoolhouse at Larrow Green. Gideon Vose organized it, wrote its by-laws and was its president from 1889 to 1911; he died in 1917. The main creamery was built at Larrow Green in 1891. The Association's office was at Ashlin, over a harness shop.

By-law Article VII paid each patron by the pounds of butterfat "ascertained by test", and "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." A station's test was therefore final for its own patrons. The year the article was adopted cannot be determined. The sources give 1891 (Rundle), 1892 and 1893 (Grigg). The printed copy says only "adopted at the annual meeting", and the 1889–92 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move.

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley. It mainly served the six farms of Hessel Bottom, a wet meadow. At the station milk was weighed, sampled and tested: a 17.6 c.c. pipette charge, acid, a four-bottle hand tester, and a reading taken with dividers. The milk was then separated. The cream went down to Larrow Green on the cream wagon six days a week, and the skim went back to the farms.

Ansel Keddie (1861–1934) was tester and separator man from the day the station opened, 4 May 1896. The first leaf of his test book reads "Begun this 4th day of May, 1896." He stayed twenty-nine seasons, 1896–1924.

His 1922 summary leaf gives the record:
- 1896: 3.80
- 1897: part year
- 1898: 3.61, and 3.61 ± 0.03 every year after, except nine weeks of 1911 at 3.80
- 1924: 3.80

Larrow Green's own average was 3.78. The station took in about 1,600,000 lb of milk a year, or 40,000,000 lb over the 25 full years 1898–1922. That is the figure the clerk Kyle compiled for the arbitration; Teague's 44 million is wrong. Butterfat averaged 25¢ a pound over the period. Complaints that the Hollow tested low started in 1902 and went on for a generation.

None of the officers ever watched the station work:
- Selby Vose, Gideon's son, manager 1913–1938
- Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945
- Hobart Sill, buttermaker 1893–1934
- Effie Loomis, secretary 1901–1940

Keddie was put out on 30 April 1925. The station closed in 1946 and was later used to store hay tools.

*Documents:* r01, r02, r03, r05, r12, r14, r15, r21, r22, r23, r24.

### Story 2 — One lot of glass: borrowed, bought, condemned, measured (1896–1958)

**Borrowed glass, 1896–97.** In its first season the station borrowed its testing glass from Larrow Green: it was buttermaker Hobart Sill's own outfit, which came back the next spring with one piece chipped. Keddie's first leaf says "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes." His later letter's "Ashlin creamery" is contradicted by that leaf and by Sill, Nym, Kyle and Orra Keddie. The borrowed glass read 3.80.

**The Ammon outfit, 1897.** The station's own outfit came from Ammon & Sons, Tarnet, invoiced on 14 April 1897:

| Item | Cost |
|---|---:|
| 6 doz. pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at 35¢ | $25.20 |
| 12 doz. test bottles | $31.68 |
| Acid measure | $0.90 |
| Four-bottle hand tester | $18.00 |
| Freight | $2.15 |
| **Total** | **$77.93** |

The lines add up to $77.93; the "$77.39" printed elsewhere is a transposition. The outfit went into use on **1 May 1897** (invoice, Nym, and Orra's diary entry for that day), not 1898. From then on the station read 3.61.

**Condemned, 1923.** Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, had the circuit from 1907 and was related to nobody in the valley. He was issued burettes in 1921 but did not run one at the station in 1922. On **12 June 1923** he did: "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate…"
- The date comes from his notebook. It is confirmed by the Association's order to Ammon dated 28 June, which a 12 July condemnation could not have come before.
- Ammon replied on 2 July: "Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name."
- New glass was in use from July 1923, and the 1924 average was 3.80. Nobody connected the two facts.
- Ammon's historian guessed that a reground mold had made the whole lot consistently short. The works books burned in 1931, so whether it was an accident or deliberate cannot be determined.

**Found and measured, 1958.** The crate, stenciled AMMON & SONS TARNET, went into the loft over the station's intake room and lay there under a tarpaulin for 35 years. Ivy Keddie found it in February 1958; nine pipettes survived, two of them chipped. On 9 March, at Farland, she measured them against a calibrated burette:
- mean delivery 16.72 c.c. against a 17.6 mark
- short by 0.88 c.c.
- 95 per cent of the mark, in the same proportion from one pipette to the next

A short charge gives a proportionally short reading: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80. The crate went to the Ordell County Historical Rooms at Ashlin in 1959.

*Documents:* r03, r04, r06, r07, r14, r18, r19, r20, r21, r22, r23, r24.

### Story 3 — The nine weeks of 1911

On 8 May 1911 the station's pipette broke. The test book records it, and so does Orra Keddie's diary for that day: "A. home cross about a broken measure." A borrowed measure was used until the station's own glass was back on 10 July. That is 63 days, exactly nine weeks, not the "six weeks" Teague and Kepp give.

- Through those nine weeks the tests stood at 3.80.
- When the Ammon glass came back they fell to 3.61.
- Keddie wrote: "Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."

His son Duncan, then seventeen, remembers him coming home saying it was "something in the glass", twice. But he would not write down what he could not prove, and he went on with the same glass for another fourteen years.

**Where the borrowed measure came from.** Most likely it came up from Larrow Green on the cream wagon, as Ivy Keddie says. This is my inference: Larrow Green was the station's usual lender, and the wagon ran daily, which fits a borrowing that began on 8 May. Roy Brant says "the state man left his own measure that June," but that does not fit a May start. Strawn's own June entry records only a routine test: "Nothing here to note."

**What the interval shows.** Only the measuring glass changed. The tester, herds, pasture, scales, acid, separator, supervision and season were all the same. The rise of 0.19 is 5 per cent of 3.80, the same proportion by which the glass was later found short. Kepp's objection that these were flush-season "June and July" figures fails for two reasons: the interval started on 8 May, and in every other year the gap was the same 0.19 in July as in February. Changing only the glass raised the figures, which rules out theft and water (same man, same milk) and hay (same feed).

Nobody who mattered noticed. Strawn wrote nothing about it, and the 1925 board never argued it. Vaught called it "unread for forty-seven years" when Bulletin 471 finally took it up in 1958.

*Documents:* r03, r04, r05, r06, r09, r11, r16, r18, r22, r24.

### Story 4 — "Five and a quarter per cent, as usual": the station gain

Every year the Association's Ostrey Hollow account showed three figures:
1. butterfat credited to the patrons on the station test;
2. butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream;
3. the difference, entered as "station gain … five and a quarter per cent, as usual".

The ledger's station column runs from 1897. The 1898 entry reads: 57,760 lb credited, 60,800 lb received, gain 3,040 lb. That gain is 5.26 per cent of the fat credited and exactly 5 per cent of the fat received (57,760 ÷ 60,800 = 0.95). Teague's "of fat actually in the cream" and Vaught's "of butterfat actually delivered" both name the wrong base.

**Who saw the line.**
- **Wilbur Teague**, bookkeeper, called the line "my own device… 1905", yet quotes it from 1898, so it predates him.
- **Hobart Sill** weighed the Hollow cream himself and entered it in the receiving book. It always ran heavier than the office sheet, which he waved away as "cream for you."
- **Bertram Kyle**, a clerk for 25 years, copied the line every year into the statement read to the annual meeting.
- **Selby Vose** signed each sheet with his thumb on the bottom figure.

**The arithmetic over the 25 full years.**
- Credited: 40,000,000 lb × 3.61% = 1,444,000 lb.
- Received at Larrow Green: 1,520,000 lb.
- Gain: 76,000 lb, which is the patrons' shortfall itself.

More fat arrived than the patrons were paid for:
- Stolen cream would have arrived light.
- Watered milk would have arrived exactly as tested, and so would milk thinned by poor hay.
- Only a test reading 5 per cent low predicts a surplus equal to 5 per cent of what was received.

Yet Teague told the 1925 board "Cream is portable… The remedy is two men or a lock," which pointed at theft. When he raised his suspicion with Vose in 1916 he was told he was "bookkeeper and not its conscience." He never questioned his own gain line. The receiving book and Strawn's 1923 notebook were both in front of the board, and nobody set one beside the other. Rundle's note added in press in 1959 finally drew the conclusion.

*Documents:* r02, r05, r07, r11, r12, r13, r14, r23.


# Answer Sheet — Part 2

## Section A — Reconstruction (stories 5–8)

### Story 5 — Emmet Brant's weigh book and the two families

Emmet Brant (1854–1929) farmed the largest place on Hessel Bottom, sixty-odd acres with 38 cows, and hauled the most milk to the station.

**Start date.** From **1 January 1897** he weighed his milk on his own scale before it left the farm and wrote the station's weight beside it. That date is given by Frick's exhibit list, Nym's accession record and Duncan Keddie. Roy says 1898; Hazel says 1900 but admits she does not keep years. The two weights always agreed, so weighing was never the problem.

**The 1910 leaf**, with "even" underlined by Emmet: 200,000 lb delivered, paid on a test of 3.42, with fat at 24¢.
- On true glass the milk was 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60, which is 7,200 lb of fat.
- He was credited with 6,840 lb.
- The difference was 360 lb, worth $86.40 that year.

**The families.**
- Emmet's sister Orra (born 1866) married Ansel Keddie in 1889. So the tester was brother-in-law to the patron who lost the most.
- Their son Duncan (born 1894), who taught at Ashlin from 1911, and Emmet's son Roy (born about 1888) were first cousins.
- Duncan's daughter Ivy (born 1931) and Roy's daughter Hazel are therefore second cousins.
- The sources do not settle whether Orra was Emmet's full sister (Roy) or his half sister by a second wife (Hazel).

**The quarrel.** The families split over the June 1919 water circular. Ansel carried it to the patrons himself rather than let another man do it. Two days later Emmet stood in the Keddie yard, asked why Ansel had carried it if he did not believe it, and never came into the house again (Orra's diary, Roy, Keddie's 1925 letter). Emmet signed the January 1925 petition. He died in spring 1929 without making it up; Ansel died in February 1934.

**What happened to the book.**
- Roy took it over in 1929 and kept it the same way. His figure for the gap, 0.17, is the Hollow's 3.61 set against Larrow Green's 3.78, not the true shortfall of 0.19.
- It was one of the four books before the 1925 board.
- Roy lent it (lent, not gave) to Ivy in 1958.
- At his death in 1962 it passed to Hazel, who gave it to the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1963. Nym's label "kept by A. Keddie" is a cataloguing mistake.

*Documents:* r01, r03, r07, r09, r13, r17, r18, r24.

### Story 6 — The hay theory: the 1908 committee and Bulletin 214

By 1907–08 the complaint, running since 1902, had turned into talk that "somebody must be doing it on purpose."

**The 1908 committee.** In January 1908 the board appointed three men under Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green. Grigg hauled to the Green and had no stake in the answer. Orra Keddie saw four men come up in Grigg's sleigh; my guess is that one was the driver. The committee:
- went to Emmet Brant's first and looked at barns rather than the station;
- drew and sealed samples at all six farms;
- watched the tests made;
- tried the station scales against a known weight and against the patrons' own scales;
- "found no dishonesty of any kind";
- blamed the wet sedge hay of Hessel Bottom and prescribed drainage.

The committee sampled only in January, February and March. Its printed report, Orra's diary and Nym's copy all say so, although Grigg's 1919 covering letter claims "every month" of 1908. Winter is exactly when any hay effect would be greatest, and the committee never compared summer milk. Its report closed the question for eleven years.

**Bulletin 214.** In 1911 the State Agricultural College at Farland ran a feeding trial on bog hay and published it as Bulletin No. 214. Its summary: bog hay "depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." It was misquoted twice:
- Grigg, reprinting his report in 1919, claimed the bulletin found "two tenths in the winter."
- Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S., repeated that in his 1926 Farm Club paper. He added a field figure of "a fifth of a point" and a gap of 0.21, and dismissed 1911 as six weeks of flush-season figures.

**Why the theory fails.**
- Hay moves the test at most 0.05, about a quarter of the 0.19 gap. Vaught's "six times" comes from his own mistaken 0.295.
- The gap was flat: the same in July as in February.
- The same herds tested 3.80 over the ridge in summer.
- The 1911 and 1923 rises happened with no change of feed.
- Hay would thin the milk actually delivered, so it could not explain the fat surplus at Larrow Green.

County agent Orin Falke's 1959 report first allowed hay as a partial factor, then withdrew it after reading Bulletin 214's summary.

*Documents:* r04, r07, r09, r11, r15, r16, r22, r24.

### Story 7 — Over the ridge: the Alder Corners cheese factory

Jerome Cudd built a cheese factory at Alder Corners, Vessey County, on the far side of Redlow Ridge, in 1913. His daughter Rosalie was twelve when the first vat was set. Roy Brant and Rundle give 1914, which may be the first season. The ridge road is four miles of switchback and is shut from November to April or May.

From 1915, four of the six Hessel Bottom farmers hauled their summer flush over the ridge, from about 1 June to mid-September, and sent their milk to the Hollow station the rest of the year. They did this for sixteen seasons, the last in 1930. Emmet Brant's place did not go.

Jerome tested on his own new glass and never compared figures with anyone. His season averages for these patrons:

| Year | Test | Year | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1915 | 3.79 | 1923 | 3.81 |
| 1916 | 3.81 | 1924 | 3.80 |
| 1917 | 3.78 | 1925 | 3.79 |
| 1918 | 3.80 | 1926 | 3.82 |
| 1919 | 3.80 | 1927 | 3.78 |
| 1920 | 3.82 | 1928 | 3.80 |
| 1921 | 3.79 | 1929 | 3.81 |
| 1922 | 3.78 | 1930 | 3.79 |

The mean is 3.80. Beside 1919 he wrote: "Good milk off wet ground."

**Why these figures matter.**
- The same herds tested 3.61 at the station, about 0.19 lower. That was in summer, on pasture, when Bulletin 214 found no hay effect at all.
- Watered milk shows up at once in cheese, and there was never a complaint.
- The overlap with the short glass is 1915–1922 plus a few days of June 1923: eight full seasons, nine at most. Ivy's "fourteen overlapping seasons" is wrong.
- From 1924 both places read 3.80, so once the Ammon glass was gone the two tests agreed.
- It is an independent control: a different factory, different glass and a different tester, testing the same cows.

**Rosalie Cudd** is Jerome's daughter (her own letter, Duncan, Rundle, Nym), not his niece. She took over the factory when he died in March 1941 and ran it for seventeen years. In 1958 she had the figures copied and sent to Ivy. The book passed to her in 1941 and went to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970; it has never left Vessey County.

*Documents:* r04, r07, r09, r10, r12, r17, r18, r19.

### Story 8 — The water letter, the arbitration and the name cleared (1919–1958)

**The water letter, 1919.** In June 1919 manager Selby Vose sent the Hollow patrons a circular warning them against adding water. Bertram Kyle typed it and Vose alone signed it. It went to nine families on the six farms; Orra's diary says eleven, perhaps counting every patron of the station. Strawn tested with the lactometer at every visit from 1908 and never found added water, and in 1919 he told the manager so. Six years of bad feeling followed.

**The arbitration, 1925.**
- The patrons petitioned in January 1925.
- A board of three sat from 17 March to 4 May, chaired by Alonzo Frick, an Ashlin attorney who "kept no cow."
- The first sitting was at the Ashlin office on 17 March. The other two were in the station weigh room, directly under the crate.
- Keddie gave his statement on 17 March: he kept the book in his own hand, could not explain the figures, and never took cream.
- Three explanations were argued and none was accepted: water (Vose), theft (implied by Teague) and hay (the 1908 report).
- The exhibits were four books and no glassware: the test book (4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924), the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book and Strawn's 1923 notebook.
- Keddie was dismissed on 30 April.
- On 4 May the board awarded $3,800 "without admission", charged to the station account. The patrons had reckoned their loss at 67,000 lb of fat.

**Afterwards.** In 1926 Vose told the members that the water had stopped after the hearing. In fact the rise came in July 1923, with the new glass and before any petition. Keddie took up carpentry and died in February 1934 with the charge still on him.

**The name cleared, 1958.** Ivy Keddie, his granddaughter and a graduate student at Farland, found the crate in February 1958 and measured the glass on 9 March. She also found Strawn's 1923 notebook in the college library, where his notebooks had been since 1947. Bulletin 471 (Vaught and Keddie, October 1958) concluded that from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 the glass read one part in twenty low:
- 40,000,000 lb × 0.19% = 76,000 lb of fat;
- at 25¢ a pound, $19,000;
- the award had been exactly one fifth of that.

The 16.27 c.c. in the bulletin's method section is 16.72 with two digits transposed. The *Ashlin Chronicle* ran the story on 3 October 1958, 33 years after the dismissal.

*Documents:* r01, r02, r03, r04, r06, r08, r11, r13, r19, r21, r22, r23, r24.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie and Ansel Keddie.** She is his **granddaughter**. Ansel (1861–1934) married Orra Brant in 1889. Their son Duncan was born in 1894 and taught school at Ashlin; Duncan's daughter Ivy was born in 1931, which fits the *Chronicle*'s "twenty-seven" in 1958. This is stated by:
- Duncan himself ("my father's granddaughter", adding that he had seen it printed otherwise);
- Ivy's own 1959 talk;
- Roy and Hazel ("Ivy is Duncan's girl");
- Oram ("the granddaughter").

The only contrary claim is "great-niece", in the *Chronicle* and in Falke's page two. Falke retracted it himself ("the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine"). Great-niece would require Ivy to descend from a sibling of Ansel, and no source names one. *Method:* the father's direct statement plus a correction on the record.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant.** They are **second cousins**. Emmet Brant and Orra were siblings. Their children, Roy and Duncan, were first cousins. Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are therefore second cousins. Roy, Duncan, Hazel and Rundle all say so, and the derivation agrees.

Vira Toland and Cleve Oram say "first cousin once removed". That is Ivy's relation to Roy, one generation off, so it is wrong. Ivy's "Rosalie is Jerome Cudd's niece" is a separate, unrelated error.

**Left open by the sources:** whether Orra and Emmet were full or half siblings.
- Roy says full sister, with no stepmother.
- Hazel says half sister, by Emmet's father's second wife.
- Duncan says only "brother and sister", the younger of two.
- The pages of Orra's diary covering her family before 1889 were not preserved.
- The twelve-year gap in their births (1854 and 1866) fits either version.

If they were half siblings, Ivy and Hazel are half second cousins; the degree of cousinship is the same either way. Duncan's and Roy's wives are never named, which does not affect the answer.

**B3. Selby Vose's father.** It was **Gideon Vose**, the Association's organizer, its president from 1889 to 1911 and the author of its by-laws; he died in 1917. Selby's own 1926 address ("my father's Jerseys"), Loomis ("son of the late president"), Kyle and Duncan all make him the son. The "nephew" of Vira and Rundle is valley talk.

It matters because Article VII, his father's rule that "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern", was the mechanism that turned a short pipette into lost pay, with no appeal to any other test. As manager (1913–1938) the son was defending his father's institution and its test. He looked for the cause in the patrons (the 1919 water circular) rather than in the station's measure. He signed ledger sheets without reading the station-gain line, and in 1916 he brushed off Teague's worry ("bookkeeper and not its conscience").

**B4. The Vose and Keddie families.** **No relationship is established.** The claim that Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins comes from Vira Toland. Cleve Oram says the families were "cousins of some sort", from his grandmother, "though I could not draw you the line." Duncan Keddie, Orra's son, denies any connection. He says his mother, who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, never once put a Vose in it. He also gives a likely source of the confusion: the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Gideon. The claim is hearsay that nobody can trace, against a denial with a reason behind it. The most likely answer is none. A negative cannot be strictly proven, because Orra's pre-1889 family pages are lost.

## Section C — Math

**C1.**

(a) The tests were **5 % low (one part in twenty)**: every recorded test was 0.95 of the true test.
- From the glass: 16.72 c.c. delivered ÷ 17.6 c.c. marked = 0.95 exactly (17.6 × 0.95 = 16.72). Each pipette was 0.88 c.c. short.
- The neck of the test bottle is ruled for a full 17.6 c.c. charge, so a short charge gives a fat reading short in the same proportion.
- Put another way, the true test was 1/0.95 = 1.0526 times the recorded one.
- Vaught's 16.27, which gives a ratio of 0.9244, is a transposition. It contradicts the 1911 and 1924 readings of 3.80 and his own conclusion.

(b) True average = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. The recorded tests were short by 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 points**. Three independent checks give the same true figure: the borrowed glass in 1896 and 1911, the new glass in 1924, and Alder Corners' 3.80.

(c) Over 40,000,000 lb of milk (25 full years, 1898–1922):
- true fat: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb
- credited fat: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb
- **not credited: 76,000 lb**

(d) 76,000 lb × $0.25 = **$19,000**. The patrons received $3,800, which is 3,800/19,000 = **one fifth (20 %)**.

**C2.** Emmet Brant's 1910 leaf: 200,000 lb of milk at a recorded test of 3.42.

(a) Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of butterfat.

(b) True test: 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60, so true fat = 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb. Owed and not credited: 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb**. Equivalently, 0.18 points short, as Ivy says for a 3.42 herd.

(c) At the 1910 price of 24¢ given by Roy: 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40**. At the long-run average of 25¢ it would be $90.00.

**C3.** Over the same 25 full years:

(a) Credited to the patrons: **1,444,000 lb**.

(b) Reached Larrow Green in the station cream: **1,520,000 lb**. The ledger's gain of 5¼ % of credited fat gives 1,444,000 × 1.0526 = 1,520,000, the full true fat.

(c) The difference is 76,000 lb:
- **5.26 % of the fat credited** (the ledger's "five and a quarter per cent")
- **exactly 5.00 % of the fat received at Larrow Green**, which is the fat actually in the milk

The same holds in the 1898 entry: 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26 %, and 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.00 %. Teague and Vaught both describe the 5¼ % as a share of the fat actually delivered, which is wrong; it is a share of the fat credited.

**C4.**

(a) Invoice total: **$77.93**. The lines add up: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93. The "$77.39" in Junia Ammon's prose and in the *Chronicle* is a transposition.

(b) **72 pipettes** (6 dozen at 35¢ = $25.20; not "five dozen"). Each was short by **0.88 c.c.**, delivering 16.72 against a 17.6 mark. That figure is the mean of the nine survivors, which were short in the same proportion one to another. It assumes the survivors stand for the whole lot, which the proportional error at the station supports.

**C5.**

(a) **How long the glass was in use:** from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923, which is **26 years and 42 days (26 years, 1 month, 11 days)**. It was out of use for nine weeks (8 May to 10 July 1911), so its actual service was about 26 years less 21 days.

**The money calculation** runs over **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922** (40,000,000 lb at 1,600,000 lb a year).

The two numbers differ because:
- the first and last seasons were only partly affected. In 1897 the glass came in on 1 May, after four months on the borrowed Larrow Green glass. In 1923 it went out on 12 June.
- the intake figure compiled for the arbitration covers whole years.
- the 25 years include the nine good weeks of 1911, which slightly overstate the loss. Leaving out the part years of 1897 and 1923 understates it by more.

On balance 76,000 lb is a slight underestimate. That last point is my inference.

(b) **Cannot be determined from the sources.**
- Sill says about 900,000 lb.
- Kyle says "a good eleven hundred thousand," from what men told him, and adds that it "is not in any book I ever ruled."
- Nym, the curator, says no 1896 intake figure exists.
- Kyle explains that the first season's milk was carried on the creamery's own books and never filed with the station papers.

The sources establish only an unrecorded range of roughly 0.9–1.1 million lb, from two memories that disagree, against a later run of 1.6 million a year. That is not enough to give a figure.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment.**

*When and how long:* 8 May to 10 July 1911, which is 63 days, **nine weeks**. The dates come from Keddie's test book and Orra's diary for 8 May; "six weeks" is wrong.

*Variable:* only the measuring pipette. The station's Ammon glass was replaced by a borrowed measure, most likely Larrow Green's (an inference).

*Held constant:* the tester, the herds and the farms, the pasture and feed, the scales, the acid, the tester's machine, supervision, and the season. The interval began in May, before the flush, and the station's gap otherwise ran the same in July as in February.

*Result:* the tests rose from 3.61 to 3.80 and fell back to 3.61 on the day the station's own glass returned. Keddie's book records "Tests are back where they were. ?"

*What it establishes that a calculation alone would not:* causation and location. The calculation (3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80) shows only that a 5 % short pipette *could* account for the gap. The interval shows the gap *was* in the measuring glass. Switching the glass switched the gap on and off while nothing else changed, which rules out the milk (water, hay) and the man (theft). It also shows the gap was 0.19 in the station's own figures at the time, 47 years before anyone measured the pipettes.

*What it does not establish:*
- which glass was right. That needs the independent 3.80 readings: 1896, 1924, Alder Corners, and Ivy's burette.
- the size of the error in cubic centimeters.
- that the whole crate was short, not just the pipettes in use.
- when the error began (1 May 1897) or how long it lasted.
- the pounds and dollars involved.
- why the glass was short, whether a reground mold or anything else.

It rests on nine weeks in one season.

**D2. Why Keddie cannot have been stealing cream.**

1. **The ledger's station gain** (Teague's ledger, Sill's weighing at the churn floor, Kyle's copies). Every year more butterfat reached Larrow Green in the Hollow cream than the patrons were credited with: 5¼ % of credited fat, exactly 5 % of fat received. Cream stolen at the station would have arrived *short* of the tests.
2. **The nine weeks of 1911** (Keddie's own test book; Falke). The same man, under the same supervision, recorded 3.80 while the borrowed glass was in use and 3.61 before and after. A thief does not stop for exactly the weeks a pipette is broken.
3. **The 1924 recovery** (Keddie's book and Strawn's 1924 note of 3.80). After Strawn condemned the glass in June 1923, the same tester recorded 3.80 on new glass for the whole of 1924, at the height of the complaints and while still in post.
4. **Outside observation** (Grigg's 1908 committee, Strawn in June 1911, Frick's board in 1925). Each of these watched the test being made and found nothing to remark on. The committee also sealed its own samples.

*Why they are independent.* They rest on different records, kept by different people, at different places, for different purposes:
- the Larrow Green receiving weights and the general ledger, kept by the buttermaker and the bookkeeper at the creamery;
- the station's test book, in Keddie's hand at Ostrey Hollow;
- the state inspector's notebook;
- the committee's and the board's direct observation.

None of them derives from another, and none depends on measuring the pipettes.

*Holding without the 1958 measurement:* all four do. The station gain is the strongest, because it proves the fat was physically delivered to the creamery. The 1911 interval holds too.

*Did the bookkeeper question the ledger entries that refute his accusation?* **No.** Teague entered the gain year after year as "five and a quarter per cent, as usual." He misdescribed its base, claimed to have invented it in 1905 although it appears in 1898, and in 1925 still implied theft ("Cream is portable…"). What he raised with Vose in 1916 was his suspicion of the station, not the meaning of his own line. Kyle says Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years."

**D3. Did the manager know, before the condemnation, that the tests ran low?**

**Yes, he knew the Hollow tested low. Nothing shows he knew the tests themselves were wrong.** The low tests were common knowledge:
- The patrons had complained at every annual meeting since 1902.
- The 1908 committee reported on it.
- His own 1919 water circular assumes the low figures and blames the milk.
- Strawn told him in 1919 that there was no added water.
- Teague took his concerns to him in 1916.

Signing the ledger sheets from 1913 does not show he understood the station-gain line. Kyle, who watched him for years, says he "read the totals and never read the lines above them", putting his thumb on the bottom figure and signing. When the glass was condemned in June 1923 he ordered new glass without drawing any inference. In 1926 he was still telling members the cause was water, and dating the cure to after the hearing, which is impossible.

So: he knew the Hollow tested low and chose to explain it by water. There is no evidence he knew the test was short or read the gain that proved it. Whether he *should* have known is a different question.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. The cause.** One lot of 72 milk-test pipettes from Ammon & Sons, invoiced 14 April 1897, was marked 17.6 c.c. but delivered 16.72 c.c., 95 % of the mark, probably because of a reground mold.

- **How it produced the error.** The test bottle's neck is ruled for a full charge, so each test read the fat 5 % low, in proportion to the true test. A 4.56 herd read 0.24 short, a 3.42 herd 0.18 short, and the station average of 3.80 read 3.61.
- **Why that size.** The size is 0.88/17.6 = 1/20 of the charge: 5 % of 3.80 = 0.19. The error was the same in every month because it had nothing to do with feed or season.
- **Why it began when it did.** It began on **1 May 1897**, when the Ammon outfit replaced Sill's borrowed Larrow Green glass, which read 3.80 in 1896.
- **Why it ended when it did.** It ended on **12 June 1923**, when Strawn ran the lot against a burette and condemned it. From July the new glass read 3.80.
- **It was also absent** for the nine weeks of 1911 on a borrowed measure.
- **Why nothing else had to change.** The cows, milk, scales (which agreed with Brant's), acid and tester were all sound throughout. The cream carried all its fat down to Larrow Green, where it showed up every year as a 5 % "station gain." Only the recorded figure, and under Article VII the pay, was wrong.

**The three competing explanations:**

1. **Water in the milk.** Advanced by Selby Vose (1919 circular, 1926 address); Sill agreed.
   *Defeated by:*
   - Strawn's lactometer, which never found added water from 1908 on and which he reported to the manager in 1919;
   - Alder Corners' cheese, which shows water at once and never drew a complaint;
   - the ledger surplus, since watered milk would arrive exactly as tested;
   - the 1911 and 1923 rises with no change on the farms.

2. **The tester removing cream.** Implied by Teague before the 1925 board ("Cream is portable… two men or a lock"); believed by Oram's father and by Vira.
   *Defeated by:*
   - the ledger's station gain: more fat arrived than was credited;
   - 1911, when the same man's figures rose on borrowed glass;
   - 1924, when the same man recorded 3.80 on new glass.

3. **Wet hay on Hessel Bottom.** Advanced by Grigg's 1908 committee, Dr. Kepp (1926), and at first Falke.
   *Defeated by:*
   - Bulletin 214's actual finding: at most 0.05 in winter and nothing in summer, against a flat 0.19 all year;
   - the same herds testing 3.80 on summer pasture at Alder Corners;
   - the 1911 interval, which began in May on the same feed;
   - the ledger surplus.

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?** **No.** Its exhibits were four books only: the test book, the receiving book, Brant's weigh book and Strawn's 1923 notebook (Frick, Kyle). There was no glassware. Two of its three sittings were held in the station weigh room directly below the crate in the loft. The notebook recording the condemnation was before it, but nobody read it against the test figures.

**What did the tester believe?** The best evidence is that he **suspected the glass** but could never prove it:
- In 1911 he came home saying it was "something in the glass" (Duncan).
- He wrote only "?" in his book beside the fall back to 3.61.
- At the hearing he said only that he could not explain the figures and never took cream.

Frick reports that Keddie privately said in the corridor that he thought the patrons were at fault. That conflicts with Duncan's account, and the two cannot be fully reconciled from the sources.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** From 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923, the Larrow Valley creamery's Ostrey Hollow station tested milk with one lot of Ammon & Sons pipettes that delivered 16.72 c.c. instead of 17.6: five per cent short. Under Article VII the station's test set each patron's pay, so every farmer was credited 3.61 instead of 3.80, about 76,000 lb of butterfat or $19,000. The valley blamed watering, the tester's theft and wet hay. A 1908 committee, a 1919 circular and a 1925 arbitration (award $3,800; tester Ansel Keddie dismissed) all missed the glass. The proof was scattered through separate records that nobody laid side by side: the tester's book (1911's nine weeks on borrowed glass), the ledger's "station gain", a farmer's weigh book, a rival factory's tests, an inspector's condemnation, a feeding bulletin, and the crate itself, which Keddie's granddaughter found in 1958.
