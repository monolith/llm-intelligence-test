# Answer Sheet — Part 1

## Section A — Reconstruction (stories 1–4)

### Story 1 — The Association, Article VII and the Ostrey Hollow station (1889–1925)

Farmers of the Larrow Valley organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association in 1889, meeting in the schoolhouse at Larrow Green. Gideon Vose organized it, wrote its by-laws and was its president from 1889 to 1911; he died in 1917. The main creamery was built at Larrow Green in 1891. The Association's office was at Ashlin, over a harness shop.

By-law Article VII paid each patron by the pounds of butterfat "ascertained by test", and "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." A station's test was therefore final for its own patrons. The year the article was adopted cannot be determined. The sources give 1891 (Rundle), 1892 and 1893 (Grigg). The printed copy says only "adopted at the annual meeting", and the 1889–92 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move.

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley. It mainly served the six farms of Hessel Bottom, a wet meadow. At the station milk was weighed, sampled and tested: a 17.6 c.c. pipette charge, acid, a four-bottle hand tester, and a reading taken with dividers. The milk was then separated. The cream went down to Larrow Green on the cream wagon six days a week, and the skim went back to the farms.

Ansel Keddie (1861–1934) was tester and separator man from the day the station opened, 4 May 1896. The first leaf of his test book reads "Begun this 4th day of May, 1896." He stayed twenty-nine seasons, 1896–1924.

His 1922 summary leaf gives the record:
- 1896: 3.80
- 1897: part year
- 1898: 3.61, and 3.61 ± 0.03 every year after, except nine weeks of 1911 at 3.80
- 1924: 3.80

Larrow Green's own average was 3.78. The station took in about 1,600,000 lb of milk a year, or 40,000,000 lb over the 25 full years 1898–1922. That is the figure the clerk Kyle compiled for the arbitration; Teague's 44 million is wrong. Butterfat averaged 25¢ a pound over the period. Complaints that the Hollow tested low started in 1902 and went on for a generation.

None of the officers ever watched the station work:
- Selby Vose, Gideon's son, manager 1913–1938
- Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945
- Hobart Sill, buttermaker 1893–1934
- Effie Loomis, secretary 1901–1940

Keddie was put out on 30 April 1925. The station closed in 1946 and was later used to store hay tools.

### Story 2 — One lot of glass: borrowed, bought, condemned, measured (1896–1958)

**Borrowed glass, 1896–97.** In its first season the station borrowed its testing glass from Larrow Green: it was buttermaker Hobart Sill's own outfit, which came back the next spring with one piece chipped. Keddie's first leaf says "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes." His later letter's "Ashlin creamery" is contradicted by that leaf and by Sill, Nym, Kyle and Orra Keddie. The borrowed glass read 3.80.

**The Ammon outfit, 1897.** The station's own outfit came from Ammon & Sons, Tarnet, invoiced on 14 April 1897:

| Item | Cost |
|---|---:|
| 6 doz. pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at 35¢ | $25.20 |
| 12 doz. test bottles | $31.68 |
| Acid measure | $0.90 |
| Four-bottle hand tester | $18.00 |
| Freight | $2.15 |
| **Total** | **$77.93** |

The lines add up to $77.93; the "$77.39" printed elsewhere is a transposition. The outfit went into use on **1 May 1897** (invoice, Nym, and Orra's diary entry for that day), not 1898. From then on the station read 3.61.

**Condemned, 1923.** Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, had the circuit from 1907 and was related to nobody in the valley. He was issued burettes in 1921 but did not run one at the station in 1922. On **12 June 1923** he did: "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate…"
- The date comes from his notebook. It is confirmed by the Association's order to Ammon dated 28 June, which a 12 July condemnation could not have come before.
- Ammon replied on 2 July: "Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name."
- New glass was in use from July 1923, and the 1924 average was 3.80. Nobody connected the two facts.
- Ammon's historian guessed that a reground mold had made the whole lot consistently short. The works books burned in 1931, so whether it was an accident or deliberate cannot be determined.

**Found and measured, 1958.** The crate, stenciled AMMON & SONS TARNET, went into the loft over the station's intake room and lay there under a tarpaulin for 35 years. Ivy Keddie found it in February 1958; nine pipettes survived, two of them chipped. On 9 March, at Farland, she measured them against a calibrated burette:
- mean delivery 16.72 c.c. against a 17.6 mark
- short by 0.88 c.c.
- 95 per cent of the mark, in the same proportion from one pipette to the next

A short charge gives a proportionally short reading: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80. The crate went to the Ordell County Historical Rooms at Ashlin in 1959.

### Story 3 — The nine weeks of 1911

On 8 May 1911 the station's pipette broke. The test book records it, and so does Orra Keddie's diary for that day: "A. home cross about a broken measure." A borrowed measure was used until the station's own glass was back on 10 July. That is 63 days, exactly nine weeks, not the "six weeks" Teague and Kepp give.

- Through those nine weeks the tests stood at 3.80.
- When the Ammon glass came back they fell to 3.61.
- Keddie wrote: "Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."

His son Duncan, then seventeen, remembers him coming home saying it was "something in the glass", twice. But he would not write down what he could not prove, and he went on with the same glass for another fourteen years.

**Where the borrowed measure came from.** Most likely it came up from Larrow Green on the cream wagon, as Ivy Keddie says. This is my inference: Larrow Green was the station's usual lender, and the wagon ran daily, which fits a borrowing that began on 8 May. Roy Brant says "the state man left his own measure that June," but that does not fit a May start. Strawn's own June entry records only a routine test: "Nothing here to note."

**What the interval shows.** Only the measuring glass changed. The tester, herds, pasture, scales, acid, separator, supervision and season were all the same. The rise of 0.19 is 5 per cent of 3.80, the same proportion by which the glass was later found short. Kepp's objection that these were flush-season "June and July" figures fails for two reasons: the interval started on 8 May, and in every other year the gap was the same 0.19 in July as in February. Changing only the glass raised the figures, which rules out theft and water (same man, same milk) and hay (same feed).

Nobody who mattered noticed. Strawn wrote nothing about it, and the 1925 board never argued it. Vaught called it "unread for forty-seven years" when Bulletin 471 finally took it up in 1958.

### Story 4 — "Five and a quarter per cent, as usual": the station gain

Every year the Association's Ostrey Hollow account showed three figures:
1. butterfat credited to the patrons on the station test;
2. butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream;
3. the difference, entered as "station gain … five and a quarter per cent, as usual".

The ledger's station column runs from 1897. The 1898 entry reads: 57,760 lb credited, 60,800 lb received, gain 3,040 lb. That gain is 5.26 per cent of the fat credited and exactly 5 per cent of the fat received (57,760 ÷ 60,800 = 0.95). Teague's "of fat actually in the cream" and Vaught's "of butterfat actually delivered" both name the wrong base.

**Who saw the line.**
- **Wilbur Teague**, bookkeeper, called the line "my own device… 1905", yet quotes it from 1898, so it predates him.
- **Hobart Sill** weighed the Hollow cream himself and entered it in the receiving book. It always ran heavier than the office sheet, which he waved away as "cream for you."
- **Bertram Kyle**, a clerk for 25 years, copied the line every year into the statement read to the annual meeting.
- **Selby Vose** signed each sheet with his thumb on the bottom figure.

**The arithmetic over the 25 full years.**
- Credited: 40,000,000 lb × 3.61% = 1,444,000 lb.
- Received at Larrow Green: 1,520,000 lb.
- Gain: 76,000 lb, which is the patrons' shortfall itself.

More fat arrived than the patrons were paid for:
- Stolen cream would have arrived light.
- Watered milk would have arrived exactly as tested, and so would milk thinned by poor hay.
- Only a test reading 5 per cent low predicts a surplus equal to 5 per cent of what was received.

Yet Teague told the 1925 board "Cream is portable… The remedy is two men or a lock," which pointed at theft. When he raised his suspicion with Vose in 1916 he was told he was "bookkeeper and not its conscience." He never questioned his own gain line. The receiving book and Strawn's 1923 notebook were both in front of the board, and nobody set one beside the other. Rundle's note added in press in 1959 finally drew the conclusion.
