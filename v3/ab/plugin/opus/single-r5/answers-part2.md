# Answer Sheet — Part 2

## Section A — Reconstruction (stories 5–8)

### Story 5 — Emmet Brant's weigh book and the two families

Emmet Brant (1854–1929) farmed the largest place on Hessel Bottom, sixty-odd acres with 38 cows, and hauled the most milk to the station.

**Start date.** From **1 January 1897** he weighed his milk on his own scale before it left the farm and wrote the station's weight beside it. That date is given by Frick's exhibit list, Nym's accession record and Duncan Keddie. Roy says 1898; Hazel says 1900 but admits she does not keep years. The two weights always agreed, so weighing was never the problem.

**The 1910 leaf**, with "even" underlined by Emmet: 200,000 lb delivered, paid on a test of 3.42, with fat at 24¢.
- On true glass the milk was 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60, which is 7,200 lb of fat.
- He was credited with 6,840 lb.
- The difference was 360 lb, worth $86.40 that year.

**The families.**
- Emmet's sister Orra (born 1866) married Ansel Keddie in 1889. So the tester was brother-in-law to the patron who lost the most.
- Their son Duncan (born 1894), who taught at Ashlin from 1911, and Emmet's son Roy (born about 1888) were first cousins.
- Duncan's daughter Ivy (born 1931) and Roy's daughter Hazel are therefore second cousins.
- The sources do not settle whether Orra was Emmet's full sister (Roy) or his half sister by a second wife (Hazel).

**The quarrel.** The families split over the June 1919 water circular. Ansel carried it to the patrons himself rather than let another man do it. Two days later Emmet stood in the Keddie yard, asked why Ansel had carried it if he did not believe it, and never came into the house again (Orra's diary, Roy, Keddie's 1925 letter). Emmet signed the January 1925 petition. He died in spring 1929 without making it up; Ansel died in February 1934.

**What happened to the book.**
- Roy took it over in 1929 and kept it the same way. His figure for the gap, 0.17, is the Hollow's 3.61 set against Larrow Green's 3.78, not the true shortfall of 0.19.
- It was one of the four books before the 1925 board.
- Roy lent it (lent, not gave) to Ivy in 1958.
- At his death in 1962 it passed to Hazel, who gave it to the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1963. Nym's label "kept by A. Keddie" is a cataloguing mistake.

*Documents:* r01, r03, r07, r09, r13, r17, r18, r24.

### Story 6 — The hay theory: the 1908 committee and Bulletin 214

By 1907–08 the complaint, running since 1902, had turned into talk that "somebody must be doing it on purpose."

**The 1908 committee.** In January 1908 the board appointed three men under Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green. Grigg hauled to the Green and had no stake in the answer. Orra Keddie saw four men come up in Grigg's sleigh; my guess is that one was the driver. The committee:
- went to Emmet Brant's first and looked at barns rather than the station;
- drew and sealed samples at all six farms;
- watched the tests made;
- tried the station scales against a known weight and against the patrons' own scales;
- "found no dishonesty of any kind";
- blamed the wet sedge hay of Hessel Bottom and prescribed drainage.

The committee sampled only in January, February and March. Its printed report, Orra's diary and Nym's copy all say so, although Grigg's 1919 covering letter claims "every month" of 1908. Winter is exactly when any hay effect would be greatest, and the committee never compared summer milk. Its report closed the question for eleven years.

**Bulletin 214.** In 1911 the State Agricultural College at Farland ran a feeding trial on bog hay and published it as Bulletin No. 214. Its summary: bog hay "depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." It was misquoted twice:
- Grigg, reprinting his report in 1919, claimed the bulletin found "two tenths in the winter."
- Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S., repeated that in his 1926 Farm Club paper. He added a field figure of "a fifth of a point" and a gap of 0.21, and dismissed 1911 as six weeks of flush-season figures.

**Why the theory fails.**
- Hay moves the test at most 0.05, about a quarter of the 0.19 gap. Vaught's "six times" comes from his own mistaken 0.295.
- The gap was flat: the same in July as in February.
- The same herds tested 3.80 over the ridge in summer.
- The 1911 and 1923 rises happened with no change of feed.
- Hay would thin the milk actually delivered, so it could not explain the fat surplus at Larrow Green.

County agent Orin Falke's 1959 report first allowed hay as a partial factor, then withdrew it after reading Bulletin 214's summary.

*Documents:* r04, r07, r09, r11, r15, r16, r22, r24.

### Story 7 — Over the ridge: the Alder Corners cheese factory

Jerome Cudd built a cheese factory at Alder Corners, Vessey County, on the far side of Redlow Ridge, in 1913. His daughter Rosalie was twelve when the first vat was set. Roy Brant and Rundle give 1914, which may be the first season. The ridge road is four miles of switchback and is shut from November to April or May.

From 1915, four of the six Hessel Bottom farmers hauled their summer flush over the ridge, from about 1 June to mid-September, and sent their milk to the Hollow station the rest of the year. They did this for sixteen seasons, the last in 1930. Emmet Brant's place did not go.

Jerome tested on his own new glass and never compared figures with anyone. His season averages for these patrons:

| Year | Test | Year | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1915 | 3.79 | 1923 | 3.81 |
| 1916 | 3.81 | 1924 | 3.80 |
| 1917 | 3.78 | 1925 | 3.79 |
| 1918 | 3.80 | 1926 | 3.82 |
| 1919 | 3.80 | 1927 | 3.78 |
| 1920 | 3.82 | 1928 | 3.80 |
| 1921 | 3.79 | 1929 | 3.81 |
| 1922 | 3.78 | 1930 | 3.79 |

The mean is 3.80. Beside 1919 he wrote: "Good milk off wet ground."

**Why these figures matter.**
- The same herds tested 3.61 at the station, about 0.19 lower. That was in summer, on pasture, when Bulletin 214 found no hay effect at all.
- Watered milk shows up at once in cheese, and there was never a complaint.
- The overlap with the short glass is 1915–1922 plus a few days of June 1923: eight full seasons, nine at most. Ivy's "fourteen overlapping seasons" is wrong.
- From 1924 both places read 3.80, so once the Ammon glass was gone the two tests agreed.
- It is an independent control: a different factory, different glass and a different tester, testing the same cows.

**Rosalie Cudd** is Jerome's daughter (her own letter, Duncan, Rundle, Nym), not his niece. She took over the factory when he died in March 1941 and ran it for seventeen years. In 1958 she had the figures copied and sent to Ivy. The book passed to her in 1941 and went to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970; it has never left Vessey County.

*Documents:* r04, r07, r09, r10, r12, r17, r18, r19.

### Story 8 — The water letter, the arbitration and the name cleared (1919–1958)

**The water letter, 1919.** In June 1919 manager Selby Vose sent the Hollow patrons a circular warning them against adding water. Bertram Kyle typed it and Vose alone signed it. It went to nine families on the six farms; Orra's diary says eleven, perhaps counting every patron of the station. Strawn tested with the lactometer at every visit from 1908 and never found added water, and in 1919 he told the manager so. Six years of bad feeling followed.

**The arbitration, 1925.**
- The patrons petitioned in January 1925.
- A board of three sat from 17 March to 4 May, chaired by Alonzo Frick, an Ashlin attorney who "kept no cow."
- The first sitting was at the Ashlin office on 17 March. The other two were in the station weigh room, directly under the crate.
- Keddie gave his statement on 17 March: he kept the book in his own hand, could not explain the figures, and never took cream.
- Three explanations were argued and none was accepted: water (Vose), theft (implied by Teague) and hay (the 1908 report).
- The exhibits were four books and no glassware: the test book (4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924), the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book and Strawn's 1923 notebook.
- Keddie was dismissed on 30 April.
- On 4 May the board awarded $3,800 "without admission", charged to the station account. The patrons had reckoned their loss at 67,000 lb of fat.

**Afterwards.** In 1926 Vose told the members that the water had stopped after the hearing. In fact the rise came in July 1923, with the new glass and before any petition. Keddie took up carpentry and died in February 1934 with the charge still on him.

**The name cleared, 1958.** Ivy Keddie, his granddaughter and a graduate student at Farland, found the crate in February 1958 and measured the glass on 9 March. She also found Strawn's 1923 notebook in the college library, where his notebooks had been since 1947. Bulletin 471 (Vaught and Keddie, October 1958) concluded that from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 the glass read one part in twenty low:
- 40,000,000 lb × 0.19% = 76,000 lb of fat;
- at 25¢ a pound, $19,000;
- the award had been exactly one fifth of that.

The 16.27 c.c. in the bulletin's method section is 16.72 with two digits transposed. The *Ashlin Chronicle* ran the story on 3 October 1958, 33 years after the dismissal.

*Documents:* r01, r02, r03, r04, r06, r08, r11, r13, r19, r21, r22, r23, r24.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie and Ansel Keddie.** She is his **granddaughter**. Ansel (1861–1934) married Orra Brant in 1889. Their son Duncan was born in 1894 and taught school at Ashlin; Duncan's daughter Ivy was born in 1931, which fits the *Chronicle*'s "twenty-seven" in 1958. This is stated by:
- Duncan himself ("my father's granddaughter", adding that he had seen it printed otherwise);
- Ivy's own 1959 talk;
- Roy and Hazel ("Ivy is Duncan's girl");
- Oram ("the granddaughter").

The only contrary claim is "great-niece", in the *Chronicle* and in Falke's page two. Falke retracted it himself ("the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine"). Great-niece would require Ivy to descend from a sibling of Ansel, and no source names one. *Method:* the father's direct statement plus a correction on the record.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant.** They are **second cousins**. Emmet Brant and Orra were siblings. Their children, Roy and Duncan, were first cousins. Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are therefore second cousins. Roy, Duncan, Hazel and Rundle all say so, and the derivation agrees.

Vira Toland and Cleve Oram say "first cousin once removed". That is Ivy's relation to Roy, one generation off, so it is wrong. Ivy's "Rosalie is Jerome Cudd's niece" is a separate, unrelated error.

**Left open by the sources:** whether Orra and Emmet were full or half siblings.
- Roy says full sister, with no stepmother.
- Hazel says half sister, by Emmet's father's second wife.
- Duncan says only "brother and sister", the younger of two.
- The pages of Orra's diary covering her family before 1889 were not preserved.
- The twelve-year gap in their births (1854 and 1866) fits either version.

If they were half siblings, Ivy and Hazel are half second cousins; the degree of cousinship is the same either way. Duncan's and Roy's wives are never named, which does not affect the answer.

**B3. Selby Vose's father.** It was **Gideon Vose**, the Association's organizer, its president from 1889 to 1911 and the author of its by-laws; he died in 1917. Selby's own 1926 address ("my father's Jerseys"), Loomis ("son of the late president"), Kyle and Duncan all make him the son. The "nephew" of Vira and Rundle is valley talk.

It matters because Article VII, his father's rule that "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern", was the mechanism that turned a short pipette into lost pay, with no appeal to any other test. As manager (1913–1938) the son was defending his father's institution and its test. He looked for the cause in the patrons (the 1919 water circular) rather than in the station's measure. He signed ledger sheets without reading the station-gain line, and in 1916 he brushed off Teague's worry ("bookkeeper and not its conscience").

**B4. The Vose and Keddie families.** **No relationship is established.** The claim that Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins comes from Vira Toland. Cleve Oram says the families were "cousins of some sort", from his grandmother, "though I could not draw you the line." Duncan Keddie, Orra's son, denies any connection. He says his mother, who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, never once put a Vose in it. He also gives a likely source of the confusion: the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Gideon. The claim is hearsay that nobody can trace, against a denial with a reason behind it. The most likely answer is none. A negative cannot be strictly proven, because Orra's pre-1889 family pages are lost.
