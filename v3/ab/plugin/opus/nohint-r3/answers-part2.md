# Answer Sheet — Part 2 (Section A, stories 5–8; Section B)

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### Story 5 — Nine Weeks in 1911: the Borrowed Measure

**Who:** Ansel Keddie; his wife Orra; his son Duncan, then seventeen.

**What happened:** On **8 May 1911** the station's own measuring glass broke. Orra's diary for that day reads: "A. home cross about a broken measure." A measure borrowed from elsewhere was used in its place until **10 July 1911**, a period of **nine weeks**. Throughout those weeks the station's tests stood markedly higher. On the day the station went back to its own glass they fell to their former level. The herds, pasture, season, men, machinery, scale and acid were all the same. Only the glass had changed.

Ansel wrote the fact in his test book and set a **question mark** against it. At home that summer he said, twice (at supper and again by the door, which was not his way), that "it was something in the glass." He never dared write that in the book, because he would not set down what he could not prove. Against his suspicion stood the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county. So he went on for fourteen more years, entering every figure the glass gave him, and those figures were what convicted him. Duncan left in October 1911 to teach school at Ashlin.

This interval is the one controlled comparison in the whole record (see D1). The only thing that changed was the glass. That rules out the tester as the cause, since the same man tested before, during and after. It rules out the hay and the herds, since the same cows ate the same bottom. It points directly at the station's own glassware, twelve years before the state inspector condemned it and forty-seven years before it was measured. It was also the year the college ran its bog-hay feeding trial (Bulletin 214), and the year Selby Vose became manager (Story 6). Nobody put these together at the time. At the 1925 hearing the interval was apparently not weighed as evidence: the board found "no cause established."

**Quantities:** 8 May–10 July 1911, nine weeks; tests "markedly higher" (the size of the rise is not given in r17–r24); Ansel continued for 14 more years.

**Documents:** r18 (Duncan, §IV), r22 (¶6, ¶8), r24 (diary, 8 May and October 1911).

### Story 6 — "Station Gain, as Usual": the Office at Larrow Green and the Water Circular (1911–1925)

**Who:** Selby Vose, manager from **1911** for about 25 years and son of Gideon Vose; Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper; Bertram Kyle, clerk at the outer desk for 25 years (he wrote in 1953, aged 68); Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector; Ansel and Orra Keddie; Emmet Brant.

**What happened:** Every year the Ostrey Hollow station account carried three figures and a sentence:

1. the pounds of butterfat credited to patrons on the station test;
2. the pounds of butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream;
3. the difference, entered as **"station gain … five and a quarter per cent, as usual,"** carried to the general account.

Larrow Green received *more* fat than the patrons were paid for. The 5¼% is the gain taken as a percentage of the *credited* pounds: a 5% under-reading gives 0.05/0.95 = 5.26%. Teague wrote the line every year and "never asked anybody anything in forty years." Vose signed the sheets with his thumb on the bottom figure and never read the lines above it. Kyle copied the line and ruled under it. For the arbitration Kyle worked out the long-run figures: **40,000,000 lb of milk** (1,600,000 lb a year) and butterfat averaging **$0.25/lb**.

In **June 1919** the office sent the Ostrey Hollow patrons a circular warning them against adding water. Kyle typed it and Vose signed it alone. Ansel refused to mail it or leave it on the platform. He carried it himself to **eleven families** (diary; Hazel's "nine" is rejected, see E) and stood at each door while it was read, coming home at eleven at night. Hazel's mother read hers twice and put it in the stove. Two days later Emmet Brant stood in the Keddie yard and asked why Ansel had carried the paper if he did not believe it. Ansel said he would not have another man carry it. Emmet never entered the house again, and the ten-year rift lasted until his death in 1929.

The water charge was baseless. Strawn had tested with the lactometer at every visit since 1908 and never found added water at the station. The ledger itself refuted both accusations: watering or theft would leave the creamery short of the credited fat, but the creamery took in more fat than it paid for.

**Quantities:** 5¼% station gain every year; 40,000,000 lb milk; 1,600,000 lb/yr; $0.25/lb; 11 circulars.

**Documents:** r23 (Kyle), r24 (diary, June 1919), r17 (Hazel), r22 (¶7), r18.

### Story 7 — The Glass Condemned, the Tester Dismissed (1923–1934)

**Who:** Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, related to nobody in the valley; the board of arbitration, chaired by an Ashlin attorney; Ansel Keddie; the patrons.

**What happened:** On **12 June 1923**, on an ordinary circuit visit, Strawn ran the station's milk pipettes against a calibrated burette. They would not deliver their mark, so he condemned the whole lot on the spot and told the manager to order new ones. He wrote it in his notebook and drew no inference about the previous 26 years. Kyle's "1922" and Hazel's "'21" are rejected in favour of 1923, which is supported by the dated Ammon correspondence of June–July 1923, the diary for July 1923 and three other narrators. New glass was in use from July 1923. From 1924 the station's average test stood at **3.85** and stayed there. Nobody connected the two. The condemned glass went into a crate under a tarpaulin in the loft over the intake room.

The patrons, Emmet among them, petitioned in **January 1925**. A board of arbitration sat from **17 March to 4 May 1925**, with Ansel present from the first day in his good coat. It held three sittings: the first in the Cooperative's office at Ashlin, and two in **April** in the station's weigh room, directly under the crate, about twenty feet down the ladder. The test book was impounded in March 1925. Four books went before the board: Keddie's test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book and Strawn's 1923 notebook recording the condemnation. Nobody laid the notebook beside the test book.

Ansel was **dismissed on 30 April 1925**, "twenty-nine seasons and a letter," four days before the board's finding. On **4 May 1925** the board found no cause established and awarded the patrons **$3,800 "without admission,"** charged to the station account. Oram's "$3,500" is his father's hearsay, and Kyle's "1924" and Hazel's "'26" are rejected. The finding found nothing, and the dismissal said everything.

Ansel rebuilt the cow shed, whistling, and did carpentry through the hollow (in 1927 "a carpenter at sixty-six") for a quarter of his old pay. He died at home in **February 1934**. The imputation was never named aloud: "a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name." Orra died in 1948.

**Documents:** r18, r19, r20, r21, r22 (¶10–11), r23, r24.

### Story 8 — Ivy Keddie Climbs the Ladder (1958–1970)

**Who:** Ivy Keddie (b. 1931), **Ansel's granddaughter** (Duncan's daughter), a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland; Professor Leland Vaught, department of dairy husbandry; Rosalie Cudd; Roy Brant; the *Ashlin Chronicle*; Orin Falke.

**What happened:** Strawn's circuit notebooks went to the state dairy office when he retired in 1936 and to the Farland college library in 1947. There, in 1958, Ivy found the **1923 volume** recording the condemnation. Roy Brant lent her the weigh book, and Rosalie Cudd sent copies of the Alder Corners test figures. In **February 1958** Ivy climbed the ladder into the loft over the old intake room and found the crate, 35 years after it went up. She took it east to Farland. On **9 March 1958** she measured the surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette: **nine**, per Falke and Oram, against the *Chronicle*'s "eleven" (see E). All were blown for 17.6 c.c. and all delivered short in the same proportion, averaging **16.72 c.c., which is 95% of the mark**.

**Bulletin No. 471** (October 1958, Vaught and Keddie) concluded that a short charge of milk in a bottle ruled for the full charge reads short in the same proportion. It found that this, and nothing else, explained Ostrey Hollow: no milk was watered and no cream was stolen. Over the **25 full delivery years**, **76,000 lb of butterfat** went uncredited, worth **$19,000** at $0.25/lb. Oram's "67,000 lb" and the *Chronicle*'s "$19,600" are rejected. The patrons' $3,800 award was **one fifth** of what they were owed.

The *Ashlin Chronicle* ran the story on 3 October 1958. It got several facts wrong ("great-niece," a "niece" for Rosalie, 16.27, $77.39) and accused Ammon & Sons without evidence. Ivy told the paper: "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not." Falke's 1959 report to the county board recommended that no action lay for the county. His added note withdrew the hay concession and corrected "great-niece" to "granddaughter." Ansel was cleared 24 years after his death and 33 years after his dismissal.

Where the records went: the test book and the crate to the Ordell County Historical Rooms at Ashlin in 1959; the weigh book there in 1963; the Alder Corners book to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970. Duncan (1968/1970) wrote that "the five books are now in three buildings" (Historical Rooms, Farland library, Vessey County), none of them the family's.

**Documents:** r17, r18 (§V), r19, r21, r22 (¶2, ¶12–13 and note), r24 (editor's note).

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie → Ansel Keddie: granddaughter.** Ansel married Orra Brant in 1889. Their son Duncan was born in 1894, and Ivy is Duncan's daughter, born in 1931. So Ivy is Ansel's son's daughter. Duncan, her father, states this explicitly and adds that he has "twice seen it printed otherwise." Oram also calls her "the granddaughter." The contrary "great-niece" appears in the *Chronicle* (r19) and in Falke's ¶2 (r22). These are not independent: both are 1958–59 derivatives of the college bulletin and press coverage. Falke himself later "informed, and verified" the correction to granddaughter. I resolved this by the father's first-hand genealogy, which the second derivative source corrected.

**B2. Ivy Keddie → Hazel Brant: second cousins.** Emmet Brant and Orra were siblings. Emmet's son Roy and Orra's son Duncan are therefore first cousins, and their daughters Hazel and Ivy are second cousins. Hazel (r17) and Duncan (r18) both derive this. Oram's "first cousin once removed" (r21) is wrong: that relation would put them a generation apart, and they are the same generation. **Left open:** whether Emmet and Orra were full or half siblings. Hazel says Orra was a half-sister "by his father's second wife." Duncan says only "sister, the younger of the two," which neither confirms nor denies it. Orra's pre-1889 pages, covering her girlhood and family, were not preserved. If they were half siblings, Roy and Duncan were half first cousins and Hazel and Ivy half second cousins. The degree, second cousins, holds either way. The sources also do not identify the spouses of Roy and Duncan (the mothers of Hazel and Ivy), and nothing turns on them.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose,** who organized the Association, was its first president and wrote its by-laws (Duncan and Kyle, independently; the "nephew" version is rumour both men reject). It matters because Gideon's by-laws include **article seven**, which paid patrons by the butterfat test taken at the station of delivery, and that rule is what turned a 5% glass error into lost money. His son Selby managed the Association from 1911. He signed the 1919 water circular blaming the patrons, and every year he signed ledger sheets showing the station "gain" without reading them. So the man who managed under his father's pay-by-test rule blamed the patrons rather than questioning the test his father's by-law made decisive.

**B4. Vose and Keddie families: no relationship.** Duncan (r18 §III) states there was "no connection whatever." He says the talk confuses them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Gideon either, and that his mother, who traced every connection in the valley, never once put a Vose in it. Oram's claim that they were "cousins of some sort" (r21) is third-hand ("my mother had it from her mother") and he admits he "could not draw you the line." I resolved this by giving the family member with direct genealogical knowledge priority over uncorroborated hearsay. A negative cannot be proven absolutely, but no source supplies any actual line of descent.
