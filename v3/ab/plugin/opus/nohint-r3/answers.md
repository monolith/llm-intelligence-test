## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1 — Paid on the Test: the Association, Article Seven and the Ostrey Hollow Station (1889–1896)

**Who:** The farmers of the Larrow Valley; Gideon Vose, who organized the Association, served as its first president and wrote its by-laws; Ansel Keddie (1861–1934), the station's tester and separator man.

**What happened:** In 1889 the valley's farmers met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. They built the main creamery at Larrow Green in 1891. By-law article seven paid every patron on the pounds of butterfat his milk was found by test to contain, not on the weight of the milk, and made the test at the station where he delivered the test that governed his pay. This article is the hinge of the whole history: under payment by weight, a faulty test would have cost nobody a dollar. The printed by-laws carry no date ("adopted at the annual meeting"), and the early minute book was lost in an office removal. **The year article seven was adopted cannot be determined from the sources.**

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley (a steady climb, with a steep pitch two miles below the hamlet). It served Hessel Bottom, a wet bottom of six farms. The station opened on **4 May 1896**. Hazel Brant's "ninety-five" is the construction winter; she disclaims any memory for years. The building was a two-floor board building by the creek. On the road side was a waist-high covered platform. Inside was the intake (weigh) room with the scale, vat and separator. Above it was a loft reached by a ladder nailed to the wall by the door. Milk was weighed, sampled, tested and separated. Cleve Oram's father hauled the cream down to Larrow Green, and the skim went back to the farmers.

Ansel Keddie was tester from the opening day and held the post for **29 seasons (1896–1924)**. He kept the station test book in his own hand from 1896. For its **first season (1896)** the station borrowed its testing glass from the Larrow Green creamery. That season's milk went on the creamery's own books, which never joined the station papers. The only figure is Bertram Kyle's hearsay of "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds," which he says is in no book, so **the first season's receipts cannot be determined**. The station closed in 1946 and was later used to store hay tools.

**Quantities:** founded 1889; creamery 1891; station opened 4 May 1896, seven miles up the valley; six farms on the bottom; 29 seasons of Keddie's tenure.

**Documents:** r18 (Duncan Keddie), r19 (*Chronicle*), r21 (Oram), r22 (Falke ¶3–4), r23 (Kyle), r24 (Orra's diary, preface).

### Story 2 — $77.93: Ammon & Sons and the Short Lot of Pipettes (1897–1923, recounted 1949)

**Who:** Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet, founded by Marcus Ammon; Junia Ammon, the firm's historian, who wrote in 1949 aged 60; the Association.

**What happened:** Ammon & Sons blew most of the creamery glass in the state: test bottles, pipettes, acid measures and burettes. A milk-test pipette must *deliver*, not merely hold, its marked charge. On **14 April 1897** the firm invoiced the Association for the Ostrey Hollow station's whole testing outfit:

- 6 doz. (72) milk-test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c., at $0.35: $25.20
- 12 doz. test bottles (8 per cent neck), at $0.22: $31.68
- 1 acid measure: $0.90
- 1 four-bottle hand tester: $18.00
- Freight: $2.15
- **Total: $77.93**

The line items add to $77.93, which is also the invoice's printed total line. The prose "seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents" (r20, repeated by r19) is a transposition. Cleve Oram's "five dozen" pipettes is wrong. The outfit went into use on **1 May 1897**. Orra's diary for that day records that the station's own glass had come and they were "done borrowing of Larrow Green." Kyle's "1 May 1898" is rejected.

The pipettes were short: they delivered about **16.72 c.c.** against their 17.6 c.c. mark, **0.88 c.c. (5%) short each** (see C1/C4; the *Chronicle*'s "16.27" is rejected by arithmetic). Every milk sample was therefore about 5% short of the charge the bottle neck was ruled for, and every fat reading was 5% low.

In summer 1923, after the state inspector condemned the lot, the Association wrote to Ammon on **28 June 1923** and ordered six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles. Ammon replied on **2 July 1923** that the order was entered and would ship that month, and: "Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name." Junia Ammon believes a reground mold made the lot run short by accident. A check of consistency cannot catch an error that is consistent. **Whether it was an accident or knowing, as the *Chronicle* alleged, cannot be determined:** the firm's works books burned in 1931 in the fire that took the old third floor, and only the office records (invoices, letter books, day books) survive. The *Chronicle*'s charge is editorial opinion, not evidence. Junia's own statement that the firm "sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900" is contradicted by the 1897 invoice printed in the same chapter.

**Documents:** r20 (the invoice and letter quoted in facsimile), r19, r21, r23, r24.

### Story 3 — Hessel Bottom and the Brant Weigh Book (1897–1963)

**Who:** Emmet Brant, the largest patron (d. 1929); his son Roy Brant (d. 1962); Roy's daughter Hazel Brant, taped in 1981.

**What happened:** Emmet farmed the largest place on Hessel Bottom, a six-farm, wet bog-meadow bottom with a cold spring where the cans stood. He hauled more milk to the station than any other man. He was **Orra Keddie's brother**, so the tester's wife's brother was the patron who lost the most. Emmet began a private weigh book on **1 January 1897** (Duncan's precise record; Hazel's "1900" is rejected because she says she keeps no years). It was a tall, narrow canvas-over-board account book. He weighed his milk on his own scale, entered the station's intake weight beside it, and would stand in the weigh-room doorway until any disagreement was explained. The weights agreed. The scales were never the trouble.

The **1910 leaf**, the one year Hazel is certain of because it is written at the head of the page, records **200,000 lb of milk "even"** delivered, "his scale and theirs agreeing," **paid on a test of 3.42**. That credited him with 6,840 lb of butterfat. At the true reading of about 3.60 he was owed 7,200 lb, so he was short about **360 lb** in that year alone (≈$90 at the long-run $0.25/lb; see C2).

Four of the six Bottom farms hauled over the ridge in the summer flush to the Cudd cheese factory at Alder Corners, Vessey County, and came back to the station for the rest of the year. The same milk was therefore tested by another house. Hazel says Ostrey Hollow read **0.19 lower** than "the same milk anywhere else," flat in every month, wet years and dry.

After June 1919 Emmet never entered the Keddie house again (see Story 6). His name was on the January 1925 patrons' petition. He lent his weigh book to the 1925 board and wanted it back. He died in spring 1929 and the book passed to Roy. Roy **lent** it (not bequeathed it) to Ivy Keddie in 1958 and had it back. At Roy's death in 1962 it went to Hazel, who gave it to the Historical Rooms at Ashlin in 1963.

**Documents:** r17 (Hazel), r18 (Duncan, §V), r23 (Kyle's four books), r24 (diary: 1908, 1919, 1925, 1929).

### Story 4 — "The Wet Hay of the Bottom": the 1908 Committee and Its Undoing (1902–1959)

**Who:** A four-man committee of the Association (1908); an Ashlin veterinarian (1926); the State Agricultural College (Bulletin No. 214, 1911); County Agent Orin Falke (1959); Jerome Cudd and his daughter Rosalie Cudd of the Alder Corners cheese company.

**What happened:** Ostrey Hollow milk tested low almost from the start. The first complaint on record is from **1902**, and Orra's diary for March 1902 records store talk that "our milk up here runs poorer." It was renewed every decade after. In **January 1908** four men came up in Grigg's sleigh to look into the herds. They went first to Emmet Brant's, as the biggest farm, and looked at the barns and not the station, which Ansel remarked on. In **March 1908** the committee reported that the coarse wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream. Emmet answered that the bottom had fed his father and would feed him. An Ashlin veterinarian wrote to the same effect in **1926**. In his 1959 report Falke at first allowed wet hay as "a real if partial factor."

The theory fails on three counts:

1. **Bulletin No. 214** (the college's 1911 feeding trial) found that bog hay fed as the whole roughage lowered the test by **no more than 0.05 point in winter and by nothing measurable on summer pasture**. A feed effect of that size cannot account for 0.19 in July. Falke withdrew his concession in a note added to the same report.
2. The deficit was **flat at 0.19 in every month**, June flush and February alike. A feed effect would vary with the season.
3. The same herds' milk tested normally elsewhere (Hazel: the four farms that went over the ridge in summer). In 1958 Rosalie Cudd, **Jerome Cudd's daughter** (the *Chronicle*'s "niece" is wrong), had the Alder Corners test figures copied out and sent to Ivy. The book itself never left Vessey County and went to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970. The nine borrowed-glass weeks of 1911 (Story 5) also ran on the same hay.

**Quantities:** hay effect ≤0.05 (winter) or 0 (summer), against a deficit of 0.19 all year.

**Documents:** r17, r18, r19, r22 (¶9 and the added note), r24.

### Story 5 — Nine Weeks in 1911: the Borrowed Measure

**Who:** Ansel Keddie; his wife Orra; his son Duncan, then seventeen.

**What happened:** On **8 May 1911** the station's own measuring glass broke. Orra's diary for that day reads: "A. home cross about a broken measure." A measure borrowed from elsewhere was used in its place until **10 July 1911**, a period of **nine weeks**. Throughout those weeks the station's tests stood markedly higher. On the day the station went back to its own glass they fell to their former level. The herds, pasture, season, men, machinery, scale and acid were all the same. Only the glass had changed.

Ansel wrote the fact in his test book and set a **question mark** against it. At home that summer he said, twice (at supper and again by the door, which was not his way), that "it was something in the glass." He never dared write that in the book, because he would not set down what he could not prove. Against his suspicion stood the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county. So he went on for fourteen more years, entering every figure the glass gave him, and those figures were what convicted him. Duncan left in October 1911 to teach school at Ashlin.

This interval is the one controlled comparison in the whole record (see D1). The only thing that changed was the glass. That rules out the tester as the cause, since the same man tested before, during and after. It rules out the hay and the herds, since the same cows ate the same bottom. It points directly at the station's own glassware, twelve years before the state inspector condemned it and forty-seven years before it was measured. It was also the year the college ran its bog-hay feeding trial (Bulletin 214), and the year Selby Vose became manager (Story 6). Nobody put these together at the time. At the 1925 hearing the interval was apparently not weighed as evidence: the board found "no cause established."

**Quantities:** 8 May–10 July 1911, nine weeks; tests "markedly higher" (the size of the rise is not given in r17–r24); Ansel continued for 14 more years.

**Documents:** r18 (Duncan, §IV), r22 (¶6, ¶8), r24 (diary, 8 May and October 1911).

### Story 6 — "Station Gain, as Usual": the Office at Larrow Green and the Water Circular (1911–1925)

**Who:** Selby Vose, manager from **1911** for about 25 years and son of Gideon Vose; Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper; Bertram Kyle, clerk at the outer desk for 25 years (he wrote in 1953, aged 68); Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector; Ansel and Orra Keddie; Emmet Brant.

**What happened:** Every year the Ostrey Hollow station account carried three figures and a sentence:

1. the pounds of butterfat credited to patrons on the station test;
2. the pounds of butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream;
3. the difference, entered as **"station gain … five and a quarter per cent, as usual,"** carried to the general account.

Larrow Green received *more* fat than the patrons were paid for. The 5¼% is the gain taken as a percentage of the *credited* pounds: a 5% under-reading gives 0.05/0.95 = 5.26%. Teague wrote the line every year and "never asked anybody anything in forty years." Vose signed the sheets with his thumb on the bottom figure and never read the lines above it. Kyle copied the line and ruled under it. For the arbitration Kyle worked out the long-run figures: **40,000,000 lb of milk** (1,600,000 lb a year) and butterfat averaging **$0.25/lb**.

In **June 1919** the office sent the Ostrey Hollow patrons a circular warning them against adding water. Kyle typed it and Vose signed it alone. Ansel refused to mail it or leave it on the platform. He carried it himself to **eleven families** (diary; Hazel's "nine" is rejected, see E) and stood at each door while it was read, coming home at eleven at night. Hazel's mother read hers twice and put it in the stove. Two days later Emmet Brant stood in the Keddie yard and asked why Ansel had carried the paper if he did not believe it. Ansel said he would not have another man carry it. Emmet never entered the house again, and the ten-year rift lasted until his death in 1929.

The water charge was baseless. Strawn had tested with the lactometer at every visit since 1908 and never found added water at the station. The ledger itself refuted both accusations: watering or theft would leave the creamery short of the credited fat, but the creamery took in more fat than it paid for.

**Quantities:** 5¼% station gain every year; 40,000,000 lb milk; 1,600,000 lb/yr; $0.25/lb; 11 circulars.

**Documents:** r23 (Kyle), r24 (diary, June 1919), r17 (Hazel), r22 (¶7), r18.

### Story 7 — The Glass Condemned, the Tester Dismissed (1923–1934)

**Who:** Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, related to nobody in the valley; the board of arbitration, chaired by an Ashlin attorney; Ansel Keddie; the patrons.

**What happened:** On **12 June 1923**, on an ordinary circuit visit, Strawn ran the station's milk pipettes against a calibrated burette. They would not deliver their mark, so he condemned the whole lot on the spot and told the manager to order new ones. He wrote it in his notebook and drew no inference about the previous 26 years. Kyle's "1922" and Hazel's "'21" are rejected in favour of 1923, which is supported by the dated Ammon correspondence of June–July 1923, the diary for July 1923 and three other narrators. New glass was in use from July 1923. From 1924 the station's average test stood at **3.85** and stayed there. Nobody connected the two. The condemned glass went into a crate under a tarpaulin in the loft over the intake room.

The patrons, Emmet among them, petitioned in **January 1925**. A board of arbitration sat from **17 March to 4 May 1925**, with Ansel present from the first day in his good coat. It held three sittings: the first in the Cooperative's office at Ashlin, and two in **April** in the station's weigh room, directly under the crate, about twenty feet down the ladder. The test book was impounded in March 1925. Four books went before the board: Keddie's test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book and Strawn's 1923 notebook recording the condemnation. Nobody laid the notebook beside the test book.

Ansel was **dismissed on 30 April 1925**, "twenty-nine seasons and a letter," four days before the board's finding. On **4 May 1925** the board found no cause established and awarded the patrons **$3,800 "without admission,"** charged to the station account. Oram's "$3,500" is his father's hearsay, and Kyle's "1924" and Hazel's "'26" are rejected. The finding found nothing, and the dismissal said everything.

Ansel rebuilt the cow shed, whistling, and did carpentry through the hollow (in 1927 "a carpenter at sixty-six") for a quarter of his old pay. He died at home in **February 1934**. The imputation was never named aloud: "a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name." Orra died in 1948.

**Documents:** r18, r19, r20, r21, r22 (¶10–11), r23, r24.

### Story 8 — Ivy Keddie Climbs the Ladder (1958–1970)

**Who:** Ivy Keddie (b. 1931), **Ansel's granddaughter** (Duncan's daughter), a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland; Professor Leland Vaught, department of dairy husbandry; Rosalie Cudd; Roy Brant; the *Ashlin Chronicle*; Orin Falke.

**What happened:** Strawn's circuit notebooks went to the state dairy office when he retired in 1936 and to the Farland college library in 1947. There, in 1958, Ivy found the **1923 volume** recording the condemnation. Roy Brant lent her the weigh book, and Rosalie Cudd sent copies of the Alder Corners test figures. In **February 1958** Ivy climbed the ladder into the loft over the old intake room and found the crate, 35 years after it went up. She took it east to Farland. On **9 March 1958** she measured the surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette: **nine**, per Falke and Oram, against the *Chronicle*'s "eleven" (see E). All were blown for 17.6 c.c. and all delivered short in the same proportion, averaging **16.72 c.c., which is 95% of the mark**.

**Bulletin No. 471** (October 1958, Vaught and Keddie) concluded that a short charge of milk in a bottle ruled for the full charge reads short in the same proportion. It found that this, and nothing else, explained Ostrey Hollow: no milk was watered and no cream was stolen. Over the **25 full delivery years**, **76,000 lb of butterfat** went uncredited, worth **$19,000** at $0.25/lb. Oram's "67,000 lb" and the *Chronicle*'s "$19,600" are rejected. The patrons' $3,800 award was **one fifth** of what they were owed.

The *Ashlin Chronicle* ran the story on 3 October 1958. It got several facts wrong ("great-niece," a "niece" for Rosalie, 16.27, $77.39) and accused Ammon & Sons without evidence. Ivy told the paper: "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not." Falke's 1959 report to the county board recommended that no action lay for the county. His added note withdrew the hay concession and corrected "great-niece" to "granddaughter." Ansel was cleared 24 years after his death and 33 years after his dismissal.

Where the records went: the test book and the crate to the Ordell County Historical Rooms at Ashlin in 1959; the weigh book there in 1963; the Alder Corners book to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970. Duncan (1968/1970) wrote that "the five books are now in three buildings" (Historical Rooms, Farland library, Vessey County), none of them the family's.

**Documents:** r17, r18 (§V), r19, r21, r22 (¶2, ¶12–13 and note), r24 (editor's note).

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie → Ansel Keddie: granddaughter.** Ansel married Orra Brant in 1889. Their son Duncan was born in 1894, and Ivy is Duncan's daughter, born in 1931. So Ivy is Ansel's son's daughter. Duncan, her father, states this explicitly and adds that he has "twice seen it printed otherwise." Oram also calls her "the granddaughter." The contrary "great-niece" appears in the *Chronicle* (r19) and in Falke's ¶2 (r22). These are not independent: both are 1958–59 derivatives of the college bulletin and press coverage. Falke himself later "informed, and verified" the correction to granddaughter. I resolved this by the father's first-hand genealogy, which the second derivative source corrected.

**B2. Ivy Keddie → Hazel Brant: second cousins.** Emmet Brant and Orra were siblings. Emmet's son Roy and Orra's son Duncan are therefore first cousins, and their daughters Hazel and Ivy are second cousins. Hazel (r17) and Duncan (r18) both derive this. Oram's "first cousin once removed" (r21) is wrong: that relation would put them a generation apart, and they are the same generation. **Left open:** whether Emmet and Orra were full or half siblings. Hazel says Orra was a half-sister "by his father's second wife." Duncan says only "sister, the younger of the two," which neither confirms nor denies it. Orra's pre-1889 pages, covering her girlhood and family, were not preserved. If they were half siblings, Roy and Duncan were half first cousins and Hazel and Ivy half second cousins. The degree, second cousins, holds either way. The sources also do not identify the spouses of Roy and Duncan (the mothers of Hazel and Ivy), and nothing turns on them.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose,** who organized the Association, was its first president and wrote its by-laws (Duncan and Kyle, independently; the "nephew" version is rumour both men reject). It matters because Gideon's by-laws include **article seven**, which paid patrons by the butterfat test taken at the station of delivery, and that rule is what turned a 5% glass error into lost money. His son Selby managed the Association from 1911. He signed the 1919 water circular blaming the patrons, and every year he signed ledger sheets showing the station "gain" without reading them. So the man who managed under his father's pay-by-test rule blamed the patrons rather than questioning the test his father's by-law made decisive.

**B4. Vose and Keddie families: no relationship.** Duncan (r18 §III) states there was "no connection whatever." He says the talk confuses them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Gideon either, and that his mother, who traced every connection in the valley, never once put a Vose in it. Oram's claim that they were "cousins of some sort" (r21) is third-hand ("my mother had it from her mother") and he admits he "could not draw you the line." I resolved this by giving the family member with direct genealogical knowledge priority over uncorroborated hearsay. A negative cannot be proven absolutely, but no source supplies any actual line of descent.

## Section C — Math

**C1.**
- **(a) The tests read 5% low; the recorded test was 0.95 of the truth.** Each pipette was marked 17.6 c.c. but delivered about 16.72 c.c.: 16.72 / 17.6 = 0.95. The test bottle's neck is ruled for a 17.6 c.c. charge, so a 5% short charge gives a fat reading 5% low, every time.
  - *Resolution:* the *Chronicle* printed the average as 16.27 c.c. That would be 16.27 / 17.6 = 0.9244, a 7.56% error. It would make the true test 3.61 / 0.9244 = 3.905 and the shortfall 0.295, which contradicts the 0.19 deficit reported independently by Hazel (r17) and Falke/the bulletin (r22). 16.72 is the only value consistent with both 3.61 and 0.19, so 16.27 is a digit transposition. Method: arithmetic cross-check.
- **(b) True average = 3.80; the recorded tests were 0.19 point short.** The recorded average was 3.61 (r22). 3.61 / 0.95 = 3.80, and 3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19. This matches the "nineteen hundredths" of r17 and r22.
- **(c) 76,000 lb of butterfat.** 40,000,000 lb milk × 3.80% = 1,520,000 lb true fat. 40,000,000 × 3.61% = 1,444,000 lb credited. 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = **76,000 lb** (equivalently 40,000,000 × 0.19%). Oram's "67,000" is a transposition.
- **(d) $19,000, of which the patrons received $3,800 = one fifth (20%).** 76,000 lb × $0.25/lb = **$19,000**. $3,800 / $19,000 = 0.20. The *Chronicle*'s $19,600 does not follow from any figure in the sources, and I reject it by arithmetic.

**C2.** Emmet Brant, 1910 (r17): 200,000 lb milk paid on a test of 3.42.
- **(a) Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = 6,840 lb butterfat.**
- **(b) True test = 3.42 / 0.95 = 3.60, so owed 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb. Not credited: 7,200 − 6,840 = 360 lb.** (If the average flat 0.19 were applied instead, 3.61 would give 7,220 lb and 380 lb. I use the proportional 5% because the error is a proportional charge error, and 0.19 is its value at the 3.80 average.)
- **(c) 360 × $0.25 = $90.00.** Caveat: r17–r24 give only the long-run average price ($0.25/lb, Kyle), not a 1910-specific price. If a 1910 price appears in r01–r16, it should replace $0.25.

**C3.** Over the 25 full delivery years:
- **(a) Credited: 1,444,000 lb** (40,000,000 × 3.61%).
- **(b) Reached Larrow Green in the cream: 1,520,000 lb** (40,000,000 × 3.80%, taking the cream fat as the true fat and ignoring separator losses).
- **(c) Difference 76,000 lb.** As a percentage **of the credited pounds**: 76,000 / 1,444,000 = **5.26% ≈ 5¼%**, which is exactly the ledger's "station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual" (r23), because 0.05 / 0.95 = 5.263%. As a percentage **of the pounds received** (the true fat) it is 76,000 / 1,520,000 = **5.00%**, the size of the glass error. The ledger's 5¼% is the gain over credited fat, not the error in the test.

**C4.**
- **(a) $77.93.** Sum of the invoice lines: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93, which is also the invoice's printed "Total" line (r20). The "$77.39" in r20's prose and in r19 is a transposition.
- **(b) 72 pipettes** (6 doz. at $0.35 = $25.20, which checks: 72 × 0.35 = 25.20). **Each was short 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c.** (With the rejected 16.27 it would be 1.33 c.c.)

**C5.**
- **(a) In use from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923: 26 years, 1 month and 11 days** (about 26 years; Ammon called the Association "a customer of twenty-six years' standing"). **The money runs over 25 full delivery years (1898–1922):** 40,000,000 lb ÷ 1,600,000 lb/yr = 25. The numbers differ because the glass entered service partway through 1897 and was condemned partway through 1923. Those two partial years are not full delivery years and fall outside the figures Kyle compiled for the arbitration. (Which years are "full" is my inference from the 25-year quotient and the in/out dates.) 1896, the first season, used borrowed Larrow Green glass and is not affected at all.
- **(b) Cannot be determined from the sources.** The first season (from 4 May 1896) was carried on the creamery's own books, and those sheets were never put with the station papers. The only figure, "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" (1,100,000 lb), is Kyle's recollection of what men told him, and he says it "is not in any book I ever ruled." It is plausible for a partial first season against the later 1,600,000 lb/yr, but it is unsourced hearsay, not a record.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran from **8 May to 10 July 1911, nine weeks** (r22; the start date is confirmed by Orra's diary for 8 May 1911, "a broken measure"). **Variable:** the measuring glass (a borrowed measure instead of the station's own). **Held constant:** the tester, the herds, the Hessel Bottom pasture and hay, the scale, the acid, the separator, the procedure and the patrons. **Result:** the tests rose markedly for exactly the nine weeks and dropped back to the old level on the day the station's own glass returned. **What it establishes that a calculation cannot:** causation. It shows the deficit follows the glass and not the man or the feed. It is a switch-on/switch-off test, and the reversal on return rules out coincidence with the season. A calculation from the 1958 measurement only shows the glass *could* produce the deficit. It cannot exclude a second cause acting alongside it, but the reversal does. It also disposes of the theft theory (same man, higher figures) and the hay theory (same hay). **What it does not establish:** the exact size of the error (the rise is described only as "marked"); whether every pipette in the lot was short, or by how much; the mechanism (manufacturing fault, reground mold, wear, or anything else); whether the borrowed measure was itself exact; when the fault began (before May 1911 the record shows only that the tests were low); or the money owed. It is also a single unreplicated interval, and its meaning was not recorded at the time: Ansel set only a question mark against it.

**D2.** Reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream:
1. **The 1911 interval** (Falke r22, Orra r24, Duncan r18). The same man, under the same supervision, produced higher tests when only the glass changed.
2. **The Association's own ledger** (Kyle, r23). A tester skimming off cream would leave Larrow Green receiving *less* fat than the patrons were credited. Every year it received **more**, a "station gain" of 5¼%. The cream arriving at the creamery contained all the fat the milk contained; the credit was what was short.
3. **The 1923 switch** (r22 ¶11). New glass came in July 1923 while Keddie stayed on as tester until April 1925. From 1924 the average stood at 3.85, normal, with the same man doing the testing.
4. **The external comparison** (Hazel, r17; the Alder Corners figures via Rosalie Cudd, r18). The same herds' milk tested normally at the cheese factory, and the deficit was a flat 0.19 in every month, the signature of an instrument, not of opportunistic theft.

**Why they are independent:** each rests on a different record kept by different people for different purposes. (1) is the station test book with the family's contemporaneous witness. (2) is the Association office's ledger, kept by its own bookkeeper at Larrow Green, seven miles away. (3) is the station averages after the inspector's action. (4) is a rival firm's books in another county. None depends on another, and none depends on the 1958 pipette measurement. **Still valid without the 1958 measurement:** all four, and especially (2) and (3). The ledger by itself refutes theft, and (3) was on record by 1924.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question the ledger entries?** **No.** Wilbur Teague wrote the "station gain … as usual" line every year of his service and "never asked anybody anything in forty years" (r23). Nobody at the arbitration laid the relevant books side by side either. Caveat: r17–r24 name the officer who urged the theft charge at the hearing only as "an officer of the Association" (r22). That Teague made the accusation is the question's premise; I cannot confirm it from these retellings.

**D3.** **Yes, he knew the tests ran low.** Selby Vose signed the June 1919 water circular to the Ostrey Hollow patrons (Kyle, r23, who typed it; Orra, r24; Hazel, r17). A warning against watering only makes sense because the station's tests were known to be low. The low tests were also complained of at every annual meeting (r19) from 1902 onward (r22), and the station account itself was read out at each annual meeting. What the sources show he did *not* grasp was the meaning of the ledger line. He "read the totals and he never read the lines above them," signing with his thumb on the bottom figure (r23). So he knew of the low tests and blamed water, while signing every year a figure (a creamery gain rather than a loss) that refuted the water and theft explanations. There is no evidence he connected the two, and nothing in the sources shows he knew the *cause* before June 1923.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. Causal theory.** The Ostrey Hollow shortfall was produced entirely by the station's own testing pipettes. On 14 April 1897 Ammon & Sons of Tarnet supplied 72 pipettes marked to deliver 17.6 c.c., the charge the test-bottle neck is ruled for. They actually delivered about 16.72 c.c., 5% short, most likely through an undetected manufacturing fault (Junia Ammon suggests a reground mold; the cause cannot be proved because the works books burned in 1931).

- **Size of the error:** a sample 5% short read 5% low in fat on every test, so a true 3.80 recorded as 3.61, a shortfall of 0.19.
- **Why that size:** the error is proportional to the missing volume (0.88 / 17.6 = 5%), which is also why the ledger showed the creamery receiving 5¼% more fat than it credited (0.05 / 0.95).
- **Why it was the same every month:** it came from the instrument, not the season or the feed.
- **Why it began when it did:** on 1 May 1897 the station stopped borrowing Larrow Green glass and began using its own lot.
- **Why it ended when it did:** Strawn condemned the lot on 12 June 1923, new glass came in the following month, and from 1924 tests stood normal at 3.85.
- **Why nothing else had to change:** the herds, hay, scale, acid, tester, procedure and the patrons' honesty were never involved. Only the glass changed at the start and at the end, and the 1911 nine-week substitution shows the same switch in miniature.
- **Why it cost money:** by-law article seven paid on the station test. The cream reaching Larrow Green carried all the fat, which the ledger booked as "station gain."

**The three competing explanations and what defeats each:**
1. **The patrons watered their milk.** Advanced by the manager, Selby Vose (the 1919 circular). Defeated by Strawn's lactometer tests at every visit from 1908, which never found added water (r22). Also by the scales agreeing (Emmet's weigh book), and by the ledger showing the creamery receiving more fat than credited.
2. **The tester stole cream and wrote low tests.** Urged at the 1925 hearing by an officer of the Association (r22), and believed by Oram's father, the cream hauler (r21). Defeated by the 1911 interval (same man, higher tests), the ledger's station *gain* (theft would produce a loss), and the normal 3.85 tests from 1924 while Keddie was still tester.
3. **The wet bog hay of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream.** Advanced by the Association's 1908 committee and an Ashlin veterinarian in 1926, and provisionally allowed by Falke. Defeated by Bulletin No. 214 (1911): at most 0.05 in winter and nothing on summer pasture, which cannot account for 0.19 in July. Also by the deficit being flat in every month, by the 1911 interval (same hay, higher tests), and by the rise after 1923 on the same bottom.

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware? No.** It sat in two of its three sessions in the weigh room directly beneath the crate in the loft, "twenty feet, if that." It had before it Strawn's 1923 notebook recording the condemnation, but nobody "laid the second beside the fourth" (r23), and it found "no cause established." The glass was first examined in 1958 by Ivy Keddie.

**What did the tester believe? That the cause was in the glass.** In summer 1911 he said at home, twice, "it was something in the glass" (r18). He never dared write it, entering only a question mark in the test book, because he would not set down what he could not prove.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** (≈145 words) From 1897 to 1923 the Ostrey Hollow skimming station paid Hessel Bottom's farmers on butterfat tests made with Tarnet pipettes that delivered 5% short, so every test read 0.19 low (3.61 instead of 3.80). For a generation the valley blamed people instead: watering (the manager's 1919 circular), cream theft by the tester Ansel Keddie, and wet hay (a 1908 committee). A borrowed measure in 1911 raised the tests for nine weeks. The Association's ledger booked a yearly 5¼% "station gain." A state inspector condemned the glass in 1923. Nobody connected any of it. Keddie was dismissed in 1925, and the patrons got $3,800 of the $19,000 they were owed. In 1958 his granddaughter Ivy found the crate in the loft and measured it. The truth was spread across an invoice, a ledger, a diary, a farmer's weigh book, an inspector's notebook and a rival's test book, each holding one piece.
