## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1 — Paid on the Test: the Association, Article Seven and the Ostrey Hollow Station (1889–1896)

**Who:** The farmers of the Larrow Valley; Gideon Vose, who organized the Association, served as its first president and wrote its by-laws; Ansel Keddie (1861–1934), the station's tester and separator man.

**What happened:** In 1889 the valley's farmers met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. They built the main creamery at Larrow Green in 1891. By-law article seven paid every patron on the pounds of butterfat his milk was found by test to contain, not on the weight of the milk, and made the test at the station where he delivered the test that governed his pay. This article is the hinge of the whole history: under payment by weight, a faulty test would have cost nobody a dollar. The printed by-laws carry no date ("adopted at the annual meeting"), and the early minute book was lost in an office removal. **The year article seven was adopted cannot be determined from the sources.**

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley (a steady climb, with a steep pitch two miles below the hamlet). It served Hessel Bottom, a wet bottom of six farms. The station opened on **4 May 1896**. Hazel Brant's "ninety-five" is the construction winter; she disclaims any memory for years. The building was a two-floor board building by the creek. On the road side was a waist-high covered platform. Inside was the intake (weigh) room with the scale, vat and separator. Above it was a loft reached by a ladder nailed to the wall by the door. Milk was weighed, sampled, tested and separated. Cleve Oram's father hauled the cream down to Larrow Green, and the skim went back to the farmers.

Ansel Keddie was tester from the opening day and held the post for **29 seasons (1896–1924)**. He kept the station test book in his own hand from 1896. For its **first season (1896)** the station borrowed its testing glass from the Larrow Green creamery. That season's milk went on the creamery's own books, which never joined the station papers. The only figure is Bertram Kyle's hearsay of "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds," which he says is in no book, so **the first season's receipts cannot be determined**. The station closed in 1946 and was later used to store hay tools.

**Quantities:** founded 1889; creamery 1891; station opened 4 May 1896, seven miles up the valley; six farms on the bottom; 29 seasons of Keddie's tenure.

**Documents:** r18 (Duncan Keddie), r19 (*Chronicle*), r21 (Oram), r22 (Falke ¶3–4), r23 (Kyle), r24 (Orra's diary, preface).

### Story 2 — $77.93: Ammon & Sons and the Short Lot of Pipettes (1897–1923, recounted 1949)

**Who:** Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet, founded by Marcus Ammon; Junia Ammon, the firm's historian, who wrote in 1949 aged 60; the Association.

**What happened:** Ammon & Sons blew most of the creamery glass in the state: test bottles, pipettes, acid measures and burettes. A milk-test pipette must *deliver*, not merely hold, its marked charge. On **14 April 1897** the firm invoiced the Association for the Ostrey Hollow station's whole testing outfit:

- 6 doz. (72) milk-test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c., at $0.35: $25.20
- 12 doz. test bottles (8 per cent neck), at $0.22: $31.68
- 1 acid measure: $0.90
- 1 four-bottle hand tester: $18.00
- Freight: $2.15
- **Total: $77.93**

The line items add to $77.93, which is also the invoice's printed total line. The prose "seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents" (r20, repeated by r19) is a transposition. Cleve Oram's "five dozen" pipettes is wrong. The outfit went into use on **1 May 1897**. Orra's diary for that day records that the station's own glass had come and they were "done borrowing of Larrow Green." Kyle's "1 May 1898" is rejected.

The pipettes were short: they delivered about **16.72 c.c.** against their 17.6 c.c. mark, **0.88 c.c. (5%) short each** (see C1/C4; the *Chronicle*'s "16.27" is rejected by arithmetic). Every milk sample was therefore about 5% short of the charge the bottle neck was ruled for, and every fat reading was 5% low.

In summer 1923, after the state inspector condemned the lot, the Association wrote to Ammon on **28 June 1923** and ordered six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles. Ammon replied on **2 July 1923** that the order was entered and would ship that month, and: "Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name." Junia Ammon believes a reground mold made the lot run short by accident. A check of consistency cannot catch an error that is consistent. **Whether it was an accident or knowing, as the *Chronicle* alleged, cannot be determined:** the firm's works books burned in 1931 in the fire that took the old third floor, and only the office records (invoices, letter books, day books) survive. The *Chronicle*'s charge is editorial opinion, not evidence. Junia's own statement that the firm "sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900" is contradicted by the 1897 invoice printed in the same chapter.

**Documents:** r20 (the invoice and letter quoted in facsimile), r19, r21, r23, r24.

### Story 3 — Hessel Bottom and the Brant Weigh Book (1897–1963)

**Who:** Emmet Brant, the largest patron (d. 1929); his son Roy Brant (d. 1962); Roy's daughter Hazel Brant, taped in 1981.

**What happened:** Emmet farmed the largest place on Hessel Bottom, a six-farm, wet bog-meadow bottom with a cold spring where the cans stood. He hauled more milk to the station than any other man. He was **Orra Keddie's brother**, so the tester's wife's brother was the patron who lost the most. Emmet began a private weigh book on **1 January 1897** (Duncan's precise record; Hazel's "1900" is rejected because she says she keeps no years). It was a tall, narrow canvas-over-board account book. He weighed his milk on his own scale, entered the station's intake weight beside it, and would stand in the weigh-room doorway until any disagreement was explained. The weights agreed. The scales were never the trouble.

The **1910 leaf**, the one year Hazel is certain of because it is written at the head of the page, records **200,000 lb of milk "even"** delivered, "his scale and theirs agreeing," **paid on a test of 3.42**. That credited him with 6,840 lb of butterfat. At the true reading of about 3.60 he was owed 7,200 lb, so he was short about **360 lb** in that year alone (≈$90 at the long-run $0.25/lb; see C2).

Four of the six Bottom farms hauled over the ridge in the summer flush to the Cudd cheese factory at Alder Corners, Vessey County, and came back to the station for the rest of the year. The same milk was therefore tested by another house. Hazel says Ostrey Hollow read **0.19 lower** than "the same milk anywhere else," flat in every month, wet years and dry.

After June 1919 Emmet never entered the Keddie house again (see Story 6). His name was on the January 1925 patrons' petition. He lent his weigh book to the 1925 board and wanted it back. He died in spring 1929 and the book passed to Roy. Roy **lent** it (not bequeathed it) to Ivy Keddie in 1958 and had it back. At Roy's death in 1962 it went to Hazel, who gave it to the Historical Rooms at Ashlin in 1963.

**Documents:** r17 (Hazel), r18 (Duncan, §V), r23 (Kyle's four books), r24 (diary: 1908, 1919, 1925, 1929).

### Story 4 — "The Wet Hay of the Bottom": the 1908 Committee and Its Undoing (1902–1959)

**Who:** A four-man committee of the Association (1908); an Ashlin veterinarian (1926); the State Agricultural College (Bulletin No. 214, 1911); County Agent Orin Falke (1959); Jerome Cudd and his daughter Rosalie Cudd of the Alder Corners cheese company.

**What happened:** Ostrey Hollow milk tested low almost from the start. The first complaint on record is from **1902**, and Orra's diary for March 1902 records store talk that "our milk up here runs poorer." It was renewed every decade after. In **January 1908** four men came up in Grigg's sleigh to look into the herds. They went first to Emmet Brant's, as the biggest farm, and looked at the barns and not the station, which Ansel remarked on. In **March 1908** the committee reported that the coarse wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream. Emmet answered that the bottom had fed his father and would feed him. An Ashlin veterinarian wrote to the same effect in **1926**. In his 1959 report Falke at first allowed wet hay as "a real if partial factor."

The theory fails on three counts:

1. **Bulletin No. 214** (the college's 1911 feeding trial) found that bog hay fed as the whole roughage lowered the test by **no more than 0.05 point in winter and by nothing measurable on summer pasture**. A feed effect of that size cannot account for 0.19 in July. Falke withdrew his concession in a note added to the same report.
2. The deficit was **flat at 0.19 in every month**, June flush and February alike. A feed effect would vary with the season.
3. The same herds' milk tested normally elsewhere (Hazel: the four farms that went over the ridge in summer). In 1958 Rosalie Cudd, **Jerome Cudd's daughter** (the *Chronicle*'s "niece" is wrong), had the Alder Corners test figures copied out and sent to Ivy. The book itself never left Vessey County and went to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970. The nine borrowed-glass weeks of 1911 (Story 5) also ran on the same hay.

**Quantities:** hay effect ≤0.05 (winter) or 0 (summer), against a deficit of 0.19 all year.

**Documents:** r17, r18, r19, r22 (¶9 and the added note), r24.
