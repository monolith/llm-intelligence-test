# Answer Sheet — Part 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

**C1. (a) Proportion of error.** The station's tests read **5 per cent low**, so a recorded test was 0.95 of the true one. From the glassware: the test bottle's neck is ruled for a 17.6 c.c. charge. A pipette that delivers only *d* c.c. puts in *d*/17.6 of the intended milk and therefore *d*/17.6 of the intended fat, and the reading is short by the same proportion. For the error to be 5 per cent, *d* = 0.95 × 17.6 = **16.72 c.c.**, which is 0.88 c.c. short per charge.

*How I resolved the pipette average.* The *Chronicle* prints the average delivery as 16.27 c.c. That would give a 7.56 per cent error, so a true 3.80 would read 3.51, a 0.29 shortfall. This contradicts every independent station figure: the flat 0.19 shortfall (Hazel, Falke), the 3.61 recorded average (Falke), and the 5¼ per cent station gain (Kyle). The value 16.72 fits all of them exactly. I therefore read 16.27 as a digit transposition, the same kind of error as the *Chronicle*'s $77.39 for $77.93. This correction is an inference from arithmetic.

**(b) True average and shortfall.** Recorded average 3.61 (Falke). True average = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. The recorded tests were **0.19 points** short, matching the "nineteen hundredths" that Hazel and Falke report. As a check, after the glass was replaced in 1924 the station averaged 3.85, and Larrow Green averaged 3.78, so 3.80 is a normal figure.

**(c) Uncredited butterfat, over 25 full delivery years.** Milk received was 40,000,000 lb (Kyle: 1,600,000 lb a year × 25 years).
- True fat: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb.
- Credited: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb.
- **Uncredited: 76,000 lb** (the same as 40,000,000 × 0.0019).

Oram's "sixty-seven thousand pounds" is a transposition of 76,000 and is rejected.

**(d) Money, and the fraction received.** 76,000 lb × $0.25 (Kyle's average price) = **$19,000**. The patrons received $3,800. 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **1/5, or 20 per cent**. The *Chronicle*'s $19,600 does not follow from any figure in the sources and is rejected on arithmetic.

**C2. The largest patron (Emmet Brant) in 1910.** He delivered 200,000 lb and was paid on a recorded test of 3.42 (Hazel, reading from the 1910 leaf of the weigh book).
- (a) Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of butterfat.
- (b) True test: 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60, so true fat was 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb. Owed and not credited: **360 lb**.
- (c) At the long-run average of 25¢ a pound, 360 × $0.25 = **$90**.

The actual 1910 butterfat price is not in the sources, so $90 uses the average. If one applied Hazel's flat 0.19 instead of the proportional error, the result would be 380 lb and $95. But the error is proportional, and at a 3.42 level it comes to 0.18.

**C3. Totals over the same 25 years.**
- (a) Credited to patrons: **1,444,000 lb**.
- (b) Reaching Larrow Green in the station's cream: **1,520,000 lb**. This treats the fat in the cream as the true fat, which is what the station-gain line measures.
- (c) The difference of 76,000 lb is **5.26 per cent of the fat credited** (76,000 ÷ 1,444,000), which is Kyle's "five and a quarter per cent, as usual." It is **5.00 per cent of the fat actually received** (76,000 ÷ 1,520,000). The ledger's "station gain" was taken as a percentage of the credited figure, the smaller base. The glass error was 5 per cent of the truth.

**C4. (a) Invoice total: $77.93.**
- 25.20 + 31.68 = 56.88
- + 0.90 = 57.78
- + 18.00 = 75.78
- + 2.15 = **77.93**

The facsimile prints $77.93. The prose "$77.39" (Junia, copied by the *Chronicle*) is a transposition.

**(b) Pipettes: 6 dozen, or 72** (at 35¢, $25.20). Each was about **0.88 c.c.** short, delivering 16.72 c.c. against its 17.6 c.c. mark, as derived in C1(a). If the *Chronicle*'s 16.27 were taken at face value the shortfall would be 1.33 c.c., but that value is rejected.

**C5. (a) How long the glass was in use, versus the money years.** The faulty glass was in use from **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923**, which is **26 years, 1 month and 11 days** (about 26 years and 6 weeks). The one exception is the nine weeks from 8 May to 10 July 1911, when a borrowed measure was used. The money calculation runs over **25 full delivery years** (Kyle's 40,000,000 lb ÷ 1,600,000 lb a year = 25), which is 1898–1922 inclusive. The two numbers differ because the glass's first season (1897, from 1 May) and last season (1923, to 12 June) were partial. The arbitration figures that Kyle compiled cover only complete years of station accounts. The 1911 interval is also inside the in-use span but was not affected.

**(b) Milk received in the first season: cannot be determined from the sources.** The only figure is Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds." He had it secondhand "from men who were there" and says it "is not in any book I ever ruled." The first season's sheets were kept on the creamery's books and never filed with the station papers. The sources establish that the station was receiving milk that season, but the only quantity is uncorroborated hearsay.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment.** It ran from **8 May to 10 July 1911, nine weeks**. Falke gives the dates, and Orra's diary independently records the broken measure on 8 May. The variable was the measuring glass: the station's own versus a borrowed one. Everything else was held constant: the herds, the pasture and hay, the season, the tester, his method and supervision, the scales and the machinery. The result was that the tests rose markedly while the borrowed measure was used and fell back the day the station's own glass returned. What it establishes, and a calculation alone does not, is **causation**. The arithmetic can show that a 5 per cent short charge would produce a 0.19 shortfall, but only the switch shows that the station's own glass actually produced it, and that cows, hay, water and the tester did not. It also shows this twelve years before anyone condemned the glass.

What it does **not** establish:
- the exact size of the error (no figures for the interval are given)
- that the borrowed measure was itself accurate
- which item in the outfit was at fault (pipette or bottle)
- why the glass was short, or whether Ammon knew
- that anybody in authority noticed; Ansel only put a question mark in his book

**D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream.** Five reasons, each from a different narrator:

1. **Kyle's ledger (r23).** Every year the cream reaching Larrow Green held 5¼ per cent *more* fat than the station credited. A tester who skimmed cream would make the creamery's receipts *smaller* than the credits, not larger.
2. **The 1911 borrowed measure (Falke r22, confirmed by Orra r24 and Duncan r18).** The same man, under the same supervision, produced higher tests for nine weeks, and the tests fell only when his own glass came back.
3. **The 1924 recovery (Falke r22).** With new glass the station averaged 3.85 from 1924, while Keddie was still the tester until April 1925.
4. **The shape of the shortfall (Hazel r17).** It was a flat 0.19 in every month and every season. That is the signature of a fixed proportional instrument error. Pilfering would vary with opportunity and volume.
5. **The glass itself (Ivy's 1958 measurement, r19/r22).** The pipettes delivered short in a uniform proportion.

These reasons are independent because each rests on a different record, kept by different people for different purposes: the office's general ledger, the station test book in two separate periods, the farmers' own comparison, and a college laboratory measurement. None of them borrows its figures from another.

Reasons 1, 2, 3 and 4 would all still hold if the glass had never been measured in 1958. The ledger is the strongest, because it refutes theft by itself.

Did the bookkeeper ever question the ledger entries? **No.** Wilbur Teague wrote the "station gain … as usual" line every year and "never asked anybody anything in forty years" (Kyle). The theft accusation was urged at the hearing by "an officer of the Association" (Falke). The retellings I read do not name that officer as Teague. What they do establish is that the Association's own books refuted the charge every year and that the man who wrote those books never questioned them.

**D3. Did the manager know before 1923 that the tests ran low?** **Yes, he knew the tests ran low. He did not, on the evidence, know what his own ledger said about why.** The low tests were the subject of complaint at every annual meeting from 1902 onward, and his own June 1919 circular warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons about water only makes sense as a response to low tests. So knowledge of the low tests is certain. What Kyle's eyewitness account rules out, as far as testimony can, is that Vose understood the station-gain line: "he read the totals and he never read the lines above them … put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign." Signing the sheet is not reading it. It is an inference, though a well-supported one, that he never saw the 5¼ per cent line as evidence that the fat was arriving and the test was wrong. Had he read it, the water accusation would have made no sense, because water lowers the fat in the cream along with the test.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. Causal theory.** The Ostrey Hollow station's own testing outfit was bought from Ammon & Sons, Tarnet, on 14 April 1897 and put into use on 1 May 1897. Its six dozen milk pipettes were marked 17.6 c.c. but delivered about 5 per cent less. Under the by-laws every patron was paid on the station's test. Each short charge went into a bottle whose neck was ruled for the full 17.6 c.c., so every fat reading came out 5 per cent low.

- **Size of the error:** a true 3.80 read as 3.61, a shortfall of 0.19.
- **Why that size:** the error was a fixed ratio of volumes, not something that varied with feed or season, so it held "flat as a floor" in June and February alike. The ledger shows the same thing: the cream that reached Larrow Green carried 5¼ per cent more fat than the patrons had been credited.
- **Why it began when it did:** the error started with the station's own glass. The first season ran on glass borrowed from Larrow Green, and complaints are recorded from 1902.
- **Why it ended when it did:** Strawn condemned the pipettes on 12 June 1923. New glass came in July, and the average went to 3.85 from 1924.
- **Why nothing else had to change:** the error lay entirely in one piece of glass. The cows, hay, scales, acid, tester and method were the same before, during and after, which the 1911 interval shows directly.

Why the lot was short cannot be determined. Ammon's works books burned in 1931. Junia's reground mold is a guess, and so is the *Chronicle*'s knowing fraud.

**The three competing explanations:**
1. **Patrons watering their milk.** Advanced by manager Selby Vose in the June 1919 circular, and suspected by the Association's officers. *Defeated by:* Strawn's lactometer tests at every visit from 1908, which never once found water. The station-gain ledger also shows the cream arriving richer than credited, and water would lower both. The farmers' scale weights also matched the station's.
2. **The tester stealing cream and writing low tests to hide it.** Urged at the 1925 hearing by an officer of the Association, and believed by hauler Oram's father. *Defeated by:* the 1911 interval, when the same man's tests rose with borrowed glass. The ledger shows more fat reaching the creamery than was credited, not less, and the 1924 tests were normal under the same tester.
3. **Wet bog hay thinning the cream.** Advanced by the Association's 1908 committee and an Ashlin veterinarian in 1926, and provisionally conceded by Falke. *Defeated by:* the college's own 1911 Bulletin No. 214, which found at most 0.05 in winter and nothing on summer pasture. That cannot account for a constant 0.19 in July. The same hay was fed through the 1911 interval and after 1924, when the tests were normal.

**Subsidiary questions.**
- **Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?** **No.** The four exhibits were the test book, the receiving book, Emmet's weigh book and Strawn's 1923 notebook, which recorded the condemnation. The crate sat in the loft twenty feet above the two sittings held in the weigh room, and nobody fetched it or compared the receiving book with the notebook.
- **What did the tester himself believe?** Ansel Keddie believed it was **"something in the glass."** He said so at home in the summer of 1911, twice, according to Duncan, and set a question mark in the book. He never dared write it down, because he could not prove it.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** (148 words) In 1897 the Larrow Valley creamery cooperative's Ostrey Hollow skimming station began testing with pipettes from Tarnet that delivered 5 per cent short. Because patrons were paid on the station test, every farmer's butterfat read 3.61 instead of 3.80 for twenty-six years: 76,000 lb, about $19,000. The office ledger recorded the missing fat every year as "station gain, 5¼ per cent, as usual," and nobody read it. The patrons were suspected of watering, the hay was blamed, and the tester, Ansel Keddie, was suspected of theft. A borrowed measure in 1911 raised the tests, but nobody acted. The inspector condemned the glass in 1923 without drawing any inference. A 1925 arbitration paid $3,800 and dismissed Keddie. In 1958 his granddaughter Ivy found the crate and measured it. The proof was scattered across an invoice, a weigh book, a diary, a ledger, notebooks, a crate and memories, and it took all eight to see.
