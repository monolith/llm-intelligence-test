# Answer Sheet — Part 1 (Section A, stories 1–4)

*Source note: this session had no notes from segments 1–2, so every answer rests on retellings r17–r24, which were read here. Where I resolved a conflict, the method is given in the text: a quoted document outranks memory, arithmetic, internal consistency, or independent majority.*

## Section A — Reconstruction

### 1. A cooperative that paid by the test (1889–1896)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was organized in 1889 by valley farmers meeting at Larrow Green, and it built its main creamery there in 1891. Gideon Vose organized it, served as its first president, and wrote its by-laws. Article seven of those by-laws is the hinge of everything that followed. A patron was paid not on the weight of his milk but on the pounds of butterfat the test found in it, and the test that governed was the one made at the station where he delivered. The printed by-laws are undated and the early minute book was lost in an office removal, so the year the article was adopted cannot be determined.

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley. The road climbs the whole way and has a hard pitch two miles below the hamlet. The station opened on **4 May 1896**. It was a two-storey board building on the flat by the creek, with a waist-high covered platform and an intake (weigh) room holding the scale, vat and separator. Above that room was a loft reached by a ladder nailed to the wall by the door. Milk was weighed, sampled, tested and separated there. The Oram wagon hauled the cream down to Larrow Green, and the skim went back to the farmer.

The station served mainly Hessel Bottom, six farms on wet ground. Emmet Brant's farm was the largest and hauled the most milk. Ansel Keddie (1861–1934) was tester and separator man from opening day and served **twenty-nine seasons**. In 1889 he had married Orra Brant, Emmet's sister, so the tester tested his brother-in-law's milk for a generation. In its first season the station borrowed testing glass from the Larrow Green creamery. That season's milk was carried on the creamery's books, and its quantity is not documented (Kyle's "eleven hundred thousand pounds" is hearsay).

Rejected: Hazel Brant's "ninety-five" as the year Keddie started. Falke's 4 May 1896 opening date, Duncan's statement that the test book began in 1896, and twenty-nine seasons (1896–1924) all agree with each other.

*Documents:* r22 (Falke), r18 (Duncan Keddie), r19 (*Chronicle*), r21 (Oram), r23 (Kyle), r24 (Orra Keddie).

### 2. Six dozen pipettes from Tarnet (1897, with sequels in 1923 and 1949)

Ammon & Sons of Tarnet, founded by Marcus Ammon, was a dairy-supply glass house that at its height furnished half the creamery glass in the state. On **14 April 1897** it invoiced the Larrow Valley Association for the Ostrey Hollow station's own testing outfit:

- 6 dozen milk-test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. at $0.35, $25.20
- 12 dozen 8-per-cent test bottles at $0.22, $31.68
- 1 acid measure, $0.90
- 1 four-bottle hand tester, $18.00
- freight, $2.15

The total was **$77.93**. The lines add to that figure and the facsimile prints it. Junia Ammon's prose says "$77.39" and the *Chronicle* repeats it, but that is a digit transposition. The outfit went into use on **1 May 1897**. Junia gives that date and Orra's diary confirms it independently: "done borrowing of Larrow Green." Kyle's 1898 and Hazel's "ninety-nine" are rejected.

The pipettes were marked 17.6 c.c. but delivered about **5 per cent** less, roughly 16.72 c.c., so each was about **0.88 c.c.** short. This is my inference from the 5 per cent error fixed independently by the ledger and the tests (stories 3 and 5). The *Chronicle*'s printed average of 16.27 would imply a 7.6 per cent error, which fits none of the station figures, so I read it as another transposition.

In 1923, after the lot was condemned, the Association reordered on 28 June. The firm answered on 2 July that the order for six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles was entered and that it regretted the trouble. It added: "our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name." The works books, which recorded glass, gauge, workman and day, burned in 1931. In her 1949 history Junia printed both papers and guessed that a reground mold had produced a consistently short lot, the one fault a consistency check cannot find. That is conjecture. The *Chronicle*'s charge of knowing fraud is unsupported, so why the glass was short cannot be determined. Junia's claim that the house sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900 is refuted by her own invoice.

*Documents:* r20 (Junia Ammon), r19, r24, r23, r22.

### 3. Low tests on Hessel Bottom and the hay committee (1897–1908)

From the time the station's own glass went into use, its milk tested low. Over the long period of complaint the station's average was **3.61**, while the milk's true value was about **3.80** and Larrow Green's own average was 3.78. The shortfall was **0.19** of a point, the same in the June flush as in February. Hazel calls it "flat as a floor" and Falke confirms it.

The patrons' weights were never in question. Emmet Brant kept a weigh book from **1 January 1897**. That is Duncan's date; Hazel says 1900 but admits she has no head for years. In it Emmet wrote his own scale weight beside the station's for every delivery, and the two agreed. The 1910 leaf, headed "1910," shows **200,000 lb even** delivered, with the note "his scale and theirs agreeing," paid on a test of **3.42**.

The valley had a built-in comparison. In the summer flush four of the six Hessel Bottom farms hauled over the ridge to Jerome Cudd's cheese company at Alder Corners in Vessey County, and the rest of the year they delivered to the station. Cudd's test book therefore records the same herds' milk tested with different glass.

The first complaint on record came in 1902. Orra's diary for March 1902 notes the store talk and Ansel's reply: "it is my figures they will look at." After that the patrons complained at every annual meeting for a quarter century, and the Association's officers suspected the patrons in return. In **January 1908** four men came up in Grigg's sleigh as an Association committee. They sampled the herds and dined at Emmet's, and looked at the barns but not at the station. In **March 1908** they reported that the coarse, wet bog hay of the bottom thinned the cream. From 1908 on, the state inspector tested with a lactometer at every visit and never found added water.

The hay verdict became the standing excuse. It could not explain a shortfall that was the same in summer, when the cows were on pasture, and in winter. It is finally disposed of by the 1911 interval and by Bulletin 214 (story 4).

*Documents:* r17 (Hazel Brant), r22, r24, r18, r19.

### 4. Nine weeks with a borrowed measure (1911)

On **8 May 1911** the station's own measuring glass broke. Orra wrote that day: "A. home cross about a broken measure." A measure borrowed from elsewhere was used for **nine weeks, until 10 July 1911**. For those nine weeks the station's tests stood markedly higher, and the day the station went back to its own glass they fell to their old level. Herds, pasture, season, men, machinery and supervision stayed the same, and the same tester made every test. Only the glass changed.

Ansel Keddie saw what it meant. That summer he came home saying it was "something in the glass." Duncan, then seventeen, remembered that his father said it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not his way. But Ansel would not write down what he could not prove. He entered the fact in the station book with a question mark beside it. He then went on for fourteen more years writing down every figure his own glass gave, and those figures were later used against him. That October, Duncan went down to Ashlin to teach.

Two other things in 1911 mattered only later. Selby Vose, Gideon's son, became manager of the Association. And the State Agricultural College at Farland ran a feeding trial on bog hay, published as **Bulletin No. 214**. It found that bog hay fed as the whole roughage lowered the test by at most 0.05 in winter and by nothing measurable on summer pasture. Nobody connected the trial to the station's low figures until county agent Falke obtained it in 1959. As he wrote, five hundredths in winter "will not carry nineteen hundredths in July."

The borrowed-measure interval is the one natural experiment in the whole history. It clears the tester, the cows and the hay, and it points at the glass twelve years before an inspector condemned it and forty-seven years before anyone measured it.

*Documents:* r22 (¶6 and the added note), r24 (8 May and October 1911), r18 (§IV), r23.

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### 5. "Station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual": the ledger and the water circular (1898–1929)

Every year the Association's Ostrey Hollow station account held three figures and a sentence. The first figure was the butterfat credited to patrons on the station test. The second was the butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in the station's cream. The third was their difference, entered as "station gain … **five and a quarter per cent, as usual**, carried to the general account." Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper, wrote that line every year he served. He was a dry, correct man who "never asked anybody anything in forty years." Bertram Kyle, clerk at the outer desk for twenty-five years, copied it into the fair annual statement that was read out to fifty farmers at every annual meeting. Selby Vose was manager from 1911 for about a quarter century. He was courteous to every caller, but he put his thumb on the bottom figure and signed, and never read the lines above it.

That line was the answer in plain sight. More fat reached the creamery than the station test credited: 3.80 against 3.61 is 5.26 per cent of the credited figure. Cream was not disappearing between the farms and the creamery. The test was under-reading it. For the arbitration, Kyle compiled **40,000,000 lb** of milk over the complaint years. That is 1,600,000 lb a year over twenty-five full years, with butterfat averaging **25¢ a pound**.

In **June 1919** Vose sent the Ostrey Hollow patrons a circular warning them against adding water to their milk. Kyle typed it and Vose signed it alone. Ansel Keddie would neither mail it nor leave it on the platform. He carried it himself to **eleven** families, stood at each door while the man read it, and came home at eleven at night with his hat still on. Orra's contemporaneous diary gives eleven; Hazel, a small child at the time, remembers nine. I follow the diary. Hazel's mother burned her copy, and no family answered. Two days later Emmet Brant stood in the Keddies' yard and asked Ansel why he had carried the paper if he did not believe it. Ansel answered that he "would not have another man carry it." Emmet never entered the house again, and he died unreconciled in spring 1929. Duncan's memory of Emmet at the Keddies' Sunday table "for thirty-six years" is outranked by the diary. Duncan had been teaching away at Ashlin since 1911.

*Documents:* r23 (Kyle), r24, r17, r19, r22, r18.

### 6. The inspector condemns the glass (1923)

Merle Strawn was the state dairy inspector on the Ordell circuit, a heavy, quiet man who kept everything in a notebook with a rubber band round it. He was related to nobody in the valley. From 1908 he tested the Ostrey Hollow milk with a lactometer on every visit and never found water. On **12 June 1923**, on an ordinary circuit visit, he ran the station's milk pipettes against a calibrated burette and found they would not deliver their mark. He condemned the whole lot on the spot and told the manager to order new ones. He recorded the condemnation and said nothing about what the glass had been doing for the previous twenty-six years.

The Association reordered from Ammon & Sons on 28 June and received the firm's evasive reply of 2 July (story 2). The new glass was in use from July. Orra's diary for July 1923 notes "new glass at the station and new pails, and the roof is still bad." In the office the matter drew no more comment than a broken window would have. From 1924 the station's average test stood at **3.85** and stayed there, a normal county figure, with the same herds, the same hay and, until April 1925, the same tester.

The condemned glass was crated and carried up the ladder into the loft over the intake room. It stayed there, under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff, for **thirty-five years**. Cleve Oram put his hand on it while looking for wire and a hay fork. Nobody set the new, normal tests beside the old low ones, and the inspector drew no inference.

Rejected: Hazel's "twenty-one" and Kyle's "twenty-two." The 1923 date rests on four sources that are independent of one another: Ammon's dated letter book, Falke's day-dated report, Orra's contemporaneous diary, and Duncan's account of the loft and of Strawn's 1923 notebook.

*Documents:* r22, r20, r24, r23, r18, r21, r19, r17.

### 7. The arbitration and the tester's dismissal (1925–1934)

In **January 1925** the Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned against the station, and Emmet Brant's name was on the paper. The station test book was impounded in March. A board of arbitration chaired by an Ashlin attorney sat from **17 March to 4 May 1925**. Ansel went down to Ashlin on the first day in his good coat, which Orra had ironed twice. The board held three sittings. The first was at the Cooperative's office in Ashlin. The other two were held in April in the station's weigh room, where the books, scales and machinery were. About a dozen men stood outside on the platform in the rain, and the crate of condemned glass sat twenty feet above the board's heads. Falke's "sat at Ashlin" reflects only where the proceedings began.

Four books went before the board: the station test book in Keddie's hand, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book (lent, and wanted back), and Strawn's notebook for 1923. Nobody laid the receiving book beside the inspector's notebook, and nobody examined the glass. All three theories were argued: water, theft by the tester (urged by an Association officer), and bog hay.

Ansel Keddie was dismissed on **30 April 1925**, "twenty-nine seasons and a letter." He came home at his usual hour, hung up his apron and ate his ham. Four days later, on 4 May, the board found no cause established and awarded the patrons **$3,800 "without admission,"** charged to the station account. The $3,800 rests on Falke, on Kyle, who handled the papers, and on the *Chronicle*. I rejected Oram's $3,500, which he had from his father, as well as Kyle's year "twenty-four" and Hazel's "twenty-six." Hazel's year may come from the Ashlin veterinarian's 1926 letter about the hay. Her father Roy Brant never went to another Association meeting.

Afterward Ansel rebuilt his cow shed, whistling. By 1927, at sixty-six, he was a jobbing carpenter, better at it than at testing and paid a quarter as much. Emmet died in spring 1929, still estranged. Ansel died at home in **February 1934**. Nobody had ever named the charge against him, so nobody ever cleared him: "a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name." Orra died in 1948 at eighty-two.

*Documents:* r24, r22, r23, r18, r21, r17, r19.

### 8. Ivy Keddie climbs the ladder (1958–1970)

Ivy Keddie, born in 1931, was Duncan's daughter and therefore Ansel's granddaughter. She grew up in Ashlin, took her first degree at the State Agricultural College at Farland, and stayed on as a graduate student under Professor Leland Vaught of dairy husbandry. In 1958 she pulled the scattered record together:

- Roy Brant lent her the weigh book (lent, not left).
- She found Strawn's 1923 notebook in the Farland college library, where his notebooks had been since 1947.
- Rosalie Cudd, Jerome Cudd's daughter, who had run the Alder Corners factory since 1941, copied its test figures for her.

In **February 1958** she climbed into the loft of the old station and found the crate. The station had closed in 1946 and was being used to store hay tools. She took the crate to Farland, and on **9 March 1958** she measured the surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette. Falke and Oram say nine survived; the *Chronicle* says eleven. Every one delivered less than its 17.6 c.c. mark, and all were short in the same proportion, about 5 per cent. A short charge of milk in a bottle ruled for the full charge reads the fat low by that same proportion every time.

**Bulletin No. 471** (Vaught and Keddie, October 1958) concluded that no milk had been watered and no cream stolen. By my reckoning, the patrons were short-credited **76,000 lb** of butterfat over twenty-five full years, worth **$19,000** at 25¢. Their $3,800 award was one fifth of that. The *Chronicle*'s $19,600 and Oram's "sixty-seven thousand pounds" are rejected.

The *Ashlin Chronicle* ran the story on 3 October 1958. It called Ivy a great-niece and Rosalie a niece, and accused Ammon of knowing fraud. In 1959 county agent Orin Falke reported to the Ordell board and recommended no county action. After the report went to type he obtained Bulletin 214, withdrew his concession on the hay, and corrected "great-niece" to granddaughter.

The records ended up in three buildings:

- **Ordell County Historical Rooms, Ashlin:** the test book (in the Cooperative office 1925–1959, deposited 1959), the crate (deposited 1959), and the weigh book (passed to Hazel on Roy's death in 1962, given by her in 1963).
- **Farland college library:** Strawn's notebooks.
- **Vessey County Historical Society:** the Alder Corners book (1970).

Ansel was cleared twenty-four years after his death.

*Documents:* r19, r22, r18, r21, r17, r24.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** Ansel and Orra's son was Duncan, born in 1894. Orra's diary has him at five in 1899 and leaving to teach in 1911. Duncan states that Ivy, born in 1931, is his daughter, "which makes her my father's granddaughter." Oram also calls her "the granddaughter." Two documents say "great-niece": the *Chronicle*, which was assembled in a day and a half from a press release, and Falke's report at ¶2. They are one error, not two. Falke almost certainly took it from the newspaper or the same press release, and he then verified the relationship himself and retracted it in his closing note. Duncan's remark that he had "twice seen it printed otherwise" matches these two printings exactly.

**B2. Ivy and Hazel were second cousins.** Emmet Brant and Orra were siblings. Emmet's son Roy and Orra's son Duncan were therefore first cousins, and their daughters Hazel and Ivy, being in the same generation, are second cousins. Both Hazel and Duncan state this. Oram's "first cousin once removed" is wrong: that relation requires a one-generation gap, and Hazel and Ivy are in the same generation. What the sources leave open is whether Orra was Emmet's full sister (Duncan: "brother and sister," "the younger of the two") or his half-sister "by his father's second wife" (Hazel). Orra's own pages from before her marriage do not survive, so this cannot be settled. If she was a half-sister, Roy and Duncan were strictly half first cousins and Hazel and Ivy half second cousins. The sources also do not name Roy's or Duncan's wives or any other children.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose,** the Association's organizer, first president and author of its by-laws. Duncan and Kyle independently confirm this, and both call the "nephew" version an Ashlin rumor. It matters for three reasons. Gideon's article seven made the station test the sole measure of each patron's pay, and that rule is what turned a short pipette into a generation of lost income. Selby then ran that system from 1911. He signed the "station gain, 5¼ per cent, as usual" sheets every year without reading them, and in 1919 he signed the circular accusing the patrons of watering their milk. He also ordered the replacement glass in 1923 without connecting it to anything. The founder's rule, administered by the founder's son, supplied both the injury and the blindness to it.

**B4. No relationship between the Voses and the Keddies is established.** Duncan, the Keddies' son, whose mother "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection" and never mentioned a Vose, says there was none. He attributes the rumor to confusion with the Ashlin Voses, who were not related to Gideon either. The only contrary source is Oram, who repeats what his mother had from her mother and admits he "could not draw you the line on paper." A family member's specific denial outranks secondhand talk with no stated line of descent. Strictly, a negative cannot be proved, but no source gives any evidence of kinship.


# Answer Sheet — Part 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

**C1. (a) Proportion of error.** The station's tests read **5 per cent low**, so a recorded test was 0.95 of the true one. From the glassware: the test bottle's neck is ruled for a 17.6 c.c. charge. A pipette that delivers only *d* c.c. puts in *d*/17.6 of the intended milk and therefore *d*/17.6 of the intended fat, and the reading is short by the same proportion. For the error to be 5 per cent, *d* = 0.95 × 17.6 = **16.72 c.c.**, which is 0.88 c.c. short per charge.

*How I resolved the pipette average.* The *Chronicle* prints the average delivery as 16.27 c.c. That would give a 7.56 per cent error, so a true 3.80 would read 3.51, a 0.29 shortfall. This contradicts every independent station figure: the flat 0.19 shortfall (Hazel, Falke), the 3.61 recorded average (Falke), and the 5¼ per cent station gain (Kyle). The value 16.72 fits all of them exactly. I therefore read 16.27 as a digit transposition, the same kind of error as the *Chronicle*'s $77.39 for $77.93. This correction is an inference from arithmetic.

**(b) True average and shortfall.** Recorded average 3.61 (Falke). True average = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. The recorded tests were **0.19 points** short, matching the "nineteen hundredths" that Hazel and Falke report. As a check, after the glass was replaced in 1924 the station averaged 3.85, and Larrow Green averaged 3.78, so 3.80 is a normal figure.

**(c) Uncredited butterfat, over 25 full delivery years.** Milk received was 40,000,000 lb (Kyle: 1,600,000 lb a year × 25 years).
- True fat: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb.
- Credited: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb.
- **Uncredited: 76,000 lb** (the same as 40,000,000 × 0.0019).

Oram's "sixty-seven thousand pounds" is a transposition of 76,000 and is rejected.

**(d) Money, and the fraction received.** 76,000 lb × $0.25 (Kyle's average price) = **$19,000**. The patrons received $3,800. 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **1/5, or 20 per cent**. The *Chronicle*'s $19,600 does not follow from any figure in the sources and is rejected on arithmetic.

**C2. The largest patron (Emmet Brant) in 1910.** He delivered 200,000 lb and was paid on a recorded test of 3.42 (Hazel, reading from the 1910 leaf of the weigh book).
- (a) Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of butterfat.
- (b) True test: 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60, so true fat was 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb. Owed and not credited: **360 lb**.
- (c) At the long-run average of 25¢ a pound, 360 × $0.25 = **$90**.

The actual 1910 butterfat price is not in the sources, so $90 uses the average. If one applied Hazel's flat 0.19 instead of the proportional error, the result would be 380 lb and $95. But the error is proportional, and at a 3.42 level it comes to 0.18.

**C3. Totals over the same 25 years.**
- (a) Credited to patrons: **1,444,000 lb**.
- (b) Reaching Larrow Green in the station's cream: **1,520,000 lb**. This treats the fat in the cream as the true fat, which is what the station-gain line measures.
- (c) The difference of 76,000 lb is **5.26 per cent of the fat credited** (76,000 ÷ 1,444,000), which is Kyle's "five and a quarter per cent, as usual." It is **5.00 per cent of the fat actually received** (76,000 ÷ 1,520,000). The ledger's "station gain" was taken as a percentage of the credited figure, the smaller base. The glass error was 5 per cent of the truth.

**C4. (a) Invoice total: $77.93.**
- 25.20 + 31.68 = 56.88
- + 0.90 = 57.78
- + 18.00 = 75.78
- + 2.15 = **77.93**

The facsimile prints $77.93. The prose "$77.39" (Junia, copied by the *Chronicle*) is a transposition.

**(b) Pipettes: 6 dozen, or 72** (at 35¢, $25.20). Each was about **0.88 c.c.** short, delivering 16.72 c.c. against its 17.6 c.c. mark, as derived in C1(a). If the *Chronicle*'s 16.27 were taken at face value the shortfall would be 1.33 c.c., but that value is rejected.

**C5. (a) How long the glass was in use, versus the money years.** The faulty glass was in use from **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923**, which is **26 years, 1 month and 11 days** (about 26 years and 6 weeks). The one exception is the nine weeks from 8 May to 10 July 1911, when a borrowed measure was used. The money calculation runs over **25 full delivery years** (Kyle's 40,000,000 lb ÷ 1,600,000 lb a year = 25), which is 1898–1922 inclusive. The two numbers differ because the glass's first season (1897, from 1 May) and last season (1923, to 12 June) were partial. The arbitration figures that Kyle compiled cover only complete years of station accounts. The 1911 interval is also inside the in-use span but was not affected.

**(b) Milk received in the first season: cannot be determined from the sources.** The only figure is Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds." He had it secondhand "from men who were there" and says it "is not in any book I ever ruled." The first season's sheets were kept on the creamery's books and never filed with the station papers. The sources establish that the station was receiving milk that season, but the only quantity is uncorroborated hearsay.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment.** It ran from **8 May to 10 July 1911, nine weeks**. Falke gives the dates, and Orra's diary independently records the broken measure on 8 May. The variable was the measuring glass: the station's own versus a borrowed one. Everything else was held constant: the herds, the pasture and hay, the season, the tester, his method and supervision, the scales and the machinery. The result was that the tests rose markedly while the borrowed measure was used and fell back the day the station's own glass returned. What it establishes, and a calculation alone does not, is **causation**. The arithmetic can show that a 5 per cent short charge would produce a 0.19 shortfall, but only the switch shows that the station's own glass actually produced it, and that cows, hay, water and the tester did not. It also shows this twelve years before anyone condemned the glass.

What it does **not** establish:
- the exact size of the error (no figures for the interval are given)
- that the borrowed measure was itself accurate
- which item in the outfit was at fault (pipette or bottle)
- why the glass was short, or whether Ammon knew
- that anybody in authority noticed; Ansel only put a question mark in his book

**D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream.** Five reasons, each from a different narrator:

1. **Kyle's ledger (r23).** Every year the cream reaching Larrow Green held 5¼ per cent *more* fat than the station credited. A tester who skimmed cream would make the creamery's receipts *smaller* than the credits, not larger.
2. **The 1911 borrowed measure (Falke r22, confirmed by Orra r24 and Duncan r18).** The same man, under the same supervision, produced higher tests for nine weeks, and the tests fell only when his own glass came back.
3. **The 1924 recovery (Falke r22).** With new glass the station averaged 3.85 from 1924, while Keddie was still the tester until April 1925.
4. **The shape of the shortfall (Hazel r17).** It was a flat 0.19 in every month and every season. That is the signature of a fixed proportional instrument error. Pilfering would vary with opportunity and volume.
5. **The glass itself (Ivy's 1958 measurement, r19/r22).** The pipettes delivered short in a uniform proportion.

These reasons are independent because each rests on a different record, kept by different people for different purposes: the office's general ledger, the station test book in two separate periods, the farmers' own comparison, and a college laboratory measurement. None of them borrows its figures from another.

Reasons 1, 2, 3 and 4 would all still hold if the glass had never been measured in 1958. The ledger is the strongest, because it refutes theft by itself.

Did the bookkeeper ever question the ledger entries? **No.** Wilbur Teague wrote the "station gain … as usual" line every year and "never asked anybody anything in forty years" (Kyle). The theft accusation was urged at the hearing by "an officer of the Association" (Falke). The retellings I read do not name that officer as Teague. What they do establish is that the Association's own books refuted the charge every year and that the man who wrote those books never questioned them.

**D3. Did the manager know before 1923 that the tests ran low?** **Yes, he knew the tests ran low. He did not, on the evidence, know what his own ledger said about why.** The low tests were the subject of complaint at every annual meeting from 1902 onward, and his own June 1919 circular warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons about water only makes sense as a response to low tests. So knowledge of the low tests is certain. What Kyle's eyewitness account rules out, as far as testimony can, is that Vose understood the station-gain line: "he read the totals and he never read the lines above them … put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign." Signing the sheet is not reading it. It is an inference, though a well-supported one, that he never saw the 5¼ per cent line as evidence that the fat was arriving and the test was wrong. Had he read it, the water accusation would have made no sense, because water lowers the fat in the cream along with the test.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. Causal theory.** The Ostrey Hollow station's own testing outfit was bought from Ammon & Sons, Tarnet, on 14 April 1897 and put into use on 1 May 1897. Its six dozen milk pipettes were marked 17.6 c.c. but delivered about 5 per cent less. Under the by-laws every patron was paid on the station's test. Each short charge went into a bottle whose neck was ruled for the full 17.6 c.c., so every fat reading came out 5 per cent low.

- **Size of the error:** a true 3.80 read as 3.61, a shortfall of 0.19.
- **Why that size:** the error was a fixed ratio of volumes, not something that varied with feed or season, so it held "flat as a floor" in June and February alike. The ledger shows the same thing: the cream that reached Larrow Green carried 5¼ per cent more fat than the patrons had been credited.
- **Why it began when it did:** the error started with the station's own glass. The first season ran on glass borrowed from Larrow Green, and complaints are recorded from 1902.
- **Why it ended when it did:** Strawn condemned the pipettes on 12 June 1923. New glass came in July, and the average went to 3.85 from 1924.
- **Why nothing else had to change:** the error lay entirely in one piece of glass. The cows, hay, scales, acid, tester and method were the same before, during and after, which the 1911 interval shows directly.

Why the lot was short cannot be determined. Ammon's works books burned in 1931. Junia's reground mold is a guess, and so is the *Chronicle*'s knowing fraud.

**The three competing explanations:**
1. **Patrons watering their milk.** Advanced by manager Selby Vose in the June 1919 circular, and suspected by the Association's officers. *Defeated by:* Strawn's lactometer tests at every visit from 1908, which never once found water. The station-gain ledger also shows the cream arriving richer than credited, and water would lower both. The farmers' scale weights also matched the station's.
2. **The tester stealing cream and writing low tests to hide it.** Urged at the 1925 hearing by an officer of the Association, and believed by hauler Oram's father. *Defeated by:* the 1911 interval, when the same man's tests rose with borrowed glass. The ledger shows more fat reaching the creamery than was credited, not less, and the 1924 tests were normal under the same tester.
3. **Wet bog hay thinning the cream.** Advanced by the Association's 1908 committee and an Ashlin veterinarian in 1926, and provisionally conceded by Falke. *Defeated by:* the college's own 1911 Bulletin No. 214, which found at most 0.05 in winter and nothing on summer pasture. That cannot account for a constant 0.19 in July. The same hay was fed through the 1911 interval and after 1924, when the tests were normal.

**Subsidiary questions.**
- **Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?** **No.** The four exhibits were the test book, the receiving book, Emmet's weigh book and Strawn's 1923 notebook, which recorded the condemnation. The crate sat in the loft twenty feet above the two sittings held in the weigh room, and nobody fetched it or compared the receiving book with the notebook.
- **What did the tester himself believe?** Ansel Keddie believed it was **"something in the glass."** He said so at home in the summer of 1911, twice, according to Duncan, and set a question mark in the book. He never dared write it down, because he could not prove it.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** (148 words) In 1897 the Larrow Valley creamery cooperative's Ostrey Hollow skimming station began testing with pipettes from Tarnet that delivered 5 per cent short. Because patrons were paid on the station test, every farmer's butterfat read 3.61 instead of 3.80 for twenty-six years: 76,000 lb, about $19,000. The office ledger recorded the missing fat every year as "station gain, 5¼ per cent, as usual," and nobody read it. The patrons were suspected of watering, the hay was blamed, and the tester, Ansel Keddie, was suspected of theft. A borrowed measure in 1911 raised the tests, but nobody acted. The inspector condemned the glass in 1923 without drawing any inference. A 1925 arbitration paid $3,800 and dismissed Keddie. In 1958 his granddaughter Ivy found the crate and measured it. The proof was scattered across an invoice, a weigh book, a diary, a ledger, notebooks, a crate and memories, and it took all eight to see.
