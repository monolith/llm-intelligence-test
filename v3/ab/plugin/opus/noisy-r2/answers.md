## Story 1 — The Sentence in Article VII (1889–1896)

**About:** the founding of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, its first
president Gideon Vose, and the by-law that made everything afterward expensive.

Farmers of the Larrow Valley organized the Association in 1889 at a meeting at Larrow Green —
Dell Rundle puts it in the Green schoolhouse in cold weather, with a horse getting loose. Gideon
Vose was its first president (1889–1911; died 1917) and wrote the by-laws himself at his kitchen
table. The main creamery was built in 1891 on Larrow Creek below the mill.

The by-laws contain Article VII, the hinge of the whole history: a patron is paid not for the
weight of his milk but for the pounds of butterfat the test finds in it, and *"the test of the
station where he delivers shall govern."* Rundle, quoting it whole, calls it a clause "put in for
convenience… the sentence on which the whole trouble afterward turned." Orin Falke, the county
agent, makes the same point flatly: under payment by weight nothing that followed would have cost
any man a dollar. The year of adoption cannot be firmly established — Effie Loomis, secretary
1901–1940, says 1892; Rundle says 1891; Nyle Grigg refers it to "the 1893 meeting"; and Falke,
who went and looked, found the printed by-laws undated on their face and the 1889–1892 minute
book lost in the Association's 1938 office removal. **1892 is the best-supported date and is not
proven.**

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles
up the valley, chiefly to serve the farms of Hessel Bottom. Cleve Oram describes it: a two-floor
board building on the flat where the road bends, a covered waist-high platform on the road side,
the intake room with the scale and vat, the separator behind, and over the intake room a loft
reached by a ladder nailed to the wall and a trap of plank with a rope on it. Milk was weighed
in, sampled, tested, separated; cream went down to Larrow Green, skim back to the farmer.

The station opened **4 May 1896** — the date the station test book begins. **Ansel Keddie**
(b. 1861) went up in 1895 and was tester and separator man from the opening for twenty-nine
seasons. It opened with no outfit of its own: the glass had been ordered and had not come, so
Hobart Sill, the buttermaker, made up an outfit out of his own bench glass at Larrow Green and
sent it up. The first season's intake was never entered in any station book and cannot now be
recovered.

## Story 2 — A Lot of Glass from Tarnet (1897–1923; told in 1949)

**About:** Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet, and the six dozen pipettes that were the
physical cause of everything.

Ammon & Sons filled four stories behind the railroad yard at Tarnet and supplied half the glass
of the state's creameries: test bottles, pipettes, acid measures, burettes. A milk-test pipette
must *deliver* a stated quantity — not hold it — which is the harder measurement, because milk
clings to the wall as it runs. The mark goes on the stem while the glass is warm; the piece is
annealed overnight, gauged the next morning, and packed by the dozen in straw in a case with the
customer's name in blue pencil.

The firm's own historian, Junia Ammon, reproduces the invoice in facsimile:

> 14 April 1897, sold to the Larrow Valley Co-op. Creamery Assn. for the Ostrey Hollow station —
> 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 = 25.20; 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck,
> at .22 = 31.68; 1 acid measure = .90; 1 hand tester, 4 bottle = 18.00; freight = 2.15;
> **total 77.93.**

That outfit went into use at Ostrey Hollow on **1 May 1897** — the day Orra Keddie wrote in her
diary, "A. says the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green."

The lot was short. Every stem was blown 17.6 c.c.; the surviving pipettes delivered a mean of
**16.72 c.c.**, exactly 0.95 of the mark. Junia Ammon's account of how, offered because "nobody
else is going to": the mold had been reground and the lot ran short, an accident no one on the
floor caught, because a boy checks the first of a lot, the tenth and the fiftieth and finds them
consistent — "they are consistently wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind cannot
find." The works books that would have proved it burned with the old third floor in 1931.

On **12 June 1923** the state dairy inspector ran those pipettes against a burette and condemned
the whole crate. The Association ordered replacements on 28 June; Ammon's letter of 2 July
entered the order, regretted the trouble, and said their books of the nineties would tell nothing
about the lot in question. Junia Ammon calls that letter "a thin thing to send to a customer of
twenty-six years' standing" and prints it beside the invoice. *The Ashlin Chronicle*'s charge
that a cheap short lot was put off knowingly is unsupported by anything in the record.

*Documents:* r20 (Junia Ammon), r07 (Nym, item 2 and item 10), r24 (Orra Keddie), r06 (Strawn),
r19 (Chronicle), r04 (Ivy Keddie).

## Story 3 — Nineteen Hundredths, Flat as a Floor (1902–1926)

**About:** the quarter-century quarrel over the low tests, and the three explanations the valley
produced, none of which was right.

From very nearly the day the station opened, Ostrey Hollow milk tested lower than milk anywhere
else in Ordell County. The station's long-run average was **3.61**; the Larrow Green creamery's
own patrons ran **3.78**, an ordinary figure for the county. The deficiency was **0.19 of a
point**, and — the fact that killed every explanation offered — it was the same 0.19 in the flush
of June as in the depth of February, in wet years and dry. Because the error was proportional, it
fell hardest on the best milk: a herd recorded at 3.42 was short 0.18, a herd recorded at 4.56
short 0.24.

The first complaint of record was 1902 and it was renewed in every decade after. Three
explanations were current, and all three were argued before the arbitration board in 1925.

**The hay.** In 1908 a committee under Nyle Grigg sampled the six Hessel Bottom herds, visited
each farm three or four times, drew and sealed its own samples, watched the milking, hauling,
weighing and testing, tried the station scales against a known weight and the patrons' scales
against the station's, and inquired into every barn's water supply. It found the stock thin in
the flank and rough in the coat and concluded that "what such land gives in quantity it withholds
in quality" — the remedy lay with the land, not the station. It insisted: "We found no dishonesty
of any kind, in any quarter." But it had sampled **January, February and March only**, and so
could not see that the gap was flat year-round. Grigg's 1919 covering letter to the reprint
claims the committee sampled "through the whole of 1908 — every month of that year," contradicting
the report printed beneath it, and states that the College's Bulletin No. 214 found bog hay to
depress the test by "two tenths in the winter feeding." The bulletin says: *"not more than five
hundredths of one per cent in winter, and… nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."*
That fourfold misquotation is the load-bearing error of the hay theory. Roland Kepp, V.S.,
repeated it in 1926, added a soil chemistry of sour, lime-poor bottoms, put the gap at 0.21, and
called the agreement of "two tenths," 0.20 and 0.21 conclusive — while dismissing the 1911
interval, which he mis-stated as six weeks, and admitting he had "never in my life read a test."
Falke conceded the hay in 1959 and withdrew the concession in press on obtaining Bulletin 214.

**The water.** Manager Selby Vose circulated a printed warning about added water to the Hollow
patrons in June 1919 over his own signature.

**The theft.** The valley's settled opinion, heard "a hundred times… never contradicted at the
store": cream left by the back door.

*Documents:* r15 (Grigg, letter and 1908 report), r16 (Kepp), r22 (Falke), r11 (Vaught §VI),
r12 (Rundle), r02, r17 (Hazel Brant), r09 (Roy Brant).

## Story 4 — Nine Weeks in 1911

**About:** the experiment that ran itself at Ostrey Hollow and was not read for forty-seven years.

On **8 May 1911** the station's own measuring glass broke. Orra Keddie's diary for that day: "A.
home cross about a broken measure. Rain all day, the low piece under water again." A measure
borrowed from elsewhere — Ivy Keddie, who had the records, says it came up from Larrow Green with
the cream wagon; Roy Brant remembered the state's man leaving his own that June, and the point
cannot be settled and does not matter — was used in its place until **10 July 1911**. **Nine
weeks.**

Through those nine weeks the station's average test stood at **3.80**. On the day the station's
own measure came back it fell to **3.61** and stayed there.

Treated as an experiment, the interval is the strongest single piece of evidence in the whole
history, and Professor Vaught rates it above all the arithmetic. One variable changed: the glass
vessel that drew the charge of milk. Everything else was held constant, and held constant not by
design but by circumstance — the same nine families, the same herds, the same pasture in the same
season, the same weighing, the same acid, the same test bottles, the same hand tester, the same
tester at the bench under no different supervision, the same book kept in the same five columns.
It disposes at a stroke of every explanation that looks to the herds, the ground, the feed or the
men, because none of those changed and the figures did.

Ansel Keddie entered the fact in his test book and set his own question mark against it. His son
Duncan, seventeen that year, remembered him coming home saying it was something in the glass, and
saying it twice — at supper and again by the door, which was not his way — and never daring to
put it in the book, because he was a man who would not write down what he could not prove. He had
nothing in his hand but a suspicion about a piece of glass, and against it stood the outfit he had
been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county. So he kept on for another
fourteen years and wrote down every figure the glass gave him, and those figures were what
convicted him.

The question mark lay unread until 1958. Kepp dismissed the interval in 1926 as "six weeks of
June and July, when every herd in the county is at its best" — wrong in length, wrong in month
(it began 8 May, in the rain), and wrong in principle, since the fall back to 3.61 on 10 July
happened in mid-summer with the stock still on the same ground.

*Documents:* r24 (Orra Keddie's diary), r03 (Ansel Keddie), r18 (Duncan Keddie), r04 (Ivy
Keddie), r11 §IV (Vaught), r22 ¶6 (Falke), r09 (Roy Brant), r16 (Kepp, dissenting and wrong).

## Story 5 — Station Gain: the Surplus in Plain Sight (1893–1953)

**About:** the Association's own books, and the two men who held the disproof of the theft theory
in their hands for forty years.

Hobart Sill was buttermaker at Larrow Green from 1893 for forty-one years. The cream wagon came
down from Ostrey Hollow daily but Sunday, and Sill weighed every can into the cold room himself —
he never took another man's figure, and his hand is on every page of the receiving book. His
testimony, taken down the day he retired in 1934: *"That cream ran heavy. Every year it ran
heavy. There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there… Always more.
Never less."* He explained it away — cream is not milk and will not stand still and be counted —
and added that nobody ever asked him to make anything of it.

In 1905 Wilbur Teague, the Association's bookkeeper, invented at his own desk a line in the
Ostrey Hollow station account. Three figures and a sentence, every year: pounds of butterfat
credited to the patrons on the station test; pounds of butterfat received at Larrow Green in the
station cream; and the difference, entered as **station gain**, carried to the general account.
A specimen year: **credited 57,760 lb / received 60,800 lb / gain 3,040 lb, "five and a quarter
per cent, as usual."** Teague's own comment: *"A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every
year is furniture."* Its constancy is exactly why nobody looked; an erratic figure would have
been investigated.

Teague read the surplus as proof that the tester was stealing cream — and it is the refutation of
that charge, since stolen cream would have reached Larrow Green **light**. He never questioned the
entries. He did put the matter to Selby Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone: "I was the
Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience." Vose signed the annual sheet carrying that line
every year of his managership.

Bertram Kyle, who sat at the outer desk at Larrow Green for twenty-five years and copied the line
into a fair hand for the annual meeting, gives the reason it survived: *"It had a name. That is
the thing about a name. A figure with a name on it has been explained, and you rule under it and
go on to the next."* His moral is the story's: "Nothing in that office was hidden… It was not a
secret. It was worse than a secret. It was in plain sight with a name on it."

One man drew the inference. Dell Rundle, a schoolteacher writing a jubilee pamphlet, was allowed
to look at the ledgers on another errand, quoted the identical entry, and retracted his own
chapter in print: *"Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the
Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand."* He left the paragraph standing
and corrected it underneath.

*Documents:* r14 (Sill), r05 (Teague), r23 (Kyle), r12 (Rundle's note in press), r02 (Loomis).

## Story 6 — Hessel Bottom, and the Road over the Ridge (1889–1963)

**About:** the six farms that lost the money, the weigh book that cleared the scales, the cheese
factory that was the outside control, and the ten-year silence between a brother and a
brother-in-law.

Hessel Bottom was 160 acres of wet ground carrying **six farms** — you could cut hay off it in a
dry August and lose a horse in a wet one — and about **nine to eleven families**, a farm holding
more than one. Emmet Brant (1854–1929) had the largest piece, sixty-odd acres and 38 cows, and
hauled the most milk of any man on the creek. His sister **Orra Brant** married **Ansel Keddie**
in 1889, so the tester accused of shorting the patrons was married to the sister of the patron who
lost most by it and ate at his table for thirty-six years.

From **1 January 1897** Emmet kept a two-column weigh book: his own barn-scale weight of every
can, and the figure the station gave back. The two columns always agreed, barring a pound or two
when a can slopped on the hill — "two scales, kept by two men who by the end were not speaking,
and the scales never fell out at all." The weighing was therefore never at fault. The 1910 leaf,
which his granddaughter Hazel could recite with her eyes shut: **two hundred thousand pounds
even, paid on a test of 3.42.**

From **1915**, four of the six Bottom men hauled over Redlow Ridge — four miles of switchback,
shut from November to April — to the Alder Corners Cheese Co. in Vessey County, from about 1 June
to mid-September, for **sixteen seasons, the last in 1930**. Their reasons were commercial: cheese
paid better in the flush. **Jerome Cudd**, who built that factory in 1913, tested on his own bench
with his own glassware and never compared his figures with anyone's — "it never entered his head."
His book, summer 1919: *"The four Hessel Bottom men: 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet
ground, whatever they say over the ridge."* The full table, 1915–1930, runs between **3.78 and
3.82 every season, averaging about 3.80**. His daughter Rosalie, who ran the factory after him,
adds the disproof of the watering charge: "Cheese is a witness" — watered milk shows within a day,
the curd comes soft and will not knit — and in sixteen summers her father never complained of
those four men or docked one a cent.

In June 1919 the manager's printed circular about water in the milk came to the station; Ansel
carried the copies round himself and stood at each door while each man read it. Two days later
Emmet came up, would not come in, said in the yard that he had hauled whole milk since the day
the station opened, and asked why Ansel had carried the paper himself if he did not believe it.
"A. said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it." Neither went up the road
first again; the silence ran ten years, to Emmet's death in the spring of 1929.

The weigh book went to Roy Brant in 1929, was **lent** to Ivy Keddie in 1958 and returned, passed
to Hazel at Roy's death in 1962, and was given to the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1963.

*Documents:* r09 (Roy Brant), r17 (Hazel Brant), r10 (Rosalie Cudd), r24 (Orra Keddie), r18
(Duncan Keddie), r07 (Nym, items 4 and 13), r15 (1908 report).

## Story 7 — The Arbitration, and the Ruin of a Careful Man (1925–1934)

**About:** the board of three that heard everything and settled nothing, and what it cost Ansel
Keddie.

The patrons petitioned in January 1925. A board of three sat under **Alonzo Frick**, an attorney
of Ashlin, who chaired "because I was the one of the three who kept no cow." The **first session
was at the Association's office at Ashlin on 17 March 1925**; Frick moved the **second and third
to the station's own weigh room**, so the board could see the plant instead of hearing it
described, and watched the scales tried and the test made twice. "I did not understand the test
then and do not understand it now; I am a lawyer and the thing is chemistry." Men stood outside
on the platform in the rain to listen.

Three explanations were put and none was accepted: **water**, by Selby Vose, "without heat and
without proof"; **theft by the tester**, insinuated by Wilbur Teague in the manner of an
accountant, "then observed that he made no charge against anybody — there is no
cross-examination that reaches a witness of that kind"; and **the hay**, by reading the 1908
report into the record, an old opinion "with the advantage of blaming nobody in the room."

Four books were in evidence: the station test book (4 May 1896 – 31 Dec 1924, in Keddie's hand);
the station receiving book; Emmet Brant's private weigh book, lent and wanted back; and **the
circuit notebook of the state dairy inspector for 1923** — which contains the condemnation of the
glass. Kyle, who made the list and checked it twice: "I could not have told you then and cannot
tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked." The
condemned crate itself lay in the loft twenty feet above two of the three sittings, and is never
mentioned once in any session.

Keddie made a nine-sentence statement on 17 March: the day began at 5:30; the scale was true and
there was never a disputed weight; samples composited over ten days, warmed blood-warm, the charge
drawn to the mark, the tip wiped, the acid down the side, read against the rulings with dividers;
"thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons"; and *"I have tested as I was taught. I cannot
tell you why the figures are what they are."* Frick believed him then and now, and named the trap:
"a man who cannot account for a figure is presumed by everybody in the room to be the figure's
author."

He was **put out on 30 April 1925**, four days before the award and while the board still sat,
with no finding ever made against him. Orra: "Twenty-nine seasons and a letter. He came up the
road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm and hung it behind the shed door and
asked what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it." The award, **4 May 1925**, "finds no
cause established" and gave the patrons **$3,800, without admission, charged to the station
account.**

He kept four or five cows and did carpentry at sixty-six for a quarter of his old pay, and
whistled at it, "which he never did when he was paid." He died in February 1934. At the burying
men shook Orra's hand who had not spoken to him in nine years and every one said what a hand he
was with a door; not one said the other thing. "He said to me once and only once… that a man
cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."

*Documents:* r13 (Frick), r03 (Keddie), r24 (Orra), r23 (Kyle), r21 (Oram), r02 (Loomis),
r08 (Vose), r22 (Falke).

## Story 8 — The Girl Who Climbed the Ladder (1947–1970)

**About:** Ivy Keddie, Bulletin No. 471, and the clearing of her grandfather's name
twenty-four years after his death.

Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks went to the state dairy office when he retired in 1936 and were
transferred in 1947 to the library of the State Agricultural College at Farland, where they sat
for eleven years and nobody put a question to them. Ivy Keddie, born 1931, daughter of the
schoolteacher Duncan Keddie and granddaughter of Ansel, took her first degree at Farland and
stayed for graduate work in dairy husbandry.

In **February 1958** she went up the nailed ladder into the loft over the station's intake room
and found, under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff, a crate stencilled AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY
TARNET — the glass condemned in 1923, put up there and never touched since. Of the original six
dozen, **nine pipettes survived unbroken** in the straw. She carried them east, and on **9 March
1958** measured them one by one against a calibrated burette at Farland: every stem blown 17.6
c.c., none delivering it, **mean 16.72 c.c. — 0.95 of the mark, one part in twenty short.**

She then did what nobody had done in sixty years: she put five sets of records in one room — the
station test book, Emmet Brant's weigh book (lent by Roy), the Alder Corners figures copied out
by Rosalie Cudd, Strawn's notebooks, and the Association's ledgers — and made them speak to each
other. Fourteen overlapping seasons showed the same milk running about 0.19 higher across the
ridge; Bulletin 214 killed the bog hay; the 1911 question mark became an experiment; the ledgers'
"station gain" became the missing fat, credited to the Association year by year in its own books.

The work appeared as **Bulletin No. 471, State Agricultural College at Farland, October 1958**,
by Professor Leland Vaught and Miss Keddie. Its conclusion: *"No milk was watered at Ostrey
Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923,
the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in
twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the
Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."*

*The Ashlin Chronicle* put it on the front page on 3 October 1958 ("Old Ordell Wrong Righted"),
garbling most of the figures. Rundle's pamphlet was reprinted in 1959 with a footnote; Orin
Falke reported it to the county board in 1959, conceded the hay and then withdrew the concession
on obtaining Bulletin 214; Ivy read a paper to the Ordell County Historical Society at Ashlin in
1959 with the crate on the table.

The glass had been condemned in 1923 and the cause found in 1958 — thirty-five years. Ansel
Keddie had been dismissed in 1925 and was twenty-four years dead. Ivy to the *Chronicle*: "The
glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not… I would like it printed that he never knew. It would
have been a kindness if somebody in this county had thought to climb a ladder." The test book and
crate went to the Historical Rooms in 1959, the weigh book in 1963, and the Alder Corners book to
the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970: "the five books are now in three buildings and are
none of them ours."

*Documents:* r04 (Ivy Keddie), r11 (Vaught), r19 (Chronicle), r22 (Falke), r18 (Duncan Keddie),
r17 (Hazel Brant), r12 (Rundle 1959 footnote), r07 (Nym), r21 (Oram).

# Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.**
Derivation: Ansel married Orra Brant in 1889; their son Duncan was born 1894; Duncan's daughter
Ivy was born 1931. Duncan states it himself and says he writes it plainly "because I have already
twice seen it printed otherwise and once heard it said aloud in this town." Ivy's own 1959 talk
and Roy Brant's account agree. Two sources point elsewhere and both say *great-niece*: *The
Ashlin Chronicle* (3 Oct 1958) and Falke's report (¶2). They are **not independent** — Falke's
report was written after "newspaper notice," and he corrects himself in a note appended after the
report had gone to type: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter
and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." Vira Toland's "a young woman from the
college away east" is vaguer still. Resolution method: a member of the household stating his own
daughter's parentage outranks a weekly's day-and-a-half feature, and the only other carrier of the
error retracted it on checking.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins.**
Derivation: Orra (Keddie) and Emmet Brant were sister and brother. Their children — Duncan Keddie
and Roy Brant — are therefore **first cousins**, which Duncan gives as "the whole definition and
there is nothing more to it." Duncan's daughter Ivy and Roy's daughter Hazel, being the children
of first cousins, are **second cousins**. Three parties in the chain say so independently: Roy
(Hazel's father), Hazel herself, and Duncan; Rundle's 1959 footnote agrees. Against it, Vira
Toland and Cleve Oram both say "first cousin once removed" — two narrators carrying the same
wrong value, both outside the family and both repeating village usage; a once-removed relation
belongs to a different generation, and Ivy and Hazel are of the same one.
*What the sources leave open:* (i) whether Orra and Emmet shared both parents. Hazel alone calls
Orra a **half** sister "by his father's second wife"; Roy says full brother and sister, same
mother and father; Duncan calls her "Emmet Brant's sister, the younger of the two"; Orra's own
diary says "my brother Emmet." I take them for full siblings on the weight of three sources
including Orra herself — but note that even if Hazel were right the degree would be unaffected
(half-first-cousins, half-second-cousins). (ii) Neither Ivy's mother nor Hazel's mother is named
anywhere, so nothing whatever is established on those two lines. (iii) Hazel's birth year is never
given; only Ivy's (1931).

**B3. Selby Vose was Gideon Vose's son.**
Sources: Loomis's officer list; Vose himself addressing the members — "my father, who was your
first president"; Duncan Keddie, who lists it among the things "always got wrong"; and Kyle, who
sat outside his door for twenty-five years — "the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have
heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense." Against: Vira Toland and
Rundle say nephew. Why it matters: Gideon organized the Association, was its first president, and
**wrote the by-laws containing Article VII himself**. The manager who defended that clause, who
signed the annual sheet carrying the "station gain" line every year, who was told by his own
bookkeeper in 1916 that the Hollow figures would not reconcile and told him to let it alone, who
issued the 1919 water circular, and who dismissed the inspector's condemnation of the glass as "a
piece of housekeeping," was the founder's son defending his father's instrument. The
institution's credit and his family's were the same thing, which is a strong reason for a man not
to look. A minor irony: the 4.56 Jersey herd he cited in 1926 as proof of good milk was his
father's, and being the richest milk it was the milk the short glass shorted worst (0.24 of a
point).

**B4. There was no relationship between the Voses and the Keddies that the sources establish.**
Duncan Keddie denies it categorically and explains where the talk comes from: people confuse the
Association's Voses with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Gideon either. "I have heard
it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them
will tell you first cousins if you let her sit down… My mother would have told me," being a woman
who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection," and who "never once put a
Vose in it." The two contrary claims are Vira Toland's (Orra "first cousin to old Mrs. Vose" —
which she herself frames as "somebody's cousin saying it") and Cleve Oram's ("cousins of some
sort… my mother had it from her mother… though I could not draw you the line on paper"). Both
are self-labelled hearsay and both fail to name a link; they are plausibly the same rumour twice,
not two witnesses. Resolution: a man inside the family, giving a specific account of how the
error arises, outranks two admitted rumours. Caveat, stated honestly: this is a negative resting
on one witness, so the strongest defensible answer is that **no kinship is established and the
positive claims are unsupported** — not that kinship is disproved.

# Section C — Math

## C1

**(a) The tests were low by one part in twenty — they read 0.95 of the truth (5 % short).**
Working: every pipette was blown for a charge of **17.6 c.c.** The nine surviving pipettes,
measured against a calibrated burette at Farland on 9 March 1958, delivered a mean of
**16.72 c.c.**

    16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly

The test bottle's neck is ruled in per cent for one charge and no other; the operator weighs
nothing and reads the fat column directly. A charge short by a factor of 0.95 therefore produces
a reading short by the factor 0.95 — **proportional, not a fixed offset.**

**(b) True average 3.80; recorded tests short by 0.19 of a point.**

    3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80        3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19

Two independent observations confirm 3.80 rather than any calculated figure: the borrowed measure
of 1911 produced exactly 3.80 for nine weeks, and the station's own book gave the year 1924 at
3.80 after the new glass (Strawn's notebook). Note that 0.19 is not a constant subtraction — it
is what 5 % happens to come to at 3.80. At 4.80 the loss is 0.24; at 3.60 it is 0.18.

**(c) 76,000 lb of butterfat.**

    Milk received over the affected delivery years: 40,000,000 lb (25 yrs × 1,600,000 lb/yr)
    Credited at 3.61 %: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb
    Delivered at 3.80 %: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb
    Not credited:       1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 lb

Check against the ledger: the annual station-gain entry is 3,040 lb; 3,040 × 25 = 76,000. The two
routes agree exactly, which is the strongest arithmetic check available.

**(d) $19,000; the patrons received one fifth of it.**

    76,000 lb × $0.25 = $19,000
    Award actually paid, 4 May 1925: $3,800
    3,800 ÷ 19,000 = 0.20 — one fifth

Butterfat is taken at 25¢ the pound "taking the fat years with the lean," the figure Kyle was set
to find for the arbitration and did find, and the figure Ivy Keddie and Nym use. (Sill and Kepp
say 27¢, which would give $20,520; Roy Brant gives 24¢ for 1910 specifically and ~25¢ over the
whole stretch.)

## C2 — Emmet Brant, 1910

Recorded on the 1910 leaf of the weigh book: **200,000 lb even, paid on a test of 3.42.**

**(a) Credited:** 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb of butterfat.**

**(b) Owed and not credited:**

    True test: 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60
    True fat:  200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb
    Short:     7,200 − 6,840 = 360 lb      (= 5 % of 7,200 ✓)

**(c) Worth:** at the 1910 butterfat price of 24¢ given by his son Roy, 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40**.
At the long-run 25¢ it would be $90.00. I give both because the 24¢ is specific to 1910 and the
25¢ is a whole-period average; **$86.40** is the better answer for that year.

## C3

**(a) Credited to the patrons: 1,444,000 lb.** (40,000,000 × 3.61 %; ledger route: 57,760 lb/yr
× 25 = 1,444,000.)

**(b) Reached Larrow Green in the station cream: 1,520,000 lb.** (40,000,000 × 3.80 %; ledger
route: 60,800 lb/yr × 25 = 1,520,000.)

**(c) The difference of 76,000 lb is:**

    76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = 5.00 %   of the butterfat actually delivered
    76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 5.26 %   of the butterfat credited  → the ledger's "five and a quarter"

Being exact about it: **the ledger's recurring "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" is a
percentage of the fat credited**, because a bookkeeper reckons a difference against the figure he
has already entered. **5 % is the figure that is also the glass** — the same one part in twenty
that 16.72 ÷ 17.6 gives. Teague (r05) and Vaught's §V both describe the 5.25 % as a percentage of
the fat *delivered*; that is arithmetically wrong (3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.00 %) and is the same slip
made twice.

## C5

**(a) In use 26 years, 1 month and 12 days: 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923. The money runs over 25
full delivery years, 1898–1922.**
They are different numbers because the first and last years of the glass were partial: it entered
service on 1 May 1897, eight months into nothing and four months after that year's deliveries had
begun, and was condemned on 12 June 1923, part-way through that year. Only the twenty-five whole
calendar years between are complete delivery years, and it is those that the 40,000,000 lb
(1,600,000 × 25) and the ledger's twenty-five annual station-gain entries of 3,040 lb cover. The
loss in the two partial years is real but is not in the 76,000 lb figure, so **$19,000 is a floor,
not a ceiling.**

**(b) Cannot be determined from the sources.**
The station opened 4 May 1896 and its first season was carried on the creamery's own books, before
there was a station account at all; the sheets were never put with the station papers. Nym states
flatly that no 1896 first-season intake figure exists anywhere, and Kyle — who ruled the books —
says the same of himself: "It is not in any book I ever ruled." What is left is two
recollections that disagree: Sill says about **900,000 lb** (May to the end of 1896) and Kyle says
"a good **eleven hundred thousand** pounds, which is what I understood at the time from men who
were there." Both are hearsay of the same lost sheets, neither is a document, and there is no
third source and no arithmetic that discriminates between them. (Note also that 1896 falls outside
the affected period entirely: that season was tested on Sill's borrowed Larrow Green glass.)

# Section D — Logic

## D1 — The 1911 interval as an experiment

**When and how long:** 8 May to 10 July 1911 — **nine weeks**. Fixed by Ansel Keddie's test book,
by Ivy Keddie's reconstruction, by Vaught §IV, by Falke ¶6, and corroborated at the near end by
Orra Keddie's diary entry for 8 May 1911 ("A. home cross about a broken measure"). Teague and
Kepp both say "six weeks"; Kepp additionally relocates it into "June and July." The nine-week
figure rests on the contemporaneous book; the six-week figure on two memories written fourteen and
fifteen years later.

**The variable:** the glass vessel that measured the charge of milk — the station's own pipette
replaced by a borrowed measure, and then restored.

**Held constant:** the same nine families and their herds; the same wet ground and the same
pasture; the season (the interval spans spring rain into full summer, so it is not a "best of the
year" window); the same weighing on the same scale; the same acid, test bottles and hand tester;
the same operator, taught the same way, working the same routine, under no different supervision;
the same book.

**Result:** the average rose from 3.61 to 3.80 and held there for nine weeks, and fell back to
3.61 **on the day the station's own measure returned.**

**What it establishes that calculation alone cannot:** the arithmetic of 1958 is an inference from
nine pipettes measured thirty-five years after they were withdrawn — it assumes those nine
represent the lot and that the lot behaved in 1900 as it did in 1958. The 1911 interval is a
single-variable intervention with a return to baseline, performed on the actual milk of the actual
farms at the actual time, by accident, with a control built into it. It is causal evidence, not
correlational: it disposes at once of every explanation that looks to the herds, the ground, the
feed, the weather or the men, because none of those changed and the figures did — and the
reversal on 10 July rules out drift, learning and seasonal trend. Vaught rates it above all the
arithmetic for exactly this reason.

**What it does *not* establish:** (i) it shows the two instruments differ, not which of them was
right — by itself it is equally consistent with a borrowed measure that ran long. It takes the
1923 condemnation against a burette, the 1958 measurement, and the 1924 average of 3.80 under new
glass to fix which side of the comparison was the error. (ii) It does not establish the *size* of
the error in any other year, nor that all 72 pipettes were short by the same amount, nor that the
lot was short from the first day. (iii) It does not identify the cause of the shortness (the
reground mold is Junia Ammon's reconstruction, not a finding). (iv) It says nothing about whether
water was ever added or cream ever removed at other times — it only shows that neither can account
for the gap.

## D2 — Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream

**Reason 1 — the cream arrived heavy, every year, for forty-one years.** Hobart Sill weighed every
can into the cold room himself: "There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been
bought up there… Always more. Never less." Stolen cream reaches the other end **light**. This is a
physical measurement at the far end of the wagon route by a man with no stake in the quarrel.

**Reason 2 — the figures tracked the instrument, twice, with the same man at the bench.** In 1911
the tests rose to 3.80 for nine weeks with a borrowed measure and fell back the day the station's
own returned — same tester, same supervision. In 1924, after the condemned glass was replaced, the
station's own book gave the year at 3.80 — again with Keddie still at the bench, and it stayed at
3.80 in 1925 under a *different* man. A thief's figures do not rise when his glassware is changed
and stay risen when he is dismissed.

**Reason 3 — the error was exactly proportional to the richness of the milk.** A herd recorded
3.42 was short 0.18; a herd recorded 4.56 was short 0.24; and the gap was the identical 0.19 in
February and July for twenty-six years. No hand skimming cans can short every patron in precise
proportion to his butterfat, invariably, for a quarter of a century, in every season, in a way
that is invisible in the scatter of the figures. Vaught's mechanism supplies this and it is
independent of any single witness.

**Reason 4 (mechanism) — theft could not have produced a low test at all.** The sample was drawn
at intake, before separation. Cream removed after separation cannot lower a test already made on
the intake sample; a thief would have to falsify the book instead, and falsified figures would not
have obeyed the glass in 1911 and 1923–24.

**Why these are independent:** Reason 1 is a physical weight recorded for another purpose by a
man at Larrow Green; Reason 2 is a natural experiment recorded in the station's own book at Ostrey
Hollow and in the state inspector's notebook; Reason 3 is a property of the mechanism, derivable
from any two patrons' figures. They come from different places, different hands, and different
kinds of evidence, and no one of them depends on any other being true.
**A caution the question invites:** Sill's testimony and Teague's/Kyle's/Rundle's ledger argument
are **not** two independent reasons. The ledger's "fat received at Larrow Green" column is
Sill's receiving book copied into the general account — one physical fact recorded twice. Counted
honestly it is one source, not three.
**One that holds without the 1958 measurement:** Reason 1 (Sill retired in 1934); also Reason 2's
1911 and 1924 halves, both recorded contemporaneously; and Rundle's inference, published in 1939:
"Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every
year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand."

**Did the bookkeeper ever question those entries? No.** Wilbur Teague invented the station-gain
line himself in 1905 and wrote it every year of his service; the surplus it recorded is the
refutation of the very charge he insinuated at the 1925 hearing. He never questioned it. He
treated its constancy as reassurance — "a figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is
furniture" — and read the surplus as evidence *for* theft rather than against it, which inverts
its meaning. The one time he raised anything with Vose (1916) he was told to let it alone and did:
"I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience." Kyle, who copied the same line for
twenty-five years, says it never once crossed his mind to wonder at it, "and I do not believe it
crossed anybody else's either."

## D3 — Did Selby Vose know, before 1923, that the tests ran low?

**Yes — he certainly knew the tests ran low, and he knew the fat did not reconcile; there is no
evidence he knew the glass was short, and good evidence he did not.**

That the tests ran low was not a private fact. It had been complained of at annual meetings from
1902, "in one form or another, in every decade thereafter," and Vose was manager from 1913
(Loomis's officer list; Kyle says 1911). His own 1919 circular presupposes it: a warning to the
Hollow patrons about adding water is only intelligible as a response to figures that ran low, and
Vose signed it and sent it out over his name and no one else's. His 1926 address argues a
*theory* of the low tests ("the rise of 1924 is the measure of it"), which he could not have held
without knowing the phenomenon.

That the fat did not reconcile is the sharper question, and the answer is still yes. He signed the
annual sheet carrying the station-gain line every year of his managership — the line that set the
fat credited against the fat received and named the difference. Kyle qualifies what a signature
proves: Vose "read the totals and he never read the lines above them… he would take a sheet, turn
it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign," and Kyle watched him do it a thousand times.
But the signature is not the only evidence. Teague states that he put the matter to Vose directly
in **1916** and was told to let it alone. That is testimony of being *told*, not of being able to
read. It is single-source and self-serving — it excuses Teague's own forty years of silence — so
I weight it as probable rather than certain; but it is consistent with Kyle's independent picture
of a courteous manager who did not read, and with Vose's own conduct after 1923, when he brushed
aside the inspector's condemnation of the glass with "apparatus wears… I decline to build a
mystery on a piece of housekeeping."

The fair conclusion: **Vose knew there was a discrepancy and had been told of it at least once; he
did not know its cause; and his response, before and after 1923, was to prefer an explanation that
put the fault on the patrons and left the Association's instrument — his father's Association's
instrument — unexamined.**

# Section E — Contradictions

Format: *claim A vs claim B — who — resolution — method.* "TWO-WRONG" marks a value carried by two
or more narrators; "SELF" marks a narrator contradicting itself.

### Numbers of the case

1. **Mean delivery of the pipettes: 16.72 vs 16.27 c.c.** — 16.72 (Nym's accession note on item 5;
Vaught's conclusion by implication; Ivy Keddie) vs 16.27 (Vaught's own §III body; *Ashlin
Chronicle*). **TWO-WRONG.** Resolved to **16.72**. Tie broken four ways: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95
exactly, and 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80, which is the figure actually observed in 1911 and in 1924, while
3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.905, which was never observed anywhere; the ledger's 3,040 lb/yr reconciles with
76,000 and not with 118,000; and Vaught *says so himself* — "this figure is not readily reconciled
with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the glassware."
The Chronicle is not an independent second source: it worked from the college press release, i.e.
from the same body text. Method: arithmetic against independent observation, plus source
dependency.
2. **Loss: 76,000 lb vs 67,000 lb vs 118,000 lb** — 76,000 (Ivy Keddie; Vaught's conclusion) vs
67,000 (Frick; Oram) vs 118,000 (Vaught's body). **TWO-WRONG** on 67,000: Frick writing in 1946
predates the 1958 work and cannot be its source, so his figure is a mis-remembered claim figure,
and Oram (1977) is repeating valley talk. Resolved by the ledger check: 3,040 × 25 = 76,000.
3. **Money: $19,000 vs $19,600** — $19,000 (Ivy Keddie; Vaught's conclusion, implied) vs $19,600
(Rundle's note in press, "to the dollar"; *Chronicle*). **TWO-WRONG.** 76,000 × $0.25 = $19,000
exactly; $19,600 would require 78,400 lb, which no source gives. Both carriers are popular
retellings. Method: arithmetic.
4. **Pipettes recovered: nine vs eleven** — nine (Ivy Keddie; Nym item 5; Vaught; Falke ¶12; Oram,
who handled the crate) vs eleven (Rundle's 1959 footnote; *Chronicle*). **TWO-WRONG**, and the two
carriers are not independent — both are 1958–59 second-hand reports of the college work. Resolved
to **nine** on the accession record and the report that had checked.

(Ammon). The line items sum to 77.93. Method: arithmetic.

are different things: 27¢ is a good round figure for the 1920s and 25¢ is the long-run average
"taking the fat years with the lean." Not a true contradiction once the period is specified.

12. **First-season intake: ~900,000 vs 1,100,000 vs no figure exists** — Sill vs Kyle vs Nym (and
Kyle's own admission that it is in no book). **Cannot be settled.** Two hearsay estimates of lost

### Narrators contradicting themselves on the page

34. **Vaught, Bulletin No. 471 (SELF):** the body gives 16.27 c.c., ratio 0.9244, true average
3.905, shortfall 0.295, loss 118,000 lb, five dozen pipettes, glass in use from 1 May 1898; the
conclusion gives one part in twenty, 76,000 lb, and 1 May 1897. The conclusion is right; the body
is a transposition of 16.72 to 16.27 carried through the arithmetic. Vaught even flags the
inconsistency himself without resolving it.
35. **Grigg, 1919 covering letter (SELF):** the letter says the committee "sampled those herds
through the whole of 1908 — every month of that year"; the report printed directly beneath it says
"We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March." The document
convicts itself, and the winter-only sampling is precisely why the committee could not see that
the gap was flat in July.
36. **Rundle, 1939 pamphlet (SELF, deliberate):** Chapter IV records the valley's settled theft
opinion; the NOTE ADDED WHILE IN PRESS quotes the ledger and retracts it — "I cannot make my
paragraph stand" — leaving both on the page.
37. **Falke, 1959 report (SELF, deliberate):** ¶9 declines to dismiss the bog hay and says "the
1908 committee was not simply wrong"; the appended note, on obtaining Bulletin 214, withdraws it —
"Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July. I
withdraw the concession made on page four" — and separately corrects "great-niece" to
"granddaughter."
38. **Junia Ammon (SELF, twice):** (a) "$77.93" in her facsimile, "seventy-seven dollars and
thirty-nine cents" in her prose immediately after; (b) "The house sold nothing into Ordell County
before 1900" — on the page facing an invoice her own firm made out to an Ordell County customer in
April 1897. Resolution: the reproduced documents outrank the general recollection; her territorial
claim is about where the drummers went, not about what the order book says. A third, softer
tension: the works books did not burn until 1931, so in 1923 the firm still possessed the records
it told the Association it did not have — she calls the letter "a thin thing" without noticing
that.
39. **Teague (SELF):** dates his invention of the station-gain line to 1905 but quotes complete an
entry dated 1898, and Nym records the ledger's station column as running from 1897. Either the
line is older than he says or he has misdated the specimen; unresolved, and it does not affect the
totals.
40. **Hazel Brant (SELF-aware, not self-contradicting):** announces at the outset that she has
quantities but not years, and every year she gives is wrong (book 1900, crate 1899, condemnation
1921, hearing 1926) while every quantity she gives is right (200,000 lb even, 3.42, six farms,
nine letters, 0.19). A useful calibration: reliability is not a property of a witness but of a
class of fact within a witness.
41. **The *Ashlin Chronicle* (SELF):** accuses Ammon & Sons of knowingly putting off "a cheap short
lot," while its own recital shows a firm that answered by return, entered the order and shipped
within the month — and Junia Ammon supplies the innocent mechanism (a reground mold; a check that
finds consistency in a consistently wrong lot). It also states that "the committee that sat on the
matter in 1908 sat again in 1925," which Frick, who chaired the 1925 board, refutes: a board of
three, chaired by a lawyer who kept no cow, not the Grigg committee of Association men.
42. **Oram (against Sill):** his father "always said there was less of it coming off that platform
than went onto it." Sill weighed the same cream at the other end for forty-one years and found it
heavy every year. A weighed record beats a driver's impression, and the ledger's station-gain line
independently records the surplus.

# Section F — Theory

## F1 — What actually produced the shortfall

**The theory in one causal chain.** The Ostrey Hollow station bought its testing outfit from
Ammon & Sons of Tarnet on 14 April 1897 and put it into use on 1 May 1897. Its milk pipettes were
blown for a charge of 17.6 c.c. and delivered, on the mean of the survivors, 16.72 c.c. — most
probably because a mold had been reground and the lot ran short, an error no in-house gauge check
could catch, since such a check tests for consistency and the lot was consistently wrong. In the
Babcock test the operator weighs nothing: he draws one charge with the pipette into a bottle whose
neck is ruled in per cent for that charge and no other, adds acid, spins, and reads the fat column
directly. A charge short by a factor of 0.95 therefore yields a reading short by the factor 0.95.

**Why that size:** 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 — one part in twenty, fixed by the glass and nothing else.
Applied to milk that truly ran 3.80, it reads 3.61: a gap of 0.19 of a point, which is a
*proportion*, not an offset, and therefore identical in every month, every season, wet years and
dry, and larger in absolute terms on richer milk (0.24 on a 4.80 herd, 0.18 on a 3.60 herd).

**Why it began when it did:** on 1 May 1897, the day the station stopped borrowing Larrow Green's
glass and started using its own — the day Orra Keddie noted in her diary.

**Why it ended when it did:** on 12 June 1923, when the state inspector, carrying a burette for
the first time on that circuit, ran the station's pipettes against it, found they would not deliver
their mark, and condemned the whole crate. New glass was in use in July 1923, and from 1924 the
station's own book gave the year at 3.80.

**Why nothing at the station or on the farms had to change at either end:** because the error was
in the instrument that *is* the standard. Scales are checked against sealed weights, acid against
its strength, bottle graduations with mercury, the tester's speed by counting — but the pipette is
checked against nothing, because in the trade the pipette is the measure. "An instrument that is
itself the measure cannot be caught by measuring with it." The error was also *precise*: the same
short charge every time, for a careful operator and a careless one, so the books had "the
appearance of excellent work." Scatter attracts attention; evenness does not. Herds still ranked
correctly against each other, so nothing inside the plant ever looked wrong; the figures were
wrong only against the outside world, and the one man positioned to see the outside world — Jerome
Cudd across the ridge — never compared his figures with anyone's, because it never entered his
head.

## The three competing explanations, and what defeats each

**1. Water in the cans.** Advanced by **Selby Vose**, manager, in the printed circular of June
1919 and again in his 1926 address to the members ("this Association pays for butterfat and cannot
pay for creek water"; "the rise of 1924 is the measure of it"), and put to the 1925 board "without
heat and without proof."
*Defeated by:* Merle Strawn's lactometer, used at every visit for some twenty-nine years, which
never once found added water at that station — he said so to the manager's face in 1919; the
Alder Corners record, since watered milk shows in a cheese vat within a day, the curd coming soft
and refusing to knit, and in sixteen summers Jerome Cudd never complained of the four Hessel
Bottom men nor docked one a cent; the 1908 committee, which inquired into every barn's water
supply and found "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter"; the flatness of the gap, since nine
families do not water in exact proportion to their butterfat every day for twenty-six years; and
the 1924 rise, which followed the July 1923 replacement of the glass, not any act of
surveillance.

**2. Theft of cream by the tester.** Insinuated by **Wilbur Teague** at the 1925 hearing "in the
manner of an accountant," held by the valley at large (Rundle's Chapter IV; Oram's father), and
never charged by anyone in terms.
*Defeated by:* the cream arriving **heavy** at Larrow Green every year for forty-one years on
Sill's own weighing — stolen cream would have arrived light, as Rundle saw in one paragraph; the
Association's own station-gain line, which records that surplus in ink, annually, for a quarter
century; the 1911 interval and the 1924 rise, in both of which the figures obeyed the glassware
while the man stayed the same; the proportionality, which no hand can imitate; and the mechanism,
since the test was made on a sample drawn at intake and cream removed after separation cannot
lower it.

**3. The wet hay of Hessel Bottom.** Advanced by the **1908 Grigg committee** ("what such land
gives in quantity it withholds in quality"), pressed by **Nyle Grigg** in 1919, given a soil
chemistry by **Roland Kepp, V.S.**, in 1926, and provisionally allowed by **Orin Falke** in 1959.
*Defeated by:* Bulletin No. 214, which found bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed
the test "by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be
measured on summer pasture" — against a flat 0.19 in July; the sampling on which the 1908 finding
rested, which covered January, February and March only, so it could not have detected the
year-round flatness that refutes it; the Alder Corners figures, in which the same cows off the same
wet ground ran 3.78–3.82 for sixteen summers on another man's bench; and the 1911 interval, in
which the ground and the feed did not change and the figures did. Grigg's and Kepp's fourfold
misquotation of Bulletin 214 — "two tenths in the winter feeding" — is what kept the theory alive
for fifty years; Falke, alone among them, sent for the bulletin and withdrew.

## Subsidiary questions

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware? No.** It came closer than anyone
realised. Its exhibit list contained four books, the fourth being **the circuit notebook of the
state dairy inspector for 1923** — which contains the condemnation of that very glass — so the
document was physically on the table. Two of its three sittings were held in the station's weigh
room, directly beneath the loft in which the crate lay under a tarpaulin, twenty feet up a ladder
nailed to the wall by the door. Loomis records that the crate is never mentioned once in any of
the three sessions; Kyle, who carried the four books in and out and checked the list twice, "could
not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside
the fourth and looked." Frick's own verdict on why: "A board can only decide between the accounts
that are put to it." Nobody put the glass.

**What did the tester himself believe?** Publicly, nothing — his nine-sentence statement of 17
March 1925 named no cause and accused no one: "I have tested as I was taught. I cannot tell you
why the figures are what they are." Privately the sources give two different beliefs at two
different times, and they are best read chronologically rather than as a contradiction. In the
summer of **1911** he came home saying **it was something in the glass**, and said it twice, at
supper and again by the door, which was not his way — his son Duncan, then seventeen, remembered
the kitchen. He would not write it down, because he would not put down what he could not prove,
and entered only a question mark in the book. By **1925**, after fourteen more years of figures
from an instrument he had been taught to trust, he told Frick privately in the corridor at Ashlin
that **he had always thought the patrons were at fault** and would not say so in the room, having
lived among them thirty years. So: it is true that he accused nobody, and false that he suspected
nobody. He had the right answer in 1911, could not prove it, could not hold it, and let it go —
which Duncan calls "the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had."

# Section G — Summary

*(148 words)*

From 1897 to 1923 the Larrow Valley creamery's Ostrey Hollow skimming station tested every
patron's milk with pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. that delivered only 16.72 — five per cent short. Its
tests therefore read five per cent low, always, invisibly, because a pipette is itself the
standard and is checked against nothing. Recorded 3.61 instead of 3.80, the station under-credited
76,000 lb of butterfat, worth $19,000; the Association booked it as "station gain." Farmers blamed
the tester, officers blamed the farmers, experts blamed the hay; an arbitration paid $3,800 in
1925, established no cause, and let Ansel Keddie be dismissed. In 1958 his granddaughter climbed a
ladder and measured the condemned glass.

It took eight records because no one held the whole: the glass, the ledger, the outside cheese
factory, the inspector, the weigh book, the diary, the hearing, the family.
