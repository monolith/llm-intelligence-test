## THE ANSWER (settled; r04, r06, r07, r11-conclusion, and the 1911 experiment all agree)
- The station's milk pipettes were blown for **17.6 c.c.** and delivered a mean of **16.72 c.c. = 0.95 of the
  mark**. Every test read **95% of the truth** — a *proportional* 1-in-20 error, not a fixed offset.
- Recorded average **3.61** ÷ 0.95 = true **3.80**; gap **0.19** of a point, flat in every month, wet year and dry.
- Period **1 May 1897 – 12 June 1923**. 1898–1922 = 25 full delivery years, 40,000,000 lb milk (1.6M/yr);
  credited at 3.61 = 1,444,000 lb fat, delivered at 3.80 = 1,520,000 lb, difference **76,000 lb** @ 25¢ = **$19,000**.
  The 1925 award was $3,800 — one fifth of it. The 76,000 lb sat in the ledgers as "station gain."
- 76,000 lb is **5%** of fat delivered (1,520,000) and **5.25%** of fat credited (1,444,000). The ledger says 5.25%
  because a bookkeeper reckons against the figure already entered; **5% is the one that is also the glass.**
  (r05 and r11 §V both misstate 5.25% as a percentage *of fat delivered* — that is the recurring slip.)
- Because proportional, it fell hardest on the best milk: a herd recorded 4.56 was short 0.24; one recorded 3.42
  short 0.18.
- **The 1911 natural experiment** (the strongest evidence, per Vaught): the station's own measure broke **8 May**,
  a borrowed one was used until **10 July** — **nine weeks**. Average rose 3.61 → 3.80 and fell back to 3.61 the
  day the station's own measure returned. Same herds, season, pasture, acid, bottles, tester. One object changed.
  Keddie entered it with his own question mark and it lay unread 47 years.

- **r11 Leland Vaught**, professor of dairy husbandry, **Bulletin No. 471, Farland, October 1958** (introduction
  and method): **the mechanism.** The test has four operations — a measured charge drawn by pipette into a test
  bottle, acid, spin, read the fat column *directly* in percent. The operator weighs nothing; the neck is ruled for
  one charge and no other (**17.6 c.c.** in this state). If the pipette delivers short, the reading is low *in the
  exact proportion by which the charge was short*. **Three consequences nobody held in mind at once:**
  (1) **proportional, not additive** — rich milk loses more absolutely, herds still rank correctly against each
  other, the station's figures look perfectly orderly and are wrong only against the outside world;
  (2) **precise** — a short pipette delivers the same short charge every time for a quarter century, for a careful
  operator and a careless one; "it has the appearance of excellent work" (scatter attracts attention; evenness does not);
  (3) **invisible from inside the plant** — scales check against test weights, speed can be counted, acid checked for
  strength, bottle graduations checked with mercury, but **the pipette is not checked against anything because in the
  trade the pipette *is* the standard**. "An instrument that is itself the measure cannot be caught by measuring with
  it." Only an outside burette catches it, and burettes reached field men very late. Method given so any county
  worker can repeat it: fill to the mark with room-temperature water, wipe the tip, deliver in the ordinary working
  manner into a certified burette, five runs per pipette, averaged; an afternoon's work.
  **§IV** rates the 1911 nine weeks above all the arithmetic: an experiment that ran itself, disposing of every
  explanation looking to the herds, the ground, or the men, since none of those changed and the figures did.
  **§VI** quotes Bulletin No. 214 exactly: *"Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test
  by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."*
  **§VII conclusion, quoted entire and reliable:** *"No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen
  there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in
  twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of
  butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."*

- **r16 Roland Kepp, V.S.**, of Ashlin, paper read to the Ordell County Farm Club, **1926**, with discussion:
  **the hay theory at its most scientific — and still wrong.** Argument: a wet bottom is not a rich meadow with too
  much water but a distinct chemistry — sour, acid, poor in lime and phosphates; sweet grasses crowd out; sedge,
  rush and tussock carry bulk and water but no minerals; late cutting takes out what little was there. A cow short
  of minerals "robs her own frame," and **the fat is the first economy she practices**, so wet land is generous of
  flow and poor in quality. Claims the county's wet bottoms yield milk poorer by **a fifth of a point** and puts the
  Ostrey Hollow gap at **twenty-one hundredths (0.21)**. Repeats Grigg's misquotation — Bulletin 214 "found the
  butter fat test depressed by **two tenths** in the winter feeding" — and calls the agreement of 0.20, 0.21 and
  "two tenths" conclusive: "I do not know what more a reasonable man requires." **Dismisses the 1911 interval**,
  which he mis-states as **six weeks**: "six weeks of June and July, when every herd in the county is at its best and
  the stock are off the wet ground… I could produce you six weeks in any year that would prove anything you liked."
  Remedy: drain, lime twice, seed, keep stock off in spring, never feed the hay alone, use a mineral supplement;
  fat at **27¢** makes it pay for a great deal of tile. **Discussion:** asked whether the cows or the men were at
  fault, he rejects it (good cattle stand on wet land and indifferent cattle test well on hills); asked whether the
  trouble might be in the station, he replies he has **"never in my life read a test and would not know how,"** and
  that when a cause is found in the soil, the herbage, the stock and the College's trial, **"it is not good sense to
  go looking for a second cause in a building."**

## ⚠ The arithmetic trap in r11 (Bulletin No. 471) — expect a question here
Vaught's **body** says the nine pipettes' mean delivery is **16.27 c.c.**, ratio 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = **0.9244**, true
average **3.905**, shortfall **0.295**, loss **118,000 lb**, and "five dozen" bought, glass in use from **1 May 1898**.
His **conclusion** says one part in twenty, **76,000 lb**, from **1 May 1897**. The conclusion is right and the body is
a transposition of **16.72 → 16.27**. Three independent checks:
1. r07 Item 5 measured the same nine pipettes at a **mean of 16.72**; 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly.
2. 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**, which is what the 1911 borrowed measure produced and what the station's own book gave in
   **1924** after the new glass (r06). 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.905, which nothing ever observed — and **Vaught says so
   himself**: "this figure is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following
   the replacement of the glassware."
3. 76,000 lb reconciles with the ledger's 3,040 lb/yr × 25 years; 118,000 does not.
Also note the invoice (r07) says **6 doz** and **1 May 1897**, and r14 confirms the station's own outfit arrived
"the spring after" the May 1896 opening — so 1897, not 1898.

## Failure lessons
- **The Bulletin 214 misquotation is the load-bearing error of the hay theory.** Grigg (1919) turned "not more than
  five hundredths in winter, nothing measurable in summer" into "two tenths in the winter feeding"; Kepp (1926)
  repeated it and called its agreement with his own 0.20 and the patrons' 0.21 conclusive. A fourfold overstatement,
  never checked against the bulletin, kept the wrong cause alive for fifty years.
- **Grigg's letter contradicts the report printed directly beneath it** (whole of 1908 vs January–March). Winter-only
  sampling could not see that the gap was flat year-round — the one fact that would have killed the feed theory.
- **Two men held the disproof of the theft theory in their hands and did nothing with it.** Sill weighed the Hollow
  cream in heavy every year for forty-one years and shrugged ("nobody ever asked me to"); Teague booked the same
  surplus as "station gain" and read it as evidence of theft. Rundle, a schoolteacher glancing at a ledger on another
  errand, drew the right inference in one paragraph.
- **Frick had exhibit 4 — the inspector's 1923 notebook, containing the condemnation — on the table and no one opened

## Contradicted claims (proven false — do not relearn)
- **No milk was watered and no cream was stolen at Ostrey Hollow** (Bulletin 471). Strawn's lactometer found no added
  water on any visit in ~29 years; Rosalie Cudd's father took the same four men's milk for sixteen summers without a
  complaint, and watered milk cannot be hidden in a cheese vat; and Sill's receiving book shows the cream arriving
  **heavy**, not light — stolen cream would have reached the Green light.
- **The wet hay of Hessel Bottom did not do it** — Bulletin 214: ≤0.05 in winter, nothing measurable in summer,
  against a flat 0.19 in every month; and the same milk tested ~0.19 higher at Alder Corners in the same summers.
- **The weighing was never at fault** — Emmet Brant's two columns agreed for thirty years; the 1908 committee tried
  both sets of scales; Strawn tried them against sealed weights every visit; Keddie: never a disputed weight.
- **The 1924 rise was not caused by "the looking"** (Vose) nor by "something getting stopped" (Sill) — it followed the
  July 1923 replacement of the condemned glass, confirmed by Strawn's October 1923 recheck.
- **The station-gain line was not an old founders' dodge** — Teague invented it at his own desk in 1905.
- **Ansel Keddie was not dismissed for cause** — no finding was ever made against him; he was put out 30 April 1925
  while the board still sat, and died in 1934 still under it. (True that he accused nobody; false that he suspected
  nobody — he told Frick privately he blamed the patrons.)
- **The 1911 interval was not six weeks and not a seasonal artefact** — nine weeks, 8 May–10 July, with the return to
  3.61 on the day the station's own measure came back.
- **The mean delivery was not 16.27 c.c.** — 16.72; see the arithmetic trap above.
