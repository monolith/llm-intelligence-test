*Source note: this session read retellings r17–r24 directly. The notes on r01–r16 from earlier sessions were not in this session's context, and the test rules forbid opening any other file. So every answer below comes from r17–r24. Where r01–r16 might settle a point, I say so.*

Arithmetic key used throughout (derived in Section C): the station read the fat test 5% low, 3.61 recorded against 3.80 true (a shortfall of 0.19). Over 25 full delivery years the station took in 40,000,000 lb of milk. That gives 1,444,000 lb of fat credited, 1,520,000 lb delivered, and 76,000 lb uncredited, worth $19,000 at 25¢ a pound. The award was $3,800, one-fifth of that.

## Section A — Reconstruction

### 1. A Rule by the Pound of Fat — the Association and its Ostrey Hollow station (1889–1896)

Farmers of the Larrow Valley in Ordell County organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association at Larrow Green in 1889 and built the main creamery there in 1891. Gideon Vose organized it, served as its first president and wrote its by-laws. Article 7 is the hinge of the whole history. It pays every patron by the pounds of butterfat his milk is found by test to contain, not by the weight of the milk, and the test at the station where he delivers is the one that governs his pay. The printed by-laws carry no date and the early minute book was lost, so the year of adoption cannot be fixed. Under payment by weight, the later error would have cost nobody a dollar.

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at the hamlet of Ostrey Hollow. It stood seven miles up the valley, uphill the whole way, with a hard pitch two miles below the hamlet. It served the upper end of the valley, chiefly Hessel Bottom: six farms on wet ground, with a cold spring at the upper end where the cans stood. The station opened on **4 May 1896**. Hazel Brant's "went up in '95" fits the building winter. The building was a two-storey board house on the flat by the creek. It had a waist-high covered platform on the road side and an intake (weigh) room holding the scale, vat and separator. Above that room was a loft, reached by a ladder nailed to the wall by the door. Milk was weighed, sampled, tested for butterfat and separated. The cream went down to Larrow Green on the Oram family's wagon, and the skim went back to the farmer for his hogs.

**Ansel Keddie** (b. 1861) was tester and separator man from the day the station opened. He served 29 seasons (1896–1924) and kept the station test book in his own hand from 1896. In its first season the station borrowed its testing glass from the Larrow Green creamery. That season's milk was carried on the creamery's own books and never filed with the station papers. The only figure for it is clerk Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds," which he admits is hearsay, so it cannot be confirmed. Kyle's claim that the station's own outfit went into use in 1898 is rejected (see story 2). The station closed in 1946 and afterwards stored hay tools.

*Documents:* r22 (Falke ¶3–4), r18, r19, r21 (Oram), r23 (Kyle), r24, r17.

### 2. Six Dozen at Thirty-Five Cents — the Tarnet glass (1897–1923, told in 1949)

Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet, was founded by Marcus Ammon, whose motto was that "a creamery buys arithmetic from us and not glassware." At its height the firm supplied half the creamery glass in the state. On **14 April 1897** it invoiced the Association for the Ostrey Hollow outfit:

- 6 dozen milk-test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c., at 35¢: $25.20
- 12 dozen test bottles with 8 per cent necks, at 22¢: $31.68
- one acid measure: $0.90
- one four-bottle hand tester: $18.00
- freight: $2.15

The items add to **$77.93**, which is the total printed on the facsimile. The "$77.39" in Junia Ammon's prose and in the *Chronicle* is a transposition. The outfit was **72 pipettes**; Oram's "five dozen" is wrong. It went into use on **1 May 1897**, and Orra Keddie's diary for that day confirms it: "the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green."

The lot was short. Each pipette was meant to deliver 17.6 c.c. into a bottle whose neck was ruled for that charge. In fact the lot delivered about **16.72 c.c., 0.88 c.c. or 5 per cent short**. I infer that figure from the 3.61/3.80 test ratio. The *Chronicle*'s printed "16.27" would mean a 7.6 per cent error, which fits none of the station's figures. The glass stayed in use for twenty-six years.

The state inspector condemned the glass in June 1923, and on 28 June the Association ordered six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles. Ammon's reply of 2 July entered the order and "regret[ted] the trouble." It also said the firm's books of the nineties could tell nothing about the lot. The firm's historian, Junia Ammon, printed the invoice and the letter side by side in chapter nine of *Sixty Years of Ammon & Sons* (1949). She blames a reground mould that turned out a lot that was *consistently* wrong, which checking one pipette against another cannot catch. That is her belief, not proof, because the works books burned in 1931. The *Chronicle*'s charge that the firm knowingly sold a short lot is just as unproven, so **accident versus fraud cannot be determined.** Her claim that the firm sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900 is contradicted by her own invoice and by the letter's "customer of twenty-six years' standing."

*Documents:* r20, r19, r24, r21, r22, r23.

### 3. Hessel Bottom and the Brant Weigh Book (1897–1963)

**Emmet Brant** farmed the largest place on Hessel Bottom and hauled more milk to the station than any other patron. He weighed his milk on his own scale before it went on the wagon. The station weighed it again at intake, and he wrote both figures side by side in a tall, narrow account book with canvas over board. The two figures agreed, which shows that the station's *weights* were honest. Duncan Keddie dates the book's start to **1 January 1897**. Hazel Brant, who says she remembers quantities but not years, gives 1900. I prefer Duncan's dated written inventory; this is an inference.

The leaf everyone remembers is **1910**: "200,000 lb even" delivered, "his scale and theirs agreeing," paid on a test of **3.42**. That credited Emmet with **6,840 lb** of butterfat. Since the glass read 5 per cent low, his true test was 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = **3.60**. He actually delivered **7,200 lb**, so he was **360 lb short**, about **$90** at the period's average of 25¢ a pound.

Hessel Bottom's six farms provided a natural comparison. In the summer flush, four of the six hauled over the ridge to Jerome Cudd's cheese factory at Alder Corners in Vessey County, and they came back to the station for the rest of the year. Their milk tested normally everywhere else. At the station it tested **0.19 lower in every month**, in wet years and dry. The Alder Corners test book passed to Cudd's daughter Rosalie in 1941. She had its figures copied for Ivy Keddie in 1958, and in 1970 the book went to the Vessey County Historical Society.

Emmet was Orra Keddie's brother, so the tester accused of shorting him was his own brother-in-law and ate Sunday dinner with him. Hazel says Orra was a half-sister by their father's second wife; Duncan simply says sister. That point is unresolved. In June 1919 the two men quarrelled over the water circular (story 4), and Emmet never came into the Keddie house again. His name was on the 1925 petition, and he lent his weigh book to the arbitration. He died in spring 1929, unreconciled, and the book passed to his son **Roy**. In 1958 Roy *lent* it to Ivy (he was particular that it was a loan, not a bequest) and had it back. Roy died in 1962. His daughter **Hazel** gave the book to the Historical Rooms at Ashlin in 1963 and described it on tape in 1981.

*Documents:* r17, r18, r23, r24, r22.

### 4. Water, Hay and Theft — the explanations that were argued (1902–1926)

From early on, Ostrey Hollow milk tested low. Over the years of complaint it averaged **3.61**, against **3.78** at Larrow Green: a shortfall of **0.19 point** that did not change with the month or season. Store talk in March 1902, recorded in Orra's diary, matches the first complaint of record in **1902**. After that the patrons complained at annual meetings for about a quarter of a century. Ansel told his wife the fault was "not your cows and not your hands" but that "it is my figures they will look at."

Three theories were argued. All of them were about people or cows, and none about the glass.

*Bog hay.* In January 1908 an Association committee of four men came up in Grigg's sleigh. They went first to Emmet Brant's farm and looked at the herds and barns but not the station. In March they reported that the wet hay of the bottom thinned the cream. An Ashlin veterinarian repeated the claim in 1926. County agent Orin Falke conceded it in 1959, then withdrew the concession after reading the college's **Bulletin No. 214**, a 1911 feeding trial. It found that bog hay lowered the test by **at most 0.05 in winter** and by **nothing measurable on summer pasture**, which cannot account for 0.19 in July.

*Water.* **Selby Vose**, manager from 1911, sent the Ostrey Hollow patrons a circular in **June 1919** warning them about added water. Clerk Bertram Kyle typed it, and it went out over Vose's name alone. Ansel refused to mail it. He carried it himself to **eleven families**, according to the diary (Hazel remembered nine), and stood at each door while it was read. Two days later Emmet stood in the Keddie yard and asked why Ansel had carried a paper he did not believe. Ansel answered that he would not have another man carry it. The state inspector, **Merle Strawn**, had tested with a lactometer on every visit from 1908 and never once found added water.

*Theft.* At the 1925 hearing an officer of the Association argued that the tester was skimming cream and writing low tests to hide it. The cream hauler Oram also privately believed that less cream left the platform than went onto it. Three things rule this out: the 1911 interval (story 5), the ledger's "station gain" (story 6), and the normal tests from 1924 on, when Ansel was still the tester but the glass had been replaced.

*Documents:* r22, r24, r23, r17, r21, r19.

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### 5. Nine Weeks in 1911 — the accidental experiment

On **8 May 1911** the station's own measuring glass broke. Orra's diary for that day reads "A. home cross about a broken measure," with rain and the low piece flooded. A measure borrowed from elsewhere was used until **10 July 1911**, which is **nine weeks (63 days)**. Nothing else changed: the herds, pasture, season, men, machinery and supervision were all the same, and the same tester made every test. For those nine weeks the station's tests stood **markedly higher**. On the day the station went back to its own glass, they fell back to their old level. It was a step up when the glass changed and a step down when it changed back. That timing rules out the season as a cause, because the drop came in mid-July.

Ansel wrote the fact in his test book with a **question mark** beside it. His son Duncan, then seventeen, remembers him coming home that summer saying "it was something in the glass." He said it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not like him. He never dared write it down. All he had was a suspicion about a piece of glass, set against an outfit he had been taught to trust and against the practice of every station in the county. He kept using that glass for twelve more years, until the 1923 condemnation, and stayed at his post fourteen more years, until 1925. The figures the glass gave him were what later convicted him. That October, Duncan left to teach school at Ashlin.

The 1958 bulletin and Falke's 1959 report both treat the interval as decisive. With every other variable held still, it isolates the glass as the cause, and it clears the tester: the same man, under the same supervision, produced higher figures. The Association did nothing with it, and the question mark stayed in a book that the arbitration later impounded. The episode does **not** settle the size of the error, because nobody knows whether the borrowed measure was itself accurate. It also does not show which piece of the outfit was at fault or how the lot came to be short. Those questions needed the 1923 condemnation and the 1958 measurements.

*Documents:* r22 ¶6, r24, r18.

### 6. "Station Gain, as Usual" — the ledger at Larrow Green (1897–1925)

**Bertram Kyle** sat at the outer desk of the Association's office at Larrow Green for twenty-five years, just outside the manager's door. He ruled the annual station accounts in a fair hand, and in 1953, at sixty-eight, he wrote his account of the office for the Historical Society. Every year the Ostrey Hollow account showed three figures and a sentence: the butterfat credited to patrons on the station test; the butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream; and the difference, entered as "**station gain … five and a quarter per cent, as usual**, carried to the general account." Bookkeeper **Wilbur Teague** wrote that line every year and "never asked anybody anything in forty years." Manager **Selby Vose**, the founder Gideon's son and not his nephew, took office in 1911. He was courteous to everyone, and he signed each sheet with his thumb on the bottom total, never reading the lines above it.

For the arbitration, Kyle worked out the figures for the whole period. The station received **40,000,000 lb** of milk over **25 full delivery years**, a steady **1,600,000 lb a year**, and butterfat averaged **25¢ a pound**. At the recorded 3.61 test, patrons were credited with **1,444,000 lb** of fat. At the true 3.80, the creamery actually received **1,520,000 lb**. The gain was **76,000 lb**. That is 5.26 per cent of the fat credited, which is Kyle's "five and a quarter," and exactly 5 per cent of the fat received. It came to **$19,000** that the Association kept and never paid out.

The line refutes both the water theory and the theft theory. Adding water adds weight but no fat, so it cannot produce extra fat at the creamery. A tester stealing cream would have produced a station *loss*, not a gain. But the line had a name, and as Kyle puts it, "a figure with a name on it has been explained." Kyle carried four books into the arbitration: the station test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's lent weigh book, and the inspector's notebook for the year the glass was condemned. He never saw anyone lay the second beside the fourth. The $3,800 award was charged to the station account. His moral is that nothing was hidden; it was "in plain sight with a name on it." Kyle's own dates for the condemnation (1922), the award (1924) and the outfit (1898) are rejected.

*Documents:* r23, r22, r18.

### 7. Strawn's Burette and the 1925 Arbitration (1923–1934)

On **12 June 1923**, on an ordinary circuit visit, state dairy inspector **Merle Strawn** tested the station's pipettes against his calibrated burette. Strawn was related to nobody in the valley. He found the pipettes would not deliver their mark, condemned the whole lot on the spot and told the manager to order new ones. The order went to Ammon & Sons on 28 June, and new glass was in use from **July 1923**. Orra's diary notes "new glass at the station and new pails, and the roof is still bad." From **1924** the station's average test stood at **3.85**. Strawn recorded the condemnation in his notebook but drew no conclusion about the previous twenty-six years, and neither did anyone else. The old glass went, crated under a tarpaulin, into the loft over the intake room. Hazel's date of 1921 and Kyle's 1922 are wrong.

In **January 1925** the patrons petitioned, with Emmet Brant's name among them. A board of arbitration chaired by an Ashlin attorney sat from **17 March to 4 May 1925**. It first met at Ashlin, where Ansel went down in his good coat. It then held **two of its three sittings** in the station's weigh room, with men standing outside on the platform in the rain, twenty feet below the crate. In March the board impounded the station test book. It had four records before it: the test book, the receiving book, Emmet's weigh book and Strawn's 1923 notebook. Nobody climbed the ladder. The board heard the three theories and found no cause established. On 4 May it awarded the patrons **$3,800 "without admission,"** one-fifth of what they were owed. Ansel had been **dismissed on 30 April 1925**, four days earlier: "twenty-nine seasons and a letter." He came home at his usual hour, hung his apron behind the shed door, asked what there was for supper, and ate it.

He rebuilt his cow shed, whistling as he worked. By 1927, at sixty-six, he was the hollow's carpenter; one barn door he hung swung true for thirty years. His brother-in-law Emmet died in 1929 without the two being reconciled. Ansel died at home in **February 1934**, aged about 72, with the charge never spoken aloud. As he once told Orra, "a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name." Orra died in **1948**, aged 82. Hazel's father Roy never went to another Association meeting.

*Documents:* r22, r18, r24, r21, r23, r20, r17, r19.

### 8. The Girl Who Climbed the Ladder — Ivy Keddie and Bulletin No. 471 (1958–1970)

**Ivy Keddie** was born in 1931, Duncan's daughter and Ansel's granddaughter. She grew up in Ashlin, took her degree at the State Agricultural College at Farland, and stayed on as a graduate student in dairy husbandry. In **February 1958** she climbed into the loft over the old intake room. There she found the crate of condemned glass, which had sat for thirty-five years under a tarpaulin and chaff. She took it to Farland, and on **9 March 1958** she measured the **nine** surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette. (The *Chronicle* says eleven; Oram and Falke say nine.) Every pipette was marked 17.6 c.c. on the stem, and every one delivered less, all short by the same proportion. On my reconstruction they averaged **16.72 c.c., 5 per cent short**.

She also gathered the other records:

- Strawn's circuit notebooks, which had passed from the state dairy office to the Farland college library in 1947; she found the 1923 volume there.
- The Alder Corners figures, which Rosalie Cudd had copied for her.
- The Brant weigh book, lent by Roy.

**Bulletin No. 471**, published in October 1958 under the names of Professor **Leland Vaught** and Ivy Keddie, concluded as follows. A short charge of milk in a bottle ruled for a full charge reads short in the same proportion. The true average test of **3.80** had been read as **3.61**, a constant **0.19** low, and there was no water and no theft. Over 25 full delivery years the patrons had gone uncredited for **76,000 lb** of butterfat, worth **$19,000** at 25¢. (Oram's "67,000" is a transposition, and the *Chronicle*'s $19,600 cannot be reproduced.) The $3,800 award was **one-fifth** of that sum.

The *Ashlin Chronicle* ran "Old Ordell Wrong Righted" on **3 October 1958**. It got the invoice total and the pipette count wrong, and it called Ivy a great-niece and Rosalie Cudd a niece. Falke's 1959 report corrected both the relationship and his own concession on bog hay. Ivy's own verdict: "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not."

The records went public. The crate and the test book, which had lain in the Cooperative office at Ashlin from 1925 to 1959, went to the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1959. The weigh book followed in 1963, and the Cudd book went to Vessey County in 1970. Duncan's 1968 notes fixed the family relationships: he and Roy were first cousins, so Hazel and Ivy are second cousins.

*Documents:* r19, r22, r18, r21, r17.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie → Ansel Keddie: granddaughter.** Ansel married Orra in 1889. Their son was Duncan (b. 1894), and Duncan's daughter is Ivy (b. 1931). Duncan says so outright in r18 and warns that he has seen it printed otherwise. Hazel confirms that "Ivy is Duncan's girl" (r17), and Oram calls her "the granddaughter" (r21). The *Chronicle*'s statement that she is 27 in 1958 fits a 1931 birth. "Great-niece" appears in r19 and in Falke's paragraph 2 (r22). Falke's added note retracts it: "granddaughter and not the great-niece … the error … is mine." His text appeared after the *Chronicle*'s, so the two most likely share one source and count as a single error. The father's first-hand statement outranks it.

**B2. Ivy Keddie ↔ Hazel Brant: second cousins.** Emmet Brant and Orra Brant Keddie were siblings. Their children, Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie, were first cousins, and those first cousins' daughters, Hazel and Ivy, are second cousins (r17 and r18 agree). Oram's "first cousin once removed" (r21) is wrong: that relation requires a generation gap, and Hazel and Ivy are in the same generation. **The sources leave two things open.** First, whether Orra was Emmet's full sister (r18) or a half-sister by their father's second wife (r17). Orra's diary cannot decide it, because her pages from before 1889 were not preserved. If she was a half-sister, the precise relations are half-first cousins and half-second cousins, though still "second cousins" in the ordinary sense. Second, Orra's birth year (1866 or 1868; see E1). The mothers of Hazel and of Ivy are never named. None of these gaps changes the degree of the relationship.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose,** who organized the Association, was its first president and wrote its by-laws (r18, r23). The "nephew" story is rumor that both narrators reject. It matters because Gideon's by-laws contain Article 7, which pays by the butterfat test at the station of delivery. That rule is what turned a 5 per cent glass error into $19,000. His son managed the Association from 1911. He signed the ledger every year with its "station gain" carried to the Association's general account, and he issued the 1919 circular blaming the patrons for watering. So the manager who defended the Association's figures was defending an institution, and a rule, that his father had made. The idea that this is why he never questioned it is my inference. The sources show only that he read totals and never the lines above them.

**B4. Vose and Keddie families: no relationship is established; the best answer is none.** Duncan (r18) denies any connection outright. He traces the rumor to confusion with the Ashlin Voses, who are no relation to Gideon either, and to gossip that Orra and Mrs. Gideon Vose were cousins. His mother, who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection," never put a Vose in any family line. Oram (r21) repeats that the families were "cousins of some sort," but he has it from his grandmother by way of his mother and admits he "could not draw you the line." The only positive claim is second-hand talk with no stated link, set against a family member's direct knowledge. I also found no Vose anywhere in the Keddie or Brant lines in r17, r18 or r24.

## Section C — Math

**C1.**
(a) **The tests read 5% low: recorded = 0.95 × true.** The test bottle's neck is graduated for a 17.6 c.c. charge of milk, and the fat reading is proportional to the fat actually in the bottle. The pipettes delivered 16.72 c.c. on average, 0.88 c.c. short, and 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95**, so every reading came out 5% low. Three figures cross-check this: 3.61 ÷ 3.80 = 0.95 (r22); Kyle's station gain of "5¼%" equals 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 5.26%; and the award is exactly 1/5 of $19,000. The *Chronicle* prints 16.27 c.c. That would give 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.924, a 7.56% error, a true test of 3.905 and a shortfall of 0.295, which contradicts the bulletin's 0.19 (r22). I therefore read 16.27 as a transposition of 16.72 (method: arithmetic consistency).
(b) **True average 3.80** (3.61 ÷ 0.95, or 3.61 + 0.19). The recorded tests were **0.19 point short**, compared with Larrow Green's 3.78. The 1924-and-later average of 3.85 belongs to a later period and is not the true figure for the affected years.
(c) 40,000,000 lb of milk (25 years × 1,600,000 lb) × 0.0019 = **76,000 lb of butterfat** not credited.
(d) 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000**. The patrons received $3,800, and 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **1/5 (20%)**. The *Chronicle*'s $19,600 would require 78,400 lb, which no source figure yields. Oram's 67,000 lb is a digit transposition of 76,000.

**C2.** Emmet Brant, 1910: 200,000 lb of milk tested at 3.42.
(a) Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** of butterfat.
(b) True test 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60, so the fat owed was 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb. The uncredited amount is **360 lb**.
(c) 360 × $0.25 = **$90**. This uses the period-average 25¢ a pound, since no 1910 price is given in these sources. Applying a flat 0.19 instead of the proportional error would give 380 lb and $95. I reject that, because the glass error is proportional, not a fixed amount.

**C3.** Over the 25 full delivery years, 40,000,000 lb of milk:
(a) Credited: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**.
(b) Reached Larrow Green: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lb**.
(c) The difference, 76,000 lb, is **5.00% of the fat actually received** (76,000 ÷ 1,520,000) and **5.26% of the fat credited** (76,000 ÷ 1,444,000). The ledger's "five and a quarter per cent" station gain is the percentage of the *credited* figure.

**C4.**
(a) $25.20 + $31.68 + $0.90 + $18.00 + $2.15 = **$77.93**. The "$77.39" in r19 and in r20's prose is a transposition.
(b) **72 pipettes** (6 dozen at 35¢ = $25.20). **Each was 0.88 c.c. short**: marked 17.6 c.c., delivering 16.72 on average. That average is inferred as in C1(a), because the printed "16.27" would mean 1.33 c.c. short, which is inconsistent with every other figure.

**C5.**
(a) **The glass was in use from 1 May 1897 to its condemnation on 12 June 1923: 26 years, 1 month and 11 days.** Two caveats: this period includes the nine weeks of 1911 when a borrowed measure was used, and new glass arrived in July 1923. **The money calculation runs over 25 full delivery years (1898–1922).** Kyle's 40,000,000 lb ÷ 1,600,000 lb a year = 25. The two numbers differ because the glass also covered part of 1897 (May onward) and part of 1923 (to June). The complete-year receipts figure counts only full years and leaves those partial seasons out, so the true loss is slightly larger than $19,000.
(b) **Cannot be determined from the sources I have.** The first season was 1896, when the station borrowed Larrow Green's glass. That season's milk was carried on the creamery's books and never filed with the station papers. The only figure is Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" (1,100,000 lb), which he got from other men and says "is not in any book I ever ruled." That figure is plausible for a season starting in May (about 8/12 of 1.6 million lb is roughly 1.07 million), but it is unverified hearsay. An earlier retelling may settle it, but I have no notes on r01–r16 this session.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment.** It ran from **8 May to 10 July 1911, nine weeks (63 days)**; the start date is confirmed by Orra's diary. **Variable:** the measuring glass, borrowed versus the station's own. **Held constant:** the herds, the Hessel Bottom pasture and hay, the tester, the supervision, the machinery, the bottles and acid, and the patrons. **Result:** the tests rose markedly for nine weeks and fell back on the very day the station's own glass returned. A sharp step both ways, with the drop in mid-July, also rules out the season. **What it establishes that a calculation cannot:** a calculation only shows that the glass *could* explain the shortfall. The interval shows that changing the glass *did* change the reading, which is direct causal evidence. It also clears the tester, because the same man under the same supervision produced higher figures. **What it does not establish:** the size of the error, since the accuracy of the borrowed measure is unknown. It also does not show which article was at fault (pipette or bottle), whether the maker's lot was short and why, how long the fault had existed, or the money lost. Those needed Strawn's 1923 test, Ivy's 1958 measurements and Kyle's ledger figures.

**D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream.**
1. **The ledger's "station gain" (Kyle, r23).** Every year Larrow Green received about 5¼% *more* fat than the patrons were credited with. A tester skimming off cream would have produced a *loss* at the creamery. This reason does not depend on the 1958 measurements.
2. **The 1911 borrowed measure (Falke r22, Orra's diary r24, Duncan r18).** The same man's tests rose when only the glass changed.
3. **The after-1923 figures (Falke r22).** New glass went in in July 1923 and the average jumped to 3.85 from 1924. Ansel was still the tester until April 1925, so the "thief" produced normal tests the moment the glass was replaced. This reason also does not depend on the 1958 measurements.
4. **A constant shortfall and outside comparison (Hazel r17).** The shortfall was 0.19 every month, in wet years and dry. The same Hessel Bottom milk tested normally at Alder Corners in summer, and Emmet's own scale always agreed with the station's.

These reasons are **independent** because they come from separate records, kept by different hands for different purposes: the Association's office ledger, the station test book as reported by the county agent, the tester's family's diary and memory, and a patron's private weigh book. None of them relies on the crate. Reasons 1 and 3 would hold even if the glass had never been measured in 1958.
**Did the bookkeeper question the ledger?** No. Wilbur Teague wrote the "station gain … as usual" line every year and "never asked anybody anything in forty years" (r23). The sources I read name the accuser at the hearing only as "an officer of the Association" (r22). Taking the question's premise that this officer was the bookkeeper, he never questioned the entries in his own hand that refute his accusation.

**D3. Did manager Selby Vose know, before 1923, that the tests ran low?** **Yes, he knew the tests were low.** The patrons complained at annual meetings year after year (r19, r22), and in 1919 he signed and sent the water circular (r23, r22, r24), which only makes sense as an explanation for low tests. **But no source shows that he understood what the ledger showed:** that the fat had in fact arrived at Larrow Green. Kyle watched him sign "a thousand times" with his thumb on the bottom total, never reading the lines above it, and says he "was not a man who read" (r23). So the information that refuted his own water theory crossed his desk every year with his signature on it, and on the evidence he never read it. He knew the tests were low; he did not know, or never looked, why. That he *could* have known is shown by the sources. That he actually grasped it is not, and I do not infer it.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. The causal theory.** On 14 April 1897 Ammon & Sons of Tarnet sold the Association a lot of 72 milk-test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. that actually delivered about 16.72 c.c. (**size of the error**). The test bottles' necks were graduated for 17.6 c.c. of milk, so the fat in every sample was only 95% of what the scale assumed. Every reading came out 5% low: a true 3.80 read as 3.61, a shortfall of 0.19 point (**why that particular size**). Because the error was in the volume of milk measured, it was proportional and the same at every sampling, in every month, wet years and dry, regardless of herd or feed. The same milk tested normally elsewhere, and a 0.19 gap on a 3.61 test is 5.26% of the credited figure, exactly the ledger's "station gain, as usual." **It began** on 1 May 1897, when the station stopped borrowing Larrow Green's glass and put its own outfit into use. **It paused** for the nine weeks of 1911, when a borrowed measure was used and the tests rose. **It ended** in June–July 1923, when Strawn condemned the pipettes and new glass came in, and the average rose to 3.85 from 1924. **Nothing on the farms or at the station had to change:** the cows, hay, water, scales, acid and tester were all the same before, during and after. Only the glass changed, and the reading followed it. Under Article 7, which pays by the test, the 5% misreading became 76,000 lb of fat and $19,000 over 25 full delivery years, retained as "station gain."

**The three competing explanations, and what defeats each:**
1. **Added water.** Advanced by manager Selby Vose in the 1919 circular, and by the Association's officers generally. Defeated by Strawn's lactometer tests on every visit from 1908, which never once found water. Adding water also adds weight but not fat, so it cannot produce the fat surplus the creamery actually received.
2. **The tester stealing cream.** Urged at the 1925 hearing by an officer of the Association, and privately believed by the hauler Oram's father. Defeated by the 1911 interval (same man, higher tests), by the ledger's station *gain* where theft would show a loss, and by the normal tests from 1924 while Ansel was still testing.
3. **Bog hay thinning the cream.** Advanced by the 1908 Association committee, by an Ashlin veterinarian in 1926, and conceded (then withdrawn) by Falke in 1959. Defeated by Bulletin No. 214, the college's 1911 feeding trial: at most 0.05 lower in winter and nothing measurable on summer pasture, which cannot produce 0.19 in July. It is also defeated by the flat, season-independent shortfall, by the same milk testing normally at Alder Corners, and by the rise in 1924 on the same hay.

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?** No. The crate lay undisturbed in the loft from 1923 until February 1958, found under dust and chaff. Two of the board's three sittings were held in the weigh room directly beneath it, and nobody went up the ladder (r18, r21). Among its four books the board did have Strawn's notebook recording the 1923 condemnation (r23), but no one connected that record to the old tests. The board found no cause established.

**What the tester believed:** that the cause was **something in the glass**. He said so at home in the summer of 1911 after the borrowed-measure weeks, but "never dared put it in the book," entering only a question mark (r18, r22). He kept testing honestly with the glass he had been taught to trust.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** From 1897 to 1923 the Ostrey Hollow skimming station tested its patrons' milk with 72 pipettes from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet that delivered 5% short, so every butterfat test read low: 3.61 instead of 3.80. Because the Association paid by test, the Hessel Bottom farmers lost 76,000 lb of fat ($19,000) over 25 years, while the Larrow Green ledger quietly booked the same fat as "station gain." People blamed watering, bog hay, or the tester, Ansel Keddie, who was dismissed in 1925 when an arbitration awarded $3,800 without admission. In 1958 his granddaughter Ivy found the condemned glass in the station loft and measured it. The proof was scattered across an invoice, a family weigh book, a diary, a ledger, an inspector's notebook, a 1911 accident, a cheese factory's tests and the crate itself, and no single keeper held more than one piece.
