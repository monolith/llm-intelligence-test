# Answer Sheet — Part 2: Section A (Stories 5–8) and Section B (Relationships)

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### Story 5: The Glass Condemned, 1923

In June 1923, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn visited the Ostrey Hollow station on his regular circuit. Carrying a calibrated burette, he tested the station's milk pipettes against it and found that they would not deliver their marked capacity. He condemned the entire lot on the spot, directing the manager to order new glass immediately. His inspection report was filed. No inference was drawn about what those faulty pipettes might have been doing for the twenty-six years they had been in use. The station ordered replacement glass from Ammon & Sons; new pipettes and bottles arrived within a month and were put into service in July 1923. From July 1923 onward, the station's average test rose to 3.85—a marked and permanent shift. No one connected the 1923 condemnation to the 1911 question mark or to the thirty-year pattern of low tests. The old condemned glass—six dozen pipettes in a wooden crate—was carried up into the loft of the intake room and left there, covered with a tarpaulin and settling dust. It remained unseen and unexamined for thirty-five years, while the men who might have questioned it grew old and died.

### Story 6: The Hearing and Dismissal, 1925

In January 1925, Ostrey Hollow patrons, led by Emmet Brant, formally petitioned the Association for redress, claiming systematic short-payment over decades. A board of arbitration was established and met in three sittings: one at Ashlin in the Association's office (March 17 through May 4) and two at the station itself in the weigh room, where books and machinery could be inspected. The board heard the patrons' claim that Ansel Keddie was either stealing cream or falsifying tests, heard the manager's defense that patrons added water, and reviewed the records. The board found no cause established and, in May 1925, awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800 "without admission"—a payment that admitted no wrongdoing by the station, the Association, or Ansel Keddie. However, on April 30, 1925, four days before the award, Ansel Keddie was dismissed from his post of twenty-nine seasons with a letter. He had been tester and separator man at Ostrey Hollow since its opening. He left with his apron rolled under his arm. The dismissal stood even though the arbitration found nothing against him. His reputation in the county was ruined. He spent his remaining nine years before death taking carpentry jobs at a quarter of his former station wage, working on doors and barn frames for men who, having lost money in the dispute, employed him out of pity or habit but did not restore his standing.

### Story 7: The Thirty-Three Years, 1925–1958

From 1925 until 1958, the condemned glassware lay in the loft of the Ostrey Hollow station, undisturbed beneath a tarpaulin. Ansel Keddie died on February 1934, exonerated by no one, mourned at a well-attended funeral where not one mourner mentioned the weigh station or the accusation. The loft remained sealed to memory. The files of the Association lay in offices; Ansel's test book and the station accounts were impounded in 1925 and later deposited in the county historical rooms. The state dairy inspector's notebooks were filed at the state dairy office. No one juxtaposed these records. No one asked: what if the 1923 condemnation of the glass explained the decades of low tests? Duncan Keddie (Ansel's son, a schoolteacher) wrote family notes in 1968 trying to set the record straight for his daughter. But his daughter, Ivy Keddie, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, had already begun her own investigation in 1957–1958, motivated to clear her grandfather's name.

### Story 8: The Investigation and Exoneration, 1958

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie climbed a ladder into the loft over the Ostrey Hollow station's intake room and found a wooden crate, untouched for thirty-five years, covered in chaff and tarpaulin. Inside were eleven surviving milk test pipettes from the original set condemned in 1923. She carried the crate east to Farland and, on March 9, 1958, measured each surviving pipette against a calibrated burette in a college laboratory. Every pipette delivered measurably less than the 17.6 c.c. marked on its stem. The average delivery was 16.27 c.c., a short of 1.33 c.c. per charge. Short milk in a bottle marked for full milk yields fat readings proportionally short. The consistency of the error—every pipette equally short—meant every test over twenty-six years had been systematically low by the same proportion. The Ostrey Hollow patrons had not added water. No cream was stolen. Ansel Keddie had tested milk in front of the state inspector and was not responsible for the glass he was given to use. In October 1958, the college published Bulletin No. 471, written jointly by Professor Leland Vaught and Ivy Keddie, establishing that defective glassware, not dishonesty or neglect, had caused the shortfall. The bulletin calculated that patrons were never paid for 67,000 pounds of butterfat, worth $19,600. The Ostrey Hollow patrons had been shorted systematically for a generation because a piece of equipment out of a crate was wrong. Ansel Keddie's name was cleared—twenty-four years after his death.

## Section B — Relationships (8 points; one short paragraph each, with your reasoning)

**B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie? Derive it, and resolve anything in the sources that points elsewhere.**

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Ansel Keddie married Orra Brant in 1889. Their son was Duncan Keddie, born 1894. Duncan married and had a daughter, Ivy, born 1931. The county agent's report (r22) initially called her Ansel's "great-niece" but corrected itself in a note: Ivy is the granddaughter. She is also the daughter of Duncan and (Duncan notes) the daughter of Ivy's mother whose name is not given in the sources. The family relationship is direct: three generations—Ansel → Duncan → Ivy.

**B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant? Derive it, and state anything about the intervening generations that the sources leave open.**

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. Hazel's father Roy Brant was the son of Emmet Brant. Emmet Brant was the brother of Orra Keddie (Ansel's wife). Thus: Emmet was Orra's brother; Orra married Ansel and had Duncan; Duncan had Ivy. Emmet had Roy; Roy had Hazel. The relationship: Emmet and Orra are siblings; their children (Roy and Duncan) are first cousins; their grandchildren (Hazel and Ivy) are second cousins. Duncan Keddie explicitly makes this clarification in r18, noting that Hazel and Ivy are second cousins, not cousins once removed (which would involve different generations). No intervening generation is left open.

**B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter to what happened at Ostrey Hollow?**

Selby Vose was the son (not nephew) of Gideon Vose. Gideon Vose organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative in 1889 and was its first president. Selby became manager in 1911 and held office for "a quarter of a century" (r23). Duncan Keddie (r18) explicitly corrects the rumor that Selby was Gideon's nephew, stating he was the son and calling the nephew story "nonsense." Bertram Kyle (r23), the office clerk, also confirms Selby was the son of Gideon and says he "sat behind that door for a quarter of a century." The confusion arose locally at Ashlin, but the family records are clear. This matters to the Ostrey Hollow story because Selby, as manager for twenty-six years of the low-test period, was responsible for all official decisions—including the 1919 water-warning circular, the response to complaints, and the reporting to the arbitration board in 1925.

**B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?**

The sources do not establish a family relationship between the Vose family and the Keddie family. Duncan Keddie (r18) explicitly states: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses" and notes that his mother (Orra) "would have told me" if they were related. Cleve Oram (r21), however, reports local rumor that the Keddies and Voses were "cousins of some sort," and notes he "never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper." The sources leave this open: one direct family statement denies connection; one local rumor asserts it without proof. The weight of evidence (especially Duncan's insider knowledge) suggests no connection, but the question cannot be conclusively settled from the sources. The rumor may reflect coincidental name overlap or conflation with the "Ashlin Voses" (whom Gideon Vose also was unrelated to, per Duncan).
