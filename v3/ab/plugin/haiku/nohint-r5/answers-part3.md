# Answer Sheet — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math (14 points; show working)

### C1. Proportion and Losses

**(a) By what proportion did Ostrey Hollow tests differ from truth?**

The pipettes were marked 17.6 c.c. but delivered 16.27 c.c. on average.
- Short per pipette = 17.6 − 16.27 = 1.33 c.c.
- Proportion = 1.33 / 17.6 = 0.0756 or **7.56% short**

This matches the observed difference in test points: Ostrey Hollow averaged 3.61 while Larrow Green averaged 3.78. Difference = 0.17 points. As a proportion: 0.17 / 3.78 = 0.045 or 4.5%. However, the discrepancy is slightly larger when calculated per-charge: the proportion of short milk in each test charge (7.56%) translates to short fat readings proportional to the milk volume short, and accumulated across years.

**(b) True average test and points short:**

The recorded average at Ostrey Hollow was 3.61. The true average (corrected for the 7.56% short charge) would be: 3.61 / (1 − 0.0756) = 3.61 / 0.9244 = 3.905. Points short = 3.905 − 3.61 = **0.295 points** (or approximately **0.30 points**).

**(c) Butterfat never credited:**

Cleve Oram (r21) states: "Sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat was what they made it, over the whole run of years, that the Hollow men were never paid for." **67,000 pounds of butterfat shorted over all delivery years.**

**(d) Money value and fraction received:**

Ivy Keddie's bulletin (r19) calculates: "the figure is nineteen thousand six hundred dollars ($19,600)." The patrons were awarded $3,800 in 1925 "without admission." Fraction received = $3,800 / $19,600 = **0.194 or 19.4%**. The patrons recovered roughly one-fifth of what was owed.

### C3. Over Full Delivery Years

**(a) Total pounds credited to patrons:**

Forty million pounds of milk received at the station over the entire period (r21, r23). At an average test of 3.61%, butterfat credited = 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,440,000 pounds of butterfat credited.**

**(b) Butterfat actually received at main creamery:**

True butterfat content (corrected for short glass) = 40,000,000 × (0.0361 / 0.9244) = 40,000,000 × 0.0390 = **1,560,000 pounds of butterfat actually present in the milk.**

**(c) Express the difference as a percentage:**

The difference is 1,560,000 − 1,440,000 = 120,000 pounds. **As a percentage of what actually reached Larrow Green:** 120,000 / 1,560,000 = 0.077 or **7.7%**. (This matches the per-pipette short of 7.56%, confirming the math.)

## Section D — Logic (10 points; a short paragraph each)

**D1. The 1911 Broken Measure Experiment**

When occurred: May 8, 1911. Duration: 9 weeks until July 10, 1911. **Variable:** the glass used to measure milk samples for testing. **Held constant:** the herds, pasture, season, men, machinery, management, and testing procedure remained identical. **Result:** during the 9-week interval with borrowed glass, tests came out "markedly higher" than usual; when the original glass was restored, tests fell back to the low level. **What it establishes:** that something intrinsic to the original glass, not to the milk or the testing process or the people involved, caused the low readings. No other variable changed; only the glass changed. The tests responded to the glass change alone. **What it does NOT establish:** The interval alone does not establish the *magnitude* of the shortfall or allow calculation of how much the glass was short—for that, direct measurement of the glass was needed (which Ivy Keddie did in 1958). The interval proves the glass was faulty; it does not prove by how much or for how long before 1911 it had been used.

**D2. Three Independent Reasons Keddie Was Not Stealing Cream**

(1) **The 1911 experiment:** When a borrowed measure was used for 9 weeks, the tests rose. Keddie was the same person, under no different supervision. If he were skimming cream and falsifying tests, the tests should have remained low. But they rose with the borrowed glass, proving the variation was in the glass, not in Keddie's actions. This reason would hold even if the glassware had never been measured in 1958. (2) **Bertram Kyle's ledger evidence (r23):** the "station gain" line of 5.25% was entered in the official books every year and read aloud at annual meetings for decades. If Keddie had been stealing cream and writing low tests to hide it, the cream received at Larrow Green would have been less than the butterfat credited to patrons—but the ledger shows the opposite: butterfat credited was consistently *less* than the cream received (the 5.25% "gain"). The bookkeeper, Teague, never questioned this—but the ledger itself refutes the theft theory. (3) **Cleve Oram's testimony (r21):** Oram's father hauled cream for twenty-plus years and could see both ends—the milk going up to the station and the cream coming back down. Oram reports his father believed Keddie was honest but that cream was going missing, and only later learned it was the glass. If Keddie had been stealing (rather than the glass being faulty), the farmer who saw both ends would have detected it or reported it; instead, his instinct was that something in the *measurement* was wrong.

**Does the bookkeeper ever question the entries?** Bertram Kyle (r23) states: "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years. That is not a criticism. It was his notion of the position: the figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people." Teague accepted the station gain as a routine entry and signed off on it.

**D3. Did the Manager Know the Tests Ran Low Before 1923?**

**Yes, the manager Selby Vose knew or had strong reason to know.** The station's low tests were complained of repeatedly from 1902 onward, "in one form or another, in every decade thereafter" (r22, r23). Patrons raised it at annual meetings. The manager signed the ledger sheets every year, which Kyle says he did by thumbing to the bottom line without reading the lines above, but the fact of complaint was visible and persistent. More decisively, in 1919 Vose issued the water-warning circular to the Ostrey Hollow patrons specifically—this circular was a direct response to allegations about that station's milk testing low. Vose would not have sent the warning without believing a problem existed. However, the sources do not establish that Vose knew the cause was faulty glass. He may have believed the water-or-theft theories. The 1923 condemnation of the glass came as a surprise to the manager and to Vose (no prior measurement had been attempted), suggesting neither man had suspected the glass itself until the state inspector tested it.

## Section F — Theory (6 points)

**F1. Causal Theory**

A piece of equipment—milk test pipettes marked to deliver 17.6 c.c.—was manufactured by Ammon & Sons in 1897 with a systematic defect: each pipette actually delivered only 16.27 c.c. on average, a shortfall of 1.33 c.c. (7.56% below marked capacity). When a measured charge of milk short by 7.56% is placed in a test bottle marked for the full amount, the fat reading emerges proportionally short. The Association, unaware, used this glass from May 1897 until June 1923. Every test over 26 years was thus systematically short by a fixed proportion. The milk delivered to Ostrey Hollow was normal; the tests were normal procedure; Ansel Keddie tested honestly. But the glass made every result consistently low by one-fifth of a percentage point (0.19 or 0.17 points, depending on year and crop mix). The deficit appeared to be inherent to Ostrey Hollow—a property of the place or its people—when it was entirely a property of the measuring apparatus.

**Why that size?** Because that was what Ammon & Sons' mold produced when defective. The glass was bought as a lot in 1897 and all came from the same batch or mold. One defective lot out of a production run was shipped; the whole crate was wrong by the same degree.

**Why it began when it did:** The faulty glass entered use on May 1, 1897, when the Association's own testing outfit arrived from Tarnet.

**Why it ended when it did:** In June 1923, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn visited on routine circuit, tested the station's pipettes against a calibrated burette, found they did not deliver their mark, and condemned them. New glass was ordered and in use by July 1923. From that month, the tests rose to 3.85, a permanent shift.

**Why nothing at the station or farms had to change:** The shortfall was purely an artifact of measurement, not of operation, animals, feed, or procedure.

**Three competing explanations advanced and how they were defeated:**

1. **Water added by patrons (advanced by Selby Vose, manager, in 1919 circular).** Defeated by: Merle Strawn's lactometer tests from 1908 onward at every visit never found added water (r22). The 1911 borrowed-measure interval: when borrowed glass was used for 9 weeks, tests rose even though if water were being added by patrons, it would remain added. The milk from the same herds, tested with different glass, produced different results, proving the problem was in the glass, not the milk.

2. **Cream theft by Ansel Keddie with falsified tests (advanced by Association officers at the 1925 hearing).** Defeated by: The 1911 experiment again—the same tester using borrowed glass produced higher tests for 9 weeks, then lower again when original glass was restored. If Keddie were stealing and falsifying, the tests would not vary with the glass. Bertram Kyle's ledger evidence: the "station gain" figure of 5.25% recorded annually showed that butterfat credited to patrons was consistently *less* than cream received at Larrow Green, the opposite of what theft would produce. Cleve Oram's father, who hauled cream both directions, believed Keddie honest; only the glass explained the loss. The 1958 measurement proved the glass was faulty and Keddie innocent of any manual wrong.

3. **Wet hay and poor pasture of Hessel Bottom thin the cream (advanced by Association committee in 1908 and a veterinarian in 1926).** Defeated by: Orin Falke's addition to her 1959 county report (r22): the State Agricultural College conducted a feeding trial on bog hay (Bulletin No. 214) and found it depressed butterfat by "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Five hundredths cannot account for nineteen hundredths, especially not in July when pasture is good. The 1911 borrowed-measure interval with the same pasture and hay showed tests rose when glass changed, not when feed changed. The true cause—faulty glass—was constant regardless of season or pasture.

**Subsidiary questions:**

- **Did the 1925 board of arbitration ever examine the condemned glassware?** No. Bertram Kyle (r23) carried the books—the test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book, and the state inspector's notebook—to the arbitration sittings and back. He states: "I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked." The condemned glass lay in the loft above the weigh room, twenty feet overhead, during the hearings. No source records that any board member examined the glass itself or even knew it was there. The board worked from documents, not from the artifact. This was the fatal procedural failure: the evidence was present but invisible.

- **What did Ansel Keddie himself believe the cause was?** Duncan Keddie (r18) notes that in summer 1911, Ansel "came home... saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book." Ansel suspected the glass in 1911 and set a question mark but made no public accusation. Orra's diary and Duncan's notes suggest Ansel kept quiet, perhaps from professional deference to the Association or fear of being disbelieved. He suspected truth but did not assert it. After dismissal, he never spoke publicly about the matter, working as a carpenter in silence until his death in 1934.

## Section G — Summary (4 points)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, formed in 1889, built a milk-testing station at Ostrey Hollow in 1896 and equipped it in 1897 with testing pipettes from a dairy-supply house. Unknown to anyone, the pipettes were systematically short by 1.33 c.c. each (7.56% below mark). For twenty-six years, tester Ansel Keddie recorded butterfat tests that were proportionally low—3.61% instead of a true 3.78%—purely because of the faulty glass. Farmers complained; managers blamed patrons or suspected theft by Keddie. A 1911 broken measure, temporarily replaced, produced higher tests, hinting the glass was wrong—but no one followed the lead. In 1925, after decades of accusations and a failed hearing, Keddie was dismissed. In 1958, his granddaughter Ivy Keddie found the condemned crate of glass in the station loft, measured it against a calibrated burette, and proved every pipette short. The college bulletin cleared Keddie's name thirty-three years after dismissal, twenty-four years after his death. It took eight separate records—invoices, diaries, ledgers, memories, official reports, and raw glass—plus a ladder climbed by one determined investigator, to learn what a simple measurement in 1911 could have shown: the glass was faulty, the man was honest, and a generation of farmers had been systematically underpaid by an inch of defective equipment.
