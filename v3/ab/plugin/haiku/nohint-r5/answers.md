# Answer Sheet — Part 1: Section A (Stories 1–4)

## Section A — Reconstruction (50 points)

### Story 1: The Cooperative and Its Equipment, 1889–1897

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association formed in 1889 when local farmers, led by Gideon Vose, organized to centralize milk production. They built the main creamery at Larrow Green in 1891 and, in 1895–1896, built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley, to serve the Hessel Bottom farms. The Ostrey Hollow station opened May 4, 1896. On April 14, 1897, the Association ordered its testing equipment from Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, at Tarnet: six dozen milk test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c., twelve dozen test bottles (8% neck), one acid measure, one four-bottle hand tester, and freight—total $77.93. The pipettes and other glass arrived and entered service on May 1, 1897. This equipment, particularly the pipettes, was bought to a specification but, unknown to anyone, arrived from the manufacturer short of that mark. The Association kept meticulous records of milk delivered, samples tested, butterfat recorded, and cream sent to Larrow Green. Payment to each patron was determined solely by the fat test made at Ostrey Hollow, per Article Seven of the Association's by-laws. The station's tester and separator operator from the beginning was Ansel Keddie, born 1861, who married Orra Brant (sister of major patron Emmet Brant) in 1889. Keddie held the post from 1896 until his dismissal in 1925—twenty-nine seasons. All books, weights, and measures appeared reliable. The Association's bookkeeper, Wilbur Teague, later noted in the station accounts a consistent "station gain" of 5.25%, entered annually as "as usual" with no further inquiry.

### Story 2: The Anomaly Observed, 1897–1911

From the station's opening, Ostrey Hollow milk tested lower than milk from any other station or region. The station's average test over all years of complaint was 3.61 percentage points of butterfat; Larrow Green's own average was 3.78. The difference—0.19 points—was exact and unwavering, occurring in wet years and dry, in the flush of June and in February, as if a constant had been subtracted. By 1902, the first complaint was formally recorded. Patrons complained repeatedly at annual meetings across three decades. The manager, Selby Vose (son of founder Gideon Vose), heard the grievances but took no decisive action. Theories circulated: the farmers of Hessel Bottom added water to their milk; Ansel Keddie skimmed cream secretly and falsified tests to cover theft; the wet hay and bog ground of Hessel Bottom naturally thinned the cream of cattle fed there. In 1908, an Association committee examined the Hessel Bottom herds and concluded the low tests resulted from poor pasture and thin hay. A veterinarian later supported this. The tests remained low without explanation. Keddie, consulting his books, noted the consistency but made no public claim. The farmer-patrons grew frustrated and suspicious. Keddie's relationship with some patrons—particularly Emmet Brant—became strained as Brant, the largest patron, appeared to lose the most. The tension was buried in repeated records and polite silence. No one thought to examine the glass.

### Story 3: The Broken Measure, 1911

On May 8, 1911, the station's own milk-measuring glass broke. For nine weeks, until July 10, 1911, the station operated using a borrowed measure obtained from elsewhere. During this nine-week interval, the station's recorded butterfat tests were markedly higher than their average. The moment the original glass was resumed, tests fell back to their former low level. Ansel Keddie noted this in his test book and, finding no other explanation (the herds, pasture, season, men, and machinery were unchanged), set a question mark beside the entry. This was the critical evidence that something in the glass itself, not in the milk or cattle or operations, caused the deficit. Yet nothing came of it at the time. The question mark remained in the record—a silent, unheeded signal that Keddie suspected the problem but could not or would not accuse the Association's equipment of fault. The 1911 interval was the one moment when faulty glass was absent and the true state became briefly visible.

### Story 4: The Manager's Intervention, 1911–1923

In June 1919, after mounting complaints from farmers, manager Selby Vose issued a formal circular warning to eleven families on Ostrey Hollow about adding water to milk. The circular was carefully worded but unmistakably accusatory. Ansel Keddie, ordered to distribute it, took the copies from door to door himself rather than leave it to another, an act that signaled his discomfort and possibly his implicit disagreement. Orra recorded that Emmet Brant confronted Ansel, asking why he delivered a letter accusing the patrons if he himself believed them innocent. Ansel replied he delivered it precisely because he would not have another man carry it—ambiguous, but suggesting he accepted the charge's necessity even if he doubted its truth. The two men, brothers-in-law, did not reconcile; Emmet did not visit the station house again. The circular had no effect; the state dairy inspector's lactometer tests found no added water. The accusation hung unresolved. Through this period, Wilbur Teague and Selby Vose continued entering the "station gain" figures in the ledgers without question, year after year. No one compared the two sets of figures or asked what justified the consistent 5.25% loss.


# Answer Sheet — Part 2: Section A (Stories 5–8) and Section B (Relationships)

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### Story 5: The Glass Condemned, 1923

In June 1923, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn visited the Ostrey Hollow station on his regular circuit. Carrying a calibrated burette, he tested the station's milk pipettes against it and found that they would not deliver their marked capacity. He condemned the entire lot on the spot, directing the manager to order new glass immediately. His inspection report was filed. No inference was drawn about what those faulty pipettes might have been doing for the twenty-six years they had been in use. The station ordered replacement glass from Ammon & Sons; new pipettes and bottles arrived within a month and were put into service in July 1923. From July 1923 onward, the station's average test rose to 3.85—a marked and permanent shift. No one connected the 1923 condemnation to the 1911 question mark or to the thirty-year pattern of low tests. The old condemned glass—six dozen pipettes in a wooden crate—was carried up into the loft of the intake room and left there, covered with a tarpaulin and settling dust. It remained unseen and unexamined for thirty-five years, while the men who might have questioned it grew old and died.

### Story 6: The Hearing and Dismissal, 1925

In January 1925, Ostrey Hollow patrons, led by Emmet Brant, formally petitioned the Association for redress, claiming systematic short-payment over decades. A board of arbitration was established and met in three sittings: one at Ashlin in the Association's office (March 17 through May 4) and two at the station itself in the weigh room, where books and machinery could be inspected. The board heard the patrons' claim that Ansel Keddie was either stealing cream or falsifying tests, heard the manager's defense that patrons added water, and reviewed the records. The board found no cause established and, in May 1925, awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800 "without admission"—a payment that admitted no wrongdoing by the station, the Association, or Ansel Keddie. However, on April 30, 1925, four days before the award, Ansel Keddie was dismissed from his post of twenty-nine seasons with a letter. He had been tester and separator man at Ostrey Hollow since its opening. He left with his apron rolled under his arm. The dismissal stood even though the arbitration found nothing against him. His reputation in the county was ruined. He spent his remaining nine years before death taking carpentry jobs at a quarter of his former station wage, working on doors and barn frames for men who, having lost money in the dispute, employed him out of pity or habit but did not restore his standing.

### Story 7: The Thirty-Three Years, 1925–1958

From 1925 until 1958, the condemned glassware lay in the loft of the Ostrey Hollow station, undisturbed beneath a tarpaulin. Ansel Keddie died on February 1934, exonerated by no one, mourned at a well-attended funeral where not one mourner mentioned the weigh station or the accusation. The loft remained sealed to memory. The files of the Association lay in offices; Ansel's test book and the station accounts were impounded in 1925 and later deposited in the county historical rooms. The state dairy inspector's notebooks were filed at the state dairy office. No one juxtaposed these records. No one asked: what if the 1923 condemnation of the glass explained the decades of low tests? Duncan Keddie (Ansel's son, a schoolteacher) wrote family notes in 1968 trying to set the record straight for his daughter. But his daughter, Ivy Keddie, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, had already begun her own investigation in 1957–1958, motivated to clear her grandfather's name.

### Story 8: The Investigation and Exoneration, 1958

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie climbed a ladder into the loft over the Ostrey Hollow station's intake room and found a wooden crate, untouched for thirty-five years, covered in chaff and tarpaulin. Inside were eleven surviving milk test pipettes from the original set condemned in 1923. She carried the crate east to Farland and, on March 9, 1958, measured each surviving pipette against a calibrated burette in a college laboratory. Every pipette delivered measurably less than the 17.6 c.c. marked on its stem. The average delivery was 16.27 c.c., a short of 1.33 c.c. per charge. Short milk in a bottle marked for full milk yields fat readings proportionally short. The consistency of the error—every pipette equally short—meant every test over twenty-six years had been systematically low by the same proportion. The Ostrey Hollow patrons had not added water. No cream was stolen. Ansel Keddie had tested milk in front of the state inspector and was not responsible for the glass he was given to use. In October 1958, the college published Bulletin No. 471, written jointly by Professor Leland Vaught and Ivy Keddie, establishing that defective glassware, not dishonesty or neglect, had caused the shortfall. The bulletin calculated that patrons were never paid for 67,000 pounds of butterfat, worth $19,600. The Ostrey Hollow patrons had been shorted systematically for a generation because a piece of equipment out of a crate was wrong. Ansel Keddie's name was cleared—twenty-four years after his death.

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. Hazel's father Roy Brant was the son of Emmet Brant. Emmet Brant was the brother of Orra Keddie (Ansel's wife). Thus: Emmet was Orra's brother; Orra married Ansel and had Duncan; Duncan had Ivy. Emmet had Roy; Roy had Hazel. The relationship: Emmet and Orra are siblings; their children (Roy and Duncan) are first cousins; their grandchildren (Hazel and Ivy) are second cousins. Duncan Keddie explicitly makes this clarification in r18, noting that Hazel and Ivy are second cousins, not cousins once removed (which would involve different generations). No intervening generation is left open.

**B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter to what happened at Ostrey Hollow?**

Selby Vose was the son (not nephew) of Gideon Vose. Gideon Vose organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative in 1889 and was its first president. Selby became manager in 1911 and held office for "a quarter of a century" (r23). Duncan Keddie (r18) explicitly corrects the rumor that Selby was Gideon's nephew, stating he was the son and calling the nephew story "nonsense." Bertram Kyle (r23), the office clerk, also confirms Selby was the son of Gideon and says he "sat behind that door for a quarter of a century." The confusion arose locally at Ashlin, but the family records are clear. This matters to the Ostrey Hollow story because Selby, as manager for twenty-six years of the low-test period, was responsible for all official decisions—including the 1919 water-warning circular, the response to complaints, and the reporting to the arbitration board in 1925.

**B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?**

The sources do not establish a family relationship between the Vose family and the Keddie family. Duncan Keddie (r18) explicitly states: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses" and notes that his mother (Orra) "would have told me" if they were related. Cleve Oram (r21), however, reports local rumor that the Keddies and Voses were "cousins of some sort," and notes he "never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper." The sources leave this open: one direct family statement denies connection; one local rumor asserts it without proof. The weight of evidence (especially Duncan's insider knowledge) suggests no connection, but the question cannot be conclusively settled from the sources. The rumor may reflect coincidental name overlap or conflation with the "Ashlin Voses" (whom Gideon Vose also was unrelated to, per Duncan).

## Section C — Math (14 points; show working)

### C1. Proportion and Losses

**(a) By what proportion did Ostrey Hollow tests differ from truth?**

The pipettes were marked 17.6 c.c. but delivered 16.27 c.c. on average.
- Short per pipette = 17.6 − 16.27 = 1.33 c.c.
- Proportion = 1.33 / 17.6 = 0.0756 or **7.56% short**

This matches the observed difference in test points: Ostrey Hollow averaged 3.61 while Larrow Green averaged 3.78. Difference = 0.17 points. As a proportion: 0.17 / 3.78 = 0.045 or 4.5%. However, the discrepancy is slightly larger when calculated per-charge: the proportion of short milk in each test charge (7.56%) translates to short fat readings proportional to the milk volume short, and accumulated across years.

**(b) True average test and points short:**

The recorded average at Ostrey Hollow was 3.61. The true average (corrected for the 7.56% short charge) would be: 3.61 / (1 − 0.0756) = 3.61 / 0.9244 = 3.905. Points short = 3.905 − 3.61 = **0.295 points** (or approximately **0.30 points**).

**(c) Butterfat never credited:**

Cleve Oram (r21) states: "Sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat was what they made it, over the whole run of years, that the Hollow men were never paid for." **67,000 pounds of butterfat shorted over all delivery years.**

**(d) Money value and fraction received:**

Ivy Keddie's bulletin (r19) calculates: "the figure is nineteen thousand six hundred dollars ($19,600)." The patrons were awarded $3,800 in 1925 "without admission." Fraction received = $3,800 / $19,600 = **0.194 or 19.4%**. The patrons recovered roughly one-fifth of what was owed.

## Section D — Logic (10 points; a short paragraph each)

**D1. The 1911 Broken Measure Experiment**

When occurred: May 8, 1911. Duration: 9 weeks until July 10, 1911. **Variable:** the glass used to measure milk samples for testing. **Held constant:** the herds, pasture, season, men, machinery, management, and testing procedure remained identical. **Result:** during the 9-week interval with borrowed glass, tests came out "markedly higher" than usual; when the original glass was restored, tests fell back to the low level. **What it establishes:** that something intrinsic to the original glass, not to the milk or the testing process or the people involved, caused the low readings. No other variable changed; only the glass changed. The tests responded to the glass change alone. **What it does NOT establish:** The interval alone does not establish the *magnitude* of the shortfall or allow calculation of how much the glass was short—for that, direct measurement of the glass was needed (which Ivy Keddie did in 1958). The interval proves the glass was faulty; it does not prove by how much or for how long before 1911 it had been used.

**D2. Three Independent Reasons Keddie Was Not Stealing Cream**

(1) **The 1911 experiment:** When a borrowed measure was used for 9 weeks, the tests rose. Keddie was the same person, under no different supervision. If he were skimming cream and falsifying tests, the tests should have remained low. But they rose with the borrowed glass, proving the variation was in the glass, not in Keddie's actions. This reason would hold even if the glassware had never been measured in 1958. (2) **Bertram Kyle's ledger evidence (r23):** the "station gain" line of 5.25% was entered in the official books every year and read aloud at annual meetings for decades. If Keddie had been stealing cream and writing low tests to hide it, the cream received at Larrow Green would have been less than the butterfat credited to patrons—but the ledger shows the opposite: butterfat credited was consistently *less* than the cream received (the 5.25% "gain"). The bookkeeper, Teague, never questioned this—but the ledger itself refutes the theft theory. (3) **Cleve Oram's testimony (r21):** Oram's father hauled cream for twenty-plus years and could see both ends—the milk going up to the station and the cream coming back down. Oram reports his father believed Keddie was honest but that cream was going missing, and only later learned it was the glass. If Keddie had been stealing (rather than the glass being faulty), the farmer who saw both ends would have detected it or reported it; instead, his instinct was that something in the *measurement* was wrong.

**Does the bookkeeper ever question the entries?** Bertram Kyle (r23) states: "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years. That is not a criticism. It was his notion of the position: the figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people." Teague accepted the station gain as a routine entry and signed off on it.

**D3. Did the Manager Know the Tests Ran Low Before 1923?**

**Yes, the manager Selby Vose knew or had strong reason to know.** The station's low tests were complained of repeatedly from 1902 onward, "in one form or another, in every decade thereafter" (r22, r23). Patrons raised it at annual meetings. The manager signed the ledger sheets every year, which Kyle says he did by thumbing to the bottom line without reading the lines above, but the fact of complaint was visible and persistent. More decisively, in 1919 Vose issued the water-warning circular to the Ostrey Hollow patrons specifically—this circular was a direct response to allegations about that station's milk testing low. Vose would not have sent the warning without believing a problem existed. However, the sources do not establish that Vose knew the cause was faulty glass. He may have believed the water-or-theft theories. The 1923 condemnation of the glass came as a surprise to the manager and to Vose (no prior measurement had been attempted), suggesting neither man had suspected the glass itself until the state inspector tested it.

## Section F — Theory (6 points)

**F1. Causal Theory**

A piece of equipment—milk test pipettes marked to deliver 17.6 c.c.—was manufactured by Ammon & Sons in 1897 with a systematic defect: each pipette actually delivered only 16.27 c.c. on average, a shortfall of 1.33 c.c. (7.56% below marked capacity). When a measured charge of milk short by 7.56% is placed in a test bottle marked for the full amount, the fat reading emerges proportionally short. The Association, unaware, used this glass from May 1897 until June 1923. Every test over 26 years was thus systematically short by a fixed proportion. The milk delivered to Ostrey Hollow was normal; the tests were normal procedure; Ansel Keddie tested honestly. But the glass made every result consistently low by one-fifth of a percentage point (0.19 or 0.17 points, depending on year and crop mix). The deficit appeared to be inherent to Ostrey Hollow—a property of the place or its people—when it was entirely a property of the measuring apparatus.

**Why that size?** Because that was what Ammon & Sons' mold produced when defective. The glass was bought as a lot in 1897 and all came from the same batch or mold. One defective lot out of a production run was shipped; the whole crate was wrong by the same degree.

**Why it began when it did:** The faulty glass entered use on May 1, 1897, when the Association's own testing outfit arrived from Tarnet.

**Why it ended when it did:** In June 1923, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn visited on routine circuit, tested the station's pipettes against a calibrated burette, found they did not deliver their mark, and condemned them. New glass was ordered and in use by July 1923. From that month, the tests rose to 3.85, a permanent shift.

**Why nothing at the station or farms had to change:** The shortfall was purely an artifact of measurement, not of operation, animals, feed, or procedure.

**Three competing explanations advanced and how they were defeated:**

1. **Water added by patrons (advanced by Selby Vose, manager, in 1919 circular).** Defeated by: Merle Strawn's lactometer tests from 1908 onward at every visit never found added water (r22). The 1911 borrowed-measure interval: when borrowed glass was used for 9 weeks, tests rose even though if water were being added by patrons, it would remain added. The milk from the same herds, tested with different glass, produced different results, proving the problem was in the glass, not the milk.

2. **Cream theft by Ansel Keddie with falsified tests (advanced by Association officers at the 1925 hearing).** Defeated by: The 1911 experiment again—the same tester using borrowed glass produced higher tests for 9 weeks, then lower again when original glass was restored. If Keddie were stealing and falsifying, the tests would not vary with the glass. Bertram Kyle's ledger evidence: the "station gain" figure of 5.25% recorded annually showed that butterfat credited to patrons was consistently *less* than cream received at Larrow Green, the opposite of what theft would produce. Cleve Oram's father, who hauled cream both directions, believed Keddie honest; only the glass explained the loss. The 1958 measurement proved the glass was faulty and Keddie innocent of any manual wrong.

3. **Wet hay and poor pasture of Hessel Bottom thin the cream (advanced by Association committee in 1908 and a veterinarian in 1926).** Defeated by: Orin Falke's addition to her 1959 county report (r22): the State Agricultural College conducted a feeding trial on bog hay (Bulletin No. 214) and found it depressed butterfat by "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Five hundredths cannot account for nineteen hundredths, especially not in July when pasture is good. The 1911 borrowed-measure interval with the same pasture and hay showed tests rose when glass changed, not when feed changed. The true cause—faulty glass—was constant regardless of season or pasture.

**Subsidiary questions:**

- **Did the 1925 board of arbitration ever examine the condemned glassware?** No. Bertram Kyle (r23) carried the books—the test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book, and the state inspector's notebook—to the arbitration sittings and back. He states: "I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked." The condemned glass lay in the loft above the weigh room, twenty feet overhead, during the hearings. No source records that any board member examined the glass itself or even knew it was there. The board worked from documents, not from the artifact. This was the fatal procedural failure: the evidence was present but invisible.

- **What did Ansel Keddie himself believe the cause was?** Duncan Keddie (r18) notes that in summer 1911, Ansel "came home... saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book." Ansel suspected the glass in 1911 and set a question mark but made no public accusation. Orra's diary and Duncan's notes suggest Ansel kept quiet, perhaps from professional deference to the Association or fear of being disbelieved. He suspected truth but did not assert it. After dismissal, he never spoke publicly about the matter, working as a carpenter in silence until his death in 1934.

## Section G — Summary (4 points)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, formed in 1889, built a milk-testing station at Ostrey Hollow in 1896 and equipped it in 1897 with testing pipettes from a dairy-supply house. Unknown to anyone, the pipettes were systematically short by 1.33 c.c. each (7.56% below mark). For twenty-six years, tester Ansel Keddie recorded butterfat tests that were proportionally low—3.61% instead of a true 3.78%—purely because of the faulty glass. Farmers complained; managers blamed patrons or suspected theft by Keddie. A 1911 broken measure, temporarily replaced, produced higher tests, hinting the glass was wrong—but no one followed the lead. In 1925, after decades of accusations and a failed hearing, Keddie was dismissed. In 1958, his granddaughter Ivy Keddie found the condemned crate of glass in the station loft, measured it against a calibrated burette, and proved every pipette short. The college bulletin cleared Keddie's name thirty-three years after dismissal, twenty-four years after his death. It took eight separate records—invoices, diaries, ledgers, memories, official reports, and raw glass—plus a ladder climbed by one determined investigator, to learn what a simple measurement in 1911 could have shown: the glass was faulty, the man was honest, and a generation of farmers had been systematically underpaid by an inch of defective equipment.
