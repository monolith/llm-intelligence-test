# Test Answers Part 1
## Section A: Eight Stories (1–4)

### Story 1: The Founding of Ostrey Hollow Station (1889–1897)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was organized by farmers in 1889 and built its main creamery at Larrow Green in 1891. By 1895–1896, recognizing the need to serve farmers seven miles up the valley, particularly those on Hessel Bottom, the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow. The station opened on May 4, 1896 (per Falke, r22).

From the opening, Ansel Keddie—born 1861—was hired as tester and separator man. He was a methodical, quiet man of scrupulous practice: he tested milk samples for butterfat, weighed incoming milk, separated cream from skim, sent cream down to Larrow Green and skim back to farmers. Every patron was paid according to the test result, not milk weight (Article VII of the Association's by-laws).

In 1897, the Association purchased its testing outfit from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet. The invoice, dated April 14, 1897, shows: six dozen milk test pipettes at 17.6 cc (marked), twelve dozen test bottles, one acid measure, one four-bottle hand tester, and freight—total $77.93 (r20, Junia Ammon). The pipettes and other glassware arrived at Ostrey Hollow and came into use on May 1, 1897 (r24, Orra's diary).

The narrators all confirm this founding and Ansel's long tenure. Emmet Brant, Roy's father, began his own careful weigh book on January 1, 1900 (r17, Hazel), recording his milk weight and the station's test side by side to verify agreement. The family pattern—meticulousness, record-keeping, trust in scales—set the tone for what would later become crucial evidence. By 1897, the Hollow's six farms (including Emmet's largest plot) formed a coherent delivery community, and Ansel Keddie was their tester for life.

**Documents bearing on this story:** Falke's official report (r22) on the Association's founding; Junia Ammon's firm history (r20) with the invoice in facsimile; Orra Keddie's diary opening (r24); Emmet Brant's ledger quoted by Roy (r09); Duncan Keddie's family notes (r18) on the household; Hazel Brant's account of her grandfather's ledger (r17).

---

### Story 2: The Emerging Deficit (1897–1911)

From the first months at Ostrey Hollow, the station's milk tested lower—about 3.61 butterfat on average—than milk from the same valley tested elsewhere. The Larrow Green creamery's own milk averaged 3.78. The deficit was consistent: 0.19 points (nineteen hundredths), flat month to month, winter and summer alike (r22, Falke; r21, Oram; r23, Kyle).

By 1902, the first complaints of record were lodged (Falke, r22). The deficit persisted for two decades. Roy Brant later recalled his father Emmet's meticulous ledger recording both his scale and the station's side by side for thirty years—and finding them in agreement. This meant the milk truly weighed what Emmet recorded; the station's scales were honest. Yet the test result remained 0.19 points lower than the same milk tested at Alder Corners cheese factory, just across the ridge, or at Larrow Green (r09, r10, r21).

Three explanations circulated. First: the Hollow's farmers added water. Second: Ansel Keddie was dishonest, skimming cream. Third: the wet hay and bog meadows of Hessel Bottom naturally depressed butterfat in the cows' milk. By 1908, a committee of the Association visited and reported that wet hay was the likely cause (r15, Nyle Grigg, 1908 committee report).

Ansel himself remained silent. Orra's diary shows that in March 1902, after hearing valley talk, Ansel came home and refused to eat, saying "it is my figures they will look at" (r24). He never publicly articulated doubt about his own equipment. Yet by 1911, something shifted: on May 8, 1911, the station's measuring glass broke.

**Documents bearing on this story:** Emmet and Roy Brant's ledger (quoted by Roy r09 and Hazel r17); Rosalie Cudd's letter to Ivy (r10) confirming Hessel Bottom milk tested 3.80 at Alder Corners; Falke's report (r22) establishing the 0.19 differential; Grigg's 1908 committee report (r15); Orra's diary (r24) on Ansel's distress; Cleve Oram's account (r21) of his father's beliefs about cream theft; Bertram Kyle's recollection (r23) of the consistent deficit in the books.

### Story 3: The Critical Interval—The Broken Glass (May 8 – July 10, 1911)

On May 8, 1911, the station's measuring glass—the pipette used to draw milk samples for the test—broke. Rather than order a replacement immediately, the station borrowed a measure from elsewhere (sources do not specify from whom, only that it was borrowed). This borrowed glass was used for nine weeks, until July 10, 1911.

During those nine weeks, the station's test results rose sharply: from the chronic 3.61 to approximately 3.80. The herds, pasture, hay, season, men, and machinery were all unchanged. The only variable that differed was the glass. On July 10, the station resumed its own glass—a replacement had arrived or been repaired. The test results immediately fell back to 3.61.

Ansel Keddie noted this in the station's test book with a question mark. Duncan Keddie (r18) recalled his father coming home in summer 1911 saying "it was something in the glass" but refusing to commit the thought to paper without proof: "He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove." Orra's diary (r24) records May 8, 1911: "A. home cross about a broken measure." The 1911 interval is the single most powerful piece of evidence that the glassware, not the milk or the tester, was at fault.

Remarkably, no one drew the inference at the time. The contemporaneous notes survived in the test book, but the connection was not made for 47 years—until Leland Vaught's 1958 analysis (r11, r22).

**Documents bearing on this story:** Ansel's test book notation (cited by Duncan r18, Falke r22, Kyle r23); Ansel's spoken reflection to Duncan (r18); Orra's diary entry for May 8, 1911 (r24); Vaught's analysis in Bulletin 471 (r11, r22); Cleve Oram's general knowledge (r21).

### Story 4: Accusations and Estrangement (1908–1925)

In 1908, the Association's committee, led by Nyle Grigg (r15), visited Hessel Bottom and concluded that the low tests were due to bog hay depressing butterfat. This explanation was codified in State College Bulletin 214, which held that bog hay reduced butterfat by 0.2 points in winter and nothing measurable in summer. It became the prevailing narrative: the Hollow farmers' own hay, not the station or the tests, was the culprit. Dr. Roland Kepp, a veterinary surgeon, reinforced this view in a 1926 paper (r16).

In June 1919, Selby Vose, the Association's manager (son of founder Gideon Vose, per Duncan r18 and Bertram Kyle r23), sent nine warning letters to Hollow patrons accusing them of adding water to their milk (r08, r24). The tone was civil but the implication was clear: the patrons were committing fraud. This was the public accusation that would linger for six years.

Ansel Keddie did not mail the letters or leave them on the station platform. Instead, he personally delivered all nine to the families and stood at each door while they read it—a gesture laden with meaning. Orra's diary (r24) records: "A. would not put them in the mail nor leave them on the platform. He took them round himself, to eleven families, and stood at the door while each man read it, and came home at eleven at night with his hat still on." (Note: Kyle says nine families; Orra says eleven. Kyle was in the office and typed the letter; Orra lived at the station. The discrepancy may reflect different counts—nine formal recipients vs. all properties visited—but this cannot be definitively resolved. I use Kyle's "nine" as the office record, but note Orra's "eleven.")

Two days later, Emmet Brant came to the station and confronted Ansel in the yard. Emmet had hauled whole milk there since the station opened and knew Ansel better than any man. He asked: why did Ansel carry the water letter himself if he didn't believe it? Ansel replied: "He carried it because he would not have another man carry it." Emmet left. Orra writes: "He has not been in this house since, and he is my brother" (r24). The ten-year estrangement—between Ansel and Emmet, between the two branches of the Brant-Keddie family—dated from this moment.

Ansel was thus publicly associated with the accusation against his own in-laws. Yet he harbored private doubt: Duncan's account (r18) notes that in 1911, Ansel had said "something in the glass" but would not be overheard. The three competing theories—water, theft, wet hay—had calcified, and Ansel's suspicion (if he held one) remained mute.

**Documents bearing on this story:** Orra's diary (r24) on the June 1919 circular and the confrontation; Kyle's recollection (r23) of typing the circular and Vose's role; Duncan's account (r18) of Ansel's 1911 suspicion and his caution; Grigg's committee report (r15) on wet hay; Kepp's paper (r16) on bog-meadow chemistry; Falke's summary (r22) of the three theories heard at the 1925 hearing.


# Test Answers Part 2
## Section A: Eight Stories (5–8)

### Story 5: The Condemnation and Silence (June 1923 – December 1924)

In June 1923, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn arrived at Ostrey Hollow on a routine circuit visit. Merle Strawn was kin to no one in the valley (Duncan r18)—a crucial fact: he had no stake in any outcome. Strawn carried a burette (a calibrated measuring vessel) and ran the station's pipettes against it to verify they delivered their marked capacity.

The pipettes failed. They would not deliver 17.6 cubic centimeters as blown on their stems. Strawn condemned the entire lot on the spot and directed the manager to order new glass (r22, Falke; r23, Kyle).

Kyle records this in his memoir: "In twenty-two the state man came into the office" (actually June 1923; Kyle's year is approximate). The Association ordered replacement glass from Ammon & Sons on June 28, 1923. Ammon's reply (July 2, 1923) acknowledged receipt but refused to explain what had happened to the original lot, saying only: "Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name" (r20, Junia Ammon's facsimile letter). The new glass arrived within the month.

From July 1923 onward, the station's average test rose to 3.85—a visible jump of 0.24 points. Yet no one drew the inference backward. Kyle writes (r23): "There was no more said of it in the office than there would have been of a broken window." Bertram Kyle knew the station gain line; he knew it read "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" every year. He never wondered.

Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks recording the condemnation were filed with the state dairy office in 1936 when he retired, then transferred to the State Agricultural College library at Farland in 1947. They lay untouched until Ivy Keddie found Strawn's 1923 notebook eleven years later (r18, Duncan).

The silence held for 26 years. The condemned pipettes sat in a crate in the loft of the station, covered with a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff (r22, Falke; r21, Oram). Two of the three sessions of the 1925 arbitration sat in the weigh room beneath that crate, twenty feet below, and no one climbed the ladder.

**Documents bearing on this story:** Falke's account of the June 1923 condemnation (r22); Kyle's brief note (r23); Junia Ammon's letter of July 2, 1923 (r20); Duncan's note on Strawn's notebooks (r18); Cleve Oram's recollection of the crate in the loft (r21); Orra's diary noting "new glass" in July 1923 (r24).

### Story 6: The Hearing and Dismissal (January – May 1925)

In January 1925, the Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned the Association for redress. A board of arbitration was formed and convened on March 17, 1925, with three sessions. The first was held at the Association's office at Ashlin. Two subsequent sessions were held at the Ostrey Hollow station itself, in the weigh room, because the books, scales, and machinery were all there (r22, Falke; r23, Kyle; r24, Orra; r21, Oram).

The board was chaired by an attorney (Alonzo Frick, per r13). The hearing examined three theories: (1) patrons added water (refuted by state inspector lactometer tests from 1908 forward, never finding added water at Hollow); (2) Ansel Keddie stole cream (Wilbur Teague's "station gain" entries showing fat arriving heavier at Larrow Green than credited, not lighter); (3) wet hay depressed butterfat (a partial factor, accounting for perhaps 0.05 points in winter, per Bulletin 214, insufficient to explain the 0.19 differential).

Ansel Keddie testified honestly about his methods. He had no defense against the charge of dishonesty; he only had his record. The board heard testimony from patrons, Association officers, and experts (Kepp, who argued for wet hay; Teague, whose own ledger entries contradicted his insinuation of theft).

On April 30, 1925—four days before the board would announce its "finding"—Ansel Keddie was dismissed from his position after 29 seasons. He came up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm, hung it behind the shed, and asked what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it (r24, Orra).

On May 4, 1925, the board awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800, "without admission" of any cause. The phrase meant: we are paying you, and we are not saying why. Ansel received nothing. He was not asked back.

The award was later charged to the station account—an oddity Kyle noted: "it struck me at the time as neat bookkeeping and strikes me now as something else, though I am not the man to say what" (r23).

Cleve Oram, listening outside the weigh room in the rain, heard voices but not words. He later recalled: "the crate was up the ladder over their heads the entire time. Twenty feet, if that. I have thought about that since, I will not pretend I thought about it then" (r21).

**Documents bearing on this story:** Falke's official account (r22) of the March 17 – May 4 hearings; Kyle's recollection (r23) of the hearing and the award; Orra's diary (r24) on March 17 and April 30; Cleve Oram's account (r21); Alonzo Frick's retrospective (r13) as arbitration chairman; Teague's defense of his ledger (r05); Kepp's argument for wet hay (r16); Grigg's 1908 committee report (r15).

### Story 7: The Nine-Year Aftermath and Vindication (1925–1958)

After dismissal, Ansel Keddie did not seek reinstatement. He had kept four or five cows on too small a plot of land and turned to carpentry and cabinet-making. He proved excellent at it. Orra's diary records (r24): "He has begun on the cow shed, which has wanted doing since his father's time. He works at it from light to dark and whistles, which he never did when he was paid." Later: "A carpenter at sixty-six. He is better at it than he ever was at the other and it pays a quarter of what the other did."

The work was honest and good. Ansel mended stairs, gates, and doors throughout the hollow. At his funeral in February 1934, men shook Orra's hand and remarked: "What a hand he was with a door." None mentioned the Ostrey Hollow matter. Ansel had said to Orra once, and only once: "A man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name" (r24).

Orra outlived him fourteen years, dying in 1948. She never saw what came next.

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie—Ansel's granddaughter, a college student at Farland—went up a ladder into the loft over the station's intake room. She found the wooden crate, still under its tarpaulin and chaff. It held the condemned glassware: test bottles and the original pipettes (r22, Falke; r21, Oram; r23, Kyle; r18, Duncan).

She carried the crate to Farland. There were eleven pipettes in condition to be measured (five dozen originally; forty-nine had been broken over 35 years). On March 9, 1958, she measured each one against a calibrated burette in a college laboratory.

Every pipette was blown with a 17.6 cc mark on its stem. Not one delivered it. The average of the lot was 16.27 cc. The shortfall was 1.33 cc—a ratio of 0.95 (16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9744, or roughly 0.95 or 5% under).

This single fact resolved the mystery: a 3.61 recorded test was actually 3.80 true (3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.8). Every recorded figure for 26 years was systematically low in the same proportion. No milk was watered. No cream was stolen. Ansel Keddie had been honest all along.

Professor Leland Vaught and Ivy Keddie collaborated on State Agricultural College Bulletin 471, published October 1958. The Ashlin Chronicle ran a front-page feature, "Old Ordell Wrong Righted" (r19). Ivy was 27 and said: "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not."

Ansel had been dead 24 years.

**Documents bearing on this story:** Orra's diary (r24) on Ansel's carpentry and death; Cleve Oram's account (r21) of Ansel's skills; Leland Vaught's analysis (r11); Bulletin 471 findings (r19, r22); Ivy Keddie's account (r19); Falke's report (r22) incorporating the new finding; Duncan's notes (r18) on Ivy's college work and Vaught's Bulletin; the Ashlin Chronicle (r19); Bertram Kyle's reflection (r23) on plain sight but a name on it.

### Story 8: The Records Scatter and the Theory Consolidated (1897–1964)

Throughout the scandal, honest men kept careful books and notes. None of them knew they were writing evidence of an instrument's failure.

**The station's test book.** Ansel Keddie kept it in his own hand from 1896 until his dismissal in 1925. The Association's board of arbitration impounded it in March 1925. After the hearing, it went to the Cooperative's office at Ashlin, where it lay from 1925 to 1959. In 1959, it was deposited with the Ordell County Historical Rooms at Ashlin, where it remains (r18, Duncan).

**Emmet Brant's weigh book.** Emmet began it on January 1, 1897 (not 1900; r17, Hazel clarifies; earlier sources said 1900, but the invoice and Orra's diary confirm 1897). Emmet kept it until his death in 1929, recording milk weight and station test side by side. Roy Brant, Emmet's son, continued it. In 1958, Roy lent it to Ivy for her college research—explicitly lent, not given (r17, r18). Roy died in 1962; Hazel received it and gave it to the Historical Rooms in 1963 (r18, Duncan). Both men were adamant about the distinction: a loan is a loan.

**Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks.** The state dairy inspector kept one for each year on his circuit. They went to the state dairy office when Strawn retired in 1936, then transferred to the State Agricultural College library at Farland in 1947. The 1923 volume lay untouched for 47 years until Ivy found it in 1958 (r18, Duncan).

**Alder Corners cheese factory test book.** Jerome Cudd kept it from his company's founding; Rosalie Cudd, his daughter, took over in 1941. In 1958, Rosalie had the figures copied out and sent to Ivy at the college (r18, Duncan; r10, Rosalie's letter). The book itself remained in Vessey County.

**The condemned crate.** The station's glassware was put up in the loft over the weigh room in 1923 and stayed there until Ivy found it in February 1958. It was taken east to Farland to be measured, then in 1959 was deposited with the Historical Rooms at Ashlin alongside the station's test book (r18, Duncan). By 1970, the Alder Corners book had also gone to the Vessey County Historical Society. The five books were scattered across three buildings.

**The arbitration record.** Alonzo Frick's 1925 hearing (r13) produced no written findings beyond the award announcement. What passed through Kyle's hands to the board (r23) was: the station test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book (lent), and Strawn's 1923 notebook. Kyle believed no one ever compared them: "I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked" (r23).

The four books together could have settled the matter in 1925. The Brant ledger proved the station's scales were honest. Strawn's notebook proved the inspector had examined the pipettes. The station book showed the 0.19 differential. The receiving book showed the cream arrived heavier at Larrow Green than credited by the station test. No one compared them because no one thought to look.

Bertram Kyle, the clerk who lived by the desk, made a final moral (r23): "Nothing in that office was hidden. Every figure I have named was written down in ink by an honest man, in a book that stood on an open shelf, in a room that anybody might walk into, and it was carried forward every year for a quarter of a century, and read out at the annual meeting to fifty farmers with their hats on their knees. It was not a secret. It was worse than a secret. It was in plain sight with a name on it."

The name—"station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual"—made the figure seem explained. And nothing is more easily overlooked than what has already been named.

**Documents bearing on this story:** Duncan Keddie's thorough account (r18) of where all five books went and when; Bertram Kyle's reflection (r23) on the books and the hearing; Hazel Brant's account (r17) of the weigh book's journey; Orra's diary (r24) noting Emmet's death and the estrangement's end; Rosalie Cudd's letter (r10); Cleve Oram's knowledge (r21) of the station's later closure (1946) and the loft's survival.

## Section B — Relationships

### B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie

**Answer:** Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.

**Reasoning:** Duncan Keddie writes (r18): "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter." Duncan's father was Ansel. Therefore, Ivy is Ansel's granddaughter. 

The Ashlin Chronicle (r19) initially called her Ansel's "great-niece," but Orin Falke, the county agent, issued a correction (r22 note added): "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." This correction is authoritative, as Falke had access to family records when preparing his official report.

Cleve Oram (r21) also confirms: "She was a Keddie and she was schooled at Farland. Ivy Keddie."

---

### B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant

**Answer:** Ivy and Hazel were second cousins.

**Reasoning:** Duncan Keddie (r18) states plainly: "Roy's daughter is Hazel Brant. My daughter is Ivy. Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece, which people say because it is shorter."

Hazel confirms this (r17): "Ivy Keddie and I are second cousins... People are forever telling me she is my niece or my cousin once removed or some such, and she is not. Second cousins."

**Open question:** Duncan and Hazel both felt compelled to correct the common misstatement, suggesting confusion ran rampant in the valley. Both sources reject "niece" or "once removed," and both assert "second cousins." The intervening generation is fully clear from the documentation: Emmet Brant (Hazel's grandfather) and Orra Brant (Ansel's wife, Emmet's sister) are the connecting link between the Brants and the Keddies. No generation is left open.

---

### B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter?

**Answer:** Gideon Vose was Selby Vose's father. Selby was the son (not nephew) of the Association's founder and first president.

**Reasoning:** Duncan Keddie (r18) is explicit: "Mr. Selby Vose became manager in nineteen eleven. He was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president — the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense."

Bertram Kyle (r23) corroborates and adds context: "Mr. Selby Vose became manager in nineteen eleven. He was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president — the son, whatever is said at Ashlin."

**Why it matters:** Gideon Vose was the Association's founder and architect. His son Selby inherited not just a position but a family legacy of authority. When Selby signed ledger sheets showing "station gain" of 5.25% "as usual," he did so as the son of the man who designed the Association's structure. This lineal authority made his signature carry weight. Moreover, Selby never questioned the entries he signed: Kyle notes he "read the totals and he never read the lines above them" (r23). The son of the founder, though present, was inattentive—a blind spot that mattered for 26 years. Duncan also notes a rumor (disputed) that the Keddies and Voses were related, but both Duncan and Kyle reject this. The point: Selby was the manager, Ansel was the tester, and the two roles were separate and should have been checking each other. The founder's son, inattentive to detail, failed to do so.

---

### B4. The relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family

**Answer:** There was no familial relationship between the Voses and the Keddies. Duncan Keddie explicitly states (r18): "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either. I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her sit down. It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me."

Cleve Oram (r21), however, reports a rumor: "the Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley. My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper."

**Resolution:** Duncan Keddie lived in the household, knew the family history intimately, and is explicit that no relationship existed. He knew his mother would have told him, and she did not. Oram admits he cannot draw the line and heard it "at the time" (vaguely, decades later). Duncan is a more reliable source: he was inside the family and would have known. Oram's is hearsay. The correct answer is that no relationship existed, though rumor in a small valley suggested one.

**Why it matters:** The absence of a family tie means the Keddies and Voses had no kinship obligation or conflict of interest to navigate. Ansel and Selby were professional colleagues only. When Ansel was accused, no family defense was available. When Selby signed the ledger each year without reading it, no family understanding tempered the oversight. The separation between them was total.


# Test Answers Part 3
## Section C — Math (show working)

### C1. Proportion of defect and lost butterfat

**(a) By what proportion did the Ostrey Hollow station's tests differ from the truth, and how do you get that figure from the glassware?**

The recorded tests read at 95% of the truth (or 0.95 ratio). This is derived as follows:

- The Alder Corners cheese factory tested milk from Hessel Bottom farmers over 16 summers (1915–1930) and consistently recorded 3.80 butterfat (r10, Rosalie Cudd).
- The same farmers' milk tested at Ostrey Hollow station averaged 3.61 over the same decades (r22, Falke).
- Ratio: 3.61 / 3.80 = 0.95.

From the glassware: The condemned pipettes, measured by Ivy Keddie (r19), averaged 16.27 cubic centimeters. The marked capacity was 17.6 cc. Ratio: 16.27 / 17.6 = 0.924, or approximately 0.92. This is close to but not identical to the 0.95 derived from the actual test results. The small discrepancy may reflect measurement variation or that the pipettes themselves degraded slightly during 26 years of use. I use the 0.95 ratio from the actual tests (3.61 / 3.80), as it is based on independent external verification (Alder Corners).

**(b) What was the true average test at that station over the affected years, and by how many points were the recorded tests short?**

- True average: 3.80 (per Alder Corners independent testing and the 1911 interval when borrowed glass was used).
- Recorded average: 3.61 (per station records and all contemporary accounts).
- Shortfall: 3.80 - 3.61 = 0.19 points.

**(c) How many pounds of butterfat were the patrons not credited with, over the full delivery years affected?**

Working backward from the stated loss:

The Ashlin Chronicle (r19) reports: "The bulletin works out what the Ostrey Hollow patrons were never paid for the butter fat they actually hauled to that station, and the figure is nineteen thousand six hundred dollars ($19,600)."

To find pounds, I use Kyle's statement (r23) that "butterfat over that whole stretch averaged twenty-five cents the pound."

Pounds of butterfat: $19,600 / $0.25 per pound = 78,400 pounds.

Verification: Kyle states (r23) "forty million pounds of milk received at that station... year in and year out." If the milk averaged 3.80 true butterfat but was recorded as 3.61, the shortfall per pound of milk is (3.80 - 3.61) / 100 = 0.0019 pounds of butterfat per pound of milk. Over 40 million pounds, this is 40,000,000 × 0.0019 = 76,000 pounds.

This is close to 78,400 (the difference may reflect rounding or variation in year-to-year volumes; the sources are not perfectly consistent on this). I report both and note that the Chronicle's figure ($19,600) is from the contemporary college bulletin, while the 76,000–78,400 range comes from calculation.

**(d) What was that worth in money, and what fraction of it did the patrons actually receive?**

Total value: $19,600 (per r19, Ashlin Chronicle, based on Bulletin 471).

Amount received: $3,800 (per r22, Falke; r24, Orra; r23, Kyle; r21, Oram; r18, Duncan).

Fraction: $3,800 / $19,600 = 0.1939 or approximately 19.4%. The patrons received about one-fifth of what they were owed.

### C2. Emmet Brant's loss in 1910

**(a) How many pounds of butterfat was Emmet credited with?**

Cannot be directly determined from the sources. The question asks about "the largest patron" in 1910. Emmet Brant was the largest patron (r17, Hazel; r18, Duncan: "Emmet farmed the largest place on Hessel Bottom and hauled the most milk into the station"). However, neither his personal ledger entry for 1910 nor the station's specific record for his milk that year is reproduced in the sources.

**(b) How many pounds was he owed and not credited?**

Cannot be determined. Without Emmet's recorded butterfat in 1910, the shortfall cannot be calculated.

**(c) What was the difference worth in money that year?**

Cannot be determined for the same reason. However, from the general figures:
- Annual milk into Hollow: 1.6 million pounds (Kyle r23).
- Average butterfat: 3.61 recorded, 3.80 true.
- Shortfall per year: 1.6 × 0.0019 = 3,040 pounds (matches Teague's "station gain" r05).
- Annual loss in dollars: 3,040 × $0.25 = $760.

If Emmet hauled "the most" and was the largest patron, his share would exceed the average. But without the specific figures, I cannot give an exact answer. The sources establish that Emmet lost the most and that his careful ledger proved the scales were honest, but they do not provide his individual 1910 butterfat credit.

### C3. Over the full delivery years

**(a) How many pounds of butterfat were credited to the patrons in total?**

Kyle (r23) states: "Forty million pounds of milk received at that station... and butterfat over that whole stretch averaged twenty-five cents the pound."

If 40 million pounds of milk was received, and the recorded (short) average was 3.61 butterfat:
- Butterfat credited: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 pounds.

**(b) How many pounds actually reached the main creamery in the station's cream?**

If the true average was 3.80:
- Butterfat that actually reached Larrow Green: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 pounds.

**(c) Express the difference as a percentage—and be exact about what you are taking a percentage of.**

Difference: 1,520,000 - 1,444,000 = 76,000 pounds.

As a percentage of the butterfat that actually reached Larrow Green: 76,000 / 1,520,000 = 0.050 or 5.0%.

As a percentage of the butterfat credited to the patrons: 76,000 / 1,444,000 = 0.0526 or 5.26%.

This matches Teague's "station gain" figure of "5.25%, as usual" (r05, r23). The 5.25% difference is the proportion of butterfat that arrived at Larrow Green but was credited as arriving from the separating process at the station (the "station gain" line), not as credit to the farmers.

## Section D — Logic

### D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment

**When was it, how long did it last?**

May 8, 1911 (station's glass broke) to July 10, 1911 (borrowed glass returned; station's own glass resumed). Duration: 63 days or 9 weeks.

**What was the variable, what was held constant?**

Variable: the measuring glass (pipette). The station's short pipettes were replaced with a borrowed, presumably accurate measure.

Held constant: the herds, pasture, hay quality, season (late spring/early summer), men, machinery, testing method, acid, bottles, handler (Ansel).

**What was the result?**

During the 9-week interval, tests rose from the chronic 3.61 to approximately 3.80 (the true figure, later confirmed by Alder Corners). On July 10, when the station resumed its own glass, tests fell back to 3.61 immediately.

**What does it establish that a calculation alone would not?**

It establishes **causation, not just correlation.** A calculation comparing recorded tests to milk weights proves something was wrong, but it cannot isolate the cause. The 1911 interval proves that the glass—not the milk, not the tester, not the farms—was the sole variable that changed. It is experimental evidence: all else equal, change only the glass, and the results change. This is more powerful than any statistical analysis.

**What does it NOT establish?**

It does not establish the magnitude of the defect in the original glass (only that it was short). It does not establish why the defect occurred (manufacturing error, wear, or degradation). It does not establish how long the original glass had been in use before becoming defective (it may have degraded over time, or been defective from the start). Critically, no one at the time drew any inference from this interval, so it did not influence the 1925 hearing; its power was only realized in 1958.

---

### D2. Three independent reasons why the tester cannot have been stealing cream

**Reason 1: The 1911 interval (independent of 1958 measurement).** When the tester (Ansel) used a borrowed glass for 9 weeks, tests rose to 3.80. The same tester, under no different oversight or control, made tests with different glassware and got different results. If Ansel had been skimming cream, his methods and opportunity did not change between May 8 and July 10, 1911. Yet the results improved 0.19 points. This proves that if he was stealing, the amount he stole tracked perfectly with the glass change—an implausibly tight correlation. More simply, it proves the tester's integrity was not the variable. This reason holds even if the glassware is never measured in 1958.

**Reason 2: The station gain ledger entries.** Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper (r05, r13), recorded that cream arriving at Larrow Green consistently weighed more butterfat than the station test had credited. This is the opposite of theft: if Ansel were stealing, the cream arriving would weigh *less*. Instead, it arrived *heavier*, meaning the station test was short, not the farmer's milk. Teague's ledger entries (r23: "5.25%, as usual") show this pattern year after year. These entries are independent of the 1958 measurement; they are contemporaneous records from the office books.

**Reason 3: Independent third-party testing.** Rosalie Cudd (r10), manager of the Alder Corners cheese factory across the ridge, tested milk from four Hessel Bottom farmers during summers 1915–1930 and consistently recorded 3.80 butterfat. These farmers also hauled to Ostrey Hollow station, where the same milk tested 3.61. Cudd had no stake in Ostrey Hollow; she was across the county line. Her independent testing proves the milk itself was sound and averaged 3.80 true butterfat. This is a third-party external check, entirely independent of Ansel or the station's records.

**Why they are independent of one another:** Reason 1 is an internal station experiment (borrowed glass vs. own glass). Reason 2 is an office ledger observation (cream arrival vs. test credit). Reason 3 is an external independent test (a different factory, different management, different geographic jurisdiction). No single narrator appears in all three; no single document supports all three. They converge on the same conclusion from separate angles.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question these entries?** No. Teague, who made the entries (r23, Kyle: "Mr. Wilbur Teague was the bookkeeper and wrote those station lines every year"), "never asked anybody anything in forty years" (r23). His notion of the position was: "the figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people, and if the other people did not ask it was not his affair" (r23). He wrote the 5.25% gain every year and signed off. This is why the hidden truth lay in plain sight: no one, not even the bookkeeper recording the evidence, thought to ask what the numbers meant.

---

### D3. Did the manager know the tests ran low before 1923?

**Answer:** Selby Vose likely knew or should have known that the Hollow's tests ran low, but the sources do not provide definitive proof that he consciously understood the problem or its cause.

**Justification:**

Selby Vose became manager in 1911 (r23, Kyle). He signed the ledger sheets every year carrying the "station gain" line: 5.25%, "as usual," every year for a quarter-century (r23, Kyle). Kyle observed that Vose "would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign. I watched him do it a thousand times" (r23). Vose read only totals, not the detail.

In 1919, Vose signed and sent the circular accusing Hollow patrons of adding water (r24, Orra; r23, Kyle). This was an active claim about the patrons' fraud. If Vose had read the detail of the ledger—the "station gain" line—he would have seen that cream arrived *heavier* than credited, contradicting a theft theory. But Kyle explicitly states Vose "never read the lines above" the total (r23). Thus, Vose may have issued the water-adding accusation without having examined the very ledger entry that refuted it.

The 1911 interval: Did Vose notice the test improvement when the glass was borrowed? The sources do not say. Vose was manager then, so the change would have been in his records. But no source records him asking Ansel about it or investigating.

The 1923 condemnation: When Strawn condemned the glass in June 1923, Vose was the manager who received the news (r22, Falke; r23, Kyle). Vose ordered new glass immediately. Kyle notes this was treated like "a broken window"—a matter-of-fact repair, not a moment of revelation. If Vose had reviewed the ledger and considered the 26-year history of the 0.19 deficit, he might have connected the glass condemnation to the chronic low tests. But there is no evidence he did so.

**Conclusion:** Vose signed the evidence annually but did not read it. He issued the water accusation in 1919 without apparent awareness that his own ledger contradicted it. In 1923, when the glass was condemned, he treated it as routine maintenance. The sources support that Vose *had access* to the information that the tests ran low (it was in his ledger) but do not establish that he *understood* the problem or thought to investigate. His negligence—signing without reading—is clear; his knowledge is not.

## Section F — Theory

### F1. The causal theory of the Ostrey Hollow shortfall

**The unified theory:**

From May 1, 1897, the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association installed a testing outfit purchased from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet. The outfit included six dozen milk test pipettes (72 total), each marked 17.6 cubic centimeters on the stem. Unknown to anyone, the mold used to blow these pipettes was defective or ran systematically short; the pipettes delivered an average of 16.27 cc instead of 17.6 cc—a shortfall of 1.33 cc per pipette, or about 7.6%.

Because the test bottle's graduated neck was marked for a full 17.6 cc charge, a short charge of 16.27 cc resulted in a test reading 95% of the true butterfat content (3.61 recorded when true was 3.80). This systematic error propagated through every test made with these pipettes from May 1897 until June 12, 1923, when state inspector Merle Strawn condemned the lot.

**Why that particular size?** The defect was inherent to the mold. Junia Ammon (r20) theorizes: "The mold had been reground and the lot ran short. It was an accident and nobody in the works caught it. A gauge sits on a nail and gets knocked off the nail and hung back on it; a mold comes back from the shop a hair different and looks exactly as it looked before; a boy checks the first of a lot and the tenth and the fiftieth and finds them consistent, because they are consistently wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind cannot find."

**Why it began when it did:** The defect was present in the very first lot, installed May 1, 1897. It did not develop gradually; it was a manufacturing defect from the start.

**Why it ended when it did:** On June 12, 1923, Strawn's burette test exposed the defect. New glass was ordered and arrived within the month. From July 1923, the station used correct pipettes, and tests rose from 3.61 to 3.85—no change to the farms, cows, hay, or tester, only a change to the glassware.

**Why nothing on the station or farms had to change for either:** The defect was not a property of the milk, the tester's honesty, the farms' practices (wet hay, water-adding), or the station's management. It was a property of a piece of glassware. The shortfall persisted identically regardless of season, weather, pasture quality, or practice—because the cause was an inanimate object that never changed until it was replaced.

---

**The three competing explanations and the evidence that defeats each:**

**1. Water-adding (advanced by Selby Vose, 1919, and argued by an Association officer at the 1925 hearing):**

Specific evidence defeating it: Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector for the Ordell circuit, tested Hollow milk with a lactometer (the standard test for added water) at every circuit visit from 1908 forward and never once found added water (r22, Falke). This is independent, third-party testing by a man with no stake in the outcome. Additionally, Rosalie Cudd (r10), testing milk from Hessel Bottom farmers at her Alder Corners cheese factory, found consistent 3.80 butterfat over 16 summers, never showing signs of watering. Water-adding would have left chemical traces; none were found.

**2. Cream theft by Ansel Keddie (Wilbur Teague's theory and urged at the 1925 hearing):**

Specific evidence defeating it: (a) The station gain ledger entries (r05, r23) show that cream arriving at Larrow Green consistently weighed *more* butterfat than the station test had credited. If Ansel were stealing, the cream would arrive weighing *less*. The direction is opposite to theft. (b) The 1911 interval (May 8 – July 10): the same tester using a borrowed glass produced tests 0.19 points higher (3.80 instead of 3.61) for nine weeks, then tests fell back when his own glass was resumed. The tester's methods did not change; only the instrument changed. If theft was the cause, the amount stolen would have had to track perfectly with the glass change—an implausible coincidence. (c) Rosalie Cudd's independent testing confirmed the milk was sound (3.80), meaning nothing was removed en route from farm to test.

**3. Wet hay of Hessel Bottom (advanced by the 1908 committee, Grigg, and Kepp):**

Specific evidence defeating it: State College Bulletin 214 (fed to Falke in 1959, after his report was in type; r22 note added) established that bog hay depressed butterfat by "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Five hundredths (0.05 points) is far insufficient to explain the 0.19-point gap in July (summer), when hay's effect is zero. Falke withdrew his earlier concession to the wet-hay theory: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July. I withdraw the concession made on page four" (r22).

---

**Did the 1925 board of arbitration ever examine the condemned glassware?**

No. The condemned glass sat in a crate in the loft over the weigh room, where two of the three arbitration sessions were held. Cleve Oram (r21) notes: "the crate was up the ladder over their heads the entire time. Twenty feet, if that." The board had access to the glass but never climbed the ladder. Bertram Kyle (r23) carried four books to the board (station test, receiving, Brant weigh, Strawn's notebook) but does not mention the glass. No source records the board examining or testing the pipettes.

---

**What did Ansel Keddie himself believe the cause was?**

Ansel suspected the glass. In summer 1911, when the station's glass broke and was replaced with a borrowed measure, the tests rose. Ansel came home and said to Duncan: "it was something in the glass" (r18, Duncan). Duncan recalled: "He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove, and I have thought since that this was the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had. He had, after all, nothing to hold in his hand but a suspicion about a piece of glass."

Ansel made a question mark in the test book but did not commit the thought to paper as a formal finding. He never publicly advanced the theory. He testified at the 1925 hearing but the record does not indicate he raised the glass theory (though he may have, as the detailed hearing transcript is not in the sources). After dismissal, he did not campaign to clear his name. He took up carpentry. Orra's diary (r24) records that he once said to her: "A man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."

Ansel's suspicion was sound, his silence honorable, and his vindication delayed 24 years.

## Section G — Summary

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, founded in 1889, established an Ostrey Hollow skimming station in 1897 to serve six farms on Hessel Bottom. The station's tester, Ansel Keddie, consistently recorded milk butterfat at 3.61 percent—0.19 points lower than the same milk tested at the Alder Corners cheese factory across the ridge. For 26 years, three theories circulated: patrons were adding water; the tester was stealing cream; or the bottom's wet hay depressed butterfat. In 1925, after mounting complaints, an arbitration board awarded patrons $3,800 without finding a cause and dismissed Ansel.

The truth lay unexamined for 33 years: the station's original pipettes (purchased in 1897 for $77.93) averaged 16.27 cc instead of their marked 17.6 cc. This 5% shortfall meant every test read 95% of truth. When state inspector Merle Strawn condemned the glass in June 1923, no one connected the 26-year deficit to the condemned pipettes. The condemned crate sat in the station's loft, untouched, through the hearing that concluded thirty feet below it.

In 1958, Ansel's granddaughter Ivy Keddie, a college student, climbed to the loft, measured the pipettes, and proved the glass was the sole cause. The patrons had actually produced 78,400 pounds of butterfat worth $19,600 but received only $3,800—about one-fifth of their due. Ansel Keddie died in 1934 without vindication. His name was cleared 24 years after his death. The eight stories—founding, deficit, theories, critical interval, condemnation, hearing, aftermath, and records—converge on a single fact: an instrument mattered more than justice, but history ultimately prevailed.
