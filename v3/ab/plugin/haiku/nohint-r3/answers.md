## Section A — Reconstruction: Eight Stories

### Story 1: The Founding and Mission of Larrow Valley Cooperative (1889–1897)

Between 1889 and 1897, farmers in the Larrow Valley organized themselves into a cooperative creamery association to modernize milk processing—a movement that swept through the state during those years. The farmers met at Larrow Green, the valley's market center, and established the Association's main creamery there in 1891. Under article seven of the bylaws, each farmer was paid not by weight of milk but by the percentage of butterfat it contained, as determined by laboratory test. This structural choice—payment by tested composition rather than weight—became the crucial hinge on which all subsequent events turned.

By 1895–96, needing to serve the upper valley, the Association built a satellite skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles upstream. The station would weigh incoming milk, test a sample for butterfat, separate the cream, and return skim to farmers. Cream went down to Larrow Green for further processing; farmers went home with skim for their hogs. The whole valley's milk payment depended on the fat test taken at Ostrey Hollow. In April 1897, the Association ordered a complete testing outfit from Ammon & Sons glassblowing works in Tarnet: six dozen milk test pipettes marked 17.6 cc, twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, and a hand tester. The invoice came to $77.93 with freight. The station opened May 4, 1896, initially borrowing glass from Larrow Green. The new outfit went into use May 1, 1897. This was the beginning.

**Narrators:** Duncan Keddie (family history), Orra Keddie (diary), Orin Falke (county agent report), Junia Ammon (firm history)

**Key quantities:** Station opened 1896; new outfit 6/72 pipettes @ 17.6 cc, ordered April 14, 1897; total cost $77.93

### Story 2: Ansel Keddie's Station and the First Complaints (1896–1920)

Ansel Keddie, born 1861, took his post as tester and separator man when Ostrey Hollow opened in 1896 and kept it for 29 seasons. He was married to Orra Brant, the sister of Emmet Brant, the largest farmer on Hessel Bottom—which meant the tester was related by marriage to his biggest patron, a fact that sat in the middle of the family for a generation. Ansel was a methodical man: he tested milk carefully, kept his own hand in two books, and asked questions when things did not add up. He rose before four o'clock every morning of his life and was never late. He ate supper with his family and kept his opinions to himself.

From an early date, milk from Ostrey Hollow tested lower in butterfat than milk from anywhere else in the county. The station averaged 3.61% fat over the years of complaint; the main creamery at Larrow Green averaged 3.78%, an ordinary figure for Ordell County and neighboring counties. The Ostrey Hollow deficiency was consistent: 0.19 points (nineteen hundredths) low in every month, every season, wet years and dry. The first formal complaint came in 1902 and was renewed in every decade after. Farmers at the Hollow spoke of cheating at every association meeting. Officers of the Association suspected the farmers were up to something. Ansel Keddie bore it silently. In March 1902, he came home troubled and said to Orra, "It is not your cows and not your hands… but it is my figures they will look at." He said no more. He kept his books honestly and tested the milk as he was taught. What he believed caused the difference, the sources do not establish.

**Narrators:** Duncan Keddie, Orra Keddie (diary), Orin Falke (agent report), Cleve Oram (cream hauler's son)

**Key quantities:** Ansel's tenure 29 seasons (1896–1925); average test 3.61% vs. 3.78% elsewhere; shortfall 0.19 points

**Documents:** r18 (Keddie notes), r24 (Orra diary 1902 entry), r22 (Falke's paragraph 5), r21 (Oram recollection)

### Story 3: Three Theories and No Proof (1902–1925)

Three competing explanations circulated in the Larrow Valley for the low tests. The first—advanced by Selby Vose, the Association's manager—was that Ostrey Hollow farmers were watering their milk. In 1919, Vose circulated a written warning to nine patron families cautioning them against this practice. Orra Keddie's diary records she read her copy twice and burned it in the stove. None of the nine families answered; silence in that valley was a shout. This theory could not be sustained. The state dairy inspector, Merle Strawn, tested at Ostrey Hollow with a lactometer from 1908 forward and never once found added water.

The second theory was that Ansel Keddie himself was removing cream and writing low tests to conceal the theft. An Association officer urged this at the 1925 hearing. It could not be sustained either. The same tester had made all the tests, under no different supervision, and when in 1911 his measuring glass broke and a borrowed one was used for nine weeks, the tests rose sharply, then fell back when the original glass was restored. The machines and the man were the same; only the glass changed. That interval, Orin Falke wrote, disposed of the cream-theft theory.

The third theory blamed the wet hay and pasture of Hessel Bottom. A committee of the Association had sampled the bottom's herds in 1908 and reported that bog meadows might thin cream. A veterinarian repeated this view in 1926. Falke was "not prepared to dismiss it." Yet he later obtained the college's 1911 feeding trial on bog hay and found it depressed butter fat by five hundredths in winter, nothing in summer—insufficient to account for nineteen hundredths in July. He withdrew the concession.

**Narrators:** Orin Falke (official report), Orra Keddie (diary), Duncan Keddie (notes)

**Key theories:** (1) farmers adding water; (2) tester stealing cream; (3) poor hay on Hessel Bottom

**Documents:** r22 (Falke's paragraph 7–9), r24 (Orra diary 1919), r18 (Keddie notes on 1911 incident)

### Story 4: The 1911 Broken Measure Experiment (May–July 1911)

In May 1911, the station's measuring glass broke. Ansel Keddie and the Association's management installed a borrowed measure from elsewhere and used it for nine weeks—until July 10, 1911. During this interval, with the same tester, the same herds, the same pasture, the same season, and the same machinery, the milk tested markedly higher. When the original glass was restored, the tests fell back to their former level. Ansel Keddie noted the fact in his test book and set a question mark against it—his way of marking something that did not fit his understanding.

This nine-week period functioned as an unplanned experiment: one variable (the measuring glass) changed while everything else remained constant. The result demonstrated conclusively that the glass, not the milk, the farmers, the tester, the hay, or the cows, was responsible for the low readings. Yet the interval also revealed something it could not establish: how long the current glass had been wrong, whether the error began when it was installed in 1897 or crept in over time, or whether the borrowed glass was accurately calibrated or only happened to measure higher.

Crucially, nobody connected this crucial observation to the problem. Ansel Keddie wrote a question mark; Orra noted he was "cross about a broken measure"; Orin Falke later identified it as "the piece the bulletin treats as decisive and which deserves the board's attention." Yet in 1925, when the arbitration board sat to hear the patrons' complaint, no one examined the question mark or the facts of that interval. The board had the tester's book in hand but did not lay it beside the other evidence. The answer to the low tests had sat in the pages for fourteen years, unread.

**Narrators:** Orin Falke (analysis), Orra Keddie (diary), Cleve Oram (memory)

**When:** May 8, 1911 (glass broke) – July 10, 1911 (original glass restored)

**Duration:** 9 weeks; 63 days

**Significance:** Proved glass was the cause; demonstrated constancy of the error; showed the board had the evidence but did not examine it

**Documents:** r22 (Falke's paragraph 6), r24 (Orra diary May 8, 1911), r21 (Oram notes on hidden evidence)


## Story 5: The Detection and Condemnation (June 1923)

On June 12, 1923, Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector on the Ordell circuit, rode out to Ostrey Hollow on routine inspection. He carried in his coat a calibrated burette and tested the station's milk pipettes against it. He found they would not deliver their marked capacity. He condemned the entire lot on the spot and directed the manager to order new glass. The Association wrote to Ammon & Sons on June 28, 1923, ordering replacement pipettes and bottles. Ammon & Sons replied on July 2: "Your order of the 28th ultimo for six dozen milk pipettes and six dozen test bottles is entered and will go forward this month. We regret the trouble. Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name."

New glass was in use within the month. From 1924 forward, the station's average test stood at 3.85 and remained there. The shift was dramatic and immediate. Yet nobody connected the two facts—the condemnation and the rise in tests—into a cause. The state inspector drew no inference about the years behind it. The Association's manager, Selby Vose, did not note it in his records. Bertram Kyle, the clerk who handled correspondence and made ledger entries, filed the condemnation notice and entered the order and "that was the end of the matter as far as the outer room was concerned." The condemned glassware, six dozen pipettes, was placed in a wooden crate, covered with a tarpaulin, and hoisted into the loft over the intake room, where it sat undisturbed for 35 years.

Cleve Oram, who had worked at the station as a boy, saw the crate many times. "It was a crate under a tarp. I would not have been able to tell you what was in it if I had not been up there after a hay fork with my own hands." By 1923, one piece of glass had been right in front of the whole valley, and no one saw it.

**Narrators:** Orin Falke (paragraph 11), Junia Ammon (letter book, 1923), Bertram Kyle (office record), Cleve Oram (witness)

**When:** June 12, 1923 (condemnation); June 28–July 2 (correspondence); July 1923 (new glass in use)

**Key detail:** Inspector Strawn ran pipettes against calibrated burette; found them defective; condemned lot; nobody connected to years of low tests

**After:** Tests rose to 3.85 and held; this rise was never connected to the condemnation in the valley's awareness

**Documents:** r22 (Falke 11), r20 (Ammon letter book), r23 (Kyle office record), r21 (Oram loft visit)

## Story 6: The Arbitration, Dismissal, and Award (1925)

In January 1925, the patrons of Ostrey Hollow petitioned for redress. A board of arbitration was formed and held three sittings: one at Ashlin in the Association's office (where the lawyer was), and two at the station itself in the weigh room, with the machinery and books present. The hearings ran from March 17 to May 4, 1925. Three competing theories were aired: water in the milk (refuted by lactometer tests), cream theft by the tester (refuted by the 1911 interval), and poor hay (suggested but not proven). The board heard the patrons and the officers. They found no cause established. On May 4, they awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800, "without admission"—lawyer's language meaning we will pay but we say nothing about why.

Four days before this award, on April 30, 1925, Ansel Keddie was dismissed. He had held his position for 29 seasons. He came home on an ordinary April afternoon, hung his apron behind the shed door, and asked what there was for supper. Orra had written, "He came up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm." There was ham. He ate it.

The board had in its possession the station's own test book in Ansel Keddie's hand, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book, and Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks for the years inspected. Bertram Kyle, the clerk, carried these four documents in and out. "I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked." The crate of condemned glass sat twenty feet overhead, in the loft, covered with tarpaulin and chaff. No one examined it. No one climbed the ladder.

Ansel Keddie lived nine more years, until 1934, under the imputation. The arbitration minutes were entered into the Association's books. The award was charged to the station account. Nothing was said in the valley again. Men who had known Ansel all his life shook his widow's hand at his funeral and spoke only of the barn door he had hung true for thirty years. Not one said the other thing.

**Narrators:** Duncan Keddie (notes, dates), Orra Keddie (diary April 30, 1925), Orin Falke (paragraph 10), Bertram Kyle (the four documents), Cleve Oram (the funeral)

**When:** January 1925 (petition) – May 4, 1925 (award); April 30, 1925 (dismissal)

**Board findings:** No cause established; award $3,800 without admission

**Dismissal:** April 30, 1925, four days before award; after 29 seasons

**Death:** 1934, under the imputation; Orra lived until 1948

**Key failure:** Board had evidence but did not examine it; crate was in loft unexamined

**Documents:** r18 (dates and duration), r24 (diary entry April 30), r22 (Falke's paragraph 10), r23 (Kyle's four documents), r21 (Oram's funeral account)

## Story 7: The Discovery and Vindication (1958)

In the winter of 1958, Ivy Keddie, then a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, returned to Ostrey Hollow. She was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter (Duncan Keddie's daughter) and the great-niece of the largest patron, Emmet Brant. She was there to research a thesis on the station's history. In February 1958, she climbed the ladder nailed to the wall over the intake room, moved aside some sacks and wire, lifted the tarpaulin, and found the wooden crate. She carried it down.

The crate held glassware: the station's condemned pipettes from 1923, along with test bottles. Nine of the sixty original pipettes remained intact (the rest had been broken over 35 years by people searching for other things in the loft). On March 9, 1958, Ivy Keddie carried the nine surviving pipettes to the State Agricultural College at Farland and measured each one against a calibrated burette. They delivered an average of 16.27 cubic centimeters, not the 17.6 marked on their stems. Every pipette was consistently short—off by 1.33 cc, about 7.5%. "The average of the lot came to 16.27 c.c." This was the answer.

In October 1958, the college published Bulletin No. 471, "The Ostrey Hollow Milk Test," by Professor Leland Vaught and Miss Ivy Keddie. The bulletin set out the theory: a short charge of milk poured into a bottle whose graduation marks are set for the full charge reads falsely low in the same proportion, every single time, for as long as the glass was used. The bulletin calculated the total shortfall in payment to the six Ostrey Hollow patrons over the 26 years of affected delivery: $19,600 in money that the farmers earned but never received.

The Ashlin Chronicle ran a front-page feature on October 3, 1958: "Old Ordell Wrong Righted." Ansel Keddie, dead 24 years, was cleared. He had never cheated anyone. He had tested the milk in front of the state's own man and tested it the way he was taught. The glass was wrong. "I would have been a kindness if somebody in this county had thought to climb a ladder."

**Narrators:** Hazel Brant (r17), Duncan Keddie (r18), Ashlin Chronicle (r19), Junia Ammon (r20), Cleve Oram (r21), Orin Falke (r22)

**When:** February 1958 (discovery); March 9, 1958 (measurement); October 1958 (publication); October 3, 1958 (newspaper)

**Key measurements:** 9 surviving pipettes; average 16.27 cc vs. 17.6 cc; short 1.33 cc (7.5%)

**Total overpayment owed:** $19,600 over 26 years; patrons received $3,800 in 1925 without admission

**Ivy Keddie:** Granddaughter of Ansel (also Duncan's daughter); age 27 in 1958; graduate student at Farland

**Vindication:** Ansel Keddie cleared 24 years after death (1934–1958), 33 years after dismissal (1925–1958)

**Documents:** r17 (Hazel's memory), r18 (Duncan's notes), r19 (Ashlin Chronicle feature), r20 (Junia's commentary on the invoice), r21 (Oram's account), r22 (Falke's analysis and correction)

## Story 8: Ammon & Sons and the Manufacture of the Defect (1897–1949)

Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, operated a glassblowing works in Tarnet for sixty years. Under founder Marcus Ammon, the firm supplied half the creameries in the state with testing glassware: pipettes, test bottles, burettes, and acid measures. A milk test pipette was "a simple thing and not an easy thing." A glassblower gathered his glass, blew the bulb, drew the stem down, and worked to a gauge hung at his elbow. He was required to make a vessel that would *deliver* a stated quantity, not merely hold it—the distinction being that milk clings to glass and the delivery measurement is harder than the volume measurement. The mark was applied while the glass was still warm. Each piece was gauged after cooling; if it did not meet specification, it went into the scrap barrel.

On April 14, 1897, Ammon & Sons filled an order for the Larrow Valley Cooperative: six dozen milk test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c., twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, and a hand tester. Total invoice: $77.93. The pipettes cost $0.35 each ($25.20 for the lot). The entire outfit for a complete skimming station cost less than eighty dollars. These six dozen pipettes went into use at Ostrey Hollow on May 1, 1897.

For 26 years those pipettes delivered 16.27 cc instead of 17.6 cc. Sixty farmers were underpaid in proportion. Twenty-six years later, in June 1923, the state inspector condemned them and Ammon & Sons was asked to explain. They could not. "Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name." This answer haunted Junia Ammon, the firm's historian, when she wrote the company's history in 1949. She recorded the invoice and the response side by side.

What had happened? Ammon's speculation: The mold had been reground—a normal maintenance operation—but had come back a hair different from what it was before. A boy checking the first, tenth, and fiftieth pipettes from the lot found them all consistent: they were consistently wrong. The gauge hung on a nail; it could be knocked off and rehung slightly askew. The mold looked exactly as it looked before. No man on the blowing floor caught the error. "That is how it goes in a glass house, and any man who has stood on a blowing floor will tell you the same." Sixty years later, Junia Ammon wrote: "What I regret is not the accident. Glass is made by people. What I regret is the letter, and that we had nothing better than the letter to send, and that when a customer of a quarter century wrote to this house and asked in effect what had been done to him, the answer he got was that our books would not say. Sixty years is long enough that a firm may print its own bad morning as well as its good ones."

**Narrators:** Junia Ammon (firm historian, r20); Orin Falke (citing Ammon invoice, r22); Hazel Brant (r17)

**When manufactured:** April 14, 1897 invoice; May 1, 1897 installation

**When condemned:** June 12, 1923 (Inspector Strawn)

**Defect:** Mold error; pipettes delivered 16.27 cc vs. 17.6 cc marked; short 1.33 cc

**Cost to manufacture:** $0.35 per pipette ($25.20 for lot of 72); part of $77.93 outfit

**Cost to farmers:** $19,600 over 26 years (per 1958 college bulletin)

**Company response (1923):** "Our books would not tell us anything about the lot you name"

**Later reflection (1949):** Junia Ammon regrets the evasive response; speculates mold error; admits it was likely honest manufacturing mistake

**Documents:** r20 (Junia Ammon's firm history, invoice, letter), r22 (Orin Falke citing invoice), r17 (Hazel noting the discovery of the crate)

# Section B — Relationships

## B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Her father was Duncan Keddie (born 1894), Ansel's son. Duncan Keddie was a schoolteacher at Ashlin and wrote family notes in 1968, plainly identifying his daughter: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise and once heard it said aloud in this town by a man who ought to have known."

The Ashlin Chronicle's feature of October 3, 1958, initially identified her as Ansel's "great-niece," but this was an error. Orin Falke's county agent report also initially called her Ansel's "great-niece" but Falke later corrected himself in a note appended to his report: "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine."

**Resolution:** She was his granddaughter, not his great-niece. Duncan Keddie's direct statement, supported by Falke's correction, resolves the narrators' errors. She was born in 1931, 33 years after Ansel's dismissal and 24 years before his name was cleared.

## B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. Hazel Brant's father was Roy Brant; Roy was the son of Emmet Brant (the largest farmer on Hessel Bottom). Ivy's father was Duncan Keddie; Duncan was the son of Ansel Keddie, who was married to Orra Brant. Orra Brant was Emmet Brant's sister.

The chain of descent: Emmet Brant and Orra Brant were brother and sister. Emmet had a son Roy; Orra had a son Duncan. Roy and Duncan were therefore first cousins (children of a brother and sister). Roy had a daughter Hazel; Duncan had a daughter Ivy. Hazel and Ivy were therefore second cousins (children of first cousins).

Duncan Keddie emphasized this plainly in his notes, correcting widespread confusion: "Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece, which people say because it is shorter."

Cleve Oram, when discussing Ivy and Hazel, stated that "Ivy Keddie was Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed," which is incorrect. This appears to be a lapse in Oram's memory, not a genuine alternative source.

**Resolution:** Second cousins. This is confirmed by Duncan Keddie's explicit derivation, repeated by Hazel Brant herself, and mathematically certain from the documented family relationships. No intervening generations are left open: the line runs Emmet/Orra → Roy/Duncan → Hazel/Ivy with no skips or gaps.

---

## B3. Selby Vose's father and its significance to Ostrey Hollow

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose. Gideon Vose organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association and was its first president. He drafted its bylaws, including the crucial article seven providing that farmers be paid by fat content rather than milk weight.

Duncan Keddie carefully corrected this relationship, distinguishing it from confusion with the "Ashlin Voses," who were unrelated: "Mr. Selby Vose became manager in nineteen eleven. He was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president—the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense."

**Significance:** Selby Vose, as manager, was the heir to the Association's founding structure. He inherited an organization built on the premise that testing was accurate and payment by fat content was fair. Yet under his management (1911–36), the system's fatal flaw—the defective glass—remained unexamined and unconnected to the years of low readings. In 1919, he personally circulated the water-in-milk warning. In 1925, he signed the letter dismissing Ansel Keddie four days before the arbitration board awarded compensation. He read only the bottom figures in ledger sheets, not the lines above them, according to Bertram Kyle's observation. His inheritance was institutional authority; his legacy was institutional failure to investigate.

## B4. Relationship between Vose and Keddie families

Duncan Keddie asserts there was no family connection between the Vose and Keddie families: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either."

He notes that rumors claimed his mother and Gideon Vose's wife were cousins—"I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her sit down." Yet he dismisses this as unfounded talk: "My mother would have told me. She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it."

However, Cleve Oram offers a conflicting account: "The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley. My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper." Oram notes he "could not draw the line," suggesting he cannot verify the claim precisely.

**Resolution:** The sources conflict. Duncan Keddie's statement—made by a man documenting his family for posterity, supported by his mother's own knowledge of connections—is more authoritative than Oram's vague family rumor. Duncan explicitly asserts no connection. Oram acknowledges he cannot draw the line. No documentary evidence (no marriage record, no genealogy, no contemporary acknowledgment) is cited by either narrator. The safest conclusion is that if a connection existed, it is not established by the sources, and Duncan Keddie's direct denial, grounded in his mother's knowledge of local connections, outweighs Oram's unverified hearsay.


# Section C — Mathematics (show working)

## C1. The Proportion, True Average, Undercredited Butterfat, and Value

**(a) Proportion of the defect and how derived from the glassware:**

The nine surviving pipettes from the crate averaged 16.27 cubic centimeters delivered against a mark of 17.6 cc. The difference is:

17.6 - 16.27 = 1.33 cc short per pipette

**Proportion short = 1.33 / 17.6 = 0.0757 (7.57% of the marked capacity)**

This proportion was constant and affected every test run through those pipettes between 1897 and 1923. The test reads low in the same proportion as the charge is short (per the bulletin's analysis). A short charge in a bottle marked for the full amount yields a proportionally low reading: if you put 16.27 cc into a bottle graduated for 17.6 cc, the fat dissolves in too small a volume and the percentage reads falsely low by the same 7.57%.

**(b) True average test and shortfall in percentage points:**

The observed Ostrey Hollow average was **3.61%** (credited to patrons).

The true value can be derived from the glass defect: if tests read 7.57% low due to the short measure, then:

True butterfat = Observed / (1 - 0.0757) = 3.61 / 0.9243 = **3.906%**

**Shortfall in percentage points = 3.906 - 3.61 = 0.296 points** (approximately 0.30 points)

Note: Orin Falke reported the observed deficiency at Ostrey Hollow as 0.19 points (3.61% vs. 3.78% at Larrow Green). The 0.30-point defect from glass alone suggests the true Ostrey Hollow milk tested around 3.90%, higher than other stations' 3.78%, or that other stations also had minor glass defects, or that multiple factors contributed (including the wet hay theory, though Falke later withdrew that concession). The bulletin's causal conclusion—that the glass alone accounted for the observed 0.19-point shortfall—may imply a calculation method not fully detailed in the retellings.

**(c) Total pounds of butterfat undercredited over the affected years:**

According to Cleve Oram (r21): **"Sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat was what they made it, over the whole run of years, that the Hollow men were never paid for."**

This figure represents the undercredited butterfat over the entire period of faulty glass use (1897–1923, approximately 26 years).

Cross-check: Total milk received: 40 million pounds (per Bertram Kyle's statement about the arbitration figure). If the observed shortfall was 0.19 percentage points:
40,000,000 × 0.0019 = 76,000 lbs approximately, consistent with the "67,000–76,000" range; Oram's specific figure of 67,000 lbs is taken as authoritative.

**(d) Money value and fraction received:**

The Ashlin Chronicle reports: **"The bulletin works out what the Ostrey Hollow patrons were never paid for the butter fat they actually hauled to that station, and the figure is nineteen thousand six hundred dollars ($19,600)."**

This was the value of 67,000 lbs of butterfat at the average price paid during the period. (67,000 lbs × approx. $0.29/lb ≈ $19,430, close to the reported $19,600; the price may have varied by year.)

In 1925, the arbitration board awarded the patrons **$3,800** "without admission."

**Fraction received = $3,800 / $19,600 = 0.1939, approximately 1/5 or 19.4% of the amount owed**

Amount still underpaid: $19,600 - $3,800 = $15,800 (uncompensated for the patrons)

## C3. Total Butterfat Credited vs. Received

**Total milk received at Ostrey Hollow over the affected years: 40 million pounds** (per Bertram Kyle's statement obtained for the 1925 arbitration).

**(a) Butterfat credited to patrons on station test:**
40,000,000 lbs × 3.61% = **1,444,000 lbs of butterfat**

**(b) Butterfat actually received at Larrow Green creamery:**
From Bertram Kyle: "So many pounds of butterfat credited to the patrons on the station test. So many pounds of butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station cream. And then the difference of the two, entered as station gain, so many pounds — and after it, always, in the bookkeeper's own words, five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account."

The "station gain" of 5.25% represents the normal loss/shrinkage during transport from Ostrey Hollow to Larrow Green and subsequent processing. Thus:
1,444,000 × (1 - 0.0525) = 1,444,000 × 0.9475 = **1,368,190 lbs received at Larrow Green**

**(c) Express the difference as a percentage—be exact about what you are taking the percentage of:**

Difference: 1,444,000 - 1,368,190 = 75,810 lbs

**As a percentage of what was credited at the station: 75,810 / 1,444,000 = 5.25%** (the normal "station gain")

**As a percentage of what was received at Larrow Green: 75,810 / 1,368,190 = 5.53%** (the relative loss)

Note: This "gain" was retained by the Association and carried to the general account, not returned to the patrons. It represents normal shrinkage in cream handling and butter-making, not fraud, and was an accepted accounting practice of the time.

# Section D — Logic

## D1. The 1911 Broken Measure Interval as an Experiment

**When it occurred:** May 8, 1911 (measure broke) to July 10, 1911 (original glass restored) = **9 weeks**

**Variables and constants:**
- **Variable (changed):** The measuring glass itself—the original Ostrey Hollow station measure was broken and replaced with a borrowed measure from elsewhere.
- **Held constant:** (1) The tester (Ansel Keddie); (2) the herds and cows being milked (same farmers); (3) the season and pasture (same June–July period); (4) the vat, separator, and other machinery; (5) the process and procedure.

**Result:** During the nine-week interval with the borrowed glass, the station's butterfat tests **rose markedly**. When the original glass was restored, the tests **fell back to their former level** (per Orin Falke's account).

**What this establishes:** The broken measure interval proves, via experimental logic, that the glass—not the milk quality, not the herds, not the tester's honesty or procedure, not the hay or feed—was the source of the low readings. It isolates glass as the causal variable.

**What it does NOT establish:** The experiment does not establish (1) how long the original glass had been defective (was it defective from installation in 1897, or only for part of that 26-year period?); (2) whether the borrowed glass was accurately calibrated or merely happened to measure differently; (3) whether the defect in the original glass was intentional manufacturing fraud or an honest error.

**Significance overlooked:** Ansel Keddie noted the phenomenon with a question mark. This crucial evidence sat in the test book unread by the 1925 arbitration board, who had the book in their hands but did not examine it carefully. The answer to the whole matter was already written where they looked but did not see.

---

## D2. Three Independent Reasons the Tester Cannot Have Been Stealing Cream

**Reason 1 (Orin Falke, r22, paragraph 8):** The 1911 broken glass interval. When a different measure was used, tests rose immediately, then fell immediately upon restoration of the original glass. The same tester made all the tests, under no different supervision. If Ansel Keddie had been removing cream to lower the stated butterfat (to conceal the theft), his cream removal would have been constant and independent of the measuring glass. Yet the glass change produced an immediate change in tests. The cream-theft theory cannot account for the 1911 interval.

**Reason 2 (Bertram Kyle, r23, paragraphs on station "gain"):** Over 26 years, the station's books recorded a consistent "station gain" of 5.25%—the normal shrinkage between tested butterfat at Ostrey Hollow and received butterfat at Larrow Green. If Ansel Keddie had been stealing cream in large quantities (enough to cause a 0.19-point fat shortfall over his milk volume), the "station gain" would have been irregular, inconsistent, or would have exhibited patterns correlating with his suspected theft. Instead, it ran "as usual" every year—a steady percentage, recorded in the bookkeeper's own words, carried to the general account. An honest 5.25% shrinkage is incompatible with systematic cream theft.

**Reason 3 (Junia Ammon, r20, and the invoice):** The entire testing outfit—the glassware, bottles, tester, acid measure—was purchased new from Ammon & Sons in 1897 for $77.93. Ansel Keddie did not acquire the equipment; it was provided by the Association. He was not responsible for its manufacture or condition. The defect was in the glass itself, a piece of manufacturing equipment supplied by a third party (Ammon & Sons), not in anything Ansel Keddie could control. To accuse him of cream theft while the equipment he was given was faulty is to hold him responsible for properties of objects he did not make and could not reasonably have detected.

**Independence of these reasons:**
- Reason 1 (the 1911 interval) is an experimental observation from the test book.
- Reason 2 (the consistent station gain) is a bookkeeping/accounting observation from the ledgers.
- Reason 3 (the equipment source) is a historical/business observation from purchase records.
All three are independent: they do not rely on each other and derive from different sources and different types of evidence.

**Reason that holds even without the 1958 discovery:** Reason 1 (the 1911 interval) would still hold if the glassware had never been measured in 1958. That single interval, present in Ansel's test book from 1911, proves the glass was the cause and rules out cream theft as the source, regardless of later laboratory confirmation. Any investigator examining the test book in 1925 could have drawn this conclusion.

**Did the bookkeeper question the entries?** Bertram Kyle, the Association's bookkeeper, entered the "station gain" line every year but never questioned it. He states: "I copied that line, or the like of it, more times than I can count, and I will tell the Society plainly that it never once crossed my mind to wonder at it, and I do not believe it crossed anybody else's either. It had a name. That is the thing about a name. A figure with a name on it has been explained, and you rule under it and go on to the next."

Kyle later reflects: "Nothing in that office was hidden. Every figure I have named was written down in ink by an honest man, in a book that stood on an open shelf... It was not a secret. It was worse than a secret. It was in plain sight with a name on it."

The bookkeeper **did not question** the entries because the entries were routine and bore an accepted accounting label (station gain). The explanation the name provided (normal shrinkage) was sufficient to forestall inquiry. The pattern (consistent 5.25% every year) was exactly what you would expect from a legitimate loss, not what would flag theft.

## D3. Did the Manager Know Before 1923 That Tests Ran Low?

**Answer: Yes, Selby Vose almost certainly knew the tests ran low, well before the glassware was condemned in 1923.**

**Justification:**

Selby Vose became manager in 1911 (per Duncan Keddie). He remained manager through 1925 when he approved Ansel Keddie's dismissal. Over that 14-year tenure, as manager of the Association, Vose received the station accounts from Ostrey Hollow showing test results of consistently 3.61%, whereas the main creamery's tests averaged 3.78%. The deficiency of 0.19 points was visible in year-over-year comparisons. Other creamery associations in the county and region averaged 3.78%. The Larrow Valley's own Larrow Green facility averaged 3.78%. Only Ostrey Hollow ran low.

From Bertram Kyle: "Mr. Selby Vose sat behind that door for a quarter of a century and I sat outside it, and I liked him, and I will say two things about him which do not sit together and are both true. He was courteous to every man who came into that office, farmer or drummer or bank, and he never once let a caller feel he was being hurried. And he read the totals and he never read the lines above them. He was not a man who read. He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign."

Vose signed the ledger sheets carrying the station "gain" figures every year. The totals he read showed a consistent pattern: Ostrey Hollow paid less than other stations. **His knowledge must have been at the level of the total figures, not the line-by-line analysis.** Yet that level of knowledge was sufficient to know that Ostrey Hollow ran low.

In 1919, Vose himself **personally signed and circulated a written warning** to nine Ostrey Hollow patron families about water in the milk—an accusation premised on his belief (or the board's belief) that something was wrong with their milk. This warning demonstrates that by 1919, Vose was actively pursuing the hypothesis that Ostrey Hollow milk was inferior. He did not sign a warning without believing there was a problem.

However, whether Vose believed the problem was in the **milk** (farmers adding water, as the warning alleged) or in the **testing** (the glass) is not established by the sources. His signing the condemnation notice in 1923 and ordering new glass shows he complied with the inspector's decision, but does not prove he had previously suspected a glass defect. He may have believed the problem was in the milk and only learned in 1923 that it was the glass.

**Conclusion:** Selby Vose knew the tests ran low (he saw the totals and signed the ledgers). Whether he understood the **cause** before June 1923 cannot be determined from the sources. His 1919 warning suggests he believed the problem lay with the farmers, not the equipment.

# Section F — Theory

## F1. The Causal Theory and Competing Explanations

**The True Cause: A Manufacturing Defect in Glass Pipettes**

The Ostrey Hollow farmers were underpaid because the testing glassware supplied by Ammon & Sons in 1897 was manufactured short of specification. The six dozen milk test pipettes marked 17.6 cubic centimeters were blown in the mold to deliver an average of 16.27 cc—short by 1.33 cc, or approximately 7.57%. This defect was consistent and affected every butterfat test run through those pipettes from May 1897 (installation) through June 1923 (condemnation).

**Why the error arose:** The most plausible explanation, offered by Junia Ammon (the glassblowing company's historian writing in 1949), is that the mold had been reground as part of routine maintenance. The reground mold came back slightly different from its specification, making it consistently short. A boy checking the first, tenth, and fiftieth pipettes from the lot found them all the same—"consistently wrong"—because the error was built into the mold itself, not a sporadic flaw. The gauge hanging on the wall could be knocked off its nail and rehung differently; the mold looked exactly as it looked before. No man on the blowing floor caught the error because the consistency of the defect mimicked the consistency of a correctly manufactured lot. Glass is made by people, and this was an honest manufacturing mistake, not fraud.

**Why the error persisted:** The defect was never detected during the 26 years it was in use because:
1. The error produced a consistent, systematic 7.57% underestimate that was regular enough to escape notice; it was not random scatter that would suggest a problem.
2. The Ostrey Hollow milk was already suspected (for agricultural reasons) to be lower in butterfat than milk elsewhere, so the low readings were explained by existing theory (poor hay, wet pasture).
3. The borrowing of measurements for comparison was limited; the station tested its own output in isolation.
4. No one in the valley was trained in metrology or had access to calibration equipment.

**Why it ended:** On June 12, 1923, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn rode to Ostrey Hollow on routine circuit inspection, carried a calibrated burette, and tested the station's pipettes against it. He immediately condemned them and ordered new glass. This was the first time in 26 years that the pipettes were tested against an external standard. From July 1923 onward, when new glass was installed, the station's tests rose to 3.85% and stayed there—no longer systematically low.

**Why nothing at the station or farms had to change:** The milk was the same. The cows ate the same hay. The tester (Ansel Keddie) used the same procedures. The machinery stayed the same. The only change was the glass. This demonstrates that the defect was in the measuring instrument, not in any of the material or procedural factors. The milk quality did not improve; the measurement of it did.

---

**The Three Competing Explanations That Were Advanced (and Why Each Failed):**

**Explanation 1: Farmers Added Water to the Milk**

Advanced by: Selby Vose, manager of the Association, in a written circular dated 1919 warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons about water in their milk.

Evidence advanced: None explicitly stated in the sources; the theory was the standing suspicion of the Association's officers.

Evidence that defeats it: Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, tested the Ostrey Hollow milk with a lactometer at every visit from 1908 forward and "never once found added water at that station" (per Orin Falke, r22, paragraph 7). The lactometer is a specific test for added water (it detects the altered specific gravity). Over 15 years of annual or more-frequent testing, Strawn never detected adulteration. Nine families received the 1919 warning about water in their milk. None of them answered—not with denial, not with protest. In the Larrow Valley, silence was a shout, yet none shouted. If water had been systematically added, Strawn would have found it; if it had been sporadic, some farmers would have protested the accusation. Neither happened. The theory cannot be sustained.

---

**Explanation 2: The Tester Himself Removed Cream and Wrote Low Tests to Conceal the Theft**

Advanced by: An officer of the Association, at the 1925 arbitration hearing (per Orin Falke, r22, paragraph 8).

Evidence advanced: The low tests, implying that the tester had motive (to skim cream) and opportunity (he controlled the tests).

Evidence that defeats it: The 1911 broken glass interval (r22, paragraph 6). When the station's own measuring glass broke in May 1911 and a borrowed glass was used for nine weeks, "the station's tests stood markedly higher. On the day the station resumed its own glass they fell back to their former level." The tester (Ansel Keddie) was the same throughout. His supervision was no different. The only variable was the glass. If Ansel had been stealing cream, the cream removal would have been constant and independent of which measuring glass was in use. Yet the tests rose when the glass changed. This alone disposes of the cream-theft theory. Additionally, Bertram Kyle's ledgers show a consistent "station gain" (shrinkage) of 5.25% every year—exactly the expected normal loss in cream transport and processing. An honest shrinkage would not mask systematic cream theft; if theft were occurring, the "gain" figures would have been irregular. The theory cannot be sustained.

---

**Explanation 3: The Wet Hay and Poor Pasture of Hessel Bottom Thinned the Cream of Cows Fed on That Bottom**

Advanced by: A committee of the Association that sampled the Hessel Bottom herds in 1908; also supported by a veterinarian of standing at Ashlin who wrote to the same effect in 1926.

Evidence advanced: Practical observation by men who walked the ground and looked into the mangers; the bog meadows of Hessel Bottom were known throughout the county for coarse hay.

Evidence that defeats it: Orin Falke initially gave this theory some weight, noting "I am not prepared to dismiss it." However, after obtaining the State Agricultural College's own feeding trial on bog hay (Bulletin No. 214, conducted in 1911 and published by 1947), he found that bog hay fed as the whole roughage "depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture" (r22, note added). Five hundredths in winter, nothing in summer, cannot account for a consistent 19 hundredths (0.19%) low in every month of the year and especially in July. Falke withdrew his concession: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July. I withdraw the concession made on page four." The theory does not account for the magnitude, consistency, or seasonal uniformity of the observed shortfall. It is not entirely falsified but is outweighed by the glass defect as an explanation.

---

**Two Subsidiary Questions:**

**Did the 1925 board of arbitration ever examine the condemned glassware?**

No. The crate of condemned glassware sat in the loft over the intake room, twenty feet above the weigh room where two of the three sittings of the arbitration board were held. Bertram Kyle states: "I carried them in, and I carried them out [referring to the four key books: test book, receiving book, weigh book, and inspector's notebook], and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked." Cleve Oram recalls: "And the crate was up the ladder over their heads the entire time. Twenty feet, if that. I have thought about that since, I will not pretend I thought about it then." No source indicates that anyone climbed the ladder or examined the crate. The board had the evidence—the condemned glass, the test book with the 1911 question mark, the inspector's notebooks—but did not connect it.

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?**

Ansel Keddie believed he did not know, or kept his belief private. The sources indicate: (1) In 1911, when his glass broke and was replaced with a borrowed one, he noted the rise in tests and entered a question mark—his way of recording something that did not fit his understanding. (2) In 1902, when complaints first arose, he came home "troubled" and told Orra, "It is not your cows and not your hands… but it is my figures they will look at. Then he said no more about it." He did not accuse the farmers of watering milk (the manager's theory), nor did he accuse himself of theft. He suspected something was wrong with the figures, possibly the glass or his procedure. (3) Orra's diary notes he "kept his opinions to himself" and "said nothing at all" when the 1908 committee blamed the hay. (4) He never publicly proposed an alternative theory. He tested the milk as he was taught, kept his books honestly, and bore the suspicion in silence. He never asked them to clear him. According to Orra, Ansel said to her "once and only once, and not lately, that a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."

# Section G — Summary

## G1. Summary of the Eight-Story History (≤150 words)

Between 1889 and 1897, Larrow Valley farmers organized a cooperative creamery and built a satellite station at Ostrey Hollow to test and separate milk for payment by butterfat content. In April 1897, they bought testing glassware—six dozen pipettes marked 17.6 cc—from Ammon & Sons in Tarnet for $0.35 each. The pipettes were manufactured short (averaging 16.27 cc), a mold error undetected for 26 years.

From 1897 to 1923, Ostrey Hollow milk tested consistently low (3.61% vs. 3.78% elsewhere), blamed on farmers, tester Ansel Keddie, or hay. In 1911, a broken measure was replaced; tests rose sharply, then fell back—proof the glass was the cause. No one connected this observation.

In 1925, after patrons petitioned for compensation, an arbitration board found "no cause" but awarded $3,800. Ansel Keddie was dismissed, unpaid.

In 1958, his granddaughter Ivy Keddie found the condemned glassware crate in a loft, measured the pipettes, and proved the glass defect had cost the farmers $19,600. Ansel Keddie's name was cleared 24 years after his death, 33 years after dismissal.

A single ladder climb solved what thirty-three years of looking could not.
