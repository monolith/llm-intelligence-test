# Section C — Mathematics (show working)

## C1. The Proportion, True Average, Undercredited Butterfat, and Value

**(a) Proportion of the defect and how derived from the glassware:**

The nine surviving pipettes from the crate averaged 16.27 cubic centimeters delivered against a mark of 17.6 cc. The difference is:

17.6 - 16.27 = 1.33 cc short per pipette

**Proportion short = 1.33 / 17.6 = 0.0757 (7.57% of the marked capacity)**

This proportion was constant and affected every test run through those pipettes between 1897 and 1923. The test reads low in the same proportion as the charge is short (per the bulletin's analysis). A short charge in a bottle marked for the full amount yields a proportionally low reading: if you put 16.27 cc into a bottle graduated for 17.6 cc, the fat dissolves in too small a volume and the percentage reads falsely low by the same 7.57%.

**(b) True average test and shortfall in percentage points:**

The observed Ostrey Hollow average was **3.61%** (credited to patrons).

The true value can be derived from the glass defect: if tests read 7.57% low due to the short measure, then:

True butterfat = Observed / (1 - 0.0757) = 3.61 / 0.9243 = **3.906%**

**Shortfall in percentage points = 3.906 - 3.61 = 0.296 points** (approximately 0.30 points)

Note: Orin Falke reported the observed deficiency at Ostrey Hollow as 0.19 points (3.61% vs. 3.78% at Larrow Green). The 0.30-point defect from glass alone suggests the true Ostrey Hollow milk tested around 3.90%, higher than other stations' 3.78%, or that other stations also had minor glass defects, or that multiple factors contributed (including the wet hay theory, though Falke later withdrew that concession). The bulletin's causal conclusion—that the glass alone accounted for the observed 0.19-point shortfall—may imply a calculation method not fully detailed in the retellings.

**(c) Total pounds of butterfat undercredited over the affected years:**

According to Cleve Oram (r21): **"Sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat was what they made it, over the whole run of years, that the Hollow men were never paid for."**

This figure represents the undercredited butterfat over the entire period of faulty glass use (1897–1923, approximately 26 years).

Cross-check: Total milk received: 40 million pounds (per Bertram Kyle's statement about the arbitration figure). If the observed shortfall was 0.19 percentage points:
40,000,000 × 0.0019 = 76,000 lbs approximately, consistent with the "67,000–76,000" range; Oram's specific figure of 67,000 lbs is taken as authoritative.

**(d) Money value and fraction received:**

The Ashlin Chronicle reports: **"The bulletin works out what the Ostrey Hollow patrons were never paid for the butter fat they actually hauled to that station, and the figure is nineteen thousand six hundred dollars ($19,600)."**

This was the value of 67,000 lbs of butterfat at the average price paid during the period. (67,000 lbs × approx. $0.29/lb ≈ $19,430, close to the reported $19,600; the price may have varied by year.)

In 1925, the arbitration board awarded the patrons **$3,800** "without admission."

**Fraction received = $3,800 / $19,600 = 0.1939, approximately 1/5 or 19.4% of the amount owed**

Amount still underpaid: $19,600 - $3,800 = $15,800 (uncompensated for the patrons)

## C3. Total Butterfat Credited vs. Received

**Total milk received at Ostrey Hollow over the affected years: 40 million pounds** (per Bertram Kyle's statement obtained for the 1925 arbitration).

**(a) Butterfat credited to patrons on station test:**
40,000,000 lbs × 3.61% = **1,444,000 lbs of butterfat**

**(b) Butterfat actually received at Larrow Green creamery:**
From Bertram Kyle: "So many pounds of butterfat credited to the patrons on the station test. So many pounds of butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station cream. And then the difference of the two, entered as station gain, so many pounds — and after it, always, in the bookkeeper's own words, five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account."

The "station gain" of 5.25% represents the normal loss/shrinkage during transport from Ostrey Hollow to Larrow Green and subsequent processing. Thus:
1,444,000 × (1 - 0.0525) = 1,444,000 × 0.9475 = **1,368,190 lbs received at Larrow Green**

**(c) Express the difference as a percentage—be exact about what you are taking the percentage of:**

Difference: 1,444,000 - 1,368,190 = 75,810 lbs

**As a percentage of what was credited at the station: 75,810 / 1,444,000 = 5.25%** (the normal "station gain")

**As a percentage of what was received at Larrow Green: 75,810 / 1,368,190 = 5.53%** (the relative loss)

Note: This "gain" was retained by the Association and carried to the general account, not returned to the patrons. It represents normal shrinkage in cream handling and butter-making, not fraud, and was an accepted accounting practice of the time.

# Section D — Logic

## D1. The 1911 Broken Measure Interval as an Experiment

**When it occurred:** May 8, 1911 (measure broke) to July 10, 1911 (original glass restored) = **9 weeks**

**Variables and constants:**
- **Variable (changed):** The measuring glass itself—the original Ostrey Hollow station measure was broken and replaced with a borrowed measure from elsewhere.
- **Held constant:** (1) The tester (Ansel Keddie); (2) the herds and cows being milked (same farmers); (3) the season and pasture (same June–July period); (4) the vat, separator, and other machinery; (5) the process and procedure.

**Result:** During the nine-week interval with the borrowed glass, the station's butterfat tests **rose markedly**. When the original glass was restored, the tests **fell back to their former level** (per Orin Falke's account).

**What this establishes:** The broken measure interval proves, via experimental logic, that the glass—not the milk quality, not the herds, not the tester's honesty or procedure, not the hay or feed—was the source of the low readings. It isolates glass as the causal variable.

**What it does NOT establish:** The experiment does not establish (1) how long the original glass had been defective (was it defective from installation in 1897, or only for part of that 26-year period?); (2) whether the borrowed glass was accurately calibrated or merely happened to measure differently; (3) whether the defect in the original glass was intentional manufacturing fraud or an honest error.

**Significance overlooked:** Ansel Keddie noted the phenomenon with a question mark. This crucial evidence sat in the test book unread by the 1925 arbitration board, who had the book in their hands but did not examine it carefully. The answer to the whole matter was already written where they looked but did not see.

## D2. Three Independent Reasons the Tester Cannot Have Been Stealing Cream

**Reason 1 (Orin Falke, r22, paragraph 8):** The 1911 broken glass interval. When a different measure was used, tests rose immediately, then fell immediately upon restoration of the original glass. The same tester made all the tests, under no different supervision. If Ansel Keddie had been removing cream to lower the stated butterfat (to conceal the theft), his cream removal would have been constant and independent of the measuring glass. Yet the glass change produced an immediate change in tests. The cream-theft theory cannot account for the 1911 interval.

**Reason 2 (Bertram Kyle, r23, paragraphs on station "gain"):** Over 26 years, the station's books recorded a consistent "station gain" of 5.25%—the normal shrinkage between tested butterfat at Ostrey Hollow and received butterfat at Larrow Green. If Ansel Keddie had been stealing cream in large quantities (enough to cause a 0.19-point fat shortfall over his milk volume), the "station gain" would have been irregular, inconsistent, or would have exhibited patterns correlating with his suspected theft. Instead, it ran "as usual" every year—a steady percentage, recorded in the bookkeeper's own words, carried to the general account. An honest 5.25% shrinkage is incompatible with systematic cream theft.

**Reason 3 (Junia Ammon, r20, and the invoice):** The entire testing outfit—the glassware, bottles, tester, acid measure—was purchased new from Ammon & Sons in 1897 for $77.93. Ansel Keddie did not acquire the equipment; it was provided by the Association. He was not responsible for its manufacture or condition. The defect was in the glass itself, a piece of manufacturing equipment supplied by a third party (Ammon & Sons), not in anything Ansel Keddie could control. To accuse him of cream theft while the equipment he was given was faulty is to hold him responsible for properties of objects he did not make and could not reasonably have detected.

**Independence of these reasons:**
- Reason 1 (the 1911 interval) is an experimental observation from the test book.
- Reason 2 (the consistent station gain) is a bookkeeping/accounting observation from the ledgers.
- Reason 3 (the equipment source) is a historical/business observation from purchase records.
All three are independent: they do not rely on each other and derive from different sources and different types of evidence.

**Reason that holds even without the 1958 discovery:** Reason 1 (the 1911 interval) would still hold if the glassware had never been measured in 1958. That single interval, present in Ansel's test book from 1911, proves the glass was the cause and rules out cream theft as the source, regardless of later laboratory confirmation. Any investigator examining the test book in 1925 could have drawn this conclusion.

**Did the bookkeeper question the entries?** Bertram Kyle, the Association's bookkeeper, entered the "station gain" line every year but never questioned it. He states: "I copied that line, or the like of it, more times than I can count, and I will tell the Society plainly that it never once crossed my mind to wonder at it, and I do not believe it crossed anybody else's either. It had a name. That is the thing about a name. A figure with a name on it has been explained, and you rule under it and go on to the next."

Kyle later reflects: "Nothing in that office was hidden. Every figure I have named was written down in ink by an honest man, in a book that stood on an open shelf... It was not a secret. It was worse than a secret. It was in plain sight with a name on it."

The bookkeeper **did not question** the entries because the entries were routine and bore an accepted accounting label (station gain). The explanation the name provided (normal shrinkage) was sufficient to forestall inquiry. The pattern (consistent 5.25% every year) was exactly what you would expect from a legitimate loss, not what would flag theft.

## D3. Did the Manager Know Before 1923 That Tests Ran Low?

**Answer: Yes, Selby Vose almost certainly knew the tests ran low, well before the glassware was condemned in 1923.**

**Justification:**

Selby Vose became manager in 1911 (per Duncan Keddie). He remained manager through 1925 when he approved Ansel Keddie's dismissal. Over that 14-year tenure, as manager of the Association, Vose received the station accounts from Ostrey Hollow showing test results of consistently 3.61%, whereas the main creamery's tests averaged 3.78%. The deficiency of 0.19 points was visible in year-over-year comparisons. Other creamery associations in the county and region averaged 3.78%. The Larrow Valley's own Larrow Green facility averaged 3.78%. Only Ostrey Hollow ran low.

From Bertram Kyle: "Mr. Selby Vose sat behind that door for a quarter of a century and I sat outside it, and I liked him, and I will say two things about him which do not sit together and are both true. He was courteous to every man who came into that office, farmer or drummer or bank, and he never once let a caller feel he was being hurried. And he read the totals and he never read the lines above them. He was not a man who read. He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign."

Vose signed the ledger sheets carrying the station "gain" figures every year. The totals he read showed a consistent pattern: Ostrey Hollow paid less than other stations. **His knowledge must have been at the level of the total figures, not the line-by-line analysis.** Yet that level of knowledge was sufficient to know that Ostrey Hollow ran low.

In 1919, Vose himself **personally signed and circulated a written warning** to nine Ostrey Hollow patron families about water in the milk—an accusation premised on his belief (or the board's belief) that something was wrong with their milk. This warning demonstrates that by 1919, Vose was actively pursuing the hypothesis that Ostrey Hollow milk was inferior. He did not sign a warning without believing there was a problem.

However, whether Vose believed the problem was in the **milk** (farmers adding water, as the warning alleged) or in the **testing** (the glass) is not established by the sources. His signing the condemnation notice in 1923 and ordering new glass shows he complied with the inspector's decision, but does not prove he had previously suspected a glass defect. He may have believed the problem was in the milk and only learned in 1923 that it was the glass.

**Conclusion:** Selby Vose knew the tests ran low (he saw the totals and signed the ledgers). Whether he understood the **cause** before June 1923 cannot be determined from the sources. His 1919 warning suggests he believed the problem lay with the farmers, not the equipment.

# Section F — Theory

## F1. The Causal Theory and Competing Explanations

**The True Cause: A Manufacturing Defect in Glass Pipettes**

The Ostrey Hollow farmers were underpaid because the testing glassware supplied by Ammon & Sons in 1897 was manufactured short of specification. The six dozen milk test pipettes marked 17.6 cubic centimeters were blown in the mold to deliver an average of 16.27 cc—short by 1.33 cc, or approximately 7.57%. This defect was consistent and affected every butterfat test run through those pipettes from May 1897 (installation) through June 1923 (condemnation).

**Why the error arose:** The most plausible explanation, offered by Junia Ammon (the glassblowing company's historian writing in 1949), is that the mold had been reground as part of routine maintenance. The reground mold came back slightly different from its specification, making it consistently short. A boy checking the first, tenth, and fiftieth pipettes from the lot found them all the same—"consistently wrong"—because the error was built into the mold itself, not a sporadic flaw. The gauge hanging on the wall could be knocked off its nail and rehung differently; the mold looked exactly as it looked before. No man on the blowing floor caught the error because the consistency of the defect mimicked the consistency of a correctly manufactured lot. Glass is made by people, and this was an honest manufacturing mistake, not fraud.

**Why the error persisted:** The defect was never detected during the 26 years it was in use because:
1. The error produced a consistent, systematic 7.57% underestimate that was regular enough to escape notice; it was not random scatter that would suggest a problem.
2. The Ostrey Hollow milk was already suspected (for agricultural reasons) to be lower in butterfat than milk elsewhere, so the low readings were explained by existing theory (poor hay, wet pasture).
3. The borrowing of measurements for comparison was limited; the station tested its own output in isolation.
4. No one in the valley was trained in metrology or had access to calibration equipment.

**Why it ended:** On June 12, 1923, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn rode to Ostrey Hollow on routine circuit inspection, carried a calibrated burette, and tested the station's pipettes against it. He immediately condemned them and ordered new glass. This was the first time in 26 years that the pipettes were tested against an external standard. From July 1923 onward, when new glass was installed, the station's tests rose to 3.85% and stayed there—no longer systematically low.

**Why nothing at the station or farms had to change:** The milk was the same. The cows ate the same hay. The tester (Ansel Keddie) used the same procedures. The machinery stayed the same. The only change was the glass. This demonstrates that the defect was in the measuring instrument, not in any of the material or procedural factors. The milk quality did not improve; the measurement of it did.

---

**The Three Competing Explanations That Were Advanced (and Why Each Failed):**

**Explanation 1: Farmers Added Water to the Milk**

Advanced by: Selby Vose, manager of the Association, in a written circular dated 1919 warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons about water in their milk.

Evidence advanced: None explicitly stated in the sources; the theory was the standing suspicion of the Association's officers.

Evidence that defeats it: Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, tested the Ostrey Hollow milk with a lactometer at every visit from 1908 forward and "never once found added water at that station" (per Orin Falke, r22, paragraph 7). The lactometer is a specific test for added water (it detects the altered specific gravity). Over 15 years of annual or more-frequent testing, Strawn never detected adulteration. Nine families received the 1919 warning about water in their milk. None of them answered—not with denial, not with protest. In the Larrow Valley, silence was a shout, yet none shouted. If water had been systematically added, Strawn would have found it; if it had been sporadic, some farmers would have protested the accusation. Neither happened. The theory cannot be sustained.

---

**Explanation 2: The Tester Himself Removed Cream and Wrote Low Tests to Conceal the Theft**

Advanced by: An officer of the Association, at the 1925 arbitration hearing (per Orin Falke, r22, paragraph 8).

Evidence advanced: The low tests, implying that the tester had motive (to skim cream) and opportunity (he controlled the tests).

Evidence that defeats it: The 1911 broken glass interval (r22, paragraph 6). When the station's own measuring glass broke in May 1911 and a borrowed glass was used for nine weeks, "the station's tests stood markedly higher. On the day the station resumed its own glass they fell back to their former level." The tester (Ansel Keddie) was the same throughout. His supervision was no different. The only variable was the glass. If Ansel had been stealing cream, the cream removal would have been constant and independent of which measuring glass was in use. Yet the tests rose when the glass changed. This alone disposes of the cream-theft theory. Additionally, Bertram Kyle's ledgers show a consistent "station gain" (shrinkage) of 5.25% every year—exactly the expected normal loss in cream transport and processing. An honest shrinkage would not mask systematic cream theft; if theft were occurring, the "gain" figures would have been irregular. The theory cannot be sustained.

---

**Explanation 3: The Wet Hay and Poor Pasture of Hessel Bottom Thinned the Cream of Cows Fed on That Bottom**

Advanced by: A committee of the Association that sampled the Hessel Bottom herds in 1908; also supported by a veterinarian of standing at Ashlin who wrote to the same effect in 1926.

Evidence advanced: Practical observation by men who walked the ground and looked into the mangers; the bog meadows of Hessel Bottom were known throughout the county for coarse hay.

Evidence that defeats it: Orin Falke initially gave this theory some weight, noting "I am not prepared to dismiss it." However, after obtaining the State Agricultural College's own feeding trial on bog hay (Bulletin No. 214, conducted in 1911 and published by 1947), he found that bog hay fed as the whole roughage "depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture" (r22, note added). Five hundredths in winter, nothing in summer, cannot account for a consistent 19 hundredths (0.19%) low in every month of the year and especially in July. Falke withdrew his concession: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July. I withdraw the concession made on page four." The theory does not account for the magnitude, consistency, or seasonal uniformity of the observed shortfall. It is not entirely falsified but is outweighed by the glass defect as an explanation.

**Two Subsidiary Questions:**

**Did the 1925 board of arbitration ever examine the condemned glassware?**

No. The crate of condemned glassware sat in the loft over the intake room, twenty feet above the weigh room where two of the three sittings of the arbitration board were held. Bertram Kyle states: "I carried them in, and I carried them out [referring to the four key books: test book, receiving book, weigh book, and inspector's notebook], and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked." Cleve Oram recalls: "And the crate was up the ladder over their heads the entire time. Twenty feet, if that. I have thought about that since, I will not pretend I thought about it then." No source indicates that anyone climbed the ladder or examined the crate. The board had the evidence—the condemned glass, the test book with the 1911 question mark, the inspector's notebooks—but did not connect it.

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?**

Ansel Keddie believed he did not know, or kept his belief private. The sources indicate: (1) In 1911, when his glass broke and was replaced with a borrowed one, he noted the rise in tests and entered a question mark—his way of recording something that did not fit his understanding. (2) In 1902, when complaints first arose, he came home "troubled" and told Orra, "It is not your cows and not your hands… but it is my figures they will look at. Then he said no more about it." He did not accuse the farmers of watering milk (the manager's theory), nor did he accuse himself of theft. He suspected something was wrong with the figures, possibly the glass or his procedure. (3) Orra's diary notes he "kept his opinions to himself" and "said nothing at all" when the 1908 committee blamed the hay. (4) He never publicly proposed an alternative theory. He tested the milk as he was taught, kept his books honestly, and bore the suspicion in silence. He never asked them to clear him. According to Orra, Ansel said to her "once and only once, and not lately, that a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."

# Section G — Summary

## G1. Summary of the Eight-Story History (≤150 words)

Between 1889 and 1897, Larrow Valley farmers organized a cooperative creamery and built a satellite station at Ostrey Hollow to test and separate milk for payment by butterfat content. In April 1897, they bought testing glassware—six dozen pipettes marked 17.6 cc—from Ammon & Sons in Tarnet for $0.35 each. The pipettes were manufactured short (averaging 16.27 cc), a mold error undetected for 26 years.

From 1897 to 1923, Ostrey Hollow milk tested consistently low (3.61% vs. 3.78% elsewhere), blamed on farmers, tester Ansel Keddie, or hay. In 1911, a broken measure was replaced; tests rose sharply, then fell back—proof the glass was the cause. No one connected this observation.

In 1925, after patrons petitioned for compensation, an arbitration board found "no cause" but awarded $3,800. Ansel Keddie was dismissed, unpaid.

In 1958, his granddaughter Ivy Keddie found the condemned glassware crate in a loft, measured the pipettes, and proved the glass defect had cost the farmers $19,600. Ansel Keddie's name was cleared 24 years after his death, 33 years after dismissal.

A single ladder climb solved what thirty-three years of looking could not.
