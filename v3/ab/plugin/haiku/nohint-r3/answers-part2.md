## Story 5: The Detection and Condemnation (June 1923)

On June 12, 1923, Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector on the Ordell circuit, rode out to Ostrey Hollow on routine inspection. He carried in his coat a calibrated burette and tested the station's milk pipettes against it. He found they would not deliver their marked capacity. He condemned the entire lot on the spot and directed the manager to order new glass. The Association wrote to Ammon & Sons on June 28, 1923, ordering replacement pipettes and bottles. Ammon & Sons replied on July 2: "Your order of the 28th ultimo for six dozen milk pipettes and six dozen test bottles is entered and will go forward this month. We regret the trouble. Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name."

New glass was in use within the month. From 1924 forward, the station's average test stood at 3.85 and remained there. The shift was dramatic and immediate. Yet nobody connected the two facts—the condemnation and the rise in tests—into a cause. The state inspector drew no inference about the years behind it. The Association's manager, Selby Vose, did not note it in his records. Bertram Kyle, the clerk who handled correspondence and made ledger entries, filed the condemnation notice and entered the order and "that was the end of the matter as far as the outer room was concerned." The condemned glassware, six dozen pipettes, was placed in a wooden crate, covered with a tarpaulin, and hoisted into the loft over the intake room, where it sat undisturbed for 35 years.

Cleve Oram, who had worked at the station as a boy, saw the crate many times. "It was a crate under a tarp. I would not have been able to tell you what was in it if I had not been up there after a hay fork with my own hands." By 1923, one piece of glass had been right in front of the whole valley, and no one saw it.

**When:** June 12, 1923 (condemnation); June 28–July 2 (correspondence); July 1923 (new glass in use)

**Key detail:** Inspector Strawn ran pipettes against calibrated burette; found them defective; condemned lot; nobody connected to years of low tests

## Story 6: The Arbitration, Dismissal, and Award (1925)

In January 1925, the patrons of Ostrey Hollow petitioned for redress. A board of arbitration was formed and held three sittings: one at Ashlin in the Association's office (where the lawyer was), and two at the station itself in the weigh room, with the machinery and books present. The hearings ran from March 17 to May 4, 1925. Three competing theories were aired: water in the milk (refuted by lactometer tests), cream theft by the tester (refuted by the 1911 interval), and poor hay (suggested but not proven). The board heard the patrons and the officers. They found no cause established. On May 4, they awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800, "without admission"—lawyer's language meaning we will pay but we say nothing about why.

Four days before this award, on April 30, 1925, Ansel Keddie was dismissed. He had held his position for 29 seasons. He came home on an ordinary April afternoon, hung his apron behind the shed door, and asked what there was for supper. Orra had written, "He came up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm." There was ham. He ate it.

The board had in its possession the station's own test book in Ansel Keddie's hand, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book, and Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks for the years inspected. Bertram Kyle, the clerk, carried these four documents in and out. "I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked." The crate of condemned glass sat twenty feet overhead, in the loft, covered with tarpaulin and chaff. No one examined it. No one climbed the ladder.

Ansel Keddie lived nine more years, until 1934, under the imputation. The arbitration minutes were entered into the Association's books. The award was charged to the station account. Nothing was said in the valley again. Men who had known Ansel all his life shook his widow's hand at his funeral and spoke only of the barn door he had hung true for thirty years. Not one said the other thing.

## Story 7: The Discovery and Vindication (1958)

In the winter of 1958, Ivy Keddie, then a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, returned to Ostrey Hollow. She was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter (Duncan Keddie's daughter) and the great-niece of the largest patron, Emmet Brant. She was there to research a thesis on the station's history. In February 1958, she climbed the ladder nailed to the wall over the intake room, moved aside some sacks and wire, lifted the tarpaulin, and found the wooden crate. She carried it down.

The crate held glassware: the station's condemned pipettes from 1923, along with test bottles. Nine of the sixty original pipettes remained intact (the rest had been broken over 35 years by people searching for other things in the loft). On March 9, 1958, Ivy Keddie carried the nine surviving pipettes to the State Agricultural College at Farland and measured each one against a calibrated burette. They delivered an average of 16.27 cubic centimeters, not the 17.6 marked on their stems. Every pipette was consistently short—off by 1.33 cc, about 7.5%. "The average of the lot came to 16.27 c.c." This was the answer.

In October 1958, the college published Bulletin No. 471, "The Ostrey Hollow Milk Test," by Professor Leland Vaught and Miss Ivy Keddie. The bulletin set out the theory: a short charge of milk poured into a bottle whose graduation marks are set for the full charge reads falsely low in the same proportion, every single time, for as long as the glass was used. The bulletin calculated the total shortfall in payment to the six Ostrey Hollow patrons over the 26 years of affected delivery: $19,600 in money that the farmers earned but never received.

The Ashlin Chronicle ran a front-page feature on October 3, 1958: "Old Ordell Wrong Righted." Ansel Keddie, dead 24 years, was cleared. He had never cheated anyone. He had tested the milk in front of the state's own man and tested it the way he was taught. The glass was wrong. "I would have been a kindness if somebody in this county had thought to climb a ladder."

**Total overpayment owed:** $19,600 over 26 years; patrons received $3,800 in 1925 without admission

**Ivy Keddie:** Granddaughter of Ansel (also Duncan's daughter); age 27 in 1958; graduate student at Farland

**Vindication:** Ansel Keddie cleared 24 years after death (1934–1958), 33 years after dismissal (1925–1958)

## Story 8: Ammon & Sons and the Manufacture of the Defect (1897–1949)

Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, operated a glassblowing works in Tarnet for sixty years. Under founder Marcus Ammon, the firm supplied half the creameries in the state with testing glassware: pipettes, test bottles, burettes, and acid measures. A milk test pipette was "a simple thing and not an easy thing." A glassblower gathered his glass, blew the bulb, drew the stem down, and worked to a gauge hung at his elbow. He was required to make a vessel that would *deliver* a stated quantity, not merely hold it—the distinction being that milk clings to glass and the delivery measurement is harder than the volume measurement. The mark was applied while the glass was still warm. Each piece was gauged after cooling; if it did not meet specification, it went into the scrap barrel.

On April 14, 1897, Ammon & Sons filled an order for the Larrow Valley Cooperative: six dozen milk test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c., twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, and a hand tester. Total invoice: $77.93. The pipettes cost $0.35 each ($25.20 for the lot). The entire outfit for a complete skimming station cost less than eighty dollars. These six dozen pipettes went into use at Ostrey Hollow on May 1, 1897.

For 26 years those pipettes delivered 16.27 cc instead of 17.6 cc. Sixty farmers were underpaid in proportion. Twenty-six years later, in June 1923, the state inspector condemned them and Ammon & Sons was asked to explain. They could not. "Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name." This answer haunted Junia Ammon, the firm's historian, when she wrote the company's history in 1949. She recorded the invoice and the response side by side.

What had happened? Ammon's speculation: The mold had been reground—a normal maintenance operation—but had come back a hair different from what it was before. A boy checking the first, tenth, and fiftieth pipettes from the lot found them all consistent: they were consistently wrong. The gauge hung on a nail; it could be knocked off and rehung slightly askew. The mold looked exactly as it looked before. No man on the blowing floor caught the error. "That is how it goes in a glass house, and any man who has stood on a blowing floor will tell you the same." Sixty years later, Junia Ammon wrote: "What I regret is not the accident. Glass is made by people. What I regret is the letter, and that we had nothing better than the letter to send, and that when a customer of a quarter century wrote to this house and asked in effect what had been done to him, the answer he got was that our books would not say. Sixty years is long enough that a firm may print its own bad morning as well as its good ones."

**Narrators:** Junia Ammon (firm historian, r20); Orin Falke (citing Ammon invoice, r22); Hazel Brant (r17)

**When manufactured:** April 14, 1897 invoice; May 1, 1897 installation

**When condemned:** June 12, 1923 (Inspector Strawn)

**Defect:** Mold error; pipettes delivered 16.27 cc vs. 17.6 cc marked; short 1.33 cc

**Cost to manufacture:** $0.35 per pipette ($25.20 for lot of 72); part of $77.93 outfit

**Cost to farmers:** $19,600 over 26 years (per 1958 college bulletin)

**Company response (1923):** "Our books would not tell us anything about the lot you name"

**Later reflection (1949):** Junia Ammon regrets the evasive response; speculates mold error; admits it was likely honest manufacturing mistake

**Documents:** r20 (Junia Ammon's firm history, invoice, letter), r22 (Orin Falke citing invoice), r17 (Hazel noting the discovery of the crate)

# Section B — Relationships

## B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Her father was Duncan Keddie (born 1894), Ansel's son. Duncan Keddie was a schoolteacher at Ashlin and wrote family notes in 1968, plainly identifying his daughter: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise and once heard it said aloud in this town by a man who ought to have known."

The Ashlin Chronicle's feature of October 3, 1958, initially identified her as Ansel's "great-niece," but this was an error. Orin Falke's county agent report also initially called her Ansel's "great-niece" but Falke later corrected himself in a note appended to his report: "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine."

**Resolution:** She was his granddaughter, not his great-niece. Duncan Keddie's direct statement, supported by Falke's correction, resolves the narrators' errors. She was born in 1931, 33 years after Ansel's dismissal and 24 years before his name was cleared.

---

## B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. Hazel Brant's father was Roy Brant; Roy was the son of Emmet Brant (the largest farmer on Hessel Bottom). Ivy's father was Duncan Keddie; Duncan was the son of Ansel Keddie, who was married to Orra Brant. Orra Brant was Emmet Brant's sister.

The chain of descent: Emmet Brant and Orra Brant were brother and sister. Emmet had a son Roy; Orra had a son Duncan. Roy and Duncan were therefore first cousins (children of a brother and sister). Roy had a daughter Hazel; Duncan had a daughter Ivy. Hazel and Ivy were therefore second cousins (children of first cousins).

Duncan Keddie emphasized this plainly in his notes, correcting widespread confusion: "Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece, which people say because it is shorter."

Cleve Oram, when discussing Ivy and Hazel, stated that "Ivy Keddie was Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed," which is incorrect. This appears to be a lapse in Oram's memory, not a genuine alternative source.

**Resolution:** Second cousins. This is confirmed by Duncan Keddie's explicit derivation, repeated by Hazel Brant herself, and mathematically certain from the documented family relationships. No intervening generations are left open: the line runs Emmet/Orra → Roy/Duncan → Hazel/Ivy with no skips or gaps.

---

## B3. Selby Vose's father and its significance to Ostrey Hollow

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose. Gideon Vose organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association and was its first president. He drafted its bylaws, including the crucial article seven providing that farmers be paid by fat content rather than milk weight.

Duncan Keddie carefully corrected this relationship, distinguishing it from confusion with the "Ashlin Voses," who were unrelated: "Mr. Selby Vose became manager in nineteen eleven. He was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president—the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense."

**Significance:** Selby Vose, as manager, was the heir to the Association's founding structure. He inherited an organization built on the premise that testing was accurate and payment by fat content was fair. Yet under his management (1911–36), the system's fatal flaw—the defective glass—remained unexamined and unconnected to the years of low readings. In 1919, he personally circulated the water-in-milk warning. In 1925, he signed the letter dismissing Ansel Keddie four days before the arbitration board awarded compensation. He read only the bottom figures in ledger sheets, not the lines above them, according to Bertram Kyle's observation. His inheritance was institutional authority; his legacy was institutional failure to investigate.

## B4. Relationship between Vose and Keddie families

Duncan Keddie asserts there was no family connection between the Vose and Keddie families: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either."

He notes that rumors claimed his mother and Gideon Vose's wife were cousins—"I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her sit down." Yet he dismisses this as unfounded talk: "My mother would have told me. She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it."

However, Cleve Oram offers a conflicting account: "The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley. My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper." Oram notes he "could not draw the line," suggesting he cannot verify the claim precisely.

**Resolution:** The sources conflict. Duncan Keddie's statement—made by a man documenting his family for posterity, supported by his mother's own knowledge of connections—is more authoritative than Oram's vague family rumor. Duncan explicitly asserts no connection. Oram acknowledges he cannot draw the line. No documentary evidence (no marriage record, no genealogy, no contemporary acknowledgment) is cited by either narrator. The safest conclusion is that if a connection existed, it is not established by the sources, and Duncan Keddie's direct denial, grounded in his mother's knowledge of local connections, outweighs Oram's unverified hearsay.
