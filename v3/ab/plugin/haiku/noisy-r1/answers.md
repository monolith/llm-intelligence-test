# Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

## Section A: Reconstruction of Eight Stories

### Story 1: The Founding and Organization of the Larrow Valley Cooperative and the Ostrey Hollow Station (1889–1898)

In 1889, farmers of Larrow Valley met at Larrow Green to organize a cooperative creamery association. By 1891, they had built the main creamery at Larrow Green. The association's by-laws included Article VII, which stipulated that every patron's payment would be based on the pounds of butterfat found in his milk by test, not on weight; the test made at the station where he delivered would govern payment absolutely, with no appeal or retest. In 1895–1896, during winter and early spring, the association built a skimming station seven miles up the valley at Ostrey Hollow to serve the Hessel Bottom farms and surrounding area. The station opened on May 4, 1896, with Ansel Keddie as tester and separator man. For its first season or two, the station borrowed testing glassware from the main creamery at Larrow Green; on May 1, 1897 (and per Junia Ammon's invoice of April 14, 1897), the station received its own complete testing outfit from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet: six dozen milk-test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c., twelve dozen test bottles with 8 percent necks, one acid measure, and one hand tester for four bottles, freight included, totaling $77.93. Bertram Kyle's office records show that in the first season (before a formal station account was created), approximately 1.1 million pounds of milk were received and recorded on the main creamery's books. From May 1, 1897 onward, the Ostrey Hollow station began regular operations under Ansel Keddie's hand, testing milk from six farms on Hessel Bottom—the largest being Emmet Brant's, from which Roy Brant (his son and Ansel's brother-in-law) hauled milk in a canvas-topped wagon driven by Cleve Oram's father. The station's records (test book), the Brant family's private weigh book (begun by Emmet Brant on January 1, 1897), and the receiving ledgers at Larrow Green became the contemporaneous documents that would later establish the truth of what went wrong.

**Documents:** r12 (Rundle, Association founding and Hollow station opening), r20 (Ammon, 1897 invoice), r24 (Orra, diary May 1, 1897), r23 (Kyle, office records and first season), r17 (Hazel, weigh book begun by grandfather Emmet), r09 (Roy Brant, family ledger context).

### Story 2: The First Recognition of the Testing Discrepancy and Competing Theories (1902–1907)

In March 1902, Orra Keddie recorded in her diary that talk at the store suggested the Ostrey Hollow milk tested poorer than milk from the valley below. From this time forward, the milk delivered to the Ostrey Hollow station tested consistently lower than milk tested anywhere else—lower than Larrow Green's average, lower than Hessel Bottom milk tested at the Alder Corners cheese factory over the ridge, lower than milk from neighboring farms tested at the same station when tested fresh. The difference was precise and unvarying: nineteen-hundredths of a point (0.19%) separated Ostrey Hollow's average from the truth, in every month of the year, in the flush of June and the depth of February alike. Ostrey Hollow averaged 3.61, while Larrow Green and standard valley milk averaged 3.78–3.80. The first complaint of record came in 1902, made by a man saying his milk tested low at Hollow despite identical feed and water to his brother-in-law's milk at another station. Between 1902 and 1908, competing explanations emerged in the valley: (1) the Ostrey Hollow patrons were adulterating their milk with water; (2) the tester, Ansel Keddie, was stealing cream and writing low tests to conceal the loss; (3) the wet hay and boggy meadows of Hessel Bottom, which thined cream in the cows' milk. Emmet Brant kept his own scale beside the station's scale and recorded both figures in his weigh book every delivery day—when the two agreed, he noted "agreeing." Roy Brant later kept the same book. This private record would become crucial evidence, showing that the station's own measure and Emmet's agreed consistently, yet both gave weights that the station then tested low for butterfat. No theft was occurring at the weighing stage. Four of the six Hessel Bottom farms hauled milk to the cheese factory over Redlow Ridge in summer (June–September, 1915–1930) and sold to Alder Corners; the same milk tested 3.78–3.82 there, averaging 3.80—proving the milk itself was sound and the Ostrey Hollow tests were systematically wrong.

**Documents:** r24 (Orra, diary March 1902 complaint), r17 (Hazel, 19-hundredths gap, "flat as a floor"), r09 (Roy, Emmet's ledger and consistency), r10 (Rosalie Cudd, Alder Corners cheese factory 16-summer record of same milk at 3.80).

### Story 3: The 1908 Investigation of Wet Hay at Hessel Bottom

In January 1908, a committee of the Larrow Valley Association came to Hessel Bottom to investigate the milk quality issue. Nyle Grigg (a farmer who hauled to Larrow Green, not Hollow, thus impartial) chaired the committee. The committee sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom every month throughout 1908, examining barns, mangers, and hay. They reported finding no dishonesty anywhere—scales were honest, milk was whole. Their finding: the wet hay of Hessel Bottom, which grows coarse sedge and rush rather than sweet grasses on acid, poorly drained soil, thins the cream of cows fed upon it. This was a theory, not proof, and it pointed to a remedy on land—drainage, lime, mixed ration—rather than at the station. Grigg republished his 1908 report in 1919 with a covering letter supporting the theory and referencing a state college feeding trial (Bulletin 214, 1911) that had tested bog hay's effect on butterfat and found it depressed fat by "two tenths" (0.2%) in winter and nothing in summer. In 1926, Dr. Roland Kepp, a veterinary surgeon at Ashlin, presented a paper to the Ordell County Farm Club elaborating this theory: wet meadows create distinct chemistry (acid soil, poor lime and phosphates); cows on such ground lose minerals and condition; the gap between Hollow milk (actually 3.85) and hill milk was 0.21 point. Kepp's challenge was that 0.2% depression from bog hay could not account for 0.19 hundredths (not 0.19%, but 0.0019) gap Hazel mentioned, and that summer milk should not be depressed if bog hay was the cause—yet the gap was consistent year-round. Nevertheless, the 1908 investigation established wet hay as a serious contributing theory and gave the Association's leadership a technical explanation that seemed reasonable and required no admission of station error.

**Documents:** r15 (Grigg, 1908 committee report republished 1919), r16 (Kepp, veterinary paper 1926 to county farm club), r24 (Orra, diary January 1908 and March 1908 committee visit).

### Story 4: The 1911 Glass-Breaking Experiment

On May 8, 1911, the Ostrey Hollow station's own measuring glass (pipette) broke. Orra Keddie's diary records Ansel came home "cross about a broken measure" that rainy day. The station needed a replacement immediately to continue testing. For exactly nine weeks—from May 8 to July 10, 1911—the station borrowed a measuring glass from another station (source unspecified, possibly Larrow Green's own equipment that was being used for comparison). During those nine weeks, all of Ostrey Hollow's milk tested markedly higher than its normal average: tests rose from the typical 3.61 to approximately 3.80 for all patrons. On the day the station returned to its own glass (July 10), the tests fell back to their former low level. Ansel Keddie noted this fact in his test book and marked it with a question mark but did not speculate or write down a hypothesis. Duncan Keddie, Ansel's son, recalled his father saying in summer 1911 that "it was something in the glass" and that he had been "cross about a broken measure"—the only time Duncan ever heard his father voice suspicion. Leland Vaught's Bulletin 471 (1958) treated the 1911 interval as decisive evidence of glass defect and "the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had": Ansel would not put down what he could not prove, and suspicion about a piece of glass could not be proven against the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every other station in the county. Over nine weeks, with herds unchanged, pasture unchanged, season unchanged, men unchanged, machinery unchanged—only the glass changed. The tests rose. The experiment demonstrates that the 1911 interval proved glass defect with near-certainty (something independent of cream theft or water adulteration or animal biology), and it proves that Ansel either could not or would not report it. However, it does not prove what specific defect the glass had, only that it was systematically altering results. The recovered pipettes from 1958 would later show the defect was systematic underdeli very: each pipette delivered approximately 16.27 c.c. instead of marked 17.6 c.c., a shortfall of 5%.

**Documents:** r24 (Orra, diary May 8 1911 "broken measure"), r03 (Ansel, statement about 1911 glass), r04 (Ivy, burette measurement of recovered pipettes), r11 (Vaught, Bulletin 471, detailed 1911 analysis), r18 (Duncan, father's 1911 suspicion), r22 (Falke, 1959 report on 1911 experiment as decisive).

### Story 5: The 1919 Water-Adulteration Warning and Family Rift (1919)

In June 1919, the manager of the Larrow Valley Association (Selby Vose, who had held office since 1911) circulated a warning letter to nine families on Hessel Bottom cautioning them against adding water to their milk. The letter was civil in form but carried no doubt about its accusation. Bertram Kyle, the Association's clerk, typed the letter and remembered typing it (unusual for him to recall any single task after 25 years at the desk). Vose signed it and sent it out over his name alone. Orra Keddie read hers and put it in the stove without replying. She was one of nine recipients, though only six farms occupied Hessel Bottom. The letter was answered by none of them, which in that valley constituted a shout of protest. Ansel Keddie, Orra's husband, refused to have the letter delivered by anyone else—he insisted on carrying the nine letters himself, going to each household on foot, standing at the door while the man read it. He came home at eleven at night with his hat still on. Two days later, Orra's brother Emmet came to the house. He did not enter; he stood in the yard and said to Ansel that he had hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and Ansel knew it better than any man living. Then Emmet asked why Ansel had carried the paper himself if he did not believe it. Ansel replied that he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet went home. He has not been in the house since—a ten-year rift between siblings that lasted until Emmet's death in spring 1929. Ansel's action was a double bind: carrying the letter personally appeared to accept its premise, yet he could not delegate it to a subordinate. The 1919 warning was later used to support the water-adulteration theory at the 1925 arbitration, but the state dairy inspector's records showed he had tested for added water with a lactometer at every visit from 1908 onward and never once found any.

**Documents:** r08 (Selby Vose, manager's action), r23 (Kyle, typing and dispatch), r24 (Orra, diary June 1919 letter and two days after, Emmet's visit and question), r22 (Falke, 1959 report noting Vose's warning and its later use), r19 (Chronicle, 1958 report on water theory).

### Story 6: The 1923 Condemnation of the Station's Glassware (June 1923)

On June 12, 1923, the state dairy inspector for the Ordell circuit, Merle Strawn, made a routine visit to the Ostrey Hollow station. Strawn was a careful man, associated with no family in the valley and thus impartial. He brought a calibrated burette and ran the station's milk pipettes against it. He found that the glassware would not deliver what was marked on it. The pipettes were running short. On the spot, he condemned the whole lot and directed the manager to order new glassware. Strawn wrote down that he had condemned them; he wrote nothing about what they might have been doing for the twenty-six years prior (May 1, 1897 to June 12, 1923). Bertram Kyle remembered that Strawn came to the office and said so: this was noted with no more concern than if a window had broken. The Association ordered replacement glassware from Ammon & Sons at Tarnet the same week (June 28, 1923). Ammon & Sons replied by July 2 that they regretted the trouble but could say nothing about the original lot from the 1890s, as their works records had burned in 1931 (actually 1923 or before) and their books of the nineties were not such that they could explain it. New glass was in use from July 1923 onward. In an important observation: from 1924 forward, the station's average test rose to 3.85 and remained there. No person connected one thing with the other at the time. The inspector did not infer backward; the Association did not ask. Nothing was said of the condemnation in the office or the books beyond the order and its arrival. Yet Cleve Oram (cream hauler's son) was up at the station that afternoon when the rain was coming down, and he saw the condemned crate placed in the loft over the intake room, under a tarpaulin. It sat there for thirty-five years undisturbed, and nobody ever gave it a thought. It was a crate under a tarp.

**Documents:** r06 (Merle Strawn, state inspector's circuit notebook 1923), r11 (Vaught, Bulletin 471, detailed technical analysis of condemnation and burette testing), r20 (Junia Ammon, 1949 firm history with July 2 letter), r23 (Kyle, office record of condemnation notice), r21 (Cleve Oram, the crate placement in the loft, his hand on it), r22 (Falke, 1959 report noting June 12 condemnation and subsequent test rise).

### Story 7: The 1925 Arbitration, Investigation Failure, and Ansel Keddie's Dismissal (January–May 1925)

In January 1925, Ostrey Hollow patrons, led by Emmet Brant (now reconciled with his sister Orra but still holding grievance against the station), submitted a petition to the Larrow Valley Association demanding redress for years of low test payments. Forty million pounds of milk had been received at the station over the complaint years; butterfat averaged 25 cents per pound; the loss to patrons was estimated (by Emmet Brant himself) at 67,000 pounds of butterfat uncredited, worth approximately $19,600 in money. The Association, facing legal pressure, formed a board of arbitration. The chairman was Alonzo Frick, an Ashlin attorney. The board sat for three periods: first at Ashlin on March 17, 1925, then two sessions at the station itself in the weigh room, with men standing outside in the road. Orra Keddie did not attend the station hearings; she could hear wagons from the yard. Four documents were presented to the board: (1) the station's test book in Ansel Keddie's hand, (2) the Association's receiving book, (3) Emmet Brant's private weigh book (which he lent for the purpose and wanted back), and (4) the state dairy inspector Strawn's circuit notebook for 1923. Frick's private reflection was that the answer may have been in the room but not in the three stories told: water adulteration (Vose's theory, no proof found), tester theft (no cream-loss pattern found in the books; on the contrary, Hobart Sill's receiving ledger showed cream arrived heavy every year), wet hay (old opinion, no current proof). The board heard from Ansel Keddie on March 17, 1925. He testified that he had tested milk in front of the state man's eyes 31 times per week for 29 seasons, could not explain the figures, never took cream, never adulterated the accounts. But accurate denial did him no good. On April 30, 1925—four days before the board's decision—the Association dismissed Ansel Keddie after twenty-nine seasons. On May 4, 1925, the board announced its finding: it found "no cause established" and awarded the patrons $3,800 "without admission," a figure charged to the station account rather than the Association's general account. The award was less than a fifth of the patrons' own reckoning. Ansel was not recalled; the Association stood by the dismissal. He lived nine years after dismissal and died in 1934 "with the imputation on him, in this county, among men he had weighed milk for since before their fathers died" (Duncan Keddie's words). At his funeral, it was well attended and nobody said much.

**Documents:** r13 (Frick, arbitration board chairman, 1946 bar association yearbook recollection), r23 (Kyle, four documents to board, May 1924 award amount [Kyle says 1924 but context is 1925]), r22 (Falke, 1959 official report on arbitration timing and findings), r24 (Orra, diary March 17, April 30, and May entries), r12 (Rundle, 1939 jubilee pamphlet on arbitration result and patrons' loss).

### Story 8: The 1958 Discovery and the 1958 Exoneration via Bulletin 471 (February–October 1958)

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland and great-niece (actually granddaughter, per Duncan Keddie's 1970 correction) of Ansel Keddie, did college work on the Ostrey Hollow case. She visited the old station at Hollow and, with a ladder and her own initiative, climbed into the loft over the intake room. Under a tarpaulin and about an inch of chaff lay the condemned crate of glassware, untouched since June 12, 1923. Inside were originally six dozen milk-test pipettes (72 total). Thirty-five years of loft life and accidental handling had broken many; eleven survived in a condition to be measured. On March 9, 1958, Ivy carried the crate east to the college laboratory at Farland. There, using a calibrated burette, she measured the surviving pipettes one by one against the burette. Every one had been marked on the stem with "17.6 c.c."—the standard charge for a milk butterfat test. Not one delivered what was marked. The average delivery of all eleven pipettes was 16.27 c.c.—a shortfall of 1.33 c.c., or 7.6% short of the marked capacity. The mathematics of the defect proved elegant: a short charge of milk into a bottle whose graduated neck was ruled for a full charge yields a butterfat reading short in the same proportion. The station had been using glass that delivered only 92.44% (or approximately 92.4%) of the marked charge, translating to 5% low readings systematically. Bulletin 471, "The Ostrey Hollow Incident: A Corrective Analysis," was published in October 1958 jointly by Professor Leland Vaught (state college dairy husbandry department) and Ivy Keddie (graduate student). The bulletin detailed the glassware defect, showed that the 1911 glass-switching experiment proved the defect's existence, calculated the true average butterfat as 3.905% (not the recorded 3.61%), and computed the butterfat uncredited to patrons over the full period at 76,000 pounds. On October 3, 1958, the *Ashlin Chronicle* published a front-page feature crediting Ivy with discovering the truth. Public opinion shifted; Ansel Keddie's name was cleared 33 years after his dismissal and 24 years after his death. The county agricultural agent, Orin Falke, prepared an official summary for the county board. Ivy gave a final statement: "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not. He tested the milk in front of the state's own man and he tested it the way he was taught, and the reason his figures would not come right was in a crate over his head the entire time. I would like it printed that he never knew."

**Documents:** r18 (Duncan Keddie, 1968 notes on document disposition and 1970 correction of Ivy as granddaughter), r17 (Hazel Brant, 1981 tape, Ivy climbing ladder with burette, tarpaulin and chaff), r19 (Ashlin Chronicle, October 3, 1958, front page on Bulletin 471 and Ivy's work), r20 (Junia Ammon, 1949 firm history with context on supply and accountability), r22 (Falke, 1959 official county report with Bulletin 214 note and correction of Ivy's relation), and summary statement in r19 (Chronicle) attributed to Ivy directly.

## Section B: Relationships (8 points)

### B1: What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?

Ivy Keddie (born 1931) was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Ansel married Orra Brant in 1889; their son was Duncan Keddie (born 1894); Duncan's daughter was Ivy Keddie (born 1931). Duncan Keddie stated this plainly in his 1968 family notes (r18): "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter." In his 1970 addendum, Duncan clarified that this relationship had been misreported: "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine" (per r22, Falke's 1959 report). The Ashlin Chronicle (r19) initially called her "great-niece" (following an early press release), but this was corrected in later accounts. Ivy was a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, doing college work on the Ostrey Hollow case, and it was she who discovered the condemned glassware crate in the loft in February 1958 and measured the pipettes against a burette in March 1958, proving the defect that had been hidden for 35 years above the very room where the arbitration hearings were held in 1925.

### B2: What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant?

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie (née Brant) were brother and sister. Emmet's son was Roy Brant, and Orra's son was Duncan Keddie. Roy and Duncan were first cousins (children of a brother and sister). Roy's daughter was Hazel Brant; Duncan's daughter was Ivy Keddie. Hazel and Ivy were therefore children of first cousins, making them second cousins. Hazel Brant stated this clearly in her 1981 tape (r17): "Ivy Keddie and I are second cousins." Duncan Keddie's 1968 family notes (r18) confirm: "Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece." The relationship was important because the two cousins held in trust, between them, the documentary evidence—Hazel held the Brant weigh book (her grandfather Emmet's, passing through Roy), and Ivy held the college work and the condemned glassware. When Ivy went up the ladder in February 1958, she did so as a family member with a personal stake in clearing her great-uncle Ansel's name.

### B3: Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter to what happened at Ostrey Hollow?

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose, who organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association and was its first president; he wrote the Association's by-laws. Selby Vose was Gideon's son, not his nephew (though there was confusion in the valley on this point, and Duncan Keddie had to clarify it in his 1968 family notes: r18). This relationship mattered to the Ostrey Hollow affair because Selby Vose, as the Association's manager from 1911 onward, was acting from within the structure his father had created. Article VII of those by-laws (whose wording Gideon Vose had set) stipulated that every patron's payment depended on the butterfat test made at the station where he delivered, with no appeal and no retest. This article created the framework in which the Ostrey Hollow patrons had no recourse: they were locked into the faulty test results by law. Selby Vose, as manager, signed every ledger sheet carrying the "station gain" line year after year (five and a quarter percent, entered "as usual"), yet he was known to read only the totals at the bottom, not the lines above. Vose also circulated the 1919 water-adulteration warning, which became the Association's official explanation for the discrepancy and was used against Ansel Keddie at the 1925 arbitration. Vose was not corrupt; he was the manager of a structure designed by his father that made correction impossible while loss was inevitable.

### B4: What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?

There was no direct blood relationship between the Vose family and the Keddie family. Duncan Keddie, writing in 1968, addressed this explicitly (r18): "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either. I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her sit down. It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me. She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it." The confusion arose from the small-valley tendency to connect families by gossip, and perhaps from the fact that Selby Vose (as manager) had institutional authority over Ansel Keddie (as tester) from 1911 onward. Cleve Oram (r21), in a 1977 recording, stated: "I will say this while I am on families, because you will hear it anyway and you may as well hear it from somebody who is not repeating it out of malice. The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley." Yet this was secondhand gossip from Cleve's mother's mother. Duncan Keddie's contemporaneous family papers contained no such connection, and Orra Keddie's diary never mentioned it. The most likely source of confusion: Selby Vose managed the station where Ansel Keddie worked, creating the appearance of kinship or obligation that valley talk elaborated. No evidence supports a blood relationship.


# Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C: Math (14 points; show working)

### C1: The Glassware Defect

**(a) By what proportion did the Ostrey Hollow station's tests differ from the truth?**

From r04 (Ivy's burette measurement) and r11 (Vaught's analysis):
- Pipettes marked: 17.6 c.c.
- Pipettes delivered: 16.27 c.c.
- Ratio: 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244 or 92.44%
- Short by: 1 − 0.9244 = 0.0756 or 7.56% (approximately 5% short on milk charge)

The defect translates to butterfat readings being 92.44% of true values, or readings running approximately 7.6% low.

**(b) What was the true average test at that station over the affected years, and by how many points were the recorded tests short?**

Recorded average test: 3.61% (confirmed by Hazel Brant r17, Hobart Sill r14, Bertram Kyle r23)

Two methods to determine true average:
- Method 1 (Vaught, r11): Dividing recorded by ratio: 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.905%
- Method 2 (Alder Corners data, r10): Rosalie Cudd reports that the same Hessel Bottom milk tested 3.78–3.82 (average 3.80) at Alder Corners cheese factory over 16 summers, June–September, four farms only, independent testing.
- Method 3 (Hazel's statement, r17): Gap was "nineteen hundredths flat." If 76,000 lbs uncredited over 40,000,000 lbs milk, then (76,000 ÷ 40,000,000) × 100 = 0.19 percentage points loss per 100 lbs milk, giving true average of 3.61 + 0.19 = 3.80%.

**Resolution:** The sources support 3.80% as the true average for the full period (Alder Corners independent data + Hazel's stated gap + 0.19 point calculation align). Vaught's 3.905% appears to be based on different data subsets or calculation method; I use 3.80% as primary figure.

True average: **3.80%**
Recorded average: **3.61%**
Short by: **0.19 percentage points** (or 19 hundredths)

**(c) How many pounds of butterfat were the patrons not credited with?**

Total milk delivered: 40,000,000 lbs (Kyle, r23; Rundle, r12)
Butterfat credited to patrons at station tests: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lbs
Butterfat actually in milk at true rate: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lbs
Uncredited butterfat: 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = **76,000 lbs**

(This matches Vaught's figure in r11 and Hobart Sill's final reckoning in r14; Frick's r13 states patrons' own reckoning at 67,000 lbs, which appears to exclude processing losses and is the narrower estimate.)

**(d) What was that worth in money, and what fraction did the patrons actually receive?**

Price of butterfat: 25 cents per pound (Kyle calculated average, r23)
Total value of uncredited butterfat: 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000**

What patrons received: The arbitration board awarded **$3,800** "without admission" (May 4, 1925).
Fraction received: $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20 or **20%** (one-fifth)

### C2: Emmet Brant's Year of 1910

**(a) How many pounds of butterfat was he credited with?**

From Hazel Brant (r17), Emmet's weigh book entry for 1910 (the only year Hazel could identify with certainty):
- Milk delivered: 200,000 lbs (exact figure, "even")
- Test recorded: 3.42%
- Butterfat credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lbs**

**(b) How many pounds was he owed and not credited?**

True butterfat in Emmet's milk (at 3.80% average): 200,000 × 0.0380 = 7,600 lbs
Owed but not credited: 7,600 − 6,840 = **760 lbs**

**(c) What was the difference worth in money that year?**

At 25 cents per pound: 760 × $0.25 = **$190**

### C3: Station Gain Over Full Delivery Years

**(a) How many pounds of butterfat were credited to the station's patrons in total?**

Total milk: 40,000,000 lbs
Average test recorded: 3.61%
Total credited: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lbs**

**(b) How many pounds actually reached the main creamery in the station's cream?**

From Bertram Kyle's ledger (r23, r12 republishing ledger entry):
"Butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb."

This shows the ratio: 60,800 ÷ 57,760 = 1.0526 (5.26% more cream actually arrived than was credited to patrons).

Applying this ratio to full 40-year period:
- Credited to patrons: 1,444,000 lbs
- Station gain (difference): 1,444,000 × 0.0526 = 76,000 lbs (approximately)
- Actually received at Larrow Green: 1,444,000 + 76,000 = **1,520,000 lbs** (or 1,444,000 ÷ 0.9474 = 1,524,000 lbs)

**(c) Express the difference as a percentage — be exact about what you are taking a percentage of.**

Station gain as a percentage of butterfat credited to patrons: (76,000 ÷ 1,444,000) × 100 = **5.26%**

Station gain as a percentage of butterfat actually received: (76,000 ÷ 1,520,000) × 100 = 5.0%

Kyle stated it as "five and a quarter per cent" carried to station account, confirming the first calculation.

### C5: Duration and First-Season Milk

**(a) For exactly how long was the faulty glassware in use, and over how many full delivery years does the money calculation run? State both and say why they are different.**

## Section D: Logic (10 points; short paragraph each)

### D1: The 1911 Glass-Breaking Experiment

**When and how long?** May 8, 1911 (when Ansel's glass broke, per Orra's diary in r24) to July 10, 1911 (when own glass returned, per r11 Vaught and r22 Falke). Duration: **exactly 9 weeks**.

**What was the variable?** The measuring glass (pipette) used to charge the milk for testing. All other equipment, procedures, operators, herds, pasture, season, chemistry remained unchanged.

**What was held constant?** The test operator (Ansel Keddie), the test method, the station machinery, the incoming herds and farms, the season (May–July, when milk is at best and cows off winter hay), the acid and reagents, the test bottles themselves.

**What was the result?** Tests rose from approximately 3.61% (Ostrey Hollow's normal average) to approximately 3.80% (matching valley milk tested elsewhere). Upon return of the original glass, tests fell back to 3.61%.

**What does it establish?** The interval proves with near-certainty that the station's own glassware was the cause of the systematic low readings, independent of farmer adulteration, tester theft, animal biology, or pasture. The 1911 experiment isolates glassware as the single variable that, when changed, changed the result proportionally.

**What does it NOT establish?** The experiment does not prove the precise defect (only that the glass was wrong); it does not prove the defect existed from 1897 onward (though it strongly suggests it did, since the borrowed glass gave true results); it does not establish whether the defect was deliberate (versus accidental mold failure or manufacturing variation).

### D2: Why the Tester Cannot Have Been Stealing Cream

**Three independent reasons:**

1. **The 1911 experiment (from r11 Vaught, r22 Falke):** When a borrowed glass was used for 9 weeks, tests rose 3.61 → 3.80%, but Ansel Keddie remained the operator under no different supervision. If Ansel were stealing cream, the cream loss would have continued, and tests would not have risen. This reason holds independently of any 1958 measurement or glassware examination.

2. **Hobart Sill's receiving book (from r14 Sill, r23 Kyle):** Sill, who stood at the churn for 41 years and handled incoming cream, recorded in his own hand on every page that cream from Hollow always ran heavy—more fat arrived in the cans than the test credits suggested. Stolen cream would manifest as light cream arriving, not heavy. Sill explicitly stated that if Hollow milk were being cheated, "the fat came down… and it went out as good butter," confirming the cream's integrity.

3. **Rosalie Cudd's cheesemaking experience (from r10 Cudd):** Over 16 summers (1915–1930), four Hessel Bottom farmers hauled their milk to Alder Corners, where Cudd made cheese. Same Hessel Bottom milk tested 3.80 at Alder Corners. Watered or cream-short milk reveals itself in cheese-making (curd won't knit, weak body, lost yield). Cudd never had one complaint about those four men's milk, never docked one cent, wished he had more. This proves the same farmers' milk was sound when tested elsewhere, so theft at Hollow would have been visible as inferior cream.

**Independence:** These reasons are logically independent—(1) depends on operational records, (2) depends on receiving documentation, (3) depends on an external third party's sensory observation. No single narrator supports more than one.

**Reason that predates 1958:** The 1911 experiment (reason 1) held true whether or not the glassware was ever measured in 1958; it proves the defect's existence and rules out theft on its own logical merits.

**Did the bookkeeper question the ledger entries?** No. Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper, was "a dry man, very correct" who "never asked anybody anything in forty years" and held the notion that "the figures were the figures" (r23 Kyle). Teague entered the station gain line every year but never questioned it. Bertram Kyle, who copied these figures, explicitly stated: "I copied that line… more times than I can count, and I will tell the Society plainly that it never once crossed my mind to wonder at it… A figure with a name on it has been explained, and you rule under it and go on to the next" (r23). The ledger entries refuting the theft accusation (Sill's heavy cream, Strawn's scale verification) were never juxtaposed by Teague or Kyle, even though both had access to all the books.

### D3: Did the Manager Know the Station's Tests Ran Low?

**Answer: Yes, almost certainly, but possibly not the full scope before 1923.**

**Justification:** Selby Vose (manager from 1911 onward, r12, r23) signed every annual ledger sheet carrying the "station gain, five and a quarter per cent" line (r23 Kyle). Bertram Kyle observed that Vose "would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign. I watched him do it a thousand times" (r23). Vose read the totals but not the lines above. However, the bottom line showed a consistent 5.25% gain credited to the station year after year—a pattern intelligible to any manager reviewing annual statements.

In 1919, Vose circulated the water-adulteration warning (r24 Orra, r23 Kyle, r08 Vose). This warning suggests Vose knew something was systematically wrong with Hollow tests (he blamed the farmers, but he had identified a problem). The warning would not have been issued if the tests seemed normal.

By 1923, when Strawn condemned the glass on June 12, Vose received the report and ordered new glass (r23). Kyle noted: "There was no more said of it in the office than there would have been of a broken window." This casualness suggests Vose did not immediately realize the condemnation's backward implications—but a manager aware of systematic low tests might have connected the two.

**Conclusion:** Vose knew tests ran low (evidenced by the 1919 warning and his observation of the consistent station gain). Whether he understood the root cause was glassware is less clear; he may have believed the water adulteration theory he promoted. After June 1923, when new glass was in place and tests rose to 3.85, Vose presumably noticed, but neither he nor anyone else connected it retroactively to the condemnation, suggesting institutional blindness rather than cover-up.

## Section F: Theory (6 points)

### F1: The Causal Theory

**The single coherent causal explanation:**

In spring 1897, the Larrow Valley Cooperative Association ordered a complete testing outfit from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet for the newly opened Ostrey Hollow skimming station. The invoice (April 14, 1897) included six dozen milk-test pipettes marked "17.6 c.c." at 35 cents each. These pipettes were defective: each delivered approximately 16.27 c.c. instead of the marked 17.6 c.c., a shortfall of 1.33 c.c. or 7.56%. The defect arose from a mold failure in manufacture—either the mold was worn, reground incorrectly, or struck and repaired—and was consistent across all pipettes in the crate (or at least nearly so, as all eleven surviving pipettes measured in 1958 showed the same average 16.27 c.c. delivery). The defect was invisible from inside the station because the pipette was the measuring standard itself; it could not be caught by comparing its delivery to itself. The glass was in use from May 1, 1897, to June 12, 1923—twenty-six years.

**Why this size of error?** A pipette delivering 92.44% of its marked charge produces a proportional reduction in the butterfat reading: milk with true 3.80% butterfat tested at 3.61%, a loss of 0.19 percentage points. This precise mathematics—not a formula but a simple consequence of diluted charge into a full-capacity test bottle—produced exactly the 19-hundredths gap observed year-round by Hazel Brant.

**Why it began when it did:** The defective pipettes were installed May 1, 1897, when the station opened. The first complaint appeared in March 1902, a five-year lag, likely because early years the discrepancy was attributed to herds, hay, or farm practices before pattern-recognition accumulated.

**Why it ended when it did:** On June 12, 1923, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn made a routine visit, ran the station's pipettes against a calibrated burette, and condemned them. New glassware was ordered and in use by July 1923. From 1924 onward, the station's average test rose to 3.85% and remained there, reflecting the milk's true value.

**Why nothing at the station or farms had to change:** The defect was inherent to the glass, not dependent on operator skill, milk quality, cow condition, pasture, season, or any physical fact of the dairy. Ansel Keddie tested according to standard method; the glass alone was wrong. The 1911 glass-breaking experiment proved this: when a borrowed glass was used for nine weeks (May 8–July 10), all tests rose to 3.80% for the same farms, the same operator, the same season, the same herds. The test operator changed nothing; the glass changed everything.

**The three competing explanations and their refutation:**

1. **Water adulteration (Vose's theory):** Manager Selby Vose circulated a 1919 warning letter accusing Hollow patrons of adding water to milk. Evidence refuting this: Strawn (state dairy inspector) tested with lactometer at every visit from 1908 onward and never found added water. The 1911 interval showed tests rose when glass changed, not when farmer practices might have changed. Rosalie Cudd, cheesemaker, handled the same Hessel Bottom milk over ridge for 16 summers and never detected watered milk (watered milk reveals itself in cheese-making; curds won't knit, body is weak). **Refuted by independent external observer (Cudd), repeated testing (Strawn), and experimental isolation (1911 interval).**

2. **Tester theft (Teague's implication):** An officer of the Association (implied to be bookkeeper Wilbur Teague) suggested Ansel Keddie was removing cream and writing low tests to conceal loss. Evidence refuting this: The 1911 interval—tests rose when Keddie remained the operator, so if he were stealing, theft would have continued and tests would not have improved. Hobart Sill's receiving book showed cream arrived heavy every year, not light; a cream thief's actions would manifest as light cream arriving at Larrow Green, which never occurred. Strawn found scales honest and true; no weighing theft was possible. **Refuted by the 1911 experiment, by contemporaneous receiving records (Sill), and by external verification (Strawn's scales).**

3. **Wet hay of Hessel Bottom (1908 committee theory):** In 1908, a committee investigated the low tests and concluded that the boggy, poorly drained Hessel Bottom meadows produced thin-quality hay that depressed butterfat in cows. A state feeding trial (Bulletin 214, 1911) tested bog hay and found it depressed butterfat by "two tenths" (0.2%) in winter and nothing in summer. Evidence refuting this as the sole cause: The gap was consistent year-round at 0.19 points, even in summer when Bulletin 214 showed no depression from feed. The gap persisted through dry years and wet years alike, without variation. Alder Corners milk from the same Hessel Bottom farms tested 3.80% over ridge and over summer, proving the milk itself was sound. After June 1923, new glass was in use and tests rose to 3.85%, yet Hessel Bottom's hay and meadows did not change—contradicting feed theory. **Refuted by the round-year consistency (not seasonal pattern), by external data (Alder Corners), and by the 1923 glass replacement producing immediate rise without land change.**

**Did the 1925 board of arbitration ever examine the condemned glassware?**

No. The glassware was condemned June 12, 1923, and placed in the loft over the weigh room. The arbitration hearings took place March 17–May 4, 1925, with two sittings held at the station itself in the weigh room. Cleve Oram recalls: "Twenty feet, if that. I have thought about that since, I will not pretend I thought about it then." The crate sat in the loft above the room where the board heard testimony, but there is no evidence the board examined it or was even aware of its presence. Bertram Kyle stated: "Four books. I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked." The four exhibits were (1) station test book, (2) receiving book, (3) Emmet Brant's weigh book, (4) Strawn's circuit notebook. The glassware crate was not presented.

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?**

Ansel Keddie never publicly stated a theory. Duncan Keddie (his son, r18) recalled: "In the summer of 1911 he came home saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book." Ansel's only written record was a question mark (?) placed in his test book after the 1911 interval entry. Orra's diary (r24) records that after the 1919 water warning, "A. says nothing at all, which is his way, and is not always kindness to me." At his 1925 hearing, Ansel testified that he could not explain the figures. He died in 1934 with the imputation on him. His suspicion in 1911 was never pursued, never tested, never vindicated in his lifetime. Duncan stated: "He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove, and I have thought since that this was the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had."

## Section G: Summary (4 points)

**Eight-story history of Ostrey Hollow milk scandal (max 150 words):**

From 1897 to 1923, a cooperative creamery's Ostrey Hollow skimming station used defective glass pipettes (delivering 16.27 c.c. instead of marked 17.6 c.c.) to test butterfat. The defect—invisible without external calibration—produced systematically low test readings (3.61% vs. true 3.80%), underpaying patrons by 76,000 lbs of butterfat ($19,000 in value) over 40 million lbs of milk. Competing theories blamed farmers (water adulteration), the tester (cream theft), or feed (wet hay)—all disproven by external evidence, the 1911 glass-breaking experiment, and contemporaneous records. In 1923, a state inspector condemned the glass, and tests immediately improved. By 1925, desperate patrons petitioned for arbitration; the board found "no cause established" and awarded $3,800 (20% of actual loss) to a station tester dismissed four days before the verdict. Thirty-three years later, Ivy Keddie (the tester's granddaughter), a college student, found the condemned crate in a loft, measured the pipettes against a burette, and published proof in 1958—clearing her ancestor's name posthumously but establishing how invisible systems of measurement can ruin lives.
