# Test Answers — Part 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math (14 points)

### C1. The glassware defect proportion and its consequences

**(a) By what proportion did the Ostrey Hollow station's tests differ from the truth?**

**Working:** The pipettes were marked 17.6 c.c. but delivered 16.27 c.c. (from Vaught's measurements, r11; Ammon's history, r20; and the Chronicle, r19). The shortfall is 17.6 − 16.27 = 1.33 c.c. The proportion is 1.33 / 17.6 = 0.0756 or **7.56% short**.

Because milk does not evaporate during a butterfat test, a short charge into a bottle marked for a full charge reads low in the same proportion: recorded tests were 7.56% lower than the true butterfat percentage.

**(b) What was the true average test, and by how many points were the recorded tests short?**

**Working:** The recorded average at Ostrey Hollow was 3.61 points (Falke, r22; Kyle, r23). Reverse the error: true average = 3.61 / (1 − 0.0756) = 3.61 / 0.9244 = 3.904 points. The recorded tests were short by 3.904 − 3.61 = 0.294 points or approximately **0.29 points**.

Alternatively, if we use the comparison: Larrow Green averaged 3.78, and the steady recorded difference was 0.19 points, then the true average at Ostrey Hollow would be 3.78 − 0.19 + error = 3.59 + (error from the glass). The 7.56% correction of 3.61 gives 3.904, so shortfall is 0.294, consistent.

**(c) How many pounds of butterfat were the patrons not credited with?**

**Working:** Bertram Kyle found that the station received approximately 40 million pounds of milk over the complaint years (1902–1925, roughly 23 years), averaging 1,600,000 pounds per year. The butter fat test average was 3.61 recorded, but the true average was 3.904. 

Difference per pound of milk: (3.904 − 3.61) / 100 = 0.00294 pounds of butterfat per pound of milk.

Total undercredited: 40,000,000 × 0.00294 = 117,600 pounds of butterfat.

However, different narrators report different total calculations. The Chronicle (r19) states $19,600 in money value; Cleve Oram (r21) mentions "sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat over the whole run of years." The true value depends on the exact years counted and average test used. If we use the 7.56% proportional error on 40 million pounds of milk at average recorded test 3.61: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 × 0.0756 ≈ 109,000 pounds of butterfat. Cleve's figure of 67,000 is lower but plausible if it counts fewer years or lower average test. The sources vary; I estimate **approximately 110,000 pounds of butterfat** (order of magnitude) were undercredited.

**(d) What was that worth in money, and what fraction did the patrons receive?**

**Working:** Kyle notes butterfat averaged "twenty-five cents the pound" over the full run (r23). At 110,000 pounds, the undercredited butterfat was worth approximately 110,000 × $0.25 = **$27,500** (not $19,600, which may be a more conservative estimate). The patrons received $3,800 in the 1925 award, which is $3,800 / $27,500 ≈ **14%** of what they were owed (if using the higher estimate) or $3,800 / $19,600 ≈ **19%** (if using the Chronicle's lower figure).

Resolution of source conflict: Falke (county agent, 1959) and Oram give different totals, likely because they counted different years or used different yields. The $19,600 from the Chronicle (r19) cites the Vaught bulletin and is the most authoritative published figure. Using it: patrons received $3,800 / $19,600 = **19.4%, or approximately 1/5 of their due**.

### C2. The largest patron's undercreditation in 1910

**Working:** Roy Brant (Emmet's son) was on Hessel Bottom. From Hazel Brant's tape (r17), her grandfather Emmet had "the largest piece" of Hessel Bottom. In 1910, recorded test was 3.61 (no variation by year noted). Emmet hauled perhaps 2,000–3,000 pounds of milk per week seasonally (six months, say 150 days, ~200 pounds/day = 30,000 pounds). More plausibly, the largest patron hauled ~100,000 pounds per year (rough estimate from Kyle's 40M/6 farms = 6.7M per farm, or 18,000 pounds/year average; largest would be 30,000–40,000 pounds).

**(a) Butterfat credited:** 100,000 pounds milk × 3.61% = 3,610 pounds of butterfat credited.

**(b) Butterfat owed:** 100,000 pounds milk × 3.904% = 3,904 pounds of butterfat owed. Difference: 3,904 − 3,610 = **294 pounds of butterfat owed but not credited**.

**(c) Money value:** 294 pounds × $0.25/pound = **$73.50 that year** (using Kyle's average price).

**Caveat:** The exact volume hauled by the largest patron is not specified in the sources. The calculation above assumes ~100,000 pounds/year for the largest patron. If the actual figure is 150,000 pounds, the undercreditation would be ~$110; if 50,000 pounds, ~$37. The order of magnitude is tens of dollars per year per farm.

### C3. The station's "gain" as a percentage over full delivery years

**Working:** Bertram Kyle (r23) notes the annual ledger entry: "So many pounds of butterfat credited to the patrons on the station test. So many pounds of butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station cream. And then the difference of the two, entered as station gain... five and a quarter per cent, as usual."

The "station gain" is the difference between credited butterfat and received butterfat. Kyle found 40 million pounds of milk received and states butterfat averaged 3.61% recorded. Total credited butterfat: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 pounds.

If the true butterfat was 3.904%, then received butterfat was 40,000,000 × 0.03904 = 1,561,600 pounds.

"Station gain" (difference): 1,561,600 − 1,444,000 = 117,600 pounds, which is 117,600 / 1,561,600 = **7.53% of the received butterfat** (or 117,600 / 1,444,000 = 8.14% of the **credited** butterfat).

Kyle's notation of "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" may refer to an accounting category separate from the true butterfat difference. The sources vary on whether 5.25% or 7.5% is the "station gain." Most likely, the 5.25% was a standing accounting line for processing losses, and the actual difference from the glass defect was ~7.5%.

**Exact percentage:** Using received butterfat as the denominator (the standard in dairy accounting): **7.53%** of received butterfat was attributed to "station gain" but was actually due to the glass defect.

### C4. The testing outfit invoice

**(a) Total invoice amount:** Junia Ammon (r20) reproduces the invoice dated April 14, 1897:
- 6 dozen pipettes @ $0.35 = $25.20
- 12 dozen test bottles @ $0.22 = $31.68
- 1 acid measure = $0.90
- 1 hand tester (4-bottle) = $18.00
- Freight = $2.15
- **Total = $77.93** (Ammon) or $77.39 (Kyle's reference). Falke says $77.39; Ammon says $77.93. The difference is likely a transcription error. Using Ammon's invoice from the firm's own records: **$77.93**.

**(b) Number of pipettes and shortfall per pipette:**
- 6 dozen pipettes = 72 pipettes total.
- Each marked 17.6 c.c. but delivered 16.27 c.c.
- Shortfall per pipette: 17.6 − 16.27 = **1.33 c.c., or 7.56% short**.

### C5. Duration of the faulty glassware and milk volumes

**(a) Exact duration of use and full delivery years:**
- Installed: May 1, 1897 (or May 1, 1898 per Kyle; sources conflict on whether it's 1897 or 1898; most say 1897).
- Condemned: June 1923 (confirmed by Falke and Ammon).
- Duration: June 1923 − May 1897 = **26 years, 1 month** of use.

**Full delivery years affected:** The 1925 arbitration covered the period of complaint, which began formally in 1902 (though the shortfall existed from the start). Kyle found 40 million pounds over "the long run of years that the patrons afterward complained of"—approximately 1902–1925, or **23 full delivery years**. The glass was in use for 26 years but the monetary claim covered 23 years.

**Why different:** The glass was installed in spring 1897, but the station was not fully operational until later that year, and complaints did not begin until 1902. The arbitration was based on a subset of the full glass-in-use period, namely the years of documented complaint and actionable harm.

**(b) First season milk volume:**
Bertram Kyle (r23) states: "People have asked me what the Hollow took in that first year and I have always said the same: a good eleven hundred thousand pounds, which is what I understood at the time from men who were there. It is not in any book I ever ruled." This refers to the station's first season, before a station account was created and before the sheets were filed with station papers.

**Answer: Approximately 1,100,000 pounds of milk** (but Kyle notes this is not documentary; it is his recollection from testimony at the arbitration).

## Section D — Logic (10 points)

### D1. The 1911 broken-glass interval as an experiment

**When:** May 8, 1911 – July 10, 1911 (9 weeks).

**Variable tested:** The measuring glass used in the butterfat test.

**Held constant:** Same herds, same pasture, same season, same men, same machinery, same tester (Ansel Keddie).

**Result:** When the borrowed glass was in use, the recorded tests rose markedly above their usual level. When the original glass was restored, the tests fell back to their former level.

**What it establishes:** This interval proves beyond calculation that the glass—not the milk, not the tester's honesty, not the farmers' practices, not the hay—was the cause of the persistent low readings. The logic is airtight: change only the glass, all tests rise; restore the original glass, tests fall back. Therefore, the glass is responsible.

**What it does NOT establish:** 
- The exact magnitude of the glass defect (only that it existed).
- Whether the defect was intentional or accidental (only that it was real).
- Specific numbers of pounds of butterfat owed (only that an owed amount existed).
- Why the mold was defective or when the defect originated.

The interval could not have been replicated as a formal experiment (the farmers would not have agreed to borrow a false measure intentionally), so this natural experiment was as close to proof as the valley would get without measuring the glass—which did not happen until 1958.

**Additional significance:** The fact that Ansel recorded the interval with a question mark, not a declaration, shows his character: he was not willing to draw conclusions he could not document. Yet he was also not willing to destroy the evidence by erasing or hiding the notation. The question mark in the ledger became a seventeen-year-old man's son Duncan's salvation and his father's eventual redemption.

### D2. Why the tester could not have been stealing cream

**Reason 1 (from Cleve Oram, r21, and the ledger logic r23):** The cream came off the platform where farmers and public could see it. Cleve, whose father hauled the cream daily, said: "My father hauled that cream and he always said there was less of it coming off that platform than went onto it." But the ledger shows cream arrived at Larrow Green heavier than credited at the station. Hobart Sill (r14) noted "hollow cream arrived heavier than credited." If Keddie stole cream, it would arrive lighter, not heavier. The ledger refutes the theft theory: **stolen cream would reduce, not increase, the "station gain."** This argument holds independently of the glass being measured.

**Reason 2 (from the 1911 interval, r04, r22, r24):** The same tester, same conditions, same cream—but different glass = different test results. Theft would be a human action independent of the glass. If Keddie stole cream to hide its absence with false tests, he would have done so equally with the borrowed glass. But the tests rose with borrowed glass and fell with original glass. **Theft would not vary with the glass; therefore, theft cannot be the cause.** This argument also would hold if the glass had never been measured in 1958 (it is independent of the 1958 finding).

**Reason 3 (from Wilbur Teague's own accounting, r05, r13):** Teague, the bookkeeper, accused Keddie but kept meticulous records. Teague's own ledger shows the annual "station gain" line: credits issued were consistently less than cream received. Teague signed off on these books yearly. If Keddie had been stealing cream, the cream arriving at the creamery would have been light (less cream than credited). But Teague's own records show cream arriving heavy (more cream than credited). **Teague's ledger—which Teague himself kept and which he would have been the first to notice if cream was missing—contradicts his own accusation.** This argument relies on documentary evidence within a single narrator's records.

**Independence of these reasons:** Reason 1 rests on volume; Reason 2 rests on the glass experiment; Reason 3 rests on Teague's own bookkeeping. None depends on the others. Any one of them, alone, defeats the theft theory.

**Did Teague question his own ledger?** No. Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years. That is not a criticism. It was his notion of the position: the figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people." Teague kept records of cream arriving heavier than credited, saw the annual "station gain" enter the ledger, and never questioned whether this pattern meant something was wrong with the test, not the tester.

### D3. Did the manager know the tests ran low before 1923?

**Answer:** Yes, in a limited sense—but he did not understand why.

**Evidence:** Selby Vose became manager in 1911 (Kyle, r23). From 1911 onward, he signed the annual ledger sheets, including the "station gain" line that read "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" (Kyle, r23). He signed these sheets yearly without reading the detail—he "would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign" (Kyle). The "station gain" was consistently present, so Vose could not have been ignorant of a pattern. Yet he did not investigate.

In 1919, Vose himself circulated the water-accusation letter (r23, r24), which proves he believed something was wrong with the milk, not the test. By sending a warning about water, he implicitly accepted the premise that tests were low because farmers were cheating, not because the test was faulty. This suggests Vose knew the tests ran low but blamed the farmers, not the equipment.

**However:** There is no evidence that Vose understood the persistent nature of the defect or connected it to the glassware. The inspector Strawn condemned the glass in 1923 and ordered replacement; Vose accepted the order and paid for new glass without comment (Kyle). Vose never wrote to Ammon & Sons asking why the glass was defective (Ammon replied to the manager's inquiry but Vose is not identified as the writer; Kyle may have written it). There is no record of Vose taking action to investigate the "station gain" pattern.

**Justification:** Vose knew tests ran low (the ledger said so, and he signed it). But knowing a pattern exists and understanding its cause are different things. Vose blamed water and farmers; he did not consider that a piece of glass sitting in the loft might be responsible. The structure of his management—signing totals without reading details—protected him from noticing what Kyle saw immediately: a pattern so regular it had to be mechanical, not human.

## Section E — Contradictions (8 points)

### Direct Factual Contradictions

**E1.1: Date the station opened**
- **Claim 1:** "May 1, 1897" (Orra's diary, r24; Ammon invoice, r20; Hazel Brant, r17).
- **Claim 2:** "May 4, 1896" (Orin Falke, r22).
- **Resolution:** The invoice is dated April 14, 1897, and Orra wrote May 1, 1897. Falke may have confused the date or used a different source. The inventory date (April 1897) points to May 1897 as correct. **May 1, 1897 is more reliable.**
- **Method:** Documentary evidence (invoice, diary) outranks administrative summary (Falke's report).

**E1.2: When the state inspector condemned the glass**
- **Claim 1:** "June 1923" (Falke, r22; Ammon, r20; Junia Ammon's letter dated July 2, 1923 in reply to June 28 inquiry).
- **Claim 2:** "1922" (Bertram Kyle, r23, says "in twenty-two the state man came into the office").
- **Resolution:** Ammon's letter is dated July 2, 1923, in response to an inquiry dated June 28, 1923 (Ammon, r20). The inspector's visit must be June 1923 or earlier, and the letter references "on the twelve of June." **June 12, 1923 is the correct date; Kyle's "1922" is an error.**
- **Method:** Dated letter outranks recollection.

**E1.3: Total award amount from the 1925 arbitration**
- **Claim 1:** "$3,500" (Cleve Oram, r21).
- **Claim 2:** "$3,800" (Bertram Kyle, r23; Orin Falke, r22; Alonzo Frick, r13).
- **Resolution:** Kyle was the clerk who entered the award in the ledger and charged it to the station account. Falke prepared the county's official report using Association records. Frick was the arbitration chairman. Oram was a cream hauler's son who heard it "from my father" (r21). Three independent records (ledger, county report, chairman) agree on $3,800; one hearsay says $3,500. **$3,800 is correct.**
- **Method:** Documentary evidence (three sources) outranks oral transmission (Oram).

**E1.4: The Ivy-Hazel kinship relationship**
- **Claim 1:** "Ivy Keddie was Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed" (Cleve Oram, r21).
- **Claim 2:** "Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins" (Duncan Keddie, r18); Hazel agrees (r17).
- **Resolution:** Duncan was setting down the family genealogy deliberately to correct misstatements. Ivy and Hazel are second cousins (children of first cousins), not first cousins once removed (parent-child plus sibling). Cleve admits he cannot draw the line on paper. **Second cousins is correct.**
- **Method:** Internal authority (Duncan knows his own family tree); Hazel confirms; Cleve is not confident.

**E1.5: Butterfat undercredited totals**
- **Claim 1:** "Sixty-seven thousand pounds" (Cleve Oram, r21).
- **Claim 2:** "$19,600 in money value" (Ashlin Chronicle citing Vaught bulletin, r19); "$27,500" (implied by Kyle's 40M pounds at 3.61% versus 3.904% at $0.25/pound).
- **Resolution:** Different calculation methods yield different totals depending on years included, yield rates, and prices used. Oram's "67,000 pounds" is half of the calculated ~110,000–120,000 pounds; the Chronicle's $19,600 is one of the lower estimates. Sources conflict; the exact total **cannot be determined from the sources.** The consistent finding is: the patrons were owed on the order of tens of thousands of dollars and received ~$3,800, a fraction (roughly 15–25%).
- **Method:** Estimates vary; no single source agrees with all others. The order of magnitude is established; the exact figure is not.

### Contradictions Unique to a Single Narrator

**E1.6: Kyle's internal inconsistency on dates**
- Kyle says (r23) "the state man came into the office... in twenty-two [1922]" but also places this after "In nineteen nineteen there was the circular."
- The letter from Ammon dated July 2, 1923 references a June 28, 1923 inquiry, placing the glass inspection in June 1923, not 1922.
- **Kyle's date is an error; he misremembered or misdated this event by a year.**

### Cases of Two Narrators Carrying the Same Wrong Value

**E1.7: Oram and Kyle both say the award was delivered/charged before Keddie knew**
- This is not a "wrong value" but a factual alignment: both say the award was made without Keddie being told first or having a chance to appeal. Kyle says "charged to the station account" (r23); Oram notes Keddie came home "at his ordinary hour" and asked about supper without mentioning the award (r21). The sources confirm the sequence: dismissal April 30, award announced May 1 (implied in r24 and r23).

## Section F — Theory (6 points)

### F1. The causal theory

**The defective glassware caused the entire 26-year shortfall. No other factor was necessary or sufficient.**

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association purchased a complete testing outfit from Ammon & Sons on April 14, 1897, for $77.93. The outfit included six dozen milk-test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. These pipettes were installed at the Ostrey Hollow station and put into use May 1, 1897. Due to a manufacturing defect—most likely a reground mold that was imperceptibly misaligned—the pipettes delivered only 16.27 c.c., not 17.6 c.c., a shortfall of 7.56%. This defect was consistent across all sixty pipettes, and they remained consistent throughout 26 years of use (1897–1923).

In butterfat testing, a short charge of milk into a test bottle whose neck is marked for a full charge yields a butterfat reading short in the same proportion: 7.56% short. Every test at Ostrey Hollow, for 26 years, read 7.56% low because the glass would not deliver the full charge. The recorded average test of 3.61 points was actually 3.90 points true. The persistent shortfall of 0.19 points (19 hundredths) observed from 1902 onward was caused entirely by the glass.

The size of the error (7.56%, or 0.19–0.29 points) is explained by the glass defect exactly: a mathematical consequence of the pipette's geometry and the test protocol.

The error began when it did (1897) because that is when the defective pipettes were installed. The error ended when it did (June 1923) because that is when state dairy inspector Merle Strawn condemned the glass and ordered replacement. The fact that nothing at the station or on the farms had to change for the error to begin or end proves that the cause was mechanical (the glass), not biological (the herd), agricultural (the hay), or moral (the tester's honesty or the farmers' practices).

### Three competing explanations and their refutation

**1. Water addition (advanced by Selby Vose, Association manager, 1919; r23, r24)**
- **Specific evidence that defeats it:** State dairy inspector Merle Strawn tested milk with a lactometer at every circuit visit from 1908 onward and never once found added water at the Ostrey Hollow station (Falke, r22). A lactometer test is specific and definitive for adulteration. If water had been added, Strawn would have detected it. Therefore, water addition did not occur.

**2. Cream theft by the tester (advanced by Wilbur Teague, Association bookkeeper, at the 1925 hearing; r13, r05)**
- **Specific evidence that defeats it:** The ledger shows cream arriving at Larrow Green heavier than credited at the station. If Ansel Keddie had stolen cream, it would arrive lighter. Teague's own records contradict Teague's accusation (Kyle, r23). Additionally, the 1911 interval (May–July) shows that the same tester, with the same cream, produced different test results when a different glass was used. Theft is a human action independent of which glass is in use; it would not vary with the glass. Therefore, the tester was not stealing cream.

**3. Bog hay depressing butterfat (advanced by a committee in 1908, r24; by veterinarian Dr. Roland Kepp in 1926, r16; cited as a residual cause by county agent Orin Falke initially, r22)**
- **Specific evidence that defeats it:** College Bulletin No. 214, published by the State Agricultural College in 1911 and cited in the 1958 Vaught bulletin, documented a feeding trial on bog hay. Its finding: "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture" (r22, note added). Five hundredths (0.05 points) in winter and zero in summer cannot account for the year-round 0.19-point shortfall. The Hessel Bottom farms used bog hay, but it accounted for at most 0.05 points of loss, not 0.19. Therefore, hay alone cannot explain the observed shortfall. Orin Falke, upon receipt of Bulletin No. 214 in 1959, explicitly withdrew his earlier concession that hay was "a real if partial factor" (r22).

### Subsidiary questions

**Did the 1925 board of arbitration ever examine the condemned glassware?**
**Answer: No.** The glassware was condemned by state inspector Strawn in June 1923 and moved to the loft above the station's intake room. The arbitration hearings were held in March–April 1925, two years later. Two of the three sittings were held at the station itself, in the weigh room, with the crate sitting ten feet directly above the hearing room (Cleve Oram, r21: "Twenty feet, if that... and the crate was up the ladder over their heads the entire time"). Yet no one examined it. Bertram Kyle, who carried the documents for the board, notes: "I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second [receiving book] beside the fourth [state inspector's notebook] and looked" (r23). The board was presented with evidence that should have guided them (Strawn's 1923 notation that the glass was faulty) but either did not recognize it or did not connect it to the shortfall. **The glassware was not examined.**

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?**
**Answer: He suspected the glass.** In summer 1911, when the borrowed glass was in use and tests rose, Ansel Keddie noted the change in the test book. But characteristically, he wrote only a question mark—a sign of suspicion that he would not elevate to a claim without proof. Duncan Keddie (his son) recorded his father's caution: "He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove, and I have thought since that this was the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had" (r18). Orra recorded in her diary that in 1923, when new glass was installed, "He has been in this hollow the whole of his life and has never yet seen them mend a roof before it fell in"—a comment suggesting he noticed the replacement (r24, July 1923 entry). But Ansel never made a formal accusation. Duncan notes: "He had, after all, nothing to hold in his hand but a suspicion about a piece of glass, and against it stood the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county. So he kept on" (r18). **Ansel suspected the glass but kept silent, lacking proof.**

## Section G — Summary (4 points)

**G1. Summary (max. 150 words)**

From 1897 to 1923, the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association's Ostrey Hollow station used defective pipettes, purchased from Ammon & Sons, that delivered 7.56% less milk than their marked capacity. Every butterfat test ran low by that proportion, causing farmers to be undercredited by approximately $20,000 over 26 years. The deficit sparked three theories—water addition, cream theft, bad hay—all disproven by evidence from the station's own records and a 1911 interval when borrowed glass yielded higher tests. In 1925, the board of arbitration awarded patrons $3,800 without finding cause and dismissed the tester, Ansel Keddie, though two years earlier the state inspector had already condemned the glassware. In 1958, Ivy Keddie found the condemned glass in the loft, had it measured against a calibrated burette, and proved the defect explained the entire shortfall. Ansel's name was cleared twenty-four years after his death. Eight separate retellings were needed because each narrator knew only part of the story, and only by integrating records (ledgers, diaries, bulletins) could the full picture emerge.
