### C1. The Proportion of Shortfall

**(a) By what proportion did the defective pipettes deliver short, and how?**

The pipettes were marked "17.6 c.c." (the standard charge for a butterfat test). The nine surviving pipettes averaged 16.27 c.c. (r19). Shortfall = 17.6 − 16.27 = 1.33 c.c.

Proportion: 1.33 / 17.6 = 0.0756 = **7.56%** (rounding to 7.6%).

This is how the shortfall was derived: Ivy Keddie measured the pipettes against a calibrated burette at Farland in March 1958 (r19, r22).

**(b) True average test and shortfall in points:**

Station's recorded average: 3.61 butterfat % (r22).
System-wide (Larrow Green) average: 3.78 (r22).
Recorded deficiency: 0.19 percentage points (r22).

Because 7.6% less milk entered each test bottle, the fat percentage read 7.6% low (fat concentration is a ratio; less milk means less total fat, hence lower percentage). If the true test were T, the recorded test would be 0.924T (i.e., 92.4% of true).

Recorded = 3.61.  
True average = 3.61 / 0.924 = 3.91.

Shortfall in percentage points = 3.91 − 3.61 = **0.30 percentage points**.

**Reconciliation:** Kepp and Falke both cite evidence of ~0.19 to 0.21 shortfall in field observations. The mathematical calculation (0.30) is higher, likely because the recorded 3.61 average already includes whatever variation and higher-test years existed (e.g., the 1911 borrowed-glass interval showed 3.80, suggesting summer peaks). The 0.19 figure likely represents the modal or long-run systematic bias; the calculation 0.30 represents the full mathematical correction.

For C1(c) I will use the recorded 0.19 point shortfall (from r22, the most explicit statement) but note the tension.

**(c) Pounds of butterfat never credited:**

Total milk received at Ostrey Hollow over the affected years: 40 million pounds (r23, covering the "long run of years" of complaint). This figure is derived from Kyle's recovery of the receiving book data for the arbitration board.

Recorded butterfat average: 3.61% (r22).
True butterfat average: ~3.80% (interpolating the 1911 interval's 3.80 and correcting for systematic shortfall).

Actually, let me use the sources more directly. The *Ashlin Chronicle* [r19] states: "The figure of the gap...was twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21 — between the milk of that station and the milk of this valley at large." Falke [r22] confirms the deficiency was 0.19 percentage points. Using 0.20 (middle ground) over 40 million pounds received:

Pounds short = 40,000,000 lbs × 0.0020 = **80,000 lbs of butterfat**.

However, *Ashlin Chronicle* [r19] calculates "nineteen thousand six hundred dollars ($19,600)" and states "61 pounds of butterfat was what they made it" over the whole run of years—this phrasing is garbled in the source. *Falke* [r22] later recalculates and mentions "60-something thousand pounds" (paraphrasing; the source says "sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat" in r21 Cleve Oram's recording). ## Section D — Logic (10 points)

### D1. The 1911 Interval as Experiment

**When and duration:** May 8 to July 10, 1911 (nine weeks, or approximately six to seven weeks, depending on whether the 8th and 10th are inclusive) [r22, r24, r16].

**The variable:** The station's measuring glass (the 17.6 c.c. pipettes).

**What was held constant:** The herds (same cows), pasture (Hessel Bottom, unchanged), season (May–July flush, when butterfat typically peaks), tester (Ansel Keddie, same man), machinery (the vat, separator, tester's technique), and supervision (no change in oversight or procedure).

**The result:** The station's tests "stood markedly higher" during the nine weeks with the borrowed glass, then "fell back to their former level" when the station's own glass was resumed [r22]. Specifically, Hazel Brant [r16] quotes Kepp: "in the summer of 1911 the testing at that station was broken off for six weeks, their own apparatus having failed them and a borrowed one being used, and the figures stood higher during those six weeks than before or after."

**What it establishes:** That the station's own equipment, not the herds, not the milk, not the men, not the management, caused the low tests. Every other potential cause remained constant; only the glass changed, and the tests rose proportionally. This is clean experimental evidence.

**What it does NOT establish:** It does not prove that the glass was wrong from the beginning (1897). It does not establish the magnitude of the error (though inference from before/after would suggest ~0.19 to 0.30 percentage points). It does not address whether other factors (wet hay, water, cream loss) might have contributed a portion of the shortfall, though the consistency of the 0.19 point deficit year-round (including summer) suggests a systematic measurement error rather than a seasonal feed effect. Kepp's 1926 objection—that six weeks of summer data cannot generalize to winter—misses the logical force: the same tester, same herds, same everything except the glass, produced different results; therefore the glass was the variable, not the season.

**Sources:** r22 (Falke's timeline and interpretation), r24 (Orra's diary, May 8, 1911), r16 (Kepp's discussion and dismissal of its significance).

### D2. Why the Tester Cannot Have Been Stealing Cream

**Three independent reasons:**

1. **The 1911 interval:** Same tester, same herds, same supervision, but with a different glass for nine weeks—the figures rose. If Ansel were removing cream, the credit would remain low regardless of the glass. The fact that the figures rose to normal during the interval proves the low figures were instrumental (caused by the glass), not behavioral (caused by theft). [r22, r24, r16]

2. **The state dairy inspector's lactometer tests:** Merle Strawn tested with the lactometer at every visit from 1908 forward and never found added water at the station, which would be the natural way to mask stolen cream volume. The lactometer (measuring specific gravity) would detect watered milk but is independent of the butterfat test. If cream were being stolen, the milk would be concentrated fatter (less volume means same fat = higher %), but no evidence of this appears in Strawn's record. [r22]

3. **Hobart Sill's contrary observation:** The buttermaker at Larrow Green [r14] testified that cream from Ostrey Hollow arrived consistently *heavier* than the station's papers recorded—the opposite of what theft would produce. If Ansel had removed cream, the cans would arrive lighter. Sill believed water was added to compensate, not that cream was stolen. This independent witness (who had no direct knowledge of Ansel and worked at a different facility) contradicts the theft hypothesis. [r14]

**Independence:** Each of these three derives from a different kind of evidence—(1) the 1911 instrumental interval is experimental; (2) Strawn's independent chemical test is technical and external; (3) Sill's testimony is from a different facility and contrary to the theft theory. None logically depends on the others.

**Which survives without the glassware measurement?** Reason (1), the 1911 interval, would still hold if the glassware had never been measured in 1958. The interval shows that the glass, not the tester, caused the low figures. This argument does not depend on knowing the glass was defective; it depends only on observing that a different glass produced different results.

**Did the bookkeeper question the ledger entries?** Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper [r23], "never asked anybody anything in forty years. That is not a criticism. It was his notion of the position: the figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people." Teague wrote the "station gain" line every year—5.25%, consistent, with no variation. Kyle notes: "I copied that line, or the like of it, more times than I can count, and I will tell the Society plainly that it never once crossed my mind to wonder at it" [r23]. The ledger entry that refutes the theft accusation—the consistent "station gain"—was entered by Teague and copied by Kyle without question. Teague never asked why the gain was so regular, suggesting he was a mechanical recorder, not an investigator. No evidence shows he ever questioned or investigated these entries.

**Sources:** r22 (Falke, the 1911 interval and Strawn's lactometer record), r14 (Hobart Sill), r23 (Kyle on Teague's incuriousness).

### D3. Did the Manager Know the Tests Ran Low Before the Glassware Was Condemned?

**Answer:** Almost certainly yes; the sources support this conclusion strongly, though not with absolute proof.

**Justification:**

Selby Vose became manager in 1911 [r23]. The complaint had been public and continuous since 1902, discussed at annual meetings, debated at crossroads stores, and known to be a significant management problem [r15]. Vose was the son of the founder and would have had access to the Association's records and meetings.

In June 1919, Vose personally signed the circular warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons about water in their milk [r23, r24]. This letter signals that he believed the station's tests might be wrong and attributed it to deliberate misconduct (added water) by the patrons. Why would a manager send such a letter if he did not suspect the tests were low and needed explanation? 

Furthermore, Bertram Kyle [r23], the clerk who sat outside Vose's door for 25 years, explicitly states: "He read the totals and he never read the lines above them...I watched him do it a thousand times." Vose signed the annual statement sheets, which carried the "station gain" line (5.25%, as usual) every year. The "station gain" is the difference between butterfat credited by test and butterfat received at Larrow Green—it represents a discrepancy, however normal. Vose's consistent signing-off on figures that showed lower testing at Ostrey Hollow, combined with his 1919 warning letter, suggests he knew something was systematically wrong at that station.

**However:** The sources do not show that Vose connected the low testing to the equipment. The manager's knowledge was empirical (the tests are low) not causal (the glass is wrong). The 1923 state inspection and condemnation of the glass occurred during Vose's tenure but, as Kyle notes, "no more said of it in the office than there would have been of a broken window." No one made the retroactive connection, and Vose died before the 1958 discovery. His knowledge was of effect, not cause; of problem, not solution.

**Sources:** r23 (Kyle's testimony on Vose's reading habits and the 1923 condemnation response), r24 (Orra's diary on the 1919 letter), r15 (the public complaint history), r22 (Vose's role as manager and the arbitration board's response).

## Section F — Theory (6 points)

### F1. The Causal Theory of the Ostrey Hollow Shortfall

**The unified theory:**

From 1897 to 1923, the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association used milk test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. that delivered only approximately 16.27 c.c. on average (a 7.6% shortfall). This shortfall was the result of a manufacturing error at Ammon & Sons: the mold had been reground and the pipettes were cast wrong, undetected by the glassblower or quality checks. When 7.6% less milk enters a test bottle marked for the full volume, the butterfat percentage reads proportionally lower: a milk sample that was actually 3.80% fat would register as 3.51% because there is less total fat in less milk. This systematic measurement error produced a consistent 0.19 to 0.21 percentage point shortfall at Ostrey Hollow every month of the year, regardless of season, herd, pasture quality, or management. The error began when the equipment arrived in 1897, continued unchanged until 1923, and ended only when state dairy inspector Merle Strawn condemned the glass and the Association ordered new equipment. No herds changed, no farms changed management, no milk composition changed—only the measurement instrument mattered.

**Why the size of the error (0.19–0.21 percentage points)?**

The defective pipettes delivered 16.27 c.c. instead of 17.6 c.c., a shortfall of 1.33 c.c. or 7.56%. A proportional error in measurement produces a proportional error in the result: ~0.19 percentage points shortfall on a baseline of ~3.80% fat milk. The consistency of this error across all months (June and February alike) confirms the cause is instrumental, not biological or seasonal.

**Why did it begin in 1897?**

The defective pipettes arrived with the station's original equipment on April 14, 1897. Ansel Keddie took them into use. No retrospective review of the equipment occurred, and the error was never detected until Strawn's 1923 inspection.

**Why did it end in 1923?**

State dairy inspector Merle Strawn, during a routine circuit visit on June 12, 1923, ran the station's pipettes against a calibrated burette as part of standard inspection. The glass failed to deliver its mark. Strawn condemned the entire lot and directed the manager to order new equipment. New pipettes came within a month. From 1924 onward, the station's average test stood at 3.85, consistent with the rest of the valley and matching the 1911 interval peak. The error ended because the equipment was replaced.

**Why nothing at the station or farms had to change for the error to begin or end?**

The error was entirely instrumental. The herds, management, milk composition, pasture, and season never changed between 1897 and 1923 (the 1911 interval proves this; tests rose with different glass, fell with the original glass, all while herds and pasture remained constant). The introduction and removal of the defective pipettes are the only variables that explain the 26-year deviation and its sudden cessation.

---

### Three Competing Explanations

**Explanation 1: Water in the milk**

Proposed by: Selby Vose, the Association's manager, who circulated a warning letter in June 1919 [r24, r23].

Evidence that defeats it: Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, tested with the lactometer at every visit from 1908 onward and never once found evidence of added water at Ostrey Hollow [r22]. The lactometer (measuring specific gravity) detects watered milk through reduced density. No positive finding of water supports the theory. Additionally, added water would produce variable results depending on the amount added and the season; the 0.19 point shortfall was uniform month to month [r22], inconsistent with deliberate adulteration.

**Explanation 2: Cream theft by the tester**

Proposed by: Officers of the Association at the 1925 arbitration hearing [r22].

Evidence that defeats it: (a) The 1911 interval: the same tester using a different glass for nine weeks produced higher figures, proving the measurement instrument, not the operator, caused the low results [r22, r16]. (b) Strawn's lactometer tests (same source as above for water) showed no tampering [r22]. (c) Hobart Sill, the buttermaker at Larrow Green, observed that cream from Ostrey Hollow arrived consistently *heavier* than the station's papers recorded—the opposite of what theft would produce [r14]. Sill believed water was added (to compensate for removed cream), not that cream was stolen outright [r14].

**Explanation 3: Wet hay reducing milk fat**

Proposed by: Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee and reissued in Grigg's 1919 covering letter; supported by Dr. Roland Kepp's 1926 veterinary paper [r15, r16].

Evidence that defeats it: Bulletin No. 214, the State Agricultural College's own feeding trial on bog hay, showed that bog hay depressed butterfat test by two tenths in winter feeding and nothing measurable in summer [r22 note, r15]. Two tenths cannot account for the 0.19 to 0.30 percentage point shortfall at Ostrey Hollow, which was uniform year-round including summer [r22]. Orin Falke, the county agent, initially conceded wet hay as a partial factor but withdrew that concession after reading Bulletin No. 214 [r22]. Furthermore, the 1911 interval—when herds remained on Hessel Bottom eating the same wet hay for nine weeks—showed tests rise with a different glass, proving the hay was not the variable causing the low figures [r22, r16].

### Two Subsidiary Questions

**(1) Did the 1925 arbitration board examine the condemned glassware?**

No. The glassware was condemned by Strawn in June 1923 and placed in the loft over the station's intake room. The arbitration board hearings occurred in March–April 1925, with two sittings held at the station itself in the weigh room [r24, r21]. Bertram Kyle carried four books to the board: Ansel's test book, the receiving book, Emmet's weigh book, and "the state inspector's notebook for the year he condemned the glass" [r23]. Yet Kyle adds [r23]: "I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked"—meaning no one compared the documents to make the connection. Most critically, the condemned glass crate itself sat physically in the loft above the weigh room where the board sat for two days [r21], but no member of the board asked to examine it or pulled it down for measurement [r22, r19]. The board found "no cause" and awarded money "without admission." Had the board examined or even inspected the glass, the entire hearing would have been different. **The board did not examine the glassware.**

**(2) What did the tester himself believe the cause was?**

Ansel Keddie believed the problem was in the glass. In the summer of 1911, when the station's glass broke and a borrowed measure was used, tests rose. Ansel came home that day "saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book" [r18]. He marked a question mark in the station's record but did not write the suspicion down in words, bound as he was by the principle that he "would not put down what he could not prove" [r18]. His wife Orra recorded in March 1902 that "he said no, but it is my figures they will look at"—worry that his data would be scrutinized, not a defense of innocence [r24]. Ansel never publicly stated his suspicion during the 1925 hearing and was not asked [r13]. When confronted by Orra's brother Emmet over the water-warning letter in 1919, Ansel did not reveal his suspicion to Emmet; he only said he had carried the letter himself because he would not have another man carry it [r24]. Ansel took his suspicion to his grave in 1934. Duncan Keddie wrote: "He said to me once and only once, and not lately, that a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name" [r24]. Ansel believed the glass was wrong but never proved it and died with the imputation on him.

## Section G — Summary (4 points)

### G1. The Eight-Story History (150 words maximum)

**The Ostrey Hollow Dairy Testing Scandal: A Eight-Decade Reckoning**

In 1897 the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery opened a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, paying farmers by butterfat test. The station received defective glass pipettes from Ammon & Sons (marked 17.6 c.c., delivering only 16.27 c.c.)—a 7.6% shortfall that produced systematic low-test readings month after month for 26 years. By 1902 farmers complained; three competing theories emerged: added water, cream theft, wet hay. All were investigated, none were proven. In 1911 the station's glass broke; a borrowed measure showed normal tests—yet no one connected this to the equipment. Tester Ansel Keddie suspected the glass but proved nothing and kept records meticulously. In 1923 a state inspector condemned the glass; new equipment came; tests rose to normal. But the office never linked past and present. In 1925 the arbitration board, sitting directly above the condemned crate in the loft, found "no cause" and dismissed Ansel. His granddaughter Ivy discovered the glass in 1958, measured it, and published Bulletin 471, vindicating Ansel 24 years after his death.
