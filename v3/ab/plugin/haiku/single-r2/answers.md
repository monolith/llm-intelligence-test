# Answer Sheet — Part 1: Section A Stories 1–4

## Section A — Reconstruction (50 points)

### Story 1: The Founding and Station Setup (1889–1897)

Farmers in the Larrow Valley organized a cooperative creamery association in 1889, building the main creamery at Larrow Green in 1891. The association adopted Article VII making payment contingent on butterfat test content, not milk weight. In 1895–1896 they constructed a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up-valley, to serve Hessel Bottom's six farms. The station opened May 4, 1896 (per r22; some sources say May 1, 1897, but Bertram Kyle's office records [r23] indicate May 4, 1896 as opening date; the confusion may reflect when various records began or when the first outfit was received). Ansel Keddie, a careful and methodical man, began as tester and separator from the station's opening and would hold that post for 29 seasons.

The association purchased its testing equipment from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet on April 14, 1897. The invoice [r20] totaled $77.93 (one source says $77.39 [r19], but the invoice facsimile [r20] shows $77.93, taking precedence over summaries). The outfit included six dozen milk test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c., twelve dozen test bottles rated 8 per cent neck, one acid measure, and one hand tester for four bottles. The pipettes came from a mold that, undetected at manufacture, had been reground short—each delivering approximately 16.27 c.c. instead of 17.6 c.c., a shortfall of 1.33 c.c. or 7.6% (calculated from r19: average of nine surviving pipettes). Ansel received his weigh book from Emmet Brant and kept meticulous records from the start, noting when the station's scale agreed with his own [r09]. The station stood on a flat by the creek where the road bent, with an intake room, vat, separator, and a covered loading platform [r21]. Over the intake room was a loft accessible by ladder [r21, r17].

**Documents:** r20 (invoice), r17 (Hazel Brant on the book and glass), r09 (Roy Brant on Emmet's practice), r21 (Cleve Oram on the station's layout), r23 (Bertram Kyle on May 4, 1896 opening).

### Story 2: The Long Complaint Begins (1902–1908)

From "an early date" [r22] Ostrey Hollow's milk tested low. By 1902 complaints were recorded; they continued in every decade thereafter. The station's average over the complaint years stood at 3.61 butterfat, against Larrow Green's 3.78—a deficiency of 0.19 percentage points [r22]. This 0.19 shortfall ran uniformly in every month: June flush and February depth alike [r22]. The six Hessel Bottom farmers were among the Association's poorest patrons; their land was wet bog ground, heavy with sedge and coarse grasses [r15, r16, r21]. Four of the six farms hauled to the cheese factory over the ridge in summer and back to Ostrey Hollow the rest of the year [r17].

In 1908 the Association formed a committee of practical men—Nyle Grigg, chairman—to investigate. Grigg and the committee sampled herds across 1908, visiting the farms unannounced, watching tests, checking scales, and examining water supplies [r15]. They found no dishonesty anywhere, but observed that the stock on wet land came out of winter thin and slow to condition. Their conclusion: the wet hay of Hessel Bottom thins the cream [r15]. The committee's report was accepted and closed the question for eleven years [r15]. Grigg would later note [r15] that when he reissued the report in 1919, the State Agricultural College's Bulletin No. 214 had vindicated his finding: bog hay depressed butterfat test by two tenths in winter feeding.

Emmet Brant, Roy's father, owned the largest piece of Hessel Bottom and hauled the most milk [r18]. Ansel Keddie, the tester, was married to Orra Brant—Emmet's sister [r24, r18]—and had been in the Brant family's orbit since before their son Duncan was born [r24]. In March 1902, when talk at the store turned to Ostrey Hollow's low testing, Ansel came home and would not eat, telling Orra "it is my figures they will look at" [r24]. The complaint was perfectly just and never disputed [r16].

**Documents:** r22 (Falke's chronology), r15 (Grigg's 1919 reissue), r16 (Kepp's 1926 paper), r24 (Orra's diary, March 1902), r17 (Hazel Brant on Hessel Bottom), r21 (Cleve Oram on the four farms).

### Story 3: The 1911 Interval—The Natural Experiment (May–July 1911)

On May 8, 1911, the station's own measuring glass broke [r24]. A measure borrowed from elsewhere was used for nine weeks until July 10 [r22]. This interval proved decisive. Through those nine weeks the station's tests stood markedly higher. On the day the station resumed its own glass, they fell back to their former level [r22]. The herds, pasture, season, men, and machinery were identical throughout. Ansel noted the fact in his book and marked a question mark [r22, r13]. Hazel Brant recalled: "The testing at that station was broken off for six weeks, their own apparatus having failed them and a borrowed one being used, and the figures stood higher during those six weeks than before or after" [r16, quoting in Kepp's 1926 paper].

The 1911 interval is the cleanest evidence that the station's own equipment, not the herds or the milk or the managers, caused the low tests. Yet for fourteen years afterward, Ansel kept using the short-delivery glass, wrote down every low figure, and this same meticulous record would later be used against him. He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove [r18]. When he came home cross about the broken measure in May 1911, he said nothing more to his wife Orra [r24]. By 1925, when the arbitration board was asked to review the 1911 interval, Kepp (speaking in 1926) dismissed it as "six weeks in the flush of the season" when "every herd in the county is at its best and the stock are off the wet ground and up on the aftermath" and therefore not representative [r16]. This objection misses the experimental design: it was the glass alone that changed, not the season.

**Documents:** r22 (Falke on the 1911 interval dates and results), r24 (Orra's diary, May 8, 1911), r18 (Duncan Keddie on Ansel's reluctance to write down unproven things), r16 (Kepp's dismissal of the interval as summer data), r13 (Frick on the arbitration and its treatment of 1911).

### Story 4: Three Competing Theories Emerge (1919–1926)

In June 1919 the Association's manager, Selby Vose (son of founder Gideon Vose [r23]; one source incorrectly called him nephew, but Bertram Kyle confirms he was son [r23]), sent nine letters to nine families on Hessel Bottom warning of water in milk [r24, r23]. The letters were delivered by Ansel himself, not mailed; he stood at each door while the man read it, then came home at 11 PM with his hat still on [r24]. Orra did not blame the manager for sending it, only reflected: "I said it is not your cows and not your hands, and he said no, but it is my figures they will look at" [r24]. Two days later, Orra's brother Emmet confronted Ansel: "You have hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and you knew it better than any man living, and then he asked him why he had carried the paper himself if he did not believe it" [r24]. Ansel replied he carried it because he would not have another man carry it [r24]. Emmet never entered that house again; the two brothers-in-law did not speak for ten years [r24, r65].

Three theories circulated: (1) the Ostrey Hollow patrons added water to their milk; (2) Ansel Keddie removed cream and wrote low tests to conceal the theft; (3) the wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream. The state dairy inspector Merle Strawn tested with the lactometer at every visit from 1908 forward and never found added water [r22]. The nine-week 1911 interval disproved theft: the same tester made the tests under no different supervision, and the figures rose [r22]. Wet hay remained a plausible hypothesis: the 1908 committee had endorsed it, and in 1926 Dr. Roland Kepp, a veterinary surgeon of thirty years' practice, published a paper defending it, citing Bulletin No. 214 [r16]. Kepp argued that bog hay, being acid, phosphate-poor, and sour, produced mineral-deficient herbage that forced cows to rob their own frames for milk fat [r16].

Yet in June 1923—four years after the water warning—state inspector Strawn came to the station and ran the pipettes against a burette [r22]. They would not deliver their mark. He condemned the whole lot and ordered new glass [r22]. New pipettes came within a month; from 1924 onward the station's average test stood at 3.85 [r22]. No person connected this to the 26 years behind it [r22]. Bertram Kyle, the Association's clerk for 25 years, noted the glass order in the ledger and filed Strawn's condemnation—"no more said of it in the office than there would have been of a broken window" [r23]. The board's later use of Bulletin No. 214 to support wet hay proved inadequate: the College's own feeding trial showed bog hay depressed test by only five hundredths in winter, not the nineteen hundredths at Ostrey Hollow [r22 note].

**Documents:** r24 (Orra's diary, June 1919), r22 (Falke's chronology and theories), r23 (Kyle on the 1923 glass condemnation and office records), r16 (Kepp's defense of wet hay), r15 (Grigg's reissue with Bulletin 214 reference).

### Story 5: The 1925 Arbitration and Dismissal

In January 1925 the Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned the Association [r22]. A board of arbitration was formed; hearings ran from March 17 to May 4 [r22]. Two sittings occurred at the station itself, in the weigh room (the intake room), with men standing outside in the road [r24]. Ansel went down to Ashlin in his good coat—the same one he'd worn since Duncan was married—at three in the morning with no sleep the night before [r24]. The Association's manager, Selby Vose, presented the view that the tester had stolen cream [r23]. Officers of the Association argued the three competing theories. The arbitration board, chaired by attorney Alonzo Frick of Ashlin, heard all parties [r13]. Frick believed Ansel's testimony was credible and found no evidence of theft or collision; he could not establish cause but awarded the patrons $3,800 without admission of any cause [r13, r22, r24]. Kyle, the clerk, carried four books to the board: Ansel's station test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book (lent for the purpose), and Merle Strawn's state inspector notebook for 1923 [r23]. Yet Kyle later reflected: "I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked"—that is, never comparing the 1923 glass condemnation to the 26-year history [r23].

Ansel was dismissed on April 30, 1925—four days before the board announced its award [r24, r18, r22]. He came up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm, hung it behind the shed door, and asked what there was for supper [r24]. Orra wrote: "Twenty-nine seasons and a letter" [r24]. Emmet Brant had sided with the petitioners (his name was on the paper [r24]). The break with Orra's brother, unhealed since 1919, was now definitive: Emmet died in 1929 and they had not spoken in ten years [r24]. Duncan, now a schoolteacher at Ashlin, came to be with his father [r24, r18]. The charge hung over Ansel in Ordell County for the rest of his life.

**Documents:** r22 (Falke, chronology and award), r24 (Orra's diary, March 17 through April 30, 1925), r13 (Frick's recollections), r23 (Kyle's account of the four books and non-connection), r21 (Cleve Oram on the two sittings at the station).

### Story 6: Ansel's Life After Dismissal (1925–1934)

In June 1925, four weeks after dismissal, Ansel began repair work on the cow shed—work that had waited since his father's time [r24]. Orra noted he worked from light to dark and whistled, which he never did when he was paid [r24]. He took carpentry jobs around the valley: mending stairs, gates, doors; Cleve Oram's family, a neighboring family, had him hang a barn door that "swung true for thirty years" [r21]. By October 1927 he was a carpenter at sixty-six, taking what work he could get, earning a quarter of his former tester's wages [r24, r21]. Hobart Sill, the Larrow Green buttermaker, recalled in 1934 [r14] that he had watched Ostrey Hollow cream come in consistently heavier than the station's papers recorded; he believed water was added to cans before testing, but noted that the practice improved in the 1920s when Ansel might have stopped it. (Sill's account is confused about causation and timing; the improvement in 1924 followed the glass condemnation and replacement in 1923, not Ansel's reformed ethics.)

Ansel never publicly defended himself. Duncan wrote [r18]: "He came home that summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book." Ansel's own suspicion lay dormant in his question mark. In 1934, Orra recorded in her diary [r24]: "A. died this morning, at home, in the forenoon, without much pain at the end of it. Duncan came up in the night. There were more at the burying than I looked for, and men shook my hand who had not spoken to him in nine years, and every one of them said what a hand he was with a door. Not one of them said the other thing. Nine years they have had to say it and they did not say it then. He never asked them to. He said to me once and only once, and not lately, that a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."

Ansel Keddie died in 1934, twenty-four years after his dismissal, twenty-three years after the glass was condemned (unknown to him), and with the imputation permanently on him in Ordell County.

**Documents:** r24 (Orra's diary, June 1925 through February 1934), r14 (Hobart Sill's 1934 remarks), r21 (Cleve Oram on Ansel's carpentry and death), r18 (Duncan Keddie on Ansel's 1911 suspicion).

### Story 7: The Glass in the Loft (1923 and 1958)

In June 1923 state inspector Merle Strawn condemned the station's original glassware and ordered replacement [r22, r23]. The crate of condemned glass was placed in the loft over the station's intake room, covered with a tarpaulin [r17, r21]. For thirty-five years it sat there, gathering dust and chaff, largely forgotten [r19, r21]. Cleve Oram, the cream hauler's son, saw it a hundred times as a boy and put his hand on it [r21]. The crate held six dozen pipettes originally; by the time Ivy Keddie found it, nine survived (five dozen broken or lost to rot and time) [r19]. Nobody connected the 1923 condemnation to the 26 years of low testing that preceded it [r22, r23].

In February 1958 Ivy Keddie, granddaughter of Ansel (Duncan's daughter), a graduate student at the State Agricultural College, went up the ladder and brought the crate down [r17, r19, r21, r22]. She carried the nine surviving pipettes east to Farland and measured them against a calibrated burette in a college laboratory on March 9, 1958 [r19, r22]. Every one marked "17.6 c.c." delivered materially less. The average of the lot was 16.27 c.c., a shortfall of 1.33 c.c. or 7.6%. No milk was watered. No cream was stolen. No hay thinned the cream. A piece of glass—blown wrong at the mold, reground without detection—had delivered 7.6% too little milk into every test bottle for twenty-six years. Every patron on Hessel Bottom had been paid accordingly on the short figures the glass gave. The Commission's own records, unchanged for decades, now stood revealed as arithmetically true but scientifically misleading: they recorded what the glass delivered, not what the milk contained.

The 1923 interval, when Strawn condemned the glass, should have prompted a retroactive review. It did not. No one in the Cooperative's office made the connection [r23]. Fifty-eight years elapsed between the purchase of the defective glass (April 1897) and its measurement (March 1958).

**Documents:** r19 (Ashlin Chronicle, the discovery), r22 (Falke, on the 1923 condemnation and 1958 measurement), r23 (Kyle, on the non-connection in the office), r17 (Hazel Brant, on seeing the crate in the loft), r21 (Cleve Oram, on touching the crate as a boy).

### Story 8: Vindication and Documentation (1958–1964)

In October 1958, Professor Leland Vaught of the State Agricultural College and Ivy Keddie published Bulletin No. 471 [r19, r22]. The bulletin laid out the history, named the three competing theories, showed how each failed, and presented the glassware evidence [r19]. The 1958 *Ashlin Chronicle* headline proclaimed "Old Ordell Wrong Righted" and noted that "this week a bulletin from the State Agricultural College at Farland says he [Ansel] never did anything wrong at all" [r19]. The article calculated that the Ostrey Hollow patrons had never been paid for 19,600 pounds of butterfat [r19]. (Later calculations by county agent Orin Falke [r22] revised this to 67,000 pounds over the entire affected run, but the sources differ; see C1 below.) Ivy was quoted: "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not. He tested the milk in front of the state's own man and he tested it the way he was taught, and the reason his figures would not come right was in a crate over his head the entire time. I would like it printed that he never knew" [r19].

Within months and years, the historical record was compiled. Orin Falke, county agricultural agent, prepared a report for the county board of supervisors in 1959, documenting the history chronologically and noting his initial concession that wet hay might have been a partial factor—a concession he withdrew upon receipt and reading of Bulletin No. 214, which showed bog hay depressed test by only five hundredths, insufficient to account for nineteen hundredths [r22]. Bertram Kyle wrote "What I Saw of the Office" in 1953 for the Ordell County Historical Society, revealing the ledger's hidden-in-plain-sight records [r23]. Cleve Oram recorded his memories in 1977, recalling his father's belief that cream had been stolen—a suspicion later disproven [r21]. Duncan Keddie wrote "Notes for the Family" in 1968, meticulously setting out the family relationships and archive trail [r18]. Junia Ammon, historian of Ammon & Sons, printed the 1897 invoice and the 1923 letter in her firm's 1949 privately printed history, adding her own judgment: "I regret the letter, and that we had nothing better than the letter to send, and that when a customer of a quarter century wrote to this house and asked in effect what had been done to him, the answer he got was that our books would not say" [r20].

Orra Keddie did not live to see her husband's vindication; she died in 1948 [r24 note]. But the record was eventually made whole: the station's books, the state inspector's notebooks, Emmet Brant's weigh book, the Alder Corners test records, and finally the condemned glass itself were gathered in the Ordell County Historical Rooms [r18, r22]. By 1970, the scattered archives had migrated to three buildings: test books at the Historical Rooms, Strawn's notebooks at the State Agricultural College library, and the Alder Corners book at the Vessey County Historical Society [r18].

Ansel Keddie's name was publicly cleared in 1958, twenty-four years after his death. The vindication came not from reopening the 1925 arbitration (which would have been impossible) but from the physical evidence—the glass itself, measured against calibrated standards—that the 1925 board never examined [r22, question F1].

**Documents:** r19 (Ashlin Chronicle, the 1958 announcement), r22 (Falke's 1959 report with Bulletin 214 analysis), r18 (Duncan Keddie's 1968 family notes and archive accounting), r23 (Kyle's 1953 memoir), r20 (Junia Ammon's 1949 firm history and regret over the 1923 letter), r21 (Cleve Oram's 1977 recording).

## Section B — Relationships (8 points)

### B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?

Ivy Keddie was Ansel's granddaughter. Ansel married Orra Brant in 1889. Their son was Duncan, born 1894. Duncan's daughter was Ivy, born 1931 [r18]. Duncan wrote plainly in his 1968 family notes: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise and once heard it said aloud in this town by a man who ought to have known" [r18]. Duncan taught school at Ashlin [r18]. Ivy became a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, where she conducted the research published in Bulletin 471. She was the one who discovered the condemned crate in 1958 and measured the pipettes in March 1958 [r19, r22]. Ansel never knew her (he died in 1934 when she was not yet born). She vindicated him twenty-four years after his death.

---

### B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant?

Ivy and Hazel were second cousins. Hazel Brant was the daughter of Roy Brant. Roy was the son of Emmet Brant and inherited Emmet's weigh book [r09, r17]. Duncan Keddie wrote [r18]: "Roy Brant and I are therefore first cousins — the children of a brother and a sister, which is the whole definition and there is nothing more to it. Roy's daughter is Hazel Brant. My daughter is Ivy. Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins." The sources leave open whether there were any intermediate generations beyond the two Keddies (Ansel and Orra) and two Brants (Emmet and Roy) who created this cousinship. Duncan's genealogy is explicit; nothing in the other sources contradicts it. Hazel, keeper of the weigh book in her own time, lent it to Ivy in 1958, and Ivy used it in her research [r17, r18].

---

### B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter?

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose, the founder and first president of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon organized the association and wrote its by-laws [r18]. Selby became manager in 1911 and held the office for a quarter of a century [r23]. Bertram Kyle, the clerk who sat outside Selby's door for 25 years, wrote [r23]: "Mr. Selby Vose sat behind that door for a quarter of a century and I sat outside it, and I liked him...He was courteous to every man who came into that office, farmer or drummer or bank...And he read the totals and he never read the lines above them...I watched him do it a thousand times and thought nothing of it, because that is what managers were." (One source incorrectly identifies Selby as Gideon's nephew [r18], but Kyle explicitly corrects this: "He was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president—the son, whatever is said at Ashlin" [r23].) 

This matters because Gideon Vose, as founder, had established the payment-by-test system that made the low Ostrey Hollow tests economically damaging to the patrons. Selby, as manager, signed off on the ledgers containing the steady "station gain" entries and approved the 1919 warning letter about water in milk [r23, r24]. The Vose family stood on the Association's side in the 1925 dispute; Selby's management decisions (signing letters, approving awards, managing communication with the state) shaped the handling of the crisis. His reading only the bottom line of each ledger sheet meant no manager in his position caught the inconsistency between the 1923 glass condemnation and the 26-year history [r23].

---

### B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?

The sources establish no blood relationship. Duncan Keddie wrote [r18]: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses...I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her sit down. It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me." Cleve Oram, the cream hauler's son, reported hearsay [r21]: "And I will say this while I am on families, because you will hear it anyway and you may as well hear it from somebody who is not repeating it out of malice. The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort...My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper." This is unsourced gossip, explicitly labeled as such, and contradicted by Duncan's direct knowledge of his own family. The sources establish no documented relationship between the Vose and Keddie families. Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector whose reports were critical, was "kin to nobody in the valley whatever—not to us, not to the Brants, not to the Voses, not to anybody on the creek" [r18], which underscores that independence of interest mattered and that kinship was traceable enough to note where it existed or did not.



# Answer Sheet — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math (14 points; show working)

### C1. The Proportion of Shortfall

**(a) By what proportion did the defective pipettes deliver short, and how?**

The pipettes were marked "17.6 c.c." (the standard charge for a butterfat test). The nine surviving pipettes averaged 16.27 c.c. (r19). Shortfall = 17.6 − 16.27 = 1.33 c.c.

Proportion: 1.33 / 17.6 = 0.0756 = **7.56%** (rounding to 7.6%).

This is how the shortfall was derived: Ivy Keddie measured the pipettes against a calibrated burette at Farland in March 1958 (r19, r22).

**(b) True average test and shortfall in points:**

Station's recorded average: 3.61 butterfat % (r22).
System-wide (Larrow Green) average: 3.78 (r22).
Recorded deficiency: 0.19 percentage points (r22).

Because 7.6% less milk entered each test bottle, the fat percentage read 7.6% low (fat concentration is a ratio; less milk means less total fat, hence lower percentage). If the true test were T, the recorded test would be 0.924T (i.e., 92.4% of true).

Recorded = 3.61.  
True average = 3.61 / 0.924 = 3.91.

Shortfall in percentage points = 3.91 − 3.61 = **0.30 percentage points**.

**Reconciliation:** Kepp and Falke both cite evidence of ~0.19 to 0.21 shortfall in field observations. The mathematical calculation (0.30) is higher, likely because the recorded 3.61 average already includes whatever variation and higher-test years existed (e.g., the 1911 borrowed-glass interval showed 3.80, suggesting summer peaks). The 0.19 figure likely represents the modal or long-run systematic bias; the calculation 0.30 represents the full mathematical correction.

For C1(c) I will use the recorded 0.19 point shortfall (from r22, the most explicit statement) but note the tension.

**(c) Pounds of butterfat never credited:**

Total milk received at Ostrey Hollow over the affected years: 40 million pounds (r23, covering the "long run of years" of complaint). This figure is derived from Kyle's recovery of the receiving book data for the arbitration board.

Recorded butterfat average: 3.61% (r22).
True butterfat average: ~3.80% (interpolating the 1911 interval's 3.80 and correcting for systematic shortfall).

Actually, let me use the sources more directly. The *Ashlin Chronicle* [r19] states: "The figure of the gap...was twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21 — between the milk of that station and the milk of this valley at large." Falke [r22] confirms the deficiency was 0.19 percentage points. Using 0.20 (middle ground) over 40 million pounds received:

Pounds short = 40,000,000 lbs × 0.0020 = **80,000 lbs of butterfat**.

However, *Ashlin Chronicle* [r19] calculates "nineteen thousand six hundred dollars ($19,600)" and states "61 pounds of butterfat was what they made it" over the whole run of years—this phrasing is garbled in the source. *Falke* [r22] later recalculates and mentions "60-something thousand pounds" (paraphrasing; the source says "sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat" in r21 Cleve Oram's recording). The most explicit source is Oram [r21]: "Sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat was what they made it...that the Hollow men were never paid for."

Using the most direct source: **67,000 pounds of butterfat** never credited to the patrons.

**(d) Money value and fraction actually received:**

Butterfat price over the full delivery years: 25 cents per pound on average (r23, from Kyle's records).

Total value of butterfat never paid for: 67,000 lbs × $0.25/lb = **$16,750**.

Award by arbitration board: $3,800 (r22, r24, r23). One source says $3,500 [r21], but multiple sources say $3,800; the invoice facsimile (r20) shows firm records, so $3,800 takes precedence.

Fraction received: $3,800 / $16,750 = **0.227 = 22.7%**, roughly one-quarter.

*(Note: The sources do not perfectly agree on total butterfat pounds. The Chronicle gives 19,600 (which might be a more conservative earlier estimate), Oram gives 67,000. I use 67,000 as it appears in the 1977 recording, likely from later scholarship, but note the discrepancy.)*

### C2. Emmet Brant's 1910 Loss

**(a) Pounds of butterfat credited:**

Hazel Brant [r17] states: "Nineteen ten. Two hundred thousand pounds even, delivered to the station...paid on a test of three forty-two."

Total milk: 200,000 lbs.  
Test: 3.42%.  
Butterfat credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lbs**.

**(b) Pounds owed and not credited:**

True test (approximately): 3.42 + 0.20 = 3.62% (or 3.80 if we use the 1911 interval as guide; Hazel notes "Three forty-two. You set that against what that milk actually was and you have the whole of the business in one line").

Using 0.20 point correction (conservative):  
True butterfat: 200,000 × 0.0362 = 7,240 lbs.  
Shortfall: 7,240 − 6,840 = **400 lbs owed and not credited**.

(Using the 1911 summer peak of 3.80 as the true average would yield 7,600 − 6,840 = 760 lbs; the true figure likely lies between 400 and 760 lbs.)

**(c) Dollar value of the difference that year:**

400 lbs × $0.25/lb = **$100** (using conservative shortfall).  
If 760 lbs: $190.

The conservative estimate is **$100 that year**.

*(Emmet was the largest patron and bore the largest loss over 26 years; his suspicion and the 1902 entry in Orra's diary reflect this.)*

### C3. Station Gain Calculation

**(a) Total butterfat credited to patrons:**

40 million pounds of milk received × 3.61% (recorded average test) = 1,444,000 lbs of butterfat credited.

**(b) Butterfat that actually reached Larrow Green:**

Assuming 40 million pounds were received and cream was separated at the station, the "station gain" recorded in the ledger every year was "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" (r23). This gain represents the difference between pounds credited by test and pounds received at Larrow Green.

Station gain = 5.25% (r23).  
Pounds credited: 1,444,000 lbs.  
Pounds at Larrow Green: 1,444,000 × 0.9475 = **1,369,230 lbs**.

(The 5.25% gain was legitimate evaporation, handling loss, and normal separation inefficiency, not fraud.)

**(c) Express as a percentage, being exact about the denominator:**

The difference is 1,444,000 − 1,369,230 = 74,770 lbs.

As a percentage of **what was credited to the patrons**: 74,770 / 1,444,000 = 0.0518 = **5.18%**, consistent with "five and a quarter per cent, as usual."

As a percentage of **what reached Larrow Green**: 74,770 / 1,369,230 = 0.0546 = **5.46%**.

The question asks to be exact about the denominator. The ledger entry records station gain as a fraction of **credited pounds**, so the answer is **5.25%** (the stated percentage) or **74,770 lbs / 1,444,000 lbs credited = 5.18%** (calculated from recovered figures).

## Section D — Logic (10 points)

### D1. The 1911 Interval as Experiment

**When and duration:** May 8 to July 10, 1911 (nine weeks, or approximately six to seven weeks, depending on whether the 8th and 10th are inclusive) [r22, r24, r16].

**The variable:** The station's measuring glass (the 17.6 c.c. pipettes).

**What was held constant:** The herds (same cows), pasture (Hessel Bottom, unchanged), season (May–July flush, when butterfat typically peaks), tester (Ansel Keddie, same man), machinery (the vat, separator, tester's technique), and supervision (no change in oversight or procedure).

**The result:** The station's tests "stood markedly higher" during the nine weeks with the borrowed glass, then "fell back to their former level" when the station's own glass was resumed [r22]. Specifically, Hazel Brant [r16] quotes Kepp: "in the summer of 1911 the testing at that station was broken off for six weeks, their own apparatus having failed them and a borrowed one being used, and the figures stood higher during those six weeks than before or after."

**What it establishes:** That the station's own equipment, not the herds, not the milk, not the men, not the management, caused the low tests. Every other potential cause remained constant; only the glass changed, and the tests rose proportionally. This is clean experimental evidence.

**What it does NOT establish:** It does not prove that the glass was wrong from the beginning (1897). It does not establish the magnitude of the error (though inference from before/after would suggest ~0.19 to 0.30 percentage points). It does not address whether other factors (wet hay, water, cream loss) might have contributed a portion of the shortfall, though the consistency of the 0.19 point deficit year-round (including summer) suggests a systematic measurement error rather than a seasonal feed effect. Kepp's 1926 objection—that six weeks of summer data cannot generalize to winter—misses the logical force: the same tester, same herds, same everything except the glass, produced different results; therefore the glass was the variable, not the season.

**Sources:** r22 (Falke's timeline and interpretation), r24 (Orra's diary, May 8, 1911), r16 (Kepp's discussion and dismissal of its significance).

---

### D2. Why the Tester Cannot Have Been Stealing Cream

**Three independent reasons:**

1. **The 1911 interval:** Same tester, same herds, same supervision, but with a different glass for nine weeks—the figures rose. If Ansel were removing cream, the credit would remain low regardless of the glass. The fact that the figures rose to normal during the interval proves the low figures were instrumental (caused by the glass), not behavioral (caused by theft). [r22, r24, r16]

2. **The state dairy inspector's lactometer tests:** Merle Strawn tested with the lactometer at every visit from 1908 forward and never found added water at the station, which would be the natural way to mask stolen cream volume. The lactometer (measuring specific gravity) would detect watered milk but is independent of the butterfat test. If cream were being stolen, the milk would be concentrated fatter (less volume means same fat = higher %), but no evidence of this appears in Strawn's record. [r22]

3. **Hobart Sill's contrary observation:** The buttermaker at Larrow Green [r14] testified that cream from Ostrey Hollow arrived consistently *heavier* than the station's papers recorded—the opposite of what theft would produce. If Ansel had removed cream, the cans would arrive lighter. Sill believed water was added to compensate, not that cream was stolen. This independent witness (who had no direct knowledge of Ansel and worked at a different facility) contradicts the theft hypothesis. [r14]

**Independence:** Each of these three derives from a different kind of evidence—(1) the 1911 instrumental interval is experimental; (2) Strawn's independent chemical test is technical and external; (3) Sill's testimony is from a different facility and contrary to the theft theory. None logically depends on the others.

**Which survives without the glassware measurement?** Reason (1), the 1911 interval, would still hold if the glassware had never been measured in 1958. The interval shows that the glass, not the tester, caused the low figures. This argument does not depend on knowing the glass was defective; it depends only on observing that a different glass produced different results.

**Did the bookkeeper question the ledger entries?** Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper [r23], "never asked anybody anything in forty years. That is not a criticism. It was his notion of the position: the figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people." Teague wrote the "station gain" line every year—5.25%, consistent, with no variation. Kyle notes: "I copied that line, or the like of it, more times than I can count, and I will tell the Society plainly that it never once crossed my mind to wonder at it" [r23]. The ledger entry that refutes the theft accusation—the consistent "station gain"—was entered by Teague and copied by Kyle without question. Teague never asked why the gain was so regular, suggesting he was a mechanical recorder, not an investigator. No evidence shows he ever questioned or investigated these entries.

**Sources:** r22 (Falke, the 1911 interval and Strawn's lactometer record), r14 (Hobart Sill), r23 (Kyle on Teague's incuriousness).

### D3. Did the Manager Know the Tests Ran Low Before the Glassware Was Condemned?

**Answer:** Almost certainly yes; the sources support this conclusion strongly, though not with absolute proof.

**Justification:**

Selby Vose became manager in 1911 [r23]. The complaint had been public and continuous since 1902, discussed at annual meetings, debated at crossroads stores, and known to be a significant management problem [r15]. Vose was the son of the founder and would have had access to the Association's records and meetings.

In June 1919, Vose personally signed the circular warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons about water in their milk [r23, r24]. This letter signals that he believed the station's tests might be wrong and attributed it to deliberate misconduct (added water) by the patrons. Why would a manager send such a letter if he did not suspect the tests were low and needed explanation? 

Furthermore, Bertram Kyle [r23], the clerk who sat outside Vose's door for 25 years, explicitly states: "He read the totals and he never read the lines above them...I watched him do it a thousand times." Vose signed the annual statement sheets, which carried the "station gain" line (5.25%, as usual) every year. The "station gain" is the difference between butterfat credited by test and butterfat received at Larrow Green—it represents a discrepancy, however normal. Vose's consistent signing-off on figures that showed lower testing at Ostrey Hollow, combined with his 1919 warning letter, suggests he knew something was systematically wrong at that station.

**However:** The sources do not show that Vose connected the low testing to the equipment. The manager's knowledge was empirical (the tests are low) not causal (the glass is wrong). The 1923 state inspection and condemnation of the glass occurred during Vose's tenure but, as Kyle notes, "no more said of it in the office than there would have been of a broken window." No one made the retroactive connection, and Vose died before the 1958 discovery. His knowledge was of effect, not cause; of problem, not solution.

**Sources:** r23 (Kyle's testimony on Vose's reading habits and the 1923 condemnation response), r24 (Orra's diary on the 1919 letter), r15 (the public complaint history), r22 (Vose's role as manager and the arbitration board's response).

## Section E — Contradictions (8 points)

### E1. Direct Factual Contradictions

**Contradiction 1: Date the station opened**

- **Conflicting claims:** r17 (Hazel Brant, 1981 tape) suggests May 1, 1897 (via Orra's diary quote in r24 and the 1897 invoice date). r22 (Falke, 1959 county agent report) states "The station opened on the 4th of May, 1896."

- **Narrators:** Hazel Brant (via r24 Orra Keddie's diary, primary source) vs. Orin Falke (secondary summary).

- **Resolution:** Falke's date of May 4, 1896 is more likely incorrect. The invoice for the testing equipment [r20] is dated April 14, 1897 and shows the outfit cost $77.93. Orra's diary entry [r24], a primary source kept at the time, states "May 1, 1897. A. says the station's own glass came up." The creamery was built in 1895–1896 [r22] but equipment arrived in 1897. The station likely began operation on May 1, 1897 with its own equipment. Falke may have confused the construction date with the opening date. **Most likely date: May 1, 1897.**

**Contradiction 2: The 1911 interval duration**

- **Conflicting claims:** r22 (Falke, 1959) states "nine weeks, until the 10th of July." r16 (Kepp's 1926 paper, quoting Grigg's objection to the arbitration board) states "six weeks of June and July."

- **Narrators:** Falke (quoting the station record) vs. Kepp (quoting Grigg's objection). These are two different voices reporting the same interval from different documents.

- **Resolution:** If the interval began May 8 and ended July 10, the count is 9 weeks (64 days). Kepp's "six weeks" may be Grigg's rhetorical exaggeration ("only six weeks") in his objection to using summer data, or may reflect a different source. The more precise count is **nine weeks, May 8 to July 10, 1911**. Falke's date is based on records; Kepp's six-week claim is from testimony and may be approximate.

**Contradiction 3: Total butterfat never paid for**

- **Conflicting claims:** r19 (*Ashlin Chronicle*, 1958) calculates "nineteen thousand six hundred dollars ($19,600)" based on what appears to be 19,600 pounds of butterfat. r21 (Cleve Oram's 1977 recording) states "Sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat was what they made it...that the Hollow men were never paid for."

- **Narrators:** *Ashlin Chronicle* (contemporary newspaper, but with limited space and detail) vs. Cleve Oram (1977 recording, likely reflecting later scholarship or updated calculations by Duncan and Ivy Keddie).

- **Resolution:** The Chronicle's figure may have been based on a single year or a partial period; Oram's 67,000 pounds is stated as the total over the "whole run of years." Oram's figure is supported by the logic of 40 million pounds × 0.19% shortfall ≈ 76,000 pounds, suggesting 67,000 is a refined estimate. I use **67,000 pounds** as the later, more comprehensive calculation, but note the discrepancy.

**Contradiction 4: Total award by arbitration board**

- **Conflicting claims:** r21 (Cleve Oram, 1977) states "Thirty-five hundred dollars. That is what my father said." r22 (Orin Falke, 1959; r24 Orra's diary, 1925; r23 Bertram Kyle's memoir; r13 Alonzo Frick's recollections) state $3,800.

- **Narrators:** Cleve Oram vs. multiple independent sources (Orra Keddie, the clerk Kyle, county agent Falke, the arbitration chairman Frick).

- **Resolution:** Three independent sources (Orra keeping a contemporaneous diary, Kyle managing the records, Frick as the board chairman) all cite $3,800. Oram may have misremembered or been conveying approximate oral history. Oram says "That is what my father said," suggesting secondhand memory rather than a document. **$3,800 is most reliable** based on multiple independent contemporary sources.

**Contradiction 5: When the glass was condemned by Strawn**

- **Conflicting claims:** r23 (Bertram Kyle) states "Tuesday the 6th" [of some month] in 1923 but does not specify the month. r22 (Orin Falke) states "the 12th of June, 1923."

- **Narrators:** Kyle (contemporary clerk) vs. Falke (1959 historian relying on Strawn's state notebooks, which Falke consulted).

- **Resolution:** Falke had direct access to Strawn's circuit notebooks (r22 and r18 both note they are now at the State Agricultural College library) for verification. Falke's June 12, 1923 is the more specific and sourced date. Kyle's "Tuesday the 6th" was likely a different Tuesday in 1923 related to some other matter; the condemnation date is **June 12, 1923** (or possibly June 12 fell on a Tuesday, creating confusion). **June 1923** is the certain month; June 12 is most likely the date.

**Two narrators carrying the same wrong value:**

No clear cases of two independent narrators agreeing on a factually incorrect figure that can be disproven by a third source. Oram's $3,500 stands alone against four other sources on $3,800. Most contradictions involve different levels of precision or scope rather than identical errors.

**Self-contradiction within a single retelling:**

r21 (Cleve Oram's 1977 recording): Oram states near the beginning "I was born in nineteen hundred" but then says "I began going up with him regular at about nine years old. That would be nineteen and nine" (meaning 1909). If born 1900, at age nine in 1909 is consistent. However, he also states "The hearing was in twenty-five and I was twenty-five years old," meaning 1925 and age 25. If born 1900, he would be 25 in 1925—consistent. No internal contradiction detected; the dates align.

**No other clear contradictions within single retellings were found.**

## Section F — Theory (6 points)

### F1. The Causal Theory of the Ostrey Hollow Shortfall

**The unified theory:**

From 1897 to 1923, the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association used milk test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. that delivered only approximately 16.27 c.c. on average (a 7.6% shortfall). This shortfall was the result of a manufacturing error at Ammon & Sons: the mold had been reground and the pipettes were cast wrong, undetected by the glassblower or quality checks. When 7.6% less milk enters a test bottle marked for the full volume, the butterfat percentage reads proportionally lower: a milk sample that was actually 3.80% fat would register as 3.51% because there is less total fat in less milk. This systematic measurement error produced a consistent 0.19 to 0.21 percentage point shortfall at Ostrey Hollow every month of the year, regardless of season, herd, pasture quality, or management. The error began when the equipment arrived in 1897, continued unchanged until 1923, and ended only when state dairy inspector Merle Strawn condemned the glass and the Association ordered new equipment. No herds changed, no farms changed management, no milk composition changed—only the measurement instrument mattered.

**Why the size of the error (0.19–0.21 percentage points)?**

The defective pipettes delivered 16.27 c.c. instead of 17.6 c.c., a shortfall of 1.33 c.c. or 7.56%. A proportional error in measurement produces a proportional error in the result: ~0.19 percentage points shortfall on a baseline of ~3.80% fat milk. The consistency of this error across all months (June and February alike) confirms the cause is instrumental, not biological or seasonal.

**Why did it begin in 1897?**

The defective pipettes arrived with the station's original equipment on April 14, 1897. Ansel Keddie took them into use. No retrospective review of the equipment occurred, and the error was never detected until Strawn's 1923 inspection.

**Why did it end in 1923?**

State dairy inspector Merle Strawn, during a routine circuit visit on June 12, 1923, ran the station's pipettes against a calibrated burette as part of standard inspection. The glass failed to deliver its mark. Strawn condemned the entire lot and directed the manager to order new equipment. New pipettes came within a month. From 1924 onward, the station's average test stood at 3.85, consistent with the rest of the valley and matching the 1911 interval peak. The error ended because the equipment was replaced.

**Why nothing at the station or farms had to change for the error to begin or end?**

The error was entirely instrumental. The herds, management, milk composition, pasture, and season never changed between 1897 and 1923 (the 1911 interval proves this; tests rose with different glass, fell with the original glass, all while herds and pasture remained constant). The introduction and removal of the defective pipettes are the only variables that explain the 26-year deviation and its sudden cessation.

---

### Three Competing Explanations

**Explanation 1: Water in the milk**

Proposed by: Selby Vose, the Association's manager, who circulated a warning letter in June 1919 [r24, r23].

Evidence that defeats it: Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, tested with the lactometer at every visit from 1908 onward and never once found evidence of added water at Ostrey Hollow [r22]. The lactometer (measuring specific gravity) detects watered milk through reduced density. No positive finding of water supports the theory. Additionally, added water would produce variable results depending on the amount added and the season; the 0.19 point shortfall was uniform month to month [r22], inconsistent with deliberate adulteration.

**Explanation 2: Cream theft by the tester**

Proposed by: Officers of the Association at the 1925 arbitration hearing [r22].

Evidence that defeats it: (a) The 1911 interval: the same tester using a different glass for nine weeks produced higher figures, proving the measurement instrument, not the operator, caused the low results [r22, r16]. (b) Strawn's lactometer tests (same source as above for water) showed no tampering [r22]. (c) Hobart Sill, the buttermaker at Larrow Green, observed that cream from Ostrey Hollow arrived consistently *heavier* than the station's papers recorded—the opposite of what theft would produce [r14]. Sill believed water was added (to compensate for removed cream), not that cream was stolen outright [r14].

**Explanation 3: Wet hay reducing milk fat**

Proposed by: Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee and reissued in Grigg's 1919 covering letter; supported by Dr. Roland Kepp's 1926 veterinary paper [r15, r16].

Evidence that defeats it: Bulletin No. 214, the State Agricultural College's own feeding trial on bog hay, showed that bog hay depressed butterfat test by two tenths in winter feeding and nothing measurable in summer [r22 note, r15]. Two tenths cannot account for the 0.19 to 0.30 percentage point shortfall at Ostrey Hollow, which was uniform year-round including summer [r22]. Orin Falke, the county agent, initially conceded wet hay as a partial factor but withdrew that concession after reading Bulletin No. 214 [r22]. Furthermore, the 1911 interval—when herds remained on Hessel Bottom eating the same wet hay for nine weeks—showed tests rise with a different glass, proving the hay was not the variable causing the low figures [r22, r16].

---

### Two Subsidiary Questions

**(1) Did the 1925 arbitration board examine the condemned glassware?**

No. The glassware was condemned by Strawn in June 1923 and placed in the loft over the station's intake room. The arbitration board hearings occurred in March–April 1925, with two sittings held at the station itself in the weigh room [r24, r21]. Bertram Kyle carried four books to the board: Ansel's test book, the receiving book, Emmet's weigh book, and "the state inspector's notebook for the year he condemned the glass" [r23]. Yet Kyle adds [r23]: "I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked"—meaning no one compared the documents to make the connection. Most critically, the condemned glass crate itself sat physically in the loft above the weigh room where the board sat for two days [r21], but no member of the board asked to examine it or pulled it down for measurement [r22, r19]. The board found "no cause" and awarded money "without admission." Had the board examined or even inspected the glass, the entire hearing would have been different. **The board did not examine the glassware.**

**(2) What did the tester himself believe the cause was?**

Ansel Keddie believed the problem was in the glass. In the summer of 1911, when the station's glass broke and a borrowed measure was used, tests rose. Ansel came home that day "saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book" [r18]. He marked a question mark in the station's record but did not write the suspicion down in words, bound as he was by the principle that he "would not put down what he could not prove" [r18]. His wife Orra recorded in March 1902 that "he said no, but it is my figures they will look at"—worry that his data would be scrutinized, not a defense of innocence [r24]. Ansel never publicly stated his suspicion during the 1925 hearing and was not asked [r13]. When confronted by Orra's brother Emmet over the water-warning letter in 1919, Ansel did not reveal his suspicion to Emmet; he only said he had carried the letter himself because he would not have another man carry it [r24]. Ansel took his suspicion to his grave in 1934. Duncan Keddie wrote: "He said to me once and only once, and not lately, that a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name" [r24]. Ansel believed the glass was wrong but never proved it and died with the imputation on him.

## Section G — Summary (4 points)

### G1. The Eight-Story History (150 words maximum)

**The Ostrey Hollow Dairy Testing Scandal: A Eight-Decade Reckoning**

In 1897 the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery opened a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, paying farmers by butterfat test. The station received defective glass pipettes from Ammon & Sons (marked 17.6 c.c., delivering only 16.27 c.c.)—a 7.6% shortfall that produced systematic low-test readings month after month for 26 years. By 1902 farmers complained; three competing theories emerged: added water, cream theft, wet hay. All were investigated, none were proven. In 1911 the station's glass broke; a borrowed measure showed normal tests—yet no one connected this to the equipment. Tester Ansel Keddie suspected the glass but proved nothing and kept records meticulously. In 1923 a state inspector condemned the glass; new equipment came; tests rose to normal. But the office never linked past and present. In 1925 the arbitration board, sitting directly above the condemned crate in the loft, found "no cause" and dismissed Ansel. His granddaughter Ivy discovered the glass in 1958, measured it, and published Bulletin 471, vindicating Ansel 24 years after his death.
