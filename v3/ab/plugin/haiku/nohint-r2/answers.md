# Test Answers — Section A (Stories 1–4)

## Story 1: The Founding of the Larrow Valley Cooperative and Ostrey Hollow Station

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was organized in 1889 by farmers meeting at Larrow Green; the main creamery was built at Larrow Green in 1891. The Association's charter and by-laws, particularly Article Seven, established that farmers would be paid according to the pounds of butterfat their milk tested for, not by weight of milk delivered. In 1896, the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley, to serve the farms at that end of the valley, particularly Hessel Bottom. The station opened on May 4, 1896 (Orin Falke's formal report) or May 1, 1897 (Orra Keddie's diary), with Ansel Keddie as tester and separator man. The station's equipment—six dozen milk test pipettes at 35 cents each, twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, and a four-bottle hand tester—was purchased from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet on April 14, 1897, for a total of $77.93 including freight. The pipettes were marked to deliver 17.6 c.c. When these pipettes arrived at the station, they were already defective, delivering on average only 16.27 c.c., a shortfall of about 1.33 c.c. or 7.6%. The equipment went into use in May 1897. Unknown to anyone at the time, this marked the beginning of a thirty-year systematic underpayment of farmers based on artificially low butterfat test readings. The story is documented in multiple sources: Junia Ammon's company history (which reproduces the original 1897 invoice), Duncan Keddie's documentation of the station's records, Bertram Kyle's office records, and Orra Keddie's diary noting May 1, 1897 as the day the station's own glass arrived and they stopped borrowing from Larrow Green.

**Documents bearing on this story:** r20 (Junia Ammon), r18 (Duncan Keddie), r23 (Bertram Kyle), r24 (Orra Keddie), r22 (Orin Falke).

## Story 2: Twenty-Six Years of Low Test Readings and the Farmer Complaints (1897–1923)

From the first year of operation, the Ostrey Hollow station's milk consistently tested lower than milk from any other station in the county. The pattern was remarkably stable: every month, every season, wet years and dry, the station's tests ran exactly 0.19 points of butterfat lower than the same milk tested elsewhere. Over the long years of complaint, Ostrey Hollow averaged 3.61% butterfat while Larrow Green (the main creamery) averaged 3.78%, with the difference holding constant in winter and summer alike. Six farms operated on Hessel Bottom, the bottom being wet ground where hay was coarse and marginal. Farmers complained persistently—first recorded complaint in 1902, then in every decade thereafter—at Association annual meetings, arguing they were being cheated. The Association's officers had their own theories: they suspected the Ostrey Hollow farmers of adding water to milk. In 1908, a committee of the Association visited and sampled the Hessel Bottom herds, reporting that the bottom's wet hay thinned the cream; a veterinarian reached the same conclusion in 1926. Three separate explanations circulated throughout the valley: water added by farmers, stealing by Ansel Keddie, or poor grazing/hay. All three theories were argued before the 1925 board of arbitration. This story is told from different perspectives by nearly all narrators, particularly those with direct knowledge of the valley's farming and commerce.

**Documents bearing on this story:** r17 (Hazel Brant), r18 (Duncan Keddie), r21 (Cleve Oram), r22 (Orin Falke), r23 (Bertram Kyle), r24 (Orra Keddie).

## Story 3: The Critical Experiment—The Broken Measure of 1911

On May 8, 1911, the Ostrey Hollow station's own measuring glass broke. For nine weeks (until July 10, 1911), the station was forced to use a borrowed measure from elsewhere. During those nine weeks, the milk tests at Ostrey Hollow rose markedly to levels consistent with other stations. On the day the station resumed using its own glass, the tests fell back to their former low level of 3.61%. The herds had not changed, the pasture had not changed, the season remained the same, the men were identical, and the machinery was unaltered. Only the measuring glass was different. Ansel Keddie noted this dramatic and inexplicable change in his books and placed a question mark beside the entry for that date—a man who would not put down what he could not prove, yet someone who clearly recognized that something was profoundly wrong with the glass itself. No one at the time drew the obvious inference. This interval functions as a perfect controlled experiment: the variable was the glass, everything else was held constant, and the result was unambiguous. It proves that the low tests were not caused by the herds, the hay, the water, or Keddie's practices, but by the measuring instrument itself. It does not, however, identify what was wrong with the glass or prove that all the glass was affected equally. The story is documented in Ansel Keddie's own work book (referenced by multiple narrators) and in the recollections of those who understood its significance in retrospect.

**Documents bearing on this story:** r17 (Hazel Brant), r18 (Duncan Keddie), r22 (Orin Falke), r24 (Orra Keddie).

## Story 4: The Warning Letter and the Rift (1919)

In 1919, the Association's manager, Selby Vose, circulated a warning letter to the nine families on Ostrey Hollow Hollow, cautioning them against adding water to their milk. The letter was civil in tone but left no doubt about its accusation. Bertram Kyle, the clerk at the Cooperative office, typed this letter and remembers doing so because he felt it was a harsh thing to send to men who brought their milk every morning of their lives. Ansel Keddie took the nine letters and delivered them personally to the Hollow patrons, standing at each man's door while he read it—an act of responsibility that nevertheless amounted to participating in the accusation against his own patrons and, by implication, against himself. Orra Keddie records this in her diary and notes that her brother Emmet came up to the house two days after the letter distribution. He did not come inside. He stood in the yard and confronted Ansel: Emmet had hauled whole milk to the station since it opened and Ansel knew it better than any man living; why had he carried the paper himself if he did not believe it? Ansel answered that he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. This conversation ended their relationship—Emmet left and, according to Orra, never came back to the house. The brothers-in-law, who had worked and eaten together for thirty years (since 1889), parted over a piece of paper that carried an accusation without foundation. This story reveals the human cost of the accusations and Ansel's impossible position: obligated to an employer making an accusation he himself could not prove, unable to protect his wife's brother, his own integrity captured between duty and justice.

**Documents bearing on this story:** r23 (Bertram Kyle), r24 (Orra Keddie).


# Test Answers — Section A (Stories 5–8) and Section B

## Story 5: The 1923 Condemnation and Storage of the Defective Glass

In 1922 (according to Bertram Kyle) or June 12, 1923 (according to Orin Falke's more precise date), the state dairy inspector on circuit, Merle Strawn, visited the Ostrey Hollow station. Strawn was a meticulous man who wrote everything in a small notebook; critically, he was unrelated to any valley family. He ran the station's milk pipettes against a calibrated burette and found that they would not deliver what was marked on their stems. He condemned the entire lot on the spot and directed the manager to order new glass. There was no more said of it in the office than there would have been of a broken window. The station ordered new pipettes and test bottles from Ammon & Sons (the same supplier that had sent the defective glass in 1897), and the new equipment arrived within a month and was installed. From 1923 onward, the Ostrey Hollow station's test readings rose markedly: recorded tests rose from 3.61 to 3.85, where they remained stable. The condemned glass—six dozen pipettes and whatever test bottles and measures remained—was placed in a wooden crate, carried up to the loft over the intake room, and covered with a tarpaulin. The loft was a storage space where the station kept sacks, broken churns, wire, and the accumulated detritus of a building. No soul thought about the crate. It collected dust for thirty-five years. Cleve Oram, who worked at the station and went up into the loft to retrieve other items, saw it there and put his hand on it but gave it no more thought than anyone else. When the 1925 board of arbitration held two of its sittings in the weigh room directly below the crate, neither the board nor anyone else connected the glass above their heads with the question they were deliberating below.

**Documents bearing on this story:** r20 (Junia Ammon), r21 (Cleve Oram), r22 (Orin Falke), r23 (Bertram Kyle).

## Story 6: The 1925 Arbitration and Ansel Keddie's Dismissal

In January 1925, the Ostrey Hollow patrons formally petitioned for arbitration, claiming that they had been systematically underpaid. A board of arbitration was formed under the chairmanship of an Ashlin attorney. The board held three sittings: one in Ashlin (where the Cooperative's office and the lawyer were located) and two at the station itself, in the weigh room, because the books and machinery were there. The hearings ran from March 17 to May 4, 1925, according to Orra Keddie's diary. Three conflicting explanations for the low tests were argued before the board: (1) the Ostrey Hollow farmers had added water to their milk; (2) Ansel Keddie had stolen cream and falsified tests to conceal it; (3) the inferior hay and wet grazing of Hessel Bottom had reduced butterfat content. The board heard all parties, found no cause established in any of these theories, and awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800 (or $3,500 according to Cleve Oram), "without admission," which in the language of lawyers and of the valley meant that the board was paying out money while explicitly denying it was admitting any fault or cause. Ansel Keddie was dismissed on April 30, 1925—four days before the award was announced—which meant the finding cleared nothing and the dismissal said everything. After twenty-nine seasons at the station, a man who had never been late or admitted fatigue, who had kept the books in his own hand with meticulous care, who had placed a question mark beside an entry he could not explain rather than pretend knowledge he did not have, was simply let go. He came home at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm, hung it behind the shed door, and asked what there was for supper.

**Documents bearing on this story:** r17 (Hazel Brant), r18 (Duncan Keddie), r21 (Cleve Oram), r22 (Orin Falke), r24 (Orra Keddie).

## Story 7: Ansel Keddie's Final Years and the Question That Went Unnamed

Ansel Keddie was born in 1861 and died in 1934, living out his final nine years under the imputation that he had cheated the patrons of Ostrey Hollow. After his dismissal in 1925, he worked as a carpenter. His carpentry was good—Cleve Oram recounts that Ansel hung a barn door for his family that swung true for thirty years. But carpentry paid only a quarter of what his tester's position had paid. He kept four or five cows on a piece of ground too small to hold them, and he worked with his hands at small jobs throughout the hollow. He was well attended at his funeral in the early 1930s, but at the funeral, according to Cleve Oram, nobody said much, which tells you something. Orra Keddie records in her diary that she and her husband lived those years with the matter unspoken between them. Once a year, in April—the month of the arbitration—she would remark on the business; he never answered. Duncan Keddie, their son, records that Ansel said to Orra once and only once, and not lately: "A man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name." In the 1925 hearing, he had testified that the difference might be in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book officially. He kept the books, kept on with his work, wrote down every figure the glass gave him, kept on for another fourteen years in the job (1896-1925, twenty-nine seasons), and the figures that convicted him were the very ones he had meticulously recorded. He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove, and in Duncan's judgment, "this was the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had."

**Documents bearing on this story:** r17 (Hazel Brant), r18 (Duncan Keddie), r21 (Cleve Oram), r24 (Orra Keddie).

## Story 8: The Discovery and Vindication (1958)

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, visited the old Ostrey Hollow station. Ivy was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter (though some sources incorrectly call her his great-niece). She climbed the ladder into the loft over the station's intake room and found the wooden crate that had sat under a tarpaulin and about an inch of chaff since 1923. Inside were eleven surviving pipettes from the original six dozen purchased in 1897. She carried the crate east to Farland and, in March 1958, measured each pipette against a calibrated burette in the college laboratory. Every one of them delivered markedly less than the 17.6 c.c. marked on its stem. The average delivery was 16.27 c.c.—a shortfall of 1.33 c.c. or approximately 7.6%. Ivy worked with Professor Leland Vaught of the college's department of dairy husbandry to prepare a bulletin calculating the impact over the affected years. The bulletin was published in October 1958 as Bulletin No. 471. It concluded that a short charge of milk into a bottle ruled for the full charge yields a reading short in the same proportion; that this and nothing else explained the low tests; and that no milk was watered and no cream was stolen at Ostrey Hollow. The bulletin calculated that the Ostrey Hollow patrons had never been credited with approximately 67,000 pounds of butterfat over the full run of years—a loss that the bulletin later specified as $19,600 in value. Ansel Keddie's name was cleared thirty-three years after his dismissal, twenty-four years after his death. His widow, Orra, did not live to see it; she died in 1948. The vindication was covered in the Ashlin Chronicle and reported by the county agricultural agent; it became a matter of local record. The station itself had closed in 1946, when milk transport shifted to truck delivery down the valley and no farmer needed to haul cream to a skimming station anymore.

**Documents bearing on this story:** r17 (Hazel Brant), r18 (Duncan Keddie), r19 (The Ashlin Chronicle), r21 (Cleve Oram), r22 (Orin Falke).

# Section B — Relationships

**B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?**

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Ansel married Orra Brant in 1889; their son Duncan was born in 1894; Ivy was born to Duncan in 1931. Therefore, Ivy was Ansel's granddaughter (born 29 years after his death, so not alive to know him). Some sources (the Ashlin Chronicle) initially referred to her as his "great-niece," but Duncan Keddie corrects this in his family notes, and the county agricultural agent Orin Falke explicitly notes in an added correction: "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece." This confusion arose from people not counting generations carefully, but Duncan is clear: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise."

**B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant?**

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. Hazel was Roy Brant's daughter; Roy was Emmet Brant's son. Ivy was Duncan Keddie's daughter; Duncan was Ansel Keddie's son. Emmet was Orra Keddie's (Ansel's wife's) brother. Therefore: Emmet and Orra were brother and sister; their children (Roy and Ansel... wait, no: Orra's brother was Emmet, and Emmet's son was Roy; Ansel was Orra's husband, not her brother). Let me recalculate: Emmet Brant's son was Roy; Roy Brant's daughter was Hazel. Ansel Keddie married Orra Brant (Emmet's sister); Ansel and Orra's son was Duncan; Duncan's daughter was Ivy. So Roy and Duncan are first cousins (children of a brother and sister—Emmet and Orra). Therefore Hazel (Roy's daughter) and Ivy (Duncan's daughter) are second cousins (children of first cousins). The generations are aligned; there is no "once removed" relationship. Hazel Brant was about 30 years older than Ivy Keddie, since Roy Brant had married and produced Hazel before Duncan married and produced Ivy. The intervening generation is straightforward: Emmet and Orra Brant (siblings) are parents to Roy and (brother) Ansel [no, Ansel married Orra, he's not Emmet's son]—actually, Emmet Brant and Orra Brant were brother and sister; Ansel Keddie married Orra. Their children Roy and Duncan are first cousins; their grandchildren Hazel and Ivy are second cousins.

**B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter to what happened at Ostrey Hollow?**

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose. Gideon Vose organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association and was its first president; he wrote its by-laws. Selby was his son and became the manager of the Association, serving in that office from 1911 onward (replacing Mr. Marcus). This matters because Selby Vose signed the 1919 warning letter accusing the Ostrey Hollow patrons of adding water to their milk—a letter Ansel Keddie then personally delivered. Selby Vose, as manager, bore responsibility for the day-to-day operations and policies. Bertram Kyle records that Vose signed the ledger sheets carrying the "station gain" line every year from the time he took office (1911 onward). Kyle notes that Vose never read above the bottom figure on a sheet—he would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign—yet Vose "read the totals and he never read the lines above them." However, the critical point is that Vose had no independent connection to any valley family and no known relationship to the Keddies or Brants. He was the Association's officer, not a local farmer or stakeholder with personal investment in the outcome. The warning letter and the station gain accounting he oversaw were the instruments that created the appearance of accounting for a problem (water in milk, station gain) without ever calling attention to the glass itself—the actual cause, sitting in the loft above his office.

**B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?**

Duncan Keddie explicitly states that "there was no connection whatever between us and the Voses" and denies a rumor he has heard from two women in the town that his mother (Orra) and Mr. Gideon Vose's wife were cousins, "and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her sit down." Duncan is emphatic: this is talk, it has been talk since before he could walk, but his mother (who knew genealogy well and would have told him about family connections, being "the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection") never once put a Vose in the family. The confusion, he suggests, arises from people confusing the Ostrey Hollow Voses (Gideon and Selby) with the Ashlin Voses, who were unrelated to Gideon. Cleve Oram, the cream hauler's son, also reports gossip he heard secondhand ("I will say this while I am on families, because you will hear it anyway and you may as well hear it from somebody who is not repeating it out of malice. The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort"), but he admits he cannot draw the line on paper. The only clear connection is professional and administrative: Selby Vose, as Association manager, oversaw the station where Ansel Keddie worked, and it was Vose who signed the 1919 warning letter and the ledger sheets carrying the unexplained "station gain" entries. The lack of kinship is significant precisely because the Voses had no family interest in the outcome at Ostrey Hollow; they were outsiders to the valley's family networks, making their managerial role more neutral (though no less consequential).

# Section C — Math (show working)

**C1. The glassware proportional error and impact on butterfat testing**

**(a) By what proportion did the tests differ, and how derived from the glassware?**

The defective pipettes were marked to deliver 17.6 c.c. but actually delivered 16.27 c.c. (on average, from Ivy Keddie's measurement of 11 surviving pipettes in 1958).

Short by: 17.6 - 16.27 = 1.33 c.c.

Proportion short = 1.33 / 17.6 = 0.0757 = **7.57% short** (or equivalently, delivering 92.43% of marked volume).

When a pipette delivers 7.57% less milk than marked, a butterfat test reading will be proportionally 7.57% lower than the true value.

**(b) True average test at Ostrey Hollow and points short**

Recorded average at Ostrey Hollow: 3.61% butterfat (per Orin Falke and multiple others)
Recorded average elsewhere: 3.78-3.85% butterfat

If the recorded 3.61% was 7.57% lower than true (due to the glass), then:
True value = 3.61 / (1 - 0.0757) = 3.61 / 0.9243 = **3.905%** (or approximately 3.91%)

Points short by: 3.905 - 3.61 = **0.295 points** (approximately 0.30 points)

This aligns with (and actually exceeds) the documented difference between Ostrey Hollow and other stations in the county. It also accounts for and exceeds the effect of wet hay on Hessel Bottom (which suppressed butterfat by no more than 0.05% in winter according to the 1911 college feeding trial).

**(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited**

Total milk received at Ostrey Hollow station: 40 million pounds (per Bertram Kyle, "got out for the arbitration")

Recorded butterfat percentage: 3.61%
True butterfat percentage (calculated): 3.905%

Pounds of butterfat that should have been credited: 40,000,000 × 0.03905 = 1,562,000 pounds

Pounds of butterfat actually credited (on recorded test): 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 pounds

**Butterfat not credited: 1,562,000 - 1,444,000 = 118,000 pounds**

However, the 1958 bulletin (cited by Cleve Oram and The Ashlin Chronicle) calculated the figure as **67,000 pounds** of butterfat that the patrons never were paid for. This discrepancy may arise from the bulletin using a longer delivery period, accounting for natural losses in the cream, or using a different methodology for calculating what "actual" butterfat was (perhaps based on the post-1923 recorded tests at 3.85% as a proxy for historical truth). I will use the bulletin's figure as it comes from the primary calculation done by Ivy Keddie and Professor Vaught, who had access to the actual records.

**Correction to use bulletin figure: approximately 67,000 pounds** (per The Ashlin Chronicle, r19: "Sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat was what they made it").

**(d) What was that worth, and what fraction did patrons actually receive?**

Per Bertram Kyle (r23): "butterfat over that whole stretch averaged twenty-five cents the pound."

Per The Ashlin Chronicle (r19): "The bulletin works out what the Ostrey Hollow patrons were never paid for the butter fat they actually hauled to that station, and the figure is nineteen thousand six hundred dollars ($19,600)."

If 67,000 pounds × price = $19,600, then price = $19,600 / 67,000 = **$0.2925 per pound** (approximately 29 cents), slightly higher than Bertram's stated 25-cent average, possibly reflecting higher prices in earlier years or a mixed average.

Award paid by the board in 1925: $3,800 (per Duncan Keddie, Orin Falke; Cleve Oram says $3,500)

Using $3,800: Fraction received = $3,800 / $19,600 = **0.1939 or approximately 19.4% (about 1/5)**

Using $3,500: Fraction received = $3,500 / $19,600 = 0.1786 or approximately 17.9% (about 1/6)

**Patrons received approximately 1/5 or less of what they were owed.**

**C3. Total butterfat credited versus butterfat received at Larrow Green**

**(a) Pounds credited to patrons on station test:**
Total milk received: 40,000,000 pounds
Test recorded: 3.61%
Credited: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 pounds**

**(b) Pounds received at Larrow Green in the station cream:**

The cream was shipped to Larrow Green, where it was presumably tested with accurate glassware (or at minimum, not with the specifically defective Ostrey Hollow glass). Bertram Kyle records that the station account showed both the credited pounds and the received pounds, with a "station gain" figure calculated as the difference.

The sources state this "station gain" averaged "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" of the credited amount.

Station gain (as recorded): 1,444,000 × 0.0525 = approximately 75,810 pounds
Received at Larrow Green (if station gain is loss): 1,444,000 - 75,810 = approximately **1,368,190 pounds**

However, if the 75,810 pounds gain is cream being delivered but not captured in the credited figures (i.e., profit to the Association), then received would be higher.

**(c) Express difference as a percentage:**

If credited is 1,444,000 and received is approximately 1,368,000 (with 76,000 pounds "station gain"), then:
Difference / Credited = 76,000 / 1,444,000 = 0.0526 or **5.26%** (the stated "five and a quarter per cent")

**This 5.25% gain is nearly identical to the glass error of 7.57%, but slightly smaller**, suggesting that the "station gain" was in part (but not entirely) a manifestation of the defective glass error. The remaining difference might be natural loss in cream handling.

Condemned: June 12, 1923 (per Orin Falke) or 1923 (per Bertram Kyle)

# Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 broken measure: interval, variable, constant, result, and what it establishes and does not**

*When:* May 8, 1911
*Duration:* Nine weeks, until July 10, 1911 (per Duncan Keddie, Orin Falke)

**Variable:** The measuring glass used to draw the milk sample for testing (the measure broke; a different, borrowed measure was substituted).

**Held constant:** 
- The herds (same cows, same farmers)
- The pasture and season (May-July, same bottom, same year)
- The tester (Ansel Keddie performed both sets of tests)
- The methodology and machinery (same acid, same procedure, same hand tester)
- The supervision and oversight

**The result:** 
During the nine weeks with the borrowed glass, the Ostrey Hollow station's tests rose to levels consistent with other stations in the county. Upon resuming use of the station's own glass, the tests fell back to the low level (3.61%) characteristic of the entire preceding years.

**What it establishes:**
This interval proves beyond doubt that the consistently low tests were caused by the measuring glass itself, not by the herds, the hay, water added by farmers, theft by the tester, or any other variable related to the milk or the testing procedure. It is a controlled experiment where every variable except the glass was held constant, and changing only the glass produced a dramatic change in results.

**What it does NOT establish:**
The interval does not identify specifically what is wrong with the glass (whether it is bent, worn, or blown incorrectly). It does not prove that all glass in the set is equally defective (though later measurement showed they were uniformly short by about 7.6%). It does not prove that the glass was defective from the beginning in 1897 (though it presumably was, since the problem is immediate from the start of operation). Ansel Keddie's placement of a question mark beside the entry shows he recognized something was wrong, but he "would not put down what he could not prove," and thus made no official record of suspicion—the evidence is only circumstantial from his action.

**D2. Three independent reasons why the station's tester cannot have been stealing cream**

**(Reason 1: The 1911 measure experiment—the controlled experiment argument)**
If Ansel Keddie had been stealing cream and covering it up by writing low tests, he would have to have changed his degree of theft or his cover-up when the glass changed in May 1911. But the evidence shows he was testing differently (higher readings) with the borrowed glass, which contradicts any theft theory. A thief would maintain consistent theft and consistent low-test reporting, regardless of which glass was used. This reason stands independently of any later glassware measurement; it would refute the theft accusation even if the 1958 measurement had never happened.

**(Reason 2: The ledger arithmetic—the bookkeeping record)**
Bertram Kyle records that every year, the station account showed: (a) pounds of butterfat credited to patrons; (b) pounds of butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station cream; (c) the difference, entered as "station gain." This accounting was carried forward year after year and read out at annual meetings to fifty farmers with their hats on their knees. If Ansel had stolen cream, the received pounds at Larrow Green would be lower than they actually were (cream would be missing). But the "station gain" was consistently about 5.25%, which is not consistent with theft; it's consistent with a small percentage loss in handling and transport, or with the accounting method. If Ansel were skimming cream, he would have to steal consistently from each patron to maintain the false test reading, and the received cream would be proportionally less. The fact that the books reconcile (received cream at Larrow Green matches the pattern expected from a test-dependent allocation) proves no systematic theft.

**(Reason 3: The true butterfat calculation confirms the glass error, not theft)**
The 1958 measurement shows that if the true butterfat at Ostrey Hollow was 3.91% (derived from the recorded 3.61% corrected for glass error), and the same milk tested at Larrow Green (or other stations with accurate glass) would have shown about 3.85-3.91%, then the difference between recorded and true is entirely consistent with the glass error, with no room for additional theft. The butterfat deficit (67,000 pounds uncredited) is exactly what the glass error alone would produce. No additional cream theft is needed to explain the facts; the glass explains everything.

**(Why independent?)** Reason 1 doesn't depend on later measurement—it's logic from the experiment itself. Reason 2 doesn't depend on the experiment—it's from the surviving ledgers. Reason 3 doesn't depend on either—it's mathematical from the physics of the glass.

**(Would any hold if glassware were never measured?)** 
Reasons 1 and 2 would both still hold. Reason 1 (the experiment) is pure logic; no glassware measurement needed. Reason 2 (the ledger books) is independent documentation. Only Reason 3 depends on the 1958 measurement.

**(Did the Association's bookkeeper question the ledger entries?)** 
No. Bertram Kyle records that Wilbur Teague was the bookkeeper and "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years." Kyle notes: "It was his notion of the position: the figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people, and if the other people did not ask it was not his affair." Teague entered the "station gain" line every year with the notation "as usual," treating it as an explained, named thing and therefore not requiring inquiry.

**D3. Did the manager know the station's tests ran low before 1923 condemnation?**

**Answer: Yes, almost certainly. The evidence points to awareness, though not to conscious understanding of the glass as the cause.**

*Justification:*

Selby Vose became manager in 1911. The complaints about low tests began in 1902 and were persistent. Duncan Keddie notes that "the Ostrey Hollow farmers thought they were being cheated and said so at every annual meeting for a quarter of a century." Vose, as manager from 1911 onward, would have heard these complaints at annual meetings every year.

More directly: Bertram Kyle records that Vose "signed the ledger sheets carrying the 'station gain' line every year from the time he took office." This line showed, year after year, that the credited butterfat was lower than the received butterfat by approximately 5.25%. If Vose "put his thumb on the bottom figure" and signed without reading the lines above, he was still approving (by signature) a pattern that showed year after year a consistent gain—an anomaly that could have prompted inquiry.

Furthermore, the 1919 warning letter (warning farmers about water in milk) was sent by Vose, which indicates he was aware there was a problem perceived as potentially fraud or contamination.

However: The glass was not condemned until June 1923, and there is no evidence that Vose (or anyone else at the time) understood that the glass was the cause. The sources show Vose aware of a problem but not investigating it; he signed ledgers carrying an unexplained anomaly (the station gain), and he tried to address a perceived problem (added water) with a warning letter. The fact that his attempted explanation (water) was not the true cause does not prove ignorance—it proves he was working with incomplete information, as everyone was.

The key evidence: Vose knew tests ran low (the complaints were constant), but he did not know the glass was the cause, and nothing in the sources indicates he investigated to find out why.

**Direct factual contradictions found in the retellings:**

**Resolution:** The station building opened May 4, 1896, but it did not have its own testing equipment until May 1897. The sources describing the opening of the station (Orin Falke in 1959, citing records, or Bertram Kyle, stating the equipment came in 1897-1898) are describing different things. Bertram Kyle says "for its first season or two it borrowed its testing glass from the creamery here, and then its own outfit came from the supply house and went into use on the first of May, 1898"—but the invoice is dated 1897. This is a minor discrepancy (1897 vs. 1898 for installation), likely reflecting the difference between invoice date and actual use date.

**Method used:** Checking dates against documented records (the invoice from Junia Ammon). The 1897 invoice is primary documentation, so May 1897 for equipment arrival is more reliable than later recollections of exact installation dates.

# Section F — Theory

**F1. Causal theory of the Ostrey Hollow shortfall**

**The defective glass explanation:**

In April 1897, Ammon & Sons of Tarnet manufactured and shipped six dozen milk test pipettes to the Larrow Valley Cooperative. The pipettes were marked to deliver 17.6 c.c. of milk for a butter-fat test. The mold used to blow these pipettes was defective or worn; the glass came from the mold delivering an average of 16.27 c.c., short by 1.33 c.c. (or 7.56%). This defect was present from the first pipette blown. The glass was packed and shipped and arrived in April 1897; it was installed at the Ostrey Hollow station and put into use in May 1897. 

**Why this size of error?** The mold defect was small—7.56% short—but consistent. Junia Ammon's hypothesis (recorded in her company history, r20) is that "the mold had been reground and the lot ran short. It was an accident and nobody in the works caught it. A gauge sits on a nail and gets knocked off the nail and hung back on it; a mold comes back from the shop a hair different and looks exactly as it looked before." This explains both the consistent error (the mold was uniformly wrong) and the oversight (no individual inspection caught it).

**Why did it begin when it did?** May 1897, when the defective glass arrived and was installed. The station borrowed glass from Larrow Green for its first season (1896-1897), and that glass presumably worked correctly. When Ammon's product arrived in May 1897, the defective glass took over.

**Why did it end when it did?** In June 1923, the state dairy inspector Merle Strawn ran the pipettes against a calibrated burette and found they did not deliver their mark. He condemned the entire lot and ordered new. The Cooperative ordered replacement glass within the week, and it arrived within a month. From July 1923 onward, tests at Ostrey Hollow rose to 3.85% and stayed there.

**Why nothing else had to change:** Because the problem was the glass itself. The defect was consistent (7.56% short, every pipette, every test), so the low readings were uniform and predictable. No change in herds, hay, diet, water, or testing procedure was necessary or occurred for the problem to manifest or persist. It was entirely mechanical—a piece of glass being wrong.

---

**Three competing explanations and evidence against each:**

**(1) The water theory (proposed by Manager Selby Vose, circulated in 1919):**
The Ostrey Hollow farmers were adding water to their milk, making the fat reading lower.

**Evidence defeating it:**
- Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, tested with the lactometer at every visit from 1908 forward and "never once found added water at that station" (per Orin Falke, r22).
- The 1911 borrowed-glass experiment: when the glass changed, tests rose markedly, while the herds and farmers' practices did not change. If water were being added, no change in glass would change the water content.

**(2) The theft theory (proposed by an officer of the Association at the 1925 hearing):**
Ansel Keddie was removing cream and writing low tests to conceal the loss.

**Evidence defeating it:**
- The 1911 experiment again: Ansel Keddie tested with the borrowed glass and the tests rose, proving he was not systematically falsifying readings. If he were stealing and covering it up, his degree of theft and falsification would have to change with the glass change, which makes no sense.
- The ledger reconciliation (Bertram Kyle, r23): The station accounts showed consistent "station gain" of about 5.25% every year, which reconciles with the accounting. If cream were stolen, received cream at Larrow Green would be short by more than this.
- Orin Falke's note (r22): "The same man [Ansel] made the tests in that interval [May-July 1911], under no different supervision, and the figures rose," which proves "the nine weeks of 1911 dispose of it" [the theft accusation].

**(3) The wet hay theory (proposed by a committee of the Association in 1908, supported by a veterinarian in 1926):**
The wet hay and poor grazing of Hessel Bottom thin the cream of cows fed on them, naturally depressing the fat test.

**Evidence against it:**
- While the 1911 college feeding trial (Bulletin No. 214) confirmed that bog hay fed as whole roughage depressed butterfat by "not more than five hundredths of one per cent" in winter and nothing measurable in summer, this accounts for only 0.05 percentage points, far less than the 0.30 points observed (from 3.61% recorded to 3.91% true).
- Even allowing for the full 0.05 hay effect, true butterfat at Ostrey Hollow would have been 3.61% + 0.05% (hay effect) + 0.25% (additional unexplained) = 3.91%, leaving 0.25 percentage points unaccounted for by hay alone.
- More conclusively, in 1924 after new glass was installed, the same herds on the same wet hay recorded 3.85%, which is close to the calculated true value of 3.91%. If wet hay naturally depressed tests, why did new glass raise them? The hay did not change.

**Orin Falke's resolution (r22):** "I withdraw the concession made on page four" (where he had tentatively accepted the wet hay theory). The bog hay study "will not carry nineteen hundredths in July. I withdraw the concession..."

---

**Subsidiary questions:**

**(a) Did the 1925 board of arbitration ever examine the condemned glassware?**

No. Cleve Oram (r21) records: "Two of the sittings of it altogether and two of them were held up at the station itself, in the weigh room — that is the intake room, the same room, with the scale in it — because the books and the scales and the machinery were all up there and it was easier to carry the men to the machinery than the machinery to the men... And the crate was up the ladder over their heads the entire time. Twenty feet, if that... Two of the three sessions of that arbitration sat in the room underneath it." Yet "it could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second [book—the receiving book] beside the fourth [book—the state inspector's notebook] and looked." The board had access to the inspector's notebook documenting the condemnation but apparently did not examine the actual glass.

Bertram Kyle (r23) confirms: "Four books. I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked." But the "four books" were: the test book, the receiving book, the weigh book, and Strawn's notebook. The crate itself was not carried in.

**Conclusion: The board did not examine the condemned glassware.**

**(b) What did the tester himself believe the cause was?**

Ansel Keddie believed, or at least suspected, that it was the glass. In 1911, when the borrowed glass was used and tests rose, he "noted the fact in his book and set a question mark against it" (Duncan Keddie, r18). Duncan records: "He came home that summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book." 

However, Duncan also notes: "He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove." So Ansel suspected the glass but had no proof. In the 1925 hearing, he had his one chance to make the accusation officially, and he did not use it—or rather, the sources do not record him doing so. The board awarded money without admitting cause, and Ansel was dismissed without ever having the chance to defend himself fully.

**Conclusion: Ansel Keddie suspected the glass (at least by 1911) but could not prove it and never publicly accused the glass. He died with the suspicion unnamed.**

# Section G — Summary (150 words maximum)

The Larrow Valley dairy cooperative's Ostrey Hollow station (opened 1896–1897) received milk from six Hessel Bottom farms for thirty years. Farmers complained that tests ran systematically low, costing them approximately $19,600 in unpaid butterfat. Three explanations were offered: farmers added water; the tester Ansel Keddie stole cream; or the wet hay naturally reduced fat content. All failed scrutiny. In 1923, a state inspector condemned the station's pipettes, which delivered 7.6% less milk than marked. New glass was installed; tests rose 0.24 points. The defect had been present since 1897, when Ammon & Sons shipped flawed pipettes. A 1925 arbitration awarded $3,800 without admission and dismissed Keddie. In 1958, Keddie's granddaughter Ivy found the condemned glass in the loft, measured it, and published findings clearing Ansel (dead 24 years). The scandal required eight separate records—farm books, station books, office ledgers, company history, diary, newspapers, inspectors' notes, county report—to piece together because no single account grasped the full chain: glass defect → low tests → farmer grievance → dismissal → storage → discovery → vindication.
