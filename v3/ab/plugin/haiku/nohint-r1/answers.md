# Answer Sheet: Part 1 (Section A, Stories 1–4)

## Section A — Reconstruction: The Eight Stories

### Story 1: Larrow Valley Cooperative and the Ostrey Hollow Station (1889–1897)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association formed in 1889 when farmers organized at Larrow Green, building their main creamery there by 1891. By-law Article Seven stipulated payment by tested butterfat content, not milk weight. In 1895–96, recognizing that milk production on the upper valley required local handling, the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles upstream. The station opened May 4, 1896. It operated on a simple principle: farmers delivered milk, which was weighed in, sampled, and tested for butterfat; the cream was hauled to Larrow Green for centralized processing, while skim milk was returned to farmers. The station served six farms on Hessel Bottom, with Emmet Brant's property being the largest. Ansel Keddie, who had married into the valley (marrying Orra Brant, Emmet's sister, in 1889), took the position of tester and separator man at opening. He was a careful, methodical man who kept meticulous records. In spring 1897, the Association purchased its testing equipment from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet: six dozen pipettes marked 17.6 cc, twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, a hand tester, and freight, totaling $77.93. This equipment arrived and went into use May 1, 1897. The station's first season preceded formal station accounts; approximately 1,100,000 pounds of milk arrived by farmer memory and oral tradition. Ansel and his wife Orra established their household at Ostrey Hollow where he would work for the next twenty-nine seasons, and Duncan, their son, was born in 1894. The foundation was set for what appeared to be an ordinary cooperative dairy operation.

**Sources:** Duncan Keddie (r18) on family and dates; Junia Ammon (r20) on purchase details and invoice; Bertam Kyle (r23) on first season volume; Orra Keddie diary (r24) on May 1, 1897 glass arrival; Orin Falke (r22) on station opening date and Ansel's tenure.

### Story 2: Ammon & Sons Manufactures Faulty Glassware (1897)

The testing glassware that Ammon & Sons supplied to Larrow Valley in spring 1897 represented the firm's core business—supplying dairy testing equipment to creameries throughout the state. Milk test pipettes were precision instruments requiring skilled glassblowing: a worker gathered glass, blew the bulb, drew the stem to specification, and marked the 17.6 cc delivery point while glass was still warm. The piece then entered the lehr for slow cooling, was gauged the next morning, and either packed or discarded. The manufacturing process was human and fallible. What happened to the specific lot shipped to Ostrey Hollow remains unclear from the sources, but Junia Ammon's 1949 account, written by the firm's own historian, speculates credibly: the mold had likely been reground, producing a systematic short measurement, and no quality check caught it because a boy might gauge the first, tenth, and fiftieth pipette in a batch, finding them "consistent—they are consistently wrong." One lot of pipettes was packed in a wooden crate, labeled for Larrow Valley's Ostrey Hollow station, and shipped from Tarnet in 1897. The pipettes delivered approximately 16.27 cc instead of 17.6 cc—a short of about 7.6% or 1.33 cc per pipette. When a short charge of milk went into a test bottle ruled for a full charge, the resulting fat percentage read proportionally lower across the entire thirty-year lifespan of the glassware. No one in the glassmaking process knew of the defect; no one at the Association tested the pipettes against a calibrated burette upon receipt; the mark blown onto the glass side certified accuracy, and that mark was trusted. The faulty glassware would go undetected for twenty-six years.

**Sources:** Junia Ammon (r20) on manufacturing process, speculation on the error, and the 1897 invoice and 1923 correspondence.

### Story 3: Three Decades of Complaint and False Explanations (1902–1923)

From nearly the station's opening, milk delivered to Ostrey Hollow tested lower than milk from elsewhere. The station's average over the complaint years stood at 3.61 butterfat percentage versus Larrow Green's 3.78 and the county norm of 3.78. The shortfall was consistent: 0.19 percentage points, flat as a floor, through wet years and dry, summer and winter. The first formal complaint appeared in 1902; it recurred in every decade thereafter. By the time arbitration was sought in 1925, farmers had spent twenty-three years arguing that something was wrong. Three separate explanations dominated valley discourse. First: the Ostrey Hollow farmers added water to their milk. The Association's manager, Selby Vose, circulated a circular warning to this effect in 1919, phrased civilly but unambiguously. However, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn tested with the lactometer at every circuit visit from 1908 forward and never found added water; this explanation could not be sustained. Second: Ansel Keddie stole cream and wrote low tests to conceal loss. An Association officer urged this charge at the 1925 hearing. But the 1911 incident refuted it decisively: on May 8, 1911, Ansel's own measuring glass broke, and he borrowed a measure from elsewhere. For nine weeks (May 8 – July 10), while using the borrowed glass, the station's tests rose markedly and visibly. When he resumed his own glass on July 10, tests fell immediately back to their habitual level. The herds, pasture, season, machinery, and management—all identical. The same man made all tests under no different supervision. If theft were occurring, the borrowed-measure interval should show no change or should show an increase in his own benefit; instead, it showed the opposite. The third explanation: the boggy Hessel Bottom, with its coarse hay, depressed the cream of cows fed upon it. A committee sampled the herds in 1908 and reported to this effect; a veterinarian affirmed it in 1926. This held surface appeal, yet a State Agricultural College feeding trial conducted in 1911 and published as Bulletin No. 214 found that bog hay fed as the whole roughage depressed butterfat test by at most 0.05 in winter and nothing measurable in summer. This could not explain a flat 0.19 year-round. All three explanations were false, yet during these decades the defect remained undiscovered, undocumented, and unknown.

**Sources:** Orin Falke (r22) on statistics, timeline, and the three explanations and their refutations; Orra Keddie diary (r24) on March 1902 talk and family reaction; Orin Falke on the 1911 broken-measure experiment.

### Story 4: The 1919 Circular and the Family Rupture

In June 1919, the Association's manager, Selby Vose, prepared and circulated a formal written warning to the Ostrey Hollow patrons. The letter warned of added water in milk and bore Vose's signature. It went to eleven families on Hessel Bottom (nine named in Hazel Brant's retelling, eleven in Orra's diary). The circular was a hard document to receive: it implied fraud by the farmers themselves, the only accusation ever directed publicly against them rather than against the station's equipment or personnel. Ansel Keddie, the tester himself under scrutiny from the start, refused to let anyone else deliver the letters. He took them round himself to each of the eleven families, standing at each door while they read it, then came home late that night with his hat still on—a gesture of formal, contained distress. His wife Orra noted the unusual behavior: he did not speak about it, but his actions spoke of his refusal to have others bear this burden. Two days later, her brother Emmet Brant—the largest patron on Hessel Bottom, the one who should have been first to protest—came to Ansel's house. He did not enter. He stood in the yard and confronted Ansel: if the circular's accusation were false, why had Ansel carried it himself rather than letting someone else? Ansel's answer was stark: he carried it so that no one else would have to bear it. The logic of his action implied that the accusation, while wrong, came from the Association, and the tester could at least prevent other farmers from becoming its messengers. Emmet did not accept this reasoning. He left and did not enter his sister's house again for ten years—a rift that persisted until his death in 1929 and was only healed by time's passage and external circumstance. The 1919 circular revealed the depth of suspicion running through the valley. It also revealed Ansel's character: he was willing to absorb social punishment to protect others, even when the suspicion was directed at them, not at him. No one in the valley added water to their milk, but the circular damaged families and festered in Orra's memory and heart.

**Sources:** Orra Keddie diary (r24), entries June 1919 and June 1919 (two days after); Bertram Kyle (r23) on the circular's composition and delivery; Hazel Brant (r17) mentioning nine families receiving nine letters, none answered.

### Story 5: The Glass Condemnation and Concealment (1923)

In June 1923, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn made one of his routine circuit visits to the Ostrey Hollow station. Strawn was an independent actor—kin to no one in the valley, holding no local allegiance or financial stake. He carried a rubber-banded notebook in which he recorded every observation. On this visit, he performed a standard test: he ran the station's milk pipettes against a calibrated burette. They failed. The pipettes would not deliver their marked capacity. Strawn condemned the entire lot on the spot, a routine condemnation based on obvious instrumental failure, and instructed the manager to order new glassware. His entry was brief and factual; it contained no backward-looking commentary. The Association received his written condemnation and acted immediately. On June 28, 1923, they wrote to Ammon & Sons ordering new glass—six dozen pipettes and six dozen test bottles. Ammon & Sons replied July 2: the order was entered and would ship within the month; the firm regretted the trouble; their books of the nineties could not tell them anything about the original lot. The response was technically truthful (the works records had burned in the 1931 fire), but it was, in Junia Ammon's later judgment, insufficient. It was a thin reply to a customer of twenty-six years' standing. New glassware arrived and went into use. From 1924 onward, the station's average test rose to 3.85—normal for the county. Strawn's condemnation had cured the problem. Yet the condemned glassware—the evidence of systematic error spanning twenty-six years—was placed in a wooden crate, covered with a tarpaulin, and carried up into the loft above the intake room. It sat there undisturbed for thirty-five years. No one at the Association or the state office connected the condemned glassware to the prior decades of complaint. The condemnation was a routine equipment failure. The fact that routine equipment failure happened to solve a mystery no one had solved was noted by no one because no one was looking. The evidence was concealed not by intention but by simple lack of connection.

### Story 6: The 1925 Arbitration, Award, and Dismissal

By 1924, the Ostrey Hollow patrons had endured decades of low payments. In January 1925, they petitioned for a board of arbitration. The board convened on March 17, 1925, at Ashlin under the chairmanship of an attorney. Three sittings followed: one in town and two at the station itself, held in the weigh room so the board could examine the scales, machinery, and books in situ. The proceedings took place from March 17 to May 4. Farmers argued that the station's tests were systematically low and their payments therefore short. Association officers defended the testing procedures and the tester's competence. The manager testified. The tester, Ansel Keddie, attended and gave testimony. At some point during the hearings (two of which were held at the station), Ansel and others involved passed by or sat above the condemned glassware in the loft—twenty feet above their heads, separated by a ceiling. None of them examined it. None of them knew what it was or its significance. Four documentary records were brought to bear: the station's test book in Ansel's hand; the receiving book; Emmet Brant's private weigh book (lent for the arbitration); and Merle Strawn's state inspector's notebook for the year he condemned the glass. Bertram Kyle, the office clerk, carried them in and out. "I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked." The board heard the testimony, examined the records, and found no cause established—insufficient evidence of wrongdoing. Yet they awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800 (one source says $3,500; the discrepancy is minor and likely attributable to memory or reporting variation), "without admission," which is lawyer's language meaning "we are paying you and we are not saying why." The award was charged to the station account. On April 30, 1925—four days before the board announced its finding—the Association dismissed Ansel Keddie. The sequence is important: he was discharged before the award was made public, so the dismissal appeared conclusive of guilt while the award remained technically without admission. He had held his post for twenty-nine seasons. He came up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm, hung it behind the shed door, and asked his wife what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it.

**Sources:** Orin Falke (r22) on arbitration dates, locations, and award amount; Orra Keddie diary (r24) on March 17, April 1925 (two entries), and April 30; Bertram Kyle (r23) on the four books and the board's handling of them; Cleve Oram (r21) on the dates and award; Duncan Keddie (r18) on Ansel's dismissal April 30, 1925, and Ansel's stipulation that the book was "lent" not given to Ivy; Falke on the condemnation of the glass in 1923 and the board's failure to connect it.

### Story 7: Ansel Keddie's Years Under Suspicion (1925–1934)

After his dismissal in 1925, Ansel Keddie lived under the imputation of fraud in a valley where he had tested milk for twenty-nine years. Men whose milk he had weighed, whose cream he had hauled, whose livelihood he had calculated—they now believed or half-believed that he had stolen from them. He never asked them to defend him publicly. He did not seek vindication or demand a hearing. He accepted the dismissal and returned to the work his hands knew: carpentry. He took whatever jobs came: mending doors, fixing sheds, hanging barn doors so true they would swing for thirty years. He worked "from light to dark," and his wife noted that he whistled—something he never did when he was paid, suggesting a liberty or relief in the unpaid work. He kept four or five cows on insufficient land, practicing the husbandry he knew. His son Duncan, teaching school at Ashlin, knew his father's name was clouded. His daughter-in-law and granddaughter—Ivy Keddie grew up in this household of quiet, unspoken vindication, watching her grandfather work as a carpenter while the valley's opinion sat on him. In 1934, at sixty-eight, Ansel died at home in the forenoon without great pain. Duncan came up in the night. Many people attended the funeral—more than expected, Orra noted. Every one of them spoke of his skill with doors. Not one of them said the other thing. Orra reflected bitterly: they had nine years to say it, they had the chance at the funeral, and they did not. "He never asked them to," she wrote. "He said to me once and only once, and not lately, that a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name." He carried the suspicion in silence, worked with his hands to feed his family, and died under the imputation. He never knew that his granddaughter would, twenty-four years later, discover the evidence that would clear his name.

**Sources:** Orra Keddie diary (r24), entries June 1925 onward, October 1927, February 1934; Cleve Oram (r21) on Ansel's carpentry skill and reputation; Duncan Keddie (r18) on Ansel's dates and tenure.

### Story 8: Ivy Keddie's Discovery and the Vindication (1958 onward)

Ivy Keddie, born in 1931, was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter and the daughter of Duncan Keddie, the schoolteacher. She attended the State Agricultural College at Farland and pursued graduate study in dairy husbandry. In 1958, at age twenty-seven, she was conducting research on early dairy testing practices. In February 1958, she went to the Ostrey Hollow station—the building now used only for storing hay tools. She climbed the ladder nailed to the wall in the intake room, pushed through the trap door, and found in the loft, under dust and chaff, a wooden crate covered with a tarpaulin. Inside were glass pipettes and test bottles—the station's original equipment, condemned in 1923. She brought the crate east to Farland. In the college's laboratory, she measured nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette. Every one had been blown on the stem to 17.6 cc. Not one delivered it. The average of the lot was 16.27 cc—consistently short by about 1.33 cc or 7.6%. She worked with Professor Leland Vaught to analyze the error's implications. In March 1958, they completed their calculations. Over the affected years (1897–1923), the station's patrons had been underpaid for approximately 67,000 pounds of butterfat. At an average price of 25 cents per pound (the valley's long-term average), this represented $19,600 in uncollected value. The Association's entire testing outfit, purchased for $77.93 in 1897, had cost six dozen pipettes at thirty-five cents each—a $25.20 line item that proved to be the most expensive line item in the county's economic history. In October 1958, Vaught and Keddie published Bulletin No. 471 from the college, presenting the evidence and conclusion: Ansel Keddie, the tester, had done his work correctly; the glass was wrong; no patrons had added water, no cream had been stolen, no hay had depressed the tests—the glass, consistently short, had read every test low by the same proportion, year after year. The local newspaper, the Ashlin Chronicle, ran the story front-page: "Old Ordell Wrong Righted." Ivy, interviewed by telephone, said: "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not. He tested the milk in front of the state's own man and he tested it the way he was taught, and the reason his figures would not come right was in a crate over his head the entire time. I would like it printed that he never knew." Ansel Keddie died in 1934, twenty-four years before vindication. His wife Orra died in 1948, ten years before the discovery, and thus never witnessed her husband's name cleared. Ivy's discovery transformed a family tragedy into a valley-wide historical record. The vindication came too late for those who had borne the imputation, but in time for the truth to be permanently recorded.

**Sources:** The Ashlin Chronicle (r19) on Ivy's age, the discovery in February 1958, the measurements in March, the publication in October 1958, the cost to farmers of $19,600, and her statement; Orin Falke (r22) on Ivy finding the glass in February 1958 and measuring nine surviving pipettes on March 9 (slightly different from Chronicle's "March"); Duncan Keddie (r18) on Ivy being born 1931 and her role; Hazel Brant (r17) on Ivy's discovery and measurement; Cleve Oram (r21) on the 67,000 pounds of butterfat figure; Junia Ammon (r20) on the 1897 invoice and the pipette cost.

## Section B — Relationships (8 points)

### B1: What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Ansel married Orra Brant in 1889 and had one son, Duncan, born in 1894. Duncan married and had one daughter, Ivy, born in 1931. Duncan Keddie (r18) states plainly: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter." This is sometimes confused in the sources: The Ashlin Chronicle (r19) first refers to her as Ansel's "great-niece," a term that Orin Falke (r22) explicitly corrects in a note appended to his report, stating Ivy is "the granddaughter and not the great-niece." The confusion likely arises from the generational remove (grandparent to grandchild), but the genealogy is clear: Ansel → Duncan → Ivy.

### B2: What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant?

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. Hazel Brant was the daughter of Roy Brant; Roy was the son of Emmet Brant. Emmet Brant was the brother of Orra Brant (Ansel Keddie's wife). Therefore, Roy Brant was the first cousin of Duncan Keddie (Ansel's son), making their children—Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy—second cousins. Duncan Keddie (r18) states this explicitly: "Roy Brant and I are therefore first cousins—the children of a brother and a sister, which is the whole definition and there is nothing more to it. Roy's daughter is Hazel Brant. My daughter is Ivy. Hazel and Ivy are second cousins." The sources leave no ambiguity on intervening generations: Emmet and Orra were brother and sister; Roy and Duncan were their respective children (first cousins); Hazel and Ivy were their respective grandchildren (second cousins). Some sources confused this (Cleve Oram states it uncertainly), but Duncan's explicit genealogy is authoritative.

### B3: Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter to what happened at Ostrey Hollow?

Selby Vose was the son of Gideon Vose, not his nephew. Gideon Vose organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association and was its first president; he also wrote its by-laws. Selby was his son and became manager of the Association, taking office in 1911. This matters to the Ostrey Hollow story because Selby Vose, as manager, was responsible for several key decisions: (1) he circulated the 1919 circular warning patrons about water in their milk, a document that came to embody the valley's suspicion of the patrons themselves and that Ansel Keddie personally delivered; (2) as manager, he received Merle Strawn's condemnation of the glass in 1923 and ordered replacement, yet failed to investigate why the condemnation solved the long-standing testing discrepancy; (3) he authorized the charging of the $3,800 arbitration award to the station account, a decision that some found expeditious but others found questionable. Bertram Kyle's remark—that Vose was courteous but "never read the lines, just the totals"—suggests a managerial style focused on the bottom line rather than scrutiny of detail. Gideon's foundational role as the Association's organizer frames Selby's later position: Selby inherited a well-established institution, but his failure to understand (or perhaps failure to care about) what was happening in its books cost the Ostrey Hollow patrons dearly and cost Ansel Keddie his reputation.

### B4: What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?

There was no kinship relationship between the Vose family and the Keddie family, despite persistent rumors in the valley. Duncan Keddie (r18) addresses this explicitly: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either." He continues: "I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her sit down. It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me." Orra Brant (later Orra Keddie), who was Gideon Vose's contemporary and who valued genealogical knowledge, never mentioned any kinship with the Voses in her diary or letters to her son. Duncan notes: "She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it." The rumor persisted in the valley (Cleve Oram mentions hearing it), but the evidence is that it was unfounded gossip. The Keddie family's conflict with the Association (via Ansel's dismissal) was between employee and institution, not between related families.

## Section C — Math (14 points; show working)

### C1. Proportion of error and true average test

**(a) By what proportion did the Ostrey Hollow station's tests differ from the truth?**

From the Ashlin Chronicle (r19): "Each one of them was blown on the stem 17.6 c.c.... Not one of them delivered it. The average of the lot came to 16.27 c.c."

Shortfall per pipette: 17.6 − 16.27 = 1.33 cc

Proportion of error:
- Error = 1.33 / 17.6 = 0.0756 or approximately 7.56%
- Alternatively, expressed as the test multiplier: 16.27 / 17.6 = 0.9244, so tests read at 92.44% of true value

The sources derive this from the glassware itself: pipettes short by 1.33 cc on a 17.6 cc delivery charge yields tests systematically low by this proportion every time.

**(b) True average test and point shortfall**

From Orin Falke (r22): "The station's average over the long period of complaint stood at three sixty-one, where the Larrow Green creamery's own average over the same years was three seventy-eight, which is an ordinary figure for this county."

Recorded average at Ostrey Hollow: 3.61
Expected average (Larrow Green/county norm): 3.78
Apparent shortfall in records: 3.78 − 3.61 = 0.17 points

However, Falke also notes: "The deficiency, as the bulletin now fixes it, was nineteen hundredths of a point, and it ran at the same nineteen hundredths in every month of the year, in the flush of June and in the depth of February alike."

True deficiency: 0.19 percentage points

To derive the true average: if recorded average was 3.61 and it was systematically low by 0.19, then:
True average = 3.61 + 0.19 = 3.80

(This aligns with the post-1923 average of 3.85 after new glass, representing the normal range.)

Recorded tests short by: 0.19 points

**(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited to patrons over affected years**

From the Ashlin Chronicle (r19): "the figure is nineteen thousand six hundred dollars ($19,600) — in a valley of six farms on a wet bottom, in the money of the eighteen-nineties and the years after."

From Cleve Oram (r21): "Sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat was what they made it, over the whole run of years, that the Hollow men were never paid for. Sixty-seven thousand pounds."

Butterfat underpaid for: 67,000 pounds

**(d) Worth in money and fraction actually received**

From the Ashlin Chronicle: total value = $19,600

From Orin Falke (r22): "The committee that sat on the matter in 1908 sat again in 1925 and awarded the patrons their money — thirty-eight hundred dollars, 'without admission,' which is lawyer's language for we are paying you and we are not saying why."

Fraction of loss recovered:
Award to patrons = $3,800
Total loss = $19,600
Fraction recovered = 3,800 / 19,600 = 0.1939 or approximately 19.4%

The patrons received approximately 19.4% (or about one-fifth) of the value they had been shorted.

### C2. Emmet Brant's 1910 figures (largest patron)

From Bertram Kyle (r23): "Forty million pounds of milk received at that station, which is sixteen hundred thousand pounds a year, year in and year out."

From Hazel Brant (r17): "That one I can say with my eyes shut... Nineteen ten. Two hundred thousand pounds even, delivered to the station."

Emmet Brant delivered 200,000 pounds of milk in 1910.

From Hazel: "And paid on a test of three forty-two" (3.42 percentage points).

**(a) Pounds of butterfat credited at recorded test:**
200,000 lbs milk × 3.42% = 200,000 × 0.0342 = 6,840 pounds of butterfat credited

**(b) Pounds of butterfat owed but not credited:**
True test should have been 3.42 + 0.19 = 3.61
True butterfat in 200,000 lbs: 200,000 × 0.0361 = 7,220 pounds
Shortfall: 7,220 − 6,840 = 380 pounds

**(c) Worth in money that year:**
From Bertram Kyle: "butterfat over that whole stretch averaged twenty-five cents the pound"
380 lbs × $0.25 = $95.00

Emmet Brant lost approximately $95 in that single year due to the glass error.

### C3. Total butterfat and percentage

**(a) Pounds of butterfat credited to patrons in total (recorded):**

From Bertram Kyle: 40 million pounds milk at Ostrey Hollow over the complaint years.

Average recorded test: 3.61 percentage points (Falke)

Total butterfat credited (at recorded rate):
40,000,000 lbs × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 pounds

**(b) Pounds that actually reached Larrow Green (true butterfat):**

From Cleve Oram and Falke: 67,000 pounds additional butterfat never credited.

True butterfat in the milk:
1,444,000 + 67,000 = 1,511,000 pounds

**(c) Express as percentage:**

The question asks for precision about what the percentage is "of."

Two interpretations:
1. Shortage as percentage of true amount: 67,000 / 1,511,000 = 0.0443 = 4.43% of true butterfat was not credited
2. Difference as percentage of recorded amount: 67,000 / 1,444,000 = 0.0464 = 4.64% more than recorded

The shortfall of 67,000 lbs represents approximately **4.4% of the true butterfat** present in the milk, or equivalently, the recorded amount was **95.6% of true reality**.

### C5. Duration of faulty glassware use and first-year milk volume

**(a) How long was faulty glassware in use, and over how many full delivery years?**

Faulty glassware placed in use: May 1, 1897 (Orra's diary, r24)

Glassware condemned: June 12, 1923 (Falke, r22; Bertram Kyle says "June 1922" but Falke's official date of June 12, 1923 is more precise)

Duration in use: May 1, 1897 to June 12, 1923 = **26 years, 1+ month**

However, the money calculation runs over **full delivery years**. The station opened May 1896 but the first season preceded formal station accounts. Full-year accounting begins with the 1897–1898 season.

First season with full accounting: 1897 (partial, May onward) through 1898 full year
Last season with faulty glass: 1922–1923 (though June 1923 condemnation ended it mid-season)

Full delivery years affected: 1897–1923 = **27 calendar years** or **26 full seasons** depending on how yearly boundaries are counted

**Why these numbers differ:** The duration of use (26 years, 1 month) differs from full delivery years (26–27 seasons) because (1) the glassware began use mid-year in 1897 and was condemned mid-year in 1923; (2) financial accounting runs on seasonal/annual cycles, not precise calendar days; (3) the "long run of years" calculation (Falke and Kyle both cite a total period with 40 million pounds received at ~1.6 million per year) yields approximately 25 years, further confirming the complaint period was roughly 1898–1923 (26 years).

The Ashlin Chronicle calculates over "67,000 pounds of butterfat" as the total shortfall, which Falke's report derives as the cumulative error over 1897–1923. Different sources frame it slightly differently, but all converge on approximately 26–27 years of affected payments.

Forty million pounds of milk received at that station... Fourteen hundred thousand pounds a year [error on his part: 40M / 25 years = 1.6M, not 1.4M], and it did not vary greatly."

**First season milk volume: Approximately 1,100,000 pounds** (oral estimate only, not formally recorded). Cannot be determined from sources with certainty.

## Section D — Logic (10 points)

### D1. The 1911 broken-measure interval as an experiment

**When and duration:**
May 8, 1911 – July 10, 1911 = nine weeks (Falke, r22)

Orra's diary entry (r24): "May 8, 1911. A. home cross about a broken measure."

**The experimental design:**
- **Variable changed:** The glass pipette used for testing (from Ansel's own measure to a borrowed one)
- **Variables held constant:** The herds (same cows), pasture (same season), machinery (same separator), staff (same men), testing procedure (same tester, Ansel), supervision (no change)
- **Result:** Tests rose markedly and visibly during the nine-week interval; upon resumption of Ansel's own glass, tests fell immediately back to their prior level

Falke: "Through those nine weeks the station's tests stood markedly higher. On the day the station resumed its own glass they fell back to their former level."

**What it establishes:**
The interval proves that the consistently low tests were a property of **the measuring glass**, not of the herds, pasture, procedure, or the tester's competence or honesty. A change in one variable (glass) while holding all others constant produced a change in the result (test height), then a reversion when the original glass returned. This is the fundamental logic of controlled experimentation.

**What it does NOT establish:**
- It does NOT identify the specific defect in Ansel's glass (quantitative measurement comes later from the 1958 lab measurement)
- It does NOT prove the glass was defective at manufacture (though the 1958 measurement confirms it)
- It does NOT by itself name the extent of the error across 26 years (only the 1958 measurement by Ivy provides this)
- The interval was nine weeks, not a full season, so it does not rule out seasonal variation (though the sources note the error was "flat as a floor" year-round, summer and winter)

---

### D2. Three independent reasons the tester did not steal cream

**Reason 1: The 1911 interval (from D1 above)**
During the nine weeks when Ansel used a borrowed glass, tests rose. If he were stealing cream to mask cream theft with low tests, the borrowed-glass interval should show either no change or lower tests (his own benefit). Instead, it shows the opposite: higher tests using borrowed glass. Same man, same supervision, same station—cream cannot be simultaneously present and stolen. This interval is independent of any 1958 measurement and would refute the theft accusation even if the glassware were never measured later. Source: Orin Falke (r22).

**Reason 2: The "station gain" ledger entries across all years**
Bertram Kyle (r23) copied the annual station accounts for twenty-five years. Each year showed a "station gain"—the difference between butterfat credited to patrons on station test versus butterfat received at Larrow Green in the cream. This gain averaged five and one-quarter percent, "as usual," carried forward every year and read aloud at the annual meeting to fifty farmers. The gain was not anomalous; it was regular, named, explained (in the accounts), and publicly visible. If Ansel were stealing cream, the gain would either (a) vary opportunistically with his access and risk, or (b) disappear if he were stealing to disguise theft with false low tests. Instead, it is consistent, documented, and public. This reasoning is independent of the 1911 interval and independent of 1958. Source: Bertram Kyle (r23).

**Reason 3: State dairy inspector's independent observation**
Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, was "kin to nobody in the valley whatever" (Duncan, r18). He tested for added water annually from 1908 onward and "never once found added water at that station" (Falke). Strawn's notebooks were independent records held by the state. If cream were being stolen and tests falsified to hide theft, stolen cream would have to be *added* to inflate volumes (to account for the weight loss), which would show up as added water or other adulterant, or as impossibly high volume-to-fat ratios. Strawn found no such sign. His testing was independent, uninterested in the local imputation, and verifiable. Source: Orin Falke (r22).

**Why independent:**
- Reason 1 (1911) is an internal experimental observation
- Reason 2 (ledger) is documentary and administrative
- Reason 3 (Strawn) is external state oversight

These three operate at different levels and from different evidentiary bases. All three would hold even if the glassware had never been measured in 1958.

**Did the bookkeeper question the ledger entries?**
No. Bertram Kyle explicitly states: "That 'as usual.' I copied that line, or the like of it, more times than I can count, and I will tell the Society plainly that it never once crossed my mind to wonder at it, and I do not believe it crossed anybody else's either. It had a name. That is the thing about a name. A figure with a name on it has been explained, and you rule under it and go on to the next." The named entry required no justification and received none. Kyle's manager, Selby Vose, "never read the lines, just the totals." The bookkeeper Wilbur Teague (r23) "never asked anybody anything in forty years. That is not a criticism. It was his notion of the position: the figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people."

### D3. Did the manager know the station's tests ran low?

**Answer: Yes, the manager knew or should have known, and there is evidence he was willfully inattentive rather than ignorant.**

**Justification:**

Selby Vose became manager in 1911 (r23, r18). From 1902 onward, there were formal complaints about the low tests (Falke, r22). Vose would have inherited these complaints and heard them repeatedly in Association meetings. In 1919, he himself circulated the written warning to farmers about water in milk, an action that implicitly acknowledged the low-test problem and proposed an explanation (fraud by farmers). He was thus cognizant of the problem.

Moreover, as manager, Vose received and signed the annual station accounts, which Bertram Kyle prepared and which showed the consistent "five and a quarter percent, as usual" station gain. Kyle notes: "The station up at Ostrey Hollow... for the long run of years that the patrons afterward complained of... the figure was got out for the arbitration and I got it out." The accounts were "made up, and were copied, and were carried forward, and once a year were laid before the annual meeting in a fair hand."

Falke's report (r22) notes: "The manager of the Association had circulated a warning to the Ostrey Hollow patrons about added water as early as 1919." This shows Vose was directing his attention to the problem.

However, Kyle also observes: "He was courteous to every man who came into that office, farmer or drummer or bank, and he never once let a caller feel he was being hurried. And he read the totals and he never read the lines above them. He was not a man who read. He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign. I watched him do it a thousand times and thought nothing of it, because that is what managers were."

**Conclusion:** Vose knew the tests ran low because he received complaints and circulated a public warning about it. He did not investigate the cause deeply, as evidenced by his failure to examine the state inspector's notebooks alongside the test records, and by his "thumb on the bottom figure" style of review. He knew the problem but did not see fit to examine it rigorously. This suggests negligence or indifference rather than ignorance.

## Section F — Theory (6 points)

### F1. Causal theory of the Ostrey Hollow shortfall

**The unified causal explanation:**

In spring 1897, the Larrow Valley Cooperative Association ordered testing glassware from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet for its new Ostrey Hollow skimming station. The invoice (April 14, 1897) included six dozen milk test pipettes marked 17.6 cc at thirty-five cents each. At manufacture, a production error occurred—either mold degradation or undetected quality-control failure—yielding pipettes that delivered approximately 16.27 cc instead of 17.6 cc, a shortfall of 7.56% per pipette. When testing resumed using these pipettes, every measurement read proportionally low: a sample containing true 3.80 butterfat tested as 3.61 (low by 0.19 percentage points) consistently across all thirty years of use and across all seasons (summer flush and winter depth), because the error was inherent to the glass, not to procedure, herds, feed, or operator. The glassware was placed in use May 1, 1897, and operated continuously until June 12, 1923, when state dairy inspector Merle Strawn ran them against a calibrated burette, found them deficient, and ordered replacement. The error persisted unchanged for 26 years because no one performed a calibration test against an external standard until 1923. The error began in 1897 because that is when the faulty glass was manufactured and installed. The error ended in 1923 because the external audit detected it. Nothing at the station or on the farms had to change: the glassware was simply wrong from the moment it arrived, and remained wrong, reading the truth low by a fixed proportion, every single time.

**Three competing explanations and their refutations:**

1. **The "added water" theory**
   - Advanced by: Selby Vose, the Association manager (r23, r24)
   - Vose circulated a written warning in June 1919 to all Ostrey Hollow patrons
   - Specific evidence defeating it: State dairy inspector Merle Strawn tested the milk with the lactometer at every annual visit from 1908 onward and found no evidence of added water (Falke, r22). Lactometric testing directly detects water; Strawn's independent, annual, systematic testing found nothing. Additionally, the 1911 borrowed-measure interval (D1) shows that tests rose when the glass changed, not the milk. If water were being added, the added water would persist regardless of glassware.

2. **The "cream theft" theory**
   - Advanced by: An officer of the Association at the 1925 hearing (Falke, r22; Kyle, r23)
   - Theory: Ansel Keddie removed cream from the sample and wrote low tests to conceal the loss
   - Specific evidence defeating it: The 1911 borrowed-measure interval. For nine weeks (May 8 – July 10), Ansel used a borrowed glass while his own was being repaired. Tests rose markedly. If he were stealing cream and lowering tests to hide it, the borrowed glass should show no change (he could still steal) or lower tests (his own benefit). Instead, tests rose dramatically, then fell again when he resumed his own glass. The same man, same station, same access, same opportunity—only the glass changed, and the result inverted. Theft cannot explain this. (Orin Falke, r22)

3. **The "poor hay" theory**
   - Advanced by: An Association committee in 1908; a veterinarian in 1926 (Falke, r22; Orin Falke footnote, r22)
   - Theory: The wet, coarse hay of Hessel Bottom depressed the butterfat in cows' milk
   - Specific evidence defeating it: The State Agricultural College conducted a feeding trial with bog hay in 1911 and published Bulletin No. 214. The summary: "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Five hundredths (0.05) in winter, nothing in summer. The observed error was nineteen hundredths (0.19) flat year-round. Five hundredths cannot explain nineteen hundredths, and zero in summer contradicts a year-round flat error. (Orin Falke, r22, footnote added after receiving Bulletin No. 214)

**Additional questions:**

- **Did the 1925 board of arbitration examine the condemned glassware?** No. The crate sat twenty feet above the hearing room in the loft, unexamined. Bertram Kyle states: "Four books. I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked." The second book was the receiving book (butterfat delivered at Larrow Green), the fourth was the state inspector's notebook (which recorded the condemnation of the glass). These were brought to the hearing, but no one apparently compared them or examined the condemned glass itself. The glass, invisible in the loft, remained unconsidered evidence.

- **What did the tester himself believe the cause was?** Ansel Keddie suspected the glass. In summer 1911, after the borrowed-measure interval, Duncan Keddie (r18) records: "He came home that summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book. I was seventeen and I remember the kitchen and I remember that he said it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not his way. What he wrote in the book was a question mark. He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove, and I have thought since that this was the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had." Ansel suspected the glass after the 1911 experiment but did not commit to the suspicion in his official records. He lived the rest of his life unable to prove what he suspected.

## Section G — Summary (4 points; max 150 words)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative, organized in 1889, built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow in 1896. In 1897, it purchased testing glassware from Ammon & Sons—six dozen pipettes marked 17.6 cc. The pipettes were short by 7.56%, delivering only 16.27 cc. For twenty-six years, this faulty glass produced systematically low butterfat test readings (0.19 percentage points below true value) for all six farms on Hessel Bottom. Ansel Keddie, the tester, suspected the glass after a 1911 experiment but could not prove it. Three false explanations circulated (added water, theft, poor hay), all disproven. In 1925, without examining the condemned glass, a board awarded patrons $3,800—one-fifth of their actual loss—and dismissed Keddie. Ansel died in 1934, under imputation. His granddaughter Ivy Keddie, a college student, found the condemned glass in the loft in 1958, measured it, and proved her grandfather innocent. The glass alone caused the loss.
