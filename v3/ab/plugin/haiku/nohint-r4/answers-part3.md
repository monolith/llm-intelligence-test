# Section C: Math

## C1. Proportion and measurements

(a) **Proportion of error from glassware:**

The marked capacity: 17.6 c.c.
Average actual delivery (measured by Ivy Keddie, March 1958): 16.27 c.c.
Shortfall per pipette: 17.6 - 16.27 = 1.33 c.c.
Proportion short: 1.33 / 17.6 = 0.0756 or 7.56%
**Answer: The pipettes delivered approximately 7.56% less than their marking, or delivered 92.44% of marked volume.**

This proportion comes directly from Ivy's measurements against a calibrated burette, reported in Bulletin No. 471 (cited by Ashlin Chronicle and Orin Falke).

(b) **True average test and shortfall:**

Recorded average at Ostrey Hollow: 3.61 butterfat percent (per Orin Falke, Ashlin Chronicle)
Recorded average at other stations: 3.78 to 3.80 percent
Observed deficiency: 0.19 percent (Hazel Brant and Orin Falke)

**True average at Ostrey Hollow: approximately 3.80 percent**
**Recorded tests short by: 0.19 percentage points**

Working: If pipettes delivered 92.44% of true milk amount, they yield readings proportionally low. If the true fat content was 3.80%, a short pipette measuring 92.44% of that would read 3.80 × 0.9244 = 3.51%. But the recorded average was 3.61, suggesting either: (i) the true average was slightly higher (~3.90%), or (ii) there were measurement variations. Using the stated deficiency of 0.19 points, the sources indicate the true average was approximately 3.80.

(c) **Total butterfat not credited over full period:**

Hazel Brant (direct quote from taped interview): "Sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat was what they made it, over the whole run of years, that the Hollow men were never paid for."

Cleve Oram (corroborating): "Sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat was what they make it, over the whole run of years, that the Hollow men were never paid for."

**Answer: 67,000 pounds of butterfat (over the period 1897–1923)**

These figures appear in the 1958 bulletin calculations and are cited by multiple independent narrators from the valley (a farmer's daughter and a cream hauler's son).

(d) **Money value and fraction recovered:**

The Ashlin Chronicle (citing the bulletin): "The bulletin works out what the Ostrey Hollow patrons were never paid for the butter fat they actually hauled to that station, and the figure is nineteen thousand six hundred dollars ($19,600) — in a valley of six farms on a wet bottom, in the money of the eighteen-nineties and the years after."

Amount awarded in 1925: $3,800 (per Orin Falke and Orra Keddie's diary)

**Money owed: $19,600**
**Money received: $3,800**
**Fraction received: $3,800 / $19,600 = 0.1939 or approximately 19.4%**

Implicit butterfat price: $19,600 / 67,000 pounds ≈ $0.29 per pound (consistent with Bertram Kyle's statement that butterfat averaged "twenty-five cents the pound" over the long run, with variation by year).

## C2. Emmet Brant's 1910 figures

**Cannot be determined with precision from sources.** 

Bertram Kyle states the station received "Forty million pounds of milk received at that station" over "the long run of years" (1897–1923, 26 years), averaging "sixteen hundred thousand pounds a year, year in and year out." This gives 1,600,000 pounds per year as an average, but not specific to 1910.

Cleve Oram says his father "hauled that cream" with "two bays and a flat wagon," implying his father transported for many patrons, not just one, suggesting the annual flow through the station was high relative to any single farm's contribution.

Hazel Brant identifies Emmet as having "the largest piece" on Hessel Bottom and notes her grandfather Emmet (not Cleve's father) "farmed the largest place on Hessel Bottom and hauled the most milk into the station of any man on the creek."

If Emmet delivered approximately 25–30% of the station's 1,600,000-pound annual intake (reasonable for the largest patron), his contribution in 1910 would be 400,000–480,000 pounds.

At 3.61% recorded test: 400,000 × 0.0361 = 14,440 pounds credited (for largest estimate of 400,000; lower estimates proportionally less)
At 3.80% true test: 400,000 × 0.0380 = 15,200 pounds owed
**Difference: approximately 760 pounds shortchanged for that year**

At $0.25/pound: approximately $190 lost in 1910 alone.

**Note:** The sources do not provide Emmet's specific 1910 production, making exact calculation impossible. The 1908 committee noted he had "the biggest" herd, but no quantitative data for 1910 survives in the retellings.

## C3. Station gain percentage over full years

Bertram Kyle's ledger showed every year: butterfat credited by test, butterfat received at creamery, difference entered as "station gain—5.25 percent, as usual."

Over 40 million pounds of milk:
- Credited butterfat (at 3.61%): 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 pounds
- True butterfat (at 3.80%): 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 pounds
- Difference: 76,000 pounds (close to the stated 67,000; variation likely due to averaging)

**Station gain recorded: 5.25%**

(a) **Total credited: 1,444,000 pounds**
(b) **Total received at creamery: 1,520,000 pounds** (this is the true amount, since cream is paid by weight at the creamery, which uses accurate scales)
(c) **The difference as percentage: (1,520,000 − 1,444,000) / 1,520,000 = 76,000 / 1,520,000 = 0.05 or 5.0%**

The recorded "station gain" of 5.25% (loss in handling during separation, normal shrinkage) is suspiciously close to the shortfall caused by faulty pipettes (~5%), which is why Kyle's innocent bookkeeping masked the defect: the error was absorbed into an expected category.

## C5. Duration of use and first-season milk

(a) **Duration of faulty glass in use: 26 years and approximately 6 weeks** (May 1, 1897 to June 12, 1923)

**Full delivery years for money calculation: 26 years** (1897–1923, though 1897 is a partial season and 1923 is a partial season; the calculation typically runs from the 1898 full season to the 1923 partial season, yielding 25 full years plus two partial years)

**Why they differ:** The glassware was purchased in April 1897, went into use May 1, 1897 (partway through the spring season), and was condemned June 12, 1923 (partway through the summer season). The time span is thus 26 years and 6 weeks. However, accounting for full delivery seasons (spring through winter), the damage runs across 26 calendar years but only about 25.5 delivery seasons. The money calculation uses 26 years as a round figure, acknowledged by Bertram Kyle: "Forty million pounds of milk received at that station, which is sixteen hundred thousand pounds a year, year in and year out" for an implied 25 full years (1898–1922, say) plus two partial years.

(b) **First season milk (1897): approximately 1,100,000 pounds**

Bertram Kyle (the only source with knowledge of this): "People have asked me what the Hollow took in that first year and I have always said the same: a good eleven hundred thousand pounds, which is what I understood at the time from men who were there. It is not in any book I ever ruled."

Kyle notes these figures were never entered in his station account books (they were carried on the creamery's own books), so no surviving document confirms this exactly, but this is the only available estimate from a contemporary witness.

# Section D: Logic

## D1. The 1911 experiment: broken measure interval

**When and duration:** May 8, 1911 (date of breakage) to July 10, 1911 (resumption of original glass) = approximately 9 weeks (63 days).

**The variable:** The glass used to measure milk samples (pipettes and measuring bottles).

**What was held constant:** The herds of cows (same animals), the pasture (same bottom, Hessel Bottom), the season (spring through July), the tester and his methods (Ansel Keddie, using the same test procedure), the acid used, the apparatus, and the creamery's receiving scale.

**The result:** Tests rose markedly and noticeably higher during the nine weeks when the borrowed measure was used, then fell immediately back to their former low level when Ansel resumed his own glass.

**What it establishes:**
1. The persistent low reading is not caused by the milk itself (herds, feed, pasture all constant).
2. The persistent low reading is not caused by the tester's honesty or method (same person, same procedure, same acid).
3. The persistent low reading is caused by the measuring glass.
4. The glass is systematically short (delivering less milk than it appears to hold).

**What it does NOT establish:**
1. By how much the glass is short (that requires direct calibration against a burette, which Ansel did not possess).
2. Where the glass came from or whether it was deliberately faulty.
3. How long the glass had been wrong (it could have been wrong from the start or degraded over time).
4. What should be done about it (Ansel had suspicion but no proof and no authority to condemn equipment).

The 1911 interval is decisive because it proves the defect exists, isolates the cause, and exonerates Ansel of dishonesty. But it cannot prove the defect's magnitude or age without calibration.

---

## D2. Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream

1. **The 1911 borrowed-measure interval (Ansel's own test book, corroborated by Orin Falke):** If Ansel were stealing cream and reporting low tests to hide the theft, the amount stolen should be independent of which glass he used. But during the nine weeks he used borrowed glass, the tests rose markedly. If the cream loss from theft were constant, the tests would remain low. The fact that tests rose proves cream theft was not the explanation.

2. **The state dairy inspector's lactometer tests (Orin Falke, county report section 7):** "The state dairy inspector for this circuit tested with the lactometer at every visit from 1908 forward and never once found added water at that station." The lactometer detects both cream removal (which would lower specific gravity) and added water (which would raise it). No inspector ever detected either, over 17 years of annual visits. If Ansel had removed cream systematically, it would show. The inspector's notes prove this.

3. **The mathematics of the 1925 award and the 1923 test rise (Bertram Kyle's ledger, Orin Falke's report, Ashlin Chronicle):** In 1923, new glass was installed. From 1923 onward, tests rose to 3.85 (normal). If Ansel had been stealing cream, the amount stolen would not suddenly drop to zero when new glass arrived. Instead, the cream delivered should have continued to fall short if theft were the cause. But cream delivery rose in proportion to the new test readings. This proves the "shortfall" in cream was not theft but was an artifact of the faulty glass.

**Independence:** These three reasons are independent because each rests on a different kind of evidence: (1) comparative measurement at different times, (2) independent chemical inspection, (3) institutional financial records and temporal correlation. Reasons 1 and 3 would hold even if the glassware had never been measured in 1958 (they prove the glass, not via calibration but via the physical experiment and the ledger math).

**Did the Association's bookkeeper question the entries?** No. Bertram Kyle explicitly states he never thought to wonder at the "station gain" line: "I copied that line, or the like of it, more times than I can count, and I will tell the Society plainly that it never once crossed my mind to wonder at it, and I do not believe it crossed anybody else's either. It had a name. That is the thing about a name."

---

## D3. Did the manager know the station's tests ran low?

**Yes, Selby Vose knew the station's tests ran low. He is likely to have believed it was due to water-adding.**

**Justification:**

1. **Selby Vose took office in 1911** (Duncan Keddie: "Mr. Selby Vose became manager in nineteen eleven").

2. **By 1911, the low readings had persisted for 14 years** and were well-known to the Association's officers (Orin Falke: "The first complaint of record was made in 1902. It was renewed, in one form or another, in every decade thereafter").

3. **Vose signed the ledger sheets carrying the station gain line every year from 1911 onward** (Bertram Kyle: "Mr. Selby Vose sat behind that door for a quarter of a century... He took a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign").

4. **In 1919, Vose issued the water-warning circular** (Orra Keddie: "A circular came to the station for the patrons, over the manager's name, about water in the milk"). This proves he had a theory about the cause: added water by patrons.

5. **Before 1919, no such accusation appears in the sources**, suggesting Vose developed this theory after taking office (or it was latent and crystallized under his management).

**Conclusion:** Vose knew tests were low (he signed the ledger records every year) but likely attributed it to patron dishonesty (water-adding) rather than equipment failure. He had no access to the 1911 measurement in Ansel's book (it was just a question mark, not explained to anyone), and no one ever told him the glass might be faulty. He acted on the institution's own suspicion, which was plausible given the era: water-adding was a real problem at some stations.

# Section E: Contradictions

## E1. Direct factual contradictions

**Contradiction 1: Ivy Keddie's generational relationship**

- **Claimants:** Orin Falke (page 2 of his report) claimed Ivy was Ansel's "great-niece"; Ashlin Chronicle initially reported she was "great-niece."
- **Resolution:** Duncan Keddie's "Notes for the family" (section I) and later correction by Orin Falke establish Ivy is Ansel's granddaughter (Duncan is Ansel's son, Ivy is Duncan's daughter).
- **Method used:** Contemporary family document (Duncan's notes written by Ansel's son) outranks memory and local report. Orin Falke himself withdrew the error: "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece."
- **Breaking the tie:** A direct genealogical statement by a family member (Duncan) who was alive and knew the fact beats a newspaper report written forty years later.

---

**Contradiction 2: Date of glassware condemnation**

- **Claims:** (a) Orin Falke, section 11: "On the 12th of June of that year [1923] the state dairy inspector... ran the station's milk pipettes against a calibrated burette and found that they would not deliver their mark."
- **Other sources:** Bertram Kyle says "In twenty-two the state man came into the office" (referring to Strawn).
- **Resolution:** June 12, 1923 is the explicit date from Orin Falke's report (which cites the bulletin). Kyle's "twenty-two" appears to be a recollection error (he was 83 when writing, looking back 30 years). The documentary evidence from Falke and the bulletin is more reliable.
- **Method:** Contemporary documentary record (bulletin, county report citing the bulletin) outranks memory.

---

**Contradiction 3: The size of the 1925 award**

- **Claimants:** Cleve Oram says "Thirty-five hundred dollars," Orin Falke says "three thousand eight hundred dollars."
- **Resolution:** Orin Falke's figure of $3,800 is correct. Cleve Oram rounds to "$35 hundred" which he states as "thirty-five hundred." These refer to the same award; Oram is rounding and speaking colloquially. Falke's more precise figure ($3,800) is corroborated by Bertram Kyle ("thirty-eight hundred dollars to the Ostrey Hollow patrons").
- **Method:** When two narrators carry the same value ($3,800), they either got it from the same source or independently verify it. Oram's "$35 hundred" is a rounding of $3,800.

---

**Contradiction 4: Number of families / farms**

- **Claims:** Hazel Brant: "Six. Hessel Bottom was six farms and no more." But Bertram Kyle refers to "nine families" receiving the water-warning circular in 1919, and the circular was sent to patrons, not all of whom are on one farm.
- **Resolution:** No contradiction. The station served six farms on Hessel Bottom (Hazel's statement), but a farm could hold more than one family. Orra Keddie: "a farm can hold more than one family and generally did." The nine families that received the 1919 circular likely included multiple families on some farms, or included patrons beyond just Hessel Bottom.
- **Method:** Grammatical reading of the terms "farms" vs. "families" clarifies the apparent contradiction.

---

**Contradiction 5: The butterfat never credited—two different figures**

- **Claims:** (a) Hazel Brant and Cleve Oram both independently state: "Sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat... the Hollow men were never paid for."
- **Also:** The Ashlin Chronicle reports "nineteen thousand six hundred dollars" not paid.
- **Resolution:** These are not contradictory; they measure different things. 67,000 pounds of butterfat never credited. At $0.29–$0.30 per pound (implied rate), 67,000 × $0.29.5 ≈ $19,700, consistent with the stated $19,600. No contradiction; independent corroboration by two valley residents (Hazel and Cleve) supports the 67,000-pound figure.
- **Method:** Cross-dimensional reasoning (pounds vs. dollars) shows consistency, not contradiction.

---

**Contradiction 6: Self-contradiction by Orin Falke (addendum vs. main report)**

- **Claim:** In section 9 of his main report, Falke concedes that the bog hay explanation "must be allowed as a real if partial factor" and cites an 1908 committee and a 1926 veterinarian.
- **Same person, later retraction:** In the addendum, after receiving Bulletin No. 214, Falke withdraws this: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July. I withdraw the concession made on page four."
- **Resolution:** Falke changes his view when presented with the college's 1911 feeding trial data. The bog hay explanation would account for only 0.05 points in winter, far short of the observed 0.19 points year-round. This is not a factual contradiction but a change in reasoned conclusion when new evidence arrives.
- **Method:** Logical reasoning from experimental evidence (feeding trial); the later conclusion is stronger.

# Section F: Theory

## F1. Causal theory of the shortfall

**The Single Coherent Cause:**

A batch of milk test pipettes, purchased from Ammon & Sons in April 1897 for $25.20 (six dozen at $0.35 each), was blown to mark at 17.6 cubic centimeters but systematically delivered only an average of 16.27 c.c.—a manufacturing defect that went undetected for 26 years. This 1.33 c.c. shortfall (7.56% of marked capacity) in the sample volume meant that every milk test was proportionally short: if true butterfat content was 3.80%, a short pipette would yield a reading of 3.61%, a consistent 0.19-point underestimate. This shortfall had nothing to do with the milk itself (herds, feed, water-adding, theft, or tester dishonesty), nor did it change when equipment, seasons, or testers changed. It was purely mechanical: the glass did not deliver what it was marked to deliver.

**Size of the error: 7.56% short per pipette; 0.19 percentage-point shortfall in recorded fat content.**

Why that size? Because the glass was blown at this specific tolerance, either by accident (a mold miscalibrated, a gauge knocked off its nail, a boy checking the first, tenth, and fiftieth pipette all consistently wrong) or by a shoddy production run from which Ammon & Sons took no accountability. Junia Ammon's own speculation, writing sixty years later: "The mold had been reground and the lot ran short. It was an accident and nobody in the works caught it. A gauge sits on a nail and gets knocked off the nail and hung back on it; a mold comes back from the shop a hair different and looks exactly as it looked before."

**When it began: May 1, 1897**, the first day the purchased glassware went into use at the station. It did not appear to begin, because one day's test is invisible in the noise; but by 1902 (five years in), the pattern was undeniable.

**Why it ended: June 12, 1923**, when state dairy inspector Merle Strawn ran the station's pipettes against a calibrated burette during routine inspection, found them short, and condemned the entire lot. New glass was ordered from Ammon & Sons and arrived within a month. From July 1923 onward, the station's test average rose to 3.85 (normal for the county). Nothing at the station or on the farms had to change; only the measuring glass was replaced, and everything corrected itself.

**Why nothing had to change:** The defect was in the equipment, not in the milk, the cows, the tester, or the patrons. Ansel Keddie was an honest, careful man who tested according to method. The patrons hauled milk daily. The seasons ran as they always had. No dishonesty, no deliberate sabotage, no hidden cause—just a piece of glass that would not deliver its marked amount, used faithfully for 26 years by a man who suspected it but could not prove it.

---

## The three competing explanations and evidence that defeats each:

**1. Water-adding (by patrons)**
- **Advanced by:** Selby Vose, manager (1919 circular); officers of the Association at the 1925 hearing.
- **Evidence that defeats it:** 
  - State dairy inspector Merle Strawn tested with a lactometer at every visit from 1908 forward (17 years of annual inspections, per Orin Falke) and never found added water.
  - The borrowed measure interval (May–July 1911): if water were being added, the amount should be independent of which glass was used; but tests rose markedly with the borrowed glass, proving water-adding is not the cause.
  - Orra Keddie: patrons received the water-warning letter "and read it in silence," a response that Orin Falke interprets as "a shout" of denial.

**2. Cream theft (by Ansel Keddie)**
- **Advanced by:** Association officers at the 1925 hearing.
- **Evidence that defeats it:**
  - The 1911 interval again: if Ansel were stealing a constant amount daily, the amount should not change when he used a borrowed measure. But the tests rose, proving theft was not the explanation.
  - After June 1923, when new glass was installed, tests rose to normal. If Ansel were stealing, the cream delivered should continue to fall short; but it rose in parallel with the new tests, proving the shortfall was not theft but measurement.
  - In June 1925, Ansel was dismissed. He lived 9 more years (until 1934) and "kept four or five cows on a piece of ground and did carpentry." No one ever accused him of cream theft after 1925; the accusation died with the arbitration, suggesting even those who made it did not truly believe it.

**3. Poor hay and pasture (Hessel Bottom bog meadows)**
- **Advanced by:** 1908 committee; veterinarian at Ashlin (1926).
- **Evidence that defeats it:**
  - A college feeding trial (Bulletin No. 214, State Agricultural College) conducted in 1911 showed bog hay depressed butterfat by at most 0.05 points in winter, nothing in summer.
  - Hazel Brant noted the shortfall was "nineteen hundredths flat as a floor. Every month, every season, wet years and dry: nineteen hundredths lower... not a little in the winter and none in the summer." The constancy across seasons refutes a feed-based explanation.
  - Orin Falke himself withdrew his initial concession: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July. I withdraw the concession made on page four."

## Two subsidiary questions:

**Q: Did the 1925 board of arbitration ever examine the condemned glassware?**

No. Bertram Kyle carried four books to the arbitration: Ansel's test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book, and Merle Strawn's state inspector's notebook for 1923. But "I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked." 

The condemned glassware was in the loft above the weigh room where the arbitration took place (Cleve Oram: "Twenty feet, if that" above their heads). Cleve notes: "Two of the three sessions of that arbitration sat in the room underneath it." No one climbed the ladder. The evidence was literally above their heads, unseen for 35 years until Ivy Keddie climbed it in February 1958.

**Q: What did the tester himself believe the cause was?**

Ansel Keddie never publicly stated his belief. Duncan Keddie, his son, wrote: "He came home that summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book." He placed only a question mark in his test book, not an explanation. For fourteen more years, he recorded every figure the glass gave him. 

Cleve Oram believed differently: "My father hauled that cream and he always said there was less of it coming off that platform than went onto it" — implying cream loss or theft. But Ansel himself never publicly accused the patrons of water-adding, never accused himself of theft, and never published his suspicion about the glass. "He was not a man who spoke," according to multiple sources. He carried the water-warning circular himself (proving he did not refuse to acknowledge the accusation) but lived with the knowledge, spoken only once to his son, that the problem was "something in the glass."

# Section G: Summary (max 150 words)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow in 1896 and equipped it with pipettes from Ammon & Sons in 1897. Unknown to anyone, these pipettes delivered only 92.4% of their marked 17.6 c.c. capacity. For 26 years, every milk test was systematically short by 0.19 percentage points. Farmers were underpaid $19,600 (67,000 pounds of butterfat). Ansel Keddie, the tester, suspected the glass (especially after a 1911 borrowed-measure interval proved it), but without proof, he stayed silent. In 1919, the manager issued a false water-warning circular. In 1925, Ansel was dismissed after an arbitration that awarded patrons only $3,800. In June 1923, the glass had been condemned but left undisturbed in a loft. In February 1958, Ansel's granddaughter Ivy Keddie climbed the ladder, found the crate, measured the pipettes, and proved the glass was faulty—clearing her grandfather's name 24 years after his death.
