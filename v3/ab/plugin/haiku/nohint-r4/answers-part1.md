# Section A: Eight Stories

## Story 1: The Founding of Ostrey Hollow Station (1896–1897)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, organized by valley farmers in 1889, decided to build a skimming station seven miles up the valley at Ostrey Hollow to serve farms in that area, chiefly Hessel Bottom. In winter 1895–1896, they constructed the station; it opened May 4, 1896. The station was designed to weigh milk, test it for butterfat, separate it, send cream down to the main creamery at Larrow Green, and return skim to patrons. Ansel Keddie, hired as tester and separator man from the opening, was responsible for all milk tests.

The critical act occurred in spring 1897 when the Association ordered its own testing outfit from Ammon & Sons, a glassblowing firm in Tarnet. The invoice, dated April 14, 1897, totaled $77.93 and included six dozen milk test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. at $0.35 each ($25.20), twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, and a hand tester. The equipment arrived and went into use May 1, 1897 (according to Orra Keddie's diary). The station began regular operations with borrowed glass initially, then switched to this purchased outfit. Junia Ammon, writing sixty years later for her firm's history, recalled the invoice and noted that it represented the firm's entry into the Ordell County market after years of being unable to penetrate beyond the ridge due to freight rates.

None of the founders knew that the pipettes in this crate would, unknowingly and unknowingly to anyone else, shape the station's entire future for twenty-six years. The equipment was ordinary, the prices reasonable, the arrangement standard for cooperative creameries of the time. Six dozen pipettes, if properly blown to deliver exactly 17.6 cubic centimeters, would have served the Ostrey Hollow patrons accurately. This one did not. What Ammon & Sons shipped and what the Association received was a batch of glass that would consistently deliver approximately 1.5 c.c. less than its marking—a difference invisible to any unaided observer and discoverable only by direct calibration against a burette, an instrument no one at the station possessed.

**Documents:** Junia Ammon's "Sixty Years of Ammon & Sons" (invoice and letter book); Orra Keddie's diary (May 1, 1897); Duncan Keddie's "Notes for the family" (section V on books); Orin Falke's county report (sections 3–4).

## Story 2: The Years of Complaint (1897–1911)

From the very beginning, the milk tested at Ostrey Hollow came out lower in butterfat than milk tested anywhere else in the county. The discrepancy was tiny but persistent: approximately 3.61 test average versus 3.78 at the main creamery and neighboring stations—a difference of 0.19 points that never varied by season or year.

By March 1902, talk at the store reached Ansel, who came home and would not eat. He told Orra: "It is my figures they will look at." The patrons, increasingly aware they were being underpaid relative to their peers, began complaining at every annual meeting. The officers of the Association, in turn, grew suspicious of the Ostrey Hollow farmers, suspecting deliberate adulteration.

In January 1908, a committee arrived—four men in Grigg's sleigh—to inspect the herds. Orra's diary records they looked at barns, not the station itself, a fact Ansel noted. Their report blamed the wet hay of Hessel Bottom bottom for thinning the cream. This explanation circulated and was accepted by many, including the state veterinarian in 1926. However, Orin Falke later showed this theory could not account for the full nineteen-hundredths difference.

The persistent low readings—fourteen years of them—generated theories but no resolution. Patrons suffered silently or grumbled, officers suspected fraud, and Ansel Keddie kept precise records of measurements he knew did not match reality but could not explain. He made his calculations honestly, wrote down every figure, and kept his doubts to himself.

**Documents:** Orra Keddie's diary (March 1902); Hazel Brant's taped interview; Duncan Keddie's notes; Bertram Kyle's office account; Orin Falke's report (section 5); The Ashlin Chronicle.

## Story 3: The 1911 Experiment (May–July 1911)

On May 8, 1911, Ansel Keddie's own measuring glass broke. For nine weeks—until July 10—he was forced to use a borrowed measure from another station. Orra noted in her diary: "A. home cross about a broken measure." But something remarkable happened during those nine weeks: the tests rose markedly and noticeably higher.

When Ansel resumed using his own glass on July 10, the tests fell immediately and completely back to their former low level. Orin Falke's county report calls this "an experiment" and notes: "The same herds, the pasture, the season, the men and the machinery were the same throughout."

In his test book, Ansel recorded this observation and placed a question mark against it. Duncan Keddie, his son, later wrote: "He had, after all, nothing to hold in his hand but a suspicion about a piece of glass, and against it stood the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county." Ansel's caution was expensive: he kept meticulous records of figures generated by faulty glass for another fourteen years without ever daring to act on his suspicion.

This interval is decisive. It proves that the difference was not in the milk, the cows, the feed, or the tester's honesty—all held constant. The only variable was the glass. It proves the glass was systematically short. But it did not prove by how much, where the glass came from, or how to proceed. For Ansel, suspicion without proof was not enough.

**Documents:** Orra Keddie's diary (May 8, 1911); Duncan Keddie's notes (section IV); Orin Falke's county report (section 6); Bertram Kyle's account.

## Story 4: The 1919 Crisis and Family Rupture (June 1919)

In June 1919, Selby Vose, who had become manager of the Association in 1911, issued a circular warning to patrons about adding water to their milk. The warning, civil in form but unmistakable in implication, went to nine families. Ansel Keddie was given the letters to distribute. Instead of leaving them on the platform or handing them to another man, he took them around himself, standing at each door while the farmer read it.

He came home at eleven at night with his hat still on, an unusual gesture Orra noticed. Two days later, her brother Emmet—the largest patron at Ostrey Hollow and the wealthiest farmer on Hessel Bottom—came up to the station. He did not come inside the house. He stood in the yard and told Ansel: "You have hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and you know it better than any man living. Why did you carry the paper yourself if you do not believe it?"

Ansel's answer: "I carried it because I would not have another man carry it."

Emmet went home. The brothers-in-law did not speak again for ten years. Orra, witnessing this, wrote: "He has not been in this house since, and he is my brother." She did not blame Emmet and told Ansel so.

The circle had tightened. Ansel was caught between his knowledge of the station and his powerlessness to prove his suspicion. The water-warning letter put him in an impossible position: if he refused to distribute it, he looked complicit; if he distributed it himself, he looked guilty. He chose to distribute it himself—a statement of integrity that only Emmet understood as a statement, and Emmet's interpretation destroyed the family peace for a decade.

**Documents:** Orra Keddie's diary (June 1919, twice); Hazel Brant's interview; The Ashlin Chronicle (reference to the letter).
