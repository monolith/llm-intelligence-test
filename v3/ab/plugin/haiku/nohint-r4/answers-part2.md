# Section A: Eight Stories (continued)

## Story 5: The Glass Condemned and Hidden (June–July 1923)

On June 12, 1923 (or thereabouts), Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector on the Ordell circuit, arrived at Ostrey Hollow station as part of routine inspection. Strawn was a quiet, methodical man with a rubber-banded notebook, and he carried with him a calibrated burette. When he tested the station's milk pipettes against the burette, he found they would not deliver their marked capacity. He condemned the entire lot on the spot and directed the manager to order new glass. Bertram Kyle, the cooperative's clerk, entered the order that same week to Ammon & Sons at Tarnet. New glass came up within the month.

No one made the connection. Strawn's visit was routine, his condemnation ordinary. The condemned crate of glass—containing approximately six dozen pipettes, now reduced to about nine that remained unbroken—was carried up into the loft over the station's intake room and left there, covered with a tarpaulin and chaff, to gather dust.

From July 1923 onward, the station's test average rose to 3.85, normal for the county. But no one examined why. Strawn had condemned the glass; the manager had ordered new. The matter was closed. Cleve Oram, the cream hauler's son, who had seen that crate a hundred times and put his hand on it, reflected years later: "The crate is the whole thing, they tell me now. Well. It sat there."

The silence was complete. Kyle recorded the inspector's visit as one line in the ledger: an order received and filed, nothing more. The state dairy office held Strawn's notebooks but filed them away. The Association's records showed a new order but offered no explanation for the old one. The facts were written down—in Strawn's notebook, in Kyle's ledger, in the supply house's order books—but they lay in separate places, unread and uncompared. And the crate sat in darkness for thirty-five years.

This was not a conspiracy. It was worse: it was the ordinary practice of institutions and men who do not ask questions of each other until forced to do so.

**Documents:** Bertram Kyle's account; Junia Ammon's letter book (July 2, 1923); Cleve Oram's interview; Orin Falke's county report (section 11); The Ashlin Chronicle.

## Story 6: The Arbitration and the Dismissal (January–April 1925)

In January 1925, the Ostrey Hollow patrons, no longer willing to accept the discrepancy, petitioned for a hearing. A board of arbitration was appointed. The hearings took place over three sittings from March 17 to April 30, 1925. The first sitting was at Ashlin in the cooperative's office; the other two were held at the station itself, in the intake room (the weigh room), where the scale and machinery stood. Cleve Oram stood outside in the road and rain, hearing voices but not words. Orra noted: "I did not go down. I could hear the wagons from the yard all that afternoon and that was enough of it for me."

The board heard testimony about water-added (refuted by state inspector's lactometer tests, which never found added water), about cream stolen by the tester (seemingly supported by the suspicious low readings), and about poor feed from wet hay (offered by an 1908 committee). The board found no cause established. But it did award the patrons $3,800, "without admission"—a term Orin Falke later explained as lawyer's language for paying without saying why.

On April 30, 1925—four days before the award was handed down—Ansel Keddie was dismissed. Orra wrote: "They have put him out. Twenty-nine seasons and a letter. He came up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm and hung it behind the shed door and asked what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it."

The crate sat in the loft above the weigh room during at least two of the three sittings. Bertram Kyle later reflected: "Two of the three sessions of that arbitration sat in the room underneath it. Twenty feet, if that." No one climbed the ladder. No one examined the condemned glass. The evidence lay above their heads, invisible not because hidden but because no one thought to look.

Ansel was paid nothing beyond his final wage. He was imputed with dishonesty in his county for nine years until his death in 1934. The patrons received $3,800, later charged to the station account by Kyle's bookkeeping. The Association closed the matter. The board issued no clear finding, no apology, no vindication.

**Documents:** Orra Keddie's diary (January, March 17, April 30, 1925); Cleve Oram's interview; Bertram Kyle's account; Orin Falke's county report (section 10); Ashlin Chronicle.

## Story 7: The Ledger and the Ledger-Keeper (1897–1925)

Bertram Kyle worked at the cooperative's office in Larrow Green for twenty-five years, sitting at the outer desk with his back to the window and his face toward the manager's door. Everything that went in or came out passed within a foot of his elbow. He copied ledgers—station accounts that came from the creamery and stations, made up, copied, and carried forward. Once a year he transcribed them in fair hand for the annual meeting.

Every year, in the station account for Ostrey Hollow, three figures appeared and a sentence. First: the pounds of butterfat credited to patrons by station test. Second: the pounds of butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station cream. Third: the difference, entered as "station gain," 5.25 percent, "as usual, carried to the general account."

Kyle copied this line dozens of times. It never crossed his mind to wonder at it. It had a name: "station gain." "That is the thing about a name," he wrote. "A figure with a name on it has been explained, and you rule under it and go on to the next."

In 1919, he typed the water-warning circular. In 1923, he entered the order for new glass. In 1925, he carried four books to the arbitration: Ansel's test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book, and Merle Strawn's state inspector's notebook for 1923. He carried them in, carried them out, and could not have told anyone—could not tell himself—that any man in that room had ever laid the state inspector's notebook beside Ansel's test book and looked.

The evidence was in plain sight, on an open shelf, in a room anybody might walk into. It was read out at the annual meeting to fifty farmers with their hats on their knees. But it was not a secret. "It was worse than a secret," Kyle wrote, looking back at sixty-eight. "It was in plain sight with a name on it."

**Documents:** Bertram Kyle's "What I Saw of the Office" (entire).

## Story 8: The 1958 Discovery and Vindication (1958)

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie, a graduate student in dairy husbandry at the State Agricultural College at Farland, returned to Ostrey Hollow. She was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter, Duncan's daughter, and Hazel Brant's second cousin. She climbed a ladder into the loft above the station's intake room. Under a tarpaulin and a layer of chaff, she found a wooden crate. In it were glass pipettes and test bottles—the condemned equipment from 1923, left undisturbed for thirty-five years.

Eleven pipettes remained in condition to be measured. In March, at Farland, she measured them against a calibrated burette. All were short. The average delivery was 16.27 cubic centimeters instead of the marked 17.6 c.c.—a shortfall of approximately 1.5 c.c., or 7.6 percent. Hazel Brant, keeper of the weigh book and witness to the 1911 incident, recognized the significance immediately: "It had always been the glass."

In October 1958, the State Agricultural College published Bulletin No. 471, co-authored by Professor Leland Vaught and Ivy Keddie. The bulletin proved that a short charge of milk into a bottle whose neck is ruled for a full charge yields a reading short in the same proportion—every single time, for twenty-six years. It calculated that the Ostrey Hollow patrons were not paid for approximately 19,600 pounds of butterfat, worth roughly $19,600 at 1890s prices. Of this, they received $3,800 in the 1925 award—about 19 percent.

Ansel Keddie died in 1934 with the imputation on him, nine years after dismissal. He was cleared in 1958, twenty-four years after his death. Orra, his widow, died in 1948 and never saw his vindication. But Ansel's reputation was restored, his competence proved, his honor defended at last by his granddaughter climbing a ladder that nobody else had thought to climb.

The evidence had always been there. It took a girl from the college with curiosity, courage, and training to see that the answer lay not in the farmers' milk, not in the tester's honesty, not in feed or water or cream theft, but in a simple fact: a piece of glass out of a crate, blown wrong, used for twenty-six years, and never measured until 1923—and then never examined again until 1958.

**Documents:** Hazel Brant's interview; Duncan Keddie's notes (section V on books); Ivy Keddie's interview (Ashlin Chronicle); Bulletin No. 471 citations (entire); Ashlin Chronicle; Orin Falke's county report with addendum; Bertram Kyle's account; Orra Keddie's diary (with editor's note on 1934 and 1958).

# Section B: Relationships

## B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie

Ivy Keddie is Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Ansel's son Duncan (born 1894) was a schoolteacher who fathered Ivy (born 1931 per Duncan's notes). Duncan explicitly identifies himself as Ansel's son and Ivy as his own daughter. Therefore Ivy is Ansel's granddaughter, not great-niece as initially claimed in the county agent's report. Orin Falke later corrected this error: "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine."

---

## B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant

Ivy and Hazel are second cousins. Here is the family line: Emmet Brant and Orra (Brant) Keddie were siblings. Emmet's son Roy Brant farmed Hessel Bottom and fathered Hazel. Orra married Ansel Keddie and had son Duncan, who fathered Ivy. Therefore: Roy and Duncan are first cousins (children of siblings). Their daughters—Hazel and Ivy—are second cousins (children of first cousins). Duncan himself emphasizes this explicitly: "Roy's daughter is Hazel Brant. My daughter is Ivy. Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece." The sources leave open no further generational ambiguity on this line.

---

## B3. Selby Vose's father, and why it matters

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose, who organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association and was its first president. Duncan Keddie emphasizes this with care: "Mr. Selby Vose became manager in nineteen eleven. He was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president — the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense."

This matters because it establishes Selby Vose as an officer of the institution from its founding forward, through his father's presidency and into his own management role. It proves there was no connection whatever between Vose and Keddie families (contrary to persistent rumor), and it shows that Selby Vose was an established officer carrying institutional authority when he issued the water-warning circular in 1919. He signed it himself and bore responsibility for its accusation.

---

## B4. Relationship between Vose and Keddie families

There was no family relationship between the Vose family and the Keddie family. Duncan Keddie states: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses." He heard rumors that his mother (Orra) and Gideon Vose's wife were cousins, even first cousins, but Orra, who spoke to him at length about family connections, never mentioned any Vose relation. He heard the rumors twice from townspeople in recent years but dismisses them as false. The absence of connection is significant because Selby Vose, as manager, issued the water-warning circular against Ostrey Hollow farmers without any family loyalty or kinship to the tester (Ansel), and Ansel Keddie, as tester, worked under Vose's management without any family protection.
