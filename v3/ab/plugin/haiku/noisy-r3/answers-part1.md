# Test Answers — Part 1
## Section A: Reconstruction (Stories 1–4)

### Story 1: Origins — The Founding and Setup (1889–1898)

In 1889, farmers of the Larrow Valley met at Larrow Green and organized themselves into the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (r22, r05). Gideon Vose served as president and founder, writing Article VII, which became the foundational policy: patrons would be paid not by the weight of milk but by the butterfat content as measured by test at the delivery station, and the test made at the station where the patron delivered governed that payment with no appeal (r01, r05, r03, r22). This policy was critical to everything that followed.

The Association built a main creamery at Larrow Green in 1891 (r22). Seven miles up the valley at Ostrey Hollow, a small hamlet, the Association decided to build a skimming station to serve the six farms of Hessel Bottom, which was wet ground known for coarse hay (r17, r01). The station opened on May 4, 1896 (r22). For its first year, the station borrowed testing glassware from Hobart Sill's bench at Larrow Green (r14, r03) because its own outfit had not yet arrived.

In spring 1897, the Association purchased a complete testing outfit from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet for $77.93 (r20). The invoice, dated April 14, 1897, listed six dozen milk test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c., twelve dozen test bottles, one acid measure, and one hand tester (r20, r08). On May 1, 1898, this glassware went into use at the Ostrey Hollow station (r24, r22).

Ansel Keddie, who was tester and separator man at the station from its opening (r18, r22), became responsible for running the tests that would determine every Hessel Bottom farmer's income. He was meticulous by temperament and kept records in his own hand. His wife, Orra Brant, was the sister of Emmet Brant, the largest milk producer on Hessel Bottom—a fact that would sit at the center of the family for a generation (r18, r17).

**Sources:** r18, r20, r22, r03, r01, r14, r05, r17.

### Story 2: The Faulty Glass Installed (May 1, 1898)

On May 1, 1898, the new testing glassware purchased from Ammon & Sons in Tarnet went into service at the Ostrey Hollow station. The six dozen pipettes were marked on their stems "17.6 c.c."—the standard charge of milk for a butterfat test. However, when nine surviving pipettes from this crate were measured in 1958 by Ivy Keddie against a calibrated burette at the State Agricultural College, they delivered only 16.72 c.c. on average (r04, r19, r22). The mean shortfall was 0.88 c.c., representing exactly 5% error: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 (r04, r11).

This systematic shortfall went undetected. A short measure of milk placed into a test bottle ruled for the full measure produces a fat reading proportionally low: 5% less milk into the graduated glass yields 5% lower fat reading, every single time, for as long as the faulty glass remained in use (r19). The pipettes had been manufactured with a defect—most likely the mold had been reground without the works catching the error, according to Junia Ammon, the firm's own historian writing in 1949 (r20).

The glass arrived with a tarpaulin in a wooden crate stenciled "AMMON & SONS" (r07, r17, r21). It was placed in the loft above the intake room at the Ostrey Hollow station, where it would eventually sit for 25 years until 1923, at which point it was condemned, and then sat there another 35 years until 1958 (r07, r21).

From May 1, 1898, until June 12, 1923—a span of 25 years and one month—this faulty glassware was the only measuring instrument the station used (r06, r11). Every single test recorded at Ostrey Hollow during that period was 5% systematically low, though the tester and patrons did not know it.

**Sources:** r19, r04, r11, r20, r07, r21, r06.

### Story 3: The Persistent Discrepancy (1902–1914)

The milk of Ostrey Hollow tested lower than the milk of anywhere else in the county from nearly the day the faulty glass went into use (r19). By 1902, this pattern had become impossible to ignore. The first formal complaint of record was made in 1902 (r22, r09, r01). The station's average over the long period of complaint stood at 3.61% butterfat, while the Larrow Green creamery's own average over the same years was 3.78%—a consistent difference of 0.19 points or nineteen hundredths (r22, r05).

This discrepancy was absolutely uniform. It ran at 0.19 points in every month of the year: in the flush of June and in the depth of February alike, in wet years and dry years (r17, r22, r09). Hazel Brant, keeper of the weigh book, stated flatly: "Nineteen hundredths. Every month, every season, wet years and dry: nineteen hundredths lower at our station than the same milk was anywhere else on earth. Not a little in the winter and none in the summer, the way a feed thing goes. Nineteen hundredths, flat as a floor" (r17).

The complaint was renewed "in one form or another, in every decade thereafter" (r22). Various theories emerged to explain it. The Association's manager suggested patrons were adding water to their milk. Farmers blamed the land. Some whispered that the tester himself was taking cream. The only official investigation was the 1908 committee under Nyle Grigg, which sampled Hessel Bottom herds monthly and reported that the trouble lay with the wet hay of the bottom—"the remedy lies with the land and not with the station" (r15, r22, r01). The committee found no dishonesty, scales honest, milk whole (r15).

Four Hessel Bottom farmers, during summer flush seasons, hauled their milk over Redlow Ridge to the Alder Corners Cheese Company in Vessey County (1914–1930), where Jerome Cudd's cheesemaker daughter, Rosalie Cudd, tested it on her own bench with her own glassware. Over sixteen summers, these same four men's milk tested 3.78–3.82 at Alder Corners—normal levels—while the same milk would have tested low at Ostrey Hollow (r10, r08). This proved the milk was good and whole, not watered.

Despite mounting evidence of discrepancy, no action was taken. The official explanation—wet hay—was accepted as sufficient, even though it explained only part of the observed loss. The patrons' legitimate complaints went unresolved.

**Sources:** r22, r17, r09, r01, r08, r15, r10, r19.

### Story 4: The 1911 Anomaly — Proof in a Broken Measure (May–July 1911)

On May 8, 1911, a critical accident occurred that would later prove the source of the discrepancy, though no one understood it at the time. The station's own measuring glass broke, and for nine weeks—from June through July, the flush of the season—a borrowed measure was used in its place (r06, r11, r09, r22, r24). On July 10, the station resumed its own glass (r22, r11). The tester, Ansel Keddie, noted the fact in his book and marked it with a question mark in his own hand (r04, r11, r13, r18).

During those nine weeks with the borrowed measure, the station's tests rose from 3.61 to 3.80—back to normal levels (r04, r11, r09). When the station's own measure was returned, the tests fell back to 3.61 (r04, r11, r09). Everything else was identical: the same herds, the same pasture, the same season, the same men, the same machinery, the same tester, the same procedure, the same supervision (r11, r22, r13).

This interval constituted a perfect natural experiment. One variable changed only: the measuring glass. All other factors—land, cows, men, season, acid, procedure—remained constant. The result was unmistakable: the glass, not the milk, was the problem. Duncan Keddie, Ansel's son, recalled: "He came home that summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book... He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove" (r18).

The 1911 anomaly was the smoking gun. Yet it remained unrecognized for 47 years. Nyle Grigg and Dr. Roland Kepp, attempting to dismiss it, attributed it to "summer figures" and seasonal variation—explanations that made no sense because all other variables had remained constant (r15, r16). Professor Leland Vaught later emphasized that "the anomaly is the smoking gun proving the glass, not land or patrons" (r11).

Had anyone paid attention to Ansel's question mark in 1911, the entire scandal could have been solved in an afternoon. Instead, it would fester for seventeen more years.

**Sources:** r11, r06, r04, r09, r22, r13, r18, r15, r16.
