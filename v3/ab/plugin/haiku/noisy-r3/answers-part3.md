## Section C: Math

### C1. The Defect in the Glassware

**(a) Proportion and derivation:**
The proportion is 0.95, or a 5% systematic low error.

**Derivation:** The nine surviving pipettes from the condemned crate were marked 17.6 c.c. but delivered only 16.72 c.c. on average when measured against a calibrated burette in March 1958 (r04, r22). Ratio: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9545... = 0.95 (r04, r11). Therefore every test was 5% low. This shortfall came directly from the glassware (r19). The proportional error in the glass is the root cause of all that followed.

**(b) True average and shortfall:**
- Recorded average: 3.61%
- True average: 3.61% ÷ 0.95 = 3.80%
- Shortfall in recorded tests: 0.19 points (3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19)

**Working:** If the glass delivers only 0.95 of the true amount, the recorded figure is 95% of the true figure. To recover the true figure, divide recorded by 0.95. The shortfall of 0.19 points matches exactly the observed consistent discrepancy between Ostrey Hollow (3.61) and Larrow Green (3.78–3.80) across all 25 years (r22, r17, r11).

**(c) Total butterfat underpaid:**
40,000,000 pounds of milk × (0.19% shortfall) = 76,000 pounds of butterfat never credited.

**Working:** Over 25 years (1898–1923), Ostrey Hollow received approximately 40 million pounds of milk (r23, r22, r11). At 0.19% loss (3.61% recorded vs. 3.80% true), the underpaid butterfat was 40,000,000 × 0.0019 = 76,000 lb. (Some sources cite 118,000 lb, which reflects different measurement bases; Bulletin 471 states "the difference" as 76,000 lb, the most authoritative figure, r11.)

**(d) Money worth and fraction received:**
- Total owed at 25¢/lb: 76,000 × $0.25 = $19,000
- Actually awarded: $3,800 (1925 arbitration, r13, r22, r04, r19)
- Fraction received: $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20 = 1/5

The patrons received one-fifth of what they were owed.

**Sources:** r04, r11, r19, r22, r23, r13, r09.

### C2. The Largest Patron's Year (1910)

The largest patron was Emmet Brant. His son Roy Brant kept a weigh book recording annual deliveries from 1898 onward. For 1910, Roy recorded the exact figure: "Two hundred thousand pounds even, delivered to the station" (r09, r17).

**(a) Butterfat credited in 1910:**
- Recorded test: 3.42% (r17: "paid on a test of three forty-two")
- 200,000 lb × 0.0342 = **6,840 pounds butterfat credited**

**(b) Butterfat actually owed and not credited:**
- True test: 3.42% ÷ 0.95 = 3.60%
- 200,000 lb × 0.0360 = 7,200 pounds butterfat true
- Owed but not credited: 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 pounds butterfat**

**(c) Money value that year:**
- 360 lb × $0.25/lb = **$90 underpaid in 1910 alone**

**Working:** Emmet Brant was not just any patron; he was the largest milk producer and Orra Keddie's (Ansel's wife's) brother. The 1910 figure was exact: 200,000 pounds "even"—a round number that stayed in Roy's memory precisely because both his scale and the station's scale agreed (r17, r09). This year was representative of the entire 25-year period.

**Sources:** r09, r17.

### C3. Station Gain: The Accounting Record

**(a) Pounds of butterfat credited to patrons over 25 years:**
Approximately 760,000 pounds (working backward from station gain).

**(b) Pounds actually received at Larrow Green in station cream:**
Approximately 800,000 pounds.

**Working:** Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper, created a "station gain" line every year: the difference between butterfat the station recorded (from tests) and butterfat the receiving books showed (what actually arrived at Larrow Green in cream). Teague calculated this as 5.25% of received fat annually (r05). Bertram Kyle, the office clerk, copied this line annually for 25 years: "So many pounds of butterfat credited to the patrons on the station test. So many pounds of butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station cream. And then the difference of the two, entered as station gain, so many pounds — and after it, always, in the bookkeeper's own words, five and a quarter per cent, as usual" (r23). The phrase "as usual" meant it was routine and consistent, never questioned. If 76,000 lb were systematically undercredited (part C1), and this represented the shortfall, then the received fat (840,000) minus undercredited (76,000) yields the credited figure (764,000 ≈ 760,000).

**(c) Express the difference as a percentage:**
Difference = 40,000 lb (840,000 − 800,000) as a percentage of **recorded butterfat credited** = 40,000 ÷ 760,000 = 0.0526 = **5.26%** (essentially 5.25% "as usual").

**Clarity:** This is 5.25% of the butterfat *credited* to patrons (not received). In other words, for every 100 pounds the station recorded, about 5.25 pounds extra arrived at Larrow Green that the station had not recorded. This invisible gain went to the Association's account and was documented in plain sight in the ledgers that were read annually at the Association meeting. The station-gain line had a name, and according to Bertram Kyle, "a figure with a name on it has been explained" (r23).

**Sources:** r05, r23, r13, r12.

### C4. The Invoice for Glassware

**(a) Total invoice amount:**
**$77.93** (charged April 14, 1897, by Ammon & Sons to Larrow Valley Coop, r20).

**Itemization:**
- 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at $0.35 = $25.20

- 1 acid measure = $0.90
- 1 hand tester, 4 bottle = $18.00
- Freight = $2.15
- **Total = $77.93**

**(b) Number of pipettes and shortfall per unit:**
- **Number of pipettes: 72** (6 doz. = 6 × 12 = 72)
- **Shortfall per unit: 0.88 c.c.**

**Working:** Each pipette was marked 17.6 c.c. but delivered 16.72 c.c. Shortfall = 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c. per pipette (r04, r11, r22). This meant $1.08 invested in each pipette ($77.93 ÷ 72) was paying for a piece of equipment that would systematically mismeasure every single test for 25 years.

**Sources:** r20, r04, r11, r22.

### C5. Duration and Delivery Years

**(a) Duration of faulty glass in use and number of full delivery years:**
- **Duration in use: 25 years, 1 month** (May 1, 1898 to June 12, 1923, r06, r11, r22)
- **Full delivery years affected: 25 years** (May 1898 through May 1923, r22)

**Why they differ:** The glass went into use May 1, 1898, but the financial calculations typically run from the first calendar year of operation through the last full season, not counting partial years. The station's records ran seasonally and were settled annually. The first season before the station's own glass arrived (1896–1897) the station used borrowed equipment. The 25-year count runs May 1898 to May 1923, covering 25 full seasons of operation under the faulty glass. The duration (25 years, 1 month) is the actual time the glass was in use; the full-delivery-years count (25) is the number of complete seasonal cycles for financial purposes.

**(b) First season milk delivery:**
**Approximately 1,100,000 pounds.**

**Working:** Bertram Kyle stated: "what the Hollow took in that first year... a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" (r23). Kyle had this from men who were there, though the figure was not in any formal account book. This was the 1896–1897 season, before the station's own glass arrived and before the faulty 1898 glass went into use. Therefore it is not part of the underpaid calculation but establishes the typical volume Ostrey Hollow received in one season, roughly 1.1 million pounds. Over 25 full seasons, 1.1M × 25 ≈ 27.5M pounds (the 40M figure in C1 may include seasonal carryover and partial seasons, or may include longer-term average after the station's early years).

**Sources:** r23, r22, r06, r11.

## Section D: Logic

### D1. The 1911 Anomaly as an Experiment

**When and duration:** May 8, 1911 (date the station's measure broke, r24) through July 10, 1911 (when own measure was returned, r11). **Duration: 9 weeks** (or "a little over two months," approximately June–July, the flush season, r22, r09).

**Variable (what changed):** The measuring glass. The station's own faulty pipettes were replaced by a borrowed measure from another source (not specified which, but implied to be from Larrow Green like the original borrowed glass, r24).

**Held constant:**
- The same tester (Ansel Keddie)
- The same herds (Hessel Bottom patrons)
- The same pasture and hay (wet ground)
- The same season (flush of June–July, highest production period)
- The same milk itself (same cows, same farms)
- The same machinery and separator
- The same procedure and supervision (state inspector saw Ansel test 31 times weekly for 29 seasons)

**Result:** Tests rose from 3.61 to 3.80 (normal level) and fell back to 3.61 when the station's glass returned. Every measurement moved 5% upward with borrowed glass, 5% downward with own glass.

**What it establishes:** The 1911 anomaly proves definitively and beyond doubt that **the glass, not the milk or the land or the tester, was the cause of the discrepancy.** This is the strongest possible evidence because all confounding variables are controlled: land, season, cows, men, procedure—nothing changed but the instrument. This is the kind of proof that cannot be refuted by theory or argument. One variable changed; one result followed.

**What it does NOT establish:** The anomaly does not prove *how much* error was in the glass (though the later 1958 measurement showed 5%). The anomaly does not prove that the glass defect was the *only* factor (though subsequent evidence ruled out watering and cream theft and showed wet hay explained only 0.05%, insufficient to account for 0.19%). The anomaly did not resolve the earlier 1902–1910 complaints, because in 1911 no one yet understood what the experiment had proved.

**Sources:** r11, r06, r04, r09, r22, r13, r24, r18, r15, r16.

### D2. Three Independent Reasons Why Ansel Could Not Have Stolen Cream

**Reason 1 — The Station Gain Ledger (Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper, 1905–1945):** Every year, the station's own accounts showed butterfat that arrived at Larrow Green in excess of what the station recorded (station gain, 5.25% annually). If Ansel had stolen cream by writing low tests, the ledger would show a **deficit**, not a surplus. Instead, it showed a consistent gain, year after year, recorded in ink by an honest bookkeeper and read aloud at every annual meeting (r05, r12, r23). Teague, despite his insinuation at the 1925 hearing, never once questioned these entries in writing. This is independent evidence from a different documentary source (ledgers, not tests).

**Reason 2 — Hobart Sill's Testimony (buttermaker at Larrow Green, 41 years, remarks 1934):** Sill weighed Ostrey Hollow cream himself most years and wrote in his receiving book. He testified that Hessel Bottom cream "always ran heavy"—more fat than the station's papers said (r14). If Ansel had removed cream, the cream arriving at Larrow Green would be lighter (less fat content), not heavier. Instead, Sill found the cream consistently heavier than the station's test figures suggested. This is independent eyewitness testimony from a person with 41 years' experience and his own written records. This reason still holds even if the glass had never been measured in 1958, because it depends on Sill's actual observations of actual cream over 41 years, not on the glass measurement.

**Reason 3 — Rosalie Cudd's Test Records (cheesemaker, Alder Corners, 1915–1930):** Four Hessel Bottom farmers hauled their milk summers over the ridge to Rosalie Cudd's cheese factory, where she tested it on her own bench with her own glassware. Over 16 summers (1915–1930), these four men's milk consistently tested 3.78–3.82 (normal levels) at her factory—proving the milk itself was good (r10). If Ansel had stolen cream, the cream would have been depleted when the milk arrived at Larrow Green, and Rosalie Cudd would have noticed the milk was watered or depleted by comparison. Instead, she found it consistently normal. This is independent from the Sill evidence (different location, different time period, different observer, different method) and would still prove Ansel innocent even without the 1958 glass measurement, because it directly demonstrates the milk quality at an independent third party.

**Why these are independent:** 
- Reason 1 (ledgers) depends on bookkeeping and institutional records
- Reason 2 (Sill) depends on direct observation and personal records over 41 years
- Reason 3 (Cudd) depends on independent testing at a third location over 16 seasons

No two of these reasons rely on the same source, method, or evidence base. If any one were wrong, the other two would still stand.

**Did the bookkeeper question the ledger entries?** Wilbur Teague, despite making the insinuation about cream theft at the 1925 hearing, never once questioned or challenged the station-gain line entries in his own books or in writing. According to Bertram Kyle, "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years" (r23). His insinuation at the hearing was made without ever confronting the entries that refuted it. This is a critical fact: Teague knew about the station gain (5.25% annually) because he recorded it, yet he insinuated cream theft without ever questioning his own ledgers in front of anyone.

**Sources:** r05, r12, r23, r14, r10, r13.

### D3. Did Selby Vose Know the Tests Ran Low?

**Answer:** Yes, Selby Vose knew or should have known that the station's tests ran systematically low. The evidence is strong, though not absolutely conclusive about his subjective state of mind.

**Justification:**

1. **The Station-Gain Line (ledgers):** Selby Vose became manager in 1911 (r23, r24). From that point forward, every year, the ledgers carried the "station gain" line: butterfat received at Larrow Green exceeded butterfat credited to patrons by 5.25% annually (r05, r23, r12). Bertram Kyle stated: "Mr. Selby Vose sat behind that door for a quarter of a century and I sat outside it... He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign. I watched him do it a thousand times" (r23). This means Vose signed the annual statements containing the station-gain line every year for 14 years (1911–1925). He did not read the lines above the totals, only looked at the bottom figure (r23). If he was conscious of the station-gain line, he knew the station's figures did not match the received cream.

2. **The 1911 Anomaly:** In summer 1911, Ansel Keddie marked a question mark in the test book when the borrowed measure produced higher figures (r18, r24, r04). Ansel was the tester; Vose was the manager. It is plausible that Vose saw the question mark. The 1911 anomaly was contemporaneous and visible in the station's own records. Whether Vose understood what it meant is unclear, but the anomaly was in his station's books.

3. **The 1919 Warning:** Vose sent the 1919 water-warning circular to patrons, accusing them of adding water (r23). This accusation came after 17 years of managing a station where the tests were systematically low. Either Vose believed the tests were honestly recording the milk's actual quality (in which case he would accuse patrons), or he had reason to believe they were not (in which case his accusation was knowing dishonesty). Alonzo Frick noted that Vose had no proof: "The first [explanation] was that the Ostrey Hollow patrons added water to their milk. This was the view of the Association's manager, who circulated a warning to that effect in 1919. It cannot be sustained" (r22). Frick found no evidence of watering (r22). If Vose knew the tests were faulty, the 1919 circular was a false accusation. If he did not know, he was negligent in not investigating for 14 years.

4. **The 1923 Condemnation:** On June 12, 1923, Merle Strawn condemned the glass in Vose's presence (or at least in communication with Vose's office). Strawn's circuit notebook records the condemnation (r06, r07). Bertram Kyle records that Strawn "came in to say so and to tell the manager to order new" (r23). This means Vose was explicitly told that the measuring glass "would not deliver what it was marked for." At that moment, Vose would have understood that all 25 years of low tests were not due to watering or cream theft or wet hay—they were due to the glass. Yet Vose later claimed (in his 1926 address) that the 1924 rise in tests proved patrons had been watering and had stopped (r08). This claim is incompatible with knowledge of the June 1923 condemnation.

**Conclusion:** Vose knew or had strong reason to know by 1911 (the anomaly) and certainly knew by June 1923 (the condemnation) that the station's tests were systematically low due to the glass, not due to patrons' actions. Yet he allowed the 1919 water accusation to stand and later defended it as if the tests were honest. His state of mind is not explicitly recorded in the sources, but his actions are consistent with either (a) willful dishonesty, or (b) profound negligence in not investigating the station-gain line or the 1911 question mark.

**Sources:** r23, r18, r24, r04, r22, r06, r07, r08, r05, r12.

## Section F: Theory

### F1. Causal Theory and Three Competing Explanations

**The Actual Cause (Causal Theory):**
Between May 1, 1898, and June 12, 1923, the Ostrey Hollow skimming station used defective pipettes manufactured by Ammon & Sons. These pipettes delivered only 16.72 c.c. for every 17.6 c.c. marked on their stems—a systematic 5% shortfall. When milk is measured with pipettes that deliver too little, the fat reading is proportionally low (5% less milk gives 5% lower fat reading). Therefore, every butterfat test recorded at Ostrey Hollow was 5% too low. The recorded average was 3.61%; the true average was 3.80% (= 3.61% ÷ 0.95). Over 40 million pounds of milk delivered by 25 years, this meant 76,000 pounds of butterfat (worth approximately $19,000 at 25¢/lb) were systematically undercredited to farmers.

**Why the error began:** The defective glass arrived in 1898 from the manufacturer with an undetected flaw (likely a mold reground without quality control catching it, per Junia Ammon's 1949 account in r20). It was installed May 1, 1898, and went unnoticed because: (a) it was the first glass the station possessed that was its own (previous glass was borrowed and likely good), so there was no baseline for comparison; (b) the error is invisible—a short pipette looks normal and operates normally; (c) the station-gain line (5.25% annually) was documented but given an innocuous name and filed without investigation.

**Why it ended:** State dairy inspector Merle Strawn tested the pipettes against a calibrated burette on June 12, 1923. This was a standard procedure (comparing pipettes to a burette), but apparently had never been done on this crate before. Strawn found the glass did not deliver its mark and condemned it. New glass was ordered and installed in July 1923. From 1924 onward, tests rose to normal 3.80% levels.

**Why nothing had to change for the beginning and end:** The error was purely instrumental. The glass was faulty, but the milk was good, the cows were good, the men were honest, the land had its characteristics, and nothing changed at those levels between 1898 and 1923. What changed was only the measuring instrument. When the instrument was replaced, the readings immediately normalized—without any change to milk quality, cows, land, season, procedure, or personnel.

**The Three Competing Explanations and Evidence That Defeats Them:**

**Explanation 1: "The patrons were adding water to their milk."**
- **Advanced by:** Selby Vose (manager), 1919 circular and 1926 defense (r23, r08, r24)
- **Specific evidence that defeats it:**
  - State dairy inspector Merle Strawn tested with lactometer at every visit from 1908 forward and never found added water (r22)
  - Hobart Sill weighed Ostrey Hollow cream at Larrow Green for 41 years and found it consistently heavy (higher fat) than the station's papers said; if cream had been watered, it would be light (r14)
  - Rosalie Cudd tested four Hessel Bottom farmers' milk at Alder Corners Cheese Company for 16 summers and found it consistently 3.78–3.82 (normal quality), proving milk was not watered (r10)
  - Bertram Kyle examined receiving books at Larrow Green: cream arrived heavy, not light; if patrons were watering milk, the cream would be depleted (r23, r12)
  - Bulletin 471 states definitively: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow" (r11)

**Explanation 2: "The tester (Ansel Keddie) was stealing cream and writing low tests to conceal the loss."**
- **Advanced by:** Wilbur Teague (bookkeeper), implied during 1925 arbitration hearing (r05, r13)
- **Specific evidence that defeats it:**
  - The station-gain line (5.25% annually) shows butterfat arriving at Larrow Green in excess of what was recorded, not deficit; if cream had been stolen, there would be less, not more (r05, r12, r23)
  - Hobart Sill's testimony: cream ran heavy (more fat), not light; theft would produce light cream (r14)
  - The 1911 anomaly: same tester (Ansel), same procedure, no different supervision, but when a different glass was used (borrowed measure), tests rose to normal and cream balance was unaffected. Therefore the same man made tests that were either honest or dishonest; the variable that changed was the glass, not the man (r11, r04, r13)
  - Alonzo Frick (arbitration chairman) believed Ansel and found no cause established (r13)
  - Bulletin 471 states: "no cream was stolen there" (r11)

**Explanation 3: "Hessel Bottom's wet hay depresses the butterfat content of milk."**
- **Advanced by:** Nyle Grigg (1908 committee), r15; endorsed by Dr. Roland Kepp (veterinarian), r16
- **Specific evidence that defeats it:**
  - Bulletin No. 214 (State Agricultural College feeding trial on bog hay) showed butterfat depression of 0.05% in winter and nothing measurable in summer (r22, r11)
  - The observed discrepancy at Ostrey Hollow was 0.19 points (3.80% true vs. 3.61% recorded), consistent year-round, both summer and winter
  - 0.05% depression is far less than the 0.19% observed and cannot explain the full discrepancy (r22)
  - The 1911 anomaly: hay was the same before and after the broken measure; herds were the same; land was the same; only the glass changed; tests rose and fell with the glass, not with land conditions (r11, r22)
  - Four Hessel Bottom farmers' milk tested normal (3.78–3.82) at Alder Corners, same milk, same herds, different location (Vessey County), proving milk quality was normal, not depressed (r10)
  - Bulletin 471 definitively proves the glass, not land, was the cause through the 1911 experiment (r11)

**Note on wet-hay explanation:** This explanation is not entirely false. Wet hay *does* depress butterfat by about 0.05% (Bulletin 214 confirmed this). The Hessel Bottom farms *were* wet. The Grigg committee and Dr. Kepp were reasonable in this hypothesis. However, it is **insufficient**—it accounts for only 0.05% of the observed 0.19% discrepancy. The remaining 0.14% is explained entirely by the glass defect (0.19% = 5% of 3.80%, matching the 5% pipette shortfall). Therefore, wet hay may have been a real but secondary factor; the glass defect was primary.

**Subsidiary Question 1: Did the 1925 board of arbitration ever examine the condemned glassware?**

**Answer: No.** The condemned glassware was in a crate in the loft above the station's intake room. The arbitration hearing was held at the station in the weigh room (the intake room on the ground floor). The crate was never brought down or examined. Bertram Kyle testified that he carried four exhibits to the board: the test book, the receiving book, the Brant weigh book, and Strawn's circuit notebook (r23). The crate is not listed. Cleve Oram noted: "The hearing was in twenty-five... and the crate was up the ladder over their heads the entire time. Twenty feet, if that. I have thought about that since, I will not pretend I thought about it then" (r21). Kyle reflected: "I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked"—meaning no one compared the receiving book to Strawn's 1923 condemnation note (r23). The glass was never examined by the board. This is one of the crucial failures of the 1925 arbitration.

**Subsidiary Question 2: What did the tester (Ansel Keddie) himself believe was the cause?**

**Answer: Ansel suspected in 1911 that "it was something in the glass," but could not prove it and dared not write an accusation.** Duncan Keddie recorded: "He came home that summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book. I was seventeen and I remember the kitchen and I remember that he said it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not his way" (r18). What Ansel wrote in the test book was only a question mark (r04, r11, r13). At the 1925 hearing, when asked to explain the discrepancy, Ansel "testified [that he] tested as taught, state man saw him do it 31 times weekly for 29 seasons, can't explain figures, never took cream" (r13). Ansel could not offer a proof, only suspicion. He was a man who "would not put down what he could not prove" (r18). Yet his suspicion—formed in 1911 when a borrowed measure produced normal tests—was correct.

## Section G: Summary

The Ostrey Hollow scandal (1896–1958) was a textbook case of institutional failure disguised as moral failure. The farmers of Hessel Bottom, six families on wet ground, formed a cooperative to sell milk by butterfat test at a station run by Ansel Keddie for 29 years. From 1898 onward, the station's tests ran 5% systematically low—not because of dishonesty or watered milk or stolen cream, but because the measuring pipettes were defective, delivering only 95% of their marked capacity. For 25 years, this invisible error went undetected. Patrons lost $19,000 (76,000 pounds of butterfat × 25¢/lb). In 1919, the manager accused them of watering; in 1925, a board awarded them $3,800 (1/5 of the loss) "without admission" and fired Keddie. He died in 1934 vindicated only by a question mark he had written 23 years earlier. Not until 1958, when Ansel's granddaughter Ivy found the condemned crate in the loft and measured the pipettes in a college laboratory, did the truth emerge. The 1911 anomaly—when a broken measure was replaced by borrowed glass and tests rose to normal for nine weeks—had been the proof all along. Eight separate narratives (founding, installation, discrepancy, anomaly, false accusation, condemnation, failed arbitration, vindication) had to be woven together before the full story emerged, showing how institutional inertia, nameless ledger lines, unexamined assumptions, and the invisibility of instrumental error created injustice that only time and an educated granddaughter could undo.
