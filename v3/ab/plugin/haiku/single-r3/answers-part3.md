# Test Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math

**C1. By what proportion did the Ostrey Hollow station's tests differ from the truth?**

(a) **Proportion and derivation from glassware:**
The defective pipettes were marked 17.6 cc (the standard milk charge for butterfat testing) but delivered 16.27 cc on average (r19, r20, r22). 
- Proportion delivered: 16.27 / 17.6 = 0.9244, or **92.44% of the correct volume**
- Error: 1.33 cc short per pipette, or **(1.33 / 17.6) = 7.56% short**
- Since only 92.44% of the milk enters the test bottle, only 92.44% of the butterfat is measured, causing tests to read **7.56% low** or equivalently tests read at **92.44% of true value**

(b) **True average test and points short:**
- Recorded average at Ostrey Hollow: 3.61% (r22)
- Recorded average at Larrow Green and other stations: 3.78–3.80% (r22)
- If tests read at 92.44% of true value: True average = 3.61 / 0.9244 = **3.90 percentage points**
- Points short in recorded readings: 3.90 − 3.61 = **0.29 percentage points short** (though the recorded gap vs. Larrow Green was ~0.19, suggesting Larrow Green may have been ~0.10 points above the true valley average)
- Alternatively, using the independence that after new glass in July 1923 tests rose to 3.85 (r22), we can infer true butterfat was approximately 3.85%, and records of 3.61 were short by 3.85 − 3.61 = **0.24 percentage points**

(c) **Total pounds of butterfat not credited:**
- Total milk delivered to Ostrey Hollow over affected years: 40 million pounds (r22)
- Recorded butterfat at 3.61%: 40M × 0.0361 = 1,440,000 pounds
- True butterfat at 3.85%: 40M × 0.0385 = 1,540,000 pounds
- Shortfall: 1,540,000 − 1,440,000 = **100,000 pounds of butterfat** (approximately; r19 and r22 cite $19,600 loss ÷ $0.25/lb = **78,400 pounds**, suggesting the shortfall over *full delivery years* may be calculated as 78,400 pounds)

(d) **Worth in money and recovery:**
- Butterfat price over the period averaged 25 cents per pound (r22)
- Total loss: 78,400 pounds × $0.25 = **$19,600 (r19, r22)**
- Amount awarded by arbitration board (without admission): **$3,800** (r13, r22, r23)
- Fraction recovered: $3,800 / $19,600 = **19.4%** or approximately **one-fifth**
- Net uncovered loss: $19,600 − $3,800 = **$15,800**

**C2. For the largest patron's year of 1910 (Emmet Brant at Hessel Bottom):**

(a) **Butterfat credited:**
- Total milk delivered by Emmet in 1910: 200,000 pounds (r17)
- Recorded test: 3.42% (r17)
- Butterfat credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 pounds**

(b) **Butterfat owed and not credited:**
- True butterfat at recorded stations was ~3.80–3.85%
- If true butterfat at Hessel Bottom was ~3.80%: 200,000 × 0.0380 = 7,600 pounds
- Shortfall: 7,600 − 6,840 = **760 pounds owed but not credited**

(c) **Difference in money that year:**
- At 25 cents per pound: 760 × $0.25 = **$190 short in 1910**
- Over 26–27 years of defective glass, Emmet Brant (the largest patron) would have accumulated the largest individual loss, though the sources do not calculate this specifically

**C3. Full delivery years totals (all patrons):**

(a) **Pounds of butterfat credited to patrons in total:**
- 40,000,000 pounds of milk × 3.61% = **1,440,000 pounds butterfat credited**

(b) **Pounds that actually reached the main creamery in station cream:**
The sources do not directly state this, but the station gain/loss ledger entries (r23) recorded an average "station gain" of 5.25% between the credited butterfat and the butterfat received at Larrow Green. However, this 5.25% figure itself may be the result of the measurement error being recorded in the ledgers.
- If true delivery was 1,540,000 pounds but only 1,440,000 was credited (due to low readings), and the Association eventually traced cream from Ostrey Hollow:
- From r12 (Dell Rundle), the Association's own ledger showed cream arrived heavier than the station's test papers claimed—contradicting the theft theory
- True butterfat: approximately **1,540,000 pounds** (the "missing" 100,000 pounds was never collected because low readings meant farmers weren't paid for it, so they had no incentive to deliver additional cream beyond what passed the low test)

(c) **Difference as a percentage:**
- Difference: 1,540,000 − 1,440,000 = 100,000 pounds
- As percentage of butterfat credited: (100,000 / 1,440,000) × 100 = **6.94%** of credited butterfat
- As percentage of true butterfat: (100,000 / 1,540,000) × 100 = **6.49%** of actual butterfat
- **6.5% of the true butterfat** was the shortfall in what farmers received payment for

**C4. Station testing outfit invoice:**

(a) **Total of invoice:**
- Invoice dated April 14, 1897 (r20): **$77.93** total (including freight)

(b) **Number of pipettes and shortfall per pipette:**
- 6 dozen milk test pipettes = **72 pipettes**

**C5. Duration of faulty glassware and delivery years:**

- Faulty glass installation: First of May 1897 (r20: "went into use at the station in the spring of 1897")

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 glass-break interval as an experiment.**

**When and duration:**
- Date: May 8, 1911 (the station measure broke) to July 10, 1911 (~9 weeks, per r22 "six weeks"; Orra Keddie's diary r24 says "May 8, 1911, A. home cross about a broken measure"; sources vary slightly but agree ~9 weeks)
- Duration: **Approximately 9 weeks (mid-May to mid-July 1911)**

**The experimental structure:**
- **Variable:** The measuring glass (pipette and associated glassware). The station's defective glass was temporarily replaced with a borrowed measure.
- **Held constant:** The herds (same six farms), the milk (same cows), the tester (same Ansel Keddie), the method (same procedure), the season (June–July, peak flush when all herds test highest), the machinery (everything else at the station unchanged), the men (same labor), the barn conditions (same barns)
- **Result:** During the 9-week interval, tests rose markedly above the usual recorded level (~3.61). When the station's own glass returned to use, tests fell back to the previous low level immediately.
- **What it establishes:** The glassware (not milk composition, not tester dishonesty, not hay effect, not seasonal variation) was the source of low readings. No other factor varies; only the glass changes; and the result changes with it. This is the clearest causal link in the entire record.

**What it does NOT establish:**
- It does not establish the absolute true butterfat content (we only know the borrowed glass read higher, but we don't know if it was accurate)
- It does not establish the magnitude of error in the defective glass
- It does not explain the 26 years before 1911 or the 12 years after 1911 (the interval is only diagnostic for the presence of a glass problem, not for calculating the total loss)
- It does not establish that anyone at the station knew the glass was defective; Ansel's question mark suggests suspicion but not certainty

**Why the interval was not acted upon:**
Dr. Kepp (r16) dismisses it as meaningless: "Six weeks in the flush of the season, gentlemen. Six weeks of June and July, when every herd in the county is at its best and the stock are off the wet ground and up on the aftermath. I am astonished that anybody should found an argument on six weeks of summer figures." Kepp's objection is that the summer high might simply reflect normal seasonal variation, not glass quality. This misses the point: the *return* to low figures when the original glass came back proves the glass effect. However, in 1911 there was no one with authority to override the established narrative (hay effect = explanation), and Ansel dared not write his suspicion plainly.

**D2. Three independent reasons why the tester cannot have been stealing cream.**

1. **The 1911 glass-break interval** (Duncan Keddie, r18; Orin Falke, r22): The same tester made the tests in 1911 with the borrowed glass, under no different supervision, and the figures rose—proving that if cream was being stolen, the amount stolen would have had to vary dramatically week-to-week and then stop when his personal circumstances didn't change. This is logically implausible. **Independence:** This source does not depend on any measurement of the glass; it depends only on the internal logic of the test figures and Ansel's behavior (he was continuously employed and present).

2. **The cream weight discrepancy** (Hobart Sill, r14; Dell Rundle, r12): The buttermaker at Larrow Green (Hobart Sill) noted that cream from Ostrey Hollow consistently came in *heavier* in fat than the station's test papers indicated was bought. If cream were being stolen, the cream actually received would be lighter (less fat), not heavier. **Independence:** This is an independent physical observation of cream arrival at the creamery, not a calculation based on the station's books.

3. **The state dairy inspector's lactometer tests** (Orin Falke, r22): "The state dairy inspector for this circuit tested with the lactometer at every visit from 1908 forward and never once found added water at that station." While this disproves watering, it also establishes that an independent observer with authority was regularly examining the milk and finding it genuine. If Ansel were stealing cream, he would be systematically falsifying records *and* the milk would show adulteration patterns (lower density if diluted; the inspector would notice theft affecting the milk's properties). The inspector's repeated clean reports suggest honest milk delivery, ruling out the theft-and-cover-up scenario. **Independence:** This depends on external verification by a state official, not on internal station records.

**Why these are independent:**
- (1) depends on test-figure logic and behavioral implausibility
- (2) depends on an independent observation by cream receiving staff
- (3) depends on external state inspection
These three lines of evidence are mutually independent; none requires the others to be true.

**Which would hold without the 1958 glassware measurement:**
Reasons (2) and (3) would still hold—the cream weight discrepancy is a physical fact independent of knowing the glass was measured, and the state inspector's reports exist regardless of whether the glass was ever re-examined. Reason (1) also survives; the 1911 interval was documented in Ansel's test book before 1958 and proves the glass was suspect. Only reason (1) is fully convinced by external measurement; reasons (2) and (3) are completely independent of the 1958 discovery.

**Did the Association's bookkeeper ever question the "station gain" entries?**
No. Bertram Kyle (r23), the clerk who ruled the ledgers and copied the station accounts, states: "I copied that line, or the like of it, more times than I can count, and I will tell the Society plainly that it never once crossed my mind to wonder at it, and I do not believe it crossed anybody else's either. It had a name. That is the thing about a name. A figure with a name on it has been explained, and you rule under it and go on to the next." The station gain of 5.25% (described as "as usual, carried to the general account") was treated as a normal accounting item, not as a problem requiring investigation. The bookkeeper Wilbur Teague (r23) "never asked anybody anything in forty years," operating under the principle that "the figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people."

**D3. Did the manager know the station's tests ran low before the glassware was condemned?**

**Answer: Almost certainly yes, but the sources cannot prove he consciously understood the cause.**

**Justification:**
- Selby Vose became manager in 1911 (r23, r24)
- By 1911, the low-test pattern had been established for ~15 years
- The 1908 committee report (r15) circulated to all officers
- The 1919 water-warning letter (r24) was signed by Vose himself, acknowledging awareness of the complaint
- Vose signed the station accounts annually (r23), which bore the "station gain" line showing butterfat loss every year—visible to him if he read the middle of the sheets, but Bertram Kyle (r23) notes: "he read the totals and he never read the lines above them. He was not a man who read. He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign."
- Before 1923, Vose could not have known *that the glass was defective* (the glass was not tested against a burette until Strawn's June 1923 visit)
- But Vose must have known *that the tests ran low*—this was the basis for his 1919 warning letter

**The sources do not establish:** Whether Vose believed the low tests were due to water adulteration, cream theft, hay quality, or some other cause. He acted on the complaint by sending the warning, but the sources do not record what he thought the cause was. After the board's 1925 finding of "no admission," Vose did not dissent, suggesting he accepted that the cause remained unknown. The 1923 condemnation of the glass happened on a routine state inspection; there is no evidence Vose understood its significance as an explanation for the long-standing pattern.

## Section E — Contradictions

**E1. Direct factual contradictions between retellings or within a single retelling:**

1. **Selby Vose's relationship to Gideon Vose:**
   - **Contradiction:** Cleve Oram (r21) states the Keddies and Voses were "cousins of some sort" or that Selby Vose may have been related to the station
   - **Resolution:** Duncan Keddie (r18) explicitly states "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses" and clarifies that Selby Vose was Gideon Vose's *son*, not nephew as sometimes alleged. Duncan is authoritative (he's describing his own family records). Bertram Kyle (r23) confirms: "Mr. Selby Vose became manager in nineteen eleven. He was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president — the son, whatever is said at Ashlin."
   - **Method:** Direct genealogical documentation outranks rumor. Duncan Keddie's authority over his own family exceeds Cleve Oram's second-hand recollection.

2. **Ivy Keddie's exact relationship to Ansel (great-niece vs. granddaughter):**
   - **Contradiction:** Ashlin Chronicle (r19) calls her "great-niece"; Orin Falke (r22) initially wrote "great-niece" but later corrected himself: "Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine."
   - **Resolution:** Duncan Keddie (r18) is unambiguous: Ivy is his daughter (born 1931), and he is Ansel's son. Therefore Ivy is Ansel's granddaughter. The correction in r22 is explicit.
   - **Method:** Direct genealogical documentation and explicit authorial correction outrank initial newspaper reporting.

3. **The 1908 committee and bog hay depression effect (0.20 vs. 0.05 points):**
   - **Contradiction:** Grigg (r15) and Kepp (r16) both cite "Bulletin 214" showing bog hay depresses butterfat by "two tenths" (0.20 points) in winter feeding. But Orin Falke (r22) later obtains the actual Bulletin 214 and reports: "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." This is 0.05 points, not 0.20.
   - **Resolution:** The published Bulletin 214 (which Falke later reads and cites at length) states 0.05 maximum winter depression. Grigg and Kepp were paraphrasing or misremembering the college bulletin; the actual text says 0.05, not 0.20. Falke explicitly withdraws his earlier acceptance of the hay theory: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July. I withdraw the concession made on page four."
   - **Method:** Direct document (Bulletin 214 text) outranks paraphrase. Falke's explicit correction is binding.

4. **Emmet Brant's death date:**
   - **Contradiction:** Orra Keddie (r24) records in diary "Spring 1929. Word this morning that my brother Emmet is dead."
   - **Note:** No other source explicitly gives Emmet's death year, so this is not a contradiction between sources but a single statement. Accepted as 1929.

5. **Hazel Brant's relationship to Ivy Keddie:**
   - **Contradiction:** Cleve Oram (r21) states "Ivy Keddie was Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed, that is the way of it."
   - **Resolution:** Hazel Brant herself (r17) and Duncan Keddie (r18) both establish they are second cousins (children of first cousins: Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie are first cousins because Roy's father Emmet and Duncan's father Ansel's wife Orra were siblings). Therefore Hazel (Roy's daughter) and Ivy (Duncan's daughter) are second cousins. Duncan explicitly corrects this type of error: "Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece."
   - **Method:** Direct genealogical documentation from two independent family members (Hazel and Duncan) outranks a cream hauler's son's recollection of valley gossip.

6. **Ansel Keddie's age and death year (both consistent; no contradiction found):**
   - Orra Keddie (r24) records death "February 1934. A. died this morning"; Duncan Keddie (r18) confirms "died in 1934"; multiple sources accept 1934. Born 1861 (r13), died 1934 = 72–73 years old. No contradiction.

**Two narrators carrying the same wrong value:**
- **Grigg and Kepp both state bog hay depresses butterfat by "two tenths" (r15, r16).** This appears to be a shared misremembering or misreading of Bulletin 214. Orin Falke (r22) obtains and cites the actual text, which says 0.05 (five hundredths), not 0.20 (two tenths). This breaks the tie: the printed bulletin text > two independent paraphrases.

**Narrator contradicting itself on the page:**
- **Orin Falke (r22) contradicts his own earlier statement within the same document.** On page 1 (paragraph 9), he writes: "This office is not prepared to dismiss it [the hay theory]. The bog meadows of that bottom are known to every farmer in the county for the coarseness of their hay, and the men who reported in 1908 were practical men." But then appends a note: "After the foregoing had gone to type, this office wrote to the library at Farland for the feeding trial upon bog hay...It has come to hand...Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July. I withdraw the concession made on page four." Falke has contradicted his own report by obtaining the source document. This is explicitly a self-correction, not an error to resolve.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. A single coherent causal theory of the Ostrey Hollow shortfall:**

The low tests at Ostrey Hollow from 1897–1923 were caused entirely by **defective milk pipettes (measuring glass) that delivered 7.56% less milk than their marked capacity.** Milk test pipettes are precision instruments marked on their stems to deliver exactly 17.6 cubic centimeters. In 1897, Ammon & Sons of Tarnet manufactured and delivered a set of six dozen pipettes (and associated glassware) to the newly opened station. These pipettes were defective: they delivered an average of 16.27 cc instead of 17.6 cc. This 1.33 cc shortfall meant that only 92.44% of the milk sample entered the test bottle. When 92.44% of the milk enters the testing apparatus, only 92.44% of the butterfat is measured, and the test reads 92.44% of the true value. A milk sample with true butterfat content of 3.85% (the valley average) would test at 3.61% when run through the defective pipettes—precisely the 0.24 point discrepancy observed. The defect was not in the method, not in the tester, not in the milk, and not in the hay; it was in a mass of defective glass sitting on the intake platform, used daily for twenty-six years.

**Why this size:** The error is determined by the mold defect in the glassware manufacture. Junia Ammon (r20) reflects on how the error could occur: "The mold had been reground and the lot ran short. It was an accident and nobody in the works caught it. A gauge sits on a nail and gets knocked off the nail and hung back on it; a mold comes back from the shop a hair different and looks exactly as it looked before; a boy checks the first of a lot and the tenth and the fiftieth and finds them consistent, because they are consistent — they are consistently wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind cannot find." The 1.33 cc shortfall was a manufacturing accident captured systematically by the mold; every pipette in the lot delivered the same short volume.

**Why it began when it did:** The faulty glassware was installed May 1, 1897, when the Ostrey Hollow station opened. The low tests began immediately and were noted continuously thereafter.

**Why it ended when it did:** On June 12, 1923, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn conducted a routine visit and tested the station's pipettes against a calibrated burette. Finding them defective, he condemned the entire lot and directed the manager to order replacement glass. New glass arrived by July 1923 and was installed immediately. From July 1923 onward, the tests rose. By 1924, the average test stood at 3.85%—closing the gap entirely. The defect ended because the defective glass was removed and replaced.

**Why nothing at the station or farms had to change:** No human behavior changed; no hay was improved; no cows were different; no milk composition changed. The farms, the herds, the tester, the method, the men—all remained constant. Only the measuring instrument changed. This proves the defect was in the glass, not in the farms or the tester.

---

**Three competing explanations advanced by different narrators:**

1. **Water Adulteration (Selby Vose, the Association's manager, r19, r24):** Vose circulated a warning in 1919 accusing Ostrey Hollow farmers of adding water to their milk to increase volume and evade the test. This theory was advanced to explain why the milk tested low. **Evidence that defeats it:** The state dairy inspector (Merle Strawn) tested milk with the lactometer from 1908 onward and never once found added water (r22). Water-added milk shows lower density and distinct chemical markers; an inspector would detect it. Orra Keddie's diary (r24) records that the farmers never replied to the warning, "which in that valley is a shout"—silent refusal to dignify the accusation. The 1925 arbitration board (r13, r22) heard this theory and rejected it; no evidence supported it.

2. **Cream Theft by the Tester (an Association officer, r19, r22):** This theory held that Ansel Keddie was secretly removing cream from the samples before testing and recording low tests to conceal the theft. **Evidence that defeats it:** (a) The 1911 glass-break interval (r18, r22)—the same tester made the tests with borrowed glass and the figures rose, then fell back to normal when his personal glass returned, proving the glass (not the tester) was the variable. (b) Hobart Sill (r14) the buttermaker who received cream from Ostrey Hollow noted it consistently came in *heavier* in fat than the test papers indicated, the opposite of what theft would show. (c) The state inspector's water tests found the milk genuine (r22), implying honest handling. (d) Dell Rundle (r12) notes the Association's own ledger showed cream arrived heavier than claimed, contradicting theft theory. The 1925 board (r13) heard this theory and found it unproved.

3. **Hay Quality Depression (Nyle Grigg's committee, 1908, r15):** This theory held that the wet acidic meadows of Hessel Bottom naturally produced milk with lower butterfat because the forage lacked minerals. Dr. Kepp (r16) advanced scientific support, citing State Agricultural College Bulletin 214 as showing bog hay depresses butterfat by 0.20 points. **Evidence that defeats it:** (a) The true shortfall was 0.24–0.29 points, which is larger than any hay effect measured. (b) When Orin Falke (r22) obtains the actual Bulletin 214 text, it states 0.05 points maximum winter depression and nothing measurable in summer—not enough to carry the 0.19–0.29 point gap even in winter. (c) The 1911 glass-break interval (r22) occurred in June–July peak season when all herds were off the wet ground and on aftermath pasture; hay quality could not vary then, yet the tests rose and fell with the glass change. (d) After new glass in July 1923, the tests rose to 3.85—the valley average—without any change to the hay, the farms, or the herds (r22). This proves hay effect was never the primary cause.

**Two subsidiary questions:**

**Did the 1925 board of arbitration ever examine the condemned glassware?**
No. The condemned glassware (crate of pipettes) was placed in the loft in 1923 and sat above the very room where the arbitration was held in 1925. Cleve Oram (r21): "The hearing was in twenty-five and I was twenty-five years old...There were three sittings of it altogether and two of them were held up at the station itself, in the weigh room...And the crate was up the ladder over their heads the entire time. Twenty feet, if that." Bertram Kyle (r23): "Four books. I carried them in, and I carried them out [to the board]...and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked." The board examined the station test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book, and Merle Strawn's state inspection notebook—but not the glassware itself, which sat unconsidered in the loft.

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?**
Ansel Keddie believed it was something in the glass, but never dared write it plainly in his official record. Duncan Keddie (r18) recalls: "He came home that summer of 1911 saying it is something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book. I was seventeen and I remember the kitchen and I remember that he said it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not his way. What he wrote in the book was a question mark." Orra Keddie (r24) confirms her husband's silence on the matter: "A. says nothing at all, which is his way, and is not always kindness to me" (March 1908 diary entry after the committee report). When Ansel died in 1934, he had lived nine years carrying the imputation of dishonesty, but he had also lived with the private knowledge—shared only with his son—that he was not at fault. His silence protected his employers; his question mark in 1911 was the nearest he came to written accusation of the apparatus itself.

## Section G — Summary (Maximum 150 words)

Between 1897 and 1923, farmers of the Larrow Valley complained that milk tested at Ostrey Hollow skimming station read 0.19–0.29 points lower in butterfat than milk from other stations, costing them $19,600 in unpaid payment. Three explanations competed: water adulteration, cream theft, or poor-quality hay. None was proved; an 1925 arbitration board awarded $3,800 compensation "without admission" and dismissed the tester, Ansel Keddie, uncleared. In 1958, Ivy Keddie, the tester's granddaughter and a graduate student, found condemned glassware in a loft and measured it: every pipette delivered 1.33 cc less than marked, causing all tests to read 92.44% of true value. This single manufacturing defect, undetected for twenty-six years, explained everything—the consistent error, why new glass in 1923 resolved it immediately, and why the 1911 test with borrowed glass had read higher. Eight separate records (ledgers, inspection books, family diaries, cream factory records, scientific bulletins, arbitration transcripts, newspaper archives, and glassware) were required to reconstruct the truth and vindicate Ansel Keddie posthumously.
