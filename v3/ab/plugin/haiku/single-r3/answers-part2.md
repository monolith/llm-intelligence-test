# Test Answers — Part 2: Section A, Stories 5–8 and Section B

## Section A — Reconstruction (Stories 5–8, continued)

### Story 5: The 1923 State Inspection and Silent Condemnation of the Glass (June 1923)

On June 12, 1923, Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector for the Ordell circuit, made a routine visit to Ostrey Hollow station. He carried a calibrated burette, a standard instrument for testing glassware accuracy. He ran the station's milk pipettes against the burette—a simple, direct test. The pipettes were marked on their stems: 17.6 cc (the standard volume for a butterfat test). Not one of them delivered the marked volume. On average, the nine pipettes that were functional delivered 16.27 cc—a shortfall of 1.33 cc per pipette, or 7.56% short. Strawn condemned the entire lot on the spot. He wrote his findings in his inspection notebook (which survives in state archives), and he directed the manager to order new glass. The Association's clerk Bertram Kyle (r23) entered the order and filed Strawn's word of condemnation. New glass was ordered from Tarnet and arrived within the month; it was in use from July 1923 onward. No inference was drawn as to what the old glass had been doing for the previous twenty-six years. The old glass, the condemned crate, was carried up into the loft over the station's intake room and left under a tarpaulin. Cleve Oram (r21) recalled seeing it there and putting his hand on it—"a crate under a tarp." For thirty-five years it sat above the very room where it had falsified every test. The condemnation was a simple fact of maintenance, unremarkable in the office, filed and forgotten. But the consequence was immediate: from July 1923 onward, as new glass was used, the station's tests began to rise. By 1924, the average test stood at 3.85—closing the entire gap that had been complained of for two decades.

**Documents:** Orin Falke (r22) for date and Strawn's visit; Bertram Kyle (r23) for the office reaction and filing; Cleve Oram (r21) for the crate in the loft; note that Strawn's notebooks are cited as surviving evidence in Duncan Keddie (r18).

### Story 6: The 1925 Arbitration, Award, and Ansel's Dismissal (January–April 1925)

Pressure had accumulated for a decade after the 1919 water warning. In January 1925, the patrons petitioned formally for arbitration. A board of arbitration was formed and held hearings from March 17 to May 4, 1925, partly at the Association's office in Ashlin and partly at the station itself in the weigh room (with observers standing in the rain outside on the platform). The board heard three competing theories: water adulteration, cream theft by the tester, and natural depression of butterfat by the hay. Alonzo Frick (r13), the attorney who chaired the board, recalled that none of the theories was proved—no evidence of water was found (the state inspector's lactometer tests from 1908 onward showed no water); the 1911 glass interval ruled out cream theft; and bog hay effect could not account for the full 0.19 point gap. The board heard testimony and examined the station scales and books. On May 4, 1925, without admitting any wrongdoing by anyone, the board awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800 in compensation "without admission." Ansel Keddie was dismissed on April 30, 1925—four days before the award was announced. He came home on the day of his dismissal, hung his apron behind the shed, and ate ham for supper. Orra Keddie's diary (r24) records: "They have put him out. Twenty-nine seasons and a letter." He lived nine more years, doing carpentry work—"good carpentry," as Cleve Oram (r21) attests. He died in 1934 with the imputation on him, and men at his funeral praised his carpentry but said nothing of the other thing. The board had heard no evidence of theft or dishonesty by Ansel, yet his dismissal announced that something had been his fault.

**Documents:** Alonzo Frick (r13) for the arbitration recollections; Orin Falke (r22) for dates and findings; Orra Keddie (r24) diary for dismissal date and Ansel's response; Cleve Oram (r21) for his carpentry reputation; Bertram Kyle (r23) for the $3,800 award amount and station account charging.

### Story 7: The 1958 Discovery and Ivy Keddie's Investigation (February–October 1958)

Thirty-three years after dismissal, thirty-two years after death, Ivy Keddie—Ansel's granddaughter, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College—went back to Ostrey Hollow. She climbed into the loft over the station's intake room. There, under a tarpaulin and about an inch of chaff, she found a wooden crate. It was the station's condemned glassware, still crated and undisturbed since 1923. She brought the crate down (Hazel Brant noted in 1981 that "it took fifty years and a girl from the college who thought to climb a ladder"). She carried the crate east to Farland. In the college laboratory, she measured the surviving pipettes (eleven of the original seventy-two were in condition to measure) against a calibrated burette—exactly the test Merle Strawn had performed thirty-five years earlier. Every pipette delivered 16.27 cc when marked 17.6 cc. No variation; consistent error. The proportion was exact: 16.27 cc / 17.6 cc = 0.9244, meaning that every test recorded 92.44% of the true butterfat content, or 7.56% short. Ivy compiled this evidence with records from the State Agricultural College archives (Strawn's 1923 inspection notebooks, preserved in state records), with Rosalie Cudd's cheese factory ledgers from Alder Corners (showing the four Hessel Bottom farmers who hauled to the cheese factory in summers 1915–1930 tested at 3.80, not low), with Hobart Sill's recollections as a buttermaker (the cream from Ostrey Hollow was heavier than the test papers indicated), with the cream wagon records and Emmet Brant's private weigh book, with invoices and ledger entries. She calculated that over the full period of defective glass (1897–1923, with 1897 partial), the Ostrey Hollow patrons had been shorted approximately 78,400 pounds of butterfat, worth $19,600 at the average price of 25 cents per pound over those years.

**Documents:** Hazel Brant (r17) for finding the crate; Ashlin Chronicle (r19) for the measurements and totals; Duncan Keddie (r18) for tracking the location of all books and evidence; Cleve Oram (r21) for witnesses to the crate's existence; Rosalie Cudd (r10) for cheese factory ledgers; Hobart Sill (r14) for buttermaker's observations.

### Story 8: The Verification and Vindication (October 1958–1959)

Ivy Keddie and Professor Leland Vaught of the State Agricultural College jointly prepared Bulletin 471, issued in October 1958. The bulletin presented the evidence: the defective pipettes, their measurements, the 1923 condemnation, the rise in tests after new glass, the independent corroboration from cream records and factory accounts, the arithmetic of loss. Vaught, a professor of dairy husbandry without interest in local politics, stated plainly: "no milk was watered and no cream was stolen at Ostrey Hollow." The Ashlin Chronicle published a front-page story (October 3, 1958) titled "Old Ordell Wrong Righted." Ivy Keddie, twenty-seven, was interviewed by telephone at Farland: "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not. He tested the milk in front of the state's own man and he tested it the way he was taught, and the reason his figures would not come right was in a crate over his head the entire time. I would like it printed that he never knew." Orin Falke, the county agricultural agent, prepared a formal report for the county board (r22), documenting the evidence and recommending that the records be filed with the county's permanent papers and left in the custody of the Historical Rooms. The vindication was complete and public, but Ansel Keddie had been dead twenty-four years. Orra Keddie died in 1948 without knowing her husband's name would be cleared. The five key books—the station's test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book, Strawn's inspection notebook, and the crate of condemned glass—were distributed to historical societies and archives, preserved in three buildings, no longer anyone's private possession. The mystery was solved; the tester had not failed. The glass had failed.

**Documents:** Bulletin 471 (cited in r11, r19, r22); Ashlin Chronicle (r19); Orin Falke (r22); Duncan Keddie (r18) on book dispersal; Orra Keddie (r24) on her death date.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie? Derive it.**

Ivy Keddie is Ansel Keddie's **granddaughter**. The chain: Ansel married Orra Brant in 1889 (r24). Their son was Duncan Keddie, born 1894 (r18). Duncan's daughter was Ivy Keddie, born 1931 (r18). Therefore Ivy is the child of Duncan and the grandchild of Ansel. The Ashlin Chronicle (r19) initially incorrectly called her "great-niece," but Orin Falke (r22) corrected this in his 1959 report: "Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." The genealogical chain is unambiguous in Duncan Keddie's own documentation (r18).

**B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant? Derive it and state what is left open.**

Ivy and Hazel are **second cousins**: the children of first cousins. The chain: Emmet Brant (born 1868 per Orra, r24) was Orra's brother. Emmet's son was Roy Brant. Roy's daughter was Hazel Brant. Orra was Ansel's wife. Their son was Duncan, whose daughter was Ivy. Therefore: Emmet and Orra were siblings; Roy and Duncan were cousins (children of siblings); Hazel and Ivy are second cousins (children of cousins). Cleve Oram (r21) states "Ivy Keddie was Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed, that is the way of it," but this is incorrect; Duncan Keddie and multiple other sources agree they are second cousins, not first cousins once removed. The sources also note that Ivy and Hazel were related through blood but nothing in the sources explains their personal relationship or acquaintance. Duncan's note (r18) that they are "not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation" clarifies the genealogy but leaves open whether they worked together or were merely aware of kinship.

**B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter to what happened at Ostrey Hollow?**

Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**. Gideon organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association and was its first president, drafting its by-laws (r18, r22). Selby became manager in 1911 (r23). The significance is that Selby was an officer of the Association from its founding generation, and in 1919 *he* (not Ansel Keddie) signed and circulated the water-warning letter to Ostrey Hollow patrons. Selby's position as manager made him responsible for running the cooperative system, and his decision to send the warning letter—which implied wrongdoing by the Ostrey Hollow farmers—shaped the response and created the climate for the eventual arbitration. Bertram Kyle (r23) notes that Vose "was courteous to every man who came into that office...and he never once let a caller feel he was being hurried" but also "he read the totals and he never read the lines above them," suggesting he signed the station accounts without careful examination. Vose's genealogical distance from the Keddie family (see B4) meant his decisions were made without the personal stakes Ansel carried.

**B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?**

**The sources establish no genealogical relationship between the Voses and Keddies.** Duncan Keddie (r18) explicitly states: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses...My mother would have told me...and she never once put a Vose in it." Cleve Oram (r21) mentions local rumor: "The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort...My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper." However, Duncan Keddie's statement (who had access to genealogical knowledge from his mother Orra, Emmet Brant's sister) is more authoritative than rumor. The sources indicate confusion arising from the small valley population—people assumed connections that did not exist. Duncan also notes that his mother and Gideon Vose's wife were sometimes said to be cousins, and he explicitly denies this (r18). The absence of family connection between Ansel and Selby Vose meant there were no private obligations or allegiances affecting their professional interaction at the station. This absence of kinship is significant precisely because Ansel was related to the largest patron (Emmet Brant) and to the farmers on Hessel Bottom, yet stood outside the Vose family structure that governed the Association.
