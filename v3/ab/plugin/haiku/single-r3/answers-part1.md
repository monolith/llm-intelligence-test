# Test Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

## Section A — Reconstruction (Eight Underlying Stories)

### Story 1: The Cooperative Movement and Founding of Ostrey Hollow Station (1889–1896)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was organized in 1889 by farmers meeting at Larrow Green, responding to the cooperative movement sweeping the state. The Association built its main creamery at Larrow Green in 1891. In the winter of 1895–1896, recognizing that milk from the upper valley (particularly the six farms of Hessel Bottom) was being hauled seven miles down to Larrow Green, the Association decided to build a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow to receive milk locally, test it, separate the cream, and haul cream down to Larrow Green while returning skim to farmers. The station building—a two-story board building on the flat by the creek—was completed and opened on May 4, 1896. The station was equipped with testing apparatus ordered from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet: six dozen milk test pipettes (each marked 17.6 cc), twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, a hand tester, and freight—total cost $77.93, invoiced April 14, 1897. Ansel Keddie, who would remain the tester for twenty-nine seasons, began work at the station from its opening. The association adopted payment by butterfat test (Article 7 of by-laws) rather than payment by milk weight, making the accuracy of the test crucial to every farmer's monthly income. Emmet Brant, the largest farmer on Hessel Bottom, brought his milk consistently to Ostrey Hollow, maintaining a meticulous weighing ledger beginning January 1, 1897, recording both his own farm scale weight and the station's recorded test. The station's role was to settle a geographical convenience for farmers at the head of the valley.

**Documents:** Junia Ammon (r20) for invoice and dates; Duncan Keddie (r18) for Ansel's appointment and tenure; Orin Falke (r22) for Association founding and by-laws; Hazel Brant (r17) for Emmet's weigh book beginning.

### Story 2: The Low Tests Emerge and Early Investigations (1896–1908)

From the opening of the station, milk tested low—by 1902 the complaint began, and it never stopped. By Hazel Brant's account (r17), the difference was consistently nineteen hundredths of a point (0.19 points) below the milk of every other station in the valley: Ostrey Hollow recorded 3.61% butterfat on average, while Larrow Green recorded 3.78–3.80%. The difference was uniform month to month, season to season, wet years and dry, rain and drought. No theory satisfied: the herds were sound, the men honest, the scales accurate. Three competing explanations circulated: (1) the farmers at Ostrey Hollow were adding water to their milk; (2) the tester Ansel Keddie was stealing cream and recording low tests to cover the loss; (3) the wet acidic meadows of Hessel Bottom (bog hay) were depressing the butterfat content of the milk naturally. In 1908, three practical farmers including Nyle Grigg were appointed as committee to investigate. They visited all six farms repeatedly, sampled herds in every season, watched tests being made, sealed their own samples, weighed scales, and examined the water supply. They reported finding no dishonesty, no adulteration, but concluded the wet hay of Hessel Bottom was the culprit. The State Agricultural College published Bulletin 214 (cited by both Grigg and Kepp) showing bog hay depressed butterfat by two tenths of a point (0.20) in winter feeding. This modest finding from an independent source gave academic credibility to the practical committee's observation, and the matter was put aside for eleven years.

**Documents:** Hazel Brant (r17) for the 0.19 point gap; Nyle Grigg (r15) for the 1908 committee work and Bulletin 214; Dr. Kepp (r16) for bog hay analysis; Orin Falke (r22) for summary and dates.

### Story 3: The 1911 Glass-Break Interval—An Unheeded Diagnosis (May–July 1911)

In May 1911, the station's own measure (milk pipette and associated glassware) broke, and the station borrowed a measure from elsewhere. For nine weeks, from May 8 until July 10, the tests rose markedly above their usual recorded level. The moment the station's own glass was returned to use, the tests fell back to their former low level. Ansel Keddie noted this fact in his test book with a question mark—a rare mark for a man who did not speculate. The incident was treated by everyone aware of it as an anomaly, interesting but not actionable, and it was forgotten. Duncan Keddie (r18) recalled his father coming home in the summer of 1911 saying "it is something in the glass," but Ansel never dared write those words in the record. The nine-week interval had the structure of an experiment: the herds were the same, the milk the same, the men the same, the season at its peak (June and July when every herd in the county is at its best and stock graze the aftermath), and only the measuring glass changed—and the results changed with it. Nothing in 1911 could have been clearer, yet the conclusion was not drawn. The interval definitively proved the problem was not in the milk (not water, not cream theft, not hay effect varying seasonally) but in the measuring apparatus. Yet this evidence lay dormant for forty-seven years, observed only by Ansel and his son.

**Documents:** Duncan Keddie (r18) for Ansel's private statement; Orin Falke (r22) for the 1911 interval as a diagnostic; Dr. Kepp (r16) for dismissing the interval as meaningless ("six weeks in the flush of the season").

### Story 4: The Manager's 1919 Water-Warning Campaign and Family Rupture

In 1919, more than fifteen years after the 1908 committee found nothing, Selby Vose, the Association's manager (who had taken office in 1911), circulated a warning to the nine families of Ostrey Hollow and its environs. The letter, signed by Vose over his own name, warned the patrons about adding water to their milk. Bertram Kyle (r23), the clerk at the Cooperative office, typed the letter and remembered the occasion—he thought it "a hard thing to send to men who were bringing their milk in every morning of their lives." Orra Keddie's diary (r24) records: "A circular came to the station for the patrons, over the manager's name, about water in the milk. A. would not put them in the mail nor leave them on the platform. He took them round himself, to eleven families, and stood at the door while each man read it." Hazel Brant's mother put her copy in the stove without reply. Two days later, Emmet Brant (Orra's brother, Ansel's brother-in-law, and the largest patron of the station) came to the house. He asked Ansel why he had carried the paper himself if he did not believe it. Ansel answered: he carried it because he would not have another man carry it—he would not shift the burden. Emmet went home and never returned to the house. He was Orra's brother; the estrangement lasted ten years until his death in 1929. The 1919 warning implicated Ansel in an accusation against his own patrons and against his wife's family, and it fractured the valley's social fabric without settling the question.

**Documents:** Orra Keddie (r24) diary entries June 1919; Emmet Brant's response also in r24; Bertram Kyle (r23) recalling the typing and his judgment of the letter.
