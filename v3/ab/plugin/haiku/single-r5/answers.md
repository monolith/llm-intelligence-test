# Test Answers: Part 1 (Section A, Stories 1–4)

## Section A — Reconstruction (Eight Stories)

### Story 1: Formation and Opening (1889–1897)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was formed in 1889 by farmers seeking to centralize butter production. The main creamery was built at Larrow Green in 1891. Article 7 of the bylaws established payment by butterfat test weight, not milk weight—a critical decision that would shape the valley's history. In 1895–96, driven by demand from upland farms, the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the creek valley, primarily to serve six farms on Hessel Bottom (a wet meadow). The station opened May 4, 1896, with Ansel Keddie hired as tester and separator man. Milk was weighed in, sampled, tested for butterfat, then separated; cream went to Larrow Green, skim went back to farmers. The station's own testing outfit—six dozen pipettes marked 17.6 cc, test bottles, acid measure, hand tester—was ordered April 14, 1897 from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet for $77.93 and installed May 1, 1897. These pipettes would define the next 26 years. Ansel Keddie was "a careful man in small things" who kept meticulous records, watching scales and procedure with the obsession of a man who understood that his numbers determined men's livelihoods. (Sources: r02, r03, r18, r22, r24; r20 Junia Ammon's invoice reproduction; r23 Bertram Kyle.)

### Story 2: The Early Years and the First Complaint (1897–1908)

For the first five years, the station operated without complaint. But starting around 1902, patrons from Ostrey Hollow began noticing and voicing suspicion: their milk, hauled to the same station by the same road, tested lower than milk from everywhere else in the valley—consistently 0.17–0.21 points lower. The Larrow Green creamery's average held at 3.78%; Ostrey Hollow's held at 3.61%. This difference was not trivial: at 25 cents per pound of butterfat, it cost each family money every month. The complaints grew in frequency and intensity, raised at annual meetings and crossroads stores. By 1908, the Larrow Valley Association's board could ignore the matter no longer. They appointed Nyle Grigg and two other farmers to investigate. Grigg's committee spent the entire year of 1908 sampling herds at all hours, checking scales against known weights, watching tests being made, inspecting water supplies, observing herd condition. They found the herds sound, the milk whole, the scales honest, and the tester's work beyond reproach. They reported that "the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream," referring to Hessel Bottom's coarse, sour sedge hay cut late and fed as winter staple. The committee found no dishonesty. This explanation—blamed on land, not on men—was accepted and formally closed the matter for eleven years. Ansel Keddie's name was cleared of all suspicion in 1908. (Sources: r02, r09, r13, r15, r17, r21, r22, r24.)

### Story 3: The 1911 Glass Incident—An Unplanned Experiment (May–July 1911)

On May 8, 1911, the station's testing measure—the pipette that had been drawing milk samples since 1897—broke. Ansel Keddie was "cross about it" according to his wife Orra. The station was forced to borrow a measure from elsewhere while awaiting a replacement. For nine weeks—from early June through July 10, 1911—all milk tests at Ostrey Hollow were made with this borrowed glass. Then, as suddenly as it had come, the borrowed measure was returned and the station resumed using its own glassware. What happened during those nine weeks was extraordinary: the station's test results, which had been running 0.19–0.21 points below the valley average for fourteen years, jumped to normal valley-level results. When the station's own glass was resumed, the tests fell back to their former low level. The tester, herds, pasture, season, creaming procedure, acid, and every other variable remained identical. Only the measuring glass changed. Ansel Keddie noted this fact in his test book and placed a question mark beside it. This was the most powerful evidence yet that something was wrong with the equipment—but it was an observation without proof, a suspicion without a name. Ansel did not dare make an accusation he could not substantiate. The measure was not remarked upon again. The question mark remained. Thirteen years would pass before anyone attempted to answer it. (Sources: r03, r09, r13, r15, r18, r21, r22, r24; Orra Keddie's diary r24 confirms May 8, 1911.)

### Story 4: Theories and Suspicion (1908–1923)

In the years following the committee's report, three theories about the shortfall circulated in the valley, each with powerful champions. The first, advanced by Nyle Grigg's committee and later supported by veterinary surgeon Dr. Roland Kepp (1926), held that the wet ground of Hessel Bottom—perpetually waterlogged, growing coarse sedge instead of sweet grass—somehow produced milk of inherently lower fat content. This was not dishonesty but land itself. The second theory, whispered among some Association officers and managers, was that Ansel Keddie was stealing cream and writing low tests to conceal the loss—embezzlement of the cruellest sort, betraying the men whose milk he weighed every day. The third theory, which surfaced with Selby Vose's appointment as manager in 1911, was that the Ostrey Hollow farmers themselves were adding water to their milk to increase volume before selling by test weight. Vose, convinced of this, circulated a warning letter in June 1919 to all families on the bottom, cautioning against this fraud. Ansel Keddie personally delivered these accusations to eleven families, standing at each door while men read them. The impact was corrosive: Orra's brother Emmet, largest patron and milk supplier, confronted Ansel about carrying the letter. The two men argued about drainage for forty years afterward, but the real rupture came then—1919—with the implicit accusation that the milk he hauled from his farm was fraudulent. Yet parallel to these suspicions, Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, conducted annual tests from 1908 onward using the lactometer and found no added water at that station. Something was deeply wrong with one of these theories. No one could prove which. (Sources: r06, r08, r13, r14, r16, r22, r23, r24.)


# Test Answers: Part 2 (Section A, Stories 5–8 & Section B)

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### Story 5: The Manager's Accusation and Rising Tension (1919–1925)

Selby Vose, son of the Association's founder Gideon Vose and manager since 1911, became convinced that the Ostrey Hollow farmers were adding water to increase milk volume before sale by test. In June 1919, he drafted warning letters to patrons on that bottom, signed them with his authority as manager, and sent them out. Ansel Keddie, rather than simply posting these accusations on the platform, took it upon himself to deliver each letter personally—hand-delivering them to eleven families, standing at their doorways while each man read an official accusation that he was defrauding the cooperative. This act—meant to show the gravity of the warning, meant perhaps to make clear that Ansel himself accepted the manager's accusation—instead drove a wedge through the valley. Orra's brother Emmet Brant, largest patron on Hessel Bottom, who had hauled whole milk to that station since its opening, confronted Ansel: "Why did you carry the paper yourself if you did not believe it?" Ansel answered he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. After this, the two men never spoke again inside that house. For six years, this rupture festered. Tensions escalated through the early 1920s as the low tests persisted and explanations failed. By January 1925, the Ostrey Hollow patrons could bear it no longer. They petitioned the Association for a hearing before an arbitration board, demanding justice. (Sources: r08, r13, r17, r23, r24.)

### Story 6: The 1925 Arbitration and Dismissal (March–May 1925)

A board of arbitration was appointed under attorney Alonzo Frick and held three sittings: one at Ashlin in the Cooperative's office, two at the station itself in the weigh room. The hearings ran from March 17 to May 4, 1925. All evidence was brought to bear: Ansel Keddie's own test book in his precise hand, the receiving books showing butterfat credited versus butterfat received, Emmet Brant's private weigh book (lent for the purpose and wanted back), and Merle Strawn's state inspector's notebook for the year the glass had been condemned. Four documents. Yet clerk Bertram Kyle, who carried them in and out, later reflected: "I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked"—meaning no one seemed to compare the inspector's notebook with the test book to understand the relationship between the condemnation and the low tests. The board heard testimony from officers and patrons, examined the machinery, reviewed the figures. On May 4, 1925, the board announced its finding: it found "no cause established"—no proof of dishonesty, no certainty about the cause. Yet simultaneously, it awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800 "without admission," meaning: we pay you but admit nothing about why. Four days before announcing this award, on April 30, 1925, the Association dismissed Ansel Keddie. He had held the position for twenty-nine seasons. He came home at his ordinary hour, hung his apron behind the shed, and asked his wife what was for supper. (Sources: r02, r13, r17, r18, r21, r22, r23, r24.)

### Story 7: The Exile and Decline of Ansel Keddie (1925–1934)

After his dismissal, Ansel Keddie took work as a carpenter. At sixty-four years old, a tester for twenty-nine seasons, he began hanging doors and repairing barns for whatever the work was worth—a quarter of his former wages. For nine years, this is how he lived: carpentry at dawn, work through dusk, whistling while he worked (something he never did while tester). His wife Orra noted in her diary that he went from being a man who never complained of tiredness to a man who whistled—perhaps the whistling was the only voice he could give to the burden. Meanwhile, the valley's judgment hung over him unspoken. Men who came to him for carpentry work spoke kindly but never of the accusation; they praised his work but not his name. Alonzo Frick, who had chaired the arbitration, later reflected that he could not explain the low tests and found no evidence of dishonesty, yet Keddie's reputation remained destroyed. In the spring of 1929, Orra's brother Emmet died—the man who had not entered Ansel's house in ten years, the man most harmed by the low tests. After the funeral, Roy Brant (Emmet's son, Ansel's cousin) "was kind" at the gate. The estrangement was finally healed, but too late. Ansel Keddie died on February 1934, at home, in the forenoon, "without much pain at the end of it." Orra outlived him fourteen years more. He never knew his name would be cleared. (Sources: r17, r18, r21, r24.)

### Story 8: The Discovery, Measurement, and Vindication (1958–1959+)

In February 1958—twenty-four years after Ansel's death, thirty-three years after his dismissal—Ivy Keddie, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, entered the loft above the Ostrey Hollow station's intake room. She found a wooden crate, covered with a tarpaulin and buried under decades of chaff and neglect. The crate contained glassware: milk-testing pipettes, marked 17.6 cc, condemned by state dairy inspector Merle Strawn on June 12, 1923, stored in that loft and forgotten. Ivy Keddie carried the crate east to Farland. In March 1958, she measured the surviving pipettes—eleven that remained in measurable condition—against a calibrated burette in the college laboratory. Every single pipette delivered not 17.6 cc but 16.27 cc. The average was consistent: 16.27 cc, short by 1.33 cc, a deficit of 7.56%. In October 1958, the State Agricultural College published Bulletin No. 471, co-authored by Ivy Keddie and Professor Leland Vaught. The bulletin calculated that over the 26 years the faulty glass was in use, the Ostrey Hollow patrons were not credited with approximately 76,000 pounds of butterfat—worth $19,600 in money paid at 25 cents per pound. This shortfall of 76,000 pounds accounted for the entire 0.19–0.21 point deficit observed in every month of every year. No milk was watered. No cream was stolen. No hay was to blame. A piece of glass, manufactured in 1897 with a reground mold, had delivered 92.44% of its marked volume for 26 years. When replaced in 1923, the tests immediately normalized. When measured in 1958, the solution was incontrovertible. The Ashlin Chronicle published the discovery on October 3, 1958, in a story headlined "Old Ordell Wrong Righted." Ansel Keddie was cleared entirely—cleared by his own granddaughter, decades after his death. Orra never lived to see it; she had died ten years earlier in 1948. His name, which he had carried under accusation for nine years and carried in silence for the twenty-five years after, was finally restored. (Sources: r04, r11, r17, r18, r19, r20, r22, r24; r07 Halbert Nym's catalogue of the physical evidence.)

## Section B — Relationships (8 points)

### B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie

Ivy Keddie is Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Ansel's son Duncan Keddie married and had a daughter, Ivy, born in 1931. (Duncan Keddie explicitly states: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter.") Orin Falke initially misidentified her as "great-niece" but corrected himself in the same report: "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece." The correction is definitive from the family's own genealogist, Duncan Keddie, and is the only resolution where the sources agree. (Sources: r18 Duncan Keddie, r22 Orin Falke's correction.)

### B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant

Ivy and Hazel are second cousins. Duncan Keddie's genealogy is explicit: "Roy's daughter is Hazel Brant. Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed." Roy Brant was Duncan's first cousin (their parents Emmet and Orra were brother and sister). Therefore Ivy (Duncan's daughter) and Hazel (Roy's daughter) are second cousins. Cleve Oram refers to them as "first cousin once removed," which is incorrect according to Duncan's genealogy. The intervening generations are: Emmet Brant (brother) and Orra Brant/Keddie (sister), who together had two sons—Roy and Duncan respectively—whose children are Hazel and Ivy, making them second cousins. (Sources: r18 Duncan Keddie; r21 Cleve Oram's confused reference.)

### B3. Selby Vose's father and why it matters

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose, the Association's founder and first president. Bertram Kyle confirms: "Mr. Selby Vose... was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president—the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense." This matters because Gideon Vose had shaped the organization's structure, including the crucial article 7 establishing payment by butterfat test. Selby, as his son and successor manager, bore organizational legitimacy and authority. When Selby Vose became convinced (wrongly) that Ostrey Hollow farmers were adding water, he had the power of his family's founding authority behind him when he issued the 1919 warning letter. The Vose family's foundational role in the cooperative gave Selby's accusations particular weight and sting. (Sources: r18 Duncan Keddie, r23 Bertram Kyle.)

### B4. Relationship between Vose and Keddie families

No established family relationship exists between the Vose and Keddie families. Duncan Keddie, whose mother Orra knew the valley's genealogy intimately, states explicitly: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses... My mother would have told me... and she never once put a Vose in it." Cleve Oram reports hearing gossip that they were "cousins of some sort," but admits this is village talk unverified and perhaps confused with other families. However, Orra was Ansel Keddie's wife and sister to Emmet Brant, who was married to Rosalie Cudd's family (the cheesemakers over the ridge). Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, is explicitly stated to be "kin to nobody in the valley whatever." The Voses and Keddies were not related by blood, only by the organizational structure: Gideon Vose founded the cooperative, Selby Vose managed it, and Ansel Keddie tested milk for it. Their connection was institutional, not familial, and it was this institutional power imbalance that gave tragic weight to the accusations. (Sources: r18 Duncan Keddie, r21 Cleve Oram, r23 Bertram Kyle.)


# Test Answers: Part 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math (14 points)

### C1. Proportion and totals from the glassware

**(a) Proportion of error from glassware:**
- Marked volume: 17.6 cc
- Actual delivery: 16.27 cc (measured by Ivy Keddie, Bulletin 471)
- Short: 17.6 − 16.27 = 1.33 cc
- Proportion short: 1.33 ÷ 17.6 = 0.0756 or **7.56%**
- Volume delivered: 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244 or **92.44% of marked volume**

This means each pipette delivered 7.56% less milk than the test procedure expected, causing fat readings to be proportionally low.

**(b) True average test and points short:**
- Recorded average at Ostrey Hollow: 3.61% (per Orin Falke, Hazel Brant)
- Recorded average at Larrow Green: 3.78%
- Observed difference: 0.17–0.21 points (sources vary; Hazel says 0.19, Dr. Kepp says 0.21)
- If 7.56% of milk was short in the measure, the fat reading would be 7.56% low
- True average = 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = **3.90%**
- Short by: 3.90 − 3.61 = **0.29 points** (approximately)

**(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited:**
- Total milk received over 26 years of defective glass: 40 million pounds (Bertram Kyle)
- Average test crediting: 3.61% × 40,000,000 = 1,444,000 pounds butterfat credited
- True average should have been: 3.90% × 40,000,000 = 1,560,000 pounds
- Unpaid butterfat: 1,560,000 − 1,444,000 = **116,000 pounds**

**Note:** Sources cite 76,000 pounds (Bulletin 471, Chronicle) or 67,000 pounds (Cleve Oram). The discrepancy may reflect different calculation methods, different time periods included, or different fat percentages assumed. The most frequently cited figure in the primary College bulletin is **76,000 pounds**.

**(d) Money value and percentage received:**
- At 25 cents per pound (average price cited by Kyle): 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000**
- Patrons received $3,800 in the 1925 award
- Percentage received: $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = **20% of the loss**
- Percentage not recovered: **80%**

### C2. Emmet Brant's account in 1910

**(a) Butterfat credited:** Cannot be determined precisely from the sources. Hazel states that her grandfather Emmet "had the largest piece" on Hessel Bottom and "delivered the most milk into the station of any man on the creek." Station received 200,000 pounds "even" (Hazel's entry for 1910—a round figure, suggesting it may be stylized). If Emmet delivered 30–40% of the station's milk (plausible for the largest of six farms), he would have been credited with approximately 60,000–80,000 pounds. At 3.42% recorded test (1910 was a test year), this yields: 60,000–80,000 × 0.0342 = **2,052–2,736 pounds of butterfat credited**, though exact amount cannot be determined.

**(b) Butterfat owed and not credited:** Using true 3.71% test (adjusting for 7.56% measurement short): 60,000–80,000 × 0.0371 = **2,226–2,968 pounds owed**. Shortfall: **174–232 pounds per year** depending on actual volume delivered.

**(c) Worth in money that year:** At 25 cents per pound average: 200 × $0.25 = **$43.50–$58.00** in value not paid to Emmet in 1910 alone. Over 26 years, compounded, the loss to the largest patron would have been substantial.

### C3. Station butterfat over full delivery years

**(a) Total butterfat credited to patrons:** 
- 40 million pounds milk received × 3.61% recorded average = **1,444,000 pounds butterfat credited**

**(b) Total butterfat received at Larrow Green:** 
- Station gain (station loss/underpayment) accounted for 76,000 pounds over 26 years
- Butterfat that actually reached main creamery: 1,444,000 − 76,000 = **1,368,000 pounds**

**(c) Express as a percentage:**
The difference (76,000 pounds) as a percentage **of what was credited**: 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.26%** 

This matches Bertram Kyle's observation: the bookkeeper annually recorded "five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account" as "station gain."

### C4. Testing outfit invoice

**(a) Total invoice:** **$77.93** (Junia Ammon's invoice reproduction, r20, dated April 14, 1897)
- Breakdown: 6 doz pipettes @ $0.35 = $25.20; 12 doz test bottles @ $0.22 = $31.68; 1 acid measure = $0.90; 1 hand tester = $18.00; freight = $2.15

**Note:** Bertram Kyle and the Ashlin Chronicle state $77.39, but Ammon's own invoice document shows $77.93. The invoice is the authoritative source.

**(b) Number of pipettes and shortfall:**
- 6 dozen pipettes = **72 pipettes**
- Marked at: 17.6 cc
- Actually delivered: 16.27 cc
- Shortfall per pipette: 17.6 − 16.27 = **1.33 cc short**

### C5. Length of glass use and milk in first season

**(a) Length of faulty glass in use vs. full delivery years affected:**
- **Physical use: May 1, 1897 to June 12, 1923 = 26 years and 43 days (26.1 years)**
- **Full delivery years for accounting: 1898–1922 = 25 full years, plus 1897 and 1923 partial years ≈ 26 full-year equivalents**
- **Why different:** The physical timeline and accounting year timeline don't align perfectly. The glassware was installed mid-May 1897 (partial season) and condemned mid-June 1923 (partial season). Accounting typically runs calendar or delivery year, so the full-impact calculation includes 25 complete years (1898–1922) plus partial years at ends, totaling approximately 26 full-year equivalents for financial calculation.

**(b) Pounds of milk in first season:**
- First season: May 1, 1897 to April 1898 (dairy year)
- Bertram Kyle states: "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds, which is what I understood at the time from men who were there"
- Exact figure not in documented books (Kyle explicitly says "It is not in any book I ever ruled")
- **Approximately 1.1 million pounds** (based on Kyle's recollection, unverified)
- This is lower than the average of 1.6 million pounds per year for subsequent years, suggesting growth or seasonal variation in the early years.

## Section D — Logic (10 points)

### D1. The 1911 glass incident as an experiment

**When and duration:** May 8, 1911 (station's measure breaks, per Orra's diary); borrowed measure used for nine weeks through July 10, 1911.

**Variable, controlled factors, and result:**
- **Variable (independent):** The measuring glassware—specifically, the pipettes used to draw milk samples for the test
- **Held constant:** The tester (Ansel Keddie), the herds, the milk source, the test procedure, the acid, the season (June-July, flush of season), the separation apparatus, the scales, the personnel, and every other factor that could affect results
- **Result:** Tests during the nine-week interval ran "markedly higher" than before or after, reaching normal valley levels (approximately 3.75–3.78) instead of the habitual 3.61

**What it establishes:**
1. The low tests are not caused by the tester's dishonesty (same man, same procedure, different result)
2. The low tests are not caused by poor-quality milk from the herds (same herds, higher tests with different glass)
3. The low tests are not caused by any procedure defect at the station (same everything except glass)
4. Some equipment issue causes the low readings—specifically, equipment that measures milk

**What it does NOT establish:**
1. That the problem existed before May 1897 (first glass arrival) or only started at some later point
2. That the problem is specifically that the pipettes deliver short volume (not proven until 1958 measurement)
3. Whether the defect was intentional, accidental, or developed over time
4. The mechanism of the defect
5. That this was a design flaw or manufacturing error rather than wear and aging

### D2. Three independent reasons against embezzlement

**Reason 1 — The 1911 Experiment (Orin Falke, Nyle Grigg, Ansel Keddie):**
When the broken measure was replaced with a borrowed one, the tests rose to normal levels under the same tester with no different supervision. If Ansel were stealing cream to account for low tests, he would remove it before the test, and the tests would remain low regardless of which glassware was used. The fact that tests rose with the borrowed glass while the same tester was in place proves the cream remained in the milk when tested. Embezzlement is therefore impossible.

**Reason 2 — Ledger Arithmetic (Bertram Kyle, Wilbur Teague):**
The "station gain" entries show a consistent 5.25% shortfall year after year—not varying with season, herd size, or milk volume, but fixed as a percentage. A thief's gain would vary: more in high-volume seasons, less in low seasons, different amounts as milk composition varies. A systematic percentage shortfall in a test result is characteristic of a measurement error, not of crime. An embezzler's accounting would be irregular; a defective measure's error is consistent.

**Reason 3 — State Inspector's Observation (Merle Strawn, Bertram Kyle):**
Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, visited the station annually from 1908 forward. He observed the milk at intake and found it "whole"—meaning no water added, no cream visibly removed, milk intact. An inspector's independent observation of milk quality at the platform contradicts any scenario of systematic cream removal before testing.

**Why independent:** Reason 1 relies on an unplanned experiment structure (controlled variables); Reason 2 relies on ledger mathematics (accounting patterns); Reason 3 relies on state inspector testimony (external observation). Each could stand entirely alone.

**One that would hold without 1958 measurement:** All three would. The 1911 experiment alone (Reason 1) would still prove no theft, regardless of whether the glass was ever measured in 1958. The ledger arithmetic (Reason 2) would still show a measurement-error pattern rather than theft pattern. The inspector's testimony (Reason 3) would still report milk was whole at platform.

**Did bookkeeper Teague question the entries?**
No. Bertram Kyle states: "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years. That is not a criticism. It was his notion of the position: the figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people." Teague wrote what he was directed to write, following the established pattern. He recorded the "station gain" line every year for forty years without questioning it. This is perhaps the tragedy: the numbers that would have revealed the truth (if compared against the state inspector's condemnation of the glass) went unquestioned because Teague understood his role as a clerk to record, not to interpret.

### D3. Did the manager know tests ran low before glassware was condemned?

**Answer: Yes, by 1919. But no evidence that he understood the cause was the glassware.**

**Justification:**
- The low tests were public knowledge: complaints began in 1902 and were raised at every annual meeting and "every crossroads store between here and Ashlin" (Nyle Grigg)
- As manager since 1911, Selby Vose would receive annual test summaries and hear complaints directly
- In June 1919, Vose issued a circular warning patrons about water in milk—proving he knew tests ran low and had developed a theory about why
- Vose signed the 1925 arbitration award, which acknowledged the low tests as an established fact requiring compensation

**But did he know the glass was defective?**
No evidence suggests Vose suspected glassware defects before June 1923. His 1919 theory was water addition (the wrong cause). No evidence indicates he or anyone else at the Association understood the glass was short-delivery before Merle Strawn measured it on June 12, 1923. The condemnation appears to have been a surprise to the Association, not a known problem.

## Section E — Contradictions (8 points)

### Direct factual contradictions

**1. Invoice total**
- **Claim A:** $77.93 (Junia Ammon, r20, reproduction of actual 1897 invoice)
- **Claim B:** $77.39 (Bertram Kyle, r23; Ashlin Chronicle, r19)
- **Narrators:** Ammon (primary source document); Kyle (clerk's memory); Chronicle (quoting Ammon's document)
- **Resolution:** $77.93 is correct. Primary document (actual invoice) outranks recollection from memory decades later.

**2. Award amount (Contradiction involving two independent narrators with same wrong value)**
- **Claim A:** $3,800 (Effie Loomis r02, Alonzo Frick r13, Duncan Keddie r18, Orin Falke r22, Bertram Kyle r23)
- **Claim B:** $3,500 (Hazel Brant r17, Cleve Oram r21)
- **Two narrators carry same wrong value:** Hazel Brant and Cleve Oram both independently state $3,500 or "thirty-five hundred dollars"
- **What breaks the tie:** Alonzo Frick was the arbitration board chairman and would have known the award amount with precision; Orin Falke cites official county records ("The award... was thirty-eight hundred dollars"); Bertram Kyle, the office clerk who handled the paperwork, states $3,800. The weight of evidence, especially from those closest to the official documents, supports $3,800.
- **Correct answer:** $3,800

**3. Condemnation date (potential contradiction)**
- **Claim A:** June 1923 (Merle Strawn r06, Orin Falke r22: "June 12 of that year [1923]")
- **Claim B:** 1922 (Bertram Kyle r23: "In nineteen twenty-two the state man came into the office")
- **Narrators:** Strawn (the inspector himself); Falke (citing official records); Kyle (office clerk)
- **Resolution:** June 12, 1923 is correct. Strawn and Falke converge on 1923; Kyle is vague ("twenty-two... came into the office") and may be misremembering or referring to advance notice. The specific date June 12, 1923 is documented in Falke's official record. Kyle's 1922 reference is likely a dating error or conflation with earlier concerns.

**4. Surviving pipettes in loft**
- **Claim A:** Nine surviving (Hazel Brant r17, Cleve Oram r21: "nine of them left")
- **Claim B:** Eleven "in condition to be measured" (Ashlin Chronicle r19, Leland Vaught r11)
- **Resolution:** These are consistent, not contradictory. The crate held 60–72 original pipettes. After 35 years in the loft, 9–11 survived the breaking and decay. The Chronicle specifies 11 "in condition to be measured"—meaning out of 9–11 surviving, 11 were actually measurable at the college lab. Minor variation likely reflects different ways of counting (total surviving vs. measurable).

**5. Defective glass delivery volume**
- **Claim A:** 16.72 cc (Ivy Keddie r04: "16.72 cc vs 17.6 cc")
- **Claim B:** 16.27 cc (Leland Vaught r11, Ashlin Chronicle r19)
- **Resolution:** 16.27 cc is correct. This is the measured average from Ivy's own college lab measurement reported by Vaught and the Chronicle. Ivy's personal statement in r04 saying "16.72 cc" appears to be a rounding error or misstatement by her in 1959. The reliable figure from the actual 1958 college measurement is 16.27 cc.

**Self-contradiction within a single document:**
- **Orin Falke (r22):** Initially states Ivy Keddie is "his great-niece" (page 2). Then adds a note at the end: "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." Falke corrects his own error within the same document.

### No significant contradictions found in:

## Section F — Theory (6 points)

### F1. Causal theory of the shortfall

**The Complete Causal Theory:**

The shortfall at Ostrey Hollow was caused by a manufacturing defect in the milk-testing pipettes (measuring glassware) installed in 1897. 

In April 1897, Ammon & Sons of Tarnet manufactured six dozen milk-testing pipettes marked 17.6 cubic centimeters. Due to a manufacturing error—most likely a reground or worn mold—the entire lot was produced short of specification. Each pipette delivered only 16.27 cubic centimeters instead of the marked 17.6 cc, a consistent shortfall of 1.33 cc per pipette (7.56% under capacity).

These pipettes were installed at the Ostrey Hollow station on May 1, 1897. The testing procedure was calibrated for the full 17.6 cc charge. When a pipette delivered only 16.27 cc—7.56% less milk—into a test bottle whose graduated neck was ruled for 17.6 cc, the resulting fat reading was proportionally lower: approximately 7.56% below the true fat content. In practical terms, this produced a consistent shortfall of approximately 0.19 to 0.21 percentage points in the butterfat test, year after year.

This error persisted unchanged from May 1897 to June 1923 (26 years) because the cause remained constant: the same defective glassware, unexamined, remained in use. The defect was not in any procedure, not in the tester's work, not in the milk quality, not in the farmers' honesty, not in the cattle or land. It was solely in the physical measurement tool.

**Why this size:** The shortfall was 7.56% because that is how much short the pipettes were manufactured—a mold error producing consistently undersized glass.

**Why it began when it did:** The shortfall began May 1, 1897, when the defective pipettes arrived and were installed. The station opened in May 1896 using borrowed glassware from Larrow Green, which tested normally. The first year's tests (1896–97) showed no shortfall. When the station's own (defective) glassware was installed May 1, 1897, the shortfall began immediately and persisted.

**Why it ended when it did:** On June 12, 1923, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn measured the Ostrey Hollow pipettes against a calibrated burette, found they would not deliver their mark, and condemned the entire lot. The Association ordered replacement glass, which arrived by July 1923. From August 1923 onward (and certainly by 1924), new pipettes were in use. Test results immediately normalized: the 1924 average rose to 3.85, nearly identical to valley averages. The shortfall ended precisely when the defective glass was replaced.

**Why nothing at the station or farms had to change:** Because the defect was not in the herds, not in the pasture, not in the testing procedure, not in the tester's technique, and not in any farmer's practice. The six farms on Hessel Bottom, with their wet meadow hay and their reputation for thinness, were not the cause—when their milk was tested with the borrowed glassware in 1911, it tested at normal valley levels. The same tester (Ansel Keddie), the same herds, the same hay, the same procedure, the same season—all unchanged—produced normal test results with different glass. The problem was entirely in the measuring device. No change was needed anywhere except in the glass.

### Three competing explanations and the evidence that defeats each

**1. Water Addition Theory** (Selby Vose, manager, 1919; suspected by some Association officers)

Vose became convinced the Ostrey Hollow farmers were adding water to their milk to increase volume before sale by test weight.

**Defeated by:** 
- Merle Strawn's lactometer testing from 1908 forward "never once found added water at that station" (Strawn's notebook, r06, r22). The state inspector, visiting annually with no interest in any local politics, would have detected water through chemical testing. His neutral testimony refutes the water theory.
- Most decisively: The 1911 glass incident. When the station's broken measure was replaced with a borrowed one for nine weeks, the tests rose to normal valley levels despite the milk coming from the exact same creek, the same farms, the same springs. If water had been added to the milk by farmers, it would persist in the milk regardless of which glassware was used to test it. The fact that tests rose when the glass changed proves no water was in the milk—the low readings came from the measuring device, not the milk.

**2. Embezzlement Theory** (advanced by some Association officers; implied suspicion in multiple testimonies)

Some believed Ansel Keddie was stealing cream and writing low tests to conceal the theft.

**Defeated by:**
- The 1911 glass incident is decisive: The same tester (Ansel Keddie), under no different supervision, handling the same milk from the same farms, made tests that rose to normal levels when using borrowed glassware, then fell back to low levels when returning to the station's own glass. If Ansel were stealing cream, he would remove it from the milk before it reached his testing glassware, and the tests would remain low regardless of which glass was used. The fact that his tests rose with the borrowed glass—while the same man was at the same desk, with the same procedure, in the same building—proves no cream was being removed. (This evidence would hold without the 1958 glass measurement.)
- The ledger pattern of "station gain": The shortfall appears as a consistent 5.25% year after year, regardless of season or milk volume. An embezzler's gain would vary: more in high-volume months, less in low-volume months, different amounts as milk composition changes. The perfect consistency of the shortfall as a percentage is diagnostic of a measurement error, not a crime.
- The state inspector's testimony: Merle Strawn, visiting annually, found the milk "whole" at intake—no cream visibly removed, no suspicious volumes. An independent observer with no local allegiances would have noticed systematic cream theft.

**3. Wet Hay Theory** (Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee; advanced by Dr. Roland Kepp, 1926)

The wet bottom of Hessel Bottom—perpetually waterlogged, growing coarse sedge—inherently produced milk of lower fat content.

**Defeated by:**
- The State Agricultural College's own Bulletin No. 214 (1911 feeding trial of bog hay). The trial showed bog hay depressed butterfat test by "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture" (r22). The observed shortfall was 0.19 to 0.21 points (19–21 hundredths). Five hundredths cannot account for 19–21 hundredths, especially not in summer months when the hay effect is zero. Orin Falke, in 1959, reviewed this evidence and explicitly withdrew his earlier concession to the wet hay theory: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July. I withdraw the concession made on page four." The mechanism proposed by the theory (poor hay → poor milk) is insufficient to explain the observed shortfall.

### Subsidiary questions

**Did the 1925 arbitration board ever examine the condemned glassware?**

No. The crate of defective pipettes sat in the loft directly overhead during the 1925 arbitration hearings. Two of the three board sittings were held in the weigh room of the station itself—directly beneath where the crate lay. Yet Bertram Kyle, the clerk who carried the four key documents in and out, reflects: "I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked"—referring to comparing the four documents. No one appears to have compared the state inspector's notebook (which documented the 1923 condemnation of the glass) with the test book. The physical glassware itself was never brought into the hearing room. Cleve Oram notes: "The crate was up the ladder over their heads the entire time. Twenty feet, if that."

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?**

Ansel Keddie suspected it was "something in the glass." After the May 1911 measure broke and a borrowed glass was used for nine weeks with tests running higher, Duncan Keddie recalls his father saying at supper and again by the door (not his usual way): "it was something in the glass." Ansel put a question mark in his test book at that point but did not dare make a definitive accusation. Duncan reflects: "He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove, and I have thought since that this was the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had." Ansel suspected the glassware but had no proof. He lived under accusation for nine years (1925–1934) and died with this suspicion unconfirmed, 24 years before the 1958 measurement by his own granddaughter proved him correct.

## Section G — Summary (4 points)

**G1. Summary in 150 words**

In 1896, farmers organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery, establishing payment by butterfat test rather than milk weight. The Ostrey Hollow skimming station, serving six upland farms on wet Hessel Bottom, opened with the system. In 1897, defective milk-testing pipettes manufactured short (delivering 16.27 cc instead of marked 17.6 cc) were installed. From 1902 onward, Ostrey Hollow's milk tested 0.19 points below valley average—worth roughly $19,600 over 26 years. Three theories arose: farmers added water (disproven by state inspection); the tester stole cream (disproven by a 1911 experiment when borrowed glass yielded normal tests); or wet hay caused poor milk (insufficient by college feeding trials). A 1925 arbitration board found "no cause established," dismissed the tester after 29 years, and awarded compensation without admission. The truth lay unexamined: defective glassware installed in 1897. When condemned and replaced in 1923, tests immediately normalized. In 1958, the tester's granddaughter discovered the condemned glass in the station's loft, measured it, and proved the entire shortfall stemmed from equipment defect, not dishonesty. The tester died vindicated—24 years too late.
