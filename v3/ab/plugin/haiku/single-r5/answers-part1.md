# Test Answers: Part 1 (Section A, Stories 1–4)

## Section A — Reconstruction (Eight Stories)

### Story 1: Formation and Opening (1889–1897)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was formed in 1889 by farmers seeking to centralize butter production. The main creamery was built at Larrow Green in 1891. Article 7 of the bylaws established payment by butterfat test weight, not milk weight—a critical decision that would shape the valley's history. In 1895–96, driven by demand from upland farms, the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the creek valley, primarily to serve six farms on Hessel Bottom (a wet meadow). The station opened May 4, 1896, with Ansel Keddie hired as tester and separator man. Milk was weighed in, sampled, tested for butterfat, then separated; cream went to Larrow Green, skim went back to farmers. The station's own testing outfit—six dozen pipettes marked 17.6 cc, test bottles, acid measure, hand tester—was ordered April 14, 1897 from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet for $77.93 and installed May 1, 1897. These pipettes would define the next 26 years. Ansel Keddie was "a careful man in small things" who kept meticulous records, watching scales and procedure with the obsession of a man who understood that his numbers determined men's livelihoods. (Sources: r02, r03, r18, r22, r24; r20 Junia Ammon's invoice reproduction; r23 Bertram Kyle.)

### Story 2: The Early Years and the First Complaint (1897–1908)

For the first five years, the station operated without complaint. But starting around 1902, patrons from Ostrey Hollow began noticing and voicing suspicion: their milk, hauled to the same station by the same road, tested lower than milk from everywhere else in the valley—consistently 0.17–0.21 points lower. The Larrow Green creamery's average held at 3.78%; Ostrey Hollow's held at 3.61%. This difference was not trivial: at 25 cents per pound of butterfat, it cost each family money every month. The complaints grew in frequency and intensity, raised at annual meetings and crossroads stores. By 1908, the Larrow Valley Association's board could ignore the matter no longer. They appointed Nyle Grigg and two other farmers to investigate. Grigg's committee spent the entire year of 1908 sampling herds at all hours, checking scales against known weights, watching tests being made, inspecting water supplies, observing herd condition. They found the herds sound, the milk whole, the scales honest, and the tester's work beyond reproach. They reported that "the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream," referring to Hessel Bottom's coarse, sour sedge hay cut late and fed as winter staple. The committee found no dishonesty. This explanation—blamed on land, not on men—was accepted and formally closed the matter for eleven years. Ansel Keddie's name was cleared of all suspicion in 1908. (Sources: r02, r09, r13, r15, r17, r21, r22, r24.)

### Story 3: The 1911 Glass Incident—An Unplanned Experiment (May–July 1911)

On May 8, 1911, the station's testing measure—the pipette that had been drawing milk samples since 1897—broke. Ansel Keddie was "cross about it" according to his wife Orra. The station was forced to borrow a measure from elsewhere while awaiting a replacement. For nine weeks—from early June through July 10, 1911—all milk tests at Ostrey Hollow were made with this borrowed glass. Then, as suddenly as it had come, the borrowed measure was returned and the station resumed using its own glassware. What happened during those nine weeks was extraordinary: the station's test results, which had been running 0.19–0.21 points below the valley average for fourteen years, jumped to normal valley-level results. When the station's own glass was resumed, the tests fell back to their former low level. The tester, herds, pasture, season, creaming procedure, acid, and every other variable remained identical. Only the measuring glass changed. Ansel Keddie noted this fact in his test book and placed a question mark beside it. This was the most powerful evidence yet that something was wrong with the equipment—but it was an observation without proof, a suspicion without a name. Ansel did not dare make an accusation he could not substantiate. The measure was not remarked upon again. The question mark remained. Thirteen years would pass before anyone attempted to answer it. (Sources: r03, r09, r13, r15, r18, r21, r22, r24; Orra Keddie's diary r24 confirms May 8, 1911.)

### Story 4: Theories and Suspicion (1908–1923)

In the years following the committee's report, three theories about the shortfall circulated in the valley, each with powerful champions. The first, advanced by Nyle Grigg's committee and later supported by veterinary surgeon Dr. Roland Kepp (1926), held that the wet ground of Hessel Bottom—perpetually waterlogged, growing coarse sedge instead of sweet grass—somehow produced milk of inherently lower fat content. This was not dishonesty but land itself. The second theory, whispered among some Association officers and managers, was that Ansel Keddie was stealing cream and writing low tests to conceal the loss—embezzlement of the cruellest sort, betraying the men whose milk he weighed every day. The third theory, which surfaced with Selby Vose's appointment as manager in 1911, was that the Ostrey Hollow farmers themselves were adding water to their milk to increase volume before selling by test weight. Vose, convinced of this, circulated a warning letter in June 1919 to all families on the bottom, cautioning against this fraud. Ansel Keddie personally delivered these accusations to eleven families, standing at each door while men read them. The impact was corrosive: Orra's brother Emmet, largest patron and milk supplier, confronted Ansel about carrying the letter. The two men argued about drainage for forty years afterward, but the real rupture came then—1919—with the implicit accusation that the milk he hauled from his farm was fraudulent. Yet parallel to these suspicions, Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, conducted annual tests from 1908 onward using the lactometer and found no added water at that station. Something was deeply wrong with one of these theories. No one could prove which. (Sources: r06, r08, r13, r14, r16, r22, r23, r24.)
