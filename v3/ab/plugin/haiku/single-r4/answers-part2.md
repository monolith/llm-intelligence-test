# Answers to Test Questions — Part 2
## Section A: Reconstruction (Stories 5–8) and Section B: Relationships

---

## Story 5: The 1919 Water-Warning Letter and Family Estrangement

In June 1919, Selby Vose (the Association's manager) circulated a warning letter to Ostrey Hollow patrons—nine families on six farms—cautiously but unmistakably accusing them of adding water to milk. Ansel Keddie received his copy and made an unusual choice: he did not mail the letters or leave them on the platform. Instead, he personally delivered each letter to all nine families, standing at each door while each man read it. He came home late with his hat still on, visibly shaken.

Two days later, Orra's brother Emmet Brant came up to the house. He did not enter. Standing in the yard, he confronted Ansel: "You have hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and you know it better than any man living. Then you ask him why he carried the paper himself if he did not believe it." Ansel replied: "I carried it because I would not have another man carry it." Emmet left and did not return for ten years.

Orra's diary: "He has not been in this house since, and he is my brother." The estrangement lasted until Emmet's death in 1929. Orra wrote then: "Ten years and neither of them would go up the road first, and now it is settled the way those things get settled."

This story captures the moral trap Ansel occupied. He was convinced the milk was not watered, but he could not prove it without naming what he suspected—the glass. His personal delivery was an act of honor: he would shoulder the burden of the message himself rather than let another carry an accusation against neighbors he worked with daily. But honor could not bridge the rift between brothers-in-law. Emmet interpreted the personal delivery as Ansel's private admission that the accusation was just—why else take the letters house to house? The misreading destroyed a family relationship that had endured since Ansel and Orra's marriage in 1889.

## Story 6: The 1923 State Inspection and Condemnation of Glass (June–July 1923)

In June 1923, Merle Strawn—the state dairy inspector for the Ordell circuit and kin to no one in the valley—made a routine visit to Ostrey Hollow. Following standard procedure, he ran the station's milk pipettes against a calibrated burette. They would not deliver their marked 17.6 c.c. He condemned the entire lot on the spot and ordered new glass.

The Association ordered new pipettes from Ammon & Sons (per Bertram Kyle's office records). New glass came and went into use by July 1923.

The change was immediate and unambiguous. From 1924 onward, the station's average test stood at 3.85—a jump that matched the valley baseline and vindicated the shortfall's mechanical cause.

Yet no one made the connection. Strawn drew no inference about the years behind the defect. The Association's office recorded the event matter-of-factly: ordered new glass, no further comment. Bertram Kyle, who recorded everything, later wrote: "In twenty-two the state man came into the office... and he came in to say so and to tell the manager to order new. Which we did, that same week... There was no more said of it in the office than there would have been of a broken window."

The condemned crate was placed in the loft above the intake room, under a tarpaulin and chaff. It lay there for thirty-five years. It contained nine surviving pipettes out of the original five dozen; the rest had been broken by people climbing the ladder for wire and tools over decades.

When the state inspector condemned defective measuring glass in 1923, he saw only the present problem (broken glass, must be replaced). He did not—and could not, without laboratory analysis—recognize that the same glass had been producing systematic false readings for twenty-six years.

## Story 7: The 1925 Arbitration, Dismissal, and Injustice

In January 1925, the Ostrey Hollow patrons filed a formal petition. The persistent shortfall had cost them money for nearly thirty years. The Association established a board of arbitration, chaired by attorney Alonzo Frick of Ashlin. Three sittings followed: one at the Association office in Ashlin, two at the station itself in the weigh room.

Bertram Kyle prepared four books for the board: the station's test book (Ansel Keddie's own records in his hand), the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book, and Merle Strawn's 1923 inspection notebook.

The board heard three competing explanations: (1) the patrons were adding water, (2) the tester was stealing cream, (3) the wet hay was thinning the milk. Alonzo Frick later recalled: "Three sittings of it altogether... the board of arbitration... heard the patrons and the officers, found no cause established, and awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons three thousand eight hundred dollars, without admission."

On April 30, 1925—four days before the board announced its award—Ansel Keddie was dismissed. He came up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm, hung it behind the shed door, and asked Orra what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it.

The award was $3,800 "without admission," legal language meaning the Association paid without conceding guilt or cause. Ansel received nothing. Frick observed: "The finding found nothing and the dismissal said everything."

Ansel Keddie lived nine more years and died in February 1934 with the imputation on him. When he died, men who had known him shook Orra's hand and spoke of his carpentry skills—but not one spoke of the other thing. Orra recorded: "He said to me once and only once, and not lately, that a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."

## Story 8: The 1958 Discovery and Scientific Vindication

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie—Ansel Keddie's granddaughter, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College—went up a ladder into the loft above the station's intake room. Under dust and chaff, she found the crate of condemned glassware, undisturbed since 1923, thirty-five years earlier.

She carried nine surviving pipettes to the college laboratory. On March 9, 1958, she measured them against a calibrated burette.

The results were absolute. Every pipette delivered materially less than 17.6 c.c. The average was 16.27 c.c.—a shortfall of 1.33 c.c. per pipette, or 7.56% low.

A short charge of milk in a test bottle marked for full capacity yields a butterfat reading proportionally short. The Ostrey Hollow shortfall of 19/100ths matched exactly what a seven-percent measurement error would produce.

Ivy Keddie and Professor Leland Vaught of the college jointly wrote Bulletin 471 (October 1958). They explained the glassware defect and calculated the patrons' loss: approximately 67,000 pounds of butterfat unpaid, worth roughly $19,600 in the money of the era.

The Ashlin Chronicle published the story on October 3, 1958. Ivy's statement: "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not. He tested the milk in front of the state's own man and he tested it the way he was taught, and the reason his figures would not come right was in a crate over his head the entire time. I would like it printed that he never knew."

Ansel Keddie had died twenty-four years earlier in 1934. His widow, Orra, had died in 1948. Neither lived to see his name cleared. The vindication was unambiguous but late beyond the reach of justice in any ordinary sense.

# Section B: Relationships

---

## B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Duncan Keddie (Ansel's son, Ivy's father) wrote: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise and once heard it said aloud in this town by a man who ought to have known." The Ashlin Chronicle initially called her Ansel's "great-niece" but published a correction: "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." 

*Resolution method:* Contemporary documentary correction by the original source outranks initial misstatement in the same document.

---

## B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. Duncan Keddie clarifies: "Roy's daughter is Hazel Brant. My daughter is Ivy. Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece." The intervening generations: Emmet Brant and Orra Brant (later Orra Keddie) were brother and sister. Emmet's son was Roy Brant (Hazel's father). Orra married Ansel Keddie, and their son was Duncan Keddie (Ivy's father). Thus Hazel and Ivy are children of first cousins (Roy and Duncan), making them second cousins.

Duncan also notes: "Ivy Keddie and I are second cousins. People are forever telling me she is my niece or my cousin once removed or some such, and she is not. Second cousins. It is not complicated; it is only that nobody wants to count."

*Resolution method:* Explicit genealogical statement by a person in a position to know (Duncan, who taught arithmetic and kept family records).

---

## B3. Selby Vose's father and why it matters

Selby Vose was the son of Gideon Vose, who organized the Association and was its first president. Duncan Keddie states: "Gideon organized the Association and was its first president and wrote its by-laws; Selby was his son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew." Bertram Kyle confirms: "Mr. Selby Vose became manager in nineteen eleven. He was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president—the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense."

Why it matters: Selby Vose, as manager from 1911 onward, signed the ledgers every year displaying the "station gain" line (the constant 5.25% difference between credited and received butterfat). Bertram Kyle observed: "He read the totals and he never read the lines above them. He was not a man who read. He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign. I watched him do it a thousand times." The station gain was hidden in plain sight with a name ("as usual") on it. A manager who reads only the bottom line for signature perpetuates the invisibility of systematic error, not through fraud but through habitual inattention. Vose's indifference to the lines above the total enabled the persistent unexplained entry to pass unchallenged for years.

*Resolution method:* Multiple independent corroborating sources (Duncan Keddie and Bertram Kyle) and observation of documented ledger-signing behavior by Kyle.

---

## B4. Relationship between Vose and Keddie families

Duncan Keddie emphatically states: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either... My mother would have told me. She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it."

However, Cleve Oram (cream hauler's son) claims: "I will say this while I am on families... The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley. My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper."

*Resolution:* Cannot be determined from the sources with certainty. Duncan's explicit denial (backed by his mother's detailed family knowledge) outranks Cleve's hearsay and admitted inability to specify the connection. However, Cleve's statement stands unchallenged in its time, and the sources do not provide conclusive disproof. The safest answer: the sources do not establish a documented family relationship between the Voses and Keddies, and Duncan's denial suggests none existed.

*Resolution method:* Contemporary family knowledge (Orra Keddie's genealogical expertise, reported by Duncan) outranks recalled hearsay acknowledged as vague.
