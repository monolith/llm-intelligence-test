# Answers to Test Questions — Part 1
## Section A: Reconstruction (Stories 1–4)

---

## Story 1: Foundation and Opening of Ostrey Hollow Station (1889–1897)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was organized in 1889 by farmers meeting at Larrow Green and built its main creamery there in 1891. Its by-laws (Article VII) mandated payment by butterfat test at the delivery station, not by milk weight. This single rule made all that followed a matter of money.

In winter 1895–1896, the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley, to serve six farms on Hessel Bottom. The station opened May 4, 1896 (Orin Falke's report), with Ansel Keddie as tester and separator man from the beginning. Its function was simple: weigh incoming milk, test it for butterfat, separate the cream for hauling to Larrow Green, return skim to farmers.

The station's testing outfit—six dozen pipettes marked 17.6 c.c., twelve dozen test bottles, one acid measure, one four-bottle hand tester—was ordered from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet on April 14, 1897. The invoice totaled $77.93 (freight included). Orra Keddie's diary records May 1, 1897: "A. says the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green." Hazel Brant notes her grandfather Emmet Brant began his private weigh book January 1, 1900, recording weights before and after station testing—the beginning of a record that would prove crucial forty years later.

This infrastructure embodied one critical decision: payment by test. Under payment by weight, nothing that followed would have cost anyone a dollar. Under payment by test, a faulty measure became the engine of loss.

---

## Story 2: The Pattern of Low Tests Emerges (1902–1908)

From the station's opening, milk tested there ran low. Hazel Brant captures the relentless pattern: "Nineteen hundredths. Every month, every season, wet years and dry: nineteen hundredths lower at our station than the same milk was anywhere else on earth. Not a little in the winter and none in the summer, the way a feed thing goes. Nineteen hundredths, flat as a floor."

The station's average over the complaint period was 3.61 percent butterfat; Larrow Green's baseline was 3.78. The shortfall was consistent to the hundredth. Orin Falke's official 1959 report confirms: "The deficiency... was nineteen hundredths of a point... and it ran at the same nineteen hundredths in every month of the year, in the flush of June and in the depth of February alike."

The first formal complaint came in 1902, then recurred "in one form or another, in every decade thereafter" (Falke). Orra Keddie's diary for March 1902 records: "Talk at the store that our milk up here runs poorer than theirs down the valley. A. came home and would not eat."

But evidence of milk quality came from outside the valley. Rosalie Cudd of the Alder Corners cheese factory across the ridge tested the same four Hessel Bottom farmers' milk when they hauled cream there in summer, getting 3.80—normal. This proved the milk itself was good. Yet at Ostrey Hollow, year-round, the tests stayed at 3.61. The consistency of the error—identical in magnitude regardless of season, herd, or condition—suggested something systematic, not variable. Not theft, not fraud, not feed—something unchanging and mathematical.

---

## Story 3: The 1908 Committee Investigation and the Wet Hay Explanation

In response to sustained complaint, the Association appointed a committee chaired by Nyle Grigg. The committee conducted a thorough 1908 investigation, sampling Hessel Bottom herds monthly through January, February, and March, drawing their own samples and witnessing tests.

Their conclusion: the herds were sound, the milk whole, the scales honest. Dismissing fraud, they blamed the wet meadow itself. Grigg wrote: "It is our opinion, formed on the ground and not out of a book, that what such land gives in quantity it withholds in quality, and that a cow cannot put into her pail what her keep has never put into her." Hessel Bottom was wet ground, prone to water saturation, growing sedge and coarse sour grasses instead of sweet hay. Such herbage was mineral-poor.

The question closed for eleven years. In 1919, Grigg reprinted the report with a covering letter noting that the State Agricultural College's Bulletin No. 214 had since conducted a feeding trial on bog hay and found butterfat depressed by "two tenths"—close to the observed shortfall.

But Grigg had not examined Bulletin 214 closely. When Orin Falke finally obtained the full bulletin in 1959, it stated: "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Five hundredths could not account for nineteen hundredths, particularly in July when cows were on pasture and no hay effect should exist. Falke withdrew his earlier concession: "I withdraw the concession made on page four."

The committee was not simply wrong. Wet hay may have had some effect. But wet hay could not explain the full, persistent, invariant nineteen-hundredths shortfall observed in every season and every year.

## Story 4: The 1911 Glass Breakage Experiment (May–July 1911)

On May 8, 1911, the station's measuring glass broke. Orra Keddie's diary: "A. home cross about a broken measure. Rain all day, the low piece under water again." The station borrowed a measure from elsewhere—likely Larrow Green—and used it for approximately nine weeks until July 10.

What happened next was decisive. During those nine weeks, the station's butterfat tests stood markedly higher than before and after. The herds were the same, the tester was the same, the season was the same (early summer flush), the machinery unchanged. Only the measuring glass differed.

When the station resumed its own glass after July 10, the test figures fell back to their customary 3.61 level.

Orin Falke's 1959 report: "Through those nine weeks the station's tests stood markedly higher. On the day the station resumed its own glass they fell back to their former level. The tester noted the fact in his book and set a question mark against it. The herds, the pasture, the season, the men and the machinery were the same throughout."

Ansel Keddie recorded this anomaly with a question mark—his way of noting something he could not explain. Duncan Keddie recalled his father saying in 1911: "It is something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book." But Keddie did mark it, with a question mark.

This interval acts as a controlled experiment. It proves the measuring glass was the limiting factor. It rules out watering (which would persist regardless of glass). It rules out tester theft (which would be unaffected by glass change). It establishes that the glass was implicated in the shortfall. It could not prove the glass caused the full nineteen hundredths, but it proved the glass was central.

The 1925 arbitration board apparently never learned of this crucial experiment.

## Story 5: The 1919 Water-Warning Letter and Family Estrangement

In June 1919, Selby Vose (the Association's manager) circulated a warning letter to Ostrey Hollow patrons—nine families on six farms—cautiously but unmistakably accusing them of adding water to milk. Ansel Keddie received his copy and made an unusual choice: he did not mail the letters or leave them on the platform. Instead, he personally delivered each letter to all nine families, standing at each door while each man read it. He came home late with his hat still on, visibly shaken.

Two days later, Orra's brother Emmet Brant came up to the house. He did not enter. Standing in the yard, he confronted Ansel: "You have hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and you know it better than any man living. Then you ask him why he carried the paper himself if he did not believe it." Ansel replied: "I carried it because I would not have another man carry it." Emmet left and did not return for ten years.

Orra's diary: "He has not been in this house since, and he is my brother." The estrangement lasted until Emmet's death in 1929. Orra wrote then: "Ten years and neither of them would go up the road first, and now it is settled the way those things get settled."

This story captures the moral trap Ansel occupied. He was convinced the milk was not watered, but he could not prove it without naming what he suspected—the glass. His personal delivery was an act of honor: he would shoulder the burden of the message himself rather than let another carry an accusation against neighbors he worked with daily. But honor could not bridge the rift between brothers-in-law. Emmet interpreted the personal delivery as Ansel's private admission that the accusation was just—why else take the letters house to house? The misreading destroyed a family relationship that had endured since Ansel and Orra's marriage in 1889.

---

## Story 6: The 1923 State Inspection and Condemnation of Glass (June–July 1923)

In June 1923, Merle Strawn—the state dairy inspector for the Ordell circuit and kin to no one in the valley—made a routine visit to Ostrey Hollow. Following standard procedure, he ran the station's milk pipettes against a calibrated burette. They would not deliver their marked 17.6 c.c. He condemned the entire lot on the spot and ordered new glass.

The Association ordered new pipettes from Ammon & Sons (per Bertram Kyle's office records). New glass came and went into use by July 1923.

The change was immediate and unambiguous. From 1924 onward, the station's average test stood at 3.85—a jump that matched the valley baseline and vindicated the shortfall's mechanical cause.

Yet no one made the connection. Strawn drew no inference about the years behind the defect. The Association's office recorded the event matter-of-factly: ordered new glass, no further comment. Bertram Kyle, who recorded everything, later wrote: "In twenty-two the state man came into the office... and he came in to say so and to tell the manager to order new. Which we did, that same week... There was no more said of it in the office than there would have been of a broken window."

The condemned crate was placed in the loft above the intake room, under a tarpaulin and chaff. It lay there for thirty-five years. It contained nine surviving pipettes out of the original five dozen; the rest had been broken by people climbing the ladder for wire and tools over decades.

When the state inspector condemned defective measuring glass in 1923, he saw only the present problem (broken glass, must be replaced). He did not—and could not, without laboratory analysis—recognize that the same glass had been producing systematic false readings for twenty-six years.

---

## Story 7: The 1925 Arbitration, Dismissal, and Injustice

In January 1925, the Ostrey Hollow patrons filed a formal petition. The persistent shortfall had cost them money for nearly thirty years. The Association established a board of arbitration, chaired by attorney Alonzo Frick of Ashlin. Three sittings followed: one at the Association office in Ashlin, two at the station itself in the weigh room.

Bertram Kyle prepared four books for the board: the station's test book (Ansel Keddie's own records in his hand), the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book, and Merle Strawn's 1923 inspection notebook.

The board heard three competing explanations: (1) the patrons were adding water, (2) the tester was stealing cream, (3) the wet hay was thinning the milk. Alonzo Frick later recalled: "Three sittings of it altogether... the board of arbitration... heard the patrons and the officers, found no cause established, and awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons three thousand eight hundred dollars, without admission."

On April 30, 1925—four days before the board announced its award—Ansel Keddie was dismissed. He came up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm, hung it behind the shed door, and asked Orra what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it.

The award was $3,800 "without admission," legal language meaning the Association paid without conceding guilt or cause. Ansel received nothing. Frick observed: "The finding found nothing and the dismissal said everything."

Ansel Keddie lived nine more years and died in February 1934 with the imputation on him. When he died, men who had known him shook Orra's hand and spoke of his carpentry skills—but not one spoke of the other thing. Orra recorded: "He said to me once and only once, and not lately, that a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."

---

## Story 8: The 1958 Discovery and Scientific Vindication

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie—Ansel Keddie's granddaughter, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College—went up a ladder into the loft above the station's intake room. Under dust and chaff, she found the crate of condemned glassware, undisturbed since 1923, thirty-five years earlier.

She carried nine surviving pipettes to the college laboratory. On March 9, 1958, she measured them against a calibrated burette.

The results were absolute. Every pipette delivered materially less than 17.6 c.c. The average was 16.27 c.c.—a shortfall of 1.33 c.c. per pipette, or 7.56% low.

A short charge of milk in a test bottle marked for full capacity yields a butterfat reading proportionally short. The Ostrey Hollow shortfall of 19/100ths matched exactly what a seven-percent measurement error would produce.

Ivy Keddie and Professor Leland Vaught of the college jointly wrote Bulletin 471 (October 1958). They explained the glassware defect and calculated the patrons' loss: approximately 67,000 pounds of butterfat unpaid, worth roughly $19,600 in the money of the era.

The Ashlin Chronicle published the story on October 3, 1958. Ivy's statement: "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not. He tested the milk in front of the state's own man and he tested it the way he was taught, and the reason his figures would not come right was in a crate over his head the entire time. I would like it printed that he never knew."

Ansel Keddie had died twenty-four years earlier in 1934. His widow, Orra, had died in 1948. Neither lived to see his name cleared. The vindication was unambiguous but late beyond the reach of justice in any ordinary sense.

## B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Duncan Keddie (Ansel's son, Ivy's father) wrote: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise and once heard it said aloud in this town by a man who ought to have known." The Ashlin Chronicle initially called her Ansel's "great-niece" but published a correction: "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." 

*Resolution method:* Contemporary documentary correction by the original source outranks initial misstatement in the same document.

---

## B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. Duncan Keddie clarifies: "Roy's daughter is Hazel Brant. My daughter is Ivy. Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece." The intervening generations: Emmet Brant and Orra Brant (later Orra Keddie) were brother and sister. Emmet's son was Roy Brant (Hazel's father). Orra married Ansel Keddie, and their son was Duncan Keddie (Ivy's father). Thus Hazel and Ivy are children of first cousins (Roy and Duncan), making them second cousins.

Duncan also notes: "Ivy Keddie and I are second cousins. People are forever telling me she is my niece or my cousin once removed or some such, and she is not. Second cousins. It is not complicated; it is only that nobody wants to count."

*Resolution method:* Explicit genealogical statement by a person in a position to know (Duncan, who taught arithmetic and kept family records).

---

## B3. Selby Vose's father and why it matters

Selby Vose was the son of Gideon Vose, who organized the Association and was its first president. Duncan Keddie states: "Gideon organized the Association and was its first president and wrote its by-laws; Selby was his son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew." Bertram Kyle confirms: "Mr. Selby Vose became manager in nineteen eleven. He was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president—the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense."

Why it matters: Selby Vose, as manager from 1911 onward, signed the ledgers every year displaying the "station gain" line (the constant 5.25% difference between credited and received butterfat). Bertram Kyle observed: "He read the totals and he never read the lines above them. He was not a man who read. He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign. I watched him do it a thousand times." The station gain was hidden in plain sight with a name ("as usual") on it. A manager who reads only the bottom line for signature perpetuates the invisibility of systematic error, not through fraud but through habitual inattention. Vose's indifference to the lines above the total enabled the persistent unexplained entry to pass unchallenged for years.

*Resolution method:* Multiple independent corroborating sources (Duncan Keddie and Bertram Kyle) and observation of documented ledger-signing behavior by Kyle.

---

## B4. Relationship between Vose and Keddie families

Duncan Keddie emphatically states: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either... My mother would have told me. She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it."

However, Cleve Oram (cream hauler's son) claims: "I will say this while I am on families... The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley. My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper."

*Resolution:* Cannot be determined from the sources with certainty. Duncan's explicit denial (backed by his mother's detailed family knowledge) outranks Cleve's hearsay and admitted inability to specify the connection. However, Cleve's statement stands unchallenged in its time, and the sources do not provide conclusive disproof. The safest answer: the sources do not establish a documented family relationship between the Voses and Keddies, and Duncan's denial suggests none existed.

*Resolution method:* Contemporary family knowledge (Orra Keddie's genealogical expertise, reported by Duncan) outranks recalled hearsay acknowledged as vague.

# Section C: Math

## C1. Proportion of glassware shortfall and butterfat loss calculations

**(a) By what proportion did the Ostrey Hollow station's tests differ from the truth?**

The faulty pipettes delivered 16.27 c.c. on average (measured by Ivy Keddie, March 1958) instead of the marked 17.6 c.c.

Shortfall per pipette: 17.6 − 16.27 = 1.33 c.c.
Proportion short: 1.33 / 17.6 = 0.0756 = **7.56% low**
Or: 16.27 / 17.6 = 0.9244 = **92.44% of true value**

**(b) True average test at Ostrey Hollow and recorded shortfall:**

Using the recorded Ostrey Hollow average (3.61) and applying the 7.56% proportional correction:
- Recorded average: 3.61
- True average: 3.61 / 0.9244 = **3.906** (approximately)
- Recorded short by: 3.906 − 3.61 = **0.296 points** (approximately 0.30 or 30/100ths)

Alternatively, using the known 19/100ths shortfall against the baseline 3.78 (Larrow Green):
- If true average was 3.78 (matching the valley) and recorded was 3.61, the deficit was exactly 0.19 points, consistent with sources.

**(c) Total pounds of butterfat not credited:**

Milk received at station over affected period: 40 million pounds (per Bertram Kyle)
Period of faulty glass: May 1897 to June 1923 (approximately 26 years, 25 full delivery years)
Average recorded test: 3.61%

Using 7.56% proportional undercount:
- Recorded butterfat: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,440,000 pounds
- True butterfat: 1,440,000 / 0.9244 = 1,557,500 pounds (approximately)
- Unpaid: 1,557,500 − 1,440,000 = **117,500 pounds**

However, Ivy Keddie's bulletin (cited by the Ashlin Chronicle) calculated approximately **67,000 pounds** over the affected years. This figure may use a different baseline or account for seasonal variation. The sources cite **67,000 pounds** as the official calculation.

**(d) Monetary value and fraction received:**

Value of 67,000 pounds at ~$0.25–0.27 per pound (era prices): approximately **$16,750–18,090**
Orin Falke's 1959 report states: **$19,600** (total unpaid value, per Ivy's bulletin calculation)
Patrons received in 1925 award: **$3,800**
Fraction received: $3,800 / $19,600 = **0.194 or approximately 19.4%** (roughly one-fifth of what was owed)

## C2. Largest patron's loss in 1910 (Emmet Brant)

**(a) Butterfat credited:**

Hazel Brant (Roy's daughter, Emmet's granddaughter, keeper of his weigh book): "Nineteen ten. Two hundred thousand pounds even, delivered to the station... paid on a test of three forty-two."

- Unpaid: 7,398 − 6,840 = **558 pounds**

**(c) Value of unpaid butterfat in 1910:**

At 25 cents per pound (per Bertram Kyle's estimate): 558 × $0.25 = **$139.50**

## C3. Total credited vs. truly received butterfat

**(a) Total butterfat credited to patrons:**

40 million pounds received at 3.61% recorded average:
40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,440,000 pounds credited**

**(b) Butterfat actually in the milk:**

Applying 7.56% undercount (glassware delivered 92.44% of true measure):
1,440,000 / 0.9244 = **1,557,500 pounds true butterfat** (approximately)

**(c) Difference as a percentage—be exact about what you are taking percentage of:**

Difference: 1,557,500 − 1,440,000 = 117,500 pounds

As a percentage of **true butterfat received** (the denominator that matters for patron loss):
117,500 / 1,557,500 = **7.54%** (the patrons were short-credited by 7.54% of the butterfat they actually delivered)

As a percentage of **credited amount** (from the perspective of the ledger):
117,500 / 1,440,000 = **8.16%** (the account showed 8.16% less than the truth)

*Most precise answer: The patrons were not credited with 7.54% of the butterfat they actually delivered.*

# Section D: Logic

---

## D1. The 1911 glass breakage as a controlled experiment

**When and duration:**
- May 8, 1911 (station's glass broke)
- June–July 1911 (borrowed glass in use for 9 weeks)
- July 10, 1911 (original glass resumed)

**The variable:** The measuring glass (station's own, presumably faulty, vs. borrowed from elsewhere, presumed functional)

**Held constant:** Herds, season (early summer flush), tester (Ansel Keddie), machinery, handling, pasturelands, farmer practices

**Result:** Tests stood markedly higher during the 9-week borrowed-glass interval. Upon resumption of the original glass, tests fell back to their former low level (3.61 average).

**What it establishes:**
1. The measuring glass was the limiting factor determining test results
2. It rules out watering of milk by farmers (which would persist regardless of glass change)
3. It rules out theft of cream by the tester (who made tests under identical conditions with both glasses)
4. It strongly implicates the original glass as measuring short of its capacity

**What it does NOT establish:**
1. The exact magnitude of the glass shortfall
2. That the glass was responsible for the *entire* 19/100ths shortfall observed year-round (the 9-week interval occurred in early summer when cows are on pasture; winter effects might differ)
3. The cause of the glass defect (manufacturing error, mold issues, etc.)

The tester (Ansel Keddie) noted this anomaly with a question mark in his test book—his way of flagging something unexplained—but never dared state his suspicion explicitly. Duncan Keddie recalled his father saying in 1911: "It is something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book." Yet Keddie did record it, with that question mark, the only notation like it in decades of otherwise routine entries.

---

## D2. Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream

**Reason 1: The 1911 glass replacement experiment**
When the station's original glass broke and was replaced with a borrowed measure for 9 weeks, test figures improved immediately without any change in the tester's access, procedures, or opportunity to steal. If Keddie were stealing cream and disguising it with false low tests, the borrowed glass would not affect his ability to steal (cream availability unchanged) or his testing control (same tester, same methods). Yet the improvement was automatic upon glass change. This reason requires no post-1958 knowledge; the 1925 board should have recognized it if they had examined the 1911 notation.

**Reason 2: Alder Corners external testing**
Four Hessel Bottom farmers hauled their milk to the Alder Corners cheese factory across the ridge in summer. Rosalie Cudd's factory tested that same milk at 3.80%—normal. Keddie had no control over milk hauled away from his station; he could only steal from milk delivered to Ostrey Hollow. The consistent 3.80 result at Alder Corners (across 16 summers) proves the milk itself was good and the deficiency was not in the milk or the farmers' practices, but at Ostrey Hollow in Keddie's testing domain. Yet Keddie could not steal from milk that never reached his station.

**Reason 3: Roy Brant's private weigh book**
Roy Brant recorded his milk weights before delivery to the station in his own ledger, examined by multiple committees (the 1908 investigation, the 1925 arbitration). If Keddie were stealing cream post-weighing, the milk weight would be unchanged but the recorded butterfat low. The consistency of Roy's records proves no milk went missing at hand-off. Keddie's opportunity to steal was bounded by the milk already weighed in; the records show no weight loss.

**Why these are independent:**
- Reason 1 tests Keddie's *method* (testing control) using the glass experiment
- Reason 2 tests Keddie's *domain* (station control) using external verification
- Reason 3 tests Keddie's *opportunity* (access to cream) using documentary evidence of weight

Any one, independently, refutes theft. Together they form a logical cage.

**Did the bookkeeper question the ledger entries?**
No. Wilbur Teague, the Association's bookkeeper, wrote the "station gain" line every year (the 5.25% difference). Bertram Kyle recalled: "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years. That is not a criticism. It was his notion of the position: the figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people, and if the other people did not ask it was not his affair." Kyle himself notes: "I copied that line, or the like of it, more times than I can count, and I will tell the Society plainly that it never once crossed my mind to wonder at it." The ledger entry, named "as usual," was treated as settled fact, not a mystery to investigate.

---

## D3. Did the manager know the station's tests ran low before 1923?

**Answer:** The sources cannot definitively establish prior knowledge, though circumstantial evidence suggests he should have known.

**Justification:**
Selby Vose became manager in 1911 (per Bertram Kyle). By that date, the Ostrey Hollow shortfall had been publicly complained about continuously since 1902. The 1908 committee had issued its report. The valley knew the station tested low.

Vose signed the ledgers every year with the "station gain" line (5.25% constant difference). Kyle observed: "He read the totals and he never read the lines above them. He was not a man who read." Vose's signature appears on the bottom of each sheet, unreflectively given.

In June 1919, Vose signed and circulated the water-warning letter to Hollow patrons, implicitly acknowledging he believed something was amiss. This is the only direct evidence of his awareness that the station had a problem.

However, there is no statement in the sources where Vose explicitly says "I knew the tests were running low" or "I suspected the glass." His willful inattention to the lines above the totals suggests he may have been defensively ignorant rather than knowingly complicit.

**Conclusion:** Vose should have known (by 1919 at the latest, from his own warning letter). But the sources do not establish what he knew or what he suspected. His management style—signing without reading—allowed him to avoid explicit confrontation with the persistent anomaly.

# Section F: Theory

---

## F1. Causal theory and competing explanations

**The actual cause:**
The Ostrey Hollow shortfall was caused by faulty milk test pipettes that delivered an average of 16.27 c.c. per pipette instead of the marked 17.6 c.c. This proportional shortfall (92.44% of correct capacity) translated directly into butterfat test readings that were proportionally short of the true value.

These pipettes came into use on May 1, 1897, when the station's testing outfit was delivered from Ammon & Sons. They were faulty from manufacture (likely due to mold error or gauge error at the glass works). No event at Ostrey Hollow or on Hessel Bottom farms had to change for the shortfall to appear—it was inherent in the glass itself and manifested immediately.

The shortfall persisted unchanged through 26 years because the same faulty pipettes remained in continuous use. The perfect consistency of the error—exactly 19/100ths lower, month after month, season after season, year after year—was not coincidence but mathematical consequence of a fixed proportional measurement error.

The shortfall ended in July 1923 when new glass, measuring true to capacity, was put into use following Merle Strawn's condemnation in June. The station's average test immediately rose to 3.85, matching the valley baseline.

**Why this size of error:** 1.33 c.c. per pipette, or 7.56% short per measurement. Applied to milk with ~3.78% butterfat (the valley norm), this proportion yields a test reading ~7.56% low, matching the observed ~5% visible shortfall (scaled by actual milk composition).

**Why it began when it did:** May 1, 1897, when the faulty glass was installed. No other date was causal.

**Why it ended when it did:** June-July 1923, when faulty glass was removed and new glass installed. No change in farm practices, herd quality, or testing procedure was required—only substitution of the physical glass.

**Why nothing at the station had to change:** A faulty measure is passive. It measures short regardless of the tester's diligence, honesty, or care. Ansel Keddie's procedures, skill, and integrity were constant. The glass alone was the variable.

---

**The three competing explanations and the evidence defeating each:**

**1. Water addition (Selby Vose, manager, June 1919 letter)**

Allegation: Ostrey Hollow farmers were adding water to milk to increase volume and circumvent the butterfat-based payment system (diluting the test).

**Defeating evidence:**
- Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, tested the milk with a lactometer in regular circuit visits from 1908 onward. His notebooks show no evidence of added water in any year (per Orin Falke's 1959 examination). A lactometer detects water addition reliably.
- The 1911 glass replacement experiment: when a borrowed (presumably true-measuring) glass was used for 9 weeks, tests improved without any change in the milk, farmers' practices, or the tester. If water were being added, it would persist regardless of the measuring glass change. Yet the tests improved, proving the milk was not watered.
- Rosalie Cudd's external cheese factory tested the same milk at 3.80 when hauled there in summer. She noted: "Watered milk would be immediately detectable in cheese production." She found no evidence of it. The milk delivered to Alder Corners was the same milk; if watered at origin, it would show at both factories.

**2. Cream theft by Ansel Keddie (advanced by an Association officer at the 1925 hearing)**

Allegation: Keddie was stealing cream after separation and writing false low tests to conceal the loss.

**Defeating evidence:**
- The 1911 glass experiment directly refutes this. Keddie had identical opportunity to steal in both intervals (same cream pool, same access). Yet the test results changed only when the glass changed, not when anything about Keddie's circumstances changed. Cream theft would be unaffected by glass change; the theft would simply shift from one interval to the next, not vanish during the borrowed-glass period.
- Roy Brant's private weigh book (examined by multiple committees) recorded milk weights before delivery to the station and at intake. No weight loss is documented. If cream were being stolen post-weigh, the weights would match (theft after weighing) but the butterfat recorded would be low. The consistent weights prove no milk went missing at hand-off.
- Keddie's social position: married to Orra Brant, sister of Emmet Brant (the largest patron and thus most harmed by theft). Keddie ate at Emmet's table, argued about drainage with him for decades, lived embedded in the community. The assumption that such a man would systematically steal for 26 years without detection—from neighbors, relatives, and friends—is implausible. His later work as a carpenter earned him the respect of the valley for his reliable craftsmanship, suggesting a character consistent with the earlier reputation.

**3. Poor feed from wet meadow (advanced by Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee)**

Allegation: Hessel Bottom's wet meadow produced mineral-poor hay that reduced butterfat in milk from cows fed on it.

**Defeating evidence:**
- The State Agricultural College's Bulletin No. 214 (the committee's own later evidence) shows bog hay depresses butterfat by "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Five hundredths cannot account for 19/100ths, particularly in July when the recorded shortfall is identical (19/100ths) but hay has no effect (cows on pasture). The full seasonal constancy of the shortfall rules out a seasonal feed effect.
- Rosalie Cudd's testing of the same milk (hauled to Alder Corners in summer) at 3.80%—normal—proves the milk's butterfat was normal when tested on proper equipment. If the wet hay depressed the milk's butterfat chemically, the depression would appear at any factory. Yet it did not. The deficiency was unique to the Ostrey Hollow station's tests.
- The 1908 committee was not simply wrong; wet meadow hay may have had some effect. But they attributed the entire visible shortfall (19/100ths) to this cause when in fact the visible shortfall was caused by faulty glass, and any true butterfat difference from poor feed was much smaller or nonexistent.

---

**Did the 1925 board of arbitration ever examine the condemned glassware?**

No. Bertram Kyle prepared four books for the board: the test book, receiving book, Brant weigh book, and Strawn's notebook. The condemned glassware crate is not mentioned as submitted or examined. Cleve Oram notes: "The crate was up the ladder over their heads the entire time. Twenty feet, if that. I have thought about that since, I will not pretend I thought about it then." The board sat in the weigh room (intake room) directly below the loft but apparently never called for the crate or examined it.

---

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?**

Ansel Keddie believed the cause was faulty glassware. Duncan Keddie records: "He came home that summer of 1911 saying it is something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book. I was seventeen and I remember the kitchen and I remember that he said it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not his way." The 1911 glass breakage and substitution of a borrowed measure confirmed his suspicion: the tests improved with borrowed glass, fell back when his own was restored.

But Keddie had no way to prove his belief. He had no burette, no capacity to measure glass, no authority to demand testing. He was a tester, not a glass expert. To accuse the Association of providing faulty glass would have been a grave and unprovable accusation. So he recorded a question mark and spent the next fourteen years recording accurate tests from glass he privately doubted. When accused in 1925, he could not defend himself without naming the glass—something he had suspected for fourteen years but had no proof to offer.

# Section G: Summary

---

## G1. Eight-story history in at most 150 words

Between 1897 and 1923, the Ostrey Hollow skimming station in Ordell County consistently reported butterfat tests nineteen-hundredths of a point lower than the valley baseline, costing farmers approximately $19,600 in unpaid cream. Three competing explanations circulated: water adulteration, tester theft, and poor-quality hay. A 1925 arbitration awarded patrons $3,800 without finding cause and dismissed the tester, Ansel Keddie, who lived accused for thirty-three years. In 1958, his granddaughter Ivy Keddie discovered the station's original measuring pipettes in a loft—condemned as defective in 1923 but never examined for historical impact. Scientific measurement proved they delivered only 16.27 cubic centimeters per pipette instead of marked 17.6 c.c., causing proportional test errors. The shortfall was entirely mechanical, not fraudulent. Ivy's college bulletin cleared Keddie posthumously, twenty-four years after his death in 1934.
