# Section C: Math

---

## C1. Proportion of glassware shortfall and butterfat loss calculations

**(a) By what proportion did the Ostrey Hollow station's tests differ from the truth?**

The faulty pipettes delivered 16.27 c.c. on average (measured by Ivy Keddie, March 1958) instead of the marked 17.6 c.c.

Shortfall per pipette: 17.6 − 16.27 = 1.33 c.c.
Proportion short: 1.33 / 17.6 = 0.0756 = **7.56% low**
Or: 16.27 / 17.6 = 0.9244 = **92.44% of true value**

**(b) True average test at Ostrey Hollow and recorded shortfall:**

Using the recorded Ostrey Hollow average (3.61) and applying the 7.56% proportional correction:
- Recorded average: 3.61
- True average: 3.61 / 0.9244 = **3.906** (approximately)
- Recorded short by: 3.906 − 3.61 = **0.296 points** (approximately 0.30 or 30/100ths)

Alternatively, using the known 19/100ths shortfall against the baseline 3.78 (Larrow Green):
- If true average was 3.78 (matching the valley) and recorded was 3.61, the deficit was exactly 0.19 points, consistent with sources.

**(c) Total pounds of butterfat not credited:**

Milk received at station over affected period: 40 million pounds (per Bertram Kyle)
Period of faulty glass: May 1897 to June 1923 (approximately 26 years, 25 full delivery years)
Average recorded test: 3.61%

Using 7.56% proportional undercount:
- Recorded butterfat: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,440,000 pounds
- True butterfat: 1,440,000 / 0.9244 = 1,557,500 pounds (approximately)
- Unpaid: 1,557,500 − 1,440,000 = **117,500 pounds**

However, Ivy Keddie's bulletin (cited by the Ashlin Chronicle) calculated approximately **67,000 pounds** over the affected years. This figure may use a different baseline or account for seasonal variation. The sources cite **67,000 pounds** as the official calculation.

**(d) Monetary value and fraction received:**

Value of 67,000 pounds at ~$0.25–0.27 per pound (era prices): approximately **$16,750–18,090**
Orin Falke's 1959 report states: **$19,600** (total unpaid value, per Ivy's bulletin calculation)
Patrons received in 1925 award: **$3,800**
Fraction received: $3,800 / $19,600 = **0.194 or approximately 19.4%** (roughly one-fifth of what was owed)

## C2. Largest patron's loss in 1910 (Emmet Brant)

**(a) Butterfat credited:**

Hazel Brant (Roy's daughter, Emmet's granddaughter, keeper of his weigh book): "Nineteen ten. Two hundred thousand pounds even, delivered to the station... paid on a test of three forty-two."

Butterfat credited: 200,000 lbs × 0.0342 = **6,840 pounds**

**(b) Butterfat owed and not credited:**

Using 7.56% proportional correction (glassware was 92.44% of true capacity):
- True test: 3.42 / 0.9244 = 3.699
- True butterfat: 200,000 × 0.03699 = **7,398 pounds**
- Unpaid: 7,398 − 6,840 = **558 pounds**

**(c) Value of unpaid butterfat in 1910:**

At 25 cents per pound (per Bertram Kyle's estimate): 558 × $0.25 = **$139.50**

## C3. Total credited vs. truly received butterfat

**(a) Total butterfat credited to patrons:**

40 million pounds received at 3.61% recorded average:
40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,440,000 pounds credited**

**(b) Butterfat actually in the milk:**

Applying 7.56% undercount (glassware delivered 92.44% of true measure):
1,440,000 / 0.9244 = **1,557,500 pounds true butterfat** (approximately)

**(c) Difference as a percentage—be exact about what you are taking percentage of:**

Difference: 1,557,500 − 1,440,000 = 117,500 pounds

As a percentage of **true butterfat received** (the denominator that matters for patron loss):
117,500 / 1,557,500 = **7.54%** (the patrons were short-credited by 7.54% of the butterfat they actually delivered)

As a percentage of **credited amount** (from the perspective of the ledger):
117,500 / 1,440,000 = **8.16%** (the account showed 8.16% less than the truth)

*Most precise answer: The patrons were not credited with 7.54% of the butterfat they actually delivered.*

## C5. Duration of glass use and delivery years affected

**(a) Duration in use vs. full delivery years:**

Glass put into use: May 1, 1897
Glass condemned: June 1923
Actual duration: 26 years, 1 month

Full delivery years affected: 1898–1922 (inclusive) = **25 complete years**

**Why they differ:** The glass was installed partway through 1897 (May 1) and condemned partway through 1923 (June). The 26+ years of actual use encompasses only 25 complete calendar/delivery years. The accounting and loss calculations typically count only full years.

**(b) Pounds of milk in first season:**

Bertram Kyle (office clerk, who recorded everything): "That first season, before ever there was a station account, the milk was weighed at the Hollow and carried on the creamery's own books... People have asked me what the Hollow took in that first year and I have always said the same: a good eleven hundred thousand pounds, which is what I understood at the time from men who were there."

First season (May–December 1897, 8 months): approximately **1,100,000 pounds** (or "11 hundred thousand" in local parlance)

Later years ran closer to 1.6 million per year (from Kyle: "sixteen hundred thousand pounds a year, year in and year out").

# Section D: Logic

---

## D1. The 1911 glass breakage as a controlled experiment

**When and duration:**
- May 8, 1911 (station's glass broke)
- June–July 1911 (borrowed glass in use for 9 weeks)
- July 10, 1911 (original glass resumed)

**The variable:** The measuring glass (station's own, presumably faulty, vs. borrowed from elsewhere, presumed functional)

**Held constant:** Herds, season (early summer flush), tester (Ansel Keddie), machinery, handling, pasturelands, farmer practices

**Result:** Tests stood markedly higher during the 9-week borrowed-glass interval. Upon resumption of the original glass, tests fell back to their former low level (3.61 average).

**What it establishes:**
1. The measuring glass was the limiting factor determining test results
2. It rules out watering of milk by farmers (which would persist regardless of glass change)
3. It rules out theft of cream by the tester (who made tests under identical conditions with both glasses)
4. It strongly implicates the original glass as measuring short of its capacity

**What it does NOT establish:**
1. The exact magnitude of the glass shortfall
2. That the glass was responsible for the *entire* 19/100ths shortfall observed year-round (the 9-week interval occurred in early summer when cows are on pasture; winter effects might differ)
3. The cause of the glass defect (manufacturing error, mold issues, etc.)

The tester (Ansel Keddie) noted this anomaly with a question mark in his test book—his way of flagging something unexplained—but never dared state his suspicion explicitly. Duncan Keddie recalled his father saying in 1911: "It is something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book." Yet Keddie did record it, with that question mark, the only notation like it in decades of otherwise routine entries.

---

## D2. Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream

**Reason 1: The 1911 glass replacement experiment**
When the station's original glass broke and was replaced with a borrowed measure for 9 weeks, test figures improved immediately without any change in the tester's access, procedures, or opportunity to steal. If Keddie were stealing cream and disguising it with false low tests, the borrowed glass would not affect his ability to steal (cream availability unchanged) or his testing control (same tester, same methods). Yet the improvement was automatic upon glass change. This reason requires no post-1958 knowledge; the 1925 board should have recognized it if they had examined the 1911 notation.

**Reason 2: Alder Corners external testing**
Four Hessel Bottom farmers hauled their milk to the Alder Corners cheese factory across the ridge in summer. Rosalie Cudd's factory tested that same milk at 3.80%—normal. Keddie had no control over milk hauled away from his station; he could only steal from milk delivered to Ostrey Hollow. The consistent 3.80 result at Alder Corners (across 16 summers) proves the milk itself was good and the deficiency was not in the milk or the farmers' practices, but at Ostrey Hollow in Keddie's testing domain. Yet Keddie could not steal from milk that never reached his station.

**Reason 3: Roy Brant's private weigh book**
Roy Brant recorded his milk weights before delivery to the station in his own ledger, examined by multiple committees (the 1908 investigation, the 1925 arbitration). If Keddie were stealing cream post-weighing, the milk weight would be unchanged but the recorded butterfat low. The consistency of Roy's records proves no milk went missing at hand-off. Keddie's opportunity to steal was bounded by the milk already weighed in; the records show no weight loss.

**Why these are independent:**
- Reason 1 tests Keddie's *method* (testing control) using the glass experiment
- Reason 2 tests Keddie's *domain* (station control) using external verification
- Reason 3 tests Keddie's *opportunity* (access to cream) using documentary evidence of weight

Any one, independently, refutes theft. Together they form a logical cage.

**Did the bookkeeper question the ledger entries?**
No. Wilbur Teague, the Association's bookkeeper, wrote the "station gain" line every year (the 5.25% difference). Bertram Kyle recalled: "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years. That is not a criticism. It was his notion of the position: the figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people, and if the other people did not ask it was not his affair." Kyle himself notes: "I copied that line, or the like of it, more times than I can count, and I will tell the Society plainly that it never once crossed my mind to wonder at it." The ledger entry, named "as usual," was treated as settled fact, not a mystery to investigate.

## D3. Did the manager know the station's tests ran low before 1923?

**Answer:** The sources cannot definitively establish prior knowledge, though circumstantial evidence suggests he should have known.

**Justification:**
Selby Vose became manager in 1911 (per Bertram Kyle). By that date, the Ostrey Hollow shortfall had been publicly complained about continuously since 1902. The 1908 committee had issued its report. The valley knew the station tested low.

Vose signed the ledgers every year with the "station gain" line (5.25% constant difference). Kyle observed: "He read the totals and he never read the lines above them. He was not a man who read." Vose's signature appears on the bottom of each sheet, unreflectively given.

In June 1919, Vose signed and circulated the water-warning letter to Hollow patrons, implicitly acknowledging he believed something was amiss. This is the only direct evidence of his awareness that the station had a problem.

However, there is no statement in the sources where Vose explicitly says "I knew the tests were running low" or "I suspected the glass." His willful inattention to the lines above the totals suggests he may have been defensively ignorant rather than knowingly complicit.

**Conclusion:** Vose should have known (by 1919 at the latest, from his own warning letter). But the sources do not establish what he knew or what he suspected. His management style—signing without reading—allowed him to avoid explicit confrontation with the persistent anomaly.

# Section E: Contradictions

---

## E1. Direct factual contradictions

**1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie**
- The Ashlin Chronicle (initial): "great-niece"
- Duncan Keddie's notes: "granddaughter"
- **Resolution:** Granddaughter. The Chronicle published a correction.
- **Method:** Documentary correction by original source.

**2. Date when station's own glass arrived**
- Orra Keddie's diary (May 1, 1897): "A. says the station's own glass came up"
- Bertram Kyle's account: "May 1, 1898"
- Junia Ammon's invoice (April 14, 1897): delivery "by May 1, 1897"
- **Resolution:** May 1, 1897. Invoice and diary align. Kyle's "1898" is likely an error in his written recollection.
- **Method:** Documentary sources (invoice, contemporary diary) outrank summary account written decades later.

**3. Total invoice for testing outfit**
- Hazel Brant: "$77.39"
- Junia Ammon (invoice reproduction): "$77.93"
- **Resolution:** Cannot be definitively settled. Discrepancy is $0.54. Junia Ammon's primary reproduction ($77.93) should be more reliable, but Hazel may have seen the original. Minor discrepancy, does not affect analysis.
- **Method:** Primary source reproduction vs. quotation from memory.

**4. Number of families receiving 1919 water-warning letter**
- Orra Keddie's diary: "eleven families"
- Duncan Keddie's notes: "nine families, six farms"
- **Resolution:** Nine families on six farms (Ostrey Hollow proper). Duncan's statement is explicit: "the manager... sent that round to nine families on six farms." Orra may have counted broader recipients or misremembered the number after many years.
- **Method:** Explicit count by a person in a position to know (Duncan) outranks personal recollection by someone close but not centrally involved.

**5. Award amount from 1925 arbitration**
- Cleve Oram: "$3,500"
- Orin Falke and Bertram Kyle: "$3,800"
- **Resolution:** $3,800. Two independent sources (county agent and office clerk) agree; Oram's recollection, 52 years later, is likely misremembered.
- **Method:** Majority among credible contemporary-adjacent sources.

**6. Dismissal date of Ansel Keddie**
- Orin Falke: "April 30, 1925"
- Orra Keddie's diary: "April 30, 1925"
- **Resolution:** April 30, 1925. Both sources agree to the specific date.
- **Method:** Documentary agreement.

**No self-contradictions within a single retelling found.**

**No cases of two narrators carrying the same wrong value identified.**

# Section F: Theory

---

## F1. Causal theory and competing explanations

**The actual cause:**
The Ostrey Hollow shortfall was caused by faulty milk test pipettes that delivered an average of 16.27 c.c. per pipette instead of the marked 17.6 c.c. This proportional shortfall (92.44% of correct capacity) translated directly into butterfat test readings that were proportionally short of the true value.

These pipettes came into use on May 1, 1897, when the station's testing outfit was delivered from Ammon & Sons. They were faulty from manufacture (likely due to mold error or gauge error at the glass works). No event at Ostrey Hollow or on Hessel Bottom farms had to change for the shortfall to appear—it was inherent in the glass itself and manifested immediately.

The shortfall persisted unchanged through 26 years because the same faulty pipettes remained in continuous use. The perfect consistency of the error—exactly 19/100ths lower, month after month, season after season, year after year—was not coincidence but mathematical consequence of a fixed proportional measurement error.

The shortfall ended in July 1923 when new glass, measuring true to capacity, was put into use following Merle Strawn's condemnation in June. The station's average test immediately rose to 3.85, matching the valley baseline.

**Why this size of error:** 1.33 c.c. per pipette, or 7.56% short per measurement. Applied to milk with ~3.78% butterfat (the valley norm), this proportion yields a test reading ~7.56% low, matching the observed ~5% visible shortfall (scaled by actual milk composition).

**Why it began when it did:** May 1, 1897, when the faulty glass was installed. No other date was causal.

**Why it ended when it did:** June-July 1923, when faulty glass was removed and new glass installed. No change in farm practices, herd quality, or testing procedure was required—only substitution of the physical glass.

**Why nothing at the station had to change:** A faulty measure is passive. It measures short regardless of the tester's diligence, honesty, or care. Ansel Keddie's procedures, skill, and integrity were constant. The glass alone was the variable.

---

**The three competing explanations and the evidence defeating each:**

**1. Water addition (Selby Vose, manager, June 1919 letter)**

Allegation: Ostrey Hollow farmers were adding water to milk to increase volume and circumvent the butterfat-based payment system (diluting the test).

**Defeating evidence:**
- Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, tested the milk with a lactometer in regular circuit visits from 1908 onward. His notebooks show no evidence of added water in any year (per Orin Falke's 1959 examination). A lactometer detects water addition reliably.
- The 1911 glass replacement experiment: when a borrowed (presumably true-measuring) glass was used for 9 weeks, tests improved without any change in the milk, farmers' practices, or the tester. If water were being added, it would persist regardless of the measuring glass change. Yet the tests improved, proving the milk was not watered.
- Rosalie Cudd's external cheese factory tested the same milk at 3.80 when hauled there in summer. She noted: "Watered milk would be immediately detectable in cheese production." She found no evidence of it. The milk delivered to Alder Corners was the same milk; if watered at origin, it would show at both factories.

**2. Cream theft by Ansel Keddie (advanced by an Association officer at the 1925 hearing)**

Allegation: Keddie was stealing cream after separation and writing false low tests to conceal the loss.

**Defeating evidence:**
- The 1911 glass experiment directly refutes this. Keddie had identical opportunity to steal in both intervals (same cream pool, same access). Yet the test results changed only when the glass changed, not when anything about Keddie's circumstances changed. Cream theft would be unaffected by glass change; the theft would simply shift from one interval to the next, not vanish during the borrowed-glass period.
- Roy Brant's private weigh book (examined by multiple committees) recorded milk weights before delivery to the station and at intake. No weight loss is documented. If cream were being stolen post-weigh, the weights would match (theft after weighing) but the butterfat recorded would be low. The consistent weights prove no milk went missing at hand-off.
- Keddie's social position: married to Orra Brant, sister of Emmet Brant (the largest patron and thus most harmed by theft). Keddie ate at Emmet's table, argued about drainage with him for decades, lived embedded in the community. The assumption that such a man would systematically steal for 26 years without detection—from neighbors, relatives, and friends—is implausible. His later work as a carpenter earned him the respect of the valley for his reliable craftsmanship, suggesting a character consistent with the earlier reputation.

**3. Poor feed from wet meadow (advanced by Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee)**

Allegation: Hessel Bottom's wet meadow produced mineral-poor hay that reduced butterfat in milk from cows fed on it.

**Defeating evidence:**
- The State Agricultural College's Bulletin No. 214 (the committee's own later evidence) shows bog hay depresses butterfat by "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Five hundredths cannot account for 19/100ths, particularly in July when the recorded shortfall is identical (19/100ths) but hay has no effect (cows on pasture). The full seasonal constancy of the shortfall rules out a seasonal feed effect.
- Rosalie Cudd's testing of the same milk (hauled to Alder Corners in summer) at 3.80%—normal—proves the milk's butterfat was normal when tested on proper equipment. If the wet hay depressed the milk's butterfat chemically, the depression would appear at any factory. Yet it did not. The deficiency was unique to the Ostrey Hollow station's tests.
- The 1908 committee was not simply wrong; wet meadow hay may have had some effect. But they attributed the entire visible shortfall (19/100ths) to this cause when in fact the visible shortfall was caused by faulty glass, and any true butterfat difference from poor feed was much smaller or nonexistent.

---

**Did the 1925 board of arbitration ever examine the condemned glassware?**

No. Bertram Kyle prepared four books for the board: the test book, receiving book, Brant weigh book, and Strawn's notebook. The condemned glassware crate is not mentioned as submitted or examined. Cleve Oram notes: "The crate was up the ladder over their heads the entire time. Twenty feet, if that. I have thought about that since, I will not pretend I thought about it then." The board sat in the weigh room (intake room) directly below the loft but apparently never called for the crate or examined it.

---

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?**

Ansel Keddie believed the cause was faulty glassware. Duncan Keddie records: "He came home that summer of 1911 saying it is something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book. I was seventeen and I remember the kitchen and I remember that he said it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not his way." The 1911 glass breakage and substitution of a borrowed measure confirmed his suspicion: the tests improved with borrowed glass, fell back when his own was restored.

But Keddie had no way to prove his belief. He had no burette, no capacity to measure glass, no authority to demand testing. He was a tester, not a glass expert. To accuse the Association of providing faulty glass would have been a grave and unprovable accusation. So he recorded a question mark and spent the next fourteen years recording accurate tests from glass he privately doubted. When accused in 1925, he could not defend himself without naming the glass—something he had suspected for fourteen years but had no proof to offer.

# Section G: Summary

---

## G1. Eight-story history in at most 150 words

Between 1897 and 1923, the Ostrey Hollow skimming station in Ordell County consistently reported butterfat tests nineteen-hundredths of a point lower than the valley baseline, costing farmers approximately $19,600 in unpaid cream. Three competing explanations circulated: water adulteration, tester theft, and poor-quality hay. A 1925 arbitration awarded patrons $3,800 without finding cause and dismissed the tester, Ansel Keddie, who lived accused for thirty-three years. In 1958, his granddaughter Ivy Keddie discovered the station's original measuring pipettes in a loft—condemned as defective in 1923 but never examined for historical impact. Scientific measurement proved they delivered only 16.27 cubic centimeters per pipette instead of marked 17.6 c.c., causing proportional test errors. The shortfall was entirely mechanical, not fraudulent. Ivy's college bulletin cleared Keddie posthumously, twenty-four years after his death in 1934.
