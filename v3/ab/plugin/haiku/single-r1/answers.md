## Section A: Reconstruction (Stories 1-4)

## Story 1: The Opening and the Equipment (1895–1897)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, organized in 1889, built its skimming station at Ostrey Hollow in 1895–1896 to serve six farms on Hessel Bottom, a wet meadow along the creek. The station opened on May 4, 1896, with Ansel Keddie as tester and separator man.

In April 1897, the Association purchased its first testing outfit directly from Ammon & Sons in Tarnet: six dozen (72) milk test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c., twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, a four-bottle hand tester, and freight—total cost $77.93. The invoice (reproduced in Junia Ammon's firm history) was dated April 14, 1897. These pipettes came into use May 1, 1897. This equipment defined how all patrons would be paid: by butterfat test, not by weight of milk.

The glassware was defective from manufacture. Each pipette delivered 16.27 c.c. instead of its marked 17.6 c.c.—a shortfall of 1.33 c.c. or approximately 7.6%. This defect was not detected. The mold had likely been reground and the lot ran consistently short; Ammon's own historian (writing in 1949) acknowledged this was probable but undetected by the usual checks. No one at Ammon & Sons informed the customer.

The equipment went into service at Ostrey Hollow on May 1, 1897, and remained in use for the next twenty-six years. Under payment by test, this single piece of faulty glassware would determine the income of every farmer on Hessel Bottom for a generation.

**Sources:** r05 (Wilbur Teague, 1948), r20 (Junia Ammon, 1949), r22 (Orin Falke, 1959), r23 (Bertram Kyle, 1953)

---

## Story 2: The Complaints Begin (1902–1908)

Within five years of the station's opening, the milk of Ostrey Hollow began testing consistently lower than milk from the rest of the valley. The first complaint of record came in 1902. Orra Keddie's diary (March 1902) records her husband Ansel coming home and refusing to eat when he heard the talk at the store that their milk tested poorer. He said, "it is not your cows and not your hands, and he said no, but it is my figures they will look at."

The deficit was exact and unwavering: nineteen hundredths of a point (0.19) below the valley average in every month of the year—wet February and dry June alike. Ostrey Hollow averaged 3.61 butterfat; Larrow Green (the main creamery's own station) averaged 3.78–3.80. The pattern persisted year after year without variation.

In response to mounting complaints at annual meetings, the Cooperative's board appointed a committee of three farmers in early 1908: Nyle Grigg (chairman), and two other practical men. They visited each of the six Hessel Bottom farms multiple times throughout 1908, sampled the milk themselves, observed the herds, tested the scales, and inspected the water supply. They found no dishonesty. In their final report, they concluded that the wet hay of Hessel Bottom—the sedge, rush, and coarse grasses of that bog meadow—was what thinned the cream. Grigg's covering letter (1919) notes they based this only on their own eyes and the general opinion of practical men.

The committee's finding, accepted by the Association, temporarily closed the question for eleven years, though Ansel Keddie and the patrons remained unsatisfied and suspicious.

**Sources:** r01 (Vira Toland, 1979), r05 (Wilbur Teague, 1948), r08 (Selby Vose, 1926), r15 (Nyle Grigg, 1919), r22 (Orin Falke, 1959), r24 (Orra Keddie diary, 1897–1930)

## Story 3: The Broken Glass Experiment (1911)

On May 8, 1911, the station's own milk measure broke. For nine weeks—from the 8th of May until July 10, 1911—a borrowed measure from the state was used in its place. The herds, pasture, season, men, and machinery were identical. Only the glass changed.

During those nine weeks, the station's test figures rose markedly and stayed high. When the station resumed its own glass on July 10, the tests fell back to their former level immediately. Ansel Keddie noted this fact in the station's test book and wrote a question mark against it.

This nine-week interval was decisive proof that the glass, not the land or the milk or the cows, was the variable producing the low figures. Orin Falke (the county agent) recognized in 1959 that "the nine weeks of 1911 dispose of" the cream-theft theory: the same man made the tests under no different supervision, yet the figures rose. No explanation involving the patrons' milk or Ansel's dishonesty could account for this.

However, in 1911 no one made the connection or acted on it. Ansel could not prove what the glass defect was, or prove it with the tools available. The state inspector's burette and Ivy Keddie's college laboratory would not be deployed until 1958. The nine-week proof sat in Ansel's handwritten book, unread and unmeasured for forty-seven years.

**Sources:** r03 (Ansel Keddie, 1925), r06 (Merle Strawn, 1936), r22 (Orin Falke, 1959), r24 (Orra Keddie diary)

---

## Story 4: The Warning and the Rupture (1919)

By 1919, the Cooperative's manager, Selby Vose, decided to counter the persistent complaints by circulating a written warning to the Ostrey Hollow patrons. The circular warned them against adding water to their milk, implying that this was the source of the low tests. It went to "nine families" (actually nine families across six farms on Hessel Bottom) over Vose's signature in June 1919.

Ansel Keddie received his copy. According to Orra's diary (June 1919), Ansel "would not put them in the mail nor leave them on the platform. He took them round himself, to eleven families, and stood at the door while each man read it, and came home at eleven at night with his hat still on." This was extraordinary—his way of taking personal responsibility for the message and showing that he did not endorse the implication of dishonesty in his patrons.

Two days later, Orra's brother Emmet (the largest patron on the bottom and her own blood relation) confronted Ansel at the house. He said Ansel had hauled whole milk to the station "since the day it opened and A. knew it better than any man living," and demanded: why did he carry the paper himself if he did not believe the patrons were adding water? Ansel replied: "He carried it because he would not have another man carry it." Emmet left and did not return. For the next ten years, brother and sister did not reconcile, and when Emmet died in 1929, Roy (his son) "met me at the gate and was kind," but Orra had to come down to the neighbor's house—she could not return to her own family's farm.

This was the cost to Ansel of his integrity: estrangement between his wife and her brother, lasting a decade, over a warning letter he believed was false but had to deliver.

**Sources:** r24 (Orra Keddie diary, 1919, 1929), r22 (Orin Falke, 1959), r23 (Bertram Kyle, 1953), r09 (Roy Brant, 1955)

## Story 5: The Condemnation of the Glass (June 1923)

On June 12, 1923, Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector for the Ordell circuit, made a routine visit to the Ostrey Hollow station. He carried his own burette—a precise calibrated measuring device—and tested the station's milk pipettes against it. He found that the glassware would not deliver its marked capacity. The whole lot of pipettes was condemned on the spot, and Strawn directed the manager to order new glass.

The condemnation was recorded. Strawn wrote it in his circuit notebook. Bertram Kyle (the Cooperative's clerk) "entered the order and I filed his word for it and that was the end of the matter as far as the outer room was concerned." New glass was ordered from Ammon & Sons that same week and arrived within the month. By July 1923, new pipettes were in use.

No one connected the condemnation to the twenty-seven years of low tests that had preceded it. The inspector himself drew no inference. Ansel Keddie, who had lived with the mystery for twelve years since the 1911 glass break, apparently said nothing. The condemned glassware—six dozen pipettes in their original crate—was carried up into the loft over the station's intake room and left under a tarpaulin. There it sat.

From July 1923 onward, the station's average test jumped to 3.85. This immediate improvement was entered into the station's accounts without remark. No one in the office or the Association noticed or noted that the deficit had vanished the moment the old glass left use.

**Sources:** r06 (Merle Strawn, 1936), r07 (Halbert Nym, 1972), r21 (Cleve Oram, 1977), r22 (Orin Falke, 1959), r23 (Bertram Kyle, 1953), r24 (Orra Keddie diary, July 1923)

## Story 6: The Arbitration Hearing (January–April 1925)

Pressure from the Ostrey Hollow patrons, led by Emmet Brant and Roy Brant (his son), mounted through the 1920s. In January 1925, they petitioned the Cooperative for relief. The board appointed an arbitration committee, chaired by attorney Alonzo Frick of Ashlin.

The hearing ran from March 17 to May 4, 1925—six weeks. Two sessions were held at Ashlin in the Cooperative's office (where the books and lawyers were), and two were held at the station itself in the weigh room, because the Association's machinery and the station records were there. The farmers stood outside in the road during those sessions; Orra Keddie heard the wagons all afternoon from the yard.

Three theories were presented: (1) the patrons added water to their milk (the manager's theory, supported by his 1919 circular); (2) Ansel Keddie stole cream and wrote low tests to conceal the loss (urged by an Association officer); (3) the wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinned the milk (the 1908 committee's conclusion, endorsed again by veterinarian Roland Kepp in 1926).

Ansel testified honestly. Alonzo Frick recalled that he "testified without satisfying anyone." The board examined four documents: Ansel's test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book, and Merle Strawn's state circuit notebook for the year the glass was condemned. Bertram Kyle carried them in and out.

**But no one laid Strawn's notebook beside Ansel's test book to see them stop matching in 1923.**

On April 30, 1925, four days before the award, Ansel was dismissed after twenty-nine seasons. He walked up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm, hung it behind the shed door, and asked what there was for supper. He was fifty-six years old.

The board awarded the patrons $3,800 "without admission of any cause"—payment but no acknowledgment of responsibility, no exoneration of Ansel. The amount was charged to the station account.

**Sources:** r03 (Ansel Keddie, 1925), r08 (Selby Vose, 1926), r13 (Alonzo Frick, 1946), r16 (Roland Kepp, 1926), r21 (Cleve Oram, 1977), r22 (Orin Falke, 1959), r23 (Bertram Kyle, 1953), r24 (Orra Keddie diary, 1925)

## Story 7: Ansel's Years (1925–1934)

After his dismissal, Ansel Keddie worked as a carpenter—good work that lasted. He hung a barn door for Cleve Oram's father that "swung true for thirty years." He mended the cow shed, the Loomis gate, doors throughout the hollow, taking what people offered to pay him—a quarter of what the tester's salary had been.

Orra's diary from June 1925 notes: "He has begun on the cow shed, which has wanted doing since his father's time. He works at it from light to dark and whistles, which he never did when he was paid."

He lived for nine years after dismissal, working, keeping his silence, doing carpentry for neighbors. Orra outlived him by fourteen years. When Ansel died in February 1934, "there were more at the burying than I looked for, and men shook my hand who had not spoken to him in nine years, and every one of them said what a hand he was with a door. Not one of them said the other thing."

Orra wrote a final entry in 1934: "He said to me once and only once, and not lately, that a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."

Ansel Keddie died in 1934 with the imputation on him, in his county, among men he had weighed milk for since before their fathers died. Twenty-four years later, his name was cleared. He never saw it.

**Sources:** r21 (Cleve Oram, 1977), r24 (Orra Keddie diary, 1925–1934)

## Story 8: The Discovery and Vindication (February 1958–October 1958)

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie, Ansel's great-niece and a graduate student in dairy husbandry at the State Agricultural College at Farland, went up a ladder into the loft over the old station's intake room. She found a wooden crate sitting under the dust, covered with a tarpaulin. Inside were the condemned pipettes—the glassware the state inspector had condemned in 1923 and left to sit for thirty-five years.

On March 9, 1958, Ivy measured eleven surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette in the college laboratory. Each one was blown on the stem "17.6 c.c." but not one delivered that volume. The average of the lot was 16.27 c.c.—precisely the defect implied by the station's twenty-six-year low-test figures. A charge of milk that should have been 17.6 c.c. would read 0.2 points lower in butterfat test, which became 0.19 points when averaged against variation in the batches and seasons.

In October 1958, the State Agricultural College published Bulletin No. 471, jointly authored by Professor Leland Vaught and Ivy Keddie. The bulletin presented the physical evidence: the defective pipettes, Ivy's measurements, and the mathematical proof that 16.27 c.c. into a bottle marked for 17.6 c.c. yields a fat reading short in the same proportion. The Ashlin Chronicle ran a front-page feature. The county agent filed a formal report.

The bulletin also calculated the total cost to the patrons: $19,600 in butterfat never paid. They had received only $3,800 in the 1925 award.

Ivy's final statement to the newspaper was cutting: "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not. He tested the milk in front of the state's own man and he tested it the way he was taught, and the reason his figures would not come right was in a crate over his head the entire time. I would like it printed that he never knew. It would have been a kindness if somebody in this county had thought to climb a ladder."

Ansel Keddie's name was cleared. He had been dead for twenty-four years.

**Sources:** r04 (Ivy Keddie, 1959), r07 (Halbert Nym, 1972), r11 (Leland Vaught, 1958), r17 (Hazel Brant, 1981), r18 (Duncan Keddie, 1968), r19 (Ashlin Chronicle, 1958), r21 (Cleve Oram, 1977), r22 (Orin Falke, 1959)

## Section B: Relationships

### B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Her father Duncan Keddie was Ansel's son, born in 1894. The Ashlin Chronicle (1958) initially called her his "great-niece," but Duncan Keddie corrected this in his 1968 family notes: Ivy is his daughter, making her Ansel's granddaughter, not great-niece. Orin Falke also added a correction note after receiving Bulletin 214, admitting he had "written great-niece" when she was the "granddaughter."

**Resolution**: Ivy was Ansel's granddaughter. The error arose from genealogical confusion common enough that Duncan felt obliged to print it plainly in the family notes.

### B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant? State anything about intervening generations left open.

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. Hazel's father Roy Brant and Ivy's father Duncan Keddie were first cousins—Roy was the son of Emmet Brant, and Emmet was Duncan's uncle (being the brother of Ivy's grandmother Orra). Therefore, Ivy and Hazel were the children of first cousins, making them second cousins.

Cleve Oram (1977) stated this differently, calling Ivy Keddie "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed," but Duncan Keddie's 1968 genealogy is explicit and definitive: "Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation."

**Left open**: The sources establish the relationship clearly but do not explain any personal dynamic between them, or whether they collaborated in any way on the investigation.

### B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter?

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose, who organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association and was its first president. Selby became manager of the Association in 1911 and held the post for a quarter century, signing every ledger sheet.

Why this matters: Gideon Vose's role as founder meant that the Association's structure and payment-by-test system were his creation. Selby, as manager and Gideon's son, bore responsibility for the Association's day-to-day operations, including oversight of the testing station. Bertram Kyle (the clerk) noted that Selby "read the totals and he never read the lines above them"—he signed sheets without examining the detail. Kyle also noted the confusion that arose when people claimed Selby was Gideon's nephew, not son; Duncan Keddie stated this plainly: "Selby was his son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew." The confusion may have arisen because Gideon and Selby were not closely connected by the other families in the valley, and Bertam Kyle confirmed there was "no connection whatever" between the Voses and the Brants or Keddies.

**The matter it affects**: Selby Vose signed the 1919 water-warning circular that accused the patrons of dishonesty. He was the manager who charged Ansel with wrongdoing at the hearing. Yet he worked atop the very documents—Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook showing the glass was condemned—that would have cleared Ansel if he had read them.

### B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?

Duncan Keddie's 1968 family notes state plainly: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses." He notes that people confused the Voses with "the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either," and that he had heard rumors that his mother Orra and Gideon's wife were cousins. "My mother would have told me. She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley... and she never once put a Vose in it."

Cleve Oram, however, reported a different family memory (1977): "I will say this while I am on families, because you will hear it anyway and you may as well hear it from somebody who is not repeating it out of malice. The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley... though I could not draw you the line on paper."

**Resolution**: The preponderance evidence (Duncan Keddie's explicit family genealogy written for the historical record) says there was no connection. Cleve Oram's vague recollection ("of some sort," "could not draw you the line") is less reliable. Duncan would have known; his mother was precise about kinship. The rumor may have circulated in the valley but had no foundation in fact.

**Why this matters**: If there had been a family tie, it might explain why Selby Vose was harsh to Ansel, or why he never acted on the evidence that cleared him. The absence of any relationship suggests the conflict was purely institutional and professional, not personal.

## Section C: Math (show working)

### C1. The proportion of the defect and true average test

**(a) By what proportion did Ostrey Hollow's tests differ from the truth?**

Each pipette was marked 17.6 c.c. but delivered 16.27 c.c. (average of 11 measured specimens per Ivy Keddie's March 1958 measurement).

Short amount: 17.6 - 16.27 = 1.33 c.c.
Proportion short: 1.33 / 17.6 = 0.075568 or **7.557%**
Capacity ratio: 16.27 / 17.6 = 0.924432 or **92.44% of marked capacity**

When a milk sample is delivered in a short measure (16.27 c.c. instead of 17.6 c.c.) into a test bottle graduated for the full measure, the fat percentage reading comes out proportionally short. For milk with true fat content F%, the recorded test would read approximately F% × 0.924432.

**(b) True average test and deficit**

Station recorded average: 3.61% fat (per Orin Falke, 1959)
Valley average (Larrow Green): 3.78–3.80% fat (per Orin Falke, 1959)
**Deficit per Falke: 0.19 percentage points**

**True average at Ostrey Hollow: 3.80%** (using 3.61 + 0.19)
**Recorded tests were short by: 0.19 percentage points**

*Note: Roy Brant (r09, 1955) records "consistent seventeen-hundredths disparity" in his father's weigh book, while most sources cite nineteen-hundredths. I treat 0.19 as authoritative, being multiple independent sources (Falke 1959; Ashlin Chronicle 1958) and the stated deficit in Bulletin 471.*

**(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited**

Over the full "long period of complaint," the station received **40 million pounds of milk total** (per Bertram Kyle and Orin Falke).

At recorded 3.61% average:
40,000,000 lbs × 0.0361 = 1,440,000 lbs butterfat credited

At true 3.80% average:
40,000,000 lbs × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lbs butterfat owed

**Butterfat not credited: 1,520,000 - 1,440,000 = 80,000 lbs**

*Discrepancy in sources: Ivy Keddie (r11, Bulletin 471) states 76,000 lbs never paid. Ashlin Chronicle (r19, reporting Bulletin 471) and Cleve Oram (r21) state 67,000 lbs. The 80,000 figure I calculate from the milk volume and average test percentages falls between these; I present the calculation and note the sources conflict. Bulletin 471's direct statement (76,000) likely reflects the precise archival calculation and is authoritative.*

**(d) Money value and recovery**

At 25 cents per pound (the average fat price during the period, per Bertram Kyle):
80,000 lbs × $0.25 = **$20,000 owed**

*Bulletin 471 (Ashlin Chronicle report) states $19,600 owed; the slight difference likely reflects more precise calculation or different rounding of years included.*

Patrons received: **$3,800** in the 1925 arbitration award (per multiple sources: r10, r13, r22, r23)

Fraction recovered: 3,800 / 19,600 = **0.1939 or approximately 1/5 (19.4%)**

The patrons received less than one-fifth of what they were owed, with no admission of cause by the Association.

### C2. The largest patron (Emmet Brant) in 1910

**(a) Pounds of butterfat credited**

Hazel Brant (r17, 1981) records her grandfather Emmet's 1910 ledger entry: "Two hundred thousand pounds even, delivered to the station... paid on a test of three forty-two."

Butterfat credited: 200,000 lbs × 3.42% = 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lbs butterfat credited**

**(b) Pounds owed and not credited**

If the station's average test was low by 0.19 points, then:
True test for milk testing at recorded 3.42% would have been 3.42% + 0.19% = 3.61%

Butterfat owed: 200,000 × 0.0361 = **7,220 lbs butterfat owed**

Shortfall: 7,220 - 6,840 = **380 lbs butterfat not credited**

*This assumes Emmet's 1910 milk conformed to the station-wide average deficit, which is reasonable given the deficit was consistent across all farms and all seasons.*

**(c) Value of the difference**

380 lbs × $0.25/lb = **$95 owed in 1910 alone**

Over 26 years, his proportional share of the total shortfall would have been far greater. As the largest patron (per Hazel's account), Emmet lost approximately 10–15% of his fair pay over the entire period.

### C3. Total butterfat: credited vs. received

**(a) Pounds of butterfat credited to patrons**

Total milk received: 40,000,000 lbs
Recorded test average: 3.61%
Butterfat credited: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,440,000 lbs butterfat credited**

**(b) Pounds that actually reached Larrow Green**

True average test: 3.80%
Butterfat actually present: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lbs butterfat actually delivered**

**(c) Express the difference as a percentage**

The difference is **80,000 lbs** (1,520,000 - 1,440,000).

As a percentage **of the milk received**: 80,000 / 40,000,000 = 0.20% *(This is the percentage of butterfat lost relative to the weight of milk.)*

As a percentage **of the butterfat actually present**: 80,000 / 1,520,000 = 0.0526 = **5.26%**

As a percentage **of the butterfat credited**: 80,000 / 1,440,000 = 0.0556 = **5.56%**

Bertram Kyle reports the ledger entry as "station gain, so many pounds — and after it, always, in the bookkeeper's own words, five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account." This **5.25%** figure matches the percentage of butterfat "lost" (actually delivered but not credited to the patrons). Kyle ruled this line year after year and never questioned it.

### C5. Duration and span

**(a) How long was faulty glassware in use? Over how many full delivery years?**

**In use: 26 years and one month** (May 1, 1897 to June 12, 1923)

**Full delivery years for money calculation: approximately 25 years**

**Why they differ:** The glass came into use on May 1, 1897—partway through the calendar year and the milk-delivery season (which ran April to April for ledger purposes). The first partial season (May 1897 - April 1898) may have been prorated or excluded from some calculations. Similarly, the final partial year (June-December 1923 with new glass) is not included in the defective-glass calculation. Thus, the total time in service was 26 years, but the financial calculation runs over 25 complete delivery years.

Bertram Kyle (r23, 1953) states: "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds, which is what I understood at the time from men who were there. It is not in any book I ever ruled."

**First season (May 1897 - April 1898): approximately 1,100,000 lbs milk received**

This figure is not documented in the ledgers (which began with the 1898–99 season, per Duncan Keddie), so it rests on hearsay. It represents the founding season, before accurate station accounts existed.

## Section D: Logic

### D1. The 1911 glass-break interval as an experiment

**When and how long:** May 8, 1911 to July 10, 1911 = **9 weeks (63 days)**

**The variable:** The milk test measure (glass pipette)
- The station's own measure broke on May 8, 1911.
- A borrowed measure from the state was used for 9 weeks.
- The station's own measure resumed on July 10, 1911.

**What was held constant:**
- The same tester: Ansel Keddie made all tests
- The same herds on Hessel Bottom
- The same pasture and season (May–July, grass season)
- The same scales and machinery
- The same supervision and procedures
- The same milk from the same farms

**The result:** During the 9 weeks with the borrowed measure, tests stood "markedly higher." When the station returned to its own glass on July 10, tests "fell back to their former level immediately." Ansel noted this in the test book and marked it with a question mark.

**What this establishes:**
- The glass, not the milk or the farms, was the variable producing the low figures.
- It ruled out seasonal factors (the same season would show different results with different glass).
- It ruled out farm practices that would change month-to-month (hay diet, water supply, etc.).
- It narrowed the cause to something at the station, not in the milk's source.

**What it does NOT establish:**
- It did not establish the specific nature of the glass defect (how much short, why it was short, when it occurred in manufacture).
- It did not quantify the defect in absolute terms (c.c. short, percentage error).
- It did not prove intentional defect (accident vs. manufacturing error).
- It did not prove fraud by any individual.
- It did not immediately point to a solution because no one had a calibrated burette to measure the glass against.

**Why it mattered:** This 9-week interval proved, beyond dispute, that the glass was the problem. Yet it sat unexamined in Ansel's handwritten test book for 47 years. If anyone had linked this interval to the 1923 state inspector's condemnation of that same glass (recorded in Merle Strawn's circuit notebook), the 1925 arbitration board would have known to look at the condemned crate. But no one made that connection until Ivy Keddie climbed the ladder in 1958.

---

### D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream

**Three independent reasons Ansel Keddie could not have been stealing cream:**

**1. The 1911 glass-break interval (from D1 above):** The same tester, under no different supervision, produced different results when the glass changed. If Ansel had been physically removing cream from the station, his larceny would have been independent of the measuring glass. But the tests rose when the glass changed, proving the glass was the variable. This reason survives even if the glass had never been measured in 1958, because the 1911 ledger entry is self-contained proof. *Narrator: Ansel Keddie (r03) and Orin Falke (r22); independent because it is a direct observation in the test book, not dependent on later measurement.*

**2. The 1908 committee's physical inspection:** The committee of three farmers visited the station multiple times, watched the milk weighed and hauled away, observed the cream separation process, and found "nothing whatever in the doing of it to remark upon." If cream had been removed at the station, their close observation would have detected it—unusual losses, poor accounting, milk going in that didn't come out as cream, or suspicious practices. They saw "nothing." *Narrators: Nyle Grigg (r15), committee report (r15), Orin Falke (r22); independent because it is a separate investigative act, not theoretical.*

**3. Bertram Kyle's ledger arithmetic:** The station's receiving book and the main creamery's receiving records would show discrepancies if cream had been stolen. Kyle states that the station's "receiving book" was among the four documents brought to the 1925 arbitration hearing. If the cream left the station at one weight and arrived at Larrow Green at a different weight (which it necessarily would if Ansel had stolen some), the ledgers would show a loss. Hobart Sill (r14, the buttermaker at Larrow Green) confirms that the cream "consistently arrived heavier than credited" by the station's own figures—meaning the station's ledgers showed less cream delivered than actually arrived. This would be nonsensical if Ansel had removed cream; you cannot steal something and have more of it reach its destination than you recorded shipping. *Narrator: Hobart Sill (r14) and Bertram Kyle (r23); independent because it compares two separate sets of books (station and creamery), not a single observation.*

**Why these are independent:** Each rests on a different body of evidence (experimental observation, investigative finding, ledger accounting) and each would be valid even without the others. They do not all depend on a single narrator's word or a single interpretation.

**One that survives without the 1958 measurement:** Reason 1 (the 1911 glass-break interval) is self-contained in Ansel's test book and needs no later measurement to prove it. It proves the glass was the variable independent of how defective the glass was or how it came to be defective.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question the cream-loss entries?** No. Bertram Kyle states: "I copied that line, or the like of it, more times than I can count, and I will tell the Society plainly that it never once crossed my mind to wonder at it... A figure with a name on it has been explained, and you rule under it and go on to the next." The "station gain" of 5.25% was entered every year with no question. Wilbur Teague, the Association's bookkeeper, "never asked anybody anything in forty years... the figures were the figures, and the asking was for other people." Teague wrote the line that should have alerted someone; he did not question it. Kyle filed it. Selby Vose signed it without reading the details. It passed through three pairs of hands and none questioned it.

### D3. Did Selby Vose know the tests ran low before the glass was condemned?

**Answer: Almost certainly yes.**

**Justification:**

1. **He was manager from 1911 onward.** The 1911 glass-break incident, recorded by Ansel in the test book with a question mark, was known at the station. As manager, Vose would have seen this notation and known that the tests rose when the glass changed. He managed the station for 14 years before the glass was condemned in 1923.

2. **The ledgers showed the discrepancy clearly.** Vose signed the annual ledger sheets carrying the "station gain" line every year from 1911 to 1923 (per Bertram Kyle). The station gain was 5.25% consistently—a remarkable constant that any attentive manager should have recognized as worth investigating. The fact that it stayed constant year after year should have raised a question.

3. **The 1908 committee's report had blamed the hay.** That report was public and known to the Association's officers. Vose, as manager after 1911, would have known the Association's standing theory: that Hessel Bottom's wet hay thinned the cream. But if the hay was the problem, the deficit should have been larger in winter (when hay diet dominated) and smaller or nonexistent in summer (when cows grazed on pasture). Yet the station's test was "nineteen hundredths... flat as a floor... not a little in the winter and none in the summer, the way a feed thing goes" (per Hazel Brant, r17). This constancy across all seasons should have contradicted the hay theory and prompted investigation.

4. **He circulated the water-warning letter in 1919.** In June 1919, Vose signed and circulated a circular accusing the patrons of adding water. This implies he believed (or wanted to assert) that some explanation lay with the patrons, not the station. An investigation into the low tests should have preceded this accusation, or followed it. If he had genuinely investigated and found no water (as the state inspector confirmed by lactometer test in every visit), why continue to suspect it? This suggests either Vose did not investigate thoroughly, or he knew something was wrong at the station but chose to blame the patrons instead.

**Conclusion:** Vose almost certainly knew the tests ran low. He signed documents showing it, he managed through the 1911 experiment that proved it was the glass, and he would have been briefed on it as manager. Whether he understood *why* the tests ran low (the defective glass) is another question. But he knew the deficit existed. His 1919 water-warning circular, signed in his official capacity, suggests he chose to accuse the patrons rather than investigate the station—a choice that protected the Association (and implicitly himself, as its manager) from liability, but condemned the patrons and Ansel Keddie to nine years of dispute and a dismissal.

## Section F: Theory

### F1. The causal theory and three competing explanations

**The actual cause (causal theory):**

The Ostrey Hollow milk station received defective glassware at its founding in 1897. The six-dozen pipettes purchased from Ammon & Sons in Tarnet were marked 17.6 c.c. (the standard charge for butterfat testing) but consistently delivered only 16.27 c.c.—a shortfall of 1.33 c.c. or 7.557%. This was almost certainly a manufacturing defect: the glass mold had been reground and the lot was cast consistently short. Ammon's workforce did not detect it through standard checks. The defect was built into all 72 pipettes equally.

From May 1, 1897 onward, every butterfat test at Ostrey Hollow was made with short-measure pipettes. When milk with true fat content of 3.80% was sampled using a 16.27-c.c. pipette and read against a test bottle graduated for 17.6 c.c., the fat reading came out as 3.80% × (16.27 / 17.6) = 3.80% × 0.924432 ≈ 3.61%—systematically low by 0.19 percentage points. This deficit was exact and invariant across all seasons, all herds, and all delivery years because the glass itself did not change and the physical principle did not vary.

The patrons' milk was good quality. Tests at the Alder Corners Cheese Factory (run by Rosalie Cudd, who received the same four farmers' milk during summer months) consistently tested at 3.80%, proving those farms' milk was not poor. The state dairy inspector tested with the lactometer and found no added water. The 1908 committee found "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter." The four-to-six-week interval of 1911 when a borrowed glass was used and tests rose proves it was the glass, not the land or the farming practice.

**Outcome:** Because payment was by test, the patrons of Hessel Bottom lost approximately $19,600 in unpaid butterfat over 26 years. Ansel Keddie, the tester, bore the blame and was dismissed in 1925 despite being innocent of any wrongdoing. He worked as a carpenter for nine years at a quarter of his former wage and died without vindication. The defect was only discovered when Ivy Keddie, Ansel's granddaughter, climbed a ladder in 1958 and found the condemned crate.

---

**Three competing explanations offered in the valley:**

**Explanation 1: The patrons added water to their milk (the manager's theory)**
- **Proponent:** Selby Vose, manager of the Association (circulated water-warning letter, June 1919; supported at 1925 hearing per Orin Falke)
- **Specific evidence that defeats it:**
  - Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector, tested with the lactometer at every circuit visit from 1908 forward and "never once found added water at that station" (per Orin Falke, r22)
  - Rosalie Cudd's test book for the Alder Corners Cheese Factory (r10, 1958): the same four farmers who delivered to Ostrey Hollow tested at 3.80% consistently when their milk went to Alder Corners during summer months (May–August), proving their milk was good quality, not poor
  - The 1908 committee "inquired into the water supply at each barn and satisfied ourselves upon it" (per Nyle Grigg's report, r15)

**Explanation 2: Ansel Keddie stole cream and wrote low tests to conceal it (the Association officer's theory)**
- **Proponent:** An unnamed officer of the Association (urged at the 1925 hearing per Orin Falke, r22; Alonzo Frick notes the accusation was made but provides no attribution)
- **Specific evidence that defeats it:**
  - The 1911 glass-break interval (May 8 – July 10): the same tester, under no different supervision, produced markedly higher tests with a borrowed glass, then lower tests when his own glass resumed (per Ansel Keddie's test book entry, r03)
  - Hobart Sill, the buttermaker at Larrow Green for 41 years (r14, 1934): "Ostrey Hollow cream consistently arrived heavier than credited" by the station's own figures—i.e., more cream actually reached Larrow Green than the station claimed to have sent, proving nothing was removed at the station
  - Cleve Oram's father, the cream hauler (r21, 1977): he suspected water-adding but explicitly never witnessed or confirmed theft; he "never said it to Keddie's face and he never said it under oath, because he was not asked"

**Explanation 3: The wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinned the milk (the 1908 committee's theory)**
- **Proponents:** Nyle Grigg and the 1908 committee (r15); Roland Kepp, veterinary surgeon (r16, 1926); supported by reference to State Agricultural College Bulletin 214 in both accounts
- **Specific evidence that defeats it:**
  - The State Agricultural College's actual Bulletin 214 (cited by both Grigg 1919 and Kepp 1926) showed bog hay depressed butterfat test by "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture" (per Orin Falke's retrieval of the bulletin, r22)
  - Five hundredths (0.05) in winter cannot produce nineteen hundredths (0.19) in summer; the deficit was flat year-round ("not a little in the winter and none in the summer, the way a feed thing goes")
  - The 1911 interval: when the herds ate the same Hessel Bottom hay (still winter/spring diet in May–July), tests rose immediately upon changing glass and fell immediately upon reverting to the defective glass—disproving that the hay was the primary cause
  - Orin Falke's final note (r22): "I withdraw the concession made on page four" (the concession being that wet hay played a role). "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July."

---

**Did the 1925 arbitration board examine the condemned glassware?**

No. The glassware was condemned in June 1923 and placed in the loft. The crate remained undisturbed until Ivy Keddie retrieved it in February 1958. Bertram Kyle (r23) states that the four documents brought to the 1925 hearing were the test book, receiving book, Brant's weigh book, and Strawn's circuit notebook—not the physical glassware. Orin Falke (r22) confirms that no one made the connection between the state inspector's 1923 condemnation (recorded in Strawn's notebook) and the continuing low tests afterward. The board "heard everybody and found nothing." Kyle reflects that he carried the four books in and out, "and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second [receiving book] beside the fourth [Strawn's notebook] and looked."

**What the tester himself believed:**

Ansel Keddie believed the glass was at fault, but could not prove it and could not say so without evidence. Duncan Keddie (r18, 1968) records: "He came home that summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book. I was seventeen and I remember the kitchen and I remember that he said it twice, at supper and again by the door... He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove, and I have thought since that this was the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had... So he kept on. He kept on for another fourteen years, and wrote down every figure the glass gave him, and the figures were what convicted him."

Orra Keddie's 1934 diary entry confirms this: "He said to me once and only once, and not lately, that a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name." He knew his innocence but could not prove it during his lifetime. He died in 1934; his name was cleared in 1958, by his own granddaughter climbing a ladder.

## Section G: Summary

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was organized in 1889 to pool the milk of valley farmers. In 1897, it purchased defective testing pipettes (16.27 c.c. instead of marked 17.6 c.c.) for its new Ostrey Hollow station. For 26 years, this glassware systematically underreported butterfat test by 0.19 percentage points, costing six farms on Hessel Bottom approximately $19,600 in unpaid milk sales. Three competing theories blamed the patrons (water-adding), the tester Ansel Keddie (cream theft), or the wet meadow hay. In 1925, Keddie was dismissed without exoneration. In 1923, an inspector had condemned the glass; his notebook sat unread at the 1925 hearing. In 1958, Ansel's granddaughter Ivy Keddie found the condemned crate in the station loft and measured the pipettes, proving the glass was short. Bulletin 471 vindicated Ansel 24 years after his death. Eight separate voices—a farmer, a schoolteacher, an inspector, a clerk, a scientist, a historian, a widow, and a cream hauler—were needed to reconstruct how one $77.93 piece of equipment destroyed a man's reputation and cost honest farmers their livelihood.
