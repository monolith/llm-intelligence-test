# Section A: The Eight Stories (Stories 5–8)

## Story 5: The State Inspector's Condemnation (June 1923)

On June 12, 1923—twenty-six years after the equipment had been installed at Ostrey Hollow—the state dairy inspector, Merle Strawn, arrived on his circuit visit. Strawn was unrelated to any family in the valley, held no allegiance to patrons or managers, and carried only the authority of measurement. He took the station's milk pipettes and ran each one against a calibrated burette to verify that they delivered their marked volume. They did not. The pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. would not deliver 17.6 c.c. Strawn condemned the entire lot on the spot and directed the manager to order new glassware. He wrote down in his official notebook that the glass had been condemned. He drew no inference as to the years behind it.

The condemned glassware was packed into a wooden crate, placed under a tarpaulin, and carried up into the loft over the intake room at the station. It sat there, unremarked, for thirty-five years. Cleve Oram, the cream hauler's son, had seen that crate many times during those decades—put his hand on it—and asked someone what it was. He was told it was the old glass the state man made them throw out. Cleve thought nothing more of it. It was just a crate under a tarp.

New glass was ordered from Ammon & Sons to Tarnet. On June 28, 1923, the Association wrote to request replacement pipettes and test bottles. Ammon & Sons responded July 2: the order would be entered and shipped within the month. The letter included the sentence: "We regret the trouble. Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name."

What Ammon & Sons could not have known—and what no one would discover for thirty-five years—was that those condemned pipettes had been systematically short by approximately five percent. The mold had been reground, or molds hung crooked after being knocked off their nail, or a boy had sampled wrong and found them consistently—consistently wrong. Glass is made by people, and when a gauge hangs on a nail and gets knocked off the nail and hung back on it, the hanging can be true or false, and a false hanging is invisible until someone measures.

Once new glass arrived and came into use in 1924, the station's tests immediately rose. The new average became 3.85, which matched the level during the 1911 trial with the borrowed measure. The cause, once and for all, had been the glass. But no one yet knew it.

## Story 6: The Arbitration and Dismissal (1925)

By January 1925, patience had exhausted. The Ostrey Hollow patrons, led by Emmet Brant, filed a formal petition demanding investigation into the station's persistently low tests. They wanted their back pay. A board of arbitration was constituted to hear the case. Three sittings were held: one at Ashlin in the Cooperative office (where the lawyer was), and two at the station itself, in the weigh room where the scale and machinery stood—and where, unknown to all the men assembling there, the condemned glassware lay in the loft overhead, twenty feet up, under a tarpaulin, waiting.

Alonzo Frick, the arbitration chairman, heard three explanations offered. Selby Vose and the Association's officers maintained the water theory—that the Ostrey Hollow patrons deliberately added water to their milk. Hobart Sill, a buttermaker, claimed the station operator removed cream dishonestly and wrote low tests to conceal the theft. The third theory blamed the Hessel Bottom meadow's wet hay and poor minerals for thinning the cows' cream. 

The board examined only four pieces of evidence: Ansel Keddie's station test book, the Association's receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book (lent for the purpose), and Merle Strawn's state inspector notebook for the year 1923 when the glass was condemned. 

The water theory was unsustainable. Strawn himself had tested at Ostrey Hollow with his lactometer and found no added water from 1908 forward. The theft theory was disproven by the 1911 trial: the same man had tested during those nine weeks with the borrowed glass, tests rose, and yet no cream had been stolen—the experiment proved the glass, not the man. The bog-hay theory remained the last plausible explanation.

On April 30, 1925, Ansel Keddie was dismissed. He walked home at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm, hung it behind the shed door, and asked Orra what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it. Four days later, on May 4, 1925, the board handed down its finding: the Ostrey Hollow patrons would receive $3,800 in compensation, "without admission" of any cause. The payment was charged to the station account in the ledger. The dismissal said everything; the finding found nothing.

Ansel Keddie had been the tester for twenty-nine seasons. He never went to another Association meeting as long as he lived.

## Story 7: Discovery and Measurement (February–March 1958)

Thirty-three years after Ansel Keddie was dismissed, thirty-four years after he died, a young woman named Ivy Keddie—Ansel's granddaughter, Duncan's daughter—was working on a graduate degree in dairy husbandry at the State Agricultural College at Farland. For her thesis work, she was investigating the historical records of milk testing in the county. She obtained Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks from the state dairy office. In the volume for 1923, she found his entry: the Ostrey Hollow pipettes had been condemned.

She went to the station at Ostrey Hollow in February 1958. In the loft over the intake room, under a tarpaulin and about an inch of chaff accumulated over thirty-five years, she found a wooden crate. Inside were the station's original glassware: test bottles, acid measures, and pipettes. Eleven of the original pipettes were still intact.

On March 9, 1958, Ivy Keddie carried the crate east to the college laboratory at Farland. There she measured each of the eleven pipettes against a calibrated burette. Every one bore the mark 17.6 c.c. on its stem. Not one of them delivered 17.6 c.c. The average of the lot came to 16.27 c.c.—a shortfall of 1.33 c.c., or approximately 7.6 percent below the mark.

The mystery was solved. A short measure of milk, poured into a bottle whose graduated neck was ruled for the full measure, would produce a reading short in the same proportion—always the same proportion, nineteen hundredths. Nobody watered anything. Nobody stole anything. The scales were true, the acid was right, the cows were ordinary. A piece of glass blown to the wrong mold was wrong.

## Story 8: Vindication and the Price of Time (October 1958–1959 onward)

In October 1958, the State Agricultural College at Farland published Bulletin No. 471, written jointly by Professor Leland Vaught of the dairy husbandry department and Ivy Keddie, a graduate student. The bulletin presented the evidence: the condemned pipettes delivered 16.27 c.c. instead of 17.6, a proportional error of approximately 7.6 percent. Over the full years of use (1897–1923), that shortfall meant the Ostrey Hollow patrons were never paid for approximately $19,600 in butter-fat they had actually hauled to Larrow Green. They received, without admission, $3,800 in 1925. They had lost $15,800.

The discovery was reported by the Ashlin Chronicle as "Old Ordell Wrong Righted." Ivy Keddie was interviewed by telephone and said: "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not. He tested the milk in front of the state's own man and he tested it the way he was taught, and the reason his figures would not come right was in a crate over his head the entire time. I would like it printed that he never knew."

In 1959, Orin Falke, the county agricultural agent, prepared an official report for the county board of supervisors summarizing the entire history. He confirmed all the facts and added a note: Upon receiving State Bulletin No. 214 on bog hay, he recalculated. Bog hay depresses butter-fat tests by no more than 0.05 percent in winter and nothing in summer. This could not account for 0.19. Falke withdrew his earlier concession to the hay theory.

Ansel Keddie had died on February 13, 1934, at his home, "without much pain at the end of it," according to Orra's final diary entry. His funeral was well attended. Men came who had not spoken to him in nine years. Every one of them remarked on his carpentry—how true his doors hung, how well his work held—and not one of them named the scandal. Ansel had said once and only once: "A man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."

He was cleared in 1958, twenty-four years too late. His widow Orra, who had kept every letter and every receipt, had died in 1948. The vindication belonged to the record, not to them.

# Section B: Relationships (8 points)

## B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Ansel married Orra Brant in 1889. Their son was Duncan Keddie, born in 1894. Duncan had a daughter, Ivy, born in 1931. Therefore Ivy was Ansel's granddaughter. Duncan Keddie himself emphasized this relationship in his 1968 writing, stating: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise and once heard it said aloud in this town by a man who ought to have known." The editor of Orin Falke's 1959 county report later corrected an error, noting: "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece." This is confirmed directly by the family genealogy.

## B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant?

Ivy and Hazel were second cousins. Hazel's father was Roy Brant, and Roy was the son of Emmet Brant. Emmet was Orra's brother (Orra married Ansel Keddie). Therefore: Emmet and Orra were siblings; Emmet had a son Roy; Roy had a daughter Hazel; Orra had a son Duncan; Duncan had a daughter Ivy. This makes Roy and Duncan first cousins (children of a brother and sister), and their children Hazel and Ivy second cousins. Duncan Keddie's 1968 genealogy confirms: "Roy Brant and I are therefore first cousins...Roy's daughter is Hazel Brant. My daughter is Ivy. Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed." Hazel Brant's 1981 interview with the weigh book also confirms this relationship. Cleve Oram's 1977 account mistakenly called them "first cousins once removed," but the contemporary family records are clear.

## B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter?

Selby Vose was the son (not nephew, despite local confusion) of Gideon Vose. Gideon organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association and was its first president, serving as the architect of its bylaws and governance structure. Selby became manager of the Association in 1911 and held the position for approximately twenty-five years. This matters because Selby Vose signed the 1919 water-warning circular accusing the Ostrey Hollow patrons of deliberate adulteration. Bertram Kyle, the clerk, typed the circular and remembered typing it specifically: "I typed it and I remember typing it...Mr. Vose signed it, and it went out over his name and no one else's." As manager—in a position of authority established by his father—Selby had the power to formalize an accusation and distribute it to patrons. Yet as manager, he also signed off on the annual ledger entries showing the regular "station gain," the difference between credited and received butter-fat, which passed before him every year. According to Kyle: "He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign. I watched him do it a thousand times." Selby's authority in the system meant his actions shaped how the scandal developed.

## B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?

Duncan Keddie explicitly denied any Vose-Keddie family relationship in his 1968 genealogical notes: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses...I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins...It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me...and she never once put a Vose in it." This denial carried weight because Duncan knew his mother Orra's genealogy intimately and Orra herself was meticulous about family connections—"being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection." However, Cleve Oram in 1977 reported a different account, saying: "I will say this while I am on families...The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley. My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper." This is hearsay ("my mother had it from her mother") without documentary support, and it contradicts the family's own genealogical statement. Duncan's explicit, documented denial—written for permanent record by a man who kept school and taught arithmetic—outweighs Cleve's unsubstantiated hearsay. The most reliable conclusion is that no proven relationship existed between the families, though rumors circulated.
