# Section A: The Eight Stories (Stories 1–4)

## Story 1: Founding and Equipment Installation (1889–1897)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was organized in 1889 by farmers meeting at Larrow Green, and the main creamery was built in 1891. Its governing Article VII stipulated that patrons would be paid according to butter-fat test, not milk weight—a decision that became the hinge of all that followed. By the winter of 1895–96, the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley, to serve the farms of the upper end, principally Hessel Bottom. The station opened on May 4, 1896, with Ansel Keddie appointed as tester and separator man. The operation required precise measurement: milk would be weighed in, sampled, tested for butter-fat content, and separated; the cream hauled down to Larrow Green to Gideon Vose's creamery, the skim returned to farmers.

On April 14, 1897, the Association purchased its complete testing outfit from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet: six dozen milk-test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. at 35 cents each, twelve dozen test bottles, one acid measure, one hand tester for four bottles, plus freight. The invoice totaled $77.93. These pipettes were the standard charge for a butter-fat test: 17.6 c.c. of milk introduced into a graduated bottle whose neck was ruled for exactly that volume. In May 1897, this equipment was installed and put into use at Ostrey Hollow. For the first few seasons, the station had borrowed its testing glass from the Larrow Green creamery before receiving its own outfit. Ansel Keddie, described by those who knew him as a careful man to a degree others found trying, kept meticulous records from the opening onward. He weighed milk twice—once on the farm scale, once at the station—and recorded both figures in a ledger, checking for agreement. The system appeared sound. No one at this moment suspected that the pipettes themselves would be the source of thirty years of accusation, theory, and heartbreak.

---

## Story 2: The Low Tests and the Theories (1902–1908)

From nearly the beginning, the milk of Ostrey Hollow tested lower than milk from anywhere else in the county. The Ostrey Hollow station's average butter-fat test stood at 3.61; the Larrow Green creamery's own average over the same years was 3.78. Ordinarily a station will fluctuate seasonally—higher in flush summer months when cows are on pasture, lower in winter—but the Ostrey Hollow deficiency was constant at nineteen hundredths of a point in every month, wet February and dry June alike. No one connected this to the glassware. Instead, explanations multiplied.

The first complaint of record came in 1902. When Orra Keddie's husband came home that March, he was visibly upset: rumors at the store said the Hollow's milk ran poorer than the valley's. She comforted him—"it is not your cows and not your hands"—but Ansel replied that whatever the cause, "it is my figures they will look at." Year after year, through the next decade, farmers at Ostrey Hollow renewed their complaints at the Association's annual meetings, convinced they were being cheated.

In 1908, the Association sent a committee to investigate. They visited Hessel Bottom and examined the herds, paying particular attention to the farms of Emmet Brant, Roy Brant's father, whose milk showed the lowest tests of all. The committee's finding was written into the record: the wet hay of Hessel Bottom's boggy ground was thinning the cows' cream. This theory was bolstered by State Bulletin No. 214, published by the agricultural college at Farland, which documented that bog hay fed as whole roughage depressed butter-fat tests—by no more than five hundredths of a percentage point in winter, nothing measurable in summer. The committee's conclusion and the state's feeding trial seemed to explain the fifteen-year mystery. Ansel Keddie said nothing when he heard it, which was his way. He kept on testing and recording.

---

## Story 3: The Measurement Experiment (May–July 1911)

On May 8, 1911, the station's measuring glass broke. Ansel Keddie came home cross about the broken measure, and Orra noted it in her diary. Rather than wait for replacement, the station borrowed a measuring glass from elsewhere and used it for nine weeks, until July 10, 1911. During those nine weeks, something remarkable occurred: the station's tests stood markedly higher. When the station resumed its own glass, the figures fell back to their former level, nineteen hundredths below everywhere else.

The herds were unchanged. The pasture was unchanged. The tester was the same man, under no different supervision. The season, the machinery, the acid—all identical. Only the glass had changed. Ansel Keddie noted this fact in his test book and set a question mark beside it. He never committed to an interpretation. Years later, his son Duncan would recall that Ansel had said at supper and again at the door—"not his way"—that it was something in the glass. But what could he prove? He possessed a suspicion and no more: a piece of glass under suspicion set against the outfit the whole county used, the practice of every station in the region, the authority of the supply house. 

So Ansel did nothing. He kept on testing for another fourteen years, and every figure his glass gave him was recorded faithfully in his ledger. Those figures would later be cited as evidence against him. Yet this nine-week interval proved conclusively that the cause lay in the measurement glass, not in the milk, the men, the machinery, or the cows. The 1911 experiment solved the mystery—but no one would recognize the solution for forty-seven years.

---

## Story 4: The Water Accusation and Estrangement (1919)

By 1919, tensions had grown throughout the valley. Ansel Keddie had been testing milk at Ostrey Hollow for twenty-three years with results that seemed inexplicable. The managers and officers of the Association, frustrated and facing growing patron complaints, hypothesized that water must be added to the milk to explain the chronic shortfall. (Cream that weighs less would register lower in fat content if diluted.) In June 1919, Selby Vose, the manager—son of Gideon Vose who had organized the Association—prepared and signed a circular warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons about adding water to their milk.

Ansel Keddie received the circular. He did not put it in the mail. He did not leave it on the platform for farmers to collect. Instead, he took it round himself to eleven families on Hessel Bottom and stood at the door while each man read it. He came home at eleven at night with his hat still on, according to Orra's diary.

Two days later, Emmet Brant came up to the station yard. Emmet was Orra's brother, Roy's father, and the largest patron—the one with the most milk to haul, the one who lost the most by the low tests. He did not come inside. He stood in the yard and said to Ansel: "You have hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and Ansel knew it better than any man living. Then he asked why Ansel had carried the paper himself if he did not believe it." Ansel replied: "I carried it because I would not have another man carry it." Emmet went home. He did not enter the Keddie house again for ten years.

The circular won no admissions of water-adding. The state dairy inspector, when he tested with his lactometer in those years, found no evidence of added water. But the accusation had now been formal and public. The family was broken. Orra wrote: "I do not blame him and I will not say so to A." She kept the breach and the silence both.


# Section A: The Eight Stories (Stories 5–8)

## Story 5: The State Inspector's Condemnation (June 1923)

On June 12, 1923—twenty-six years after the equipment had been installed at Ostrey Hollow—the state dairy inspector, Merle Strawn, arrived on his circuit visit. Strawn was unrelated to any family in the valley, held no allegiance to patrons or managers, and carried only the authority of measurement. He took the station's milk pipettes and ran each one against a calibrated burette to verify that they delivered their marked volume. They did not. The pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. would not deliver 17.6 c.c. Strawn condemned the entire lot on the spot and directed the manager to order new glassware. He wrote down in his official notebook that the glass had been condemned. He drew no inference as to the years behind it.

The condemned glassware was packed into a wooden crate, placed under a tarpaulin, and carried up into the loft over the intake room at the station. It sat there, unremarked, for thirty-five years. Cleve Oram, the cream hauler's son, had seen that crate many times during those decades—put his hand on it—and asked someone what it was. He was told it was the old glass the state man made them throw out. Cleve thought nothing more of it. It was just a crate under a tarp.

New glass was ordered from Ammon & Sons to Tarnet. On June 28, 1923, the Association wrote to request replacement pipettes and test bottles. Ammon & Sons responded July 2: the order would be entered and shipped within the month. The letter included the sentence: "We regret the trouble. Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name."

What Ammon & Sons could not have known—and what no one would discover for thirty-five years—was that those condemned pipettes had been systematically short by approximately five percent. The mold had been reground, or molds hung crooked after being knocked off their nail, or a boy had sampled wrong and found them consistently—consistently wrong. Glass is made by people, and when a gauge hangs on a nail and gets knocked off the nail and hung back on it, the hanging can be true or false, and a false hanging is invisible until someone measures.

Once new glass arrived and came into use in 1924, the station's tests immediately rose. The new average became 3.85, which matched the level during the 1911 trial with the borrowed measure. The cause, once and for all, had been the glass. But no one yet knew it.

---

## Story 6: The Arbitration and Dismissal (1925)

By January 1925, patience had exhausted. The Ostrey Hollow patrons, led by Emmet Brant, filed a formal petition demanding investigation into the station's persistently low tests. They wanted their back pay. A board of arbitration was constituted to hear the case. Three sittings were held: one at Ashlin in the Cooperative office (where the lawyer was), and two at the station itself, in the weigh room where the scale and machinery stood—and where, unknown to all the men assembling there, the condemned glassware lay in the loft overhead, twenty feet up, under a tarpaulin, waiting.

Alonzo Frick, the arbitration chairman, heard three explanations offered. Selby Vose and the Association's officers maintained the water theory—that the Ostrey Hollow patrons deliberately added water to their milk. Hobart Sill, a buttermaker, claimed the station operator removed cream dishonestly and wrote low tests to conceal the theft. The third theory blamed the Hessel Bottom meadow's wet hay and poor minerals for thinning the cows' cream. 

The board examined only four pieces of evidence: Ansel Keddie's station test book, the Association's receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book (lent for the purpose), and Merle Strawn's state inspector notebook for the year 1923 when the glass was condemned. 

The water theory was unsustainable. Strawn himself had tested at Ostrey Hollow with his lactometer and found no added water from 1908 forward. The theft theory was disproven by the 1911 trial: the same man had tested during those nine weeks with the borrowed glass, tests rose, and yet no cream had been stolen—the experiment proved the glass, not the man. The bog-hay theory remained the last plausible explanation.

On April 30, 1925, Ansel Keddie was dismissed. He walked home at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm, hung it behind the shed door, and asked Orra what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it. Four days later, on May 4, 1925, the board handed down its finding: the Ostrey Hollow patrons would receive $3,800 in compensation, "without admission" of any cause. The payment was charged to the station account in the ledger. The dismissal said everything; the finding found nothing.

Ansel Keddie had been the tester for twenty-nine seasons. He never went to another Association meeting as long as he lived.

## Story 7: Discovery and Measurement (February–March 1958)

Thirty-three years after Ansel Keddie was dismissed, thirty-four years after he died, a young woman named Ivy Keddie—Ansel's granddaughter, Duncan's daughter—was working on a graduate degree in dairy husbandry at the State Agricultural College at Farland. For her thesis work, she was investigating the historical records of milk testing in the county. She obtained Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks from the state dairy office. In the volume for 1923, she found his entry: the Ostrey Hollow pipettes had been condemned.

She went to the station at Ostrey Hollow in February 1958. In the loft over the intake room, under a tarpaulin and about an inch of chaff accumulated over thirty-five years, she found a wooden crate. Inside were the station's original glassware: test bottles, acid measures, and pipettes. Eleven of the original pipettes were still intact.

On March 9, 1958, Ivy Keddie carried the crate east to the college laboratory at Farland. There she measured each of the eleven pipettes against a calibrated burette. Every one bore the mark 17.6 c.c. on its stem. Not one of them delivered 17.6 c.c. The average of the lot came to 16.27 c.c.—a shortfall of 1.33 c.c., or approximately 7.6 percent below the mark.

The mystery was solved. A short measure of milk, poured into a bottle whose graduated neck was ruled for the full measure, would produce a reading short in the same proportion—always the same proportion, nineteen hundredths. Nobody watered anything. Nobody stole anything. The scales were true, the acid was right, the cows were ordinary. A piece of glass blown to the wrong mold was wrong.

---

## Story 8: Vindication and the Price of Time (October 1958–1959 onward)

In October 1958, the State Agricultural College at Farland published Bulletin No. 471, written jointly by Professor Leland Vaught of the dairy husbandry department and Ivy Keddie, a graduate student. The bulletin presented the evidence: the condemned pipettes delivered 16.27 c.c. instead of 17.6, a proportional error of approximately 7.6 percent. Over the full years of use (1897–1923), that shortfall meant the Ostrey Hollow patrons were never paid for approximately $19,600 in butter-fat they had actually hauled to Larrow Green. They received, without admission, $3,800 in 1925. They had lost $15,800.

The discovery was reported by the Ashlin Chronicle as "Old Ordell Wrong Righted." Ivy Keddie was interviewed by telephone and said: "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not. He tested the milk in front of the state's own man and he tested it the way he was taught, and the reason his figures would not come right was in a crate over his head the entire time. I would like it printed that he never knew."

In 1959, Orin Falke, the county agricultural agent, prepared an official report for the county board of supervisors summarizing the entire history. He confirmed all the facts and added a note: Upon receiving State Bulletin No. 214 on bog hay, he recalculated. Bog hay depresses butter-fat tests by no more than 0.05 percent in winter and nothing in summer. This could not account for 0.19. Falke withdrew his earlier concession to the hay theory.

Ansel Keddie had died on February 13, 1934, at his home, "without much pain at the end of it," according to Orra's final diary entry. His funeral was well attended. Men came who had not spoken to him in nine years. Every one of them remarked on his carpentry—how true his doors hung, how well his work held—and not one of them named the scandal. Ansel had said once and only once: "A man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."

He was cleared in 1958, twenty-four years too late. His widow Orra, who had kept every letter and every receipt, had died in 1948. The vindication belonged to the record, not to them.

# Section B: Relationships (8 points)

## B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Ansel married Orra Brant in 1889. Their son was Duncan Keddie, born in 1894. Duncan had a daughter, Ivy, born in 1931. Therefore Ivy was Ansel's granddaughter. Duncan Keddie himself emphasized this relationship in his 1968 writing, stating: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise and once heard it said aloud in this town by a man who ought to have known." The editor of Orin Falke's 1959 county report later corrected an error, noting: "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece." This is confirmed directly by the family genealogy.

## B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant?

Ivy and Hazel were second cousins. Hazel's father was Roy Brant, and Roy was the son of Emmet Brant. Emmet was Orra's brother (Orra married Ansel Keddie). Therefore: Emmet and Orra were siblings; Emmet had a son Roy; Roy had a daughter Hazel; Orra had a son Duncan; Duncan had a daughter Ivy. This makes Roy and Duncan first cousins (children of a brother and sister), and their children Hazel and Ivy second cousins. Duncan Keddie's 1968 genealogy confirms: "Roy Brant and I are therefore first cousins...Roy's daughter is Hazel Brant. My daughter is Ivy. Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed." Hazel Brant's 1981 interview with the weigh book also confirms this relationship. Cleve Oram's 1977 account mistakenly called them "first cousins once removed," but the contemporary family records are clear.

## B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter?

Selby Vose was the son (not nephew, despite local confusion) of Gideon Vose. Gideon organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association and was its first president, serving as the architect of its bylaws and governance structure. Selby became manager of the Association in 1911 and held the position for approximately twenty-five years. This matters because Selby Vose signed the 1919 water-warning circular accusing the Ostrey Hollow patrons of deliberate adulteration. Bertram Kyle, the clerk, typed the circular and remembered typing it specifically: "I typed it and I remember typing it...Mr. Vose signed it, and it went out over his name and no one else's." As manager—in a position of authority established by his father—Selby had the power to formalize an accusation and distribute it to patrons. Yet as manager, he also signed off on the annual ledger entries showing the regular "station gain," the difference between credited and received butter-fat, which passed before him every year. According to Kyle: "He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign. I watched him do it a thousand times." Selby's authority in the system meant his actions shaped how the scandal developed.

## B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?

Duncan Keddie explicitly denied any Vose-Keddie family relationship in his 1968 genealogical notes: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses...I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins...It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me...and she never once put a Vose in it." This denial carried weight because Duncan knew his mother Orra's genealogy intimately and Orra herself was meticulous about family connections—"being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection." However, Cleve Oram in 1977 reported a different account, saying: "I will say this while I am on families...The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley. My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper." This is hearsay ("my mother had it from her mother") without documentary support, and it contradicts the family's own genealogical statement. Duncan's explicit, documented denial—written for permanent record by a man who kept school and taught arithmetic—outweighs Cleve's unsubstantiated hearsay. The most reliable conclusion is that no proven relationship existed between the families, though rumors circulated.


# Section C: Mathematics (14 points)

## C1. The Pipette Error

**(a) Proportion of error:**
The pipettes were marked 17.6 c.c. but delivered an average of 16.27 c.c. (Ivy Keddie's measurement, March 1958, confirmed in State Bulletin No. 471). The shortfall was 17.6 − 16.27 = 1.33 c.c. As a proportion: 1.33 / 17.6 = 0.0756, or approximately **7.6% short of the mark**. Equivalently, the pipettes delivered 16.27 / 17.6 = **92.4% of the marked volume**, or about **95% of delivered to marked** if rounding to the nearest 5%.

Since a full charge of milk is required to fill a graduated bottle marked for 17.6 c.c., a short charge produces a proportionally short reading: if only 16.27 c.c. is present, the fat reading will be low by the same 7.6 percent (or 0.076 points on the fat scale).

**(b) True average test and shortfall:**
The Ostrey Hollow recorded average was 3.61. The shortfall was a constant 0.19 points. Therefore, the true average test was 3.61 + 0.19 = **3.80 points**. The recorded tests were short by **0.19 points**, exactly matching the 0.19 hundredths mentioned in multiple sources (Hazel Brant, Orin Falke, Bertram Kyle).

Verification: 0.19 / 3.80 = 0.05, or 5%. However, the measured pipette error is 7.6%, not 5%. This discrepancy warrants examination. The likely resolution is that the 7.6% error on milk volume is equivalent to approximately 5% error on the fat reading if one accounts for non-linear effects in the test procedure or rounding in the average. Alternatively, not all pipettes were equally short; Ivy measured 11 survivors, and the average of "a lot" may have been computed with different precision than individual pipettes.

**(c) Total pounds of butterfat not credited:**
Over the full delivery years 1897–1923 (27 years of faulty glass in use), the station received approximately 40 million pounds of milk (confirmed by Bertram Kyle). At an average test of 3.80%, this milk actually contained 40,000,000 × 0.038 = 1,520,000 pounds of butterfat.

If the recorded average was 3.61%, and the station credited 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,440,000 pounds of butterfat to patrons, the difference is 1,520,000 − 1,440,000 = **80,000 pounds of butterfat not credited**. 

However, the Ostrey Hollow station's cream was then sent to Larrow Green for churning. Bertram Kyle noted a standard "station gain" of 5.25% annually—the difference between butterfat credited at the station and butterfat received at the main creamery. This was routine loss due to normal inefficiency in separation and transfer. If we apply 5.25% to the already-short crediting: the patrons lost 80,000 pounds to the pipette error, but an additional loss due to normal separation/transfer would already have been accounted for in the 5.25% gain. The question asks for pounds "not credited to patrons"—i.e., credited neither at Ostrey nor received at Larrow. The most direct reading: **80,000 pounds of butterfat were never credited to the patrons due to the pipette error**.

Yet multiple sources refer to "sixty-seven thousand pounds." Cleve Oram reports: "Sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat was what they made it, over the whole run of years, that the Hollow men were never paid for." The exact figure given by the State Bulletin or the Farland College measurement is not quoted directly in the retellings provided. I cannot determine precisely from the sources whether it is 67,000 or 80,000 or another figure, but the order of magnitude is 60,000–80,000 pounds.

**(d) Money owed and received:**
Bertram Kyle noted: "butterfat over that whole stretch averaged twenty-five cents the pound, taking the fat years with the lean." At $0.25/pound, 67,000–80,000 pounds would be worth $16,750–$20,000.

Multiple sources confirm this range. The Ashlin Chronicle (October 1958) states: "The bulletin works out what the Ostrey Hollow patrons were never paid for the butter fat they actually hauled to that station, and the figure is nineteen thousand six hundred dollars ($19,600)."

Therefore, patrons were owed approximately **$19,600** for unpaid butterfat.

They received **$3,800** in the 1925 arbitration award.

**Fraction of loss recovered:** $3,800 / $19,600 = 0.1939, or approximately **19.4%, or roughly one-fifth** of what they were owed.

## C2. The Largest Patron's Year (1910)

Emmet Brant was the largest patron—Roy Brant's father, Hessel Bottom's principal farm. In 1910:

**(a) Butterfat credited at station:**
The station's recorded average test was 3.61. For a full year of delivery at a major farm, a reasonable estimate from the station's intake would be approximately 120,000–150,000 pounds of milk. Using 140,000 pounds as a middle estimate: 140,000 × 0.0361 = 5,054 pounds of butterfat credited.

However, the sources do not provide Emmet Brant's specific 1910 delivery. I note that Bertram Kyle states "the station accounts...came in from the creamery side and from the stations, and were made up, and were copied, and were carried forward, and once a year were laid before the annual meeting." The individual farm records are not quoted in the retellings. I cannot determine from the sources provided the exact 1910 figure for the largest patron.

**(b) Butterfat owed and not credited:**
If we assume a typical major farm delivery of ~140,000 pounds at the true test of 3.80%, then 140,000 × 0.038 = 5,320 pounds were actually present. Shortfall: 5,320 − 5,054 = **266 pounds owed and not credited** (assuming the 7.6% pipette error applies uniformly).

**(c) Money value:**
At $0.25/pound: 266 × $0.25 = **$66.50** per year for a farm the size of Emmet Brant's.

**Note:** These are estimates. The sources do not provide the specific 1910 delivery figure for the largest patron, so exact calculation is impossible.

## C3. Station Accounts Over Full Delivery Years

**(a) Total butterfat credited at station:**
Bertram Kyle: "Forty million pounds of milk received at that station" over the complaint years, "averaged 25 cents the pound" for butterfat.

At 3.61% test: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,440,000 pounds of butterfat credited to patrons on the station test.

**(b) Butterfat actually received at Larrow Green:**
The station gained 5.25% annually—the difference between what was credited at Ostrey and what arrived at Larrow in the station's cream. This was routine loss to separation efficiency, transfer spillage, etc.

Of 1,440,000 pounds credited, if 5.25% was lost in transit, then 1,440,000 × (1 − 0.0525) = 1,440,000 × 0.9475 = 1,364,400 pounds reached Larrow Green.

Alternatively: the "station gain" of 5.25% means 5.25% was retained as "gain" (loss/shrinkage), so 94.75% was received. This is consistent.

**(c) Difference as percentage:**
Butterfat credited at station: 1,440,000 pounds.
Butterfat received at Larrow: 1,364,400 pounds.
Difference: 1,440,000 − 1,364,400 = 75,600 pounds.

This is 75,600 / 1,440,000 = 0.0525, or **5.25%** of the amount **credited at the station**.

(Note: if asked as percentage of what was received, it is 75,600 / 1,364,400 = 0.0554, or about 5.54%. The question says "express as a percentage...and be exact about what you are taking a percentage **of**." The station gain is conventionally expressed as a percentage of the credited amount, not the received amount.)

## C5. Duration of Faulty Glass and First Season Milk

**(a) How long the glass was in use, and over how many full delivery years:**

The equipment was put into use on **May 1, 1897**.
The state inspector condemned the glass on **June 12, 1923**.

**Time in use: 26 years and approximately 41 days** (from May 1, 1897 to June 12, 1923).

However, the question asks about "full delivery years." A milk-hauling year typically runs from late spring (after thaw, when milk volume increases) through the next spring. Using fiscal/accounting years:

The station opened May 4, 1896, and received milk starting then. The 1897 season began spring 1897; subsequent seasons are: 1897–1898, 1898–1899, ..., 1922–1923 (the glass was condemned in June 1923, partway through 1923).

**Full delivery years affected: 1897–1923 = 26 complete years** (or 27 if counting partial years, but the question asks for "full" years).

**Why they differ:** The glass was in use for 26 years and 41 days (calendar time), but 27 milk-delivery seasons overlapped with that period (spring 1897 through spring 1924, when new glass went into use). The math calculations typically run over the complete delivery years 1897–1923 (26 full years), not including the partial year of 1924 when new glass was installed.

Bertram Kyle wrote: "People have asked me what the Hollow took in that first year and I have always said the same: a good eleven hundred thousand pounds, which is what I understood at the time from men who were there."

**First season milk (1896–1897): approximately 1,100,000 pounds** (11 hundred thousand).

# Section D: Logic (10 points)

## D1. The 1911 Measurement Experiment

**When and how long:** The station's measuring glass broke on May 8, 1911. A borrowed measure from elsewhere was used starting immediately. The borrowed glass was used for nine weeks, until July 10, 1911 (confirmed by Orin Falke's official report: "On the 8th of May the station's own measuring glass was broken...a measure borrowed from elsewhere was used in its place for nine weeks, until the 10th of July").

**The variable:** The type/source of glass pipette used to measure milk samples for the butter-fat test.

**What was held constant:** The herds (same cows, same farms), the pasture (same June-July conditions), the tester (same man, Ansel Keddie), the season, the machinery (separator, acid measure), the test procedure itself.

**The result:** During the nine weeks with borrowed glass, "the station's tests stood markedly higher. On the day the station resumed its own glass they fell back to their former level" (Falke). Hazel Brant adds that tests were 3.42 in 1910 (on the station's own glass) and that the 1911 episode showed tests could rise.

**What it establishes:** That a systematic low-bias in the measurement glass alone is sufficient to produce low test results, independent of milk quality, herd genetics, pasture, or tester honesty.

**What it does NOT establish:** The magnitude of the error or why the glass was short (manufacturing defect, mold degradation, etc.). It also does not establish how long the glass had been defective before May 1911—whether since installation in 1897 or after some later date. The 1911 experiment proves the glass is implicated but does not prove the error was present for 14 years prior.

## D2. Three Independent Reasons Why the Tester Cannot Have Been Stealing Cream

**Reason 1 (from 1911 experiment):** If Ansel Keddie had been removing cream and covering it with low tests, he would have had to adjust the volume of cream reported. Yet during the nine weeks (May 8–July 10, 1911) when he used a borrowed measure, his tests rose, while he operated the separator and had the same opportunity to remove cream as any other day. The change in test result with only the glass changing, the operator and procedures constant, rules out the theft mechanism. This is drawn from Orin Falke's 1959 report and is independent of any later measurement of the glass.

**Reason 2 (from Bertram Kyle's ledger entries):** Kyle, the clerk who copied the station accounts every year, noted that the ledger showed "station gain"—the difference between butterfat credited and butterfat received at Larrow Green. If cream had been stolen at the station, the amount received at Larrow would be *lower* than credited. But Kyle states: "the skim came back out the same platform for the farmers to take home...The cream went into our cans [to Larrow]." More tellingly, Cleve Oram (cream hauler's son) reported: "My father...always said there was less of it coming off that platform than went onto it"—meaning *less cream coming out than went in* to the separator, which is physically impossible if the separator works correctly. This confusion (thinking cream disappeared rather than was proportionally reduced by the test) shows the opacity of the process to observers outside the station but does not constitute evidence of theft. Ansel could not steal cream without the ledgers showing it as a deficit, and they do not.

**Reason 3 (from herds tested elsewhere):** Rosalie Cudd (from r10 in the handoff brief) reported that Hessel Bottom farmers' milk tested 3.78–3.82 at the cheese factory on the ridge when hauled there in summer. This is the *same herds* that tested 3.61 at Ostrey Hollow. If the cream had been stolen by the station operator, the farmers' own samples would have shown signs of tampering or fraud. Instead, the same milk tests high elsewhere and low only at Ostrey Hollow, proving the station itself—not the milk or the theft—is the source. This is from an independent narrator (Rosalie Cudd) and would hold true even if the 1911 experiment had never occurred.

**Were these reasons recognized by the bookkeeper?** Bertram Kyle, the bookkeeper, stated clearly: "he never read the lines above them"—the manager Selby Vose signed off on totals without examining the supporting entries. Kyle does not state that he himself questioned the ledger. Indeed, Kyle wrote: "It had a name. That is the thing about a name. A figure with a name on it has been explained, and you rule under it and go on to the next."

The "station gain" was named, explained as routine loss, and therefore unquestioned. Kyle did not voice doubt about the ledger entries at the time, though in 1953 he reflected: "It was worse than a secret. It was in plain sight with a name on it."

---

## D3. Did the Manager Know the Tests Ran Low?

**Answer:** Yes, the manager Selby Vose almost certainly knew—or should have known—that the station's tests ran low.

**Justification:**

1. **Explicit record:** Every year from 1911 onward (when Selby became manager), he signed the station account ledger showing the "station gain"—the documented difference between butterfat credited at Ostrey and butterfat received at Larrow. This difference persisted year after year at approximately 5.25%. For an account to show a consistent pattern, a manager reviewing it would need to notice the pattern.

2. **The three decades of complaint:** Bertram Kyle notes: "The patrons afterward complained of" low tests from 1902 onward, and "the figure was got out for the arbitration." The Association's annual meetings, held every year, discussed the Ostrey Hollow issue "in one form or another, in every decade" (Orin Falke). As manager from 1911 forward, Selby would have attended these meetings and heard the complaints and arguments directly.

3. **The 1919 circular:** Selby drafted and signed a circular accusing patrons of adding water, distributed to 11 families. This action shows he was aware enough of the problem to take formal action against the patrons. His awareness that something was wrong is demonstrated; the question is whether he knew what was wrong.

4. **Lack of investigation:** Despite signing off on ledgers showing the discrepancy and hearing complaints about it for a decade, Selby did not order an investigation of the glassware until the state dairy inspector arrived uninvited in June 1923. This suggests he either (a) did not suspect the glass, having accepted the "bog hay" and "water" theories, or (b) did not want to investigate.

**Conclusion:** Selby Vose knew the tests were running low and persistently lower than other stations. Whether he understood the cause (and whether he could have known without professional inspection) is less certain. But that he knew the *fact* of the shortfall is beyond question.

# Section E: Contradictions (8 points)

## E1. Direct Contradictions

**Contradiction 1: Equipment installation date**

- **Kyle's account (R23):** "its own outfit came from the supply house and went into use on the first of May, 1898."
- **Junia Ammon's account (R20):** "The Larrow Valley Association put that outfit into use at its Ostrey Hollow station on the first of May, 1897."
- **Ivy Keddie / State Bulletin (R19):** "Six dozen pipettes came in that crate, at thirty-five cents apiece, and they went into use at the station in the spring of 1897."
- **Orra Keddie's diary (R24):** "May 1, 1897. A. says the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green."

**Resolution:** The correct date is May 1, 1897, not 1898. The sources are:
- The invoice itself (dated April 14, 1897) from Ammon & Sons.
- Junia Ammon's company records, writing in 1949, citing the firm's own files.
- Ivy Keddie's research.
- Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary (May 1, 1897).

Kyle's "1898" is the only dissenting account, and it is based on memory (Kyle was seventy-three when writing in 1953, recalling events 55 years earlier). **Bertram Kyle misremembered or miswrote the year; 1897 is correct.**

**Contradiction 5: Hay feed trial reduction**

# Section F: Theory (6 points)

## F1. Causal Theory

**Actual cause:** The Ostrey Hollow station's testing glassware—specifically the milk-test pipettes—were systematically short in capacity by approximately 7.6% (delivering 16.27 c.c. instead of the marked 17.6 c.c.). This proportional error was introduced at the glass supplier (Ammon & Sons, Tarnet) in 1897, likely due to a reground mold or incorrect hanging of the gauge during manufacture. Every milk sample tested with these pipettes throughout 1897–1923 received a proportionally reduced volume, resulting in proportionally lower butter-fat readings: 7.6% lower on the fat scale. Over twenty-six full delivery years, this systematic bias deprived the Ostrey Hollow patrons of accurate payment for their milk.

**Why that size?** The 7.6% shortfall (or ~5% as rounded in some contexts) corresponds exactly to the difference between the marked volume (17.6 c.c.) and the delivered volume (16.27 c.c.). This was a manufacturing defect inherent in the mold or glassblowing process; no calibration or user action could correct it without replacing the glass.

**Why it began when it did:** The error originated at the point of manufacture in 1897 and was installed immediately upon receipt (May 1, 1897). There is no evidence the glass was initially correct and later became short; the defect was present from the beginning.

**Why it ended when it did:** On June 12, 1923, State Dairy Inspector Merle Strawn performed a routine calibration check and found the pipettes out of spec. He condemned the entire lot on the spot and directed replacement. New glass was ordered and installed by July 1923. From that point forward, tests at Ostrey Hollow matched the county average (~3.80%), proving the correction.

**Why nothing at the station or on the farms had to change:** The low tests were not caused by milk quality, herd genetics, pasture conditions, farming practices, or the tester's honesty. All of these were constant and adequate. The error was entirely in the measurement apparatus, so no change to operations was required to eliminate it—only replacement of the glass.

---

**Three competing false explanations and the evidence that defeats each:**

1. **Explanation 1: The Ostrey Hollow patrons deliberately added water to their milk (advanced by Selby Vose, Association manager, starting 1919).**
   - **Evidence defeating it:** The state dairy inspector tested with a lactometer at every visit from 1908 forward and found no added water (Orin Falke, R22). Additionally, the same milk from Ostrey Hollow farmers tested 3.78–3.82 at the Alder Corners cheese factory on the ridge (Rosalie Cudd, R10 per handoff), proving the milk itself was not watered—only its test at Ostrey was low.

2. **Explanation 2: Ansel Keddie, the station tester, stole cream and wrote low tests to conceal the loss (advanced by an Association officer at the 1925 hearing; supported by some suspicion, e.g., Hobart Sill's account in R14).**
   - **Evidence defeating it:** During the nine weeks of May 8–July 10, 1911, when the station's own glass broke and a borrowed measure was used, the tests rose despite the same operator and same opportunity to steal cream. A theft mechanism would not suddenly reverse itself with only the glass changed (Orin Falke, R22). Moreover, the ledgers showed "station gain"—cream received at Larrow consistently matched credited amounts within the routine 5.25% inefficiency (Bertram Kyle, R23), not a deficit that would indicate theft.

3. **Explanation 3: The wet meadow soil and coarse hay of Hessel Bottom caused cows to produce lower-fat milk (advanced by a 1908 Association committee and cited by Dr. Kepp, veterinarian, in 1926).**
   - **Evidence defeating it:** Hessel Bottom farmers' milk tested 3.78–3.82 when hauled to other creameries (Rosalie Cudd, R10). The same herds, same farms, same hay produced normal-fat milk elsewhere. The low test was unique to Ostrey Hollow, not to the milk source. Furthermore, State Bulletin No. 214 (bog hay study) showed a maximum reduction of 0.05% in winter and nothing in summer—far insufficient to account for the 0.19 observed deficit (Orin Falke's correction, R22).

---

**Subsidiary questions:**

- **Did the 1925 board of arbitration examine the condemned glassware?** No. The glassware was condemned in June 1923 and placed in the loft. During the 1925 hearing (March 17–May 4), Bertram Kyle specifically notes that four pieces of evidence were examined: "The station's own test book, in Keddie's hand. The receiving book. Mr. Emmet Brant's private weigh book, which he lent for the purpose and wanted back. And the state inspector's notebook for the year he condemned the glass." The notebook for 1923 (documenting the condemnation) was shown, but not the condemned glass itself. Cleve Oram notes that the crate was in the loft "twenty feet" above where the hearing was held at the station itself, unknown to the board members. Kyle adds: "I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked." The board did not make the connection between the condemnation note and the possible cause of the shortfall.

- **What did the tester himself believe the cause was?** Ansel Keddie suspected the glass beginning in summer 1911 (his son Duncan noted he said at supper and again at the door that "it was something in the glass," and Ansel marked a question mark in the ledger that year). However, he could not prove it and set the suspicion aside. In all his years afterward—fourteen more years of testing—he did not assert the glass theory publicly or lodge a complaint about it. According to Orra Keddie's diary, when others blamed the hay or accused him of water-adding, "he said nothing at all, which is his way." Ansel carried the private suspicion to his death in 1934, never vindicated. His granddaughter Ivy later stated: "He tested the milk in front of the state's own man and he tested it the way he was taught, and the reason his figures would not come right was in a crate over his head the entire time. I would like it printed that he never knew."

# Section G: Summary (4 points)

## G1. The Eight-Story History in 150 Words

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery (founded 1889) paid farmers by milk butter-fat test, not weight. The Ostrey Hollow skimming station (opened 1896) tested milk using equipment purchased in 1897 from a supplier in Tarnet. Unknown to anyone, the six dozen pipettes were systematically short—delivering only 95% of their marked capacity—causing all tests to read 7.6% low. Over twenty-six years, this bias accumulated into complaints of "water-adding," "theft," or "poor meadow hay." In 1911, a broken measure was borrowed; tests rose. In 1923, a state inspector condemned the glass but drew no inference. In 1925, the station tester was dismissed without admission of cause; patrons received partial restitution. In 1958, the tester's granddaughter, a college researcher, found the condemned glass in the loft and measured it, proving the pipettes short. The scientific explanation emerged three decades too late for the tester, who died in 1934. Eight separate voices—farmer, clerk, inspector, widow, scientist—were needed to reconstruct what one piece of glass had concealed.
