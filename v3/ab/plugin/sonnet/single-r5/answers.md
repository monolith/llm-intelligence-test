# Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

*(Method note: throughout, where sources conflict I favor (1) verbatim-quoted primary documents over paraphrase, (2) contemporaneous records over later recollection, (3) figures that are internally arithmetic-consistent with each other over ones that are not, and (4) independent witnesses over repetitions of the same rumor. I say explicitly which rule I used each time.)*

## Story 1 — The Founding of the Association and the Rule That Made the Test Matter

**Who:** Gideon Vose and the farmers of Larrow Valley.
**What:** In 1889, farmers of the valley met at the Larrow Green schoolhouse and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (r12, r22). Gideon Vose was elected first president and wrote the by-laws himself (r12, r18, r23). The main creamery was built at Larrow Green — 1891 per r22/r12. The by-laws included Article VII, the rule that every patron would be paid according to the pounds of butterfat his milk tested for, at the station where he delivered, rather than by the weight of milk delivered. This article is the entire reason the later dispute cost anyone money: under payment-by-weight, a mistaken test would have cost nothing (r22 makes this point explicitly).
**When:** Organization 1889; creamery built c. 1891. The date Article VII/payment-by-test was *adopted* is contested: 1891 (r12), 1892 (r02), 1893 (r15). **This cannot be settled from the sources.** The most careful source, county agent Orin Falke's 1959 report (r22), states plainly that the printed by-laws carry no date and that the Association's own early minute book was lost in an office move, and that his office "does not attempt" to fix the year. Since the only source that actually checked the primary record found no answer, and the three other claims are casual restatements with no cited evidence, I record this as unresolved rather than picking a "majority."
**Quantities:** No monetary quantities attach to founding itself; its importance is structural — it is why Article VII, not any dollar figure, is the hinge of the whole affair (r22 ¶3).
**Documents:** r01, r02 (adoption date, Selby Vose as "son"), r12 (founding, 1891 date, Selby Vose as "nephew" — later shown wrong, see Story 8/Section B), r15 (Grigg's 1893 claim), r18 (Duncan Keddie's genealogical correction of the Vose relationship), r22 (the only source that investigated the minute book and found it lost), r23 (clerk's account of the by-laws and Gideon/Selby Vose's actual father-son relationship).

## Story 2 — Building Ostrey Hollow Station and the Bad Lot of Glass

**Who:** The Association's officers, Ansel Keddie (hired as tester), and Ammon & Sons of Tarnet (the supply house).
**What:** In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up-valley, to serve Hessel Bottom's six farms (r22). It opened 4 May 1896 (r22) with Ansel Keddie as tester and separator man, a post he held 29 seasons (r18, r22, r24). For its first partial season the station borrowed testing glass from the Larrow Green creamery (r03's note "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green," corroborated by Hobart Sill, who says he personally supplied equipment from his own bench, r14). The station's own outfit was ordered from Ammon & Sons: an invoice dated 14 April 1897, reproduced verbatim by the firm's own historian (r20) — six dozen milk-test pipettes at 17.6 c.c., 35 cents each ($25.20); twelve dozen test bottles ($31.68); one acid measure (90 cents); one four-bottle hand tester ($18.00); freight ($2.15); **total $77.93**. This glass went into use 1 May 1897, fixed by Orra Keddie's own diary entry written that week ("A. says the station's own glass came up," r24) — the single most authoritative date, being contemporaneous. (Vaught's bulletin, r11, and clerk Kyle's 1953 memory, r23, both say 1898; both are later and I judge them slips.)

Unknown to anyone, the pipettes — though blown to the 17.6 c.c. mark — actually delivered only about 16.72 c.c. on average, a manufacturing defect Ammon's own historian later attributes to a reground mold that ran the lot short without anyone on the blowing floor catching it, because a spot-check of a short lot finds it "consistently wrong," which no ordinary check catches (r20).
**Quantities:** $77.93 total invoice; 72 pipettes at 17.6 c.c. marked, ~16.72 c.c. actual, 35¢ each.
**Documents:** r03, r04, r07 (curator's account of the invoice and glass), r11 (Vaught, giving a conflicting 1898 date and quantity), r14 (Sill on borrowed glass), r19 (newspaper, with a garbled invoice total), r20 (Ammon & Sons' own reproduced invoice — the primary source), r22, r23, r24 (Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary).

## Story 3 — The Chronic Complaint and the Three Rival Theories

**Who:** The patrons of Hessel Bottom (led in early complaints by Emmet Brant and neighbors), against the Association's officers, bookkeeper, and a series of investigating committees.
**What:** From very shortly after the glass went into use, Ostrey Hollow's tests ran consistently below the rest of the valley. The first complaint of record was 1902 (r09, r22). The gap held steady at roughly two-tenths of a point year-round — winter and summer alike, which matters because a feed-based cause should vary seasonally and this did not (r09: "seventeen hundredths"; r16: "twenty-one hundredths"; r22, citing the bulletin: "nineteen hundredths," the figure I treat as authoritative since it is the one actually derived from the calibrated measurement — see Section C). Three explanations circulated for a quarter-century: **(1) watering**, the Association manager's theory, formalized in a 1919 circular (Story 5); **(2) cream theft by the tester**, insinuated at the 1925 hearing by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague (r13); and **(3) wet/bog hay thinning the cream**, the theory of a 1908 investigating committee under Nyle Grigg (r15) and later given a mineral-deficiency mechanism by veterinarian Roland Kepp in a 1926 paper to the county farm club (r16). All three theories are refuted in Section F; none is correct, but all three were argued in good faith by people close to the case, and each attracted genuine, non-trivial evidence in its favor at the time (the 1908 committee genuinely found no dishonesty; Kepp genuinely found parallel effects on other wet bottoms).
**When:** 1902 onward, with committee investigations in 1908 and continuing grievance through the 1910s–20s.
**Quantities:** gap of ~0.19 points (I resolve to this value; see Section C); recorded station average 3.61%, valley average 3.78–3.80%.
**Documents:** r05, r06/r07 (curator/Strawn material), r09 (Roy Brant's ledger-based estimate), r13 (Frick on Teague's theft insinuation), r14 (Sill's watering counter-theory), r15 (Grigg committee, wet hay), r16 (Kepp's mineral theory), r22 (Falke's synthesis and figure of 0.19).

## Story 4 — The Nine Weeks of 1911: The Accidental Experiment

**Who:** Ansel Keddie and the Hessel Bottom patrons, unwittingly.
**What:** On 8 May 1911 the station's own measuring glass broke, and a borrowed measure was used in its place until 10 July 1911 — nine weeks (r03's precise dates, corroborated by r07's summary leaf and independently by county agent Falke's 1959 report, r22, which gives the identical dates). Through those nine weeks, with the same herds, same pasture, same tester, and same oversight, the station's recorded tests rose markedly; the day the station's own glass resumed, they fell back to the old level (r22 ¶6). Ansel Keddie noted the fact in his book and set a question mark against it, but — per his son Duncan's much later account (r18) — told his family at supper, uncharacteristically and twice, that he believed "it was something in the glass." He never wrote this suspicion down because, in his son's words, "he was a man who would not put down what he could not prove" (r18). Veterinarian Kepp later dismissed the whole episode as "only six weeks" of flush-season data (r16) — an outlier duration I reject as hearsay-based, since he cites it as something "urged upon me," not his own record.
**Why it matters:** this is the one piece of evidence in the whole record that is a true controlled comparison rather than a retrospective calculation — see Section D1 for full analysis of what it proves and what it does not.
**Quantities:** 9 weeks, 8 May – 10 July 1911.
**Documents:** r03 (precise dates), r07 (summary leaf), r09 (Roy Brant's puzzled diary-adjacent account, "and there it sat for near on fifty years"), r11 (Vaught's bulletin treatment, calling it "an experiment that ran itself"), r16 (Kepp's dismissal as six weeks), r18 (Ansel Keddie's private suspicion), r22 (Falke's dated, authoritative account), r24 (Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary entry, "A. home cross about a broken measure," 8 May 1911).


# Answers — Part 2: Section A, Stories 5–8; Section B

## Story 5 — The 1919 Water Circular and the Brant–Keddie Rift

**Who:** Manager Selby Vose, tester Ansel Keddie, and Emmet Brant (Keddie's own brother-in-law and the largest Hessel Bottom patron).
**What:** In 1919 the Association's manager Selby Vose circulated a written warning to Ostrey Hollow patrons about adding water to their milk (r02, r09, r14, r17, r23, r24). Ansel Keddie, though the accusation implicated his own testing, personally delivered the letters by hand rather than mailing them or leaving them on the platform, and stood at each man's door while he read it (r24's contemporaneous diary). Two days later Emmet Brant — Keddie's own wife's brother, a patron since the station opened — confronted Keddie in the yard, asking why he had carried the paper himself "if he did not believe it." Keddie answered that he "would not have another man carry it." The two men, brothers-in-law of thirty years' standing, did not speak again until Brant's death in 1929 (r09, r18, r24). The number of families who received the letter is disputed — nine (r17) versus eleven (r24, the contemporaneous diary) — and I favor eleven since it is the eyewitness, dated-at-the-time account by the deliverer's own wife.
**Why it matters:** it shows the accusation was taken with total seriousness inside the tester's own household, and it converted a technical dispute into a permanent family rupture, which is why later narrators (Roy Brant, Hazel Brant, Duncan Keddie) all treat it as a wound rather than an argument.
**Quantities:** 9 or 11 families addressed (unresolved, see above); zero replies received by the manager (r17: "answered by none of them, which in that valley is a shout").
**Documents:** r02, r09 (Roy Brant, on the family rift and its cause), r14 (Sill, approving of the circular), r17 (Hazel Brant, "nine families"), r23 (Kyle, who typed the circular), r24 (Orra Keddie's diary, contemporaneous, "eleven families," and the yard confrontation).

## Story 6 — The 1925 Board of Arbitration and Ansel Keddie's Dismissal

**Who:** The Hessel Bottom patrons (petitioners), the Association's officers, bookkeeper Wilbur Teague, chairman Alonzo Frick, and Ansel Keddie.
**What:** The patrons petitioned in January 1925. A board of arbitration, chaired by attorney Alonzo Frick, sat from 17 March to 4 May 1925 — one session at the Cooperative's Ashlin office, two at the station itself in the weigh room, at Frick's own initiative (r13, r21). All three rival theories (watering, theft, wet hay) were argued (r13, r22). Keddie testified publicly that he had tested "thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons" and denied ever taking cream; Frick believed him, and privately, in a corridor conversation Frick recalls in 1946, Keddie said he had "always thought the patrons were at fault" but would not say so publicly out of loyalty to neighbors of thirty years (r13) — a claim that sits uneasily against Duncan Keddie's account that his father privately blamed the glass as early as 1911 (r18; see Section F). The board found no cause established and awarded the patrons **$3,800**, "without admission" (r04, r13, r22, r23 — four independent tellings agree on this figure; r21's "$3,500" is a lone, secondhand figure I reject, see Section E). Ansel Keddie was dismissed **30 April 1925**, four days *before* the award was announced, while the board was still sitting (r13, r18, r22, r24's diary all agree on this date and sequence) — a fact Frick says he "never been able to think well of the Association for."
**Quantities:** award $3,800; dismissal 30 April 1925; award 4 May 1925.
**Documents:** r05, r06/r07, r08, r13 (Frick, presiding — most authoritative), r18 (Duncan Keddie, family account), r21 (Oram, dissenting on the amount), r22 (Falke's report), r23 (Kyle, the clerk who processed the paperwork), r24 (Orra Keddie's diary, the dismissal day itself: "he came up the road at his ordinary hour... asked what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it.").

## Story 7 — The 1923 Condemnation of the Glassware

**Who:** State dairy circuit inspector Merle Strawn.
**What:** In the course of an ordinary circuit visit, Strawn ran the station's milk pipettes against a calibrated burette, found they would not deliver their marked charge, and condemned the whole lot on the spot, directing the manager to order new glass (r06/r07, r18, r19, r20, r22, r23). New glass was ordered from Ammon & Sons within the week and in use within the month (r20, r23). **No one at the time connected this defect to the twenty-six years of low tests that preceded it** (r22 ¶11 states this explicitly) — the condemnation was treated, in the office, as no more remarkable than "a broken window" (r23). This event is entirely separate from, and earlier than, the 1925 arbitration; the crate of condemned glass was simply put up in the loft over the intake room and left there (r18, r21).
**When:** The date is contested — 12 June 1923 (r06/r07, r22, and the bulletin's own closing paragraph as quoted in r04/r07/r11 — three independently-transmitted matches) versus 12 July 1923 (r08, r12) versus "1922" (r23, an elderly clerk's memory lapse thirty years on). I resolve to **12 June 1923**, since it is attested identically by the bulletin's actual text and by Falke's separately-researched 1959 report — two genuinely independent chains.
**Quantities:** whole lot condemned; new glass in use within a month; station's average rose to 3.85% from 1924 onward and held there (r22) — itself indirect proof of the defect, though "no person connected the one thing with the other at the time."
**Documents:** r06/r07 (curator), r08 (Vose, "12 July"), r18 (Duncan Keddie, "the state man condemned that glass in twenty-one" — NB: r18 gives yet a fourth date, 1921, which I judge a further outlier and reject against the weight of r06/07+r22+the bulletin text), r19, r20 (Ammon & Sons' own letter of 2 July 1923, replying to the Association's order), r22, r23.

## Story 8 — The 1958 Rediscovery and Bulletin No. 471

**Who:** Ivy Keddie (Ansel Keddie's granddaughter, a Farland graduate student), Professor Leland Vaught, and Rosalie Cudd.
**What:** In February 1958, Ivy Keddie, researching the station for her college work, climbed into the loft over the old intake room and found the condemned crate of pipettes, undisturbed since 1923 (r17, r18, r19, r22). She carried it to Farland and, on 9 March 1958, measured the surviving pipettes — **nine** of them, in condition to be measured — against a calibrated burette (r22; see Section E for the conflicting "eleven" claims). Each was blown to the standard 17.6 c.c. mark; the average actual delivery was **16.72 c.c.**, short in the same proportion across the lot. This — a proportional, universal, unchanging instrument error — explained every feature of the old complaint at once: its constancy across seasons, its resistance to the 1908 committee's honest inspection, and its correlation with the 1911 borrowed-measure anomaly. The findings were published as **Bulletin No. 471** (State Agricultural College at Farland, October 1958), jointly authored by Vaught and Keddie, with research assistance from Rosalie Cudd of Alder Corners, who supplied her father's independent cheese-test records as corroborating evidence (r10, r19). The bulletin explicitly and formally cleared Ansel Keddie, twenty-four years after his death (r24's editorial note). The story was picked up by the county newspaper in October 1958 (r19) and summarized officially by the county agricultural agent in 1959 (r22), who also corrected the newspaper's error calling Ivy Keddie Ansel's "great-niece" rather than his granddaughter.
**Quantities:** 9 pipettes measured; 17.6 c.c. marked vs 16.72 c.c. actual; see Section C for the full monetary and butterfat reconstruction.
**Documents:** r04, r07 (the bulletin's content via the curator), r09, r10 (Cudd), r11 (Vaught's introduction, with the internal arithmetic error — Section E), r17 (Hazel Brant), r18 (Duncan Keddie), r19 (newspaper), r22 (Falke's report and correction).

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**
Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's **granddaughter**. Chain: Ansel and Orra Keddie's son was Duncan Keddie (r18, r24); Duncan's daughter is Ivy (r18, explicit: "which makes her my father's granddaughter"). This is corroborated by r09 (Roy Brant, an independent family member, describing Ivy as "Duncan's girl"). Two sources point elsewhere: the newspaper (r19) and the first draft of the county report (r22) both call her Ansel's "great-niece." I resolve in favor of granddaughter because (a) it is stated directly by Ansel's own son, the person best positioned to know, specifically to correct a rumor he says he has "already twice seen it printed" wrong, and (b) the county report itself (r22) *retracts* its own "great-niece" claim in an appended note after verification: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece." A source correcting itself on direct inquiry outranks an uncorrected newspaper guess.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**
They are **second cousins**. Orra (Keddie) and Emmet Brant were brother and sister (r09, r18, r24); their children, Duncan Keddie and Roy Brant, are therefore first cousins (r09, r17, r18 — three independent, family-insider statements, worded almost identically as "children of a brother and a sister"); Duncan's daughter Ivy and Roy's daughter Hazel are therefore second cousins, as r09, r17, and r18 all state explicitly and take pains to distinguish from "cousins once removed" or "aunt/niece," phrasing that suggests this exact confusion was common at the time. One outside source, Cleve Oram (r21), calls them "first cousin once removed" — I reject this as a non-family outsider's looser reckoning against three internally consistent family accounts, one of which (r18) is Ivy's own father explicitly writing to correct just this kind of error. The sources leave open only whether Orra was Emmet's full or half sister (r09 says full sister, same parents; r18 does not specify) — this detail does not affect the cousin relationship either way.

**B3. Selby Vose's father.**
Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**, the Association's founding president and author of its by-laws (r12, r18, r23). This matters because it means the manager who accused the Hessel Bottom patrons of watering their milk in 1919, and who presided over the Association through the 1911–1925 period, was not an outside hire but the founder's own son — inheriting institutional standing and probably institutional loyalty to the Association's own account of itself, which may help explain why he (per his own clerk, r23) signed the yearly ledgers "without reading the lines above" the bottom figure rather than questioning them. Two sources instead call him Gideon's "nephew" (r01, r12 itself, inconsistently — see Section E). I resolve to "son" on the authority of Duncan Keddie (r18), an outsider to the Vose family with no reason to favor either version, who states it was "nonsense" that anyone called him "nephew," and Bertram Kyle (r23), Selby Vose's own clerk of twenty-five years, who independently states the same thing in almost the same words ("the son, whatever is said at Ashlin... it is nonsense"). Two independently-positioned witnesses directly refuting a rumor outweighs the rumor repeated twice.

**B4. Relationship between the Vose and Keddie families.**
**None.** Duncan Keddie states this explicitly and specifically (r18): "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses," attributing the persistent rumor to confusion with an unrelated family, "the Ashlin Voses." Against this, Cleve Oram (r21) says "The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort," but immediately qualifies it as thirdhand ("my mother had it from her mother") and admits he "could not draw... the line on paper." I resolve to no relationship: a direct family member's specific, sourced denial (naming the actual mix-up) outranks an outsider's admittedly vague and unverifiable hearsay.


# Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math (show working)

**C1.**
**(a) Proportion off.** The invoice pipettes were blown to a marked charge of 17.6 c.c. (the standard test charge, attested identically across sources). The calibrated mean actual delivery was **16.72 c.c.** (r04, r07; also the only value consistent with the bulletin's own quoted conclusion — see below). Ratio = 16.72 / 17.6 = **0.95**. So every test read at 95% of true value — short by **one part in twenty (5%)**, exactly the phrase used in the bulletin's closing paragraph as quoted identically in r04, r07, and r11. (I reject the competing figure of 16.27 c.c., given in r11's own body text and echoed by the newspaper r19, because it produces 16.27/17.6 = 0.9244, which matches *neither* r11's own quoted conclusion — a document should not disagree with itself — nor any of the other self-consistent figures below; it is very likely a 72/27 digit transposition.)

**(b) True average and the shortfall in points.** Recorded station average: **3.61%** (r22, and the only value that makes the rest of the arithmetic below close exactly). Since recorded = true × 0.95, true = 3.61 / 0.95 = **3.80%**. Shortfall = 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 points**, matching Falke's bulletin-derived figure in r22 exactly ("nineteen hundredths of a point... in every month of the year"). (Other narrators' independent field estimates — Roy Brant's 0.17, Kepp's 0.21 — bracket this value without a shared source, which is itself corroborating: several people arrived at approximately the same gap by different, non-instrumental means.)

**(c) Total pounds not credited.** Using the total milk received at the station, **40,000,000 lb** (see C3 for why this figure, not 44,000,000 lb, is correct): credited butterfat = 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**. True butterfat = 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lb**. Difference = **76,000 lb**. This matches the bulletin's own stated conclusion exactly, as quoted identically in three tellings (r04, r07, r11's closing paragraph) — strong internal confirmation that 40,000,000 lb / 3.61% / 3.80% is the correct, self-consistent figure set. (I reject r11's own body-text total of 118,000 lb as an artifact of its 16.27 c.c. error, and r13's "sixty-seven thousand pounds" as a different, earlier, non-instrumental estimate made by the patrons themselves in 1925, before any measurement existed — not a competing calculation of the same quantity.)

**(d) Money value and fraction received.** Butterfat price averaged about 25 cents/lb across the affected years (r09: "average over the years, twenty-five cents"; r23 independently: "twenty-five cents the pound"). 76,000 lb × $0.25 = **$19,000**, matching r04's figure exactly. (The alternate figures of $19,600, in r12 and r19, derive from the alternate 44,000,000 lb intake tradition — see C3 — not from an independent recomputation, so I treat them as one recurring error, not a second data point.) Patrons actually received the 1925 arbitration award of **$3,800** "without admission." Fraction received = 3,800 / 19,000 = **0.20, i.e. one-fifth (20%)**.

**C2. Emmet Brant, 1910 (the largest patron).**
His 1910 leaf (quoted identically by r09 and r17, both descendants working from the same original book) states: 200,000 lb delivered, "his scale and theirs agreeing," paid on a test of 3.42%.
**(a) Credited:** 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** butterfat.
**(b) True/owed:** applying the same 0.95 ratio, true test = 3.42 / 0.95 = 3.60%; true butterfat = 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb. Not credited = 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb**.
**(c) Money value that year:** 1910's price is separately given as 24 cents/lb that year (r09). 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40**.

**C3. Full delivery years, station-wide.**
**(a) Credited to patrons:** 40,000,000 lb milk × 3.61% = **1,444,000 lb** butterfat.
**(b) Actually reached the creamery in the cream:** 40,000,000 lb × 3.80% = **1,520,000 lb**.
**(c) Difference as a percentage — of what:** the absolute difference is 76,000 lb either way, but the *percentage* differs depending on the base. As a fraction of what was actually delivered/true (1,520,000 lb): 76,000/1,520,000 = **5.0% exactly** — this is the real "one part in twenty" figure, since true × 0.95 = credited by construction. As a fraction of what patrons were credited (1,444,000 lb): 76,000/1,444,000 ≈ **5.26%**. The bulletin's own "one part in twenty" language refers to the *first* base (percentage of the true amount), and that is the figure I'd report if only one number is wanted — but the question asks to be exact about the base, so I give both and flag which the sources actually use.
*(Confirms 40,000,000 lb over 44,000,000 lb: only 40,000,000 makes 3.61/3.80/76,000 close exactly; 44,000,000 lb — the figure independently used by Teague, Rundle, and Frick, who I treat as one recurring "alternate tradition" rather than three independent confirmations, since all three cluster with the same non-standard figures elsewhere, e.g. the 3.16 average — does not reconcile with 76,000 lb at any test-pair used in the sources.)*

**C4. The invoice.**
**(a) Total:** **$77.93** — the figure given in the invoice as reproduced verbatim by Ammon & Sons' own historian (r20), the primary source. (r19's paraphrase gives $77.39, a likely digit transposition of the same document; I favor the actual facsimile.)
**(b) Pipette count and shortfall:** **six dozen (72)** pipettes (r04, r07, r20's invoice text all agree; r11 and r21's "five dozen" I judge a shared error propagating from an early or garbled reading of the bulletin — see Section E), marked 17.6 c.c., delivering ~16.72 c.c. — short by **17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c.** each.

**C5.**
**(a) Duration of faulty glass vs. years in the money calculation.** The faulty glass was in service from **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923** — about **26 years** (matches r20's own "six and twenty years afterward"). The money/butterfat calculation (76,000 lb, $19,000) necessarily runs over the same span of **full delivery seasons the bad glass actually covered — roughly 26 seasons (1897 through 1922), excluding the partial 1897 borrowed-glass stub-season before 1 May and the partial 1923 season cut short by the mid-year condemnation**. The two numbers ("how long the glass was in use" vs "how many full years the money math covers") differ only because the glass's service period spans two partial seasons at its edges, which cannot be included in a *full-year* delivery calculation; the underlying span is otherwise the same 26-year stretch.
**(b) First season's milk intake: cannot be determined from the sources.** The curator's notes (referenced consistently across r06/r07) state this figure is recorded nowhere in the ledgers. Two witnesses offer irreconcilable personal recollections decades later: Hobart Sill recalls "about nine hundred thousand pounds" (r14); Bertram Kyle recalls "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" (r23). Neither cites a document; both explicitly say they are reporting impression/hearsay ("which is what I understood... from men who were there"). Since the only source that checked the actual books found nothing, and the two personal estimates differ by over 20% with no way to adjudicate between them, I report this as unsettled rather than average or choose between them.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment.**
When/how long: 8 May – 10 July 1911, nine weeks (r03's precise dates, corroborated independently by r22's report using the same dates; Kepp's "six weeks," r16, is explicitly hearsay in his own text — "it has been urged upon me" — and I discount it). Variable: the measuring instrument (station's own broken glass, replaced temporarily by a borrowed measure). Held constant: the herds, the pasture/season, the tester (Ansel Keddie), and the level of supervision. Result: tests rose markedly during the nine weeks and fell back the moment the station's own glass resumed. What this establishes that arithmetic alone cannot: it is a genuine natural controlled comparison — because literally everything else stayed fixed while only the instrument changed, and the effect tracked the instrument, it directly implicates the equipment as the causal factor, independent of and decades before anyone measured *how much* the pipettes were off. A later calibration (1958) can only measure a static object; it cannot, by itself, prove that object's error was actually responsible for the historical pattern of complaint — the 1911 episode is the only evidence in the record that does that. What it does **not** establish: the *size* of the pipette error (only the burette measurement gives that); that the station's specific pipettes were defective for their *entire* working life (it shows only that an instrument swap correlated with a test-level swap on one occasion); or the mechanism (proportional short-delivery) by which the error operated.

**D2. Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream.**
(1) The 1911 experiment (Story 4/D1): the same tester, under the same supervision, produced *higher* readings the moment the equipment — not his conduct — changed; theft requires a change in conduct, not equipment, to explain a change in outcome.
(2) The Association's own "station gain" ledger entries (r05, r12's retraction, r23's clerk account), signed off every single year, recorded that *more* butterfat reached the main creamery in the station's cream than was ever credited to the patrons — the opposite of what skimming cream would produce, which would show a deficit at the receiving end, not a surplus.
(3) Hobart Sill, the rival buttermaker at Larrow Green, independently reweighed every batch of Hollow cream on arrival for decades using his own scale and found it consistently ran *heavier* than the station's own paperwork claimed (r14) — a second, physically independent check pointing the same direction as the ledger.
These are independent because they come from three different people, using three different methods (an in-book natural experiment, an accounting record kept by people uninvolved in testing, and an independent physical reweighing by a competitor with no reason to flatter Keddie), at different times, none relying on the others' data. All three — (1), (2), and (3) — predate and do not depend on the 1958 pipette measurement; I would single out (2), the ledger entries, as the one that would be most immediately convincing even to a skeptic, since it is the Association's *own* paperwork working against its own accusation. Did the bookkeeper Wilbur Teague, who insinuated the theft accusation, ever question these entries? **No** — per Bertram Kyle (r23), who sat beside him for decades, Teague wrote the "station gain" line himself every year of his 40-year service and "never asked anybody anything," treating the figures as self-explanatory once labeled; no source shows him ever reconciling this with the accusation he made at the 1925 hearing.

**D3. Did the manager know before 1958 that the tests ran low?**
He signed ledger sheets carrying the explicit "station gain" line every year from taking office in 1911 (r23) — documentary, in his own hand, of a surplus of butterfat over what patrons were credited — so in a *constructive* sense, yes, the knowledge was in front of him annually for over a decade. But his own clerk of twenty-five years (r23) states directly that Vose "read the totals and... never read the lines above them," taking "a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign" — meaning there is direct testimony he did not actually process the information. My answer: **he had the evidence but there is no basis to say he understood it** — and his own conduct (circulating the 1919 watering accusation) is more consistent with someone who believed a different, wrong theory than with someone knowingly concealing the truth. This is a case the sources leave genuinely ambiguous between "willful blindness" and "honest failure to read," and I do not think it can be pushed further than that.

## Section F — Theory

**The actual cause.** The pipettes bought new in 1897 from Ammon & Sons were blown to the correct 17.6 c.c. mark on their stems but, owing to a manufacturing fault (Ammon's own historian, r20, guesses a reground mold that quietly ran a lot short, a defect invisible to ordinary spot-checking because a short lot checks out "consistently wrong"), actually delivered only about 16.72 c.c. — roughly 95% of the intended charge. Because the fat percentage from a test is computed against the assumed full charge, every single test made with that glass read about 5% low, in exact and unvarying proportion, regardless of the milk's true richness, the season, or the herd. This explains the size of the error (fixed by the physical shortfall of the glass, ~0.88 c.c. out of 17.6), why it was constant year-round rather than seasonal (it is a property of the instrument, not the cows or the hay), why it began precisely on 1 May 1897 (when the new glass entered service) and ended precisely on 12 June 1923 (when the state inspector's burette caught it and it was replaced), and why nothing on the farms or at the station itself ever had to change to produce or end the pattern — the fault lay entirely outside the human and agricultural system being blamed, and (per Vaught's introduction, r11) was invisible from inside daily operation because "the pipette is the standard": there was no internal way to notice a systematic instrument bias without an outside reference, which nobody applied until the state inspector's spot check in 1923, and definitively only when Ivy Keddie repeated the calibration in 1958.

**The three competing (and defeated) explanations:**
1. *Watering*, advanced by manager Selby Vose (1919 circular). Defeated by the state inspector's lactometer tests at every visit from 1908 on, which never once found added water (r22), and by the cheese-quality evidence from Alder Corners (r10) — watered milk fails to make good cheese, yet the same Hessel Bottom farmers' milk made fine cheese there for sixteen summers.
2. *Cream theft by the tester*, insinuated by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague (r13). Defeated by the 1911 borrowed-measure experiment (same tester, tests rose when only the equipment changed), the Association's own "station gain" ledger surplus every year (r05, r12, r23), and Hobart Sill's independent reweighing consistently finding the cream arrived heavier than claimed (r14).
3. *Wet/bog hay*, the 1908 Grigg committee's theory, elaborated as a mineral-deficiency mechanism by veterinarian Kepp (1926, r16). Defeated by the actual feeding trial, Bulletin No. 214, which found the effect to be at most 0.05 points in winter and nothing measurable in summer (r22's verbatim reprint) — far too small and too seasonal to account for a flat, year-round 0.19-point gap.

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?** **No.** Per the presiding chairman's own exhibit list (r13), only four items were entered: the station test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book, and the state inspector's 1923 circuit notebook. The crate itself was never brought before the board; it sat, unexamined, in the loft even while two of the board's three sessions were held in the room directly beneath it (r13, r21). It was not physically measured until Ivy Keddie did so in 1958.

**What did the tester himself believe?** The sources conflict and I do not think they can be fully reconciled. Duncan Keddie (r18), his own son, reports that Ansel privately suspected "something in the glass" as early as the summer of 1911, said so twice at the family table, but never wrote it down because he would not put down what he could not prove — a claim that fits the documented question mark Keddie actually entered in the test book that year. Alonzo Frick (r13), the arbitration chairman, reports a single private corridor conversation from 1925 in which Keddie said he had "always thought the patrons were at fault," withheld out of loyalty to his neighbors. These are hard to square: one has him privately blaming the instrument fourteen years before his dismissal, the other has him privately blaming the patrons at the very moment of it. I favor Duncan's account as more probative — it is a direct family member reporting words spoken at home, corroborated by a physical artifact in the book — but I record this as a genuine, unresolved contradiction rather than picking one to discard outright; it is also possible, as Frick himself speculates about Keddie's loyalty, that Keddie told different people different things at different times to protect his neighbors.

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

A dairy cooperative's outlying station tested milk about a fifth of a point poorer in butterfat than the rest of its valley for twenty-six years (1897–1923), costing its farmers real money under a pay-by-test rule. Neighbors blamed watering, theft, or bad hay in turn; a 1908 inspection found no dishonesty, a 1911 equipment mishap accidentally proved the fault wasn't the farms or the tester, and in 1925 an arbitration board still dismissed the tester without ever examining the one piece of physical evidence sitting in the station's own loft: a crate of condemned test pipettes, quietly short of their marked volume since the day they were bought. No single witness — farmer, clerk, inspector, or board — held every fact needed to see it. Only in 1958, when the tester's own granddaughter climbed the ladder and measured the glass herself, did the pieces from eight separate, partial, sometimes self-contradicting records finally add up to one answer.
