# Answers — Part 2: Section A, Stories 5–8; Section B

## Story 5 — The 1919 Water Circular and the Brant–Keddie Rift

**Who:** Manager Selby Vose, tester Ansel Keddie, and Emmet Brant (Keddie's own brother-in-law and the largest Hessel Bottom patron).
**What:** In 1919 the Association's manager Selby Vose circulated a written warning to Ostrey Hollow patrons about adding water to their milk (r02, r09, r14, r17, r23, r24). Ansel Keddie, though the accusation implicated his own testing, personally delivered the letters by hand rather than mailing them or leaving them on the platform, and stood at each man's door while he read it (r24's contemporaneous diary). Two days later Emmet Brant — Keddie's own wife's brother, a patron since the station opened — confronted Keddie in the yard, asking why he had carried the paper himself "if he did not believe it." Keddie answered that he "would not have another man carry it." The two men, brothers-in-law of thirty years' standing, did not speak again until Brant's death in 1929 (r09, r18, r24). The number of families who received the letter is disputed — nine (r17) versus eleven (r24, the contemporaneous diary) — and I favor eleven since it is the eyewitness, dated-at-the-time account by the deliverer's own wife.
**Why it matters:** it shows the accusation was taken with total seriousness inside the tester's own household, and it converted a technical dispute into a permanent family rupture, which is why later narrators (Roy Brant, Hazel Brant, Duncan Keddie) all treat it as a wound rather than an argument.
**Quantities:** 9 or 11 families addressed (unresolved, see above); zero replies received by the manager (r17: "answered by none of them, which in that valley is a shout").
**Documents:** r02, r09 (Roy Brant, on the family rift and its cause), r14 (Sill, approving of the circular), r17 (Hazel Brant, "nine families"), r23 (Kyle, who typed the circular), r24 (Orra Keddie's diary, contemporaneous, "eleven families," and the yard confrontation).

## Story 6 — The 1925 Board of Arbitration and Ansel Keddie's Dismissal

**Who:** The Hessel Bottom patrons (petitioners), the Association's officers, bookkeeper Wilbur Teague, chairman Alonzo Frick, and Ansel Keddie.
**What:** The patrons petitioned in January 1925. A board of arbitration, chaired by attorney Alonzo Frick, sat from 17 March to 4 May 1925 — one session at the Cooperative's Ashlin office, two at the station itself in the weigh room, at Frick's own initiative (r13, r21). All three rival theories (watering, theft, wet hay) were argued (r13, r22). Keddie testified publicly that he had tested "thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons" and denied ever taking cream; Frick believed him, and privately, in a corridor conversation Frick recalls in 1946, Keddie said he had "always thought the patrons were at fault" but would not say so publicly out of loyalty to neighbors of thirty years (r13) — a claim that sits uneasily against Duncan Keddie's account that his father privately blamed the glass as early as 1911 (r18; see Section F). The board found no cause established and awarded the patrons **$3,800**, "without admission" (r04, r13, r22, r23 — four independent tellings agree on this figure; r21's "$3,500" is a lone, secondhand figure I reject, see Section E). Ansel Keddie was dismissed **30 April 1925**, four days *before* the award was announced, while the board was still sitting (r13, r18, r22, r24's diary all agree on this date and sequence) — a fact Frick says he "never been able to think well of the Association for."
**Quantities:** award $3,800; dismissal 30 April 1925; award 4 May 1925.
**Documents:** r05, r06/r07, r08, r13 (Frick, presiding — most authoritative), r18 (Duncan Keddie, family account), r21 (Oram, dissenting on the amount), r22 (Falke's report), r23 (Kyle, the clerk who processed the paperwork), r24 (Orra Keddie's diary, the dismissal day itself: "he came up the road at his ordinary hour... asked what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it.").

## Story 7 — The 1923 Condemnation of the Glassware

**Who:** State dairy circuit inspector Merle Strawn.
**What:** In the course of an ordinary circuit visit, Strawn ran the station's milk pipettes against a calibrated burette, found they would not deliver their marked charge, and condemned the whole lot on the spot, directing the manager to order new glass (r06/r07, r18, r19, r20, r22, r23). New glass was ordered from Ammon & Sons within the week and in use within the month (r20, r23). **No one at the time connected this defect to the twenty-six years of low tests that preceded it** (r22 ¶11 states this explicitly) — the condemnation was treated, in the office, as no more remarkable than "a broken window" (r23). This event is entirely separate from, and earlier than, the 1925 arbitration; the crate of condemned glass was simply put up in the loft over the intake room and left there (r18, r21).
**When:** The date is contested — 12 June 1923 (r06/r07, r22, and the bulletin's own closing paragraph as quoted in r04/r07/r11 — three independently-transmitted matches) versus 12 July 1923 (r08, r12) versus "1922" (r23, an elderly clerk's memory lapse thirty years on). I resolve to **12 June 1923**, since it is attested identically by the bulletin's actual text and by Falke's separately-researched 1959 report — two genuinely independent chains.
**Quantities:** whole lot condemned; new glass in use within a month; station's average rose to 3.85% from 1924 onward and held there (r22) — itself indirect proof of the defect, though "no person connected the one thing with the other at the time."
**Documents:** r06/r07 (curator), r08 (Vose, "12 July"), r18 (Duncan Keddie, "the state man condemned that glass in twenty-one" — NB: r18 gives yet a fourth date, 1921, which I judge a further outlier and reject against the weight of r06/07+r22+the bulletin text), r19, r20 (Ammon & Sons' own letter of 2 July 1923, replying to the Association's order), r22, r23.

## Story 8 — The 1958 Rediscovery and Bulletin No. 471

**Who:** Ivy Keddie (Ansel Keddie's granddaughter, a Farland graduate student), Professor Leland Vaught, and Rosalie Cudd.
**What:** In February 1958, Ivy Keddie, researching the station for her college work, climbed into the loft over the old intake room and found the condemned crate of pipettes, undisturbed since 1923 (r17, r18, r19, r22). She carried it to Farland and, on 9 March 1958, measured the surviving pipettes — **nine** of them, in condition to be measured — against a calibrated burette (r22; see Section E for the conflicting "eleven" claims). Each was blown to the standard 17.6 c.c. mark; the average actual delivery was **16.72 c.c.**, short in the same proportion across the lot. This — a proportional, universal, unchanging instrument error — explained every feature of the old complaint at once: its constancy across seasons, its resistance to the 1908 committee's honest inspection, and its correlation with the 1911 borrowed-measure anomaly. The findings were published as **Bulletin No. 471** (State Agricultural College at Farland, October 1958), jointly authored by Vaught and Keddie, with research assistance from Rosalie Cudd of Alder Corners, who supplied her father's independent cheese-test records as corroborating evidence (r10, r19). The bulletin explicitly and formally cleared Ansel Keddie, twenty-four years after his death (r24's editorial note). The story was picked up by the county newspaper in October 1958 (r19) and summarized officially by the county agricultural agent in 1959 (r22), who also corrected the newspaper's error calling Ivy Keddie Ansel's "great-niece" rather than his granddaughter.
**Quantities:** 9 pipettes measured; 17.6 c.c. marked vs 16.72 c.c. actual; see Section C for the full monetary and butterfat reconstruction.
**Documents:** r04, r07 (the bulletin's content via the curator), r09, r10 (Cudd), r11 (Vaught's introduction, with the internal arithmetic error — Section E), r17 (Hazel Brant), r18 (Duncan Keddie), r19 (newspaper), r22 (Falke's report and correction).

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**
Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's **granddaughter**. Chain: Ansel and Orra Keddie's son was Duncan Keddie (r18, r24); Duncan's daughter is Ivy (r18, explicit: "which makes her my father's granddaughter"). This is corroborated by r09 (Roy Brant, an independent family member, describing Ivy as "Duncan's girl"). Two sources point elsewhere: the newspaper (r19) and the first draft of the county report (r22) both call her Ansel's "great-niece." I resolve in favor of granddaughter because (a) it is stated directly by Ansel's own son, the person best positioned to know, specifically to correct a rumor he says he has "already twice seen it printed" wrong, and (b) the county report itself (r22) *retracts* its own "great-niece" claim in an appended note after verification: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece." A source correcting itself on direct inquiry outranks an uncorrected newspaper guess.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**
They are **second cousins**. Orra (Keddie) and Emmet Brant were brother and sister (r09, r18, r24); their children, Duncan Keddie and Roy Brant, are therefore first cousins (r09, r17, r18 — three independent, family-insider statements, worded almost identically as "children of a brother and a sister"); Duncan's daughter Ivy and Roy's daughter Hazel are therefore second cousins, as r09, r17, and r18 all state explicitly and take pains to distinguish from "cousins once removed" or "aunt/niece," phrasing that suggests this exact confusion was common at the time. One outside source, Cleve Oram (r21), calls them "first cousin once removed" — I reject this as a non-family outsider's looser reckoning against three internally consistent family accounts, one of which (r18) is Ivy's own father explicitly writing to correct just this kind of error. The sources leave open only whether Orra was Emmet's full or half sister (r09 says full sister, same parents; r18 does not specify) — this detail does not affect the cousin relationship either way.

**B3. Selby Vose's father.**
Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**, the Association's founding president and author of its by-laws (r12, r18, r23). This matters because it means the manager who accused the Hessel Bottom patrons of watering their milk in 1919, and who presided over the Association through the 1911–1925 period, was not an outside hire but the founder's own son — inheriting institutional standing and probably institutional loyalty to the Association's own account of itself, which may help explain why he (per his own clerk, r23) signed the yearly ledgers "without reading the lines above" the bottom figure rather than questioning them. Two sources instead call him Gideon's "nephew" (r01, r12 itself, inconsistently — see Section E). I resolve to "son" on the authority of Duncan Keddie (r18), an outsider to the Vose family with no reason to favor either version, who states it was "nonsense" that anyone called him "nephew," and Bertram Kyle (r23), Selby Vose's own clerk of twenty-five years, who independently states the same thing in almost the same words ("the son, whatever is said at Ashlin... it is nonsense"). Two independently-positioned witnesses directly refuting a rumor outweighs the rumor repeated twice.

**B4. Relationship between the Vose and Keddie families.**
**None.** Duncan Keddie states this explicitly and specifically (r18): "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses," attributing the persistent rumor to confusion with an unrelated family, "the Ashlin Voses." Against this, Cleve Oram (r21) says "The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort," but immediately qualifies it as thirdhand ("my mother had it from her mother") and admits he "could not draw... the line on paper." I resolve to no relationship: a direct family member's specific, sourced denial (naming the actual mix-up) outranks an outsider's admittedly vague and unverifiable hearsay.
