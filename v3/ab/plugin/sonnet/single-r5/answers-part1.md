# Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

*(Method note: throughout, where sources conflict I favor (1) verbatim-quoted primary documents over paraphrase, (2) contemporaneous records over later recollection, (3) figures that are internally arithmetic-consistent with each other over ones that are not, and (4) independent witnesses over repetitions of the same rumor. I say explicitly which rule I used each time.)*

## Story 1 — The Founding of the Association and the Rule That Made the Test Matter

**Who:** Gideon Vose and the farmers of Larrow Valley.
**What:** In 1889, farmers of the valley met at the Larrow Green schoolhouse and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (r12, r22). Gideon Vose was elected first president and wrote the by-laws himself (r12, r18, r23). The main creamery was built at Larrow Green — 1891 per r22/r12. The by-laws included Article VII, the rule that every patron would be paid according to the pounds of butterfat his milk tested for, at the station where he delivered, rather than by the weight of milk delivered. This article is the entire reason the later dispute cost anyone money: under payment-by-weight, a mistaken test would have cost nothing (r22 makes this point explicitly).
**When:** Organization 1889; creamery built c. 1891. The date Article VII/payment-by-test was *adopted* is contested: 1891 (r12), 1892 (r02), 1893 (r15). **This cannot be settled from the sources.** The most careful source, county agent Orin Falke's 1959 report (r22), states plainly that the printed by-laws carry no date and that the Association's own early minute book was lost in an office move, and that his office "does not attempt" to fix the year. Since the only source that actually checked the primary record found no answer, and the three other claims are casual restatements with no cited evidence, I record this as unresolved rather than picking a "majority."
**Quantities:** No monetary quantities attach to founding itself; its importance is structural — it is why Article VII, not any dollar figure, is the hinge of the whole affair (r22 ¶3).
**Documents:** r01, r02 (adoption date, Selby Vose as "son"), r12 (founding, 1891 date, Selby Vose as "nephew" — later shown wrong, see Story 8/Section B), r15 (Grigg's 1893 claim), r18 (Duncan Keddie's genealogical correction of the Vose relationship), r22 (the only source that investigated the minute book and found it lost), r23 (clerk's account of the by-laws and Gideon/Selby Vose's actual father-son relationship).

## Story 2 — Building Ostrey Hollow Station and the Bad Lot of Glass

**Who:** The Association's officers, Ansel Keddie (hired as tester), and Ammon & Sons of Tarnet (the supply house).
**What:** In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up-valley, to serve Hessel Bottom's six farms (r22). It opened 4 May 1896 (r22) with Ansel Keddie as tester and separator man, a post he held 29 seasons (r18, r22, r24). For its first partial season the station borrowed testing glass from the Larrow Green creamery (r03's note "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green," corroborated by Hobart Sill, who says he personally supplied equipment from his own bench, r14). The station's own outfit was ordered from Ammon & Sons: an invoice dated 14 April 1897, reproduced verbatim by the firm's own historian (r20) — six dozen milk-test pipettes at 17.6 c.c., 35 cents each ($25.20); twelve dozen test bottles ($31.68); one acid measure (90 cents); one four-bottle hand tester ($18.00); freight ($2.15); **total $77.93**. This glass went into use 1 May 1897, fixed by Orra Keddie's own diary entry written that week ("A. says the station's own glass came up," r24) — the single most authoritative date, being contemporaneous. (Vaught's bulletin, r11, and clerk Kyle's 1953 memory, r23, both say 1898; both are later and I judge them slips.)

Unknown to anyone, the pipettes — though blown to the 17.6 c.c. mark — actually delivered only about 16.72 c.c. on average, a manufacturing defect Ammon's own historian later attributes to a reground mold that ran the lot short without anyone on the blowing floor catching it, because a spot-check of a short lot finds it "consistently wrong," which no ordinary check catches (r20).
**Quantities:** $77.93 total invoice; 72 pipettes at 17.6 c.c. marked, ~16.72 c.c. actual, 35¢ each.
**Documents:** r03, r04, r07 (curator's account of the invoice and glass), r11 (Vaught, giving a conflicting 1898 date and quantity), r14 (Sill on borrowed glass), r19 (newspaper, with a garbled invoice total), r20 (Ammon & Sons' own reproduced invoice — the primary source), r22, r23, r24 (Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary).

## Story 3 — The Chronic Complaint and the Three Rival Theories

**Who:** The patrons of Hessel Bottom (led in early complaints by Emmet Brant and neighbors), against the Association's officers, bookkeeper, and a series of investigating committees.
**What:** From very shortly after the glass went into use, Ostrey Hollow's tests ran consistently below the rest of the valley. The first complaint of record was 1902 (r09, r22). The gap held steady at roughly two-tenths of a point year-round — winter and summer alike, which matters because a feed-based cause should vary seasonally and this did not (r09: "seventeen hundredths"; r16: "twenty-one hundredths"; r22, citing the bulletin: "nineteen hundredths," the figure I treat as authoritative since it is the one actually derived from the calibrated measurement — see Section C). Three explanations circulated for a quarter-century: **(1) watering**, the Association manager's theory, formalized in a 1919 circular (Story 5); **(2) cream theft by the tester**, insinuated at the 1925 hearing by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague (r13); and **(3) wet/bog hay thinning the cream**, the theory of a 1908 investigating committee under Nyle Grigg (r15) and later given a mineral-deficiency mechanism by veterinarian Roland Kepp in a 1926 paper to the county farm club (r16). All three theories are refuted in Section F; none is correct, but all three were argued in good faith by people close to the case, and each attracted genuine, non-trivial evidence in its favor at the time (the 1908 committee genuinely found no dishonesty; Kepp genuinely found parallel effects on other wet bottoms).
**When:** 1902 onward, with committee investigations in 1908 and continuing grievance through the 1910s–20s.
**Quantities:** gap of ~0.19 points (I resolve to this value; see Section C); recorded station average 3.61%, valley average 3.78–3.80%.
**Documents:** r05, r06/r07 (curator/Strawn material), r09 (Roy Brant's ledger-based estimate), r13 (Frick on Teague's theft insinuation), r14 (Sill's watering counter-theory), r15 (Grigg committee, wet hay), r16 (Kepp's mineral theory), r22 (Falke's synthesis and figure of 0.19).

## Story 4 — The Nine Weeks of 1911: The Accidental Experiment

**Who:** Ansel Keddie and the Hessel Bottom patrons, unwittingly.
**What:** On 8 May 1911 the station's own measuring glass broke, and a borrowed measure was used in its place until 10 July 1911 — nine weeks (r03's precise dates, corroborated by r07's summary leaf and independently by county agent Falke's 1959 report, r22, which gives the identical dates). Through those nine weeks, with the same herds, same pasture, same tester, and same oversight, the station's recorded tests rose markedly; the day the station's own glass resumed, they fell back to the old level (r22 ¶6). Ansel Keddie noted the fact in his book and set a question mark against it, but — per his son Duncan's much later account (r18) — told his family at supper, uncharacteristically and twice, that he believed "it was something in the glass." He never wrote this suspicion down because, in his son's words, "he was a man who would not put down what he could not prove" (r18). Veterinarian Kepp later dismissed the whole episode as "only six weeks" of flush-season data (r16) — an outlier duration I reject as hearsay-based, since he cites it as something "urged upon me," not his own record.
**Why it matters:** this is the one piece of evidence in the whole record that is a true controlled comparison rather than a retrospective calculation — see Section D1 for full analysis of what it proves and what it does not.
**Quantities:** 9 weeks, 8 May – 10 July 1911.
**Documents:** r03 (precise dates), r07 (summary leaf), r09 (Roy Brant's puzzled diary-adjacent account, "and there it sat for near on fifty years"), r11 (Vaught's bulletin treatment, calling it "an experiment that ran itself"), r16 (Kepp's dismissal as six weeks), r18 (Ansel Keddie's private suspicion), r22 (Falke's dated, authoritative account), r24 (Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary entry, "A. home cross about a broken measure," 8 May 1911).
