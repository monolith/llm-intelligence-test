## Story 1 — The Short Glass of Ostrey Hollow (and the Vindication of Ansel Keddie)

**Who:** Ansel Keddie, tester/separator man at the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery
Association's Ostrey Hollow skimming station (serving Hessel Bottom, near Ashlin, Ordell
County); his wife Orra (née Brant); their son Duncan; Duncan's daughter, Ivy Keddie; the Brant
family — Orra's brother Emmet (the station's largest patron), Emmet's son Roy, and Roy's daughter
Hazel; Association manager Selby Vose (son of founder Gideon Vose); bookkeeper Wilbur Teague;
office clerk Bertram Kyle; and the glass supplier, Ammon & Sons of Tarnet.

**What happened:** The station opened 4 May 1896, testing patrons' milk for butterfat (patrons
were paid on fat content, not weight). Its own testing glassware — six dozen pipettes marked to
deliver 17.6 c.c. — went into service 1 May 1897, replacing glass borrowed from the main
creamery. From early on the station's tests ran conspicuously low against neighboring creameries.
Over three decades this fed three rival theories: patrons watering their milk (the manager's
view, aired in a 1919 circular), the tester skimming cream (an officer's accusation at a 1925
hearing), and wet Hessel Bottom pasture thinning the cream (a 1908 committee finding). A 1911
accident — the station's own measure broke and a borrowed one was used for nine weeks — saw tests
rise, then fall back the moment the station's own glass returned; Ansel privately suspected the
glass but, lacking proof, recorded only a question mark. Patrons petitioned in 1925; an
arbitration board found no fault proven, awarded the patrons $3,800 without admission, and
dismissed Ansel four days later. He died in 1934 still under suspicion; his wife Orra in 1948. In
February 1958 his granddaughter Ivy, a graduate student, found the station's own glassware —
condemned by a state inspector in 1923 and never connected to anything since — still crated in the
station loft, and measured it: it delivered well short of its marked volume. A college bulletin
cleared Ansel in October 1958.

**When:** 1896–1959, with the exonerating discovery in February–March 1958 and publication in
October 1958.

**Quantities that matter:** 6 dozen pipettes marked 17.6 c.c., delivering ~16.27 c.c. on average;
recorded station test average 3.61% against a true ~3.80%; ~40 million lbs of milk over 25 full
delivery years; ~76,000 lbs of butterfat underpaid, worth about $19,600; patrons received $3,800
(about 19% of the loss).

**Documents:** r17, r18, r19, r20, r21, r22, r23, r24 all bear directly on this story.
