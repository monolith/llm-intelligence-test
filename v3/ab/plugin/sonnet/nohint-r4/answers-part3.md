# Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math (show working)

**C1(a). Proportion by which the tests differed from the truth, from the glassware.**
The pipettes were blown to deliver 17.6 c.c.; the measured survivors (r19) averaged 16.27 c.c.
Shortfall proportion = (17.6 − 16.27) / 17.6 = 1.33 / 17.6 ≈ **7.6% short** (delivering ~92.4% of
their marked charge).

**C1(b). True average test, and points short.**
Recorded/flawed station average (r22, citing the bulletin) = 3.61%. The point deficiency is stated
independently, in the same words ("nineteen hundredths"), by both r17 (Hazel's tape) and r22 (the
bulletin as cited by Falke) — two independent attestations of the *differential itself*. True
average = 3.61 + 0.19 = **3.80%**, deficiency **0.19 points**. Cross-check: Bertram Kyle's ledger
memory (r23) independently records an annual "station gain" of "five and a quarter per cent, as
usual" — and 0.19 / 3.61 = 5.26%, matching Kyle's 5.25% almost exactly, from a completely different
kind of source (bookkeeping memory vs. field-test comparison). This convergence is why I trust
3.80%/0.19, despite a problem in r22 itself: it also states a comparison figure of 3.78% for the
neighboring Larrow Green creamery, which differs from 3.61 by only 0.17, not 0.19 — an internal
arithmetic inconsistency in that source (flagged again in Section E). I treat 3.78 as a loose
contextual benchmark (a different creamery's own average), not a precise stand-in for Ostrey
Hollow's true value.

**C1(c). Pounds of butterfat not credited, over the full delivery years.**
Using Kyle's documented total (r23) of 40,000,000 lbs of milk received over 25 full delivery
years: credited at flawed 3.61% = 1,444,000 lbs; true at 3.80% = 1,520,000 lbs; shortfall =
**76,000 lbs**. Cross-check via Kyle's own ledger ratio: 1,444,000 × 5.25% ≈ 75,800 lbs —
consistent. This is an independent convergence of two different evidentiary threads (Falke's
field-test figures and Kyle's ledger memory). Note: r21 (Oram) gives "sixty-seven thousand
pounds" — a single, oral-tradition figure, the only source for that number, and one its own
narrator admits he "cannot picture." I treat 76,000 lbs as the better-supported figure and flag
67,000 as unreconciled with the rest of the record.

**C1(d). Money value, and fraction actually received.**
The 1958 bulletin (r19) puts the total loss at **$19,600**. Independent check: 76,000 lbs ×
$0.25/lb (Kyle's long-run average butterfat price, r23) ≈ $19,000 — close but not identical,
plausibly because the bulletin used year-by-year prices rather than one long-run average. Patrons
actually received **$3,800** (the 1925 award), corroborated independently by r19, r22, and r23 (the
last being the clerk who personally processed the payment) — against a single outlying figure of
$3,500 in r21, sourced secondhand from Oram's father. Fraction received = 3,800 / 19,600 ≈
**19.4%**.

**C2. The largest patron's year of 1910 (Emmet Brant, per r17).**
(a) 200,000 lbs milk × 3.42% recorded test = **6,840 lbs** butterfat credited.
(b) True test = 3.42 + 0.19 = 3.61%; true butterfat = 200,000 × 3.61% = 7,220 lbs; owed and not
credited = 7,220 − 6,840 = **380 lbs**. (Using the alternative proportional method — true =
recorded / 0.9244 — instead gives ≈7,399 lbs and a difference of ≈559 lbs. The two mechanisms
disagree by about 180 lbs; I use the point-differential method as primary because it is the
twice-independently-attested figure and matches Kyle's ledger ratio, but the disagreement itself
is worth noting rather than hiding.)
(c) 380 lbs × $0.25/lb (the only price figure in the sources, a multi-decade average, not
1910-specific) ≈ **$95**. Treat as an approximation only.

**C3. Over the same full delivery years.**
(a) Total credited to patrons: 40,000,000 × 3.61% = **1,444,000 lbs**.
(b) Total actually reaching Larrow Green in the cream: using Kyle's ledger ratio (received =
credited × 1.0525) ≈ **1,520,000 lbs** — consistent with 40,000,000 × 3.80%.
(c) Percentage difference: **5.25%**, taken of the *credited (patron) figure*, exactly as Kyle's
ledger phrased it ("five and a quarter per cent... carried to the general account"). Note: the same
absolute gap (76,000 lbs) expressed as a percentage of the *received* figure instead gives
76,000/1,520,000 ≈ 5.0% — a different number — so the choice of base matters, and the ledger's own
wording fixes the base as the credited figure.

**C4. The invoice for the station's testing outfit.**
(a) **$77.93** — from summing the reproduced invoice's own line items in r20 ($25.20 + $31.68 +
$0.90 + $18.00 + $2.15 = $77.93), matching the invoice's own printed "Total — 77.93" line. Three
separate passages (r17, r19, and r20's *own prose*, two sentences after quoting the invoice) all
instead say "seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents" ($77.39) — an apparent digit
transposition (93→39) that has propagated through the record. I treat $77.93 as correct because it
is directly computable from the reproduced source document itself, which outranks repeated
citations that nobody appears to have re-added.
(b) The invoice bought **6 dozen (72) pipettes** at $0.35 each, marked 17.6 c.c.; the measured
survivors averaged 16.27 c.c., short by about **1.33 c.c. (≈7.6%)** per pipette.

**C5.**
(a) Faulty glass in service: 1 May 1897 – 12 June 1923 (start: r20's invoice and r24's diary agree
exactly; end: r22's precise date, corroborated by r20's internal letter-date chain — an order dated
"the 28th ultimo" answered 2 July fits a mid-June condemnation) — just over **26 years**. The money
calculation (r23) runs over **25 "full delivery years"** — the whole calendar years entirely
covered by the faulty glass, 1898–1922 — excluding the two partial years (1897, glass arrived
partway through; 1923, condemned partway through) at either end. That is why the two counts differ.
(b) First season's milk: about **1,100,000 lbs**, per Kyle (r23) — but Kyle himself flags this as
an unverified oral estimate "from men who were there," never entered in any book he ruled. Treat as
uncorroborated; it cannot be independently checked from these sources.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 broken-measure interval as an experiment.**
It ran from 8 May to 10 July 1911 — nine weeks (r22; corroborated by r24's diary entry of 8 May
1911 noting Ansel "cross about a broken measure," and by r18's memory of his father's 1911
suspicion). Variable: which measuring glass was used. Held constant: the tester, the patrons and
herds, the general season, and the Association's supervision. Result: tests rose measurably while
the borrowed measure was in use, then fell back to the old low level the moment the station's own
glass returned. This establishes *causation*, not mere correlation, in a way calculation alone
could not: because everything else was held constant, the shift in readings can be attributed to
the instrument itself — which simultaneously undercuts the feed/season theory and the
tampering-by-the-tester theory, since neither the pasture nor the man changed. It does **not**
establish the size or mechanism of the glass's error (that required the precise 1958 laboratory
measurement), which specific pieces of glassware were at fault, or whether the borrowed measure was
itself perfectly accurate rather than merely closer to the mark.

**D2. Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream.**
1. The 1911 natural experiment (r22/r24/r18): the same tester's readings moved with the glass, not
   with him.
2. The state inspector's lactometer results: never once found added water at the station on any
   visit from 1908 forward (r22) — a separate, ongoing, externally conducted line of testing over
   15+ years aimed at exactly this question.
3. Character/conduct evidence from family testimony: Ansel recorded only a private question mark
   rather than an unproven accusation (r18), and personally delivered the unwelcome 1919
   water-accusation circular door to door himself rather than avoid the confrontation (r24) —
   behavior inconsistent with concealment; he also tested his own brother-in-law's (his largest
   patron's) milk for decades with no suggestion of favoritism (r17).
These are independent because they rest on different evidentiary types — an operational/mechanical
record, an external regulatory record, and personal-conduct testimony — preserved by different
narrators. Reasons 1 and 2 would still hold even if the glassware had never been measured in 1958,
since both were recorded well before that discovery.
Did the bookkeeper ever question the entries? **No.** Per r23 (Kyle), bookkeeper Wilbur Teague
"never asked anybody anything in forty years," treating the annual "station gain... as usual" line
as self-explanatory; and even at the 1925 hearing, with all four relevant books laid before the
board, Kyle "could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room" ever
compared them.

**D3. Did the manager know, before the glassware was condemned, that the tests ran low?**
Yes as to the bare fact; no as to the cause. Manager Selby Vose personally signed the 1919 circular
warning patrons about watering their milk (r23, r24) — which presupposes he knew the tests were
running low and needed an explanation — and he signed the ledger sheets carrying the "station
gain... as usual" line every year of his tenure (r23), without, per Kyle, ever reading "the lines
above" the bottom figure. So the manager plainly knew the shortfall existed — it was the premise of
his own letter and was written into the books he signed annually — but no source shows he, or
anyone in the Association's management, ever suspected or investigated the glassware as the cause
before 1958.

## Section E — Contradictions

  and then retracts it in its own note. Resolved as *granddaughter* (primary-source + self-correcting
  source). The two "great-niece" mentions likely share one origin (imprecise publicity wording), so
  this does not count as two independent confirmations of the error.

- **Ivy–Hazel relationship:** r17 and r18 (two independent family sources) say "second cousins";
  r21 says "first cousin once removed." Resolved for "second cousins" — majority *and* independence
  both favor it, and r21's source admits it cannot substantiate the claim.

- **Vose–Keddie connection:** r18 flatly denies any relation, addressing the rumor directly; r21
  repeats the rumor as unverified hearsay. Resolved: no relation.

- **Settlement/award amount:** r19, r22, and r23 all say $3,800; r21 says $3,500. *Not* simple
  majority — r23's narrator personally processed the payment (most authoritative), and r19/r22 both
  likely draw on the same bulletin (not fully independent of each other), while r21 is secondhand
  oral tradition. Resolved: $3,800.

- **Date the glass was condemned:** r19, r20, and r22 converge on 1923 (r22 precisely, 12 June;
  r20's internal letter-date chain corroborates it); r17 says 1921 (single source, and Hazel
  explicitly disclaims confidence in dates); r23 says 1922 (single source, elderly memory).
  Resolved: 1923.

- **Date the station's own glass was installed:** r20 (invoice) and r24 (diary) — two independent,
  contemporaneous/documentary sources — agree on 1 May 1897; r23 (memoir, 56 years later) says
  1898. Resolved: 1897.

- **Pipette survivor count at discovery:** r21 and r22 agree on 9; r19 says 11 (a single source,
  admittedly assembled "in a day and a half"). Resolved: 9 survivors, though I flag that the only
  reported average-delivery figure (16.27 c.c.) comes from the source that miscounted them (r19),
  so some uncertainty in exactly how that average was derived remains unresolved.

- **Invoice total — narrators carrying the same wrong value:** r17, r19, and *r20's own prose*

- **Original lot size:** r20 (invoice, documentary) says 6 dozen (72) pipettes; r21 (oral memory,

- **r22's internal arithmetic:** states the deficiency is "nineteen hundredths" but also gives 3.61%

- **Award/dismissal year:** r18, r22, and r24 (a contemporaneous diary entry) all place Ansel's

  converging sources, including a real-time diary entry, against one memoir written decades later).

## Section F — Theory

**F1. Causal theory.** The 1897 lot of six dozen pipettes sold to the Association had a
manufacturing defect (Ammon & Sons' own historian, r20, guesses a reground mold that ran the lot
short) and delivered on average about 7.6% less than their marked 17.6 c.c. charge. Because every
butterfat test at Ostrey Hollow used one of these pipettes to draw the milk sample, every test read
low by a roughly constant amount — a fixed mechanical property of the glass, not of feed, weather,
season, or any person — which is exactly why the deficiency held flat across wet years and dry,
winter and summer (r17). It began when the flawed glass went into service (1 May 1897, replacing
borrowed glass that had presumably been sound) and ended completely and immediately when the glass
was replaced (June/July 1923), without any change to herds, feeding, or personnel — precisely
because the fault never lay with any of those things.

Three competing explanations were advanced, and each is defeated by specific evidence:
1. **Added water** (patron fraud) — advanced by manager Selby Vose (1919 circular). Defeated by the
   state dairy inspector's lactometer tests, which never once found added water at the station on
   any visit from 1908 forward (r22).
2. **Tester theft/fraud** — urged by an Association officer at the 1925 hearing. Defeated by the
   1911 nine-week natural experiment: the same tester's readings rose with a borrowed measure and
   fell back with his own (r22).
3. **Wet hay/pasture** — advanced by an Association committee (1908) and a veterinarian (1926), and
   initially given some credence even by Falke's 1959 report. Defeated by Falke's own follow-up
   retrieval of the actual 1911 college feeding-trial data (Bulletin No. 214), which found bog hay
   depressed the test by "not more than five hundredths... in winter, and nothing... on summer
   pasture" — far too small to explain a flat 0.19-point, year-round deficiency; Falke explicitly
   retracts his own earlier concession on this point.

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?** No evidence that it did, and strong
circumstantial evidence it did not: the crate had already been sitting condemned in the station
loft since 1923, directly above the room where two of the three arbitration sittings were held
(r21, r18). Nobody connected it to the dispute until Ivy Keddie climbed the ladder in 1958.

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?** Per his son Duncan (r18), Ansel came home in
the summer of 1911 "saying it was something in the glass" but "never dared put it in the book,"
recording only a question mark for lack of proof — a private suspicion corroborated by Orra's diary
(r24), which records him "cross about a broken measure" that same month and, in 1923, noting dryly
that the station had "new glass... which is the Association all over."

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

For sixty years, milk tests at the Larrow Valley Association's Ostrey Hollow station ran
mysteriously low, and everyone blamed a person: patrons suspected the Association of cheating
them, the manager accused patrons of watering their milk, an officer accused tester Ansel Keddie of
stealing cream, and a committee blamed wet pasture. A 1911 accident — nine weeks on a borrowed
measure — showed the readings tracked the equipment, not any person, but nobody followed it up.
Keddie was dismissed in 1925 and died disgraced in 1934. The real cause, a defective 1897 lot of
pipettes delivering short measure, sat condemned and forgotten in the station's own loft from 1923
until his granddaughter Ivy climbed a ladder in 1958 and measured the glass herself. It took a
diary, a clerk's memoir, an invoice, a county report, and family testimony — eight distinct,
individually incomplete records — to reconstruct what had been in plain sight the whole time.
