## Section C — Math

**C1.**

(a) Proportion. The surviving pipettes were blown to a standard charge of 17.6 c.c. but delivered
a measured mean of 16.72 c.c. (r04; r07 Items 5 & 14 — two independent catalogue citations of the
same 1958 measurement). 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly, i.e. a shortfall of one part in twenty (5%).
This is not a coincidence of rounding: 17.6 × 0.95 = 16.72 exactly, which is itself evidence that
16.72 c.c. is the correct measured figure. A rival figure of 16.27 c.c., found only in one
document's own body text (r11), gives an ugly, non-round ratio (16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244) — and that
same document's own quoted "Conclusion" section states the shortfall as "one part in twenty," which
is consistent only with 16.72 c.c., not with its own 16.27 c.c. computation. I resolve in favor of
16.72 c.c. / one-part-in-twenty (5%) on both external corroboration (two other documents) and
internal arithmetic consistency (a self-contradicting document siding against its own draft
figure).

(b) True average. Recorded station average was 3.61 (well corroborated: r02, r04, r07, r09
implicitly, r22). At a 5% shortfall, true average = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80. This matches the "true"
figure independently stated in r04/r07's conclusion and (for the post-1923 station, once corrected)
in r03's 1924 figure of 3.80. The tests were short by 3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19 points — a figure
independently corroborated by r22 ("the bulletin now fixes it" at nineteen hundredths) and r17
("Nineteen hundredths... flat as a floor"), against weaker single-source figures of 0.17 (r09),
0.21 (r16), both plausible independent estimates but outnumbered by the two sources that cite the
bulletin's own settled figure directly.

(c) Butterfat not credited. 76,000 lb, per the bulletin's own quoted "Conclusion" text, which
appears verbatim and identically in three separate documents (r04, r07 Item 15, and r11's own
quoted Section VII — ironically contradicting that same document's separate body computation of
118,000 lb, which used the erroneous 16.27 c.c. figure from (a) and should be rejected for the same
reason). A third figure, 67,000 lb, appears in two documents (r13, r21) but is explicitly labeled
in the earlier of the two as "the patrons' own claimed loss" at the 1925 hearing (r13) — a
litigation figure computed before the true mechanism was known, not the bulletin's later, better-
evidenced finding. I resolve for 76,000 lb.

(d) Value and fraction received. At the era's average butterfat price of 25 cents/lb (r23,
explicitly stated as the average "over that whole stretch"), 76,000 lb × $0.25 = $19,000 — matching
the figure given directly in r04/r07 and confirming that 25 cents, not 27 cents (r16's figure for a
different, later period, "these years" ≈ 1926), is the right multi-year average to use. A rival
figure of $19,600 appears in two documents (r12, r19) but does not reduce cleanly from any
corroborated pounds figure (76,000 × 0.25 ≠ 19,600), so I treat $19,000 as the better-supported,
arithmetically consistent figure. Patrons actually received $3,800 (the 1925 award — heavily
corroborated, Section A Story 6) of the $19,000 owed: $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20, i.e. one-fifth
(20%).

**C2.** For Emmet Brant, 1910 (the largest patron, per his own weigh book leaf, quoted identically
in r04/r07 Item 4, r09, and r17):

(a) Credited: 200,000 lb milk delivered, paid on a test of 3.42 → 200,000 × 0.0342 = 6,840 lb
butterfat credited.

(b) Owed and not credited: true test (recorded 3.42 + the 0.19-point shortfall from C1b) = 3.61 →
200,000 × 0.0019 = 380 lb butterfat owed but not credited.

(c) Money value: 380 lb × an assumed price of $0.25/lb (the best available multi-year average; no
source gives a price specific to 1910) = $95.00. I flag this as an approximation: it uses a
25-year average price applied to a single year, because no source prices butterfat for 1910
specifically.

**C3.** Over the full delivery years (best-supported total intake: 40,000,000 lb, per the
ledger-based figures in r02/r04/r07, against a "44,000,000 lb" figure shared by two sources, r05
and r12, which I treat as a repeated derivative error since neither cites the ledgers directly):

(a) Credited to patrons in total: 40,000,000 lb × 3.61% (recorded average) = 1,444,000 lb
butterfat credited.

(b) Actually reaching the main creamery: the sources give this only as a per-year "station gain"
ledger line — 57,760 lb credited against 60,800 lb actually received at Larrow Green, a gain of
3,040 lb, "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" — repeated, per the Association's own clerk,
"every year" of the station's operation (r02, r05, r07 Item 6, r12, r23, all converging on the
identical figures and the identical 5.25% label). Note that 60,800 lb is close to the per-year
average implied by dividing the 40,000,000 lb total over roughly 25 years (≈1,600,000 lb/year of
milk, ≈57,760 lb of butterfat at the recorded test), which suggests this ledger line is a
representative, not a cumulative, figure. Scaling the constant 5.25%-of-credited gain across the
full 1,444,000 lb credited total (from part a) gives an estimated 1,444,000 × 1.0525 ≈ 1,520,000
lb actually received over the full run — but this is my extrapolation, not a total stated anywhere
in the sources, which only ever give the per-year figure.

(c) As a percentage: 5.25% (3,040 ÷ 57,760), taken of the credited amount — this is the base the
sources themselves use ("station gain," computed as a percentage of what was credited to
patrons, not of what arrived at the creamery). This percentage is exceptionally well corroborated
and explicitly labeled in the source text itself, unlike the extrapolated totals in (b).

**C4.**

(a) Invoice total: $77.93, per the invoice reproduced in full by the selling firm's own company
history (r20) — itemized (6 doz. pipettes $25.20 + 12 doz. bottles $31.68 + acid measure $0.90 +
hand tester $18.00 + freight $2.15 = $77.93, which checks exactly). A newspaper account gives
"$77.39" (r19), which does not match any itemization and I reject as a transcription error against
the primary reproduced document.

(b) The invoice bought six dozen (72) pipettes — confirmed independently by both parties to the
original 1897 sale (the buyer's catalogue, r07 Item 2, and the seller's own company history, r20)
— against a "five dozen" figure shared by two later, secondhand narrators (r11, r21) that I treat
as a repeated recollection error. Each pipette was short by 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c. (using the
corrected figure from C1a).

**C5.**

(a) The faulty glassware was in service from 1 May 1897 (three independent sources: the seller's
invoice-adjacent record r20, the catalogue's provenance note r07, and Orra Keddie's contemporaneous
1897 diary entry r24 — against a "1898" figure shared by two later narrators, r11 and r23) until
its condemnation, best dated 12 June 1923 (the inspector's own notebook, r06, and the catalogue
drawing on it, r07 — against "12 July 1923," shared by two secondary narrators, r08 and r12, and an
outlying "1922" from one elderly clerk's uncertain memory, r23). That is roughly 26 years of
physical service. The money/butterfat calculation, however, is explicitly run over "25 years" (per
r11's own body text) and over a total intake of 40,000,000 lb, which — at the station's fairly
steady ~1,600,000 lb/year (r23) — also implies roughly 25 years. These numbers differ because the
station's first, partial season (1896, before the station's own glass and before continuous
ledger-keeping began) is excluded from the "full delivery years" the financial reckoning covers,
while the glassware itself was physically present slightly longer than the span the money
calculation counts.

(b) Cannot be determined. The 1972 catalogue states explicitly that no figure for the station's
1896 first-season intake survives in any ledger (r07). Two later personal recollections supply
figures anyway — "about nine hundred thousand pounds" (r14) and "a good eleven hundred thousand
pounds" (r23) — but they conflict with each other by roughly 20%, and neither is tied to any
surviving document; both narrators explicitly source the number to what they "understood at the
time from men who were there" rather than to a ledger. Two mutually contradictory, undocumented
recollections do not outweigh an explicit archival statement that the figure does not exist.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval was 8 May–10 July 1911 (nine weeks; see Section A Story 3 and Section E for
the "six weeks" rival figure and why it is rejected). The variable was the measuring instrument —
the station's own broken glass was replaced by a borrowed measure for the repair period. Held
constant: the herds, the patrons, the pasture and season (essentially unchanged across a nine-week
window in late spring/early summer), the tester, and the level of supervision. Result: recorded
tests rose markedly for the whole nine weeks and fell back to the prior level the instant the
station's own glass returned to use. This establishes, by isolating a single changed variable
against everything else held constant, that the deficiency travels with the instrument, not with
the milk, the cows, the patrons, or the tester — which is precisely the evidence a calculation
performed forty years later on inert glass could not by itself supply, because a calculation shows
what an instrument *would* do, not that it *actually did* produce the historical discrepancy in the
years when it was in use. What the interval does not establish: the exact size or mechanism of the
shortfall (only that swapping the glass changed the reading), nor that the borrowed measure itself
was accurately calibrated — only that it differed from the station's own faulty glass. The precise
magnitude required the 1958 measurement against a calibrated burette (Section A Story 8).

**D2.** Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream:

1. The 1911 substitution (D1): the same tester, under the same supervision, produced higher
readings the moment the instrument changed — inconsistent with a story in which the tester's own
dishonest behavior, not the instrument, explained the shortfall.

2. The Association's own annual ledger entries, kept by the bookkeeper (not the tester), show
cream arriving at Larrow Green consistently *heavier* in butterfat than what patrons had been
credited at the station — a "gain," not a loss, every single year (r02, r05, r07, r12, r23). Cream
actually stolen at the station would show up missing on arrival, not in surplus; the schoolteacher
who wrote the Association's own 1939 jubilee history explicitly retracted his own stolen-cream
narrative for exactly this reason once he saw the ledger (r12).

3. Entirely separate record-keeping outside the Association — the Alder Corners Cheese Company's
own independent tests of the same four Hessel Bottom patrons' summer milk (r10), and buttermaker
Hobart Sill's independent re-weighing of Ostrey Hollow's cream on arrival at Larrow Green (r14) —
both show the patrons' milk was never thin and the cream never came in light.

These three are independent because they rest on three different record-keeping systems kept by
three different, mutually unaware parties: the tester's own book (1), the Association bookkeeper's
ledger (2), and an entirely separate company's books plus a third employee's personal observation
(3). All three would still hold even if the glassware had never been measured in 1958 — none of
them depends on the 1958 pipette measurement; all three were true and, in principle, knowable from
existing records decades earlier.

Did the bookkeeper, Wilbur Teague, ever question the ledger entries that refute the theory he
himself advanced? No. Teague wrote the "station gain" line every year of his forty-year service
(r23) and, per the office clerk who sat outside his door, "never asked anybody anything in forty
years" (r23); at the 1925 hearing he nonetheless advanced the cream-skimming theory "obliquely...
via insinuation" (r13), apparently without ever reconciling it against the entries in his own hand.

**D3.** Yes, the manager (Selby Vose, from 1911) plainly knew the station's tests ran low relative
to the rest of the valley — that fact was the entire, decades-old premise of the patrons'
complaints and of his own 1919 water-accusation circular, which he could not have written without
first accepting that a gap existed. What the sources do not show is that he understood, or ever
connected, the low tests to a fault in the station's own equipment: per the office clerk who worked
outside his door for years, Vose "read the totals and... never read the lines above them," signing
the very ledger sheets that carried the exculpatory "station gain" entries every year without
(so far as the record shows) drawing the inference they permitted (r23). So: he knew the fact of
the shortfall before 1958; the sources give no evidence he knew, or looked for, its cause.

## Section E — Contradictions

*Format: claim vs. claim — who — resolution/method.*

1. **1908 committee's sampling period, self-contradiction.** Grigg's 1919 covering letter claims
sampling ran "the whole of 1908... every month," but the very report it reprints, in the same
document, says sampling covered only "January, February and March" (r15). Resolution: the quoted
1908 report is the primary document; the 1919 cover letter is a later, self-serving restatement.
Method: primary text outranks a paraphrase, especially where the paraphraser is defending his own
earlier work.

2. **Bulletin No. 214's finding, misquoted twice with the same wrong figure.** The actual bulletin
text — "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and... nothing... in summer" — is
quoted verbatim in four places (r04, r07 Item 12, r11, and r22's appended note, which retrieves the
bulletin directly). Grigg (r15, 1919) and, independently, veterinarian Kepp (r16, 1926) both
instead cite "two tenths." Resolution: four verbatim quotations of the primary text outweigh two
secondhand paraphrases; I judge Kepp's error a coincidental echo of Grigg's, since Kepp cites the
figure at second hand and neither claims to have read the bulletin's primary numbers himself.
Method: verbatim-quotation count plus explicit secondhand sourcing in the losing pair.

3. **The 1925 settlement amount, shared wrong figure.** $3,800 is given by six differently-placed,
independent sources (r02, r05, r07, r08, r13 — the arbitration chairman himself — and r19, r22).
$3,500 is given by two lay narrators (r01 and r21). Resolution: $3,800, on independence and
authority (an eyewitness chairman among the majority) vastly outweighing two secondhand
recollections. Method: count of independent sources plus source authority.

4. **Surviving pipette count, shared wrong figure.** "Nine" appears in four places with some claim
to primacy (r04 — Ivy's own account; r07 Items 5 and 14; r11; r22, citing the bulletin). "Eleven"
appears in two secondary accounts (r12's later footnote, r19's newspaper story). Resolution: nine,
on primary-source weight. Method: primary/official sourcing beats press/pamphlet secondary
reporting.

5. **Pipettes purchased in 1897, shared wrong figure.** "Six dozen" is independently confirmed by
both parties to the original transaction (buyer's catalogue r07 Item 2; seller's own reproduced
invoice, r20). "Five dozen" appears in two independent later narrators (r11, r21). Resolution: six
dozen, since the winning figure is drawn from the actual invoice, reproduced from both sides of the
sale. Method: primary document (both sides) over decades-later recollection.

6. **Mean pipette delivery, self-contradiction within a document.** r11's own body computes 16.27
c.c. and a ratio of ~1-in-13, but the same document's quoted "Conclusion" (Section VII) states the
shortfall as "one part in twenty," consistent only with 16.72 c.c. (Section C1a). Resolution: 16.72
c.c., matching the figure independently given in r04 and r07 Item 5/14, and matching the "one part
in twenty" text the document itself quotes as authoritative. Method: internal consistency plus
external corroboration against the document's own draft-stage figure.

7. **Glassware condemnation date.** "12 June 1923," from the inspector's own notebook (r06) and the
catalogue drawing on it (r07 Items 5, 9), and independently arrived at again in the county report
(r22). "12 July 1923" is shared by two secondary narrators (r08, r12). "1922," an outlier, appears
once (r23, an elderly clerk's uncertain memory). Resolution: 12 June 1923, on primary-source
proximity. Method: closeness to the inspector's own contemporaneous record.

8. **Glass in-service start date.** "1 May 1897" is given by three independent sources, one of them
contemporaneous (Orra Keddie's 1897 diary, r24; plus r07's provenance note and r20's invoice-
adjacent record). "1 May 1898" is shared by two later narrators (r11, r23). Resolution: 1897, on
contemporaneity plus independent corroboration. Method: a contemporaneous diary entry outranks two
decades-later recollections.

9. **Orra Keddie's kinship to Emmet Brant.** Full sister (r09, explicit, framed as correcting a
rumor) vs. half-sister "by his father's second wife" (r17, Emmet's own granddaughter). Cannot be
settled: both are direct-family sources, one generation apart, and neither is contemporaneous.
Method: flagged as genuinely unresolved; noted that the closer-generation source (Emmet's own son)
explicitly frames his claim as a correction, which is weak evidence in its favor but not proof.

10. **Number of families sent the 1919 water circular.** "Nine" (r09, r17, both Brant-family later
recollections) vs. "eleven" (r24, Orra Keddie's own contemporaneous 1919 diary entry). Resolution:
I lean toward eleven, since it is the only contemporaneous account, against two later recollections
that may share a common family memory rather than being independently arrived at — but I flag this
as a case where a numerical "majority" (two against one) is not decisive, per the test's own
instruction to weigh independence over count.

11. **Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.** Granddaughter (r18, her own father, explicit and
self-correcting of prior errors) vs. "great-niece" (r19; also r22's own first draft, which
self-corrects). Resolution: granddaughter. Method: the closest possible witness (her father)
against two press/report errors, one of which corrects itself on the page.

12. **Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.** "Second cousins," independently and carefully
derived by three family sources (r09, r17, r18) vs. "first cousins once removed" (r21, an outside,
secondhand account). Resolution: second cousins. Method: convergent independent derivation by
family insiders over one outside layman's guess.

13. **Total station milk intake.** 40,000,000 lb (r02, r04, r07, ledger-based) vs. 44,000,000 lb
(r05, r12). Resolution: 40,000,000 lb, since the ledger-based figures are explicitly sourced to the
ledgers themselves, while neither source for 44,000,000 cites its origin. Method: sourcing
specificity.

14. **Recorded average test.** 3.61 (r02, r04, r07, implied elsewhere) vs. 3.16 (r05, r13).
Resolution: 3.61, as the figure consistent with the corrected 3.80 true average and the 0.19-point
gap independently corroborated in Section C1b; 3.16 does not fit that arithmetic (3.80 − 3.16 =
0.64, far outside any gap figure given anywhere else). Method: arithmetic consistency with the
best-supported surrounding figures.

15. **1896 first-season intake, three-way conflict, none corroborated.** See Section C5b: "no
figure exists" (r07) vs. "~900,000 lb" (r14) vs. "~1,100,000 lb" (r23). Resolution: cannot be
determined; the two positive figures contradict each other and neither is documented.

16. **Number of arbitration sessions and location.** "Sessions at Ashlin only" (implied by an
account that does not mention the others) vs. "three sessions, two at the station itself,"
independently confirmed by the board's own chairman (r13), by family notes (r18), by an eyewitness
who stood outside (r21), and by a contemporaneous diary (r24). Resolution: three sessions, two at
the station. Method: four independent, some eyewitness/primary, sources against a document that
simply omits the detail rather than actively denying it — treated as incompleteness, not a true
conflict.

17. **Vose–Keddie family connection.** Denied explicitly and at length by Duncan Keddie (r18) vs.
asserted vaguely and admittedly unverifiably by Cleve Oram (r21: "cousins of some sort... I could
not draw you the line on paper"). Resolution: no relationship. Method: first-person, detailed
denial over an explicitly hedged secondhand claim.

18. **Selby Vose's parentage.** Son of Gideon Vose (r18, r23, both explicit and both independently
rejecting a "nephew" rumor) — no genuine documentary claim of "nephew" exists; it is cited by both
winning sources only as a rumor they debunk. Not a true document-vs-document conflict.

19. **Rosalie Cudd's relationship to Jerome Cudd.** Daughter (r10, her own letter; r18,
independently) vs. "niece" (r19, a newspaper error). Resolution: daughter. Method: her own
first-person letter over a press account.

20. **Arbitration year.** 1925 (heavily corroborated, including dated diary entries in r24) vs.
"1924" (r23, one elderly clerk's account, elsewhere shown to be imprecise about dates). Resolution:
1925. Method: dated primary-adjacent sources over one admittedly hazy memoir decades later.

## Section F — Theory

**The three competing explanations, and what defeats each:**

1. **Water added by the patrons** — advanced by manager Selby Vose (the 1919 circular, and again
at the 1925 hearing). Defeated by: the state dairy inspector's own lactometer tests, taken at every
circuit visit from 1908 forward, which never once found added water at the station (r22); and by
the Alder Corners/Larrow Green corroboration (Section A Story 7) showing the same patrons' milk
tested normally wherever else it was measured.

2. **The tester stealing/skimming cream** — advanced obliquely by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague at the
1925 hearing (r13). Defeated decisively by the Association's own ledger, which the bookkeeper
himself had written: cream arrived at Larrow Green consistently *heavier* than credited at the
station, every year, a pattern that stolen cream cannot produce (Section D2).

3. **Wet, sour Hessel Bottom hay depressing natural butterfat** — advanced by the 1908 Grigg
committee and defended into the 1920s by veterinarian Dr. Kepp, citing Bulletin No. 214. Defeated
by the bulletin's own actual text (a seasonal 0.05-point effect in winter and none in summer,
against a constant year-round 0.19-point gap it cannot explain) and, more directly, by the 1911
substituted-measure experiment, in which tests changed with the instrument and not with the hay,
herd, or season.

**Unified theory.** The station's original 1897 pipettes were manufactured short of their marked
17.6 c.c. charge — most plausibly, per the glassmaker's own historian, because a mold was reground
and a lot ran consistently short in a way no ordinary spot-check could catch (r20). A pipette that
under-delivers milk by a fixed proportion produces a butterfat reading low by that same fixed
proportion on every single test, forever, regardless of season, herd, or feed — which is exactly
why the gap was constant year-round rather than seasonal (ruling out hay) and exactly why it was
invisible from inside the operation for a generation (every internal check — scales against
scales, book against book — would appear self-consistent, because the same instrument was used for
all of them). It began the day the flawed glass entered service (1 May 1897) and ended the instant
that glass was condemned and replaced (1923), after which the station's average immediately and
permanently rose to match the reconstructed true value — with no change whatsoever required of the
patrons, the herds, the hay, or the tester, because nothing about any of them had ever actually
been the problem.

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?** No. Per the board's own chairman, its
exhibit list held only four items — the station test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's
private weigh book, and the state inspector's 1923 circuit notebook — and did not include the
Association's ledgers (which held the exculpatory "station gain" entries) or the glass itself
(r13). The board had documentary notice, via the inspector's notebook, that the glass had already
been condemned two years earlier, but there is no evidence it was ever physically tested or even
discussed as a possible cause at the hearing.

**What did the tester himself believe?** Publicly, nothing — he set only a bare question mark in
his book after the 1911 anomaly rather than assert a conclusion he could not prove (r18), and his
own wife's diary and his own surviving letter record no accusation of anyone (r03, r24). Privately,
per the arbitration chairman's own account of a corridor conversation, Keddie confided that he "had
always thought the patrons were at fault" — i.e., he privately suspected the water-added theory
himself, against the very men accusing him (r13) — a suspicion, it turned out, as mistaken as
everyone else's.

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

For a generation, the Ostrey Hollow milk-testing station in the Larrow Valley paid its patrons on
butterfat tests that ran about 0.19 points low, year-round, costing them roughly $19,000 they were
never credited. Three theories circulated — the patrons were watering their milk, the tester was
skimming cream, or the bottom's wet hay thinned it naturally — and each was argued, believed, and
acted on for decades, culminating in a 1925 hearing that fined no one, paid the patrons a fraction
of what they were owed, and dismissed the tester in disgrace. The true cause, unsuspected by
anyone, was a bad lot of testing glassware bought in 1897 that delivered a slightly short charge on
every single test, forever. It took eight separate records — ledgers, diaries, a weigh book, an
inspector's notebook, oral histories, and a 1958 college bulletin — because no single person alive
ever held all of them at once, and the one document that could have settled it sat in a crate in a
loft for thirty-five years.
