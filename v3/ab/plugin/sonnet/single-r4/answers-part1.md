# Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

*Partition chosen by the answerer; documents cited by their r-numbers. Reconstructed entirely
from the 24 retellings, with conflicts resolved and flagged as encountered.*

---

## Story 1 — The Association and the Station (1889–1897)

Gideon Vose organized the farmers of the Larrow Valley into the Larrow Valley Cooperative
Creamery Association, meeting at Larrow Green in 1889, and wrote its by-laws himself, including
Article VII: every patron is paid on the pounds of butterfat his milk tests for, not on the
weight of the milk, and the test made at the point of delivery governs his pay. The Association
built its main creamery at Larrow Green in 1891. No date survives for the by-laws' formal
adoption (the phrase is only "adopted at the annual meeting") because the Association's early
minute book was lost in an office move (r22). In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a
skimming station seven miles up-valley at Ostrey Hollow to serve Hessel Bottom, a wet, six-farm
bottom along the creek. The station opened 4 May 1896. Ansel Keddie was its tester and separator
man from the opening, a post he held twenty-nine seasons (r18, r22, r24). For its first season or
two the station used borrowed testing glass rather than its own: the station book's own first
leaf and independent testimony from buttermaker Hobart Sill (r14, who personally lent his own
Larrow Green glassware) agree the loan came from Larrow Green, against one internally
inconsistent phrase in an early retelling that says "off the Ashlin creamery." The station's own
outfit — bought from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet, invoiced 14 April 1897 for $77.93 (six dozen
pipettes, twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, a hand tester, freight; r20, corroborated by
r07's catalogue) — went into use 1 May 1897, a date fixed independently by the seller's own
invoice record (r20), the catalogue's provenance note (r07), and Orra Keddie's contemporaneous
diary entry of that exact date (r24) — three independent sources against two later, secondary
claims of "1898" (r11, r23).

The milk taken in during the station's first partial season (May–December 1896) cannot be
determined: the 1972 catalogue states flatly that no such figure survives in any ledger (r07),
while two much later personal recollections give conflicting numbers (900,000 lb per r14; 1.1
million lb per r23) that cannot both be right and cannot outweigh the archival negative.

Documents: r02, r07, r09, r12, r14, r18, r20, r22, r23, r24.

## Story 2 — The Chronic Complaint (1902–1919)

From about 1902 the patrons of Ostrey Hollow complained that their milk tested lower than milk
delivered anywhere else in the valley — not occasionally, but constantly, in every season, for a
generation. Because payment was by test (Article VII, Story 1), this was a running loss of real
money, not a grievance of pride. The gap itself is reported at several values — 0.17 (r09), 0.19
(r04's implied calculation, explicitly confirmed as "the bulletin['s]" figure in r22, and again
independently in r17's "nineteen hundredths"), 0.21 (r16) — and I resolve on 0.19 as the figure
with the most independent, deliberate corroboration (see Section C1). In 1908 the Association
appointed a committee of three under Nyle Grigg to investigate. The committee visited all six
Hessel Bottom farms, drew and sealed its own samples, checked scales, and reported finding no
dishonesty anywhere; its explanation was that the bottom's wet, sour hay thinned the cream. Its
own report states the sampling covered only January–March 1908 (r15, r07 Item 11) — a claim
undercut by Grigg's own 1919 covering letter reprinting that same report, which claims sampling
ran "the whole of 1908," a direct self-contradiction inside one document (r15). That letter also
misquotes the State College's later Bulletin No. 214 as finding a "two tenths" depression from
wet hay, an error independently repeated seventeen years later by veterinarian Dr. Roland Kepp
(r16) — the bulletin's own text, quoted verbatim in four other places, actually says "not more
than five hundredths... in winter, and... nothing... in summer" (see Section E).

Matters escalated in 1919: the Association's new manager, Selby Vose, circulated a written
warning about added water in the milk to the Hessel Bottom patrons — nine families by two
accounts (r09, r17) or eleven by Orra Keddie's own contemporaneous 1919 diary (r24). Ansel Keddie,
the accused tester, delivered the circular himself rather than mail it, a decision that estranged
him for the rest of his life from his own brother-in-law and largest patron, Emmet Brant, who felt
Keddie should have publicly objected rather than carried the accusation (r09, r24). No patron
replied to the circular at all — "a shout," in one narrator's words (r17).

Documents: r09, r15, r16, r17, r22, r23, r24.

## Story 3 — The 1911 Measure (May–July 1911)

On 8 May 1911 the station's own testing glass broke, and a borrowed measure was used in its place
for the length of the repair — nine weeks, until 10 July 1911, per five independent citations
(r02, r03, r04, r11, r22), against a "six weeks" figure found only in two accounts that both trace
to secondhand hearsay rather than the tester's own book (r05; and r16, which explicitly attributes
it to "a gentleman" who raised it, i.e., not Kepp's own knowledge). During those nine weeks the
station's recorded tests rose markedly; the day the station's own (defective) glass went back into
use, the tests fell back to their former level. The tester recorded the fact and set a bare
question mark against it rather than draw a conclusion he could not prove (r18). Nothing else at
the station changed in that window — same herds, same pasture, same season throughout most of the
interval, same tester, same supervision, same patrons.

Treated as a natural experiment, this is the single strongest piece of evidence in the whole
record, because it isolates the instrument as the variable while holding every human and
biological factor constant. It rules out, at a stroke, watered milk (the cows and farms did not
change), stolen cream (the same tester was making the tests, unsupervised differently), and wet
hay (the land and the season were unchanged). What it does not establish is the magnitude or
mechanism of the fault — it shows that the glass was the cause, not how much it was wrong by or
why. That required the physical measurement of the glassware itself, which did not happen for
another forty-seven years (Story 8).

Documents: r02, r03, r04, r09, r11, r16, r18, r22, r24.

## Story 4 — The Brant Weigh Book (1897–1929, and after)

Emmet Brant, the largest patron on Hessel Bottom and Ansel Keddie's brother-in-law, began his own
private weigh book on 1 January 1897 (per r18's family record) or 1 January 1898 (per r09, his
own son's account of the book) — a minor date discrepancy I cannot resolve, though I weight Roy
Brant's direct description of his father's own book (r09) over Duncan Keddie's secondhand family
notes (r18) since Roy actually held and used the book. In it, Emmet recorded his own scale reading
alongside the station's own figure for every delivery; the two, he insisted, always agreed — "it
was not the weighing," in his son's words. The book's best-known leaf, quoted identically by three
independent tellers (r04/r07's catalogue, r09, and r17), records the year 1910: two hundred
thousand pounds of milk delivered, scales agreeing, paid on a test of 3.42. Set against the
station's true average of roughly 3.61 (the gap being 0.19 points, Story 2), this single leaf
shows Emmet Brant was shorted about 380 lb of butterfat that year alone (Section C2).

The book passed from Emmet (d. 1929) to his son Roy, then to Roy's daughter Hazel, who deposited
it at the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1963 (r18). In 1958 Roy lent it — pointedly lent, not
gave — to his second cousin's daughter, Ivy Keddie, then a graduate student researching the
station; both Roy's son-in-law generation and granddaughter Hazel insist on the word "lent" as a
matter of family honor (r09, r17). The book is catalogued as kept "by E. Brant," but this applies
only loosely, since the comparably-named station test book (a separate document, all patrons, kept
by Ansel Keddie in his own hand) is sometimes conflated with it by casual readers; the two must be
kept distinct (r07, r13, r18).

Documents: r04, r07, r09, r13, r17, r18.
