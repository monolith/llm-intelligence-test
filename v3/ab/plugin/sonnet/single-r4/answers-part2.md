## Story 5 — The Keddie Family and the Tester's Ordeal (1889–1934)

Ansel Keddie (1861–1934) married Orra Brant (b. 1866 per her own diary's preface, r24; b. 1868
per her son Duncan's notes, r18 — I favor her own account as the primary source) in 1889. Orra was
a sister of Emmet Brant, the valley's largest Ostrey Hollow patron; whether she was his full sister
or his half-sister "by his father's second wife" is a genuine, unresolved conflict between two
direct descendants — Emmet's son Roy explicitly denies any stepmother story (r09), while Roy's own
daughter Hazel asserts one (r17). I cannot settle this; I note only that Roy, one generation
closer and explicitly correcting a rumor, seems the stronger witness, but this is a judgment call,
not a proof. Ansel and Orra's son Duncan (b. 1894) became a schoolteacher at Ashlin; his daughter
is Ivy Keddie (b. 1931), the researcher of Story 8 — Ansel's granddaughter, not his great-niece as
one newspaper account and one early draft of a county report mistakenly had it (r18, r19, r22 —
the county report explicitly catches and corrects its own error).

Ansel tested the milk of every patron on the bottom, including his own brother-in-law, for
twenty-nine seasons, all the while the station's tests ran low. When he personally delivered the
1919 water-accusation circular (Story 2) rather than let it come by post, Emmet Brant broke with
him for the rest of Emmet's life (d. 1929) — a rift Orra's own diary records almost to the day
(r24). Publicly Ansel never accused anyone and set only a question mark against the 1911 anomaly
rather than a conclusion he could not prove (r18); privately, per the 1925 arbitration board's own
chairman, Ansel confided in a hallway that he had "always thought the patrons were at fault and
would not say so in the room" (r13) — suspecting, in secret, the very men who suspected him.
Dismissed 30 April 1925, four days before the board's award, he lived out his life doing carpentry
around the valley, and died in 1934 still under the county's suspicion; "not one of them said the
other thing" at his funeral (r24). Orra outlived him, dying in 1948, twenty-four years before his
name was formally cleared and ten years before it was even attempted.

Documents: r03, r09, r13, r17, r18, r21, r24.

## Story 6 — The 1925 Arbitration

The patrons petitioned formally in January 1925. A board of arbitration, chaired by attorney
Alonzo Frick, sat three times: once at the Association's office in Ashlin (17 March–4 May 1925)
and twice more at the station itself, in its own weigh room, so the board could examine the
physical plant — a detail confirmed independently by the chairman himself (r13), by Duncan Keddie's
family notes (r18), by cream-hauler's son Cleve Oram, who stood outside on the platform in the
rain during one such sitting (r21), and by Orra Keddie's own contemporaneous diary (r24: "Two of
the sittings held up here at the station itself"). Three theories were argued: water added by the
patrons (the manager's view), cream skimmed by the tester (raised obliquely by bookkeeper Wilbur
Teague), and wet hay (the 1908 committee's finding, reprinted for the board). The board's own
exhibit list, per its chairman, held only four items — the station test book, the receiving book,
Emmet Brant's private weigh book, and the state inspector's 1923 circuit notebook — and did not
include the Association's own annual ledgers, meaning the board never saw the "station gain"
entries that would have discredited the cream-theft theory (r13; see Section F).

The board found no cause and awarded the patrons $3,800, "without admission" — a figure
overwhelmingly corroborated (r02, r05, r07, r08, r13, r19, r22) against a "$3,500" figure shared by
only two lay narrators, Cleve Oram and one other account (r21 and the outlier r01), which I treat
as a repeated recollection error rather than a genuine second data point. Ansel Keddie was
dismissed 30 April 1925, four days before the award was announced, so that — as his son put it —
"the dismissal said everything" the finding declined to (r18).

Documents: r02, r05, r07, r08, r13, r18, r19, r21, r22, r24.

## Story 7 — Outside Corroboration: Alder Corners and Larrow Green

Independent of the Ostrey Hollow station, two other sets of records bear on whether the Hessel
Bottom patrons' milk was actually thin or watered. Four of the six Hessel Bottom farms hauled their
milk over the ridge to the Alder Corners Cheese Company (Vessey County) during the summer flush,
roughly June to mid-September, for sixteen seasons, 1915 through 1930 (r09, r10). Alder Corners'
founder, Jerome Cudd, tested independently on his own glassware and never compared figures with
Ostrey Hollow; his own 1919 book entry records those four patrons testing 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, and
3.82 — "good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge." His daughter Rosalie Cudd, who
ran the factory after his 1941 death, supplied Ivy Keddie in 1958 with the full run of summer
averages, 1915–1930, consistently 3.78–3.82, and noted that cheesemaking reveals watered milk
immediately through soft curd and poor yield — and her father never once suspected these four men
in sixteen years (r10). Separately, Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green for 41 years, states
that Ostrey Hollow's cream, re-weighed on arrival, consistently ran heavier in fat than the
station's own paperwork claimed — "always more, never less" — matching the Association's own
ledger pattern (Story 6; r14).

Both bodies of evidence point the same direction — the patrons' milk, tested elsewhere or
re-weighed independently, was never thin — but neither Cudd nor Sill personally drew the
conclusion that exonerated Keddie; Sill in fact privately believed, like Keddie's own confided
suspicion, that "somebody up that hill road was helping the cans along" (r14). The corroboration
is real; the inference from it was not drawn until 1958.

Documents: r09, r10, r14.

## Story 8 — The Crate in the Loft (1958–1959)

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie — a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland,
and Ansel Keddie's granddaughter — climbed a ladder into the loft over the Ostrey Hollow station's
intake room and found the station's original, condemned glassware still crated under a tarpaulin,
where it had lain since the state dairy inspector condemned it in 1923 (best-supported date: 12
June 1923, per the inspector's own notebook and the catalogue that draws on it, against a less
well-supported "12 July 1923" shared by two secondary narrators and an outlying "1922" from one
elderly clerk's memory — see Section E). Of the six dozen pipettes bought new in 1897, nine
survived in measurable condition (the best-supported count, against an "eleven" shared by two
secondary press/pamphlet accounts). On 9 March 1958 she measured them against a calibrated burette
at Farland: each was blown to the standard 17.6 c.c. charge but delivered a mean of only 16.72
c.c. — a proportional shortfall of one part in twenty, exactly matching the station's own recorded
average (3.61) against a reconstructed true average of 3.80.

Working with Professor Leland Vaught, she published the findings as Bulletin No. 471 (October
1958): a short pipette delivers a short charge every time, in the same proportion, indistinguishable
from the inside of the plant from a genuinely low-testing milk — explaining why the fault was
constant year-round, why it began the moment the flawed glass went into service (1 May 1897), and
why it ended the moment that glass was replaced (1923). The Ashlin Chronicle ran the story on 3
October 1958; the county agricultural agent filed an official summary in 1959, at first repeating
two errors (a concession to the wet-hay theory, and Ivy's relationship to Ansel) that he then
caught and retracted himself on receipt of the actual text of Bulletin No. 214. Ansel Keddie's name
was formally cleared twenty-four years after his death; his widow, Orra, had died ten years too
soon to see it.

Documents: r04, r07, r11, r19, r20, r22.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.** Granddaughter. Ansel and Orra Keddie's son
Duncan (b. 1894) is Ivy's father (r18); Duncan states this plainly and notes he has "already
twice seen it printed otherwise," which is exactly what happens elsewhere in the record: the
Ashlin Chronicle (r19) and the county agent's first draft (r22) both call her Ansel's
"great-niece." Duncan, writing as her father, is the decisive source; Falke's report (r22) itself
independently confirms the correction after verifying it. I resolve for granddaughter.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.** Second cousins. Orra Keddie (née Brant) and
Emmet Brant were siblings; their children, Duncan Keddie and Roy Brant, are therefore first
cousins; Ivy (Duncan's daughter) and Hazel (Roy's daughter) are the children of first cousins, i.e.
second cousins — a chain independently worked out and stated in these exact terms by three
family-insider sources (r09, r17, r18), two of whom explicitly reject "cousins once removed" or
"aunt and niece" as popular but wrong labels people kept applying. Cleve Oram's "first cousins once
removed" (r21) is an outside, secondhand account and is outweighed. What the sources leave open:
whether Orra was Emmet's full or half sister (Story 5) is unresolved, but it does not change the
cousin arithmetic either way — Duncan and Roy are first cousins under either version, so the
"second cousins" conclusion for Ivy and Hazel holds regardless.

**B3. Selby Vose's father.** Gideon Vose, who organized the Association in 1889, was its first
president, and personally wrote its by-laws, including Article VII (payment by butterfat test) —
confirmed independently by Duncan Keddie's family notes and by Bertram Kyle, the office clerk who
worked alongside Selby for a quarter-century and explicitly rejects a competing "nephew" rumor
(r18, r23). It matters because Selby inherited not just the Association's leadership but, per
Kyle, its founder's institution nearly unexamined: Selby signed the ledger sheets carrying the
station's own "station gain" figures every year without, in Kyle's account, ever reading the lines
above the bottom total (r23) — and, as the son of the man whose by-law made every test a matter of
money, he had every institutional reason to look for the fault in the patrons (the 1919 circular)
rather than in the Association's own equipment.

**B4. Vose–Keddie relationship.** None, on the weight of the evidence. Duncan Keddie devotes a
section of his own family notes to denying any connection, calling it valley talk that his mother
— by his account a woman who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection" —
never once asserted (r18). Cleve Oram's account that "the Keddies and the Voses were cousins of
some sort" is explicitly hedged ("I could not draw you the line on paper") and secondhand from his
mother (r21). I resolve for no relationship, weighting Duncan's careful, first-person denial over
Oram's admittedly unverifiable claim.
