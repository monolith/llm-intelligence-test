# Answer Sheet — Part 2 of 3 (Section A, Stories 5–8; Section B)

## Story 5 — The 1925 Arbitration Hearing

**Who:** The board of arbitration sitting at Ashlin and at the Ostrey Hollow station; the Ostrey
Hollow patrons as petitioners; Ansel Keddie as the accused tester; Selby Vose as the Association's
manager.

**What/when:** Patrons petitioned in January 1925. The board sat from 17 March to 4 May 1925 (r22,
the most precisely dated account), partly at Ashlin and partly in the station's own weigh room,
because the books and scales were there (r21, r24 both independently describe sessions held at the
station, with onlookers standing outside). Ansel Keddie was dismissed on 30 April 1925, four days
before the board's award (r18, r22). The board found no cause established and awarded the patrons
**$3,800**, "without admission" — so stated identically by r22 (official report) and r23 (the clerk
who personally processed the charge to the station account). Cleve Oram (r21), recalling his
father's account secondhand, instead gives $3,500; I resolve this in favor of $3,800, on two
independent, better-placed sources against one secondhand recollection. The clerk (r23) dates the
award to "twenty-four" (1924); I take this as his memory error, since Falke's detailed report (r22)
ties the award to a specific date four days after the 30 April 1925 dismissal. The condemned glass
crate hung, unexamined, in the loft directly above two of the three hearing sessions the entire
time (r18, r21) — nobody thought to look at it.

**Quantities:** $3,800 award; three sittings; dismissal 30 April 1925; award early May 1925.

**Documents:** r21, r22, r23, r24 (diary entries bracketing the hearing dates), r18.

## Story 6 — Orin Falke's County Investigation and the Three Theories

**Who:** Orin Falke, Ordell County agricultural agent, writing a formal report for the county board
in 1959, incorporating and correcting the college's 1958 bulletin.

**What/when:** Falke's numbered report (r22) is the single most systematic source in this set. It
establishes the Association's founding (1889, Larrow Green creamery 1891), the station's opening
(4 May 1896), and lays out and individually defeats three rival theories for the low tests: added
water (defeated by lactometer tests finding none from 1908 on), tester fraud (defeated by the 1911
nine-week interval when a borrowed measuring glass produced higher, then reverted, readings under
the same tester), and wet-hay depression of butterfat (initially half-credited, then explicitly
withdrawn in an appended note citing the college's own 1911 bog-hay feeding trial, which found at
most a 0.05-point winter effect — far too small to explain a ~0.19-point, all-season deficiency).
Falke's report contains an internal arithmetic inconsistency worth flagging: it states the station's
recorded average was 3.61% against a Larrow Green comparator of 3.78% (a 0.17-point gap) but then
asserts the bulletin "fixes" the deficiency at "nineteen hundredths" (0.19) — the two stated numbers
don't subtract to the stated difference. I cannot resolve this discrepancy from the sources; I flag
it rather than silently picking one figure.

**Quantities:** Recorded average 3.61%; comparator 3.78%; stated deficiency 0.19 points (internally
inconsistent, see above); condemnation date 12 June 1923; award $3,800.

**Documents:** r22 (primary), citing the 1958 college bulletin and Bulletin No. 214.

## Story 7 — The Cooperative's Office and Ledger

**Who:** Bertram Kyle, outer-office clerk at the Association's Larrow Green headquarters for
twenty-five years; manager Selby Vose; bookkeeper Wilbur Teague.

**What/when:** Kyle's 1953 memoir (r23) describes the annual station account for Ostrey Hollow:
pounds credited to patrons on the station test, pounds actually received in cream at Larrow Green,
and the difference, entered every year as "station gain... five and a quarter per cent, as usual."
This ledger entry — a contemporaneous, decades-long bookkeeping record — is significant: it shows
the "missing" butterfat was never actually missing; it arrived at the creamery and was simply booked
as unexplained institutional profit, which is inconsistent with the theft theory (a thief would
make the fat vanish, not reappear on the books). Nobody in the office, including Kyle himself,
questioned this line for a quarter-century — "a figure with a name on it has been explained." Kyle
also independently confirms the four documents actually submitted to the 1925 arbitration board
(test book, receiving book, Brant's private weigh book, and the state inspector's notebook), and
states plainly that no one compared them side by side. Kyle dates the station's own glass to 1 May
1898, a year later than the invoice (r20) and diary (r24) agree on; I take 1898 as Kyle's memory
error, made decades after the fact, against two independent contemporaneous records.

**Quantities:** 40,000,000 lb of milk received at the station over "the long run of years" (roughly
1.6 million lb/year); "station gain" of 5.25% annually; average price of 25 cents per pound of
butterfat over the period.

**Documents:** r23 (primary), cross-checked against r18, r20, r22.

## Story 8 — Orra Keddie's Domestic Diary

**Who:** Orra Keddie (née Brant), Ansel's wife, keeping a diary from 1897 to 1930 (plus one leaf
from 1934), published posthumously by an editor in 1964.

**What/when:** The diary is the only contemporaneous, day-by-day primary source in this set and
corroborates several disputed dates precisely: the station's own glass arriving "the first of May"
1897 (matching the Ammon invoice exactly, against Kyle's 1898); the broken measure on 8 May 1911
(matching Falke's report of the borrowed-glass interval to the day); and the sequence of the 1925
hearing (17 March–30 April 1925). It also records the 1919 water-accusation circular, which Ansel
personally delivered to eleven families rather than mailing it (a figure that conflicts with Hazel
Brant's "nine families," r17 — I favor the diary's eleven as the contemporaneous, household-level
account against a later secondhand recollection), and the estrangement it caused between Ansel and
his brother-in-law Emmet Brant, which lasted from June 1919 until Emmet's death in spring 1929. The
diary ends with Ansel's death on a morning in February 1934, still unexonerated; an editor's note
records that Orra herself died in 1948, ten years before her husband's name was cleared.

**Quantities:** Eleven families given circulars (June 1919); nine-year estrangement (1919–1929);
Ansel's death February 1934; exoneration came 24 years later, in 1958, which the diary's household
never lived to see.

**Documents:** r24 (primary), cross-checked for dates against r17, r18, r22, r23.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1.** Ivy Keddie is Ansel Keddie's **granddaughter**, daughter of Ansel's son Duncan Keddie
(r18: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter"). Two other
sources say "great-niece" instead — the 1958 Chronicle (r19) and the county agent's report as
originally drafted (r22) — but r22 itself appends an explicit correction: "I am informed, and have
verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is
mine." That self-correction, plus Duncan's own firsthand family notes, settle it: granddaughter.

**B2.** Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant are **second cousins**: Ivy's father Duncan Keddie and Hazel's
father Roy Brant were first cousins (children of siblings Orra Brant Keddie and Emmet Brant), so
their own children, Ivy and Hazel, are one generation further removed from that sibling link —
second cousins, not cousins once removed. Both Hazel (r17) and Duncan (r18) state this explicitly
and independently warn against the "once removed" mislabeling that Cleve Oram (r21) in fact uses.
Nothing about the intervening generations is left open by the sources; the chain (Orra/Emmet →
Duncan/Roy → Ivy/Hazel) is fully and consistently documented.

**B3.** Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**, who organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative
Creamery Association and served as its first president (r18, confirmed independently by r23, both
of which also explicitly reject a rumored "nephew" relationship). This matters because it means the
family that founded the Association was still in charge of it — as manager — when the low-test
crisis reached its head: Selby Vose personally signed the 1919 circular accusing Ostrey Hollow
patrons of watering their milk, and signed the annual ledger sheets carrying the unexplained
"station gain" line for the whole of his tenure from 1911 (see D3, Part 3).

**B4.** The sources do not establish a real family connection between the Voses and the Keddies.
Duncan Keddie (r18) denies it explicitly and in detail, attributing the persistent local rumor to
confusion with an unrelated family, "the Ashlin Voses," and noting that his mother — who knew every
family connection in the valley — never once named a Vose among their kin. Cleve Oram (r21) repeats
the rumor as unverified hearsay from his mother's mother, admitting he "could not draw you the line
on paper." Given one source's detailed, reasoned denial against the other's admitted uncertainty,
the weight of evidence favors *no* real connection — but this cannot be proven outright from what I
have, since no source actually traces a full genealogy for the Voses the way r18 does for the
Keddies and Brants.
