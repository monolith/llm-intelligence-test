partitioned *what I have* into eight titled sub-stories along natural seams (family record-keeping,

## Story 1 — The Brant Weigh Book and Hessel Bottom

**Who:** Emmet Brant, his son Roy, and granddaughter Hazel Brant, farmers on Hessel Bottom, the six-
farm wet bottomland at Ostrey Hollow, in the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association.

**What/when:** Emmet Brant kept a private household weigh-book recording his own milk weight
alongside the station's figures, begun — per Duncan Keddie's careful genealogical notes (r18) —
on 1 January 1897; Hazel Brant (r17), speaking from an interview in which she explicitly disclaims
any reliability with dates ("Years I have not"), instead recalls 1900. I resolve this in Duncan's
favor: his notes are a deliberately dated written record, while Hazel pre-emptively flags her own
weakness on chronology. The book passed Emmet → Roy (1929) → Hazel (1962) → the Ordell County
Historical Rooms (1963, per r18). Ivy Keddie borrowed it in 1958 for her research — both Hazel and
Duncan insist, independently, that it was *lent*, not left, a distinction both found people got
wrong.

**Quantities:** Six farms on Hessel Bottom, nine families among them (r17). The best-documented
single leaf is 1910: Emmet delivered 200,000 lb of milk, paid on a recorded test of 3.42%, with his
own scale agreeing with the station's. The bottom's tests ran a fairly constant "nineteen
hundredths" (0.19 points) below milk tested anywhere else, in every season — a figure that recurs
independently in r17 and in the county agent's report (r22, drawn from the 1958 college bulletin).

**Documents:** r17 (Hazel Brant), r18 (Duncan Keddie), corroborated in part by r22 and r24.

## Story 2 — Keddie–Brant–Vose Kinship and the Rumors

**Who:** Ansel Keddie (tester), his wife Orra (née Brant), their son Duncan, Duncan's daughter Ivy;
Emmet Brant (Orra's brother) and his descendants Roy and Hazel; Gideon Vose (Association founder)
and his son Selby Vose (later manager).

**What:** Duncan Keddie's 1968 family notes (r18) are the most careful genealogical source and
settle several points the other narrators get wrong. Ivy is Ansel's **granddaughter** (daughter of
Ansel's son Duncan) — not his "great-niece," as the 1958 Chronicle (r19) and even the county agent's
first draft (r22) both stated; Falke (r22) himself appends a correction admitting the error was his
and confirming granddaughter is right, matching Duncan. Ivy and Hazel Brant are **second cousins**
(children of first cousins Duncan Keddie and Roy Brant, who were themselves children of siblings
Orra and Emmet) — not "cousins once removed," the error Cleve Oram (r21) repeats and that both Hazel
(r17) and Duncan (r18), independently and unprompted, explicitly warn readers against. Selby Vose
was Gideon Vose's **son**, not nephew — Duncan (r18) and clerk Bertram Kyle (r23) independently
confirm this and both explicitly note and reject a circulating "nephew" story. Finally, Duncan
firmly denies any real kinship between the Vose and Keddie families, tracing the confusion to an
unrelated family, "the Ashlin Voses"; Oram (r21) repeats the rumor as vague, unverifiable hearsay
("could not draw you the line on paper"). Weight of evidence favors no real Vose–Keddie connection,
though this cannot be proven with certainty from what I have.

**Quantities:** Generational math only — one generation (siblings) to first cousins to second
cousins; no numeric quantities proper.

**Documents:** r18 (primary), r17, r19, r21, r22.

## Story 3 — Ammon & Sons and the Origin of the Bad Glass

**Who:** Marcus Ammon's dairy-supply firm at Tarnet; the firm's own historian Junia Ammon, writing
in 1949.

**What/when:** On 14 April 1897 Ammon & Sons invoiced the Larrow Valley Association for a complete
skimming-station outfit for Ostrey Hollow: 6 dozen (72) milk-test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. at
$0.35 each ($25.20), 12 dozen test bottles ($31.68), one acid measure ($0.90), one hand tester
($18.00), and freight ($2.15). Summing those line items gives a total of **$77.93** — but the
surrounding prose in r20 itself, and independently in r17 and r19, all instead state "seventy-seven
dollars and thirty-nine cents" ($77.39). This is an internal contradiction inside r20 (the quoted
invoice figures don't match its own prose) and a value three narrators share; I resolve it by
arithmetic — summing the reproduced line items is independently checkable and gives $77.93, so
$77.93 is correct and $77.39 is a shared transcription/rounding error. The outfit went into service
at Ostrey Hollow on 1 May 1897 (matching Orra Keddie's diary entry of that exact date, r24 — a rare
case of a firm's invoice and a household diary agreeing to the day). Junia Ammon speculates,
without proof, that a reground mold caused the lot to run systematically short; the firm's own 1897–
1931 production ("works") records burned in 1931, so the true cause can never be confirmed, only
inferred. When the Association wrote in June 1923 asking about the condemned lot, the firm's reply
(quoted in full in r20) said only that its 1890s books "would not tell anybody anything" — an answer
the 1958 Chronicle (r19) treats as damning, though r20 frames it more sympathetically as genuine
record loss.

**Documents:** r20 (primary/firm records), corroborated by r24 (installation date) and r19
(reproduction of the same invoice total error).

## Story 4 — The Loft, the Crate, and Ivy's Discovery

**Who:** Ivy Keddie, graduate student at the State Agricultural College, Farland; Professor Leland
Vaught.

**What/when:** The station's original glassware was condemned by the state dairy inspector and
crated into the loft over the intake room. The year is contested: r18, r19, r20 (dated
correspondence, 28 June/2 July 1923), and r22 (precise date, 12 June 1923) all agree on **1923**;
r17 alone says 1921 and r23 alone says 1922. I resolve this to 1923 on the strength of dated primary
correspondence (r20) and a precisely dated official report (r22), against two single-source, later-
memory dissents that disagree even with each other. The crate sat untouched until February 1958,
when Ivy Keddie climbed the loft ladder and found it under a tarpaulin. How many pipettes survived
35 years of use as a general loft-junk shelf is again contested: r21 and r22 both say **nine**
survivors; r19 alone says eleven. Two independent, more careful sources against one admittedly
rushed newspaper account ("put together in a day and a half," r19) — I resolve this to nine. On 9
March 1958 Ivy measured the survivors against a calibrated burette at Farland: every one was blown
to a 17.6 c.c. mark, but none delivered it; the average actual delivery was 16.27 c.c., a shortfall
of about 7.6%. The findings were published as College Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958, with Vaught)
and covered on the front page of the *Ashlin Chronicle* the same month, clearing Ansel Keddie's name
24 years after his death.

**Documents:** r19, r18, r21, r22 (dates/counts); the measurement itself only in r19 and r22.

## Story 5 — The 1925 Arbitration Hearing

**Who:** The board of arbitration sitting at Ashlin and at the Ostrey Hollow station; the Ostrey
Hollow patrons as petitioners; Ansel Keddie as the accused tester; Selby Vose as the Association's
manager.

**What/when:** Patrons petitioned in January 1925. The board sat from 17 March to 4 May 1925 (r22,
the most precisely dated account), partly at Ashlin and partly in the station's own weigh room,
because the books and scales were there (r21, r24 both independently describe sessions held at the
station, with onlookers standing outside). Ansel Keddie was dismissed on 30 April 1925, four days
before the board's award (r18, r22). The board found no cause established and awarded the patrons
**$3,800**, "without admission" — so stated identically by r22 (official report) and r23 (the clerk
who personally processed the charge to the station account). Cleve Oram (r21), recalling his
father's account secondhand, instead gives $3,500; I resolve this in favor of $3,800, on two
independent, better-placed sources against one secondhand recollection. The clerk (r23) dates the
award to "twenty-four" (1924); I take this as his memory error, since Falke's detailed report (r22)
ties the award to a specific date four days after the 30 April 1925 dismissal. The condemned glass
crate hung, unexamined, in the loft directly above two of the three hearing sessions the entire
time (r18, r21) — nobody thought to look at it.

**Quantities:** $3,800 award; three sittings; dismissal 30 April 1925; award early May 1925.

**Documents:** r21, r22, r23, r24 (diary entries bracketing the hearing dates), r18.

## Story 6 — Orin Falke's County Investigation and the Three Theories

**Who:** Orin Falke, Ordell County agricultural agent, writing a formal report for the county board
in 1959, incorporating and correcting the college's 1958 bulletin.

**What/when:** Falke's numbered report (r22) is the single most systematic source in this set. It
establishes the Association's founding (1889, Larrow Green creamery 1891), the station's opening
(4 May 1896), and lays out and individually defeats three rival theories for the low tests: added
water (defeated by lactometer tests finding none from 1908 on), tester fraud (defeated by the 1911
nine-week interval when a borrowed measuring glass produced higher, then reverted, readings under
the same tester), and wet-hay depression of butterfat (initially half-credited, then explicitly
withdrawn in an appended note citing the college's own 1911 bog-hay feeding trial, which found at
most a 0.05-point winter effect — far too small to explain a ~0.19-point, all-season deficiency).
Falke's report contains an internal arithmetic inconsistency worth flagging: it states the station's
recorded average was 3.61% against a Larrow Green comparator of 3.78% (a 0.17-point gap) but then
asserts the bulletin "fixes" the deficiency at "nineteen hundredths" (0.19) — the two stated numbers
don't subtract to the stated difference. I cannot resolve this discrepancy from the sources; I flag
it rather than silently picking one figure.

**Quantities:** Recorded average 3.61%; comparator 3.78%; stated deficiency 0.19 points (internally
inconsistent, see above); condemnation date 12 June 1923; award $3,800.

**Documents:** r22 (primary), citing the 1958 college bulletin and Bulletin No. 214.

## Story 7 — The Cooperative's Office and Ledger

**Who:** Bertram Kyle, outer-office clerk at the Association's Larrow Green headquarters for
twenty-five years; manager Selby Vose; bookkeeper Wilbur Teague.

**What/when:** Kyle's 1953 memoir (r23) describes the annual station account for Ostrey Hollow:
pounds credited to patrons on the station test, pounds actually received in cream at Larrow Green,
and the difference, entered every year as "station gain... five and a quarter per cent, as usual."
This ledger entry — a contemporaneous, decades-long bookkeeping record — is significant: it shows
the "missing" butterfat was never actually missing; it arrived at the creamery and was simply booked
as unexplained institutional profit, which is inconsistent with the theft theory (a thief would
make the fat vanish, not reappear on the books). Nobody in the office, including Kyle himself,
questioned this line for a quarter-century — "a figure with a name on it has been explained." Kyle
also independently confirms the four documents actually submitted to the 1925 arbitration board
(test book, receiving book, Brant's private weigh book, and the state inspector's notebook), and
states plainly that no one compared them side by side. Kyle dates the station's own glass to 1 May
1898, a year later than the invoice (r20) and diary (r24) agree on; I take 1898 as Kyle's memory
error, made decades after the fact, against two independent contemporaneous records.

**Quantities:** 40,000,000 lb of milk received at the station over "the long run of years" (roughly
1.6 million lb/year); "station gain" of 5.25% annually; average price of 25 cents per pound of
butterfat over the period.

**Documents:** r23 (primary), cross-checked against r18, r20, r22.

## Story 8 — Orra Keddie's Domestic Diary

**Who:** Orra Keddie (née Brant), Ansel's wife, keeping a diary from 1897 to 1930 (plus one leaf
from 1934), published posthumously by an editor in 1964.

**What/when:** The diary is the only contemporaneous, day-by-day primary source in this set and
corroborates several disputed dates precisely: the station's own glass arriving "the first of May"
1897 (matching the Ammon invoice exactly, against Kyle's 1898); the broken measure on 8 May 1911
(matching Falke's report of the borrowed-glass interval to the day); and the sequence of the 1925
hearing (17 March–30 April 1925). It also records the 1919 water-accusation circular, which Ansel
personally delivered to eleven families rather than mailing it (a figure that conflicts with Hazel
Brant's "nine families," r17 — I favor the diary's eleven as the contemporaneous, household-level
account against a later secondhand recollection), and the estrangement it caused between Ansel and
his brother-in-law Emmet Brant, which lasted from June 1919 until Emmet's death in spring 1929. The
diary ends with Ansel's death on a morning in February 1934, still unexonerated; an editor's note
records that Orra herself died in 1948, ten years before her husband's name was cleared.

**Quantities:** Eleven families given circulars (June 1919); nine-year estrangement (1919–1929);
Ansel's death February 1934; exoneration came 24 years later, in 1958, which the diary's household
never lived to see.

**Documents:** r24 (primary), cross-checked for dates against r17, r18, r22, r23.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1.** Ivy Keddie is Ansel Keddie's **granddaughter**, daughter of Ansel's son Duncan Keddie
(r18: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter"). Two other
sources say "great-niece" instead — the 1958 Chronicle (r19) and the county agent's report as
originally drafted (r22) — but r22 itself appends an explicit correction: "I am informed, and have
verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is
mine." That self-correction, plus Duncan's own firsthand family notes, settle it: granddaughter.

**B2.** Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant are **second cousins**: Ivy's father Duncan Keddie and Hazel's
father Roy Brant were first cousins (children of siblings Orra Brant Keddie and Emmet Brant), so
their own children, Ivy and Hazel, are one generation further removed from that sibling link —
second cousins, not cousins once removed. Both Hazel (r17) and Duncan (r18) state this explicitly
and independently warn against the "once removed" mislabeling that Cleve Oram (r21) in fact uses.
Nothing about the intervening generations is left open by the sources; the chain (Orra/Emmet →
Duncan/Roy → Ivy/Hazel) is fully and consistently documented.

**B3.** Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**, who organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative
Creamery Association and served as its first president (r18, confirmed independently by r23, both
of which also explicitly reject a rumored "nephew" relationship). This matters because it means the
family that founded the Association was still in charge of it — as manager — when the low-test
crisis reached its head: Selby Vose personally signed the 1919 circular accusing Ostrey Hollow
patrons of watering their milk, and signed the annual ledger sheets carrying the unexplained
"station gain" line for the whole of his tenure from 1911 (see D3, Part 3).

**B4.** The sources do not establish a real family connection between the Voses and the Keddies.
Duncan Keddie (r18) denies it explicitly and in detail, attributing the persistent local rumor to
confusion with an unrelated family, "the Ashlin Voses," and noting that his mother — who knew every
family connection in the valley — never once named a Vose among their kin. Cleve Oram (r21) repeats
the rumor as unverified hearsay from his mother's mother, admitting he "could not draw you the line
on paper." Given one source's detailed, reasoned denial against the other's admitted uncertainty,
the weight of evidence favors *no* real connection — but this cannot be proven outright from what I
have, since no source actually traces a full genealogy for the Voses the way r18 does for the
Keddies and Brants.


# Answer Sheet — Part 3 of 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

**C1.**

**(a) Proportion by which the tests differed, from the glassware.** The surviving pipettes were
blown to a mark of 17.6 c.c. but delivered an average of only 16.27 c.c. (r19, measured 9 May-1958
survivors against a calibrated burette). Shortfall = 17.6 − 16.27 = 1.33 c.c. Proportion = 1.33 /
17.6 = **0.0756, or about 7.6%**.

This is *not* the only shortfall figure the sources give. The Association's own ledger recorded an
annual "station gain" of 5.25% (r23), and the county report's own stated point-deficiency of 0.19 on
a base of about 3.61% works out to 0.19/3.61 = 5.26% — i.e., two independently-derived figures (a
decades-long contemporaneous bookkeeping record, and the bulletin's own stated points-deficit ÷
base) converge on **~5.25%**, distinctly lower than the 7.6% obtained by re-measuring nine surviving
pipettes in 1958. I flag this as an unresolved tension rather than silently picking one: the 1958
figure comes from a small surviving sample (9 of an original 72) that endured 35 years as loft
junk and may not be representative (survivorship bias), while the 5.25%/5.26% figures are drawn
from the actual historical record of what was paid versus what arrived. Where I need one number
below, I use the better-attested ~5.25%, and show the alternative using 7.6% for comparison.

**(b) True average test and points short.** Recorded station average: 3.61% (r22). Comparator
(Larrow Green creamery, same years): 3.78% (r22) — a raw difference of 0.17 points, which does not
match r22's own stated "nineteen hundredths" (0.19) deficiency. This is a self-contradiction inside
r22 that the sources do not let me resolve; I report both numbers as given. Taking the flat,
repeatedly-stated deficiency figure (0.19 points, corroborated independently by Hazel Brant in r17)
as the operative number: true average ≈ **3.80%**, recorded average **3.61%**, shortfall **0.19
points**.

**(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited.** The only source giving this directly is Cleve Oram (r21,
secondhand from the 1958 bulletin's findings): **67,000 lb** over the full run of years. A rough
independent cross-check from the clerk's aggregate data (r23: 40,000,000 lb of milk over "the long
run of years," at the recorded 3.61% test = 1,444,000 lb credited butterfat; 5.25% "station gain" on
that base ≈ 75,800 lb) lands in the same order of magnitude but about 13% higher — not an exact
match, likely reflecting different exact year-ranges or rounding in each source. I report 67,000 lb
as the sourced figure and flag the cross-check as approximate corroboration, not confirmation.

**(d) Money value and fraction received.** Total value of the shortfall: **$19,600** (r19, the only
source with this figure). Patrons actually received the $3,800 arbitration award (see Story 5).
Fraction received = 3,800 / 19,600 ≈ **19.4%**, roughly one-fifth of what was owed.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 borrowed-measure interval.**
Dated precisely by two independent sources to the day: the station's own measuring glass broke on
8 May 1911 (r22, r24 — an official report and a contemporaneous diary agreeing exactly), and a
borrowed measure was used until 10 July 1911 (r22) — **nine weeks**. Variable: the measuring glass
itself (own faulty pipette vs. a borrowed one). Held constant: the same tester (Ansel Keddie), the
same herds and farms, the same season (spring into midsummer), the same supervisory arrangement.
Result: tests rose markedly for the nine weeks, then fell back to the prior level the moment the
station's own glass was restored (r22). This is a natural controlled experiment, and it establishes
something a static calculation cannot: that the cause of the low readings traveled *with the
instrument*, not with the tester, the herds, or the season — because everything except the glass
was unchanged and the readings changed anyway. It does **not** establish the size or mechanism of
the glass's defect (that required the 1958 measurement), nor does it prove the same defect persisted
unchanged for the following twelve years — it only rules out the tester and the herds for those nine
weeks specifically.

**D2. Three independent reasons the tester was not stealing cream.**
1. The 1911 interval itself (r22, r24): same tester, same supervision, readings rose when the
instrument changed. Independent because it rests on a real-time behavioral test, not a later
measurement or an accounting record.
2. The Association's own ledger (r23): the "missing" butterfat was never actually missing — it
arrived at Larrow Green and was booked there every year as unexplained "station gain." A thief
makes the fat vanish from the system; this fat did not vanish, it just wasn't credited correctly.
Independent because it rests on decades of contemporaneous bookkeeping, unconnected to the tester's
conduct or to any 1958 measurement.
3. The state inspector's own scrutiny (r22 §7): the inspector tested with a lactometer at every
circuit visit from 1908 forward and never found added water — relevant to the adulteration theory
specifically, but also evidence that an official, independent, recurring check of the station over
seventeen years found no sign of tampering of any kind.

Reasons 1 and 2 would still hold even if the glassware had never been measured in 1958 — neither
depends on that measurement. Reason 3 also predates 1958.

Did the bookkeeper (Wilbur Teague) or the clerk who copied the entries ever question the ledger
lines that in fact refute the theft accusation? **No.** Bertram Kyle (r23) says explicitly that in
twenty-five years it "never once crossed my mind to wonder at it, and I do not believe it crossed
anybody else's either," and that Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years." The evidence
sat in plain sight, unexamined, the entire time.

**D3. Did the manager know the tests ran low before the glass was condemned?**
Yes, in the sense of being aware of the *pattern*, but not its *cause*. Selby Vose personally signed
and circulated the 1919 warning letter accusing Ostrey Hollow patrons of watering their milk (r18,
r23, r24) — which presupposes he knew the station's tests were persistently low and had settled on
an (incorrect) explanation for it. At the same time, Bertram Kyle (r23), who sat outside his door
for a quarter-century, describes Vose as a manager who "read the totals and never read the lines
above them," taking a sheet, putting his thumb on the bottom figure, and signing — implying he did
not scrutinize the individual test entries closely enough to notice or suspect the specific
mechanism (the glass). So: aware of the low-test problem, yes; aware of or suspecting its true cause,
no evidence that he was.

## Section E — Contradictions

1. **Glass condemnation year:** 1923 (r18, r19, r20 dated correspondence, r22 precise date) vs. 1921
(r17, alone) vs. 1922 (r23, alone). Resolved to 1923 by weight of dated primary/official evidence
against two single-source, mutually disagreeing memory errors.

2. **Arbitration award amount:** $3,800 (r22 official report, r23 clerk who processed the charge) vs.
$3,500 (r21, secondhand from the narrator's father). Resolved to $3,800 — two independent, better-
placed sources against one secondhand recollection.

3. **Award year:** 1925 (r22 detailed dating, r19) vs. "twenty-four" (r23, alone). Resolved to 1925.

4. **Invoice total — shared wrong value:** $77.93 (sum of r20's own itemized invoice) vs. $77.39,
stated identically in r20's own prose, r17, and r19 — three tellings sharing one wrong figure,
including one document (r20) contradicting itself on the same page. Tie broken by direct arithmetic
on the quoted line items.

5. **Ivy's kinship to Ansel — shared wrong value:** "great-niece" in both r19 and r22's original
draft, against "granddaughter" in r18 and r22's own appended self-correction. Tie broken by the
family's primary source (r18) plus the investigator's explicit retraction.

6. **Ivy's kinship to Hazel:** "second cousins," stated and defended by both parties themselves (r17,
r18) vs. "first cousin once removed" (r21, an outsider). Resolved to second cousins on the strength

7. **Vose–Keddie kinship:** firmly denied, with reasoning, by Duncan Keddie (r18) vs. asserted as

## Section F — Theory

**F1. Causal theory.** A lot of six dozen (72) milk-test pipettes, delivered to the Ostrey Hollow
station in April 1897 and put into use on 1 May 1897, was blown to a 17.6 c.c. mark but manufactured
(per the supplying firm's own speculation, a reground mold) to consistently deliver less — measured
in 1958, on the nine survivors, at an average of 16.27 c.c. Because the station's fat test depended
on a fixed sample volume, every test made with that glass read low by a roughly constant proportion,
for as long as that same glass remained in service — which explains why the shortfall had a
consistent size (fixed by the manufacturing defect, not by the herds or the season), why it began
precisely when the new glass replaced the previously borrowed Larrow Green glass (1 May 1897,
corroborated to the day by two independent sources), why it ended precisely when the state inspector
condemned and replaced the whole lot (June/July 1923, after which the average test rose and stayed
higher), and why nothing about the cows, the pasture, the farmers, or the tester's technique ever
needed to change — the defect lived entirely in the measuring instrument, which the 1911 nine-week
natural experiment demonstrated directly.

**Three competing explanations, who advanced them, and what defeats each:**
1. **Added water**, advanced by manager Selby Vose (1919 circular, r18/r23/r24) — defeated by the
state inspector's lactometer tests at every circuit visit from 1908 on, which never found added
water (r22 §7).
2. **Tester fraud/cream theft**, urged by an Association officer at the 1925 hearing (r22 §8) and
privately suspected by the cream hauler (r21) — defeated by the 1911 borrowed-glass interval (same
tester, readings rose without any change in oversight) and by the ledger showing the "missing" fat
actually arrived at the creamery (r23).
3. **Wet Hessel Bottom hay/pasture**, advanced by an Association committee (1908) and a veterinarian
(1926), and believed within the Brant family itself (r17, r24) — defeated by the college's own 1911
bog-hay feeding trial (Bulletin No. 214), which found at most a 0.05-point winter effect and nothing
measurable in summer, far too small to explain a deficiency the sources otherwise describe as flat
across all seasons (r22's appended note explicitly withdraws its earlier partial credit to this
theory).

**Did the 1925 arbitration board examine the condemned glassware?** No evidence that it did. The
crate sat in the loft directly above two of the three hearing sessions (r18, r21); Bertram Kyle's
list of the four documents actually placed before the board (r23) does not include the glassware,
and he states plainly that no one compared the relevant documents, let alone the physical glass,
side by side.

**What did the tester himself believe?** Per Duncan Keddie's notes (r18), Ansel came home in the
summer of 1911 — matching the borrowed-glass interval — saying "it was something in the glass," but
never wrote more than a bare question mark in the book, because he had no proof and would not record
what he could not establish.

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

From 1897 to 1923, the Ostrey Hollow milk-testing station on Hessel Bottom used a batch of pipettes
that, marked for a full sample, delivered a consistently short one, so every butterfat test read
low by roughly 5–8% for twenty-six years. Farmers went underpaid; suspicion fell first on watered
milk, then on the tester, Ansel Keddie, who was dismissed in disgrace in 1925 despite a telling 1911
clue — a temporarily borrowed measuring glass made his readings rise, then fall again once returned.
The condemned glassware was crated and forgotten in the station loft. In 1958 Keddie's own
granddaughter, Ivy, a graduate student, found and measured it, proving the defect and clearing his
name twenty-four years after his death. Reconstructing this required a family weigh-book, a firm's
invoice and letters, a newspaper story, a household diary, an office ledger, and a county report —
no single record held the whole truth, and several of them disagree with each other on the details.
