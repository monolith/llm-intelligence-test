# Answer Sheet — Part 3 of 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

**C1.**

**(a) Proportion by which the tests differed, from the glassware.** The surviving pipettes were
blown to a mark of 17.6 c.c. but delivered an average of only 16.27 c.c. (r19, measured 9 May-1958
survivors against a calibrated burette). Shortfall = 17.6 − 16.27 = 1.33 c.c. Proportion = 1.33 /
17.6 = **0.0756, or about 7.6%**.

This is *not* the only shortfall figure the sources give. The Association's own ledger recorded an
annual "station gain" of 5.25% (r23), and the county report's own stated point-deficiency of 0.19 on
a base of about 3.61% works out to 0.19/3.61 = 5.26% — i.e., two independently-derived figures (a
decades-long contemporaneous bookkeeping record, and the bulletin's own stated points-deficit ÷
base) converge on **~5.25%**, distinctly lower than the 7.6% obtained by re-measuring nine surviving
pipettes in 1958. I flag this as an unresolved tension rather than silently picking one: the 1958
figure comes from a small surviving sample (9 of an original 72) that endured 35 years as loft
junk and may not be representative (survivorship bias), while the 5.25%/5.26% figures are drawn
from the actual historical record of what was paid versus what arrived. Where I need one number
below, I use the better-attested ~5.25%, and show the alternative using 7.6% for comparison.

**(b) True average test and points short.** Recorded station average: 3.61% (r22). Comparator
(Larrow Green creamery, same years): 3.78% (r22) — a raw difference of 0.17 points, which does not
match r22's own stated "nineteen hundredths" (0.19) deficiency. This is a self-contradiction inside
r22 that the sources do not let me resolve; I report both numbers as given. Taking the flat,
repeatedly-stated deficiency figure (0.19 points, corroborated independently by Hazel Brant in r17)
as the operative number: true average ≈ **3.80%**, recorded average **3.61%**, shortfall **0.19
points**.

**(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited.** The only source giving this directly is Cleve Oram (r21,
secondhand from the 1958 bulletin's findings): **67,000 lb** over the full run of years. A rough
independent cross-check from the clerk's aggregate data (r23: 40,000,000 lb of milk over "the long
run of years," at the recorded 3.61% test = 1,444,000 lb credited butterfat; 5.25% "station gain" on
that base ≈ 75,800 lb) lands in the same order of magnitude but about 13% higher — not an exact
match, likely reflecting different exact year-ranges or rounding in each source. I report 67,000 lb
as the sourced figure and flag the cross-check as approximate corroboration, not confirmation.

**(d) Money value and fraction received.** Total value of the shortfall: **$19,600** (r19, the only
source with this figure). Patrons actually received the $3,800 arbitration award (see Story 5).
Fraction received = 3,800 / 19,600 ≈ **19.4%**, roughly one-fifth of what was owed.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 borrowed-measure interval.**
Dated precisely by two independent sources to the day: the station's own measuring glass broke on
8 May 1911 (r22, r24 — an official report and a contemporaneous diary agreeing exactly), and a
borrowed measure was used until 10 July 1911 (r22) — **nine weeks**. Variable: the measuring glass
itself (own faulty pipette vs. a borrowed one). Held constant: the same tester (Ansel Keddie), the
same herds and farms, the same season (spring into midsummer), the same supervisory arrangement.
Result: tests rose markedly for the nine weeks, then fell back to the prior level the moment the
station's own glass was restored (r22). This is a natural controlled experiment, and it establishes
something a static calculation cannot: that the cause of the low readings traveled *with the
instrument*, not with the tester, the herds, or the season — because everything except the glass
was unchanged and the readings changed anyway. It does **not** establish the size or mechanism of
the glass's defect (that required the 1958 measurement), nor does it prove the same defect persisted
unchanged for the following twelve years — it only rules out the tester and the herds for those nine
weeks specifically.

**D2. Three independent reasons the tester was not stealing cream.**
1. The 1911 interval itself (r22, r24): same tester, same supervision, readings rose when the
instrument changed. Independent because it rests on a real-time behavioral test, not a later
measurement or an accounting record.
2. The Association's own ledger (r23): the "missing" butterfat was never actually missing — it
arrived at Larrow Green and was booked there every year as unexplained "station gain." A thief
makes the fat vanish from the system; this fat did not vanish, it just wasn't credited correctly.
Independent because it rests on decades of contemporaneous bookkeeping, unconnected to the tester's
conduct or to any 1958 measurement.
3. The state inspector's own scrutiny (r22 §7): the inspector tested with a lactometer at every
circuit visit from 1908 forward and never found added water — relevant to the adulteration theory
specifically, but also evidence that an official, independent, recurring check of the station over
seventeen years found no sign of tampering of any kind.

Reasons 1 and 2 would still hold even if the glassware had never been measured in 1958 — neither
depends on that measurement. Reason 3 also predates 1958.

Did the bookkeeper (Wilbur Teague) or the clerk who copied the entries ever question the ledger
lines that in fact refute the theft accusation? **No.** Bertram Kyle (r23) says explicitly that in
twenty-five years it "never once crossed my mind to wonder at it, and I do not believe it crossed
anybody else's either," and that Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years." The evidence
sat in plain sight, unexamined, the entire time.

**D3. Did the manager know the tests ran low before the glass was condemned?**
Yes, in the sense of being aware of the *pattern*, but not its *cause*. Selby Vose personally signed
and circulated the 1919 warning letter accusing Ostrey Hollow patrons of watering their milk (r18,
r23, r24) — which presupposes he knew the station's tests were persistently low and had settled on
an (incorrect) explanation for it. At the same time, Bertram Kyle (r23), who sat outside his door
for a quarter-century, describes Vose as a manager who "read the totals and never read the lines
above them," taking a sheet, putting his thumb on the bottom figure, and signing — implying he did
not scrutinize the individual test entries closely enough to notice or suspect the specific
mechanism (the glass). So: aware of the low-test problem, yes; aware of or suspecting its true cause,
no evidence that he was.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. Causal theory.** A lot of six dozen (72) milk-test pipettes, delivered to the Ostrey Hollow
station in April 1897 and put into use on 1 May 1897, was blown to a 17.6 c.c. mark but manufactured
(per the supplying firm's own speculation, a reground mold) to consistently deliver less — measured
in 1958, on the nine survivors, at an average of 16.27 c.c. Because the station's fat test depended
on a fixed sample volume, every test made with that glass read low by a roughly constant proportion,
for as long as that same glass remained in service — which explains why the shortfall had a
consistent size (fixed by the manufacturing defect, not by the herds or the season), why it began
precisely when the new glass replaced the previously borrowed Larrow Green glass (1 May 1897,
corroborated to the day by two independent sources), why it ended precisely when the state inspector
condemned and replaced the whole lot (June/July 1923, after which the average test rose and stayed
higher), and why nothing about the cows, the pasture, the farmers, or the tester's technique ever
needed to change — the defect lived entirely in the measuring instrument, which the 1911 nine-week
natural experiment demonstrated directly.

**Three competing explanations, who advanced them, and what defeats each:**
1. **Added water**, advanced by manager Selby Vose (1919 circular, r18/r23/r24) — defeated by the
state inspector's lactometer tests at every circuit visit from 1908 on, which never found added
water (r22 §7).
2. **Tester fraud/cream theft**, urged by an Association officer at the 1925 hearing (r22 §8) and
privately suspected by the cream hauler (r21) — defeated by the 1911 borrowed-glass interval (same
tester, readings rose without any change in oversight) and by the ledger showing the "missing" fat
actually arrived at the creamery (r23).
3. **Wet Hessel Bottom hay/pasture**, advanced by an Association committee (1908) and a veterinarian
(1926), and believed within the Brant family itself (r17, r24) — defeated by the college's own 1911
bog-hay feeding trial (Bulletin No. 214), which found at most a 0.05-point winter effect and nothing
measurable in summer, far too small to explain a deficiency the sources otherwise describe as flat
across all seasons (r22's appended note explicitly withdraws its earlier partial credit to this
theory).

**Did the 1925 arbitration board examine the condemned glassware?** No evidence that it did. The
crate sat in the loft directly above two of the three hearing sessions (r18, r21); Bertram Kyle's
list of the four documents actually placed before the board (r23) does not include the glassware,
and he states plainly that no one compared the relevant documents, let alone the physical glass,
side by side.

**What did the tester himself believe?** Per Duncan Keddie's notes (r18), Ansel came home in the
summer of 1911 — matching the borrowed-glass interval — saying "it was something in the glass," but
never wrote more than a bare question mark in the book, because he had no proof and would not record
what he could not establish.

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

From 1897 to 1923, the Ostrey Hollow milk-testing station on Hessel Bottom used a batch of pipettes
that, marked for a full sample, delivered a consistently short one, so every butterfat test read
low by roughly 5–8% for twenty-six years. Farmers went underpaid; suspicion fell first on watered
milk, then on the tester, Ansel Keddie, who was dismissed in disgrace in 1925 despite a telling 1911
clue — a temporarily borrowed measuring glass made his readings rise, then fall again once returned.
The condemned glassware was crated and forgotten in the station loft. In 1958 Keddie's own
granddaughter, Ivy, a graduate student, found and measured it, proving the defect and clearing his
name twenty-four years after his death. Reconstructing this required a family weigh-book, a firm's
invoice and letters, a newspaper story, a household diary, an office ledger, and a county report —
no single record held the whole truth, and several of them disagree with each other on the details.
