### Story 1: Founding the Association and Building the Station (1889–1897)

Farmers of Larrow Valley organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association in 1889 at Larrow Green, under Gideon Vose, the first president, who wrote the by-laws (r12, r18, r22). The main creamery was built at Larrow Green in 1891 (r12, r22). Among the by-laws was Article VII, establishing payment by butterfat test rather than by milk weight — the mechanism that made everything that followed matter financially. Its adoption date cannot be fixed: Rundle associates it with 1891 (r12), Loomis states 1892 (r02), and Grigg recalls "the 1893 meeting" (r15); county agent Falke later searched for the minute book and reported it lost in an office move, explicitly declining to name a year (r22). This is one of the few points the sources themselves flag as unrecoverable.

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station seven miles up-valley at Ostrey Hollow to serve Hessel Bottom's six farms (r22). It opened 4 May 1896 (r22, corroborated generally by r12, r14). Ansel Keddie was hired as tester and separator man from the opening (r18, r22); Hazel Brant's account that he "went up to that station in ninety-five" (r17) may describe his hiring/preparation before the May 1896 opening rather than a true conflict.

Before its own equipment arrived, the station used borrowed glass. Ansel's own weigh-book records "borrowed of Larrow Green" (r03/r07), but Ansel's own letter elsewhere says "borrowed off the Ashlin creamery" — a contradiction within his own materials. Buttermaker Hobart Sill separately claims it was his own personal churn-room glassware, since he worked at Larrow Green (r14) — not strictly incompatible with "Larrow Green," but more specific.

The station's own outfit — six dozen pipettes (17.6 c.c.), twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, and a hand tester — was invoiced by Ammon & Sons of Tarnet on 14 April 1897 for $77.93, and went into use 1 May 1897 (r20's invoice and prose, corroborated by Orra Keddie's diary entry of the same date, r24). Kyle (r23) and Vaught (r11) instead date first use to 1 May 1898, a year later than the invoice and the tester's own wife record contemporaneously — the primary invoice and diary are preferred.

### Story 2: The Ostrey Hollow Complaint and Its Theories (1902–1926)

From an early date, milk tested at Ostrey Hollow ran below the rest of the valley: the station's long-run average was 3.61 against Larrow Green's 3.78 (r22), a gap of about 0.19 points (r04, r07, r17, r22) — though r09 gives 0.17 and r16 gives both "0.21" and, self-contradictorily, "a fifth of a point" (0.20) elsewhere in the same paper. The first complaint of record was made in 1902 (r22, corroborated by Orra Keddie's diary, r24). It recurred every decade.

Three theories circulated. (1) Watering: the Association's manager, Selby Vose, circulated a warning letter to Hessel Bottom patrons in 1919 (r09, r23, r24); Orra's diary records Ansel personally delivering it to eleven families (r24), though Hazel Brant later recalled nine families (r17). (2) Cream theft by the tester: never formally charged but insinuated at the 1925 hearing by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague (r05, r13). (3) Wet bog hay thinning the cream: a committee chaired by Nyle Grigg sampled the six Hessel Bottom herds through 1908 and blamed the wet, sour hay of the bottom (r15, reprinted with a 1919 covering letter). Veterinarian Roland Kepp elaborated this into a soil-mineral-deficiency theory in a 1926 paper (r16).

Grigg and Kepp both cite the college's Bulletin No. 214 as confirming the hay theory, quoting a depression of "two tenths... in the winter feeding" (r15, r16) — a matched error. County agent Falke independently obtained the bulletin's actual summary in 1959: bog hay depressed the test "by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing... in summer" (r22, matching the verbatim text in r04 and r07 Item 12). Falke did the arithmetic himself — "five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July" — and explicitly withdrew his own earlier concession to the hay theory. This is the most decisive single piece of evidence against explanation (3).

### Story 3: The Nine Weeks of Borrowed Glass (1911)

On 8 May 1911 the station's own measuring glass broke, and a borrowed measure was used until 10 July — nine weeks (Ansel's own dated book entries, r03; corroborated by Orra's diary entry of 8 May 1911, r24; cited by Vaught, r11, and Falke, r22). Teague (r05) and Kepp (r16) instead say the interval lasted six weeks — a matched wrong figure shared by the two narrators least willing to credit the anomaly (Teague insinuated theft; Kepp needed the hay theory to hold).

During those nine weeks, with the same herds, same pasture, same season, same tester, and same scales, but a different measuring glass, the station's tests rose markedly; the day the station's own glass returned, they fell back to their former level (r03, r09, r11, r22). Ansel noted the fact and set only a question mark against it in his own book — Duncan later recorded that his father came home saying "it was something in the glass" but "never dared put it in the book" without proof (r18).

Treated as an experiment, this interval isolates the glass as the single variable responsible for the depressed readings, since every other candidate cause (feed, season, herd, personnel, honesty of the scales) was held constant and the result still changed. It is the one piece of evidence in the whole record that a static calculation of the 1958 pipettes could never supply by itself, because it shows the effect appearing and disappearing in real time, under observation, decades before anyone measured a pipette. What it does not establish: the specific mechanism (a short-delivery pipette skewing the test proportionally) was not identified until 1958; nor does it prove the borrowed glass was itself perfectly accurate, only that it was closer to accurate than the station's own.

### Story 4: Ansel Keddie — Tester, In-Law, and Outcast (1861–1934)

Ansel Keddie, born 1861, married Orra Brant in 1889 (r18, r24) and served as tester and separator man at Ostrey Hollow from its 1896 opening for twenty-nine seasons (r18, r22). Orra was the sister of Emmet Brant, the largest patron on Hessel Bottom — accounts differ on whether she was his full sister (r09) or half-sister "by his father's second wife" (r17); the sources do not resolve this. Ansel and Emmet were thus brothers-in-law and, per Orra's diary, dined together for decades until the 1919 circular episode caused a permanent breach: Emmet confronted Ansel in the yard over having personally delivered the water-accusation letters, and the two men never spoke again (r09, r24).

Ansel suspected his own equipment as early as 1911, telling his family "something in the glass," but would not commit an unproven suspicion to his own book (r18). Yet per arbitration chairman Alonzo Frick's account of a private corridor conversation in 1925, Keddie also told him he privately believed the patrons themselves were at fault — a tension the sources do not reconcile, possibly reflecting a hedge to different listeners at different times (r13).

The 1925 board of arbitration, after hearing patrons and officers, found no cause established, awarded the patrons $3,800 without admission, and Keddie was dismissed on 30 April 1925 — four days before the award was announced (r13, r18, r24, all agreeing on this date). He took up carpentry, worked the family farm, and never attended another Association meeting (r24). He died in February 1934, twenty-four years before the 1958 bulletin cleared him, with, in his wife's words, "the imputation on him" still unnamed by the neighbors who came to his funeral (r24). Orra outlived him to 1948.

### Story 5: The Brant Family, the Weigh Book, and the Break

Emmet Brant farmed the largest place on Hessel Bottom and hauled the most milk of any patron into Ostrey Hollow. He kept a private weigh book recording his own scale weight alongside the station's, so that any disagreement would be visible at once; his son Roy reports the two figures always agreed, which rules out short-weighing at intake (r09). Sources disagree on when Emmet began the book: 1 January 1897 (r18, Duncan Keddie's account), 1 January 1898 (r09, Roy's own account), or "nineteen hundred" (r17, Hazel's, the most generationally removed and self-admittedly weak on dates). The 1910 leaf is the one page all who quote it agree on verbatim: 200,000 lb delivered, "his scale and theirs agreeing," paid on a test of 3.42 (r09, r17).

Four of Hessel Bottom's six farms hauled their summer milk over Redlow Ridge to the Alder Corners cheese factory rather than to Ostrey Hollow, for sixteen seasons, 1915–1930 (r09, corroborated independently by the factory's own records in r10). Alder Corners tested that milk on its own bench glass and recorded season averages of 3.78–3.82 throughout, refuting both the watering and wet-hay theories for that milk (r10).

The 1919 circular broke the family: Emmet confronted Ansel in the yard for having personally carried the water-accusation letters, and the two brothers-in-law did not speak again until Emmet's death in 1929 (r09, r24). The book passed from Emmet (d. 1929) to Roy, who lent it — not gave it — to Ivy Keddie in 1958 for her research (r09, r17, r18), and at Roy's death in 1962 to his daughter Hazel, who deposited it with the Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1963 (r18). Roy and Duncan Keddie (Ansel and Orra's son) were first cousins; their daughters, Hazel Brant and Ivy Keddie, are second cousins (r09, r12 footnote, r17, r18) — see Section B.

### Story 6: The 1925 Board of Arbitration

Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned in January 1925, Emmet Brant's name among them (r24). A board of arbitration, chaired by attorney Alonzo Frick, sat three times: 17 March 1925 at Ashlin, and twice more at the station itself, in the weigh room, because the relevant books and machinery were there (r13, r21, r22, all agreeing on this structure). Cleve Oram, listening from the platform in the rain during one station sitting, later noted that the crate of condemned pipettes sat in the loft directly above the hearing the entire time, unexamined (r21) — confirmed by Frick's own list of exhibits: only four books were placed before the board (the station test book in Keddie's hand, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book, and the state inspector's notebook for the condemnation year), with no glassware among them (r13, corroborated independently by clerk Bertram Kyle's identical list of four, r23).

Three explanations were argued: watering (urged by the manager's side), tester theft (insinuated by bookkeeper Teague), and wet hay (reread from the 1908 report). The board found no cause established and awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800 "without admission" (r19, r22, r23 — Kyle misdates the award to 1924, an apparent memory slip against the more precisely dated 1925 accounts). Vira Toland and Cleve Oram both instead recall $3,500 — a wrong figure shared by the two most oral/folk-memory-based narrators (r01, r21).

Ansel Keddie was dismissed 30 April 1925, four days before the award (r13, r18, r24). Kyle notes the award was charged back to the Ostrey Hollow station's own account rather than spread across the Association — "neat bookkeeping," in his words (r23). The crate that would have settled the matter at once was never brought down; it took another thirty-three years to be found.

### Story 7: Ivy Keddie's Discovery and Bulletin 471 (1958)

Ivy Keddie, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland and Ansel Keddie's granddaughter (not great-niece, as the Ashlin Chronicle first reported — corrected by county agent Falke after verification, r19 vs. r22), spent 1958 researching the Ostrey Hollow question. She borrowed Roy Brant's weigh book (r09, r17), obtained Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks from the college library, where they had sat since 1947 (r18), and had Rosalie Cudd copy out the Alder Corners figures (r10, r18).

In February 1958 she climbed into the loft over the station's old intake room and found the crate of glassware condemned in 1923, still under its tarpaulin (r17, r18, r19, r21, r22). She carried it to Farland and on 9 March 1958 measured the surviving pipettes — nine of them, by the weight of sources (r04, r07, r19 prose, r21, r22), though Rundle's 1959 footnote and the Chronicle both separately give "eleven," likely stemming from the same press release rather than independent counts (r12, r19). Each pipette was blown to deliver 17.6 c.c.; the measured mean was 16.72 c.c. — about 95% of the marked volume, a proportional shortfall of "one part in twenty" (r04, r07, and the verbatim bulletin conclusion quoted even inside r11, despite that document's own inconsistent body figures of 16.27 c.c. and 118,000 lb).

Bulletin No. 471, published October 1958 under Professor Leland Vaught and Ivy Keddie, concluded that no milk was ever watered and no cream ever stolen: a short pipette delivering a short charge of milk into a bottle graduated for a full charge produces a proportionally low reading every time, regardless of season, explaining both the size and the constancy of the gap. The finding was reported in the Ashlin Chronicle (r19) and taken up by the county board via Falke's 1959 report (r22), which independently checked and corrected two errors in circulation — Ivy's exact relation to Ansel, and the wet-hay theory's supposed support from Bulletin 214.

---

### Story 8: Where the Records Went (1923–1970)

The controversy left behind five separate bodies of record, and their scattered custody is why no single witness before 1958 could compare all of them at once (r18, r23). The station's own test book, kept by Ansel Keddie from 1896 until his 1925 dismissal, was impounded by the arbitration board in March 1925, held at the Cooperative's Ashlin office until 1959, then deposited at the Ordell County Historical Rooms (r18). The Brant weigh book followed the family path described in Story 5, reaching the Historical Rooms in 1963 (r18). Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks went to the state dairy office on his 1936 retirement, then to the college library at Farland in 1947, where Ivy found the 1923 volume in 1958 — eleven years after the transfer, and after nobody else had asked for it (r18). The Alder Corners test book stayed in Vessey County with the Cudd family; Rosalie had its figures copied for Ivy in 1958, and the book itself was later given to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970 (r10, r18). The condemned glassware itself sat in the station loft from 1923 until Ivy's 1958 discovery, went to Farland to be measured, and was deposited with the Historical Rooms in 1959 alongside the test book (r18, r19, r22).

By 1970 the five source-books were held in three different buildings, none of them the Association's own — the Cooperative itself having, by implication, long since ceased to function as custodian of its own history (r18's closing line). This dispersal is the structural reason the question took sixty years and a ladder to answer: the ledger line that would have raised suspicion (the "station gain" entry, r23), the inspector's condemnation (r06), the tester's own question mark (r03), and the physical glass (found 1958) were never in the same room, or the same set of eyes, until Ivy Keddie assembled them for the 1958 bulletin.

## Section B — Relationships (8 points)

**B1.** Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's **granddaughter**, not his great-niece. Duncan Keddie's own family notes state plainly: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter... I have already twice seen it printed otherwise" (r18). This is independently confirmed by county agent Orin Falke, who added a correction to his 1959 report after verifying the point: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine" (r22). Both sources outrank the Ashlin Chronicle's original "great-niece" (r19), which was evidently an unchecked press-release phrase. Ivy is the daughter of Duncan Keddie, who is Ansel and Orra's son.

**B2.** Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant are **second cousins**. Emmet Brant and Orra (Keddie) were siblings; Emmet's son Roy and Orra's son Duncan are therefore first cousins (explicitly derived in r18, Section II); Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy, as children of first cousins, are second cousins. This is stated independently by Roy Brant (r09), Duncan Keddie (r18), Rundle's 1959 footnote (r12), and Hazel herself, who insists on the precise term ("Second cousins. It is not complicated; it is only that nobody wants to count," r17). The minority claim that Ivy is Hazel's "first cousin once removed" (r01, r21) is folk-level hearsay from the two most orally-sourced narrators and is contradicted by every source that actually draws the family tree. Left open by the sources: whether Orra was Emmet's full sister (r09) or half-sister "by his father's second wife" (r17) — this affects the exact nature of the connecting generation but not the cousin degree itself.

**B3.** Selby Vose was the son of Gideon Vose, the Association's founding organizer, first president, and author of its by-laws (r18, corroborated independently by clerk Bertram Kyle, "the son, whatever is said at Ashlin," r23). This matters because Selby, as manager during the controversy, was defending an institution his own father had designed — including Article VII, the payment-by-test rule whose accuracy was ultimately at issue. This may help explain why he championed the watering theory (which located the fault with the patrons) over any suspicion of the Association's own equipment, and why he signed the 1919 accusatory circular under his own name (r09, r23, r24).

**B4.** No genuine relationship existed between the Vose and Keddie families. Duncan Keddie directly rebuts the persistent rumor, attributing it to confusion with the unrelated "Ashlin Voses": "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses... It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk" (r18). Cleve Oram repeats the same rumor but flags his own uncertainty — "cousins of some sort... I could not draw you the line on paper" (r21). Given that Duncan is a direct family member offering a specific, reasoned rebuttal (naming the source of the confusion) against Oram's admittedly unverified hearsay, the sources resolve in favor of no relationship.

## Section C — Math (14 points)

**C1.**

(a) The canonical figure, quoted verbatim as Bulletin 471's own conclusion in three separate documents (Ivy Keddie's research, r04; the catalogue, r07; and even inside Vaught's introduction despite its own inconsistent body text, r11), is that the pipettes delivered a mean of **16.72 c.c.** against a marked 17.6 c.c. — a ratio of 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95**, i.e. the tests ran short by **one part in twenty (5%)**. This is independently corroborated by Bertram Kyle's contemporaneous clerk's ledger entry, which recorded the annual "station gain" as "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" (r23) — very close to the same figure from a completely different, decades-earlier document. I reject Vaught's body-text figures (five dozen pipettes, 16.27 c.c., ratio 0.9244) as a self-contradiction within r11 itself, since the same document's own closing quotation of the bulletin still says "one part in twenty" and "seventy-six thousand pounds," which only the 16.72 c.c./0.95 figures produce (see (c)).

(b) True average test = station's recorded long-run average (3.61) + shortfall (0.19) = **3.80** (r22 gives 3.61 and 0.19 directly; 3.80 is also the actual recorded average once correct glass was installed, per Strawn's inspector's log, r06 — a clean convergence). Recorded tests were short by **0.19 points**. (Vose's claim of 3.90 post-fix, r08, and Falke's own alternative figure of 3.85, r22, are both outliers against this arithmetic and against the primary inspector's log.)

(c) **76,000 lb** of butterfat, per the bulletin's own stated conclusion (r04, r07, r11's quoted closing line). This is independently reproducible by arithmetic: using Kyle's 40,000,000 lb total intake (r23) at the recorded 3.61% average, patrons were credited 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb; at the true 3.80% average they should have been credited 40,000,000 × 0.038 = 1,520,000 lb; the difference is exactly **76,000 lb**. This double confirmation (bulletin's own stated number, and independent arithmetic from a different narrator's figures) is why I trust 76,000 lb over Vaught's own body-text alternative of 118,000 lb, or the pre-bulletin folk estimate of 67,000 lb shared by Frick (r13) and Oram (r21).

(d) At Kyle's stated long-run average butterfat price of 25 cents/lb (r23): 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000** — matching Ivy Keddie's own stated loss figure exactly (r04). (Rundle's footnote and the Chronicle instead give $19,600, r12/r19, likely a press-release rounding rather than an independent recomputation.) The patrons actually received the 1925 arbitration award of **$3,800**, i.e. $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = **20%** of the true loss — and that award was paid nine years before anyone knew the true loss, essentially by chance/political settlement rather than by calculation.

**C2.** Using Emmet Brant's 1910 leaf (r09, r17): 200,000 lb of milk, paid on a recorded test of 3.42.
(a) Credited butterfat: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb**.
(b) I apply the same flat 0.19-point correction the sources themselves use for the station generally (no source gives a proportional recalculation for an individual patron, so I follow their method rather than substitute my own): true test ≈ 3.42 + 0.19 = 3.61%; true butterfat = 200,000 × 0.0361 = 7,220 lb. Owed and not credited = 7,220 − 6,840 = **380 lb**.
(c) At 25 cents/lb (r23): 380 × $0.25 = **$95.00** for that year. (Note: this uses Kyle's multi-decade average price, not a documented 1910-specific price, which the sources do not supply — flagged as an approximation.)

**C3.** Over the "full delivery years" (Kyle's 40,000,000 lb total, r23, matching r04/r07/r11's intake figure):
(a) Credited to patrons: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**.
(b) True butterfat actually in the milk: 40,000,000 × 0.038 = **1,520,000 lb**.
(c) The difference is 76,000 lb (matches C1c exactly). As a percentage, the answer depends on the denominator: of the **true** total (1,520,000 lb), it is 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = exactly **5.00%** — matching the bulletin's own "one part in twenty." Of the amount actually **credited** (1,444,000 lb) — i.e., what the shortfall represents as a markup on what patrons were paid — it is 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.26%**, which matches Kyle's independently-recorded ledger entry of "five and a quarter per cent" almost exactly (r23). Both percentages are correct; they answer different questions, and the sources themselves (bulletin vs. ledger) each used one of the two without saying so.

**C4.**
(a) The invoice's own line items (25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15) sum to **$77.93** (r20), and this is what the invoice table itself states as the total. Ammon's own prose caption and the Ashlin Chronicle both instead say "seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents" ($77.39, r20 prose, r19) — this is a self-contradiction within r20 (its own table doesn't match its own prose), resolved by arithmetic in favor of **$77.93**; the Chronicle's matching "$77.39" shows only that it copied Ammon's prose error rather than checking the table.
(b) The invoice bought **six dozen (72) pipettes** at 17.6 c.c. marked capacity (r20, corroborated by the catalogue r07 and the Chronicle r19). At the canonical measured mean of 16.72 c.c. (see C1a), each was short by 17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88 c.c.** (Vaught's and Oram's alternative "five dozen" purchased, r11/r21, is rejected against the primary invoice document and two corroborating narrators.)

**C5.**
(a) The faulty glass was in use from **1 May 1897** (invoice date 14 April 1897, use from 1 May per r20 and Orra's diary, r24) to **12 June 1923** (condemnation, per the inspector's own notebook, r06, and corroborated by r04, r07, r22, and the dating of the replacement-order correspondence in r20) — a span of just over **26 years**. The money calculation (C1–C3) instead runs over **40,000,000 lb / 1,600,000 lb per year ≈ 25 full delivery years** (r23), because the glass went into service partway through the 1897 season and was pulled partway through the 1923 season; those two partial seasons are excluded from the whole-year financial reckoning, which is why the physical-use figure (26 years) and the money-calculation figure (25 full years) differ by roughly a year.
(b) The first season's (May–December 1896) intake is contested and **cannot be determined precisely**: Hobart Sill, a firsthand participant, gives "about nine hundred thousand pounds" (r14); clerk Bertram Kyle gives "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" but explicitly flags it as hearsay never entered in any book he ruled (r23). I lean toward Sill's figure as the firsthand account, but this is not a resolved fact.

## Section D — Logic (10 points)

**D1.** The interval is the nine weeks of borrowed measuring glass, 8 May–10 July 1911 (see Story 3 and C5a for sourcing). The variable was the measuring glass itself (the station's own, broken, versus a borrowed one). Held constant: the herds, the pasture and season (within one growing period), the tester, and the scales. The result: tests rose markedly for the nine weeks and fell back the moment the station's own glass resumed (r03, r09, r11, r22). This establishes, by direct real-time observation rather than retrospective calculation, that the *source* of the depressed readings traveled with the glass and not with the farms, the feed, or the tester — something no 1958 laboratory measurement of a pipette, however precise, could show, because a bench measurement only proves a piece of glass is wrong, not that it was the actual and sole cause of the historical pattern. What the interval does *not* establish: the specific mechanism (a proportional short-delivery pipette) was not identified until 1958; nor is there any guarantee the borrowed glass was itself perfectly accurate — only that it was closer to correct than the station's own.

**D2.** At least three independent reasons rule out cream theft by the tester: (1) Roy Brant's report that his father Emmet's own farm scale always agreed with the station's scale (r09) — ruling out short-weighing at intake, a method-of-checking (weight comparison) unrelated to any test or bulletin. (2) State inspector Merle Strawn's lactometer readings, taken at every circuit visit from 1908 forward, which never found added water and gave no support to any theft-related irregularity (r06) — an independent, contemporaneous, government-conducted check. (3) Rosalie Cudd's Alder Corners cheese-factory records, showing the same Hessel Bottom farmers' milk testing normally (3.78–3.82) on entirely separate equipment at a separate facility for sixteen seasons (r10) — proving the milk itself was normal before it ever reached Ostrey Hollow. These three are independent because they come from three different people, using three different methods (weight-matching, water-testing, parallel fat-testing), none of which depends on the others or on the 1958 discovery. At least one — Strawn's lactometer record and Cudd's factory data — would stand unchanged even if the crate had never been found in 1958, since both are entirely contemporaneous with the events themselves. As for the bookkeeper: no, Wilbur Teague, who insinuated the theft accusation at the 1925 hearing, apparently never questioned the ledger entries that refuted it. Clerk Bertram Kyle, who sat outside Teague's desk for years, recorded that Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years... the figures were the figures" (r23) — he wrote the "station gain" line every year without ever reconciling it with his own hearing testimony.

**D3.** Yes — the sources support that manager Selby Vose knew the tests ran low well before the glass was condemned in 1923, even if he did not know why. He personally signed the station's annual ledger sheets carrying the "station gain" line from the time he took office in 1911 (r23); the very existence of that named, recurring line item is documentary proof the discrepancy between what patrons were credited and what the creamery actually received was a known and recorded fact reviewed by him every year. Further, his own 1919 circular warning patrons about added water (r09, r23, r24) is itself proof he was aware of, and actively concerned about, the shortfall for at least twelve years before condemnation — he had simply settled on the wrong cause.

## Section E — Contradictions (8 points)

**General contradictions (conflicting claims across sources), with resolution method:**

1. Settlement amount: $3,800 (r19, r22, r23) vs. $3,500 (r01, r21). *Resolved* by majority + written/institutional sources (newspaper, county report, clerk's ledger) outranking oral-tradition figures — see matched pair below.
2. Ivy–Hazel relationship: second cousins (r09, r12, r17, r18) vs. first cousin once removed (r01, r21). *Resolved* — see B2 and matched pair below.
3. Patrons' claimed loss: 67,000 lb (r13, r21) vs. 76,000 lb (bulletin figure, r04/r07/r11's quote) vs. 118,000 lb (r11's own body text). *Resolved* in favor of 76,000 lb by double arithmetic confirmation (C1c); 118,000 lb rejected as self-contradictory within r11; 67,000 lb treated as a pre-bulletin folk estimate — see matched pair below.
4. Condemnation date: 12 June 1923 (r06's own notebook, r04, r07, r22) vs. 12 July 1923 (r08, r12) vs. 1921 (r17) vs. 1922 (r23). *Resolved* in favor of 12 June 1923 via the primary inspector's notebook plus the dating of Ammon's replacement-order correspondence ("order of the 28th ultimo," reply 2 July — consistent with a June condemnation, r20).
5. Pipettes purchased: six dozen (r07, r20's invoice, r19) vs. five dozen (r11, r21). *Resolved* in favor of six dozen via the primary invoice — see matched pair below.

## Section F — Theory (6 points)

**F1.** The shortfall was caused by a bad lot of pipettes. Six dozen milk-test pipettes bought from Ammon & Sons in April 1897, each marked to deliver 17.6 c.c., were flawed at the glassworks — Junia Ammon's own history suggests a mold reground slightly short and never caught, since a spot-check of a few pieces from a short-but-*consistent* lot would pass inspection (r20) — and in fact delivered a mean of only 16.72 c.c., about 5% short. Because the butterfat test formula assumes a full 17.6 c.c. charge, a consistently short charge produces a proportionally low fat reading on every single test, in every season, which is exactly why the gap was flat — about 0.19 points, all year, "wet years and dry" (r09, r17) — rather than varying with feed or weather the way a hay-based theory would predict. It began in May 1897, when the flawed lot went into service, and ended in June 1923, when state inspector Merle Strawn ran the pipettes against a calibrated burette and condemned them, after which the recorded average returned to 3.80, matching the true value. Nothing at the station or on the farms ever had to change, because nothing there was ever actually wrong — only an unnoticed piece of glassware on a shelf.

Three competing explanations were offered and each is defeated by specific evidence: (1) **Watering**, advanced by manager Selby Vose (1919 circular), is defeated by Strawn's lactometer tests at every circuit visit from 1908 on, which never found added water (r06, r22). (2) **Cream theft by the tester**, insinuated by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague at the 1925 hearing (r05, r13), is defeated by the 1911 nine-week anomaly (same tester, borrowed glass, tests rose — D1), by Roy Brant's matching scale weights (r09), and by Rosalie Cudd's independent Alder Corners test data (r10). (3) **Wet bog hay**, the 1908 committee's verdict (r15) elaborated into a soil-mineral theory by Kepp (r16), is defeated by the actual text of Bulletin No. 214 — a depression of at most 0.05 points in winter and none in summer, far too small to explain a flat 0.19-point, year-round gap — as independently verified by Falke (r22), who explicitly withdrew his own prior concession to the theory once he checked the real figures.

Subsidiary questions: The 1925 board of arbitration **never examined the condemned glassware** — Frick's own list of exhibits names only four books, with no crate among them (r13), and Cleve Oram notes the crate sat in the loft directly above two of the hearing's sessions the entire time, unexamined (r21). As for what the tester himself believed: the earliest and most textually grounded evidence — his own book's question mark and his family's account of him saying "it was something in the glass" (r18), corroborated by his wife's diary entry the same day the measure broke (r24) — indicates he suspected the glass specifically from 1911 onward. Complicating this, Frick recalls a private 1925 conversation in which Keddie said he privately believed the patrons were at fault — a tension the sources leave unresolved, perhaps reflecting different things said to different people, or a hedge under the pressure of the hearing itself.

## Section G — Summary (4 points)

**G1.** From 1897 to 1923, the Ostrey Hollow skimming station tested patrons' milk with pipettes that, unknown to anyone, delivered about 5% less than their marked volume, making every fat reading run about 0.19 points low, year-round. Patrons suspected theft or watering by tester Ansel Keddie; officials suspected the patrons; an early committee blamed wet bog hay. All three theories were wrong. A 1911 accident — nine weeks on borrowed glass, during which tests rose then fell back — was the best clue, but went unexplained for decades. In 1925 a board fired Keddie and paid patrons $3,800 without ever finding a cause. Only in 1958, when Keddie's granddaughter Ivy found the condemned glassware in a station loft and measured it, was the true mechanism proven. It took eight scattered records — family weigh-books, a bookkeeper's ledger, an inspector's log, oral memories, and the final bulletin — to piece together, because no single witness ever held both the ledger and the crate at once.
