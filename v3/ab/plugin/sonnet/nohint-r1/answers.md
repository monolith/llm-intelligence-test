### Story 1 — The Short Glass of Ostrey Hollow

From 1896, Ansel Keddie tested and weighed milk at the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery
Association's skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, serving the farms of Hessel Bottom, including
that of his brother-in-law Emmet Brant, the bottom's largest patron. Patrons were paid on
butterfat test, not weight. From nearly the station's opening, its milk tested roughly
nineteen-hundredths of a point lower than milk anywhere else in the county, a deficiency that
held constant across every season for decades. Three explanations circulated: added water
(pressed by manager Selby Vose, who circulated an accusatory warning to patrons in 1919), theft
or false testing by Keddie himself (urged by an Association officer), and thin pasture on the
bottom's wet bog hay (an Association committee's 1908 finding). In May 1911, the station's own
measuring glass broke and a borrowed one was used for nine weeks; tests rose during that
interval and fell back the day the original glass returned — evidence Keddie noted in his own
book only as a question mark, unwilling to accuse without proof. The patrons petitioned in
January 1925; a board of arbitration heard the matter that spring, found no cause, awarded the
patrons $3,800 "without admission," and dismissed Keddie four days later, on April 30, 1925,
after twenty-nine seasons. He died in 1934 with the suspicion still on him. Separately, in 1923 a
state dairy inspector had condemned the station's original testing glassware — six dozen
pipettes bought from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet in 1897 for $77.93, each blown to deliver 17.6 c.c. —
as undersized, and ordered it replaced; the discarded crate went into the station's loft and was
forgotten. In February 1958, Keddie's granddaughter Ivy, a graduate student at the State
Agricultural College, found the crate, had the nine surviving pipettes measured (averaging 16.27
c.c., roughly a 7.6% shortfall), and demonstrated that the glass alone had produced the
deficiency for some 26 years — no watering, no theft. A 1958 college bulletin cleared Keddie's
name, twenty-four years after his death.

**Documents bearing on it:** r17 (Hazel Brant), r18 (Duncan Keddie), r19 (*Ashlin Chronicle*),
r20 (Junia Ammon / Ammon & Sons), r21 (Cleve Oram), r22 (Orin Falke, county report), r23 (Bertram
Kyle, clerk), r24 (Orra Keddie's diary).

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?**

She was his granddaughter. Duncan Keddie's own family notes (r18) — written by Ansel's son, in a
document whose explicit purpose is to correct family-relationship errors already in print — state
plainly: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter." Two other
sources initially say "great-niece": the *Ashlin Chronicle* (r19) and the county agent's 1959
report (r22). But r22 itself retracts this in its own appended note: "I am informed, and have
verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is
mine." I resolve this in favor of granddaughter on three grounds: it is asserted by the person
with the most direct and reliable standing to know (her own father); it is the version explicitly
verified and substituted for the wrong one by another source; and the two sources giving "great-
niece" are not independent of each other — both plausibly trace to the same garbled detail in
secondary reporting of the same 1958 college bulletin, so their agreement doesn't strengthen the
claim (per the instructions, repeated figures from the same ultimate place count as one source).

**B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant?**

They were second cousins. This is stated independently and identically by representatives of
*both* families: Hazel herself (r17: "Ivy Keddie and I are second cousins... It is not
complicated; it is only that nobody wants to count") and Duncan, Ivy's father (r18: "Hazel and
Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins"). Both lay out the same underlying
genealogy: Emmet Brant and Orra (Keddie) were brother and sister; Emmet's son Roy Brant and
Orra's son Duncan Keddie were therefore first cousins; Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter
Ivy are accordingly children of first cousins, i.e. second cousins. This checks out by ordinary
kinship arithmetic. Cleve Oram (r21), an outsider to both families, instead calls Ivy "Hazel
Brant's first cousin once removed" — I reject this as the weaker, single, non-family source
against two independent, reasoned, firsthand family accounts that agree exactly and show their
work. What the sources leave open: neither account gives Roy's or Duncan's birth order relative
to their fathers' generation in a way that would let me independently verify ages, but the stated
generational structure (siblings → their children as first cousins → their children as second
cousins) is internally consistent across both firsthand tellings, which is enough to settle it.

**B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter to what happened at Ostrey Hollow?**

Gideon Vose, who organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association and was its first
president. This is stated independently by Duncan Keddie (r18: "Selby was his son and not, as I
have heard it given, his nephew") and by Bertram Kyle (r23), the clerk who worked immediately
outside Selby's office for twenty-five years: "He was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized
the Association... the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice
in the last year and it is nonsense." Two independent sources, one a family record-keeper and one
Selby's own longtime subordinate, agree and both explicitly reject a competing "nephew" rumor —
a strong, well-corroborated resolution. It matters because Gideon Vose wrote the Association's
by-laws, including the article making every patron's pay depend on the station's butterfat test
rather than on weight (r22) — the very rule that turned a piece of faulty glassware into a
decades-long financial injury. His son Selby then managed the Association through the height of
the dispute (from 1911), personally signed the 1919 circular accusing the Ostrey Hollow patrons
of watering their milk, and signed the yearly ledger sheets carrying the (as it turned out,
telling) "station gain" line — without, per Kyle, ever reading past the bottom total. So the same
family that authored the rule making test accuracy decisive was also the family formally
responsible for the institution when the equipment producing the bad tests went uninvestigated
for a generation.

**B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?**

None is established. Duncan Keddie's notes (r18) address this directly and at length, as a rumor
he wants corrected: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused
them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either... My mother would have
told me. She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley... and she never once put a
Vose in it." Against this stands only Cleve Oram's hedged secondhand claim (r21): "the Keddies
and the Voses were cousins of some sort... I could not draw you the line on paper." I resolve in
favor of no established relationship: Duncan's account is a considered, reasoned denial from a
family insider citing a named authority (his mother, elsewhere shown in the diary excerpts, r24,
to have been the valley's genealogical memory), explicitly written to correct exactly this kind
of talk; Cleve's account is local gossip he himself cannot substantiate. The most likely
explanation the sources support is confusion between the Voses of this story and an unrelated
family of the same surname at Ashlin.

## Section C — Math

**C1.**

(a) The measured lab data (r19) is the most direct evidence: the pipettes were blown to deliver
17.6 c.c. but, measured one by one against a calibrated burette in 1958, averaged 16.27 c.c.
Shortfall = 17.6 − 16.27 = 1.33 c.c.; proportion = 1.33 / 17.6 ≈ **7.56% short**. Since the fat
reading is taken from a graduated neck calibrated for the full 17.6 c.c. charge, a short charge
produces a proportionally low reading "every single time" (r19's own words).

(b) The sources do not fully agree here, and I flag rather than paper over it. The figure most
often quoted for the deficiency is a flat **0.19 points ("nineteen hundredths")**, stated
confidently and repeatedly by Hazel Brant (r17) and echoed as the bulletin's headline figure in
Falke's report (r22). But r22's own body text gives two different, mutually inconsistent
anchors: a station average of "three sixty-one" against Larrow Green's "three seventy-eight"
(difference 0.17), and later a post-fix average of "three eighty-five" (difference from 3.61 of
0.24) — a self-contradiction within a single document (see Section E). Using the most commonly
repeated numbers (measured average 3.61, deficiency 0.19), the implied true average is
**≈3.80**, close to but not identical to the stated post-fix figure of 3.85. I cannot fully
reconcile 0.17 / 0.19 / 0.24 from the sources as given; 0.19 is the best-attested single figure,
but the true average and exact points-short cannot be pinned down with certainty.

(c) The only pounds-of-butterfat figure given is Cleve Oram's **"sixty-seven thousand pounds"**
(r21) over "the whole run of years." This is a single, unverified, orally-remembered source
("You cannot picture it," he adds, suggesting it isn't his own precise figure either), not
corroborated by any other retelling in pounds terms. I report it as the only available figure but
flag it as unconfirmed.

(d) The only money figure given for the total is **$19,600** (r19, from the college bulletin —
also single-sourced within this batch). The patrons actually received the 1925 arbitration award
of **$3,800** (see C1(c) below the ledger discussion and Section E for how this figure is
established against a competing $3,500 claim). Fraction received = 3,800 / 19,600 ≈ **19.4%**,
roughly one-fifth of what the bulletin calculated they were owed.

**C2. Largest patron, 1910 (Emmet Brant, per Hazel's weigh-book reading, r17):**

(a) Milk delivered: 200,000 lb; recorded test: 3.42%. Credited butterfat = 200,000 × 0.0342 =
**6,840 lb**.

(b) Two different models are implied by the sources, and they disagree, which I show rather than
hide:
 - *Additive model* (using the flat 0.19-point deficiency): true test = 3.42 + 0.19 = 3.61%; true
   butterfat = 200,000 × 0.0361 = 7,220 lb; shortfall = 7,220 − 6,840 = **380 lb**.
 - *Proportional model* (using the 7.56% cc-based shortfall): true test = 3.42 / 0.9244 ≈ 3.70%;
   true butterfat = 200,000 × 0.0370 = 7,400 lb; shortfall = 7,400 − 6,840 = **≈560 lb**.
 I cannot determine from the sources which model they intend; the honest answer is a range of
 **roughly 380–560 lb**.

(c) At the only price given anywhere in the sources for this business (r23's "twenty-five cents
the pound," itself an average "taking the fat years with the lean" over the whole multi-decade
stretch, not specific to 1910): 380 lb × $0.25 = $95; 560 lb × $0.25 = $140. So **roughly
$95–$140** for 1910, again a range, not a point figure, and using a price average that wasn't
really meant for a single year.

**C3.**

(a) Total milk received at the station over "the long run of years" is given as 40,000,000 lb
(r23: "sixteen hundred thousand pounds a year" over 25 years). At the station's stated average
test of 3.61% (r22): credited butterfat = 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**.

(b) No source states the pounds actually received at Larrow Green directly. The only figure

Taking that percentage against the credited total as its base: received ≈ 1,444,000 × 1.0525 ≈

(c) As a percentage, the sources' own bookkeeping already states it directly: **5.25% of the

fully reconcile them; I present all three rather than force a false match.

**C4.**

(a) **$77.93**, per the invoice Junia Ammon reproduces in full (r20), verified by adding its own

(b) The station's first season received an estimated **1,100,000 lb** of milk (r23), a figure
Kyle himself flags as remembered rather than recorded ("It is not in any book I ever ruled").

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran from **May 8 to July 10, 1911** (nine weeks), independently dated
identically by the county report (r22) and by Orra Keddie's diary, which records "A. home cross
about a broken measure" under May 8, 1911 (r24) — a strong, independent, contemporaneous
corroboration of the start date. The variable manipulated (by accident, not design) was **which
piece of measuring glass was used**; everything else was explicitly held constant — same tester,
same herds, same pasture and season, "under no different supervision" (r22). The result: tests
rose measurably during the nine weeks and fell back to the old low level the day the station's
own glass returned. This establishes, in a way no later calculation could by itself, that the
deficiency tracked the *instrument*, not the man, the cows, or the season — because all of those
were unchanged while only the glass changed. What it does **not** establish: the exact size of
the shortfall (no numeric 1911 readings are quoted anywhere), what specifically was wrong with
the station's own glass (that mechanism was only identified in 1958), or whether the borrowed
glass itself was perfectly accurate rather than merely "less wrong."

**D2.** At least three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream:
1. The 1911 borrowed-glass interval (r22, r24): with the same tester and conditions, the readings
   changed only when the glass did — inconsistent with a theft/deception theory centered on the
   man.
2. The state dairy inspector, Merle Strawn — explicitly "kin to nobody in the valley whatever"
   (r18) — tested for added water with a lactometer at every visit from 1908 onward and never
   once found evidence of it (r22); this is independent regulatory oversight, unconnected to
   either family.
3. Character and conduct testimony from independent, even mutually estranged, witnesses: Ansel
   tested his own brother-in-law's milk (his largest patron) to that patron's stated detriment for
   decades (r17, r18); he refused to write down an unproven suspicion in 1911, entering only a
   question mark rather than either accusing the equipment or fudging a figure (r18); and his
   wife's diary independently attests to his rigor and honesty over decades of firsthand
   observation ("up before four every day... never once said he was tired," r24).
 Reasons 1 and 2 are independent of each other (an equipment-swap event vs. a regulator's
 chemical test) and both would **still hold even if the glassware had never been measured in
 1958** — Strawn's lactometer inspections in particular ran from 1908 onward, decades before
 the 1958 measurement, and needed no later confirmation to be valid evidence against theft.
 Reason 3 (character) is the weakest standing alone but corroborates independently.
 Did the bookkeeper (Wilbur Teague) ever question the ledger entries that refute the theft
 charge? No — per Kyle (r23), Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years," treating the
 "station gain... as usual" line as self-explanatory, and no one at the arbitration is shown to
 have laid the station's test book beside the inspector's notebook to compare them.

**D3.** The sources support a distinction between knowing an anomaly existed and knowing its
cause. Selby Vose signed the ledger sheets carrying the "station gain" line every year, but per
Kyle, his own clerk for a quarter century, he "read the totals and he never read the lines above
them... take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign" (r23) — indicating no
real scrutiny. Yet he personally signed and circulated the 1919 warning to patrons about added
water (r23, r24), which shows he was aware *something* was consistently off with the Ostrey
Hollow tests well before 1923 — he simply attributed it to the patrons rather than the equipment,
and there is no evidence in any source that he investigated the glassware itself before it was
condemned by the state inspector (an event Kyle describes as unremarkable office routine, "no
more said of it... than there would have been of a broken window").

## Section F — Theory

The shortfall was produced by a single, physical, one-time manufacturing flaw: the six dozen
pipettes bought from Ammon & Sons in 1897 were blown to a nominal 17.6 c.c. mark but consistently
under-delivered (measured in 1958 at an average of 16.27 c.c.), most likely, per the maker's own
later speculation (r20), because a reground mold produced a lot that ran short in a way routine
spot-checks of the first, tenth, and fiftieth piece could not catch, since the whole lot was
*consistently* wrong. Because the Babcock-style test reads the separated fat column against a
neck calibrated for the full 17.6 c.c. charge, a short charge yields a proportionally low reading
"every single time" (r19) — explaining why the error was the same size in every season (it
depended on the glass, not the feed or the weather) and identical for every patron on the bottom
(all their milk passed through the same station instruments). It began the moment that pipette
lot entered service (May 1897) and ended the moment the state inspector condemned it in 1923,
because nothing about the cows, the tester, or the farms needed to change for either — only the
glass changed, both times.

The sources offer three competing explanations, each explicitly named and each defeated:
1. **Added water**, advanced by manager Selby Vose (1919 circular, r23/r24) — defeated by the
   state inspector's lactometer tests at every visit from 1908 on, which never found added water
   (r22).
2. **Tester theft/false reporting**, urged by an Association officer at the 1925 hearing (r22) —
   defeated by the 1911 borrowed-glass interval, in which the same tester's readings rose and
   fell with the equipment alone (r22, r24).
3. **Wet-hay/poor pasture** depressing natural butterfat, from an Association committee (1908)
   and a veterinarian (1926) (r22) — defeated by the county agent's own follow-up into the
   college's actual 1911 feeding-trial data, which found bog hay depresses butterfat by at most
   0.05 points in winter and by nothing measurable in summer, far short of the deficiency, which
   held even on summer pasture (r22's own appended retraction).

Subsidiary questions: **Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?** No. Two of the
three arbitration sittings were held at the station itself, directly beneath the loft where the
crated glass sat the entire time, and no one climbed up to look (r21); Kyle's inventory of the
four documents actually placed before the board (test book, receiving book, Brant's weigh book,
inspector's notebook) does not include the physical glassware, and he states no one is shown to
have cross-checked the inspector's notebook against the test book either (r23). **What did the
tester himself believe the cause was?** He suspected "something in the glass" as early as 1911,
saying so twice at home the summer of the borrowed-measure incident (r18), a suspicion his wife's
diary corroborates by date (r24) — but he would not commit it to the record without proof, and
died in 1934 still unable to prove what he privately believed all along.

## Section G — Summary

For roughly 26 years, a defective lot of test-glass at a small dairy station under-measured every
farmer's milk sample by a fixed proportion, quietly costing them money on every delivery while
three generations blamed water, theft, or bad hay instead. The tester died in disgrace still
unable to prove his own suspicion about the glass. The truth survived only in scattered forms — a
private diary, a family's account book, a supplier's old invoice, a clerk's ledger habits, a
county agent's report, an oral memory from the cream wagon, and a granddaughter's climb up a
ladder in 1958 — each holding one piece and getting other pieces wrong or garbled. No single
witness had the whole picture; only cross-checking all of them, and trusting quoted documents and
contemporaneous records over decades-old memory, resolves what actually happened.
