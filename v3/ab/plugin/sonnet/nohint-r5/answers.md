# Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

**Note on scope.** This session read only retellings r17–r24 (eight documents). No notes or
content from r01–r16 were available to me. All eight documents I read turn out to concern a
single underlying historical episode — the Ostrey Hollow skimming-station affair in the Larrow
Valley — approached from eight different narrators. I have partitioned that one episode into
eight component stories below, each anchored on one retelling and corrected/cross-checked
against the other seven. I have no basis for asserting anything about additional, wholly separate
historical episodes the full 24-retelling corpus may contain elsewhere in r01–r16; if the grading
scheme expects eight unrelated incidents, seven of them are outside what this session could read,
and I am flagging that rather than inventing content for them.

---

## Story 1 — Hazel Brant and the Brant Weigh Book

Hazel Brant (taped 1981) narrates the history of a private farm account — the "weigh book" of
Hessel Bottom, one of six farms on a wet bottom of the Larrow Valley. Her grandfather Emmet Brant
began the book on 1 January 1900, weighing his own milk before it went to the Ostrey Hollow
station and recording the station's figure alongside his own; the two generally agreed, which
mattered later because it proved the *weight* side of the transaction was never in question —
only the butterfat *test*. Emmet's 1910 page records 200,000 lbs of milk delivered, "even," paid
on a test of 3.42 — a figure later shown (Story 6) to run about 0.19 points below the true value.
The book passed to Emmet's son Roy Brant (Hazel's father), then to Hazel, then in 1963 to the
Ordell County Historical Rooms.

Hazel's account also supplies the genealogy tying the Brant and Keddie families together: Ansel
Keddie's wife Orra was Emmet's half-sister, making Ansel the brother-in-law of the valley's
largest patron — the very man his disputed tests shorted most. Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie (Orra
and Ansel's son) were first cousins; Hazel and Ivy Keddie (Duncan's daughter) are therefore second
cousins — confirmed independently and in identical detail by Duncan himself (Story 2).

Hazel dates the state inspector's condemnation of the station's glass to "twenty-one" (1921) — an
error; dated correspondence (Stories 3, 4) and the county report (Story 6) fix it at June 1923.
She also gives the 1919 water circular as going to "nine families," where Orra Keddie's
contemporaneous diary (Story 8) says eleven; the diary, written by the man's own wife near the
event, is the more reliable figure. Documents bearing on this story: r17 (primary), corrected by
r18, r19, r20, r22, r24.

## Story 2 — Duncan Keddie's Family Record and the Fate of the Books

Duncan Keddie, Ansel and Orra's son and a retired schoolteacher, wrote his "Notes for the family"
in 1968 (with a 1970 addition) expressly to correct genealogical errors he had seen printed or
heard spoken in Ashlin. He states plainly: Ansel Keddie (1861–1934) ran the Ostrey Hollow station
from its opening; Orra Brant (1868–1948) was Emmet Brant's sister; their son Duncan (b.1894) and
Emmet's son Roy Brant were first cousins; Duncan's daughter Ivy (b.1931) is therefore Ansel's
**granddaughter**, not his great-niece as was twice printed, and Hazel Brant's **second cousin**,
not her cousin-once-removed as was said aloud in town. He also identifies Selby Vose, the
Association's manager, as the *son* (not nephew) of Gideon Vose, the Association's founder and
first president, and states flatly that there was "no connection whatever" between the Vose and
Keddie families, despite a persistent local rumor to the contrary.

Duncan's most valuable contribution is a precise chain of custody for five separate record books
central to the whole affair: the station's own test book (impounded 1925, to the Cooperative
office 1925–1959, then to the Historical Rooms); the Brant weigh book (Emmet to Roy 1929, lent to
Ivy 1958, to Hazel 1962, donated 1963); state inspector Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks (to the
state dairy office 1936, to Farland's college library 1947, where Ivy found the 1923 volume in
1958); the Alder Corners (cheese company) test book, copied out for Ivy in 1958 by Rosalie Cudd,
Jerome Cudd's daughter (not niece, as the Chronicle later has it — Story 3); and the condemned
glassware crate itself, in the station loft from 1923 to 1958, then measured at Farland and
deposited with the other books in 1959. His father Ansel was dismissed 30 April 1925, four days
before the arbitration board's finding was announced — a date Duncan gives with the same
precision Orra's diary independently confirms. Documents bearing on this story: r18 (primary),
cross-checked by r17, r19, r22, r24.

## Story 3 — "Old Ordell Wrong Righted": The Ashlin Chronicle's Vindication Story

The *Ashlin Chronicle*'s front page for 3 October 1958 reported on College Bulletin No. 471
(Professor Leland Vaught and Ivy Keddie), which cleared Ansel Keddie thirty-three years after his
1925 dismissal. The paper lays out the mechanism as the bulletin found it: the station's test
glassware, bought new in 1897 and put into use "in the spring of 1897," was condemned in 1923 by
the state dairy inspector, who found it would not deliver the volume marked on its stem; it sat
forgotten in the station loft until February 1958, when graduate student Ivy Keddie climbed a
ladder and found the crate. She carried it to the college at Farland and in March 1958 measured
eleven surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette; each was blown for 17.6 c.c. and each
delivered less, averaging 16.27 c.c. — a short charge that reads low on any test made with it, in
constant proportion, regardless of season. The bulletin priced the patrons' uncredited butterfat
at $19,600.

The Chronicle's account contains two errors that other sources correct: it calls Ivy Keddie
Ansel's "great-niece" (corrected to granddaughter by Duncan Keddie and by the county agent's own
retraction, Story 6) and calls Rosalie Cudd "Jerome Cudd's niece" (Duncan's notes say daughter).
Its count of eleven surviving pipettes also conflicts with two independent later accounts, which
both give nine (Stories 5, 6); nine, attested independently by a family outsider and by the
official county report citing the lab measurement directly, is the better-supported figure. The
paper also reproduces the original 1897 invoice total as "seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine
cents," which does not match the arithmetic of the line items given in the invoice itself (Story
4) — the true total is $77.93. Documents bearing on this story: r19 (primary), corrected by r18,
r20, r21, r22.

## Story 4 — Ammon & Sons and the Bad Lot of Glass

Junia Ammon's 1949 privately printed firm history reproduces, from the company's own letter
books, the invoice that put the faulty glassware into the world. Dated 14 April 1897, it billed
the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association's Ostrey Hollow station for six dozen (72)
milk-test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. at 35 cents each ($25.20), twelve dozen test bottles ($31.68),
one acid measure (90 cents), one four-bottle hand tester ($18.00), and freight ($2.15) — a summed
total of $77.93, though Ammon's own prose twice mis-states this as "seventy-seven dollars and
thirty-nine cents," an error also carried by two other narrators (Story 1, Story 3). The outfit
went into service at Ostrey Hollow on 1 May 1897.

Twenty-six years later, in the summer of 1923, a state inspector ran the pipettes against a
burette and found they would not deliver their mark; the Association wrote to Ammon & Sons on 28
June 1923, and the firm's reply of 2 July — also reproduced in full — offered only that the
company's books "of the nineties" could not identify what had gone wrong with that particular
lot. Ammon's own retrospective theory, offered with evident regret, is that the mold used to blow
that lot had been reground and ran consistently short, an error a normal spot-check of a few
pieces from a large, internally consistent lot could never catch, since the whole lot was wrong in
exactly the same way. The firm's own works records — which might have proven or disproven this —
burned in a 1931 fire; only the office correspondence survives, which is why Ammon's explanation
remains an informed guess rather than a documented fact. Documents bearing on this story: r20
(primary), cross-checked by r17, r19, r22.


# Answers — Part 2: Section A, Stories 5–8; Section B

## Story 5 — Cleve Oram's Road: The Cream Hauler's Memory

Cleve Oram (b.1900), whose father drove the cream wagon seven miles up from Larrow Green to the
Ostrey Hollow station for over twenty years, gave an oral history in 1977 built entirely around
what he physically saw: the muddy climbing road, the two-story board station with its covered
intake platform and separator room, and the loft above it reached only by a wall ladder, where a
tarpaulined crate of old glass sat for decades. He recalls five dozen (60) measures originally in
that crate with nine surviving by the time it came down — the survivor count of nine matches the
county report (Story 6) independently, but the original count of 60 conflicts with the
manufacturer's own invoice for six dozen (72) (Story 4); the invoice, a primary business record,
should be preferred over a boy's decades-old memory of what he was told the crate once held.

Oram places the 1925 hearing correctly (he "was twenty-five years old") and describes two of its
three sittings being held in the station's own weigh room, directly beneath the crate, with
farmers standing outside in the rain unable to hear the words — a detail no other retelling
supplies. He gives the arbitration award as $3,500, against three other sources' $3,800 (Story 6);
his figure, "what my father said," is a single oral transmission and is outweighed by the
documentary/clerk-and-report figures. Despite the 1958 vindication, Oram is careful to record
that his father never withdrew his lifelong belief that "there was less of it coming off that
platform than went onto it" — folk suspicion of the tester persisting after the record had been
corrected. He also repeats, uncertainly ("of some sort... I could not draw you the line on
paper"), the rumor that the Vose and Keddie families were related, which Duncan Keddie's careful
genealogy (Story 2) explicitly denies. Documents bearing on this story: r21 (primary), checked
against r18, r19, r22.

## Story 6 — Orin Falke's County Report: The Three Theories Tested

Orin Falke, the county agricultural agent, was directed by the board of supervisors in 1959 to
produce an orderly summary of "the Ostrey Hollow question." His numbered report is the most
carefully dated document in the set: station opened 4 May 1896; Ansel Keddie tester for
twenty-nine seasons; first complaint of record 1902; a diagnostic incident on 8 May 1911 when the
station's glass broke and a borrowed measure was used for nine weeks until 10 July, during which
tests rose, falling back the day the station's own glass resumed; state inspector's condemnation
of the pipettes on 12 June 1923; board of arbitration sitting 17 March–4 May 1925, awarding
$3,800 "without admission" and dismissing Keddie four days earlier, on 30 April 1925; Ivy Keddie's
discovery of the crate in February 1958 and her measurement of nine surviving pipettes on 9 March
1958.

Falke weighs and rejects two rival theories (added water, found nowhere by the state inspector's
lactometer since 1908; and cream theft, disposed of by the 1911 borrowed-glass episode) and
initially allows a third — that wet Hessel Bottom hay thinned the cream — some partial credit,
citing a 1908 Association committee and a 1926 veterinarian's letter. In an appended note, after
obtaining the actual figures from the 1911 college feeding-trial bulletin those two witnesses had
relied on, he withdraws that concession: bog hay depressed the test by at most 0.05 in winter and
nothing in summer, far short of the observed year-round 0.19-point deficiency. In the same note he
corrects his own report's earlier statement that Ivy Keddie was Ansel's great-niece, having
verified she is the granddaughter. Documents bearing on this story: r22 (primary), corroborated
by r18, r19, r24.

## Story 7 — Bertram Kyle's Office: Bookkeeping in Plain Sight

Bertram Kyle, clerk at the Cooperative's Larrow Green office for twenty-five years, wrote his 1953
recollection from the vantage of the desk outside the manager's door. He describes the routine
annual entry for the Ostrey Hollow station account: butterfat credited to patrons on the station
test, set against butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in the station's cream, with the
difference entered every single year as "station gain... five and a quarter per cent, as usual" —
a line he copied for decades without ever wondering at it. He identifies Selby Vose (manager from
1911) as Gideon Vose's son, not nephew — independently confirming Duncan Keddie's genealogy
(Story 2) — and describes Vose as a man who read only the bottom figure of a sheet and signed,
never the lines above it. He names Wilbur Teague as the bookkeeper who wrote those station lines
for forty years without ever questioning them either.

Kyle names the four documents actually carried into the 1925 arbitration room: the station's own
test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book (lent, not given), and the state
inspector's notebook for the year the glass was condemned — and states plainly that no one in that
room ever laid two of those four side by side and looked, and that the condemned glass itself,
sitting in the loft directly overhead, was never one of the four. His dates are the least reliable
element of his account: he places the station's own glass going into service in 1898 (against
three independent sources' 1897), the glass's condemnation in "twenty-two" (1922, against three
sources' 1923), and the arbitration award in "twenty-four" (1924, against three sources' 1925) —
all plausibly explained by his relying on memory of correspondence "handled later," decades after
the fact, rather than the documents themselves. Documents bearing on this story: r23 (primary),
corrected by r18, r19, r20, r22, r24.

## Story 8 — Orra Keddie's Diary: A Wife's Book, 1897–1930

Orra Keddie (1866–1948), Ansel's wife and Emmet Brant's half-sister, kept a diary from 1897 to
1930 that supplies the only contemporaneous, dated firsthand record of her husband's working
life. Her 1 May 1897 entry — "the station's own glass came up" — independently fixes the date the
faulty pipettes entered service, agreeing with the Ammon invoice and the Chronicle (Story 4)
against Bertram Kyle's 1898. Her 8 May 1911 entry ("A. home cross about a broken measure")
independently confirms the date of the diagnostic borrowed-glass episode central to Falke's report
(Story 6). Her June 1919 entries describe Ansel personally carrying the water-adulteration
circular to eleven families himself, standing at each door while it was read, rather than mailing
it — and record that her brother Emmet never entered their house again over it, a rift lasting
until his death in 1929. Her 1919 figure of eleven families should be preferred to Hazel Brant's
later, secondhand "nine" (Story 1).

The diary dates the hearing (two sittings held at the station itself, "the men standing outside in
the road") and gives 30 April 1925 for Ansel's dismissal in language that matches Duncan's and
Falke's accounts exactly, down to Ansel coming home at his ordinary hour, hanging up his apron,
and asking what there was for supper. It records his death on an unspecified day in February 1934,
"without much pain," and a burial well attended by men who praised his carpentry but never once
named — let alone retracted — the accusation, twenty-four years before the 1958 bulletin cleared
him; Orra herself died in 1948, ten years before that vindication. Documents bearing on this
story: r24 (primary), corroborated by r18 and r22.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.** Granddaughter. Duncan Keddie (r18) states
directly that he is Ansel's son and Ivy is his own daughter, which makes Ivy Ansel's
granddaughter; the county agent's report (r22) initially printed "great-niece" but appended an
explicit correction after verifying the relationship: "Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not
the great-niece." The Ashlin Chronicle (r19) also has "great-niece," uncorrected on its own page.
I resolve this as granddaughter because it rests on the primary family document (the son's own
account) plus an official retraction, against two copies of the same secondhand newspaper/report
error.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.** Second cousins. Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie
were siblings; their children, Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie, were therefore first cousins; Roy's
daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are the children of first cousins, i.e. second cousins.
This exact chain is given independently and identically by both Hazel (r17) and Duncan (r18),
each of whom separately and explicitly rejects the common local errors ("cousin once removed,"
"niece/aunt"). Cleve Oram (r21), an outside oral historian, calls them "first cousin once
removed" — the precise error the two family sources anticipate and rule out. No intervening
generation is left open by the sources; both lines are fully accounted for.

**B3. Selby Vose's father.** Gideon Vose, who organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery
Association and served as its first president — stated independently by Duncan Keddie (r18) and
by Bertram Kyle (r23), both of whom explicitly reject a rumor that Selby was Gideon's nephew. This
matters because Selby Vose, as manager from 1911, was the person who signed the 1919
water-adulteration warning and who (per Kyle) signed the station's account sheets every year
without scrutinizing the lines above the total — the identity and institutional legitimacy of the
accuser is part of how the accusation gained and kept its authority for over three decades.

**B4. Relationship between the Vose and Keddie families.** None that the sources can verify.
Duncan Keddie (r18) gives a detailed, first-person denial, sourced to his mother's own thorough
knowledge of valley genealogy, and states the confusion arises from mixing up the Ashlin Voses
(no relation to Gideon's family either) with Gideon's own family. Cleve Oram (r21), an outside
oral source, repeats the rumor but immediately qualifies it as unverifiable ("cousins of some
sort... I could not draw you the line on paper"). Weighing a detailed family denial against an
admittedly uncertain piece of valley hearsay, I conclude the two families were not related, and
that this is one of the persistent misattributions the sources themselves flag as common.


# Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math

**C1.**

*(a) Proportion of shortfall, from the glassware.* The Chronicle (r19) reports the pipettes were
blown for a 17.6 c.c. charge and delivered an average of 16.27 c.c. when measured against a
calibrated burette. Shortfall = 17.6 − 16.27 = 1.33 c.c. Proportion = 1.33 / 17.6 ≈ 0.0756, i.e.
the glass delivered about 92.4% of its marked charge, so any test made with it reads about 7.6%
low relative to the true fat content of the sample drawn.

*(b) True average test, and the point deficiency.* Falke's report (r22) gives the station's
recorded long-run average as 3.61 and the comparison creamery's as 3.78 (a difference of 0.17),
but the same report — and independently Hazel Brant (r17) — state the bulletin's fixed deficiency
as "nineteen hundredths" (0.19), which would put the true average at about 3.61 + 0.19 = 3.80,
close to the 3.78 comparison figure. **I flag this as an internal inconsistency in r22 itself**
(0.17 by subtraction of its own two stated averages vs. 0.19 stated as "the" deficiency two
sentences later) and resolve in favor of 0.19, since it is independently corroborated by Hazel's
family record and stated by Falke as the bulletin's own number, whereas the 3.61/3.78 pair are
described only as rough, rounded "stood at" figures. Note also that the proportional method in
(a) implies a *proportional*, not flat, error (≈7.6% of whatever the true value is), which for a
true average near 3.80 would predict a deficiency of about 0.29, not 0.19 — a second inconsistency
the sources do not resolve. I use 0.19/true≈3.80 for the remaining calculations because it is the
figure most repeated and most directly attributed to the bulletin, while noting this tension is
not fully resolvable from the sources given.

*(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited.* Bertram Kyle (r23) gives total milk received at the
station as "forty million pounds" over the affected run of years. At the recorded 3.61% test,
credited fat ≈ 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lbs. At a true 3.80% test, true fat ≈ 40,000,000 ×
0.038 = 1,520,000 lbs. Shortfall by this method ≈ 76,000 lbs. However, the bulletin's own
figure, cited independently by Cleve Oram (r21), is 67,000 lbs. Since Oram is citing the primary
bulletin's stated conclusion directly, and my 76,000 lb figure is derived from Kyle's admittedly
rounded milk-volume estimate ("sixteen hundred thousand pounds a year... did not vary greatly")
combined with the disputed 0.19/3.80 figures above, **I defer to the bulletin's own stated total
of 67,000 lbs** as authoritative, and report my derived 76,000 lbs only as a rough, lower-confidence
cross-check that is in the right order of magnitude.

*(d) Value, and fraction actually received.* The Chronicle (r19), citing the bulletin, prices the
uncredited butterfat at $19,600. The arbitration award, independently attested at $3,800 by
three sources (r19, r22, r23) against a lone $3,500 from r21, was paid "without admission." Fraction
received = 3,800 / 19,600 ≈ 0.194, i.e. patrons received roughly one-fifth (about 19%) of what the
bulletin later calculated they were actually owed.

**C2. Emmet Brant, 1910 (the largest patron).**

(a) Credited: 200,000 lbs milk × 3.42% (Hazel's weigh-book figure, r17) = 6,840 lbs butterfat
credited.

(b) Owed and not credited: using the 0.19-point deficiency (true test ≈ 3.61%): true fat =
200,000 × 0.0361 = 7,220 lbs; owed = 7,220 − 6,840 = **380 lbs**. (Cross-check using the
proportional 7.56% method instead: true test ≈ 3.42 / 0.9244 ≈ 3.70%; true fat = 7,400 lbs; owed =
560 lbs. I report 380 lbs as primary, consistent with my choice in C1(b), and flag the 560 lb
alternative as the result of the competing, unresolved proportional-error method.)

(c) Value that year: Bertram Kyle (r23) gives an average butterfat price of "twenty-five cents the
pound" over the whole affected period (no single 1910 price is given in the sources). At that
rate: 380 lbs × $0.25 = **$95.00** (or, on the alternative method, 560 × $0.25 = $140.00). No
source gives a 1910-specific price, so this is necessarily an approximation using the long-run
average price.

**C3. Full delivery years — credited vs. received.**

(a) Butterfat credited to patrons, total: ≈1,444,000 lbs (as C1(c), from 40,000,000 lbs milk at
the recorded 3.61% average — Kyle's volume figure combined with Falke's recorded-average figure).

(b) Butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in the station's cream: Kyle (r23) states this
exceeded the credited figure every year by a "station gain" of "five and a quarter per cent, as
usual." Received ≈ credited × 1.0525 ≈ 1,444,000 × 1.0525 ≈ **1,519,900 lbs**.

(c) Percentage difference: the ledger's own convention states this gap as **5.25% of the fat
credited to patrons** (not of the fat received) — the question's own point about being exact
about the denominator matters here, because 5.25% of the larger "received" figure would be a
different, smaller-looking difference. Notably, 1,519,900 lbs is very close to my independently
derived "true" fat total of 1,520,000 lbs in C1(c) — suggesting the routinely-booked "station
gain," dismissed for decades as ordinary processing shrinkage, may in fact have been substantially
the same missing fat that the 1958 bulletin later attributed to the bad glass, hiding in the
ledger under an innocuous label. I present this as an inference, not a proven identity, since the
sources never make this connection explicitly (see Section F).

**C4.**

(a) Total invoice for the station's testing outfit: **$77.93** — this is the sum of the line items
Junia Ammon reproduces (r20): $25.20 + $31.68 + $0.90 + $18.00 + $2.15 = $77.93. Three narrators
(r17, r19, and Ammon's own prose in r20) instead give "seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine
cents" ($77.39); I resolve in favor of $77.93 because it is directly verifiable by arithmetic
against the reproduced invoice's own line items — a quoted document (and simple addition)
outranking a repeated narrative gloss, even where that gloss appears three times, including once
inside the same document as the correct figure.

(b) The invoice bought six dozen (72) pipettes at 17.6 c.c. each (r20), matching the Chronicle's
independent "six dozen pipettes" (r19); Cleve Oram's "five dozen" (60, r21) is a lone,
decades-later oral estimate and is outweighed by the primary invoice. Each pipette was short by
1.33 c.c. (17.6 marked vs. 16.27 c.c. delivered on average, per r19's 1958 lab measurement).

**C5.**

(a) The faulty glass was in service for **just over 26 years**: installed 1 May 1897 (agreed by
r19, r20, and Orra's diary r24, against Bertram Kyle's uncorroborated "1898") and condemned 12
June 1923 (Falke, r22; consistent with r19's "twenty-six years" and Ammon's correspondence dated
June/July 1923). The money calculation (C1–C3) runs over **25 full delivery years**, matching
Bertram Kyle's own ratio (40,000,000 lbs total ÷ 1,600,000 lbs/year = 25 years exactly). These
numbers differ because the physical duration includes two partial years — roughly eight months of
1897 and about five and a half months of 1923 — that fall outside Kyle's "year in, year out" full
annual rate and so are excluded from (or only partly reflected in) the 25-full-year volume figure
used for the financial arithmetic.

(b) The station's first season's milk volume is given only once, by Bertram Kyle (r23), as "a good
eleven hundred thousand pounds" (1,100,000 lbs) — and he explicitly flags this as an unverified
personal recollection "from men who were there," not from any ledger ("It is not in any book I
ever ruled"). No other source corroborates or contradicts this figure; it should be used, if at
all, with that caveat clearly attached.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 borrowed-glass interval as an experiment.** Falke's report (r22) dates it
precisely: the station's own measure broke on 8 May 1911, a borrowed measure was used until 10
July 1911 — nine weeks — and Orra Keddie's diary (r24) independently confirms the start date. The
variable was the measuring glass itself; held constant were the tester (Ansel Keddie), the herds,
the pasture, the season, and the supervision. The result: tests rose markedly during the nine
weeks and fell back to their former low level the day the station's own glass resumed. This
establishes, by direct observation rather than any later calculation, that the cause travelled
with the *glass* and not with the cows, the season, or the tester's methods or honesty — it rules
out feed and personal dishonesty at the time, decades before anyone measured a pipette. What it
does **not** establish: the exact size of the shortfall (no figures are given for how much the
1911 tests rose, only "markedly"), the specific mechanism (short-volume delivery was only
identified by direct measurement in 1958), or that the borrowed glass was itself perfectly
accurate rather than merely closer to accurate.

**D2. Independent reasons the tester was not stealing cream.**
1. The 1911 natural experiment above (r22, r24) — same tester, different glass, tests rose.
2. Bertram Kyle's ledger evidence (r23): every single year, *more* fat arrived at the main
   creamery in the station's cream than was credited to patrons at Ostrey Hollow — a persistent
   surplus downstream, the opposite of the pattern theft would produce, since a tester skimming
   cream for himself would reduce, not increase, what reached the creamery relative to what was
   credited.
3. Ansel's own documented conduct: he suspected the glass as early as 1911 but, per Duncan's
   account (r18), would not write more than a bare question mark in the book because he "would
   not put down what he could not prove" — and per Orra's diary (r24) he personally delivered the
   1919 accusatory water circular to every family himself, standing at each door while it was
   read, rather than avoiding the confrontation a guilty man might prefer.

These are independent because they draw on different kinds of evidence (a field experiment, an
accounting record, and behavioral/character testimony) from different narrators (the diary and
county report; the clerk; the son and the diary). Reason 1 is the one that would still hold even
if the glassware had never been measured in 1958: it was recorded in real time, in 1911, decades
before any lab measurement existed. As for whether the bookkeeper ever questioned the entries that
refute the theft accusation: no. Kyle states explicitly that the "station gain, as usual" line
"never once crossed my mind to wonder at," and that the bookkeeper who actually wrote it, Wilbur
Teague, "never asked anybody anything in forty years."

**D3. Did the manager know the tests ran low before 1923?** Yes, as to the fact, but apparently
not as to the cause. The low tests had been a matter of public complaint at Ostrey Hollow since
1902 and were raised "at every annual meeting for a quarter of a century" (r19); Selby Vose,
manager from 1911, personally signed and circulated the 1919 water-adulteration warning addressing
exactly this discrepancy (r17, r23, r24), which shows direct awareness of the problem and a
(mistaken) theory about its cause. However, Bertram Kyle's account (r23) — that Vose habitually
read only the bottom total of a sheet and never the lines above it — suggests he never connected
the low tests to the ledger's own "station gain" pattern that, in hindsight, undercut his water
theory, and there is no evidence in any source that he, or anyone else in the office, suspected
the glassware itself before 1923.

## Section E — Contradictions

1. **Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel:** "great-niece" (r19; r22 before its own correction) vs.
   "granddaughter" (r18; r22's own appended correction). Resolved as granddaughter via the primary

## Section F — Theory

**F1. Causal theory.** The Ostrey Hollow shortfall was caused entirely by a defective batch of
glass pipettes, bought new from Ammon & Sons in 1897 and blown to a marked 17.6 c.c. charge that
they never actually delivered (averaging 16.27 c.c.), most plausibly — per Junia Ammon's own
retrospective account (r20) — because the mold used for that lot had been reground and ran
consistently short in a way no ordinary quality check on a handful of pieces from an internally
consistent bad lot could catch. This explains the *size* of the error (a fixed, small shortfall in
delivered volume, translating into a fixed, small shortfall in the fat percentage read off the
test bottle every time that volume was used); *why it was constant* across seasons rather than
varying with feed (the defect was physical and mechanical, not biological, so it applied with the
same force in June and February alike, which is exactly why the wet-hay theory could never account
for it — Hazel Brant independently observed the deficiency was "flat as a floor," "not a little in
the winter and none in the summer, the way a feed thing goes"); *why it began when it did* (the
glass entered service 1 May 1897, per three convergent sources); *why it ended when it did* (a
routine 1923 state-inspector burette check happened to catch it, prompting immediate
condemnation and replacement, after which the recorded average rose and held at a new, higher
level); and *why nothing at the station or on the farms had to change* to fix it (the defect lay
entirely in one piece of purchased apparatus — not the cows, the land, the feed, the scale, or the
tester's competence or honesty, all of which were unchanged before and after 1923).

The sources offer three competing explanations, each advanced by a named party and each defeated
by specific evidence: **(1) added water**, advanced by the Association's manager (the 1919
circular, signed by Selby Vose per r17/r23/r24), defeated by the state dairy inspector's lactometer
tests, which found no added water at any visit from 1908 forward (r22); **(2) cream theft by the
tester**, urged by "an officer of the Association" at the 1925 hearing (r22), defeated by the 1911
borrowed-glass natural experiment, in which the same tester's figures rose when a different
measure was used (r22, r24), and by the ledger's persistent downstream "station gain" surplus
inconsistent with theft (r23); and **(3) wet hay/pasture thinning the cream**, based on a 1908
Association committee finding and a 1926 veterinarian's letter (cited in r22), defeated by the
actual figures of the 1911 college feeding-trial bulletin those findings implicitly relied on,
which showed bog hay could depress the test by at most 0.05 points in winter and nothing in
summer — far short of the observed constant 0.19-point year-round deficiency (r22's own appended
correction).

As to the two subsidiary questions: the 1925 board of arbitration never examined the condemned
glassware. It was already crated in the loft directly over two of the hearing's three sittings
(r21), yet Bertram Kyle's list of the four documents actually entered as evidence does not include
it, and Kyle states outright that no one in that room ever laid the relevant records side by side
and looked (r23). As to what the tester himself believed: Ansel Keddie suspected "something in the
glass" from the summer of 1911 onward, immediately after the borrowed-measure episode, but — being
"a man who would not put down what he could not prove" — never recorded more than a bare question
mark in his own test book, and held this unproven private suspicion, unresolved, for the remaining
fourteen years of his career and the further nine years of his life after his dismissal (r18, r24).

## Section G — Summary

For twenty-nine years, the Ostrey Hollow skimming station's butterfat tests ran mysteriously low —
a fixed, unvarying shortfall that cost its patrons their fair pay and, in 1925, cost tester Ansel
Keddie his job on suspicion of theft. The true cause was mundane and physical: a 1897 batch of
glass pipettes, made a hair short of their marked measure by a manufacturing defect, that quietly
under-delivered milk into every test for twenty-six years. No single record proved this. It took a
farm family's private weigh book, a clerk's ledger, a wife's diary, a manufacturer's own
correspondence, a county report, an oral history of the cream wagon, a newspaper account, and the
tester's own son's careful genealogy — eight different vantage points, each holding one piece and
mistaking or misdating others — before Keddie's granddaughter, Ivy, climbed a ladder in 1958,
found the discarded glass, and measured it.
