# Answers — Part 2: Section A, Stories 5–8; Section B

## Story 5 — Cleve Oram's Road: The Cream Hauler's Memory

Cleve Oram (b.1900), whose father drove the cream wagon seven miles up from Larrow Green to the
Ostrey Hollow station for over twenty years, gave an oral history in 1977 built entirely around
what he physically saw: the muddy climbing road, the two-story board station with its covered
intake platform and separator room, and the loft above it reached only by a wall ladder, where a
tarpaulined crate of old glass sat for decades. He recalls five dozen (60) measures originally in
that crate with nine surviving by the time it came down — the survivor count of nine matches the
county report (Story 6) independently, but the original count of 60 conflicts with the
manufacturer's own invoice for six dozen (72) (Story 4); the invoice, a primary business record,
should be preferred over a boy's decades-old memory of what he was told the crate once held.

Oram places the 1925 hearing correctly (he "was twenty-five years old") and describes two of its
three sittings being held in the station's own weigh room, directly beneath the crate, with
farmers standing outside in the rain unable to hear the words — a detail no other retelling
supplies. He gives the arbitration award as $3,500, against three other sources' $3,800 (Story 6);
his figure, "what my father said," is a single oral transmission and is outweighed by the
documentary/clerk-and-report figures. Despite the 1958 vindication, Oram is careful to record
that his father never withdrew his lifelong belief that "there was less of it coming off that
platform than went onto it" — folk suspicion of the tester persisting after the record had been
corrected. He also repeats, uncertainly ("of some sort... I could not draw you the line on
paper"), the rumor that the Vose and Keddie families were related, which Duncan Keddie's careful
genealogy (Story 2) explicitly denies. Documents bearing on this story: r21 (primary), checked
against r18, r19, r22.

## Story 6 — Orin Falke's County Report: The Three Theories Tested

Orin Falke, the county agricultural agent, was directed by the board of supervisors in 1959 to
produce an orderly summary of "the Ostrey Hollow question." His numbered report is the most
carefully dated document in the set: station opened 4 May 1896; Ansel Keddie tester for
twenty-nine seasons; first complaint of record 1902; a diagnostic incident on 8 May 1911 when the
station's glass broke and a borrowed measure was used for nine weeks until 10 July, during which
tests rose, falling back the day the station's own glass resumed; state inspector's condemnation
of the pipettes on 12 June 1923; board of arbitration sitting 17 March–4 May 1925, awarding
$3,800 "without admission" and dismissing Keddie four days earlier, on 30 April 1925; Ivy Keddie's
discovery of the crate in February 1958 and her measurement of nine surviving pipettes on 9 March
1958.

Falke weighs and rejects two rival theories (added water, found nowhere by the state inspector's
lactometer since 1908; and cream theft, disposed of by the 1911 borrowed-glass episode) and
initially allows a third — that wet Hessel Bottom hay thinned the cream — some partial credit,
citing a 1908 Association committee and a 1926 veterinarian's letter. In an appended note, after
obtaining the actual figures from the 1911 college feeding-trial bulletin those two witnesses had
relied on, he withdraws that concession: bog hay depressed the test by at most 0.05 in winter and
nothing in summer, far short of the observed year-round 0.19-point deficiency. In the same note he
corrects his own report's earlier statement that Ivy Keddie was Ansel's great-niece, having
verified she is the granddaughter. Documents bearing on this story: r22 (primary), corroborated
by r18, r19, r24.

## Story 7 — Bertram Kyle's Office: Bookkeeping in Plain Sight

Bertram Kyle, clerk at the Cooperative's Larrow Green office for twenty-five years, wrote his 1953
recollection from the vantage of the desk outside the manager's door. He describes the routine
annual entry for the Ostrey Hollow station account: butterfat credited to patrons on the station
test, set against butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in the station's cream, with the
difference entered every single year as "station gain... five and a quarter per cent, as usual" —
a line he copied for decades without ever wondering at it. He identifies Selby Vose (manager from
1911) as Gideon Vose's son, not nephew — independently confirming Duncan Keddie's genealogy
(Story 2) — and describes Vose as a man who read only the bottom figure of a sheet and signed,
never the lines above it. He names Wilbur Teague as the bookkeeper who wrote those station lines
for forty years without ever questioning them either.

Kyle names the four documents actually carried into the 1925 arbitration room: the station's own
test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book (lent, not given), and the state
inspector's notebook for the year the glass was condemned — and states plainly that no one in that
room ever laid two of those four side by side and looked, and that the condemned glass itself,
sitting in the loft directly overhead, was never one of the four. His dates are the least reliable
element of his account: he places the station's own glass going into service in 1898 (against
three independent sources' 1897), the glass's condemnation in "twenty-two" (1922, against three
sources' 1923), and the arbitration award in "twenty-four" (1924, against three sources' 1925) —
all plausibly explained by his relying on memory of correspondence "handled later," decades after
the fact, rather than the documents themselves. Documents bearing on this story: r23 (primary),
corrected by r18, r19, r20, r22, r24.

## Story 8 — Orra Keddie's Diary: A Wife's Book, 1897–1930

Orra Keddie (1866–1948), Ansel's wife and Emmet Brant's half-sister, kept a diary from 1897 to
1930 that supplies the only contemporaneous, dated firsthand record of her husband's working
life. Her 1 May 1897 entry — "the station's own glass came up" — independently fixes the date the
faulty pipettes entered service, agreeing with the Ammon invoice and the Chronicle (Story 4)
against Bertram Kyle's 1898. Her 8 May 1911 entry ("A. home cross about a broken measure")
independently confirms the date of the diagnostic borrowed-glass episode central to Falke's report
(Story 6). Her June 1919 entries describe Ansel personally carrying the water-adulteration
circular to eleven families himself, standing at each door while it was read, rather than mailing
it — and record that her brother Emmet never entered their house again over it, a rift lasting
until his death in 1929. Her 1919 figure of eleven families should be preferred to Hazel Brant's
later, secondhand "nine" (Story 1).

The diary dates the hearing (two sittings held at the station itself, "the men standing outside in
the road") and gives 30 April 1925 for Ansel's dismissal in language that matches Duncan's and
Falke's accounts exactly, down to Ansel coming home at his ordinary hour, hanging up his apron,
and asking what there was for supper. It records his death on an unspecified day in February 1934,
"without much pain," and a burial well attended by men who praised his carpentry but never once
named — let alone retracted — the accusation, twenty-four years before the 1958 bulletin cleared
him; Orra herself died in 1948, ten years before that vindication. Documents bearing on this
story: r24 (primary), corroborated by r18 and r22.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.** Granddaughter. Duncan Keddie (r18) states
directly that he is Ansel's son and Ivy is his own daughter, which makes Ivy Ansel's
granddaughter; the county agent's report (r22) initially printed "great-niece" but appended an
explicit correction after verifying the relationship: "Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not
the great-niece." The Ashlin Chronicle (r19) also has "great-niece," uncorrected on its own page.
I resolve this as granddaughter because it rests on the primary family document (the son's own
account) plus an official retraction, against two copies of the same secondhand newspaper/report
error.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.** Second cousins. Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie
were siblings; their children, Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie, were therefore first cousins; Roy's
daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are the children of first cousins, i.e. second cousins.
This exact chain is given independently and identically by both Hazel (r17) and Duncan (r18),
each of whom separately and explicitly rejects the common local errors ("cousin once removed,"
"niece/aunt"). Cleve Oram (r21), an outside oral historian, calls them "first cousin once
removed" — the precise error the two family sources anticipate and rule out. No intervening
generation is left open by the sources; both lines are fully accounted for.

**B3. Selby Vose's father.** Gideon Vose, who organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery
Association and served as its first president — stated independently by Duncan Keddie (r18) and
by Bertram Kyle (r23), both of whom explicitly reject a rumor that Selby was Gideon's nephew. This
matters because Selby Vose, as manager from 1911, was the person who signed the 1919
water-adulteration warning and who (per Kyle) signed the station's account sheets every year
without scrutinizing the lines above the total — the identity and institutional legitimacy of the
accuser is part of how the accusation gained and kept its authority for over three decades.

**B4. Relationship between the Vose and Keddie families.** None that the sources can verify.
Duncan Keddie (r18) gives a detailed, first-person denial, sourced to his mother's own thorough
knowledge of valley genealogy, and states the confusion arises from mixing up the Ashlin Voses
(no relation to Gideon's family either) with Gideon's own family. Cleve Oram (r21), an outside
oral source, repeats the rumor but immediately qualifies it as unverifiable ("cousins of some
sort... I could not draw you the line on paper"). Weighing a detailed family denial against an
admittedly uncertain piece of valley hearsay, I conclude the two families were not related, and
that this is one of the persistent misattributions the sources themselves flag as common.
