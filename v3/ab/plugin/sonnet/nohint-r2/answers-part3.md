# Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math

**C1.**

*(a) Proportion by which the tests differed from the truth, from the glassware:*
The pipettes were blown to deliver 17.6 c.c. but, measured against a calibrated burette in 1958,
delivered on average 16.27 c.c. (r19). Since the Babcock test's percentage reading is calibrated
on the assumption that the full marked charge is delivered, a short charge produces a
proportionally low reading: (17.6 − 16.27) / 17.6 ≈ **7.6% short**, directly from the glass.
Separately, the sources independently give the *test-percentage* deficit as 0.19 points on a
recorded average of 3.61 (Hazel, r17; Falke, r22), which is 0.19/3.80 ≈ **5.0% short** — and this
is corroborated by the Association's own bookkeeping "station gain" of 5¼%, entered every year
(Kyle, r23), since 3.61 × 1.0525 ≈ 3.80. These two proportions (7.6% vs. ~5.0–5.25%) do **not**
match, and the sources give no way to reconcile them — I flag this as an unresolved internal
inconsistency rather than force a false precision. I take **5.0–5.25%, corroborated twice
independently** (the repeated "nineteen hundredths" claim and Kyle's separate ledger figure), as
the better-supported number for parts (b)–(d) below, while noting the raw glass calculation is
the larger 7.6%.

*(b) True average test and shortfall in points:* Recorded average 3.61%. True average **3.80%**,
derived two ways that agree: 3.61 + 0.19 = 3.80, and 3.61 × 1.0525 = 3.80. Shortfall: **0.19
points**.

*(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited:* Kyle (r23) gives total milk received over "the long run
of years that the patrons afterward complained of" as 40,000,000 lb (1,600,000 lb/year for 25
years). At the recorded 3.61%: credited = 1,444,000 lb. At the true 3.80%: actual = 1,520,000 lb.
Difference: **76,000 lb of butterfat not credited** (cross-checked via the 5.25% station-gain
figure: 1,444,000 × 0.0525 ≈ 75,810 lb — consistent).

*(d) Value and fraction received:* At Kyle's stated whole-period average price of $0.25/lb,
76,000 lb ≈ **$19,000** — close to, though not identical to, the college bulletin's independently
stated figure of $19,600 (r19), a reasonable match given rounding and differing assumptions. The
patrons actually received the 1925 arbitration award of **$3,800**, "without admission." Fraction
received: 3,800 / 19,000 ≈ **20%** (or 3,800/19,600 ≈ 19%, using the bulletin's own total) — the
patrons got roughly one-fifth of what they were actually owed.

**C2. Largest patron, 1910 (Emmet Brant, per Hazel's weigh-book leaf, r17): 200,000 lb milk,
recorded test 3.42%.**

(a) Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb** butterfat.
(b) True test, applying the same 0.19-point deficit: 3.42 + 0.19 = 3.61%; true butterfat =
200,000 × 0.0361 = 7,220 lb. Owed but not credited: 7,220 − 6,840 = **380 lb**.
(c) Value at $0.25/lb: 380 × 0.25 = **$95** (using the only price figure the sources give, a
whole-period average, not an 1910-specific price — flagged as an approximation).

**C3.**

(a) Credited to patrons in total: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**.
(b) Actually reached the creamery in the station's cream: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lb**.
(c) Percentage difference: this is exactly what the Association's own ledger recorded every
year as "station gain," stated by Kyle (r23) as **5¼%**, taken as a percentage of the butterfat
*credited to the patrons* — i.e. (received − credited) / credited. This is the one figure in the
whole record that is both explicit in a primary bookkeeping source and consistent with the
independently derived 3.61%/3.80% averages.

**C4.**

(a) The invoice's own line items (r20): 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = **$77.93**. Note
that both r19 and r20's prose state the total as "seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents"
($77.39) — this is a repeated arithmetic error (see Section E); the verified total, by summing
the invoice's own reproduced figures, is $77.93.

(b) The invoice bought **6 dozen (72) pipettes**, marked 17.6 c.c., each delivering on average
16.27 c.c. when measured in 1958 — short by **1.33 c.c. each**.

**C5.**

(a) The faulty glass was in use from 1 May 1897 (invoice dated 14 April 1897; diary confirms
delivery 1 May 1897, r24) until it was condemned in 1923, with new glass in service by July 1923
— roughly **26 years**, matching the Chronicle's own arithmetic ("the twenty-six years before,"
r19). The *money* calculation, however, runs over **25 full delivery years** (40,000,000 lb ÷
1,600,000 lb/year, per Kyle, r23). These differ because the station's very first season was never
recorded as a separate "station account" — its milk was carried on the main creamery's own books
(r23) — so it is excluded from the 25-year butterfat/money tally even though the defective glass
was already physically in use for part of it.

(b) Pounds of milk received in the first season: "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds"
(1,100,000 lb) — Kyle's own recollection, explicitly not drawn from any surviving book (r23).

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran from 8 May 1911 (the station's own measure broke, per Orra Keddie's
diary, r24) to 10 July 1911 (r22: "nine weeks"), during which a borrowed measure was used.
Variable: which glass measured the milk. Held constant: the same tester (Ansel Keddie), the same
herds, the same season, the same procedure. Result: tests rose markedly for the nine weeks, then
fell back to the old low level the day the station's own glass resumed (r22). This is a natural
controlled experiment isolating the *instrument* as the cause, something a calculation alone
could not do — it directly rules out the tester, the cows, and the season as causes, since only
the glass changed. What it does *not* establish: the exact size of the deficit system-wide, which
glass specifically was at fault, or how much it was off by — that required the direct 1958
burette measurement of the pipettes.

**D2.** Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream:
1. The 1911 nine-week interval: the *same* tester's readings rose the moment a different measure
   was substituted and fell back when his own glass returned (r22, corroborated by r24's diary) —
   inconsistent with the tester himself being the cause.
2. The state inspector's lactometer tests, run at every visit from 1908 onward, never once found
   added water or any sign of tampering (r22, para. 7–8) — an independent regulatory check, not
   family or valley testimony.
3. Ansel Keddie tested his own wife's brother's milk — his largest, most exposed patron — for
   decades at the same rate as everyone else's, and recorded only a cautious question mark
   against his 1911 suspicion rather than an unproven accusation (r18, r24) — personal conduct
   testimony, independent of the other two.
These are independent because they rest on three different kinds of evidence: an operational
test, an outside regulator's data, and personal/family testimony. Reasons 1 and 2 would still
hold even if the glass had never been measured in 1958, since both were already part of the
record used at the 1925 hearing itself (r22 states all three theories "were argued before the
board of arbitration in 1925").
Did the bookkeeper ever question the ledger entries that refute the theft accusation? No — per
Bertram Kyle (r23), Wilbur Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years," and Kyle himself
copied the "station gain... as usual" line for decades without it "once cross[ing] my mind to
wonder at it."

**D3.** Yes, the manager (Selby Vose, from 1911) knew the tests ran low before the glass was
condemned in 1923. Evidence: he personally circulated the 1919 warning letter accusing patrons of
watering their milk (r18, r19, r23, r24) — which presupposes he already believed the tests were
running low, since there would otherwise be nothing to warn about. He also signed the "station
gain... as usual" ledger line every year of his tenure (r23). However, per Kyle, Vose "read the
totals and he never read the lines above them," so while he plainly knew the tests ran low (this
was decades-long, valley-wide common knowledge, which his own letter responds to), there is no
evidence he ever examined the bookkeeping closely enough to diagnose why, or to notice that the
pattern was inconsistent with either watering or theft.

## Section E — Contradictions

*Two narrators sharing the same wrong value (called out separately, as instructed):*

1. **"Great-niece."** Both the newspaper (r19) and the county report's own initial text (r22,
   para. 2) call Ivy Keddie Ansel's "great-niece." The tie is broken by r22's own appended
   correction — "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not
   the great-niece" — and independently by Duncan Keddie's family notes (r18), which state the
   grandfather-granddaughter relationship plainly and note this exact error had already been
   printed twice. Both errors likely trace to the same upstream source (the college bulletin's
   release copy), so they are not fully independent confirmations of each other, which is part of
   why the correction outweighs them.

2. **"Seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents."** Both the newspaper (r19) and the glass
   firm's own history (r20) state the 1897 invoice total in prose as $77.39. Arithmetic breaks
   the tie: r20 itself reproduces the invoice's line items (25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15),
   which sum to $77.93, not $77.39. The correct total is verified by simple addition of the
   primary document's own figures.

*A narrator contradicting itself on the page (called out separately, as instructed):*

3. **r22 (Falke's county report):** calls Ivy Keddie the "great-niece" in paragraph 2, then
   corrects itself in the appended note to "granddaughter" — self-contradiction, resolved by the
   author's own explicit retraction.
4. **r22:** credits the wet-hay theory as a "real if partial factor" in paragraph 9, then, upon
   obtaining the actual 1911 feeding-trial figures, writes "I withdraw the concession made on page
   four" — an author-flagged self-correction rather than a raw contradiction.
5. **r20 (Ammon & Sons' own history):** states "The house sold nothing into Ordell County before
   1900," then two paragraphs later reproduces its own 1897 invoice to the Larrow Valley
   Cooperative (the Ostrey Hollow station, in Ordell County) — a direct, unflagged
   self-contradiction within the same document.
6. **r18 (Duncan Keddie's notes):** states Orra was "born in eighteen sixty-eight," then later

## Section F — Theory

**What actually happened:** A lot of milk-test pipettes bought new from Ammon & Sons in 1897,
blown to deliver 17.6 c.c. but (per the firm's own historian, likely from a reground mold) short
by a fixed margin, entered service at Ostrey Hollow on 1 May 1897. Because the Babcock
percentage reading assumes the full marked charge is delivered, every test made with that glass
read low by the same fixed proportion, regardless of season, herd, or feed — which is exactly why
the deficit was flat year-round ("nineteen hundredths... flat as a floor," r17) rather than
tracking summer/winter feed changes the way a genuine pasture-related cause would. It began the
moment the new glass replaced the previously-borrowed measure (1897) and ended the moment the
state inspector's routine 1923 calibration check condemned that specific lot and it was replaced
— nothing else needed to change, because the sole cause was a fixed physical property of glass
that had nothing to do with the cows, the tester, or the hay.

**The three competing explanations, who advanced them, and what defeats each:**
1. *Watering*, advanced by manager Selby Vose (1919 circular). Defeated by the state inspector's
   lactometer tests, run at every visit from 1908 on, which never once found added water (r22).
2. *Tester theft/false reporting*, urged by an Association officer at the 1925 hearing. Defeated
   by the 1911 nine-week borrowed-glass interval, in which the same tester's readings rose and
   fell with the equipment, not with him (r22, r24).
3. *Wet Hessel Bottom hay*, advanced by an Association committee (1908) and a veterinarian (1926).
   Defeated by the college's own 1911 feeding-trial figures (Bulletin No. 214), showing bog hay
   depresses the test by at most 0.05 points in winter and nothing in summer — far too small to
   explain a 0.19-point, year-round deficit (r22's appended correction).

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?** No. The glass had already been condemned
and crated in 1923, and sat forgotten in the loft directly above the room where two of the three
1925 hearing sessions were held (r18, r21). The documents Kyle (r23) lists as actually presented
to the board — the station's test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book, and the
inspector's notebook — do not include the glassware itself or any measurement of it.

**What did the tester himself believe?** Ansel Keddie suspected "something in the glass" as early
as 1911, after the borrowed-measure interval, but never asserted it without proof — he wrote only
a question mark in his book and kept testing faithfully with the same glass for fourteen more
years, "a man who would not put down what he could not prove" (r18).

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

For twenty-nine years, the Ostrey Hollow skimming station's milk tests ran mysteriously low,
costing its farmers roughly $19,000 in unpaid butterfat. The valley blamed the patrons for
watering their milk, then blamed the tester, Ansel Keddie, for stealing cream — dismissing him in
1925 with a small, no-fault award to the farmers and no vindication for him. The real cause was
mundane: a defective 1897 batch of test glassware that measured milk short, so every reading came
out proportionally low no matter the season, herd, or man. The state condemned that same glass in
1923, but nobody connected the two events until 1958, when Keddie's granddaughter found the
discarded crate in a forgotten loft and measured it herself. It took eight separate accounts —
family memory, genealogy notes, a newspaper, a supplier's own history, an oral history, a formal
report, a clerk's ledger memory, and a diary — because no single record-keeper had ever held both
halves of the story at once.
