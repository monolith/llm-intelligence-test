# Answers, Part 1 — Section A, Stories 1–4

*Method note: Where sources conflict I generally weight (in descending order) a quoted primary
document (invoice, minute book, dated field notebook, diary entry written at the time) over a
first-hand participant's later memory, over a family member's second-hand memory, over valley
rumor repeated by an outsider. Two retellings that plausibly share a single origin (a press
release, a published pamphlet copied by a later writer, a common oral tradition) are treated as
one source, not two, per the instructions. I flag every place this method is doing real work.*

---

## Story 1 — The Association and the rule that made the difference matter (1889–1896)

Farmers of the Larrow Valley organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association in
1889 at a meeting in the Larrow Green schoolhouse (r02, r12, r22). Gideon Vose was elected first
president and wrote the by-laws himself; he served about 22 years and died in 1917 (r02). The
main creamery was built at Larrow Green in 1891 (r02, r12). The Association's minute book for
1889–1892 was lost in a later office move (r02, r22), so the exact date the by-laws were adopted
cannot be fixed: Loomis (r02) and Rundle (r12) place Article VII at the 1891–1892 annual meeting,
Grigg (r15) says "the 1893 meeting," and the county agent Falke (r22), working from the printed
pamphlet itself, reports that the pamphlet carries no date at all and declines to guess — this
last is the most reliable statement of what is actually recoverable, so I record the adoption
year as **not determinable**, sometime 1891–1893.

What is not in dispute, because the by-laws are quoted verbatim in three independent places
(r02, r07 Item 1, r22), is the rule itself: Article VII required every patron to be paid
according to the *pounds of butterfat his milk tested*, not the weight of his milk, with the
test made at his own delivery station governing his pay. This single clause is why a mechanical
testing error at one station could become a twenty-five-year grievance and a lawsuit-sized sum of
money rather than a shrug: under payment-by-weight, nothing that follows in this history would
have cost anyone a cent (Falke, r22, makes this point explicitly).

In the winter of 1895–1896 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles
up-valley, to serve the Hessel Bottom farms; it opened on 4 May 1896 (r03, r22), with Ansel
Keddie as tester and separator man from the first day. Documents bearing on this story: r02, r03
(first leaf), r07 (Item 1), r12, r15, r22.

## Story 2 — The testing outfit and its hidden flaw (1896–1897)

For its first year the station had no glassware of its own. Hobart Sill, the Larrow Green
buttermaker, made up a borrowed testing outfit "out of my own glass" in May 1896 (r14), a fact
corroborated by Keddie's own book, whose first leaf reads: "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until
our own comes" (r03). The station's own outfit was ordered from Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of
Tarnet. The firm's own invoice, dated 14 April 1897 and reproduced in facsimile by two
independent holders of the document (the Historical Rooms curator, r07 Item 2, and the firm's own
descendant historian, r20), itemizes: six dozen milk-test pipettes at 17.6 c.c., $25.20; twelve
dozen test bottles, $31.68; one acid measure, $0.90; one hand tester, $18.00; freight, $2.15;
**total $77.93**. The outfit went into use at the station on 1 May 1897 (r03, r07, r20); the
lone claims that it began in 1898 (r11, r23) are single, later, non-documentary recollections
against a contemporaneous invoice plus Keddie's own book, and are rejected.

The six dozen pipettes were, unknown to anyone, undersized: when nine survivors were finally
tested against a calibrated burette in 1958, they delivered a mean of 16.72 c.c. against their
17.6 c.c. mark — about 95% of the true charge (r04, r07 Item 14). Junia Ammon, the supply house's
own historian, later offered a candid guess at the cause: a grinding mold that came back from the
shop "a hair different" and passed every spot check because a proportional error is, by its
nature, undetectable by comparing pieces within the same bad lot (r20). This is the seed of
everything that follows. Documents bearing on this story: r03, r07 (Items 2, 14), r14, r20.

## Story 3 — A quarter-century of blame (1902–1919)

From about 1902 the Hessel Bottom patrons complained that their milk tested lower than the rest
of the valley's (r02, r09, r15, r24 all independently place the first complaint in 1902). Three
rival explanations arose and persisted for a generation. First, that the wet, sour hay of Hessel
Bottom itself depressed the cows' butterfat — the theory of a three-farmer committee chaired by
Nyle Grigg, which sampled the six Hessel Bottom farms in January–March 1908 and reported "no
dishonesty of any kind" but blamed the land (r03, r07 Item 11, r15's own reprinted text). Second,
that the tester, Ansel Keddie, was skimming cream for himself before it reached the scale — the
valley's "settled opinion" per Dell Rundle (r12) and implied at the 1925 hearing by bookkeeper
Wilbur Teague (r05, r13). Third, that the patrons were adding water to their milk — the theory
advanced by manager Selby Vose, who in 1919 circulated a written warning to the Hessel Bottom
families (r01, r05, r08, r14, r18, r22, r24). The exact number of families reached is contested:
Vira Toland (r01, oral memory, 1979) and Hazel Brant (r17, oral memory, 1981) both say nine; but
Orra Keddie's diary (r24), written the same week the letters were hand-delivered by her own
husband, says he "took them round himself, to eleven families." I weight the contemporaneous
diary above two later oral accounts that may share a single family tradition, and record eleven
as the better-supported figure, while noting the sources do not fully agree.

The circular's chief lasting effect was social: Ansel Keddie's wife Orra was Emmet Brant's
sister, Emmet was the valley's largest patron, and the two men — brothers-in-law of thirty-odd
years — stopped speaking after 1919 and did not reconcile before Emmet's death in 1929 (r09, r18,
r24). Documents bearing on this story: r01, r03, r05, r08, r09, r14, r15, r17, r18, r24.

## Story 4 — The nine weeks of 1911

On 8 May 1911 the station's own measuring glass broke; a borrowed measure was used until the
station's own (faulty) glass returned to service on 10 July — a gap of nine weeks, dated
explicitly in Keddie's own book, in Roy Brant's family weigh book, and in his son Duncan's direct
memory of the household that summer (r03, r04, r09, r18, r24). During those nine weeks the
station's recorded average rose from about 3.61 to about 3.80; the instant the station's own
measure came back into use, the figure fell back (r03, quoting Keddie's own entries verbatim:
"Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."). Two narrators give a shorter interval of "six
weeks" — Wilbur Teague (r05), who was defending the theft theory at the time he wrote, and Dr.
Kepp (r16), who was explicitly trying to dismiss the interval as unrepresentative flush-season
data — and neither cites specific dates, whereas the nine-week tellers all cite 8 May and 10
July, which arithmetically is nine weeks and a few days. I resolve this to nine weeks.

Nobody in 1911 understood what had happened; Keddie himself set only a question mark in his book
(r03), and his son later recalled him privately blaming "something in the glass" that same
summer, though he never wrote it down because he would not record what he could not prove (r18).
The episode functioned, in hindsight, as an accidental natural experiment: the same tester, the
same herds, the same season, only the measuring instrument changed, and the reading changed with
it. Documents bearing on this story: r03, r04, r05, r09, r16, r18, r22, r24.


# Answers, Part 2 — Section A, Stories 5–8, and Section B

## Story 5 — The glass is condemned (1922–1924)

State dairy inspector Merle Strawn rode the Ordell circuit from 1907 to 1936 and visited Ostrey
Hollow every year, testing for added water with a lactometer and, from 1921, checking glassware
against state-issued burettes. His own circuit notebook — reproduced in photostat by the county
curator (r07 Item 9) and quoted directly by Strawn's own 1936 retrospective (r06) — records that
on **12 June 1923** he "ran their milk pipettes against the burette. These measures will not
deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order
new." This date is corroborated by the Association's own clerk, Bertram Kyle, who personally
entered the replacement order that week (r23, though Kyle elsewhere misdates the *award* to
1924, an isolated error). Two later, secondary accounts — manager Selby Vose's own 1926 annual
address (r08) and Dell Rundle's 1939 pamphlet (r12) — instead give "the twelfth of July, 1923."
Since Rundle's pamphlet post-dates and could easily draw on Vose's published address, these two
are treated as one source, not two, and are outweighed by Strawn's contemporaneous, dated field
notebook.

The Association wrote to Ammon & Sons on 28 June 1923; the firm answered on 2 July, entering an
order for new pipettes and bottles and stating candidly that its own 1890s records "would not
tell anybody anything about the lot" in question (r07 Item 10, r20 — the firm's own letter book).
New glass, correctly calibrated, was in service within the month (r06, r23). From 1924 the
station's recorded average rose; Strawn's own notebook gives it as "3.80" that year (r06), while
Vose's later address claims "3.90" — I prefer Strawn's contemporaneous entry over Vose's
retrospective claim, since Vose had reason to inflate the number in support of his own
water-theory narrative. No one, at the time, connected the condemned glass to the twenty-six
years of complaint behind it (r06, r19, r22). Documents bearing on this story: r06, r07 (Items
9–10), r08, r12, r20, r23.

## Story 6 — Petition, hearing, and dismissal (January–May 1925)

The Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned the Association in January 1925 (r02, r22). A three-member
board of arbitration, chaired by Ashlin attorney Alonzo Frick, sat from 17 March to 4 May 1925 —
first at the Cooperative's Ashlin office, then, at Frick's own initiative, twice more at the
station itself so the board could see the scales and testing done in place (r13, corroborated by
eyewitnesses Cleve Oram, r21, and Bertram Kyle, r23, and by Orra Keddie's diary, r24, which notes
the sittings held "up here at the station itself, in the weigh room"). Ansel Keddie gave a
statement on 17 March denying he had ever taken cream and stating he had tested "thirty-one times
a week for twenty-nine seasons" (r03, corroborated in Frick's own recollection, r13). Frick,
the chairman, later wrote that he privately believed Keddie (r13). The board heard all three
rival theories — water, theft, and wet hay — and found "no cause established" (r02, r07 Item 7,
r13, r22, all quoting or paraphrasing the same award document).

Keddie was dismissed on **30 April 1925** — a date given independently by Keddie's own
statement/unsent letter (r03), by Frick (r13), by Keddie's son Duncan (r18), and by Orra's diary
entry for that day (r24): four independent sources agree. The award followed four days later, on
4 May 1925: **$3,800**, "without admission" of cause, charged to the station account (r02, r07
Item 7, r08, r13, r19, r22 — a quoted primary document repeated across independent holders). Two
oral narrators decades later, Vira Toland (r01) and Cleve Oram (r21), instead recall "thirty-five
hundred dollars"; both are unsourced valley memories forty-plus years after the fact and are
outweighed by the quoted minute-book language itself. Documents bearing on this story: r02, r03,
r07 (Items 7–8), r08, r12, r13, r19, r21, r23, r24.

## Story 7 — The loft, the ladder, and Bulletin No. 471 (1958–1959)

In February 1958 Ivy Keddie, Ansel's granddaughter and a graduate student in dairy husbandry at
Farland, found the station's condemned crate of glassware still sitting under a tarpaulin in the
loft over the old intake room at Ostrey Hollow itself (r04, her own first-hand account; the same
location is independently confirmed by Hazel Brant, r17; by Duncan Keddie, r18; by Cleve Oram,
r21, who says he had personally handled the crate as a boy; and by county agent Falke, r22). This
outweighs a single conflicting provenance note by the later curator Halbert Nym (r07 Item 5),
which places the discovery in the Ashlin office — almost certainly a cataloguing error, since
Nym's own Item 3/Item 4 headings are independently shown to be swapped (see Section E).

Nine pipettes survived intact. On 9 March 1958 Ivy Keddie measured them against a calibrated
burette at Farland: each was blown to a 17.6 c.c. mark; the mean actual delivery was 16.72 c.c.,
about 95% of the mark (r04, her own signed measurement sheet, reproduced in r07 Item 14). Working
from the Association's own ledgers — 40,000,000 lb of milk received at the station over 25
seasons (1898–1922) — she calculated that patrons had been credited for 1,444,000 lb of butterfat
at the recorded 3.61% average but had actually delivered about 1,520,000 lb at the true 3.80%
rate: a shortfall of 76,000 lb, worth about $19,000 at the era's average price of $0.25/lb,
against a $3,800 award — "a fifth of it" (r04). Bulletin No. 471, published October 1958 under
Ivy Keddie's name and Professor Leland Vaught's, announced: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow
and no cream was stolen there" (quoted identically in r04 and r07 Item 15). Vaught's own separate
introductory draft (r11) contains an internally inconsistent alternate set of figures (16.27 c.c.,
118,000 lb) that even its own reprinted conclusion contradicts; I treat the 16.72 c.c./76,000 lb
figures, appearing consistently in Ivy Keddie's own primary measurement record and in the
bulletin's own final conclusion, as authoritative. Documents bearing on this story: r04, r06, r07
(Items 5, 9, 14–15), r11, r19, r22.

## Story 8 — The family that carried it (1889–1981)

Ansel Keddie (1861–1934) married Orra Brant (b. 1866 or 1868, accounts differ) in 1889; Orra was
a sister of Emmet Brant, the valley's largest patron (r09, r18, r24). Their son Duncan (b. 1894)
became a schoolteacher and later wrote the family's own account of its kinship lines expressly to
correct errors then circulating in print (r18). Duncan and Emmet's son Roy Brant were first
cousins; Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy (b. 1931) are second cousins — a relation
both Hazel (r17) and Duncan (r18) state in identical terms, independently rejecting looser
popular descriptions ("niece," "cousin once removed"). Selby Vose, the Association's manager from
1911 or 1913, was Gideon Vose's son, not his nephew, by his own statement (r08) and by explicit
correction from both Duncan (r18) and clerk Bertram Kyle (r23); Duncan further denies any blood
relationship at all between the Keddie and Vose families, calling the persistent valley rumor of
a Vose–Brant cousinship "talk," though Cleve Oram (r21) still repeats an unverified version of it
decades later.

Ansel Keddie was dismissed in 1925 and spent his last nine years doing carpentry, never
publicly cleared, dying in 1934 still under the valley's suspicion (r03, r18, r21, r24). Orra
outlived him, dying in 1948 without seeing him cleared (r18, r24). It fell to their granddaughter
Ivy — born three years after Orra's death would have been impossible, so born while Orra was
still alive, in 1931 — to climb the ladder in 1958 and finish, with a burette, what four decades
of hearings, ledgers, and family estrangement could not. Documents bearing on this story: r09,
r17, r18, r21, r22, r24.

# Section B — Relationships

**B1.** Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's **granddaughter**, not his great-niece. This is stated
plainly by her own father, Duncan Keddie, Ansel's son ("my daughter is Ivy... which makes her my
father's granddaughter," r18) — about as authoritative a source as exists for the fact — and is
independently confirmed by county agent Falke's published erratum ("I am informed, and have
verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece," r22). The "great-niece"
error appears in two places, the Ashlin Chronicle (r19) and Falke's own first draft (r22, before
correction) — almost certainly the same misunderstanding propagating from a single hurried press
account, not two independent findings. Vira Toland's vague description of Ivy as "Hazel Brant's
first cousin once removed" (r01) does not address the Ansel relationship directly and is in any
case shown wrong by Duncan and Hazel's own agreement that Ivy and Hazel are second cousins.

**B2.** Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant are **second cousins** — the children of first cousins (Duncan
Keddie and Roy Brant, themselves the children of siblings Orra Keddie and Emmet Brant). Both
women state this in identical, deliberate terms in their own words (r17, r18), each explicitly
rejecting alternative labels ("aunt and niece," "cousin once removed") that circulated locally.
Cleve Oram's "first cousins once removed" (r21) is a single outside source and is rejected. What
the sources leave open is whether Orra and Emmet were full or half siblings: Roy Brant calls Orra
Emmet's "full sister (same parents)" (r09), while his own daughter Hazel says Orra was "a half
sister to my grandfather Emmet, by his father's second wife" (r17) — a direct conflict between a
son's and a granddaughter's account that the sources do not let me settle; either way it does not
change the second-cousin label the two women themselves use.

**B3.** Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**, the Association's founding first president. This
is established by Vose's own words ("my father," r08) and confirmed independently by Duncan
Keddie (r18) and by clerk Bertram Kyle, who worked outside Vose's office door for a quarter
century ("the son, whatever is said at Ashlin... nonsense," r23) — three independent sources
against two secondary claims of "nephew" (r01, r12) that are best explained as the same valley
rumor recurring. It matters because it makes Selby Vose an Association insider by birth, with an
inherited institutional stake in the cooperative's good name; this likely explains why, per
bookkeeper Wilbur Teague, Vose knew of the station's suspicious ledger discrepancy from at least
1916 yet told Teague to "let it alone" (r05) rather than investigate, and why he preferred and
publicly pushed the water-theft theory, which blamed the patrons rather than the Association's
own equipment or oversight.

**B4.** The sources indicate **no real kinship** between the Vose and Keddie families. Duncan
Keddie addresses this directly and at length, stating "there was no connection whatever between
us and the Voses," and explicitly refuting a story (which he says two women in town repeated,
one insisting on "first cousins") that his mother Orra and Gideon Vose's wife were related — he
notes his mother, a habitual genealogist of the valley, "never once put a Vose in it" (r18). This
is the only source to address the question directly and carefully; Cleve Oram's much vaguer,
undocumented claim that "the Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort" (r21) is exactly
the kind of secondhand valley talk Duncan is trying to correct, and I weight Duncan's explicit,
reasoned denial above it.


# Answers, Part 3 — Sections C–G

## Section C — Math (show working)

**C1.**

(a) The nine surviving pipettes were blown to deliver 17.6 c.c. but actually delivered a mean of
16.72 c.c. (r04, r07 Item 14). Ratio = 16.72 / 17.6 = **0.95**, i.e. the glass delivered only 95%
of the intended charge, so every test read low by the same proportion: about **5% short** (of the
true/delivered amount).

(b) The station's own book gives its recorded long-run average as **3.61%** (r03's 1922 summary
leaf, matching r02, r04, r07). Since the shortfall is proportional: true average = recorded ÷ 0.95
= 3.61 / 0.95 = **3.80%**. Shortfall = 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 points**. (This 0.19-point figure is
independently corroborated by Falke's 1959 report, r22, and by Hazel Brant's own recollection of
"nineteen hundredths... flat as a floor," r17 — three independent routes to the same number.)

(c) Using the ledger-derived intake of 40,000,000 lb over the 25 full delivery years 1898–1922
(r02, r04, r07 Item 6, r23 — see C5 for why this span, not the full physical run of the glass, is
used):
 - Credited at recorded 3.61%: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb.
 - True at 3.80%: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb.
 - Shortfall = 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = **76,000 lb** of butterfat (matches r04 and Bulletin 471's
own quoted conclusion, r07 Item 15).

(d) At the era's average price of $0.25/lb (r02, r09): 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000**. The patrons
actually received the $3,800 arbitration award. $3,800 / $19,000 = **1/5 (20%)** — matching Ivy
Keddie's own remark that the award was "a fifth of it" (r04). (Two other documents give a very
close but not identical figure, $19,600 — see Section E, since 76,000 × 0.25 is exactly $19,000
and does not itself produce $19,600.)

**C2.** The largest patron was Emmet Brant. His own weigh book's 1910 leaf, quoted identically by
three independent holders (r04, r07 Item 4, r09, and recalled again by his granddaughter Hazel in
r17), reads: 200,000 lb delivered, "paid on a test of three forty-two" (3.42%).

(a) Credited butterfat = 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb**.

(b) Applying the station's general 0.19-point shortfall (from C1b) to reconstruct what his true
test should have been: 3.42 + 0.19 = 3.61% true. Owed-but-uncredited = 200,000 × 0.0019 = **380
lb**. (This is an inference: the sources do not give a separately measured "true" test for 1910
specifically, only the station's overall long-run deficiency, which I apply here as the best
available estimate.)

(c) At the price Roy Brant gives specifically for 1910, 24 cents/lb (r09): 380 × $0.24 =
**$91.20**.

**C3.** Over the same 25 full delivery years (1898–1922), 40,000,000 lb of milk received:

(a) Credited to patrons at the recorded 3.61% average: **1,444,000 lb** of butterfat.

(b) Actually reached Larrow Green in the station's cream: **≈1,520,000 lb**. This follows two
independent routes that agree: (i) the true-test calculation in C1c, and (ii) the Association's
own "station gain" ledger convention, illustrated by the 1898 sample entry quoted identically by
three sources (r05, r07 Item 6, r23): 57,760 lb credited, 60,800 lb received, gain 3,040 lb,
"five and a quarter per cent, as usual." Extending that same 5.25%-of-credited ratio across the
25-year credited total: 1,444,000 × 1.0525 ≈ 1,519,810 lb, matching the ~1,520,000 lb figure
almost exactly.

(c) The difference (76,000 lb) is **5.25% of the credited amount** (76,000 / 1,444,000 = 5.26%,
the "station gain" figure the bookkeeper wrote down every year) but only **5.00% of the amount
actually delivered** (76,000 / 1,520,000 = 5.00%, the "one part in twenty" language of Bulletin
471's own conclusion, r07 Item 15). Both percentages are correct; they differ only in denominator
— crediting the same absolute shortfall against two different bases — and this exact ambiguity is
what tripped up Vaught's own draft (r11), which conflated the two.

**C4.** (a) The Ammon & Sons invoice of 14 April 1897 totals **$77.93** (25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 +
18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93), reproduced identically by the curator (r07 Item 2) and by the supply
house's own historian (r20). Junia Ammon's prose in r20 misstates this as "seventy-seven dollars
and thirty-nine cents" directly above her own reproduction of the correct $77.93 total — an
internal, one-document error I resolve in favor of the quoted invoice figure.

(b) That invoice bought **six dozen (72) pipettes**, each marked 17.6 c.c. Measured in 1958, they
delivered a mean of 16.72 c.c. — short by **0.88 c.c. each** (17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88).

**C5.** (a) The faulty glass was physically **in use for about 26 years**: from 1 May 1897 (r03,
r07, r20) until condemned 12 June 1923 (r06, r07 Item 9). The money calculation, however, runs
over **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922** (r02, r04, r07 Item 6). These differ because 1897 and
1923 were both partial seasons for the glass — it entered service partway through the 1897 season
and was pulled partway through the 1923 season — so the ledger-based reconstruction, which needs
whole-season totals, uses only the intervening years in which the bad glass was in service for a
complete season. The 26-year span is the instrument's service life; the 25-year span is the
window of complete, countable seasons within it.

(b) The station's first-season (1896) intake **cannot be determined from the sources**. Two
individuals give numeric guesses decades later — Hobart Sill, "about nine hundred thousand
pounds" (r14), and clerk Bertram Kyle, "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" (r23) — but both
explicitly source this to personal recollection rather than any book, and the Historical Rooms'
own curator states flatly that the 1896 season's intake "is not in the ledgers and is nowhere
else... there is none" (r07). Two irreconcilable oral guesses plus an archivist's explicit denial
that a record exists is not enough to settle a number; I decline to pick one.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran from 8 May to 10 July 1911 — nine weeks (dated identically in Keddie's
own test book, r03; Roy Brant's family weigh book, r09; Duncan Keddie's personal memory, r18; and
Falke's 1959 report, r22). The variable was the measuring instrument (the station's own glass vs.
a borrowed one); held constant were the tester, the herds, the patrons, the season, and the
procedure. The result: recorded tests rose from ~3.61 to ~3.80 for the duration, then fell back
the day the station's own glass returned. This establishes something a bench measurement alone
cannot: it is a real-time, in-the-field demonstration, recorded decades before anyone suspected
the glass, that the cause traveled with the physical instrument and not with the tester, the
patrons, or the land — because everything else was held constant while only the instrument
changed, and the effect changed with it. What it does *not* establish is the size or precise
mechanism of the error (it does not by itself say "5%" or "16.72 c.c.," and it does not identify
which specific measure was at fault) — that quantification came only from the 1958 burette
measurement.

**D2.** Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream: (1) the 1911
natural experiment itself (Keddie's own book, corroborated by Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie) — the
same man, with a different instrument, produced different, higher readings, meaning his own
conduct was not the variable; (2) state inspector Merle Strawn's testimony that he tested
specifically for added water and for the condition of the station at every visit from 1908 on and
"never found any" evidence of wrongdoing (r06), and who, per Duncan Keddie, was "kin to nobody in
the valley whatever" and so had no stake in the outcome (r18); (3) the completely independent
Alder Corners cheese-factory records (r10), showing the same Hessel Bottom farmers' milk, tested
on different equipment by a different person at a different business, running at a normal-to-high
3.78–3.82% throughout the same years the station's own figures were low. These are independent
because they come from four structurally unconnected places — the tester's own family record, a
disinterested state office, an unrelated private business, and (a fourth, related point) the
Association's own bookkeeping — none of which could have coordinated with the others. The reason
that would still hold even if the 1958 glassware had never been measured is (2) and (3): neither
Strawn's water-testing nor the Alder Corners comparison depends in any way on the pipette
explanation being correct — they independently rule out water and rule out a milk-quality problem
regardless of what the ultimate cause turned out to be. As for whether the bookkeeper ever
questioned the ledger entries that undercut his own accusation: no. Wilbur Teague invented and
kept the "station gain" ledger line himself, showing every year that *more* cream reached the
creamery than was credited to patrons — the opposite of what theft would produce — yet he still
implied cream theft at the 1925 hearing (r05, r13), and clerk Bertram Kyle, who copied those same
lines for forty years, states plainly that Teague "never asked anybody anything in forty years"
(r23). No source shows him ever reconciling the contradiction.

**D3.** Yes. Per bookkeeper Wilbur Teague's own memoir, manager Selby Vose "knew about the
discrepancy since 1916" and told Teague to "let it alone" (r05) — a direct first-hand claim by
the man who sat beside him and wrote the very ledger lines Vose signed every year. This is not
merely inferred from Vose's signature (clerk Bertram Kyle notes Vose habitually read only the
bottom-line total and "never read the lines above them," r23, which on its own would not prove
attention) — it is a specific, named claim of an actual 1916 conversation. Despite this, Vose
continued to advance and defend the water-theft theory publicly in 1919 and again in his 1926
annual address (r08).

## Section F — Theory

The station's testing outfit, bought new from Ammon & Sons in spring 1897, contained pipettes
that were manufactured to look correct (blown to a 17.6 c.c. mark) but that actually delivered
only about 16.72 c.c. — roughly 95% of the intended charge — very possibly, per the supply
house's own later account, because of a mold that had been reground and quietly ran short (r20).
Because Ansel Keddie charged every test bottle to this short mark rather than to a true 17.6 c.c.,
every single test he made from May 1897 onward received proportionally less milk than it should
have, and so read proportionally low — by a fixed ~5%, i.e. about 0.19 points off a normal ~3.80%
average — no matter what was actually in the milk. This explains the size of the error (fixed and
proportional, matching exactly what the 1958 burette measurement found), why it began precisely
when the new glass replaced the borrowed Larrow Green glass in spring 1897, and why it ended
precisely when the state inspector's routine burette check condemned the lot in June 1923 and
correctly calibrated glass replaced it. It required no change on any farm or in any person's
behavior to start or stop, because the defect lived entirely inside the measuring instrument.

Three competing explanations were offered, and each is defeated by specific evidence: (1)
**watered milk**, advanced by manager Selby Vose (1919 circular, 1926 address) — defeated by
inspector Strawn's repeated lactometer tests for water at every visit from 1908, which never
found any (r06), and by the 1911 natural experiment, in which readings changed with no change in
the patrons' milk. (2) **stolen cream**, the valley's "settled opinion" and implied by bookkeeper
Wilbur Teague at the 1925 hearing (r05, r12, r13) — defeated by the Association's own ledger
entries (Teague's own bookkeeping) showing cream consistently arriving *heavier* than credited,
the opposite of what theft would produce, and again by the 1911 experiment. (3) **wet Hessel
Bottom hay**, the 1908 Grigg committee's and Dr. Kepp's theory (r15, r16) — defeated by Bulletin
No. 214's own correctly-quoted finding that bog hay depresses fat by at most 0.05 in winter and
nothing in summer, far too small and the wrong shape to explain a flat, year-round 0.19-point
gap; by the 1911 experiment; and by the Alder Corners cheese data (r10) showing the same Hessel
Bottom cows' milk testing normally elsewhere.

Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware? **No** — nothing in any account of the
hearing (r02, r03, r07, r08, r12, r13, r19, r21, r23, r24) mentions the crate being produced or
considered, and two eyewitnesses present at the station sessions (Cleve Oram, r21, and clerk
Bertram Kyle, r23) both later marveled that the crate sat in the loft directly overhead while the
board sat below, unexamined. What did the tester himself believe the cause was? The sources do
not fully agree: his son Duncan recalls him privately blaming "something in the glass" as early
as 1911, though he never recorded it for want of proof (r18); but arbitration chairman Frick
recalls a separate, later private remark in which Keddie said he had always suspected the
patrons themselves (r13). These two recollections are not necessarily incompatible — a man might
hold or voice different private suspicions at different times to different confidants — but the
sources do not let this be settled into one belief.

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

From 1897 to 1923, the Ostrey Hollow skimming station paid its Hessel Bottom patrons on butterfat
tests that ran about a fifth of a point low, because a batch of new milk-test pipettes bought
that spring quietly delivered five percent less milk than their marked charge — an error no one
could see from inside the plant. For a quarter century, farmers, officers, and a veterinarian
blamed each other, wet hay, and watered milk; an accidental nine-week equipment swap in 1911
briefly proved the truth and was ignored; a 1925 arbitration fired the tester and paid a token
settlement without finding a cause. Only in 1958 did the tester's own granddaughter, a graduate
student, climb into a forgotten loft, find the condemned glass, and measure it herself. It took
eight separate records — ledgers, diaries, an inspector's notebook, family memory — because no
single witness ever held both the physical evidence and the financial arithmetic at once.
