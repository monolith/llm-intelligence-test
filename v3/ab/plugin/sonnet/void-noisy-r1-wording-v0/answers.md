*Method note: throughout, where sources conflict I favor (in rough order) a reproduced primary document (an invoice, a photostat, a dated diary entry, a quoted bulletin) over institutional record-keepers over disinterested contemporaneous officials (state inspector, county agent) over personal memoir over oral history recorded decades later — and I weight independence, not head-count, per the instructions. ## Story 1 — The Rule That Started It All (founding of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association)

Farmers of the Larrow Valley organized the Association in **1889**, meeting at Larrow Green (r02, r22); Gideon Vose was founding and first president, 1889–1911 (r02). The main creamery was built at Larrow Green in **1891** (r02, r22). Its governing rule, Article VII, paid every patron on the butterfat *test* of his milk rather than its weight, and specified that "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern," with no appeal (r02, r12) — the hinge on which the entire later dispute turns, as county agent Orin Falke observes: under payment-by-weight, none of what follows would have cost anyone a dollar (r22).

The exact adoption date cannot be recovered. r02 and r12 imply 1891/1892; Nyle Grigg's 1919 cover letter instead claims by-law payment-by-test "came in with the 1893 meeting" (r15). Falke, writing in 1959 with institutional resources and access to what records survived, states plainly that the Association's 1889–92 minute book was lost in a 1938 office move (also independently noted by secretary Effie Loomis, r02) and that "this office is unable to state the year of adoption and does not attempt to" (r22). I follow Falke rather than adjudicate between 1892 and 1893: the best-resourced, most careful narrator in the record explicitly declines to settle it, which is stronger evidence of unrecoverability than picking a side.

Officers named across the record: Effie Loomis, secretary 1901–1940 (r02); Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper from 1905 (r02, r05, r23); Hobart Sill, buttermaker 1893–1934 (r14); Selby Vose, manager (start date disputed — see Story 8/Section E), Gideon's son (r02, r18, r23 — the "nephew" rumor explicitly and independently denied twice).

The Ostrey Hollow skimming station, built to serve the valley's upper end, is the subject that dominates the rest of this record — Article VII is why a faulty measuring instrument at that one station, rather than a mere bookkeeping curiosity, became a fifty-year grievance affecting real money for real families.

**Documents:** r02 (Loomis), r12 (Rundle), r15 (Grigg), r22 (Falke), r23 (Kyle, institutional background).

## Story 2 — Ansel Keddie's Twenty-Nine Seasons

The Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up-valley, over the winter of 1895–96; it opened on **4 May 1896** (r22; matches the station's own test book, whose first leaf reads "Begun this 4th day of May, 1896... A. Keddie," r03). Ansel Keddie (b. 1861) was tester and separator man from the opening until his dismissal — 29 seasons (r03, r13, r18, r22). The station's first season or two used glassware borrowed from the Larrow Green creamery itself (r23); Larrow Green's own buttermaker Hobart Sill personally lent glass so the season could open before the station's ordered outfit arrived (r14). The station's own outfit went into service **1 May 1897** (r04, r07, r14, r20 — the manufacturer's own history and invoice; also confirmed by Orra Keddie's diary entry of that exact date, r24; Bertram Kyle's 1898 claim, r23, is an isolated later memory error).

Keddie married Orra Brant in 1889 (r18, r24); their son Duncan was born 1894 (r18), corroborated by Orra's diary noting Duncan at five in 1899 (r24) and at seventeen leaving for teacher training in October 1911 (r24). Keddie was, by every account including hostile ones, meticulous: he tested "faithfully 31x/week for 29 seasons" (r13), his test-book entries stayed within 0.03 of each other every year except the 1911 anomaly (r03), and when he privately suspected something wrong in 1911 he refused to write down what he could not prove, entering only a question mark (r18).

He was dismissed **30 April 1925**, four days before the arbitration board's award (r03, r13, r18, r22, r24 — extremely well corroborated, including by his own wife's diary entry of that date). He took up carpentry, worked the family farm, and died **February 1934**, still under the imputation, having told his wife once that "a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name" (r24). He was posthumously exonerated in October 1958, twenty-four years after his death (r24, r19, r22).

**Documents:** r03 (Keddie's own statement/letter), r18 (Duncan's family notes), r24 (Orra's diary), r14 (Sill), r22 (Falke), r13 (Frick).

## Story 3 — The Standing Grievance: Hessel Bottom's Low Tests, 1902–1925

From early in the station's life, Hessel Bottom's milk tested measurably lower than milk anywhere else in the county — not seasonally, but flat, year-round, in every month alike (r09, r17, r22). Falke's independent 1959 figure: the station's recorded average stood at **3.61**, against Larrow Green's own **3.78** (an ordinary county figure), a constant deficiency of **0.19 points** in every season (r22) — a figure independently echoed by Roy Brant ("seventeen hundredths," close, r09-digest) and his daughter Hazel, who gives it flatly as "nineteen hundredths... flat as a floor" (r17). The complaint was first voiced aloud in **1902** (r09, r22) and renewed every decade after.

Emmet Brant, the largest Hessel Bottom patron, kept his own parallel weigh book from **1 January 1897** (per his grandson Duncan Keddie's careful family notes, r18) — Hazel Brant's own account instead dates it to "nineteen hundred" (1900, r17), but Hazel explicitly disclaims her own reliability on years at the outset of her testimony ("Years I have not"), so I prefer Duncan's more precise, internally consistent figure. Brant's own farm-scale weight and the station's returned weight always agreed (r09) — ruling out any tampering with quantities, only with the *test*.

Matters escalated in **1919**, when manager Selby Vose circulated a written warning about "water in the cans" to the Hessel Bottom patrons — nine families per two later oral-history accounts (r01, r17), but eleven per Orra Keddie's diary, written the same month the circulars went out (r24). I favor the contemporaneous diary over the two much-later oral memories, which is itself an instance of "an apparent majority is not thereby settled." The circular was met with silence from every recipient — "which in that valley is a shout" (r17) — and caused the permanent rupture between Ansel Keddie and his brother-in-law Emmet Brant, not over the water charge itself but because Keddie, who kept the book, had personally delivered the circulars without publicly objecting (r09, r24). The two men never spoke again; Emmet died in 1929 without reconciling (r24, r18).

The grievance culminated in the patrons' formal petition of January 1925.

**Documents:** r09 (Roy Brant), r17 (Hazel Brant), r01 (Vira Toland), r24 (Orra's diary), r22 (Falke).

## Story 4 — The 1911 Interruption: An Accidental Experiment

On **8 May 1911** the station's own measuring pipette broke; a borrowed measure was used in its place until Keddie's own returned on **10 July 1911** — nine weeks (r03, Keddie's own dated letter; independently confirmed by r22 Falke's synthesis and cited in r04, r11). Wilbur Teague's memoir and veterinarian Roland Kepp instead give "six weeks" (r05, r16); I treat this as a single transmission error, since Kepp — who admits he has "never read a test in his life" (r16) — has no independent basis for a duration and was most likely repeating Teague's figure or valley rumor, not counting for himself.

Through those nine weeks, with the *same* tester, same herds, same season, same total absence of supervision, the station's recorded tests rose noticeably; the day Keddie's own measure returned, they fell straight back to the old level (r03, r22). Keddie recorded the fact and set only a question mark against it, telling his family it was "something in the glass," but would not commit it to the book without proof (r18).

As a natural experiment this is powerful: it isolates a single changed variable (the physical measuring instrument) while holding constant the man, the cows, the pasture, the season, and the oversight — and the result tracked the instrument, not any of the other candidates. That is decisive against both the theft theory (the same allegedly larcenous man's figures moved with a piece of glass he did not control any differently) and, less directly, against seasonal/feed theories (nothing about pasture or weather changed on 8 May or 10 July). What it does *not* establish: the magnitude of the true, ongoing error (that required the 1958 laboratory measurement), nor that the borrowed measure was itself perfectly accurate — only that the station's own glass was implicated. It is corroborating evidence, not by itself a quantified proof.

**Documents:** r03 (Keddie), r02, r04 (Ivy Keddie's citation), r11 (Vaught bulletin), r22 (Falke), r18 (Duncan); contrast r05/r16 for the six-week error.


# Answers — Part 2: Section A, Stories 5–8, and Section B

## Story 5 — Three Theories on Trial: The 1925 Arbitration at Ashlin

The patrons petitioned in **January 1925**; a board of arbitration chaired by attorney Alonzo Frick sat at Ashlin from **17 March** to **4 May 1925**, with two of its three sessions held at the station itself, in the weigh room, patrons and haulers standing outside in the road (r13, r21, r24 — Cleve Oram's eyewitness account and Orra Keddie's diary independently corroborate the same detail of outdoor waiting during station sessions). Exhibits were four books only: the station test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book, and the 1923 inspector's circuit notebook (r13, r23) — notably, the condemned glassware itself was never examined, sitting in the loft directly above the arbitrators' heads during the station sessions (r21).

Three explanations were aired (r13, r22): **(1) added water**, the Association manager's own view, behind his 1919 circular (r08); **(2) tester theft**, hinted by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague without a direct accusation (r05, r13); **(3) wet hay/mineral deficiency** of the Hessel Bottom pasture, per an Association committee's 1908 report (r15) and later echoed on veterinary grounds by Dr. Roland Kepp (r16).

The board "found no cause established," yet awarded the patrons **$3,800**, "without admission" (r02, r04, r07, r08, r13, r19, r22, r23 — well-corroborated across institutional, curatorial, journalistic and clerical sources). Two independent oral-history narrators decades later instead recall **$3,500** (r01, r21); I resolve for $3,800 on documentary weight, and note the two "$3,500" tellings are themselves not independent evidence for each other so much as a shared, plausible mishearing of "thirty-five" for "thirty-eight" hundred passed down in valley memory. Keddie was dismissed 30 April 1925, four days before the award — "so that the finding found nothing and the dismissal said everything" (r18).

Privately, per Frick's own recollection two decades later, Keddie told him in a hearing-corridor aside that he had always suspected the *patrons* of watering their own milk but would not accuse his neighbors publicly (r13) — a detail in tension with Duncan's account that his father blamed "something in the glass" as early as 1911 (r18). Both cannot be his single settled belief; see Section F.

**Documents:** r13 (Frick), r08 (Vose), r05 (Teague), r15/r16 (Grigg/Kepp), r21 (Oram), r24 (Orra's diary), r23 (Kyle).

## Story 6 — The Witnesses Who Never Found a Problem: Alder Corners

From 1915, four of Hessel Bottom's six farms hauled their milk over the ridge in the summer flush to Rosalie Cudd's father's cheese factory at Alder Corners, returning to the Ostrey Hollow station the rest of the year — sixteen seasons, through 1930 (r09, r10, r17, all three independently agreeing on "four" farms and "sixteen seasons"). Jerome Cudd, who built the factory in 1913, tested this milk entirely independently, never comparing his figures to the station's (r10).

His results, quoted from his own 1919 book and tabulated for the full run 1915–1930, cluster tightly at **3.78–3.82**, averaging about 3.80 — matching ordinary county levels, not the station's depressed 3.61 (r10). Cheesemaking, Rosalie Cudd states, reveals watered milk immediately through next-morning curd defects, and none ever showed in sixteen years (r10). This single, quiet data trail — from an entirely separate business, using different equipment, different personnel, never touching Keddie's pipettes — independently disproves both the water-adulteration theory (no defect ever found) and the wet-hay theory (the *same* Hessel Bottom hay and herds, measured elsewhere, tested normally) years before anyone identified the pipette as the cause. It predates the 1958 discovery entirely and does not depend on it.

A minor internal inconsistency: Cudd's letter quotes four individual 1919 figures (3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82) but her own summary table gives the 1919 season average as 3.80 flat — likely because the quoted figures are a sample, not the full season, rather than a real contradiction.

Rundle's 1939 pamphlet originally repeated the cream-theft folk belief in its Chapter IV, but added, "while in press," a retraction after consulting the ledger: cream reached Larrow Green *heavy*, not light, so theft does not fit the physical evidence (r12) — independently arriving, from the institutional side, at the same conclusion Alder Corners had quietly demonstrated from the farm side for two decades.

**Documents:** r10 (Rosalie Cudd), r09 (Roy Brant), r17 (Hazel Brant), r12 (Rundle's retraction).

## Story 7 — A Crate in the Loft: Discovery and Bulletin No. 471

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie — a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, and Ansel Keddie's granddaughter (see Section B1) — climbed a ladder into the loft over the old station's intake room and found, under a tarpaulin, the crate of pipettes condemned by the state dairy inspector on **12 June 1923** (r04, r17, r18, r21 — four independent accounts locate the crate in the loft, against a lone curatorial claim of "the Association office at Ashlin," r07, which I treat as the error — see Section E).

On 9 March 1958 she measured the nine surviving pipettes (of six dozen originally purchased, per the manufacturer's own 1897 invoice reproduced in r20) against a calibrated burette at Farland. Each was blown to a marked charge of 17.6cc but delivered on average only about 16.72cc — roughly 5% short, consistently and proportionally, not additively (r04, r11) — meaning the recorded test readings had run at about 95% of the true value for as long as that glass was in service. This explained the deficiency completely: no theft, no watering, only a manufacturing short-charge that was invisible from inside the plant because it was perfectly self-consistent (r11).

The finding was published as **Bulletin No. 471** (October 1958), co-authored by Professor Leland Vaught and Ivy Keddie (r11, r19, r22), and covered on the front page of the *Ashlin Chronicle* on 3 October 1958 (r19). County agent Orin Falke's 1959 report for the board of supervisors independently verified the bulletin's key citation (Bulletin No. 214's actual winter-depression figure) by writing to the college directly, and formally corrected his own earlier error calling Ivy Keddie a "great-niece" rather than granddaughter (r22).

The manufacturer, Ammon & Sons of Tarnet, later published its own account (1949, predating the discovery) admitting the likely mechanism — a reground mold running the lot short without anyone on the blowing floor catching it — and regretting that in 1923 it had nothing better to tell the Association than that its own records did not go back that far (r20).

**Documents:** r04 (Ivy Keddie), r07 (curator Nym), r11 (Vaught bulletin), r19 (Ashlin Chronicle), r22 (Falke), r20 (Ammon & Sons).

## Story 8 — Who Kept What: Family, Books, and the Long Afterlife of the Records

The Keddie, Brant, and Vose families were entangled for three generations. Orra Keddie (Ansel's wife) was Emmet Brant's sister (r09, r18, r24); their son Duncan and Emmet's son Roy were first cousins; Duncan's daughter Ivy and Roy's daughter Hazel were second cousins (r09, r17, r18 — three independent, explicit accounts, two from the people themselves, both pointedly correcting a common valley confusion). Selby Vose, the Association's manager, was Gideon Vose's son, not nephew, despite a persistent rumor (r18, r23 — both emphatic, independent denials). A separate rumor that the Voses and Keddies were themselves related by blood is explicitly and carefully refuted by Duncan, who states his mother — a woman who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection" — never once made the claim (r18); Cleve Oram nonetheless repeats the rumor secondhand, admitting he "could not draw... the line on paper" (r21).

The station test book stayed in Keddie's own hand from 1896 until impounded by the arbitration board in March 1925, then held at the Ashlin Cooperative office 1925–1959, then transferred to the Ordell County Historical Rooms (r18). The Brant weigh book passed from Emmet (began 1 Jan 1897, per Duncan's careful account) to Roy at Emmet's death in 1929, was lent — not given — to Ivy Keddie in 1958 for her college work, returned, and passed to Hazel at Roy's death in 1962, reaching the Historical Rooms in 1963 (r09, r17, r18 — all agree it was a loan, a point both lending families insisted on precisely). Strawn's circuit notebooks went to the state dairy office in 1936, then to the Farland college library in 1947, where Ivy found the crucial 1923 volume in 1958 (r18). The crate of condemned glass itself sat in the loft from 1923 until Ivy retrieved it in 1958, was measured at Farland, and was deposited with the Historical Rooms in 1959 alongside the test book (r18). By 1970 the five key documents/objects were housed in three separate buildings — "and none of them ours," as Duncan wryly notes (r18).

**Documents:** r18 (Duncan Keddie), r17 (Hazel Brant), r21 (Cleve Oram), r23 (Kyle), r09 (Roy Brant).

# Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**
Ivy is Ansel's **granddaughter**, daughter of his son Duncan (b. 1894), Ivy herself born 1931 (r18, explicit: "my daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter... I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise"). Cleve Oram independently calls her "the granddaughter" (r21). The *Ashlin Chronicle*, however, calls her Ansel's "great-niece" (r19), and county agent Falke's report originally repeated that error before appending an explicit correction: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine" (r22). This is a rare case where the sources resolve their own conflict — I follow Duncan (a primary family source with every reason to be exact) and Falke's verified correction, and treat the Chronicle's "great-niece" as a fast-turnaround newspaper error.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**
They are **second cousins**: Ivy's father Duncan and Hazel's father Roy were first cousins (children of siblings Orra Keddie and Emmet Brant), making their own children — Ivy and Hazel — second cousins (r09, r17, r18). Both Hazel and Duncan explicitly flag and correct the common local error of calling them cousins-once-removed or aunt/niece; Cleve Oram nonetheless makes exactly that error, calling them "first cousins once removed" (r21). I resolve for second cousins on the strength of two independent, first-person family accounts that specifically address and reject the alternative, against one outsider's admittedly uncertain recollection. What the sources leave open: neither generation's birth order or the precise year Emmet and Orra's own parents' second marriage occurred is given, though it does not affect the cousin math.

**B3. Selby Vose's father, and why it matters.**
Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**, the Association's founder, first president (1889–1911), and author of Article VII (r02, r18, r23 — the "nephew" rumor is explicitly and independently denied twice, by Duncan and by clerk Bertram Kyle, the latter noting he'd "heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense"). It matters because Selby inherited not just the manager's chair (from at least 1913, see Section E) but the institutional authority and credibility of the founding family; he signed the 1919 water-suspicion circular under his own name alone (r23), and as manager he signed every annual ledger sheet carrying the unexplained "station gain" line without, by his own clerk's account, reading the line itself (r23) — meaning the founding family's standing shaped whose theory (water) got institutional backing, and whose evidence (the ledger anomaly) went unexamined for decades.

**B4. The Vose–Keddie family relationship.**
The sources establish **no blood relationship**. A persistent valley rumor claimed the families were cousins "of some sort" (r21, hedged and unverified — Oram admits he "could not draw... the line on paper"). Duncan Keddie devotes a section of his family notes specifically to refuting this ("A thing that is said and is not so"), stating his mother, a woman who tracked every family connection in the valley obsessively, never once made this claim, and that the confusion likely arises from mixing up the Ashlin Voses (unrelated to Gideon's line) with Gideon's own family (r18). I follow Duncan's detailed, source-explicit denial over Oram's admittedly unverifiable hearsay.


# Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math

**C1. The systematic shortfall.**

(a) *Proportion.* The pipettes were blown to deliver a marked charge of 17.6cc but delivered on average only **16.72cc** — a shortfall of 0.88cc, i.e. 16.72/17.6 = **0.95**, or **95% of the mark** (5% short). I use 16.72cc (r04, and matching the bulletin's own reproduced conclusion paragraph as quoted in r11) rather than the competing 16.27cc figure found in Vaught's own Section V math (r11) and repeated in the *Chronicle* (r19), because that 16.27cc figure produces a shortfall (118,000 lb) that contradicts the *same bulletin's own conclusion paragraph* (76,000 lb) — an internal inconsistency in the primary document — whereas 16.72cc/76,000 lb is corroborated by an independent, different-methodology, contemporaneous measurement (state inspector Strawn's field-observed 1924 post-correction average of 3.80, r06; see (b)). Since the test bottle's fat reading scales directly with the volume of milk actually delivered into it, a 5%-short delivery produces a fat reading that is a fixed 5% low, in every test, at any true value — proportional, not additive (explicitly stated in r11).

(b) *True average.* Recorded station average = 3.61% (r02, r04, r07, r23). True average = 3.61 / 0.95 = **3.80%**. The points short = 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 points** — which matches, independently, the flat "nineteen hundredths" deficiency reported by patron-side narrators who never did this arithmetic themselves (r09, r17, r22), a strong cross-check that the proportional math and the patrons' lived experience converge.

(c) *Pounds not credited.* Using the well-corroborated total intake of 40,000,000 lb (see C3, and Section E on why I reject the 44,000,000 lb figure): credited fat = 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb; true fat = 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb. Difference = **76,000 lb** of butterfat never credited — matching the figure independently reached in r04 and in the bulletin's own conclusion (r11), and close to the "station gain" ledger check in C3 below.

(d) *Money and what was received.* At the average price of $0.25/lb quoted across the whole period (r02's Teague-ledger figure; independently reaffirmed by clerk Kyle, r23): 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000**. The bulletin's own more precise total, quoted twice (r12's added footnote, r19), is **$19,600** — likely reflecting year-by-year price variation rather than one flat average, which the sources do not give us enough data to reconstruct exactly; I report both and flag the $600 gap as **not fully resolvable from the arithmetic given**. Against either figure, the patrons actually received $3,800 — roughly **one-fifth** (3,800/19,000 ≈ 20%, or 3,800/19,600 ≈ 19%) of what the shortfall was actually worth.

**C2. Emmet Brant's 1910 year.**
His weigh-book leaf shows 200,000 lb delivered, "paid on a test of 3.42" — a figure independently read the same way by both Roy Brant (r09) and his daughter Hazel, quoting the same physical leaf six decades later (r17), a strong verbatim cross-check.
(a) Credited fat = 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb**.
(b) Since the error is proportional (not a flat 0.19-point addition — see C1(a)), his true test = 3.42 / 0.95 = 3.60%; true fat = 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb. Shorted = 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb**. (Note: using the flat-0.19-point shortcut instead gives 380 lb — a different, less-justified answer, since the sources specify the mechanism is proportional.)
(c) No 1910-specific butterfat price is given in the sources; using the only price available, the whole-period average of $0.25/lb (r23), 360 × $0.25 = **$90.00 (approximate)** — flagged as an inferred figure using a multi-decade average price as a stand-in for a single year's actual price, which the sources do not supply.

**C3. Station-wide, full delivery years.**
(a) Credited to patrons: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**.
(b) What actually reached Larrow Green: Teague's own "station-gain" ledger line, invented 1905, recorded a consistent gain of about **5.25%** between the station's credited figure and Larrow Green's received figure (worked example, 1898: credited 57,760 lb, received 60,800 lb, gain 3,040 lb = 5.25%; r05, r23). Applying that same 5.25% to the full-period credited total: 1,444,000 × 1.0525 ≈ **1,520,000 lb** received — which matches, independently, the pipette-derived "true fat" figure in C1(b) almost exactly. This convergence is significant: it means a completely separate, contemporaneous bookkeeping trail (real receiving weights at Larrow Green, tracked from 1905 onward) corroborates the size of the 1958 laboratory finding decades before anyone knew its cause.
(c) The difference, 76,000 lb, expressed as a percentage: **5.25%, taken of the credited (station-test) figure**, not of the received figure — this denominator choice is exactly what Teague's own ledger used ("as usual," per clerk Kyle's recollection of the line, r23), and it is what the sources actually give us; expressed instead as a fraction of the received total the same 76,000 lb would read as roughly 5% (76,000/1,520,000 ≈ 5.0%), a smaller number, which is why being explicit about the base matters.

**C4. The invoice.**
(a) Total: **$77.93** — from the manufacturer's own reproduced invoice, dated 14 April 1897 (r20; matching r04 and r07's separate citations of the same figure; the *Chronicle*'s "$77.39," r19, is a transposition error against the primary document).
(b) That invoice bought **6 dozen (72) milk-test pipettes** at $0.35 apiece (line item $25.20 of the $77.93 total, which also covered test bottles, an acid measure, a hand tester, and freight) — confirmed by the primary invoice (r20) against the competing "5 dozen" figure found only in Vaught's bulletin math (r11) and Cleve Oram's oral account (r21), which I treat as a single transmitted error, not independent corroboration. Each pipette was short by **0.88cc** (17.6cc marked, 16.72cc actual delivery).

**C5. Duration and first season.**
(a) The faulty glassware was in service from **1 May 1897** to its condemnation on **12 June 1923** — about **26 years** (matching r19's own phrase, "the twenty-six years before"). The money calculation, however, is described in the sources as running over "25 years" of ledger data (Teague's ledger total, as reported by secretary Loomis, r02) or simply "the whole run of years" (r04, r11) without a precise year-count. These are two different, overlapping-but-not-identical spans: the glass's own service life is fixed by two hard dates (invoice/installation 1897, condemnation 1923), while the "25 years" ledger figure appears to be a round or period-specific accounting total that the sources do not let us reconcile to the exact year with the glass's own service dates. I treat the one-year discrepancy as **not fully determinable from the sources given** — this is one of the places where "cannot be determined" is the honest answer.
(b) The station's first season (partial, May–Dec 1896, before the station's own glass arrived) took in an estimated **~900,000 lb** per Hobart Sill (r14, who was present and personally lent equipment) or **~1,100,000 lb** per clerk Bertram Kyle (r23, who explicitly disclaims this figure as an unverified impression, "not in any book I ever ruled"). Neither is ledger-based; I report both and note this figure **cannot be determined precisely** from the sources.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** See Story 4 above for full detail. In brief: the interval was **8 May – 10 July 1911, nine weeks** (Keddie's own dated letter, r03; independently confirmed by Falke's 1959 synthesis, r22 — against a "six weeks" figure in r05/r16 that I treat as a single transmitted error, since Kepp admits no competence to judge a test at all). The variable was the physical measuring pipette (broken glass replaced by a borrowed one, then restored); held constant were the tester, the herds, the pasture, the season, and the (complete absence of) supervision. The result: tests rose measurably for the nine weeks and fell back the day the original-type glass resumed. This establishes, as a calculation alone could not, a *causal* link between the instrument and the reading — because it isolates one changed variable while holding every human and biological factor fixed, which no amount of after-the-fact statistical averaging can do. It does **not** establish the magnitude of the ongoing true error (only the 1958 burette measurement did that), and it does not prove the borrowed measure was itself perfectly accurate — only that the station's own glass, specifically, was implicated.

**D2.** Three independent reasons Keddie cannot have been stealing cream, each predating and independent of the 1958 discovery:
1. **The station-gain ledger (from 1905).** Teague's own annual bookkeeping showed *more* fat consistently arriving at Larrow Green than was ever credited to the patrons — the opposite of what cream theft would produce (stolen cream would arrive *light*, not heavy). Rundle's 1939 pamphlet reaches the identical conclusion independently, retracting his own theft claim "while in press" once he examined the ledger (r12).
2. **The Alder Corners cross-check (1915–1930).** A wholly separate business, using different equipment and personnel, tested the same Hessel Bottom herds' summer milk for sixteen years and found no defect, no shortfall, and no sign of tampering ever (r10) — evidence entirely outside Keddie's own equipment or supervision.
3. **The 1911 natural experiment.** The same allegedly larcenous tester's own figures moved when a different physical instrument was substituted, under no different oversight — inconsistent with deliberate human pilferage, consistent with an instrumental cause (see D1).
These are independent because they rest on three unconnected data trails (Larrow Green's own receiving records; an outside company's cheese-quality records; a real-time 1911 field event) kept by three different institutions/individuals, none of them derived from or dependent on the others. Both (1) and (2) would still hold even if the 1958 glassware had never been measured, since both existed and were recorded decades before Ivy Keddie went up the ladder. Did the bookkeeper who *made* the theft insinuation, Teague, ever question his own ledger entries that refute it? **No** — per clerk Kyle's eyewitness account, Teague wrote the "station gain... as usual" line every year for forty years without ever asking why (r23), and per Teague's own memoir, when he raised it with manager Vose in 1916 he was told to "let it alone" (r05) — yet he still leaned toward the theft theory as late as his 1948 memoir, apparently without reconciling it against his own numbers.

**D3.** The sources conflict, but on balance, **yes, the manager likely knew** the station's own ledger carried an unexplained anomaly before 1958, even if he did not grasp its cause. Clerk Bertram Kyle's eyewitness account of Selby Vose's general office habits describes him signing every sheet by reading only the bottom total, never the lines above (r23) — suggesting routine inattention. But Teague's own, more specific memoir states directly that Vose *knew* of the "station-gain" line and personally told him, in 1916, to "let it alone" (r05) — a specific, dated, first-person recollection of a substantive exchange, which is more probative than a general characterization of Vose's habits. The two accounts are not strictly incompatible (a man can habitually skim totals yet still have had one remembered pointed conversation about a specific line), so I resolve toward Teague's account: Vose was aware, at least from 1916, that the ledger showed something that needed explaining, and chose not to pursue it.

## Section F — Theory

**F1.** The shortfall was caused by a **manufacturing defect in the station's own milk-test pipettes**: the 6-dozen lot purchased from Ammon & Sons in 1897, most likely from a reground mold that ran the batch short without anyone on the blowing floor catching it (the firm's own later admission, r20), delivered a fixed ~5% less milk than their marked 17.6cc charge into the test bottle. Because the butterfat reading is directly proportional to the volume of milk actually delivered, this produced a constant, precise, proportional under-reading — about 0.19 points low at the station's typical 3.6–3.8% range — in every single test, for as long as that specific glass remained in service, and it was invisible from inside the plant because a systematically biased instrument is perfectly self-consistent and passes any check that only tests for internal consistency rather than absolute accuracy (r11).

This theory explains: **the size** of the error (fixed by the physical dimensions of that one batch of glass — proportional shortfall of ~5%, i.e., ~0.19 points at the station's typical test level); **why that size** (a one-time manufacturing defect in a single lot, not a design flaw, since no other station or lot shows the same pattern); **why it began when it did** (the defective glass entered service 1 May 1897, replacing the station's earlier borrowed, accurate glass); **why it ended when it did** (the state inspector, newly equipped with a calibrated burette, condemned and replaced the exact physical objects on 12 June 1923); and **why nothing at the station or on the farms needed to change** for the deficiency to appear or disappear — because the defect lived entirely in the measuring apparatus, not in the milk, the cows, the feed, or anyone's honesty, which is exactly consistent with the observed flat, unvarying, year-round, proportional nature of the deficit.

**The three competing explanations, and what defeats each:**
- **Cream theft** (advanced by Vira Toland, r01; hinted by Wilbur Teague, r05, r13) — defeated by the "station-gain" ledger showing cream arriving at Larrow Green *heavier*, not lighter, than credited (r05, r12's retraction), and by the 1911 natural experiment, where the same tester's figures moved with a change of instrument alone.
- **Added water by the patrons** (advanced by manager Selby Vose, r08; and, from the creamery end, buttermaker Hobart Sill, r14) — defeated by state inspector Strawn's testimony that he tested for water at every visit from 1908 forward and never once found any (r06, r22), and by sixteen years of Alder Corners cheese records showing no watering defect ever (r10).
- **Wet hay / mineral deficiency of Hessel Bottom pasture** (1908 Grigg committee, r15; veterinarian Roland Kepp, r16) — defeated by Bulletin No. 214's own actual finding, verified by Falke's direct quotation, that bog hay depressed butterfat by at most 0.05% in winter and nothing measurable in summer (r22) — far too small and too seasonal to explain a flat, year-round 0.19-point deficit — and by the Alder Corners records, where the *same* hay and herds tested normally when measured elsewhere (r10).

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?** **No.** The exhibit list named only four books (r13, r23), with no mention of the physical glassware, and Cleve Oram's eyewitness account states plainly that the crate sat in the loft "over their heads the entire time" during the station sessions (r21) — the literal physical evidence was never brought down or inspected.

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?** The sources give two different private statements from Keddie that are not fully reconcilable. Per his son Duncan, he came home in 1911 saying it was "something in the glass" and refused to write down what he couldn't prove (r18) — a contemporaneous, dated account. Per Alonzo Frick's private recollection decades later, Keddie told him in a hearing-corridor aside that he had always privately suspected the *patrons* of watering their milk (r13) — an undated, secondhand-to-us recollection reported in 1946. I weight the 1911 "glass" account somewhat more heavily as his longer-held, contemporaneously recorded suspicion, but note the sources do not let us be certain which, if either, was his final settled belief by 1925.

## Section G — Summary

An 1889 farmers' cooperative paid its members by the butterfat *test* of their milk, not its weight. From 1896, one outlying station's tests ran a fixed 0.19 points low, every month, every year — a standing grievance that split families, produced a hostile 1919 circular, and ended in a 1925 arbitration that fired the tester and paid the patrons a fraction of what they'd lost, without ever explaining why. Three theories — theft, added water, bad hay — were argued for decades and each was quietly disproved by independent evidence (a bookkeeper's own ledger, a rival cheese factory's clean sixteen-year record, a freak 1911 natural experiment) that nobody assembled. In 1958 the dead tester's granddaughter climbed a ladder into a forgotten loft and found the real cause: a batch of measuring glass, bought in 1897, that had silently delivered 5% short of its mark for twenty-six years. It took eight separate, partial, sometimes self-contradicting records — family memory, ledgers, a diary, an inspector's notebook, an invoice, a lab bulletin — because no single witness ever held all the pieces at once.
