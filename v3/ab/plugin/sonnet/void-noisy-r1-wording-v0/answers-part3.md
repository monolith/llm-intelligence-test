# Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math

**C1. The systematic shortfall.**

(a) *Proportion.* The pipettes were blown to deliver a marked charge of 17.6cc but delivered on average only **16.72cc** — a shortfall of 0.88cc, i.e. 16.72/17.6 = **0.95**, or **95% of the mark** (5% short). I use 16.72cc (r04, and matching the bulletin's own reproduced conclusion paragraph as quoted in r11) rather than the competing 16.27cc figure found in Vaught's own Section V math (r11) and repeated in the *Chronicle* (r19), because that 16.27cc figure produces a shortfall (118,000 lb) that contradicts the *same bulletin's own conclusion paragraph* (76,000 lb) — an internal inconsistency in the primary document — whereas 16.72cc/76,000 lb is corroborated by an independent, different-methodology, contemporaneous measurement (state inspector Strawn's field-observed 1924 post-correction average of 3.80, r06; see (b)). Since the test bottle's fat reading scales directly with the volume of milk actually delivered into it, a 5%-short delivery produces a fat reading that is a fixed 5% low, in every test, at any true value — proportional, not additive (explicitly stated in r11).

(b) *True average.* Recorded station average = 3.61% (r02, r04, r07, r23). True average = 3.61 / 0.95 = **3.80%**. The points short = 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 points** — which matches, independently, the flat "nineteen hundredths" deficiency reported by patron-side narrators who never did this arithmetic themselves (r09, r17, r22), a strong cross-check that the proportional math and the patrons' lived experience converge.

(c) *Pounds not credited.* Using the well-corroborated total intake of 40,000,000 lb (see C3, and Section E on why I reject the 44,000,000 lb figure): credited fat = 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb; true fat = 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb. Difference = **76,000 lb** of butterfat never credited — matching the figure independently reached in r04 and in the bulletin's own conclusion (r11), and close to the "station gain" ledger check in C3 below.

(d) *Money and what was received.* At the average price of $0.25/lb quoted across the whole period (r02's Teague-ledger figure; independently reaffirmed by clerk Kyle, r23): 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000**. The bulletin's own more precise total, quoted twice (r12's added footnote, r19), is **$19,600** — likely reflecting year-by-year price variation rather than one flat average, which the sources do not give us enough data to reconstruct exactly; I report both and flag the $600 gap as **not fully resolvable from the arithmetic given**. Against either figure, the patrons actually received $3,800 — roughly **one-fifth** (3,800/19,000 ≈ 20%, or 3,800/19,600 ≈ 19%) of what the shortfall was actually worth.

**C2. Emmet Brant's 1910 year.**
His weigh-book leaf shows 200,000 lb delivered, "paid on a test of 3.42" — a figure independently read the same way by both Roy Brant (r09) and his daughter Hazel, quoting the same physical leaf six decades later (r17), a strong verbatim cross-check.
(a) Credited fat = 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb**.
(b) Since the error is proportional (not a flat 0.19-point addition — see C1(a)), his true test = 3.42 / 0.95 = 3.60%; true fat = 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb. Shorted = 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb**. (Note: using the flat-0.19-point shortcut instead gives 380 lb — a different, less-justified answer, since the sources specify the mechanism is proportional.)
(c) No 1910-specific butterfat price is given in the sources; using the only price available, the whole-period average of $0.25/lb (r23), 360 × $0.25 = **$90.00 (approximate)** — flagged as an inferred figure using a multi-decade average price as a stand-in for a single year's actual price, which the sources do not supply.

**C3. Station-wide, full delivery years.**
(a) Credited to patrons: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**.
(b) What actually reached Larrow Green: Teague's own "station-gain" ledger line, invented 1905, recorded a consistent gain of about **5.25%** between the station's credited figure and Larrow Green's received figure (worked example, 1898: credited 57,760 lb, received 60,800 lb, gain 3,040 lb = 5.25%; r05, r23). Applying that same 5.25% to the full-period credited total: 1,444,000 × 1.0525 ≈ **1,520,000 lb** received — which matches, independently, the pipette-derived "true fat" figure in C1(b) almost exactly. This convergence is significant: it means a completely separate, contemporaneous bookkeeping trail (real receiving weights at Larrow Green, tracked from 1905 onward) corroborates the size of the 1958 laboratory finding decades before anyone knew its cause.
(c) The difference, 76,000 lb, expressed as a percentage: **5.25%, taken of the credited (station-test) figure**, not of the received figure — this denominator choice is exactly what Teague's own ledger used ("as usual," per clerk Kyle's recollection of the line, r23), and it is what the sources actually give us; expressed instead as a fraction of the received total the same 76,000 lb would read as roughly 5% (76,000/1,520,000 ≈ 5.0%), a smaller number, which is why being explicit about the base matters.

**C4. The invoice.**
(a) Total: **$77.93** — from the manufacturer's own reproduced invoice, dated 14 April 1897 (r20; matching r04 and r07's separate citations of the same figure; the *Chronicle*'s "$77.39," r19, is a transposition error against the primary document).
(b) That invoice bought **6 dozen (72) milk-test pipettes** at $0.35 apiece (line item $25.20 of the $77.93 total, which also covered test bottles, an acid measure, a hand tester, and freight) — confirmed by the primary invoice (r20) against the competing "5 dozen" figure found only in Vaught's bulletin math (r11) and Cleve Oram's oral account (r21), which I treat as a single transmitted error, not independent corroboration. Each pipette was short by **0.88cc** (17.6cc marked, 16.72cc actual delivery).

**C5. Duration and first season.**
(a) The faulty glassware was in service from **1 May 1897** to its condemnation on **12 June 1923** — about **26 years** (matching r19's own phrase, "the twenty-six years before"). The money calculation, however, is described in the sources as running over "25 years" of ledger data (Teague's ledger total, as reported by secretary Loomis, r02) or simply "the whole run of years" (r04, r11) without a precise year-count. These are two different, overlapping-but-not-identical spans: the glass's own service life is fixed by two hard dates (invoice/installation 1897, condemnation 1923), while the "25 years" ledger figure appears to be a round or period-specific accounting total that the sources do not let us reconcile to the exact year with the glass's own service dates. I treat the one-year discrepancy as **not fully determinable from the sources given** — this is one of the places where "cannot be determined" is the honest answer.
(b) The station's first season (partial, May–Dec 1896, before the station's own glass arrived) took in an estimated **~900,000 lb** per Hobart Sill (r14, who was present and personally lent equipment) or **~1,100,000 lb** per clerk Bertram Kyle (r23, who explicitly disclaims this figure as an unverified impression, "not in any book I ever ruled"). Neither is ledger-based; I report both and note this figure **cannot be determined precisely** from the sources.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** See Story 4 above for full detail. In brief: the interval was **8 May – 10 July 1911, nine weeks** (Keddie's own dated letter, r03; independently confirmed by Falke's 1959 synthesis, r22 — against a "six weeks" figure in r05/r16 that I treat as a single transmitted error, since Kepp admits no competence to judge a test at all). The variable was the physical measuring pipette (broken glass replaced by a borrowed one, then restored); held constant were the tester, the herds, the pasture, the season, and the (complete absence of) supervision. The result: tests rose measurably for the nine weeks and fell back the day the original-type glass resumed. This establishes, as a calculation alone could not, a *causal* link between the instrument and the reading — because it isolates one changed variable while holding every human and biological factor fixed, which no amount of after-the-fact statistical averaging can do. It does **not** establish the magnitude of the ongoing true error (only the 1958 burette measurement did that), and it does not prove the borrowed measure was itself perfectly accurate — only that the station's own glass, specifically, was implicated.

**D2.** Three independent reasons Keddie cannot have been stealing cream, each predating and independent of the 1958 discovery:
1. **The station-gain ledger (from 1905).** Teague's own annual bookkeeping showed *more* fat consistently arriving at Larrow Green than was ever credited to the patrons — the opposite of what cream theft would produce (stolen cream would arrive *light*, not heavy). Rundle's 1939 pamphlet reaches the identical conclusion independently, retracting his own theft claim "while in press" once he examined the ledger (r12).
2. **The Alder Corners cross-check (1915–1930).** A wholly separate business, using different equipment and personnel, tested the same Hessel Bottom herds' summer milk for sixteen years and found no defect, no shortfall, and no sign of tampering ever (r10) — evidence entirely outside Keddie's own equipment or supervision.
3. **The 1911 natural experiment.** The same allegedly larcenous tester's own figures moved when a different physical instrument was substituted, under no different oversight — inconsistent with deliberate human pilferage, consistent with an instrumental cause (see D1).
These are independent because they rest on three unconnected data trails (Larrow Green's own receiving records; an outside company's cheese-quality records; a real-time 1911 field event) kept by three different institutions/individuals, none of them derived from or dependent on the others. Both (1) and (2) would still hold even if the 1958 glassware had never been measured, since both existed and were recorded decades before Ivy Keddie went up the ladder. Did the bookkeeper who *made* the theft insinuation, Teague, ever question his own ledger entries that refute it? **No** — per clerk Kyle's eyewitness account, Teague wrote the "station gain... as usual" line every year for forty years without ever asking why (r23), and per Teague's own memoir, when he raised it with manager Vose in 1916 he was told to "let it alone" (r05) — yet he still leaned toward the theft theory as late as his 1948 memoir, apparently without reconciling it against his own numbers.

**D3.** The sources conflict, but on balance, **yes, the manager likely knew** the station's own ledger carried an unexplained anomaly before 1958, even if he did not grasp its cause. Clerk Bertram Kyle's eyewitness account of Selby Vose's general office habits describes him signing every sheet by reading only the bottom total, never the lines above (r23) — suggesting routine inattention. But Teague's own, more specific memoir states directly that Vose *knew* of the "station-gain" line and personally told him, in 1916, to "let it alone" (r05) — a specific, dated, first-person recollection of a substantive exchange, which is more probative than a general characterization of Vose's habits. The two accounts are not strictly incompatible (a man can habitually skim totals yet still have had one remembered pointed conversation about a specific line), so I resolve toward Teague's account: Vose was aware, at least from 1916, that the ledger showed something that needed explaining, and chose not to pursue it.

## Section E — Contradictions

*(Selected significant contradictions; reasoning method noted for each. "Independent-error" cases and self-contradictions are called out separately at the end, as required.)*

1. **Settlement:** $3,800 (r02, r04, r07, r08, r13, r19, r22, r23) vs $3,500 (r01, r21). *Method:* documentary/institutional weight; resolved for $3,800.
2. **Condemnation date:** 12 June 1923 (r02, r06, r07, r22) vs 12 July 1923 (r08, r12) vs 1922 (r23, sole outlier). *Method:* Ammon & Sons' own letter book (r20) shows the Association's replacement order was dated **28 June 1923**, which is only possible if condemnation preceded it — arithmetically ruling out "12 July" and confirming "12 June." Kyle's "1922" is an isolated late memory error (he is also wrong elsewhere — see #16 below).

## Section F — Theory

**F1.** The shortfall was caused by a **manufacturing defect in the station's own milk-test pipettes**: the 6-dozen lot purchased from Ammon & Sons in 1897, most likely from a reground mold that ran the batch short without anyone on the blowing floor catching it (the firm's own later admission, r20), delivered a fixed ~5% less milk than their marked 17.6cc charge into the test bottle. Because the butterfat reading is directly proportional to the volume of milk actually delivered, this produced a constant, precise, proportional under-reading — about 0.19 points low at the station's typical 3.6–3.8% range — in every single test, for as long as that specific glass remained in service, and it was invisible from inside the plant because a systematically biased instrument is perfectly self-consistent and passes any check that only tests for internal consistency rather than absolute accuracy (r11).

This theory explains: **the size** of the error (fixed by the physical dimensions of that one batch of glass — proportional shortfall of ~5%, i.e., ~0.19 points at the station's typical test level); **why that size** (a one-time manufacturing defect in a single lot, not a design flaw, since no other station or lot shows the same pattern); **why it began when it did** (the defective glass entered service 1 May 1897, replacing the station's earlier borrowed, accurate glass); **why it ended when it did** (the state inspector, newly equipped with a calibrated burette, condemned and replaced the exact physical objects on 12 June 1923); and **why nothing at the station or on the farms needed to change** for the deficiency to appear or disappear — because the defect lived entirely in the measuring apparatus, not in the milk, the cows, the feed, or anyone's honesty, which is exactly consistent with the observed flat, unvarying, year-round, proportional nature of the deficit.

**The three competing explanations, and what defeats each:**
- **Cream theft** (advanced by Vira Toland, r01; hinted by Wilbur Teague, r05, r13) — defeated by the "station-gain" ledger showing cream arriving at Larrow Green *heavier*, not lighter, than credited (r05, r12's retraction), and by the 1911 natural experiment, where the same tester's figures moved with a change of instrument alone.
- **Added water by the patrons** (advanced by manager Selby Vose, r08; and, from the creamery end, buttermaker Hobart Sill, r14) — defeated by state inspector Strawn's testimony that he tested for water at every visit from 1908 forward and never once found any (r06, r22), and by sixteen years of Alder Corners cheese records showing no watering defect ever (r10).
- **Wet hay / mineral deficiency of Hessel Bottom pasture** (1908 Grigg committee, r15; veterinarian Roland Kepp, r16) — defeated by Bulletin No. 214's own actual finding, verified by Falke's direct quotation, that bog hay depressed butterfat by at most 0.05% in winter and nothing measurable in summer (r22) — far too small and too seasonal to explain a flat, year-round 0.19-point deficit — and by the Alder Corners records, where the *same* hay and herds tested normally when measured elsewhere (r10).

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?** **No.** The exhibit list named only four books (r13, r23), with no mention of the physical glassware, and Cleve Oram's eyewitness account states plainly that the crate sat in the loft "over their heads the entire time" during the station sessions (r21) — the literal physical evidence was never brought down or inspected.

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?** The sources give two different private statements from Keddie that are not fully reconcilable. Per his son Duncan, he came home in 1911 saying it was "something in the glass" and refused to write down what he couldn't prove (r18) — a contemporaneous, dated account. Per Alonzo Frick's private recollection decades later, Keddie told him in a hearing-corridor aside that he had always privately suspected the *patrons* of watering their milk (r13) — an undated, secondhand-to-us recollection reported in 1946. I weight the 1911 "glass" account somewhat more heavily as his longer-held, contemporaneously recorded suspicion, but note the sources do not let us be certain which, if either, was his final settled belief by 1925.

## Section G — Summary

An 1889 farmers' cooperative paid its members by the butterfat *test* of their milk, not its weight. From 1896, one outlying station's tests ran a fixed 0.19 points low, every month, every year — a standing grievance that split families, produced a hostile 1919 circular, and ended in a 1925 arbitration that fired the tester and paid the patrons a fraction of what they'd lost, without ever explaining why. Three theories — theft, added water, bad hay — were argued for decades and each was quietly disproved by independent evidence (a bookkeeper's own ledger, a rival cheese factory's clean sixteen-year record, a freak 1911 natural experiment) that nobody assembled. In 1958 the dead tester's granddaughter climbed a ladder into a forgotten loft and found the real cause: a batch of measuring glass, bought in 1897, that had silently delivered 5% short of its mark for twenty-six years. It took eight separate, partial, sometimes self-contradicting records — family memory, ledgers, a diary, an inspector's notebook, an invoice, a lab bulletin — because no single witness ever held all the pieces at once.
