# Answers — Part 2: Section A, Stories 5–8, and Section B

## Story 5 — Three Theories on Trial: The 1925 Arbitration at Ashlin

The patrons petitioned in **January 1925**; a board of arbitration chaired by attorney Alonzo Frick sat at Ashlin from **17 March** to **4 May 1925**, with two of its three sessions held at the station itself, in the weigh room, patrons and haulers standing outside in the road (r13, r21, r24 — Cleve Oram's eyewitness account and Orra Keddie's diary independently corroborate the same detail of outdoor waiting during station sessions). Exhibits were four books only: the station test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book, and the 1923 inspector's circuit notebook (r13, r23) — notably, the condemned glassware itself was never examined, sitting in the loft directly above the arbitrators' heads during the station sessions (r21).

Three explanations were aired (r13, r22): **(1) added water**, the Association manager's own view, behind his 1919 circular (r08); **(2) tester theft**, hinted by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague without a direct accusation (r05, r13); **(3) wet hay/mineral deficiency** of the Hessel Bottom pasture, per an Association committee's 1908 report (r15) and later echoed on veterinary grounds by Dr. Roland Kepp (r16).

The board "found no cause established," yet awarded the patrons **$3,800**, "without admission" (r02, r04, r07, r08, r13, r19, r22, r23 — well-corroborated across institutional, curatorial, journalistic and clerical sources). Two independent oral-history narrators decades later instead recall **$3,500** (r01, r21); I resolve for $3,800 on documentary weight, and note the two "$3,500" tellings are themselves not independent evidence for each other so much as a shared, plausible mishearing of "thirty-five" for "thirty-eight" hundred passed down in valley memory. Keddie was dismissed 30 April 1925, four days before the award — "so that the finding found nothing and the dismissal said everything" (r18).

Privately, per Frick's own recollection two decades later, Keddie told him in a hearing-corridor aside that he had always suspected the *patrons* of watering their own milk but would not accuse his neighbors publicly (r13) — a detail in tension with Duncan's account that his father blamed "something in the glass" as early as 1911 (r18). Both cannot be his single settled belief; see Section F.

**Documents:** r13 (Frick), r08 (Vose), r05 (Teague), r15/r16 (Grigg/Kepp), r21 (Oram), r24 (Orra's diary), r23 (Kyle).

## Story 6 — The Witnesses Who Never Found a Problem: Alder Corners

From 1915, four of Hessel Bottom's six farms hauled their milk over the ridge in the summer flush to Rosalie Cudd's father's cheese factory at Alder Corners, returning to the Ostrey Hollow station the rest of the year — sixteen seasons, through 1930 (r09, r10, r17, all three independently agreeing on "four" farms and "sixteen seasons"). Jerome Cudd, who built the factory in 1913, tested this milk entirely independently, never comparing his figures to the station's (r10).

His results, quoted from his own 1919 book and tabulated for the full run 1915–1930, cluster tightly at **3.78–3.82**, averaging about 3.80 — matching ordinary county levels, not the station's depressed 3.61 (r10). Cheesemaking, Rosalie Cudd states, reveals watered milk immediately through next-morning curd defects, and none ever showed in sixteen years (r10). This single, quiet data trail — from an entirely separate business, using different equipment, different personnel, never touching Keddie's pipettes — independently disproves both the water-adulteration theory (no defect ever found) and the wet-hay theory (the *same* Hessel Bottom hay and herds, measured elsewhere, tested normally) years before anyone identified the pipette as the cause. It predates the 1958 discovery entirely and does not depend on it.

A minor internal inconsistency: Cudd's letter quotes four individual 1919 figures (3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82) but her own summary table gives the 1919 season average as 3.80 flat — likely because the quoted figures are a sample, not the full season, rather than a real contradiction.

Rundle's 1939 pamphlet originally repeated the cream-theft folk belief in its Chapter IV, but added, "while in press," a retraction after consulting the ledger: cream reached Larrow Green *heavy*, not light, so theft does not fit the physical evidence (r12) — independently arriving, from the institutional side, at the same conclusion Alder Corners had quietly demonstrated from the farm side for two decades.

**Documents:** r10 (Rosalie Cudd), r09 (Roy Brant), r17 (Hazel Brant), r12 (Rundle's retraction).

## Story 7 — A Crate in the Loft: Discovery and Bulletin No. 471

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie — a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, and Ansel Keddie's granddaughter (see Section B1) — climbed a ladder into the loft over the old station's intake room and found, under a tarpaulin, the crate of pipettes condemned by the state dairy inspector on **12 June 1923** (r04, r17, r18, r21 — four independent accounts locate the crate in the loft, against a lone curatorial claim of "the Association office at Ashlin," r07, which I treat as the error — see Section E).

On 9 March 1958 she measured the nine surviving pipettes (of six dozen originally purchased, per the manufacturer's own 1897 invoice reproduced in r20) against a calibrated burette at Farland. Each was blown to a marked charge of 17.6cc but delivered on average only about 16.72cc — roughly 5% short, consistently and proportionally, not additively (r04, r11) — meaning the recorded test readings had run at about 95% of the true value for as long as that glass was in service. This explained the deficiency completely: no theft, no watering, only a manufacturing short-charge that was invisible from inside the plant because it was perfectly self-consistent (r11).

The finding was published as **Bulletin No. 471** (October 1958), co-authored by Professor Leland Vaught and Ivy Keddie (r11, r19, r22), and covered on the front page of the *Ashlin Chronicle* on 3 October 1958 (r19). County agent Orin Falke's 1959 report for the board of supervisors independently verified the bulletin's key citation (Bulletin No. 214's actual winter-depression figure) by writing to the college directly, and formally corrected his own earlier error calling Ivy Keddie a "great-niece" rather than granddaughter (r22).

The manufacturer, Ammon & Sons of Tarnet, later published its own account (1949, predating the discovery) admitting the likely mechanism — a reground mold running the lot short without anyone on the blowing floor catching it — and regretting that in 1923 it had nothing better to tell the Association than that its own records did not go back that far (r20).

**Documents:** r04 (Ivy Keddie), r07 (curator Nym), r11 (Vaught bulletin), r19 (Ashlin Chronicle), r22 (Falke), r20 (Ammon & Sons).

## Story 8 — Who Kept What: Family, Books, and the Long Afterlife of the Records

The Keddie, Brant, and Vose families were entangled for three generations. Orra Keddie (Ansel's wife) was Emmet Brant's sister (r09, r18, r24); their son Duncan and Emmet's son Roy were first cousins; Duncan's daughter Ivy and Roy's daughter Hazel were second cousins (r09, r17, r18 — three independent, explicit accounts, two from the people themselves, both pointedly correcting a common valley confusion). Selby Vose, the Association's manager, was Gideon Vose's son, not nephew, despite a persistent rumor (r18, r23 — both emphatic, independent denials). A separate rumor that the Voses and Keddies were themselves related by blood is explicitly and carefully refuted by Duncan, who states his mother — a woman who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection" — never once made the claim (r18); Cleve Oram nonetheless repeats the rumor secondhand, admitting he "could not draw... the line on paper" (r21).

The station test book stayed in Keddie's own hand from 1896 until impounded by the arbitration board in March 1925, then held at the Ashlin Cooperative office 1925–1959, then transferred to the Ordell County Historical Rooms (r18). The Brant weigh book passed from Emmet (began 1 Jan 1897, per Duncan's careful account) to Roy at Emmet's death in 1929, was lent — not given — to Ivy Keddie in 1958 for her college work, returned, and passed to Hazel at Roy's death in 1962, reaching the Historical Rooms in 1963 (r09, r17, r18 — all agree it was a loan, a point both lending families insisted on precisely). Strawn's circuit notebooks went to the state dairy office in 1936, then to the Farland college library in 1947, where Ivy found the crucial 1923 volume in 1958 (r18). The crate of condemned glass itself sat in the loft from 1923 until Ivy retrieved it in 1958, was measured at Farland, and was deposited with the Historical Rooms in 1959 alongside the test book (r18). By 1970 the five key documents/objects were housed in three separate buildings — "and none of them ours," as Duncan wryly notes (r18).

**Documents:** r18 (Duncan Keddie), r17 (Hazel Brant), r21 (Cleve Oram), r23 (Kyle), r09 (Roy Brant).

# Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**
Ivy is Ansel's **granddaughter**, daughter of his son Duncan (b. 1894), Ivy herself born 1931 (r18, explicit: "my daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter... I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise"). Cleve Oram independently calls her "the granddaughter" (r21). The *Ashlin Chronicle*, however, calls her Ansel's "great-niece" (r19), and county agent Falke's report originally repeated that error before appending an explicit correction: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine" (r22). This is a rare case where the sources resolve their own conflict — I follow Duncan (a primary family source with every reason to be exact) and Falke's verified correction, and treat the Chronicle's "great-niece" as a fast-turnaround newspaper error.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**
They are **second cousins**: Ivy's father Duncan and Hazel's father Roy were first cousins (children of siblings Orra Keddie and Emmet Brant), making their own children — Ivy and Hazel — second cousins (r09, r17, r18). Both Hazel and Duncan explicitly flag and correct the common local error of calling them cousins-once-removed or aunt/niece; Cleve Oram nonetheless makes exactly that error, calling them "first cousins once removed" (r21). I resolve for second cousins on the strength of two independent, first-person family accounts that specifically address and reject the alternative, against one outsider's admittedly uncertain recollection. What the sources leave open: neither generation's birth order or the precise year Emmet and Orra's own parents' second marriage occurred is given, though it does not affect the cousin math.

**B3. Selby Vose's father, and why it matters.**
Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**, the Association's founder, first president (1889–1911), and author of Article VII (r02, r18, r23 — the "nephew" rumor is explicitly and independently denied twice, by Duncan and by clerk Bertram Kyle, the latter noting he'd "heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense"). It matters because Selby inherited not just the manager's chair (from at least 1913, see Section E) but the institutional authority and credibility of the founding family; he signed the 1919 water-suspicion circular under his own name alone (r23), and as manager he signed every annual ledger sheet carrying the unexplained "station gain" line without, by his own clerk's account, reading the line itself (r23) — meaning the founding family's standing shaped whose theory (water) got institutional backing, and whose evidence (the ledger anomaly) went unexamined for decades.

**B4. The Vose–Keddie family relationship.**
The sources establish **no blood relationship**. A persistent valley rumor claimed the families were cousins "of some sort" (r21, hedged and unverified — Oram admits he "could not draw... the line on paper"). Duncan Keddie devotes a section of his family notes specifically to refuting this ("A thing that is said and is not so"), stating his mother, a woman who tracked every family connection in the valley obsessively, never once made this claim, and that the confusion likely arises from mixing up the Ashlin Voses (unrelated to Gideon's line) with Gideon's own family (r18). I follow Duncan's detailed, source-explicit denial over Oram's admittedly unverifiable hearsay.
