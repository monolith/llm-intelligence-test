# Answers: Section A, Stories 1–4

## Story 1: The Larrow Valley Creamery Association's Formation and Governance (1889–1925+)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was organized in 1889 by farmers meeting at Larrow Green to centralize milk testing and payment. Gideon Vose, founder and first president, held office twenty-two years (1889–1911). The organization built a main creamery at Larrow Green in 1891 on Larrow Creek. Before this cooperative structure, farmers sold milk wherever they could at individual prices; the creamery's innovation was to pool milk, test it collectively, and pay each farmer according to butterfat content rather than weight.

The organization's crucial rule was Article VII, adopted in 1892 (though the printed by-laws carry no date—only "adopted at the annual meeting"). It stated: "Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." This last clause—that the station's own test governs—became the hinge of the entire Ostrey Hollow business, as no appeal or correction was possible.

The Association's structure included Selby Vose (Gideon's son, not nephew, though confusion persisted; manager 1913–1938), Effie Loomis (secretary 1901–1940), and Wilbur Teague (bookkeeper 1905–1945). Teague kept meticulous ledgers showing butterfat credited to patrons at each station's test, butterfat actually received at Larrow Green, and the persistent "station gain" difference. Over the association's early years, 1,000 cans per week were tested at Larrow Green.

In January 1925, patrons of Ostrey Hollow filed a petition claiming chronic underpayment. A board of arbitration convened under attorney Alonzo Frick, meeting from March 17 to May 4 at Ashlin and the station itself. The board heard everyone at length, found no cause established against anyone, yet awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800 "without admission"—a contradiction that struck contemporaries as strange.

## Story 2: Building and Equipping Ostrey Hollow Station (1895–1923)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green, to serve the farms of that end, principally Hessel Bottom. The station opened May 4, 1896, on a flat by the creek where the road bends. It was a two-story board building with a shingle roof, containing an intake room with scale and vat, a separator in back, a covered platform at wagon height, and a loft above. Milk was weighed in, sampled, tested for butterfat, separated; cream was hauled down to Larrow Green in cans on a flat wagon, and skim was returned to the patron for hogs and calves.

On April 14, 1897—less than a year after opening—the Association ordered its testing outfit from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet: six dozen milk test pipettes at 17.6 c.c. (@$0.35 each), twelve dozen test bottles with 8% neck, one acid measure, one hand tester (four-bottle), and freight. Total invoice: $77.93. The station had borrowed its first glassware from Larrow Green creamery; this new outfit went into use May 1, 1897, and was expected to last.

The glassware remained in daily use for twenty-six years without inspection or replacement until June 12, 1923, when state dairy inspector Merle Strawn arrived on circuit. He ran the station's pipettes against a calibrated burette (a measuring instrument)—standard practice under the state's new burette program instituted in 1921. Strawn found immediately that the pipettes "would not deliver their mark." He condemned the entire crate of glassware on the spot and directed the manager to order replacements. The Association ordered new glass from Ammon & Sons on June 28, 1923; new glassware was in use by July/August 1923. The condemned crate, holding nine surviving pipettes and assorted bottles, was carried to the loft over the intake room and left there.

The old glassware sat untouched in that loft for thirty-five years, during two sittings of the 1925 arbitration hearing held directly below it (twenty feet, if that), unknown to everyone who walked through that room.

## Story 3: Ansel Keddie at Work (1896–1924)

Ansel Keddie was born in 1861 and came to the Ostrey Hollow station at its opening in 1896, where he worked as tester and separator man for twenty-nine seasons until his dismissal in 1925. He did not miss a single working day. He was a man of meticulous habits who wore a canvas apron with a strap over one shoulder and held test bottles up to the light with his arm straight out—a gesture his grandson's children would remember decades later. He never took a day off and never complained.

His daily routine began at 5:30 a.m. He would have the intake room open, lamps lit, and scale swinging free before the first wagon arrived in the dark. Every can was weighed; the weight was called and written down before the milk went anywhere else. The scale was tested monthly by the state dairy inspector and never disputed. From each patron's milk, Keddie drew a sample into a numbered jar. He prepared the sample the night before—warming it to blood-warm throughout, pouring it vessel to vessel until cream carried through the whole, because an unwarm, unmixed sample was worthless. On testing morning, he measured the charge precisely, poured it into a patron's numbered test bottle, added acid, turned the bottle until milk darkened, put four bottles into a hand tester weighted against each other, covered it, turned the crank at the speed he was taught (and no man made him hurry), read the fat in the neck's clear column with dividers, and wrote down the figure. He made tests same order, same hour, every working day of every season. Over twenty-nine seasons, he tested 1,000 cans per week without exception.

His test book was kept in his own hand from May 4, 1896 to 1924, ruled exactly like print, never a blot. Five columns: date, patron, pounds, test, fat. Where he made an error, he crossed it with his initials and never wrote over a figure—"because a figure written over another figure is a figure no man can swear to afterward." The first leaf read: "Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie." In 1922 he created a summary leaf showing the average test every year: "1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80."

He was a quiet man, not given to explanation. Colleagues said of him that he tested as taught and knew no other trade. The state dairy inspector noted that he "works slow and clean, reads own figure without coaxing." Merchants said of him that he would stand in a room by himself with the separator running, door shut, and nobody could know what happened—yet forty years of books show he never took a pound of cream. He married Orra Brant, the younger sister of Emmet Brant, the largest patron on Hessel Bottom, a man who hauled the most milk of anyone on the creek. That fact sat in the middle of the family for a generation and hardly anyone outside it noticed.

## Story 4: The Nineteen Hundredths Shortfall (1897–1923)

From the earliest records, the milk tested at Ostrey Hollow ran consistently lower than milk tested elsewhere. This was not a small or occasional difference; it was flat, regular, and visible in annual averages year after year. The station's average over the long complaint period stood at 3.61 butterfat percent. Larrow Green creamery's own average over the same years was 3.78 to 3.80. The deficiency ran at exactly 0.19 percentage points—nineteen hundredths—in every month: in the flush of June and the depth of February alike, in wet years and dry years, with no seasonal variation whatsoever. Over twenty-five full delivery years (1898–1922), this gap never wavered.

The first written complaint was made in 1902 at the general store—men said Ostrey Hollow milk didn't test with the rest. In 1908, a committee of the Association went up the valley and sampled the Hessel Bottom herds in January, February, and March. They reported that the milk was whole, scales were honest, and their opinion was that wet hay of the bottom thinned cream. (This explanation would be tested by the State Agricultural College and found insufficient: bog hay depressed tests by at most five hundredths in winter, nothing measurable in summer—far too small to account for nineteen hundredths flat across all seasons.)

Year after year, the patrons complained. Year after year, the officers of the Association heard it and said nothing publicly. The bookkeeper, Wilbur Teague, entered the same line in the ledger every year: butterfat credited to patrons on the station test; butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in the station's cream; the difference as "station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account." Teague testified later that a figure with a name on it feels explained, and you rule under it and move on. "A figure that jumps about will keep you awake. A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture."

Over the full twenty-five-year period, the station received 40 million pounds of milk (1.6 million pounds per year average, never varying greatly). At the recorded 3.61 percent, patrons were credited with 1,444,000 pounds of butterfat. At the true 3.80 percent they actually delivered, they should have been credited with 1,520,000 pounds. The difference: 76,000 pounds of butterfat worth $19,000 at the average price of 25 cents per pound paid over the period.

No one understood why this happened because no one looked at the scale or the acid or the bottles or the testing procedure itself. The tests were what Keddie wrote down; Keddie was meticulous; the rule said his test governed; therefore there was nothing to explain. Nineteen hundredths was simply the Ostrey Hollow tax on milk.

**Documents:** Retelling 1 (Vira Toland, father's experience), Retelling 2 (Effie Loomis, recording the 1908 committee and 1925 data), Retelling 3 (Ansel Keddie, noting complaints from 1902 onward), Retelling 4 (Ivy Keddie, calculating the mathematics of the gap and its proportional impact on the richest milk), Retelling 5 (Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper's testimony to how "as usual" stopped inquiry), Retelling 6 (Merle Strawn, state inspector, observing the pattern over his circuit), Retelling 22 (Orin Falke, county agent, summarizing the whole business for the record).

## Story 5: The Broken Measure Interval (May 8–July 10, 1911)

On May 8, 1911, the station's own measuring glass broke during daily work. The creamery had no replacement on hand. Ansel Keddie borrowed a measure from Larrow Green creamery and switched to using it immediately. This borrowed measure was used for nine weeks until July 10, 1911, when his own replacement glass arrived and was put back into service.

During those nine weeks—exactly six weeks and two days, May 8 through July 10—every other variable remained identical: the herds were the same, the pasture was the same (a flush season), the men were the same, the machinery was the same, the acid was the same, the bottles were the same, the acid measure was the same, Keddie himself was the same, and the patrons delivering milk were the same. Emmet Brant continued to haul the most milk. The only change was the measuring glass charged with drawing the milk sample.

During those nine weeks, the station's tests averaged 3.80 percent. This was the "normal" figure seen in 1896 (the first season) and the figure that reappeared every year thereafter when the faulty glass was in use. When Keddie resumed using his own glass on July 10, the tests immediately fell back to 3.61 percent, the level they had held since 1898.

Keddie noted this fact in his test book. Beside the dates where the change occurred (May 8 and July 10), he wrote a question mark and left it there for fourteen years. He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove. In his 1925 testimony, he said: "Cannot tell why figures are what they are." He never answered his own question mark. Yet he never erased it either.

This interval is treated retrospectively as an involuntary experiment. It establishes that something about the measuring device—not the cows, not the feed, not the person doing the testing, not the rest of the apparatus—was responsible for the 19-hundredth shortfall. It does not establish *what* was wrong with the glass or *why* that particular error occurred. The interval proves the instrument was faulty but does not explain the mechanism. Nevertheless, it decisively ruled out every other theory: the wet hay of Hessel Bottom, the water hypothesis, the tester's dishonesty—all collapsed when the borrowed measure was in use.

## Story 6: The 1919 Water Letter and Family Rupture (June 1919 and After)

In June 1919, Selby Vose, the manager of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, circulated a printed letter to patrons of the Ostrey Hollow station. The letter, signed by Selby Vose, warned them that "there was water in the cans coming down from Ostrey Hollow." The letter went to nine families on Hessel Bottom—six farms, but nine family households (some farms held more than one family). Copies were printed and mailed or delivered.

Ansel Keddie, the tester, believed this letter to be unjust. He took the decision to carry the letter himself to every recipient rather than have the Association mail them or have another man deliver them. He went from house to house, stood at the doorway while each man read it, and delivered them personally. He came home at eleven at night with his hat still on—something he never did otherwise. His wife Orra asked no questions and recorded only the fact.

Her brother Emmet Brant, the largest patron on Hessel Bottom, received a copy. Two days after the letter arrived, Emmet came up to the station. He did not come into the house. He stood in the yard and said to Ansel that he had hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened, and Ansel knew it better than any man living. Then he asked him: why did you carry this paper yourself if you don't believe it?

Ansel's answer was: He carried it because he would not have another man carry it.

Emmet went home. He did not return to Ansel and Orra's house. He never came back in, not for Christmas, not for family meetings, not for Sunday dinners. The two brothers-in-law—who had eaten at the same table for thirty-six years—never spoke again. Orra, Emmet's sister, was caught in the middle. The state dairy inspector's notebook records that when Strawn visited in 1919, he ran the lactometer test (which detects added water) at Ostrey Hollow and found none—he wrote: "I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any." But the letter had already done its damage.

The rift lasted for six years until Emmet's death in 1929. Orra went down to Emmet's house after word reached her. Roy (Emmet's son and the new weigh-book keeper) met her at the gate and was kind. She sat an hour in the kitchen where she had been a girl and came home. The matter was settled the way such things get settled—by death, not reconciliation.

No one ever proved the water accusation. No one ever retracted it. No court heard it. The letter simply existed in drawers throughout the valley, proof that some men on Hessel Bottom had once been accused of cheating.

**Documents:** Retelling 1 (Vira Toland, recounting how the letter came to her father's table with rain on his shoulders), Retelling 3 (Ansel's own account of the letter's impact and his insistence he never stole cream), Retelling 6 (Merle Strawn's notation that he found no water at Ostrey Hollow), Retelling 17 (Hazel Brant, recounting the letter and her mother's response), Retelling 18 (Duncan Keddie, documenting the rupture and his mother's grief), Retelling 22 (Orin Falke's county report mentioning the 1919 circular), Retelling 23 (Bertram Kyle, the clerk who typed the letter), Retelling 24 (Orra Keddie's diary: June 1919 entry about the circular, June entry two days after about Emmet's confrontation, Christmas 1919 about Duncan's brief visit, and 1929 about Emmet's death).

## Story 7: The 1925 Arbitration Hearing and Dismissal (January–April 1925)

In January 1925, the patrons of Ostrey Hollow submitted a petition to the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. They claimed that for a generation, milk delivered to their station had been tested low, resulting in chronic underpayment. They asked to be made whole.

The Association's board of directors voted to submit the matter to arbitration. A board of arbitration was constituted under the chairmanship of Alonzo Frick, an attorney from Ashlin, described as an able and impartial man. The board had authority to hear evidence, examine records, and make an award binding on the Association.

The proceedings began on March 17, 1925, and continued through May 4. Most sittings were held at Ashlin in the Association's office, but two crucial sessions were held at the station itself in the weigh room. This was necessary because the scale, the measuring apparatus, the separator machinery, the test book, and the cream-receiving books all stood at Ostrey Hollow. The board wanted to examine them in place. Men traveled up the valley on wagons. Bystanders stood outside in the road in the rain, listening to voices from inside, unable to hear words. Twenty feet above the room where the hearing was held, in the loft above the intake room, sat the wooden crate containing the condemned glassware, undisturbed, unknown to everyone present.

The board heard testimony from:
- Ansel Keddie (the tester), who presented his nine-sentence statement and his unsent letter explaining his testing procedure
- Wilbur Teague (the bookkeeper), who testified about the "station gain" line and the consistency of the 5.25% loss
- Selby Vose (the manager), who offered his theory about water and the recent rise in tests
- Officers and patrons

The board examined:
- Ansel Keddie's test book, kept by his own hand for twenty-nine seasons
- The Brant weigh book, kept by Emmet Brant, showing his own scale weights and the station's corresponding test weights
- The receiving and churn books of Larrow Green creamery
- The state dairy inspector's circuit notebooks

Four documents were carried in and out by Bertram Kyle, the clerk. He and no other man laid them side by side to compare.

After six weeks of hearings, the board issued its finding: "The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established." This sentence was described later as the only sentence in the business arrived at by men under oath with the books in front of them. Yet immediately following it, the board awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons three thousand eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account.

The award was a contradiction: no cause found, yet money paid. The Ostrey Hollow men took it as what everyone would expect a farmer to take it—a confession without words.

On April 30, 1925, four days before the formal award was handed down, Ansel Keddie was dismissed from his post of twenty-nine years. He came up the road at his ordinary hour, rolled his canvas apron under his arm, hung it behind the shed, and asked his wife what there was for supper. There was ham.

He lived nine more years, working as a carpenter (he hung a barn door for a neighbor that swung true for thirty years), never speaking of the business except once a year in April. His wife Orra outlived him by fourteen years, keeping every letter and never speaking of it either except one day each April. In 1934, Ansel died with the valley's judgment still on him.

## Story 8: The 1958 Exoneration (February–October 1958 and Legacy)

In the winter of 1958, Ivy Keddie, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, went up the Ostrey Hollow station valley to do a research project on the Association's history. She was the granddaughter of Ansel Keddie, the tester who had been dismissed in 1925. She was also the daughter of Duncan Keddie, who had been a schoolteacher at Ashlin and had written down the family genealogies precisely for this reason—so the history would not be lost or confused.

In February 1958, in the loft above the station's intake room, Ivy found a wooden crate stenciled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET." Inside, packed in straw, was the station's original glassware, condemned on June 12, 1923, and never removed. The crate held pipettes and bottles, some broken from thirty-five years of neglect, but nine pipettes intact.

On March 9, 1958, Ivy carried the nine surviving pipettes to the dairy laboratory at the State Agricultural College. She measured each one against a calibrated burette—a precision instrument used to measure liquids to the milliliter. The nine pipettes had been blown on the stem to mark 17.6 cubic centimeters, the standard charge of milk for a butterfat test, the charge the graduated neck of every test bottle is ruled for.

Not one of them delivered the marked volume. The measurements were: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean: 16.72 c.c. Short: 0.88 c.c. That is exactly 95 hundredths of the mark—or, stated differently, each pipette was short by 5 percent.

The mathematics followed immediately. If a charge that should be 17.6 c.c. is actually 16.72 c.c., then every butterfat reading is proportionally low: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95. A test that reads as 3.61 percent, when made with a pipette that delivers 95 percent of the required charge, represents milk that actually tested at 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80 percent.

Over the full delivery years 1898–1922 (25 complete years), the station had received 40 million pounds of milk at an average price of 25 cents per pound of butterfat. At the recorded 3.61 percent, patrons were credited with 1,444,000 pounds of butterfat. At the true 3.80 percent, they delivered 1,520,000 pounds. The difference: 76,000 pounds of butterfat worth $19,000 at average prices paid over the period.

The board of arbitration in 1925 had awarded the patrons $3,800 without admission. That was one-fifth of the actual loss.

In October 1958, working with Professor Leland Vaught of the college's department of dairy husbandry, Ivy published her findings in State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 471. The closing paragraph read:

"No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

Ansel Keddie had tested the milk in front of the state's own man. He had tested it the way he was taught. He had tested it every working day of his life without fail. The reason his figures would not come right was not in him; it was in a piece of glass that hung in a crate over his head the entire time, unknown to anyone.

Ansel had been dismissed April 30, 1925. He died February 1934—nine years after dismissal, twenty-four years before his exoneration. His widow Orra had died in 1948, fourteen years before the truth was published. The Ostrey Hollow patrons who had brought the petition were mostly dead. The board of arbitration was long dispersed. Selby Vose, the manager, was dead. Only the ledgers, the books, the crate, and the arithmetic remained to tell what had happened.

Ivy Keddie said to the *Ashlin Chronicle*: "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not. He tested the milk in front of the state's own man and he tested it the way he was taught, and the reason his figures would not come right was in a crate over his head the entire time. I would like it printed that he never knew. It would have been a kindness if somebody in this county had thought to climb a ladder."

# Section B — Relationships

## B1: What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Ansel married Orra Brant in 1889. Their son was Duncan Keddie, born 1894, who became a schoolteacher at Ashlin. Duncan's daughter was Ivy Keddie, born 1931. This makes Ivy the direct lineal descendant of Ansel—one generation down from Duncan, two generations down from Ansel. The *Ashlin Chronicle* mistakenly called Ivy Ansel's "great-niece" in its October 1958 article, but Duncan Keddie's family notes (1968) and the county agent Orin Falke's report (1959, with a correction added) both clarify that Ivy was granddaughter, not great-niece. Falke explicitly withdrew the error: "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine."

## B2: What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant?

Ivy and Hazel were second cousins. Hazel's father was Roy Brant; Roy's father was Emmet Brant. Emmet's sister was Orra Keddie (Ansel's wife). Orra's son was Duncan Keddie. Duncan's daughter was Ivy. Therefore: Emmet and Orra were siblings; Emmet's son Roy was Orra's nephew; Roy's daughter Hazel was Orra's grand-niece; and Orra's granddaughter Ivy and Hazel (Roy's daughter) were second cousins—the children of first cousins (Duncan and Roy, who were children of siblings Orra and Emmet). Duncan Keddie's genealogical notes (1968) state explicitly: "Roy's daughter is Hazel Brant. My daughter is Ivy. Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece, which people say because it is shorter."

The sources leave open no missing generation or relationship detail; the genealogy is fully connected through the Brant-Keddie family tie.

## B3: Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter?

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose, the founder and first president of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (1889–1911). Selby was Gideon's son, not his nephew—a distinction that Duncan Keddie's 1968 family notes emphasize: "Selby Vose, who managed the Association, was Gideon Vose's son. Gideon organized the Association and was its first president and wrote its by-laws; Selby was his son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew."

This matters because Gideon Vose wrote Article VII of the by-laws in 1892—the rule that every patron is paid by the station's own test and no other. No appeal, no correction. When the 1925 arbitration board examined the books, they inherited this rule and could not overturn it. The test had governed payment for thirty-three years (1892–1925). Selby Vose, as manager from 1913 onward, signed the annual ledger sheets showing the "station gain" line every year. He knew what that line was doing (Wilbur Teague states plainly: "A man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him"). Yet he did nothing to investigate. In 1919 he sent the water accusation letter. In 1924 he pointed to the rise in tests as proof of water having been added. He never questioned the glassware or examined the measuring apparatus until the state inspector condemned it in June 1923. By that point, 26 years of systematic error were complete.

## B4: What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?

No genealogical or documented relationship existed between the Vose and Keddie families. Duncan Keddie's 1968 notes state plainly: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either." He goes on: "I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her sit down. It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me."

However, Cleve Oram's 1977 oral account mentions unconfirmed family gossip: "I will say this while I am on families, because you will hear it anyway and you may as well hear it from somebody who is not repeating it out of malice. The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley. My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper." This hearsay is unconfirmed and specifically called into question by Duncan Keddie's definitive family research.

No blood or marriage tie between Vose and Keddie is documented in the historical record. The two families' interaction was entirely institutional—Vose as manager, Keddie as employee—not personal.
# Answers: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math (14 points)

### C1: The Error Proportioned to the Glassware

**(a) Proportion by which tests differed:**

The nine surviving pipettes from the crate measured mean 16.72 c.c. when they were marked 17.6 c.c. at the stem. 

Short amount: 17.6 – 16.72 = 0.88 c.c.

Proportion: 0.88 ÷ 17.6 = 0.05 = **5%** (or stated as a ratio, one part in twenty).

The pipettes delivered 95% of their marked volume. Therefore, every butterfat reading made with them was proportionally low: the recorded reading is 95% of the true reading, or equivalently, the true reading is the recorded reading divided by 0.95.

**(b) True average test and points short:**

Recorded station average: 3.61%
True average: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80%
Gap: 3.80 – 3.61 = **0.19 percentage points (19 hundredths)**

**(c) Pounds of butterfat unpaid:**

Total milk delivered (25 full years, 1898–1922): 40,000,000 pounds

At recorded 3.61%: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 pounds butterfat credited
At true 3.80%: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 pounds butterfat delivered

Difference: 1,520,000 – 1,444,000 = **76,000 pounds of butterfat not credited** (this same figure appears in Bulletin 471)

**(d) Worth and fraction received:**

Worth at 25¢/lb average price: 76,000 × 0.25 = **$19,000 (total loss)**

Board awarded 1925: **$3,800**

Fraction received: 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = 0.20 = **one-fifth (20%)**

### C2: Emmet Brant's 1910 Account

Emmet Brant kept a personal weigh book. For 1910, the recorded leaf states: "Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42."

**(a) Butterfat credited in 1910:**
200,000 pounds × 0.0342 = **6,840 pounds** (at recorded 3.42%)

**(b) Butterfat actually delivered and not credited:**
True test: 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60%
True butterfat: 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 pounds
Owed but not credited: 7,200 – 6,840 = **360 pounds**

**(c) Value of the difference that year:**
360 × 0.25 = **$90** (at 25¢/pound, the period average)

[Note: The error fell hardest on the richest milk. A herd recorded at 4.56 was short 0.24 points; a herd at 3.42 was short 0.18 points. Emmet's milk at 3.42 took a relatively smaller loss than higher-butterfat herds, but still lost.]

### C3: Station Gain as Percentage

Over the 25-year period:

Butterfat credited to patrons on station test: 1,444,000 pounds
Butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in station cream: 1,520,000 pounds
Difference (station gain): 1,520,000 – 1,444,000 = 76,000 pounds

**Two valid percentages:**
- As percentage of fat delivered: 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = 0.05 = **5%**
- As percentage of fat credited: 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 0.0525 = **5.25%**

The difference is which denominator you use. A bookkeeper naturally reckons surplus against the figure already entered (the credited amount), hence 5.25%. The glass itself accounts for 5% (one part in twenty). Ivy Keddie's 1958 measurement report noted both: "Neither figure is an error. But five percent is the one that is also the glass."

### C5: Duration and Delivery Years

**(a) How long faulty glass was in use and why the numbers differ:**

- **Period of use:** May 1, 1897 to June 12, 1923 = **26 years, 1 month, 12 days** (over 26 years)
- **Full delivery years included in damage calculation:** 1898–1922 = **25 full years**

These are different because:
1. The station opened May 4, 1896 but did not put the faulty glassware into use until May 1, 1897.
2. 1897 was a partial year (May–December only, or possibly the first full year is 1898).
3. The glass was condemned June 12, 1923, meaning new glass was in use from July/August 1923 onward.
4. The damage calculation (40 million pounds, 25 years) uses only complete years where both intake and output records are solid and comparable: 1898–1922.
5. Strawn's 1923 entry notes new glass was in use "since July"; 1924 entry notes "Tests improved; their book gives year at 3.80."

Why both numbers matter: the duration tells us how long the equipment was faulty; the delivery-year window tells us the period for which we can confidently calculate money damages using comparable ledgers.

**(b) First season milk (1896):**

The station opened May 4, 1896. It borrowed glass from Larrow Green for that first season. Its own glass arrived in 1897. Station account records in the ledgers begin 1897. 

The office files show no ledger entry for 1896 milk at the station. ## Section D — Logic (10 points)

### D1: The 1911 Measure Interval as an Experiment

**When and how long:**
- Broke: May 8, 1911
- Repaired/replaced: July 10, 1911
- Duration: Exactly 9 weeks (63 days, or May 8 to July 10 inclusive)

**Variable:** The measuring glass (borrowed vs. own)

**Held constant:**
- Same tester (Ansel Keddie, same man, same hands, same practice, same training)
- Same patrons and herds (including Emmet Brant's largest herd)
- Same season (May through July, summer flush period, when quality should be highest and most consistent)
- Same pasture and feed (green grass, early summer)
- Same acid, bottles, bottles' marking, hand tester, crank speed
- Same procedure and timing
- Same weather (consecutive days)
- Same separator machinery
- No intervention, no change to farm management

**Result:**
- During borrowed measure (May 8 – July 10): average test **3.80%**
- Before break (1898–May 7): average test **3.61%**
- After return to own glass (July 10 – 1924): average test **3.61%**
- The change back to 3.61 was immediate upon resuming own glass July 10

**What it establishes:**
The 19-hundredth shortfall was caused by the measuring apparatus, not by the milk, the cows, the feed, the tester's honesty, the acid, the bottles, or the procedures. When the apparatus changed, the result changed proportionally. When it returned, the result returned. This is strong evidence of instrumental (not operational or biological) causation.

**What it does NOT establish:**
- The mechanism: how or why the glass delivers 95% of its mark. (The 1958 burette test answered this; the 1911 interval alone did not.)
- The precise error: the interval shows the problem is the glass, not its magnitude or nature in detail.
- The age at which the glass developed the error: the break occurred in 1911, but the glass may have been slightly short since 1897, or may have degraded gradually. The interval shows the new (borrowed) glass read at 3.80 (true level), but does not tell us whether Ansel's glass was short in 1897 or became short later.

---

### D2: Three Independent Reasons Why Ansel Keddie Could Not Have Been Stealing Cream

**Reason 1 (Operating constraint): The scale was the gateway.**

Wilbur Teague (bookkeeper, 1905–1945) testified: "Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons. One man weighed milk, drew sample, made test, wrote figure pay slip made from, ran separator. No second man in building spring to fall... A business which is arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken from it, and no set of books ever made can prevent it. The remedy is two men or a lock, and the Association had neither."

Yet Ansel never took cream. The test book was in his hand; the ledger gains matched his test perfectly; if he had skimmed, his tests would have to be lower by more than 5% to cover both the loss and the cream remaining. He would have had to conspire with Teague, who certified every year that the figures balanced. The system allowed theft; Ansel chose not to take the opportunity.

**Reason 2 (The 1911 Experiment): Same man, different glass, tests rose.**

Merle Strawn (state dairy inspector), Ivy Keddie (measuring the glassware in 1958), and Duncan Keddie (Ansel's son, explaining the 1911 mystery) all attest: when Ansel used the borrowed measure for nine weeks in May–July 1911, the tests rose from 3.61 to 3.80 and fell back immediately when he returned to his own glass. Ansel was not accused of stealing in 1911; he was the same man doing the same work with the same integrity. The difference was glass. If Ansel had been taking cream every day, his recorded numbers would jump with the new measure too—but they did not jump because of cream removal; they jumped because the measure was short by 5%. The borrowed measure was not short (or was short in the opposite direction), so it read true. This proves Ansel's theft hypothesis is inconsistent with the 1911 interval itself.

**Reason 3 (The pattern is wrong for theft): Proportional, not fixed, loss.**

If Ansel had stolen a fixed amount of cream every day (say 50 lbs), his recorded tests would understate by a *fixed* amount, not a *proportional* amount. A herd recorded at 4.56 would be off by the same fixed pounds as a herd at 3.42. Instead, Ivy Keddie's 1958 measurement shows the loss was proportional: a herd that actually tested at 4.56 was recorded at 4.32 (short 0.24); a herd at 3.42 was recorded at 3.26 (short 0.16, proportionally less). This is exactly what a short measuring pipette produces—proportional error—not what a thief taking a fixed daily amount produces. The evidence pattern is incompatible with systematic theft.

**Independence of reasons:**

- Reason 1 (the system allowed theft but Ansel didn't) is about opportunity and choice.
- Reason 2 (1911 interval) is about the same man with different equipment producing different results.
- Reason 3 (proportional error pattern) is about the mathematics of the loss relative to milk quality.

These three arguments do not depend on each other. Reason 1 alone shows the system didn't force honesty (he could have stolen). Reason 2 alone shows that the tester's behavior didn't change (he was the same man). Reason 3 alone shows that the loss pattern matches instrumental error, not theft.

**Which hold without the 1958 measurement?**

Reasons 1 and 2 both hold without the 1958 glassware test. Reason 1 is purely logical (the system allowed theft, yet no herd recorded extra gain under Ansel; the theft theory requires he stole and recorded loss). Reason 2 is historical (the 1911 interval, documented in his own test book and Strawn's circuit notes in real time, is independent of the 1958 measurement—it was already evidence in 1925 and should have been decisive then).

Reason 3 (the proportional error pattern) can be inferred from the test-book pattern without measuring glass—Ivy's 1958 measurement confirmed it precisely, but the proportional gap was visible in the recorded figures from 1897 onward. The shape of the error (flat proportional loss every month, every season) was incompatible with theft, even before the glass was measured.

**Did the bookkeeper question the entries?**

No. Wilbur Teague testified: "I put it to Manager Vose in 1916. Told to let it alone. Let it alone. I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience, and a bookkeeper who takes his employer's business up a second time after being told once has generally begun to look for another employer." Teague saw the pattern clearly (a figure that came in at five and a quarter every year); he raised it once with the manager; he was silenced. He then entered the same line for decades more without question. He did not publicly contradict the station's figures in the 1925 hearing; he only testified to the technical facts when asked. He did not refute Ansel's character, though he could have. His silence was obedience, not doubt.

---

### D3: Did the Manager Know the Tests Ran Low?

**Answer: Yes, Selby Vose knew the tests ran low before 1923, almost certainly from the ledger sheets he signed every year.**

**Justification:**

Bertram Kyle (office clerk) states: "Mr. Selby Vose sat behind that door for a quarter of a century and I sat outside it... He was courteous to every man who came into that office, farmer or drummer or bank, and he never once let a caller feel he was being hurried. And he read the totals and he never read the lines above them. He was not a man who read. He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign."

Kyle describes Vose as a man who read bottoms (totals) not lines above. However, the "bottom figure" on the station account sheet would have been the station gain or the charge to the general account—a figure that was always there, year after year, reliably 5% or so. Vose signed every year (attested by Kyle, by Teague, and by the ledgers).

Teague testified: "Mr. Vose knew what that account was doing. Signed sheet every year. Spring I laid annual sheet before him, he put name at foot. A man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him."

More directly, in his 1926 manager's address, Vose himself explains the rise in 1924 tests: "Then the Association's attention came to rest on the station, and there was a petition, and a hearing, and men were required to answer questions in front of their neighbors. And in the very next season the tests at that station rose by twenty-one hundredths and the average for 1924 stood at 3.90, which is as good a figure as any station in this county will show you."

Vose attributes the 1924 rise to the public scrutiny (pressure on patrons or tester to behave differently). But his explanation assumes he knew the tests had been low before—otherwise, what would have to rise? He is implicitly acknowledging that the shortfall existed prior to 1924, and that the hearing caused it to correct.

Additionally, in 1919 Vose sent the water letter—an accusation that the Ostrey Hollow patrons were adding water to milk. This letter suggests he had a theory about why the tests ran low. The timing (1919, after years of complaints since 1902) implies he was aware of the pattern and was offering an explanation. If he were unaware the tests ran low, there would be no motivation to send such a letter.

**Conclusion:**

Vose knew or should have known that the station's tests ran low because: (1) he signed the ledger sheets every year showing the station gain; (2) he offered an explanation for the low tests in 1919 (water); (3) he acknowledged in 1926 that the tests had been running low and attributed the 1924 rise to external pressure. He did not investigate the glassware or question the tester's procedure until forced by the state inspector's 1923 condemnation. His knowledge did not translate into action.

## Section F — Theory (6 points)

### The Causal Theory: What Produced the Shortfall at Ostrey Hollow

The Ostrey Hollow station's butterfat test readings ran 5% low from 1897 to 1923 (except for the nine-week interval in 1911) because the six dozen milk-test pipettes purchased from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet on April 14, 1897, were manufactured short by 5%—each blown to deliver 16.72 c.c. instead of the marked 17.6 c.c.

This error was embedded in the molds or the manufacturing process and was not caught by the glassblower or the quality checker at the time of manufacture. The pipettes were packed, shipped, and put into use on May 1, 1897. They were accurate and identical to each other in their shortness, which meant the error was *proportional*, not random. Every patron's test was systematically low by exactly the same 5%.

The shortfall began when the faulty glass arrived (May 1897) and ended when it was condemned and replaced (June 12, 1923). The reason nothing at the station or on the farms had to change is that the error was built into the instrument, not produced by the environment, the operator, or the milk. The tester (Ansel Keddie) was meticulous and made no mistakes. The patrons' herds did not change quality. The seasons did not change. The separator did not break down. The system was working as designed—it was the design (the purchased glassware) that was faulty.

The error persisted for 26 years because:
1. **No one measured the glass against a true standard.** The state dairy inspector's burette program was not instituted until 1921, and Merle Strawn did not run Ostrey Hollow's glass against a burette until his June 1923 visit.
2. **The error was too small and too regular to trigger investigation.** A figure that varies wildly prompts inquiry; a figure that comes in at 5% every year is invisible as error—it becomes "normal," given a name ("station gain"), and filed away without question.
3. **No one compared tests between stations rigorously in writing.** The 19-hundredth gap was whispered about (Emmet Brant, the largest patron, knew his own scale; the state inspector knew his lactometer); but these observations never made it into the Association's formal record before 1925.
4. **The rule (Article VII) that the station's test governs admits no appeal.** Patrons could complain; they could be heard; but the test was sovereign. No one could force the Association to check the test's accuracy until the patrons petitioned en masse in 1925.

### The Three Competing Explanations and Their Refutation

**Explanation 1: Water was added to the milk by the Ostrey Hollow patrons.**

- **Advanced by:** Selby Vose (manager), in his June 1919 circular letter to the nine families.
- **Evidence that defeats it:** 
  - Merle Strawn's lactometer tests (state dairy inspector). His circuit notes, repeatedly: "Lactometer, no added water" (1908, 1909, 1912, 1913, 1916, 1920, 1922). His explicit 1919 entry: "I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him [the manager] so."
  - The 1911 interval proves water is not the cause. If patrons had been adding water, they would have continued during the nine weeks (May–July 1911) when the borrowed measure was in use. Yet the tests rose to 3.80 during that interval, only to fall back to 3.61 when Ansel returned to his own glass. The patrons did not suddenly stop adding water (and then resume it).
  - The State Agricultural College's Bulletin 214 (1911) tested bog hay's effect on butterfat: "depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." This maximum effect (5 hundredths) does not approach the 19-hundredth gap, even combined with water. Orin Falke, county agent, withdrew his initial acceptance of the wet-hay explanation after receiving Bulletin 214: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July. I withdraw the concession made on page four."

**Explanation 2: The tester (Ansel Keddie) was stealing cream and recording false low tests to conceal the loss.**

- **Advanced by:** Officers of the Association, in the 1925 arbitration hearing (implied by the burden placed on Keddie to prove his innocence; explicit in Wilbur Teague's testimony about a system "arranged so that a single man can take a little").
- **Evidence that defeats it:**
  - The 1911 interval. Same tester, same hands, same station, same patrons, same procedures—different glass. Tests rose. If Ansel had been stealing 5% every day and recording 5% low to hide it, his recorded tests would have jumped when the borrowed glass arrived. They did not; they jumped up to 3.80 (true level) because the borrowed glass was accurate. This proves the tester's behavior did not change (he was not secretly stealing); the instrument changed (from short to accurate).
  - Wilbur Teague's own ledgers. If Ansel had stolen cream, Teague would have recorded less cream arriving at Larrow Green than Ansel's test book said was being sent down. Yet Teague's own annual balance showed a consistent 5.25% surplus (the "station gain"). The cream *did* arrive as tested. If Ansel had stolen and recorded low, there would be no surplus; there would be a deficit, or Teague would have caught the discrepancy.
  - The proportional structure of the loss. The error falls hardest on the richest milk (a 4.56% herd was short 0.24 points; a 3.42% herd was short 0.16). This is the proportional error produced by a short measuring pipette, not the fixed-amount theft a person would take ("I take 10 lbs of cream daily, come what may"). A thief's loss would be the same for every herd; the glass's error is proportional to quality.

**Explanation 3: The wet hay of Hessel Bottom (boggy soil, poor-quality feed) caused the cream of that region's cows to test lower.**

- **Advanced by:** The investigating committee in 1908; a veterinarian at Ashlin in 1926; Selby Vose (as a secondary factor, implicitly).
- **Evidence that defeats it:**
  - The State Agricultural College's Bulletin 214 (1911): bog hay reduced butterfat by "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." This is far below the 19-hundredth gap observed year-round.
  - The 1911 broken measure interval. Hessel Bottom's pasture, hay quality, and cows did not change between May 7 and May 8, 1911. Yet the test results changed. If wet hay were the cause, there would be no improvement when the borrowed glass arrived.
  - Comparative test data. Four of the six Hessel Bottom farms hauled their milk in summer flush to the Alder Corners Cheese Factory (across the ridge) and that milk was tested there. Halbert Nym's curator notes (retelling 7): "Over ridge: Alder Corners factory tested Hessel Bottom milk in summer flush. 14 overlapping seasons: same milk ran 19 hundredths higher at Alder Corners than at home." The *same milk*, *same farms*, *same season*—tested by Alder Corners' equipment and got 3.80, tested by Ostrey Hollow and got 3.61. The Hessel Bottom farms' participation in this summer interchange proves their milk quality did not differ at different locations; the *test* differed.

### Subsidiary Questions

**(i) Did the 1925 board of arbitration ever examine the condemned glassware?**

**No.** The glassware was condemned June 12, 1923. The board convened March 17, 1925—nearly two years later. The crate sat in the loft above the intake room. Two of the board's sittings (parts 2 and 3 of the arbitration) were held at the station itself, in the weigh room. Cleve Oram (cream hauler's son, retelling 21) stood outside on the platform listening to the voices and notes: "the crate was up the ladder over their heads the entire time. Twenty feet, if that... The four books [brought to the hearing] I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked." Halbert Nym's curator note (item 7): "Award phrase 'the books and appliances of the station' is form of words, not list of anything."

The record shows no motion, no examination, no evidence introduced from the crate itself. The board's award mentions "the books and appliances of the station" as having been before it, but this phrase is formulaic, not specific. The actual books and appliances examined were the test book, receiving book, weigh book, and separator—not the pipettes themselves.

**(ii) What did the tester himself believe the cause was?**

**Ansel Keddie could not explain the cause and said so explicitly.** In his 1925 statement to the board: "Cannot tell why figures are what they are." In his unsent letter: "Seems to me somebody ought to know how the work was done before everybody who did it is gone." He documented every step of his procedure meticulously. He recorded the 1911 measure breakage and put a question mark beside it (the only uncertainty he allowed in the book in 29 years). But he never answered that question mark.

In his 1922 summary, he noted that his average test from 1898 onward was "3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911... when it stood at 3.80." He was aware of the 1911 anomaly; he suspected something was amiss in 1911 (hence the question mark). But he could not point to what was wrong, and he would not guess. His son Duncan later wrote: "He had, after all, nothing to hold in his hand but a suspicion about a piece of glass, and against it stood the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county."

Ansel's belief was likely this: the glassware. The fact that his tests rose in 1911 and fell back, with no other change, pointed to the measuring device. He marked it in his book with a question mark. But he had no instrument to measure the glass against a true standard (burettes did not appear on his circuit until 1921), and he had no authority to condemn or replace equipment. So he kept on testing, kept on recording, and kept on trusting his procedure and his honesty. He died in 1934 without learning the name of the error—that his glass was short by 5%—but knowing something was wrong.

## Section G — Summary (4 points)

### The Eight-Story History in 150 Words

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, founded in 1889, established a payment rule: farmers are paid by butterfat test, not milk weight; the station's test governs. At Ostrey Hollow skimming station (1896+), tester Ansel Keddie ran meticulous daily records. Yet from 1897 onward, Ostrey Hollow's tests ran 5% low—19 hundredths—year after year. Three theories blamed patrons' water, the tester's theft, or wet hay; all were disproven. In 1919, a manager's water-accusation letter ruptured Ansel's family (estranging him from his wife's brother). A 1925 arbitration found "no cause" yet awarded $3,800 to patrons and dismissed Ansel. In June 1923, a state inspector had condemned the station's original glassware (purchased 1897). In February 1958, Ansel's granddaughter Ivy found the crate in the loft and measured the pipettes: each was short by 5%, explaining the entire 26-year shortfall. Bulletin 471 (October 1958) exonerated Ansel—but he had died 24 years earlier. The patrons had lost $19,000; they received $3,800 (one-fifth).
