# Answers: Section A, Stories 5–8 and Section B

## Story 5: The Broken Measure Interval (May 8–July 10, 1911)

On May 8, 1911, the station's own measuring glass broke during daily work. The creamery had no replacement on hand. Ansel Keddie borrowed a measure from Larrow Green creamery and switched to using it immediately. This borrowed measure was used for nine weeks until July 10, 1911, when his own replacement glass arrived and was put back into service.

During those nine weeks—exactly six weeks and two days, May 8 through July 10—every other variable remained identical: the herds were the same, the pasture was the same (a flush season), the men were the same, the machinery was the same, the acid was the same, the bottles were the same, the acid measure was the same, Keddie himself was the same, and the patrons delivering milk were the same. Emmet Brant continued to haul the most milk. The only change was the measuring glass charged with drawing the milk sample.

During those nine weeks, the station's tests averaged 3.80 percent. This was the "normal" figure seen in 1896 (the first season) and the figure that reappeared every year thereafter when the faulty glass was in use. When Keddie resumed using his own glass on July 10, the tests immediately fell back to 3.61 percent, the level they had held since 1898.

Keddie noted this fact in his test book. Beside the dates where the change occurred (May 8 and July 10), he wrote a question mark and left it there for fourteen years. He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove. In his 1925 testimony, he said: "Cannot tell why figures are what they are." He never answered his own question mark. Yet he never erased it either.

This interval is treated retrospectively as an involuntary experiment. It establishes that something about the measuring device—not the cows, not the feed, not the person doing the testing, not the rest of the apparatus—was responsible for the 19-hundredth shortfall. It does not establish *what* was wrong with the glass or *why* that particular error occurred. The interval proves the instrument was faulty but does not explain the mechanism. Nevertheless, it decisively ruled out every other theory: the wet hay of Hessel Bottom, the water hypothesis, the tester's dishonesty—all collapsed when the borrowed measure was in use.

## Story 6: The 1919 Water Letter and Family Rupture (June 1919 and After)

In June 1919, Selby Vose, the manager of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, circulated a printed letter to patrons of the Ostrey Hollow station. The letter, signed by Selby Vose, warned them that "there was water in the cans coming down from Ostrey Hollow." The letter went to nine families on Hessel Bottom—six farms, but nine family households (some farms held more than one family). Copies were printed and mailed or delivered.

Ansel Keddie, the tester, believed this letter to be unjust. He took the decision to carry the letter himself to every recipient rather than have the Association mail them or have another man deliver them. He went from house to house, stood at the doorway while each man read it, and delivered them personally. He came home at eleven at night with his hat still on—something he never did otherwise. His wife Orra asked no questions and recorded only the fact.

Her brother Emmet Brant, the largest patron on Hessel Bottom, received a copy. Two days after the letter arrived, Emmet came up to the station. He did not come into the house. He stood in the yard and said to Ansel that he had hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened, and Ansel knew it better than any man living. Then he asked him: why did you carry this paper yourself if you don't believe it?

Ansel's answer was: He carried it because he would not have another man carry it.

Emmet went home. He did not return to Ansel and Orra's house. He never came back in, not for Christmas, not for family meetings, not for Sunday dinners. The two brothers-in-law—who had eaten at the same table for thirty-six years—never spoke again. Orra, Emmet's sister, was caught in the middle. The state dairy inspector's notebook records that when Strawn visited in 1919, he ran the lactometer test (which detects added water) at Ostrey Hollow and found none—he wrote: "I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any." But the letter had already done its damage.

The rift lasted for six years until Emmet's death in 1929. Orra went down to Emmet's house after word reached her. Roy (Emmet's son and the new weigh-book keeper) met her at the gate and was kind. She sat an hour in the kitchen where she had been a girl and came home. The matter was settled the way such things get settled—by death, not reconciliation.

No one ever proved the water accusation. No one ever retracted it. No court heard it. The letter simply existed in drawers throughout the valley, proof that some men on Hessel Bottom had once been accused of cheating.

## Story 7: The 1925 Arbitration Hearing and Dismissal (January–April 1925)

In January 1925, the patrons of Ostrey Hollow submitted a petition to the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. They claimed that for a generation, milk delivered to their station had been tested low, resulting in chronic underpayment. They asked to be made whole.

The Association's board of directors voted to submit the matter to arbitration. A board of arbitration was constituted under the chairmanship of Alonzo Frick, an attorney from Ashlin, described as an able and impartial man. The board had authority to hear evidence, examine records, and make an award binding on the Association.

The proceedings began on March 17, 1925, and continued through May 4. Most sittings were held at Ashlin in the Association's office, but two crucial sessions were held at the station itself in the weigh room. This was necessary because the scale, the measuring apparatus, the separator machinery, the test book, and the cream-receiving books all stood at Ostrey Hollow. The board wanted to examine them in place. Men traveled up the valley on wagons. Bystanders stood outside in the road in the rain, listening to voices from inside, unable to hear words. Twenty feet above the room where the hearing was held, in the loft above the intake room, sat the wooden crate containing the condemned glassware, undisturbed, unknown to everyone present.

The board heard testimony from:
- Ansel Keddie (the tester), who presented his nine-sentence statement and his unsent letter explaining his testing procedure
- Wilbur Teague (the bookkeeper), who testified about the "station gain" line and the consistency of the 5.25% loss
- Selby Vose (the manager), who offered his theory about water and the recent rise in tests
- Officers and patrons

The board examined:
- Ansel Keddie's test book, kept by his own hand for twenty-nine seasons
- The Brant weigh book, kept by Emmet Brant, showing his own scale weights and the station's corresponding test weights
- The receiving and churn books of Larrow Green creamery
- The state dairy inspector's circuit notebooks

Four documents were carried in and out by Bertram Kyle, the clerk. He and no other man laid them side by side to compare.

After six weeks of hearings, the board issued its finding: "The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established." This sentence was described later as the only sentence in the business arrived at by men under oath with the books in front of them. Yet immediately following it, the board awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons three thousand eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account.

The award was a contradiction: no cause found, yet money paid. The Ostrey Hollow men took it as what everyone would expect a farmer to take it—a confession without words.

On April 30, 1925, four days before the formal award was handed down, Ansel Keddie was dismissed from his post of twenty-nine years. He came up the road at his ordinary hour, rolled his canvas apron under his arm, hung it behind the shed, and asked his wife what there was for supper. There was ham.

He lived nine more years, working as a carpenter (he hung a barn door for a neighbor that swung true for thirty years), never speaking of the business except once a year in April. His wife Orra outlived him by fourteen years, keeping every letter and never speaking of it either except one day each April. In 1934, Ansel died with the valley's judgment still on him.

## Story 8: The 1958 Exoneration (February–October 1958 and Legacy)

In the winter of 1958, Ivy Keddie, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, went up the Ostrey Hollow station valley to do a research project on the Association's history. She was the granddaughter of Ansel Keddie, the tester who had been dismissed in 1925. She was also the daughter of Duncan Keddie, who had been a schoolteacher at Ashlin and had written down the family genealogies precisely for this reason—so the history would not be lost or confused.

In February 1958, in the loft above the station's intake room, Ivy found a wooden crate stenciled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET." Inside, packed in straw, was the station's original glassware, condemned on June 12, 1923, and never removed. The crate held pipettes and bottles, some broken from thirty-five years of neglect, but nine pipettes intact.

On March 9, 1958, Ivy carried the nine surviving pipettes to the dairy laboratory at the State Agricultural College. She measured each one against a calibrated burette—a precision instrument used to measure liquids to the milliliter. The nine pipettes had been blown on the stem to mark 17.6 cubic centimeters, the standard charge of milk for a butterfat test, the charge the graduated neck of every test bottle is ruled for.

Not one of them delivered the marked volume. The measurements were: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean: 16.72 c.c. Short: 0.88 c.c. That is exactly 95 hundredths of the mark—or, stated differently, each pipette was short by 5 percent.

The mathematics followed immediately. If a charge that should be 17.6 c.c. is actually 16.72 c.c., then every butterfat reading is proportionally low: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95. A test that reads as 3.61 percent, when made with a pipette that delivers 95 percent of the required charge, represents milk that actually tested at 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80 percent.

Over the full delivery years 1898–1922 (25 complete years), the station had received 40 million pounds of milk at an average price of 25 cents per pound of butterfat. At the recorded 3.61 percent, patrons were credited with 1,444,000 pounds of butterfat. At the true 3.80 percent, they delivered 1,520,000 pounds. The difference: 76,000 pounds of butterfat worth $19,000 at average prices paid over the period.

The board of arbitration in 1925 had awarded the patrons $3,800 without admission. That was one-fifth of the actual loss.

In October 1958, working with Professor Leland Vaught of the college's department of dairy husbandry, Ivy published her findings in State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 471. The closing paragraph read:

"No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

Ansel Keddie had tested the milk in front of the state's own man. He had tested it the way he was taught. He had tested it every working day of his life without fail. The reason his figures would not come right was not in him; it was in a piece of glass that hung in a crate over his head the entire time, unknown to anyone.

Ansel had been dismissed April 30, 1925. He died February 1934—nine years after dismissal, twenty-four years before his exoneration. His widow Orra had died in 1948, fourteen years before the truth was published. The Ostrey Hollow patrons who had brought the petition were mostly dead. The board of arbitration was long dispersed. Selby Vose, the manager, was dead. Only the ledgers, the books, the crate, and the arithmetic remained to tell what had happened.

Ivy Keddie said to the *Ashlin Chronicle*: "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not. He tested the milk in front of the state's own man and he tested it the way he was taught, and the reason his figures would not come right was in a crate over his head the entire time. I would like it printed that he never knew. It would have been a kindness if somebody in this county had thought to climb a ladder."

# Section B — Relationships

## B1: What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Ansel married Orra Brant in 1889. Their son was Duncan Keddie, born 1894, who became a schoolteacher at Ashlin. Duncan's daughter was Ivy Keddie, born 1931. This makes Ivy the direct lineal descendant of Ansel—one generation down from Duncan, two generations down from Ansel. The *Ashlin Chronicle* mistakenly called Ivy Ansel's "great-niece" in its October 1958 article, but Duncan Keddie's family notes (1968) and the county agent Orin Falke's report (1959, with a correction added) both clarify that Ivy was granddaughter, not great-niece. Falke explicitly withdrew the error: "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine."

## B2: What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant?

Ivy and Hazel were second cousins. Hazel's father was Roy Brant; Roy's father was Emmet Brant. Emmet's sister was Orra Keddie (Ansel's wife). Orra's son was Duncan Keddie. Duncan's daughter was Ivy. Therefore: Emmet and Orra were siblings; Emmet's son Roy was Orra's nephew; Roy's daughter Hazel was Orra's grand-niece; and Orra's granddaughter Ivy and Hazel (Roy's daughter) were second cousins—the children of first cousins (Duncan and Roy, who were children of siblings Orra and Emmet). Duncan Keddie's genealogical notes (1968) state explicitly: "Roy's daughter is Hazel Brant. My daughter is Ivy. Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece, which people say because it is shorter."

The sources leave open no missing generation or relationship detail; the genealogy is fully connected through the Brant-Keddie family tie.

## B3: Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter?

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose, the founder and first president of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (1889–1911). Selby was Gideon's son, not his nephew—a distinction that Duncan Keddie's 1968 family notes emphasize: "Selby Vose, who managed the Association, was Gideon Vose's son. Gideon organized the Association and was its first president and wrote its by-laws; Selby was his son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew."

This matters because Gideon Vose wrote Article VII of the by-laws in 1892—the rule that every patron is paid by the station's own test and no other. No appeal, no correction. When the 1925 arbitration board examined the books, they inherited this rule and could not overturn it. The test had governed payment for thirty-three years (1892–1925). Selby Vose, as manager from 1913 onward, signed the annual ledger sheets showing the "station gain" line every year. He knew what that line was doing (Wilbur Teague states plainly: "A man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him"). Yet he did nothing to investigate. In 1919 he sent the water accusation letter. In 1924 he pointed to the rise in tests as proof of water having been added. He never questioned the glassware or examined the measuring apparatus until the state inspector condemned it in June 1923. By that point, 26 years of systematic error were complete.

## B4: What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?

No genealogical or documented relationship existed between the Vose and Keddie families. Duncan Keddie's 1968 notes state plainly: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either." He goes on: "I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her sit down. It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me."

However, Cleve Oram's 1977 oral account mentions unconfirmed family gossip: "I will say this while I am on families, because you will hear it anyway and you may as well hear it from somebody who is not repeating it out of malice. The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley. My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper." This hearsay is unconfirmed and specifically called into question by Duncan Keddie's definitive family research.

No blood or marriage tie between Vose and Keddie is documented in the historical record. The two families' interaction was entirely institutional—Vose as manager, Keddie as employee—not personal.
