# Retention Notes: Retellings 1–16

## Retelling 1 — Vira Toland (1979, age 67)

**Narrator**: Born 1912 in Ostrey Hollow; lived there all her life except two years at Ashlin when first married; father hauled milk to the station.

**Ansel Keddie & the Station**: Ran Ostrey Hollow skimming station since 1895 (Vira first rode on wagon when old enough to remember). Station was whitewashed, cold as a spring house even in August, smelled of milk/steam/hot iron. Keddie kept test book meticiously ruled, "like print," never a blot; kept it standing at slope desk by window. Tested 1,000 cans/week, read figures without hunting. Vira's father said if the rest of the world kept accounts like Ansel Keddie, there would be no lawyers in Ashlin.

**The 1919 Letter**: Printed letter came from Association (signed by Selby Vose, manager, nephew of old Gideon Vose, founder). Letter said "there was water in the cans coming down from Ostrey Hollow." Went to nine families including Vira's father. He read it at table with hat still on (he never did this), mother stood at stove not turning around. He read it twice, put it in desk drawer. Some families kept theirs pinned inside trunk lids. Paper was proof of what was said of them.

**Family Rift**: Orra Keddie was Ansel's wife AND Emmet Brant's sister. Before 1919, the two families ate Sunday dinners together. After 1919, they never spoke—not at store, not at funeral. Orra walked down to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane. Years and years, lived within sight of each other's chimney smoke, never said good morning.

**The Unexplained Rise**: "Then the tests went up. All at once, one summer, the figures came up and stayed up, and nobody ever said why. Not the station, not the Association, not anybody." This to Vira was the queerest part—the thing corrected itself and no man stood up and gave a reason.

**The Hearing & Dismissal**: Hearing down at Ashlin (winter and spring after 1919). Lawyers and Association officers, men going down in wagons. Went on and on. At the end Ansel Keddie put out after all those seasons. Patrons got $3,500 (Vira's number; other sources say $3,800). They said he was taking the cream; the tests rose the summer after. "I am not going to dress it up for you. A man stands in a room by himself with the separator running and the door shut and nobody in the valley can add, and after a while he helps himself."

**Ansel's Death**: Died in the thirties, still under it when he died. That sits badly with Vira now that she's old. Whatever he did or did not do, he had all those years of the valley looking at him a certain way in church. Orra outlived him a good while and had it the whole time.

**The College Visitor (1958)**: A young woman from college away east came asking about all this. She was Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed—of the family both sides in a manner of speaking. She sat at Vira's kitchen table, careful, wrote everything down. Vira told her about the letter in her father's drawer. Vira doesn't know what she made of it.

**Final Memory**: "It rained the day of that letter, I remember the wet on my father's shoulders when he came in, and I have never in sixty years been able to think about the one without the other."

## Retelling 2 — Effie Loomis (1941, memoir)

**Narrator & Position**: Secretary of Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association 1901–1940 (40 years). Kept minute book in own hand throughout. Missing first 12 years (1889–1901) as officer, so relies on papers. Missing entire 1889–1892 minute book, lost in 1938 office move—carried out with rest, never arrived.

**Association History**: Organized 1889 at Larrow Green by farmers. Gideon Vose (first president) held office 22 years (1889–1911). Creamery built at Larrow Green 1891 on Larrow Creek. Before creamery, farmers sold milk where they could get price, no common test, no common price, every man's arithmetic his own. Creamery ended that.

**Article VII (adopted 1892)**: "Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." Before: paid for what cans weighed. After: paid for fat found in milk by test at place delivered; weight decided nothing. Rule just: pays for what actually brings, not water naturally in it. **Critical last clause**: "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern"—not creamery test, not valley average, not test made elsewhere. No appeal. Hinge of whole Ostrey Hollow business, written 1892 by Gideon Vose who died 1917 and couldn't be asked about it.

**Officers in Effie's Time**:
- Wilbur Teague: bookkeeper 1905–1945
- Hobart Sill: buttermaker at Larrow Green, kept churn book and cream receiving book
- Selby Vose: manager 1913–1938 (Gideon's son, nephew)
- Effie Loomis: secretary 1901–1940

These four worked together very long time, none idle. **Critical fact**: "not one of us ever went up the valley and stood in the Ostrey Hollow station on a working morning to watch the work being done. I include myself first in that."

**Ostrey Hollow Station**: Stood 7 miles up valley from Larrow Green at head of Hollow. Took milk of farms up there, separated it, sent cream down to Larrow Green, gave skim back to patron. Best milk came off Hessel Bottom (wet meadow below hamlet). Seven farms had rights on Hessel Bottom; largest was Emmet Brant's, station's largest patron in every recorded year.

**The 1925 Petition & Arbitration**:
- **January 1925**: Patrons of Ostrey Hollow petitioned Association
- **Complaint**: Milk never tested as neighbors' milk; paid accordingly very long time; asked to be made whole
- **Board**: Sat at Ashlin 17 March–4 May 1925 under Alonzo Frick, attorney
- **Three explanations argued**: None accepted. Effie won't rehearse them (in minutes, in safe).
- **Award** (recorded by Effie on last day): "The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account."
- **Effie's reaction**: "I have written many things into that book with more pleasure. *Finds no cause established*, and then an award of thirty-eight hundred dollars: I took it down as it was given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once."

**The Quantities** (from bookkeeper's ledgers, Wilbur Teague, verified by Effie):
- 25 years of claims: 40 million pounds of milk
- Average: 1,600,000 pounds/year
- Average price for butterfat: 25 cents/pound
- Over 25 years: credited patrons at 3.61% test

**Critical Gaps in Record**:
1. 1889–1892 minute book lost in 1938 office move
2. **The Crate**: June 1923, state inspector condemned crate of measures at station, set aside and new ones ordered. **Never mentioned in 1925 hearing**. Not a word spoken by patrons, officers, or board. Award phrase "the books and appliances of the station" is form of words, not list of anything. "Forty years of taking down what men say has taught me that the interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying."

## Retelling 3 — Ansel Keddie (Statement & Letter, Spring 1925)

**Statement to Board of Arbitration, Ashlin, 17 March 1925** (9 sentences, no more):
- Kept test book since station opened; every figure his
- Set down date, patron, pounds, test, fat; error crossed with initials, never wrote figure over figure
- Made tests same order, same hour, every working day of every season
- Heard what patrons and officers say but neither came to watch
- Asked to account for figures; figures are what he read and wrote, nothing besides
- Old man, no other trade
- Tested as taught and as state man saw him do, 31 times/week for 29 seasons
- Cannot tell why figures are what they are
- Never taken pound of cream; no man in room thinks he has when by himself

**Unsent Letter, Spring 1925**:

Written because couldn't say it in that room where man allowed to answer but not explain. "Seems to me somebody ought to know how the work was done before everybody who did it is gone."

**Testing Procedure** (detailed):
- Day began 5:30am
- Wagons came up Hollow in dark most year
- Had intake room open, lamps lit, scale swinging free before first wagon at door
- Every can weighed, weight called and written before milk went anywhere
- Scale tested by state man, true; never disputed in 29 seasons
- Sample from each patron's milk in own jar, numbered only to him
- Jar made up 10 days before testing
- Testing morning: jar warmed to blood-warm throughout, poured vessel to vessel, poured again until cream carried through whole
- Sample not warmed and mixed worth nothing
- Charge: milk drawn in measure to mark on stem, tip wiped, blown into patron's numbered bottle
- Acid poured down side, bottle turned until milk dark
- Four bottles in hand tester, weighted against each other, cover down
- Crank turned at speed taught, no faster, no man made him hurry
- Fat up in neck in clear column, read per cent with dividers, written down
- "Not a hard trade. Only a trade that must be done the same way every time, which is a harder thing than it sounds."

**Glass History**:
- Began on in 1896 was borrowed off Ashlin creamery
- Used until own outfit came up valley spring following in crate

**Test Book** (5 columns: date, patron, pounds, test, fat):
- 29 seasons 1896–1924; didn't test 1925
- Where error, crossed it with initials, never wrote over figure
- "Because a figure written over another figure is a figure no man can swear to afterward."
- First leaf: "Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie."

**1922 Summary Leaf** (made because asked often):
"Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80."

**The 1911 Mystery**:
- May 8: "Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure."
- July 10: "Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."
- Question mark let stand 14 years. Could not answer then, cannot now. Will not put anything in place he doesn't know.

**Pattern of Figures**:
- Didn't come and go with season; gap in July as in February, flush and winter, wet and dry years, 1924 average 3.80

**History of Complaints**:
- 1902: First said down at Larrow Green that Ostrey Hollow milk didn't test with rest
- 1908: Committee came up, sampled herds through winter, reported wet hay of Bottom thinned cream; remedy with land
- 1919: Circular went out from Association to nine families saying water in cans
- No charge against any man who put name to that paper. "I only say that I stood in that room every morning of those years and I know what came into it, and that nine kitchens in this Hollow have had that letter in a drawer ever since."

**Family Connection**:
- Wife Orra is Emmet Brant's sister
- Emmet brought more milk than any other man on Bottom
- Every pound Ansel weighed himself, every test himself, wrote in book
- "He and I have not spoken since the circular. That is the plainest fact in this letter and it is the one I find I have the least to say about."

**Dismissal**:
- "Put out on the thirtieth of April."
- "They found no cause established and they put me out on the last day of the month, and both of those things are true at once, and I have given up trying to hold them in my head together."
- "I have tested as I was taught. I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are."

## Retelling 4 — Ivy Keddie (1959, Historical Society Talk)

**Narrator**: Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Father Duncan Keddie taught school at Ashlin. "A granddaughter who reads her grandfather's books ought to say so, and then I intend to leave it alone, because nothing I am going to show you tonight depends in the smallest degree on my opinion of my grandfather. It depends on nine pieces of glass and a burette."

**Five Sets of Records Assembled** (first time in one room):
1. **Station test book**: Association's office since 1925, came to Historical Rooms 1959
2. **Brant weigh book**: Hessel Bottom 1897 onward, lent by Roy Brant (Emmet's son)
3. **Alder Corners Cheese Company test figures**: Copied by Rosalie Cudd (Jerome Cudd's niece); book never left Vessey County, Ivy never seen it
4. **Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks**: State dairy inspector 1908 onward, college library at Farland since 1947 (11 years nobody questioned them)
5. **Association's annual ledgers**: Present at talk

**The Crate Discovery** (February 1958):
- Went up Hollow to station building (used for storage since closed)
- Loft over intake room, reached by ladder
- Under tarpaulin at back: wooden crate stenciled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET"
- Carried up June 1923, still there
- Inside: packed in straw, station's condemned milk-test pipettes
- Six dozen bought new 1897 for $77.93 total (pipettes, bottles, acid measure, hand tester, freight from Ammon & Sons invoice April 14, 1897)
- Nine pipettes unbroken in crate

**Burette Testing** (9 March 1958, at Farland):
Measured nine pipettes against calibrated burette.

**Measurement Sheet** (exact):
"Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for."

**Analysis** ("arithmetic and not conjuring"):
- Butterfat test: charge measured in bottle ruled for percent for that charge alone
- Short charge = short reading, proportional
- Pipettes delivered 16.72 where blown for 17.6
- 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 = 95 hundredths
- **Every test at station read 95% of truth**

**Applying to Ostrey Hollow**:
- Grandfather's summary: average recorded 3.61
- True average: 3.80
- Gap: 19 hundredths
- 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80
- 19 hundredths = 5% of 3.8
- Station lost 5% of whatever was there

**The Dollars**:
- 25 full delivery years 1898–1922: 40 million pounds milk
- At recorded 3.61%: patrons credited 1,444,000 pounds fat
- At true 3.80%: patrons delivered 1,520,000 pounds fat
- **Difference: 76,000 pounds butter fat**
- At 25 cents/pound: **$19,000 unpaid**
- Board of arbitration 1925 awarded $3,800 = **one-fifth of it**

**Two Percentages**:
- 76,000 = 5% of 1,520,000 (fat delivered)
- 76,000 = 5.25% of 1,444,000 (fat credited)
- "The Association's ledger line uses the second, because a bookkeeper naturally reckons a surplus against the figure he has already entered. Neither figure is an error. But five percent is the one that is also the glass."

**Unequal Impact**:
- Error proportional, fell hardest on best milk
- Herd recorded at 4.56 was short 0.24 of point
- Herd at 3.42 was short 0.18
- "The richer the milk, the more was taken from it, and no man had any way of knowing that his good cows cost him more than his poor ones."

**The 1911 Mystery Explained**:
- May 8: measure broke, substitute borrowed until July 10
- Same patrons, herds, pasture, season, acid, bottles, man at crank
- Average went to 3.80, came back to 3.61 day own measure returned
- "No explanation of this valley's twenty-six-year anomaly survives those nine weeks except the one I have given you."

**Disproving the "Wet Hay" Explanation**:
- Kindest old explanation offered in good faith: wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinned cream
- State Agricultural College tested 1911, Bulletin 214: "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."
- 5 hundredths vs. 19 hundredths gap: four times too small, wrong shape
- Gap flat every month (July and February alike), summer and winter
- Over ridge: Alder Corners factory tested Hessel Bottom milk in summer flush
- 14 overlapping seasons: same milk ran 19 hundredths higher at Alder Corners than at home

**Exoneration** (Bulletin 471, October 1958, last paragraph):
"No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

**Timeline of Justice**:
- Glass condemned June 1923
- Ansel dismissed 1925
- Cause found 1958: **35 years later**
- Ansel dismissed 1925: **33 years before**
- Ansel died 1934: **24 years before exoneration**
- "I have no remedy to propose. I have only the crate, and it is on the table, and you may look into it on your way out."

## Retelling 5 — Wilbur Teague (1948, Memoir Chapter)

**Narrator & Position**: Bookkeeper of Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association 1905–1945 (40 years). "I have set the figures down as they stood in the books, which is the only way I know to set anything down."

**Workplace & Practice**:
- Office one room over harness shop at Ashlin
- North end cold, south end never cold enough
- Ledgers on shelf, sloped desk with brass rail across foot to keep sheets from sliding
- Remembers sound of station sheets coming out of envelope in March (stiffer paper than creamery used)
- Worked 40 years; remembers paper, ruling, ink pad smell better than most faces

**Association Structure** (in Teague's time):
- Creamery at Larrow Green
- Office at Ashlin
- Skimming station 7 miles up valley at Ostrey Hollow
- Selby Vose: manager over whole
- Effie Loomis: secretary, kept minutes
- Wilbur Teague: at ledger

**Station Operation**:
- Patrons hauled milk to station instead of 7 miles down to Larrow Green
- Milk weighed in, sample taken from each can, tested for butterfat
- Man paid on pounds and test together
- Station separated milk, sent cream down valley to Larrow Green, gave skim back (hogs and calves)

**The Station Gain Line** (Teague's own device, began 1905):
- One side: butterfat credited to patrons on station's own test (what station's books said it bought)
- Other side: butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in station's cream (weighed and tested into vats; what Association had in hand)
- Two never same; second always larger
- Difference charged to station account, carried to general account
- "A difference that will not be entered somewhere will come back and bite you at audit."

**Typical Entry** (1898):
"Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account."

**Entry in Every Year After**: Same terms, same hand throughout Teague's tenure. "As usual" got into entry and stayed there.

**The Evenness Effect**:
- Gain ran at 5.25% of fat actually in cream
- Evenly every year
- Words "as usual" got in entry and stayed
- Evenness stopped him looking
- "A figure that jumps about will keep you awake. A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture."

**Quantities** (two large numbers):
- Over years account ran: station took in **44 million pounds of milk** (Ivy said 40M; Teague says 44M)
- Average test of all Ostrey Hollow patrons, whole period: **3.16**
- Larrow Green did better
- "Not a season in twenty-five years when Ostrey Hollow men were not sore about it."

**The 1911 Break**:
- Spring 1911: station's own measure broke
- Made shift with borrowed one 6 weeks before own came up from town
- Went into accounts as small charge for replacement, nothing else
- Wouldn't mention except people asked about that year in particular
- "As though a bookkeeper kept the weather."

**Testimony to 1925 Board of Arbitration**:
- "Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons."
- One man weighed milk, drew sample, made test, wrote figure pay slip made from, ran separator
- No second man in building spring to fall
- "I do not say what any particular person did on any particular day, because I was not there and I do not know."
- "I say that a business which is arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken from it, and that no set of books ever made can prevent it."
- "The remedy is two men or a lock, and the Association had neither."

**The Arbitration Award**:
- Patrons petitioned winter (farmer has time to be angry)
- Board sat Ashlin under Alonzo Frick (fair chairman)
- Teague called, gave evidence, answered questions
- Board found nothing established against anybody
- Awarded $3,800 without admission, charged to station account
- "And I entered that too, and it balanced."

**Teague's Confrontation with Vose** (1916):
- Put it to Manager Vose in 1916
- Told to let it alone
- Let it alone
- "I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience, and a bookkeeper who takes his employer's business up a second time after being told once has generally begun to look for another employer."

**Vose's Knowledge**:
- "Mr. Vose knew what that account was doing."
- Signed sheet every year
- Spring Teague laid annual sheet before him, he put name at foot
- "A man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him."
- What he made of it never told

**Final Statement**:
- "That is the whole of the station account as I kept it."
- Figures in ledgers at Ashlin, in ink, dated
- "Anybody who wishes to quarrel with this chapter is at liberty to go and read them, which is more than most of the quarrelers have ever done."

## Retelling 6 — Merle Strawn (1936, Extracts from 28 Circuit Books)

**Narrator & Practice**: State dairy inspector. Took Ordell circuit 1907, laid it down 1936 (29 years). 50-odd plants in four counties (creameries, cheese factories, skimming stations, small butter operations). State expected each seen at least once/year, doubtful ones oftener. Traveled by train where train, livery where not, spring by neither. "A man on this circuit learns the plants by their smells before he learns them by their names."

**Four-Part Inspection Protocol** (always in same order to not forget):
1. **Scales**: against sealed weights he carried; intake scale and cream scale, loaded light and heavy
2. **Lactometer**: on day's milk, patron by patron where question, for added water
3. **Boiler, Water Supply, Cold Room**: pressure, gauge, cased well if any, whether held overnight
4. **Tester's Elbow**: stood at tester's elbow, watched test from sample to reading, didn't let him hurry

**Philosophy**:
- Put down what found; didn't put down what thought
- State pays field man to weigh and look
- "Every time I have seen a field man paid for an opinion instead, the opinion has outlived the weighing and done harm after the man was dead."

**Ostrey Hollow Entries** (select):

**1907** (First round, new circuit):
- Scales true against weights
- Cold room fair
- Tester keeps book in own hand, hand better than most
- Watched 2 tests
- Works slow and clean, reads own figure without coaxing

**1909** (Fall):
- Scales true
- Boiler in order, safety tried
- Takes milk from wet Bottom farms and three hill places
- Milk sweet, cans clean
- One man cautioned about rusted seam

**1911** (June, on way to other county):
- Scales true
- Watched test through
- Nothing to note

**1912**:
- Scales true
- Lactometer, no added water
- Cream shipped down valley same day separated
- Creek out, went round ridge, lost afternoon

**1913** (Larrow Green, two days):
- Churn room and receiving books
- Scales true
- New manager at Association this year (Selby Vose)
- Came out to platform with Strawn (last manager never did)

**1915**:
- Scales true
- Wash room floor holding water at drain
- Ordered it graded
- Philosophy: "A field man who lets a floor go will be shown a floor at every plant on his circuit inside of two years."

**1916**:
- Scales true
- Lactometer, no added water
- Tester complains patrons blame him for figures
- Told him that's the trade
- He said he knew it

**1917**:
- Cheese factory in next county, then Larrow Green
- Scales true at both
- Bad year for help; two testers doing work of three men
- "Which is where mistakes live"

**1919** (Critical):
- Ostrey Hollow: scales true
- Lactometer, no added water
- Manager (Vose) sent circular round to patrons about water in cans
- **"I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so."**

**1920** (Twice, spring and fall):
- Both: scales true
- Lactometer, no added water
- Fall: cold room short of ice
- Told them, seen to before out of county

**1921** (Burette Program Begins):
- State issued burettes to field men this year (graduated, wooden case)
- Came spring with printed sheet of directions
- "Slow business to run a plant's glass against it"
- "Most plants have never had it done."

**1922** (Critical Admission):
- Ostrey Hollow: scales true
- Lactometer, no added water
- Boiler in order
- **"I did not run their glass against the burette this year, the day being short and the road home being what it was, and I have put that down because it is what happened."**

**1923, June 12** (Burette Testing, After Two Seasons):
"Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk pipettes against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them."

**1923, October** (Follow-up):
- New glass in use since July
- Ran new pipettes against burette
- They deliver their mark
- Scales true

**1924**:
- Scales true, no added water
- Tests improved
- Their book gives year at **3.80**

**1925** (Fall):
- Scales true, no added water
- Tests improved
- New man at tester's bench
- "He is quick, and quick is not the thing I look for."

**1926** (Burette Program Discontinued):
- Word from state office
- Instruments to keep but no more issued
- Strawn's in grip, used twice since

**Later Visits**:
- 1928: Station quieter; loft over intake room full of odds and ends; told loft over milk room for air only; told had been full since before war
- 1931: Larrow Green and Ostrey Hollow same week; both in order; bad ice year
- 1934: Scales true; lactometer no added water; separator bearing ran hot, shut down two days, repaired; tester's book kept up but not in hand used to be
- 1936 (March, last round): Ostrey Hollow, Larrow Green, two factories over county line; scales true at all four; nothing to note

**Closing Note**:
- 28 pocket books (one per year, oilcloth covers) go to state dairy office with this copy
- "There is nothing in them but what a field man puts in a field book: what I weighed, what I tested, what I told the plant to mend, and whether it was mended when I came back."
- "Dull reading and they were meant to be."
- Asked once or twice to say in general way how plant stood in his estimation
- Always answered: "I do not keep an estimation, I keep a book, and the book is open to anybody the state sends for it."
- "I never had a theory about a plant and I do not recommend having one. A theory is a thing a man carries out to the platform with him and it gets in the way of the weights."

## Retelling 7 — Halbert Nym (1972, Historical Rooms Catalog)

**Narrator**: Curator, Ordell County Historical Rooms. Accession notes for dairy collection items 1–16. "Transcriptions are exact and checked twice. The glosses are mine and are worth what an afternoon is worth."

**Item 1 — By-laws Pamphlet**:
Article VII entire: "Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."
Printed copies carry no year, only "adopted at the annual meeting."

**Item 2 — Ammon & Sons Invoice, 14 April 1897**:
Sold to Larrow Valley Co-op. Creamery Assn. for Ostrey Hollow station:
- 6 doz. milk test pipettes 17.6 c.c. @ $0.35 = $25.20
- 12 doz. test bottles 8% neck @ $0.22 = $31.68
- 1 acid measure = $0.90
- 1 hand tester 4 bottle = $18.00
- Freight = $2.15
- **Total = $77.93**
Six dozen pipettes; office file shows in use station from 1 May 1897, Larrow Green lent until then.

**Item 3 — Station Test Book** (kept by "E. Brant" but author Ansel Keddie):
Half-bound marbled boards, back mended cloth. Columns: date, patron, pounds, test. One hand throughout, 29 seasons.
- First leaf: "Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie."
- Summary leaf 1922: "Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80."
- **Curatorial Note**: Summary gathers yearly averages; 3.80 first season, 3.61 from 1898 on.
- **Provenance**: Station 4 May 1896; impounded board March 1925; Association office Ashlin 1925–1959; received 1959.

**Item 4 — Farm Weigh Book** (by A. Keddie):
Small quarto, sheepskin, greasy at corners. Daily from 1 January 1897: weights delivered with test paid on set against them.
- 1910 leaf: "Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42."

## Retelling 9 — Roy Brant (1955, farm bureau account)

**Narrator**: Farmer at Hessel Bottom, son of Emmet Brant; 67 years old, still milking 1955; kept weigh book from 1898 onward in father's manner.

**The Bottom**: Hessel Bottom is a hundred and sixty acres of wet meadow lying under the hill at Ostrey Hollow. Six farms had rights on it and cut hay off it and pastured it. Roy's was the largest of the six — sixty-odd acres of the meadow, and thirty-eight cows in a good year, which for this valley was a herd. The water stands on the low end into May, and there is a soft place by the alders that has taken a horse to the hock in Roy's time and his father's. The hay off it is coarse, and it cuts late, and a cow will eat it and do well enough, whatever has been said about that hay by men who never fed it. Roy has cut that meadow with a scythe and with a mower behind two horses, and he will say for it that it never failed to grow something.

**The Book**: It is a ledger, a plain one, ruled by hand. Two columns for each day of delivery. In the first column Emmet entered the weight of every can as he took it off his own scale in his own barn before the wagon went down the road. In the second he entered the figure the station gave him back for that same milk. That is all it is. There is no comment in it anywhere but three or four lines in thirty years, and no opinion at all. The cover is gone off the front, the back is held by a strip of harness leather Emmet tacked on himself, and the first forty leaves are stained brown at one corner where it lay in a leaky window a winter he was sick.

**The two columns agreed**: Day after day, month after month, for the whole run of years Emmet kept it and for the years Roy has kept it, his weight and the station's weight came out the same, barring a pound or two here and there, and those were days when a can got slopped on the hill. Two scales, his and the station's, kept by two men who by the end were not speaking to one another, and the scales never fell out at all. Whatever was wrong at that station — and something was wrong at that station for a very long time — it was not the weighing.

**1910 leaf**: "Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42." Two hundred thousand pounds even. Emmet was pleased about the evenness of it; he wrote "even" and underlined it, which for him was carrying on. The price of butterfat that year was twenty-four cents. Over the whole stretch of years the complaint covers, one year with another, it came to about twenty-five cents.

Emmet wrote at the kitchen table after the second milking, in the lamp, with his hat still on, and his mother would not have the table cleared till he was done. It took him no time at all, and it is the only reason there is anything left to argue about now.

**The trouble**: Their milk at the Hollow ran seventeen hundredths of a point under what the same class of milk was making down at Larrow Green. Seventeen hundredths is nothing to look at on one pay slip. Set the slips in a row for a lifetime of farming and it is a farm.

The first men to say so out loud said it in 1902, and they were laughed at, and one of them was Emmet. What Roy would have anybody notice is this: it was seventeen hundredths in July and it was seventeen hundredths in February. It did not come and go with the season. It did not care whether the cows were on the meadow or on hay in the barn. Nobody would take account of that, then or since, though it was in front of them.

In 1908 the Association appointed a committee, with Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green at the head of it, and the committee came up and sampled the Bottom herds through the winter and reported that the wet hay of this bottom thins the cream, and that the remedy lay with the land. Emmet read that report twice and put it in the drawer and said that the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not.

**1911 measure break**: There were nine weeks in the summer of 1911 when the station's own measure was out of use, it having been broken. The state man left his own measure that June and that is what they used. Through those nine weeks Emmet's and all other farms' tests came up, all of us, every farm on the Bottom; and then the station's own came back into use and the figures went down again to where they had been. Emmet noticed the rise and he noticed the fall and he entered both, and he could make nothing of it, and Roy could make nothing of it either, and there it sat for near on fifty years.

**The 1919 circular and family breach**: In 1919 the manager sent a paper round to the Hollow patrons warning against putting water in the milk. Nine families at the head of the valley got that paper and nine families read it for what it was. Emmet was one who never forgave it. He and Ansel Keddie were brothers-in-law, and from 1919 they did not speak — not from any quarrel over the water, but because Emmet held that the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not.

**Over the Ridge**: From 1915, four of the Bottom men hauled their milk over Redlow Ridge to the cheese factory in the summer flush and back to the Hollow the rest of the year. The factory over there had opened in 1914. They went on that way for sixteen seasons. It was for the price and the flush market and a shorter haul while the road was open, and it was talked about here as though they had gone over to the enemy, which shows you what a valley is. Roy did not go over. We had the biggest herd on the meadow, and the biggest herd is the one that cannot afford to be talked about, and Roy has thought since that this was a poor reason and cost them money.

**The family**: Orra Keddie was Emmet's sister. They were full brother and sister, same mother and same father, whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it. So Ansel Keddie, who kept the station's test book, was married into the family that had more to lose by that station than any other family on the meadow, and Roy has never known what to do with that fact except set it down.

Duncan Keddie, who taught school at Ashlin, was Ansel and Orra's boy, and he and Roy are first cousins. Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are second cousins, and Roy mentions it because both of them have had to have it explained to them by him more than once.

**The book, again**: Roy is sixty-seven and the book has outlived two of the three men who are in it. It goes to Hazel when Roy is done with it. That is all the arrangement he has made and all he intends to make.

**Postscript, 1958**: Duncan's girl Ivy came out to the place this year and asked whether she might borrow the book for a piece of work she is doing at the college at Farland. Roy let her have it. She is careful with paper. It comes back to Roy when she is through, and after Roy it is Hazel's.

## Retelling 10 — Rosalie Cudd (1958, letter to Ivy Keddie)

**Narrator & Place**: Rosalie Cudd, cheesemaker at Alder Corners Cheese Company in Vessey County, over Redlow Ridge from Ordell Valley. Her father Jerome Cudd built the factory in 1913; he died 1941 in March, between the setting and the cutting of a vat, which he would have arranged that way if he had been asked. Rosalie took it over that same spring and has had it 17 years (from 1941). She was 12 when first vat was set and was drying hoops before tall enough to see into one.

**The Ridge**: The road is four miles of switchback, cut for a lumber road and never much improved since. It shuts in November and does not open before April, some years May. There is no arguing with it — the drifts sit in the cut below the top and stay there after everything else is bare. So no man from the valley ever hauled to Alder Corners in winter. It was not possible.

**Summer Hauling, 1915–1930**: From 1915, four men off Hessel Bottom hauled their milk over to Alder Corners in the summer flush. They came from about the first of June to the middle of September, and the rest of the year they went down to Ostrey Hollow, which was the only sensible thing to do. Their reasons were plain enough and none of them was mysterious: cheese paid better than butter in a flush month; Alder Corners was buying hard in flush because a cheese factory can use everything you can carry it; and while the road was open, the gate was a shorter pull for those four farms than the run down the valley. They came for sixteen seasons. The last of those summers was 1930.

They started before daylight to have the cans on her platform by seven, because milk that has been four miles down a switchback in a wagon on a July morning is not improving while it stands, and they came in with the horses steaming and the tarps wet with the cloud that sits on the top of the ridge till the sun is well up. One of them brought his boy, who used to sit on her scale platform and be weighed for a joke. That boy is a man in the state road department now and does not remember her.

**Jerome's Testing**: Jerome tested himself, on his own bench, in the little room off the intake, with his own glassware that he bought new and washed in the evening and kept in a rack he had made, and he wrote his own book. He never compared a figure of his with anybody else's, and Rosalie wants to be honest about the reason: it never entered his head. He had no business with what the Ostrey Hollow station made of the same milk. A cheesemaker tests what comes in his door so he knows what he is paying for and what his vat will do. He is not keeping a check on another man's arithmetic seven miles off over a mountain, and if you had suggested it to Jerome he would have laughed at you.

**Jerome's 1919 Notes**: Rosalie has the books here. Jerome wrote at the end of the summer of 1919:
"The four Hessel Bottom men, summer of 1919: 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge."

That last clause is Jerome all over. He had heard what was said in Ordell County about that meadow — that the wet hay of the bottom thinned the cream, and that the men who farmed it were bringing in poor stuff and had better drain their land before they complained. He thought it was nonsense, and he wrote so in his own book where nobody would ever read it, which is where he generally put his opinions. He used to say that the Ordell people had a theory about that bottom the way some families have a ghost: it explained things, and it cost nothing, and nobody was ever going to test it.

**The Facts About the Milk**: Rosalie will tell the man from the county what she told another who came in here once in a temper about it. That milk was good milk. Season after season those four men came in at about 3.80, and not one of them ever gave her an hour's trouble in the vat. She has set the figures out for him at the end of the letter, every summer of the sixteen, and he will see they sit between 3.78 and 3.82, year in, year out, wet years and dry ones, whether the meadow had been under water in May or hard as a road. They are the season averages of the four men taken together, copied straight off the leaves; she has not smoothed anything and has not thrown out a season. If he wants the men separately she can copy those too, but he will find they are within a few hundredths of each other and always were, which is what you would expect of four herds off one piece of ground.

**On Watering**: Since he asked particularly about water, she will answer it flatly. A cheesemaker knows watered milk inside a day. He does not need a test to know it, though the test will tell him too. The curd comes soft and it will not knit; you get a slack, weak body and your yield is down, and you can stand at the vat and see your money going out with the whey. It cannot be hidden from the man who makes the cheese, because the cheese tells him, and it tells him the next morning, not next year. Butter is more forgiving that way. Cheese is a witness. In sixteen summers Jerome never made one complaint of those four men, never docked one of them a cent, and never — not once that Rosalie heard, and she was in the room — said a word about their milk except that he wished he had more of it in July.

**Knowledge of the Trouble**: Rosalie is aware, of course, that there was trouble in the Ordell valley for years about the milk at the head of it, because trouble carries over a ridge better than anything else does. It came to her the way weather comes, in pieces and late, and always with somebody's name attached to it. She paid no attention. It was not her county, they were not her patrons in September, and she had a make to get out. If she sounds short about Ordell it is only that she has listened to forty years of the valley explaining that meadow to itself, and she never once heard anybody go up there and look.

**What She Can Provide**: She does not know what the Ostrey Hollow station made of the same cows, and she has not the first idea what the county decided about any of it. She never saw those figures and she would not know how to get them. All she can give him is their side of the ridge, honestly copied, and he is welcome to it, and welcome to print it if it is any use to him.

**The Invitation & Table**: Come over in the summer and see the place. The road is open by then, mostly, and she will give him his dinner and show him his father's book, which is a nicer object than anything she has said about it here.

## Retelling 11 — Leland Vaught (1958, Bulletin 471 Introduction & Method)

**Narrator & Position**: Professor of dairy husbandry, State Agricultural College at Farland. Writes introduction and method to Bulletin No. 471, October 1958, addressed to extension agents and county workers.

**Why This Bulletin Was Written**: This station has been asked, at intervals for fifty years, why the butterfat tests at one skimming station in Ordell County ran below those of its own creamery seven miles down the same valley. The question has been answered at various times by reference to the land and by reference to the men. It is answered here by reference to a piece of glass.

The answer is not complicated, and its simplicity is itself part of the history. Nobody in that valley lacked the intelligence to find it. What they lacked was any occasion to suspect the one article in the plant that nobody in the trade is in the habit of suspecting.

**Extension Worker Takeaway**: The milk pipette is the one instrument in the butterfat test which the plant cannot check against anything else, and it is the instrument on which every figure in the plant depends.

**The Instrument and the Percent**: The test as practiced in every creamery in the state consists of four operations. A measured charge of milk is drawn into a pipette and delivered into a test bottle. Sulfuric acid is added and dissolves everything but the fat. The bottle is spun in a hand tester, and the liberated fat rises into the graduated neck. The operator reads the fat off directly, in percent.

Notice the word *directly*. The operator weighs nothing. He reads the length of a column of fat in a narrow neck and calls it a percentage, and he is entitled to do so for one reason only: the charge of milk that went into that bottle is a fixed and known quantity. The maker rules the neck for that quantity and no other. Every mark on it means "this much fat, out of the standard charge."

The standard charge in this state, and on the glassware here examined, is **17.6 cubic centimeters**.

**What Happens with Short Pipettes**: Consider then what follows if the pipette delivers less than its mark. Less milk goes into the bottle; less fat is liberated; a shorter column stands in the neck; and the neck, which knows nothing of the substitution, is read off in the ordinary way. The reading is not merely low. It is low *in the exact proportion by which the charge was short*.

**Three Critical Consequences**:

**First, the error is proportional and not additive.** It does not remove a fixed number of points from every test. It removes a fixed *fraction*. Rich milk therefore loses more than poor milk, absolutely, and the ratio between any two tests made on the same short glass is undisturbed. A station using such glass will show a perfectly orderly, internally consistent set of figures. Its herds will rank correctly against one another. Only against the outside world is it wrong.

**Second, the error is precise.** A leaking pipe or a slipping scale gives a scattered error, and scatter attracts attention. A pipette that delivers 16.27 c.c. where it is blown 17.6 delivers 16.27 c.c. every time, for a quarter of a century, in January and in July, for a careful operator and a careless one. It has the appearance of excellent work.

**Third — and this is the reason the thing ran so long — the error is invisible from inside the plant.** Every other element of the test can be verified against a standard. The scales can be checked with test weights. The tester's speed can be counted. The acid can be checked for strength. The bottle's graduation can be checked with mercury. But the pipette is not checked against anything, because in the ordinary practice of the trade *the pipette is the standard*. It is what the plant measures with. An instrument that is itself the measure cannot be caught by measuring with it. It can be caught only by a burette brought in from outside, and burettes reached the state's field men very late in this history.

**Material Examined**: Of the lot bought for the Ostrey Hollow station in 1897 — **five dozen** pipettes were purchased — **nine** were recovered from the station's condemned crate, unbroken and legible, and these nine constitute the material of this bulletin. Their stems are blown **17.6 c.c.** They were delivered against a calibrated burette, five runs to each pipette, and the mean delivery of the nine is **16.27 c.c.**

The station's own glass went into use on **the first of May, 1898**, and remained in use until the state inspector condemned the lot in June of 1923. Before the earlier date, and after the later, the station's tests were made on other glass.

**Method**: The method of measurement is within the reach of any county worker. The pipette is filled to its mark with water at room temperature, the tip is wiped, and the contents are delivered in the ordinary working manner — the same interval of drainage, the same touch of the tip to the wall of the receiver — into a burette of certified graduation. Five deliveries are made from each pipette and the readings averaged. The whole of it occupies an afternoon and requires no apparatus a creamery does not own, save the burette. There is no reason why every station in this state should not be checked once in this way, and several reasons in this history why it should be.

**The 1911 Interval**: For nine weeks in the summer of 1911 the station's own pipette was broken and a borrowed measure was used in its place. The patrons were the same patrons; the herds were the same herds; the pasture, the season, the acid, the bottles, the tester and the tester's habits were all unaltered. **One variable changed.** The station's average test rose from 3.61 to 3.80 and held there for the nine weeks, and returned to 3.61 on the day the station's own measure came back into service. This is recorded contemporaneously, in the operator's hand, by a man who did not know what he was recording and set a question mark against it.

Vaught has spent his working life urging students to prefer an experiment to a calculation. Here is one that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven years. It disposes of every explanation that looks to the herds, to the ground or to the men, for the sufficient reason that none of those was altered in those nine weeks, and the figures were.

**Magnitude**: The ratio of delivered to marked capacity is 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = **0.9244**. The recorded station average over the affected years is **3.61**; the corresponding true average is 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = **3.905**, a shortfall of **0.295** of a point. Applied to the 40,000,000 lb of milk received at the station in the twenty-five full delivery years, this yields **118,000 lb** of butterfat delivered and not credited.

(Note: This figure is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the glassware.)

The corresponding quantity appears on the Association's own side of the account. Its ledgers carried, annually, a credit for fat received at the creamery in excess of fat credited to patrons at the station, under the heading "station gain." The gain is five and a quarter percent of the butterfat actually delivered.

**The Feed Question**: Because the herbage of the wet meadow at the head of that valley has been blamed, the College's own feeding trial of 1911 should be set down. Bulletin No. 214 reported:

"Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."

Five hundredths in winter; nothing at all in summer. The discrepancy under examination is present in every month of the year and is some six times the largest effect the trial could produce under conditions harsher than any farmer would impose.

**Conclusion** (Bulletin 471, final paragraph):
"No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

— L. VAUGHT

## Retelling 12 — Dell Rundle (1939, 1959 reprint)

**Narrator & Position**: Schoolteacher at Larrow Green. Compiled "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years," a jubilee pamphlet printed at Larrow Green for the Association's fiftieth year (1939), reprinted 1959 with one footnote and closing note unaltered. Compiled from recollections of old members and two winters of asking questions at the store. Not a record, but what the valley remembers, tidied.

**The Men of 1889**: In 1889 the farmers of this valley met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. They were tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking whatever the merchant chose to allow them for it, and they had worked out — the way farmers do, slowly and then all at once — that the way to be paid properly for a thing is to own the place that buys it.

They met in the schoolhouse, in cold weather, and the older men who were there remembered afterward chiefly that it went on very late and that somebody's horse got loose in the middle of it.

Gideon Vose was elected the first president. He was a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the law and thought better of it, and he wrote the by-laws himself, at his own kitchen table, in a hand that the Association's clerks were still complaining about thirty years later. His nephew Selby Vose, afterward manager of the Association, used to say that his uncle wrote by-laws the way other men build stone wall — slowly, and to last.

In 1891 the creamery was built at Larrow Green, on the creek below the mill, and the churn was set up in it, and the valley began to be paid in cash once a month, which had never happened to it before. There are men still living who can tell you what the first check did in their mother's kitchen, and they will tell you whether you ask or not.

**Article VII (The Rule That Made the Test the Master)**: Of all Gideon Vose's by-laws, one has done more to this valley than the rest together. He prints it whole:

"Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."

Article VII dates from 1891, the year the churn was set up. It was thought a hard rule at the time and there were men who left the Association over it. It is a just rule. A man who keeps good cows and feeds them ought not to be paid the same as the man who keeps poor ones, and the pound of milk is a liar where the pound of butterfat is not.

But mark the last clause, because the valley has had fifty years to learn what it means. *The test of the station where he delivers shall govern.* Not the test of the creamery. Not any test but the one made at his own door. Gideon Vose put that in for convenience and it was convenient for thirty years, and it is the sentence on which the whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned.

**The Station at Ostrey Hollow**: The Hollow is seven miles up from the Green, and in the nineties the pull down was three hours in mud and could not be done at all in March. So the Association built a skimming station up there, and it opened in 1896: the milk was weighed and tested and separated on the spot, the cream came down to the Green by wagon, and the skim went home to the calves and the pigs the same morning. It was a great convenience and the Hollow was glad of it. The building is a plain one, boarded and battened, with the intake room at the road end and a loft over it that Rundle believes has never been cleared out since it was put up; and behind it the creek, which in a wet April comes up to the sill and has twice come over it.

**The Trouble at the Head of the Valley**: Rundle would rather not write this chapter and it cannot be left out.

From 1902 the men of the Hollow complained that their milk tested lower at their own station than the same class of milk tested at the Green. They were not believed. In 1908 the Association sent a committee up under Nyle Grigg, which sampled the Bottom herds through the winter and reported that the wet hay of that bottom thinned the cream, and that the remedy lay with the land. The Hollow did not accept it. In 1919 the manager circulated a warning to the Hollow patrons against adding water to their milk, and after that the two ends of this valley did not speak civilly to one another for six years. Rundle has had the children of both ends in one schoolroom through those years and he can tell you that it came down to them entire, without a word of it being explained, the way a debt does.

It is the settled opinion of the valley that cream left that station by the back door, and the figures went right when the man who kept it did not.

Rundle sets that down as he has heard it set down a hundred times, by men who were there and men who were not, and he has never heard it contradicted at the store.

The state inspector condemned the station's glassware on the twelfth of July, 1923, for reasons of his own that had nothing to do with the quarrel, and new glass was put in. In January of 1925 the Hollow patrons petitioned the Association. The station took in, over the years complained of, forty-four million pounds of milk. The patrons' loss upon it was nineteen thousand six hundred dollars, and their petition set the figure out to the dollar. A board of arbitration sat at Ashlin that spring under Alonzo Frick, attorney, of that town. It heard everybody. It found nothing established, it made an award to the patrons without admitting anything, and it sent this valley home to live with itself, which the valley has done, after a fashion, ever since.

**Over the Ridge**: Rundle should mention, because it belongs to the history and because the young people no longer know it, that in 1914 the Alder Corners Cheese Company was opened over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County, by Jerome Cudd, and that in the summers a few of the Hessel Bottom men hauled over the mountain to him rather than down to their own station. That was resented here. It is Jerome Cudd's daughter, Rosalie Cudd, who makes the cheese at Alder Corners now.

**Footnote Added to 1959 Reprint**: The Hollow matter has since been taken up again at the college at Farland by Miss Ivy Keddie, who is second cousin to Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom. Eleven pipettes out of the station's old condemned crate were found still to be in existence. Rundle is too old to rewrite a pamphlet and he refers the reader to the college. — D.R.

**Fifty Years**: Set against the quarrel, put the rest: fifty Junes of cream coming down that road in the early cool, fifty Januaries of it going down over ice with the horses roughshod, a check every month in kitchens that had known none, and a creamery still standing that was built by men with nothing but each other and a piece of paper they had written themselves. Whatever this valley did wrong at the head of it, it did this right, and it did it for half a century, and the men of 1889 would think we had done well.

**Note Added While in Press**: While this pamphlet was being set up in type Rundle was allowed to look at the Association's old ledgers in the office, on another errand altogether. The station account carries, every year, an entry of this form, in the bookkeeper's hand:

"Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account."

Rundle must go back on Chapter IV. Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and Rundle cannot make his paragraph stand. He leaves it as written and corrects it here.

— D. RUNDLE, Larrow Green

## Retelling 13 — Alonzo Frick (1946, Bar Association Yearbook)

**Narrator & Position**: Alonzo Frick, attorney; recollections of the Ostrey Hollow arbitration for the yearbook of the Ordell County Bar Association (1946). He sat as chairman of the board. At seventy-four, he notes he has tried titles and boundaries for forty years, and two homicides, and none of them has stayed with him as the Ostrey Hollow matter has. It was not litigation at all. It was an arbitration under the by-laws of a creamery association, with no appeal from it, no reported decision, and nothing whatever to cite it for; and he has thought about it oftener than about anything he ever argued in a courthouse.

**The Complaint**: The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association pays a patron for the butter fat in his milk and not for the weight of it. The fat is found by test, and the test of the station where the man delivers is the test that governs him. Frick says nothing against the rule. But it makes the test the whole of the bargain, and it leaves a dissatisfied man with no remedy on earth except to quarrel with a figure.

At Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green, the Association kept a skimming station: milk weighed in, a sample drawn for each patron, the cream sent down the valley and the skim milk handed back. The patrons of that station had been paid, year upon year, on an average test of 3.16, and the milk of the rest of the valley tested better than theirs. Three and sixteen is not a scandalous figure standing by itself. It becomes one when the man over the hill is paid on a better figure for milk out of cows no different from yours; when the difference holds steady for a quarter of a century; and when nobody in the office can tell you the cause of it.

**The Petition and the Board**: In January of 1925 the patrons of the station petitioned the Association. The by-laws provided for arbitration, and the officers, whatever else may be said of them, did not resist it. A board of three was constituted and Frick was named its chairman, he has always supposed because he was the one of the three who kept no cow.

The board sat three times. The first session was at the Association's office at Ashlin on the seventeenth of March, 1925. The second and the third Frick asked to have at Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's own weigh room, so that the board might see the plant it was to pass upon instead of hearing it described by men with an interest in the description. That was Frick's doing and he is still content with it. Every physical appliance of that station was before them and they satisfied themselves upon it. They saw the scales tried. They saw the sampling done and the test made, and Frick will say plainly that he did not understand the test then and does not understand it now; he is a lawyer and the thing is chemistry. But he could see that it was done the same way twice, and that the men who did it were not hiding their hands.

**The Three Explanations**: The board heard three explanations of the Ostrey Hollow figures, and it is worth setting down who put each of them, because the three did not sit easily together and nobody seemed to mind that they did not.

The first was water. This was the manager's, Selby Vose. He had circulated a warning to those patrons on the subject some years before and he stood by it before the board: that the Hollow's milk came in thinned, that a little water in a can is the oldest trick in the trade, and that a station cannot test what is not there. He put it without heat and without proof.

The second was that the tester had been taking cream for himself and writing low tests to cover the loss. This was put by the bookkeeper, Wilbur Teague, in the way an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it — he was asked about the station's account and answered at length about how such an account would look if a man were helping himself, and then observed that he made no charge against anybody. There is no cross-examination that reaches a witness of that kind, and Frick did not attempt one.

The third was the hay. Somebody read the report of the 1908 committee into the record, and the bog meadow at Hessel Bottom was before them for the better part of an afternoon — the wet ground, the coarse hay off it, the cows that eat it all winter. It is an old opinion in that valley and it had the advantage of blaming nobody in the room.

**Keddie**: Ansel Keddie had tested at that station since it opened in 1896 and had kept the book in his own hand every season since. He made a statement to the board on the seventeenth of March. He said he had tested as he was taught and as the state man had seen him do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, that he could not tell them why the figures were what they were, and that he had never taken a pound of cream out of that station. Frick believed him then and believes him now. His denial was accurate and it did him no good at all, and the reason it did him no good is that a man who cannot account for a figure is presumed by everybody in the room to be the figure's author.

Frick adds one thing which is not in the minutes. In the corridor at Ashlin, Keddie told him privately that he had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the room. He was not a bitter man about it. He had lived among them thirty years and would not stand up and accuse his neighbors, and Frick records it here because it has been said since that he went to his grave accusing nobody, which is true, and that he suspected nobody, which is not.

**The Dismissal and the Award**: On the thirtieth of April, four days before they made their award, the Association dismissed Ansel Keddie. There was no finding against him from the board and there never was one; the board had not concluded and did not conclude that he had done anything whatever. He was simply let go while the board was still sitting, and Frick has never been able to think well of the Association for it.

On the fourth of May, 1925, the board found no cause established and awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons thirty-eight hundred dollars, without admission, to be charged to the station account. The patrons' own reckoning of what they had lost was sixty-seven thousand pounds of butter fat over the years complained of, and nothing was ever brought forward to disturb that figure. The board was not able to say who owed it or why, and they said so. An award without a finding is an unsatisfactory thing and every member of that board knew it.

**The Exhibits**:

EXHIBITS BEFORE THE BOARD OF ARBITRATION, OSTREY HOLLOW MATTER, 1925:

1. The test book of the Ostrey Hollow station, 4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924, in the hand of Ansel Keddie.
2. The receiving book of the said station.
3. The private weigh book of Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom, begun 1 January 1897.
4. The circuit notebook of the state dairy inspector for the year 1923.

Four books and nothing else. It is a short list for a matter of that size, and Frick has looked at it many times since.

**Reflection**: A board of arbitration can only decide between the accounts that are put to it. Three were put to them; each of them was urged by a man of standing; each of them would have been believed by half the valley; and not one of them was proved. Frick has wondered since whether the answer was in that room and simply was not in any of the three stories, and whether a lawyer who understood chemistry might have found it. He did not understand chemistry. He understood that an honest man was ruined by a figure nobody could explain, and that they paid his neighbors money without being able to tell them what they were being paid for.

## Retelling 14 — Hobart Sill (1934, remarks at Larrow Green)

**Narrator & Position**: Hobart Sill, buttermaker (ret.); remarks recorded at the Larrow Green creamery on the day he left it, 1934. He made the Association's butter for forty-one years (1893–1934) and had no patience with anybody who did not.

**Opening**: Forty-one years he has stood at that churn. Eighteen ninety-three he came here, and there was no floor in this room but plank, and they cooled with creek ice out of the pond. He never sent out a tub of butter he would not eat himself, which is more than the office can say about anything it ever sent out.

**The Hollow**: Everybody wants to know about the Hollow. Sill will tell what he knows, which is what came off that wagon, because that is what he handled.

The cream wagon came down from Ostrey Hollow every day of the week but Sunday, seven miles of hill road, and in July it came down warm no matter how early they started. It was set off at the platform and it was weighed and it was sampled and it went into the cold room, and Sill took it from there. Now here is the thing about that cream, and he has said it to men in this room a hundred times and none of them ever thought it was worth anything. **That cream ran heavy. Every year it ran heavy. There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there.** The office would send down its sheet and the sheet would say so many pounds of fat bought at the station, and the fat that came into this room off that wagon was more than the sheet said. Always more. Never less.

And Sill weighed it himself, most years. The platform man weighed it and he weighed it over again when it came into the cold room, because he has never in his life taken another man's figure for what was going into his churn. Cans off, cans on the scale, cans into the ice, and the book ruled and signed before he put his coat on. He may go and look at the receiving book if they will let him at it, and he will find his hand on every page of it from ninety-three, and he will find the Hollow's cream coming in heavy on page after page after page, in his own writing, for as long as he has been in this room.

Well, that is cream for you. Cream is not milk and it will not stand still and be counted. It is a thick thing and it clings and it separates on you and any man who tells you he can put a figure on cream to the pound and hold it is a man who has never had his arm in a can up to the elbow. So it ran heavy. So does everybody's. Sill never made anything of it, and he will say plain, nobody ever asked him to.

**Larrow Green Milk**: The milk here at Larrow Green, Sill's own patrons, the men on this creek — that ran 3.78 taking one year with another right through those years. Three seventy-eight. He knows it because he made the butter out of it and a buttermaker knows what his cream is worth before anybody hands him a paper about it. Ordinary milk, ordinary county, ordinary cows. There is nothing wonderful about 3.78 and there is nothing shameful about it either.

**Hollow Milk and Water**: Now the Hollow. The Hollow milk was really a 3.85 and no better, and Sill will say that to any man in this valley. Wet ground up there, but good cows, and he never had a word to say against the cows. A 3.85 and no better, and if the figures they were paid on came out under that, then somebody up that hill road was helping the cans along, and he said so at the time and he says it now.

He will get it told to him a dozen ways. He has been in the office, he expects, and they have their ways of putting it. He puts it the one way. You cannot tell him nine families all got honest in one summer. There was water in those cans and he said so at the time. Because he will tell you what happened — one summer, along in the twenties, those figures came up and they stayed up, and nothing on this earth changed but the season, and men do not suddenly start pouring better milk out of the same cows in the same barns. Something got stopped. Something got said to somebody. He never knew what and he never asked, because the day a buttermaker starts asking about other men's cans is the day he stops making butter.

**Long-Standing Complaints**: They had been hollering up there a long while before that, mind. Away back. Nineteen two is the first he remembers of it, a man in here shouting that his milk tested under his brother-in-law's down the creek and it was the same feed and the same water and what did they mean by it. And the manager put out a paper to them about it — that would be nineteen nineteen — a circular, warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons about water in the milk, plain as that. He thought well of him for it. It was the only plain thing that office ever did, and of course they hated him for it up the Hollow, and of course it changed nothing, because a paper never emptied a can.

**The Office & Butter**: That is the offices for you. Papers. The Association has a paper for everything and butter for nothing. He has had men come in here in a collar and tell him his churn was running slow when they could not have told you which end of a cow gets up first. He made butter that took premiums in three states out of this room, and not one man in that office ever came down here to see how it was done, and every one of them could tell you to the half cent what the fat was worth. Twenty-seven cents. That is what fat was through those years, take one year with another, twenty-seven cents the pound, and there is not a man in the office could tell you what makes it churn.

**Lending Glass to Ostrey Hollow (1896)**: He will tell one more thing and then they can go. When they opened that station up at Ostrey Hollow — that was ninety-six, in May, and there was a crowd went up for it — they had no outfit of their own. Not a piece. It was ordered and it was not come, and they were ready to start taking milk, and there was talk of holding off the whole season. And Sill said no, they can have his. He went to his own bench in this room and he made up an outfit for them out of his own glass, his own, that he had used and knew, and it went up the valley on the wagon and they opened on it. Their own came the spring after and they sent his back to him, and one piece of it was chipped and he never said a word about that either.

**First Season at Ostrey Hollow**: That first season they took in about nine hundred thousand pounds up there. May to the end of the year, that is, so it was not even a whole one. That is a good deal of milk for a hill station starting from nothing, and it was the biggest thing that ever happened to Ostrey Hollow, and Sill was glad enough to have had a hand in it.

**Reflection**: Do he think about it? He is sixty-four years old and he has thought about butter. He never went up there but twice in his life. He took their cream off the platform and he made it into butter and the butter was good — as good as any, and better than most, and there was never a season it was not. Whatever the Hollow was doing with its cans, and he has told them what he thinks about that, the fat came down that hill and it came into his churn and it went out of this room as good butter with this creamery's name on the tub.

**Final Statement**: Now that is forty-one years and he would like to go home. Put in about the butter. Do not put in that he said anything against the cows up there, because he did not. The cows were never the trouble. Put in that Hobart Sill made the butter and it was good butter and he stood at the churn until the day they gave him a chair.

## Retelling 15 — Nyle Grigg (1919, covering letter to 1908 committee report)

**Narrator & Position**: Nyle Grigg, farmer of standing at Larrow Green; 1919 covering letter accompanying the reprinted 1908 report of the investigating committee. He was chairman of that committee. Sends the report back into circulation because the same complaint has been raised again, and adds a letter saying that the intervening science has proved him right.

**Opening of Letter**: Larrow Green, 1919. To the officers and patrons of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. He is asked to let the report be printed again, the Ostrey Hollow question having been reopened this season by men who were boys when they made it. He consents willingly and has nothing in it to take back. He asks leave only to add a page, because a report is a dry thing and does not say why it was written, and because eleven years have brought some light from a quarter that had none to give them in 1908.

**The 1908 Context**: From about 1902 there had been grumbling out of Ostrey Hollow that the milk of that station tested under the milk of the rest of this valley. It was not one man and it was not one season. It went on and it grew, and it got into the talk at the annual meeting and into the talk at every crossroads store between here and Ashlin, and it took the shape such things always take, which is that somebody must be doing it on purpose. By the winter in which they were named, the board could not let it alone any longer without seeming to have an interest in letting it alone, and they three were named to look into it. Grigg has never in his life been given a duty he liked less. Payment by test came in with the 1893 meeting, as he has always understood it, and from that year a difference of this kind stopped being a matter of opinion and became a matter of money. He had known the men of that bottom since he was a young man and he has bought hay off two of them.

**The Committee's Work**: They went at it as plain farmers do. They sampled those herds through the whole of 1908 — every month of that year, in the barn and on the pasture, out of the night's milk and the morning's, and they did not let a man know which day they were coming. They weighed. They watched the test made and they watched it made again. And what they found, they put in the report, and the report is below and he will not repeat it here.

**Grigg's Neutrality**: He will add, since a man who prints a thing twice ought to say where he stands in it, that he had no interest whatever in the answer. He is not a patron of that station. He hauls to Larrow Green and always has, and whatever the Hollow was paid or not paid never put a cent into his pocket nor took one out of it. He was chosen for the work on that account and for no other, and he has been abused for the finding by men on both sides of it, which he takes to be a fair sign that the finding was an honest one.

**Scientific Support**: What he will say is this. When they wrote, they had nothing to go on but their own eyes and the general opinion of practical men, and he knew that a committee of farmers saying that wet hay thins the cream would be told that farmers will say anything about their own land. But the State Agricultural College at Farland has since published its Bulletin No. 214, which was a feeding trial upon bog hay carried out under the College's own eye, with weighed rations and no interest in the outcome. That trial found the bog hay to depress the butter fat test by **two tenths** in the winter feeding. Two tenths, gentlemen, out of a College that had never heard of Hessel Bottom and had no reason on earth to flatter them. There is their finding, arrived at by another road and by better men, three years after they set their names to it.

**Remedy**: He is content to let the matter rest where the College and this committee have jointly put it. The remedy lies with the land and not with the station. Drain that bottom, open the old ditches and cut new, lime the ground and lime it again, sow it down when it will carry a seeding, keep the stock off it in the wet of the spring, when a hoof does more harm in a day than a season will repair, and never feed that hay alone. Feed it with a mixed ration and a mineral supplement, as the College directs. Fat has been worth twenty-seven cents the pound through these years, and a fifth of a point taken off every hundredweight a farm ships, year in and year out, will pay for a great deal of tile. Go on as they are going and abuse the man who reads their figures for them, and they will be grumbling in twenty years' time as they were grumbling when we began, and no better off.

**Signature**: Respectfully, NYLE GRIGG, Larrow Green.

**The 1908 Report** (as reprinted):

REPORT OF THE COMMITTEE APPOINTED TO INQUIRE INTO THE COMPLAINTS OF THE PATRONS OF THE OSTREY HOLLOW STATION.

To the President and Directors of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association.

Your committee, appointed in January last, respectfully reports as follows.

The complaint before us is of some years' standing and is in substance this: that the milk delivered at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station is returned by the test at a figure below that of milk delivered elsewhere in this valley, and that the patrons of that station are in consequence paid less for the same labor and the same cows than their neighbors are paid. Since payment by test came in among us and the pound of butter fat took the place of the pound of milk as the measure of a man's month, a difference of this kind is no longer a matter of opinion. It is a matter of money, and it comes out of the same pocket every month, and your committee has approached it in that light and not as a quarrel.

Hessel Bottom, where six farms cut hay and turn out stock, is a wet meadow lying along the creek under Ostrey Hollow. Its ground is springy the year round and is under water in part every spring. Its herbage is sedge, rush, and the coarse sour grasses that come in on such land, and its hay is cut late and heavy and is fed as the main dependence of those farms from November to April. There is no better winter keep to be had there. The committee says this without reproach to any man on that bottom; the land is what it is, and they took it as their fathers left it to them.

Your committee visited each of the six farms, some of them three and four times. They drew their samples themselves and sealed them themselves. They saw the herds milked, they saw the cans filled, and they saw them hauled to the station and weighed in at the intake, and they stood by while the test was made and saw nothing whatever in the doing of it to remark upon. They tried the station scales against a known weight and they tried the patrons' own scales against the station's. They inquired into the water supply at each barn and satisfied themselves upon it.

Your committee further observed the general condition of the stock upon that bottom, as men who have kept cattle all their lives are entitled to observe it. The herds are thin in the flank at the end of the winter feeding and slow to come to their flesh in the spring. Their coats are rough later in the season than the coats of hill herds. They do well enough in flow, for the wet land is generous of milk, but it is our opinion, formed on the ground and not out of a book, that what such land gives in quantity it withholds in quality, and that a cow cannot put into her pail what her keep has never put into her.

They report that they found no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter, and wish that finding to be printed in the same type as the rest.

They have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March. The milk is whole and the scales are honest. It is their opinion that the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station.

NYLE GRIGG, chairman, and the committee.

**Aftermath**: That report was received and accepted, and the question was closed by it for eleven years — until this season, and the men who have reopened it have brought nothing new before them but their impatience.

## Retelling 16 — Dr. Roland Kepp (1926, Farm Club paper)

**Narrator & Position**: Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S. (veterinary surgeon), of Ashlin; paper read before the Ordell County Farm Club, 1926. Thirty years' practice. When called to a herd doing badly, he asks first what the cattle are standing on. He has found in thirty years that the answer to nine cases in ten is under the animal's feet, and that the tenth is in the water she drinks.

**The Land & Soil Chemistry**: A wet bottom is not a rich meadow with too much water in it. It is a distinct kind of ground with a distinct kind of chemistry, and the water is the least part of it. Where drainage is wanting, the year's growth does not rot down as it does on a hill field; it lies sour, and the soil that results is an acid soil, poor in lime, and poor in the phosphates. On such ground the sweet grasses will not hold. They are crowded out within a few seasons by sedge and rush and the coarse tussock grasses, which want nothing better than sour wet land and will take it over completely if you let them. That herbage carries bulk and it carries water. What it does not carry, in any quantity worth the name, is lime or phosphorus, and it is cut late because such ground cannot be got onto early, and late cutting takes out what little was in it.

**What the Cow Does**: Now consider the animal. A cow in milk is a manufactory working on a narrow margin, and she is obliged to find the material of her milk somewhere. If her ration is short of the minerals, she does not simply give less; she robs her own frame to make good, and she goes on robbing it until she cannot. You will see the consequences in any wet-meadow herd in this county and he has seen them in all of them. The stock come out of the winter feeding thin over the loin and rough in the coat. They are slow to come to condition in the spring. They are ready subjects for every disorder of the digestion in the fall of the year, and he is oftener called to that class of trouble on wet land than on any other. They breed later. Their feet are worse.

They will milk, gentlemen. Wet land is generous of flow and no man on a bog meadow ever complained that his pail was light. But what is in the pail is another matter, and the fat is the first thing to suffer, because the fat is the dearest thing the cow makes and it is the first economy she practices when she is pressed.

**The Measure of It**: He has gone about this county for thirty years and he has kept his eyes open. The wet bottoms of this county yield a milk poorer in fat by a fifth of a point and every practical man knows it. Set the hill herds of this county against the bottom herds and you will find that difference, farm for farm, wherever you look for it, and the difference does not depend on the breed, on the man, or on the barn. It depends on the ground.

**Hessel Bottom**: He comes to the case which is in everybody's mind. Hessel Bottom, under Ostrey Hollow, is the completest example of wet meadow in this county. Six farms cut hay and pasture stock upon it. It lies along the creek; the springs come out at the foot of the slope and run in it the year round; a part of it is under water every spring and is not fit to be walked on until well into May. Its hay is sedge hay, and on those farms it is the winter dependence and very nearly the whole of it.

The patrons of the skimming station at Ostrey Hollow have complained for something like a quarter of a century that their milk tests below the milk of the rest of the valley. Their complaint is perfectly just and he has never doubted it. **The figure of the gap, as it has been given to him and as he has satisfied himself, is twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21 — between the milk of that station and the milk of this valley at large.** A committee of practical men went over the ground in 1908 and found the herds sound, the milk whole, and the scales honest, and laid the cause to the hay of that bottom. The matter has been before committees since and before boards, and it is not settled, and it will not be settled by men shouting at one another across a weigh room. It is settled already, in the literature, for anybody who will read.

**The College Evidence**: The State Agricultural College at Farland conducted a feeding trial upon bog hay and published it as its Bulletin No. 214. He commends it to every man here. The College fed the bog hay under control and measured what it did, and it found the butter fat test depressed by **two tenths** in the winter feeding. That is the whole case. Two tenths from the College, laid alongside the fifth of a point he has found in the field and the twenty-one hundredths that station has been complaining of, and the three agree so nearly that he does not know what more a reasonable man requires.

**An Objection (The 1911 Mystery)**: It has been urged upon him, by a gentleman who he knows will forgive his noticing it here, that in the summer of 1911 the testing at that station was broken off for six weeks, their own apparatus having failed them and a borrowed one being used, and that the figures stood higher during those six weeks than before or after. Six weeks in the flush of the season, gentlemen. Six weeks of June and July, when every herd in the county is at its best and the stock are off the wet ground and up on the aftermath. He is astonished that anybody should found an argument on six weeks of summer figures. He could produce you six weeks in any year that would prove anything you liked.

**The Remedy**: It is not a mystery and it is not cheap. Drain the bottom; open the old ditches and cut new; lime the ground and lime it again; sow it down when it will carry a seeding; keep the stock off it in the wet of the spring, when a hoof does more harm in a day than a season will repair; and never feed that hay alone. Feed it with a mixed ration and a mineral supplement, as the College directs. Fat has been worth twenty-seven cents the pound through these years, and a fifth of a point taken off every hundredweight a farm ships, year in and year out, will pay for a great deal of tile.

**DISCUSSION**:

*A member* asked whether the difference might not be in the cows rather than the land, the bottom farms being poorer men with poorer stock.

*Dr. Kepp* replied that he had considered it and rejected it; that some of the best-bred cattle in the county stand on wet land, and some very indifferent cattle on the hills test well; and that a man may improve his herd for twenty years and never get his fat up while his ground is sour.

*A member* asked whether the trouble might lie in the station rather than the bottom, as had been said in the valley for years.

*Dr. Kepp* replied that he had no interest in that quarrel and no competence in it; that he had never in his life read a test and would not know how; but that when a cause is found in the soil, in the herbage, in the condition of the stock, in the College's trial, and in every wet bottom in the county alike, it is not good sense to go looking for a second cause in a building.

*The chairman* thanked Dr. Kepp and observed that the paper would be printed for the members.

**The Paradox**:
- Board found "no cause established" yet awarded $3,800 "without admission"
- Effie Loomis: "It is not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once."
- Ansel: "They found no cause established and they put me out on the last day of the month, and both of those things are true at once, and I have given up trying to hold them in my head together."
- Frick: Award without finding is unsatisfactory; honest man ruined by unexplained figure

**The Mechanism** (as proven 1958):
- Pipettes short by 1/20th (16.72 instead of 17.6 c.c., 95% delivery)
- Every test read 95% of true value (proportional error, not additive)
- Over 25 years: 76,000 pounds of fat lost (equivalent to 19 hundredths of recorded average)
- Loss proportional to fat content (best herds hurt most; Vose's hypothetical 4.56% herd would lose ~0.24 points)
- 1911 mystery solved: borrowed measure for 9 weeks delivered 100% (3.80 recorded); own measure delivered 95% (3.61 recorded)
- No evidence watering occurred; defective glass alone explains all data (Merle Strawn never found water; Rosalie Cudd never saw watered milk; Alder Corners figures confirm milk was good)
- Exoneration came 33 years after dismissal, 24 years after Ansel's death
- Glass condemned June 12, 1923; dismissal April 30, 1925; new glass July 1923, tests rose to 3.80+
- Board explicitly found no cause despite knowing (or should have known) about June 1923 condemnation
- Cream arrived heavy at Larrow Green every year (per Hobart Sill), proving fat did not leave station illegally

**Conflicts & Oddities Between Sources**:
1. Milk intake: Ivy/Effie say 40M; Teague says 44M
2. Test average: Teague says 3.16 overall; Ivy says 3.61 recorded; Kepp says 0.21 gap (implying 3.59–3.80)
3. Award: Vira says $3,500; Effie & Board & Teague & Frick say $3,800
4. Vose's 1924 rise: Vose says "21 hundredths to 3.90"; Strawn says year ended "3.80"
5. Bulletin 214 effect: 5 hundredths claimed; Kepp/Grigg invoke it; Ivy notes it's 4× too small
6. 1911 break duration: Ivy says "9 weeks"; Kepp dismisses as "6 weeks in summer"; Ansel's entry says "May 8–July 10" (~9 weeks)
7. Hollow milk quality: Sill says "really 3.85"; Kepp says "0.21 gap" (implying 3.59–3.80); Rosalie Cudd's 3.78–3.82 at Alder Corners suggests closer to 3.80–3.90
8. Cream theft rumor: Settled valley opinion (Rundle, Dell), Teague's insinuation, Sill's claim "heavy cream"—all contradicted by Sill's own receiving-book entries showing excess, not theft (fat arrived at Larrow Green, not missing)
9. Glass condemnation timing: Strawn condemned June 12, 1923 "on burette test"; Vose says July 12; Rundle says July 12; invoice Oct 2 July 1923
10. Lost minute book: Effie says 1889–1892 lost in 1938 office move; this is the gap in records, never recovered
11. Crate discovery: Ivy found in February 1958; came up June 1923; address on crate says "TARNET" (Ammon & Sons)
12. First season milk: Sill says ~900,000 lbs (May–Dec 1896); Vaught says "1896 intake not in ledgers or elsewhere"—confirming missing first season
13. Orra's death timeline: Vira says she "outlived him a good while"; Ansel died 1934; no date given for Orra

**The Verdict**:
No milk was watered, no cream was stolen. Defective pipettes (short by 5%) alone explain the 19-hundredth gap. The 1911 measure break proves this: same milk, same cows, borrowed measure (accurate) = 3.80; own measure (short) = 3.61. Society blamed Ansel falsely for 33 years. Board found "no cause" but paid $3,800 "without admission" on April 30–May 4, 1925. Ansel was dismissed April 30, while board still sitting. Bulletin 471 (October 1958) exonerated him formally, 24 years after his death.
