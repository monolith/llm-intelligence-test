# Answers: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math (14 points)

### C1: The Error Proportioned to the Glassware

**(a) Proportion by which tests differed:**

The nine surviving pipettes from the crate measured mean 16.72 c.c. when they were marked 17.6 c.c. at the stem. 

Short amount: 17.6 – 16.72 = 0.88 c.c.

Proportion: 0.88 ÷ 17.6 = 0.05 = **5%** (or stated as a ratio, one part in twenty).

The pipettes delivered 95% of their marked volume. Therefore, every butterfat reading made with them was proportionally low: the recorded reading is 95% of the true reading, or equivalently, the true reading is the recorded reading divided by 0.95.

**(b) True average test and points short:**

Recorded station average: 3.61%
True average: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80%
Gap: 3.80 – 3.61 = **0.19 percentage points (19 hundredths)**

**(c) Pounds of butterfat unpaid:**

Total milk delivered (25 full years, 1898–1922): 40,000,000 pounds

At recorded 3.61%: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 pounds butterfat credited
At true 3.80%: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 pounds butterfat delivered

Difference: 1,520,000 – 1,444,000 = **76,000 pounds of butterfat not credited** (this same figure appears in Bulletin 471)

**(d) Worth and fraction received:**

Worth at 25¢/lb average price: 76,000 × 0.25 = **$19,000 (total loss)**

Board awarded 1925: **$3,800**

Fraction received: 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = 0.20 = **one-fifth (20%)**

### C2: Emmet Brant's 1910 Account

Emmet Brant kept a personal weigh book. For 1910, the recorded leaf states: "Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42."

**(a) Butterfat credited in 1910:**
200,000 pounds × 0.0342 = **6,840 pounds** (at recorded 3.42%)

**(b) Butterfat actually delivered and not credited:**
True test: 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60%
True butterfat: 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 pounds
Owed but not credited: 7,200 – 6,840 = **360 pounds**

**(c) Value of the difference that year:**
360 × 0.25 = **$90** (at 25¢/pound, the period average)

[Note: The error fell hardest on the richest milk. A herd recorded at 4.56 was short 0.24 points; a herd at 3.42 was short 0.18 points. Emmet's milk at 3.42 took a relatively smaller loss than higher-butterfat herds, but still lost.]

### C3: Station Gain as Percentage

Over the 25-year period:

Butterfat credited to patrons on station test: 1,444,000 pounds
Butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in station cream: 1,520,000 pounds
Difference (station gain): 1,520,000 – 1,444,000 = 76,000 pounds

**Two valid percentages:**
- As percentage of fat delivered: 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = 0.05 = **5%**
- As percentage of fat credited: 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 0.0525 = **5.25%**

The difference is which denominator you use. A bookkeeper naturally reckons surplus against the figure already entered (the credited amount), hence 5.25%. The glass itself accounts for 5% (one part in twenty). Ivy Keddie's 1958 measurement report noted both: "Neither figure is an error. But five percent is the one that is also the glass."

### C5: Duration and Delivery Years

**(a) How long faulty glass was in use and why the numbers differ:**

- **Period of use:** May 1, 1897 to June 12, 1923 = **26 years, 1 month, 12 days** (over 26 years)
- **Full delivery years included in damage calculation:** 1898–1922 = **25 full years**

These are different because:
1. The station opened May 4, 1896 but did not put the faulty glassware into use until May 1, 1897.
2. 1897 was a partial year (May–December only, or possibly the first full year is 1898).
3. The glass was condemned June 12, 1923, meaning new glass was in use from July/August 1923 onward.
4. The damage calculation (40 million pounds, 25 years) uses only complete years where both intake and output records are solid and comparable: 1898–1922.
5. Strawn's 1923 entry notes new glass was in use "since July"; 1924 entry notes "Tests improved; their book gives year at 3.80."

Why both numbers matter: the duration tells us how long the equipment was faulty; the delivery-year window tells us the period for which we can confidently calculate money damages using comparable ledgers.

**(b) First season milk (1896):**

The station opened May 4, 1896. It borrowed glass from Larrow Green for that first season. Its own glass arrived in 1897. Station account records in the ledgers begin 1897. 

The office files show no ledger entry for 1896 milk at the station. Bertram Kyle (clerk) testified: "The station up at Ostrey Hollow was opened before my time, and for its first season or two it borrowed its testing glass from the creamery here, and then its own outfit came from the supply house and went into use on the first of May, 1898. I have that from the correspondence... That first season, before ever there was a station account, the milk was weighed at the Hollow and carried on the creamery's own books, and the sheets for it were never put with the station papers. People have asked me what the Hollow took in that first year and I have always said the same: a good eleven hundred thousand pounds, which is what I understood at the time from men who were there. It is not in any book I ever ruled."

**First season (1896) milk: ~1,100,000 pounds** (reported from oral history, not in ledgers; Ivy's and Teague's figures for later years differ on whether to count this or not, which is why the final ledger-based calculation uses 40 million for 25 full years 1898–1922, implying the 1897 partial year and 1896 first year are excluded from the final damage total).

## Section D — Logic (10 points)

### D1: The 1911 Measure Interval as an Experiment

**When and how long:**
- Broke: May 8, 1911
- Repaired/replaced: July 10, 1911
- Duration: Exactly 9 weeks (63 days, or May 8 to July 10 inclusive)

**Variable:** The measuring glass (borrowed vs. own)

**Held constant:**
- Same tester (Ansel Keddie, same man, same hands, same practice, same training)
- Same patrons and herds (including Emmet Brant's largest herd)
- Same season (May through July, summer flush period, when quality should be highest and most consistent)
- Same pasture and feed (green grass, early summer)
- Same acid, bottles, bottles' marking, hand tester, crank speed
- Same procedure and timing
- Same weather (consecutive days)
- Same separator machinery
- No intervention, no change to farm management

**Result:**
- During borrowed measure (May 8 – July 10): average test **3.80%**
- Before break (1898–May 7): average test **3.61%**
- After return to own glass (July 10 – 1924): average test **3.61%**
- The change back to 3.61 was immediate upon resuming own glass July 10

**What it establishes:**
The 19-hundredth shortfall was caused by the measuring apparatus, not by the milk, the cows, the feed, the tester's honesty, the acid, the bottles, or the procedures. When the apparatus changed, the result changed proportionally. When it returned, the result returned. This is strong evidence of instrumental (not operational or biological) causation.

**What it does NOT establish:**
- The mechanism: how or why the glass delivers 95% of its mark. (The 1958 burette test answered this; the 1911 interval alone did not.)
- The precise error: the interval shows the problem is the glass, not its magnitude or nature in detail.
- The age at which the glass developed the error: the break occurred in 1911, but the glass may have been slightly short since 1897, or may have degraded gradually. The interval shows the new (borrowed) glass read at 3.80 (true level), but does not tell us whether Ansel's glass was short in 1897 or became short later.

### D2: Three Independent Reasons Why Ansel Keddie Could Not Have Been Stealing Cream

**Reason 1 (Operating constraint): The scale was the gateway.**

Wilbur Teague (bookkeeper, 1905–1945) testified: "Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons. One man weighed milk, drew sample, made test, wrote figure pay slip made from, ran separator. No second man in building spring to fall... A business which is arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken from it, and no set of books ever made can prevent it. The remedy is two men or a lock, and the Association had neither."

Yet Ansel never took cream. The test book was in his hand; the ledger gains matched his test perfectly; if he had skimmed, his tests would have to be lower by more than 5% to cover both the loss and the cream remaining. He would have had to conspire with Teague, who certified every year that the figures balanced. The system allowed theft; Ansel chose not to take the opportunity.

**Reason 2 (The 1911 Experiment): Same man, different glass, tests rose.**

Merle Strawn (state dairy inspector), Ivy Keddie (measuring the glassware in 1958), and Duncan Keddie (Ansel's son, explaining the 1911 mystery) all attest: when Ansel used the borrowed measure for nine weeks in May–July 1911, the tests rose from 3.61 to 3.80 and fell back immediately when he returned to his own glass. Ansel was not accused of stealing in 1911; he was the same man doing the same work with the same integrity. The difference was glass. If Ansel had been taking cream every day, his recorded numbers would jump with the new measure too—but they did not jump because of cream removal; they jumped because the measure was short by 5%. The borrowed measure was not short (or was short in the opposite direction), so it read true. This proves Ansel's theft hypothesis is inconsistent with the 1911 interval itself.

**Reason 3 (The pattern is wrong for theft): Proportional, not fixed, loss.**

If Ansel had stolen a fixed amount of cream every day (say 50 lbs), his recorded tests would understate by a *fixed* amount, not a *proportional* amount. A herd recorded at 4.56 would be off by the same fixed pounds as a herd at 3.42. Instead, Ivy Keddie's 1958 measurement shows the loss was proportional: a herd that actually tested at 4.56 was recorded at 4.32 (short 0.24); a herd at 3.42 was recorded at 3.26 (short 0.16, proportionally less). This is exactly what a short measuring pipette produces—proportional error—not what a thief taking a fixed daily amount produces. The evidence pattern is incompatible with systematic theft.

**Independence of reasons:**

- Reason 1 (the system allowed theft but Ansel didn't) is about opportunity and choice.
- Reason 2 (1911 interval) is about the same man with different equipment producing different results.
- Reason 3 (proportional error pattern) is about the mathematics of the loss relative to milk quality.

These three arguments do not depend on each other. Reason 1 alone shows the system didn't force honesty (he could have stolen). Reason 2 alone shows that the tester's behavior didn't change (he was the same man). Reason 3 alone shows that the loss pattern matches instrumental error, not theft.

**Which hold without the 1958 measurement?**

Reasons 1 and 2 both hold without the 1958 glassware test. Reason 1 is purely logical (the system allowed theft, yet no herd recorded extra gain under Ansel; the theft theory requires he stole and recorded loss). Reason 2 is historical (the 1911 interval, documented in his own test book and Strawn's circuit notes in real time, is independent of the 1958 measurement—it was already evidence in 1925 and should have been decisive then).

Reason 3 (the proportional error pattern) can be inferred from the test-book pattern without measuring glass—Ivy's 1958 measurement confirmed it precisely, but the proportional gap was visible in the recorded figures from 1897 onward. The shape of the error (flat proportional loss every month, every season) was incompatible with theft, even before the glass was measured.

**Did the bookkeeper question the entries?**

No. Wilbur Teague testified: "I put it to Manager Vose in 1916. Told to let it alone. Let it alone. I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience, and a bookkeeper who takes his employer's business up a second time after being told once has generally begun to look for another employer." Teague saw the pattern clearly (a figure that came in at five and a quarter every year); he raised it once with the manager; he was silenced. He then entered the same line for decades more without question. He did not publicly contradict the station's figures in the 1925 hearing; he only testified to the technical facts when asked. He did not refute Ansel's character, though he could have. His silence was obedience, not doubt.

### D3: Did the Manager Know the Tests Ran Low?

**Answer: Yes, Selby Vose knew the tests ran low before 1923, almost certainly from the ledger sheets he signed every year.**

**Justification:**

Bertram Kyle (office clerk) states: "Mr. Selby Vose sat behind that door for a quarter of a century and I sat outside it... He was courteous to every man who came into that office, farmer or drummer or bank, and he never once let a caller feel he was being hurried. And he read the totals and he never read the lines above them. He was not a man who read. He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign."

Kyle describes Vose as a man who read bottoms (totals) not lines above. However, the "bottom figure" on the station account sheet would have been the station gain or the charge to the general account—a figure that was always there, year after year, reliably 5% or so. Vose signed every year (attested by Kyle, by Teague, and by the ledgers).

Teague testified: "Mr. Vose knew what that account was doing. Signed sheet every year. Spring I laid annual sheet before him, he put name at foot. A man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him."

More directly, in his 1926 manager's address, Vose himself explains the rise in 1924 tests: "Then the Association's attention came to rest on the station, and there was a petition, and a hearing, and men were required to answer questions in front of their neighbors. And in the very next season the tests at that station rose by twenty-one hundredths and the average for 1924 stood at 3.90, which is as good a figure as any station in this county will show you."

Vose attributes the 1924 rise to the public scrutiny (pressure on patrons or tester to behave differently). But his explanation assumes he knew the tests had been low before—otherwise, what would have to rise? He is implicitly acknowledging that the shortfall existed prior to 1924, and that the hearing caused it to correct.

Additionally, in 1919 Vose sent the water letter—an accusation that the Ostrey Hollow patrons were adding water to milk. This letter suggests he had a theory about why the tests ran low. The timing (1919, after years of complaints since 1902) implies he was aware of the pattern and was offering an explanation. If he were unaware the tests ran low, there would be no motivation to send such a letter.

**Conclusion:**

Vose knew or should have known that the station's tests ran low because: (1) he signed the ledger sheets every year showing the station gain; (2) he offered an explanation for the low tests in 1919 (water); (3) he acknowledged in 1926 that the tests had been running low and attributed the 1924 rise to external pressure. He did not investigate the glassware or question the tester's procedure until forced by the state inspector's 1923 condemnation. His knowledge did not translate into action.

## Section F — Theory (6 points)

### The Causal Theory: What Produced the Shortfall at Ostrey Hollow

The Ostrey Hollow station's butterfat test readings ran 5% low from 1897 to 1923 (except for the nine-week interval in 1911) because the six dozen milk-test pipettes purchased from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet on April 14, 1897, were manufactured short by 5%—each blown to deliver 16.72 c.c. instead of the marked 17.6 c.c.

This error was embedded in the molds or the manufacturing process and was not caught by the glassblower or the quality checker at the time of manufacture. The pipettes were packed, shipped, and put into use on May 1, 1897. They were accurate and identical to each other in their shortness, which meant the error was *proportional*, not random. Every patron's test was systematically low by exactly the same 5%.

The shortfall began when the faulty glass arrived (May 1897) and ended when it was condemned and replaced (June 12, 1923). The reason nothing at the station or on the farms had to change is that the error was built into the instrument, not produced by the environment, the operator, or the milk. The tester (Ansel Keddie) was meticulous and made no mistakes. The patrons' herds did not change quality. The seasons did not change. The separator did not break down. The system was working as designed—it was the design (the purchased glassware) that was faulty.

The error persisted for 26 years because:
1. **No one measured the glass against a true standard.** The state dairy inspector's burette program was not instituted until 1921, and Merle Strawn did not run Ostrey Hollow's glass against a burette until his June 1923 visit.
2. **The error was too small and too regular to trigger investigation.** A figure that varies wildly prompts inquiry; a figure that comes in at 5% every year is invisible as error—it becomes "normal," given a name ("station gain"), and filed away without question.
3. **No one compared tests between stations rigorously in writing.** The 19-hundredth gap was whispered about (Emmet Brant, the largest patron, knew his own scale; the state inspector knew his lactometer); but these observations never made it into the Association's formal record before 1925.
4. **The rule (Article VII) that the station's test governs admits no appeal.** Patrons could complain; they could be heard; but the test was sovereign. No one could force the Association to check the test's accuracy until the patrons petitioned en masse in 1925.

### The Three Competing Explanations and Their Refutation

**Explanation 1: Water was added to the milk by the Ostrey Hollow patrons.**

- **Advanced by:** Selby Vose (manager), in his June 1919 circular letter to the nine families.
- **Evidence that defeats it:** 
  - Merle Strawn's lactometer tests (state dairy inspector). His circuit notes, repeatedly: "Lactometer, no added water" (1908, 1909, 1912, 1913, 1916, 1920, 1922). His explicit 1919 entry: "I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him [the manager] so."
  - The 1911 interval proves water is not the cause. If patrons had been adding water, they would have continued during the nine weeks (May–July 1911) when the borrowed measure was in use. Yet the tests rose to 3.80 during that interval, only to fall back to 3.61 when Ansel returned to his own glass. The patrons did not suddenly stop adding water (and then resume it).
  - The State Agricultural College's Bulletin 214 (1911) tested bog hay's effect on butterfat: "depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." This maximum effect (5 hundredths) does not approach the 19-hundredth gap, even combined with water. Orin Falke, county agent, withdrew his initial acceptance of the wet-hay explanation after receiving Bulletin 214: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July. I withdraw the concession made on page four."

**Explanation 2: The tester (Ansel Keddie) was stealing cream and recording false low tests to conceal the loss.**

- **Advanced by:** Officers of the Association, in the 1925 arbitration hearing (implied by the burden placed on Keddie to prove his innocence; explicit in Wilbur Teague's testimony about a system "arranged so that a single man can take a little").
- **Evidence that defeats it:**
  - The 1911 interval. Same tester, same hands, same station, same patrons, same procedures—different glass. Tests rose. If Ansel had been stealing 5% every day and recording 5% low to hide it, his recorded tests would have jumped when the borrowed glass arrived. They did not; they jumped up to 3.80 (true level) because the borrowed glass was accurate. This proves the tester's behavior did not change (he was not secretly stealing); the instrument changed (from short to accurate).
  - Wilbur Teague's own ledgers. If Ansel had stolen cream, Teague would have recorded less cream arriving at Larrow Green than Ansel's test book said was being sent down. Yet Teague's own annual balance showed a consistent 5.25% surplus (the "station gain"). The cream *did* arrive as tested. If Ansel had stolen and recorded low, there would be no surplus; there would be a deficit, or Teague would have caught the discrepancy.
  - The proportional structure of the loss. The error falls hardest on the richest milk (a 4.56% herd was short 0.24 points; a 3.42% herd was short 0.16). This is the proportional error produced by a short measuring pipette, not the fixed-amount theft a person would take ("I take 10 lbs of cream daily, come what may"). A thief's loss would be the same for every herd; the glass's error is proportional to quality.

**Explanation 3: The wet hay of Hessel Bottom (boggy soil, poor-quality feed) caused the cream of that region's cows to test lower.**

- **Advanced by:** The investigating committee in 1908; a veterinarian at Ashlin in 1926; Selby Vose (as a secondary factor, implicitly).
- **Evidence that defeats it:**
  - The State Agricultural College's Bulletin 214 (1911): bog hay reduced butterfat by "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." This is far below the 19-hundredth gap observed year-round.
  - The 1911 broken measure interval. Hessel Bottom's pasture, hay quality, and cows did not change between May 7 and May 8, 1911. Yet the test results changed. If wet hay were the cause, there would be no improvement when the borrowed glass arrived.
  - Comparative test data. Four of the six Hessel Bottom farms hauled their milk in summer flush to the Alder Corners Cheese Factory (across the ridge) and that milk was tested there. Halbert Nym's curator notes (retelling 7): "Over ridge: Alder Corners factory tested Hessel Bottom milk in summer flush. 14 overlapping seasons: same milk ran 19 hundredths higher at Alder Corners than at home." The *same milk*, *same farms*, *same season*—tested by Alder Corners' equipment and got 3.80, tested by Ostrey Hollow and got 3.61. The Hessel Bottom farms' participation in this summer interchange proves their milk quality did not differ at different locations; the *test* differed.

### Subsidiary Questions

**(i) Did the 1925 board of arbitration ever examine the condemned glassware?**

**No.** The glassware was condemned June 12, 1923. The board convened March 17, 1925—nearly two years later. The crate sat in the loft above the intake room. Two of the board's sittings (parts 2 and 3 of the arbitration) were held at the station itself, in the weigh room. Cleve Oram (cream hauler's son, retelling 21) stood outside on the platform listening to the voices and notes: "the crate was up the ladder over their heads the entire time. Twenty feet, if that... The four books [brought to the hearing] I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked." Halbert Nym's curator note (item 7): "Award phrase 'the books and appliances of the station' is form of words, not list of anything."

The record shows no motion, no examination, no evidence introduced from the crate itself. The board's award mentions "the books and appliances of the station" as having been before it, but this phrase is formulaic, not specific. The actual books and appliances examined were the test book, receiving book, weigh book, and separator—not the pipettes themselves.

**(ii) What did the tester himself believe the cause was?**

**Ansel Keddie could not explain the cause and said so explicitly.** In his 1925 statement to the board: "Cannot tell why figures are what they are." In his unsent letter: "Seems to me somebody ought to know how the work was done before everybody who did it is gone." He documented every step of his procedure meticulously. He recorded the 1911 measure breakage and put a question mark beside it (the only uncertainty he allowed in the book in 29 years). But he never answered that question mark.

In his 1922 summary, he noted that his average test from 1898 onward was "3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911... when it stood at 3.80." He was aware of the 1911 anomaly; he suspected something was amiss in 1911 (hence the question mark). But he could not point to what was wrong, and he would not guess. His son Duncan later wrote: "He had, after all, nothing to hold in his hand but a suspicion about a piece of glass, and against it stood the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county."

Ansel's belief was likely this: the glassware. The fact that his tests rose in 1911 and fell back, with no other change, pointed to the measuring device. He marked it in his book with a question mark. But he had no instrument to measure the glass against a true standard (burettes did not appear on his circuit until 1921), and he had no authority to condemn or replace equipment. So he kept on testing, kept on recording, and kept on trusting his procedure and his honesty. He died in 1934 without learning the name of the error—that his glass was short by 5%—but knowing something was wrong.

## Section G — Summary (4 points)

### The Eight-Story History in 150 Words

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, founded in 1889, established a payment rule: farmers are paid by butterfat test, not milk weight; the station's test governs. At Ostrey Hollow skimming station (1896+), tester Ansel Keddie ran meticulous daily records. Yet from 1897 onward, Ostrey Hollow's tests ran 5% low—19 hundredths—year after year. Three theories blamed patrons' water, the tester's theft, or wet hay; all were disproven. In 1919, a manager's water-accusation letter ruptured Ansel's family (estranging him from his wife's brother). A 1925 arbitration found "no cause" yet awarded $3,800 to patrons and dismissed Ansel. In June 1923, a state inspector had condemned the station's original glassware (purchased 1897). In February 1958, Ansel's granddaughter Ivy found the crate in the loft and measured the pipettes: each was short by 5%, explaining the entire 26-year shortfall. Bulletin 471 (October 1958) exonerated Ansel—but he had died 24 years earlier. The patrons had lost $19,000; they received $3,800 (one-fifth).
