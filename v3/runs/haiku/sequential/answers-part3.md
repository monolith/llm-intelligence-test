# Answers—Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math (show working)

### C1. (a) Proportion of error from glassware.

The faulty pipettes were blown on the stem at 17.6 c.c. (the standard charge of milk for a butter fat test). Ivy Keddie's measurement sheet (March 9, 1958) shows nine surviving pipettes, each tested five times against a calibrated burette. The individual means per pipette were: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 c.c. The mean of these nine means is 16.72 c.c.

Ratio of delivered to marked capacity: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 (or 95 hundredths, or 95%).
Shortness: 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c., which is 0.88 ÷ 17.6 = 0.05, or 5%, or one part in twenty short.

**Note:** Bulletin No. 471 (Vaught, October 1958) cites "mean delivery of the nine is 16.27 c.c." and "ratio... 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244." This would give 16.27 / 17.6 = 0.9244 = 92.44%, a 7.56% shortness. However, Ivy Keddie's actual measurement sheet (reprinted in accession notes r07 and her measurement sheet r04) records the nine individual means and states their mean as 16.72 c.c. with shortness of 0.88 c.c. The arithmetic 16.72 / 17.6 = 0.95 and 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 are consistent with each other. The Bulletin's 16.27 figure appears inconsistent with the measurement sheet's data. **I resolve this by using 16.72 c.c. as the accurate measurement, giving a 5% shortness (one part in twenty), consistent with the 3.80 / 3.61 test ratio.**

### C1. (b) True average test and shortfall in points.

Recorded average at Ostrey Hollow station: 3.61% butterfat.
Ratio of delivered to marked: 0.95.
True average: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80% butterfat.
Shortfall: 3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19 percentage points.

Alternatively, using Bulletin 471's figures (16.27 / 0.9244): true average would be 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.905%, shortfall 0.295 points. **I use 3.80 as the true average, based on the 1911 anomaly (when correct glass was used, tests stood at 3.80), and the measurement sheet's 16.72 mean.**

### C1. (c) Unrealized butterfat over full delivery years.

Total milk received 1898–1922: 40,000,000 pounds (sources: Teague, Loomis, Nym, Vaught).
Recorded butterfat credited: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 pounds.
True butterfat delivered: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 pounds.
Unrealized butterfat (not credited): 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = **76,000 pounds**.

(Vaught's Bulletin 471 cites 118,000 lbs using the 3.905 figure; Ivy Keddie's Chronicle statement uses 76,000. The ledgers themselves carry "station gain" of 5.25%, which applied to 1,444,000 credited yields 76,000 pounds, supporting the 76,000 figure.)

### C1. (d) Money value and fraction received.

Value at average price: 76,000 pounds × $0.25 per pound = **$19,000**.
Award received: $3,800.
Fraction: $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20, or **1/5 (one-fifth)**.

Over 25 full delivery years (1898–1922):

**(a) Total butterfat credited to patrons on station test:**
40,000,000 pounds of milk × 3.61% average = **1,444,000 pounds**.

**(b) Total butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in cream:**
1,444,000 pounds (credited) + 76,000 pounds (gain) = **1,520,000 pounds**.

**(c) Express the difference as a percentage:**
Difference: 76,000 pounds.
As percentage of fat actually delivered (1,520,000): 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = 0.05 = **5%** of the fat delivered.

Both are correct; they have different denominators. Teague's ledger entries use the second form (the credit baseline), as "it is natural for a bookkeeper to reckon a surplus against the figure he has already entered."

## Section D — Logic

### D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment.

**When and duration:** May 8, 1911 to July 10, 1911. Duration: 9 weeks, 2 days (or "nine weeks" as the sources consistently say).

**Variable:** The measure (pipette). The station's own pipette broke on May 8, 1911 (recorded in Ansel's test book: "May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure"). A measure borrowed from Larrow Green was used. On July 10, the station's own measure was repaired or returned and went back into use ("July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.").

**Held constant:** The patrons (same nine families on Hessel Bottom), the herds (same cows), the pasture (same ground, wet meadow), the season (May–July, flush season, highest test period), the tester (Ansel Keddie, same man), the procedure (same method, same hands), the acid (same chemical), the bottles (same glassware marked for 17.6 c.c., if they were the same bottles), the receiver of the milk (same creamery intake).

**Result:** Tests rose from 3.61 (the station's recorded long-term average, including winter months when tests would be lower) to 3.80 during the nine weeks. They fell back to 3.61 when the faulty glass returned. The difference was immediate: "Tests are back where they were" on July 10.

**What it establishes:** The experiment proves that a constant proportion (5%) of the recorded figures were systematically lost due to the measure, not due to men, cows, land, season, or procedure. It establishes that one variable—the glass—was responsible for the entire shortfall. No other single variable was altered. Therefore, no explanation blaming the herds (poor cows), the ground (wet hay), the men (water-adding, cream-stealing), or the season (summer vs. winter fluctuation) can account for a consistent 19-hundredth gap that persisted in July and February alike. The 1911 interval proves it was the glass.

**What it does NOT establish:** It does not prove that the tests in 1911 (3.80) were the true average for the entire 26 years, because the summer 1911 tests may have been higher than the winter average. It does not identify the specific mechanism of error (short delivery of charge) until the glass is measured in 1958. It does not identify the date the error began or confirm it began in 1897 (though the test book's first recorded average, 1896, shows 3.80, suggesting the borrowed glass was correct). Without measurement of the faulty glass, the 1911 interval alone does not establish the 5% shortness quantitatively, only that glass was the culprit.

### D2. Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream.

**Reason 1: The cream arrived at Larrow Green heavier than credited.** Hobart Sill (buttermaker, 1934) testified that the Ostrey Hollow cream "ran heavy" every year: "There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there... Always more. Never less." He weighed the cream himself when it arrived. Sill notes: "I weighed it myself, most years. The platform man weighed it and I weighed it over again when it came into the cold room... the Hollow's cream coming in heavy on page after page after page, in my own writing, for as long as I have been in this room." If Keddie had stolen cream (which would reduce the quantity leaving the station), the cream arriving at Larrow Green would weigh *less*, not *more*, than credited. This is independent of the 1911 anomaly and of any glass measurement.

**Reason 2: The 1911 anomaly itself.** During the nine weeks a borrowed (correct) measure was used, tests rose to 3.80 and fell back when the faulty glass returned. Wilbur Teague (bookkeeper) testified at the arbitration: "I said that a business which is arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken from it... The remedy is two men or a lock, and the Association had neither." Teague implied Keddie could have done it. But the 1911 interval shows that when the glass changed (and nothing else did), the figures changed immediately. If Keddie were stealing and writing low, he would have had the same incentive and opportunity with the borrowed glass. He did not take less then. The variable that changed was the glass, not his behavior. This is independent of Sill's cream-weight testimony and requires no glass measurement.

**Reason 3: The independent test books.** Emmet Brant kept a weigh book of his own milk from 1897 onward. His own scale and the station's scale "agreed" to within a pound or two over 30 years (Roy Brant, 1955: "The two scales, his and the station's, kept by two men who by the end were not speaking to one another, and the scales never fell out at all. Whatever was wrong at that station... it was not the weighing"). If Keddie were stealing cream, he could not touch the milk before it was weighed. The theft would have to occur after weighing but before or during separation. Separation yields cream and skim; the skim goes back to the patron, the cream goes down the valley. If Keddie took cream, the skim would have to be heavier to compensate (else the patron would notice the recovered skim). But this would be obvious to the patron and would leave evidence in his own use of the skim. None of the sources report patrons complaining about short skim or unusual skim weights. The scales agreed; the skim was accounted for; therefore, no cream was removed from the station. This is independent of Sill's testimony and the 1911 interval, and does not require the glass to be measured (it only requires the consistency of Brant's own ledger).

**Are the reasons independent?** Yes. Reason 1 (cream weight) depends on the receiver's records (Sill's testimony and the receiving book). Reason 2 (the 1911 anomaly) depends on the test book entries and the operator's immediate response. Reason 3 (the scales and skim accounting) depends on Emmet Brant's independent weigh book. No two of these depend on the same source or record.

**Which would still hold if glass had never been measured?** All three. Reason 1 (heavy cream) would stand on Sill's testimony alone. Reason 2 (the 1911 anomaly) would stand on the test book's notation without needing to know *why* the glass was different. Reason 3 (the scales and skim) would stand on Brant's ledger and patron accounting alone.

**Did the bookkeeper question the entries?** Wilbur Teague kept the "station gain" line every year with the phrase "five and a quarter per cent, as usual." Teague's 1948 memoir states: "The gain ran at five and a quarter percent of the fat that was actually in the cream. It ran there year after year, so evenly that the words 'as usual' got into the entry and stayed there, and a man may as well be honest about what evenness does to a bookkeeper: it stops him looking. A figure that jumps about will keep you awake. A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture." Teague asked no questions. He testified at the 1925 hearing, describing his "station gain" line: "I said that a business which is arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken from it." He was arguing that the structure invited theft. But he never challenged the ledger entries themselves. He signed off on them. He later wrote (1948): "I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone. I let it alone. I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience."

### D3. Did the manager know the station's tests ran low?

**Answer: Yes, Selby Vose knew the tests ran low. He signed the ledgers every year, and the figures on them established the persistent gap.**

**Justification:** Wilbur Teague (1948 memoir) states: "I laid the annual sheet before him each spring and he put his name at the foot of it, and a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him. What he made of it he never told me." The station account was explicit: every year from 1913 onward (when Vose became manager), the sheet carried "butterfat credited to patrons on station test" vs. "butterfat received at Larrow Green in station cream," with the difference entering as "station gain." The average Ostrey Hollow test, over the full period, was 3.16–3.61, depending on the years counted. The Larrow Green creamery's average was 3.78, and "every year the numbers went right the summer after" high tests were found (in Vose's 1926 annual address). Vose had been in office since 1913; the pattern was unbroken from 1898 onward. He could not have *not* known. The sources do not establish *why* he did not act on it: whether he misinterpreted the figures, trusted that the system governed itself, or deliberately concealed the problem. But the evidence that he *signed* the sheets every year, and the figures were explicit, establishes that knowledge of the persistent gap was available to him.

Bertram Kyle (office clerk, 1953) corroborates: "I watched him do it a thousand times... turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign." Vose read the bottom line (perhaps the total profit or loss) and signed without reading the detail. This is negligence, not innocence.

## Section F — Theory

### F1. Causal theory of the shortfall.

**The true cause:** The Ostrey Hollow station received a shipment of milk-test pipettes from Ammon & Sons in May 1897, intended to be blown to a standard 17.6 c.c. charge. Due to an error in the glassblowing mold (likely reground before production, introducing a systematic dimensional error), the pipettes delivered only 16.72 c.c. (or possibly 16.27 c.c.; the measurement data shows 16.72). This represented a 5% shortness (0.95 of the standard charge). Because a test bottle's neck is ruled for a specific charge and no other, a short charge of milk yields a proportionally short reading of butterfat. Every test made at that station from May 1, 1897 to June 12, 1923 read 95 percent of the truth. Tests that should have read 3.80% read 3.61%. Tests that should have read 4.56% read 4.33%. The error was proportional (fell harder on rich milk), consistent (every day, every year, July and February alike), and precise (the same 5% every single time).

**Why the error persisted 26 years:** No one suspected the pipettes because:
1. Pipettes are the measure; they are not checked against anything else, unlike scales (checked with weights) or acid (checked for strength) or bottles (checked with mercury). An instrument that is itself the measure cannot be caught by measuring with it.
2. The system was designed by Article VII to rely on the station's test as the sole authority, permitting no external check or appeal.
3. The station's records (Ansel Keddie's test book) were immaculate: perfectly kept, internally consistent, marked in a clean hand, with no erasures. Consistency in error reads as reliability.
4. The persistent gap was rationally explained by competing theories that seemed to address the evidence available: water in the cans (a widespread practice in small-scale dairying, credible), cream theft (possible in a one-man operation, credible), or wet land (observable to anyone, credible). One plausible explanation satisfied each observer and removed the need to look further.
5. The 1911 anomaly—when a borrowed measure was used and tests rose to 3.80, then fell back when the faulty glass returned—sat with only a question mark in Ansel's book. No one in the office connected this to the long history; the office and the station operated separately.

**Why the error ended when it did:** On June 12, 1923, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn (riding circuit, checking appliances for wear) ran the station's pipettes against a calibrated burette, found they did not deliver their mark, and condemned the entire crate. This was routine maintenance: old glass fails, equipment wears. He ordered new. The Association ordered replacement glass that week (June 28, 1923). New glass arrived and went into use July–August 1923. The new glass delivered 17.6 c.c. Tests immediately rose to 3.80 (1924 average). No one inferred backward to the 26 years of error. The condemnation was treated as a maintenance matter, not as discovery of a long-standing systematic failure.

**Why nothing at the station or farms had to change:** The milk and cows were constant. The pasture did not improve. No farmer changed his practice. Ansel Keddie tested by the same method both before and after 1923. No water-adding stopped. Yet tests rose 19 hundredths in a day because the glass changed. This proves that the 26-year gap was *not* due to the men (Ansel), the milk (the patrons' cows), the land (Hessel Bottom's wet hay), the season (tests were low in July as much as February), or any practice that would require changed human behavior. The gap was due to the glass alone.

---

**The three competing explanations and the evidence that defeats each:**

**Explanation 1: Water in the cans (advanced by Selby Vose, manager, in his 1919 circular warning).**

Evidence urged: The milk tested lower and stayed lower year after year; a little water in the cans was a common small-farm practice; the manager's circular explicitly accused the patrons of water-adding.

Evidence that defeats it:
- Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks (1907–1936): At every visit to Ostrey Hollow, Strawn tested for added water with a lactometer. His notes consistently say "no added water." He tested from 1908 onward, including the year after the 1919 circular. He never found water.
- The 1911 anomaly: The same milk and same patrons, tested with a borrowed (correct) measure, showed tests at 3.80. If water were in the cans, the water would be in the 1911 milk too. But tests rose immediately when the glass changed, then fell immediately when the faulty glass returned. A practice (water-adding) would not turn on and off with the measure itself; the glass changed, not the milk.
- Rosalie Cudd's cheese factory records (1915–1930): Four Hessel Bottom farmers hauled the same milk in summer to her factory over the ridge. She tested it at 3.78–3.82%, concluding "Good milk off wet ground." A cheesemaker detects watered milk immediately (the curd does not knit, the body is slack). She made no complaints.
- The 1924 improvement: In 1924, with new correct glass, tests rose to 3.80. No farmer stopped watering; no new management initiative changed behavior. The tests improved because the glass was correct, proving that water-adding was not the cause of the low tests.

**Explanation 2: Cream theft by the tester (implied by Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper, at the 1925 hearing).**

Evidence urged: The station account carried a "station gain" line every year—more fat arrived at Larrow Green than the station claimed to have bought. A single-man operation with no supervision was vulnerable to theft. The tester could take cream and write low tests to hide the loss.

Evidence that defeats it:
- Hobart Sill's receiving book (1893–1934): Cream off the Ostrey Hollow wagon arrived at Larrow Green heavier than the papers said had been credited. "Always more. Never less." Sill weighed it himself. If cream had been stolen, the cans arriving would be lighter, not heavier. Theft would shrink the quantity; the quantity grew. Therefore, no cream was removed.
- The 1911 anomaly: The same tester, same opportunity, same method. When the glass changed and nothing else did, tests rose. If the tester were stealing, he had the same incentive and ability with the borrowed glass as with his own. He did not take less then. The variable was the glass, not his behavior.
- Emmet Brant's independent weigh book (1897–1929): His own scale and the station's scale agreed to within pounds over 30 years. The milk was weighed before it could be stolen. There is no record of complaints about short skim milk (which would be the remaining byproduct if cream were removed). The skim accounted for every pound.
- Merle Strawn's inspections: Strawn visited the station regularly and on his 1923 visit condemned the glass. He saw no sign of a one-man theft operation and kept no notes suggesting dishonesty.

Therefore, the cream arrived heavy because the faulty glass was undercounting its butterfat content; the cream itself was intact.

**Explanation 3: The wet hay of Hessel Bottom thins the cream (advanced by the 1908 committee and endorsed by Dr. Roland Kepp in 1926).**

Evidence urged: The 1908 committee sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through winter months (January–March) and found the milk "whole," scales "honest," and their opinion that "the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream" and "the remedy lies with the land." The meadow is demonstrably wet and sour, producing coarse hay. Dr. Kepp (1926) argued from agricultural science that sour ground low in lime and phosphorus naturally produces lower-fat milk. The State Agricultural College's Bulletin No. 214 (1911) found bog hay in a feeding trial depresses butterfat by two tenths in winter.

Evidence that defeats it:
- The 1911 anomaly: In summer 1911, when stock were off the wet hay and on good pasture, tests rose to 3.80 with the borrowed glass. The same low ground did not prevent high tests when the glass was correct. The hay had not changed, the land had not changed, the cows had not changed. Therefore, the hay was not the cause.
- The year-round flatness of the gap: The gap was 19 hundredths in July (when cows grazed good summer pasture and the College's Bulletin No. 214 found no measurable depression from bog hay) and 19 hundredths in February (when cows ate the bad hay and the College found up to two tenths depression). The gap was not seasonal or variable with feeding—it was the same in every month. Hay effects would show seasonally. The gap did not.
- The Alder Corners comparison (1915–1930): The same four Hessel Bottom farmers sent milk to Rosalie Cudd's factory in summer, where it tested 3.78–3.82, while at Ostrey Hollow it tested 3.61 on the same milk. This proof that the milk itself was good, not thinned by land, was not available to the 1908 committee but was available by 1958. It definitively refutes the land explanation.
- Bulletin No. 214's own findings: The bulletin found bog hay depresses butterfat "by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Five hundredths in winter was the maximum. The gap at Ostrey Hollow was 19 hundredths flat across all seasons. Even combining the worst seasonal effect with the worst hay effect, the hay could not account for a 19-hundredth year-round gap. Therefore, the hay was not the primary cause.

**Two subsidiary questions:**

**Did the 1925 board of arbitration examine the condemned glassware?**

No. Alonzo Frick (1946) lists the four exhibits before the board: "The station test book... The receiving book... The private weigh book of Emmet Brant... The circuit notebook of the state dairy inspector for the year 1923." The condemned crate itself was not brought before the board. It sat in the loft over the weigh room—20 feet above the room where the board sat on April 1925 (Cleve Oram observed this). The board never examined the glass. Frick later (1946) wondered whether "the answer was in that room and simply was not in any of the three stories" presented. The answer was literally in the loft.

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?**

Ansel Keddie believed in 1911 that "it was something in the glass." Duncan Keddie (1968) recalls: "In the summer of 1911, Ansel noted the anomaly in the test book with a question mark, and Duncan, then 17, recalls his father saying at supper and again by the door: 'it was something in the glass.' He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove, and he never wrote it as anything but a question mark." Ansel's unsent letter (spring 1925) notes the 1911 event: "Those nine weeks in 1911 are the strangest thing in my book and I have never had an account of them to give... That question mark is mine and I have let it stand for fourteen years. I could not answer it then and I cannot answer it now, and I will not put anything in its place that I do not know."

Ansel Keddie tested as taught, kept perfect records, and suspected the glass but refused to name it until he could prove it. He died in 1934 with the imputation on him. He never claimed innocence publicly, only accuracy: "I am an old man and I have no other trade. I have tested as I was taught... I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are."

## Section G — Summary (max. 150 words)

In 1897, the Larrow Valley Cooperative Association's Ostrey Hollow station received faulty milk-test pipettes that delivered only 95% of their marked capacity. For 26 years, every test read 5% low, crediting patrons 3.61% butterfat when milk actually contained 3.80%. The gap seemed explicable: water in cans, tester theft, or poor land. Three competing theories emerged, each defensible but none provable. In 1923, an inspector condemned the old glass; new glass was installed and tests improved, but no one connected the improvement to the 26-year error. The 1925 arbitration awarded $3,800 to patrons claiming $19,000 loss, "finding no cause established." Ansel Keddie, the tester, was dismissed without finding against him. He died under the imputation. In 1958, his granddaughter Ivy, a graduate student, found the crate in the loft, measured the pipettes, and proved the glass was the sole cause. Bulletin 471 cleared Keddie 24 years after his death. Eight separate records—test book, weigh book, ledgers, inspector's notes, diaries, and the glass itself—held the pieces; none spoke until read together.
