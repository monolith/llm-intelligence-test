# Answers—Part 1: Section A (Stories 1–4)

## Story 1: The Founding of the Cooperative and Its Rule
**Who:** Gideon Vose (first president) and the farmers of Larrow Valley.
**What:** In 1889, farmers organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, tired of selling butter to merchants at unfavorable prices. Gideon Vose was elected president and wrote the by-laws himself, including Article VII.
**When:** 1889 (organization), 1891 (creamery built at Larrow Green), 1892 (Article VII adopted), 1896 (Ostrey Hollow station built).
**Quantities:** The main creamery at Larrow Green opened in 1891 on Larrow Creek. The skimming station at Ostrey Hollow opened May 4, 1896, seven miles up the valley.
**Key Rule:** Article VII: "Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." This gave each station absolute authority over its own test results.
**Why It Matters:** Article VII created a system where payment depended entirely on a single test made at each location, with no appeal and no external check. This became the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow disaster, because once the station's test was made the rule, nothing could override it except proving the test itself was wrong.
**Documents:** Effie Loomis's secretary notes (1941) on the Association's organizing; Dell Rundle's 1939 jubilee pamphlet on the founding; Orin Falke's 1959 county board report; Halbert Nym's accession notes with the by-laws printed in full.

## Story 2: The Purchase and Installation of Faulty Testing Glassware
**Who:** Ammon & Sons (dairy-supply firm at Tarnet); the Association's managers and officers; the station tester Ansel Keddie.
**What:** The Association ordered a complete testing outfit for the Ostrey Hollow station from Ammon & Sons. The invoice (April 14, 1897) included six dozen milk-test pipettes marked 17.6 cubic centimeters, twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, a hand tester, and freight. The outfit went into use May 1, 1897. Unknown to anyone, the pipettes were systematically short—they delivered only 16.27 cc instead of 17.6 cc, a shortness of 0.88 cc per pipette.
**When:** Ordered April 14, 1897; received and in use by May 1, 1897.
**Quantities:** Total invoice: $77.93. Six dozen pipettes at $0.35 each = $25.20. Test bottles, acid measure, hand tester, and freight made up the balance. Each pipette was short by 0.88 cc, representing 95 hundredths of the marked capacity (16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244).
**Why It Happened:** Junia Ammon (firm historian, 1949) speculates that the mold had been reground, causing the lot to run short. A gauging check at the bench would catch the first, tenth, and fiftieth pipettes if they were consistent—but if the mold is reground, they are consistently wrong, and that kind of error slips through. The firm had no works records after a 1931 fire; they could only report (July 2, 1923) that "Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name."
**Documents:** Junia Ammon's chapter from the firm's 1949 history, reproducing both the invoice and the July 2, 1923 letter; Halbert Nym's accession notes with the invoice reprinted; Ansel Keddie's unsent letter (spring 1925) noting the 1897 arrival of the glass.

## Story 3: Twenty-Six Years of Unrecognized Systematic Undercounting (1897–1923)
**Who:** Ansel Keddie (tester); the Ostrey Hollow patrons (especially Emmet Brant and the six Hessel Bottom farms); Selby Vose (Association manager, 1913 onward); Wilbur Teague (bookkeeper); Merle Strawn (state dairy inspector).
**What:** For 26 years, the faulty pipettes systematically undercharged milk in every test. Because the pipettes delivered only 95 hundredths of their mark, every test read 95 hundredths of the truth—a proportional error that fell hardest on rich milk. The recorded average test at Ostrey Hollow was 3.61 percent butterfat, but the true average was 3.80 percent. The gap was a constant 19 hundredths, present in every month of every year—wet January and dry August alike. No seasonal pattern; no reason that could be blamed on men or cows or land.
**When:** May 1, 1897 (when the faulty glass went into use) to June 12, 1923 (when the state inspector condemned it). First complaint of record: 1902. Repeated complaints: 1908, 1919, 1925.
**Quantities:** Over 25 full delivery years (1898–1922), the station received 40 million pounds of milk. The recorded total butterfat credited to patrons was approximately 1,444,000 pounds. The actual butterfat delivered was approximately 1,520,000 pounds. The missing 76,000 pounds of butterfat, at an average price of 25 cents per pound, was worth $19,000.
**Why No One Suspected the Glass:** Every competing explanation seemed more plausible. Three theories were offered and defended: (1) Water in the cans—the manager Selby Vose issued a 1919 warning circular to the patrons; (2) The tester stealing cream—Wilbur Teague made this implication at the 1925 hearing; (3) The wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinning the cream—the 1908 committee blamed it, and Dr. Kepp in 1926 built a veterinary case for it using Bulletin 214 (which found bog hay depresses butterfat by only two tenths in winter, far short of the 19-hundredth gap). All three could be defended by reference to parts of the evidence. None was disproven at the time. Ansel Keddie tested as he was taught, in full view of the state inspector's visits, with meticulous records. He suspected something in the glass in 1911 when the borrowed measure arrived and tests rose, but he wrote only a question mark.
**The 1911 Anomaly:** On May 8, 1911, the station's own measure broke. From May 8 to July 10, a borrowed measure was used. During those 9 weeks, the tests rose from 3.61 to 3.80. When the station's own glass returned, tests dropped back to 3.61 the same day. Same patrons, same herds, same pasture, same season, same tester, same procedure—one variable changed. Ansel noted it and set a question mark. For 47 years this sat unread. In 1958, Professor Leland Vaught called it the most decisive evidence in the entire history: an experiment that ran itself under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, disposing of every explanation that blamed herds, land, or men, because none of those changed in those nine weeks.
**Documents:** Ansel Keddie's statement and unsent letter (spring 1925); Vira Toland's 1979 recording (retelling what her father Emmet Brant knew); Roy Brant's 1955 account of his father's weigh book; Rosalie Cudd's 1958 letter showing the four Hessel Bottom men's milk tested 3.78–3.82 at Alder Corners (confirming good milk); Merle Strawn's circuit books (1907–1936), finding no added water every visit; Effie Loomis's notes on the 1925 hearing award; Wilbur Teague's 1948 memoir on the "station gain" line; Hobart Sill's 1934 remarks noting cream arrived heavy, not light; Dr. Kepp's 1926 farm club paper; Orra Keddie's diary (1897–1930); Bertram Kyle's 1953 office recollections.

## Story 4: The Inspection, Condemnation, and Replacement of the Glassware (1923)
**Who:** Merle Strawn (state dairy inspector); Selby Vose (Association manager); the Association board; Ammon & Sons (dairy supply firm).
**What:** On June 12, 1923, during a routine circuit visit, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn tested the Ostrey Hollow station's pipettes against a calibrated burette (a precision measuring instrument). He found they would not deliver their marked capacity. He condemned the entire crate on the spot and directed the manager to order new glass. The Association wrote to Ammon & Sons on June 28, 1923. Ammon & Sons replied July 2, 1923, saying they would ship new glass within the month and expressing regret but inability to say anything about "the lot you name" (the original 1897 batch). New glass arrived and was in use by July 1923. From 1924 onward, the station's average test stood at 3.80–3.90 (compared to the recorded 3.61 over the previous 26 years).
**When:** Inspection June 12, 1923. Order placed June 28, 1923. New glass in use from July/August 1923.
**Quantities:** The new glass delivered its mark. In 1924, the first full year with the new glass, the recorded average was 3.80 (or in some sources, 3.90 or 3.85). No explanation was given or sought for the sudden improvement; it was attributed to improved management or farmer compliance with the 1919 warning about water.
**Why It Mattered—and Didn't:** Strawn had no reason to suspect that the faulty glass had been causing the 26-year undercount. He condemned it as maintenance (old glass wears, equipment needs replacement). No one connected the condemnation to the decades of low tests. The Association ordered new glass as routine equipment maintenance. Manager Vose never drew an inference. No one asked why tests suddenly improved in 1924 if the problem had been water-adding or tester theft—those would require changed behavior, not new glass. The connection between the 1923 condemnation and the 1897–1923 undershooting lay dormant for 35 years.
**Documents:** Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks (entries for June 12, 1923 and October 1923); Ammon & Sons' letter of July 2, 1923 (in Junia Ammon's 1949 history); the invoice and correspondence filed in Halbert Nym's accession notes; Selby Vose's 1926 annual address (noting the tests improved in 1924 but attributing it to the Association's scrutiny "when the looking began"); Orra Keddie's diary (July 1923: "new glass at the station"); Cleve Oram's 1977 tape (observing the crate in the loft under a tarp, placed there when "the state man made them throw it out").
# Answers—Part 2: Section A (Stories 5–8) and Section B

## Story 5: The Litigation and Dismissal (1925)
**Who:** The Ostrey Hollow patrons; Selby Vose (manager); Alonzo Frick (arbitration board chairman); Wilbur Teague (bookkeeper); Ansel Keddie (tester).
**What:** In January 1925, the Ostrey Hollow patrons, exhausted by decades of lower tests and lower pay, petitioned the Association for a hearing. A board of arbitration was convened under the Association's by-laws, chaired by attorney Alonzo Frick of Ashlin. The board sat three times: first at the Association office in Ashlin (March 17, 1925), then twice at the station itself (April 1925), examining the scales, sampling procedure, and test method in situ. Three competing explanations were presented but none proved. The board announced its findings on May 4, 1925: "The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account." An award without a finding—both parts of the sentence were true at once, and no one knew how to hold them together.
**When:** Petition January 1925. Hearing March 17–May 4, 1925. Award May 4, 1925. Dismissal April 30, 1925 (four days before the award).
**Quantities:** Award: $3,800 (or sometimes cited as $3,500 or $35 per patron per year, though this varies by account). The patrons' own claim was for $67,000 in lost butterfat over 25 years. The board never justified the gap between finding and award.
**The Dismissal:** On April 30, 1925—while the board was still deliberating—the Association dismissed Ansel Keddie from his 29-year post. No finding against him was ever made. The board did not conclude he had done anything wrong. Frick later wrote (1946) that the dismissal was done "while the board was still sitting, and I have never been able to think well of the Association for it." Ansel came home at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm, hung it behind the shed door, and asked what there was for supper.
**Why It Failed to Find a Cause:** The three explanations offered were (1) water added by patrons (Vose's theory, unsupported); (2) cream theft by Keddie (Teague's implication, contradicted by the 1911 anomaly); (3) wet hay of Hessel Bottom (the 1908 committee, later seemingly confirmed by Kepp and Bulletin 214, though Bulletin 214 actually refutes it—five hundredths in winter, nothing in summer, against a 19-hundredth year-round gap). Each could be defended; none could be proven or decisively refuted at the time. Frick later wondered if "the answer was in that room and simply was not in any of the three stories."
**Documents:** Alonzo Frick's 1946 Bar Association article (the only board member to write about it, and with profound ambivalence); Ansel Keddie's statement to the board and unsent letter (spring 1925); Effie Loomis's notes on the board award from the minute book; Wilbur Teague's 1948 memoir on his testimony; Merle Strawn's circuit notebook (page on June 12, 1923 condemnation); the list of four exhibits before the board (station test book, receiving book, Brant weigh book, Strawn's 1923 notebook); Orra Keddie's diary (March 17 and April 30, 1925); Cleve Oram's 1977 tape (the crate 20 feet overhead during the hearing).

## Story 6: The Social Rupture and Long Estrangement (1919 Onward)
**Who:** Ansel Keddie (tester); Emmet Brant (largest patron, Ansel's brother-in-law); Orra Brant Keddie (sister to both); Roy Brant (Emmet's son); the four Hessel Bottom farmers who hauled to Alder Corners; Jerome Cudd and Rosalie Cudd (cheesemakers over the ridge).
**What:** In June 1919, Selby Vose issued a warning circular to the Ostrey Hollow patrons about added water in milk. Ansel Keddie personally delivered the circular himself to 11 families (or 9 according to Orra's diary) rather than leaving it for another man to carry, standing at each door while each man read it. Two days later, Emmet Brant confronted Ansel in the yard, saying he had hauled whole milk since the station opened and Ansel knew it better than any man living—then asking why Ansel had carried the paper himself if he didn't believe it. Ansel said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet went home and never returned to the house. He and Ansel, who had eaten Sunday dinners together for 36 years, did not speak again for 10 years until Emmet's death in 1929. Orra bore this silently and wrote in her diary: "He has not been in this house since, and he is my brother."
**The Alder Corners Proof:** Contemporaneously, four of the six Hessel Bottom farms (including Emmet's and Roy's) hauled their milk during the summer flush to Alder Corners Cheese Company over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County (1915–1930, especially summer 1919–1930). The cheesemaker Jerome Cudd and later his daughter Rosalie tested this milk and found it consistently at 3.78–3.82 percent butterfat—the same milk that tested 3.61 at Ostrey Hollow. Rosalie Cudd wrote to Ivy Keddie in 1958 that "that milk was good milk" and a cheesemaker would detect watering immediately (the curd would come soft and the body would be weak). Her father Jerome had written in 1919: "Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge." This proved the milk was not thinned by wet hay or watered; it was excellent milk that the Ostrey Hollow station consistently underrated.
**The Aftermath:** After the 1925 dismissal, Ansel lived under the imputation for nine years. He worked as a carpenter (doing repairs for neighbors, including hanging a barn door for Cleve Oram that "swung true for thirty years"), taking whatever work neighbors offered at a quarter of his station pay. In his diary entry for June 1925, Orra noted he began work on the cow shed and "whistles, which he never did when he was paid." He was rebuilding a life in fragments. When Emmet died in 1929, Orra went to the funeral, sat an hour in Emmet's kitchen where she grew up, and came home. The silence had been settled by death, not by words. Ansel died in February 1934, "at home, in the forenoon, without much pain." More attended his funeral than Orra expected. Men who had not spoken to him in nine years shook her hand and praised his carpentry. "Not one of them said the other thing. Nine years they have had to say it and they had not said it and they did not say it then."
**Documents:** Orra Keddie's diary (June 1919, two entries; January 1925; March 17, 1925; April 30, 1925; June 1925; 1929; February 1934); Rosalie Cudd's 1958 letter to Ivy Keddie with the Alder Corners test figures (1915–1930); Roy Brant's 1955 account; Cleve Oram's 1977 tape; Vira Toland's 1979 recording (on the impact in the Ostrey Hollow community).

## Story 7: The Rediscovery and Scientific Proof (1958)
**Who:** Ivy Keddie (graduate student at Farland Agricultural College, granddaughter of Ansel Keddie); Professor Leland Vaught (dairy husbandry); Duncan Keddie (Ivy's father, Ansel's son, retired schoolteacher); Merle Strawn (retired state dairy inspector—his notebooks in the college library); Rosalie Cudd (cheesemaker at Alder Corners).
**What:** In February 1958, Ivy Keddie climbed the ladder into the loft over the station's intake room at Ostrey Hollow and found a wooden crate under a tarpaulin and chaff. It was the station's original condemned glassware, placed there on the day it was condemned (June 12, 1923) and never retrieved. Inside were pipettes, bottles, and other glass pieces. Nine pipettes were intact and legible. On March 9, 1958, Ivy measured the nine pipettes against a calibrated burette at Farland. Each was blown on the stem at 17.6 c.c. Not one delivered it. The mean delivery was 16.72 c.c. (rounded 16.27 in some accounts). That is 95 hundredths of the mark—a consistent, proportional shortness of 5 percent across all nine. The bottleneck of the test bottle is ruled for the standard 17.6 c.c. charge. A short charge yields a short reading in the same proportion. Every test made at that station for 26 years had read 95 percent of the truth.
**The Mathematics:** The recorded average test at Ostrey Hollow was 3.61 percent. Divided by 0.95 (the delivery ratio of the faulty glass), the true average was 3.80 percent. The shortfall was 0.19 percent (19 hundredths) for every test over 26 years. Applied to 40 million pounds of milk received at the station, the unrealized butterfat was 76,000 pounds. At 25 cents per pound, that was worth $19,000. The board had awarded $3,800 without knowing why—a fifth of the true loss.
**The 1911 Anomaly Explained:** When the borrowed measure arrived on May 8, 1911, it was correct (it delivered 17.6 c.c.). When the station's own measure returned July 10, 1911, it was faulty (delivering 16.27 c.c.). During those 9 weeks, tests rose from 3.61 to 3.80. When the faulty measure returned, tests fell back to 3.61 the same day. This proved that the 26-year shortfall was entirely due to the glass—not the men, not the land, not the milk.
**The Board Never Examined the Glass:** The crate had been in the loft directly over the room where the arbitration board sat in 1925. It was twenty feet above their heads, under a tarp, and no one thought to look. The board made an award on grounds they could not establish, never knowing the proof lay in a crate in the loft.
**Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958):** Co-authored by Leland Vaught and Ivy Keddie, it proved: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference—seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat—stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."
**The Vindication:** Ansel Keddie had been dead 24 years. His wife Orra had died 10 years before the proof appeared. He tested as he was taught and as the state inspector saw him do it. He suspected the glass in 1911 and set a question mark, but he would not put down what he could not prove. Duncan Keddie later wrote: "This was the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had."
**Documents:** Ivy Keddie's measurement sheet (March 9, 1958); Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958), with its six sections on the instrument, the material examined, the 1911 interval, magnitude, the question of feed, and the conclusion; Halbert Nym's accession notes (1972) cataloging the crate and surviving pipettes; Duncan Keddie's 1968 family notes (tracking the books); Ashlin Chronicle feature (October 3, 1958, "Old Ordell Wrong Righted"); Orin Falke's 1959 county board report; Dell Rundle's 1939 jubilee pamphlet with its 1959 footnote referencing Ivy's work; Rosalie Cudd's 1958 letter (providing independent test data); Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks (the 1923 entry).

## Story 8: The Institutional Memory and the Five Books (1889–1972)
**Who:** Ansel Keddie (tester, 1896–1925); Emmet Brant (patron, 1897–1929); Wilbur Teague (bookkeeper, 1905–1945); Effie Loomis (secretary, 1901–1940); Merle Strawn (state inspector, 1907–1936); Jerome Cudd and Rosalie Cudd (cheesemakers, 1913–1970s); Halbert Nym (curator, 1972); the Historical Societies of Ordell County and Vessey County.
**What:** Five key books survived: (1) Ansel Keddie's station test book (1896–1924, kept at the Association office from 1925, then at Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1959); (2) Emmet Brant's private weigh book (begun 1897, kept by Roy Brant, lent to Ivy Keddie in 1958, returned, given to Historical Rooms in 1963); (3) Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks (to state dairy office 1936, to Farland Agricultural College 1947, where Ivy Keddie found the 1923 volume in 1958); (4) the Alder Corners Cheese Company test book (never left Vessey County, figures copied for Ivy in 1958, went to Vessey County Historical Society in 1970); (5) the Association's ledgers (with the annual "station gain" entries in Wilbur Teague's hand, carrying figures that made no sense unless the incoming cream was heavier than what had been credited).

Also surviving: Wilbur Teague's memoir (1948) on the "station gain" line; Effie Loomis's notes (1941); Dell Rundle's jubilee pamphlet (1939, with a press-note correction and a 1959 reprint footnote); Bertram Kyle's office recollections (1953); Orra Keddie's diary (1897–1930, plus one 1934 entry, published 1964); Duncan Keddie's family notes (1968, with an addition in 1970); Junia Ammon's firm history (1949); Alonzo Frick's Bar Association article (1946); various oral recollections taped in 1955, 1977, 1979, 1981.

**The Puzzle the Books Posed:** Each book was internally consistent and honest. Ansel's test book had perfect penmanship, no erasures, his question mark in 1911, a clean ledger from 1896 to 1924. Emmet's weigh book showed his scale and the station's agreeing to within a pound or two over 30 years. The Association's ledgers carried the "station gain" line every year, "five and a quarter per cent, as usual," entered by Teague in the same confident hand. Merle Strawn's notebooks were factual jottings, no opinions. But these books were never laid beside each other in one room. Teague kept the figures but never asked why they did not reconcile. Ansel tested as taught and never challenged the system. Vose read only the bottom line and signed. Effie Loomis wrote the minutes but recorded that three explanations were urged and none proven. Kyle sat outside the manager's door for 25 years and saw everything go in and out but thought the "station gain" line had a name and therefore it had been explained. The answer to the question "why is the Ostrey Hollow milk always low?" sat in five books in three buildings and one crate in a loft, all of it honest, all of it documented, and all of it unconnected.

**The Curator's Work:** Halbert Nym, cataloging the deposit of materials in 1972, compiled these items (16 total, including invoices, bulletins, reports, and correspondence) into the Historical Rooms at Ashlin. His accession notes were themselves a retelling, setting down what the record contained and what it did not. His curatorial note on the ledgers: "The station's intake for the years 1898 to 1922 is given in these ledgers as 40,000,000 lb of milk, and the station gain accumulated across those years as 76,000 lb of butter fat, worth $19,000 at the average price paid for fat over the period. The two percentages in this file are not interchangeable: five and a quarter percent of the credited fat is five percent of the fat delivered, and each is right of its own denominator and of no other."

**Why Eight Stories, Not One:** The eight stories could not be one story because they happened in different register and time frame. The founding (story 1) was a deliberate choice to build a just system. The equipment fault (story 2) was accidental. The 26-year unrecognized error (story 3) was a function of the system meeting an accident. The condemnation and replacement (story 4) was a routine maintenance event that failed to connect to its consequence. The litigation (story 5) was a social crisis that could not find its true cause. The rupture (story 6) was a family and community catastrophe triggered by circumstances no one understood. The rediscovery (story 7) was a chance finding and scientific analysis 35 years after the condemnation. The institutional memory (story 8) was the preservation of evidence that did not speak to anyone until an outsider—a granddaughter—read across all five books at once. Each story answers a different question: How was the system designed to work? What went wrong by accident? What did the error produce over time? When was it detected and what was done about it? What was the social cost? Who bore the injustice? How was the truth finally found? What survived to tell the tale?

**Documents:** All 24 retellings, but especially: Halbert Nym's accession notes (1972); Duncan Keddie's family notes (1968) tracking the provenance of each book; Ivy Keddie's measurement sheet and Bulletin No. 471; all the institutional documents (invoices, minutes, ledgers, circuit notebooks, test books, diaries); and the secondary sources (Frick's 1946 article, Teague's 1948 memoir, Loomis's 1941 notes, Kyle's 1953 letter, Rundle's 1939 pamphlet, Ammon's 1949 history).

## Section B — Relationships

### B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Ansel Keddie married Orra Brant in 1889. They had one son, Duncan Keddie, born 1894. Duncan Keddie had one daughter, Ivy Keddie, born 1931. Thus: Ansel (1861–1934) → Duncan (1894–after 1968) → Ivy (b. 1931). The Ashlin Chronicle (October 1958) initially called her his "great-niece," an error; Orin Falke (1959 county board report) corrected it: "Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece." Duncan Keddie's 1968 family notes confirm plainly: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise and once heard it said aloud in this town by a man who ought to have known." The significance: Ivy was trained as a scientist (agricultural college, Farland); she had no investment in the valley's old quarrels; yet she was Ansel's flesh and blood, and his vindication was also a family vindication.

### B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant? State anything the sources leave open about intervening generations.

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins (the children of first cousins). Emmet Brant (born 1854, died 1929) was Orra Brant Keddie's brother. Emmet's son was Roy Brant (dates not given; Emmet lent him the weigh book at Emmet's death around 1929). Roy Brant's daughter was Hazel Brant. Ansel and Orra's son was Duncan Keddie (born 1894). Duncan's daughter was Ivy Keddie (born 1931). Thus: Emmet and Orra were brother and sister → Roy (Emmet's son) and Duncan (Orra's son) were first cousins → Hazel (Roy's daughter) and Ivy (Duncan's daughter) were second cousins. Duncan Keddie's 1968 notes explain plainly the confusion: "Roy's daughter is Hazel Brant. My daughter is Ivy. Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece, which people say because it is shorter." Cleve Oram (1977) said Ivy was "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed," which is incorrect by Duncan's definition, but the relationship is the same individuals regardless of naming error. The sources do not establish whether Roy and Hazel remained in the valley or moved, or when exactly Roy died (he lent the weigh book to Ivy in 1958 "and lived some good while afterward," per Hazel's 1981 tape, and then "at his death in 1962 it went to his daughter Hazel").

### B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter to what happened at Ostrey Hollow?

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose, the founder and first president of the Association (serving 1889–1911, 22 years). Gideon Vose wrote the by-laws and Article VII himself. Dell Rundle's 1939 pamphlet: "Gideon Vose was elected the first president... and he wrote the by-laws himself, at his own kitchen table." Selby Vose was his son and became manager in 1913 (Effie Loomis's notes), serving until 1938. Bertram Kyle (1953) clarifies: "Mr. Selby Vose became manager in nineteen eleven... He was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president — the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense." Duncan Keddie (1968) also corrects: "Selby Vose, who managed the Association, was Gideon Vose's son. Gideon organized the Association and was its first president and wrote its by-laws; Selby was his son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew." Why this matters: Gideon Vose created Article VII, which gave absolute authority to the station's test, allowing no appeal and requiring no external verification. Selby inherited a system in which the test at Ostrey Hollow was law, and in which the manager signed off on the annual ledgers without reading the detail. When low tests persisted for years, the by-law created by his father trapped him: he could not question the tester without questioning the rule his father wrote. His 1919 warning circular about water blamed the patrons; his 1926 annual address blamed the patrons for watering cans and praised the Association for paying peace money. He never looked at the glass. The system his father designed, and his own position in it, prevented him from seeing what was in front of him every year—the "station gain" line on the ledgers he signed.

### B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?

The sources are in direct conflict. Duncan Keddie (1968) states flatly: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either. I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her sit down. It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me. She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it." Duncan's testimony is internal to the family and explicit. However, Cleve Oram (1977 tape) says: "And I will say this while I am on families, because you will hear it anyway and you may as well hear it from somebody who is not repeating it out of malice. The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley. My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper." Oram cannot draw the line and admits "it is a small valley" gossip. Vira Toland (1979) notes that Orra Keddie was "Emmet Brant's sister" and Emmet was "the sister of the man that letter cost the most," and she references a connection between the Voses and the Keddie family through a 1919 situation, but she does not explicitly state a blood relationship. Halbert Nym's accession notes mention no connection. Duncan's authority is direct (he is writing for his family); Oram's is hearsay he "never heard contradicted" but cannot verify. The safest conclusion: no blood relationship is established by documentary evidence. Rumors existed, but Duncan Keddie (who would know) denies them. The professional connection (Vose as manager overseeing Keddie's work) is clear; a family connection is not.

# Answers—Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math (show working)

### C1. (a) Proportion of error from glassware.

The faulty pipettes were blown on the stem at 17.6 c.c. (the standard charge of milk for a butter fat test). Ivy Keddie's measurement sheet (March 9, 1958) shows nine surviving pipettes, each tested five times against a calibrated burette. The individual means per pipette were: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 c.c. The mean of these nine means is 16.72 c.c.

Ratio of delivered to marked capacity: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 (or 95 hundredths, or 95%).
Shortness: 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c., which is 0.88 ÷ 17.6 = 0.05, or 5%, or one part in twenty short.

**Note:** Bulletin No. 471 (Vaught, October 1958) cites "mean delivery of the nine is 16.27 c.c." and "ratio... 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244." This would give 16.27 / 17.6 = 0.9244 = 92.44%, a 7.56% shortness. However, Ivy Keddie's actual measurement sheet (reprinted in accession notes r07 and her measurement sheet r04) records the nine individual means and states their mean as 16.72 c.c. with shortness of 0.88 c.c. The arithmetic 16.72 / 17.6 = 0.95 and 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 are consistent with each other. The Bulletin's 16.27 figure appears inconsistent with the measurement sheet's data. **I resolve this by using 16.72 c.c. as the accurate measurement, giving a 5% shortness (one part in twenty), consistent with the 3.80 / 3.61 test ratio.**

### C1. (b) True average test and shortfall in points.

Recorded average at Ostrey Hollow station: 3.61% butterfat.
Ratio of delivered to marked: 0.95.
True average: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80% butterfat.
Shortfall: 3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19 percentage points.

Alternatively, using Bulletin 471's figures (16.27 / 0.9244): true average would be 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.905%, shortfall 0.295 points. **I use 3.80 as the true average, based on the 1911 anomaly (when correct glass was used, tests stood at 3.80), and the measurement sheet's 16.72 mean.**

### C1. (c) Unrealized butterfat over full delivery years.

Total milk received 1898–1922: 40,000,000 pounds (sources: Teague, Loomis, Nym, Vaught).
Recorded butterfat credited: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 pounds.
True butterfat delivered: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 pounds.
Unrealized butterfat (not credited): 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = **76,000 pounds**.

(Vaught's Bulletin 471 cites 118,000 lbs using the 3.905 figure; Ivy Keddie's Chronicle statement uses 76,000. The ledgers themselves carry "station gain" of 5.25%, which applied to 1,444,000 credited yields 76,000 pounds, supporting the 76,000 figure.)

### C1. (d) Money value and fraction received.

Value at average price: 76,000 pounds × $0.25 per pound = **$19,000**.
Award received: $3,800.
Fraction: $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20, or **1/5 (one-fifth)**.

Over 25 full delivery years (1898–1922):

**(a) Total butterfat credited to patrons on station test:**
40,000,000 pounds of milk × 3.61% average = **1,444,000 pounds**.

**(b) Total butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in cream:**
1,444,000 pounds (credited) + 76,000 pounds (gain) = **1,520,000 pounds**.

Difference: 76,000 pounds.

## Section D — Logic

### D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment.

**When and duration:** May 8, 1911 to July 10, 1911. Duration: 9 weeks, 2 days (or "nine weeks" as the sources consistently say).

**Variable:** The measure (pipette). The station's own pipette broke on May 8, 1911 (recorded in Ansel's test book: "May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure"). A measure borrowed from Larrow Green was used. On July 10, the station's own measure was repaired or returned and went back into use ("July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.").

**Held constant:** The patrons (same nine families on Hessel Bottom), the herds (same cows), the pasture (same ground, wet meadow), the season (May–July, flush season, highest test period), the tester (Ansel Keddie, same man), the procedure (same method, same hands), the acid (same chemical), the bottles (same glassware marked for 17.6 c.c., if they were the same bottles), the receiver of the milk (same creamery intake).

**Result:** Tests rose from 3.61 (the station's recorded long-term average, including winter months when tests would be lower) to 3.80 during the nine weeks. They fell back to 3.61 when the faulty glass returned. The difference was immediate: "Tests are back where they were" on July 10.

**What it establishes:** The experiment proves that a constant proportion (5%) of the recorded figures were systematically lost due to the measure, not due to men, cows, land, season, or procedure. It establishes that one variable—the glass—was responsible for the entire shortfall. No other single variable was altered. Therefore, no explanation blaming the herds (poor cows), the ground (wet hay), the men (water-adding, cream-stealing), or the season (summer vs. winter fluctuation) can account for a consistent 19-hundredth gap that persisted in July and February alike. The 1911 interval proves it was the glass.

**What it does NOT establish:** It does not prove that the tests in 1911 (3.80) were the true average for the entire 26 years, because the summer 1911 tests may have been higher than the winter average. It does not identify the specific mechanism of error (short delivery of charge) until the glass is measured in 1958. It does not identify the date the error began or confirm it began in 1897 (though the test book's first recorded average, 1896, shows 3.80, suggesting the borrowed glass was correct). Without measurement of the faulty glass, the 1911 interval alone does not establish the 5% shortness quantitatively, only that glass was the culprit.

### D2. Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream.

**Reason 1: The cream arrived at Larrow Green heavier than credited.** Hobart Sill (buttermaker, 1934) testified that the Ostrey Hollow cream "ran heavy" every year: "There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there... Always more. Never less." He weighed the cream himself when it arrived. Sill notes: "I weighed it myself, most years. The platform man weighed it and I weighed it over again when it came into the cold room... the Hollow's cream coming in heavy on page after page after page, in my own writing, for as long as I have been in this room." If Keddie had stolen cream (which would reduce the quantity leaving the station), the cream arriving at Larrow Green would weigh *less*, not *more*, than credited. This is independent of the 1911 anomaly and of any glass measurement.

**Reason 2: The 1911 anomaly itself.** During the nine weeks a borrowed (correct) measure was used, tests rose to 3.80 and fell back when the faulty glass returned. Wilbur Teague (bookkeeper) testified at the arbitration: "I said that a business which is arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken from it... The remedy is two men or a lock, and the Association had neither." Teague implied Keddie could have done it. But the 1911 interval shows that when the glass changed (and nothing else did), the figures changed immediately. If Keddie were stealing and writing low, he would have had the same incentive and opportunity with the borrowed glass. He did not take less then. The variable that changed was the glass, not his behavior. This is independent of Sill's cream-weight testimony and requires no glass measurement.

**Reason 3: The independent test books.** Emmet Brant kept a weigh book of his own milk from 1897 onward. His own scale and the station's scale "agreed" to within a pound or two over 30 years (Roy Brant, 1955: "The two scales, his and the station's, kept by two men who by the end were not speaking to one another, and the scales never fell out at all. Whatever was wrong at that station... it was not the weighing"). If Keddie were stealing cream, he could not touch the milk before it was weighed. The theft would have to occur after weighing but before or during separation. Separation yields cream and skim; the skim goes back to the patron, the cream goes down the valley. If Keddie took cream, the skim would have to be heavier to compensate (else the patron would notice the recovered skim). But this would be obvious to the patron and would leave evidence in his own use of the skim. None of the sources report patrons complaining about short skim or unusual skim weights. The scales agreed; the skim was accounted for; therefore, no cream was removed from the station. This is independent of Sill's testimony and the 1911 interval, and does not require the glass to be measured (it only requires the consistency of Brant's own ledger).

**Are the reasons independent?** Yes. Reason 1 (cream weight) depends on the receiver's records (Sill's testimony and the receiving book). Reason 2 (the 1911 anomaly) depends on the test book entries and the operator's immediate response. Reason 3 (the scales and skim accounting) depends on Emmet Brant's independent weigh book. No two of these depend on the same source or record.

**Which would still hold if glass had never been measured?** All three. Reason 1 (heavy cream) would stand on Sill's testimony alone. Reason 2 (the 1911 anomaly) would stand on the test book's notation without needing to know *why* the glass was different. Reason 3 (the scales and skim) would stand on Brant's ledger and patron accounting alone.

**Did the bookkeeper question the entries?** Wilbur Teague kept the "station gain" line every year with the phrase "five and a quarter per cent, as usual." Teague's 1948 memoir states: "The gain ran at five and a quarter percent of the fat that was actually in the cream. It ran there year after year, so evenly that the words 'as usual' got into the entry and stayed there, and a man may as well be honest about what evenness does to a bookkeeper: it stops him looking. A figure that jumps about will keep you awake. A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture." Teague asked no questions. He testified at the 1925 hearing, describing his "station gain" line: "I said that a business which is arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken from it." He was arguing that the structure invited theft. But he never challenged the ledger entries themselves. He signed off on them. He later wrote (1948): "I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone. I let it alone. I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience."

### D3. Did the manager know the station's tests ran low?

**Answer: Yes, Selby Vose knew the tests ran low. He signed the ledgers every year, and the figures on them established the persistent gap.**

**Justification:** Wilbur Teague (1948 memoir) states: "I laid the annual sheet before him each spring and he put his name at the foot of it, and a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him. What he made of it he never told me." The station account was explicit: every year from 1913 onward (when Vose became manager), the sheet carried "butterfat credited to patrons on station test" vs. "butterfat received at Larrow Green in station cream," with the difference entering as "station gain." The average Ostrey Hollow test, over the full period, was 3.16–3.61, depending on the years counted. The Larrow Green creamery's average was 3.78, and "every year the numbers went right the summer after" high tests were found (in Vose's 1926 annual address). Vose had been in office since 1913; the pattern was unbroken from 1898 onward. He could not have *not* known. The sources do not establish *why* he did not act on it: whether he misinterpreted the figures, trusted that the system governed itself, or deliberately concealed the problem. But the evidence that he *signed* the sheets every year, and the figures were explicit, establishes that knowledge of the persistent gap was available to him.

Bertram Kyle (office clerk, 1953) corroborates: "I watched him do it a thousand times... turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign." Vose read the bottom line (perhaps the total profit or loss) and signed without reading the detail. This is negligence, not innocence.

## Section F — Theory

### F1. Causal theory of the shortfall.

**The true cause:** The Ostrey Hollow station received a shipment of milk-test pipettes from Ammon & Sons in May 1897, intended to be blown to a standard 17.6 c.c. charge. Due to an error in the glassblowing mold (likely reground before production, introducing a systematic dimensional error), the pipettes delivered only 16.72 c.c. (or possibly 16.27 c.c.; the measurement data shows 16.72). This represented a 5% shortness (0.95 of the standard charge). Because a test bottle's neck is ruled for a specific charge and no other, a short charge of milk yields a proportionally short reading of butterfat. Every test made at that station from May 1, 1897 to June 12, 1923 read 95 percent of the truth. Tests that should have read 3.80% read 3.61%. Tests that should have read 4.56% read 4.33%. The error was proportional (fell harder on rich milk), consistent (every day, every year, July and February alike), and precise (the same 5% every single time).

**Why the error persisted 26 years:** No one suspected the pipettes because:
1. Pipettes are the measure; they are not checked against anything else, unlike scales (checked with weights) or acid (checked for strength) or bottles (checked with mercury). An instrument that is itself the measure cannot be caught by measuring with it.
2. The system was designed by Article VII to rely on the station's test as the sole authority, permitting no external check or appeal.
3. The station's records (Ansel Keddie's test book) were immaculate: perfectly kept, internally consistent, marked in a clean hand, with no erasures. Consistency in error reads as reliability.
4. The persistent gap was rationally explained by competing theories that seemed to address the evidence available: water in the cans (a widespread practice in small-scale dairying, credible), cream theft (possible in a one-man operation, credible), or wet land (observable to anyone, credible). One plausible explanation satisfied each observer and removed the need to look further.
5. The 1911 anomaly—when a borrowed measure was used and tests rose to 3.80, then fell back when the faulty glass returned—sat with only a question mark in Ansel's book. No one in the office connected this to the long history; the office and the station operated separately.

**Why the error ended when it did:** On June 12, 1923, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn (riding circuit, checking appliances for wear) ran the station's pipettes against a calibrated burette, found they did not deliver their mark, and condemned the entire crate. This was routine maintenance: old glass fails, equipment wears. He ordered new. The Association ordered replacement glass that week (June 28, 1923). New glass arrived and went into use July–August 1923. The new glass delivered 17.6 c.c. Tests immediately rose to 3.80 (1924 average). No one inferred backward to the 26 years of error. The condemnation was treated as a maintenance matter, not as discovery of a long-standing systematic failure.

**Why nothing at the station or farms had to change:** The milk and cows were constant. The pasture did not improve. No farmer changed his practice. Ansel Keddie tested by the same method both before and after 1923. No water-adding stopped. Yet tests rose 19 hundredths in a day because the glass changed. This proves that the 26-year gap was *not* due to the men (Ansel), the milk (the patrons' cows), the land (Hessel Bottom's wet hay), the season (tests were low in July as much as February), or any practice that would require changed human behavior. The gap was due to the glass alone.

---

**The three competing explanations and the evidence that defeats each:**

**Explanation 1: Water in the cans (advanced by Selby Vose, manager, in his 1919 circular warning).**

Evidence urged: The milk tested lower and stayed lower year after year; a little water in the cans was a common small-farm practice; the manager's circular explicitly accused the patrons of water-adding.

Evidence that defeats it:
- Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks (1907–1936): At every visit to Ostrey Hollow, Strawn tested for added water with a lactometer. His notes consistently say "no added water." He tested from 1908 onward, including the year after the 1919 circular. He never found water.
- The 1911 anomaly: The same milk and same patrons, tested with a borrowed (correct) measure, showed tests at 3.80. If water were in the cans, the water would be in the 1911 milk too. But tests rose immediately when the glass changed, then fell immediately when the faulty glass returned. A practice (water-adding) would not turn on and off with the measure itself; the glass changed, not the milk.
- Rosalie Cudd's cheese factory records (1915–1930): Four Hessel Bottom farmers hauled the same milk in summer to her factory over the ridge. She tested it at 3.78–3.82%, concluding "Good milk off wet ground." A cheesemaker detects watered milk immediately (the curd does not knit, the body is slack). She made no complaints.
- The 1924 improvement: In 1924, with new correct glass, tests rose to 3.80. No farmer stopped watering; no new management initiative changed behavior. The tests improved because the glass was correct, proving that water-adding was not the cause of the low tests.

**Explanation 2: Cream theft by the tester (implied by Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper, at the 1925 hearing).**

Evidence urged: The station account carried a "station gain" line every year—more fat arrived at Larrow Green than the station claimed to have bought. A single-man operation with no supervision was vulnerable to theft. The tester could take cream and write low tests to hide the loss.

Evidence that defeats it:
- Hobart Sill's receiving book (1893–1934): Cream off the Ostrey Hollow wagon arrived at Larrow Green heavier than the papers said had been credited. "Always more. Never less." Sill weighed it himself. If cream had been stolen, the cans arriving would be lighter, not heavier. Theft would shrink the quantity; the quantity grew. Therefore, no cream was removed.
- The 1911 anomaly: The same tester, same opportunity, same method. When the glass changed and nothing else did, tests rose. If the tester were stealing, he had the same incentive and ability with the borrowed glass as with his own. He did not take less then. The variable was the glass, not his behavior.
- Emmet Brant's independent weigh book (1897–1929): His own scale and the station's scale agreed to within pounds over 30 years. The milk was weighed before it could be stolen. There is no record of complaints about short skim milk (which would be the remaining byproduct if cream were removed). The skim accounted for every pound.
- Merle Strawn's inspections: Strawn visited the station regularly and on his 1923 visit condemned the glass. He saw no sign of a one-man theft operation and kept no notes suggesting dishonesty.

Therefore, the cream arrived heavy because the faulty glass was undercounting its butterfat content; the cream itself was intact.

**Explanation 3: The wet hay of Hessel Bottom thins the cream (advanced by the 1908 committee and endorsed by Dr. Roland Kepp in 1926).**

Evidence urged: The 1908 committee sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through winter months (January–March) and found the milk "whole," scales "honest," and their opinion that "the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream" and "the remedy lies with the land." The meadow is demonstrably wet and sour, producing coarse hay. Dr. Kepp (1926) argued from agricultural science that sour ground low in lime and phosphorus naturally produces lower-fat milk. The State Agricultural College's Bulletin No. 214 (1911) found bog hay in a feeding trial depresses butterfat by two tenths in winter.

Evidence that defeats it:
- The 1911 anomaly: In summer 1911, when stock were off the wet hay and on good pasture, tests rose to 3.80 with the borrowed glass. The same low ground did not prevent high tests when the glass was correct. The hay had not changed, the land had not changed, the cows had not changed. Therefore, the hay was not the cause.
- The year-round flatness of the gap: The gap was 19 hundredths in July (when cows grazed good summer pasture and the College's Bulletin No. 214 found no measurable depression from bog hay) and 19 hundredths in February (when cows ate the bad hay and the College found up to two tenths depression). The gap was not seasonal or variable with feeding—it was the same in every month. Hay effects would show seasonally. The gap did not.
- The Alder Corners comparison (1915–1930): The same four Hessel Bottom farmers sent milk to Rosalie Cudd's factory in summer, where it tested 3.78–3.82, while at Ostrey Hollow it tested 3.61 on the same milk. This proof that the milk itself was good, not thinned by land, was not available to the 1908 committee but was available by 1958. It definitively refutes the land explanation.
- Bulletin No. 214's own findings: The bulletin found bog hay depresses butterfat "by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Five hundredths in winter was the maximum. The gap at Ostrey Hollow was 19 hundredths flat across all seasons. Even combining the worst seasonal effect with the worst hay effect, the hay could not account for a 19-hundredth year-round gap. Therefore, the hay was not the primary cause.

**Two subsidiary questions:**

**Did the 1925 board of arbitration examine the condemned glassware?**

No. Alonzo Frick (1946) lists the four exhibits before the board: "The station test book... The receiving book... The private weigh book of Emmet Brant... The circuit notebook of the state dairy inspector for the year 1923." The condemned crate itself was not brought before the board. It sat in the loft over the weigh room—20 feet above the room where the board sat on April 1925 (Cleve Oram observed this). The board never examined the glass. Frick later (1946) wondered whether "the answer was in that room and simply was not in any of the three stories" presented. The answer was literally in the loft.

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?**

Ansel Keddie believed in 1911 that "it was something in the glass." Duncan Keddie (1968) recalls: "In the summer of 1911, Ansel noted the anomaly in the test book with a question mark, and Duncan, then 17, recalls his father saying at supper and again by the door: 'it was something in the glass.' He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove, and he never wrote it as anything but a question mark." Ansel's unsent letter (spring 1925) notes the 1911 event: "Those nine weeks in 1911 are the strangest thing in my book and I have never had an account of them to give... That question mark is mine and I have let it stand for fourteen years. I could not answer it then and I cannot answer it now, and I will not put anything in its place that I do not know."

Ansel Keddie tested as taught, kept perfect records, and suspected the glass but refused to name it until he could prove it. He died in 1934 with the imputation on him. He never claimed innocence publicly, only accuracy: "I am an old man and I have no other trade. I have tested as I was taught... I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are."

## Section G — Summary (max. 150 words)

In 1897, the Larrow Valley Cooperative Association's Ostrey Hollow station received faulty milk-test pipettes that delivered only 95% of their marked capacity. For 26 years, every test read 5% low, crediting patrons 3.61% butterfat when milk actually contained 3.80%. The gap seemed explicable: water in cans, tester theft, or poor land. Three competing theories emerged, each defensible but none provable. In 1923, an inspector condemned the old glass; new glass was installed and tests improved, but no one connected the improvement to the 26-year error. The 1925 arbitration awarded $3,800 to patrons claiming $19,000 loss, "finding no cause established." Ansel Keddie, the tester, was dismissed without finding against him. He died under the imputation. In 1958, his granddaughter Ivy, a graduate student, found the crate in the loft, measured the pipettes, and proved the glass was the sole cause. Bulletin 471 cleared Keddie 24 years after his death. Eight separate records—test book, weigh book, ledgers, inspector's notes, diaries, and the glass itself—held the pieces; none spoke until read together.
