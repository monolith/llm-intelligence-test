# Test Answers — Part 1: Section A (Stories 1–4)

## Section A: Reconstruction of Eight Underlying Stories

### Story 1: The Association's Founding and the Rule That Made the System Vulnerable (1889–1897)

The farmers of Larrow Valley, dissatisfied with merchants' butter prices, organized themselves at Larrow Green in 1889 into the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose, a farmer who had once considered law, was elected first president and drafted the by-laws himself at his kitchen table. The cornerstone was Article VII, adopted at the 1892 annual meeting: patrons would be paid not by milk weight, but by butterfat content as ascertained by test at the station where they delivered. "The test of the station where he delivers shall govern"—no appeal, no external verification, only local measurement.

The Association built its main creamery at Larrow Green in 1891 on the creek below the mill. Seven miles up the valley, at Ostrey Hollow near Hessel Bottom, they erected a skimming station opening May 4, 1896. Ansel Keddie, hired to test and run the separator, established himself as the sole operator—one man weighing, sampling, testing, and recording every patron's milk. The station's complete testing outfit arrived from Ammon & Sons, Tarnet, on April 14, 1897: six dozen milk-test pipettes at 17.6 cubic centimeters, test bottles, an acid measure, a hand tester, and freight, totaling $77.93. The pipettes entered use May 1, 1898. Orra Brant, sister of Emmet Brant (the largest patron), married Ansel in 1889 and bore his son Duncan in 1894. By this arrangement, one man's marriage joined the tester to the family most affected by the system's accuracy. The by-law was just—it paid farmers for what they actually brought, not for water naturally in milk—but it made everything hinge on one instrument in one man's hands, with no inspection or verification built in. This was the system's design at the moment it became vulnerable to hidden defect.

### Story 2: The Anomaly Emerges—Milk at Ostrey Hollow Tests Lower Than the Valley (1902–1908)

Beginning in 1902, farmers at Ostrey Hollow began to complain that their milk tested lower at the station than milk delivered elsewhere in the valley. One farmer complained to his brother-in-law that their cows, feed, and water were identical but their pay was not. The gap persisted month to month, season to season, year to year: roughly 0.19 points (19 hundredths) consistently lower at Ostrey Hollow than at Larrow Green. In July it was there; in February it was there. The difference never came and went with seasons. No man could explain it.

By 1908, the complaint had grown loud enough that the Association appointed a committee of three practical farmers, with Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green as chairman, to investigate. They came to Hessel Bottom in January, February, and March 1908, sampled the herds themselves, checked the scales, observed the herd conditions and pasture, and reported their findings. The committee found the milk to be whole, the scales to be honest, and no dishonesty in any quarter. However, they concluded that the wet meadow itself was the cause: the coarse sedge hay and standing water of Hessel Bottom, fed through winter, thinned the cream. The remedy, they said, lay not with the station but with the land—drainage and better forage would fix it. This theory had the virtue of blaming no man in the room and appealed to practical experience. The report was accepted and the question was considered closed for eleven years. But the gap remained: 19 hundredths in July, 19 hundredths in February, 19 hundredths every year regardless of feed, weather, or season.

### Story 3: The 1911 Experiment—When the Measure Broke (May 8–July 10, 1911)

On May 8, 1911, Ansel Keddie's own measuring pipette broke. The station could not function without it, so he and the manager obtained a borrowed measure from Larrow Green and put it into use immediately. For the next nine weeks—from May 8 through July 10—Ansel tested milk with the borrowed glass. During that nine-week interval, with every other element unchanged (same patrons, same herds on the same pasture, same season, same acid, same bottles, same separator, same tester working at the same pace), the station's average test rose to 3.80 and held there.

On July 10, the station's own measure arrived back from repair or replacement. Ansel switched to it, and on that same day the average test fell back to 3.61. Both events—the rise and the fall—he recorded contemporaneously in his test book, the second time adding a question mark: "July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." He had noticed. He could not explain it then and never did. Fourteen years later he was still wondering, unable to prove what he suspected, unwilling to write down guesses in a record he had kept perfectly.

This interval is what Professor Vaught later called "an experiment that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered." One variable changed; all others remained constant. The result showed that something in the apparatus—specifically, in the glass—was responsible for the low readings. It was not the hay, not the cows, not the men, not the season.

### Story 4: The Water Accusation and Family Rupture (1919 and After)

In June 1919, Selby Vose, manager of the Association since 1913, composed and circulated a printed circular to the patrons of Ostrey Hollow. The circular warned them that the Association pays for butterfat and cannot pay for added water, and warned that water in cans would be looked for and dealt with. It was printed, not personal; it named nobody; it accused nobody directly. But in a valley where milk quality was family reputation and livelihood, a public warning about watering milk was a public accusation.

Ansel Keddie received nine copies—one for each of the nine families hauling to the station. Rather than post them on the platform, he delivered them himself, one to each household, standing at the door while each man read it. He returned home at eleven at night with his hat still on, his wife Orra noted in her diary. Two days later, Orra's brother Emmet Brant drove up to Ansel's house, stood in the yard without entering, and asked Ansel why he had carried the letter himself if he did not believe its contents. Ansel said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it—he would not let the accusation come from someone else's hand. Emmet went home. He did not enter that house again, though it was his sister's home and he lived within sight of it. Orra recorded: "He has not been in this house since, and he is my brother."

The rupture lasted years. Father and brother-in-law, who had eaten Sunday dinners together, stopped speaking. Neither would pass the other's lane. Ansel would walk miles out of his way rather than meet Emmet. They remained at war by silence until Emmet's death in 1929, after which Orra's daughter-in-law Roy's wife brought Orra to Emmet's house for an hour, and Orra went home and recorded it: "Ten years and neither of them would go up the road first, and now it is settled the way those things get settled."

The circular had solved nothing. The tests did not improve. But the valley had chosen its explanation and had nailed it to the one man with access to the figures, because a man you can see is easier to blame than a system you cannot change.
