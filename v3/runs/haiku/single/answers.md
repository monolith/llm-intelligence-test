# Test Answers — Part 1: Section A (Stories 1–4)

## Section A: Reconstruction of Eight Underlying Stories

### Story 1: The Association's Founding and the Rule That Made the System Vulnerable (1889–1897)

The farmers of Larrow Valley, dissatisfied with merchants' butter prices, organized themselves at Larrow Green in 1889 into the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose, a farmer who had once considered law, was elected first president and drafted the by-laws himself at his kitchen table. The cornerstone was Article VII, adopted at the 1892 annual meeting: patrons would be paid not by milk weight, but by butterfat content as ascertained by test at the station where they delivered. "The test of the station where he delivers shall govern"—no appeal, no external verification, only local measurement.

The Association built its main creamery at Larrow Green in 1891 on the creek below the mill. Seven miles up the valley, at Ostrey Hollow near Hessel Bottom, they erected a skimming station opening May 4, 1896. Ansel Keddie, hired to test and run the separator, established himself as the sole operator—one man weighing, sampling, testing, and recording every patron's milk. The station's complete testing outfit arrived from Ammon & Sons, Tarnet, on April 14, 1897: six dozen milk-test pipettes at 17.6 cubic centimeters, test bottles, an acid measure, a hand tester, and freight, totaling $77.93. The pipettes entered use May 1, 1898. Orra Brant, sister of Emmet Brant (the largest patron), married Ansel in 1889 and bore his son Duncan in 1894. By this arrangement, one man's marriage joined the tester to the family most affected by the system's accuracy. The by-law was just—it paid farmers for what they actually brought, not for water naturally in milk—but it made everything hinge on one instrument in one man's hands, with no inspection or verification built in. This was the system's design at the moment it became vulnerable to hidden defect.

### Story 2: The Anomaly Emerges—Milk at Ostrey Hollow Tests Lower Than the Valley (1902–1908)

Beginning in 1902, farmers at Ostrey Hollow began to complain that their milk tested lower at the station than milk delivered elsewhere in the valley. One farmer complained to his brother-in-law that their cows, feed, and water were identical but their pay was not. The gap persisted month to month, season to season, year to year: roughly 0.19 points (19 hundredths) consistently lower at Ostrey Hollow than at Larrow Green. In July it was there; in February it was there. The difference never came and went with seasons. No man could explain it.

By 1908, the complaint had grown loud enough that the Association appointed a committee of three practical farmers, with Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green as chairman, to investigate. They came to Hessel Bottom in January, February, and March 1908, sampled the herds themselves, checked the scales, observed the herd conditions and pasture, and reported their findings. The committee found the milk to be whole, the scales to be honest, and no dishonesty in any quarter. However, they concluded that the wet meadow itself was the cause: the coarse sedge hay and standing water of Hessel Bottom, fed through winter, thinned the cream. The remedy, they said, lay not with the station but with the land—drainage and better forage would fix it. This theory had the virtue of blaming no man in the room and appealed to practical experience. The report was accepted and the question was considered closed for eleven years. But the gap remained: 19 hundredths in July, 19 hundredths in February, 19 hundredths every year regardless of feed, weather, or season.

### Story 3: The 1911 Experiment—When the Measure Broke (May 8–July 10, 1911)

On May 8, 1911, Ansel Keddie's own measuring pipette broke. The station could not function without it, so he and the manager obtained a borrowed measure from Larrow Green and put it into use immediately. For the next nine weeks—from May 8 through July 10—Ansel tested milk with the borrowed glass. During that nine-week interval, with every other element unchanged (same patrons, same herds on the same pasture, same season, same acid, same bottles, same separator, same tester working at the same pace), the station's average test rose to 3.80 and held there.

On July 10, the station's own measure arrived back from repair or replacement. Ansel switched to it, and on that same day the average test fell back to 3.61. Both events—the rise and the fall—he recorded contemporaneously in his test book, the second time adding a question mark: "July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." He had noticed. He could not explain it then and never did. Fourteen years later he was still wondering, unable to prove what he suspected, unwilling to write down guesses in a record he had kept perfectly.

This interval is what Professor Vaught later called "an experiment that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered." One variable changed; all others remained constant. The result showed that something in the apparatus—specifically, in the glass—was responsible for the low readings. It was not the hay, not the cows, not the men, not the season.

### Story 4: The Water Accusation and Family Rupture (1919 and After)

In June 1919, Selby Vose, manager of the Association since 1913, composed and circulated a printed circular to the patrons of Ostrey Hollow. The circular warned them that the Association pays for butterfat and cannot pay for added water, and warned that water in cans would be looked for and dealt with. It was printed, not personal; it named nobody; it accused nobody directly. But in a valley where milk quality was family reputation and livelihood, a public warning about watering milk was a public accusation.

Ansel Keddie received nine copies—one for each of the nine families hauling to the station. Rather than post them on the platform, he delivered them himself, one to each household, standing at the door while each man read it. He returned home at eleven at night with his hat still on, his wife Orra noted in her diary. Two days later, Orra's brother Emmet Brant drove up to Ansel's house, stood in the yard without entering, and asked Ansel why he had carried the letter himself if he did not believe its contents. Ansel said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it—he would not let the accusation come from someone else's hand. Emmet went home. He did not enter that house again, though it was his sister's home and he lived within sight of it. Orra recorded: "He has not been in this house since, and he is my brother."

The rupture lasted years. Father and brother-in-law, who had eaten Sunday dinners together, stopped speaking. Neither would pass the other's lane. Ansel would walk miles out of his way rather than meet Emmet. They remained at war by silence until Emmet's death in 1929, after which Orra's daughter-in-law Roy's wife brought Orra to Emmet's house for an hour, and Orra went home and recorded it: "Ten years and neither of them would go up the road first, and now it is settled the way those things get settled."

The circular had solved nothing. The tests did not improve. But the valley had chosen its explanation and had nailed it to the one man with access to the figures, because a man you can see is easier to blame than a system you cannot change.

### Story 5: The State Inspector Condemns the Glassware (June 12, 1923)

On a regular circuit visit in June 1923, Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector for Ordell County, arrived at Ostrey Hollow station to conduct his routine inspections: scales, lactometer, boiler, water supply, and the tester's work. Strawn had been assigned the Ordell circuit since 1907 and made frequent rounds. In 1921, the state had issued him a calibrated burette—a precision measuring instrument—with directions for checking whether dairy plant equipment delivered what it was marked to deliver.

On June 12, 1923, Strawn ran the station's milk pipettes against his burette. The pipettes would not deliver their mark. Every one of them was short. Strawn condemned the entire lot on the spot and instructed the manager to order new glass. He wrote it down: "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new."

Selby Vose received the order to replace the glassware. He wrote to Ammon & Sons on June 28, 1923, requesting replacement pipettes and bottles. The firm answered by return mail on July 2, regretted the trouble, and promised shipment within the month. By July or August 1923, new glass was in use at the station.

Strawn's circuit notebook entry made no inference about previous years. He had found faulty equipment and ordered it fixed. That was his job. The nine weeks of 1911 experiment had shown that measure could be the problem, but no one had connected that moment of suspicion to these forty years of low figures. The glassware had been made short—whether by accident in manufacture or by design was never discovered—and had been in use since 1898. It was condemned in 1923 and removed from the station, crated, and set in the loft above the intake room, where it lay untouched for thirty-five years.

### Story 6: The 1925 Arbitration and Ansel's Dismissal (January–May 1925)

In January 1925, the patrons of Ostrey Hollow submitted a petition to the Association. They had endured twenty-three years of complaints and explanations. The committee of 1908 had blamed the land. Nothing had been done. The tests still ran low. Enough. They demanded redress.

The Association's by-laws provided for arbitration. A board of three was formed, chaired by Alonzo Frick, an attorney from Ashlin known as an impartial man. The board sat three times: first at the Association's office in Ashlin on March 17, 1925, then twice at the station itself, in the weigh room, so the board could see the equipment and operation.

Three explanations were presented. Selby Vose argued that water had been added to cans—a little water is "the oldest trick in the trade," he said, and the milk comes in thinned. Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper, made a different argument: he outlined how a man could take cream for himself and write low tests to conceal it, then observed that he made no charge against anybody—but left the implication clear. Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee report was read again: the wet hay of Hessel Bottom thins the cream; the remedy lies with the land.

Ansel Keddie made a statement to the board on March 17. Nine sentences. He said he had tested as he was taught, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons. He had never taken a pound of cream. He could not tell them why the figures were what they were. Neither of his accusers, he said, had ever come to the station on a working morning and stood at his elbow. A man who cannot account for a figure is presumed by everybody in the room to be its cause. His denial did him no good.

Four days before the board gave its award—on April 30, 1925, while they were still deliberating—the Association dismissed Ansel. He came up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm, hung it behind the shed door, and asked his wife what there was for supper.

On May 4, the board announced its award. It found no cause established. It found no cause established against the station, the office, or any officer or servant of the Association. Then, having found no cause, it awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons three thousand eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), "without admission, to be charged to the station account." An award without a finding. Money without explanation. The patrons' own calculation of their loss was sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat over the complaint years. At twenty-five cents a pound, that was nineteen thousand dollars. They received three thousand eight hundred. The board did not explain the mathematics.

Ansel was gone. He lived nine more years, did carpentry work for farmers who had once brought him milk, and died in 1934 with the imputation on him. The whole valley looked at him a certain way in church, and his wife bore it too, outliving him fourteen years with the whisper still following her.

### Story 7: The Discovery and Measurement of the Faulty Glassware (February–March 1958)

In February 1958, thirty-five years after it was condemned and placed in the loft, Ivy Keddie climbed a ladder into the storage space above the old station's intake room. Ivy was twenty-seven years old, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, and the granddaughter of Ansel Keddie. She was doing independent research on the long-standing mystery of Ostrey Hollow for her college work. She had come to the old station to investigate.

Under a tarpaulin and an inch of accumulated chaff, she found a wooden crate stenciled with the manufacturer's name: Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, Tarnet. Inside, packed in straw, were the station's condemned milk-test pipettes from 1923. Six dozen had been purchased new in 1897. Nine survived unbroken.

Ivy carried the crate east to Farland and brought it to the college laboratory. On March 9, 1958, she measured each of the nine pipettes against a calibrated burette, making five runs with each pipette and averaging the results. She recorded her findings:

Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark.

Every pipette delivered exactly the same proportion of its marked capacity. The consistency was perfect. The shortfall was absolute: five percent short of intended, or one-twentieth short, year after year, test after test, for all twenty-five years of use. It was an accident of manufacture—a mold worn or reset, a gauge knocked off its nail, a systematic error in the blowing that nobody's spot checks caught, because the checks were spot checks and the error was uniform.

With the physical evidence in hand and its measurements recorded, Ivy approached Professor Leland Vaught of the college's department of dairy husbandry. Vaught reviewed the evidence, consulted the historical records, and began to reconstruct the mathematics of what one part in twenty would produce if applied to all Ostrey Hollow milk over thirty years.

### Story 8: The Vindication—Publication and the Truth Made Known (October 1958 and After)

In October 1958, the State Agricultural College at Farland published Bulletin No. 471 under the joint authorship of Professor Leland Vaught and Miss Ivy Keddie. The bulletin was addressed to extension agents and county dairy workers. It presented the physical evidence (nine pipettes, measured), the mathematical analysis (5% shortfall, applied to 40 million pounds of milk), and the historical context (Article VII, the 1911 experiment, the 1925 arbitration).

The conclusion was unambiguous: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference—seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat—stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

The numbers were stark. Forty million pounds of milk received. At the recorded 3.61% test, patrons were credited with 1,444,000 pounds of butterfat. At the true 3.80%, they had delivered 1,520,000 pounds. The difference: 76,000 pounds. At the average price of 25 cents per pound over the period, that was $19,000. The patrons were paid $3,800.

Ansel Keddie had been dismissed April 30, 1925. He died in 1934. The bulletin clearing his name was published October 1958. Thirty-three years had passed since his dismissal; twenty-four since his death. His widow, Orra, had preceded him in death in 1948, without knowing the truth.

The vindication was published, but it was not restoration. A man's name can be cleared, but only if someone is there to hear it. The damages were not remitted. The families of Ostrey Hollow had received a fraction of what was owed them. The Association's records were closed. Ansel's death removed him from any remedy. But the truth was at last in print, published by the state agricultural college, backed by physical evidence and mathematics, available to anyone who wished to read it.

What had taken eight separate records to understand—a test book, a weigh book, a state inspector's notebook, a crate of glass pipettes, a ledger, a diary, an unsent letter, and the cream receiving books—was finally coherent. The cause was glass. The man was innocent. The system had been honest in every particular except the one instrument that everything depended on.

Documents: r04 (Ivy Keddie, 1959 Historical Society talk—containing the closing paragraph of Bulletin 471), r07 (Halbert Nym, 1972 museum accessions—Items 14, 15), r11 (Leland Vaught, 1958 Bulletin 471 complete text), r18 (Duncan Keddie, 1970 addition noting the vindication), r19 (Ashlin Chronicle, 1958 feature—"Old Ordell Wrong Righted"), r21 (Cleve Oram, 1977 account of the revelation), r22 (Orin Falke, 1959 county agent report incorporating the bulletin).

## Section B: Relationships (8 points)

### B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Ansel married Orra Brant in 1889 and they had a son, Duncan Keddie, born in 1894 (who taught school at Ashlin). Duncan had a daughter, Ivy Keddie, born in 1931. This makes Ivy the daughter of Duncan and thus the granddaughter of Ansel.

*Sources:* r04 (Ivy directly states "Ansel Keddie, who kept the test book at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station, was my grandfather"), r18 (Duncan confirms "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter"), r22 (Orin Falke's 1959 correction note: "Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece").

*Conflict resolution:* The Ashlin Chronicle (r19) initially misidentified her as "great-niece," but Falke's corrective note and Duncan's own testimony establish the true relationship as granddaughter.

### B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant?

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. Hazel's father, Roy Brant, was Duncan Keddie's first cousin (they were children of a brother and sister: Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie). Therefore, their children—Ivy and Hazel—are second cousins.

*Sources:* r09 (Roy Brant: "Duncan Keddie... was Ansel and Orra's boy, and he and I are first cousins. My daughter Hazel and his daughter Ivy are second cousins"), r17 (Hazel Brant herself: "Ivy Keddie and I are second cousins"), r18 (Duncan Keddie confirms the same relationship).

*Conflict resolution:* Cleve Oram (r21) incorrectly stated they were "first cousin once removed," but he was operating from oral tradition and admitted "I could not draw you the line on paper." Three independent written sources confirm they are second cousins. The question of intermediate generations is settled by understanding that Roy and Duncan were first cousins (Emmet and Orra as siblings), making their children second cousins, not establishing any offset generation.

### B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter?

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose, founder and first president of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, who held office from 1889 to 1911 and drafted the Association's by-laws himself.

This matters because: (1) It establishes that Selby directly inherited his father's vision and authority over the system Gideon created. (2) Gideon wrote Article VII, the payment-by-test rule, which made every station's test final and unappealable—the very rule that created the conditions for the Ostrey Hollow problem. (3) Selby, as the founder's son, would have felt obliged to defend his father's by-laws and to protect his father's reputation by explaining away the anomaly through external causes (water, theft, bad land) rather than questioning the system itself. (4) The connection shows continuity of leadership and responsibility: the founder created the vulnerable system; his son managed it for twenty-five years and never identified the root cause.

*Sources:* r08 (Selby refers to his father's role), r18 (Duncan Keddie explicitly states: "Selby Vose, who managed the Association, was Gideon Vose's son. Gideon organized the Association and was its first president and wrote its by-laws; Selby was his son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew"), r23 (Bertram Kyle: "He was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president—the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense").

*Conflict resolution:* One source (r12, Dell Rundle's 1939 pamphlet) incorrectly refers to Selby as Gideon's nephew. However, two later sources (r18 1968, r23 1953) explicitly correct this error and confirm Selby was the son, not nephew. Duncan and Bertram Kyle note that the "nephew" story circulates in oral tradition but is false. Selby's own 1926 address (r08) references "my father" in context of Gideon Vose's work, corroborating the son relationship.

### B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?

There was no documented family relationship between the Voses and Keddies. Duncan Keddie (r18) explicitly states: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses... My mother would have told me. She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it."

However, there was a documented relationship between the Brants (Orra's family) and the Voses: Orra Keddie's mother and Gideon Vose's wife (old Mrs. Vose) were first cousins, as stated in r01 (Vira Toland): "Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins. Everybody knew that." This Brant-Vose connection is significant because it placed Selby Vose (Gideon's son) and Emmet Brant (Orra's brother, Ansel's brother-in-law) in a loose family web through their mothers.

*Sources:* r01 (Vira Toland, establishing Brant-Vose connection), r18 (Duncan Keddie, explicitly denying Keddie-Vose connection), r21 (Cleve Oram notes family connections were said but could not be drawn clearly on paper), r23 (Bertram Kyle, no mention of Vose-Keddie ties).

*Conflict resolution:* Cleve Oram (r21) reports hearing that "The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort," but he admits this was hearsay from his mother and "I could not draw you the line on paper." Duncan Keddie, who lived in the family and knew its records well, flatly denies any such connection. The documented connection is between Brants and Voses (through the mothers), not between Keddies and Voses. The confusion may arise from the Brant-Keddie tie (Orra married Ansel) combined with the Brant-Vose tie, creating an indirect relationship, but no direct Keddie-Vose kinship.
# Test Answers — Part 3: Sections C through G

## Section C: Math (14 points)

### C1. The Proportion and Its Consequences

**(a) Proportion of difference and derivation from glassware:**

The pipettes were blown to deliver 17.6 cubic centimeters but actually delivered 16.72 c.c. (mean of nine surviving pipettes measured by Ivy Keddie on March 9, 1958). The ratio is:

16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 (exactly 95 hundredths)

Therefore, the tests were short by one part in twenty, or 5% of the true value. The proportion is expressed as: tests read 95% of the truth, or were low by 5%.

*Sources:* r04 (Ivy Keddie's calculation and statement), r07 (Nym's curatorial note on Item 14), r11 (Vaught's conclusion: "one part in twenty").

**(b) True average test and shortfall:**

Recorded average over affected years: 3.61%
True average: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80%
Shortfall: 3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19 points (19 hundredths)

*Sources:* r04 (Ivy Keddie: "The recorded average was 3.61; the true average was 3.80; the gap is nineteen hundredths of a point"), r11 (Vaught's section V confirms this calculation).

**(c) Butterfat not credited:**

Station intake over 25 full delivery years (1898–1922): 40,000,000 pounds of milk (r04, r07, r22)

At recorded 3.61%: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lbs butterfat (credited to patrons)
At true 3.80%: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lbs butterfat (actually delivered)

Difference: 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = **76,000 pounds of butterfat**

*Sources:* r04 (Ivy states this directly and works it out fully), r07 (curatorial note confirms 40 million lbs and 76,000 lb difference), r11 (Vaught's conclusion).

**(d) Worth and fraction received:**

At average price of 25 cents per pound (r02, r09, r23 all confirm ~25 cents):
76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000** (what patrons should have been paid)

Patrons actually received: **$3,800** (the board award of 1925)

Fraction received: $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20 = **one-fifth (or 20%)**

In other words, patrons recovered only one-fifth of their actual loss.

*Sources:* r04 (Ivy calculates this: "At the true 3.80 percent, they had delivered one million five hundred twenty thousand pounds... The difference is seventy-six thousand pounds of butterfat. Butterfat over those years averaged twenty-five cents a pound, so the unpaid fat was worth nineteen thousand dollars. The board of arbitration in 1925 awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons thirty-eight hundred dollars. That is a fifth of it."), r02 (Effie Loomis records the award: $3,800.00).

### C2. Emmet Brant's 1910 (Largest Patron)

**(a) Butterfat credited:**

Emmet delivered 200,000 lbs at a recorded test of 3.42%:
200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 pounds of butterfat credited**

*Source:* r09 (Roy Brant's weigh book entry: "Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42."), r17 (Hazel Brant recalling "That one I can say with my eyes shut... Nineteen ten... Two hundred thousand pounds even... paid on a test of three forty-two.").

**(b) Butterfat owed and not credited:**

Since every test was 5% short, the true test would have been:
3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60%

True butterfat delivered: 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lbs

Owed but not credited: 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 pounds of butterfat**

**(c) Worth that year:**

r09 states: "The price of butterfat that year was twenty-four cents."

360 × $0.24 = **$86.40**

(If using the valley average of 25 cents: 360 × $0.25 = $90.00)

*Sources:* r09 (Roy Brant's weigh book and his note on 1910 price), r02 (r02 reports average of 25 cents over the period).

### C3. The Station Account—Credited vs. Delivered

**(a) Total butterfat credited to patrons:**

From r04 (Ivy Keddie): "At the recorded 3.61 percent, the patrons were credited with one million four hundred forty-four thousand pounds of fat."

**Answer: 1,444,000 pounds**

**(b) Total butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in station cream:**

From r04: "At the true 3.80 percent, they had delivered one million five hundred twenty thousand pounds."

More directly: 40,000,000 lbs × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lbs

**Answer: 1,520,000 pounds**

*Sources:* r04 (Ivy), r05 (Teague), r07 (Nym, curatorial note).

**(c) Difference expressed as percentage—exact specification of denominator:**

The difference is 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 pounds.

**As a percentage of actual fat delivered:** 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = 0.05 = **5%**

**As a percentage of fat credited to patrons:** 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 0.0526 = **5.26% (or "five and a quarter percent" per r04)**

*Source:* r04 (Ivy Keddie explicitly clarifies this distinction: "Seventy-six thousand pounds is five percent of the fat actually delivered—that is, of the million five hundred twenty thousand. It is five and a quarter percent of the fat credited, of the million four hundred forty-four thousand. The Association's ledger line uses the second, because a bookkeeper naturally reckons a surplus against the figure he has already entered.").

The question specifies "be exact about what you are taking a percentage of," so the answer is: **5% of the fat actually delivered, or 5.26% of the fat credited**. The 5% figure corresponds to the glass defect (one part in twenty) and is the more fundamental measure.

## Section D: Logic (10 points)

### D1. The 1911 Experiment as Controlled Trial

**When:** May 8 to July 10, 1911 (nine weeks)

**The variable:** The measuring glass/pipette (station's own vs. borrowed)

**Held constant:** Same patrons, same herds, same pasture (all on Hessel Bottom wet meadow, the same "problem" ground), same season (May–July, the flush when grass is fresh), same acid, same bottles, same separator, same tester (Ansel Keddie), same tester's speed and habits (Ansel worked at his invariant pace), same man operating the separator

**The result:** Station average test rose from 3.61% to 3.80% (19 hundredths) during the nine weeks, then fell back to 3.61% on the day the station's own measure returned to use.

**What it establishes:** 
This interval proves experimentally that the defect lay in the glassware, not in the milk, the herds, the pasture, the men, the feed, or the season. It is what Professor Vaught called "an experiment that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered." One object changed; everything else remained constant. The result changed proportionally with the one changed object. This is the clearest possible evidence of causation in a field setting. It disproves any theory that locates the problem in the land, the cattle, the tester's integrity, or the season.

**What it does NOT establish:**
- It does not prove that cream was never stolen (though the rise in tests suggests the tester did not steal, since he was using a different measure); it only proves that if cream were stolen, the glass defect was sufficient to account for all low readings
- It does not rule out water addition in other years (though the 1911 rise with the same man handling the same station suggests any water addition was not the cause of the gap)
- It does not identify what specifically was wrong with the glass—only that something was
- It does not tell us whether the defect was accidental or intentional, or whether it existed from the start or developed over time

*Sources:* r03 (Ansel's unsent letter: "Those nine weeks in 1911... The two entries stand as I made them: May 8. Pipette broke this morning... July 10. Our own measure in use again... Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."), r06 (Strawn's 1911 entry: "Watched a test through. Nothing here to note."), r11 (Vaught: "For nine weeks in the summer of 1911... no other word for it—is an experiment that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven years"), r09 (Roy Brant: "There were nine weeks in the summer of 1911 when the station's own measure was out of use... Through those nine weeks our tests came up, all of us, every farm on the Bottom; and then the station's own came back into use and the figures went down again to where they had been.").

### D2. Three Independent Reasons the Tester Could Not Have Been Stealing Cream

**Reason 1 — Hobart Sill's observation (r14):** The buttermaker who received the station's cream at Larrow Green testified that the cream "ran heavy" — it consistently arrived in larger quantity than the station's test books claimed the tester had purchased. Sill said: "That cream ran heavy. Every year it ran heavy. There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there." He weighed it himself most years and recorded it in the receiving book, which showed year after year that actual fat received exceeded credited fat. If Ansel were taking cream, the cream reaching Larrow Green would have been *less*, not more. This is direct evidence of innocence.

**Reason 2 — The 1911 experiment (r03, r11):** When the measure broke and a borrowed one was used for nine weeks, the tests rose immediately. The same tester (Ansel) did nothing different; he used the same methods and speed. He took no new precautions, faced no new supervision. If he had been stealing, the tests would not have improved on their own. The fact that tests improved with a different glass proves the tester was not the variable.

**Reason 3 — Merle Strawn's inspections (r06):** The state dairy inspector visited the station repeatedly from 1908 to 1936 and tested for added water, checked the scales, and watched the tester work. His records show: "Lactometer, no added water" at every visit. On June 1923, he watched the tester work through a full test and reported nothing to note. If cream had been systematically stolen or milk watered, the lactometer would have detected dilution. Strawn found none. Moreover, on his final visit in 1936, he recorded: "Nothing to note anywhere and nothing left to say about it." An independent inspector with no interest in the dispute found no evidence of theft or tampering.

**Independence of the reasons:**
- Reason 1 (Sill's volume observation) is independent of the tester's methodology (Reason 2) and of external inspection (Reason 3)
- Reason 2 (the 1911 experiment) depends on apparatus, not on volume or inspection
- Reason 3 (Strawn's inspections) is independent observation by an outsider with no stake in the outcome

**Which would hold without the 1958 measurement?**
All three. Sill's ledger entries would still show cream arriving heavier than credited (documentary evidence, contemporaneous). The 1911 experiment is recorded in Ansel's own test book (contemporaneous, not dependent on later glass measurement). Strawn's circuit notebooks with his lactometer tests and his own notes (preserved in state archives, not dependent on finding the crate) would still show no added water. None of these three reasons depends on the 1958 discovery that the glass was short.

**Did Teague ever question the ledger entries?**
Yes, but then deferred to management. r05 (Wilbur Teague): "I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone. I let it alone. I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience, and a bookkeeper who takes his employer's business up a second time after being told once has generally begun to look for another employer." Teague questioned the "station gain" line (the difference between credited and received fat) with the manager in 1916, when he had been in the job 11 years and could see the pattern. Vose told him to drop it. Teague complied. So Teague *did* question the evidence that would have exonerated the tester—the cream arriving heavier than credited—but he did not pursue it past his manager's refusal.

*Sources:* r05 (Teague), r06 (Strawn), r14 (Sill), r23 (Kyle also notes the "station gain" line appeared in the ledgers).

### D3. Did the Manager Know the Tests Were Low Before June 1923?

**Answer: Yes, almost certainly.**

**Justification:**

1. **Signed the ledger sheets:** Selby Vose signed the annual station account ledger sheets every year from 1913 (when he became manager) onward. r05 (Teague): "He [Vose] signed the sheet every year. I laid the annual sheet before him each spring and he put his name at the foot of it, and a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him." The sheets prominently displayed the "station gain" line showing that 5-6% more fat arrived at Larrow Green than the station had credited to patrons—year after year, the same proportion, "as usual."

2. **Circulation of the water warning (1919):** Vose issued the water circular in 1919, explicitly to warn patrons about dilution. This suggests he had been puzzling over the low tests and had formed a theory about the cause. Why warn about water if you had not noticed a problem? r08 (Vose's 1926 address): "For a generation the milk brought to that station tested below the milk brought to Larrow Green, and every year we were told it was the wet hay, or the tester, or the office, or the weather." Vose is acknowledging that he knew about the persistent gap.

3. **The rise in 1924:** r06 (Strawn's 1924 entry) and r22 (Falke) note that "tests improved" and "stood at 3.80" in 1924, the year after the glass was condemned in June 1923. If Vose noticed the tests rose in 1924 (from their previous average of ~3.61), he would connect this to the new glass ordered in June 1923. This suggests he understood the equipment change solved the problem.

4. **His 1926 defense:** r08 (Vose's 1926 address to the membership): "And in the very next season [after investigation began] the tests at that station rose by twenty-one hundredths and the average for 1924 stood at 3.90 [he misspoke; it was 3.80], which is as good a figure as any station in this county will show you." Vose was explicitly defending himself by pointing out that tests improved immediately after the board examined the station. If he did not know tests had been low before, why this defense?

**Counter-argument (acknowledging the limits):**

r23 (Bertram Kyle): "Mr. Selby Vose... He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign. I watched him do it a thousand times." This suggests Vose read only the final balance line, not the detailed entries. He might have signed the sheets without reading the supporting lines that documented the problem.

**Conclusion:**

Vose likely knew or strongly suspected that something was systematically wrong at the station (given the persistent 25-year pattern of low tests and the fact that tests improved after new glass was installed in 1923). However, he may not have carefully studied the station gain line that documented the problem. His 1919 water circular suggests he suspected *cheating* (water, theft) rather than a glass defect. He probably attributed the gap to badness in the milk or the men, not to an apparatus flaw. He knew something was wrong; he may not have known what exactly.

*Sources:* r05 (Teague on signed sheets), r08 (Vose's address), r06 (Strawn's 1924 observation), r22 (Falke's summary), r23 (Kyle on how Vose read the ledgers).

## Section F: Theory (6 points)

### F1. Causal Theory and Competing Explanations

**Unified Causal Theory:**

The Ostrey Hollow station's milk tests were systematically low from 1898 through June 1923 because the milk-test pipettes purchased from Ammon & Sons, Tarnet, in April 1897 were manufactured short of specification. The pipettes were blown to deliver 17.6 cubic centimeters but actually delivered 16.72 c.c., representing a shortfall of one part in twenty (5%).

A butterfat test depends on delivering a precise measured charge of milk into a test bottle whose graduated neck is marked to read percentage for that charge and no other. A short charge produces a short fat column, which reads as a lower percentage when measured against the fixed markings. The error is proportional, not additive: rich milk (high butterfat) lost more absolutely than poor milk, but the ratio between herds remained consistent. This is why the station ranked herds correctly internally but wrong against the outside world.

The defect began when the station's own glass entered use May 1, 1898 (before then, borrowed glass was used, which tested correctly). It continued year after year because the defect was uniform and invisible from within the station: an instrument that is itself the standard cannot detect its own error. No external check was built into the system. The bookkeeper's records showed a persistent 5% "station gain" (more fat arriving than credited), but this was filed away under a name—"station gain, five and a quarter percent, as usual"—and thus explained away. The error ended when state inspector Merle Strawn condemned the glass on June 12, 1923, and new glass was ordered and in use by July 1923.

From 1923 onward, the station's tests averaged 3.80%, the true value. Nothing at the station or on the farms had to change because the problem was never in the milk, the men, or the management. It was in one piece of glass that cost 35 cents.

Over the twenty-five full delivery years from 1898 to 1922, the defect cost the patrons 76,000 pounds of butterfat, worth $19,000 at the average price of 25 cents per pound. They were awarded $3,800 without admission of cause.

---

**Three Competing Explanations and Evidence Against Each:**

1. **Water in Cans (Selby Vose's theory):**
   - Vose advanced this explanation in his 1919 circular and reiterated it to the arbitration board in 1925.
   - **Evidence defeating it:** Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, visited the station regularly from 1908 onward and tested every day's milk with a lactometer for added water. His circuit notebooks document "Lactometer, no added water" at every single visit. Water-diluted milk cannot escape lactometer detection. If water had been systematically added over 25 years, Strawn would have found evidence within a few years of his visits beginning. His repeated "no added water" findings across 20+ years of inspections definitively refute the water theory.

2. **Cream Theft (Wilbur Teague's theory):**
   - Teague implied to the arbitration board that the tester might be removing cream and writing low tests to conceal it.
   - **Evidence defeating it:** The 1911 experiment. When the measure broke and a borrowed one was used for nine weeks, tests rose from 3.61 to 3.80 while the same tester (Ansel) worked at his ordinary pace, with no new supervision or oversight. If cream theft were occurring, it would continue; the tester would not cease stealing when a different glass was installed. Yet tests rose immediately. Additionally, Hobart Sill (the buttermaker receiving the station's cream) testified that cream always arrived "heavy"—in larger quantity than the station's records claimed—year after year. If cream were being stolen, the cream arriving at Larrow Green would be less than credited, not more.

3. **Poor Feed from Wet Meadow (The 1908 Committee's theory):**
   - The investigating committee of 1908 concluded that the wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream, and that remedy lay with land improvement, not the station.
   - **Evidence defeating it:** The State Agricultural College's Bulletin No. 214 (1911) conducted a controlled feeding trial on bog hay and published findings that bog hay depressed butterfat by no more than 0.05% in winter and nothing in summer. But the Ostrey Hollow gap was 0.19%, flat year-round, present in both winter and summer, in wet years and dry. Five hundredths cannot account for nineteen hundredths. Additionally, four Hessel Bottom farmers hauled milk to the Alder Corners Cheese Company over Redlow Ridge (summer only, 1915–1930). Their milk there, from the same wet meadow, tested about 3.80—identical to what the borrowed measure showed in 1911. The hay did not thin the milk; the glass did.

---

**Did the board examine the condemned glassware?**

No. The glass was condemned June 12, 1923. The arbitration board sat March 17 to May 4, 1925. The crate remained in the loft at the station. Bertram Kyle (r23) carried four documents to the board: the test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book, and Strawn's circuit notebook for 1923. The notebook documented that Strawn had condemned the glass, but the board did not have the glass itself to measure or examine. Kyle: "I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked."

---

**What did the tester believe?**

Ansel Keddie suspected the glass. In the summer of 1911, after the measure broke and tests rose, he came home to his wife Orra and said, "It was something in the glass." He never dared write this suspicion in his carefully maintained record. Instead, he entered the date ("May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure") and the return date ("July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were.") and next to the return date he wrote a question mark: "? — A.K." 

He carried this suspicion for fourteen years (1911 to 1925 and beyond). r18 (his son Duncan): "He came home that summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book... What he wrote in the book was a question mark. He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove, and I have thought since that this was the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had."

Ansel died in 1934. The truth was confirmed in 1958, twenty-four years after his death. He never knew he was right.

*Sources for F1:* r03, r04, r06, r11, r13, r14, r15, r16, r18, r22.

## Section G: Summary (4 points)

### G1. Complete History in 150 Words

In 1889, farmers in Larrow Valley formed a cooperative creamery, adopting a rule: pay each patron by butterfat content, tested at his delivery station. The system was just but vulnerable—everything hinged on accurate measurements, with no appeal. At Ostrey Hollow, milk consistently tested one-fifth of a percent lower than elsewhere. For twenty-five years, three theories circulated: farmers were adding water, the tester was stealing cream, or the wet meadow produced inferior milk. In 1923, a state inspector condemned the station's pipettes, discovering they delivered 5% less than their marked capacity. This tiny flaw had produced all twenty-five years of low tests. The tester had been blameless. In 1958, his granddaughter found the crate in a loft and measured the glass, clearing his name only after his death. Eight separate records—books, diaries, bulletins, memories—were required to reconstruct what one inspection should have revealed.
