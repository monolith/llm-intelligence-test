## Section C: Math (14 points)

### C1. The Proportion and Its Consequences

**(a) Proportion of difference and derivation from glassware:**

The pipettes were blown to deliver 17.6 cubic centimeters but actually delivered 16.72 c.c. (mean of nine surviving pipettes measured by Ivy Keddie on March 9, 1958). The ratio is:

16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 (exactly 95 hundredths)

Therefore, the tests were short by one part in twenty, or 5% of the true value. The proportion is expressed as: tests read 95% of the truth, or were low by 5%.

*Sources:* r04 (Ivy Keddie's calculation and statement), r07 (Nym's curatorial note on Item 14), r11 (Vaught's conclusion: "one part in twenty").

**(b) True average test and shortfall:**

Recorded average over affected years: 3.61%
True average: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80%
Shortfall: 3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19 points (19 hundredths)

*Sources:* r04 (Ivy Keddie: "The recorded average was 3.61; the true average was 3.80; the gap is nineteen hundredths of a point"), r11 (Vaught's section V confirms this calculation).

**(c) Butterfat not credited:**

Station intake over 25 full delivery years (1898–1922): 40,000,000 pounds of milk (r04, r07, r22)

At recorded 3.61%: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lbs butterfat (credited to patrons)
At true 3.80%: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lbs butterfat (actually delivered)

Difference: 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = **76,000 pounds of butterfat**

*Sources:* r04 (Ivy states this directly and works it out fully), r07 (curatorial note confirms 40 million lbs and 76,000 lb difference), r11 (Vaught's conclusion).

**(d) Worth and fraction received:**

At average price of 25 cents per pound (r02, r09, r23 all confirm ~25 cents):
76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000** (what patrons should have been paid)

Patrons actually received: **$3,800** (the board award of 1925)

Fraction received: $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20 = **one-fifth (or 20%)**

In other words, patrons recovered only one-fifth of their actual loss.

*Sources:* r04 (Ivy calculates this: "At the true 3.80 percent, they had delivered one million five hundred twenty thousand pounds... The difference is seventy-six thousand pounds of butterfat. Butterfat over those years averaged twenty-five cents a pound, so the unpaid fat was worth nineteen thousand dollars. The board of arbitration in 1925 awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons thirty-eight hundred dollars. That is a fifth of it."), r02 (Effie Loomis records the award: $3,800.00).

### C2. Emmet Brant's 1910 (Largest Patron)

**(a) Butterfat credited:**

Emmet delivered 200,000 lbs at a recorded test of 3.42%:
200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 pounds of butterfat credited**

*Source:* r09 (Roy Brant's weigh book entry: "Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42."), r17 (Hazel Brant recalling "That one I can say with my eyes shut... Nineteen ten... Two hundred thousand pounds even... paid on a test of three forty-two.").

**(b) Butterfat owed and not credited:**

Since every test was 5% short, the true test would have been:
3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60%

True butterfat delivered: 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lbs

Owed but not credited: 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 pounds of butterfat**

**(c) Worth that year:**

r09 states: "The price of butterfat that year was twenty-four cents."

360 × $0.24 = **$86.40**

(If using the valley average of 25 cents: 360 × $0.25 = $90.00)

*Sources:* r09 (Roy Brant's weigh book and his note on 1910 price), r02 (r02 reports average of 25 cents over the period).

### C3. The Station Account—Credited vs. Delivered

**(a) Total butterfat credited to patrons:**

From r04 (Ivy Keddie): "At the recorded 3.61 percent, the patrons were credited with one million four hundred forty-four thousand pounds of fat."

**Answer: 1,444,000 pounds**

**(b) Total butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in station cream:**

From r04: "At the true 3.80 percent, they had delivered one million five hundred twenty thousand pounds."

More directly: 40,000,000 lbs × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lbs

**Answer: 1,520,000 pounds**

*Sources:* r04 (Ivy), r05 (Teague), r07 (Nym, curatorial note).

**(c) Difference expressed as percentage—exact specification of denominator:**

The difference is 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 pounds.

**As a percentage of actual fat delivered:** 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = 0.05 = **5%**

**As a percentage of fat credited to patrons:** 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 0.0526 = **5.26% (or "five and a quarter percent" per r04)**

*Source:* r04 (Ivy Keddie explicitly clarifies this distinction: "Seventy-six thousand pounds is five percent of the fat actually delivered—that is, of the million five hundred twenty thousand. It is five and a quarter percent of the fat credited, of the million four hundred forty-four thousand. The Association's ledger line uses the second, because a bookkeeper naturally reckons a surplus against the figure he has already entered.").

The question specifies "be exact about what you are taking a percentage of," so the answer is: **5% of the fat actually delivered, or 5.26% of the fat credited**. The 5% figure corresponds to the glass defect (one part in twenty) and is the more fundamental measure.

### C5. Duration and Delivery Years

**(a) How long the faulty glass was in use, and over how many full delivery years:**

**Duration in use:** May 1, 1898 (when the station's own glass arrived and entered use—r03, r24) to June 12, 1923 (when condemned by state inspector—r06, r11). This is approximately **25 years and 1+ months**.

## Section D: Logic (10 points)

### D1. The 1911 Experiment as Controlled Trial

**When:** May 8 to July 10, 1911 (nine weeks)

**The variable:** The measuring glass/pipette (station's own vs. borrowed)

**Held constant:** Same patrons, same herds, same pasture (all on Hessel Bottom wet meadow, the same "problem" ground), same season (May–July, the flush when grass is fresh), same acid, same bottles, same separator, same tester (Ansel Keddie), same tester's speed and habits (Ansel worked at his invariant pace), same man operating the separator

**The result:** Station average test rose from 3.61% to 3.80% (19 hundredths) during the nine weeks, then fell back to 3.61% on the day the station's own measure returned to use.

**What it establishes:** 
This interval proves experimentally that the defect lay in the glassware, not in the milk, the herds, the pasture, the men, the feed, or the season. It is what Professor Vaught called "an experiment that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered." One object changed; everything else remained constant. The result changed proportionally with the one changed object. This is the clearest possible evidence of causation in a field setting. It disproves any theory that locates the problem in the land, the cattle, the tester's integrity, or the season.

**What it does NOT establish:**
- It does not prove that cream was never stolen (though the rise in tests suggests the tester did not steal, since he was using a different measure); it only proves that if cream were stolen, the glass defect was sufficient to account for all low readings
- It does not rule out water addition in other years (though the 1911 rise with the same man handling the same station suggests any water addition was not the cause of the gap)
- It does not identify what specifically was wrong with the glass—only that something was
- It does not tell us whether the defect was accidental or intentional, or whether it existed from the start or developed over time

*Sources:* r03 (Ansel's unsent letter: "Those nine weeks in 1911... The two entries stand as I made them: May 8. Pipette broke this morning... July 10. Our own measure in use again... Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."), r06 (Strawn's 1911 entry: "Watched a test through. Nothing here to note."), r11 (Vaught: "For nine weeks in the summer of 1911... no other word for it—is an experiment that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven years"), r09 (Roy Brant: "There were nine weeks in the summer of 1911 when the station's own measure was out of use... Through those nine weeks our tests came up, all of us, every farm on the Bottom; and then the station's own came back into use and the figures went down again to where they had been.").

### D2. Three Independent Reasons the Tester Could Not Have Been Stealing Cream

**Reason 1 — Hobart Sill's observation (r14):** The buttermaker who received the station's cream at Larrow Green testified that the cream "ran heavy" — it consistently arrived in larger quantity than the station's test books claimed the tester had purchased. Sill said: "That cream ran heavy. Every year it ran heavy. There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there." He weighed it himself most years and recorded it in the receiving book, which showed year after year that actual fat received exceeded credited fat. If Ansel were taking cream, the cream reaching Larrow Green would have been *less*, not more. This is direct evidence of innocence.

**Reason 2 — The 1911 experiment (r03, r11):** When the measure broke and a borrowed one was used for nine weeks, the tests rose immediately. The same tester (Ansel) did nothing different; he used the same methods and speed. He took no new precautions, faced no new supervision. If he had been stealing, the tests would not have improved on their own. The fact that tests improved with a different glass proves the tester was not the variable.

**Reason 3 — Merle Strawn's inspections (r06):** The state dairy inspector visited the station repeatedly from 1908 to 1936 and tested for added water, checked the scales, and watched the tester work. His records show: "Lactometer, no added water" at every visit. On June 1923, he watched the tester work through a full test and reported nothing to note. If cream had been systematically stolen or milk watered, the lactometer would have detected dilution. Strawn found none. Moreover, on his final visit in 1936, he recorded: "Nothing to note anywhere and nothing left to say about it." An independent inspector with no interest in the dispute found no evidence of theft or tampering.

**Independence of the reasons:**
- Reason 1 (Sill's volume observation) is independent of the tester's methodology (Reason 2) and of external inspection (Reason 3)
- Reason 2 (the 1911 experiment) depends on apparatus, not on volume or inspection
- Reason 3 (Strawn's inspections) is independent observation by an outsider with no stake in the outcome

**Which would hold without the 1958 measurement?**
All three. Sill's ledger entries would still show cream arriving heavier than credited (documentary evidence, contemporaneous). The 1911 experiment is recorded in Ansel's own test book (contemporaneous, not dependent on later glass measurement). Strawn's circuit notebooks with his lactometer tests and his own notes (preserved in state archives, not dependent on finding the crate) would still show no added water. None of these three reasons depends on the 1958 discovery that the glass was short.

**Did Teague ever question the ledger entries?**
Yes, but then deferred to management. r05 (Wilbur Teague): "I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone. I let it alone. I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience, and a bookkeeper who takes his employer's business up a second time after being told once has generally begun to look for another employer." Teague questioned the "station gain" line (the difference between credited and received fat) with the manager in 1916, when he had been in the job 11 years and could see the pattern. Vose told him to drop it. Teague complied. So Teague *did* question the evidence that would have exonerated the tester—the cream arriving heavier than credited—but he did not pursue it past his manager's refusal.

*Sources:* r05 (Teague), r06 (Strawn), r14 (Sill), r23 (Kyle also notes the "station gain" line appeared in the ledgers).

### D3. Did the Manager Know the Tests Were Low Before June 1923?

**Answer: Yes, almost certainly.**

**Justification:**

1. **Signed the ledger sheets:** Selby Vose signed the annual station account ledger sheets every year from 1913 (when he became manager) onward. r05 (Teague): "He [Vose] signed the sheet every year. I laid the annual sheet before him each spring and he put his name at the foot of it, and a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him." The sheets prominently displayed the "station gain" line showing that 5-6% more fat arrived at Larrow Green than the station had credited to patrons—year after year, the same proportion, "as usual."

2. **Circulation of the water warning (1919):** Vose issued the water circular in 1919, explicitly to warn patrons about dilution. This suggests he had been puzzling over the low tests and had formed a theory about the cause. Why warn about water if you had not noticed a problem? r08 (Vose's 1926 address): "For a generation the milk brought to that station tested below the milk brought to Larrow Green, and every year we were told it was the wet hay, or the tester, or the office, or the weather." Vose is acknowledging that he knew about the persistent gap.

3. **The rise in 1924:** r06 (Strawn's 1924 entry) and r22 (Falke) note that "tests improved" and "stood at 3.80" in 1924, the year after the glass was condemned in June 1923. If Vose noticed the tests rose in 1924 (from their previous average of ~3.61), he would connect this to the new glass ordered in June 1923. This suggests he understood the equipment change solved the problem.

4. **His 1926 defense:** r08 (Vose's 1926 address to the membership): "And in the very next season [after investigation began] the tests at that station rose by twenty-one hundredths and the average for 1924 stood at 3.90 [he misspoke; it was 3.80], which is as good a figure as any station in this county will show you." Vose was explicitly defending himself by pointing out that tests improved immediately after the board examined the station. If he did not know tests had been low before, why this defense?

**Counter-argument (acknowledging the limits):**

r23 (Bertram Kyle): "Mr. Selby Vose... He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign. I watched him do it a thousand times." This suggests Vose read only the final balance line, not the detailed entries. He might have signed the sheets without reading the supporting lines that documented the problem.

**Conclusion:**

Vose likely knew or strongly suspected that something was systematically wrong at the station (given the persistent 25-year pattern of low tests and the fact that tests improved after new glass was installed in 1923). However, he may not have carefully studied the station gain line that documented the problem. His 1919 water circular suggests he suspected *cheating* (water, theft) rather than a glass defect. He probably attributed the gap to badness in the milk or the men, not to an apparatus flaw. He knew something was wrong; he may not have known what exactly.

*Sources:* r05 (Teague on signed sheets), r08 (Vose's address), r06 (Strawn's 1924 observation), r22 (Falke's summary), r23 (Kyle on how Vose read the ledgers).

## Section F: Theory (6 points)

### F1. Causal Theory and Competing Explanations

**Unified Causal Theory:**

The Ostrey Hollow station's milk tests were systematically low from 1898 through June 1923 because the milk-test pipettes purchased from Ammon & Sons, Tarnet, in April 1897 were manufactured short of specification. The pipettes were blown to deliver 17.6 cubic centimeters but actually delivered 16.72 c.c., representing a shortfall of one part in twenty (5%).

A butterfat test depends on delivering a precise measured charge of milk into a test bottle whose graduated neck is marked to read percentage for that charge and no other. A short charge produces a short fat column, which reads as a lower percentage when measured against the fixed markings. The error is proportional, not additive: rich milk (high butterfat) lost more absolutely than poor milk, but the ratio between herds remained consistent. This is why the station ranked herds correctly internally but wrong against the outside world.

The defect began when the station's own glass entered use May 1, 1898 (before then, borrowed glass was used, which tested correctly). It continued year after year because the defect was uniform and invisible from within the station: an instrument that is itself the standard cannot detect its own error. No external check was built into the system. The bookkeeper's records showed a persistent 5% "station gain" (more fat arriving than credited), but this was filed away under a name—"station gain, five and a quarter percent, as usual"—and thus explained away. The error ended when state inspector Merle Strawn condemned the glass on June 12, 1923, and new glass was ordered and in use by July 1923.

From 1923 onward, the station's tests averaged 3.80%, the true value. Nothing at the station or on the farms had to change because the problem was never in the milk, the men, or the management. It was in one piece of glass that cost 35 cents.

Over the twenty-five full delivery years from 1898 to 1922, the defect cost the patrons 76,000 pounds of butterfat, worth $19,000 at the average price of 25 cents per pound. They were awarded $3,800 without admission of cause.

---

**Three Competing Explanations and Evidence Against Each:**

1. **Water in Cans (Selby Vose's theory):**
   - Vose advanced this explanation in his 1919 circular and reiterated it to the arbitration board in 1925.
   - **Evidence defeating it:** Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, visited the station regularly from 1908 onward and tested every day's milk with a lactometer for added water. His circuit notebooks document "Lactometer, no added water" at every single visit. Water-diluted milk cannot escape lactometer detection. If water had been systematically added over 25 years, Strawn would have found evidence within a few years of his visits beginning. His repeated "no added water" findings across 20+ years of inspections definitively refute the water theory.

2. **Cream Theft (Wilbur Teague's theory):**
   - Teague implied to the arbitration board that the tester might be removing cream and writing low tests to conceal it.
   - **Evidence defeating it:** The 1911 experiment. When the measure broke and a borrowed one was used for nine weeks, tests rose from 3.61 to 3.80 while the same tester (Ansel) worked at his ordinary pace, with no new supervision or oversight. If cream theft were occurring, it would continue; the tester would not cease stealing when a different glass was installed. Yet tests rose immediately. Additionally, Hobart Sill (the buttermaker receiving the station's cream) testified that cream always arrived "heavy"—in larger quantity than the station's records claimed—year after year. If cream were being stolen, the cream arriving at Larrow Green would be less than credited, not more.

3. **Poor Feed from Wet Meadow (The 1908 Committee's theory):**
   - The investigating committee of 1908 concluded that the wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream, and that remedy lay with land improvement, not the station.
   - **Evidence defeating it:** The State Agricultural College's Bulletin No. 214 (1911) conducted a controlled feeding trial on bog hay and published findings that bog hay depressed butterfat by no more than 0.05% in winter and nothing in summer. But the Ostrey Hollow gap was 0.19%, flat year-round, present in both winter and summer, in wet years and dry. Five hundredths cannot account for nineteen hundredths. Additionally, four Hessel Bottom farmers hauled milk to the Alder Corners Cheese Company over Redlow Ridge (summer only, 1915–1930). Their milk there, from the same wet meadow, tested about 3.80—identical to what the borrowed measure showed in 1911. The hay did not thin the milk; the glass did.

**Did the board examine the condemned glassware?**

No. The glass was condemned June 12, 1923. The arbitration board sat March 17 to May 4, 1925. The crate remained in the loft at the station. Bertram Kyle (r23) carried four documents to the board: the test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book, and Strawn's circuit notebook for 1923. The notebook documented that Strawn had condemned the glass, but the board did not have the glass itself to measure or examine. Kyle: "I carried them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked."

---

**What did the tester believe?**

Ansel Keddie suspected the glass. In the summer of 1911, after the measure broke and tests rose, he came home to his wife Orra and said, "It was something in the glass." He never dared write this suspicion in his carefully maintained record. Instead, he entered the date ("May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure") and the return date ("July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were.") and next to the return date he wrote a question mark: "? — A.K." 

He carried this suspicion for fourteen years (1911 to 1925 and beyond). r18 (his son Duncan): "He came home that summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book... What he wrote in the book was a question mark. He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove, and I have thought since that this was the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had."

Ansel died in 1934. The truth was confirmed in 1958, twenty-four years after his death. He never knew he was right.

## Section G: Summary (4 points)

### G1. Complete History in 150 Words

In 1889, farmers in Larrow Valley formed a cooperative creamery, adopting a rule: pay each patron by butterfat content, tested at his delivery station. The system was just but vulnerable—everything hinged on accurate measurements, with no appeal. At Ostrey Hollow, milk consistently tested one-fifth of a percent lower than elsewhere. For twenty-five years, three theories circulated: farmers were adding water, the tester was stealing cream, or the wet meadow produced inferior milk. In 1923, a state inspector condemned the station's pipettes, discovering they delivered 5% less than their marked capacity. This tiny flaw had produced all twenty-five years of low tests. The tester had been blameless. In 1958, his granddaughter found the crate in a loft and measured the glass, clearing his name only after his death. Eight separate records—books, diaries, bulletins, memories—were required to reconstruct what one inspection should have revealed.
