# Batch 1 Answers

## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1: The Founding and Article VII (1889–1897)

**Who:** Gideon Vose, founder of Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, and the farmers of Larrow Valley.

**What happened:** Farmers in Larrow Valley organized a cooperative creamery association in 1889, with Gideon Vose as founder and first president. In 1891, Vose drafted by-laws by hand "the way other men build stone wall — slowly, and to last." The critical provision was Article VII: "Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." This was written "for convenience and it was convenient for thirty years." The Association built the main creamery at Larrow Green in 1891. In 1895–1896, it constructed a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley, to serve the farms at Hessel Bottom and surrounding areas.

**When:** The Association was organized in 1889; by-laws were written in 1891; the Ostrey Hollow station opened on May 1, 1897.

**Quantities that matter:** The first check from the Association delivered unprecedented monthly cash payments to farmers' kitchens—the first reliable cash income most had seen. The Ostrey Hollow station received approximately 900,000 pounds of milk in its first season (May–December 1897). The station was equipped with a complete testing outfit ordered April 14, 1897, from Ammon & Sons glassware supplier at Tarnet: six dozen milk test pipettes marked 17.6 cc, twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, and a four-bottle hand tester—total cost $77.93 freight included.

**Documents:** Invoice dated April 14, 1897, from Ammon & Sons (quoted by Junia Ammon and Bertram Kyle); Gideon Vose's by-laws written in his own hand; Orra Keddie's diary entry for May 1, 1897 ("A. says the station's own glass came up"); station opening records; Hobart Sill's account of lending his own glassware to open the station when the ordered outfit had not yet arrived.

### Story 2: Complaints and the 1908 Investigation (1902–1908)

**Who:** Hessel Bottom farmers, especially Emmet Brant (the largest milk patron); the investigating committee led by Nyle Grigg; Ansel Keddie (tester at Ostrey Hollow).

**What happened:** From the early date, milk tested at Ostrey Hollow station ran consistently lower than milk from other parts of the valley. Farmers began complaining in 1902 that their milk tested lower at Ostrey Hollow than it did when weighed at other places. The deficiency was steady: Ostrey Hollow's average was 3.61%, while Larrow Green creamery's average over the same years was 3.78%—a gap of 0.17 points. In January 1908, an investigating committee appointed by the Association, chaired by farmer Nyle Grigg, was sent to examine the Hessel Bottom herds. They sampled "every month of that year, in barn and on pasture, out of night's milk and morning's" without advance notice. They weighed the milk, watched tests made and watched them made again. The committee found no dishonesty of any kind, no problem with scales or milk, but concluded that "wet hay of the bottom thins cream." Their report was accepted, the question was closed for eleven years, and the matter was officially attributed to the soils and drainage of Hessel Bottom, not to any tester or patron misconduct.

**When:** First complaint recorded 1902; committee investigation January–March 1908; report accepted, question closed 1908.

**Quantities that matter:** Gap of 0.17–0.21 points between Ostrey Hollow tests and other valley stations, running at the same proportion every season, "nineteen hundredths, every month every season, wet years dry: nineteen hundredths lower at station than anywhere else" (per Hazel Brant).

**Documents:** The 1908 committee report (filed and later read into the board record at the 1925 arbitration); Emmet Brant's weigh book begun January 1, 1897 (showing his own weighings before delivering to the station); Ansel Keddie's test books; oral accounts of the committee's findings by Nyle Grigg and Orin Falke; Dr. Roland Kepp's veterinary assessment (1926) confirming bog hay depresses butterfat by about 0.20 points; State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 214 (feeding trial on bog hay, published 1911, showing bog hay depresses butterfat by "not more than five hundredths" in winter, nothing measurable in summer).

### Story 3: The 1911 Diagnostic Incident (May–July 1911)

**Who:** Ansel Keddie (tester); Duncan Keddie (his son, age seventeen, away at school but home briefly); state inspector Merle Strawn; patrons of Ostrey Hollow station.

**What happened:** On May 8, 1911, Ansel Keddie's own measuring pipette broke at the station. For nine weeks (approximately June 10 – July 10, 1911), while waiting for a replacement, he borrowed a measure from elsewhere and used it to perform tests. During those nine weeks, the station's recorded butterfat tests rose markedly higher than their long-term average. On the day the station's own measure was returned and back in use, the tests fell back to their former, lower level. The herds, pasture, season, acid, bottles, tester, and habits were identical before and after; only one variable changed—the glassware. Duncan Keddie, then seventeen and home from his teaching position at Ashlin, remembered his father coming home that summer saying "it was something in the glass," and his father mentioned it twice but "never dared put it in the book." Ansel recorded the incident in his test book with a question mark against it, and it passed unnoted by supervisors. The nine-week interval was "a thing that ran itself under conditions designed trial could not have bettered; lay unread forty-seven years."

**When:** May 8, 1911 (pipette broke); June 10 – July 10, 1911 (approximately, nine weeks with borrowed measure); July 10, 1911 (station's own glass returned to use).

**Quantities that matter:** Station's average test rose from 3.61% (the long-term level) to 3.80% during the nine weeks and returned to 3.61% when own glass came back. Summer 1911: herds at best; stock off wet ground and on aftermath; every herd in county at best flush of season—yet figures rose despite seasonal conditions that should have favored butterfat. The difference: 3.80 – 3.61 = 0.19 points higher with the borrowed glass.

**Documents:** Ansel Keddie's own test book, entry for May 8, 1911, and subsequent entries through July 1911 (recorded contemporaneously in his hand); Orra Keddie's diary entry for May 8, 1911 ("A. home cross about a broken measure"); Duncan Keddie's 1968 genealogical notes recalling the kitchen conversation and his father's remark about "something in the glass"; Merle Strawn's circuit notebook entries (annual visits 1912–1925 record consistent conditions and tests); Leland Vaught's analysis in Bulletin No. 471 (1958), which identified the 1911 incident as the most powerful evidence disproving every explanation that blamed herds, ground, or men.

## Section B — Relationships

### B1. Ivy Keddie to Ansel Keddie

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. The chain of descent: Ansel married Orra Keddie in 1889; they had Duncan Keddie (born 1894); Duncan had Ivy Keddie (born 1931). The notes explicitly state "Ivy Keddie — Granddaughter, historian... Ansel's granddaughter; Duncan Keddie's daughter." This relationship is central because Ivy, forty-seven years after her grandfather's dismissal, retrieved the defective glassware from the station loft and proved scientifically what he could not prove in 1925: that he was innocent and that the pipettes alone caused the low tests.

### B2. Ivy Keddie to Hazel Brant

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. The chain: Ansel and Orra had Duncan; Orra's brother Emmet had Roy Brant; Roy had Hazel. Duncan and Roy were first cousins (through their mothers), so their daughters Ivy and Hazel are second cousins. The notes state explicitly: "Hazel Brant — Ivy is Duncan's girl; Hazel is Roy's; second cousins, not cousins once removed." The intervening generations are: Duncan (schoolteacher, Ansel's son, born 1894) and Roy (farmer, Emmet's son, born before 1894, died 1962). Roy kept his father Emmet's weigh book and loaned it to Ivy in 1958 for her research, then passed it to Hazel, who gave it to the Historical Rooms in 1963 after Roy's death.

### B3. Selby Vose's father and why it matters

Selby Vose was the son of Gideon Vose, founder and first president (1889–1911) of Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. The notes initially refer to "Nephew Selby Vose," but Duncan Keddie's 1970 addendum corrects this: "Selby Vose was Gideon Vose's son, not nephew." This matters because Gideon wrote Article VII, the by-law that made butterfat-test-at-the-station the governing rule for all payments—the rule that would have made the Ostrey Hollow scandal catastrophic if true. When Selby became manager in 1913, he inherited a system his father had designed and the authority to defend it. In 1919, Selby sent the circular warning patrons against adding water, putting his name and managerial authority on the accusation. At the 1925 board hearing, he defended his theory that water was being added, even though state inspector Merle Strawn had never found any water in 1919 or after. Selby's family connection to the system's founding gave weight to his voice and also tied the family's standing to the scandal's resolution.

### B4. Relationship between Vose and Keddie families

The Vose and Keddie families were distant cousins through the women. The notes state: "Orra Keddie — First cousin to old Mrs. Vose." Orra married Ansel Keddie in 1889. Gideon Vose (founder) was married and had a wife (identified in context as "old Mrs. Vose"). Orra was first cousin to Gideon's wife. Additionally, the notes mention: "The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort; it is a small valley." The relationship was not close enough to prevent conflict—Ansel carried the warning letters about water in 1919 despite them being sent under Selby Vose's name, and the rift over the scandal separated Ansel from Orra's brother Emmet for fourteen years. But the kinship meant they shared valley networks and that Ansel's dismissal affected multiple family branches' standing.

## Section C — Math

### C1. Glassware Error Analysis

**(a) Proportion by which Ostrey Hollow tests differed from truth, derived from glassware measurement:**

The pipettes were marked to deliver 17.6 cc. When measured with a calibrated burette, nine surviving pipettes delivered a mean of 16.27 cc. 

Ratio = delivered / marked = 16.27 / 17.6 = 0.9244 (or 92.44% of marked capacity)

**The tests were recorded at 92.44% of true value.** 

To find the correction factor: 1 / 0.9244 = 1.0818, meaning the true tests were 8.18% higher than recorded.

Alternatively: (17.6 – 16.27) / 17.6 = 1.33 / 17.6 = 0.0756 or **7.56% short**.

**(b) True average test and shortfall in points:**

Recorded station average over affected years: **3.61%**

Corrected true average: 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = **3.905%**

Shortfall in recorded tests: 3.905 – 3.61 = **0.295 points** (or 29.5 hundredths)

**(c) Total butterfat not credited over full affected period:**

Milk received at Ostrey Hollow station over 25 full delivery years affected: 40,000,000 lbs

Ratio correction: 40,000,000 lbs × (1 – 0.9244) = 40,000,000 × 0.0756 = **3,040,000 lbs delivered and not credited**

Alternatively, expressed as butterfat lost: recorded 3.61% vs. true 3.905% = 0.295 points difference
40,000,000 lbs × 0.00295 = **118,000 lbs butterfat not credited**

(The notes cite both figures; the 3,040,000 represents total milk shortfall; 118,000 represents butterfat shortfall. The butterfat figure is used for the money calculation below.)

**(d) Money value and fraction received:**

Butterfat price throughout period: 27 cents per pound (per Hobart Sill and other sources)

Total value owed: 118,000 lbs × $0.27 = **$31,860**

Board award to patrons: $3,800 (May 4, 1925)

Fraction received: $3,800 / $31,860 = **0.119 or approximately 11.9% (roughly 1/8)**

### C2. Largest Patron's 1910 Year (Emmet Brant)

**(a) Butterfat credited to him:**

Milk delivered by Emmet Brant in 1910: 200,000 lbs (exact, "even," per Hazel Brant: "Remembers 1910 leaf: two hundred thousand pounds even, paid on test of 3.42")

Test recorded at station: 3.42%

Butterfat credited: 200,000 lbs × 0.0342 = **6,840 lbs butterfat credited**

**(b) Butterfat owed but not credited:**

The recorded test (3.42%) was 92.44% of the true test due to defective pipettes.

True butterfat percentage: 3.42% ÷ 0.9244 = 3.700%

Butterfat actually owed: 200,000 lbs × 0.03700 = 7,400 lbs

Shortfall: 7,400 – 6,840 = **560 lbs not credited**

**(c) Money value of shortfall in 1910:**

Price of butterfat: 27 cents per pound

Value of shortfall: 560 lbs × $0.27 = **$151.20**

## Conflict Resolution Notes

- **Discrepancy on Orra's birth year:** Sources say both 1866 and 1868. I used 1868 per the main cast list, as this is the most formal source.
- **Selby Vose as nephew vs. son:** Duncan Keddie's explicit 1970 correction overrides earlier language ("nephew"). Son is correct.
- **Hazel's recollection of 1897 vs. 1900:** Hazel says "grandfather Emmet began it 1 January 1900 (date precision: 'nineteen hundred')" but other independent sources (Roy's postscript, Ivy's research) confirm Emmet's weigh book began January 1, 1897. Hazel's recollection of the year is unreliable; 1897 is correct.
- **Ivy Keddie's title:** One source calls her "great-niece" of Ansel; this is corrected by Orin Falke's footnote to "granddaughter," which matches Duncan's genealogy.
- **Butterfat loss figures:** The 118,000 lbs of butterfat shortfall and the $3,800 award are both documented in multiple independent sources (Vaught's calculation, Frick's board decision, Kyle's ledger entry).
