# Retention Notes: Segment 6

## Document Overview: Core Case + Supporting Retellings

The milk-testing dispute case (1889–1925) from Segment 1 continues across multiple retellings:
- Ivy Keddie's 1959 historical society talk (scientific forensics proving innocence)
- Wilbur Teague's 1948 memoir chapter (bookkeeper perspective on station-gain accounting)
- **Merle Strawn's 1936 circuit notebooks (state dairy inspector, independent corroboration)**

These retellings provide investigative conclusion and independent verification that exonerated Ansel Keddie decades after his death.

## Cast of Characters and Relationships
*(Carried from Segment 2 with additions)*

**Ansel Keddie** (Tester, Ostrey Hollow station, 1896–1924)
- Ran the skimming station 7 miles up valley from Larrow Green
- Kept meticulous test books in his own hand, handwriting "like print," no blots across 29 seasons
- Married to Orra Keddie
- Denies taking cream: "I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself" (March 1925 statement)
- Put out (fired) April 30, 1925, after arbitration award
- Died in the 1930s, still under stigma from the 1919 letter
- His question mark in the test book about the 1911 anomaly stood unanswered for 14 years
- EXONERATED in 1958 (35 years after dismissal, 24 years after death) by scientific analysis

**Ivy Keddie** (Granddaughter, historian)
- Ansel's granddaughter; Duncan Keddie was her father (teacher at Ashlin)
- In 1959, presented findings to Ordell County Historical Society at Ashlin
- Conducted scientific forensic analysis of condemned pipettes from 1923
- Used calibrated burette to measure nine unbroken pipettes from station crate
- Measured pipettes delivering 16.72 cc instead of 17.6 cc (95% of correct volume)
- Published findings in Bulletin No. 471 with Professor Vaught

**Wilbur Teague** (Bookkeeper, 1905–1945)
- Kept the Association's books from 1905 to 1945 (40 years)
- Invented the "station-gain" account in 1905 to track difference between credited vs. received butterfat
- Station-gain line ran at 5.25 percent of actual cream, year after year
- Noted in books: "Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account."
- Warned Selby Vose about the account in 1916; was told to "let it alone"
- Testified at 1925 board of arbitration hearing
- Wrote memoir "The Books of the Association" in 1948, three years after retiring
- Emphasized: "One man weighed the milk, one man drew the sample, one man made the test, one man wrote the figure that the pay slip was made from, and one man ran the separator. There was no second man in that building from the spring to the fall"
- Noted Selby Vose signed the annual sheets every year without comment

**Orra Keddie** (Ansel's wife)
- First cousin of old Mrs. Vose
- Sister of Emmet Brant (largest milk patron)
- After 1919 letter, rift with her brother Emmet: "they did not speak. Not one word"

**Merle Strawn** (State dairy inspector, retired)
- Took the Ordell circuit in 1907, laid it down in 1936 (29 years)
- Circuit covered fifty-odd plants in four counties (creameries, cheese factories, skimming stations)
- State expected each plant seen at least once per year, doubtful ones oftener
- Traveled by train, livery, or on foot (spring roads impassable)
- Conducted four standard checks in order each visit: scales (sealed weights), lactometer (for added water), boiler/water supply/cold room, and tester observation
- Kept circuit notebooks 1907–1936 (twenty-eight books, one per year, oilcloth covers, pocket size)
- Pocket books now in state dairy office (copied spring 1936)
- His burette (calibrated measure from 1921) used to check glass delivery; state stopped program in 1926
- Documented visits: 1907 first round, 1909 fall, June 1911, 1912–1925 annually, 1926 onward, 1928, 1931, 1934, 1936 last round

**Merle Strawn's Circuit Notes for Ostrey Hollow (1907–1936)**

*1907* — First round. Scales true. Cold room fair. Tester keeps book in own hand; hand better than most. Watched two tests. Works slow and clean; reads own figure.

*1909* — Fall visit. Scales true. Boiler in order, safety tried. Station takes from wet bottom farms and three hill places. Milk sweet; cans clean; one man cautioned about rusted seam.

*1911, June* — On way over to other county. Scales true. Watched test through. Nothing to note.

*1912* — Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. Cream shipped down valley same day it separated. Creek flooded; went round by ridge; lost afternoon.

*1913* — Larrow Green visit (not Ostrey Hollow).

*1915* — Scales true. Wash room floor holding water at drain; ordered graded.

*1916* — Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. Tester complains patrons blame him for figures; told it's the trade.

*1917* — Other plants; Larrow Green.

*1919* — Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. Association manager sent circular about water in cans. States: "I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so."

*1920, spring and fall* — Both times: Scales true, Lactometer no added water. Cold room short of ice in fall; said so; corrected before leaving county.

*1921* — State began issuing burettes to field men this year (graduated, wooden case). Mine came spring with printed directions. Most plants never had glass checked.

*1922* — Scales true. Lactometer no added water. Boiler in order. Did not run glass against burette this year (day short, road home bad); noted it.

**1923, June 12** — Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer no added water, as always here. **RAN MILK PIPETTES AGAINST BURETTE. THESE MEASURES WILL NOT DELIVER THEIR MARK. CONDEMNED THE LOT, THE WHOLE CRATE OF THEM, AND TOLD THE MANAGER TO ORDER NEW. GLASS IS GLASS AND I DID NOT WEIGH WHAT IT HAS COST THEM.**

*1923, October* — Ostrey Hollow. New glass in use since July. Ran new pipettes against burette. They deliver their mark. Scales true.

*1924* — Scales true, no added water, tests improved. Book gives year at 3.80.

*1925, fall* — Scales true, no added water, tests improved. New man at tester's bench. He is quick; quick not the thing I look for.

*1926* — Word from state office burette program discontinued. Instruments kept but no more issued; mine in case in grip, used twice since.

*1928* — Scales true, lactometer no added water. Station quieter than it was. Loft over intake room full of odds and ends; ordered cleared; told it's been full since before war.

*1931* — Larrow Green and Ostrey Hollow in same week. Both in order. Bad ice year; every plant short.

*1934* — Scales true, lactometer no added water. Separator bearing run hot, shut down two days, repaired. Tester's book kept up, though not in hand it used to be.

**1936, March** — Last round. Ostrey Hollow, Larrow Green, two factories over county line. Scales true at all four. Nothing to note anywhere; nothing left to say.

**Strawn's Closing Note:** Books themselves (twenty-eight, one to year) go to state dairy office with copy. "There is nothing in them but what a field man puts in a field book: what I weighed, what I tested, what I told the plant to mend, and whether it was mended when I came back. They are dull reading and they were meant to be. I have been asked once or twice, by men who ought to know better, to say in a general way how a certain plant stood in my estimation over the years, and I have always answered that I do not keep an estimation, I keep a book, and the book is open to anybody the state sends for it. I never had a theory about a plant and I do not recommend having one. A theory is a thing a man carries out to the platform with him and it gets in the way of the weights."

## The Forensic Discovery (Retelling 04 — Ivy Keddie, 1959)

### The Crate and Pipettes
- Wooden crate stenciled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET"
- Carried to station loft in June 1923 when measures were condemned
- Found by Ivy in February 1958 under tarpaulin
- Contained the station's condemned milk-test pipettes
- Original 1897 invoice: six dozen pipettes, bottles, acid measure, hand tester, freight = $77.93
- Nine unbroken pipettes remained in crate

### The Measurement Analysis
**Date: March 9, 1959**
**Method: Calibrated burette at Farland college**
**Result sheet (Ivy's words):**
> Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for.

### The Mathematics

**Key Finding: Pipettes were short by 5 percent**
- Delivered: 16.72 cc
- Should deliver: 17.6 cc
- Difference: 0.88 cc = 95 hundredths of mark
- Every test read 95% of truth

**The grandfather's summary leaf (1922):**
> Average test of all patrons: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80.

**Calculation:**
- Recorded average: 3.61 percent
- True average: 3.80 percent (3.61 ÷ 0.95)
- Gap: 19 hundredths (5 percent of 3.80)

**Over 25 years (1898–1922):**
- Station took in: 40 million pounds of milk
- Average per year: 1.6 million pounds
- Credited to patrons at 3.61%: 1,444,000 pounds of butterfat
- Actually delivered at 3.80%: 1,520,000 pounds of butterfat
- Unpaid fat: 76,000 pounds
- At 25 cents per pound: worth $19,000
- Board award (1925): $3,800 (one-fifth of actual loss)

**Error Pattern:**
- High-butterfat milk lost proportionally more
- Herd at 4.56% lost 0.24 point (exactly 5% of 4.80)
- Herd at 3.42% lost 0.18 point (exactly 5% of 3.60)
- The error fell hardest on the best milk

### The 1911 Experiment (Already noted in Segment 1, confirmed by Ivy)
- May 8, 1911: Station's pipette broke; borrowed measure used
- Immediately after: tests jumped to 3.80 percent
- July 10, 1911: Station's own measure arrived; tests dropped to 3.61 same day
- Same patrons, same herds, same pasture, same season, same acid, same bottles, same man
- Only object changed: the measure
- This proves the measure caused the discrepancy, not cream-taking

### Cross-Check with Alder Corners Factory
Over ridge, Alder Corners factory tested milk from Hessel Bottom men:
- Same men, same cows, same wet ground
- 14 overlapping seasons
- Milk at Alder Corners tested 19 hundredths HIGHER than at Ostrey Hollow
- Confirms the station's glass, not the milk or land, caused the gap

### The Wet-Hay Theory (Refuted)
Argument: wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream
- State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 214 tested this
- Result: bog hay depressed butterfat by at most 5 hundredths in winter, nothing measurable in summer
- Gap was 19 hundredths year-round, July and February alike
- Verdict: four times too small and wrong shape

### Bulletin No. 471 Conclusion
Published last October (1958), co-authored by Ivy Keddie and Professor Vaught:
> No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain.

**Timeline of vindication:**
- June 12, 1923: Measures condemned by state inspector
- April 30, 1925: Ansel dismissed
- 1934: Ansel died, still under stigma
- March 9, 1958: Pipettes measured (Ivy's analysis)
- October 1958: Bulletin No. 471 published
- 35 years after dismissal; 24 years after death; 33 years after dismissal letter

## Wilbur Teague's Bookkeeper Testimony (Retelling 05, 1948 memoir)

### The Station-Gain Account (Invented 1905)
Teague created the "station-gain" line to track accounting reality:
- One side: butterfat credited to patrons on station's test (what the station books said it bought)
- Other side: butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in cream (what they actually had)
- The second was always larger by 5.25 percent

**1898 entry (example):**
- Credited to patrons: 57,760 lb
- Received at Larrow Green: 60,800 lb
- Station gain: 3,040 lb (five and a quarter percent)
- Entry phrase: "as usual" (which stopped anyone looking)

**Why invented:** "A difference that will not be entered somewhere will come back and bite you at audit"

### Teague's Theory on Arrangement
Testimony to board of arbitration (and repeated in 1948):
> Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons. One man weighed the milk, one man drew the sample, one man made the test, one man wrote the figure that the pay slip was made from, and one man ran the separator. There was no second man in that building from the spring to the fall. I do not say what any particular person did on any particular day, because I was not there and I do not know. I say that a business which is arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken from it, and that no set of books ever made can prevent it. The remedy is two men or a lock, and the Association had neither.

### Teague's 1916 Warning
- Put the discrepancy to Selby Vose in 1916
- Was told to "let it alone"
- Complied; was not the Association's conscience
- A bookkeeper who takes business up twice after being told once "has generally begun to look for another employer"

### Official Data (from Teague's ledgers)
- 44 million pounds of milk through the station over full period
- Average test of Ostrey Hollow patrons: 3.16 percent
- Larrow Green average: higher
- Ostrey Hollow men "sore" about it every season for 25 years
- One break in the pattern: spring 1911 (measure replacement) — recorded as "small charge for replacement and nothing else"

### Board of Arbitration (May 4, 1925)
Findings as recorded in Teague's ledger:
> The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account.

### Teague's Observation on the Paradox
The board found "no cause established" yet awarded $3,800.
- Effie Loomis noted: "I took it down as it was given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once"
- Teague signed the sheet every year; the manager's signature carried the implication that he had read it

### New from Segment 3 (Retelling 06 — Merle Strawn)
11. **Strawn explicitly documented the condemnation in June 1923**, not just after-the-fact recall: "RAN MILK PIPETTES AGAINST BURETTE. THESE MEASURES WILL NOT DELIVER THEIR MARK. CONDEMNED THE LOT, THE WHOLE CRATE OF THEM."
12. **Strawn's 1919 entry explicitly denies water in milk**, contemporaneously: "I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so."
13. **Strawn never ran glass against burette in 1922**, admitting it: "did not run their glass against the burette this year, the day being short and the road home being what it was, and I have put that down because it is what happened" — this gap means no verification of glass in 1922.
14. **Tests improved immediately after July 1923 new glass installed** (Strawn's October entry: "New glass in use since July. Ran new pipettes against burette. They deliver their mark.") and Strawn's 1924 entry: "tests improved. Their book gives the year at 3.80."
15. **Strawn's books were available in Farland college library since 1947**, yet were not consulted before 1925 board hearing.
16. **Strawn's 1936 closing note is a deliberate rejection of inference**: "I do not keep an estimation, I keep a book... I never had a theory about a plant and I do not recommend having one. A theory is a thing a man carries out to the platform with him and it gets in the way of the weights." This underscores that while Strawn documented facts, he pointedly refused to speculate—yet his notebook facts, read carefully, unmistakably point to the measure.
17. **Strawn's methodology was standardized** (four checks in fixed order so he wouldn't forget which he'd done), and he always noted exceptions (1922 no burette check, 1923 June condemnation note, October 1923 new glass verification).
18. **Strawn's phrase "glass is glass"** (in the June 1923 entry when refusing to assess cost impact) is a professional stance of indifference to the economic damage, staying in his lane as a factual recorder.

## Summary

The milk-testing scandal of 1896–1925 is now fully resolved by science and corroborated by independent official records. Three independent retellings (Ivy Keddie's forensic analysis, Wilbur Teague's bookkeeper testimony, and Merle Strawn's state inspector circuit notes) prove:

1. **Ansel Keddie was innocent.** The pipettes he used were defective, not his integrity compromised.
2. **The evidence was official and on record, yet overlooked.** Strawn's circuit notebooks, stored in Farland college library since 1947 and documenting the June 1923 condemnation, were never consulted by the 1925 arbitration board.
3. **The loss was real, but misdirected blame.** Patrons genuinely lost $19,000 over 25 years—not to theft but to measurement error. The board's $3,800 award addressed only a fifth of the actual loss.
4. **The rift was permanent despite vindication.** Ansel was dismissed April 30, 1925, died in 1934 still under stigma, and was not cleared until October 1958—24 years after his death. Emmet and Orra's silence lasted decades.
5. **The system's blind spot was fatal.** A single-person operation with no independent verification; a bookkeeper's regular 5.25% adjustment that became "furniture" in the ledger; official records (inspector's notebooks) never cross-checked with arbitration hearing; Selby Vose's tacit approval by signing sheets annually without comment; the very measures that exposed the error kept in a crate in the station loft rather than delivered to the hearing.
6. **The remedy Teague named—"two men or a lock"—was never implemented until after dismissal.**

The case exemplifies how statistical regularity can obscure causation, how isolation of responsibilities can enable both theft and deflect blame from structural flaws, how institutional silence can preserve injustice across generations, and how official documentation can exist yet remain unexamined when it contradicts institutional interests or assumptions.
