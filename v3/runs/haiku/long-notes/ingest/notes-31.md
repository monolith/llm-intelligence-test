# Retention Notes: Segment 31

## Complete Milk-Testing Scandal Case (1889–1925) with Full Archival Documentation

### Overview
The milk-testing scandal spanning 1889–1925 involves allegations of cream theft or water addition at Ostrey Hollow skimming station in Ordell County. The case was exonerated in 1958, 35 years after dismissal and 24 years after the death of accused tester Ansel Keddie. Scientific analysis proved defective glassware—milk pipettes that delivered only 95% of marked capacity—caused consistently low test results recorded from 1897 to June 1923.

### Cast of Characters and Family Relationships

**Ansel Keddie** (Tester, Ostrey Hollow station, 1896–1924; born 1861, died 1934)
- Ran skimming station 7 miles up valley from Larrow Green for 29 seasons
- Kept meticulous test books in his own hand, handwriting "like print," no blots
- Married to Orra Keddie (sister of Emmet Brant, largest milk patron)
- Denies taking cream: "I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself" (March 1925 statement)
- Fired April 30, 1925, while board was still sitting (four days before award)
- Exonerated in 1958 by scientific analysis
- Statement to board (17 March 1925): one-leaf document in his hand, not fully transcribed in notes
- In private corridor conversation at Ashlin with attorney Frick: said he always thought patrons were at fault but would not accuse neighbors in hearing room; 14+ year silence with brother-in-law after 1919
- Duncan describes him as "a careful man in small things to a degree that other people found trying"
- Summer 1911: came home saying "it was something in the glass," never dared put it in the book; Duncan was seventeen and remembered kitchen conversation, father said it twice (at supper and by door)
- Refused to write down what he could not prove: "the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had"
- Lived nine years after dismissal with imputation on him, among men he had weighed milk for since before their fathers died
- Died in 1934 (nine years after dismissal)
- Per Orra's diary: married 1889; walked 7 miles each way to station regularly; "up before four every day of his life" in November 1924; received dismissal letter on April 30, 1925, hung apron behind shed door and asked what there was for supper (ham); took up carpentry after dismissal and "whistles, which he never did when he was paid"; died February 1934 "without much pain at the end"

**Merle Strawn** (State dairy inspector, 1907–1936)
- Took Ordell circuit in 1907, laid it down in 1936 (29 years)
- Circuit covered fifty-odd plants in four counties (creameries, cheese factories, skimming stations)
- State expected each plant seen at least once per year, doubtful ones oftener

- Kept circuit notebooks 1907–1936 (28 books, one per year, oilcloth covers, pocket size)
- Pocket books now in state dairy office (copied spring 1936)
- His burette (calibrated measure from 1921) used to check glass delivery; state stopped program in 1926
- Documented visits: 1907 first round, 1909 fall, June 1911, 1912–1925 annually, 1926 onward, 1928, 1931, 1934, 1936 last round
- **1919 entry:** "I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so"
- **June 12, 1923:** "RAN MILK PIPETTES AGAINST BURETTE. THESE MEASURES WILL NOT DELIVER THEIR MARK. CONDEMNED THE LOT, THE WHOLE CRATE OF THEM, AND TOLD THE MANAGER TO ORDER NEW. GLASS IS GLASS AND I DID NOT WEIGH WHAT IT HAS COST THEM."
- **October 1923:** "New glass in use since July. Ran new pipettes against burette. They deliver their mark. Scales true."

- Did not run glass against burette in 1922; noted it: "did not run their glass against the burette this year, the day being short and the road home being what it was, and I have put that down because it is what happened"
- **1936 closing note:** "I do not keep an estimation, I keep a book... I never had a theory about a plant and I do not recommend having one. A theory is a thing a man carries out to the platform with him and it gets in the way of the weights."

- Duncan notes: "was kin to nobody in the valley whatever — not to us, not to the Brants, not to the Voses, not to anybody on the creek. That is worth writing down, because his notebooks turned out to matter, and it matters that the man who kept them had no interest in any of us."

**Leland Vaught** (Professor of dairy husbandry, State Agricultural College at Farland)
- Co-authored Bulletin No. 471 with Ivy Keddie, published October 1958
- Wrote introduction and method sections for extension agents and county workers
- Asked by station for fifty years why butterfat tests at one skimming station in Ordell County ran below those of creamery seven miles down same valley
- Answer: "a piece of glass" (the pipette)
- Notes simplicity of answer is itself part of history; nobody in valley lacked intelligence to find it; what they lacked was occasion to suspect one article in plant that nobody in trade is in habit of suspecting
- States milk pipette is one instrument in butterfat test which plant cannot check against anything else and instrument on which every figure in plant depends
- Explains: recorded station average over affected years is 3.61; corresponding true average is 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.905, shortfall of 0.295 point
- Calculates ratio of delivered to marked capacity: 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244
- Applied to 40,000,000 lb milk received at station in 25 full delivery years, yields 118,000 lb butterfat delivered and not credited
- Notes this figure not readily reconciled with station's own average of 3.80 in year following replacement of glassware
- States error is proportional not additive: does not remove fixed number of points from every test; removes fixed fraction; rich milk loses more absolutely; ratio between any two tests on same short glass undisturbed; station shows perfectly orderly, internally consistent figures; herds rank correctly against one another; only against outside world is it wrong
- States error is precise: leaking pipe or slipping scale gives scattered error attracting attention; pipette delivering 16.27 c.c. where blown 17.6 delivers 16.27 c.c. every time for quarter of century, in January and July, for careful operator and careless one; has appearance of excellent work
- **Key finding:** error is invisible from inside plant; every other element of test can be verified against standard (scales with test weights, tester's speed counted, acid checked for strength, bottle's graduation checked with mercury); but pipette not checked against anything because in ordinary practice of trade pipette IS the standard; it is what plant measures with; instrument that is itself measure cannot be caught by measuring with it; can be caught only by burette brought in from outside; burettes reached state's field men very late in this history
- Material examined: lot bought for Ostrey Hollow station in 1897 had five dozen pipettes; nine recovered unbroken and legible from condemned crate; these nine constitute material of bulletin; stems blown 17.6 c.c.; delivered against calibrated burette, five runs to each; mean delivery of nine is 16.27 c.c.
- Station's own glass went into use first of May 1898; remained until state inspector condemned lot June 1923; before earlier date and after later date, station tests made on other glass
- Method of measurement within reach of any county worker; can be repeated; pipette filled to mark with water at room temperature, tip wiped, contents delivered in ordinary working manner (same interval drainage, same touch tip to wall receiver) into burette of certified graduation; five deliveries from each pipette, readings averaged; occupies afternoon, requires no apparatus creamery doesn't own save burette
- Urges reader to weigh statement about 1911 interval above anything computed in bulletin
- Emphasizes 1911 interval as evidence: nine weeks summer 1911, station's own pipette broken, borrowed measure used; patrons same, herds same, pasture same, season same, acid same, bottles same, tester and habits same; ONE variable changed; station's average test rose from 3.61 to 3.80 and held there nine weeks, returned to 3.61 day station's own measure came back; recorded contemporaneously in operator's hand by man who didn't know what recording and set question mark against it
- States this disposes of every explanation looking to herds, ground, or men for reason (all three unaltered, figures were not)
- Spent working life urging students to prefer experiment to calculation; here is one that ran itself under conditions designed trial could not have bettered; lay unread forty-seven years
- **Conclusion:** "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

**Hobart Sill** (Buttermaker, Larrow Green creamery, retired 1934)
- Made Association's butter for forty-one years (1893–1934)
- Stood at churn from 1893; plank floor in that room then, cooled with creek ice out of pond
- Never sent out tub of butter he would not eat himself, more than office can say
- Cream wagon came down from Ostrey Hollow every day but Sunday, seven miles hill road
- In July came warm no matter how early they started
- Set off at platform, weighed, sampled, went into cold room; Sill took it from there
- **Key observation:** Cream ran heavy; every year it ran heavy; more fat in cans than papers said had been bought up there
- Office would send down sheet saying so many pounds fat bought at station; fat that came into cold room off that wagon was more than sheet said; always more; never less
- Weighed it himself most years; platform man weighed it and Sill weighed it over again when came into cold room; never took another man's figure for what going into churn
- Cans off, cans on scale, cans into ice, book ruled and signed before Sill put coat on
- Receiving book shows Hollow's cream coming in heavy on page after page, in his own writing, for as long as he had been in room
- Cream is not milk and will not stand still and be counted; thick thing, clings, separates; any man who tells you he can put figure on cream to pound and hold it is man who never had arm in can up to elbow
- So it ran heavy; so does everybody's; Sill never made anything of it; nobody ever asked him to
- Own milk at Larrow Green (own patrons, men on creek) ran 3.78 taking one year with another right through those years
- Ordinary milk, ordinary county, ordinary cows; nothing wonderful about 3.78 and nothing shameful
- **The Hollow milk:** really a 3.85 and no better; Sill will say to any man in valley; wet ground up there but good cows; never had word to say against cows
- If figures they were paid on came out under 3.85, then somebody up that hill road was helping cans along; Sill said so at time and says it now
- **Nine families all got honest in one summer?** No; there was water in those cans; Sill said so at time
- One summer along in twenties, figures came up and stayed up; nothing on earth changed but season; men do not suddenly start pouring better milk out of same cows in same barns
- Something got stopped; something got said to somebody; Sill never knew what and never asked; day buttermaker starts asking about other men's cans is day he stops making butter
- Men hollering up there a long while before that, away back; 1902 is first Sill remembers, man in Larrow shouting his milk tested under brother-in-law's down creek; same feed and same water; what did Association mean by it
- Manager put out paper to them about it—1919—circular warning Ostrey Hollow patrons about water in milk, plain as that; Sill thought well of him for it; only plain thing office ever did; of course they hated him for it up Hollow; of course it changed nothing because paper never emptied can
- Offices for you; papers; Association has paper for everything and butter for nothing
- Men come in collar and tell Sill churn running slow when couldn't tell which end of cow gets up first

**Dr. Roland Kepp** (Veterinary surgeon, Ashlin)
- Thirty years' practice
- Habit when called to herd doing badly to ask first what cattle standing on
- Found in thirty years answer to nine cases in ten is under animal's feet; tenth is in water she drinks
- Knows about soil, herbage, and mineral economy of cow
- Wet bottom is not rich meadow with too much water; distinct kind of ground with distinct chemistry
- Water is least part of it
- Where drainage wanting, year's growth does not rot down as on hill field; lies sour; soil is acid, poor in lime, poor in phosphates
- Sweet grasses will not hold; crowded out within few seasons by sedge, rush, coarse tussock grasses
- That herbage carries bulk and water; does not carry lime or phosphorus in any quantity worth name; cut late because ground cannot be gotten onto early; late cutting takes out what little was in it
- Cow in milk is manufactory working on narrow margin; obliged to find material of milk somewhere
- If ration short of minerals, does not simply give less; robs own frame to make good; goes on robbing until cannot
- See consequences in any wet-meadow herd in county
- Stock come out of winter feeding thin over loin and rough in coat
- Slow to come to condition in spring
- Ready subjects for every disorder of digestion in fall
- Oftener called to that class of trouble on wet land than any other
- Breed later
- Feet are worse
- Will milk, gentlemen; wet land generous of flow; no man on bog meadow ever complained pail was light
- What is in pail is another matter; fat is first thing to suffer because fat is dearest thing cow makes; first economy she practices when pressed
- Wet bottoms of county yield milk poorer in fat by fifth of point; every practical man knows it
- Set hill herds against bottom herds and will find that difference, farm for farm, wherever you look; difference does not depend on breed, man, or barn
- Depends on ground
- Hessel Bottom is completest example of wet meadow in county
- Six farms cut hay and pasture stock upon it
- Lies along creek
- Springs come out at foot of slope and run in it year round
- Part under water every spring; not fit to be walked on until well into May
- Hay is sedge hay; on those farms winter dependence and very nearly whole of it
- Patrons of skimming station at Ostrey Hollow complained for quarter of century milk tests below rest of valley
- Complaint perfectly just; never doubted it
- Figure of gap, as given and as satisfied himself, is twenty-one hundredths of point — 0.21 — between station and valley at large
- Committee of practical men went over ground in 1908; found herds sound, milk whole, scales honest; laid cause to hay of that bottom
- Matter has been before committees since and before boards; not settled; will not be settled by men shouting at one another
- Settled already, in literature, for anybody who will read
- State Agricultural College at Farland conducted feeding trial on bog hay; published Bulletin No. 214
- College fed bog hay under control; measured what it did; found butter fat test depressed by two tenths in winter feeding
- Two tenths from College that never heard of Hessel Bottom and no reason to flatter
- Three agree so nearly (two tenths from College, fifth of point he found in field, twenty-one hundredths station complained of) that does not know what more reasonable man requires
- **Objection: in summer of 1911** testing at that station broken off for six weeks; own apparatus failed; borrowed one used; figures stood higher during those six weeks than before or after
- Six weeks in flush of season; every herd in county at best; stock off wet ground and up on aftermath
- Astonished anybody should founded argument on six weeks of summer figures
- Could produce six weeks in any year that would prove anything you liked
- Remedy not mystery and not cheap
- Drain bottom; open old ditches and cut new; lime ground and lime it again
- Sow it down when will carry seeding
- Keep stock off in wet of spring (hoof does more harm in day than season will repair)
- Never feed that hay alone
- Feed with mixed ration and mineral supplement as College directs
- Fat was twenty-seven cents pound through those years; fifth of point taken off every hundredweight a farm ships year in and year out will pay for great deal of tile

**Retelling 21: Cleve Oram** (Cream hauler's son, born 1900, recorded 1977, age 77)
- Born in nineteen hundred and has not lived five miles from this chair since
- Father hauled the cream; that was his living
- Two bays and a flat wagon with a rack built onto it to keep the cans standing, and a canvas over them in July, and in the winter nothing over them at all because they would not freeze in the time it took
- Seven miles from Larrow Green up to the Hollow, and it climbs the whole seven
- Pitch about two miles below the hamlet, past where the creek comes in from the north, that took the horses at a walk with an empty wagon and took them at a crawl with a loaded one
- In March went up through mud to the hub and came down through it again, and the ruts froze at night in the shape wheels left them, so that the second week of March was worse than the first
- Mother used to say she could tell the month by how father swore coming in the door
- Began going up with father regular at about nine years old (nineteen and nine)
- Sat on the box on his left because the ditch was on the right and he would not have Cleve on that side
- **Station description:** Board building, two floors, a shingle roof that wanted work the whole time he knew it
- On the road side there was a covered platform about waist high, so a man could back his wagon to it and roll the cans off level instead of lifting them
- Inside that was the intake room, with the scale and the vat, and the separator going in the back of it, and a smell in there of warm milk and steam and machine oil
- Skim came back out the same platform for the farmers to take home to their hogs
- Cream went into their cans
- Over the intake room there was a loft; got at it by a ladder nailed to the wall by the door, not a stair, a ladder, and the trap at the top of it was a piece of plank with a rope on it

- **The critical crate:** There was a crate up in that loft with a tarpaulin over it, and Cleve has seen it a hundred times, and put his hand on it
- It was the station's old glass, put up there when the state man made them throw it out

- Five dozen of those measures went into that crate to begin with — that was the whole outfit the station was set up with
- Nine of them left in it at the end, because a thing in a loft for thirty-five years gets broken by everybody who goes up after wire
- Nobody ever gave it a thought; it was a crate under a tarp
- Cleve would not have been able to tell what was in it if he had not been up there after a hay fork with his own hands
- That crate is the whole thing, they tell him now
- It sat there
- **The 1925 hearing:** Hearing was in twenty-five and Cleve was twenty-five years old, which is how he keeps the date
- Started it down at Ashlin, in the Cooperative's office, because that is where the lawyer was and lawyers do not travel
- But there were three sittings of it altogether and two of them were held up at the station itself, in the weigh room — that is the intake room, the same room, with the scale in it
- Because the books and the scales and the machinery were all up there and it was easier to carry the men to the machinery than the machinery to the men
- Cleve stood outside on the platform the whole of one of those afternoons, in the rain, with about a dozen others

- **The crate over their heads:** And the crate was up the ladder over their heads the entire time; twenty feet, if that

**Retelling 22: Orin Falke** (County agent, 1959)
- Prepared by county agricultural agent at direction of board of supervisors of Ordell County
- At its meeting of the autumn quarter the board directed office to prepare, for its own use and for the county's permanent file, a plain summary of what is called locally the Ostrey Hollow question
- Matter recently been subject of newspaper notice, raised with office by three separate patrons within one month
- Orderly statement warranted
- The undersigned set down what is established, in the order in which it occurred, and indicate where record is thin
- Occasion of present interest: bulletin issued October 1958 by State Agricultural College at Farland, prepared jointly by Professor Leland Vaught and Miss Ivy Keddie, a graduate student, who is the great-niece of tester at Ostrey Hollow station [NOTE: later corrected to granddaughter, not great-niece]
- Bulletin temperate in tone and does not overstate its case
- Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association organized 1889 by farmers meeting at Larrow Green, built main creamery there 1891
- By-laws contain Article VII: every patron paid according to pounds of butterfat his milk found by test to contain, not according to weight of milk; test made at station where he delivers governs his pay
- Board should observe this article is hinge of whole matter
- Under payment by weight, nothing following would have cost any man a dollar
- Printed by-laws carry no date on face, only words "adopted at annual meeting"
- Minute book of Association's first years was lost in office removal
- Office unable to state year of adoption and does not attempt to
- Winter 1895 and 1896: Association built skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up valley from Larrow Green, to serve farms of that end, Hessel Bottom principally
- Station opened 4th of May, 1896
- Milk weighed in, sampled, tested for butterfat, separated; cream hauled down to Larrow Green and skim returned to patron
- Tester and separator man from opening was Ansel Keddie, who held post for twenty-nine seasons
- From early date milk of that station tested low
- Station's average over long period of complaint stood at three sixty-one, where Larrow Green creamery's own average over same years was three seventy-eight (ordinary figure for this county and adjoining)
- Deficiency, as bulletin now fixes it, was nineteen hundredths of a point, ran at same nineteen hundredths in every month of year, in flush of June and depth of February alike
- First complaint of record made 1902
- Renewed, in one form or another, in every decade thereafter
- In 1911 an incident occurred which bulletin treats as decisive
- On 8th of May station's own measuring glass broken, measure borrowed from elsewhere used in place for nine weeks, until 10th of July
- Through those nine weeks station's tests stood markedly higher
- On day station resumed own glass they fell back to former level
- Tester noted fact in his book and set question mark against it
- Herds, pasture, season, men and machinery were same throughout
- Three explanations were current in valley across whole period, all three argued before board of arbitration in 1925
- First: Ostrey Hollow patrons added water to their milk
- View of Association's manager, who circulated warning to that effect in 1919
- Cannot be sustained
- State dairy inspector for this circuit tested with lactometer at every visit from 1908 forward and never once found added water at that station
- Second explanation: tester himself removed cream and wrote low tests to conceal loss
- Urged at hearing by officer of Association
- Cannot be sustained either
- Nine weeks of 1911 dispose of it: same man made tests in that interval, under no different supervision, and figures rose
- Third explanation: hay and pasture of Hessel Bottom, which is wet ground, thin cream of cows fed upon them
- Committee of Association sampled Hessel Bottom herds in 1908 and reported to this effect
- Veterinarian of standing at Ashlin wrote to same purpose in 1926
- Office is not prepared to dismiss it
- Bog meadows of that bottom are known to every farmer in county for coarseness of their hay
- Men who reported in 1908 were practical men who walked ground and looked into mangers
- Whatever else at work at Ostrey Hollow, wet hay of that bottom must be allowed as real if partial factor
- 1908 committee was not simply wrong
- Patrons petitioned January 1925
- Board of arbitration sat at Ashlin from 17th of March to 4th of May under chairmanship of attorney of that place
- Heard patrons and officers
- Found no cause established
- Awarded Ostrey Hollow patrons three thousand eight hundred dollars, without admission
- Tester was dismissed 30th of April, 1925, four days before award
- Necessary to go back to 1923
- On 12th of June of that year state dairy inspector, in course of ordinary circuit visit, ran station's milk pipettes against calibrated burette and found they would not deliver their mark
- Condemned whole lot on spot and directed manager to order new
- New glass in use from following month
- From 1924 station's average test stood at three eighty-five and remained there
- No person connected one thing with other at time
- Inspector himself drew no inference whatever as to years behind it
- February 1958: Miss Keddie found condemned glass, still crated, in loft over station's intake room, where it had lain since day it was condemned
- On 9th of March she measured nine surviving pipettes against calibrated burette at Farland
- They delivered materially less than capacity blown upon their stems, in same proportion, one to another
- Bulletin's conclusion: short charge of milk into bottle whose neck ruled for full charge yields reading short in same proportion; this and nothing else is explanation; no milk was watered and no cream was stolen at Ostrey Hollow
- For board's purposes office recommends: report filed with county's dairy papers; surviving records left in custody of Historical Rooms at Ashlin, where they now are; no action lies for county, all parties in interest being dead and Association's account long since closed
- **Note added on receipt of Bulletin No. 214:**
- After foregoing had gone to type, office wrote to library at Farland for feeding trial upon bog hay which college conducted in 1911 and published as Bulletin No. 214
- That trial cited in 1958 bulletin without its figures
- It has come to hand
- Summary printed here entire: "Bog hay fed as whole of roughage depressed butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."
- Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July
- Falke withdraws concession made on page four
- Falke obliged to add second correction
- Informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is granddaughter and not great-niece
- Error on page two is his
- — O. Falke, county agricultural agent, Ordell County

- And after it, always, in bookkeeper's own words, five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to general account
- As usual
- He copied that line, or like of it, more times than can count
- Will tell Society plainly that it never once crossed his mind to wonder at it
- Does not believe it crossed anybody else's either
- It had a name
- That is thing about a name
- A figure with a name on it has been explained, and you rule under it and go on to next
- People suppose a clerk lives by calendar
- He does not
- He lives by what is on desk
- A year in that office was annual statement in winter, creamery inventory when ice went out, station accounts all summer, and meeting again
- That is how he holds whole of it and has never needed another way
- Station up at Ostrey Hollow was opened before his time
- For its first season or two it borrowed its testing glass from creamery here
- Then its own outfit came from supply house and went into use on first of May, 1898
- He has that from correspondence, which he handled later when it was all got out for hearing
- That first season, before ever there was a station account, milk was weighed at Hollow and carried on creamery's own books
- Sheets for it were never put with station papers
- People have asked him what Hollow took in that first year
- He has always said same: good eleven hundred thousand pounds, which is what he understood at time from men who were there
- It is not in any book he ever ruled
- For long run of years that patrons afterward complained of, figure was got out for arbitration and he got it out
- Forty million pounds of milk received at that station, which is sixteen hundred thousand pounds year, year in and year out, and it did not vary greatly
- And butterfat over that whole stretch averaged twenty-five cents pound, taking fat years with lean, which was another thing he was set to find and did find
- Mr. Selby Vose became manager in nineteen eleven
- He was son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized Association and was its first president
- The son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and he has heard nephew story twice in last year and it is nonsense
- Mr. Selby Vose sat behind that door for quarter of century and he sat outside it, and he liked him, and will say two things about him which do not sit together and are both true
- He was courteous to every man who came into that office, farmer or drummer or bank, and he never once let caller feel he was being hurried
- And he read totals and he never read lines above them
- He was not man who read
- He would take sheet, turn it, put thumb on bottom figure, and sign
- He watched him do it thousand times and thought nothing of it, because that is what managers were
- Mr. Wilbur Teague was bookkeeper and wrote those station lines every year of his service
- A dry man, very correct, ate same dinner out of same tin
- Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years
- That is not criticism
- It was his notion of position: figures were figures, and asking was for other people, and if other people did not ask it was not his affair
- In nineteen nineteen there was circular
- He typed it and he remember typing it, which he cannot say of much else
- It went up to Ostrey Hollow patrons and warned them about water in their milk, in civil form of words but with no doubt at all in it about what was meant
- Mr. Vose signed it, and went out over his name and no one else's
- He thought at time it was hard thing to send to men who were bringing their milk in every morning of their lives
- He typed it anyway
- Then in twenty-two state man came into office
- Strawn, dairy inspector on this circuit, heavy quiet man who wrote everything in little book with rubber band round it
- He had been up to Hollow and run their milk pipettes against his burette and condemned whole lot of them
- He came in to say so and to tell manager to order new
- Which they did, that same week, to supply house at Tarnet
- New glass came up within month
- There was no more said of it in office than there would have been of broken window
- He entered order and filed his word for it and that was end of matter as far as outer room was concerned
- Arbitration is piece Society will want
- Patrons had petitioned
- A board was formed
- Sittings were partly at Ashlin and partly at station
- What passed across his desk to that board was four things, and he can name them without looking, because he made list and checked them off twice
- Station's own test book, in Keddie's hand
- Receiving book
- Mr. Emmet Brant's private weigh book, which he lent for purpose and wanted back
- State inspector's notebook for year he condemned glass
- Four books
- He carried them in, and carried them out, and could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid second beside fourth and looked
- Award, when it came, was thirty-eight hundred dollars to Ostrey Hollow patrons, without admission of any cause
- That was in twenty-four
- It was charged to station account, which struck him at time as neat bookkeeping and strikes him now as something else, though he is not man to say what
- He stayed on some years after and then office was moved and he was not moved with it
- If Society wants moral from clerk it may have this one
- Nothing in that office was hidden
- Every figure he has named was written down in ink by honest man, in book that stood on open shelf, in room that anybody might walk into
- It was carried forward every year for quarter of century, and read out at annual meeting to fifty farmers with their hats on their knees
- It was not secret
- It was worse than secret
- It was in plain sight with a name on it

**Retelling 24: Orra Keddie — "A Farm Woman's Book, 1897–1930," diary extracts published 1964**

*Extracts from a diary kept at Ostrey Hollow, printed from three notebooks now in the family's hands. Preface and bracketed notes are the editor's.*

**Preface:** Orra Keddie was born in 1866 and married Ansel Keddie in 1889. He was tester and separator man at Ostrey Hollow skimming station of Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, and kept its books in his own hand for twenty-nine seasons. She began diary in spring of 1897 and closed it in 1930, and it was kept in three ruled notebooks bought at Ashlin. Pages covering her girlhood and family before 1889 were not preserved; nothing in her hand survives from before marriage. She writes A. for her husband throughout. Single later leaf, written in 1934 and laid loose inside back cover of third book, is printed at end. Entries have been chosen for what they show of household and season; nothing has been added and nothing altered but spelling of two place names.

**Diary Entries (Selected):**

- **April 4, 1897:** Ground still frozen a spade down. Set the eggs. A. gone before light as always and back after dark, and he has walked it both ways this week because the mare is lame.

- **May 1, 1897:** A. says the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green. He is pleased about it. A man is pleased about small things when he is let.

- **July 19, 1897:** Hot. Cut the home piece. Wild strawberries thick along the station road, and I picked a pail on the way down with A.'s dinner.

- **February 3, 1898:** Twenty below at the door this morning. A. broke the path to the station himself at four o'clock. He says the milk comes in with the frost still on the cans.

- **September 12, 1899:** Duncan five years old and reads anything set before him. His father says nothing but I saw him listening at the door.

- **March 1902:** Talk at the store that our milk up here runs poorer than theirs down the valley. A. came home and would not eat. I said it is not your cows and not your hands, and he said no, but it is my figures they will look at. Then he said no more about it.

- **June 6, 1904:** Currants. Rain every afternoon this week at four o'clock, you could set the clock by it.

- **January 1908:** Four men up the road today in Grigg's sleigh, come to look into the herds. They went to my brother Emmet's first, being the biggest, and he gave them their dinner. They were civil enough to me. They wanted to see the barns and not the station, which A. remarked on afterward and let alone.

- **March 1908:** The committee gone back down. Their word is that it is the wet hay of the bottom that thins the cream. My brother Emmet says the bottom fed his father and it will feed him. A. says nothing at all, which is his way, and is not always kindness to me.

- **August 30, 1910:** Threshing. Fourteen men to dinner and I had a girl up from the lower place to help. Two pies short and I have not lived it down.

- **May 8, 1911:** A. home cross about a broken measure. Rain all day, the low piece under water again.

- **October 1911:** Duncan gone to the school at Ashlin to teach, seventeen and looks fourteen. His father drove him down and came back without a word in him.

- **April 1914:** Set out six apple trees below the house. I shall not see them bear well but somebody will.

- **November 1917:** Snow before the corn was in. Everything late this year and everything short.

- **June 1919:** A circular came to the station for the patrons, over the manager's name, about water in the milk. A. would not put them in the mail nor leave them on the platform. He took them round himself, to eleven families, and stood at the door while each man read it, and came home at eleven at night with his hat still on.

- **June 1919, two days after:** My brother Emmet came up. He did not come in. He stood in the yard and said to A. that he had hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and A. knew it better than any man living, and then he asked him why he had carried the paper himself if he did not believe it. A. said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. He went home. He has not been in this house since, and he is my brother.

- **Christmas 1919:** Duncan up from Ashlin for two days. A good day and I will write it down as one.

- **February 1921:** Ice storm. The station road impassable two days and the milk stood.

- **July 1923:** Hay in good and not a drop on it. A. says they have new glass at the station and new pails, and the roof is still bad, which is the Association all over. He has been in this hollow the whole of his life and has never yet seen them mend a roof before it fell in.

- **November 1924:** Killing weather. A. up before four every day of his life for as long as I have known him, and he has never once said he was tired in my hearing, and he has never once been late.

- **January 1925:** The patrons have put in a paper against the station. My brother Emmet's name is on it. I do not blame him and I will not say so to A.

- **March 17, 1925:** A. went down to Ashlin this morning in his good coat, which he has had since Duncan was married. There is a board of men to hear the whole thing out. He was up at three and did not sleep before that either. I ironed the coat twice for want of anything I could do.

- **April 1925:** Two of the sittings held up here at the station itself, in the weigh room, with the men standing outside in the road. I did not go down. I could hear the wagons from the yard all that afternoon and that was enough of it for me.

- **April 30, 1925:** They have put him out. Twenty-nine seasons and a letter. He came up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm and hung it behind the shed door and asked what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it.

- **May 1925:** [The award of the board is not mentioned in the diary. — Ed.]

- **June 1925:** He has begun on the cow shed, which has wanted doing since his father's time. He works at it from light to dark and whistles, which he never did when he was paid.

- **1926:** [Several leaves torn out. The editor has not attempted to supply them. — Ed.]

- **October 1927:** A. mending stairs at the far end of the hollow, and the Loomis gate, and half the doors in this hollow, and taking what they offer. A carpenter at sixty-six. He is better at it than he ever was at the other and it pays a quarter of what the other did.

- **Spring 1929:** Word this morning that my brother Emmet is dead. I went down. Roy met me at the gate and was kind. I sat an hour in that kitchen where I was a girl and came home. Ten years and neither of them would go up the road first, and now it is settled the way those things get settled.

- **July 1929:** Hollyhocks the best I have had. A. sat out with me an evening and named the ones my mother had.

- **August 1930:** I am sixty-four and my hand is not what it was. I have kept this book thirty-three years and there is less in it every year, which is not because less happens.

- **[Final leaf, written in 1934]:** A. died yesterday at three o'clock in the morning. He did not wake at the last. There were more at the burying than I looked for, and men shook my hand who had not spoken to him in nine years, and every one of them said what a hand he was with a door. Not one of them said the other thing.
