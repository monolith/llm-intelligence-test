### Complete Cast and Relationships

**Ansel Keddie** (Tester, Ostrey Hollow station, 1896–1934): Ran skimming station 7 miles up valley from Larrow Green; kept meticulous test books in handwriting "like print"; married to Orra Keddie; denied taking cream (March 1925 statement); **fired April 30, 1925**; **died 1934** (9 years after dismissal, having lived under the imputation); **exonerated 1958** (33 years after dismissal, 24 years after death). Born 1861. Went to the station "from the year it opened" (1889 or start of Association). Summer 1911: suspected "something in the glass" but did not dare put it in book, wrote question mark instead; would not accuse it outright despite 14 years of suspicion. Man who would not put down what he could not prove; "most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had."

**Ivy Keddie** (Granddaughter, historian, State Agricultural College at Farland): Ansel's granddaughter; father Duncan Keddie (teacher at Ashlin); presented findings to Ordell County Historical Society 1959; **conducted scientific forensic analysis of pipettes**: measured nine pipettes delivering 16.72 cc instead of 17.6 cc (95% accuracy); published findings Bulletin No. 471 with Professor Vaught (October 1958); created measurement worksheet March 9, 1958; wrote to Rosalie Cudd requesting test figures 1958; second cousin to Hazel Brant; borrowed Emmet Brant's weigh book from Roy Brant 1958; found defective glassware crate in loft of Ostrey Hollow weigh room (under tarpaulin, chaff) February 1958; took glass back east and measured it (winter of 1958). **Age 27 at time of exoneration** (1958); took her first degree at Farland and stayed on for graduate work; grew up in Ashlin. "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not" (Chronicle quote).

### Critical Archival Evidence: Dell Rundle's Ledger Note

**Source:** Dell Rundle, jubilee pamphlet (1939, reprinted 1959) — Archival Addition While in Press.

While Rundle's manuscript was being typeset, he examined Association's old ledgers and revised Chapter IV:

> "Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, **57,760 lb**. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, **60,800 lb**. Station gain, **3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent**, as usual. Carried to the general account."

**Rundle's explicit revision:** "I must go back on Chapter IV. Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand. I leave it as written and correct it here." — Dated D. RUNDLE, Larrow Green.

**Significance:** Systematic overweight receipts at central facility **every year**; contradicts single-tester theft theory; **supports pipette defect explanation confirmed by Ivy Keddie's 1958 analysis**: 16.72 cc vs 17.6 cc (exactly 95% accuracy = 5% underfilling, matching 5.25% station gain).

### Retelling r20 — Junia Ammon, "Sixty Years of Ammon & Sons" (1949), Chapter Nine

**Source:** Firm's own historian (age 60), private history reproduced in facsimile.

**Company Background:**
- Marcus Ammon founded house on rented floor with two men and secondhand annealing oven
- By Junia's time: four stories plus basement at Tarnet, behind railroad yard
- Sidings close enough that case of glass went from packing room to freight car without touching street
- "We were the noisiest building on that block. Glass is not quiet and neither were the men."
- At height, supplied **half the creameries in the state** (glass only: test bottles, pipettes, acid measures, burettes, little thermometers)
- "A creamery orders a churn once in twenty years. A creamery orders glass forever, because glass breaks"

**Pipette Manufacturing Process:**
- "A milk test pipette is a simple thing and it is not an easy thing"
- Man gathers glass, blows bulb, draws stem down, works to gauge hanging on nail at elbow
- "He is making a vessel that must deliver a stated quantity of milk — not hold it, *deliver* it, which is a different measurement and a harder one, because milk is not water and clings to the wall of the glass as it runs out"
- Mark goes on stem while glass still warm
- Piece goes into lehr to cool slowly, comes out next morning, gauged
- Either packed or thrown into cullet barrel under bench; "no man on that floor was ever thanked for what went into the barrel"
- Packing: by dozen, in straw, wooden case with customer's name in blue pencil on end
- Everything out of building went into order book entry; everything made on blowing floor went into works book (what glass, what gauge, what man, what day)

**Archival Records:**
- Works records **burned in 1931 in fire on old third floor**
- Remaining: office side only—invoices, letter books, day books of what was owed
- "Those works books would answer a great many questions if we still had them. We do not."

**Territory & Market:**
- House sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900 (county behind ridge, traded east via Farland)
- Standing complaint of Marcus that couldn't get foothold while freight rate stood where it did
- Territory in early years ran along two railroad lines, hardly a mile off them
- Drummer steps down at depot with two sample cases, calls on nearby creamery; if manager out in fields, drummer sits and waits (next train tomorrow)
- "The creamery trade of the nineties was won a depot at a time"

**1890s Cooperative Movement:**
- "Cooperative creamery movement went through this state in those years like weather"
- Farmers who made butter in kitchens formed associations, put up creamery on creek, then skimming stations at head of every valley with cows
- "Every station wanted a full outfit at once: a hand tester, bottles, pipettes, an acid measure, and freight. We sold a great many outfits in those years and they went out very much alike."
- **Marcus's saying (lettered in packing room):** "A creamery buys arithmetic from us and not glassware"

### The 1923 Correspondence — Ammon & Sons' Deflection

**June 28, 1923:** Association wrote to Ammon & Sons following state inspector's condemnation of the glass

**July 2, 1923:** Ammon & Sons reply (letter book entry reproduced by Junia):

> *Your order of the 28th ultimo for six dozen milk pipettes and six dozen test bottles is entered and will go forward this month. We regret the trouble. Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name.*

**Junia's Assessment (1949):**
- "That was the truth and it was also, I am obliged to say, a thin thing to send to a customer of twenty-six years' standing."
- "We entered the order, we shipped in the month, and we said what we could say, which was nothing."

**Junia's Theory (what she "believes happened"):**
- "The mold had been reground and the lot ran short."
- "It was an accident and nobody in the works caught it."
- "A gauge sits on a nail and gets knocked off the nail and hung back on it; a mold comes back from the shop a hair different and looks exactly as it looked before; a boy checks the first of a lot and the tenth and the fiftieth and finds them consistent, because they are consistent — they are consistently wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind cannot find."
- "That is how it goes in a glass house, and any man who has stood on a blowing floor will tell you the same."

**Junia's Regret:**
- "What I regret is not the accident. Glass is made by people."
- "What I regret is the letter, and that we had nothing better than the letter to send, and that when a customer of a quarter century wrote to this house and asked in effect what had been done to him, the answer he got was that our books would not say."

**Closing (1949):**
- "I put both papers in this chapter, the invoice and the letter, so that they stand together where they belong. Sixty years is long enough that a firm may print its own bad morning as well as its good ones."
