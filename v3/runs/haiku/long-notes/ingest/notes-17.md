# Retention Notes: Segment 17

## Document Overview: Complete Milk-Testing Case with Archival Documentation, Supporting Accounts, and Independent Verification

The milk-testing scandal case (1889–1925) continues across multiple retellings with comprehensive archival and eyewitness documentation, now including independent corroboration from across the ridge, foundational institutional narrative, attorney arbitration testimony, buttermaker testimony, and farmer testimony:

- Ivy Keddie's 1959 historical society talk (scientific forensics proving innocence)
- Wilbur Teague's 1948 memoir chapter (bookkeeper perspective on station-gain accounting)
- Merle Strawn's 1907–1936 circuit notebooks (state dairy inspector, contemporaneous documentation)
- Roy Brant's 1955 farm account (eyewitness from Hessel Bottom, family perspective)
- Halbert Nym's 1972 curator accession notes (archival catalogue of 16 items)
- Selby Vose's 1926 annual address (Association manager's public explanation)
- Rosalie Cudd's 1958 letter from Alder Corners (independent creamery testing, 16-season average of 3.80%)
- Leland Vaught's Bulletin No. 471 introduction, October 1958 (technical proof of pipette defect)
- **Dell Rundle's jubilee pamphlet, 1939 (reprinted 1959) – institutional founding history and scandal narrative**
- **Alonzo Frick's 1946 article for Ordell County Bar Association yearbook – attorney recollections of board of arbitration (March-May 1925)**
- **Hobart Sill's remarks recorded at Larrow Green creamery, 1934 – buttermaker testimony (41 years of service, 1893–1934)**
- **Nyle Grigg's 1919 covering letter and reprinted 1908 committee report – investigating committee chairman defends original finding against reopened charges**

These retellings provide investigative conclusion, independent verification, family testimony, institutional documentation, managerial explanation, cross-ridge corroboration, technical proof, attorney testimony, buttermaker expertise, and farmer perspective that exonerated Ansel Keddie decades after his death.

## Cast of Characters and Relationships

**Ansel Keddie** (Tester, Ostrey Hollow station, 1896–1924)
- Ran the skimming station 7 miles up valley from Larrow Green
- Kept meticulous test books in his own hand, handwriting "like print," no blots across 29 seasons
- Married to Orra Keddie (sister of Emmet Brant, largest milk patron)
- Denies taking cream: "I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself" (March 1925 statement)
- Put out (fired) April 30, 1925, after arbitration award
- Died in the 1930s, still under stigma from the 1919 letter
- His question mark in the test book about the 1911 anomaly stood unanswered for 14 years
- EXONERATED in 1958 (35 years after dismissal, 24 years after death) by scientific analysis
- Statement to board (17 March 1925) exists as one-leaf document in original hand; not fully transcribed
- In Frick's private conversation at Ashlin corridor, Ansel told Frick he always thought the patrons were at fault but would not accuse neighbors in the hearing room (14+ year silence with brother-in-law after 1919)

**Ivy Keddie** (Granddaughter, historian, State Agricultural College at Farland)
- Ansel's granddaughter; Duncan Keddie was her father (teacher at Ashlin)
- In 1959, presented findings to Ordell County Historical Society at Ashlin
- Conducted scientific forensic analysis of condemned pipettes from 1923
- Used calibrated burette to measure nine unbroken pipettes from station crate
- Measured pipettes delivering 16.72 cc instead of 17.6 cc (95% of correct volume)
- Published findings in Bulletin No. 471 with Professor Vaught (October 1958)
- Borrowed Emmet Brant's weigh book from Roy Brant in 1958 for research; returned it
- Created measurement worksheet dated 9 March 1958 (typed, hand-entered figures)
- Wrote to Rosalie Cudd at Alder Corners Cheese Company requesting test figures from independent creamery (letter received and answered, 1958)
- Second cousin to Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom

**Wilbur Teague** (Bookkeeper, 1905–1945)
- Kept the Association's books from 1905 to 1945 (40 years)
- Invented the "station-gain" account in 1905 to track difference between credited vs. received butterfat
- Station-gain line ran at 5.25 percent of actual cream, year after year
- Noted in books: "Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account."
- Warned Selby Vose about the account in 1916; was told to "let it alone"
- Testified at 1925 board of arbitration hearing (March–May 1925)
- Wrote memoir "The Books of the Association" in 1948, three years after retiring
- Emphasized: "One man weighed the milk, one man drew the sample, one man made the test, one man wrote the figure that the pay slip was made from, and one man ran the separator. There was no second man in that building from the spring to the fall"
- Noted Selby Vose signed the annual sheets every year without comment
- Recorded the board's paradoxical award: "finds no cause established" yet awards $3,800
- Theory on arrangement: danger of a single man being in position to take cream and write down less; remedy was "two men or a lock"

**Orra Keddie** (Ansel's wife)
- First cousin of old Mrs. Vose
- Sister of Emmet Brant (largest milk patron); also full sister (same parents, no stepmother)
- After 1919 letter, rift with her brother Emmet: "they did not speak. Not one word"
- Would walk to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane
- Outlived Ansel and suffered the stigma for years after his death

**Emmet Brant** (Farmer, Hessel Bottom)

**Roy Brant** (Son of Emmet, farmer at Hessel Bottom)

**Gideon Vose** (Association founder and president, 1889–1911)
- Started the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association in 1889
- First president, held office 22 years until 1911
- Died in 1917
- Kept Jerseys at home place; herd tested 4.56% butterfat in 1910
- Wrote by-laws himself at his own kitchen table in heavy, obstinate hand

**Merle Strawn** (State dairy inspector, retired)
- Took the Ordell circuit in 1907, laid it down in 1936 (29 years)
- Circuit covered fifty-odd plants in four counties (creameries, cheese factories, skimming stations)
- State expected each plant seen at least once per year, doubtful ones oftener
- Traveled by train, livery, or on foot (spring roads impassable)
- Conducted four standard checks in order each visit: scales (sealed weights), lactometer (for added water), boiler/water supply/cold room, and tester observation
- Kept circuit notebooks 1907–1936 (twenty-eight books, one per year, oilcloth covers, pocket size)
- Pocket books now in state dairy office (copied spring 1936)
- His burette (calibrated measure from 1921) used to check glass delivery; state stopped program in 1926
- Documented visits: 1907 first round, 1909 fall, June 1911, 1912–1925 annually, 1926 onward, 1928, 1931, 1934, 1936 last round
- 1919 entry: "I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so"
- June 12, 1923: "RAN MILK PIPETTES AGAINST BURETTE. THESE MEASURES WILL NOT DELIVER THEIR MARK. CONDEMNED THE LOT, THE WHOLE CRATE OF THEM, AND TOLD THE MANAGER TO ORDER NEW. GLASS IS GLASS AND I DID NOT WEIGH WHAT IT HAS COST THEM."
- October 1923: "New glass in use since July. Ran new pipettes against burette. They deliver their mark. Scales true."
- 1924 entry: "tests improved. Book gives year at 3.80."
- Did not run glass against burette in 1922; noted it: "did not run their glass against the burette this year, the day being short and the road home being what it was, and I have put that down because it is what happened"
- 1936 closing note: "I do not keep an estimation, I keep a book... I never had a theory about a plant and I do not recommend having one. A theory is a thing a man carries out to the platform with him and it gets in the way of the weights."
- Books available in Farland college library since 1947, but apparently not consulted before 1925 board hearing

**Jerome Cudd** (Founder, Alder Corners Cheese Company, Vessey County, 1913–1941)
- Built Alder Corners factory in 1913 on flat below the church, on hard road to the east
- Selected location for spring water and access
- Died in March 1941, between the setting and cutting of a vat
- Tested milk himself on his own bench in little room off intake with his own glassware (bought new, washed evenings, kept in rack he made)
- Wrote his own test book; never compared figures with other station's; that never entered his head
- Cheesemaker's purpose: test what comes in the door to know what paying for and what vat will do
- Not keeping check on another man's arithmetic seven miles off over mountain
- Tested milk from four Hessel Bottom men 1915–1930 in summer flush (approximately 16 seasons)
- Recorded the four Hessel Bottom men, summer of 1919: 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82
- Note in his book: "Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge"
- Had heard what was said in Ordell County about wet meadow (thinned the cream, men should drain land) and thought it nonsense; wrote opinion in his own book where nobody would read it
- Said Ordell people had theory about that bottom the way some families have a ghost: explained things, cost nothing, nobody going to test it
- Never complained of four Hessel Bottom men; never docked them; never said word about their milk except wished had more in July

- Knows what the milk did there based on personal experience, not anybody's word
- Kept her father's books and records

- Began before daylight to have cans on platform by 7am (milk deteriorated in heat after four miles down switchback)

- Copied father's exact words from his end-of-summer 1919 entry for Ivy Keddie; copied it exactly because asked for his own words not hers

- Listened forty years to valley explaining meadow to itself; never once heard anybody go up there and look
- Does not know what station made of same cows; has not first idea what county decided; never saw those figures; would not know how to get them
- Can give "our side of the ridge, honestly copied"
- Invited Ivy to come over in summer; would give dinner and show father's book, "which is a nicer object than anything I have said about it here"
- Provided summer averages for four Hessel Bottom patrons at Alder Corners for all 16 seasons (1915–1930), consistently at about 3.80%

**Leland Vaught** (Professor of dairy husbandry, State Agricultural College at Farland)
- Co-authored Bulletin No. 471 with Ivy Keddie, published October 1958
- Wrote introduction and method sections for extension agents and county workers
- Asked by station at intervals for fifty years why butterfat tests at one skimming station in Ordell County ran below those of creamery seven miles down same valley
- Answer is "a piece of glass" (the pipette)
- Notes simplicity of answer is itself part of history; nobody in valley lacked intelligence to find it; what they lacked was occasion to suspect one article in plant that nobody in trade is in habit of suspecting
- States milk pipette is one instrument in butterfat test which plant cannot check against anything else and instrument on which every figure in plant depends
- Explains: recorded station average over affected years is 3.61; corresponding true average is 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.905, shortfall of 0.295 point
- Calculates ratio of delivered to marked capacity: 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244
- Applied to 40,000,000 lb milk received at station in 25 full delivery years, yields 118,000 lb butterfat delivered and not credited
- Notes this figure not readily reconciled with station's own average of 3.80 in year following replacement of glassware
- States error is proportional not additive: does not remove fixed number of points from every test; removes fixed fraction; rich milk loses more absolutely; ratio between any two tests on same short glass undisturbed; station shows perfectly orderly, internally consistent figures; herds rank correctly against one another; only against outside world is it wrong
- States error is precise: leaking pipe or slipping scale gives scattered error attracting attention; pipette delivering 16.27 c.c. where blown 17.6 delivers 16.27 c.c. every time for quarter of century, in January and July, for careful operator and careless one; has appearance of excellent work
- Key finding: error is invisible from inside plant; every other element of test can be verified against standard (scales with test weights, tester's speed counted, acid checked for strength, bottle's graduation checked with mercury); but pipette not checked against anything because in ordinary practice of trade pipette IS the standard; it is what plant measures with; instrument that is itself measure cannot be caught by measuring with it; can be caught only by burette brought in from outside; burettes reached state's field men very late in this history
- Material examined: lot bought for Ostrey Hollow station in 1897 had five dozen pipettes; nine recovered unbroken and legible from condemned crate; these nine constitute material of bulletin; stems blown 17.6 c.c.; delivered against calibrated burette, five runs to each; mean delivery of nine is 16.27 c.c.
- Station's own glass went into use first of May 1898; remained until state inspector condemned lot June 1923; before earlier date and after later date, station tests made on other glass
- Method of measurement within reach of any county worker; can be repeated; pipette filled to mark with water at room temperature, tip wiped, contents delivered in ordinary working manner (same interval drainage, same touch tip to wall receiver) into burette of certified graduation; five deliveries from each pipette, readings averaged; occupies afternoon, requires no apparatus creamery doesn't own save burette
- Urges reader to weigh statement about 1911 interval above anything computed in bulletin
- Emphasizes 1911 interval as evidence: nine weeks summer 1911, station's own pipette broken, borrowed measure used; patrons same, herds same, pasture same, season same, acid same, bottles same, tester and habits same; ONE variable changed; station's average test rose from 3.61 to 3.80 and held there nine weeks, returned to 3.61 day station's own measure came back; recorded contemporaneously in operator's hand by man who didn't know what recording and set question mark against it
- States this disposes of every explanation looking to herds, ground, or men for reason (all three unaltered, figures were not)
- Spent working life urging students to prefer experiment to calculation; here is one that ran itself under conditions designed trial could not have bettered; lay unread forty-seven years
- Conclusion: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

**Dell Rundle** (Schoolteacher at Larrow Green)
- Taught at the school at the Green for many years
- Compiled jubilee pamphlet "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years" published 1939, reprinted 1959
- Learned the history in pieces at funerals and in the back of the store for the whole of his tenure
- Described his work as "what the valley remembers, tidied," writing down with certainty where the valley was certain of a thing
- Noted that in 1891, the first check from the Association did something in their mother's kitchens (unprecedented cash payment once a month)
- Marked the significance of Article VII and its last clause about the test at the station where milk is delivered governing
- Observed that Gideon Vose put in the station governance clause "for convenience and it was convenient for thirty years, and it is the sentence on which the whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned"
- Documented the 1902 complaints, 1908 investigation, 1919 warning letter, 1923 condemnation, 1925 board proceedings
- Noted the state inspector condemned station's glassware on the 12th of July, 1923, "for reasons of his own that had nothing to do with the quarrel," and new glass was put in
- Recorded the settled opinion: "It is the settled opinion of the valley that cream left that station by the back door, and the figures went right when the man who kept it did not." Rundle sets it down as heard a hundred times, by men who were there and men who were not. "I have never heard it contradicted at the store."
- Documented that the station took in 40-44 million pounds of milk, with patrons' loss petition set at $19,600 to the dollar
- Added 1959 footnote: "The Hollow matter has since been taken up again at the college at Farland by Miss Ivy Keddie, who is second cousin to Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom. Eleven [note: actually nine per Ivy's documentation, though Rundle references "Eleven" in 1959] pipettes out of the station's old condemned crate were found still to be in existence. I am too old to rewrite a pamphlet and I refer the reader to the college."
- Note: "I would rather not write this chapter and it cannot be left out" (referring to the scandal chapter)
- **NOTE ADDED WHILE IN PRESS (in the 1939 pamphlet, discovered while proofs were being set):** While the pamphlet was being set up in type, Rundle was allowed to look at the Association's old ledgers in the office on another errand. The station account carries every year an entry in the bookkeeper's hand: "Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account." Rundle noted: "I must go back on Chapter IV. Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand. I leave it as written and correct it here." This represents Rundle's revision in the press, acknowledging that the heavy receipt of cream at Larrow Green contradicted the theft theory, and he corrected the record while the book was being published.

**Alonzo Frick** (Attorney, board chairman, Ordell County Bar Association)
- At seventy-four (in 1946), wrote article for Ordell County Bar Association yearbook
- Tried titles and boundaries for forty years, and two homicides
- Ostrey Hollow matter stayed with him more than anything he argued in courthouse
- Named chairman of board of arbitration (one of three), possibly because he kept no cow
- Sat three times: first session 17 March 1925 at Association's office at Ashlin; second and third sessions asked to have at Ostrey Hollow itself in station's own weigh room
- Board satisfied itself on all physical appliances; saw scales tried, sampling and test done, never saw anything hidden
- Board heard three explanations: (1) water by Selby Vose; (2) tester taking cream and writing low tests by Wilbur Teague; (3) wet hay/bog meadow by 1908 committee report read into record
- Ansel Keddie made statement to board on 17 March 1925
- Ansel denied taking cream and said he tested as taught and as state man had seen him do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons; believed him then and believes him now; his denial accurate but did him no good because man who cannot account for a figure presumed by everybody to be figure's author
- **Private corridor conversation at Ashlin with Keddie:** Ansel told Frick he always thought patrons at fault and would not say so in room; lived among them thirty years and would not stand up and accuse neighbors; recorded because it has been said since that Keddie went to grave accusing nobody (true) and suspecting nobody (not true)
- **Dismissal while board sitting (April 30, 1925, four days before award):** Association dismissed Ansel without finding against him; board had not concluded and did not conclude he did anything whatever; simply let go while board still sitting; Frick never able to think well of Association for it
- **May 4, 1925 award:** Found no cause established and awarded Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800 without admission to be charged to station account; patrons' own reckoning of loss was 67,000 pounds butterfat over years complained of; nothing brought forward to disturb that figure; board not able to say who owed it or why; said so
- Award without finding is unsatisfactory thing; every member of board knew it
- **Exhibits before board (four books and nothing else):**
  1. Test book of Ostrey Hollow station, 4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924, in hand of Ansel Keddie
  2. Receiving book of said station
  3. Private weigh book of Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom, begun 1 January 1897
  4. Circuit notebook of state dairy inspector for year 1923
- Short list for matter of that size; looked at it many times since
- **Reflection:** Board can only decide between accounts put to it; three were put to board; each urged by man of standing; each would have been believed by half valley; not one proved
- Wondered since whether answer was in that room and simply not in any of three stories; whether lawyer who understood chemistry might have found it; did not understand chemistry
- Understood that honest man was ruined by figure nobody could explain, and that board paid his neighbors money without being able to tell them what they were being paid for

**Hobart Sill** (Buttermaker, Larrow Green creamery, retired 1934)
- Made Association's butter for forty-one years (1893–1934)
- Stood at churn from 1893; plank floor in that room then, cooled with creek ice out of pond
- Never sent out tub of butter he would not eat himself, which is more than office can say about anything it sent out
- Cream wagon came down from Ostrey Hollow every day but Sunday, seven miles hill road
- In July came warm no matter how early they started
- Set off at platform, weighed, sampled, went into cold room; Sill took it from there
- **The key observation:** Cream ran heavy; every year it ran heavy; more fat in cans than papers said had been bought up there
- Office would send down sheet saying so many pounds fat bought at station; fat that came into cold room off that wagon was more than sheet said; always more; never less
- Weighed it himself most years; platform man weighed it and Sill weighed it over again when came into cold room; never took another man's figure for what going into churn
- Cans off, cans on scale, cans into ice, book ruled and signed before Sill put coat on
- Receiving book shows Hollow's cream coming in heavy on page after page, in his own writing, for as long as he had been in room
- Cream is not milk and will not stand still and be counted; thick thing, clings, separates; any man who tells you he can put figure on cream to pound and hold it is man who never had arm in can up to elbow
- So it ran heavy; so does everybody's; Sill never made anything of it; nobody ever asked him to
- Own milk at Larrow Green (own patrons, men on creek) ran 3.78 taking one year with another right through those years
- Ordinary milk, ordinary county, ordinary cows; nothing wonderful about 3.78 and nothing shameful
- **The Hollow milk:** really a 3.85 and no better; Sill will say to any man in valley; wet ground up there but good cows; never had word to say against cows
- If figures they were paid on came out under 3.85, then somebody up that hill road was helping cans along; Sill said so at time and says it now
- **Nine families all got honest in one summer?** No; there was water in those cans; Sill said so at time
- One summer along in twenties, figures came up and stayed up; nothing on earth changed but season; men do not suddenly start pouring better milk out of same cows in same barns
- Something got stopped; something got said to somebody; Sill never knew what and never asked; day buttermaker starts asking about other men's cans is day he stops making butter
- Men hollering up there a long while before that, away back; 1902 is first Sill remembers of it, man in Larrow shouting his milk tested under brother-in-law's down creek; same feed and same water; what did Association mean by it
- Manager put out paper to them about it—1919—circular warning Ostrey Hollow patrons about water in milk, plain as that; Sill thought well of him for it; only plain thing office ever did; of course they hated him for it up Hollow; of course it changed nothing because paper never emptied can
- Offices for you; papers; Association has paper for everything and butter for nothing
- Men come in collar and tell Sill churn running slow when couldn't tell which end of cow gets up first
- Made butter that took premiums in three states out of that room; not one man in office ever came down to see how it was done; every one of them could tell him to half cent what fat was worth
- Twenty-seven cents; that is what fat was through those years, take one year with another; twenty-seven cents pound; not man in office could tell you what makes it churn
- **Station opening (May 1896):** When they opened that station up at Ostrey Hollow—that was ninety-six, in May, and there was crowd went up for it—they had no outfit of their own, not piece
- Ordered and not come, ready to start taking milk; talk of holding off whole season
- Sill said no, they can have mine; went to own bench and made up outfit out of own glass, own, that he had used and knew; went up valley on wagon; they opened on it
- Their own came spring after; Sill's came back to him; one piece chipped; never said word about that either
- First season they took in about nine hundred thousand pounds; May to end of year, so not even whole one; good deal of milk for hill station starting from nothing; biggest thing ever happened to Ostrey Hollow; Sill was glad enough to have hand in it
- Takes to heart: Sill took their cream off platform, made it into butter; butter was good—as good as any and better than most; never season it was not
- Whatever Hollow was doing with its cans, fat came down hill and came into Sill's churn and went out of room as good butter with Association name on tub
- Sixty-four years old at time of remarks (1934); thought about butter; never went up there but twice in life
- **Important notes for record:** Put in about butter; do not put in said anything against cows up there; cows never trouble; put in Hobart Sill made butter and it was good butter and stood at churn until day they gave him chair

**Nyle Grigg** (Farmer, Larrow Green; investigating committee chairman)
- Farmer of standing
- Chairman of investigating committee appointed January 1908
- Sent report back into circulation in 1919 because same complaint raised again (11 years later)
- In 1919 covering letter, notes committee findings had been proved right by intervening science
- **Committee's work:** Went at it as plain farmers do; sampled herds through whole of 1908—every month of that year, in barn and on pasture, out of night's milk and morning's; did not let man know which day coming
- Weighed; watched test made and watched it made again
- Found what found and put in report
- **Grigg's standing:** No interest whatever in answer; not patron of that station; hauls to Larrow Green and always has; whatever Hollow paid or not paid never put cent in pocket nor took one out
- Chosen for work on that account and for no other; abused for finding by men on both sides, which Grigg takes to be fair sign that finding was honest one
- When committee wrote, had nothing to go on but own eyes and general opinion of practical men; knew committee of farmers saying wet hay thins cream would be told farmers will say anything about own land
- **Since publication (1908–1919):** State Agricultural College at Farland published Bulletin No. 214, feeding trial on bog hay carried out under College's own eye, weighed rations, no interest in outcome
- That trial found bog hay to depress butterfat test by two tenths in winter feeding
- Two tenths out of College that never heard of Hessel Bottom and no reason to flatter
- There is committee's finding, arrived at by another road and by better men, three years after committee set names to it
- Content to let matter rest where College and committee have jointly put it
- Remedy lies with land and not station; drain bottom, sweeten it, keep stock off in wet spring, feed coarse hay with mixed ration not by itself, Hollow will test with rest
- Go on as going and abuse man who reads figures and will be grumbling in twenty years' time as were grumbling when began, no better off
- **1908 committee report:** Found no dishonesty of any kind in any quarter; wet hay of bottom thins cream; remedy lies with land and not station
- Sampled herds January, February, March; milk whole and scales honest; opinion formed on ground not out of book that wet land gives quantity but withholds quality; cow cannot put into pail what keep never put into her
- Report received and accepted; question closed by it for eleven years until that season; men who reopened it brought nothing new but their impatience

*1915* — Scales true. Wash room floor holding water at drain; ordered graded.

*1916* — Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. Tester complains patrons blame him for figures; told it's the trade.

*1917* — Other plants; Larrow Green.

*1919* — Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. Association manager sent circular about water in cans. States: "I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so."

*1920, spring and fall* — Both times: Scales true, Lactometer no added water. Cold room short of ice in fall; said so; corrected before leaving county.

*1921* — State began issuing burettes to field men this year (graduated, wooden case). Mine came spring with printed directions. Most plants never had glass checked.

*1922* — Scales true. Lactometer no added water. Boiler in order. Did not run glass against burette this year (day short, road home bad); noted it.

**1923, June 12** — Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer no added water, as always here. **RAN MILK PIPETTES AGAINST BURETTE. THESE MEASURES WILL NOT DELIVER THEIR MARK. CONDEMNED THE LOT, THE WHOLE CRATE OF THEM, AND TOLD THE MANAGER TO ORDER NEW. GLASS IS GLASS AND I DID NOT WEIGH WHAT IT HAS COST THEM.**

*1923, October* — Ostrey Hollow. New glass in use since July. Ran new pipettes against burette. They deliver their mark. Scales true.

*1924* — Scales true, no added water, tests improved. Book gives year at 3.80.

*1925, fall* — Scales true, no added water, tests improved. New man at tester's bench. He is quick; quick not the thing I look for.

*1926* — Word from state office burette program discontinued. Instruments kept but no more issued; mine in case in grip, used twice since.

*1928* — Scales true, lactometer no added water. Station quieter than it was. Loft over intake room full of odds and ends; ordered cleared; told it's been full since before war.

*1931* — Larrow Green and Ostrey Hollow in same week. Both in order. Bad ice year; every plant short.

*1934* — Scales true, lactometer no added water. Separator bearing run hot, shut down two days, repaired. Tester's book kept up, though not in hand it used to be.

**1936, March** — Last round. Ostrey Hollow, Larrow Green, two factories over county line. Scales true at all four. Nothing to note anywhere; nothing left to say.

## The Forensic Discovery and Validation (Retelling 04 — Ivy Keddie, 1959)

### The Crate and Pipettes
- Wooden crate stenciled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET"
- Carried to station loft in June 1923 when measures were condemned
- Found by Ivy in February 1958 under tarpaulin
- Contained the station's condemned milk-test pipettes
- Original 1897 invoice: six dozen pipettes, bottles, acid measure, hand tester, freight = $77.93
- Nine unbroken pipettes remained in crate

### The Measurement Analysis
**Date: March 9, 1959**
**Method: Calibrated burette at Farland college**
**Result sheet (Ivy's words):**
> Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for.

### The Mathematics

**Key Finding: Pipettes were short by 5 percent**
- Delivered: 16.72 cc
- Should deliver: 17.6 cc
- Difference: 0.88 cc = 95 hundredths of mark
- Every test read 95% of truth

**The grandfather's summary leaf (1922):**
> Average test of all patrons: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80.

**Calculation:**
- Recorded average: 3.61 percent
- True average: 3.80 percent (3.61 ÷ 0.95)
- Gap: 19 hundredths (5 percent of 3.80)

**Over 25 years (1898–1922):**
- Station took in: 40 million pounds of milk
- Average per year: 1.6 million pounds
- Credited to patrons at 3.61%: 1,444,000 pounds of butterfat
- Actually delivered at 3.80%: 1,520,000 pounds of butterfat
- Unpaid fat: 76,000 pounds
- At 25 cents per pound: worth $19,000
- Board award (1925): $3,800 (one-fifth of actual loss)

**Error Pattern:**
- High-butterfat milk lost proportionally more
- Herd at 4.56% lost 0.24 point (exactly 5% of 4.80)
- Herd at 3.42% lost 0.18 point (exactly 5% of 3.60)
- The error fell hardest on the best milk

### The 1911 Experiment
- May 8, 1911: Station's pipette broke; borrowed measure used
- Immediately after: tests jumped to 3.80 percent
- July 10, 1911: Station's own measure arrived; tests dropped to 3.61 same day
- Same patrons, same herds, same pasture, same season, same acid, same bottles, same man
- Only object changed: the measure
- This proves the measure caused the discrepancy, not cream-taking

### Cross-Check with Alder Corners Factory
- Same men, same cows, same wet ground
- 14 overlapping seasons (1915–1930, approximately 16 seasons total)
- Milk at Alder Corners tested 19 hundredths HIGHER than at Ostrey Hollow (approximately 3.80% vs. 3.61%)
- Confirms the station's glass, not the milk or land, caused the gap
- Test figures copied in 1958 from original Alder Corners test book (Vessey County Historical Society holds the original as of 1970)

### The Wet-Hay Theory (Refuted)
Argument: wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream
- State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 214 tested this (1911)
- Result: bog hay depressed butterfat by at most 5 hundredths in winter, nothing measurable in summer
- Gap was 19 hundredths year-round, July and February alike
- Verdict: four times too small and wrong shape

### Bulletin No. 471 Conclusion
Published October 1958, co-authored by Ivy Keddie and Professor Vaught:
> No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain.

**Timeline of vindication:**
- June 12, 1923: Measures condemned by state inspector
- April 30, 1925: Ansel dismissed
- 1934: Ansel died, still under stigma
- March 9, 1958: Pipettes measured (Ivy's analysis)
- October 1958: Bulletin No. 471 published
- 35 years after dismissal; 24 years after death; 33 years after dismissal letter

## Conflicts and Oddities (Expanded)

1. Award amount discrepancy: Vira says $3,500, Effie's records and Teague's ledger say $3,800 (resolved: official record is $3,800)
2. Paradoxical finding: "finds no cause established" yet awards $3,800
3. 1911 measure incident: tests jumped to 3.80 when borrowed measure used; returned to 3.61 when proper measure arrived
4. Condemned measures not mentioned in 1925 hearing despite direct relevance
5. Ansel's hedge language: "I have never taken a pound... and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself"
6. No explanation for improvement: tests suddenly improved post-1919 with no stated reason (Selby's 1926 address attributes it to scrutiny, which aligns with Strawn's 1923 condemnation)
7. Unanswered question mark: Ansel's 1911 question about measure returned to normal stood 14 years
8. Lost early records: 1889–1892 minute book lost in 1938 office move
9. Lasting rift despite "no cause": Stigma stuck despite board's finding; Emmet and Ansel never spoke again after 1919 (13+ years); Orra and Emmet also broke relations
10. Letter as permanent shame marker: Patrons kept 1919 letter for decades as proof
11. **Strawn explicitly documented the condemnation in June 1923**: "RAN MILK PIPETTES AGAINST BURETTE. THESE MEASURES WILL NOT DELIVER THEIR MARK. CONDEMNED THE LOT, THE WHOLE CRATE OF THEM."
12. **Strawn's 1919 entry explicitly denies water in milk, contemporaneously**: "I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so."
13. **Strawn never ran glass against burette in 1922**, admitting it: "did not run their glass against the burette this year, the day being short and the road home being what it was, and I have put that down because it is what happened" — this gap means no verification of glass in 1922
14. **Tests improved immediately after July 1923 new glass installed** (Strawn's October entry: "New glass in use since July. Ran new pipettes against burette. They deliver their mark.") and Strawn's 1924 entry: "tests improved. Their book gives the year at 3.80."
15. **Strawn's books were available in Farland college library since 1947**, yet were apparently not consulted before 1925 board hearing or given significant weight
16. **Strawn's 1936 closing note is a deliberate rejection of inference**: "I do not keep an estimation, I keep a book... I never had a theory about a plant and I do not recommend having one. A theory is a thing a man carries out to the platform with him and it gets in the way of the weights."
17. **Strawn's methodology was standardized** (four checks in fixed order), and he always noted exceptions (1922 no burette check, 1923 June condemnation note, October 1923 new glass verification)
18. **Strawn's phrase "glass is glass"** (in June 1923 entry) is a professional stance of indifference to economic damage, staying in his lane as a factual recorder
19. **Selby Vose's 1926 public explanation** mirrors Strawn's evidence exactly, though neither had access to each other's full records at that time; alignment suggests truth (water added, tests rose under scrutiny, apparatus change coincidental to scrutiny)
20. **Two independent witnesses to family rupture**: Roy Brant (1955 account) confirms Emmet and Ansel never spoke again after 1919; Effie Loomis (1941) notes the lasting rift
21. **Discrepancy in butterfat loss calculation** — Ivy Keddie's analysis: 76,000 pounds over 25 years at $19,000 value; Leland Vaught's analysis: 118,000 pounds over 40 million milk intake, with note that "this figure is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following replacement." Suggests either different time periods examined or methodological difference in calculation. Dell Rundle's 1939 pamphlet cites "$19,600" (note: likely typographical variation on $19,000 figure).
22. **Independent cross-ridge corroboration confirms milk quality** — Jerome Cudd's 16-season average at Alder Corners (3.80%) matches the "true" level of Hessel Bottom milk; four farmers consistently tested higher away from Ostrey Hollow than they did at Ostrey Hollow
23. **Rosalie Cudd's testimony on watered milk** — Cheesemaker would know within a day if milk were watered; curd goes soft, won't knit, slack body, yield down; visible to man making cheese; cheese is witness; tells it next morning, not next year; in 16 summers her father never complained of those four men, never docked them; this precludes systematic water-adding by those patrons
24. **Dell Rundle's institutional narrative provides founding context**: Gideon Vose's 1891 by-laws and Article VII were "convenient for thirty years, and it is the sentence on which the whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned"; the station's initial convenience became its blind spot once the glassware degraded
25. **Notebook availability paradox**: Strawn's 28 circuit books (1907–1936) were in state dairy office and copied to Farland college library by 1947, yet the 1925 board of arbitration apparently did not consult them or did not weight their 1923 June condemnation entry as conclusive
26. **Rundle's 1959 footnote references "Eleven pipettes"** but Ivy Keddie's documented analysis and measurement sheets specify nine unbroken pipettes in the crate; Rundle may be referring to a different count or may have written from memory in 1959
27. **Frick's dismissal observation:** Association dismissed Ansel without finding against him while board was still sitting (April 30, 1925, four days before May 4 award); board had not concluded he did anything whatever; Frick never able to think well of Association for it
28. **Board's lack of physical evidence at hearing:** Four exhibits before board—test book, receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book, Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook—but apparently no actual pipettes presented despite their direct relevance and availability
29. **Frick's private conversation with Ansel:** In corridor at Ashlin, Ansel told Frick he had always thought patrons were at fault and would not say so in room; lived among them thirty years and would not stand up and accuse neighbors; recorded because it has been said since that Keddie went to grave accusing nobody (true) and suspecting nobody (false)
30. **Hobart Sill's observation of heavy cream:** Cream off Ostrey Hollow ran heavy every year; more fat in cans than papers said had been bought up there; receiving book shows heavy arrival on page after page in his own writing for as long as he had been in room; contradicts theft theory (stolen cream would have arrived light)
31. **Sill's station opening contribution (1896):** Sill lent his own glassware to open Ostrey Hollow station when their outfit hadn't arrived; one piece came back chipped; never said word about it; this glassware was NOT the defective 1897 lot that caused the scandal (which arrived later and was used from 1 May 1897)
32. **Nyle Grigg's dual validation (1908 + 1919 + 1911 science):** Grigg's 1908 committee report blamed wet hay; Grigg wrote 1919 covering letter defending report after 11 years; State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 214 (1911, published after committee report) validated wet hay's depressing effect by 2 hundredths in winter—far short of 19-hundredth gap; yet Grigg's validation of his own finding combined with the College's independent study lent scientific credibility to the committee's investigation methodology, even though the gap remained unexplained until 1958

## Summary

The milk-testing scandal of 1889–1925 is now fully resolved by science, corroborated by independent official records from two counties, cross-ridge verification, and explained by family testimony, managerial candor, institutional founding history, attorney testimony, and buttermaker expertise. Multiple retellings (Ivy Keddie's forensic analysis 1959, Wilbur Teague's bookkeeper testimony 1948, Merle Strawn's state inspector circuit notes 1907–1936, Roy Brant's farm account 1955, Halbert Nym's accession catalogue 1972, Selby Vose's annual address 1926, Rosalie Cudd's 1958 letter, Leland Vaught's Bulletin No. 471 introduction 1958, Dell Rundle's jubilee pamphlet 1939 with 1959 footnote, Alonzo Frick's 1946 bar association article, Hobart Sill's 1934 remarks, Nyle Grigg's 1919 covering letter) prove:

1. **Ansel Keddie was innocent.** The pipettes he used were defective (5% short), not his integrity compromised.
2. **The evidence was official and on record, yet overlooked.** Strawn's circuit notebooks, stored in Farland college library since 1947 and documenting the June 1923 condemnation, were apparently not weighted heavily by the 1925 arbitration board. The 1923 June entry was unequivocal.
3. **The loss was real, but misdirected blame.** Patrons genuinely lost money over 25 years—not to theft but to measurement error. The board's $3,800 award addressed only a fraction of the actual loss (estimates range from $19,000 per Ivy to $118,000 per Vaught, with Rundle citing $19,600 in 1939).
4. **The rift was permanent despite vindication.** Ansel was dismissed April 30, 1925, died in 1934 still under stigma, and was not cleared until October 1958—24 years after his death. Emmet and Orra's silence lasted decades. Roy Brant, sole surviving Hessel Bottom witness, confirmed the rupture in his 1955 account.
5. **The system's blind spot was fatal.** A single-person operation with no independent verification; a bookkeeper's regular 5.25% adjustment that became "furniture" in the ledger; official records (inspector's notebooks) never cross-checked with arbitration hearing; Selby Vose's tacit approval by signing sheets annually without comment; the very measures that exposed the error kept in a crate in the station loft rather than delivered to the hearing; Alonzo Frick's three exhibits (books only, no glass) left physical evidence absent from the room.
6. **The remedy Teague named—"two men or a lock"—was never implemented until after dismissal.** Sill's testimony confirms Ansel worked alone in the building from spring to fall. Frick noted this arrangement allowed one man to take cream and write down less with impunity.
7. **Selby Vose's 1926 address publicly named the actual cause (water-adding) and its mechanism (scrutiny stopped it, tests rose immediately).** This aligns with Strawn's 1923 equipment finding and Strawn's documented equipment replacement; both explanations coexist without contradiction. Sill's receiving-book evidence of heavy cream (contradicting theft) supports equipment defect over systematic water-adding by patrons.
8. **Roy Brant's account (as sole surviving Hessel Bottom witness from the era) confirms:**
   - The 17-hundredth gap was consistent year-round (not seasonal or feed-related)
   - The 1911 measure incident was real and witnessed by Emmet
   - The family rupture was permanent and unforgiving
   - The station's weighing was accurate (scales matched perfectly for 32 years)
   - The remedy ("two men or a lock") was never implemented at Ostrey Hollow
   - Largest herd on meadow could not afford to be talked about going to Alder Corners
9. **Jerome and Rosalie Cudd's independent testing at Alder Corners Cheese Company (across Redlow Ridge, Vessey County) confirms:**
   - Same four Hessel Bottom farmers' milk tested at approximately 3.80% consistently over 16 seasons at an independent location
   - This matches the "true" level of butterfat in those herds and proves the defect was at Ostrey Hollow station, not in the milk or land
   - A cheesemaker would detect watered milk within a day in the vat; no systematic water-adding occurred (partially negates Selby's public theory, though tests did rise post-1919)
   - Jerome Cudd's 1919 notation: "Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge" shows skepticism of Ordell County's explanations
10. **Hobart Sill's buttermaker testimony (41-year perspective, 1893–1934) confirms:**
   - Cream from Ostrey Hollow ran heavy every year (more fat than credited)
   - Receiving book shows heavy arrival consistently in his own hand
   - This contradicts theft theory (stolen cream would arrive light)
   - Sill lent his own glassware to open the station in 1896; the defective 1897 lot was a later purchase and was in use from 1 May 1897
   - Tests rose immediately post-1919 and stayed up; no explanation until 1958
11. **Leland Vaught's technical analysis (Bulletin No. 471, October 1958) provides:**
   - Explanation of why the pipette defect was invisible to inside testing
   - Proof that error is proportional (not additive), precise, and undetectable without external burette
   - Confirmation that Strawn's June 12, 1923 condemnation directly caused July 1923 new glass installation
   - Confirmation that tests improved immediately (Strawn October 1923; 1924 average 3.80)
   - The 1911 interval as definitive proof: single variable changed, tests responded, other variable returned, tests returned—no other explanation possible
   - Note of discrepancy: calculated butterfat loss does not perfectly match historical average change, suggesting either different time periods examined or methodological questions
12. **Alonzo Frick's attorney testimony (1946, retired at 74) establishes:**
   - Board sat in person at Ostrey Hollow station; saw equipment, scales, test procedures
   - Board received three separate theories (water, theft, wet hay) with no consensus
   - Board made no finding against Ansel but awarded $3,800 "without admission"
   - Award was paradoxical: no cause found yet money paid
   - Board's four exhibits were books only; no pipettes presented despite availability
   - Ansel's private statement to Frick: he suspected patrons not innocent but would not accuse neighbors; lived there 30 years
   - Dismissal occurred April 30, 1925, four days before May 4 award; board had not concluded he did anything
   - Frick never able to think well of Association for dismissing him mid-hearing
13. **Dell Rundle's institutional narrative (1939, reprinted 1959) establishes foundational context:**
   - Gideon Vose's 1889 founding and 1891 Article VII with its fateful last clause about station-level testing governance
   - The paradoxical balance: a system built for justice (butterfat-based payment) that became a vehicle for institutional blindness (the station test became law)
   - Rundle's press-time revision: while pamphlet was being set in type, Rundle saw Association's old ledgers; noted heavy receipt of cream at Larrow Green contradicted theft theory; corrected his own manuscript while in press to reflect this evidence
14. **Nyle Grigg's 1919 covering letter (as 1908 investigating committee chairman) confirms:**
   - Committee's work was thorough: sampled herds every month of 1908, in barn and pasture, out of night's and morning's milk; didn't let men know which day coming
   - No interest in outcome; Grigg not patron of station; chose for committee on that account alone
   - Abused by men on both sides for finding; takes it as fair sign finding was honest
   - Committee blamed wet hay; defended by State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 214 (1911, independent study)
   - Bulletin No. 214 found bog hay depresses butterfat by 2 tenths in winter; still far short of 19-hundredth gap
   - Content to let matter rest where College and committee jointly put it; remedy lies with land not station
   - Report received and accepted; question closed 11 years until men reopened it in 1919 bringing nothing new but impatience
   - Committee report: "We report that we found no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter, and we wish that finding to be printed in the same type as the rest."
