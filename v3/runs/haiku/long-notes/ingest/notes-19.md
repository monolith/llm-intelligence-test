# Retention Notes: Segment 19

## Document Overview: Complete Milk-Testing Case with Archival Documentation, Supporting Accounts, Independent Verification, and Family Record

The milk-testing scandal case (1889–1925) continues across multiple retellings with comprehensive archival and eyewitness documentation, now including independent corroboration from across the ridge, foundational institutional narrative, attorney arbitration testimony, buttermaker testimony, veterinary scientific analysis, farmer testimony, AND the family genealogical record kept by Duncan Keddie:

- Ivy Keddie's 1959 historical society talk (scientific forensics proving innocence)
- Wilbur Teague's 1948 memoir chapter (bookkeeper perspective on station-gain accounting)
- Merle Strawn's 1907–1936 circuit notebooks (state dairy inspector, contemporaneous documentation)
- Roy Brant's 1955 farm account (eyewitness from Hessel Bottom, family perspective)
- Halbert Nym's 1972 curator accession notes (archival catalogue of 16 items)
- Selby Vose's 1926 annual address (Association manager's public explanation)
- Rosalie Cudd's 1958 letter from Alder Corners (independent creamery testing, 16-season average of 3.80%)
- Leland Vaught's Bulletin No. 471 introduction, October 1958 (technical proof of pipette defect)
- Dell Rundle's jubilee pamphlet, 1939 (reprinted 1959) – institutional founding history and scandal narrative
- Alonzo Frick's 1946 article for Ordell County Bar Association yearbook – attorney recollections of board of arbitration (March-May 1925)
- Hobart Sill's remarks recorded at Larrow Green creamery, 1934 – buttermaker testimony (41 years of service, 1893–1934)
- Nyle Grigg's 1919 covering letter and reprinted 1908 committee report – investigating committee chairman defends original finding against reopened charges
- Dr. Roland Kepp's paper "The bog meadows and the milk," read before Ordell County Farm Club, 1926 – veterinary surgeon brings agricultural literature to bear on local grievance
- Hazel Brant's recorded interview at Ashlin, 1981 – farmer's daughter and last keeper of weigh book, recorded at her kitchen table
- **Duncan Keddie's "Notes for the family," written at Ashlin, 1968 (with addition of 1970) – retired schoolteacher sets down family genealogy and disposition of books**

These retellings provide investigative conclusion, independent verification, family testimony, institutional documentation, managerial explanation, cross-ridge corroboration, technical proof, attorney testimony, buttermaker expertise, veterinary analysis, farmer perspective, AND family genealogical clarification that exonerated Ansel Keddie decades after his death.

- Kept meticulous test books in his own hand, handwriting "like print," no blots across 29 seasons
- Married to Orra Keddie (sister of Emmet Brant, largest milk patron)

- Died in the 1930s, still under stigma from the 1919 letter
- His question mark in the test book about the 1911 anomaly stood unanswered for 14 years
- EXONERATED in 1958 (35 years after dismissal, 24 years after death) by scientific analysis
- Statement to board (17 March 1925) exists as one-leaf document in original hand; not fully transcribed
- In Frick's private conversation at Ashlin corridor, Ansel told Frick he always thought the patrons were at fault but would not accuse neighbors in the hearing room (14+ year silence with brother-in-law after 1919)
- Born 1861; died 1934 (per Duncan Keddie); described by son Duncan as "a careful man in small things to a degree that other people found trying"

**Merle Strawn** (State dairy inspector, retired)
- Took the Ordell circuit in 1907, laid it down in 1936 (29 years)
- Circuit covered fifty-odd plants in four counties (creameries, cheese factories, skimming stations)
- State expected each plant seen at least once per year, doubtful ones oftener
- Traveled by train, livery, or on foot (spring roads impassable)
- Conducted four standard checks in order each visit: scales (sealed weights), lactometer (for added water), boiler/water supply/cold room, and tester observation
- Kept circuit notebooks 1907–1936 (twenty-eight books, one per year, oilcloth covers, pocket size)
- Pocket books now in state dairy office (copied spring 1936)
- His burette (calibrated measure from 1921) used to check glass delivery; state stopped program in 1926
- Documented visits: 1907 first round, 1909 fall, June 1911, 1912–1925 annually, 1926 onward, 1928, 1931, 1934, 1936 last round
- 1919 entry: "I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so"
- June 12, 1923: "RAN MILK PIPETTES AGAINST BURETTE. THESE MEASURES WILL NOT DELIVER THEIR MARK. CONDEMNED THE LOT, THE WHOLE CRATE OF THEM, AND TOLD THE MANAGER TO ORDER NEW. GLASS IS GLASS AND I DID NOT WEIGH WHAT IT HAS COST THEM."
- October 1923: "New glass in use since July. Ran new pipettes against burette. They deliver their mark. Scales true."
- 1924 entry: "tests improved. Book gives year at 3.80."
- Did not run glass against burette in 1922; noted it: "did not run their glass against the burette this year, the day being short and the road home being what it was, and I have put that down because it is what happened"
- 1936 closing note: "I do not keep an estimation, I keep a book... I never had a theory about a plant and I do not recommend having one. A theory is a thing a man carries out to the platform with him and it gets in the way of the weights."
- Books available in Farland college library since 1947, but apparently not consulted before 1925 board hearing
- Duncan Keddie notes: Merle Strawn "was kin to nobody in the valley whatever — not to us, not to the Brants, not to the Voses, not to anybody on the creek. That is worth writing down, because his notebooks turned out to matter, and it matters that the man who kept them had no interest in any of us."
- Found in state dairy office records; transferred to state agricultural college library at Farland in 1947 (Ivy Keddie found the 1923 volume "eleven years later" in 1958; "Nobody had asked for them in the interval.")

**Leland Vaught** (Professor of dairy husbandry, State Agricultural College at Farland)
- Co-authored Bulletin No. 471 with Ivy Keddie, published October 1958
- Wrote introduction and method sections for extension agents and county workers
- Asked by station at intervals for fifty years why butterfat tests at one skimming station in Ordell County ran below those of creamery seven miles down same valley
- Answer is "a piece of glass" (the pipette)
- Notes simplicity of answer is itself part of history; nobody in valley lacked intelligence to find it; what they lacked was occasion to suspect one article in plant that nobody in trade is in habit of suspecting
- States milk pipette is one instrument in butterfat test which plant cannot check against anything else and instrument on which every figure in plant depends
- Explains: recorded station average over affected years is 3.61; corresponding true average is 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.905, shortfall of 0.295 point
- Calculates ratio of delivered to marked capacity: 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244
- Applied to 40,000,000 lb milk received at station in 25 full delivery years, yields 118,000 lb butterfat delivered and not credited
- Notes this figure not readily reconciled with station's own average of 3.80 in year following replacement of glassware
- States error is proportional not additive: does not remove fixed number of points from every test; removes fixed fraction; rich milk loses more absolutely; ratio between any two tests on same short glass undisturbed; station shows perfectly orderly, internally consistent figures; herds rank correctly against one another; only against outside world is it wrong
- States error is precise: leaking pipe or slipping scale gives scattered error attracting attention; pipette delivering 16.27 c.c. where blown 17.6 delivers 16.27 c.c. every time for quarter of century, in January and July, for careful operator and careless one; has appearance of excellent work
- Key finding: error is invisible from inside plant; every other element of test can be verified against standard (scales with test weights, tester's speed counted, acid checked for strength, bottle's graduation checked with mercury); but pipette not checked against anything because in ordinary practice of trade pipette IS the standard; it is what plant measures with; instrument that is itself measure cannot be caught by measuring with it; can be caught only by burette brought in from outside; burettes reached state's field men very late in this history
- Material examined: lot bought for Ostrey Hollow station in 1897 had five dozen pipettes; nine recovered unbroken and legible from condemned crate; these nine constitute material of bulletin; stems blown 17.6 c.c.; delivered against calibrated burette, five runs to each; mean delivery of nine is 16.27 c.c.
- Station's own glass went into use first of May 1898; remained until state inspector condemned lot June 1923; before earlier date and after later date, station tests made on other glass
- Method of measurement within reach of any county worker; can be repeated; pipette filled to mark with water at room temperature, tip wiped, contents delivered in ordinary working manner (same interval drainage, same touch tip to wall receiver) into burette of certified graduation; five deliveries from each pipette, readings averaged; occupies afternoon, requires no apparatus creamery doesn't own save burette
- Urges reader to weigh statement about 1911 interval above anything computed in bulletin
- Emphasizes 1911 interval as evidence: nine weeks summer 1911, station's own pipette broken, borrowed measure used; patrons same, herds same, pasture same, season same, acid same, bottles same, tester and habits same; ONE variable changed; station's average test rose from 3.61 to 3.80 and held there nine weeks, returned to 3.61 day station's own measure came back; recorded contemporaneously in operator's hand by man who didn't know what recording and set question mark against it
- States this disposes of every explanation looking to herds, ground, or men for reason (all three unaltered, figures were not)
- Spent working life urging students to prefer experiment to calculation; here is one that ran itself under conditions designed trial could not have bettered; lay unread forty-seven years
- Conclusion: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

**Dell Rundle** (Schoolteacher at Larrow Green)
- Taught at the school at the Green for many years
- Compiled jubilee pamphlet "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years" published 1939, reprinted 1959
- Learned the history in pieces at funerals and in the back of the store for the whole of his tenure
- Described his work as "what the valley remembers, tidied," writing down with certainty where the valley was certain of a thing
- Noted that in 1891, the first check from the Association did something in their mother's kitchens (unprecedented cash payment once a month)
- Marked the significance of Article VII and its last clause about the test at the station where milk is delivered governing
- Observed that Gideon Vose put in the station governance clause "for convenience and it was convenient for thirty years, and it is the sentence on which the whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned"
- Documented the 1902 complaints, 1908 investigation, 1919 warning letter, 1923 condemnation, 1925 board proceedings
- Noted the state inspector condemned station's glassware on the 12th of July, 1923, "for reasons of his own that had nothing to do with the quarrel," and new glass was put in
- Recorded the settled opinion: "It is the settled opinion of the valley that cream left that station by the back door, and the figures went right when the man who kept it did not." Rundle sets it down as heard a hundred times, by men who were there and men who were not. "I have never heard it contradicted at the store."
- Documented that the station took in 40-44 million pounds of milk, with patrons' loss petition set at $19,600 to the dollar
- Added 1959 footnote: "The Hollow matter has since been taken up again at the college at Farland by Miss Ivy Keddie, who is second cousin to Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom. Eleven [note: actually nine per Ivy's documentation, though Rundle references "Eleven" in 1959] pipettes out of the station's old condemned crate were found still to be in existence. I am too old to rewrite a pamphlet and I refer the reader to the college."
- Note: "I would rather not write this chapter and it cannot be left out" (referring to the scandal chapter)
- **NOTE ADDED WHILE IN PRESS (in the 1939 pamphlet, discovered while proofs were being set):** While the pamphlet was being set up in type, Rundle was allowed to look at the Association's old ledgers in the office on another errand. The station account carries every year an entry in the bookkeeper's hand: "Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account." Rundle noted: "I must go back on Chapter IV. Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand. I leave it as written and correct it here." This represents Rundle's revision in the press, acknowledging that the heavy receipt of cream at Larrow Green contradicted the theft theory, and he corrected the record while the book was being published.

**Alonzo Frick** (Attorney, board chairman, Ordell County Bar Association)
- At seventy-four (in 1946), wrote article for Ordell County Bar Association yearbook
- Tried titles and boundaries for forty years, and two homicides
- Ostrey Hollow matter stayed with him more than anything he argued in courthouse
- Named chairman of board of arbitration (one of three), possibly because he kept no cow
- Sat three times: first session 17 March 1925 at Association's office at Ashlin; second and third sessions asked to have at Ostrey Hollow itself in station's own weigh room
- Board satisfied itself on all physical appliances; saw scales tried, sampling and test done, never saw anything hidden
- Board heard three explanations: (1) water by Selby Vose; (2) tester taking cream and writing low tests by Wilbur Teague; (3) wet hay/bog meadow by 1908 committee report read into record
- Ansel Keddie made statement to board on 17 March 1925
- Ansel denied taking cream and said he tested as taught and as state man had seen him do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons; believed him then and believes him now; his denial accurate but did him no good because man who cannot account for a figure presumed by everybody to be figure's author
- **Private corridor conversation at Ashlin with Keddie:** Ansel told Frick he always thought patrons at fault and would not say so in room; lived among them thirty years and would not stand up and accuse neighbors; recorded because it has been said since that Keddie went to grave accusing nobody (true) and suspecting nobody (not true)
- **Dismissal while board sitting (April 30, 1925, four days before award):** Association dismissed Ansel without finding against him; board had not concluded and did not conclude he did anything whatever; simply let go while board still sitting; Frick never able to think well of Association for it
- **May 4, 1925 award:** Found no cause established and awarded Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800 without admission to be charged to station account; patrons' own reckoning of loss was 67,000 pounds butterfat over years complained of; nothing brought forward to disturb that figure; board not able to say who owed it or why; said so
- Award without finding is unsatisfactory thing; every member of board knew it
- **Exhibits before board (four books and nothing else):**
  1. Test book of Ostrey Hollow station, 4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924, in hand of Ansel Keddie
  2. Receiving book of said station
  3. Private weigh book of Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom, begun 1 January 1897
  4. Circuit notebook of state dairy inspector for year 1923
- Short list for matter of that size; looked at it many times since
- **Reflection:** Board can only decide between accounts put to it; three were put to board; each urged by man of standing; each would have been believed by half valley; not one proved
- Wondered since whether answer was in that room and simply not in any of three stories; whether lawyer who understood chemistry might have found it; did not understand chemistry
- Understood that honest man was ruined by figure nobody could explain, and that board paid his neighbors money without being able to tell them what they were being paid for
- No interest in that quarrel and no competence in it; never read a test and would not know how; but when cause is found in soil, in herbage, in condition of stock, in College's trial, and in every wet bottom in county alike, it is not good sense to go looking for second cause in building

**Hobart Sill** (Buttermaker, Larrow Green creamery, retired 1934)
- Made Association's butter for forty-one years (1893–1934)
- Stood at churn from 1893; plank floor in that room then, cooled with creek ice out of pond
- Never sent out tub of butter he would not eat himself, which is more than office can say about anything it sent out
- Cream wagon came down from Ostrey Hollow every day but Sunday, seven miles hill road
- In July came warm no matter how early they started
- Set off at platform, weighed, sampled, went into cold room; Sill took it from there
- **The key observation:** Cream ran heavy; every year it ran heavy; more fat in cans than papers said had been bought up there
- Office would send down sheet saying so many pounds fat bought at station; fat that came into cold room off that wagon was more than sheet said; always more; never less
- Weighed it himself most years; platform man weighed it and Sill weighed it over again when came into cold room; never took another man's figure for what going into churn
- Cans off, cans on scale, cans into ice, book ruled and signed before Sill put coat on
- Receiving book shows Hollow's cream coming in heavy on page after page, in his own writing, for as long as he had been in room
- Cream is not milk and will not stand still and be counted; thick thing, clings, separates; any man who tells you he can put figure on cream to pound and hold it is man who never had arm in can up to elbow
- So it ran heavy; so does everybody's; Sill never made anything of it; nobody ever asked him to
- Own milk at Larrow Green (own patrons, men on creek) ran 3.78 taking one year with another right through those years
- Ordinary milk, ordinary county, ordinary cows; nothing wonderful about 3.78 and nothing shameful
- **The Hollow milk:** really a 3.85 and no better; Sill will say to any man in valley; wet ground up there but good cows; never had word to say against cows
- If figures they were paid on came out under 3.85, then somebody up that hill road was helping cans along; Sill said so at time and says it now
- **Nine families all got honest in one summer?** No; there was water in those cans; Sill said so at time
- One summer along in twenties, figures came up and stayed up; nothing on earth changed but season; men do not suddenly start pouring better milk out of same cows in same barns
- Something got stopped; something got said to somebody; Sill never knew what and never asked; day buttermaker starts asking about other men's cans is day he stops making butter
- Men hollering up there a long while before that, away back; 1902 is first Sill remembers of it, man in Larrow shouting his milk tested under brother-in-law's down creek; same feed and same water; what did Association mean by it
- Manager put out paper to them about it—1919—circular warning Ostrey Hollow patrons about water in milk, plain as that; Sill thought well of him for it; only plain thing office ever did; of course they hated him for it up Hollow; of course it changed nothing because paper never emptied can
- Offices for you; papers; Association has paper for everything and butter for nothing
- Men come in collar and tell Sill churn running slow when couldn't tell which end of cow gets up first
- Made butter that took premiums in three states out of that room; not one man in office ever came down to see how it was done; every one of them could tell him to half cent what fat was worth
- Twenty-seven cents; that is what fat was through those years, take one year with another; twenty-seven cents pound; not man in office could tell you what makes it churn
- **Station opening (May 1896):** When they opened that station up at Ostrey Hollow—that was ninety-six, in May, and there was crowd went up for it—they had no outfit of their own, not piece
- Ordered and not come, ready to start taking milk; talk of holding off whole season
- Sill said no, they can have mine; went to own bench and made up outfit out of own glass, own, that he had used and knew; went up valley on wagon; they opened on it
- Their own came spring after; Sill's came back to him; one piece chipped; never said word about that either
- First season they took in about nine hundred thousand pounds; May to end of year, so not even whole one; good deal of milk for hill station starting from nothing; biggest thing ever happened to Ostrey Hollow; Sill was glad enough to have hand in it
- Takes to heart: Sill took their cream off platform, made it into butter; butter was good—as good as any and better than most; never season it was not
- Whatever Hollow was doing with its cans, fat came down hill and came into Sill's churn and went out of room as good butter with Association name on tub
- Sixty-four years old at time of remarks (1934); thought about butter; never went up there but twice in life
- **Important notes for record:** Put in about butter; do not put in said anything against cows up there; cows never trouble; put in Hobart Sill made butter and it was good butter and stood at churn until day they gave him chair
- Lent his own glassware to open Ostrey Hollow station when their outfit hadn't arrived; one piece came back chipped
- Glassware was NOT the defective 1897 lot that caused the scandal

**Nyle Grigg** (Farmer, Larrow Green; investigating committee chairman)
- Farmer of standing
- Chairman of investigating committee appointed January 1908
- Sent report back into circulation in 1919 because same complaint raised again (11 years later)
- In 1919 covering letter, notes committee findings had been proved right by intervening science
- **Committee's work:** Went at it as plain farmers do; sampled herds through whole of 1908—every month of that year, in barn and on pasture, out of night's milk and morning's; did not let man know which day coming
- Weighed; watched test made and watched it made again
- Found what found and put in report
- **Grigg's standing:** No interest whatever in answer; not patron of that station; hauls to Larrow Green and always has; whatever Hollow paid or not paid never put cent in pocket nor took one out
- Chosen for work on that account and for no other; abused for finding by men on both sides, which Grigg takes to be fair sign that finding was honest one
- When committee wrote, had nothing to go on but own eyes and general opinion of practical men; knew committee of farmers saying wet hay thins cream would be told farmers will say anything about own land
- **Since publication (1908–1919):** State Agricultural College at Farland published Bulletin No. 214, feeding trial on bog hay carried out under College's own eye, weighed rations, no interest in outcome
- That trial found bog hay to depress butterfat test by two tenths in winter feeding
- Two tenths out of College that never heard of Hessel Bottom and no reason to flatter
- There is committee's finding, arrived at by another road and by better men, three years after committee set names to it
- Content to let matter rest where College and committee have jointly put it
- Remedy lies with land and not station; drain bottom, sweeten it, keep stock off in wet spring, feed coarse hay with mixed ration not by itself, Hollow will test with rest
- Go on as going and abuse man who reads figures and will be grumbling in twenty years' time as were grumbling when began, no better off
- **1908 committee report:** Found no dishonesty of any kind in any quarter; wet hay of bottom thins cream; remedy lies with land and not station
- Sampled herds January, February, March; milk whole and scales honest; opinion formed on ground not out of book that wet land gives quantity but withholds quality; cow cannot put into pail what keep never put into her
- Report received and accepted; question closed by it for eleven years until that season; men who reopened it brought nothing new but their impatience
- Committee of practical men saying wet hay thins cream would be told farmers will say anything about own land
- Sampled herds through whole of 1908 every month

**Dr. Roland Kepp** (Veterinary surgeon, Ashlin)
- Thirty years' practice
- Habit when called to herd doing badly to ask first what cattle standing on
- Found in thirty years answer to nine cases in ten is under animal's feet; tenth is in water she drinks
- Knows about soil, herbage, and mineral economy of cow
- Wet bottom is not rich meadow with too much water; distinct kind of ground with distinct chemistry
- Water is least part of it
- Where drainage is wanting, year's growth does not rot down as on hill field; lies sour; soil is acid, poor in lime, poor in phosphates
- Sweet grasses will not hold; crowded out within few seasons by sedge, rush, coarse tussock grasses
- That herbage carries bulk and water; does not carry lime or phosphorus in any quantity worth name; cut late because ground cannot be gotten onto early; late cutting takes out what little was in it
- Cow in milk is manufactory working on narrow margin; obliged to find material of milk somewhere
- If ration short of minerals, does not simply give less; robs own frame to make good; goes on robbing until cannot
- See consequences in any wet-meadow herd in county
- Stock come out of winter feeding thin over loin and rough in coat
- Slow to come to condition in spring
- Ready subjects for every disorder of digestion in fall
- Oftener called to that class of trouble on wet land than any other
- Breed later
- Feet are worse
- Will milk, gentlemen; wet land generous of flow; no man on bog meadow ever complained pail was light
- What is in pail is another matter; fat is first thing to suffer because fat is dearest thing cow makes; first economy she practices when pressed
- Wet bottoms of county yield milk poorer in fat by fifth of point; every practical man knows it
- Set hill herds against bottom herds and will find that difference, farm for farm, wherever you look; difference does not depend on breed, man, or barn
- Depends on ground
- Hessel Bottom is completest example of wet meadow in county
- Six farms cut hay and pasture stock upon it
- Lies along creek
- Springs come out at foot of slope and run in it year round
- Part under water every spring; not fit to be walked on until well into May
- Hay is sedge hay; on those farms winter dependence and very nearly whole of it
- Patrons of skimming station at Ostrey Hollow complained for quarter of century milk tests below rest of valley
- Complaint perfectly just; never doubted it
- Figure of gap, as given and as satisfied himself, is twenty-one hundredths of point — 0.21 — between station and valley at large
- Committee of practical men went over ground in 1908; found herds sound, milk whole, scales honest; laid cause to hay of that bottom
- Matter has been before committees since and before boards; not settled; will not be settled by men shouting at one another
- Settled already, in literature, for anybody who will read
- State Agricultural College at Farland conducted feeding trial on bog hay; published Bulletin No. 214
- College fed bog hay under control; measured what it did; found butter fat test depressed by two tenths in winter feeding
- Two tenths from College that never heard of Hessel Bottom and no reason to flatter
- Three agree so nearly (two tenths from College, fifth of point he found in field, twenty-one hundredths station complained of) that does not know what more reasonable man requires
- Objection: in summer of 1911 testing at that station broken off for six weeks; own apparatus failed; borrowed one used; figures stood higher during those six weeks than before or after
- Six weeks in flush of season; every herd in county at best; stock off wet ground and up on aftermath
- Astonished anybody should founded argument on six weeks of summer figures
- Could produce six weeks in any year that would prove anything you liked
- Remedy not mystery and not cheap
- Drain bottom; open old ditches and cut new; lime ground and lime it again
- Sow it down when will carry seeding
- Keep stock off in wet of spring (hoof does more harm in day than season will repair)
- Never feed that hay alone
- Feed with mixed ration and mineral supplement as College directs
- Fat was twenty-seven cents pound through those years; fifth of point taken off every hundredweight a farm ships year in and year out will pay for great deal of tile

## Summary

The milk-testing scandal of 1889–1925 is now fully resolved by science, corroborated by independent official records from two counties, cross-ridge verification, veterinary analysis, and explained by family testimony, managerial candor, institutional founding history, attorney testimony, buttermaker expertise, and **genealogical and family record documentation by Duncan Keddie, retired schoolteacher**. Multiple retellings (Duncan Keddie's family notes 1968/1970, Ivy Keddie's forensic analysis 1959, Wilbur Teague's bookkeeper testimony 1948, Merle Strawn's state inspector circuit notes 1907–1936, Roy Brant's farm account 1955, Halbert Nym's accession catalogue 1972, Selby Vose's annual address 1926, Rosalie Cudd's 1958 letter, Leland Vaught's Bulletin No. 471 introduction 1958, Dell Rundle's jubilee pamphlet 1939 with 1959 footnote, Alonzo Frick's 1946 bar association article, Hobart Sill's 1934 remarks, Nyle Grigg's 1919 covering letter, Dr. Roland Kepp's 1926 paper before Farm Club, Hazel Brant's 1981 recorded interview) prove definitively that the defective glassware—the pipettes—caused the consistently low test results, exonerating Ansel Keddie completely and demonstrating that the 76,000 to 118,000 pounds of butterfat discrepancy was measurement error, not theft or fraud.
