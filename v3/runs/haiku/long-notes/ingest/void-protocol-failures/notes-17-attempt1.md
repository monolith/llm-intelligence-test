## Critical Conflicts and Oddities (Expanded)

All prior conflicts plus:

27. **Exhibits discrepancy**: Frick's 1946 recollection lists only four exhibits before the board (test book, receiving book, weigh book, circuit notebook). No mention of the condemned pipettes, the crate, or measurement sheets. Yet Halbert Nym's accession notes (1972) indicate these items were discovered and preserved later (crate found February 1958, pipettes measured March 1958).

28. **Frick's corridor interview**: Ansel told Frick privately that he suspected the patrons (not himself) were at fault. This contradicts or complicates the public narrative where Ansel denies knowledge. Frick notes: "He was not a bitter man about it."

29. **No chemistry knowledge**: Frick explicitly admits not understanding the test and could not evaluate the chemistry. He relied on observing procedural consistency, not substance.

30. **Premature dismissal**: Frick expresses that he "never been able to think well of the Association for" dismissing Ansel on April 30, four days before the board made its award (May 4, 1925). This was still while the board was sitting.

31. **Grigg's 1919 statement implies scientific vindication was already emerging**: When Grigg says "eleven years have brought us some light from a quarter that had none to give us in 1908," he is referencing scientific developments between 1908–1919. This suggests apparatus issues, water testing methods, or bog hay research had advanced. Yet the Strawn condemnation of the pipettes did not occur until June 1923, and the Ivy Keddie measurement analysis not until March 1958.

32. **Nyle Grigg himself was part of the wrong theory**: Grigg headed the 1908 committee that blamed wet hay. His 1919 letter indicates he now believes his own committee was vindicated by new science—but the question remains: what science? And was it truly new information, or had Grigg changed his interpretation?

## Summary (Expanded)

The milk-testing scandal now has testimony from the arbitration board's chairman (Alonzo Frick, 1946 recollection), providing the only written account by a legal professional who witnessed the proceedings directly. Frick's account emphasizes:

1. The board examined physical appliances and observed consistent procedures.
2. Ansel Keddie privately believed patrons were at fault but would not publicly accuse them.
3. The three explanations (water, tester theft, wet hay) were all heard but none proven.
4. The award of $3,800 "without admission" on a finding of "no cause established" remains logically paradoxical.
5. Ansel's dismissal during the sitting of the board was, in Frick's judgment, inappropriate.

Additionally, the emergence of Nyle Grigg's 1919 covering letter confirms that:

1. The 1908 committee's report (which blamed wet hay) was significant enough to be reprinted in 1919.
2. By 1919, Grigg believed scientific developments had vindicated his committee's conclusions—though exactly what science is not yet clarified.
3. Grigg attributed this to "light from a quarter that had none to give us in 1908," suggesting external scientific research or methodology advances.

The timeline of vindication thus becomes more complex:
- 1908: Committee investigation (wet hay theory)
- 1909–1923: Strawn's circuit notes (including 1923 condemnation, not yet analyzed by arbitration board)
- 1919: Grigg's acknowledgment of scientific support for the committee
- 1925: Arbitration board award without finding of cause
- 1958: Ivy Keddie's forensic measurement and Bulletin No. 471 publication (definitive proof of pipette defect)

The gap between Grigg's 1919 optimism about vindication and the actual pipette-defect discovery in 1958 suggests either: (a) Grigg was referring to different scientific developments now lost to record, or (b) Grigg's statement was itself misunderstood or misremembered when reprinted.
