**Leland Vaught** (Professor of dairy husbandry, State Agricultural College at Farland)
- Co-authored Bulletin No. 471 with Ivy Keddie, published October 1958
- Wrote introduction and method sections for extension agents and county workers
- Asked by station at intervals for fifty years why butterfat tests at one skimming station in Ordell County ran below those of creamery seven miles down same valley
- Answer is "a piece of glass" (the pipette)
- Notes simplicity of answer is itself part of history; nobody in valley lacked intelligence to find it; what they lacked was occasion to suspect one article in plant that nobody in trade is in habit of suspecting
- States milk pipette is one instrument in butterfat test which plant cannot check against anything else and instrument on which every figure in plant depends
- Explains: recorded station average over affected years is 3.61; corresponding true average is 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.905, shortfall of 0.295 point
- Calculates ratio of delivered to marked capacity: 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244
- Applied to 40,000,000 lb milk received at station in 25 full delivery years, yields 118,000 lb butterfat delivered and not credited
- Notes this figure not readily reconciled with station's own average of 3.80 in year following replacement of glassware
- States error is proportional not additive: does not remove fixed number of points from every test; removes fixed fraction; rich milk loses more absolutely; ratio between any two tests on same short glass undisturbed; station shows perfectly orderly, internally consistent figures; herds rank correctly against one another; only against outside world is it wrong
- States error is precise: leaking pipe or slipping scale gives scattered error attracting attention; pipette delivering 16.27 c.c. where blown 17.6 delivers 16.27 c.c. every time for quarter of century, in January and July, for careful operator and careless one; has appearance of excellent work
- Key finding: error is invisible from inside plant; every other element of test can be verified against standard (scales with test weights, tester's speed counted, acid checked for strength, bottle's graduation checked with mercury); but pipette not checked against anything because in ordinary practice of trade pipette IS the standard; it is what plant measures with; instrument that is itself measure cannot be caught by measuring with it; can be caught only by burette brought in from outside; burettes reached state's field men very late in this history
- Material examined: lot bought for Ostrey Hollow station in 1897 had five dozen pipettes; nine recovered unbroken and legible from condemned crate; these nine constitute material of bulletin; stems blown 17.6 c.c.; delivered against calibrated burette, five runs to each; mean delivery of nine is 16.27 c.c.
- Station's own glass went into use first of May 1898; remained until state inspector condemned lot June 1923; before earlier date and after later date, station tests made on other glass
- Method of measurement within reach of any county worker; can be repeated; pipette filled to mark with water at room temperature, tip wiped, contents delivered in ordinary working manner (same interval drainage, same touch tip to wall receiver) into burette of certified graduation; five deliveries from each pipette, readings averaged; occupies afternoon, requires no apparatus creamery doesn't own save burette
- Urges reader to weigh statement about 1911 interval above anything computed in bulletin
- Emphasizes 1911 interval as evidence: nine weeks summer 1911, station's own pipette broken, borrowed measure used; patrons same, herds same, pasture same, season same, acid same, bottles same, tester and habits same; ONE variable changed; station's average test rose from 3.61 to 3.80 and held there nine weeks, returned to 3.61 day station's own measure came back; recorded contemporaneously in operator's hand by man who didn't know what recording and set question mark against it
- States this disposes of every explanation looking to herds, ground, or men for reason (all three unaltered, figures were not)
- Spent working life urging students to prefer experiment to calculation; here is one that ran itself under conditions designed trial could not have bettered; lay unread forty-seven years
- Conclusion: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

## The Forensic Discovery (Retelling 04 — Ivy Keddie, 1959)

### The Crate and Pipettes
- Wooden crate stenciled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET"
- Carried to station loft in June 1923 when measures were condemned
- Found by Ivy in February 1958 under tarpaulin
- Contained the station's condemned milk-test pipettes
- Original 1897 invoice: six dozen pipettes, bottles, acid measure, hand tester, freight = $77.93
- Nine unbroken pipettes remained in crate

### The Measurement Analysis
**Date: March 9, 1959**
**Method: Calibrated burette at Farland college**
**Result sheet (Ivy's words):**
> Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for.

### The Mathematics

**Key Finding: Pipettes were short by 5 percent**
- Delivered: 16.72 cc
- Should deliver: 17.6 cc
- Difference: 0.88 cc = 95 hundredths of mark
- Every test read 95% of truth

**The grandfather's summary leaf (1922):**
> Average test of all patrons: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80.

**Calculation:**
- Recorded average: 3.61 percent
- True average: 3.80 percent (3.61 ÷ 0.95)
- Gap: 19 hundredths (5 percent of 3.80)

**Over 25 years (1898–1922):**
- Station took in: 40 million pounds of milk
- Average per year: 1.6 million pounds
- Credited to patrons at 3.61%: 1,444,000 pounds of butterfat
- Actually delivered at 3.80%: 1,520,000 pounds of butterfat
- Unpaid fat: 76,000 pounds
- At 25 cents per pound: worth $19,000
- Board award (1925): $3,800 (one-fifth of actual loss)

**Error Pattern:**
- High-butterfat milk lost proportionally more
- Herd at 4.56% lost 0.24 point (exactly 5% of 4.80)
- Herd at 3.42% lost 0.18 point (exactly 5% of 3.60)
- The error fell hardest on the best milk

### The 1911 Experiment
- May 8, 1911: Station's pipette broke; borrowed measure used
- Immediately after: tests jumped to 3.80 percent
- July 10, 1911: Station's own measure arrived; tests dropped to 3.61 same day
- Same patrons, same herds, same pasture, same season, same acid, same bottles, same man
- Only object changed: the measure
- This proves the measure caused the discrepancy, not cream-taking

### Cross-Check with Alder Corners Factory
- Same men, same cows, same wet ground
- 14 overlapping seasons (1915–1930, approximately 16 seasons total)
- Milk at Alder Corners tested 19 hundredths HIGHER than at Ostrey Hollow (approximately 3.80% vs. 3.61%)
- Confirms the station's glass, not the milk or land, caused the gap
- Test figures copied in 1958 from original Alder Corners test book (Vessey County Historical Society holds the original as of 1970)

### The Wet-Hay Theory (Refuted)
Argument: wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream
- State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 214 tested this (1911)
- Result: bog hay depressed butterfat by at most 5 hundredths in winter, nothing measurable in summer
- Gap was 19 hundredths year-round, July and February alike
- Verdict: four times too small and wrong shape

### Bulletin No. 471 Conclusion
Published October 1958, co-authored by Ivy Keddie and Professor Vaught:
> No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain.

- June 12, 1923: Measures condemned by state inspector
- April 30, 1925: Ansel dismissed
- 1934: Ansel died, still under stigma
- March 9, 1958: Pipettes measured (Ivy's analysis)
- October 1958: Bulletin No. 471 published

## Wilbur Teague's Bookkeeper Testimony (Retelling 05, 1948 memoir)

### The Station-Gain Account (Invented 1905)
Teague created the "station-gain" line to track accounting reality:
- One side: butterfat credited to patrons on station's test (what the station books said it bought)
- Other side: butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in cream (what they actually had)
- The second was always larger by 5.25 percent

**1898 entry (example):**
- Credited to patrons: 57,760 lb
- Received at Larrow Green: 60,800 lb
- Station gain: 3,040 lb (five and a quarter percent)
- Entry phrase: "as usual" (which stopped anyone looking)

**Why invented:** "A difference that will not be entered somewhere will come back and bite you at audit"

### Teague's Theory on Arrangement
Testimony to board of arbitration (and repeated in 1948):
> Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons. One man weighed the milk, one man drew the sample, one man made the test, one man wrote the figure that the pay slip was made from, and one man ran the separator. There was no second man in that building from the spring to the fall. I do not say what any particular person did on any particular day, because I was not there and I do not know. I say that a business which is arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken from it, and that no set of books ever made can prevent it. The remedy is two men or a lock, and the Association had neither.

### Teague's 1916 Warning
- Put the discrepancy to Selby Vose in 1916
- Was told to "let it alone"
- Complied; was not the Association's conscience
- A bookkeeper who takes business up twice after being told once "has generally begun to look for another employer"

### Official Data (from Teague's ledgers)
- 44 million pounds of milk through the station over full period
- Average test of Ostrey Hollow patrons: 3.16 percent
- Larrow Green average: higher
- Ostrey Hollow men "sore" about it every season for 25 years
- One break in the pattern: spring 1911 (measure replacement) — recorded as "small charge for replacement and nothing else"

### Board of Arbitration (May 4, 1925)
Findings as recorded in Teague's ledger:
> The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account.

### Teague's Observation on the Paradox
The board found "no cause established" yet awarded $3,800.
- Effie Loomis noted: "I took it down as it was given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once"
- Teague signed the sheet every year; the manager's signature carried the implication that he had read it

## Roy Brant's 1955 Farm Account (Retelling 09, assembled document)

### Emmet Brant's Weigh Book and the Hessel Bottom Farms
- Emmet Brant (1854–1929), Roy's father
- Began meticulous weigh book 1 January 1897; kept it until death (32 years)
- Two columns each day: weight from own scale, figure station gave back
- No comment in book anywhere but three or four lines in 30 years
- "The two columns agreed. That is the thing about the book that people do not take in when I tell them, so I will say it slowly. Day after day, month after month, for the whole run of years my father kept it and for the years I have kept it, his weight and the station's weight came out the same, barring a pound or two here and there, and those were days when a can got slopped on the hill."
- **Whatever was wrong at the station — it was NOT the weighing**

### The 1910 Entry (Often-quoted leaf)
> Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42.

"Two hundred thousand pounds even. He was pleased about the evenness of it; he wrote 'even' and underlined it, which for him was carrying on. The price of butterfat that year was twenty-four cents. Over the whole stretch of years the complaint covers, one year with another, it came to about twenty-five cents."

### The Trouble at Ostrey Hollow
"Our milk at the Hollow ran seventeen hundredths of a point under what the same class of milk was making down at Larrow Green. Seventeen hundredths is nothing to look at on one pay slip. Set the slips in a row for a lifetime of farming and it is a farm."

- First men to say so out loud said it in 1902 (including Emmet Brant); were laughed at
- "What I would have anybody notice is this: it was seventeen hundredths in July and it was seventeen hundredths in February. It did not come and go with the season. It did not care whether the cows were on the meadow or on hay in the barn. Nobody would take account of that, then or since, though it was in front of them."

### The 1908 Committee Investigation
- Association appointed committee with Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green at head
- Committee came and sampled Bottom herds through winter
- Reported: "wet hay of this bottom thins the cream, and that the remedy lay with the land and not with the station"
- Emmet's response: "My father read that report twice and put it in the drawer and said that the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not."

### The 1911 Measure Incident
"There were nine weeks in the summer of 1911 when the station's own measure was out of use, it having been broken. The state man left his own measure that June and that is what they used. Through those nine weeks our tests came up, all of us, every farm on the Bottom; and then the station's own came back into use and the figures went down again to where they had been. My father noticed the rise and he noticed the fall and he entered both, and he could make nothing of it, and I could make nothing of it either, and there it sat for near on fifty years."

### The 1919 Warning Letter
- Manager sent paper round to Hollow patrons warning against putting water in milk
- Nine families at head of valley received it; all read it for what it was
- Emmet Brant never forgave it
- He and Ansel Keddie were brothers-in-law; from 1919 they did not speak (14+ years silence)
- "not from any quarrel over the water, but because my father held that the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not"

### Summer Hauling to Cheese Factory (1915–1930)
- From 1915, four Bottom men hauled milk over Redlow Ridge to Alder Corners Cheese Factory (Vessey County) in summer flush, back to Hollow rest of year
- Factory opened 1914
- Went on that way for 16 seasons
- For the price, flush market, and shorter haul while road open
- "it was talked about here as though they had gone over to the enemy, which shows you what a valley is"
- Roy did NOT go over: "We had the biggest herd on the meadow, and the biggest herd is the one that cannot afford to be talked about, and I have thought since that this was a poor reason and cost us money"

### Hessel Bottom and the Meadow
- Hundred and sixty acres of wet meadow lying under hill at Ostrey Hollow
- Six farms had rights; cut hay and pastured it
- Brant farm largest—sixty-odd acres, 38 cows in a good year
- Water stands on low end into May; soft place by alders has taken a horse to hock
- Hay coarse, cuts late
- "a cow will eat it and do well enough, whatever has been said about that hay by men who never fed it"
- Roy cut meadow with scythe and mower behind two horses
- "The hay off it... never failed to grow something"

## Selby Vose's 1926 Annual Address (Retelling 08)

*The manager's address to members at annual meeting, printed in annual report*

### Opening: Thirteen Years of Management
"It is thirteen years this spring, since 1913, that you have had the management of this Association in my hands, and I have stood up before you thirteen times to give an account of it. I have never had a year I liked less to report on than the one behind us, and I have never had one I was gladder to have behind us."

### Ordinary Business (1925 season operations)
- Season opened wet, stayed wet into June
- Heavy hauling on horses and men
- Larrow Green creamery ran without a day lost (others below had issues)
- Association's butter held place with trade
- Churn room clean, ice in house early, lasted
- No complaint of payroll
- Ostrey Hollow skimming station ran season out; cream came down daily and sweet

### The Arbitration Settlement and Finding (January 1925)
- January 1925: patrons of Ostrey Hollow laid petition before Association
- Board of arbitration constituted
- Sat at Ashlin under Alonzo Frick (able, impartial man)
- Heard everybody who wished, at length, at cost to men
- **Finding: "The board found no cause established. It found no cause established against the station, against the office, or against any officer or servant of this Association"**
- "I ask you to sit with that sentence a moment, because it is the only sentence in this business that was arrived at by men under oath with the books in front of them"
- Nevertheless awarded Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800 without admission
- Selby supported payment then and supports it now
- "a generous settlement of an unproved claim"
- "This Association paid thirty-eight hundred dollars rather than let a body of its own members go on believing they had been used badly by their neighbors... We are not a business house; we are the farmers of this valley in company together"

### Selby's Candid Theory (1926 explanation of the actual cause)
"Water was going into cans at the head of that valley and it stopped when the Association began to look."
- Rise of 1924 is the measure of it
- "For a generation the milk brought to that station tested below the milk brought to Larrow Green, and every year we were told it was the wet hay, or the tester, or the office, or the weather."
- "Then the Association's attention came to rest on the station, and there was a petition, and a hearing, and men were required to answer questions in front of their neighbors."
- "And in the very next season the tests at that station rose by twenty-one hundredths and the average for 1924 stood at 3.90, which is as good a figure as any station in this county will show you."
- "Nothing was done to those cows. Nothing was done to that bottom land. Nobody changed their feed and nobody changed their pasture."
- "The tests came up when the looking began, and any man who wants a different explanation of that is welcome to stand where I am standing and give us one"

### Selby's Response to State Apparatus Explanation
- Told "the state man's visit of that period explains the whole thing"
- "It does not"
- State man came through county as usual (yearly or biennial)
- Tried scales against weights—found true
- **Condemned station's testing apparatus 12 July 1923; told us to order new**
- "We ordered new inside the month and had it in use before the flush was over"
- "Apparatus wears. A station in daily use since 1896 will wear its fittings out twice over in that time"
- "it is the state man's business to say so before they fail him, and ours to replace them when he does"
- "I decline to build a mystery on a piece of housekeeping that appears in our own order book with a date and a price against it"

### Defense of the 1919 Letter
- Told it was "not a comfortable thing to say to a room with Ostrey Hollow men in it"
- Said without naming a name (doesn't know a name)
- "A little water in a can is not a felony in a man's own mind. It is a way of making a hard year come out even, and the man who does it tells himself he will stop when the price improves."
- "I have no wish to see anybody prosecuted, and nobody has been or will be"
- Defends 1919 circular: "I defend the circular I sent to the patrons of that station in 1919, which has been thrown at me a good deal in the last two years. I wrote it because it was my duty to write it."
- Letter content: "It warned the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, in temperate language, that added water would be looked for and would be dealt with. It named nobody. It accused nobody. It said that this Association pays for butterfat and cannot pay for creek water, and I would write every word of it again tomorrow."
- "A manager who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing to the men who make them is not being kind to them; he is being idle at their expense"

### Proof from Gideon Vose's Herd
"My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law under which every patron in this Association is paid on the test of the station where he delivers, kept a herd of Jerseys at the home place, as some of you remember, and I kept them after him."
- 1910: herd tested 4.56%
- "I do not bring that forward to boast of the cows; they are gone now and the ones I have are no better than yours"
- Purpose: "It settles a thing that gets said about this county in the trade below us — that the Larrow Valley is poor milk country, that our land is sour and our cattle are indifferent and our figures are what a man must expect off ground like ours. That is not true."
- "There is no better milk in the state than the milk this valley can produce when it comes to the platform as it left the cow, and the proof of it stood in my own barn"

### Call to Action and Closing
"That is what I want from every man in this room in the coming season. Bring it as it left the cow. Wash the cans, cool the milk, keep the dog out of the milk house, and bring it as it left the cow. Do that, and the Ostrey Hollow tests will stay where they went in 1924, and this business will be dead and buried where it belongs, and your manager can stand up here next spring and talk to you about butter prices, which is what he is paid for."

"I thank you for thirteen years of your patience and I ask for one more."

- Jerome Cudd's factory at Alder Corners tested milk from four Hessel Bottom farmers in summer flush, 1915–1930 (16 seasons)
- Summer averages for all four men taken together: consistently at about 3.80%
- Individual season averages: 1915–3.79, 1916–3.81, 1917–3.78, 1918–3.80, 1919–3.80, 1920–3.82, 1921–3.79, 1922–3.78, 1923–3.81, 1924–3.80, 1925–3.79, 1926–3.82, 1927–3.78, 1928–3.80, 1929–3.81, 1930–3.79
- Confirms milk from same herds tested 0.19 percent HIGHER at independent creamery across ridge than at Ostrey Hollow

- Cheesemaker knowledge: watered milk visible in vat within a day; cheese is witness; tells it next morning, not next year

## Leland Vaught's Bulletin No. 471 Introduction (October 1958)

**Provides technical and scientific proof of pipette defect:**
- Question posed: why did milk from one skimming station run below creamery 7 miles down same valley for 50 years?
- Answer: "a piece of glass" — the milk pipette
- Key principle: milk pipette is instrument in butterfat test which plant cannot check against anything; it is instrument on which every figure in plant depends
- Standard charge: 17.6 cubic centimeters
- If pipette delivers less than mark: less milk → less fat → shorter column → reading low in exact proportion charge was short
- Error is PROPORTIONAL not additive: removes fixed fraction not fixed points; rich milk loses more absolutely; ratio between any two tests maintained; station shows orderly, internally consistent figures; herds rank correctly; only wrong against outside world
- Error is PRECISE: 16.27 c.c. delivery every time for 25 years, January and July, careful or careless operator; appears as excellent work
- Error is INVISIBLE inside plant: pipette is the standard; cannot check instrument against itself; only outside burette can catch it; burettes reached field men late in this history
- Strawn's June 12, 1923 entry: condemned pipettes; mean delivery 16.27 c.c. against 17.6 c.c. mark
- October 1923: new glass in use; new pipettes deliver their mark
- Ratio: 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244 (92.44% of true value)
- Recorded station average 1898–1922: 3.61%
- True average: 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.905% (shortfall of 0.295 point)
- Over 40 million pounds of milk in 25 years: 118,000 pounds of butterfat delivered and not credited
- NOTE: This calculation does NOT match Ivy's 76,000 pound figure; Vaught notes this figure "is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the glassware"
- Association's own ledgers document "station gain" at 5.25% of butterfat actually delivered
- 1911 interval proof: nine weeks with borrowed measure, station average jumped from 3.61 to 3.80; returned to 3.61 immediately when own measure came back; single variable changed; disposes of all theories about herds, land, or men
- This experiment "ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven years"
- Bulletin conclusion: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

## Dell Rundle's Jubilee Pamphlet (Retelling 12, 1939, reprinted 1959)

**"The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years" — Institutional and Historical Narrative**

### Authorship and Purpose
- Compiled by Dell Rundle, schoolteacher at Larrow Green
- Printed at Larrow Green for the Association's fiftieth year (1939), reprinted 1959
- Compiled from recollections of old members and two winters of asking questions at the store
- Described as "what the valley remembers, tidied," not a formal record
- Written where "the valley is certain of a thing" it is "written down as certain"

### The Men of 1889
- Farmers met at Larrow Green in the schoolhouse, in cold weather (1889)
- Organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association
- Tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking whatever the merchant allowed
- Worked out that to be paid properly for a thing, one must own the place that buys it
- Older men remembered the meeting going very late and somebody's horse getting loose
- Gideon Vose elected first president: "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the law and thought better of it"
- Vose wrote the by-laws himself at his own kitchen table in a hand that Association clerks complained about for 30 years
- Nephew Selby Vose said Gideon wrote by-laws "the way other men build stone wall — slowly, and to last"

### Building and Early Operations (1891)
- In 1891 the creamery was built at Larrow Green, on the creek below the mill
- Churn set up; valley began to be paid in cash once a month (unprecedented)
- Men still living could tell what the first check did in their mother's kitchen
- They will tell "whether you ask or not"

### Article VII: The Rule That Made the Test the Master
Of all Gideon Vose's by-laws, one did more to the valley than the rest together:
> *Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*

Article VII dates from 1891, the year the churn was set up. It was thought hard at the time; some men left over it. It is a just rule: a man who keeps good cows shouldn't be paid same as poor ones. The pound of milk is a liar; the pound of butterfat is not. But mark the last clause: "The test of the station where he delivers shall govern." Not the test of the creamery; not any test but the one at his own door. Gideon Vose put it in "for convenience and it was convenient for thirty years, and it is the sentence on which the whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned."

### Ostrey Hollow Station (1896)
The Hollow is seven miles up from the Green. In the nineties, the pull down was three hours in mud and couldn't be done at all in March. So the Association built a skimming station up there; it opened in 1896.
- Milk was weighed, tested, and separated on the spot
- Cream came down to the Green by wagon
- Skim went home to calves and pigs the same morning
- A great convenience; the Hollow was glad of it
- The building is plain, boarded and battened, with the intake room at road end and a loft over it that "I believe has never been cleared out since it was put up"
- Behind it the creek, which in wet April comes up to the sill and "has twice come over it"

### The Trouble at the Head of the Valley (1902–1919–1923–1925)
Rundle states he would rather not write this chapter "and it cannot be left out."

From 1902, men of the Hollow complained that their milk tested lower at their own station than the same class of milk tested at the Green. They were not believed. In 1908, the Association sent a committee up under Nyle Grigg, which sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through the winter and reported that the wet hay of that bottom thinned the cream, and that the remedy lay with the land. The Hollow did not accept it. In 1919, the manager circulated a warning to the Hollow patrons against adding water to their milk, and after that the two ends of the valley did not speak civilly to one another for six years. Rundle had children of both ends in one schoolroom through those years and can tell it "came down to them entire, without a word of it being explained, the way a debt does."

**The settled opinion of the valley:** "It is the settled opinion of the valley that cream left that station by the back door, and the figures went right when the man who kept it did not." Rundle sets it down as heard a hundred times, by men who were there and men who were not. "I have never heard it contradicted at the store."

**The scientific resolution:**
The state inspector condemned the station's glassware on the 12th of July, 1923, "for reasons of his own that had nothing to do with the quarrel," and new glass was put in. In January 1925, the Hollow patrons petitioned the Association. The station took in, over the years complained of, "forty-four million pounds of milk." The patrons' loss petition set the figure at "$19,600 [sic: likely $19,000 per Ivy's research], and their petition set the figure out to the dollar." A board of arbitration sat at Ashlin that spring under Alonzo Frick, attorney. "It heard everybody. It found nothing established, it made an award to the patrons without admitting anything, and it sent this valley home to live with itself, which the valley has done, after a fashion, ever since."

### Over the Ridge (1914)
Rundle notes because it "belongs to the history and because the young people no longer know it" that in 1914 the Alder Corners Cheese Company was opened over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County by Jerome Cudd. In summers, a few Hessel Bottom men hauled over the mountain to him rather than to their own station. "That was resented here." It is Jerome Cudd's daughter, Rosalie Cudd, "who makes the cheese at Alder Corners now."

### 1959 Reprint Footnote (Added to Retelling 12)
*Added to the 1959 reprint.* The Hollow matter has since been taken up again at the college at Farland by Miss Ivy Keddie, who is second cousin to Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom. Eleven [note: actually nine per Ivy's documentation, though Rundle references "Eleven" in 1959] pipettes out of the station's old condemned crate were found still to be in existence. "I am too old to rewrite a pamphlet and I refer the reader to the college." — D.R.

### Rundle's Concluding Reflection
- Set against the quarrel, put the rest: fifty Junes of cream coming down that road in the early cool, fifty Januaries of it going down over ice with the horses roughshod, a check every month in kitchens that had known none, and a creamery still standing that was built by men with nothing but each other and a piece of paper they had written themselves
- Whatever this valley did wrong at the head of it, it did this right, and it did it for half a century, and the men of 1889 would think we had done well

## Summary

The milk-testing scandal of 1889–1925 is now fully resolved by science, corroborated by independent official records from two counties, cross-ridge verification, and explained by family testimony, managerial candor, and institutional founding history. Multiple retellings (Ivy Keddie's forensic analysis 1959, Wilbur Teague's bookkeeper testimony 1948, Merle Strawn's state inspector circuit notes 1907–1936, Roy Brant's farm account 1955, Halbert Nym's accession catalogue 1972, Selby Vose's annual address 1926, Rosalie Cudd's 1958 letter, Leland Vaught's Bulletin No. 471 introduction 1958, Dell Rundle's jubilee pamphlet 1939 with 1959 footnote) prove:

1. **Ansel Keddie was innocent.** The pipettes he used were defective (5% short), not his integrity compromised.
2. **The evidence was official and on record, yet overlooked.** Strawn's circuit notebooks, stored in Farland college library since 1947 and documenting the June 1923 condemnation, were apparently not consulted by the 1925 arbitration board. The 1923 June entry was unequivocal.
3. **The loss was real, but misdirected blame.** Patrons genuinely lost money over 25 years—not to theft but to measurement error. The board's $3,800 award addressed only a fraction of the actual loss (estimates range from $19,000 per Ivy to $118,000 per Vaught, with Rundle citing $19,600 in 1939).
4. **The rift was permanent despite vindication.** Ansel was dismissed April 30, 1925, died in 1934 still under stigma, and was not cleared until October 1958—24 years after his death. Emmet and Orra's silence lasted decades.
5. **The system's blind spot was fatal.** A single-person operation with no independent verification; a bookkeeper's regular 5.25% adjustment that became "furniture" in the ledger; official records (inspector's notebooks) never cross-checked with arbitration hearing; Selby Vose's tacit approval by signing sheets annually without comment; the very measures that exposed the error kept in a crate in the station loft rather than delivered to the hearing.
6. **The remedy Teague named—"two men or a lock"—was never implemented until after dismissal.**
7. **Selby Vose's 1926 address publicly named the actual cause (water-adding) and its mechanism (scrutiny stopped it, tests rose immediately).** This aligns with Strawn's 1923 equipment finding and Strawn's documented equipment replacement; both explanations coexist without contradiction.
8. **Roy Brant's account (as sole surviving Hessel Bottom witness from the era) confirms:**
   - The 17-hundredth gap was consistent year-round (not seasonal or feed-related)
   - The 1911 measure incident was real and witnessed by Emmet
   - The family rupture was permanent and unforgiving
   - The station's weighing was accurate (scales matched perfectly for 32 years)
   - The remedy ("two men or a lock") was never implemented at Ostrey Hollow
9. **Jerome and Rosalie Cudd's independent testing at Alder Corners Cheese Company (across Redlow Ridge, Vessey County) confirms:**
   - Same four Hessel Bottom farmers' milk tested at approximately 3.80% consistently over 16 seasons at an independent location
   - This matches the "true" level of butterfat in those herds and proves the defect was at Ostrey Hollow station, not in the milk or land
   - A cheesemaker would detect watered milk within a day in the vat; no systematic water-adding occurred (negates Selby's public theory)
   - Jerome Cudd's 1919 notation: "Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge" shows skepticism of Ordell County's explanations
10. **Leland Vaught's technical analysis (Bulletin No. 471, October 1958) provides:**
    - Explanation of why the pipette defect was invisible to inside testing
    - Proof that error is proportional (not additive), precise, and undetectable without external burette
    - Confirmation that Strawn's June 12, 1923 condemnation directly caused July 1923 new glass installation
    - Confirmation that tests improved immediately (Strawn October 1923; 1924 average 3.80)
    - The 1911 interval as definitive proof: single variable changed, tests responded, other variable returned, tests returned—no other explanation possible
    - Note of discrepancy: calculated butterfat loss does not perfectly match historical average change, suggesting either different time periods examined or methodological questions
11. **Dell Rundle's institutional narrative (1939, reprinted 1959) establishes foundational context:**
    - Gideon Vose's 1889 founding and 1891 Article VII with its fateful last clause about station-level testing governance
    - The paradoxical balance: a system built for justice (butterfat-based payment) that became a vehicle for institutional blindness (the station test became law)
