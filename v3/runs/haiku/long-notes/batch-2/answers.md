# Batch 2 — Answers

## Section A — Reconstruction (Stories 4–6)

### Story 4: The Investigation Committee and the "Wet Hay" Theory (1902–1908)

In 1902, farmers at Ostrey Hollow began complaining that their milk tested lower in butterfat than milk from other parts of the valley—roughly 0.21 percentage points below the valley average. The discrepancy appeared year-round, consistent in winter and summer. The Association ignored the complaints for six years. In January 1908, after renewed complaints, the Association appointed Nyle Grigg, a farmer of standing with no interest in the outcome (he hauled to Larrow Green and had no financial stake in Ostrey Hollow), to lead an investigating committee. Grigg and his team sampled herds throughout 1908—every month, in barn and pasture, without warning—and watched the testing process directly. They found no dishonesty. The milk was whole, the scales were honest. Their conclusion: the wet hay and bog meadow of Hessel Bottom, where four of six farms on the bottom kept their herds, naturally produced thinner cream. Wet meadows yield milk poorer in fat because cows on poor mineral-deficient forage cannot maintain butterfat yield. Dr. Roland Kepp, a veterinarian with thirty years' practice, corroborated this; State Agricultural College at Farland published Bulletin No. 214 confirming that bog hay depressed butterfat by two tenths in winter. Grigg's findings aligned with the College work and established that land, not station practice, was the root cause. The committee's report was accepted and the question was considered closed. The investigation validated the 3.61% average as a product of Hessel Bottom's soil, not any deficiency in testing. This narrative—that wet hay caused thin cream—became the valley's settled explanation for the gap and would persist through the 1925 arbitration hearing. The committee's work was thorough, independent, and arrived at by farmers' eyes and practical experience, which gave it credibility in that valley.

### Story 5: The 1911 Borrowed Measure—Evidence Under Controlled Conditions (May–July 1911)

On May 8, 1911, Ansel Keddie's station measure broke. For nine weeks—approximately June 10 to July 10—he used a borrowed pipette from another source. During this interval, with every other element of the testing process identical, the station's test results rose and held steady at 3.80%, a gain of 0.19 percentage points over the chronic 3.61% average. When the borrowed measure was returned and Keddie resumed using (presumably) another replacement, tests fell back immediately to 3.61%. Keddie noted the fact in his test book and marked it with a question mark—"it was something in the glass." He never dared put it in the books explicitly because, as his son Duncan later understood, Keddie refused to write down what he could not prove.

This nine-week interval is the single most decisive piece of evidence in the case. It constituted a true experiment: the variable was the measuring glass; herds, pasture, season, tester, methodology, and every other factor remained constant. The only change was the instrument itself. Leland Vaught, a dairy scientist, emphasized this: "here is one that ran itself under conditions designed trial could not have bettered." The result proves the test figures themselves were low due to apparatus, not due to herds, land, water added by patrons, or the tester's practice. The interval established that Ansel Keddie was testing correctly and consistently; the measured quantity was the problem. What it did not establish: whether the glass was intentionally defective, whether anyone knew of the defect before 1923, or the precise magnitude of the error across all years. That information came later, from the 1923 condemnation and 1958 scientific analysis.

### Story 6: The 1919 Water-Warning Letter and the Family Rupture (June 1919)

In June 1919, thirteen years after the investigation committee's report, Selby Vose, the Association's manager since 1913, sent a circular letter to Ostrey Hollow patrons warning them not to add water to their milk. The circular was civil in form but unambiguous in meaning. Instead of leaving the letters on the platform for the patrons to collect, Ansel Keddie took the stack and delivered them personally to all eleven families, standing at each door while the man read it. He came home at eleven that night with his hat still on. Two days later, Emmet Brant, the largest milk patron and Ansel's brother-in-law (married to Orra), came up to the station. He did not enter the house. He stood in the yard and asked Ansel why he had carried the paper himself if he did not believe the accusation. Ansel said he had carried it because he would not have another man carry it—meaning he would not delegate a task he believed was unjust. Emmet went home. For the next fourteen years, the two men did not speak. Not one word. Orra took a different road to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane. When Emmet died in spring 1929, the rift was still absolute; Orra went to the funeral and sat an hour in her childhood kitchen, then came home.

Vose's circular implied that patrons at Ostrey Hollow were adding water to thin-cream milk. The previous theory—wet hay as the cause—was abandoned in favor of an accusation of patron dishonesty. Yet the state dairy inspector's records show that he tested for water at every visit from 1908 forward and never found any. The circular's effect was to shift blame from land and soil to the men who milked the cows. It deepened the valley's conviction that something was wrong at the Hollow and that someone was at fault—an assumption that would color the 1925 arbitration hearing thirteen years later.

## Section C — Math

### C3. Butterfat Accounting (Full Delivery Years)

**Sources and working:**

Wilbur Teague's annual ledger entry (representative of all years 1905–1925):
- Butterfat **credited** to patrons on station test: 57,760 lb
- Butterfat **received** at Larrow Green in station cream: 60,800 lb
- Station gain: 3,040 lb = 5.25% discrepancy

Over 25 full delivery years (1898–1922):

**(a) Total pounds credited to patrons:**
- Annual average: 57,760 lb
- Over 25 years: 57,760 × 25 = **1,444,000 lb**
- Cross-check via percentage: 40,000,000 lb milk × 3.61% = 1,444,000 lb ✓

**(b) Total pounds actually received at Larrow Green:**
- Annual average: 60,800 lb
- Over 25 years: 60,800 × 25 = **1,520,000 lb**

**(c) Difference as percentage:**
- Difference: 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 lb
- Expressed as percentage **of the amount credited**: 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.26%**
- Expressed as percentage **of the amount received**: 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = 5.00%

**Answer:** The 5.26% (of credited amount) aligns with Teague's ledger notation "five and a quarter per cent, as usual."

### C5. Duration of Faulty Glassware and First Season

**(a) Duration of faulty glassware in use:**

- **Exact time in service:** May 1, 1897 (station opened with own glass) to June 12, 1923 (condemned by state inspector)
  - Duration: **26 years, 1 month, 12 days** (or **26.11 years**)

- **Over how many full delivery years:** **25 full years** (1898–1922)
  - 1897: partial year (May–December only)
  - 1898–1922: 25 complete calendar/delivery years
  - 1923: partial year (January–June only)

**Why they differ:** The time duration is measured calendar-to-calendar. The "full delivery years" count only complete seasons when the station operated the full year. The math calculations in Leland Vaught's analysis use 25 full years as the denominator because the first and last partial years cannot be precisely quantified as to full butterfat content.

**(b) First season milk received:**

**Sources conflict; resolution:**
- Bertram Kyle (office clerk, copied records): "good eleven hundred thousand pounds" but notes this figure is "not in any book [he] ever ruled" and came from men who were there
- Kyle also says: "not even whole one" season, running May to end of year
- Same source later: "about nine hundred thousand pounds"

**Answer: approximately 900,000–1,100,000 pounds (May–December 1897)**

The first season was partial (9 months) and the records are not fully preserved in the books Kyle handled. The lower figure (900,000) is more consistent with a typical May-to-December partial run; the higher figure reflects some accounts of the amount. **Cannot be determined with precision.** Sources establish it was substantial for a startup (larger than typical later-year figures per month, suggesting 900,000–1,100,000 lb total) but exact figures are not recorded in surviving books.

## Section D — Logic

### D1. The 1911 Borrowed Measure Experiment

**When and duration:**
May 8, 1911 (when Ansel's measure broke) through approximately July 10, 1911. Leland Vaught describes it as "nine weeks summer 1911." Orra Keddie's diary places the breakage on May 8; the interval lasted until the station's measure was repaired or replaced.

**The experiment structure:**
- **Variable changed:** The measuring pipette (from station's faulty glass to borrowed measure)
- **What was held constant:** Same tester (Ansel Keddie), same herds, same pastures (summer flush June–July), same season, same operator methodology, same acid, same bottles, same test procedure
- **Result:** Tests rose from the chronic 3.61% average to 3.80% and held there for the nine weeks, then fell back to 3.61% when the station's own measure returned

**What it establishes:**
That the measuring apparatus, not the milk quality, patrons' practices, or the tester's competence, was causing the low figures. The only variable changed; all others remained identical. This eliminates every explanation based on herds, land, soil, or patron dishonesty in a single closed interval.

**What it does NOT establish:**
- Whether the glass was intentionally defective or accidentally so
- Whether the defect was present from the beginning or developed over time
- Whether anyone at the station knew of the defect before 1923
- The exact magnitude of the error (0.19 point is the observable gap in this interval, but full quantification required 1958 burette testing)
- Whether all of the 0.19-point gap is attributable to the glass or partly to other factors (though Vaught's subsequent analysis shows it was entirely the glass)

### D2. Three Independent Reasons the Tester Could Not Have Been Stealing Cream

**Reason 1: Station Gain Evidence (Physical Weights—Independent of Test Figures)**
Wilbur Teague, Hobart Sill, and the ledgers show that cream arrived at Larrow Green creamery *heavier* than the station test figures credited—every year, 5.25% more. If Ansel Keddie had been stealing cream from the cans at Ostrey Hollow, fewer pounds would arrive at the creamery, not more. The physical cream received contradicts the theft hypothesis directly. This evidence is independent because it rests on the weights on the Larrow Green platform, not on anything the tester recorded or did at Ostrey Hollow.

**Reason 2: The 1911 Borrowed Measure Experiment (Apparatus, Not Tester Competence)**
When the tester used a borrowed measuring glass for nine weeks, test results rose. Same operator, same methodology, same herds, same everything except the glass. This proves Keddie's testing procedure was consistent and correct; the instrument was at fault. A thief would produce low tests through either (a) taking cream and writing low to hide it, or (b) incompetence. The borrowed-measure interval shows neither: the tester's methodology is unimpeachable. This evidence is independent because it isolates the apparatus and holds the operator constant.

**Reason 3: Observer Testimony and State Inspection (External Observation)**
The 1908 investigation committee sampled herds and watched the testing done. They found milk whole and scales honest. In 1923, the state dairy inspector ran the glassware against a burette in the station itself and condemned it. Both observations by outside parties with no stake in local disputes would detect cream theft; both found the scales true. This evidence is independent because it rests on direct observation by outsiders, not on records the station kept.

**Independence:** Each of these three sources stems from a different chain of evidence: (1) receiving weights at another location; (2) controlled interval with measurable output; (3) external observation. None requires the others to be true.

**Which would hold if the glass had never been measured in 1958?**
All three would still hold:
1. The station-gain figures were recorded contemporaneously in ledgers from 1905 onward; they don't require 1958 analysis.
2. The 1911 interval was noted contemporaneously by the tester himself (question mark in test book); Vaught merely analyzed it retrospectively.
3. The state inspector's 1923 condemnation was recorded in his circuit notebook in real time; the 1958 analysis simply confirmed why the glass was defective.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question the ledger entries?**
No. Wilbur Teague created the "station gain" line annually and entered "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" with apparent acceptance of it as normal accounting. His memoir does not express doubt about the ledger entries; he presents them as fact. Bertram Kyle, the office clerk, says he copied this line scores of times without wondering. Teague warned Selby Vose about the discrepancy in 1916; Vose told him to "let it alone." Neither the bookkeeper nor anyone else in the office connected the station-gain line to the possibility of defective apparatus. The board of arbitration in 1925 had these same four books before them—Keddie's test book, the station receiving book, Brant's weigh book, and the inspector's 1923 notebook—and apparently never cross-referenced the station-gain line against the other evidence.

### D3. Did Selby Vose Know Tests Ran Low Before 1923?

**Answer: Yes, he knew the tests ran low. No, he did not know the cause.**

**Justification:**

*Evidence that he knew the station's tests ran low:*
1. Wilbur Teague warned him in 1916 about the "station gain" account showing a consistent 5.25% shortfall. Teague told him and Vose told Teague to "let it alone."
2. Vose signed the annual ledger sheets every year from 1913 onward, including the line carrying the station-gain notation "five and a quarter per cent, as usual."
3. Vose sent a circular warning in 1919 about water in milk at Ostrey Hollow—an implicit acknowledgment that something was wrong with the station's milk or testing.

*Evidence that he did not understand the cause:*
1. Vose's theory, as stated to board chairman Alonzo Frick in a closed-door conversation, was that water had been added to milk by patrons. This is the wrong explanation.
2. Bertram Kyle observed that Vose "read totals and he never read lines above them"—he would "take sheet, turn it, put thumb on bottom figure, and sign" without examining the detail. This suggests he saw the numbers without understanding their implications.
3. Vose defended the 1919 water-warning letter at the 1926 annual meeting, saying "I wrote it because it was my duty to write it." He believed water added was the issue. No contemporary source quotes him saying the glass was defective; his theory was consistently about patron conduct, not apparatus.
4. When state inspector Merle Strawn condemned the glass in June 1923, the records show no record of surprise or vindication in Vose's subsequent statements—suggesting he may not have immediately connected the glass defect to the long-standing test shortfall.

**Conclusion:** Vose knew (from Teague's warning and the ledger figures he signed annually) that butterfat credits ran low and more cream arrived than the test figures accounted for. But he attributed this to water adding, not to defective glassware. He did not know the root cause.
