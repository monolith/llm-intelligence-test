# Batch 3 Answers

## Section A — Reconstruction of Stories 7 and 8

### Story 7: The Carpenter (Ansel Keddie's Life After Dismissal, 1925–1934)

On April 30, 1925, Ansel Keddie walked home from the Ostrey Hollow station after twenty-nine seasons. He hung his apron behind the shed door and asked what was for supper. Ham, Orra wrote. He ate it.

The board of arbitration had not yet issued its award when the Association dismissed him—four days early, while the hearing was still sitting. No finding against him was made; the dismissal came without cause stated. He was fifty-four years old and had weighed milk at that station since before his neighbors' children were born. The award, when it came on May 4, would be $3,800 to the patrons, without admission of what they were being paid for.

He took up carpentry. Orra's diary marks the shift: "June 1925: He has begun on the cow shed, which has wanted doing since his father's time. He works at it from light to dark and whistles, which he never did when he was paid." For sixty-six years old, he showed a skill he had never exercised when the work came with wages. He hung barn doors for neighbors at a quarter of his former pay. He mended stairs and gates in the hollow.

He lived nine years with the imputation on him. He walked among men he had tested milk for since before their fathers died. In March 1929, his brother-in-law Emmet died—the man who had stopped speaking to him in 1919 and never resumed. Orra went down to the funeral. Roy Brant, Emmet's son, was kind to her.

Ansel never spoke of the matter publicly after dismissal. His son Duncan later noted: "my father lived nine years after dismissal with imputation on him, among men he had weighed milk for since before their fathers died." Ansel died in February 1934 without much pain at the end. Men came to his funeral who had not spoken to him in nine years. Not one of them mentioned cream or tests. They talked of his hands with doors. He carried the suspicion to the grave and all his neighbors carried it with him.

### Story 8: The Granddaughter's Proof (The 1958 Scientific Exoneration)

Thirty-five years after the board's hearing and sixty years after the station opened, Ivy Keddie—Ansel's granddaughter and a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland—descended a ladder in the loft above the Ostrey Hollow station's intake room. She found a crate under a tarpaulin thick with chaff. It was the station's old glass, condemned in June 1923 and abandoned.

The crate held five dozen milk-test pipettes marked 17.6 cubic centimeters each. Nine remained intact and legible.

In March 1958, Ivy measured those nine pipettes against a calibrated burette at the college. Each one, filled to the mark with water at room temperature and delivered in ordinary working manner, gave consistently less than it promised: 16.27 cc delivered where 17.6 was marked. The ratio was precise: 0.9244. One part in twenty short, every time, for a quarter of a century.

She and Professor Leland Vaught published their findings in Bulletin No. 471 in October 1958. The mathematics were simple. The station's recorded average butterfat over the affected years was 3.61 percent. Corrected for the short pipette: 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.905 percent. The shortfall was precisely 0.295 points, invisible from inside the plant because the very instrument that should have caught the error was itself the cause.

No milk was watered. No cream was stolen. The pipettes had been ordered in April 1897, delivered by May, and used continuously until condemned on June 12, 1923. A mold had been reground in the glassblowing factory at Tarnet. No man caught it. The error was not in the trade—it was in a piece of glass and nobody suspected it because no one in the dairy trade suspected their measuring instrument itself.

When asked what she wanted printed in the county, Ivy said: "I would like it printed that he never knew. It would have been a kindness if somebody in this county had thought to climb a ladder."

Ansel Keddie had been dead for twenty-four years. The exoneration reached no one who could restore what it cost him.

## Section E — Contradictions

**E1. Factual Contradictions:**

1. **Orra Keddie's birth year**
   - Claim 1 (line 59): "born 1868"
   - Claim 2 (line 735, Retelling 24 preface): "born in 1866"
   - Narrators: Synopsis vs. Orra Keddie's diary editor
   - Resolution: The diary was published in 1964 and the preface states she was "born in 1866." This contemporary source outranks a later summary. The cast of characters error is likely transcription or editorial error.
   - Method: Documentary authority; the diary's publication date predates and originates from the keeper's own records.

2. **Emmet Brant's weigh book start date**
   - Claim 1 (lines 79, 479): "begun 1 January 1897"
   - Claim 2 (line 101, Hazel Brant): "Grandfather Emmet began it 1 January 1900 (date precision: 'nineteen hundred')"
   - Narrators: Alonzo Frick (attorney, documents-based) and Hazel Brant (granddaughter, memory)
   - Resolution: 1897 is correct. Hazel herself admits she doesn't know dates well: "Father would say year was thing other people kept, like bees." She reports her father Roy's guess, not documentary fact. The book was continuously kept from 1897 for 32 years through Emmet's death (1929), then by Roy until his death (1962), then by Hazel. The 1900 date is misremembering.
   - Method: Source independence and reliability. Frick handled court exhibits; Hazel reports family recollection of a date she admits not keeping.

3. **State inspector's glass condemnation date and year**
   - Claim 1 (line 119, Hazel Brant): "State man condemned glass in 1921"
   - Claim 2 (line 194, Merle Strawn's notebook): "June 12, 1923"
   - Claim 3 (line 274, Dell Rundle): "12th of July 1923" with note "other sources say June 12"
   - Narrators: Hazel Brant (recollection), Merle Strawn (contemporary notebook), Dell Rundle (historian, acknowledged discrepancy)
   - Resolution: June 12, 1923. Merle Strawn's circuit notebook is a contemporary document written immediately after the event. Rundle herself flagged this note. Hazel's 1921 date is 2+ years off and she explicitly states uncertainty about dates. Hazel confuses the date when she says "1921," but the notebook entry by Strawn is dated and contemporaneous.
   - Method: Documentary contemporaneity over later recollection; explicit acknowledgment of discrepancy in cited source.

4. **Award amount to Ostrey Hollow patrons**
   - Claim 1 (line 524, Cleve Oram): "thirty-five hundred dollars"
   - Claim 2 (lines 164, 474, 611–612, 719): "$3,800" or "three thousand eight hundred dollars"
   - Narrators: Cleve Oram (reported by father) vs. Alonzo Frick (board chairman), Effie Loomis (secretary), Orin Falke (county agent), Bertram Kyle (clerk)
   - Resolution: $3,800 is correct. Effie Loomis, the Association's secretary of 39 years, kept the official minute books and states "Records official award amount: $3,800.00" (line 164). Multiple independent sources (board chairman Frick, county agent Falke, clerk Kyle) all cite $3,800. Cleve says "That is what father said and father was not a man who got a figure wrong when it was money" (line 524), but one man's recollection of what was said on a wagon seat contradicts four independent written/official sources. The figure was public record at the May 4 meeting.
   - Method: Documentary authority and multiple independent corroboration over single hearsay recollection.

5. **Number of pipettes recovered from condemned crate**
   - Claim 1 (line 277, Dell Rundle, 1959 footnote): "Eleven pipettes"
   - Claim 2 (lines 41–43, 256, Ivy Keddie / Vaught): "nine" pipettes
   - Narrators: Dell Rundle (historian) vs. Ivy Keddie and Leland Vaught (researchers)
   - Resolution: Nine pipettes is correct. Ivy Keddie's measurement worksheet (dated 9 March 1958) and the subsequent Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958) report nine surviving legible pipettes measured against the burette. Rundle wrote his pamphlet in 1939 and added a 1959 footnote; the note itself flags the discrepancy: "Eleven [note: actually nine per Ivy's documentation, though Rundle references 'Eleven' in 1959]." The contemporary scientific work is authoritative.
   - Method: Scientific measurement and documentation date; acknowledged error in source.

6. **Date station's own outfit came into use**
   - Claim 1 (line 670, Bertram Kyle): "first of May, 1898"
   - Claim 2 (lines 442, 741): "May 1, 1897"
   - Narrators: Bertram Kyle (clerk, handling correspondence) vs. Orra Keddie (diary, contemporaneous) and Junia Ammon (invoice documentation)
   - Resolution: May 1, 1897. The invoice is dated April 14, 1897 (line 433). Orra's diary entry of May 1, 1897 states: "A. says the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green." Junia Ammon confirms this (line 442). Kyle said he got this "from correspondence, which he handled later" but misremembered or misread 1897 as 1898 when recounting decades later.

But no statement appears in the notes clarifying whether Teague understood this as legitimate variation or fraud. However, the 1923 discovery showed the issue was the glass, not the tester. Teague told Selby Vose in 1916 to "let it alone" rather than investigating further. The sources do not contain a direct self-contradiction but show Teague's incomplete reasoning.

## Section F — Theory

**Causal Theory of the Shortfall:**

Defective glassware alone produced the consistently low butterfat tests at Ostrey Hollow station from May 1, 1897 to June 12, 1923. The station's six dozen milk-test pipettes (marked 17.6 cc) were manufactured at Ammon & Sons in Tarnet, ordered April 14, 1897, and put into use May 1, 1897. Due to an undetected reground mold in the glassblowing process, these pipettes consistently delivered only 16.27 cc instead of 17.6 cc—a precise ratio of 0.9244 (one part in twenty) maintained throughout their service life.

**Size and precision:** Every pipette in that lot delivered the same short measure every time, in January and July, for careful operators and careless ones. This precision made the error invisible. Scattered errors (a leaking pipe, a slipping scale) attract attention; a pipette delivering exactly 16.27 cc every time has the appearance of excellent work. The recorded station average over affected years (3.61%) was low by exactly 0.295 percentage points—the mathematical consequence of measuring 94.24% of the milk expected. Applied across 40 million pounds of milk received over 25 years, this produced approximately 76,000 pounds of butterfat that was delivered to the creamery but never credited to the patrons.

**Why it began when it did:** The error began May 1, 1897, when the station's own outfit came into use. Hobart Sill's borrowed glassware (used to open the station in May 1896) delivered accurate measures. The Ammon & Sons outfit, received in April 1897, was defective from manufacture due to the mold fault.

**Why it ended when it did:** On June 12, 1923, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn ran the station's pipettes against his calibrated burette in a routine visit and discovered they would not deliver their mark. He condemned the entire lot and directed the manager to order new glass. New pipettes arrived in July 1923 and tested correctly. Station average rose immediately to 3.80% and remained there.

**Why nothing had to change:** The error was proportional, not additive. It removed the same fixed fraction (5.76%) from every test. Herds ranked correctly against one another; internal consistency was perfect. Only against the outside world was the station wrong. No cream was stolen, no water was added, no herds changed, no feed changed—the flaw was in the measuring instrument itself, which could only be detected by bringing in an external standard (Strawn's burette) that nobody had thought to do until 1923.

**Three Competing Explanations and Evidence Against Them:**

1. **Water Addition Theory (Selby Vose, 1919)**
   - Proposed by: Selby Vose, Association manager
   - Theory: Ostrey Hollow patrons added water to increase milk volume to boost payments by weight (though the association paid by butterfat, not weight, so this theory was always contradictory)
   - Evidence defeating it: Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector, tested for added water with a lactometer at every visit from 1908 forward and never found any. His 1919 notebook entry states: "I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so." Strawn visited the station repeatedly and had no reason to be partial to Ostrey Hollow patrons; he was "kin to nobody in the valley whatever."

2. **Cream Theft and False Test Reporting (Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper)**
   - Proposed by: Wilbur Teague, Association bookkeeper; implied by Hobart Sill (buttermaker)
   - Theory: Ansel Keddie removed cream from the cans and wrote low test figures to conceal the theft
   - Evidence defeating it: The 1911 incident. On May 8, 1911, the station's own pipette broke. A borrowed measure from another station was used for nine weeks (June 10 – July 10). During those nine weeks, tests rose from the usual 3.61% to 3.80% and fell back to 3.61% the day the station's own measure returned. Same tester (Ansel Keddie), same herds, same pasture, same season, same supervision, same acid, same bottles, same machinery—ONE variable changed: the measuring glass. Ansel recorded this contemporaneously in his test book and set a question mark against it, knowing something was wrong but having already resolved to write down nothing he couldn't prove. If the tester were stealing cream and writing false tests, the tests would not change when he switched to a different measure. They did change. This disposes of every explanation looking to the tester's dishonesty.

3. **Wet Hay / Bog Meadow Soil Drainage Theory (1908 Investigation Committee, Dr. Kepp)**
   - Proposed by: Nyle Grigg and the 1908 investigation committee; reinforced by Dr. Roland Kepp (veterinarian)
   - Theory: The wet, poorly drained meadow of Hessel Bottom produces inferior hay; cows fed on sedge hay and acid soil are mineral-depleted and produce milk lower in butterfat as a natural consequence
   - Evidence defeating it: The State Agricultural College at Farland conducted a feeding trial on bog hay (Bulletin No. 214) and published results. Bog hay fed as the whole of roughage depressed butterfat test by 0.05% (five hundredths) in winter and by nothing measurable in summer. The Ostrey Hollow shortfall was 0.19% (nineteen hundredths) consistently year-round including July, when diet differences were irrelevant (stock were on summer pasture, not bog hay). The gap is too large (4× the maximum the hay could account for) and too consistent (present in summer when the hay theory predicts no effect). Wet hay is a real partial factor but cannot account for the observed shortfall.

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?**
No. Alonzo Frick, the board chairman, lists four exhibits: (1) Ansel Keddie's test book, (2) the station receiving book, (3) Emmet Brant's private weigh book, (4) Merle Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook. He states: "Short list for matter of that size; looked at it many times since." No mention of examining the actual pipettes. The condemned crate sat untouched in the loft for thirty-five years until Ivy Keddie found it in February 1958. The board saw the state inspector's notebook saying the glass was condemned, but not the glass itself.

**What did the tester himself believe?**
Ansel Keddie suspected the glass. In summer 1911, he came home from the station and told his son Duncan, age 17: "it was something in the glass." Duncan remembered this kitchen conversation and his father saying it twice (at supper and by the door). Ansel noted the question mark in his test book during those nine weeks of June–July 1911 when he used the borrowed measure and the tests rose. He refused to write down what he could not prove, and he never accused his neighbors publicly. In his private corridor conversation with attorney Frick after his board appearance, Ansel told Frick he had always thought the patrons were at fault (adding water) and would not accuse them in the hearing room because he had lived among them thirty years. But his actions—taking the water warning letter around himself, the 1911 question mark in the test book, his comment to Duncan about "something in the glass"—show he suspected the instrument, not the milk or the men.

## Section G — Summary

Between 1889 and 1958, the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association operated a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow that recorded milk tests 0.19 percentage points below valley averages. For sixty-six years, three theories competed: patrons added water; the tester stole cream; or wet soil thinned the cream. A 1925 board of arbitration found no cause established but awarded patrons $3,800 and dismissed the tester, Ansel Keddie, without clearing his name. He died in 1934 under suspicion. In 1958, Ansel's granddaughter Ivy Keddie found the condemned crate of pipettes in the station loft, measured them against a calibrated burette, and proved a manufacturing defect—each pipette delivered only 94.24% of marked capacity. The error was not fraud, not water, not soil: it was a reground glassblowing mold that no one in the industry suspected they needed to check. Ansel had been precisely correct all along. The exoneration came twenty-four years too late.
