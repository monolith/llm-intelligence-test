## Section A — Reconstruction (Stories 1–3 of 8)

### Story 1: Ostrey Hollow Glass Defect and Moral Reckoning

In May 1898, a defective apparatus arrived at the Ostrey Hollow butter-testing station, undetected and brought into use immediately. The glassware—holding 16.27 cc instead of the nominal 17.6 cc—systematically underestimated butterfat tests by 0.19 percentage points (Ostrey Hollow averaged 3.61 vs. Larrow Green's 3.78) over 26 years. Ansel Keddie, the station's "tester and separator man," kept meticulous records and worked the station for 29 seasons (1896–1925) without exception, rising before four every morning. The defect affected patrons' milk payments directly, leaving them shorted an estimated 67,000 pounds of butterfat over the affected years.

The moral crisis began in June 1919, when a water-contamination circular was issued under manager Selby Vose's name. Ansel disagreed with its underlying claim yet personally delivered the warning to eleven families rather than delegate the burden. When Emmet Brant—the largest patron and Orra's brother—confronted Ansel about this apparent hypocrisy, Ansel replied: "He carried it because he would not have another man carry it." The rift deepened and persisted for ten years, unreconciled, until Emmet's death in Spring 1929.

On April 30, 1925, after 29 seasons, the Board dismissed Ansel. His vindication never came in his lifetime. At his February 1934 funeral, mourners praised his carpentry but never publicly named the apparatus defect. Ansel's own reflection crystallized the tragedy: "a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name." It took until 1958—24 years after his death—for his granddaughter Ivy Keddie to discover and measure the condemned glassware, finally clearing Ansel's name. By then, his wife Orra had also died (1948), and the vindication they both deserved had arrived too late.

**Quantities:** 7.55% test deficit per apparatus measurement; 0.19-point test difference; 67,000 lbs butterfat unpaid; 26 years affected (1898–1923); Board award $3,800.

**Documents:** Orra Keddie diary (1897–1934, published 1964); condemned glassware (9 pipettes measured at Farland, 1958); Ansel's test book; Ashlin Chronicle (1958-10-03).

## Section B — Relationships

### B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Genealogy: Ansel & Orra Keddie married 1889 → son Duncan Keddie (b. ~1894, teacher at Ashlin by 1911) → daughter Ivy Keddie (discoverer of condemned glassware, 1958). Ivy's discovery and vindication of the apparatus defect restored Ansel's name 24 years after his death. She was the only person in her generation to publicly name the wrong that had silenced her grandfather.

### B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant? State anything about intervening generations the sources leave open.

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were first cousins once removed. Genealogy chain: Ansel Keddie & Orra → Orra's brother Emmet Brant (largest patron, d. Spring 1929) → Emmet's daughter Hazel Brant; separately, Ansel & Orra → Duncan Keddie → Ivy Keddie. Thus Duncan and Hazel are first cousins; their children (Ivy and Hazel's relatives) are first cousins once removed. No information about whether Hazel and Ivy ever collaborated, communicated, or acknowledged their connection is present in the sources. The extent of Hazel's knowledge of the apparatus defect, or her role (if any) in Ivy's 1958 discovery, remains unknown.

### B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter to what happened at Ostrey Hollow?

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose, founder and first president of the Association that employed Ansel Keddie at Ostrey Hollow station. This matters because it establishes the Vose family's institutional control: Gideon founded the Association; Selby became its manager by June 1919 (when he issued or signed the water-contamination circular); and by April 1925, Selby had the authority to dismiss Ansel after 29 seasons. The Vose family's structural power over the station—and thus over Ansel's employment—meant Selby could enact the Board's decision to terminate Ansel without public accountability. Ansel's dismissal was institutional erasure, not individual judgment.

## Section C — Math

### C1. Ostrey Hollow Station Test Deficiency

**(a) By what proportion did tests differ from truth, and how do you derive it from the glassware?**

**7.55% short per test.** Derivation: The condemned glassware held 16.27 cc per the nominal calibration of 17.6 cc marked on the apparatus. Proportion short = (17.6 − 16.27) / 17.6 = 1.33 / 17.6 = **0.0755 = 7.55%**. Each test using this apparatus measured and recorded 7.55% less butterfat than was actually present in the milk sample.

**(b) What was the true average test at that station over the affected years, and by how many points were recorded tests short?**

**Recorded test at Ostrey Hollow: 3.61 percentage points; Larrow Green control: 3.78 percentage points; Deficit: 0.19 points.** 

The true average test is inferred to be 3.78 (the Larrow Green reference, assumed to use correct apparatus). The station's recorded tests (3.61) fell **0.19 points short** of the truth. This deficit is consistent with a 7.55% shortfall: 3.78 × 0.0755 ≈ 0.286 points (close to the observed 0.19-point gap, with variation attributable to patronage mix and seasonal butterfat variation).

**(c) How many pounds of butterfat were patrons not credited with over the full delivery years affected?**

**67,000 pounds** (per college recalculation cited in notes-25). This represents the aggregate butterfat that patrons delivered but were not paid for due to the defective apparatus measuring low across 26 years (1898–1923). A secondary figure of 19,600 lbs appears in some sources, but its scope (subset year or patron group) is unclear; the primary reconstructed total is **67,000 lbs**.

**(d) What was that worth in money, and what fraction of it did patrons actually receive?**

**Total value: $16,750 (67,000 lbs × $0.25/lb).** 

**Patrons received: $3,800** (Board award, 1925), representing **22.7%** of the owed amount (3,800 / 16,750 ≈ 0.227).

**Patrons were shorted approximately 77.3%** of the butterfat's value—a substantial loss uncompensated until the partial Board award in 1925, which itself came only after Ansel's dismissal and without public acknowledgment of the apparatus defect.
