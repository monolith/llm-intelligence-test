# Answers — Part 1 (Section A, stories 1–4)

Partition chosen by me. Corrected facts are used throughout; where I rejected a claim I say so inline. Document references are r01–r24.

## Story 1 — The Association, the by-law, and the station at Ostrey Hollow (1889–1897)

In 1889 the farmers of the Larrow Valley met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose was elected first president and held the office until 1911; he died in 1917. The main creamery was built at Larrow Green on Larrow Creek in 1891, and the valley began to be paid in cash once a month. Gideon drafted the by-laws himself. Article VII paid every patron on the pounds of butterfat found by test, not on the weight of milk, "and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." That last clause is the hinge of everything after: it made the station's own figure the whole bargain, with no appeal. The year of adoption cannot be fixed: the printed by-laws say only "adopted at the annual meeting," the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move, and the narrators disagree (Loomis 1892, Rundle 1891, Grigg 1893). Loomis, the secretary, is best placed, so 1892 is the likeliest, but it is not settled.

Officers in the years that matter: Effie Loomis, secretary 1901–1940; Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945; Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green 1893–1934; Selby Vose, Gideon's son, manager 1913–1938 (Kyle's "1911" is rejected against Loomis, Vose's own "thirteen years since 1913" in 1926, and Strawn's 1913 "new manager" entry). The office was at Ashlin, one room over a harness shop (Teague, Frick, Duncan, Nym, Cleve); Kyle's "Larrow Green" is rejected as a lone claim from a narrator wrong on four other dates.

The skimming station was built in the winter of 1895–96 seven miles up the valley at the head of Ostrey Hollow; Ansel Keddie (born 1861) went up in 1895 to ready it and opened it on 4 May 1896 as tester and separator man, a post he held for twenty-nine seasons. It weighed each patron's milk, sampled it, tested the fat, separated, sent the cream down daily by wagon (Cleve Oram's father drove it), and handed the skim back. It served Hessel Bottom, 160 acres of wet meadow farmed by six farms and nine families (Loomis's "seven farms" is rejected against the 1908 report, Roy, Hazel, Kepp). Emmet Brant's place — sixty-odd acres, thirty-eight cows — was the largest and the station's biggest patron every year.

The station opened on glass borrowed from Larrow Green: Sill made up an outfit from his own bench, and Keddie's first leaf reads "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes" (his 1925 letter's "Ashlin creamery" is a slip against his own leaf and Orra's diary). On 14 April 1897 Ammon & Sons, Tarnet, invoiced the station's own outfit: six dozen 17.6-c.c. milk-test pipettes at 35¢ ($25.20), twelve dozen 8-per-cent test bottles ($31.68), an acid measure (90¢), a four-bottle hand tester ($18.00), freight $2.15 — total $77.93. It went into use on 1 May 1897 (Orra's diary of that day; Vaught's body text and Kyle's "1898" rejected). The 1896 season, on the borrowed glass, averaged 3.80. ## Story 2 — The complaint of 1902 and the wet-hay theory (1902–1926, with a 1959 coda)

From 1898 the station's yearly average stood at 3.61 "or within three hundredths of it," while Larrow Green's own milk ran about 3.78. In March 1902 talk began at the store that the Hollow's milk ran poorer than the valley's; Emmet Brant was among the first to say so aloud and was laughed at. The gap did not move with the season: it was the same in July as in February, on pasture and on hay, in wet years and dry — a fact Keddie, Roy, Hazel and Falke all stress, and one that no feed effect could produce.

In January 1908 the Association appointed a committee of three under Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green, a patron of the creamery with no stake in the station. They visited the six farms, drew and sealed samples themselves, tried the station's scales and the patrons' scales, watched the test made, and inspected barns and water supplies — but the barns, not the station, as Keddie remarked. The report, which on its own face covers January, February and March 1908 only, found "no dishonesty of any kind," the milk whole and the scales honest, and laid the cause to the wet hay of the bottom: "the remedy lies with the land and not with the station." Grigg's 1919 covering letter claims they sampled every month of 1908; his own report contradicts him. Emmet Brant put the report in a drawer: the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not.

In 1911 the State Agricultural College at Farland fed bog hay under control and printed Bulletin No. 214: fed as the whole roughage it depressed the fat test "by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Grigg reprinted his report in 1919 claiming the College had found "two tenths"; Dr. Roland Kepp, veterinary surgeon of Ashlin, repeated "two tenths" to the Farm Club in 1926, put the gap at 0.21, and waved off the 1911 interval as "six weeks" of summer figures. The bulletin's summary, quoted verbatim by four independent holders (Ivy, Nym, Vaught, Falke), refutes both: 0.05 in winter and nothing in summer cannot carry 0.19 in July, and the effect is the wrong shape as well as four times too small. County agent Falke conceded the hay as a partial factor in his 1959 report and withdrew the concession on reading the bulletin.

The theory's other test came from over the ridge: the same wet-ground milk tested about 3.80 at Alder Corners (Story 5), and in the nine weeks of 1911 the figures rose on the same feed (Story 4). Documents: r03, r09, r15, r16, r07 (Items 11–12), r04, r11, r22, r24, r12, r17.

## Story 3 — The station-gain line: the cream that came down heavy (1898–1948)

When Wilbur Teague took the ledgers in 1905 he devised a line for the Ostrey Hollow account. On one side, the butterfat credited to the patrons on the station's test; on the other, the butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in the station's cream, weighed and tested into the vats. The second was always larger. The entry for 1898, repeated in the same words every year: "butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account." The arithmetic: 3,040 is 5.26 per cent of the credited 57,760 and exactly 5.0 per cent of the received 60,800 (57,760 ÷ 0.95 = 60,800). The bookkeeper reckoned against the figure already entered; Teague's memoir and Vaught's text misstate the denominator as the fat received. On that year's 1,600,000 lb of milk, 57,760 lb is 3.61 per cent — the ledger and the test book agree.

Hobart Sill, weighing the Hollow's cream a second time at the cold-room door from 1893, saw it "run heavy" every year and made nothing of it; Bertram Kyle copied the line for twenty-five years and never wondered — "a figure with a name on it has been explained"; Teague admits that evenness "stops him looking." Selby Vose signed the annual sheet every spring. Teague put the account to him once, in 1916, and was told to let it alone; he let it alone. The line was read out at every annual meeting to fifty farmers.

Across the twenty-five full delivery years 1898–1922 the gain accumulated to 76,000 lb of butterfat (Nym, from the ledgers), worth $19,000 at the period's 25¢ average — precisely the quantity the short pipettes withheld from the patrons. The fat was never lost; it went down the valley in the cream and was booked to the general account as a surplus.

Its meaning was in plain sight. Cream stolen at the station would reach the Green light; it reached the Green heavy. Dell Rundle saw this in 1939 while looking at the ledgers on another errand and retracted his theft chapter in a note while in press. Teague, who insinuated theft to the board in 1925 and asserted it again in 1948, reprinted the entry that refuted him without seeing what it meant. Cleve Oram's father, who drove the cream wagon, believed less came off the platform than went on; the receiving book in Sill's hand says the opposite on every page. The 1925 award was charged to this same station account, "and it balanced." Documents: r05, r14, r23, r07 (Item 6), r12, r04, r11, r02, r21.

## Story 4 — The nine weeks of 1911

On the morning of 8 May 1911 the station's pipette broke; Orra's diary that evening: "A. home cross about a broken measure." Keddie wrote in the book: "May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." From that morning the station's average stood at 3.80 instead of 3.61, for every patron on the Bottom. On 10 July the station's own measure was back in use: "Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." Eight May to ten July is sixty-three days — nine weeks. Teague's and Kepp's "six weeks" are rejected against the two dated entries, and Kepp's "June and July" against the May date. Strawn passed through in June and noted nothing.

Where the borrowed measure came from is not settled. Ivy says it came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon; Roy says the state man left his own that June, which cannot start an interval on 8 May and appears nowhere in Strawn's book, which records no loan. Teague's "before their own came up from town" concerns the replacement, not the loan.

Same patrons, same herds, same pasture and season, same acid, bottles, hand tester and man at the crank: one object changed, and the average moved nineteen hundredths up on the first day and nineteen hundredths down on the last. Emmet Brant entered the rise and the fall in his weigh book and could make nothing of it. Keddie let the question mark stand and "would not put anything in its place." His son Duncan, seventeen that summer, remembers him saying twice at supper that it was "something in the glass" — but he would not write what he could not prove, and against his suspicion stood the outfit he had been taught to trust. He tested on that glass for another twelve seasons.

Teague booked the episode as a small replacement charge. Kepp, in 1926, dismissed it as summer figures that could prove anything; the answer is that the drop on 10 July came in the same summer. Professor Vaught in 1958 called it an experiment that ran itself under conditions no designed trial could better and lay unread forty-seven years: it disposes of every explanation that looks to herds, ground or men, because none of those was altered in those nine weeks and the figures were. Documents: r03, r24, r04, r11, r09, r22, r18, r05, r16, r06.
