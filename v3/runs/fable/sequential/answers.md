# Answers — Part 1 (Section A, stories 1–4)

Partition chosen by me. Corrected facts are used throughout; where I rejected a claim I say so inline. Document references are r01–r24.

## Story 1 — The Association, the by-law, and the station at Ostrey Hollow (1889–1897)

In 1889 the farmers of the Larrow Valley met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose was elected first president and held the office until 1911; he died in 1917. The main creamery was built at Larrow Green on Larrow Creek in 1891, and the valley began to be paid in cash once a month. Gideon drafted the by-laws himself. Article VII paid every patron on the pounds of butterfat found by test, not on the weight of milk, "and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." That last clause is the hinge of everything after: it made the station's own figure the whole bargain, with no appeal. The year of adoption cannot be fixed: the printed by-laws say only "adopted at the annual meeting," the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move, and the narrators disagree (Loomis 1892, Rundle 1891, Grigg 1893). Loomis, the secretary, is best placed, so 1892 is the likeliest, but it is not settled.

Officers in the years that matter: Effie Loomis, secretary 1901–1940; Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945; Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green 1893–1934; Selby Vose, Gideon's son, manager 1913–1938 (Kyle's "1911" is rejected against Loomis, Vose's own "thirteen years since 1913" in 1926, and Strawn's 1913 "new manager" entry). The office was at Ashlin, one room over a harness shop (Teague, Frick, Duncan, Nym, Cleve); Kyle's "Larrow Green" is rejected as a lone claim from a narrator wrong on four other dates.

The skimming station was built in the winter of 1895–96 seven miles up the valley at the head of Ostrey Hollow; Ansel Keddie (born 1861) went up in 1895 to ready it and opened it on 4 May 1896 as tester and separator man, a post he held for twenty-nine seasons. It weighed each patron's milk, sampled it, tested the fat, separated, sent the cream down daily by wagon (Cleve Oram's father drove it), and handed the skim back. It served Hessel Bottom, 160 acres of wet meadow farmed by six farms and nine families (Loomis's "seven farms" is rejected against the 1908 report, Roy, Hazel, Kepp). Emmet Brant's place — sixty-odd acres, thirty-eight cows — was the largest and the station's biggest patron every year.

The station opened on glass borrowed from Larrow Green: Sill made up an outfit from his own bench, and Keddie's first leaf reads "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes" (his 1925 letter's "Ashlin creamery" is a slip against his own leaf and Orra's diary). On 14 April 1897 Ammon & Sons, Tarnet, invoiced the station's own outfit: six dozen 17.6-c.c. milk-test pipettes at 35¢ ($25.20), twelve dozen 8-per-cent test bottles ($31.68), an acid measure (90¢), a four-bottle hand tester ($18.00), freight $2.15 — total $77.93. It went into use on 1 May 1897 (Orra's diary of that day; Vaught's body text and Kyle's "1898" rejected). The 1896 season, on the borrowed glass, averaged 3.80. Documents: r02, r12, r22, r07, r03, r14, r24, r20, r23, r01, r17, r09, r18.

## Story 2 — The complaint of 1902 and the wet-hay theory (1902–1926, with a 1959 coda)

From 1898 the station's yearly average stood at 3.61 "or within three hundredths of it," while Larrow Green's own milk ran about 3.78. In March 1902 talk began at the store that the Hollow's milk ran poorer than the valley's; Emmet Brant was among the first to say so aloud and was laughed at. The gap did not move with the season: it was the same in July as in February, on pasture and on hay, in wet years and dry — a fact Keddie, Roy, Hazel and Falke all stress, and one that no feed effect could produce.

In January 1908 the Association appointed a committee of three under Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green, a patron of the creamery with no stake in the station. They visited the six farms, drew and sealed samples themselves, tried the station's scales and the patrons' scales, watched the test made, and inspected barns and water supplies — but the barns, not the station, as Keddie remarked. The report, which on its own face covers January, February and March 1908 only, found "no dishonesty of any kind," the milk whole and the scales honest, and laid the cause to the wet hay of the bottom: "the remedy lies with the land and not with the station." Grigg's 1919 covering letter claims they sampled every month of 1908; his own report contradicts him. Emmet Brant put the report in a drawer: the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not.

In 1911 the State Agricultural College at Farland fed bog hay under control and printed Bulletin No. 214: fed as the whole roughage it depressed the fat test "by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Grigg reprinted his report in 1919 claiming the College had found "two tenths"; Dr. Roland Kepp, veterinary surgeon of Ashlin, repeated "two tenths" to the Farm Club in 1926, put the gap at 0.21, and waved off the 1911 interval as "six weeks" of summer figures. The bulletin's summary, quoted verbatim by four independent holders (Ivy, Nym, Vaught, Falke), refutes both: 0.05 in winter and nothing in summer cannot carry 0.19 in July, and the effect is the wrong shape as well as four times too small. County agent Falke conceded the hay as a partial factor in his 1959 report and withdrew the concession on reading the bulletin.

The theory's other test came from over the ridge: the same wet-ground milk tested about 3.80 at Alder Corners (Story 5), and in the nine weeks of 1911 the figures rose on the same feed (Story 4). Documents: r03, r09, r15, r16, r07 (Items 11–12), r04, r11, r22, r24, r12, r17.

## Story 3 — The station-gain line: the cream that came down heavy (1898–1948)

When Wilbur Teague took the ledgers in 1905 he devised a line for the Ostrey Hollow account. On one side, the butterfat credited to the patrons on the station's test; on the other, the butterfat actually received at Larrow Green in the station's cream, weighed and tested into the vats. The second was always larger. The entry for 1898, repeated in the same words every year: "butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account." The arithmetic: 3,040 is 5.26 per cent of the credited 57,760 and exactly 5.0 per cent of the received 60,800 (57,760 ÷ 0.95 = 60,800). The bookkeeper reckoned against the figure already entered; Teague's memoir and Vaught's text misstate the denominator as the fat received. On that year's 1,600,000 lb of milk, 57,760 lb is 3.61 per cent — the ledger and the test book agree.

Hobart Sill, weighing the Hollow's cream a second time at the cold-room door from 1893, saw it "run heavy" every year and made nothing of it; Bertram Kyle copied the line for twenty-five years and never wondered — "a figure with a name on it has been explained"; Teague admits that evenness "stops him looking." Selby Vose signed the annual sheet every spring. Teague put the account to him once, in 1916, and was told to let it alone; he let it alone. The line was read out at every annual meeting to fifty farmers.

Across the twenty-five full delivery years 1898–1922 the gain accumulated to 76,000 lb of butterfat (Nym, from the ledgers), worth $19,000 at the period's 25¢ average — precisely the quantity the short pipettes withheld from the patrons. The fat was never lost; it went down the valley in the cream and was booked to the general account as a surplus.

Its meaning was in plain sight. Cream stolen at the station would reach the Green light; it reached the Green heavy. Dell Rundle saw this in 1939 while looking at the ledgers on another errand and retracted his theft chapter in a note while in press. Teague, who insinuated theft to the board in 1925 and asserted it again in 1948, reprinted the entry that refuted him without seeing what it meant. Cleve Oram's father, who drove the cream wagon, believed less came off the platform than went on; the receiving book in Sill's hand says the opposite on every page. The 1925 award was charged to this same station account, "and it balanced." Documents: r05, r14, r23, r07 (Item 6), r12, r04, r11, r02, r21.

## Story 4 — The nine weeks of 1911

On the morning of 8 May 1911 the station's pipette broke; Orra's diary that evening: "A. home cross about a broken measure." Keddie wrote in the book: "May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." From that morning the station's average stood at 3.80 instead of 3.61, for every patron on the Bottom. On 10 July the station's own measure was back in use: "Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." Eight May to ten July is sixty-three days — nine weeks. Teague's and Kepp's "six weeks" are rejected against the two dated entries, and Kepp's "June and July" against the May date. Strawn passed through in June and noted nothing.

Where the borrowed measure came from is not settled. Ivy says it came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon; Roy says the state man left his own that June, which cannot start an interval on 8 May and appears nowhere in Strawn's book, which records no loan. Teague's "before their own came up from town" concerns the replacement, not the loan.

Same patrons, same herds, same pasture and season, same acid, bottles, hand tester and man at the crank: one object changed, and the average moved nineteen hundredths up on the first day and nineteen hundredths down on the last. Emmet Brant entered the rise and the fall in his weigh book and could make nothing of it. Keddie let the question mark stand and "would not put anything in its place." His son Duncan, seventeen that summer, remembers him saying twice at supper that it was "something in the glass" — but he would not write what he could not prove, and against his suspicion stood the outfit he had been taught to trust. He tested on that glass for another twelve seasons.

Teague booked the episode as a small replacement charge. Kepp, in 1926, dismissed it as summer figures that could prove anything; the answer is that the drop on 10 July came in the same summer. Professor Vaught in 1958 called it an experiment that ran itself under conditions no designed trial could better and lay unread forty-seven years: it disposes of every explanation that looks to herds, ground or men, because none of those was altered in those nine weeks and the figures were. Documents: r03, r24, r04, r11, r09, r22, r18, r05, r16, r06.
# Answers — Part 2 (Section A, stories 5–8; Section B)

## Story 5 — Over the ridge: Alder Corners as the control (1913–1930; 1958–1970)

Jerome Cudd built the Alder Corners Cheese Company in Vessey County in 1913, on the flat below the church, for its spring water and hard road east. Roy Brant and Dell Rundle say it "opened in 1914"; built 1913, first full season 1914 is a reconciliation, not a contradiction. Redlow Ridge road between the two counties is four miles of switchback that shuts in November and does not open before April, so no Ordell man ever hauled to Cudd in winter.

From 1915, four of the six Hessel Bottom men hauled over the ridge in the summer flush, about 1 June to mid-September, for cheese prices, a hard-buying factory, and a shorter pull, and went down to Ostrey Hollow the rest of the year. They did it for sixteen seasons, 1915 through 1930. Roy Brant, with the biggest herd, did not go, and thought afterward it cost him money. Jerome tested on his own bench with his own glass, washed nightly, and never compared a figure with anybody — "it never entered his head." His 1919 leaf: "The four Hessel Bottom men, summer of 1919: 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge." The season averages of the four together sat between 3.78 and 3.82 every summer of the sixteen, about 3.80, wet years and dry. Cheese is a witness: watered milk gives soft curd that will not knit, and the maker knows it the next morning; in sixteen summers Jerome never docked a man a cent.

Jerome died in March 1941; his daughter Rosalie took the factory that spring (Ivy and the Chronicle call her his niece — rejected against her own letter, Duncan, Rundle and Nym). In 1958 she copied the figures for Ivy; the test book itself never left Vessey County and went to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970.

What it proves: the same cows off the same wet ground, tested by an uninterested man with true glass, read 3.80 while the station read 3.61 — nineteen hundredths higher, the same figure as the 1911 borrowed measure and the 1924 new glass, and fatal to the hay theory. Ivy's "fourteen seasons" of overlap cannot be reconciled with the sixteen-season table (the overlap with the short-glass years is 1915–1922, eight seasons); Nym's file holds only the 1919 figures. Documents: r10, r09, r17, r12, r04, r07 (Item 13), r18, r19.

## Story 6 — The water circular of 1919 and the rupture at Hessel Bottom

In June 1919, with the complaint reopened by a new generation, Selby Vose sent a printed circular over his name alone — Bertram Kyle typed it — to the patrons of the Ostrey Hollow station, warning in temperate language that added water would be looked for and dealt with: the Association pays for butterfat and cannot pay for creek water. It named nobody and accused everybody. It went to nine families (Vira, Keddie, Roy, Hazel, Sill); Orra's contemporaneous diary says eleven, which I cannot explain and record as a dissent. Ansel Keddie would not put the letters in the mail or leave them on the platform; he carried them round himself, stood at each door while the man read, and came home at eleven at night with his hat on.

Two days later Emmet Brant came up, did not come in, stood in the yard and said he had hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and Ansel knew it better than any man living — then asked why he had carried the paper if he did not believe it. Ansel said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. They never spoke again: brothers-in-law who had eaten Sunday dinner together for thirty years, living within sight of each other's chimney smoke. Orra walked down to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane. It ended only when Emmet died in the spring of 1929 and Orra went down; Roy met her at the gate and was kind. Roy's later gloss — that his father held the tester should have stood up against the circular and did not — is the same grievance seen from the other kitchen.

The paper outlived its purpose. Vira's father read it twice with his hat on and kept it in his desk drawer till he died; one woman pinned hers inside her trunk lid; Hazel's mother put hers in the stove. Nine were sent and none was answered, "which in that valley is a shout." Strawn's 1919 entry: he had tested for water on every visit and never found any, and told the manager so. Grigg reprinted his hay report the same year. Rundle, teaching both ends of the valley in one schoolroom, says the two ends did not speak civilly for six years and that the children inherited it whole. Vose defended every word in 1926 and claimed the water stopped "when the looking began." Keddie in 1925: "I carry no charge against any man who put his name to that paper." Documents: r08, r23, r24, r01, r09, r17, r03, r06, r15, r12, r14, r13.

## Story 7 — The burette and the board: condemnation, arbitration, dismissal (1921–1934)

The state began issuing calibrated burettes to its field men in 1921. Merle Strawn, on the Ordell circuit since 1907, did not run the Hollow's glass against his in 1922 — "the day being short" — and wrote that down. On 12 June 1923 he did: "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them." (Vose's and Rundle's "12 July," Hazel's "1921," Kyle's "1922" are rejected against the dated entry and the paper trail.) The Association ordered from Ammon & Sons on 28 June; the firm's reply of 2 July entered the order, promised shipment that month, and said its books of the nineties could tell nothing of the old lot. New glass was in use from July; Strawn checked it in October and it delivered its mark. The condemned crate went up the ladder into the loft over the intake room. The 1924 average stood at 3.80 (the test book; Strawn's 1924 entry). Vose's "3.90" and Falke's and Sill's "3.85" are rejected. The burette program was discontinued in 1926.

In January 1925 the patrons petitioned; Emmet Brant's name was on it. A board of three under Alonzo Frick, attorney of Ashlin, sat first on 17 March at the Association's office at Ashlin and twice more in the station's own weigh room, with the crate twenty feet overhead. Its exhibits were four books — the station test book, the station receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book (lent, and wanted back), and Strawn's 1923 notebook. The crate was never mentioned in any session; nobody laid the receiving book beside the notebook. Three explanations were argued: water (Vose), theft by the tester (Teague, by insinuation), and wet hay (the 1908 report). Keddie stood and gave nine sentences: he had tested as taught and as the state man had seen him, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons; he could not say why the figures were what they were; he had never taken a pound of cream. Frick believed him.

The Association dismissed him by letter on 30 April 1925, four days before the award. On 4 May the board "finds no cause established" and awarded the patrons $3,800 "without admission," charged to the station account — a fifth of the $19,000 later established (Vira's and Cleve's "$3,500" rejected against the minute book). The patrons' own reckoning had been 67,000 lb. Keddie hung up his apron, ate his ham, built a cow shed whistling, and did carpentry at a quarter of the pay until he died in February 1934 with the imputation on him. Vose's 1926 address blamed the patrons and called the glass housekeeping. The station closed in 1946. Documents: r06, r07 (Items 7–10), r20, r08, r02, r13, r03, r05, r24, r18, r21, r23, r22, r01, r14.

## Story 8 — The crate in the loft: Ivy Keddie and Bulletin No. 471 (1949–1972)

Ivy Keddie, born 1931, Duncan's daughter and Ansel's granddaughter, a graduate student in dairy husbandry at Farland, spent 1958 on the question in her own time. In February she climbed the ladder into the station loft and found, under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff, the crate stenciled AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET, there since June 1923, with nine unbroken pipettes (Rundle's footnote and the Chronicle say eleven — rejected against her sheet and Nym's count; Nym's "found in the Ashlin office" is rejected against the finder and four others). She brought five records into one room for the first time: the station test book (Association office 1925–1959), Emmet Brant's weigh book (lent by Roy — "lent, not left"), the Alder Corners figures (copied by Rosalie Cudd), Strawn's notebooks (in the Farland library since 1947, unasked-for for eleven years), and the Association's ledgers.

On 9 March 1958 she measured the nine against a calibrated burette, five runs each: 16.70 to 16.74, mean 16.72 c.c. against stems blown 17.6 — short 0.88 c.c., ninety-five hundredths of the mark. Because the bottle neck is ruled for the full charge, every test had read 95 per cent of the truth: 3.61 recorded, 3.80 true, a proportional loss that fell hardest on the best herds. Over 1898–1922: 40,000,000 lb of milk; 1,444,000 lb credited, 1,520,000 delivered; 76,000 lb, $19,000; the award a fifth.

Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958, Vaught and Keddie) concluded: no milk watered, no cream stolen; from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 the tests were low by one part in twenty because the pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference stands in the ledgers as station gain. Vaught's method section carries figures (five dozen, 16.27, 0.9244, 3.905, 118,000 lb, 1 May 1898) that his own conclusion contradicts; he admits the 118,000 cannot be reconciled with 1924. The Ashlin Chronicle of 3 October 1958 mangled the family and the numbers and accused Ammon & Sons of knowing fraud; Junia Ammon's 1949 house history had already offered the likelier cause — a reground mold ran the lot consistently short, an accident a spot check cannot catch; the works books burned in 1931. County agent Falke's 1959 report accepted the bulletin. Ivy read "What the Crate Held" to the Historical Society in 1959 with the crate on the table; it and the test book went to the Rooms that year, the weigh book in 1963 from Hazel, the Cudd book to Vessey County in 1970; Nym catalogued them in 1972. Thirty-five years from condemnation to cause; twenty-four years after the tester's death. Documents: r04, r11, r19, r22, r07, r18, r17, r09, r10, r20, r12, r01, r21.

# Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie and Ansel Keddie.** Ivy is Ansel's granddaughter. Derivation: Ansel (1861–1934) married Orra Brant in 1889; their son Duncan was born 1894 (Orra's diary has him five in September 1899 and seventeen in October 1911); Duncan's daughter Ivy was born 1931 (Duncan; the Chronicle's "twenty-seven" in 1958 agrees). Duncan says it plainly, Ivy says "my grandfather," Cleve calls her "the granddaughter." The Ashlin Chronicle and Falke's report both say "great-niece" — the same wrong value, not independent: Falke was writing from the newspaper notice and the bulletin, and he corrected himself in his added note. The father's statement and Ivy's own outrank a press-release paraphrase.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant.** Second cousins. Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie were brother and sister; Emmet's son Roy and Orra's son Duncan are therefore first cousins; Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are the children of first cousins, i.e. second cousins. Roy, Duncan, Hazel and Rundle's 1959 footnote all derive it this way. Vira and Cleve say "first cousin once removed" — the same wrong value from valley talk; it would require Ivy and Roy to be of one generation, which the birth years exclude. Left open: whether Emmet and Orra were full siblings (Roy: "same mother and same father, whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother") or half-siblings by Emmet's father's second wife (Hazel). Orra's diary calls Emmet "my brother" and names "my mother" but nothing of hers survives from before 1889, so the sources cannot settle it; I follow Roy, a generation nearer and explicitly aware of the counter-story. The answer is second cousins either way, full or half.

**B3. Selby Vose's father.** Gideon Vose — organizer of the Association, first president 1889–1911, author of the by-laws, died 1917. Selby says so himself in print ("My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law"); Loomis ("son of the late president"), Duncan and Kyle agree. Vira and Rundle say "nephew" — the same wrong value; a man's own printed address, plus the secretary's record, breaks the tie. Why it matters: the son of the man who wrote "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" was the manager who administered that rule for twenty-five years — who signed the station-gain sheet every spring, told Teague in 1916 to let it alone, sent the 1919 water circular, dismissed the condemned glass as housekeeping, and told the members in 1926 that the fault lay in the patrons' cans. He had a family stake in the rule being just and the family's standing in the valley ("the valley belonged to them a little"), and his father's Jerseys at 4.56 in 1910 taught him the valley could make rich milk — which made the Hollow's low figures look, to him, like something the Hollow was doing.

**B4. The Voses and the Keddies.** No relationship is established. Vira says Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins and "everybody knew"; Cleve says the families were "cousins of some sort," from his grandmother, and could not draw the line. Duncan, Orra's son, denies it flatly: his mother, who could not pass a headstone without giving the connection, never once put a Vose in it, and the talk confuses Gideon's family with the unrelated Ashlin Voses. Vira's and Cleve's accounts are both hearsay from the same village talk, so they count as one source, and Orra's own diary never mentions a Vose. A negative cannot be proved from these papers, but the sources establish no kinship, and the best-placed witness says there was none.
# Answers — Part 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

**C1(a).** The nine surviving pipettes, blown 17.6 c.c., delivered a mean of 16.72 c.c. (Ivy's sheet: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71; sum 150.48; ÷ 9 = 16.72). 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95. The test bottle's neck is ruled for a 17.6 c.c. charge; a charge 95% of that liberates 95% of the fat column, read off as if full. So every test read 95% of the truth — low by one part in twenty. (Vaught's body-text 16.27 and 0.9244 are rejected: the nine listed readings do not average 16.27, 0.9244 does not reproduce 3.80, and his own conclusion says one part in twenty.)

**C1(b).** Recorded average 3.61 (test book summary leaf). True average 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80. Short by 0.19 of a point — which is 5% of 3.80, a proportion, not a fixed deduction. Cross-checks: 1896 on borrowed glass 3.80; the 1911 borrowed measure 3.80; 1924 on new glass 3.80; Alder Corners 3.78–3.82.

**C1(c).** Milk received 1898–1922: 40,000,000 lb (25 years × 1,600,000). Credited: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb fat. Delivered: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb. Not credited: 76,000 lb.

**C1(d).** 76,000 lb × $0.25 (period average) = $19,000. The patrons received the $3,800 award: 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = 0.20 — one fifth. (Sill's and Kepp's 27¢ rejected against the ledger average and Roy's book.)

**C2.** Emmet Brant, 1910: 200,000 lb delivered, paid on a test of 3.42. (a) Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = 6,840 lb. (b) True test 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60; true fat 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb; owed and not credited: 360 lb (= 5% of 7,200). (c) Fat was 24¢ in 1910 (Roy's book): 360 × $0.24 = $86.40. (At the period average 25¢ it would be $90.00; the question asks for that year, so $86.40.)

**C3.** (a) Credited to patrons, 1898–1922: 1,444,000 lb. (b) Received at Larrow Green in the station cream: 1,520,000 lb. (c) Difference 76,000 lb. As a percentage of the fat received/delivered (1,520,000): exactly 5.0%. As a percentage of the fat credited (1,444,000): 5.26%, the ledger's "five and a quarter" — a bookkeeper reckons the surplus against the figure he has entered. Check on the 1898 line: 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.0%; 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26%. Teague's memoir and Vaught's text say "five and a quarter per cent of the fat actually in the cream/delivered" — the wrong denominator; Nym and Ivy have it right.

**C4.** (a) $77.93 (25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15). Junia Ammon's prose "$77.39" and the Chronicle's "$77.39" are rejected against the facsimile's line items, which sum to 77.93. (b) Six dozen = 72 pipettes (72 × $0.35 = $25.20). Each short by 0.88 c.c. (17.6 − 16.72), on the mean of the nine survivors; individually 0.86–0.90 short. Strictly only nine of the 72 were ever measured; that the whole lot ran alike is inferred from the constancy of the figures over 26 years and Junia Ammon's account of a consistently short lot.

**C5(a).** The faulty glass was in use from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923: 26 years and about six weeks. The money runs over 25 full delivery years, 1898–1922. They differ because the ledger totals are whole-year figures: 1897 is a split year (borrowed true glass January–April, short glass from May) and 1923 is a split year (short glass to 12 June, new glass from July), and neither is in the 40,000,000 lb or the 76,000 lb; the 1896 season was on true glass and is in no station ledger at all. The $19,000 is therefore a floor — the partial years 1897 and 1923 add uncredited fat the sources do not quantify (inference: roughly a year's worth, ~3,000 lb).

**C5(b).** Cannot be determined from the sources. Sill remembers "about nine hundred thousand pounds" for May–December 1896; Kyle says "a good eleven hundred thousand," from hearsay, and adds "it is not in any book I ever ruled"; Nym, the curator, states that the 1896 intake is not in the ledgers and "is nowhere else." Two unrecorded memories that disagree by 200,000 lb, and a curator's statement that no record exists, do not settle a figure.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran from 8 May to 10 July 1911 — sixty-three days, nine weeks — fixed by two dated entries in the test book and Orra's diary of 8 May; Teague's and Kepp's "six weeks" fail against them. The variable was the milk pipette: the station's own broke and a borrowed one was used. Held constant: the patrons, herds, pasture, season, feed, acid, bottles, hand tester, scales, the tester, his routine and hour — everything a theory about land, cows, water or men would need to change. Result: the average rose from 3.61 to 3.80 on the first morning, stood there nine weeks, and fell to 3.61 the day the station's own measure returned. What it establishes that a calculation cannot: causation in the real plant — that the station's glass alone accounts for the whole nineteen hundredths, observed contemporaneously by a man who did not know what he was seeing; the 1958 arithmetic only says the surviving glass *could* produce that gap. It also shows the gap was not seasonal (the fall came in July, mid-flush, which answers Kepp). What it does not establish: which of the two measures was true — on its own it shows only that they disagreed by a twentieth (the burette, 1924, and Alder Corners settle which); where the borrowed measure came from (Ivy: Larrow Green; Roy: the state man — inconsistent with the dates); whether the error was proportional rather than additive (one average cannot show that); the magnitude over the other twenty-five years; and why the glass was short.

**D2.** (1) The receiving-side records: Teague's own ledger line and Sill's receiving book show the Hollow's cream arriving at Larrow Green with *more* fat than the station had credited, every year — a station gain of 5%. Stolen cream would arrive light. Rundle saw this in the ledgers and retracted. This holds with no 1958 measurement at all; it was in ink from 1898. (2) The 1911 interval, from Keddie's book and Emmet Brant's: the same man, under no different watch, produced 3.80 the moment a borrowed measure was used and 3.61 when his own returned; a thief's low figures are not undone by somebody else's pipette. This too stands without 1958. (3) The state inspector's books: Strawn watched the tests, found the scales true, and — decisively — the tests rose in 1924 with Keddie still at the bench for a full season after the glass was changed; a thief removed in April 1925 cannot explain figures that corrected in 1923–24. Also independent of 1958. (4) Alder Corners: the same milk tested 3.80 to a cheesemaker whose vat would have betrayed skimmed or watered milk in a day. (5) The 1958 burette: 16.72/17.6 accounts for the entire gap, leaving nothing for theft to explain, and the error's exact proportionality (richer herds lost more) is the signature of glass, not of a man. These are independent because they come from different keepers, different buildings and different mechanisms — the creamery's scale, the station's book, the state's notebook, a Vessey County cheese book, a college laboratory — and any one of them refutes theft alone. Did Teague ever question the entries? Once and glancingly: he "put it to Mr. Vose in 1916" and was told to let it alone, and did. He never questioned what the entries meant. By his own account the evenness "stops him looking" and the figure was "furniture"; Kyle says he never asked anybody anything in forty years; and in 1948 he reprinted the entry in full while still maintaining that cream is portable — without noticing that a surplus refutes a theft.

**D3.** Yes. He knew the station's tests ran low — that was the standing complaint from 1902, and he says so himself in 1926: "a manager who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing to the men who make them" is idle. He also knew the ledger showed the Association receiving more fat than the station credited: he signed the station-gain sheet every spring from 1913 (Teague: "a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him"), and in 1916 Teague raised it with him directly and was told to let it alone. And from 1919 he knew from Strawn that no added water had ever been found. What the sources do not show is that he understood the cause: he held to the water explanation publicly through 1926, called the condemned glass housekeeping, and misdated the condemnation by a month; Kyle says he "read the totals and never read the lines above them." So: he knew the tests ran low and had before him the evidence that the fat was arriving anyway; he attributed the lowness to the patrons and declined to look further. Whether he ever privately connected the gain line to under-crediting cannot be determined.

## Section F — Theory

**F1.** The lot of six dozen 17.6-c.c. milk-test pipettes that Ammon & Sons of Tarnet sold the Association on 14 April 1897 was blown short — most likely, on the firm's own historian's account, a reground mold that ran the whole lot consistently 0.88 c.c. under the mark, a fault a spot check of the first, tenth and fiftieth cannot catch because the pieces agree with each other. Each delivered 16.72 c.c., ninety-five hundredths of its mark. The Babcock bottle's neck is ruled to read per cent for a 17.6-c.c. charge; a charge one-twentieth short liberates a fat column one-twentieth short, read off in the ordinary way. So every test at Ostrey Hollow read 95 per cent of the truth. That fixes the size and the shape of the error: 3.80 true became 3.61 recorded, nineteen hundredths, which is exactly 5 per cent; it was proportional, so rich herds lost more than poor ones and the herds still ranked correctly among themselves; it was precise, so it looked like excellent work; and it was the same in February and July, wet years and dry, because glass does not know the season. It was invisible from inside because the pipette is the plant's own standard — every other element can be checked against something, and the pipette cannot, except by a burette from outside. It began on 1 May 1897, the day the outfit replaced Sill's borrowed glass (1896 on that glass: 3.80), and it ended on 12 June 1923, the day Strawn — issued a burette in 1921, having skipped the check in 1922 — ran the pipettes and condemned the lot; the new glass from July delivered its mark and 1924 read 3.80. Nothing on the farms or at the station had to change because nothing there was wrong: the same cows, ground, man and routine produced 3.80 whenever a true measure was in hand — the nine weeks of 1911 and the season of 1924 — and 3.61 whenever the Tarnet glass was. The fat withheld did not vanish: it went down the valley in the cream, was weighed into the vats at Larrow Green, and was entered every year as "station gain," 5 per cent of the fat received — 76,000 lb over 1898–1922, worth $19,000, of which the patrons got $3,800.

Three competing explanations:
1. *Added water* — Selby Vose (1919 circular, 1926 address), echoed by Sill. Defeated by: Strawn's lactometer on every visit from 1907 to 1936, never once finding water, and telling the manager so in 1919; Jerome Cudd's cheese, which would have betrayed watered milk in a day and never did in sixteen summers; the station-gain line — honestly tested thin milk yields no surplus at the creamery, yet the cream arrived 5 per cent heavy; the flat gap in every month regardless of price or season; and chronology — the rise came in 1924, after the new glass and before any petition or hearing, and the "water" stopped for exactly nine weeks in 1911 when a pipette broke.
2. *Cream taken by the tester* — Wilbur Teague before the board and in 1948; valley opinion (Vira, Rundle's chapter IV, Cleve's father). Defeated by: the ledger and Sill's receiving book — stolen cream arrives light, this arrived heavy every year; the 1911 interval, same man, figures up; the 1924 season at 3.80 with Keddie still at the bench; the exact and constant 5 per cent proportion, the signature of glass not of a hand; Strawn's watching him work "slow and clean"; and Rundle's own retraction on reading the ledgers.
3. *The wet hay of Hessel Bottom* — Grigg's 1908 committee, Grigg's 1919 letter, Dr. Kepp in 1926, Falke provisionally in 1959. Defeated by: Bulletin No. 214 (1911) — not more than 0.05 in winter and nothing in summer, against 0.19 in every month; Alder Corners' 3.80 from the same herds on the same ground; the 1911 nine weeks and 1924, same feed, figures up; the 1896 season at 3.80 on the same land; and the committee's own sampling of three winter months only.

*Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?* No. Loomis, who took every word, says the crate was never mentioned in any of the three sessions; Frick's exhibit list is four books; the crate sat in the loft above the weigh room during the two sittings held there (Duncan, Cleve, Orra); the "appliances" the board saw tried were the new 1923 glass, which delivered its mark; and, as Kyle says, nobody laid Strawn's 1923 notebook beside the receiving book.

*What did the tester believe?* On his own record, that he did not know: "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are," a question mark he refused for fourteen years to replace, and "no charge against any man." The one contemporaneous hint points at the glass — the question mark stands against the two measure entries — and his son says he came home in 1911 saying twice it was "something in the glass" but would not write what he could not prove. Frick reports a corridor remark that he privately blamed the patrons; it is uncorroborated, twenty-one years after, and contradicts the unsent letter. Between the two private reports the sources cannot decide; I weigh the written record and the question mark, and conclude he suspected the glass, would not assert it, and publicly professed not to know.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** For twenty-six years, from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923, the Ostrey Hollow skimming station of the Larrow Valley creamery cooperative tested its patrons' milk with pipettes from a short-blown lot: they delivered 16.72 c.c. instead of 17.6, so every test read 95 per cent of the truth — 3.61 instead of 3.80. Under a by-law making the station's own test final, six farms were paid one-twentieth short: 76,000 lb of butterfat, about $19,000. The missing fat arrived at the creamery in the cream and was booked yearly as "station gain, as usual." The valley blamed water (the manager), theft (the bookkeeper), and wet hay (a 1908 committee); a borrowed measure in 1911 showed the answer, unread. An inspector condemned the glass in 1923; a 1925 arbitration paid $3,800 "without admission" and the tester was dismissed. In 1958 his granddaughter found the crate in the loft, measured it, and cleared him. Each record held one piece; none held two.
