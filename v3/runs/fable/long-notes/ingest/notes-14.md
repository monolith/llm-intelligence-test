## CAST (consolidated across all eighteen sources)

**The Keddie family**
- **Ansel Keddie** — tester at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station. **Duncan (1968): "My father was Ansel Keddie, born in 1861 and died in 1934. He was tester and separator man at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association from the year it opened. He was a careful man in small things to a degree that other people found trying, and it was the making of the record he left and, I have sometimes thought, the unmaking of him."** (Born 1861 → 35 when the station opened, 64 at his dismissal, 73 at death.) Kept the test book in his own hand from the station's opening. Tested 29 seasons, 1896 through 1924; did not test in 1925. Dismissed ("put out") 30 April 1925 — Duncan: "dismissed on the thirtieth of April, 1925, four days before the board handed down what it called its finding, so that the finding found nothing and the dismissal said everything. He lived nine years after it and died in 1934 with the imputation on him, in this county, among men he had weighed milk for since before their fathers died." Died in the 1930s (Ivy: 1934; Duncan: 1934). "An old man" with "no other trade" in 1925. Vira says he went up in '95 to get the station ready; **Hazel (1981) agrees: "Ansel Keddie went up to that station in ninety-five, and he was still there when they turned him out, and in between he tested the milk of every man on that bottom including my grandfather, who was his wife's brother."** Duncan: "from the year it opened." Strawn (never names him), 1907: "keeps his book in his own hand, and his hand is better than most… works slow and clean and reads his own figure without being coaxed to it"; 1916: "the tester complains that his patrons blame him for their figures. I told him that is the trade and he said he knew it." Nym Item 8: his statement is "One leaf, ruled, both sides, in the hand of Item 3, signed"; the test book is "One hand throughout, twenty-nine seasons" — though Nym's Item 3 heading calls it "kept by E. Brant" and Item 4 "kept by A. Keddie" (transposed; Conflict 38). Selby Vose (1926) never names him nor mentions his dismissal. Roy Brant (1955): "married into the family that had more to lose by that station than any other family on the meadow"; Emmet stopped speaking to him in 1919 "because my father held that the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not." Vaught (1958): "a man who did not know what he was recording and set a question mark against it." Rundle (1939) never names him: "the figures went right when the man who kept it did not." Frick (1946): "had tested at that station since it opened in 1896 and had kept the book in his own hand every season since"; statement to the board 17 March 1925; "I believed him then and I believe him now. His denial was accurate and it did him no good at all… a man who cannot account for a figure is presumed by everybody in the room to be the figure's author." Frick's corridor remark (not in the minutes): "In the corridor at Ashlin, Keddie told me privately that he had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the room. He was not a bitter man about it. He had lived among them thirty years and would not stand up and accuse his neighbors, and I record it here because it has been said since that he went to his grave accusing nobody, which is true, and that he suspected nobody, which is not." Frick: dismissed 30 April, "four days before we made our award… There was no finding against him from us and there never was one… He was simply let go while the board was still sitting, and I have never been able to think well of the Association for it"; "an honest man was ruined by a figure nobody could explain." Frick's exhibit 1: test book "4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924, in the hand of Ansel Keddie." Grigg (1919, unnamed): "abuse the man who reads their figures for them." Sill (1934) never names him; his theory is water in the cans. **Hazel (1981):** "The man they said was cheating the patrons was married to the sister of the patron who lost the most by it, and ate at his table, and the two of them argued about drainage for forty years"; Roy came home from the hearing and said "they had let Ansel go, which was the finding whatever they wrote in the book"; "gladder about it for Ansel Keddie, who did not live to see it." Kepp (1926) never names him ("a second cause in a building"). **Duncan (1968) on 1911: "He came home that summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book. I was seventeen and I remember the kitchen and I remember that he said it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not his way. What he wrote in the book was a question mark. He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove, and I have thought since that this was the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had. He had, after all, nothing to hold in his hand but a suspicion about a piece of glass, and against it stood the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county. So he kept on. He kept on for another fourteen years, and wrote down every figure the glass gave him, and the figures were what convicted him."** Duncan: the patron he was said to have shorted worst "was his own wife's brother, who was at our table on a Sunday and had been for thirty-six years" (1889–1925).

## SOURCE 16 — Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S., "The bog meadows and the milk," paper read before the Ordell County Farm Club (1926)
*"A veterinary surgeon of thirty years' practice brings the agricultural literature to bear on an old local grievance, and the discussion from the floor is appended as taken down by the club's secretary."* Headed **THE BOG MEADOWS AND THE MILK**, *By Roland Kepp, V.S., of Ashlin*.
- Opening: "it is my habit, when I am called to a herd that is doing badly, to ask first what the cattle are standing on. I have found in thirty years that the answer to nine cases in ten is under the animal's feet, and that the tenth is in the water she drinks." The question "has been in this county longer than I have practiced in it, and… has been dealt with hitherto by opinion, by grumbling, and by the naming of names. It deserves better. It is a question of soil, of herbage, and of the mineral economy of the cow."
- **The land**: "A wet bottom is not a rich meadow with too much water in it. It is a distinct kind of ground with a distinct kind of chemistry… the year's growth does not rot down as it does on a hill field; it lies sour, and the soil that results is an acid soil, poor in lime, and poor in the phosphates… crowded out within a few seasons by sedge and rush and the coarse tussock grasses… That herbage carries bulk and it carries water. What it does not carry… is lime or phosphorus, and it is cut late because such ground cannot be got onto early, and late cutting takes out what little was in it."
- **What the cow does**: "A cow in milk is a manufactory working on a narrow margin… If her ration is short of the minerals, she does not simply give less; she robs her own frame to make good… The stock come out of the winter feeding thin over the loin and rough in the coat. They are slow to come to condition in the spring. They are ready subjects for every disorder of the digestion in the fall of the year… They breed later. Their feet are worse." "They will milk, gentlemen. Wet land is generous of flow and no man on a bog meadow ever complained that his pail was light. But what is in the pail is another matter, and the fat is the first thing to suffer, because the fat is the dearest thing the cow makes and it is the first economy she practices when she is pressed."
- **The measure**: "**The wet bottoms of this county yield a milk poorer in fat by a fifth of a point** and every practical man knows it. Set the hill herds of this county against the bottom herds and you will find that difference, farm for farm… the difference does not depend on the breed, on the man, or on the barn. It depends on the ground."
- **Hessel Bottom** (quoted under PLACES): six farms; springs at the foot of the slope; under water every spring; not fit to walk on until well into May; sedge hay "the winter dependence and very nearly the whole of it."
- **The complaint**: "The patrons of the skimming station at Ostrey Hollow have complained for something like a quarter of a century that their milk tests below the milk of the rest of the valley. Their complaint is perfectly just and I have never doubted it. **The figure of the gap, as it has been given to me and as I have satisfied myself, is twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21** — between the milk of that station and the milk of this valley at large. A committee of practical men went over the ground in 1908 and found the herds sound, the milk whole, and the scales honest, and laid the cause to the hay of that bottom. The matter has been before committees since and before boards, and it is not settled, and it will not be settled by men shouting at one another across a weigh room. It is settled already, in the literature, for anybody who will read."
- **The College**: "The State Agricultural College at Farland conducted a feeding trial upon bog hay and published it as its Bulletin No. 214… it found the butter fat test depressed by **two tenths in the winter feeding**. That is the whole case. Two tenths from the College, laid alongside the fifth of a point I have found in the field and the twenty-one hundredths that station has been complaining of, and the three agree so nearly that I do not know what more a reasonable man requires."
- **An objection**: "It has been urged upon me, by a gentleman who I know will forgive my noticing it here, that **in the summer of 1911 the testing at that station was broken off for six weeks, their own apparatus having failed them and a borrowed one being used, and that the figures stood higher during those six weeks than before or after. Six weeks in the flush of the season, gentlemen. Six weeks of June and July**, when every herd in the county is at its best and the stock are off the wet ground and up on the aftermath. I am astonished that anybody should found an argument on six weeks of summer figures. I could produce you six weeks in any year that would prove anything you liked."
- **The remedy** (quoted under PLACES). "**Fat has been worth twenty-seven cents the pound through these years**, and a fifth of a point taken off every hundredweight a farm ships, year in and year out, will pay for a great deal of tile."
- **DISCUSSION**: *A member* asked whether the difference might be in the cows rather than the land, "the bottom farms being poorer men with poorer stock." *Dr. Kepp*: considered and rejected it; "some of the best-bred cattle in the county stand on wet land, and some very indifferent cattle on the hills test well; and… a man may improve his herd for twenty years and never get his fat up while his ground is sour." *A member* asked whether the trouble might lie in the station rather than the bottom, "as had been said in the valley for years." *Dr. Kepp*: "he had no interest in that quarrel and no competence in it; that he had never in his life read a test and would not know how; but that when a cause is found in the soil, in the herbage, in the condition of the stock, in the College's trial, and in every wet bottom in the county alike, it is not good sense to go looking for a second cause in a building." *The chairman* thanked Dr. Kepp; the paper "would be printed for the members."

## SOURCE 17 — Hazel Brant, "farmer's daughter and last keeper of the weigh book," recorded at Ashlin (1981)
*"Taped at her kitchen table over two afternoons; the interviewer's prompts are in brackets. She had no papers in front of her and did not want any."*
- "I'll tell you the trouble with me before you start the machine… Quantities I have. Years I have not. I can give you what came off that bottom in a season to the pound, and I could not tell you the year I was married without walking into the front room for the certificate, and I have been married a long while. My father was the same way. He used to say a year was a thing other people kept, like bees."
- **[Start with the book.]** "It is a tall narrow account book, canvas over board, and the spine went soft on the left where he carried it under his arm from the barn to the house in all weather. My grandfather Emmet began it in nineteen hundred, on the first day of January, because he was a man who wanted a clean date under a new habit. He weighed his own milk on his own scale before he ever put a can on the wagon, and then the station weighed it again at the intake, and he wrote both figures down side by side. If the two ever disagreed he would stand in the doorway of that weigh room with his hat on until somebody found out why. They generally agreed. That was the point of him." "Then my father Roy had the book, from the year my grandfather died — don't ask me, I'll only guess — and he kept it exactly the same way for the rest of his life, and then it was mine, and now it is down the street at the historical rooms in this town where anybody may look at it who wants to, which is nobody." The loan to Ivy (quoted under Ivy): "he lent it. He did not leave it to her."
- **[The nineteen-ten leaf.]** "That one I can say with my eyes shut, and it is the only year I am certain of, because it is written at the head of the page in his hand and I have read that page a thousand times. Nineteen ten. Two hundred thousand pounds even, delivered to the station. Even — that is what fixes it… as if the cows had been consulted. His scale and theirs agreeing, he wrote, which was his way of saying that he had checked and was not going to make a speech about it. And paid on a test of three forty-two." "Three forty-two. You set that against what that milk actually was and you have the whole of the business in one line, and nobody set it against anything for fifty years."
- **[How many farms?]** "Six. Hessel Bottom was six farms and no more" (quoted under PLACES; the cold spring at the upper end where the cans stood). "Four of those six men hauled over the ridge in the summer flush, to the cheese factory on the far side, and brought their milk back down to our own station the rest of the year. Four. My father used to say it was the only honest arithmetic anybody in that valley ever did without meaning to."
- **[The difference in the tests.]** "**Nineteen hundredths.** Every month, every season, wet years and dry: nineteen hundredths lower at our station than the same milk was anywhere else on earth. Not a little in the winter and none in the summer, the way a feed thing goes. Nineteen hundredths, flat as a floor. I have heard men who could not add a column of five figures explain to my father why that was the hay."
- **[About Mr. Keddie.]** "Ansel Keddie went up to that station in ninety-five, and he was still there when they turned him out, and in between he tested the milk of every man on that bottom including my grandfather, who was his wife's brother." "That is the part people can't hold in their heads, so I will go slowly. **Orra was Ansel Keddie's wife, and Orra was a half sister to my grandfather Emmet, by his father's second wife.** So Ansel married into us. The man they said was cheating the patrons was married to the sister of the patron who lost the most by it, and ate at his table, and the two of them argued about drainage for forty years." "Then their boy Duncan and my father Roy were first cousins — the children of a brother and a sister, which is what makes first cousins. And Ivy is Duncan's girl, and I am Roy's, so Ivy Keddie and I are second cousins. People are forever telling me she is my niece or my cousin once removed or some such, and she is not. Second cousins. It is not complicated; it is only that nobody wants to count."
- **[The glassware.]** "**The crate came out from Tarnet in ninety-nine**, and it sat up in the loft over the weigh room for most of my life without one soul thinking about it. **The state man condemned that glass in twenty-one** — went at it with some measuring instrument he carried in his coat, said it would not deliver what it was marked for, and told them to order new. They ordered new. Nobody said one word about all the tests that were already in the book."
- "**The hearing was in twenty-six.** My father drove down to this town for it, and he came back and hung his coat up and said the board had heard everybody and found nothing, and that they had let Ansel go, which was the finding whatever they wrote in the book. He never went to another meeting of that Association as long as he lived, and he was a man who liked meetings."
- **[The manager's letter?]** "Oh, the water letter. He sent that round to nine families on that bottom, nine letters, warning us about adding water to the milk. My mother read hers twice and put it in the stove. I was small but I remember the stove." "Nine families, six farms, if you are counting — a farm can hold more than one family and generally did. He sent nine and he was answered by none of them, which in that valley is a shout."
- **[When your cousin found it out?]** "Ivy found the crate up in that loft in the winter, under a tarpaulin and about an inch of chaff, and took the glass back east with her and measured it, and it was the glass. It had always been the glass. Every man on that bottom had been paid short for a generation because a piece of glass would not deliver what was blown on the side of it." "I was glad about it for my grandfather and gladder about it for Ansel Keddie, who did not live to see it, and I will tell you the truth, I was a little sour too. All that arguing, all those men in their good coats. A thing you could have settled in an afternoon with a burette and a straight face, and it took fifty years and a girl from the college who thought to climb a ladder."

## SOURCE 18 — Duncan Keddie, "Notes for the family," written at Ashlin, 1968 (with an addition of 1970)
*"A retired schoolteacher, seventy-four, sets down for his daughter and her children who everyone was and where every book went; the sections are his own."*
- Opening: "I am writing this out because my daughter has spent ten years putting the case right in print and nobody has ever put the family right on paper at all, and because when I am gone there will be no one left who was in the house. I have kept to what I know. Where I do not know a thing I have said so. I have not tried to make anybody in these pages better than he was, and I have not needed to."
- **I. The people.** "My father was Ansel Keddie, born in 1861 and died in 1934. He was tester and separator man at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association from the year it opened. He was a careful man in small things to a degree that other people found trying, and it was the making of the record he left and, I have sometimes thought, the unmaking of him." "My mother was Orra Brant, born in eighteen sixty-eight. She married my father in 1889. She was Emmet Brant's sister, the younger of the two, and Emmet farmed the largest place on Hessel Bottom and hauled the most milk into the station of any man on the creek. That fact sat in the middle of our family for a generation and hardly anyone outside it ever noticed. When they said in 1925 that my father had been shorting the patrons, the patron he was said to have shorted worst was his own wife's brother, who was at our table on a Sunday and had been for thirty-six years." "I am Duncan, born in 1894, their son. I taught school at Ashlin the better part of my life, arithmetic among other things, and I have set out the arithmetic of this family below in the way I should have wanted it set out for me. My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise and once heard it said aloud in this town by a man who ought to have known."
- **II. What the connections are, set out so that they can be copied.** "Emmet Brant and my mother Orra were brother and sister. Emmet's son was Roy Brant, who farmed the Hessel Bottom place after him and kept his father's weigh book. Roy Brant and I are therefore first cousins — the children of a brother and a sister, which is the whole definition and there is nothing more to it." "Roy's daughter is Hazel Brant. My daughter is Ivy. Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece, which people say because it is shorter." "Two more, since they are always got wrong. Selby Vose, who managed the Association, was Gideon Vose's son. Gideon organized the Association and was its first president and wrote its by-laws; Selby was his son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew. And Rosalie Cudd of Vessey County, who ran the cheese company over the ridge after her father, was Jerome Cudd's daughter." "Merle Strawn, the state's dairy inspector on this circuit, was kin to nobody in the valley whatever — not to us, not to the Brants, not to the Voses, not to anybody on the creek. That is worth writing down, because his notebooks turned out to matter, and it matters that the man who kept them had no interest in any of us."
- **III. A thing that is said and is not so.** "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either. I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her sit down. It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me. She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it."
- **IV. My father.** "He was dismissed on the thirtieth of April, 1925, four days before the board handed down what it called its finding, so that the finding found nothing and the dismissal said everything. He lived nine years after it and died in 1934 with the imputation on him, in this county, among men he had weighed milk for since before their fathers died." "He came home that summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book. I was seventeen and I remember the kitchen and I remember that he said it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not his way. What he wrote in the book was a question mark. He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove, and I have thought since that this was the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had. He had, after all, nothing to hold in his hand but a suspicion about a piece of glass, and against it stood the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county. So he kept on. He kept on for another fourteen years, and wrote down every figure the glass gave him, and the figures were what convicted him." "My mother outlived him by fourteen years and never spoke of the business except once a year, in April. She was eighty-two when she died in 1948. She had kept every letter and she left them to me, and there is nothing in them that anybody would want, only the weather and the price of things and her brother's health, and I am keeping them anyway."
- **V. The books, and where they went.** "This is the part I chiefly wanted set down, because books go astray and people afterward say that a thing was never written when it was only never found." *The station test book.* "My father kept it in his own hand from 1896 until he left. In March of 1925 the board of arbitration impounded it, and after the hearing it went into the Cooperative office at Ashlin, where it lay from 1925 until 1959. In 1959 it went to the Ordell County Historical Rooms in this town, where it is now." *The Brant weigh book.* "Emmet Brant began it on the first of January, 1897, and kept it until his death in 1929, when it went to his son Roy. Roy lent it to Ivy in 1958 — lent, and I want the word — and had it back, and at his death in 1962 it went to his daughter Hazel, and Hazel gave it to the Historical Rooms in 1963." *Strawn's circuit notebooks.* "He kept one for every year he rode the Ordell circuit. They went to the state dairy office when he retired in 1936, and in 1947 they were transferred to the library of the State Agricultural College at Farland, which is where Ivy found the volume for 1923 eleven years later. Nobody had asked for them in the interval." *The Alder Corners test book.* "Jerome Cudd kept it from the founding of his company; it passed to his daughter Rosalie in 1941 when she took the factory on; and in 1958 she had the figures copied out and sent to Ivy at the college. The book itself has never been out of Vessey County." *The crate.* "The station's condemned glassware went up into the loft over the intake room in 1923 and stayed there until Ivy found it in February of 1958. It went east to Farland to be measured and came back, and in 1959 it was deposited with the Historical Rooms along with the test book. Twenty feet of ladder, thirty-five years, and two of the three sessions of that arbitration sat in the room underneath it." *Added, 1970.* "Mrs. Cudd's test book has this year gone to the Vessey County Historical Society, and the five books are now in three buildings and are none of them ours."
