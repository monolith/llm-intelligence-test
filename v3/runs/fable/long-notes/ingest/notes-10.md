# Retention notes — Segment 10 (Retellings 01–12; the Ostrey Hollow skimming-station affair)

Twelve retellings so far, all about the same events: the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, its skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, tester Ansel Keddie, the long low butterfat tests there, the 1925 board of arbitration, and the 1958–59 discovery that the station's pipettes were short. ## CAST (consolidated across all twelve sources)

**The Keddie family**
- **Ansel Keddie** — tester at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station. Kept the test book in his own hand from the station's opening. Tested 29 seasons, 1896 through 1924; did not test in 1925. Dismissed ("put out") 30 April 1925. Died in the 1930s (Ivy: 1934). "An old man" with "no other trade" in 1925. Vira says he went up in '95 to get the station ready. Strawn (never names him), 1907: "keeps his book in his own hand, and his hand is better than most… works slow and clean and reads his own figure without being coaxed to it"; 1916: "the tester complains that his patrons blame him for their figures. I told him that is the trade and he said he knew it." Nym Item 8: his statement is "One leaf, ruled, both sides, in the hand of Item 3, signed"; the test book is "One hand throughout, twenty-nine seasons" — though Nym's Item 3 heading calls it "kept by E. Brant" and Item 4 (the Brant weigh book) "kept by A. Keddie" (transposed; Conflict 38). Selby Vose (1926) never names him nor mentions his dismissal. Roy Brant (1955): "Ansel Keddie, who kept the station's test book, was married into the family that had more to lose by that station than any other family on the meadow"; Emmet stopped speaking to him in 1919 "because my father held that the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not." Vaught (1958): "a man who did not know what he was recording and set a question mark against it." **Rundle (1939) never names him**: "the figures went right when the man who kept it did not" — i.e. the valley's "settled opinion" that he took cream by the back door, which Rundle then retracts in his in-press note.
- **Orra Keddie** — Ansel's wife; Emmet Brant's sister — Roy: "full brother and sister, same mother and same father, whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it." First cousin of "old Mrs. Vose" (Vira). After 1919 she and her brother never spoke; she walked to Larrow Green rather than pass his lane. Outlived Ansel "a good while."
- **Duncan Keddie** — Ansel and Orra's son ("boy"); taught school at Ashlin; Ivy's father; Roy Brant's first cousin (Roy).
- **Ivy Keddie** — Ansel's granddaughter; college at Farland; gave the 1959 talk "What the Crate Held." Nym: "Miss Ivy Keddie of the State Agricultural College at Farland." Rosalie Cudd addresses her as "Miss Keddie," "a graduate student at Farland," "a young woman from the college who asks [a plain question] in four lines and apologizes for troubling me." Found the crate February 1958 (Nym: Association office at Ashlin; Ivy: station loft); measured nine pipettes at Farland 9 March 1958; gave crate to the Rooms 1959; borrowed the Brant weigh book from Roy Brant in 1958 (Roy's postscript: "Duncan's girl Ivy came out to the place this year and asked whether she might borrow the book for a piece of work she is doing at the college at Farland. I let her have it. She is careful with paper") and returned it; found and copied Strawn's 1923 volume at Farland 1958; wrote to Rosalie Cudd 1958 and received the Alder Corners figures. Vira's 1958 visitor "from the college away east… Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed" is Ivy; **Roy Brant says Hazel and Ivy are second cousins; Rundle's 1959 footnote agrees: "Miss Ivy Keddie, who is second cousin to Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom"** (Conflict 20/47). Rundle 1959: "The Hollow matter has since been taken up again at the college at Farland by Miss Ivy Keddie."

**The Brant family (Hessel Bottom)**
- **Emmet Brant** — **born 1854, died 1929** (Roy). Biggest man on Hessel Bottom: most cows, most hay ground; largest patron every year (Effie); Roy: "Ours was the largest of the six — sixty-odd acres of the meadow, and thirty-eight cows in a good year, which for this valley was a herd." Orra Keddie's full brother. Roy: "From the first of January, 1898, until the week before he died, he wrote down every can of milk that went out of this barn" (Nym/Ivy: from 1 January 1897 — Conflict 61). Wrote "at the kitchen table after the second milking, in the lamp, with his hat still on, and my mother would not have the table cleared till he was done." Among the first to say out loud (1902) that the milk tested low — "and they were laughed at, and one of them was my father." Read the 1908 report twice, put it in the drawer, "said that the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not." Noticed the 1911 rise and fall and entered both; "could make nothing of it." "One who never forgave" the 1919 paper. 1910 leaf: 200,000 lb even, "Paid on a test of 3.42"; he wrote "even" and underlined it, "which for him was carrying on."
- **Roy Brant** — Emmet's son; **sixty-seven in winter 1955 (born c. 1888), "still milking"**; wrote Retelling 09 in longhand at Hessel Bottom for the county farm bureau, postscript 1958. Kept up the weigh book since his father died, "in the same book until it was filled and then in another, in the same way." Cut the meadow "with a scythe and with a mower behind two horses." Did not haul to Alder Corners ("We had the biggest herd on the meadow, and the biggest herd is the one that cannot afford to be talked about, and I have thought since that this was a poor reason and cost us money"). Duncan Keddie's first cousin. Lent the book to Ivy 1958. "It goes to Hazel when I am done with it." Nym: book to Roy 1929; to Hazel 1962.
- **Hazel Brant** — **Roy's daughter** (Roy: "My daughter Hazel and his daughter Ivy are second cousins… both of them have had to have it explained to them by me more than once"). Vira: the 1958 visitor's "first cousin once removed." Nym: "Miss Hazel Brant" held the weigh book from 1962; received at the Rooms 1963. Rundle 1959: "Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom."

**The Vose family (Larrow Green)**
- **Gideon Vose** — first president 1889–1911 (22 years); organized the Association; drafted the by-laws (not a lawyer); died 1917. Selby: "My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law… kept a herd of Jerseys at the home place… In 1910 that herd tested 4.56." **Rundle:** elected first president 1889; "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the law and thought better of it, and he wrote the by-laws himself, at his own kitchen table, in a hand that the Association's clerks were still complaining about thirty years later." "His nephew Selby Vose… used to say that his uncle wrote by-laws the way other men build stone wall — slowly, and to last." "Gideon Vose put that [the 'station… shall govern' clause] in for convenience and it was convenient for thirty years."
- **Selby Vose** — manager 1913–1938 (Effie). Signed and wrote the 1919 "water in the cans" circular. CONFLICT on relation: Effie "son of the late president"; Vira "old Gideon Vose's nephew"; Selby "My father"; **Rundle "His nephew Selby Vose, afterward manager of the Association."** Signed the annual station sheet every year; told Teague in 1916 to let the station account alone. Strawn 1913: "New manager… came out with me to the platform, which the last one never did"; 1919: told him no water ever found; 12 June 1923: "told the manager to order new." His 1926 address: thirteenth year; water-in-the-cans explanation that "stopped when the Association began to look"; dates the condemnation "the twelfth of July, 1923"; 1924 average 3.90; "I ask for one more" (served to 1938). Roy (1955): "In 1919 the manager sent a paper round to the Hollow patrons warning us against putting water in the milk." Rundle: "In 1919 the manager circulated a warning to the Hollow patrons against adding water to their milk."
- **old Mrs. Vose** — Orra's first cousin (Vira).

## PLACES
- **Larrow Valley**; **Larrow Green ("the Green")** — hamlet; Association organized 1889 (Rundle: in the schoolhouse); main creamery built 1891 on Larrow Creek (Rundle: "on the creek below the mill"; the churn set up in it). Strawn inspected 1913, 1917, 1931, 1936. Nym Item 16: Larrow Green average "a little under two tenths of a point better" than the station's 3.61. Roy: the Hollow milk ran "seventeen hundredths of a point under what the same class of milk was making down at Larrow Green." Nyle Grigg was "of Larrow Green." Dell Rundle taught the school there; the pamphlet was "Printed at Larrow Green"; the store where the history was told.
- **Ostrey Hollow ("the Hollow")** — seven miles up the valley (Rundle: "seven miles up from the Green, and in the nineties the pull down was three hours in mud and could not be done at all in March"); station at the head of the Hollow where the road bends to the ridge; low whitewashed building (Rundle: "a plain one, boarded and battened, with the intake room at the road end and a loft over it that I believe has never been cleared out since it was put up; and behind it the creek, which in a wet April comes up to the sill and has twice come over it"); intake room, loft over it (ladder), wash room, cold room, boiler, separator; opened 1896 (Rundle: "the milk was weighed and tested and separated on the spot, the cream came down to the Green by wagon, and the skim went home to the calves and the pigs the same morning"); shut by 1958 (fence wire and salt). Strawn 1909: takes from the wet bottom below and three hill places. Vaught: "one skimming station in Ordell County… below those of its own creamery seven miles down the same valley." Rundle: "the head of the valley."
- **Hessel Bottom** — Roy: "a hundred and sixty acres of wet meadow lying under the hill at Ostrey Hollow. **Six farms** had rights on it and cut hay off it and pastured it" (Effie: seven farms). Brant's sixty-odd acres of it. "The water stands on the low end into May, and there is a soft place by the alders that has taken a horse to the hock in my time and my father's. The hay off it is coarse, and it cuts late, and a cow will eat it and do well enough… it never failed to grow something." Jerome Cudd: "Good milk off wet ground."
- **Ashlin** — Association office (over a harness shop); board of arbitration 1925 ("sat at Ashlin that spring" — Rundle); Duncan Keddie taught school there; Historical Rooms/Society; Nym: crate found there Feb 1958; Alonzo Frick "attorney, of that town"; before 1889 the farmers hauled butter to the merchant there (Rundle).
- **Ordell County / Ordell County Historical Rooms / the Ordell circuit** (four counties, fifty-odd plants).
- **Redlow Ridge** — Rosalie: "Four miles of switchback, cut for a lumber road and never much improved since. It shuts in November and it does not open before April, some years May… the drifts sit in the cut below the top." Cloud "sits on the top of the ridge till the sun is well up." Rundle: "over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County"; "over the mountain."
- **Alder Corners, Vessey County** — the Alder Corners Cheese Company, built 1913 (Rosalie) / opened 1914 (Roy; Rundle), "on the flat below the church"; spring water on the lot; hard road out to the east; make room; "little room off the intake" (Jerome's test bench); platform with scale. Its book never left Vessey County (Nym); to the Vessey County Historical Society 1970.
- **Farland** — the State Agricultural College (Ivy's; Vaught's; Strawn's notebooks in its library from 1947; pipettes measured there). Vaught calls it "This station." Rundle 1959: "the college at Farland… I refer the reader to the college."
- **Tarnet** — Ammon & Sons.

## SOURCE 9 — Roy Brant, account for the county farm bureau's local-history collection (Hessel Bottom, winter 1955; postscript 1958)
*"Set down in longhand at Hessel Bottom in the winter of 1955 by Roy Brant, sixty-seven years old and still milking, with a postscript he added three years afterward."*
- "The bureau asked me for something on the old days at the head of the valley. I would rather write about my father's book… the book is on the shelf behind my chair, and a man does better to talk about what he can put his hand on."
- "My father was Emmet Brant. He was born in 1854 and he died in 1929. From the first of January, 1898, until the week before he died, he wrote down every can of milk that went out of this barn, and I have kept it up since, in the same book until it was filled and then in another, in the same way, because he showed me how and because I never found a reason to stop."
- **The Bottom**: 160 acres wet meadow under the hill at Ostrey Hollow; **six farms** had rights, cut hay and pastured it; Brant's the largest — sixty-odd acres of meadow, thirty-eight cows in a good year. Water stands on the low end into May; soft place by the alders; hay coarse, cuts late; "whatever has been said about that hay by men who never fed it."
- **The book**: "a ledger, a plain one, ruled by hand. Two columns for each day of delivery. In the first column my father entered the weight of every can as he took it off his own scale in his own barn before the wagon went down the road. In the second he entered the figure the station gave him back for that same milk. That is all it is. There is no comment in it anywhere but three or four lines in thirty years, and no opinion at all. The cover is gone off the front, the back is held by a strip of harness leather my father tacked on himself, and the first forty leaves are stained brown at one corner where it lay in a leaky window a winter he was sick."
- "The two columns agreed… his weight and the station's weight came out the same, barring a pound or two here and there, and those were days when a can got slopped on the hill. Two scales, his and the station's, kept by two men who by the end were not speaking to one another, and the scales never fell out at all. Whatever was wrong at that station — and something was wrong at that station for a very long time — it was not the weighing."
- 1910 leaf (quoted, identical to Nym): *"Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42."* — "He was pleased about the evenness of it; he wrote 'even' and underlined it, which for him was carrying on. The price of butterfat that year was twenty-four cents. Over the whole stretch of years the complaint covers, one year with another, it came to about twenty-five cents."
- Kitchen table, second milking, lamp, hat on; "It took him no time at all, and it is the only reason there is anything left to argue about now."
- **The trouble**: "Our milk at the Hollow ran seventeen hundredths of a point under what the same class of milk was making down at Larrow Green. Seventeen hundredths is nothing to look at on one pay slip. Set the slips in a row for a lifetime of farming and it is a farm." First said out loud 1902, laughed at, father among them. "it was seventeen hundredths in July and it was seventeen hundredths in February. It did not come and go with the season. It did not care whether the cows were on the meadow or on hay in the barn."
- **1908**: committee "with Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green at the head of it," sampled through the winter; wet-hay report; father: "the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not."
- **1911**: "There were nine weeks in the summer of 1911 when the station's own measure was out of use, it having been broken. The state man left his own measure that June and that is what they used. Through those nine weeks our tests came up, all of us, every farm on the Bottom; and then the station's own came back into use and the figures went down again… My father noticed the rise and he noticed the fall and he entered both, and he could make nothing of it, and I could make nothing of it either, and there it sat for near on fifty years."
- **1919**: "the manager sent a paper round to the Hollow patrons warning us against putting water in the milk. Nine families at the head of the valley got that paper and nine families read it for what it was. My father was one who never forgave it. He and Ansel Keddie were brothers-in-law, and from 1919 they did not speak — not from any quarrel over the water, but because my father held that the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not."
- **Alder Corners**: "From 1915, four of the Bottom men hauled their milk over Redlow Ridge to the cheese factory in the summer flush and back to the Hollow the rest of the year. The factory over there had opened in 1914. They went on that way for sixteen seasons. It was for the price and the flush market and a shorter haul while the road was open, and it was talked about here as though they had gone over to the enemy, which shows you what a valley is. I did not go over."
- **Family**: Orra = father's full sister. "Duncan Keddie, who taught school at Ashlin, was Ansel and Orra's boy, and he and I are first cousins. My daughter Hazel and his daughter Ivy are second cousins."
- "I am sixty-seven and the book has outlived two of the three men who are in it. It goes to Hazel when I am done with it. That is all the arrangement I have made and all I intend to make."
- **Postscript, 1958**: "Duncan's girl Ivy came out to the place this year and asked whether she might borrow the book for a piece of work she is doing at the college at Farland. I let her have it. She is careful with paper. It comes back to me when she is through, and after me it is Hazel's."

## SOURCE 11 — Leland Vaught, introduction and method to Bulletin No. 471 (State Agricultural College at Farland, October 1958)
*"The opening and method sections of the bulletin, written for extension agents and county workers, closing with the bulletin's own concluding paragraph reproduced entire."* Signed "— L. VAUGHT."
- **§I Why written**: "This station has been asked, at intervals for fifty years, why the butterfat tests at one skimming station in Ordell County ran below those of its own creamery seven miles down the same valley… answered at various times by reference to the land and by reference to the men. It is answered here by reference to a piece of glass." "Nobody in that valley lacked the intelligence to find it. What they lacked was any occasion to suspect the one article in the plant that nobody in the trade is in the habit of suspecting." Bold: **"the milk pipette is the one instrument in the butterfat test which the plant cannot check against anything else, and it is the instrument on which every figure in the plant depends."**
- **§II The instrument**: four operations — measured charge into a test bottle; sulfuric acid; spun in a hand tester; fat read directly in percent in the graduated neck. "The operator weighs nothing." Standard charge **17.6 c.c.** Short pipette → reading "low in the exact proportion by which the charge was short." Three consequences: **proportional not additive** (rich milk loses more; ranking of herds undisturbed; "Only against the outside world is it wrong"); **precise** ("A pipette that delivers 16.27 c.c. where it is blown 17.6 delivers 16.27 c.c. every time, for a quarter of a century, in January and in July"); **invisible from inside the plant** (scales checked with weights, tester's speed counted, acid checked, bottle graduation checked with mercury, but "the pipette is the standard… It can be caught only by a burette brought in from outside, and burettes reached the state's field men very late in this history").
- **§III Material**: "Of the lot bought for the Ostrey Hollow station in 1897 — **five dozen** pipettes were purchased — **nine** were recovered from the station's condemned crate, unbroken and legible… stems are blown 17.6 c.c… five runs to each pipette, and the mean delivery of the nine is **16.27 c.c.**" "The station's own glass went into use on **the first of May, 1898**, and remained in use until the state inspector condemned the lot in June of 1923." Method: water at room temperature, tip wiped, delivered in working manner into a certified burette, five deliveries averaged; "occupies an afternoon"; "no reason why every station in this state should not be checked once in this way."
- **§IV 1911**: nine weeks; "One variable changed"; 3.61 → 3.80 and back "on the day the station's own measure came back into service"; recorded "by a man who did not know what he was recording and set a question mark against it." "an experiment… that ran itself… and lay unread for forty-seven years."
- **§V Magnitude**: 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = **0.9244**; 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = **3.905**, shortfall **0.295**; on 40,000,000 lb over twenty-five years, **118,000 lb** "delivered and not credited." "It should be said that this figure is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the glassware." Station gain "five and a quarter percent of the butterfat actually delivered."
- **§VI Feed**: Bulletin No. 214 quoted (identical); "Five hundredths in winter; nothing at all in summer. The discrepancy… is present in every month of the year and is some six times the largest effect the trial could produce."
- **§VII Conclusion (quoted)**: *"No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."*

## SOURCE 12 — Dell Rundle, schoolteacher, "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years," a jubilee pamphlet (1939; reprinted 1959)
*"Printed at Larrow Green for the Association's fiftieth year, compiled from the recollections of old members and two winters of asking questions at the store; reissued in 1959 with one footnote and the author's closing note unaltered."* Sections: "TO THE MEMBERS"; I. THE MEN OF 1889; II. THE RULE THAT MADE THE TEST THE MASTER; III. THE STATION AT OSTREY HOLLOW; IV. THE TROUBLE AT THE HEAD OF THE VALLEY; V. OVER THE RIDGE (with footnote [^1] added 1959); VI. FIFTY YEARS; "NOTE ADDED WHILE IN PRESS," signed "— D. RUNDLE, Larrow Green."
- **To the members**: "Fifty years is not long for a country, but it is a long time for a valley to keep one thing going, and that is what this pamphlet is about. I have taught the school at the Green a good many years now and I have listened to this history in pieces, at funerals and in the back of the store, for the whole of them. What follows is not a record. It is what the valley remembers, tidied, and where the valley is certain of a thing I have written it down as certain."
- **I. The men of 1889**: "In 1889 the farmers of this valley met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. They were tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking whatever the merchant chose to allow them for it, and they had worked out — the way farmers do, slowly and then all at once — that the way to be paid properly for a thing is to own the place that buys it." "They met in the schoolhouse, in cold weather, and the older men who were there remembered afterward chiefly that it went on very late and that somebody's horse got loose in the middle of it." "Gideon Vose was elected the first president. He was a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the law and thought better of it, and he wrote the by-laws himself, at his own kitchen table, in a hand that the Association's clerks were still complaining about thirty years later. His nephew Selby Vose, afterward manager of the Association, used to say that his uncle wrote by-laws the way other men build stone wall — slowly, and to last." "In 1891 the creamery was built at Larrow Green, on the creek below the mill, and the churn was set up in it, and the valley began to be paid in cash once a month, which had never happened to it before. There are men still living who can tell you what the first check did in their mother's kitchen, and they will tell you whether you ask or not."
- **II. The rule**: "Of all Gideon Vose's by-laws, one has done more to this valley than the rest together, and I print it whole:" — **Article VII quoted, identical to Effie and Nym**: *"Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."* "**Article VII dates from 1891, the year the churn was set up.** It was thought a hard rule at the time and there were men who left the Association over it. It is a just rule. A man who keeps good cows and feeds them ought not to be paid the same as the man who keeps poor ones, and the pound of milk is a liar where the pound of butterfat is not." "But mark the last clause, because the valley has had fifty years to learn what it means. *The test of the station where he delivers shall govern.* Not the test of the creamery. Not any test but the one made at his own door. Gideon Vose put that in for convenience and it was convenient for thirty years, and it is the sentence on which the whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned."
- **III. The station**: "The Hollow is seven miles up from the Green, and in the nineties the pull down was three hours in mud and could not be done at all in March. So the Association built a skimming station up there, and it opened in 1896: the milk was weighed and tested and separated on the spot, the cream came down to the Green by wagon, and the skim went home to the calves and the pigs the same morning. It was a great convenience and the Hollow was glad of it. The building is a plain one, boarded and battened, with the intake room at the road end and a loft over it that I believe has never been cleared out since it was put up; and behind it the creek, which in a wet April comes up to the sill and has twice come over it."
- **IV. The trouble**: "I would rather not write this chapter and it cannot be left out." "From 1902 the men of the Hollow complained that their milk tested lower at their own station than the same class of milk tested at the Green. They were not believed. In 1908 the Association sent a committee up under Nyle Grigg, which sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through the winter and reported that the wet hay of that bottom thinned the cream, and that the remedy lay with the land. The Hollow did not accept it. In 1919 the manager circulated a warning to the Hollow patrons against adding water to their milk, and after that the two ends of this valley did not speak civilly to one another for six years. I have had the children of both ends in one schoolroom through those years and I can tell you that it came down to them entire, without a word of it being explained, the way a debt does." **"It is the settled opinion of the valley that cream left that station by the back door, and the figures went right when the man who kept it did not."** "I set that down as I have heard it set down a hundred times, by men who were there and men who were not, and I have never heard it contradicted at the store." "The state inspector condemned the station's glassware on the twelfth of July, 1923, for reasons of his own that had nothing to do with the quarrel, and new glass was put in. In January of 1925 the Hollow patrons petitioned the Association. **The station took in, over the years complained of, forty-four million pounds of milk. The patrons' loss upon it was nineteen thousand six hundred dollars, and their petition set the figure out to the dollar.** A board of arbitration sat at Ashlin that spring under Alonzo Frick, attorney, of that town. It heard everybody. It found nothing established, it made an award to the patrons without admitting anything, and it sent this valley home to live with itself, which the valley has done, after a fashion, ever since." (No award sum; no mention of Keddie's dismissal, the 1911 episode, or the crate.)
- **V. Over the ridge**: "I should mention, because it belongs to the history and because the young people no longer know it, that in 1914 the Alder Corners Cheese Company was opened over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County, by Jerome Cudd, and that in the summers a few of our Hessel Bottom men hauled over the mountain to him rather than down to their own station. That was resented here. It is Jerome Cudd's daughter, Rosalie Cudd, who makes the cheese at Alder Corners now.[^1]"
- **Footnote [^1] (quoted; "Added to the 1959 reprint")**: *"The Hollow matter has since been taken up again at the college at Farland by Miss Ivy Keddie, who is second cousin to Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom. Eleven pipettes out of the station's old condemned crate were found still to be in existence. I am too old to rewrite a pamphlet and I refer the reader to the college. — D.R."*
- **VI. Fifty years**: "Set against the quarrel, put the rest: fifty Junes of cream coming down that road in the early cool, fifty Januaries of it going down over ice with the horses roughshod, a check every month in kitchens that had known none, and a creamery still standing that was built by men with nothing but each other and a piece of paper they had written themselves. Whatever this valley did wrong at the head of it, it did this right, and it did it for half a century, and the men of 1889 would think we had done well."
- **Note added while in press (quoted in full)**: "While this pamphlet was being set up in type I was allowed to look at the Association's old ledgers in the office, on another errand altogether. The station account carries, every year, an entry of this form, in the bookkeeper's hand:" — *"Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account."* (identical to Teague's 1898 entry) — "**I must go back on Chapter IV. Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand. I leave it as written and correct it here.** — D. RUNDLE, Larrow Green."

## KEY OBJECTS AND DOCUMENTS
- **The station test book** (Keddie: five columns; Nym: four; first leaf 4 May 1896; 1922 summary leaf; 8 May and 10 July 1911 entries with "? — A.K."; half-bound, marbled boards, cloth-mended back; one hand, 29 seasons; impounded March 1925; Ashlin office 1925–59; the Rooms 1959; Nym Item 3 "kept by E. Brant").
- **The Brant farm weigh book** (Nym Item 4 "kept by A. Keddie": small quarto, sheepskin, greasy corners, from 1 Jan 1897; Roy: hand-ruled ledger from 1 Jan 1898, two weight columns, cover gone, harness-leather strip, forty stained leaves, "on the shelf behind my chair"; 1910 leaf, "even" underlined; to Roy 1929; lent to Ivy 1958 and returned; to Hazel 1962; the Rooms 1963; a second volume begun when the first filled).
- **The 1919 printed circular** ("water in the cans"; nine kitchen drawers, one trunk lid; Selby: "It named nobody. It accused nobody… cannot pay for creek water"; Roy: "a paper… warning us against putting water in the milk"; Rundle: "a warning to the Hollow patrons against adding water to their milk").
- **Article VII** (Effie 1892; Rundle 1891; pamphlet gray wrappers, undated; quoted identically by Effie, Nym and Rundle; Rundle: "I print it whole").
- **Gideon Vose's by-laws** (written by him at his kitchen table in a hand the clerks complained of for thirty years — Rundle; "a piece of paper they had written themselves").
- **The 1925 petition** (January 1925; Rundle: set the loss "out to the dollar" at $19,600).
- **The 1925 award** (Effie's minute book; Nym Item 7; $3,800; Rundle: no sum).
- **Keddie's statement** (Nym Item 8) and unsent letter.
- **Teague's station-gain line** (1898 entry; "as usual"; Nym Item 6; **quoted verbatim by Rundle in 1939 from the ledgers "in the bookkeeper's hand"**).
- **The crate** (AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET; deal, stained; six dozen pipettes 1897 — Vaught: five dozen; nine survive, two chipped — Rundle 1959: eleven; loft June 1923 / Ashlin office; the Rooms 1959; "wants a case with a lock"; Rundle: "the station's old condemned crate").
- **The invoice** 14 April 1897 (itemized; $77.93; "8 per cent neck").
- **Ammon & Sons letter** 2 July 1923 (order of 28 June; "declining to say anything of the earlier lot").
- **The 1908 committee report** (Nym Item 11; chairman Nyle Grigg per Roy and Rundle; "The milk is whole and the scales are honest").
- **Strawn's burette** (1921; wooden case; discontinued 1926; used at Ostrey Hollow 12 June and October 1923), **sealed weights**, **lactometer**, **twenty-eight circuit books** (oilcloth; state office 1936; Farland 1947; 1923 photostat Nym Item 9).
- **Ivy's burette sheet** 9 March 1958 (17.6 blown; 16.72 mean; short 0.88; 95%; Nym Item 14). Vaught's method: water, room temperature, five runs, certified burette; his mean 16.27.
- **Bulletin No. 214** (1911, bog hay ≤0.05 in winter, nothing in summer; Nym Item 12) and **Bulletin No. 471** (October 1958; Vaught's §§I–VII; conclusion quoted; Nym Item 15).
- **Jerome Cudd's Alder Corners test book** (own glassware in a rack; 1919 entry quoted; sixteen season averages 1915–1930; founder → daughter 1941 → Vessey County Historical Society 1970; never in Ordell County; Ivy never saw it; Rosalie copied figures — Nym Item 13 "Correspondence file, Vessey County"). **Rosalie's 1958 letter** with the table.
- **Roy Brant's 1955 account** (longhand; county farm bureau local-history collection; postscript 1958).
- **Dell Rundle's jubilee pamphlet "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years"** (printed at Larrow Green 1939; six sections plus "To the Members" and the in-press note; reprinted 1959 with footnote [^1] signed "D.R."; the in-press note signed "D. RUNDLE, Larrow Green").
- **Larrow Green receiving and churn books** (Sill; Nym Item 16; water-damaged).
- **The Association's order book** (Selby: 1923 replacement "with a date and a price").
- **The 1926 annual report** (Selby's address).
- **The Association's old ledgers in the office** (seen by Rundle while the pamphlet was in press, 1939; Teague's books; Nym Item 6 canvas ledgers).
- **The Vose Jerseys** (4.56 in 1910; gone by 1926). **The Brant herd** (38 cows in a good year; 200,000 lb in 1910 at 3.42; 24-cent butterfat).
- **Scales**: state-tested true (Strawn every visit; Selby; 1908 committee "honest"); Brant's own barn scale agreeing with the station's "barring a pound or two… when a can got slopped on the hill" (Roy); Rosalie's platform scale (the boy weighed for a joke).
- **Money**: $3,800 award (Vira $3,500; Rundle unstated); $19,000 unpaid (76,000 lb × 25¢; Vaught §V implies 118,000 lb; **Rundle/petition $19,600**); $77.93 outfit; 24¢ (1910) / ~25¢ butterfat; monthly cash checks from 1891 (Rundle: "what the first check did in their mother's kitchen").
- **Station plant**: intake and cream scales; boiler; wash room; cold room; separator; loft; slope desk by the window; Rundle: boarded-and-battened building, intake room at the road end, loft over it never cleared out, creek behind that has twice come over the sill. **Alder Corners plant**: make room; little room off the intake; platform; hoops; vat; boiler and roof replaced by Rosalie.
- **Redlow Ridge road**: four miles of switchback; lumber road; closed November–April/May; drifts in the cut below the top. **The Hollow road**: seven miles; three hours in mud in the nineties; impassable in March (Rundle); cream wagons "fifty Junes… in the early cool, fifty Januaries… over ice with the horses roughshod."
- **The schoolhouse at Larrow Green** (where the 1889 meeting was held; where Rundle taught, with "the children of both ends in one schoolroom" 1919–25).
