## 1. Retelling 01 — Vira Toland, "a Hessel Bottom patron's daughter," recorded at Ostrey Hollow, 1979

**Framing:** a tape made at her kitchen table by "a young man from the county who asked three questions in an hour and let her have the rest of it"; transcribed as spoken, doublings-back left in. Title of file: "What the Hollow Said."

**Vira herself:**
- Born 1912 "at the top end of Ostrey Hollow, in the house my father's father put up."
- Except two years "down at Ashlin when I was first married," never lived anywhere else ("I would not count those two years anyhow").
- Her father hauled milk to the station "the same as everybody up here did." **Nine families** hauled to that station; "in and out of each other's kitchens the whole year round." She could name every one but "you don't want names, you want the trouble."
- Her father is never named. Her mother appears (at the stove, back turned, when the letter was read). Her brother "has it yet" (the letter).
- She was **seven years old in 1919**.
- Closing: "It rained the day of that letter, I remember the wet on my father's shoulders when he came in, and I have never in sixty years been able to think about the one without the other."

**The station (as she describes it):**
- "at the very top of the Hollow where the road bends and then goes to nothing but the ridge."
- "a low building, whitewashed, cold as a spring house inside even in August"; smelled "of milk and steam and hot iron"; "you could hear the engine going before you came round the last turn."
- By 1958 it was "shut by then and had been for years, they kept fence wire and salt in it."

**Ansel Keddie (per Vira):**
- "Ansel Keddie ran it. He went up there in **ninety-five** [1895], the year before they opened it, to get it ready, and he tested that first summer too, and he was still there when I was a girl big enough to ride on the wagon with my father."
- **His book:** "ruled, and the ruling was his own, done with a straightedge, and every line in it was the same height as every other line, and his figures were like print." He wrote in it "standing up at a slope desk by the window where the light came in." If a man asked what his milk had done, "He turned to the day and he read it off." "He tested **a thousand cans a week** and never a blot in that book." Her father's saying: "if the rest of the world kept its accounts the way Ansel Keddie kept his there would be no lawyers in Ashlin." ("There are still lawyers in Ashlin.")
- **Vira's own verdict:** "He was taking the cream. They caught him at it and they put him out, and that is why the numbers went right the summer after. That is what it was. I am not going to dress it up for you. A man stands in a room by himself with the separator running and the door shut and nobody in the valley can add, and after a while he helps himself, and it is not that he is a wicked man, it is that nobody ever came up that road and looked."
- Her father "never would say it out loud. My father said Ansel Keddie was an honest man who had got into something."
- "He died **in the thirties** and he was still under it when he died." Orra "outlived him a good while and had it the whole time" (the valley "looking at him a certain way in church").

**The 1919 letter (the "circular"):**
- "Nineteen nineteen. That is the year everything went sour up here."
- "A letter came from the Association, **printed, over the manager's signature**, and it said there was **water in the cans** coming down from Ostrey Hollow." Went to **nine families**; her father one of the nine.
- Father read it at the table "with his hat still on, which he never did"; read it twice; put it "in the drawer of the desk in the front room"; it was still there when he died; "I believe my brother has it yet."
- "One woman I knew had hers pinned inside the lid of her trunk." People "keep the paper forty years to prove what was said of them."
- **Signer:** "**Selby Vose**, down at Larrow Green. He was **old Gideon Vose's nephew**, and old Gideon was the one that started the whole Association back in the beginning, and I think the family always thought the valley belonged to them a little."
- **Kinship nobody wrote down:** "**Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins.** Everybody knew that." So the letter "was not only the Association saying it... it was somebody's cousin saying it."

**Keddie/Brant family relations (per Vira):**
- "**Orra was Ansel Keddie's wife. And Orra was Emmet Brant's sister.**"
- "**Emmet Brant** was the biggest man on the Bottom, he had the most cows and the most hay ground and he hauled more milk to that station than anybody, and he was the one that letter cost the most."
- "The man that tested the milk had married the sister of the man that got the worst of it."
- Before 1919 the two families "ate their Sunday dinners together... the two houses in one kitchen and the men out at the fence after." After 1919 "they did not speak. Not one word, not at the store, not at a funeral. **Orra would walk down to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane.**" Both "long in the ground now."

**The tests rising (per Vira):**
- "Then the tests went up. All at once, **one summer**, the figures came up and stayed up, and nobody ever said why. Not the station, not the Association, not anybody." Her father "did not know what he had done different, and he had not done anything different, none of them had." "the thing corrected itself and no man stood up and gave a reason for it."

**The hearing (per Vira):**
- "after that there was the hearing down at Ashlin. That was **the winter and spring after**." "They sat in an office down there, lawyers and the officers of the Association and our men going down in the wagons, and it went on and on."
- Outcome: "they put Ansel Keddie out. An old man, and out, after all those seasons in that cold room, and they paid the patrons **thirty-five hundred dollars** and called it settled. Thirty-five hundred dollars, split up **nine ways** over all those years — my father's share of it did not buy a hay rake."

**The 1958 visitor (per Vira):**
- "a young woman came round here in **nineteen fifty-eight** from the college away east, asking after all this, and she went up and poked about the old station building." "She sat where you are sitting. Nice girl, careful, wrote everything down."
- "She was **Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed**, so she was of the family both sides in a manner of speaking." (Hazel Brant appears in r07's provenance for the weigh book, and r09 identifies her as **Roy Brant's daughter**; per Roy, Ivy and Hazel are **second cousins** — see Conflicts #61. The visitor is evidently Ivy Keddie — see r04, r09 postscript.)
- Vira "told her about the letter in my father's drawer. I don't know what she made of it."

## 2. Retelling 02 — Effie Loomis, secretary of the Association 1901–1940, "Notes toward a history of the Association," 1941

**Framing:** "Written out in numbered sections for the Association's own files, in the year after I laid down the minute book: from the minutes wherever the minutes will bear it, from my own recollection where they will not, and the two kept apart."

**§1 — Organizing.** In **1889** the farmers of the Larrow Valley met at **Larrow Green** and organized the **Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association**. **Gideon Vose** elected first president; held office **until 1911** — "twenty-two years, and no other man has held it so long." Effie "was fourteen years old in 1889" (so born c. 1875); became secretary **1901**. "The **1889–1892 minute book was lost when the office was moved in 1938.**"

**§2 — Creamery.** "The main creamery was built at Larrow Green in **1891**, on **Larrow Creek**, and it is the same building yet." Before it, members sold milk where they could; "no common test and no common price."

**§3 — By-laws, Article VII.** Gideon Vose drafted the by-laws himself; "He was not a lawyer and they read as a farmer wrote them." Quoted entire:

> *Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*

Adopted at the **annual meeting of 1892**, "the year after the creamery opened." Effie's gloss: the last clause — "*The test of the station where he delivers shall govern.* Not the test of the creamery, not the average of the valley, not a test made over again anywhere else. The station's test, and no appeal from it. That clause is the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business and it was written in 1892 by a man who **died in 1917** and could not be asked about it afterward."

**§4 — Officers in her time.**
- Effie Loomis, secretary **1901–1940**, kept the minute book in her own hand.
- **Wilbur Teague**, bookkeeper from **1905**, kept the ledgers.
- **Hobart Sill**, buttermaker at Larrow Green; had "the churn book and the cream receiving book."
- **Selby Vose, "son of the late president,"** manager **1913 to 1938**.
- "not one of us ever went up the valley and stood in the Ostrey Hollow station on a working morning to watch the work being done. I include myself first in that."

**§5 — The station.** "The skimming station stood **seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green**, at the head of the Hollow." It "took in the milk of the farms up there, separated it, sent the cream down to us and gave the skim back to the patron." "The best of that milk came off **Hessel Bottom**, the wet meadow below the hamlet. **Seven farms had rights on Hessel Bottom** and they cut hay and pastured on it, and the largest of the seven was **Emmet Brant's**, who was the station's largest patron in every year of which we have a record."

**§6 — Petition and board.** "In **January 1925** the patrons of Ostrey Hollow petitioned the Association. Their complaint was that their milk had never tested as their neighbors' milk tested and that they had been paid accordingly for a very long time, and they asked to be made whole." "The board of arbitration sat at **Ashlin** under **Alonzo Frick, attorney, of that place, from 17 March to 4 May 1925**. **Three explanations** were argued before it. None of the three was accepted." "they are in the minutes and the minutes are in the safe." Award, recorded by Effie "on the last day":

> *The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account.*

Effie: "*Finds no cause established*, and then an award of thirty-eight hundred dollars: I took it down as it was given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once."

**§7 — Quantities (Teague's ledgers).** "For the **twenty-five years** over which the patrons laid their claim, the ledgers show that the Ostrey Hollow station received **forty million pounds** of milk, which is an average of **one million six hundred thousand pounds** in a year. Over the same period the price paid for butterfat averaged **twenty-five cents a pound**." "they are Mr. Teague's, out of the annual sheets, and I have added the columns twice."

**§8 — What the record does not contain.**
1. The 1938 office move lost the 1889–1892 minute book: "It was not thrown out; it was carried out with the rest and it did not arrive." "anybody who tells you what was resolved at the first four annual meetings is telling you a story he had from his father."
2. **The crate.** "The crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all. I took every word down and it is not there. I mean the **crate of measures that the state's inspector condemned up at the station in June of 1923**, which was set aside and new ones ordered in its place. Not a word of it was spoken in any of the **three sessions**, by the patrons, by the officers, or by the board, and the award's phrase about 'the books and appliances of the station' is a form of words the board used and not a list of anything."

## 3. Retelling 03 — Ansel Keddie, tester at Ostrey Hollow: statement to the board (17 March 1925) and an unsent letter (spring 1925)

**Framing:** "Two papers. The first is what he said, standing, before the board of arbitration at Ashlin on the seventeenth of March, 1925 — **nine sentences and no more**, taken down as he gave them. The second is a letter written at Ostrey Hollow later that spring; it was never sent, and was found among his papers."

**I. Statement to the board, Ashlin, 17 March 1925 (quoted in full):**

> I have kept the test book of this station in my own hand since the station opened, and there is not a figure in it that I did not put there. I have set down the date, the patron, the pounds, the test and the fat, and where I have made an error I have drawn a line through it and put my letters against the line, and I have never written a figure over a figure. I have made the tests in the same order and at the same hour of every working day of every season. I have heard what the patrons say of me and I have heard what the officers say of me. Neither of them has come up that road on a working morning and stood at my elbow and watched the work done. I am asked to account for figures, and the figures are what I read and what I wrote and nothing besides. I am an old man and I have no other trade. I have tested as I was taught and as the state man saw me do it, **thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons**, and I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are. I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself.

**II. Unsent letter, Ostrey Hollow, spring 1925 — contents:**
- Why written: "I could not say it in that room, where a man is allowed to answer and not allowed to explain," and "somebody ought to know how the work was done before everybody who did it is gone."
- **Daily routine:** "The day began at **half past five**. The wagons came up the Hollow in the dark most of the year, and I had the intake room open and the lamps lit and the scale swinging free before the first of them was at the door. Every can went on the scale and the weight was called and written before the milk went anywhere else. That scale was tested by the state's man on his rounds and it was true, and I have never in twenty-nine seasons had a patron dispute a weight with me and be right."
- **Sampling:** a sample from each patron's milk as weighed "went into his own jar, numbered to him and to nobody else, and the jar was made up over **ten days** before it was tested." Testing morning: jar "set in warm water until it was blood-warm right through, and then it was poured from vessel to vessel, and poured again, until the cream that had risen was carried down through the whole of it. A sample that is not warmed and mixed is worth nothing and a boy could tell you so."
- **The charge and test:** "The milk was drawn up in the measure to the mark on the stem, and the tip wiped, and the charge blown out into the bottle that carried the patron's number. Then the acid, poured down the side, and the bottle turned until the milk was dark. **Four bottles in the hand tester**, weighted against each other, the cover down, and the crank turned at the speed I was taught to turn it and no faster, and no man ever stood beside me and made me hurry it. Then the fat is up in the neck of the bottle in a clear column, and it is read off in per cent against the rulings, with a **pair of dividers**, and the figure is written down." "It is not a hard trade. It is only a trade that must be done the same way every time, which is a harder thing than it sounds."
- **The glass:** "The glass I began on in **ninety-six** was **borrowed off the Ashlin creamery**, and we used it until our own outfit came up the valley in a crate **the spring following**." (Contrast the first leaf below, which says borrowed of Larrow Green.)
- **The book:** "kept in **five columns**: the date, the patron, the pounds, the test, the fat." Errors crossed and initialed; "never wrote over a figure, because a figure written over another figure is a figure no man can swear to afterward." "**Twenty-nine seasons, from 1896 through 1924. I did not test a pound in the season of 1925.**"
- **First leaf of the book (quoted):**

> *Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie.*

- **Summary leaf made up in 1922** "because I had been asked for them so often" (quoted):

> *Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80.*

- **The nine weeks of 1911:** "the strangest thing in my book." "Our measure broke on the **eighth of May** and I made shift with one borrowed, and from that morning the tests came up and stood at 3.80, and on the **tenth of July** our own was in use again and the tests were down again the same day." The two entries (quoted):

> *May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure.*

> *July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.*

"That question mark is mine and I have let it stand for fourteen years."
- **Shape of the gap:** "They did not come and go with the season. The gap was there in July as it was there in February, in the flush of the grass and in the dead of the winter on dry hay, and it was the same in a wet year as in a dry one. **In 1924 the average of this station was 3.80**, and I wrote that figure with the same pen and the same measure of care as all the others."
- **History of complaints:** "The complaints began in **1902**, when it was first said down at Larrow Green that Ostrey Hollow milk did not test with the rest. In **1908** a committee came up and sampled the herds through the winter and reported that the **wet hay of the Bottom thinned the cream**, and that the remedy lay with the land. In **1919** a circular went out from the Association to **nine families** up here saying there was water in their cans. I carry no charge against any man who put his name to that paper." "**nine kitchens in this Hollow have had that letter in a drawer ever since.**"
- **Family:** "My wife **Orra is Emmet Brant's sister**. Emmet brought more milk to my door than any other man on the Bottom, and every pound of it I weighed myself and every test of it I made myself and wrote in the book. **He and I have not spoken since the circular.** That is the plainest fact in this letter and it is the one I find I have the least to say about."
- **Dismissal:** "I was put out on the **thirtieth of April**. They found no cause established and they put me out on the last day of the month, and both of those things are true at once, and I have given up trying to hold them in my head together. I have tested as I was taught. I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are."

## 4. Retelling 04 — Ivy Keddie, "What the Crate Held," a talk to the Ordell County Historical Society, Ashlin, 1959

**Framing:** "Read from typescript at the Society's meeting, with the crate itself on a table at the front and the borrowed books laid open beside it; the papers quoted here were read aloud from the originals."

**Ivy's family:** "**Ansel Keddie**, who kept the test book at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station, was my **grandfather**. My father, **Duncan Keddie**, who **taught school at Ashlin**, was his son." "nothing I am going to show you tonight depends in the smallest degree on my opinion of my grandfather. It depends on **nine pieces of glass and a burette**."

**Five sets of records** ("no two of them had ever been in one room before last year"):
1. **The station test book** — "in the Association's office since 1925 and came to these Rooms this year."
2. **The Brant weigh book**, "kept at Hessel Bottom from **1897** onward," lent by **Roy Brant, Emmet Brant's son**, "who has kept it up himself since his father died."
3. **The Alder Corners Cheese Company's test figures**, "from over **Redlow Ridge in Vessey County**," copied out by **Rosalie Cudd**, "who runs that factory and is **Jerome Cudd's niece**; the book itself has never left Vessey County and I have never seen it."
4. **Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks**, "kept by the **state's dairy inspector for this county from 1908**," in "my own college's library at **Farland** since **1947** — eleven years in which nobody put a question to them."
5. **The Association's own annual ledgers**, present at the talk.

**The crate:**
- "In **February of last year** [1958] I went up the Hollow to the station building, which has been used for storage since it closed. There is a **loft over what was the intake room, reached by a ladder**, and under a **tarpaulin** at the back of it there was a wooden crate stenciled on the end **AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET**. It had been carried up there in **June of 1923** and it was there still."
- "Inside, packed in straw, were the station's condemned milk-test pipettes. **Six dozen** had been bought new for the station in **1897**, on an invoice that came to **seventy-seven dollars and ninety-three cents** [$77.93] for the whole outfit — pipettes, bottles, acid measure, hand tester and freight together. **Nine** of them were unbroken in the crate."

**The measurement (9 March 1958, at Farland, against a calibrated burette) — her sheet, quoted:**

> *Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for.*

**The arithmetic ("arithmetic and not conjuring"):**
- A butterfat test draws a measured charge into a bottle whose neck is ruled to read percent for that charge. Short charge → short reading in proportion. "16.72 divided by 17.6 is ninety-five hundredths, exactly. Every test made at that station with that glass read **ninety-five percent of the truth**."
- Grandfather's 1922 summary leaf quoted (identical text to r03): *Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80.*
- "3.61 divided by 0.95 is 3.80. The recorded average was 3.61; the true average was 3.80; the gap is **nineteen hundredths** of a point." "Nineteen hundredths is **five percent of 3.80**. The station did not lose a set number of points. It lost **a twentieth of whatever was there**."
- "In the **twenty-five full delivery years from 1898 to 1922** the station took in **forty million pounds** of milk, an average of **one million six hundred thousand pounds** a year. At the recorded 3.61 percent, the patrons were credited with **1,444,000 pounds** of fat. At the true 3.80 percent, they had delivered **1,520,000 pounds**. The difference is **76,000 pounds** of butterfat. Butterfat over those years averaged **twenty-five cents a pound**, so the unpaid fat was worth **nineteen thousand dollars**. The board of arbitration in 1925 awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons **thirty-eight hundred dollars**. That is **a fifth** of it."
- **Two percentages, both right:** "Seventy-six thousand pounds is **five percent of the fat actually delivered** — that is, of the 1,520,000. It is **five and a quarter percent of the fat credited**, of the 1,444,000. The Association's ledger line uses the second, because a bookkeeper naturally reckons a surplus against the figure he has already entered. Neither figure is an error. But five percent is the one that is also the glass."
- **Proportional error hit best milk hardest:** "A herd whose milk was recorded at **4.56** was short **0.24** of a point. A herd recorded at **3.42** was short **0.18**." "no man had any way of knowing that his good cows cost him more than his poor ones." (Note: 3.42 is exactly the test the Brant weigh book records for 1910 — see §5c Item 4 and r09. And 4.56 is exactly the figure Selby Vose gives for his own father's Jersey herd in 1910 — see §5d and Conflicts #51.)

**The 1911 "experiment":** "For **nine weeks in 1911** there was an experiment at that station, and nobody knew it was one. The station's own measure broke on the **eighth of May** and a substitute was used until the **tenth of July**. **The measure they borrowed in 1911 came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon.** Same patrons, same herds, same pasture, same season, same acid, same bottles, same man at the crank — one object changed — and the average went to 3.80 and came back to 3.61 the day the station's own measure returned. No explanation of this valley's **twenty-six-year anomaly** survives those nine weeks except the one I have given you."

**The wet-hay explanation refuted:** "It was said the wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream. The State Agricultural College tested that in **1911** and printed the answer in **Bulletin No. 214**":

> *Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture.*

"Five hundredths in winter, nothing in summer — against a gap of nineteen hundredths standing flat in every month of every year, July and February alike. It is **four times too small and it is the wrong shape**." Also: "over the ridge, the **Alder Corners** factory tested the milk of **four Hessel Bottom men** in their summer flush; where the two books overlap, **fourteen seasons** of them, that same milk off that same wet ground ran about **nineteen hundredths higher at Alder Corners** than at home."

**Bulletin No. 471** ("issued last October" = October 1958), last paragraph, "Professor Vaught's wording and mine together":

> *No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain.*

**Closing:** "The glass was condemned in **1923** and the cause was found in **1958**: **thirty-five years**. My grandfather was dismissed in **1925**: **thirty-three years**. He **died in 1934** and had been dead **twenty-four years** when the sheet was read that clears him. I have no remedy to propose. I have only the crate, and it is on the table, and you may look into it on your way out."

## 5. Retelling 05 — Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper (retired), Chapter Nine of "The Books of the Association," 1948

**Framing:** "Chapter Nine of an unpublished memoir, written out at **Ashlin in the winter of 1948, three years after I gave up the desk**. I have set the figures down as they stood in the books."

**Teague:** "I kept the books of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association from **1905 to 1945**, forty years by the calendar." "in the whole of it I never carried a column that would not prove." "a bookkeeper who wants admiring has generally got something behind him that he would rather you did not turn over."

**The office at Ashlin:** "one room **over a harness shop**, cold at the north end and never cold enough at the south, and the ledgers lived on a shelf that a taller man had built for himself before me. I worked at a **sloped desk with a brass rail** across the foot of it." "the sound the station sheets made coming out of their envelope in **March**, which was a stiffer paper than the creamery used."

**The Association "three things on paper":** "a creamery at Larrow Green, an office at Ashlin, and a skimming station **seven miles up the valley at Ostrey Hollow**. **Mr. Selby Vose was manager** over the whole of it, **Miss Effie Loomis was secretary** and kept the minutes, and I sat at the ledger. The station was the smallest of the three and it made the most noise."

**How the station worked (Teague's version):** patrons hauled milk up to the station instead of seven miles down to Larrow Green; weighed in; sample from each man's can tested for butterfat; man paid on pounds and test together; station separated the milk, sent cream down to Larrow Green, gave skim back "for his hogs and his calves."

**The station-gain column:** "On one side of the station account I entered the butterfat credited to the patrons on the station's own test — that is, what the station's books said it had bought. On the other side I entered the butterfat that actually came down the valley in the station's cream and was weighed and tested into our vats at Larrow Green — that is, what we had in hand. The two were never the same. **The second was always the larger.** The difference between them was the station's account to the good, and I carried it to the general account of the Association."
- "The station-gain line was **my own device; I began it when I came in 1905**. I have heard it said in the valley that the line was some old dodge of the founders. It was not." "a difference that will not be entered somewhere will come back and bite you at audit."
- **The entry, quoted complete:**

> *Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account.*

- "**That is the entry for 1898**, and there is one like it for every year after, in the same terms and in the same hand for as long as the hand was mine." "The gain ran at **five and a quarter percent of the fat that was actually in the cream**. It ran there year after year, so evenly that the words 'as usual' got into the entry and stayed there... **A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture.**"

**Two large numbers (Teague):** "Over the years the account ran, that station took in **forty-four million pounds** of milk. And the average test of all the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, taken over the whole period and not over some picked year that suits an argument, was **3.16**. Down at Larrow Green we did better than that, and there was not a season in twenty-five years when the Ostrey Hollow men were not sore about it."

**The 1911 break (Teague):** "The station's own measure broke in the **spring of 1911**, and they made shift with a borrowed one for **six weeks** before their own came up from town. It went into my accounts as a small charge for replacement and nothing else." People later "came asking me about that year in particular, as though a bookkeeper kept the weather."

**What Teague told the board in 1925 (and repeats):** "**Cream is portable**, and that station was in **one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons**." "One man weighed the milk, one man drew the sample, one man made the test, one man wrote the figure that the pay slip was made from, and one man ran the separator. There was **no second man in that building from the spring to the fall**." "I do not say what any particular person did on any particular day, because I was not there and I do not know." "a business which is arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken from it... **The remedy is two men or a lock, and the Association had neither.**"

**The hearing (Teague):** "The patrons petitioned in the winter, when a farmer has time to be angry, and the board sat at Ashlin under **Mr. Alonzo Frick**, who was as fair a chairman as ever a hearing had, and I was called and gave my evidence." "The board found nothing established against anybody. It then awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons **$3,800**, without admission, and charged it to the station account, and I entered that too, and it balanced."

**Two things more:**
1. "**Mr. Vose knew what that account was doing. He signed the sheet every year.** I laid the annual sheet before him each spring and he put his name at the foot of it... What he made of it he never told me."
2. "I did not sit silent for forty years... **I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone.** I let it alone. I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience."

"The figures are in the ledgers at Ashlin, in ink, dated."

## 5b. Retelling 06 — Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector (retired), "Extracts from twenty-eight circuit books," with a covering note, 1936

**Framing / epigraph:** "*Copied out this spring at the request of the state dairy office, from the pocket books I carried on the Ordell circuit. I have copied what is in them and put in nothing that is not.*"

**Covering note:**
- "I took the Ordell circuit in **nineteen hundred and seven** and I have laid it down **this year** [1936]." The circuit: "**fifty-odd plants in four counties** — creameries, cheese factories, skimming stations, one or two little places that made butter three days a week and called themselves a creamery." The state expected each plant "seen at least once in the year and the doubtful ones oftener." Travel by train where there was one, livery where not, "and in the spring by neither, the roads up the side valleys being what they are in April."
- "A man on this circuit learns the plants by their smells before he learns them by their names: the sour-sweet of a receiving platform in July, the lime and steam of a wash room, the flat cold nothing of a well-run cold room, which is the smell I liked best because it meant I would have no letter to write."
- **His four checks, always in the same order:** (1) the scales, against the sealed weights he carried — both the intake scale and the cream scale, loaded light and loaded heavy; (2) the lactometer on the day's milk, patron by patron where there was any question, for added water; (3) the boiler, the water supply and the cold room — pressure, gauge, the cased well if there was one, whether the room would hold overnight; (4) "and the one most men grudged me, I stood at the tester's elbow and watched him make a test through from the sample to the reading, and I did not let him hurry it."
- Creed: "I put down what I found. I did not put down what I thought about it, and I have not begun now. The state pays a field man to weigh and to look, and every time in my life I have seen a field man paid for an opinion instead, the opinion has outlived the weighing and done harm after the man was dead."

**The extracts (year by year):**
- **1907.** First round of the new circuit. "Ostrey Hollow station, up seven miles from Larrow Green, opened before my time. Scales true against my weights. Cold room fair. Their tester keeps his book in his own hand, and his hand is better than most. Watched him through two tests. **He works slow and clean and reads his own figure without being coaxed to it.**"
- **1909.** Ostrey Hollow, fall. Scales true. Boiler in order, safety tried. "The station takes from the farms of **the wet bottom below it and from three places on the hill**. Milk sweet, cans clean, one man cautioned about a rusted seam."
- **1911.** Ostrey Hollow, **June**, on his way over to the other side of the county. Scales true. Watched a test through. "**Nothing here to note.**" (This visit fell inside the 8 May–10 July borrowed-measure window — see Conflicts #26; and see Roy Brant's claim that the state man LEFT the borrowed measure — Conflicts #58.)
- **1912.** Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. "Cream shipped down the valley the same day it is separated, which is more than some do." Creek out over the flat road; went round by the ridge and lost the afternoon.
- **1913.** Larrow Green creamery, two days, churn room and receiving books. Scales true. "**New manager at the Association this year and he came out with me to the platform, which the last one never did.**" (Matches Selby Vose's managership beginning 1913.)
- **1915.** Ostrey Hollow and the plants down the county line. Scales true. Wash room floor holding water at the drain; ordered it graded. "A field man who lets a floor go will be shown a floor at every plant on his circuit inside of two years."
- **1916.** Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. "**The tester complains that his patrons blame him for their figures. I told him that is the trade and he said he knew it.**"
- **1917.** Cheese factory in the next county, then Larrow Green. Scales true at both. "A bad year for help; the plants are short-handed and I found two testers doing the work of three men, which is where mistakes live."
- **1919.** Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. "**The manager of the Association has sent a circular round to the patrons of this station about water in the cans. I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so.**"
- **1920.** Ostrey Hollow, twice — spring and fall. Scales true both times. No added water. Cold room short of ice in the fall; "I said so and it was seen to before I was out of the county."
- **1921.** "**The state has begun issuing burettes to the field men this year, graduated, in a wooden case, for checking the delivery of measures and pipettes at the plants. Mine came in the spring with a printed sheet of directions. It is a slow business to run a plant's glass against it and most plants have never had it done.**"
- **1922.** Ostrey Hollow. Scales true, no added water, boiler in order. "**I did not run their glass against the burette this year, the day being short and the road home being what it was, and I have put that down because it is what happened.**"
- **1923, June 12.** "I had carried the burette two seasons before this check." The entry (quoted):

> *Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk pipettes against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them.*

- **1923, October.** "New glass in use since **July**. Ran the new pipettes against the burette. They deliver their mark. Scales true."
- **1924.** "Scales true, no added water, tests improved. **Their book gives the year at 3.80.**"
- **1925.** Ostrey Hollow, fall. Scales true, no added water, tests improved. "**New man at the tester's bench this year. He is quick, and quick is not the thing I look for.**"
- **1926.** "Word from the state office that **the burette program is discontinued from this year**. The instruments are to be kept by the men who have them but no more are to be issued and no returns are wanted. Mine is in its case in my grip and has been used twice since."
- **1928.** Ostrey Hollow. Scales true, no added water. "The station is quieter than it was. **The loft over the intake room is full of odds and ends and I told them a loft over a milk room is a place for nothing but air, and was told in return that it had been full since before the war.**"
- **1931.** Larrow Green and Ostrey Hollow in the same week. Both in order. "Bad ice year; every plant in the four counties short and complaining, and two of them buying from the railroad."
- **1934.** Ostrey Hollow. Scales true, no added water. "A separator bearing run hot and shut down two days, since repaired. **The tester's book is kept up, though not in the hand it used to be kept in.**"
- **1936, March.** Last round. Ostrey Hollow, Larrow Green, and the two factories over the county line. Scales true at all four. "Nothing to note anywhere and nothing left to say about it."

**Closing note:** "The books themselves — **twenty-eight of them, one to the year, oilcloth covers, pocket size** — go to the **state dairy office** with this copy, as the rules require of a man laying down a circuit. There is nothing in them but what a field man puts in a field book: what I weighed, what I tested, what I told the plant to mend, and whether it was mended when I came back. They are dull reading and they were meant to be. I have been asked once or twice, by men who ought to know better, to say in a general way how a certain plant stood in my estimation over the years, and I have always answered that I do not keep an estimation, I keep a book, and the book is open to anybody the state sends for it. **I never had a theory about a plant and I do not recommend having one. A theory is a thing a man carries out to the platform with him and it gets in the way of the weights.**"

## 5d. Retelling 08 — Selby Vose, manager, annual address to the Association, 1926 (now COMPLETE — lines 1–31)

**Framing:** "The manager's address to the members at the annual meeting, printed in the annual report for the year."

**Opening and ordinary business (lines 1–14, read in segment 5):**
- Opens "Members of the Association: It is **thirteen years this spring, since 1913**, that you have had the management of this Association in my hands, and I have stood up before you thirteen times to give an account of it. I have never had a year I liked less to report on than the one behind us, and I have never had one I was gladder to have behind us." Pledges plain speech: "a manager who talks around a difficulty leaves the difficulty exactly where he found it, with interest."
- **Ordinary business:** "The season opened wet and stayed wet into June, and the hauling was heavy on the horses and heavier on the men. The creamery at Larrow Green ran through the flush without a day lost, which is more than the creameries below us can say. Our butter has held its place with the trade that buys it, our churn room is clean, our ice went into the house early and lasted, and I have no complaint of any man on the payroll. The skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, **seven miles up the valley**, ran its season out and its cream came down to us daily and sweet. That is the whole of the ordinary business and I would to heaven it were the whole of the address."
- **"Now to the other matter."** Recaps: in **January 1925** the Ostrey Hollow patrons laid a petition; a board of arbitration was constituted, sat at Ashlin under **Mr. Alonzo Frick, "an able and impartial man,"** heard everybody who wished to be heard, "at length, and at some cost in time to men who could ill spare it in the spring." Then: "**The board found no cause established. It found no cause established against the station, against the office, or against any officer or servant of this Association, and I ask you to sit with that sentence a moment, because it is the only sentence in this business that was arrived at by men under oath with the books in front of them.**"

**The remainder (lines 15–31, read this segment):**
- **On the award:** "Having found no cause established, that board nevertheless awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons thirty-eight hundred dollars, without admission, and charged it to the station account. **I supported the payment then and I support it now.** It was, I say to you frankly, **a generous settlement of an unproved claim**. This Association paid thirty-eight hundred dollars rather than let a body of its own members go on believing they had been used badly by their neighbors. There is not a business house in Ordell County that would have done it. We did it because we are not a business house; we are the farmers of this valley in company together, and a company that will not spend money to keep peace among its own members has not understood what it is for."
- **His declared cause:** "But I will not have peace bought a second time, and so I will say what I believe to be the cause of the whole affair... **Water was going into cans at the head of that valley and it stopped when the Association began to look. The rise of 1924 is the measure of it.** For a generation the milk brought to that station tested below the milk brought to Larrow Green, and every year we were told it was the wet hay, or the tester, or the office, or the weather. Then the Association's attention came to rest on the station, and there was a petition, and a hearing, and men were required to answer questions in front of their neighbors. And **in the very next season the tests at that station rose by twenty-one hundredths and the average for 1924 stood at 3.90**, which is as good a figure as any station in this county will show you. Nothing was done to those cows. Nothing was done to that bottom land. Nobody changed their feed and nobody changed their pasture. The tests came up when the looking began, and any man who wants a different explanation of that is welcome to stand where I am standing and give us one."
- **Dismissing the glass:** "I am told, and I have been told it kindly and by men who mean well, that the state man's visit of that period explains the whole thing. It does not. He came through the county as he came through it every year or two, he tried our scales against his weights and found them true, and **he condemned the station's testing apparatus on the twelfth of July, 1923**, and told us to order new. **We ordered new inside the month and had it in use before the flush was over.** Apparatus wears. **A station in daily use since 1896 will wear its fittings out twice over in that time**, and it is the state man's business to say so before they fail him, and ours to replace them when he does. **I decline to build a mystery on a piece of housekeeping that appears in our own order book with a date and a price against it.**"
- **On naming no one:** "I am aware that this is not a comfortable thing to say to a room with Ostrey Hollow men in it, and I say it **without naming a name, because I do not know a name and I would not use it if I did**. A little water in a can is not a felony in a man's own mind. It is a way of making a hard year come out even, and the man who does it tells himself he will stop when the price improves. I have no wish to see anybody prosecuted, and nobody has been or will be. I wish only to have it understood, once, in the hearing of the whole membership, why that station's figures were what they were and why they are what they are now."
- **Defending the 1919 circular:** "It follows that **I defend the circular I sent to the patrons of that station in 1919**, which has been thrown at me a good deal **in the last two years**. I wrote it because it was my duty to write it. **It warned the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, in temperate language, that added water would be looked for and would be dealt with. It named nobody. It accused nobody.** It said that this Association pays for butterfat and cannot pay for creek water, and I would write every word of it again tomorrow. A manager who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing to the men who make them is not being kind to them; he is being idle at their expense."
- **His father and the Jerseys:** "**My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law under which every patron in this Association is paid on the test of the station where he delivers, kept a herd of Jerseys at the home place**, as some of you remember, and I kept them after him. **In 1910 that herd tested 4.56.** I do not bring that forward to boast of the cows; they are gone now and the ones I have are no better than yours. I bring it forward because it settles a thing that gets said about this county in the trade below us — that the Larrow Valley is poor milk country, that our land is sour and our cattle are indifferent and our figures are what a man must expect off ground like ours. That is not true. There is no better milk in the state than the milk this valley can produce when it comes to the platform as it left the cow, and the proof of it stood in my own barn."
- **Closing:** "That is what I want from every man in this room in the coming season. **Bring it as it left the cow.** Wash the cans, cool the milk, keep the dog out of the milk house, and bring it as it left the cow. Do that, and the Ostrey Hollow tests will stay where they went in 1924, and this business will be dead and buried where it belongs, and your manager can stand up here next spring and talk to you about butter prices, which is what he is paid for. **I thank you for thirteen years of your patience and I ask for one more.**"

## 5e. Retelling 09 — Roy Brant, farmer at Hessel Bottom, an account for the county farm bureau's local-history collection, 1955 (NEW this segment)

**Framing:** "*Set down in longhand at Hessel Bottom in the winter of 1955 by Roy Brant, sixty-seven years old and still milking, with a postscript he added three years afterward.*" (So Roy was born c. 1887/88.) "The bureau asked me for something on the old days at the head of the valley. I would rather write about my father's book, because the old days are gone and the book is on the shelf behind my chair, and a man does better to talk about what he can put his hand on."

**Emmet Brant:** "My father was Emmet Brant. **He was born in 1854 and he died in 1929.** From the **first of January, 1898**, until the week before he died, he wrote down every can of milk that went out of this barn, and I have kept it up since, **in the same book until it was filled and then in another**, in the same way, because he showed me how and because I never found a reason to stop."

**The Bottom (per Roy):** "Hessel Bottom is **a hundred and sixty acres** of wet meadow lying under the hill at Ostrey Hollow. **Six farms had rights on it** and cut hay off it and pastured it. **Ours was the largest of the six — sixty-odd acres of the meadow, and thirty-eight cows in a good year**, which for this valley was a herd. The water stands on the low end into May, and there is a soft place by the alders that has taken a horse to the hock in my time and my father's. The hay off it is coarse, and it cuts late, and a cow will eat it and do well enough, whatever has been said about that hay by men who never fed it. I have cut that meadow with a scythe and with a mower behind two horses, and I will say for it that it never failed to grow something."

**The book (per Roy):** "It is a ledger, a plain one, ruled by hand. **Two columns for each day of delivery.** In the first column my father entered the **weight of every can as he took it off his own scale in his own barn** before the wagon went down the road. In the second he entered **the figure the station gave him back for that same milk**. That is all it is. There is no comment in it anywhere but **three or four lines in thirty years**, and no opinion at all. **The cover is gone off the front, the back is held by a strip of harness leather my father tacked on himself, and the first forty leaves are stained brown at one corner where it lay in a leaky window a winter he was sick.**"

**The two columns agreed:** "Day after day, month after month, for the whole run of years my father kept it and for the years I have kept it, **his weight and the station's weight came out the same, barring a pound or two here and there**, and those were days when a can got slopped on the hill. **Two scales, his and the station's, kept by two men who by the end were not speaking to one another, and the scales never fell out at all.** Whatever was wrong at that station — and something was wrong at that station for a very long time — **it was not the weighing**."

**The 1910 leaf (quoted, "the one I am always asked for, in his hand"):**

> *Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42.*

"Two hundred thousand pounds even. He was pleased about the evenness of it; **he wrote 'even' and underlined it**, which for him was carrying on. **The price of butterfat that year was twenty-four cents.** Over the whole stretch of years the complaint covers, one year with another, it came to about **twenty-five cents**." (Text identical to r07 Item 4's quotation.)

**How Emmet kept it:** "He wrote at the kitchen table after the second milking, in the lamp, **with his hat still on**, and my mother would not have the table cleared till he was done. It took him no time at all, and it is the only reason there is anything left to argue about now."

**The trouble (per Roy):** "Our milk at the Hollow ran **seventeen hundredths of a point under** what the same class of milk was making down at Larrow Green. Seventeen hundredths is nothing to look at on one pay slip. **Set the slips in a row for a lifetime of farming and it is a farm.**" "The first men to say so out loud said it in **1902**, and they were laughed at, and **one of them was my father**. What I would have anybody notice is this: **it was seventeen hundredths in July and it was seventeen hundredths in February.** It did not come and go with the season. It did not care whether the cows were on the meadow or on hay in the barn. Nobody would take account of that, then or since, though it was in front of them."

**The 1908 committee (per Roy):** "In 1908 the Association appointed a committee, with **Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green at the head of it**, and the committee came up and sampled the Bottom herds through the winter and reported that the wet hay of this bottom thins the cream, and that the remedy lay with the land and not with the station. **My father read that report twice and put it in the drawer and said that the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not.**"

**The 1911 nine weeks (per Roy):** "There were **nine weeks in the summer of 1911** when the station's own measure was out of use, it having been broken. **The state man left his own measure that June and that is what they used.** Through those nine weeks our tests came up, all of us, every farm on the Bottom; and then the station's own came back into use and the figures went down again to where they had been. My father noticed the rise and he noticed the fall and **he entered both**, and he could make nothing of it, and I could make nothing of it either, and there it sat for near on fifty years."

**The 1919 circular (per Roy):** "In 1919 the manager sent a paper round to the Hollow patrons **warning us against putting water in the milk**. **Nine families** at the head of the valley got that paper and nine families read it for what it was. **My father was one who never forgave it.** He and Ansel Keddie were **brothers-in-law**, and from 1919 they did not speak — **not from any quarrel over the water, but because my father held that the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not**."

**The Alder Corners hauling (per Roy):** "From **1915**, **four of the Bottom men** hauled their milk over Redlow Ridge to the cheese factory in the summer flush and back to the Hollow the rest of the year. **The factory over there had opened in 1914.** They went on that way for **sixteen seasons**. It was for the price and the flush market and a shorter haul while the road was open, and it was talked about here as though they had gone over to the enemy, which shows you what a valley is. **I did not go over.** We had the biggest herd on the meadow, and the biggest herd is the one that cannot afford to be talked about, and I have thought since that this was a poor reason and cost us money."

**The family (per Roy):** "**Orra Keddie was my father's sister. They were full brother and sister, same mother and same father, whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it.** So Ansel Keddie, who kept the station's test book, was married into the family that had more to lose by that station than any other family on the meadow, and I have never known what to do with that fact except set it down." "**Duncan Keddie, who taught school at Ashlin, was Ansel and Orra's boy, and he and I are first cousins. My daughter Hazel and his daughter Ivy are second cousins**, and I mention it because both of them have had to have it explained to them by me more than once."

**The book's future:** "I am sixty-seven and **the book has outlived two of the three men who are in it** [Emmet d. 1929, Ansel d. 1934; Roy the third]. **It goes to Hazel when I am done with it.** That is all the arrangement I have made and all I intend to make."

**Postscript, 1958:** "**Duncan's girl Ivy came out to the place this year and asked whether she might borrow the book for a piece of work she is doing at the college at Farland. I let her have it. She is careful with paper. It comes back to me when she is through, and after me it is Hazel's.**" (Matches Ivy's account of borrowing from Roy in 1958, returned, and r07's provenance chain.)

## 7. Places

- **Ostrey Hollow** — hamlet and hollow; station at the very top/head where the road bends toward the ridge; seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green (confirmed by Strawn 1907 and Vose r08).
- **Hessel Bottom** — wet meadow below the hamlet; **a hundred and sixty acres** (r09); **seven farms** had rights on it (Effie) vs **six farms** (Roy, r09 — Brant's the largest, sixty-odd acres); water stands on the low end into May; a soft place by the alders "has taken a horse to the hock"; Brant weigh book kept there from 1 Jan 1897 (r07) / 1898 (Roy) "by one household"; Strawn 1909: station takes from "the farms of the wet bottom below it and from three places on the hill."
- **Larrow Green** — main creamery (built 1891 on Larrow Creek); where the Association organized (1889); Selby Vose "down at Larrow Green" (Vira); the Vose "home place" with Gideon's Jersey herd is evidently there (r08); Nyle Grigg "of Larrow Green" (r09); borrowed 1896–97 glass "of Larrow Green" (first leaf; r07 office file confirms) and the 1911 borrowed measure "came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon" (Ivy — but see Roy's version, Conflicts #58); Strawn inspected it 1913 (two days), 1917, 1931, 1936; its receiving/churn books are r07 Item 16.
- **Larrow Valley / Larrow Creek.**
- **Ashlin** — the Association's office (one room over a harness shop; Teague); the board of arbitration sat there 1925; "lawyers in Ashlin"; Duncan Keddie taught school there; Ordell County Historical Society rooms / "Ordell County Historical Rooms" there (1959 talk; 1972 catalogue); Vira lived there two years; Keddie's letter says "the Ashlin creamery"; r07 Item 5 says the crate was found "in the Association office at Ashlin" (conflict — see #34).
- **Farland** — Ivy's college = the **State Agricultural College** (r07); its library holds Strawn's notebooks from 1947; pipettes measured there; Roy: "the college at Farland" (1958 postscript).
- **Redlow Ridge / Vessey County / Alder Corners** — Alder Corners Cheese Company over the ridge; **opened 1914** (r09); its test book now (1970→) at the Vessey County Historical Society (r07).
- **Ordell County** — the historical society/Rooms; Strawn's "Ordell circuit" (fifty-odd plants in four counties); Vose (r08): "There is not a business house in Ordell County that would have done it."
- **Tarnet** — home of Ammon & Sons Dairy Supply.
- "the college away east" (Vira) = Farland presumably.

## 11. Conflicts, discrepancies, and oddities (between and within sources)

1. **Amount of the award:** Vira says **$3,500**; Effie (recording the award verbatim), Teague, Ivy, r07 Item 7, and Vose (r08) all say **$3,800**.
2. **Selby Vose's relation to Gideon:** Effie — "son of the late president"; Vira — "old Gideon Vose's nephew." **RESOLVED by r08:** Vose himself says "My father, who was your first president." Vira is wrong.
3. **Total milk:** Effie and Ivy — 40,000,000 lb over 25 years (1898–1922; r07 Item 6 agrees); Teague — 44,000,000 lb "over the years the account ran" (his period may be longer, e.g. 1898–1924, but he does not say).
4. **Average test:** Keddie's leaf and Ivy — 3.61 recorded (3.80 true); Teague — "3.16" (looks like a transposition of 3.61, but stands as written).
5. **Length of the 1911 borrowed-measure interval:** Keddie and Ivy — nine weeks (8 May–10 July); Teague — "six weeks." Teague also says "their own came up from town" and it went in as a replacement charge, whereas Keddie's entry says "Our own measure in use again" and Ivy says "the station's own measure returned." Roy (r09) says nine weeks.
6. **Where the 1896 glass was borrowed from:** Keddie's first leaf — "borrowed of Larrow Green"; Keddie's letter prose — "borrowed off the Ashlin creamery." (Per Effie/Teague the creamery is at Larrow Green and Ashlin is the office; no "Ashlin creamery" is otherwise mentioned. r07's office file supports Larrow Green: "Larrow Green having lent until then.")
7. **Teague's station-gain line vs. the 1898 entry:** Teague says the line "was my own device; I began it when I came in 1905," yet quotes "the entry for 1898" and says the entries were "in the same hand for as long as the hand was mine." He did not join until 1905, so the 1898 entry cannot be in his hand or his invention. Bulletin 471 also says the gain stands "year by year" from 1897, and r07 says the station column runs from 1897 with the same entry-form every year.
8. **Teague's percentage base:** the entry says "five and a quarter per cent," and Teague's prose says that was "of the fat that was actually in the cream" — but 3,040/60,800 = 5.0%; 5.25% is of the credited 57,760. Ivy explicitly states the ledger uses the credited base; r07's curatorial note agrees. Teague misstates his own figure.
9. **Keddie's dismissal date vs. board dates:** Keddie — put out 30 April 1925 ("the last day of the month"), with "no cause established" already found; Effie — board sat until 4 May 1925 and she recorded the award "on the last day." So the dismissal precedes the recorded award by four days.
10. **Vira's sequence of events is internally inconsistent** and conflicts with the others: she says the tests went up "one summer" and *then* the hearing came "the winter and spring after," yet also says they put Keddie out "and that is why the numbers went right the summer after." Per Keddie/Ivy the tests rose when the glass was condemned (June 1923) and stood at 3.80 in 1924, before the 1925 hearing; Keddie tested nothing in 1925 — so the rise cannot be a consequence of his removal.
11. **Vira's theory (cream theft) vs. Bulletin 471** ("no cream was stolen") vs. Teague ("cream is portable"; one pair of hands; he says nothing about any particular day) vs. Keddie ("never taken a pound of cream") vs. Vose (r08 — watering, not theft).
12. **Keddie's "thirty-one times a week" vs. Vira's "a thousand cans a week"** — different measures (tests vs. cans); not strictly contradictory but not reconciled.
13. **Nine families vs. seven farms vs. six farms:** Vira, Keddie, and Roy speak of nine families/kitchens (station patrons; recipients of the circular); Effie of **seven** farms with rights on Hessel Bottom; **Roy (r09) of SIX farms** with rights on the Bottom. Different sets (Bottom farms vs. all patrons), but the seven-vs-six is a direct conflict — see #56. (See also #28: Strawn's "wet bottom farms + three hill places.")
14. **Hazel Brant:** Vira — the visitor was "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed"; r07 provenance — weigh book to her 1962, to the Rooms 1963; **r09 — Hazel is Roy's daughter, Ivy's second cousin.** See #61.
15. **Who condemned the crate:** Effie — "the state's inspector," June 1923; Bulletin 471 — 12 June 1923; Ivy says the crate was carried up in June 1923. Merle Strawn is the state's dairy inspector — **r06 confirms it was Strawn, in his own quoted 12 June 1923 entry**, and r07 Item 9 holds the photostat. (Vose's July date — see #45.)
16. **The crate was never mentioned at the hearing** (Effie §8), though the award speaks of "the books and appliances of the station." Keddie's letter mentions the crate arriving in 1897 but not its condemnation in 1923; his letter does record the 1924 average at 3.80 without saying why. Yet Vose's 1926 address openly acknowledges the condemnation and the replacement order — one year after the hearing at which no one mentioned it.
17. **Keddie's death:** Ivy — 1934; Vira — "in the thirties."
18. **Vira's "nine ways" split of the award** vs. Effie's "seven farms" vs. Roy's "six farms" — nine patrons vs. Bottom-farm counts.
19. **"Mr. Vose" in Teague's 1916 remark:** by then Gideon was no longer president (ended 1911) and Selby was manager (from 1913); Teague names "Mr. Selby Vose" as manager, so the 1916 "Mr. Vose" is presumably Selby, but Teague does not specify. Vira's "old Mrs. Vose" is presumably Gideon's wife.
20. **Effie's "three explanations" argued before the board** are never listed by her ("they are in the minutes"). Candidates from other sources: wet hay (1908 committee), water in the cans (1919 circular; Vose's 1926 cause), cream taken (Teague/Vira). Not stated.
21. **Ivy's "twenty-six-year anomaly"** = 1897–1923; her "twenty-five full delivery years" = 1898–1922; Keddie's "twenty-nine seasons" = 1896–1924; Effie's "twenty-five years over which the patrons laid their claim"; Roy's "near on fifty years" (1911→1958, for the unexplained nine weeks). These spans differ by definition.
22. **Vira says Selby Vose was "down at Larrow Green"; Teague places the office (where Selby signed the sheets) at Ashlin.** Effie mentions an office move in 1938 without naming places. (Strawn 1913 met the new manager at the Larrow Green creamery platform; r08 places the Vose "home place" herd in the Larrow Green area.)
23. **Odd non-mention:** Vira says she told the 1958 visitor about the letter in the drawer; Ivy's talk does not mention Vira, the letter, or the 1919 circular — Ivy addresses only wet hay, watering, and cream in the Bulletin paragraph.
24. **(r06) — Strawn refuted the watering charge in period:** his 1919 entry says he had "tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so" — told the manager (Selby Vose) directly, the same year the circular went out over the manager's signature. The Association thus had contemporaneous state evidence against "water in the cans," and the circular's charge stood in nine kitchens for decades anyway. **And in 1926 Vose reasserted watering as the cause regardless — see #48.**
25. **(r06) — The missed 1922 check:** Strawn had carried the burette from spring 1921 but skipped running Ostrey Hollow's glass against it in 1922 ("the day being short and the road home being what it was, and I have put that down because it is what happened"). The 26-year error could have been caught a year earlier; his honesty in recording the omission is itself notable.
26. **(r06) — Strawn's June 1911 visit fell inside the nine-week borrowed-measure window** (8 May–10 July 1911) and he watched a test through and wrote "Nothing here to note." Consistent — the borrowed measure delivered correctly, so nothing looked wrong — but the inspector was present during the one interval when the station read true, and saw nothing remarkable. (Roy's claim that the state man LEFT the measure — #58 — would make this visit the source of the substitute, which Strawn's own extract does not mention.)
27. **(r06) — Start of Strawn's records:** Ivy says the circuit notebooks were "kept by the state's dairy inspector for this county from **1908**"; Strawn himself says he took the Ordell circuit in **1907** and his first extract is dated 1907. Also Ivy: "for this county" vs. Strawn: four counties.
28. **(r06) — Patron count again:** Strawn 1909 says the station takes from "the farms of the wet bottom below it and from three places on the hill." Seven Bottom farms (Effie) + three hill places = ten sources; six Bottom farms (Roy) + three hill places = nine sources — which would exactly match the nine families of Vira/Keddie/Roy, favoring Roy's six over Effie's seven. Unstated in any source; noted here as arithmetic only.
29. **(r06) — "Twenty-eight" circuit books, "one to the year,"** but the circuit ran 1907–1936, which spans thirty calendar years. Two years are unaccounted for (no explanation given).
30. **(r06) — The loft "full since before the war":** Strawn (1928) was told the loft over the intake room "had been full since before the war," yet Ivy establishes the crate was carried up in June 1923 — after the war. Either the reply referred to other odds and ends, or it was wrong; either way it deflected attention from the loft where the crate sat.
31. **(r06→r07 RESOLVED) — Path of the notebooks:** Strawn's closing note (1936) sends the 28 books to the **state dairy office**; Ivy says they were in the **Farland college library since 1947**. r07 Item 9 makes the chain explicit: state dairy office on retirement 1936 → Farland library 1947; the 1923 volume found and photostated 1958. No conflict remains.
32. **(r06) — Strawn's entries independently corroborate:** the scale always true (matching Keddie's claim and Roy's two-agreeing-scales point); no added water ever found (matching Bulletin 471); new manager 1913 (Selby Vose); 1924 book average 3.80; new tester from 1925; the crate condemned 12 June 1923 with new glass from July 1923. Also, Strawn watching a test "through from the sample to the reading" matches Keddie's "as the state man saw me do it."
33. **(r07) — Attribution swap in the accession headings:** Item 3 labels the **station test book** "kept by **E. Brant**," yet its first leaf is signed "A. Keddie," it is "one hand throughout, twenty-nine seasons," and Item 8 (Keddie's signed statement) is "in the hand of Item 3." Item 4 labels the **farm weigh book** "kept by **A. Keddie**," yet its provenance is the Hessel Bottom household → Roy Brant (1929) → Hazel Brant — i.e., the Brant book, and every other source (Ivy, Keddie, Teague, Vira, Strawn, Roy) has Keddie keeping the test book and the Brants the weigh book. The two "kept by" credits appear transposed by the curator; they stand as written in r07.
34. **(r07) — WHERE the crate was found, Feb 1958:** Ivy (1959, firsthand): in the **loft over the intake room of the station building** up the Hollow, under a tarpaulin, reached by a ladder, carried up June 1923 "and it was there still" — corroborated obliquely by Vira ("she went up and poked about the old station building") and by Strawn's 1928 loft note. Nym (1972): "The crate was **found in the Association office at Ashlin**, in February 1958, by Miss Ivy Keddie." Direct, unresolved contradiction on the find-site.
35. **(r07) — Ivy's institution identified:** Vira — "the college away east"; Ivy — "my own college's library at Farland"; Nym — "Miss Ivy Keddie **of the State Agricultural College at Farland**"; Roy — "the college at Farland." So the Farland college = the State Agricultural College, issuer of Bulletins 214 (1911) and 471 (1958). A resolution, not a conflict.
36. **(r07) — Ledger column start & the "1898" entry:** r07 — "the station column runs from **1897**," and the 57,760/60,800/3,040 entry is quoted as the "typical" (undated) yearly form; Teague dates that exact text "**the entry for 1898**." Also r07: the 1896 part-season intake exists nowhere at all.
37. **(r07) — Alder Corners records:** Ivy cites **fourteen seasons** of overlap for four Hessel Bottom men; r07's correspondence file holds copied figures "for the season of **1919**" only. And the book's chain: r07 — "from its founder to **his daughter** in 1941"; Ivy — Rosalie Cudd "runs that factory and is Jerome Cudd's **niece**." (If the founder is Jerome Cudd, daughter vs. niece disagree; neither name appears in r07.) Roy adds: factory opened 1914, hauling from 1915, **sixteen seasons** — see #60.
38. **(r07) — Statement mode:** r03's framing — the nine sentences were spoken, "taken down as he gave them"; r07 Item 8 — "one leaf, ruled, both sides, in the hand of Item 3 [Keddie's], **signed**." A dictated transcript vs. an autograph signed leaf; unreconciled.
39. **(r07) — By-laws dating:** the printed pamphlet "carr[ies] no year on [its] face, only the words 'adopted at the annual meeting'" — Effie's 1892 adoption date is the only source for the year.
40. **(r07→r09 RESOLVED) — Emmet Brant's death year:** the weigh book passed "to Roy Brant in **1929**" (r07), implying Emmet died 1929; **r09 confirms: "He was born in 1854 and he died in 1929,"** and he kept the book "until the week before he died."
41. **(r07) — Test-book columns:** Nym Item 3 — "columns for date, patron, pounds and test" (four); Keddie — "five columns: the date, the patron, the pounds, the test, the fat." The "fat" column is missing from Nym's description.
42. **(r07) — 1908 committee report vs. Keddie's memory:** the signed report says the sampling covered January–March 1908 ("The milk is whole and the scales are honest"); Keddie's letter recalls "a committee came up and sampled the herds through the winter" — consistent; but the report's confidence in the scales and milk sits alongside its wrong wet-hay verdict, later demolished by Bulletin 214 and Ivy. Roy names its head (Nyle Grigg) and preserves Emmet's retort: "the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not."
43. **(r08) — Vose's framing vs. what he knew:** the address leans on "no cause established... against any officer or servant... men under oath with the books in front of them," and — now that the full text is read — he does discuss the award, the condemned glass, and his 1919 circular, but never mentions Keddie's dismissal, never mentions that Strawn told him in 1919 that no water had ever been found, and never mentions the station-gain line he signed yearly (Teague: "Mr. Vose knew what that account was doing... I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone").
44. **(r08) — Corroborations:** "thirteen years this spring, since 1913" matches Effie's 1913–1938 managership and Strawn's 1913 "new manager" note; "seven miles up the valley" matches Effie/Teague/Strawn; the Jan 1925 petition, Ashlin venue, and Frick chairmanship match Effie/Teague; the station still running in 1926 matches Strawn's fall-1925 "new man at the tester's bench"; "We ordered new inside the month and had it in use before the flush was over" matches the 28 June 1923 order (r07 Item 10) and Strawn's "new glass in use since July."
45. **(r08 NEW) — Date of the condemnation:** Strawn's own entry, Bulletin 471, and r07 Items 5/9 all say **12 June 1923**; Vose's 1926 address says "**the twelfth of July, 1923**." Same day-number, wrong month — and July would make the "inside the month" order (28 June) precede the condemnation. Vose's date is wrong (or the printed report's).
46. **(r08 NEW) — The 1924 average:** Keddie's book, Strawn's 1924 entry, and Ivy all give **3.80**; Vose says the tests "rose by twenty-one hundredths and the average for 1924 stood at **3.90**." Internally inconsistent too: 3.61 + 0.21 = 3.82, matching neither 3.80 nor 3.90. (The true rise, 3.61→3.80, is nineteen hundredths.)
47. **(r08 NEW) — Vose's chronology is impossible:** he says the Association's attention came to rest on the station, "and there was a petition, and a hearing... And in the very next season the tests at that station rose... the average for 1924." But the petition was January 1925 and the hearing March–May 1925 — AFTER the 1924 rise he cites as their consequence. (The actual cause on the record — new glass from July 1923 — precedes both.) Compare Vira's similarly reversed sequence (#10): both fold the rise into a punishment-then-improvement story.

I decline to build a mystery on a piece of housekeeping"). His wear theory also cannot explain an error constant from the glass's first season (1898's 3.61, off new-bought pipettes blown 17.6/delivering 16.72) — wear would grow; this was flat for 26 years. Note also his "in daily use since 1896" — the condemned outfit arrived in 1897.

Roy's paraphrase ("warning us against putting water in the milk") is closer to Vose's version, yet Roy also reports all nine families "read it for what it was" and his father never forgave it.
