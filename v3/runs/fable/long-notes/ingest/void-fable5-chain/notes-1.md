## 1. Retelling 01 — Vira Toland, "a Hessel Bottom patron's daughter," recorded at Ostrey Hollow, 1979

**Framing:** a tape made at her kitchen table by "a young man from the county who asked three questions in an hour and let her have the rest of it"; transcribed as spoken, doublings-back left in. Title of file: "What the Hollow Said."

**Vira herself:**
- Born 1912 "at the top end of Ostrey Hollow, in the house my father's father put up."
- Except two years "down at Ashlin when I was first married," never lived anywhere else ("I would not count those two years anyhow").
- Her father hauled milk to the station "the same as everybody up here did." **Nine families** hauled to that station; "in and out of each other's kitchens the whole year round." She could name every one but "you don't want names, you want the trouble."
- Her father is never named. Her mother appears (at the stove, back turned, when the letter was read). Her brother "has it yet" (the letter).
- She was **seven years old in 1919**.
- Closing: "It rained the day of that letter, I remember the wet on my father's shoulders when he came in, and I have never in sixty years been able to think about the one without the other."

**The station (as she describes it):**
- "at the very top of the Hollow where the road bends and then goes to nothing but the ridge."
- "a low building, whitewashed, cold as a spring house inside even in August"; smelled "of milk and steam and hot iron"; "you could hear the engine going before you came round the last turn."
- By 1958 it was "shut by then and had been for years, they kept fence wire and salt in it."

**Ansel Keddie (per Vira):**
- "Ansel Keddie ran it. He went up there in **ninety-five** [1895], the year before they opened it, to get it ready, and he tested that first summer too, and he was still there when I was a girl big enough to ride on the wagon with my father."
- **His book:** "ruled, and the ruling was his own, done with a straightedge, and every line in it was the same height as every other line, and his figures were like print." He wrote in it "standing up at a slope desk by the window where the light came in." If a man asked what his milk had done, "He turned to the day and he read it off." "He tested **a thousand cans a week** and never a blot in that book." Her father's saying: "if the rest of the world kept its accounts the way Ansel Keddie kept his there would be no lawyers in Ashlin." ("There are still lawyers in Ashlin.")
- **Vira's own verdict:** "He was taking the cream. They caught him at it and they put him out, and that is why the numbers went right the summer after. That is what it was. I am not going to dress it up for you. A man stands in a room by himself with the separator running and the door shut and nobody in the valley can add, and after a while he helps himself, and it is not that he is a wicked man, it is that nobody ever came up that road and looked."
- Her father "never would say it out loud. My father said Ansel Keddie was an honest man who had got into something."
- "He died **in the thirties** and he was still under it when he died." Orra "outlived him a good while and had it the whole time" (the valley "looking at him a certain way in church").

**The 1919 letter (the "circular"):**
- "Nineteen nineteen. That is the year everything went sour up here."
- "A letter came from the Association, **printed, over the manager's signature**, and it said there was **water in the cans** coming down from Ostrey Hollow." Went to **nine families**; her father one of the nine.
- Father read it at the table "with his hat still on, which he never did"; read it twice; put it "in the drawer of the desk in the front room"; it was still there when he died; "I believe my brother has it yet."
- "One woman I knew had hers pinned inside the lid of her trunk." People "keep the paper forty years to prove what was said of them."
- **Signer:** "**Selby Vose**, down at Larrow Green. He was **old Gideon Vose's nephew**, and old Gideon was the one that started the whole Association back in the beginning, and I think the family always thought the valley belonged to them a little."
- **Kinship nobody wrote down:** "**Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins.** Everybody knew that." So the letter "was not only the Association saying it... it was somebody's cousin saying it."

**Keddie/Brant family relations (per Vira):**
- "**Orra was Ansel Keddie's wife. And Orra was Emmet Brant's sister.**"
- "**Emmet Brant** was the biggest man on the Bottom, he had the most cows and the most hay ground and he hauled more milk to that station than anybody, and he was the one that letter cost the most."
- "The man that tested the milk had married the sister of the man that got the worst of it."
- Before 1919 the two families "ate their Sunday dinners together... the two houses in one kitchen and the men out at the fence after." After 1919 "they did not speak. Not one word, not at the store, not at a funeral. **Orra would walk down to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane.**" Both "long in the ground now."

**The tests rising (per Vira):**
- "Then the tests went up. All at once, **one summer**, the figures came up and stayed up, and nobody ever said why. Not the station, not the Association, not anybody." Her father "did not know what he had done different, and he had not done anything different, none of them had." "the thing corrected itself and no man stood up and gave a reason for it."

**The hearing (per Vira):**
- "after that there was the hearing down at Ashlin. That was **the winter and spring after**." "They sat in an office down there, lawyers and the officers of the Association and our men going down in the wagons, and it went on and on."
- Outcome: "they put Ansel Keddie out. An old man, and out, after all those seasons in that cold room, and they paid the patrons **thirty-five hundred dollars** and called it settled. Thirty-five hundred dollars, split up **nine ways** over all those years — my father's share of it did not buy a hay rake."

**The 1958 visitor (per Vira):**
- "a young woman came round here in **nineteen fifty-eight** from the college away east, asking after all this, and she went up and poked about the old station building." "She sat where you are sitting. Nice girl, careful, wrote everything down."
- "She was **Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed**, so she was of the family both sides in a manner of speaking." (Hazel Brant is named nowhere else. The visitor is evidently Ivy Keddie — see r04.)
- Vira "told her about the letter in my father's drawer. I don't know what she made of it."

## 2. Retelling 02 — Effie Loomis, secretary of the Association 1901–1940, "Notes toward a history of the Association," 1941

**Framing:** "Written out in numbered sections for the Association's own files, in the year after I laid down the minute book: from the minutes wherever the minutes will bear it, from my own recollection where they will not, and the two kept apart."

**§1 — Organizing.** In **1889** the farmers of the Larrow Valley met at **Larrow Green** and organized the **Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association**. **Gideon Vose** elected first president; held office **until 1911** — "twenty-two years, and no other man has held it so long." Effie "was fourteen years old in 1889" (so born c. 1875); became secretary **1901**. "The **1889–1892 minute book was lost when the office was moved in 1938.**"

**§2 — Creamery.** "The main creamery was built at Larrow Green in **1891**, on **Larrow Creek**, and it is the same building yet." Before it, members sold milk where they could; "no common test and no common price."

**§3 — By-laws, Article VII.** Gideon Vose drafted the by-laws himself; "He was not a lawyer and they read as a farmer wrote them." Quoted entire:

> *Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*

Adopted at the **annual meeting of 1892**, "the year after the creamery opened." Effie's gloss: the last clause — "*The test of the station where he delivers shall govern.* Not the test of the creamery, not the average of the valley, not a test made over again anywhere else. The station's test, and no appeal from it. That clause is the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business and it was written in 1892 by a man who **died in 1917** and could not be asked about it afterward."

**§4 — Officers in her time.**
- Effie Loomis, secretary **1901–1940**, kept the minute book in her own hand.
- **Wilbur Teague**, bookkeeper from **1905**, kept the ledgers.
- **Hobart Sill**, buttermaker at Larrow Green; had "the churn book and the cream receiving book."
- **Selby Vose, "son of the late president,"** manager **1913 to 1938**.
- "not one of us ever went up the valley and stood in the Ostrey Hollow station on a working morning to watch the work being done. I include myself first in that."

**§5 — The station.** "The skimming station stood **seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green**, at the head of the Hollow." It "took in the milk of the farms up there, separated it, sent the cream down to us and gave the skim back to the patron." "The best of that milk came off **Hessel Bottom**, the wet meadow below the hamlet. **Seven farms had rights on Hessel Bottom** and they cut hay and pastured on it, and the largest of the seven was **Emmet Brant's**, who was the station's largest patron in every year of which we have a record."

**§6 — Petition and board.** "In **January 1925** the patrons of Ostrey Hollow petitioned the Association. Their complaint was that their milk had never tested as their neighbors' milk tested and that they had been paid accordingly for a very long time, and they asked to be made whole." "The board of arbitration sat at **Ashlin** under **Alonzo Frick, attorney, of that place, from 17 March to 4 May 1925**. **Three explanations** were argued before it. None of the three was accepted." "they are in the minutes and the minutes are in the safe." Award, recorded by Effie "on the last day":

> *The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account.*

Effie: "*Finds no cause established*, and then an award of thirty-eight hundred dollars: I took it down as it was given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once."

**§7 — Quantities (Teague's ledgers).** "For the **twenty-five years** over which the patrons laid their claim, the ledgers show that the Ostrey Hollow station received **forty million pounds** of milk, which is an average of **one million six hundred thousand pounds** in a year. Over the same period the price paid for butterfat averaged **twenty-five cents a pound**." "they are Mr. Teague's, out of the annual sheets, and I have added the columns twice."

**§8 — What the record does not contain.**
1. The 1938 office move lost the 1889–1892 minute book: "It was not thrown out; it was carried out with the rest and it did not arrive." "anybody who tells you what was resolved at the first four annual meetings is telling you a story he had from his father."
2. **The crate.** "The crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all. I took every word down and it is not there. I mean the **crate of measures that the state's inspector condemned up at the station in June of 1923**, which was set aside and new ones ordered in its place. Not a word of it was spoken in any of the **three sessions**, by the patrons, by the officers, or by the board, and the award's phrase about 'the books and appliances of the station' is a form of words the board used and not a list of anything."

## 3. Retelling 03 — Ansel Keddie, tester at Ostrey Hollow: statement to the board (17 March 1925) and an unsent letter (spring 1925)

**Framing:** "Two papers. The first is what he said, standing, before the board of arbitration at Ashlin on the seventeenth of March, 1925 — **nine sentences and no more**, taken down as he gave them. The second is a letter written at Ostrey Hollow later that spring; it was never sent, and was found among his papers."

**I. Statement to the board, Ashlin, 17 March 1925 (quoted in full):**

> I have kept the test book of this station in my own hand since the station opened, and there is not a figure in it that I did not put there. I have set down the date, the patron, the pounds, the test and the fat, and where I have made an error I have drawn a line through it and put my letters against the line, and I have never written a figure over a figure. I have made the tests in the same order and at the same hour of every working day of every season. I have heard what the patrons say of me and I have heard what the officers say of me. Neither of them has come up that road on a working morning and stood at my elbow and watched the work done. I am asked to account for figures, and the figures are what I read and what I wrote and nothing besides. I am an old man and I have no other trade. I have tested as I was taught and as the state man saw me do it, **thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons**, and I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are. I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself.

**II. Unsent letter, Ostrey Hollow, spring 1925 — contents:**
- Why written: "I could not say it in that room, where a man is allowed to answer and not allowed to explain," and "somebody ought to know how the work was done before everybody who did it is gone."
- **Daily routine:** "The day began at **half past five**. The wagons came up the Hollow in the dark most of the year, and I had the intake room open and the lamps lit and the scale swinging free before the first of them was at the door. Every can went on the scale and the weight was called and written before the milk went anywhere else. That scale was tested by the state's man on his rounds and it was true, and I have never in twenty-nine seasons had a patron dispute a weight with me and be right."
- **Sampling:** a sample from each patron's milk as weighed "went into his own jar, numbered to him and to nobody else, and the jar was made up over **ten days** before it was tested." Testing morning: jar "set in warm water until it was blood-warm right through, and then it was poured from vessel to vessel, and poured again, until the cream that had risen was carried down through the whole of it. A sample that is not warmed and mixed is worth nothing and a boy could tell you so."
- **The charge and test:** "The milk was drawn up in the measure to the mark on the stem, and the tip wiped, and the charge blown out into the bottle that carried the patron's number. Then the acid, poured down the side, and the bottle turned until the milk was dark. **Four bottles in the hand tester**, weighted against each other, the cover down, and the crank turned at the speed I was taught to turn it and no faster, and no man ever stood beside me and made me hurry it. Then the fat is up in the neck of the bottle in a clear column, and it is read off in per cent against the rulings, with a **pair of dividers**, and the figure is written down." "It is not a hard trade. It is only a trade that must be done the same way every time, which is a harder thing than it sounds."
- **The glass:** "The glass I began on in **ninety-six** was **borrowed off the Ashlin creamery**, and we used it until our own outfit came up the valley in a crate **the spring following**." (Contrast the first leaf below, which says borrowed of Larrow Green.)
- **The book:** "kept in **five columns**: the date, the patron, the pounds, the test, the fat." Errors crossed and initialed; "never wrote over a figure, because a figure written over another figure is a figure no man can swear to afterward." "**Twenty-nine seasons, from 1896 through 1924. I did not test a pound in the season of 1925.**"
- **First leaf of the book (quoted):**

> *Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie.*

- **Summary leaf made up in 1922** "because I had been asked for them so often" (quoted):

> *Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80.*

- **The nine weeks of 1911:** "the strangest thing in my book." "Our measure broke on the **eighth of May** and I made shift with one borrowed, and from that morning the tests came up and stood at 3.80, and on the **tenth of July** our own was in use again and the tests were down again the same day." The two entries (quoted):

> *May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure.*

> *July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.*

"That question mark is mine and I have let it stand for fourteen years."
- **Shape of the gap:** "They did not come and go with the season. The gap was there in July as it was there in February, in the flush of the grass and in the dead of the winter on dry hay, and it was the same in a wet year as in a dry one. **In 1924 the average of this station was 3.80**, and I wrote that figure with the same pen and the same measure of care as all the others."
- **History of complaints:** "The complaints began in **1902**, when it was first said down at Larrow Green that Ostrey Hollow milk did not test with the rest. In **1908** a committee came up and sampled the herds through the winter and reported that the **wet hay of the Bottom thinned the cream**, and that the remedy lay with the land. In **1919** a circular went out from the Association to **nine families** up here saying there was water in their cans. I carry no charge against any man who put his name to that paper." "**nine kitchens in this Hollow have had that letter in a drawer ever since.**"
- **Family:** "My wife **Orra is Emmet Brant's sister**. Emmet brought more milk to my door than any other man on the Bottom, and every pound of it I weighed myself and every test of it I made myself and wrote in the book. **He and I have not spoken since the circular.** That is the plainest fact in this letter and it is the one I find I have the least to say about."
- **Dismissal:** "I was put out on the **thirtieth of April**. They found no cause established and they put me out on the last day of the month, and both of those things are true at once, and I have given up trying to hold them in my head together. I have tested as I was taught. I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are."

## 4. Retelling 04 — Ivy Keddie, "What the Crate Held," a talk to the Ordell County Historical Society, Ashlin, 1959

**Framing:** "Read from typescript at the Society's meeting, with the crate itself on a table at the front and the borrowed books laid open beside it; the papers quoted here were read aloud from the originals."

**Ivy's family:** "**Ansel Keddie**, who kept the test book at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station, was my **grandfather**. My father, **Duncan Keddie**, who **taught school at Ashlin**, was his son." "nothing I am going to show you tonight depends in the smallest degree on my opinion of my grandfather. It depends on **nine pieces of glass and a burette**."

**Five sets of records** ("no two of them had ever been in one room before last year"):
1. **The station test book** — "in the Association's office since 1925 and came to these Rooms this year."
2. **The Brant weigh book**, "kept at Hessel Bottom from **1897** onward," lent by **Roy Brant, Emmet Brant's son**, "who has kept it up himself since his father died."
3. **The Alder Corners Cheese Company's test figures**, "from over **Redlow Ridge in Vessey County**," copied out by **Rosalie Cudd**, "who runs that factory and is **Jerome Cudd's niece**; the book itself has never left Vessey County and I have never seen it."
4. **Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks**, "kept by the **state's dairy inspector for this county from 1908**," in "my own college's library at **Farland** since **1947** — eleven years in which nobody put a question to them."
5. **The Association's own annual ledgers**, present at the talk.

**The crate:**
- "In **February of last year** [1958] I went up the Hollow to the station building, which has been used for storage since it closed. There is a **loft over what was the intake room, reached by a ladder**, and under a **tarpaulin** at the back of it there was a wooden crate stenciled on the end **AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET**. It had been carried up there in **June of 1923** and it was there still."
- "Inside, packed in straw, were the station's condemned milk-test pipettes. **Six dozen** had been bought new for the station in **1897**, on an invoice that came to **seventy-seven dollars and ninety-three cents** [$77.93] for the whole outfit — pipettes, bottles, acid measure, hand tester and freight together. **Nine** of them were unbroken in the crate."

**The measurement (9 March 1958, at Farland, against a calibrated burette) — her sheet, quoted:**

> *Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for.*

**The arithmetic ("arithmetic and not conjuring"):**
- A butterfat test draws a measured charge into a bottle whose neck is ruled to read percent for that charge. Short charge → short reading in proportion. "16.72 divided by 17.6 is ninety-five hundredths, exactly. Every test made at that station with that glass read **ninety-five percent of the truth**."
- Grandfather's 1922 summary leaf quoted (identical text to r03): *Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80.*
- "3.61 divided by 0.95 is 3.80. The recorded average was 3.61; the true average was 3.80; the gap is **nineteen hundredths** of a point." "Nineteen hundredths is **five percent of 3.80**. The station did not lose a set number of points. It lost **a twentieth of whatever was there**."
- "In the **twenty-five full delivery years from 1898 to 1922** the station took in **forty million pounds** of milk, an average of **one million six hundred thousand pounds** a year. At the recorded 3.61 percent, the patrons were credited with **1,444,000 pounds** of fat. At the true 3.80 percent, they had delivered **1,520,000 pounds**. The difference is **76,000 pounds** of butterfat. Butterfat over those years averaged **twenty-five cents a pound**, so the unpaid fat was worth **nineteen thousand dollars**. The board of arbitration in 1925 awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons **thirty-eight hundred dollars**. That is **a fifth** of it."
- **Two percentages, both right:** "Seventy-six thousand pounds is **five percent of the fat actually delivered** — that is, of the 1,520,000. It is **five and a quarter percent of the fat credited**, of the 1,444,000. The Association's ledger line uses the second, because a bookkeeper naturally reckons a surplus against the figure he has already entered. Neither figure is an error. But five percent is the one that is also the glass."
- **Proportional error hit best milk hardest:** "A herd whose milk was recorded at **4.56** was short **0.24** of a point. A herd recorded at **3.42** was short **0.18**." "no man had any way of knowing that his good cows cost him more than his poor ones."

**The 1911 "experiment":** "For **nine weeks in 1911** there was an experiment at that station, and nobody knew it was one. The station's own measure broke on the **eighth of May** and a substitute was used until the **tenth of July**. **The measure they borrowed in 1911 came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon.** Same patrons, same herds, same pasture, same season, same acid, same bottles, same man at the crank — one object changed — and the average went to 3.80 and came back to 3.61 the day the station's own measure returned. No explanation of this valley's **twenty-six-year anomaly** survives those nine weeks except the one I have given you."

**The wet-hay explanation refuted:** "It was said the wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream. The State Agricultural College tested that in **1911** and printed the answer in **Bulletin No. 214**":

> *Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture.*

"Five hundredths in winter, nothing in summer — against a gap of nineteen hundredths standing flat in every month of every year, July and February alike. It is **four times too small and it is the wrong shape**." Also: "over the ridge, the **Alder Corners** factory tested the milk of **four Hessel Bottom men** in their summer flush; where the two books overlap, **fourteen seasons** of them, that same milk off that same wet ground ran about **nineteen hundredths higher at Alder Corners** than at home."

**Bulletin No. 471** ("issued last October" = October 1958), last paragraph, "Professor Vaught's wording and mine together":

> *No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain.*

**Closing:** "The glass was condemned in **1923** and the cause was found in **1958**: **thirty-five years**. My grandfather was dismissed in **1925**: **thirty-three years**. He **died in 1934** and had been dead **twenty-four years** when the sheet was read that clears him. I have no remedy to propose. I have only the crate, and it is on the table, and you may look into it on your way out."

## 5. Retelling 05 — Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper (retired), Chapter Nine of "The Books of the Association," 1948

**Framing:** "Chapter Nine of an unpublished memoir, written out at **Ashlin in the winter of 1948, three years after I gave up the desk**. I have set the figures down as they stood in the books."

**Teague:** "I kept the books of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association from **1905 to 1945**, forty years by the calendar." "in the whole of it I never carried a column that would not prove." "a bookkeeper who wants admiring has generally got something behind him that he would rather you did not turn over."

**The office at Ashlin:** "one room **over a harness shop**, cold at the north end and never cold enough at the south, and the ledgers lived on a shelf that a taller man had built for himself before me. I worked at a **sloped desk with a brass rail** across the foot of it." "the sound the station sheets made coming out of their envelope in **March**, which was a stiffer paper than the creamery used."

**The Association "three things on paper":** "a creamery at Larrow Green, an office at Ashlin, and a skimming station **seven miles up the valley at Ostrey Hollow**. **Mr. Selby Vose was manager** over the whole of it, **Miss Effie Loomis was secretary** and kept the minutes, and I sat at the ledger. The station was the smallest of the three and it made the most noise."

**How the station worked (Teague's version):** patrons hauled milk up to the station instead of seven miles down to Larrow Green; weighed in; sample from each man's can tested for butterfat; man paid on pounds and test together; station separated the milk, sent cream down to Larrow Green, gave skim back "for his hogs and his calves."

**The station-gain column:** "On one side of the station account I entered the butterfat credited to the patrons on the station's own test — that is, what the station's books said it had bought. On the other side I entered the butterfat that actually came down the valley in the station's cream and was weighed and tested into our vats at Larrow Green — that is, what we had in hand. The two were never the same. **The second was always the larger.** The difference between them was the station's account to the good, and I carried it to the general account of the Association."
- "The station-gain line was **my own device; I began it when I came in 1905**. I have heard it said in the valley that the line was some old dodge of the founders. It was not." "a difference that will not be entered somewhere will come back and bite you at audit."
- **The entry, quoted complete:**

> *Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account.*

- "**That is the entry for 1898**, and there is one like it for every year after, in the same terms and in the same hand for as long as the hand was mine." "The gain ran at **five and a quarter percent of the fat that was actually in the cream**. It ran there year after year, so evenly that the words 'as usual' got into the entry and stayed there... **A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture.**"

**Two large numbers (Teague):** "Over the years the account ran, that station took in **forty-four million pounds** of milk. And the average test of all the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, taken over the whole period and not over some picked year that suits an argument, was **3.16**. Down at Larrow Green we did better than that, and there was not a season in twenty-five years when the Ostrey Hollow men were not sore about it."

**The 1911 break (Teague):** "The station's own measure broke in the **spring of 1911**, and they made shift with a borrowed one for **six weeks** before their own came up from town. It went into my accounts as a small charge for replacement and nothing else." People later "came asking me about that year in particular, as though a bookkeeper kept the weather."

**What Teague told the board in 1925 (and repeats):** "**Cream is portable**, and that station was in **one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons**." "One man weighed the milk, one man drew the sample, one man made the test, one man wrote the figure that the pay slip was made from, and one man ran the separator. There was **no second man in that building from the spring to the fall**." "I do not say what any particular person did on any particular day, because I was not there and I do not know." "a business which is arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken from it... **The remedy is two men or a lock, and the Association had neither.**"

**The hearing (Teague):** "The patrons petitioned in the winter, when a farmer has time to be angry, and the board sat at Ashlin under **Mr. Alonzo Frick**, who was as fair a chairman as ever a hearing had, and I was called and gave my evidence." "The board found nothing established against anybody. It then awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons **$3,800**, without admission, and charged it to the station account, and I entered that too, and it balanced."

**Two things more:**
1. "**Mr. Vose knew what that account was doing. He signed the sheet every year.** I laid the annual sheet before him each spring and he put his name at the foot of it... What he made of it he never told me."
2. "I did not sit silent for forty years... **I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone.** I let it alone. I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience."

"The figures are in the ledgers at Ashlin, in ink, dated."

## 10. Documents quoted (verbatim, collected)

1. **Article VII** (Effie §3): "Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."
2. **The award, 4 May 1925** (Effie §6): "The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account."
3. **Keddie's statement, 17 March 1925** — nine sentences, quoted in full in §3 above.
4. **First leaf of the test book**: "Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie."
5. **1922 summary leaf** (quoted identically by Keddie and Ivy): "Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80."
6. **Test-book entries, 1911**: "May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." / "July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."
7. **Ivy's measurement sheet, 9 March 1958**: "Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for."
8. **Bulletin No. 214 (1911)**: "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."
9. **Bulletin No. 471 (Oct 1958), last paragraph**: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."
10. **Teague's 1898 ledger entry**: "Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account."

## 11. Conflicts, discrepancies, and oddities (between and within sources)

1. **Amount of the award:** Vira says **$3,500**; Effie (recording the award verbatim), Teague, and Ivy all say **$3,800**.
2. **Selby Vose's relation to Gideon:** Effie — "son of the late president"; Vira — "old Gideon Vose's nephew."
3. **Total milk:** Effie and Ivy — 40,000,000 lb over 25 years (1898–1922); Teague — 44,000,000 lb "over the years the account ran" (his period may be longer, e.g. 1898–1924, but he does not say).
4. **Average test:** Keddie's leaf and Ivy — 3.61 recorded (3.80 true); Teague — "3.16" (looks like a transposition of 3.61, but stands as written).
5. **Length of the 1911 borrowed-measure interval:** Keddie and Ivy — nine weeks (8 May–10 July); Teague — "six weeks." Teague also says "their own came up from town" and it went in as a replacement charge, whereas Keddie's entry says "Our own measure in use again" and Ivy says "the station's own measure returned."
6. **Where the 1896 glass was borrowed from:** Keddie's first leaf — "borrowed of Larrow Green"; Keddie's letter prose — "borrowed off the Ashlin creamery." (Per Effie/Teague the creamery is at Larrow Green and Ashlin is the office; no "Ashlin creamery" is otherwise mentioned.)
7. **Teague's station-gain line vs. the 1898 entry:** Teague says the line "was my own device; I began it when I came in 1905," yet quotes "the entry for 1898" and says the entries were "in the same hand for as long as the hand was mine." He did not join until 1905, so the 1898 entry cannot be in his hand or his invention. Bulletin 471 also says the gain stands "year by year" from 1897.
8. **Teague's percentage base:** the entry says "five and a quarter per cent," and Teague's prose says that was "of the fat that was actually in the cream" — but 3,040/60,800 = 5.0%; 5.25% is of the credited 57,760. Ivy explicitly states the ledger uses the credited base. Teague misstates his own figure.
9. **Keddie's dismissal date vs. board dates:** Keddie — put out 30 April 1925 ("the last day of the month"), with "no cause established" already found; Effie — board sat until 4 May 1925 and she recorded the award "on the last day." So the dismissal precedes the recorded award by four days.
10. **Vira's sequence of events is internally inconsistent** and conflicts with the others: she says the tests went up "one summer" and *then* the hearing came "the winter and spring after," yet also says they put Keddie out "and that is why the numbers went right the summer after." Per Keddie/Ivy the tests rose when the glass was condemned (June 1923) and stood at 3.80 in 1924, before the 1925 hearing; Keddie tested nothing in 1925 — so the rise cannot be a consequence of his removal.
11. **Vira's theory (cream theft) vs. Bulletin 471** ("no cream was stolen") vs. Teague ("cream is portable"; one pair of hands; he says nothing about any particular day) vs. Keddie ("never taken a pound of cream").
12. **Keddie's "thirty-one times a week" vs. Vira's "a thousand cans a week"** — different measures (tests vs. cans); not strictly contradictory but not reconciled.
13. **Nine families vs. seven farms:** Vira and Keddie speak of nine families/kitchens (station patrons; recipients of the circular); Effie of seven farms with rights on Hessel Bottom. Different sets, but note the numbers.
14. **Hazel Brant** appears only in Vira; Ivy names Roy Brant as Emmet's son and never mentions Hazel. Vira says the visitor was "of the family both sides" — a Keddie granddaughter (Ansel's) and a Brant relation (through Orra, née Brant) — consistent, but the exact Hazel link is unexplained.
15. **Who condemned the crate:** Effie — "the state's inspector," June 1923; Bulletin 471 — 12 June 1923; Ivy says the crate was carried up in June 1923. Merle Strawn is the state's dairy inspector "for this county from 1908," but is never explicitly identified as the condemner.
16. **The crate was never mentioned at the hearing** (Effie §8), though the award speaks of "the books and appliances of the station." Keddie's letter mentions the crate arriving in 1897 but not its condemnation in 1923; his letter does record the 1924 average at 3.80 without saying why.
17. **Keddie's death:** Ivy — 1934; Vira — "in the thirties."
18. **Vira's "nine ways" split of the award** vs. Effie's "seven farms" — again nine patrons vs. seven Bottom farms.
19. **"Mr. Vose" in Teague's 1916 remark:** by then Gideon was no longer president (ended 1911) and Selby was manager (from 1913); Teague names "Mr. Selby Vose" as manager, so the 1916 "Mr. Vose" is presumably Selby, but Teague does not specify. Vira's "old Mrs. Vose" is presumably Gideon's wife.
20. **Effie's "three explanations" argued before the board** are never listed by her ("they are in the minutes"). Candidates from other sources: wet hay (1908 committee), water in the cans (1919 circular), cream taken (Teague/Vira). Not stated.
21. **Ivy's "twenty-six-year anomaly"** = 1897–1923; her "twenty-five full delivery years" = 1898–1922; Keddie's "twenty-nine seasons" = 1896–1924; Effie's "twenty-five years over which the patrons laid their claim." These spans differ slightly by definition.
22. **Vira says Selby Vose was "down at Larrow Green"; Teague places the office (where Selby signed the sheets) at Ashlin.** Effie mentions an office move in 1938 without naming places.
23. **Odd non-mention:** Vira says she told the 1958 visitor about the letter in the drawer; Ivy's talk does not mention Vira, the letter, or the 1919 circular (beyond nothing) — Ivy addresses only wet hay, watering, and cream in the Bulletin paragraph.
