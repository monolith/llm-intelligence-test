## CAST (consolidated across all fifteen sources)

**The Keddie family**
- **Ansel Keddie** — tester at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station. Kept the test book in his own hand from the station's opening. Tested 29 seasons, 1896 through 1924; did not test in 1925. Dismissed ("put out") 30 April 1925. Died in the 1930s (Ivy: 1934). "An old man" with "no other trade" in 1925. Vira says he went up in '95 to get the station ready. Strawn (never names him), 1907: "keeps his book in his own hand, and his hand is better than most… works slow and clean and reads his own figure without being coaxed to it"; 1916: "the tester complains that his patrons blame him for their figures. I told him that is the trade and he said he knew it." Nym Item 8: his statement is "One leaf, ruled, both sides, in the hand of Item 3, signed"; the test book is "One hand throughout, twenty-nine seasons" — though Nym's Item 3 heading calls it "kept by E. Brant" and Item 4 (the Brant weigh book) "kept by A. Keddie" (transposed; Conflict 38). Selby Vose (1926) never names him nor mentions his dismissal. Roy Brant (1955): "Ansel Keddie, who kept the station's test book, was married into the family that had more to lose by that station than any other family on the meadow"; Emmet stopped speaking to him in 1919 "because my father held that the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not." Vaught (1958): "a man who did not know what he was recording and set a question mark against it." Rundle (1939) never names him: "the figures went right when the man who kept it did not." **Frick (1946):** "had tested at that station since it opened in 1896 and had kept the book in his own hand every season since"; made his statement to the board 17 March 1925; "I believed him then and I believe him now. His denial was accurate and it did him no good at all… a man who cannot account for a figure is presumed by everybody in the room to be the figure's author." **Frick's corridor remark (not in the minutes):** "In the corridor at Ashlin, Keddie told me privately that he had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the room. He was not a bitter man about it. He had lived among them thirty years and would not stand up and accuse his neighbors, and I record it here because it has been said since that he went to his grave accusing nobody, which is true, and that he suspected nobody, which is not." Frick: dismissed 30 April, "four days before we made our award… There was no finding against him from us and there never was one… He was simply let go while the board was still sitting, and I have never been able to think well of the Association for it"; "an honest man was ruined by a figure nobody could explain." Frick's exhibit 1: the test book "4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924, in the hand of Ansel Keddie." **Grigg (1919, unnamed):** "abuse the man who reads their figures for them." **Sill (1934) never names him;** his theory is water in the cans, not the tester.
- **Orra Keddie** — Ansel's wife; Emmet Brant's sister — Roy: "full brother and sister, same mother and same father, whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it." First cousin of "old Mrs. Vose" (Vira). After 1919 she and her brother never spoke; she walked to Larrow Green rather than pass his lane. Outlived Ansel "a good while."
- **Duncan Keddie** — Ansel and Orra's son ("boy"); taught school at Ashlin; Ivy's father; Roy Brant's first cousin (Roy).
- **Ivy Keddie** — Ansel's granddaughter; college at Farland; gave the 1959 talk "What the Crate Held." Nym: "Miss Ivy Keddie of the State Agricultural College at Farland." Rosalie Cudd addresses her as "Miss Keddie," "a graduate student at Farland," "a young woman from the college who asks [a plain question] in four lines and apologizes for troubling me." Found the crate February 1958 (Nym: Association office at Ashlin; Ivy: station loft); measured nine pipettes at Farland 9 March 1958; gave crate to the Rooms 1959; borrowed the Brant weigh book from Roy Brant in 1958 (Roy's postscript: "Duncan's girl Ivy came out to the place this year and asked whether she might borrow the book for a piece of work she is doing at the college at Farland. I let her have it. She is careful with paper") and returned it; found and copied Strawn's 1923 volume at Farland 1958; wrote to Rosalie Cudd 1958 and received the Alder Corners figures. Vira's 1958 visitor "from the college away east… Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed" is Ivy; **Roy Brant says Hazel and Ivy are second cousins; Rundle's 1959 footnote agrees: "Miss Ivy Keddie, who is second cousin to Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom"** (Conflict 20/47). Rundle 1959: "The Hollow matter has since been taken up again at the college at Farland by Miss Ivy Keddie."

**The Brant family (Hessel Bottom)**
- **Emmet Brant** — **born 1854, died 1929** (Roy). Biggest man on Hessel Bottom: most cows, most hay ground; largest patron every year (Effie); Roy: "Ours was the largest of the six — sixty-odd acres of the meadow, and thirty-eight cows in a good year, which for this valley was a herd." Orra Keddie's full brother. Roy: "From the first of January, 1898, until the week before he died, he wrote down every can of milk that went out of this barn" (Nym/Ivy: from 1 January 1897; **Frick's exhibit 3: "The private weigh book of Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom, begun 1 January 1897"** — Conflict 61). Wrote "at the kitchen table after the second milking, in the lamp, with his hat still on, and my mother would not have the table cleared till he was done." Among the first to say out loud (1902) that the milk tested low — "and they were laughed at, and one of them was my father." Read the 1908 report twice, put it in the drawer, "said that the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not." Noticed the 1911 rise and fall and entered both; "could make nothing of it." "One who never forgave" the 1919 paper. 1910 leaf: 200,000 lb even, "Paid on a test of 3.42"; he wrote "even" and underlined it, "which for him was carrying on." His weigh book was before the 1925 board (Frick).
- **Roy Brant** — Emmet's son; **sixty-seven in winter 1955 (born c. 1888), "still milking"**; wrote Retelling 09 in longhand at Hessel Bottom for the county farm bureau, postscript 1958. Kept up the weigh book since his father died, "in the same book until it was filled and then in another, in the same way." Cut the meadow "with a scythe and with a mower behind two horses." Did not haul to Alder Corners ("We had the biggest herd on the meadow, and the biggest herd is the one that cannot afford to be talked about, and I have thought since that this was a poor reason and cost us money"). Duncan Keddie's first cousin. Lent the book to Ivy 1958. "It goes to Hazel when I am done with it." Nym: book to Roy 1929; to Hazel 1962.
- **Hazel Brant** — **Roy's daughter** (Roy: "My daughter Hazel and his daughter Ivy are second cousins… both of them have had to have it explained to them by me more than once"). Vira: the 1958 visitor's "first cousin once removed." Nym: "Miss Hazel Brant" held the weigh book from 1962; received at the Rooms 1963. Rundle 1959: "Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom."

**The Vose family (Larrow Green)**
- **Gideon Vose** — first president 1889–1911 (22 years); organized the Association; drafted the by-laws (not a lawyer); died 1917. Selby: "My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law… kept a herd of Jerseys at the home place… In 1910 that herd tested 4.56." **Rundle:** elected first president 1889; "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the law and thought better of it, and he wrote the by-laws himself, at his own kitchen table, in a hand that the Association's clerks were still complaining about thirty years later." "His nephew Selby Vose… used to say that his uncle wrote by-laws the way other men build stone wall — slowly, and to last." "Gideon Vose put that [the 'station… shall govern' clause] in for convenience and it was convenient for thirty years." Frick 1946: "The by-laws provided for arbitration."
- **Selby Vose** — manager 1913–1938 (Effie). Signed and wrote the 1919 "water in the cans" circular. CONFLICT on relation: Effie "son of the late president"; Vira "old Gideon Vose's nephew"; Selby "My father"; **Rundle "His nephew Selby Vose, afterward manager of the Association."** Signed the annual station sheet every year; told Teague in 1916 to let the station account alone. Strawn 1913: "New manager… came out with me to the platform, which the last one never did"; 1919: told him no water ever found; 12 June 1923: "told the manager to order new." His 1926 address: thirteenth year; water-in-the-cans explanation that "stopped when the Association began to look"; dates the condemnation "the twelfth of July, 1923"; 1924 average 3.90; "I ask for one more" (served to 1938). Roy (1955): "In 1919 the manager sent a paper round to the Hollow patrons warning us against putting water in the milk." Rundle: "In 1919 the manager circulated a warning to the Hollow patrons against adding water to their milk." **Frick (1946):** the first of the three explanations, water, "was the manager's, Selby Vose. He had circulated a warning to those patrons on the subject some years before and he stood by it before us: that the Hollow's milk came in thinned, that a little water in a can is the oldest trick in the trade, and that a station cannot test what is not there. He put it without heat and without proof." **Sill (1934):** "the manager put out a paper to them about it — that would be nineteen nineteen — a circular, warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons about water in the milk, plain as that. I thought well of him for it. It was the only plain thing that office ever did, and of course they hated him for it up the Hollow, and of course it changed nothing, because a paper never emptied a can."
- **old Mrs. Vose** — Orra's first cousin (Vira).
- **"the last one"** — unnamed manager before 1913.

**Association officers**
- **Effie Loomis** — secretary 1901–1940; minute book in her hand; 14 in 1889; "Notes toward a history" 1941.
- **Wilbur Teague** — bookkeeper 1905–1945; invented the "station gain" line; memoir Chapter Nine, Ashlin, winter 1948. Rundle (1939) saw the station-gain entry "in the bookkeeper's hand" in the office ledgers. **Frick (1946):** the second explanation — "that the tester had been taking cream for himself and writing low tests to cover the loss. This was put by the bookkeeper, Wilbur Teague, in the way an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it — he was asked about the station's account and answered at length about how such an account would look if a man were helping himself, and then observed that he made no charge against anybody. There is no cross-examination that reaches a witness of that kind, and I did not attempt one."
- **Hobart Sill** — buttermaker at Larrow Green; churn and cream receiving books (Nym Item 16). **Sill's own account (Source 14):** came to Larrow Green in **1893** ("there was no floor in this room but plank, and we cooled with creek ice out of the pond"); stood at the churn **forty-one years**, retiring in **1934**, aged **sixty-four** (born c. 1870) — "he stood at the churn until the day they gave him a chair." Made "butter that took premiums in three states out of this room." Weighed the Hollow's cream over again himself when it came into the cold room; his hand "on every page" of the receiving book "from ninety-three." Lent his own glass outfit to the new station in May 1896. "I never went up there but twice in my life." Holds the water theory ("There was water in those cans and I said so at the time"); never blamed the cows ("The cows were never the trouble"). Estimates Larrow Green's own milk at 3.78 and the Hollow's "really a 3.85 and no better"; butterfat "twenty-seven cents the pound" through those years.
- **Alonzo Frick** — attorney of Ashlin; chaired the 1925 board. Teague: "as fair a chairman as ever a hearing had." Selby: "an able and impartial man." Rundle: "A board of arbitration sat at Ashlin that spring under Alonzo Frick, attorney, of that town. It heard everybody." **Frick's own account (Source 13):** **seventy-four in 1946** (born c. 1872); "tried titles and boundaries for forty years, and two homicides"; "A board of three was constituted and I was named its chairman, I have always supposed because I was the one of the three who kept no cow." Held sessions two and three at the station itself; "I am a lawyer and the thing is chemistry"; believed Keddie; "I have never been able to think well of the Association" for the dismissal.
- **Nyle Grigg** — of Larrow Green; head of the 1908 investigating committee (Roy: "In 1908 the Association appointed a committee, with Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green at the head of it"; **Rundle: "In 1908 the Association sent a committee up under Nyle Grigg"**). Nym Item 11: the fair copy is "signed by the chairman" (unnamed there). **Grigg's own account (Source 15):** one of **three** named by the board in the winter of 1907–08 ("we three were named to look into it. I have never in my life been given a duty I liked less"); "I am not a patron of that station. I haul to Larrow Green and always have… I was chosen for the work on that account and for no other, and I have been abused for the finding by men on both sides of it"; "I had known the men of that bottom since I was a young man and I have bought hay off two of them." Signs "Respectfully, NYLE GRIGG, Larrow Green" (1919) and "NYLE GRIGG, chairman, and the committee" (1908). Frick 1925: "Somebody read the report of the 1908 committee into the record."
- **The other two committee members (1908)** — unnamed (Grigg: "we three").
- **The other two arbitrators (1925)** — unnamed; both, by Frick's implication, kept cows.

## PLACES
- **Larrow Valley**; **Larrow Green ("the Green")** — hamlet; Association organized 1889 (Rundle: in the schoolhouse); main creamery built 1891 on Larrow Creek (Rundle: "on the creek below the mill"; the churn set up in it). Strawn inspected 1913, 1917, 1931, 1936. Nym Item 16: Larrow Green average "a little under two tenths of a point better" than the station's 3.61. Roy: the Hollow milk ran "seventeen hundredths of a point under what the same class of milk was making down at Larrow Green." **Sill: Larrow Green's own patrons, "the men on this creek," ran 3.78 "taking one year with another."** Sill's churn room: plank floor in 1893, cooled with creek ice out of the pond; the platform where the Hollow's cream was set off, weighed and sampled; the cold room; Sill's "own bench in this room." Nyle Grigg was "of Larrow Green," hauled there always. Dell Rundle taught the school there; the pamphlet was "Printed at Larrow Green"; the store where the history was told. Grigg: "every crossroads store between here and Ashlin."
- **Ostrey Hollow ("the Hollow")** — seven miles up the valley (Rundle: "seven miles up from the Green, and in the nineties the pull down was three hours in mud and could not be done at all in March"; Sill: "seven miles of hill road, and in July it came down warm no matter how early they started"); station at the head of the Hollow where the road bends to the ridge; low whitewashed building (Rundle: "a plain one, boarded and battened, with the intake room at the road end and a loft over it that I believe has never been cleared out since it was put up; and behind it the creek, which in a wet April comes up to the sill and has twice come over it"); intake room, loft over it (ladder), wash room, cold room, boiler, separator; **the weigh room** (Frick: sessions two and three of the 1925 board were held "at Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's own weigh room"); opened 1896 (Rundle; Sill: "ninety-six, in May, and there was a crowd went up for it"); Frick: "milk weighed in, a sample drawn for each patron, the cream sent down the valley and the skim milk handed back"; shut by 1958 (fence wire and salt). Strawn 1909: takes from the wet bottom below and three hill places. Vaught: "one skimming station in Ordell County… below those of its own creamery seven miles down the same valley." Rundle: "the head of the valley."
- **Hessel Bottom** — Roy: "a hundred and sixty acres of wet meadow lying under the hill at Ostrey Hollow. **Six farms** had rights on it and cut hay off it and pastured it" (Effie: seven farms; **Grigg's 1908 report: "Hessel Bottom, where six farms cut hay and turn out stock, is a wet meadow lying along the creek under Ostrey Hollow. Its ground is springy the year round and is under water in part every spring. Its herbage is sedge, rush, and the coarse sour grasses that come in on such land, and its hay is cut late and heavy and is fed as the main dependence of those farms from November to April. There is no better winter keep to be had there"**). Brant's sixty-odd acres of it. "The water stands on the low end into May, and there is a soft place by the alders that has taken a horse to the hock in my time and my father's. The hay off it is coarse, and it cuts late, and a cow will eat it and do well enough… it never failed to grow something." Jerome Cudd: "Good milk off wet ground." Frick: "the bog meadow at Hessel Bottom… the wet ground, the coarse hay off it, the cows that eat it all winter." Grigg's remedy: "Drain that bottom, sweeten it, keep the stock off it in the wet of the spring, and feed the coarse hay off it with a mixed ration and not by itself."
- **Ashlin** — Association office (over a harness shop); board of arbitration 1925 ("sat at Ashlin that spring" — Rundle; Frick: first session "at the Association's office at Ashlin on the seventeenth of March, 1925"; the corridor at Ashlin where Keddie spoke to Frick); Duncan Keddie taught school there; Historical Rooms/Society; Nym: crate found there Feb 1958; Alonzo Frick "attorney, of that town"; before 1889 the farmers hauled butter to the merchant there (Rundle).
- **Ordell County / Ordell County Historical Rooms / the Ordell circuit** (four counties, fifty-odd plants); **the Ordell County Bar Association** and its yearbook (Frick 1946).
- **Redlow Ridge** — Rosalie: "Four miles of switchback, cut for a lumber road and never much improved since. It shuts in November and it does not open before April, some years May… the drifts sit in the cut below the top." Cloud "sits on the top of the ridge till the sun is well up." Rundle: "over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County"; "over the mountain."
- **Alder Corners, Vessey County** — the Alder Corners Cheese Company, built 1913 (Rosalie) / opened 1914 (Roy; Rundle), "on the flat below the church"; spring water on the lot; hard road out to the east; make room; "little room off the intake" (Jerome's test bench); platform with scale. Its book never left Vessey County (Nym); to the Vessey County Historical Society 1970.
- **Farland** — the State Agricultural College (Ivy's; Vaught's; Strawn's notebooks in its library from 1947; pipettes measured there; Bulletin No. 214 issued there — Grigg: "a College that had never heard of Hessel Bottom"). Vaught calls it "This station." Rundle 1959: "the college at Farland… I refer the reader to the college."
- **Tarnet** — Ammon & Sons.
- **"the home place"** — Vose Jerseys.
- **"the creameries below us" / "the trade below us"** — Selby's phrases.
- **"the two ends of this valley"** — Rundle's phrase: the Green and the Hollow, which "did not speak civilly to one another for six years" after 1919.
- **"the man over the hill"** — Frick's phrase for the better-paid neighbor.

## SOURCE 9 — Roy Brant, account for the county farm bureau's local-history collection (Hessel Bottom, winter 1955; postscript 1958)
*"Set down in longhand at Hessel Bottom in the winter of 1955 by Roy Brant, sixty-seven years old and still milking, with a postscript he added three years afterward."*
- "The bureau asked me for something on the old days at the head of the valley. I would rather write about my father's book… the book is on the shelf behind my chair, and a man does better to talk about what he can put his hand on."
- "My father was Emmet Brant. He was born in 1854 and he died in 1929. From the first of January, 1898, until the week before he died, he wrote down every can of milk that went out of this barn, and I have kept it up since, in the same book until it was filled and then in another, in the same way, because he showed me how and because I never found a reason to stop."
- **The Bottom**: 160 acres wet meadow under the hill at Ostrey Hollow; **six farms** had rights, cut hay and pastured it; Brant's the largest — sixty-odd acres of meadow, thirty-eight cows in a good year. Water stands on the low end into May; soft place by the alders; hay coarse, cuts late; "whatever has been said about that hay by men who never fed it."
- **The book**: "a ledger, a plain one, ruled by hand. Two columns for each day of delivery. In the first column my father entered the weight of every can as he took it off his own scale in his own barn before the wagon went down the road. In the second he entered the figure the station gave him back for that same milk. That is all it is. There is no comment in it anywhere but three or four lines in thirty years, and no opinion at all. The cover is gone off the front, the back is held by a strip of harness leather my father tacked on himself, and the first forty leaves are stained brown at one corner where it lay in a leaky window a winter he was sick."

Two scales, his and the station's, kept by two men who by the end were not speaking to one another, and the scales never fell out at all. Whatever was wrong at that station — and something was wrong at that station for a very long time — it was not the weighing."

Seventeen hundredths is nothing to look at on one pay slip. Set the slips in a row for a lifetime of farming and it is a farm." First said out loud 1902, laughed at, father among them. "it was seventeen hundredths in July and it was seventeen hundredths in February. It did not come and go with the season. It did not care whether the cows were on the meadow or on hay in the barn."
- **1908**: committee "with Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green at the head of it," sampled through the winter; wet-hay report; father: "the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not."

Through those nine weeks our tests came up, all of us, every farm on the Bottom; and then the station's own came back into use and the figures went down again… My father noticed the rise and he noticed the fall and he entered both, and he could make nothing of it, and I could make nothing of it either, and there it sat for near on fifty years."
- **1919**: "the manager sent a paper round to the Hollow patrons warning us against putting water in the milk. Nine families at the head of the valley got that paper and nine families read it for what it was. My father was one who never forgave it. He and Ansel Keddie were brothers-in-law, and from 1919 they did not speak — not from any quarrel over the water, but because my father held that the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not."
- **Alder Corners**: "From 1915, four of the Bottom men hauled their milk over Redlow Ridge to the cheese factory in the summer flush and back to the Hollow the rest of the year. The factory over there had opened in 1914. They went on that way for sixteen seasons. It was for the price and the flush market and a shorter haul while the road was open, and it was talked about here as though they had gone over to the enemy, which shows you what a valley is. - **Postscript, 1958**: "Duncan's girl Ivy came out to the place this year and asked whether she might borrow the book for a piece of work she is doing at the college at Farland. I let her have it. She is careful with paper. It comes back to me when she is through, and after me it is Hazel's."

## SOURCE 11 — Leland Vaught, introduction and method to Bulletin No. 471 (State Agricultural College at Farland, October 1958)
*"The opening and method sections of the bulletin, written for extension agents and county workers, closing with the bulletin's own concluding paragraph reproduced entire."* Signed "— L. VAUGHT."
- **§I Why written**: "This station has been asked, at intervals for fifty years, why the butterfat tests at one skimming station in Ordell County ran below those of its own creamery seven miles down the same valley… answered at various times by reference to the land and by reference to the men. It is answered here by reference to a piece of glass." "Nobody in that valley lacked the intelligence to find it. What they lacked was any occasion to suspect the one article in the plant that nobody in the trade is in the habit of suspecting." Bold: **"the milk pipette is the one instrument in the butterfat test which the plant cannot check against anything else, and it is the instrument on which every figure in the plant depends."**
- **§II The instrument**: four operations — measured charge into a test bottle; sulfuric acid; spun in a hand tester; fat read directly in percent in the graduated neck. "The operator weighs nothing." Standard charge **17.6 c.c.** Short pipette → reading "low in the exact proportion by which the charge was short." Three consequences: **proportional not additive** (rich milk loses more; ranking of herds undisturbed; "Only against the outside world is it wrong"); **precise** ("A pipette that delivers 16.27 c.c. where it is blown 17.6 delivers 16.27 c.c. every time, for a quarter of a century, in January and in July"); **invisible from inside the plant** (scales checked with weights, tester's speed counted, acid checked, bottle graduation checked with mercury, but "the pipette is the standard… It can be caught only by a burette brought in from outside, and burettes reached the state's field men very late in this history").
- **§III Material**: "Of the lot bought for the Ostrey Hollow station in 1897 — **five dozen** pipettes were purchased — **nine** were recovered from the station's condemned crate, unbroken and legible… stems are blown 17.6 c.c… five runs to each pipette, and the mean delivery of the nine is **16.27 c.c.**" "The station's own glass went into use on **the first of May, 1898**, and remained in use until the state inspector condemned the lot in June of 1923." Method: water at room temperature, tip wiped, delivered in working manner into a certified burette, five deliveries averaged; "occupies an afternoon"; "no reason why every station in this state should not be checked once in this way."
- **§IV 1911**: nine weeks; "One variable changed"; 3.61 → 3.80 and back "on the day the station's own measure came back into service"; recorded "by a man who did not know what he was recording and set a question mark against it." "an experiment… that ran itself… and lay unread for forty-seven years."
- **§V Magnitude**: 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = **0.9244**; 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = **3.905**, shortfall **0.295**; on 40,000,000 lb over twenty-five years, **118,000 lb** "delivered and not credited." "It should be said that this figure is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the glassware." Station gain "five and a quarter percent of the butterfat actually delivered."
- **§VI Feed**: Bulletin No. 214 quoted (identical); "Five hundredths in winter; nothing at all in summer. The discrepancy… is present in every month of the year and is some six times the largest effect the trial could produce."
- **§VII Conclusion (quoted)**: *"No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."*

## SOURCE 12 — Dell Rundle, schoolteacher, "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years," a jubilee pamphlet (1939; reprinted 1959)
*"Printed at Larrow Green for the Association's fiftieth year, compiled from the recollections of old members and two winters of asking questions at the store; reissued in 1959 with one footnote and the author's closing note unaltered."* Sections: "TO THE MEMBERS"; I. THE MEN OF 1889; II. THE RULE THAT MADE THE TEST THE MASTER; III. THE STATION AT OSTREY HOLLOW; IV. THE TROUBLE AT THE HEAD OF THE VALLEY; V. OVER THE RIDGE (with footnote [^1] added 1959); VI. FIFTY YEARS; "NOTE ADDED WHILE IN PRESS," signed "— D. RUNDLE, Larrow Green."
- **To the members**: "Fifty years is not long for a country, but it is a long time for a valley to keep one thing going, and that is what this pamphlet is about. I have taught the school at the Green a good many years now and I have listened to this history in pieces, at funerals and in the back of the store, for the whole of them. What follows is not a record. It is what the valley remembers, tidied, and where the valley is certain of a thing I have written it down as certain."
- **I. The men of 1889**: "In 1889 the farmers of this valley met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. They were tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking whatever the merchant chose to allow them for it, and they had worked out — the way farmers do, slowly and then all at once — that the way to be paid properly for a thing is to own the place that buys it." "They met in the schoolhouse, in cold weather, and the older men who were there remembered afterward chiefly that it went on very late and that somebody's horse got loose in the middle of it." "Gideon Vose was elected the first president. He was a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the law and thought better of it, and he wrote the by-laws himself, at his own kitchen table, in a hand that the Association's clerks were still complaining about thirty years later. His nephew Selby Vose, afterward manager of the Association, used to say that his uncle wrote by-laws the way other men build stone wall — slowly, and to last." "In 1891 the creamery was built at Larrow Green, on the creek below the mill, and the churn was set up in it, and the valley began to be paid in cash once a month, which had never happened to it before. There are men still living who can tell you what the first check did in their mother's kitchen, and they will tell you whether you ask or not."
- **II. The rule**: "Of all Gideon Vose's by-laws, one has done more to this valley than the rest together, and I print it whole:" — **Article VII quoted, identical to Effie and Nym**. "**Article VII dates from 1891, the year the churn was set up.** It was thought a hard rule at the time and there were men who left the Association over it. It is a just rule. A man who keeps good cows and feeds them ought not to be paid the same as the man who keeps poor ones, and the pound of milk is a liar where the pound of butterfat is not." "But mark the last clause, because the valley has had fifty years to learn what it means. *The test of the station where he delivers shall govern.* Not the test of the creamery. Not any test but the one made at his own door. Gideon Vose put that in for convenience and it was convenient for thirty years, and it is the sentence on which the whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned."
- **III. The station**: "The Hollow is seven miles up from the Green, and in the nineties the pull down was three hours in mud and could not be done at all in March. So the Association built a skimming station up there, and it opened in 1896: the milk was weighed and tested and separated on the spot, the cream came down to the Green by wagon, and the skim went home to the calves and the pigs the same morning. It was a great convenience and the Hollow was glad of it. The building is a plain one, boarded and battened, with the intake room at the road end and a loft over it that I believe has never been cleared out since it was put up; and behind it the creek, which in a wet April comes up to the sill and has twice come over it."
- **IV. The trouble**: "I would rather not write this chapter and it cannot be left out." "From 1902 the men of the Hollow complained that their milk tested lower at their own station than the same class of milk tested at the Green. They were not believed. In 1908 the Association sent a committee up under Nyle Grigg, which sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through the winter and reported that the wet hay of that bottom thinned the cream, and that the remedy lay with the land. The Hollow did not accept it. In 1919 the manager circulated a warning to the Hollow patrons against adding water to their milk, and after that the two ends of this valley did not speak civilly to one another for six years. I have had the children of both ends in one schoolroom through those years and I can tell you that it came down to them entire, without a word of it being explained, the way a debt does." **"It is the settled opinion of the valley that cream left that station by the back door, and the figures went right when the man who kept it did not."** "I set that down as I have heard it set down a hundred times, by men who were there and men who were not, and I have never heard it contradicted at the store." "The state inspector condemned the station's glassware on the twelfth of July, 1923, for reasons of his own that had nothing to do with the quarrel, and new glass was put in. In January of 1925 the Hollow patrons petitioned the Association. **The station took in, over the years complained of, forty-four million pounds of milk. The patrons' loss upon it was nineteen thousand six hundred dollars, and their petition set the figure out to the dollar.** A board of arbitration sat at Ashlin that spring under Alonzo Frick, attorney, of that town. It heard everybody. It found nothing established, it made an award to the patrons without admitting anything, and it sent this valley home to live with itself, which the valley has done, after a fashion, ever since." (No award sum; no mention of Keddie's dismissal, the 1911 episode, or the crate.)
- **V. Over the ridge**: "I should mention, because it belongs to the history and because the young people no longer know it, that in 1914 the Alder Corners Cheese Company was opened over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County, by Jerome Cudd, and that in the summers a few of our Hessel Bottom men hauled over the mountain to him rather than down to their own station. That was resented here. It is Jerome Cudd's daughter, Rosalie Cudd, who makes the cheese at Alder Corners now.[^1]"
- **Footnote [^1] (quoted; "Added to the 1959 reprint")**: *"The Hollow matter has since been taken up again at the college at Farland by Miss Ivy Keddie, who is second cousin to Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom. Eleven pipettes out of the station's old condemned crate were found still to be in existence. I am too old to rewrite a pamphlet and I refer the reader to the college. — D.R."*
- **VI. Fifty years**: "Set against the quarrel, put the rest: fifty Junes of cream coming down that road in the early cool, fifty Januaries of it going down over ice with the horses roughshod, a check every month in kitchens that had known none, and a creamery still standing that was built by men with nothing but each other and a piece of paper they had written themselves. Whatever this valley did wrong at the head of it, it did this right, and it did it for half a century, and the men of 1889 would think we had done well."

## SOURCE 13 — Alonzo Frick, "Recollections of the Ordell Arbitration," article for the yearbook of the Ordell County Bar Association (1946)
*"Recollections of the Ostrey Hollow arbitration by the attorney who sat as chairman of the board, with the exhibit list reprinted from his own file."* Headed **RECOLLECTIONS OF THE ORDELL ARBITRATION**.
- "Our editor has asked the older men of this bar for something out of their own practice, and at seventy-four I take him to mean me. I have tried titles and boundaries for forty years, and two homicides, and none of them has stayed with me as the Ostrey Hollow matter has. It was not litigation at all. It was an arbitration under the by-laws of a creamery association, with no appeal from it, no reported decision, and nothing whatever to cite it for; and I have thought about it oftener than about anything I ever argued in a courthouse."
- **The complaint.** "The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association pays a patron for the butter fat in his milk and not for the weight of it. The fat is found by test, and the test of the station where the man delivers is the test that governs him. I say nothing against the rule. But it makes the test the whole of the bargain, and it leaves a dissatisfied man with no remedy on earth except to quarrel with a figure." "At Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green, the Association kept a skimming station: milk weighed in, a sample drawn for each patron, the cream sent down the valley and the skim milk handed back. The patrons of that station had been paid, year upon year, on an average test of **3.16**, and the milk of the rest of the valley tested better than theirs. Three and sixteen is not a scandalous figure standing by itself. It becomes one when the man over the hill is paid on a better figure for milk out of cows no different from yours; when the difference holds steady for a quarter of a century; and when nobody in the office can tell you the cause of it."
- **The petition and the board.** "In January of 1925 the patrons of the station petitioned the Association. The by-laws provided for arbitration, and the officers, whatever else may be said of them, did not resist it. A board of three was constituted and I was named its chairman, I have always supposed because I was the one of the three who kept no cow." "We sat three times. The first session was at the Association's office at Ashlin on the seventeenth of March, 1925. The second and the third I asked to have at Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's own weigh room, so that the board might see the plant it was to pass upon instead of hearing it described by men with an interest in the description. That was my doing and I am still content with it. Every physical appliance of that station was before us and we satisfied ourselves upon it. We saw the scales tried. We saw the sampling done and the test made, and I will say plainly that I did not understand the test then and do not understand it now; I am a lawyer and the thing is chemistry. But I could see that it was done the same way twice, and that the men who did it were not hiding their hands."
- **The three explanations** — "it is worth setting down who put each of them, because the three did not sit easily together and nobody seemed to mind that they did not." **First, water — the manager's, Selby Vose** (quoted under Selby above; "He put it without heat and without proof"). **Second, "that the tester had been taking cream for himself and writing low tests to cover the loss" — the bookkeeper, Wilbur Teague,** obliquely (quoted under Teague above). **Third, the hay:** "Somebody read the report of the 1908 committee into the record, and the bog meadow at Hessel Bottom was before us for the better part of an afternoon — the wet ground, the coarse hay off it, the cows that eat it all winter. It is an old opinion in that valley and it had the advantage of blaming nobody in the room."
- **Keddie.** "Ansel Keddie had tested at that station since it opened in 1896 and had kept the book in his own hand every season since. He made a statement to the board on the seventeenth of March. He said he had tested as he was taught and as the state man had seen him do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, that he could not tell us why the figures were what they were, and that he had never taken a pound of cream out of that station. I believed him then and I believe him now. His denial was accurate and it did him no good at all, and the reason it did him no good is that a man who cannot account for a figure is presumed by everybody in the room to be the figure's author." Then the corridor passage ("I add one thing which is not in the minutes…" — quoted in full under Keddie in the cast).
- **The dismissal and the award.** "On the thirtieth of April, four days before we made our award, the Association dismissed him. There was no finding against him from us and there never was one; the board had not concluded and did not conclude that he had done anything whatever. He was simply let go while the board was still sitting, and I have never been able to think well of the Association for it." "On the fourth of May, 1925, the board found no cause established and awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons thirty-eight hundred dollars, without admission, to be charged to the station account. **The patrons' own reckoning of what they had lost was sixty-seven thousand pounds of butter fat over the years complained of, and nothing was ever brought forward to disturb that figure.** We were not able to say who owed it or why, and we said so. An award without a finding is an unsatisfactory thing and every member of that board knew it."

## SOURCE 14 — Hobart Sill, buttermaker (ret.), remarks recorded at the Larrow Green creamery on the day he left it (1934)
*"Taken down as spoken by a man who made the Association's butter for forty-one years and had no patience with anybody who did not."*
- "Forty-one years I have stood at that churn. Eighteen ninety-three I came here, and there was no floor in this room but plank, and we cooled with creek ice out of the pond. You can write that down. Forty-one years and I never sent out a tub of butter I would not eat myself, which is more than the office can say about anything it ever sent out."
- "You want to know about the Hollow. Everybody wants to know about the Hollow. I will tell you what I know, which is what came off that wagon, because that is what I handled."
- **The cream**: "The cream wagon came down from Ostrey Hollow every day of the week but Sunday, seven miles of hill road, and in July it came down warm no matter how early they started. It was set off at the platform and it was weighed and it was sampled and it went into the cold room, and I took it from there. Now here is the thing about that cream, and I have said it to men in this room a hundred times and none of them ever thought it was worth anything. **That cream ran heavy. Every year it ran heavy. There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there.** The office would send down its sheet and the sheet would say so many pounds of fat bought at the station, and the fat that came into this room off that wagon was more than the sheet said. Always more. Never less."
- **His receiving book**: "And I weighed it myself, most years. The platform man weighed it and I weighed it over again when it came into the cold room, because I have never in my life taken another man's figure for what was going into my churn. Cans off, cans on the scale, cans into the ice, and the book ruled and signed before I put my coat on. You may go and look at the receiving book if they will let you at it, and you will find my hand on every page of it from ninety-three, and you will find the Hollow's cream coming in heavy on page after page after page, in my own writing, for as long as I have been in this room."
- **His explanation of the heavy cream**: "Well, that is cream for you. Cream is not milk and it will not stand still and be counted. It is a thick thing and it clings and it separates on you and any man who tells you he can put a figure on cream to the pound and hold it is a man who has never had his arm in a can up to the elbow. So it ran heavy. So does everybody's. I never made anything of it, and I will say plain, nobody ever asked me to."
- **Figures**: "Our own milk, here at Larrow Green, our own patrons, the men on this creek — that ran **3.78** taking one year with another right through those years. Three seventy-eight. I know it because I made the butter out of it and a buttermaker knows what his cream is worth before anybody hands him a paper about it. Ordinary milk, ordinary county, ordinary cows. There is nothing wonderful about 3.78 and there is nothing shameful about it either." "Now the Hollow. **The Hollow milk was really a 3.85 and no better**, and I will say that to any man in this valley. Wet ground up there, but good cows, and I never had a word to say against the cows. A 3.85 and no better, and if the figures they were paid on came out under that, then somebody up that hill road was helping the cans along, and I said so at the time and I say it now."
- **Water**: "You will get it told to you a dozen ways. You have been in the office, I expect, and they have their ways of putting it. I put it the one way. You cannot tell me **nine families** all got honest in one summer. There was water in those cans and I said so at the time. Because I will tell you what happened — one summer, along in the twenties, those figures came up and they stayed up, and nothing on this earth changed but the season, and men do not suddenly start pouring better milk out of the same cows in the same barns. Something got stopped. Something got said to somebody. I never knew what and I never asked, because the day a buttermaker starts asking about other men's cans is the day he stops making butter."
- **1902 and 1919**: "They had been hollering up there a long while before that, mind. Away back. Nineteen two is the first I remember of it, a man in here shouting that his milk tested under his brother-in-law's down the creek and it was the same feed and the same water and what did we mean by it. And the manager put out a paper to them about it — that would be nineteen nineteen — a circular, warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons about water in the milk, plain as that. I thought well of him for it. It was the only plain thing that office ever did, and of course they hated him for it up the Hollow, and of course it changed nothing, because a paper never emptied a can."
- **The office**: "That is the offices for you. Papers. The Association has a paper for everything and butter for nothing. I have had men come in here in a collar and tell me my churn was running slow when they could not have told you which end of a cow gets up first. I made butter that took premiums in three states out of this room, and not one man in that office ever came down here to see how it was done, and every one of them could tell you to the half cent what the fat was worth. **Twenty-seven cents.** That is what fat was through those years, take one year with another, twenty-seven cents the pound, and there is not a man in the office could tell you what makes it churn."
- **The 1896 glass**: "When they opened that station up at Ostrey Hollow — that was ninety-six, in May, and there was a crowd went up for it — they had no outfit of their own. Not a piece. It was ordered and it was not come, and they were ready to start taking milk, and there was talk of holding off the whole season. And I said no, they can have mine. I went to my own bench in this room and I made up an outfit for them out of my own glass, my own, that I had used and knew, and it went up the valley on the wagon and they opened on it. Their own came the spring after and they sent mine back to me, and one piece of it was chipped and I never said a word about that either."
- **1896 intake**: "That first season they took in about **nine hundred thousand pounds** up there. May to the end of the year, that is, so it was not even a whole one. That is a good deal of milk for a hill station starting from nothing, and it was the biggest thing that ever happened to Ostrey Hollow, and I was glad enough to have had a hand in it."
- "Do I think about it? I am sixty-four years old and I have thought about butter. I never went up there but twice in my life. I took their cream off the platform and I made it into butter and the butter was good — as good as any, and better than most, and there was never a season it was not. Whatever the Hollow was doing with its cans, and I have told you what I think about that, the fat came down that hill and it came into my churn and it went out of this room as good butter with this creamery's name on the tub."
- Closing: "Now that is forty-one years and I would like to go home. Put in about the butter. Do not put in that I said anything against the cows up there, because I did not. The cows were never the trouble. Put in that Hobart Sill made the butter and it was good butter and he stood at the churn until the day they gave him a chair."

## SOURCE 15 — Nyle Grigg, covering letter of 1919 to the reprinted report of the investigating committee of 1908
*"A farmer of standing sends his eleven-year-old report back into circulation because the same complaint has been raised again, and adds a letter saying that the intervening science has proved him right."*
- **COVERING LETTER**, "Larrow Green, 1919. To the officers and patrons of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gentlemen: I am asked to let the report of our committee be printed again, the Ostrey Hollow question having been reopened this season by men who were boys when we made it. I consent willingly and I have nothing in it to take back. I ask leave only to add a page, because a report is a dry thing and does not say why it was written, and because eleven years have brought us some light from a quarter that had none to give us in 1908."
- "You will remember the state of things. From about 1902 there had been grumbling out of Ostrey Hollow that the milk of that station tested under the milk of the rest of this valley. It was not one man and it was not one season. It went on and it grew, and it got into the talk at the annual meeting and into the talk at every crossroads store between here and Ashlin, and it took the shape such things always take, which is that somebody must be doing it on purpose. By the winter in which we were named, the board could not let it alone any longer without seeming to have an interest in letting it alone, and we three were named to look into it. I have never in my life been given a duty I liked less. **Payment by test came in with the 1893 meeting, as I have always understood it**, and from that year a difference of this kind stopped being a matter of opinion and became a matter of money. I had known the men of that bottom since I was a young man and I have bought hay off two of them."
- "We went at it as plain farmers do. **We sampled those herds through the whole of 1908 — every month of that year, in the barn and on the pasture, out of the night's milk and the morning's, and we did not let a man know which day we were coming.** We weighed. We watched the test made and we watched it made again. And what we found, we put in the report, and the report is below and I will not repeat it here."
- "I will add, since a man who prints a thing twice ought to say where he stands in it, that I had no interest whatever in the answer. I am not a patron of that station. I haul to Larrow Green and always have, and whatever the Hollow was paid or not paid never put a cent into my pocket nor took one out of it. I was chosen for the work on that account and for no other, and I have been abused for the finding by men on both sides of it, which I take to be a fair sign that the finding was an honest one."
- **Bulletin 214**: "When we wrote, we had nothing to go on but our own eyes and the general opinion of practical men, and I knew that a committee of farmers saying that wet hay thins the cream would be told that farmers will say anything about their own land. But the State Agricultural College at Farland has since published its Bulletin No. 214, which was a feeding trial upon bog hay carried out under the College's own eye, with weighed rations and no interest in the outcome. **That trial found the bog hay to depress the butter fat test by two tenths in the winter feeding. Two tenths, gentlemen**, out of a College that had never heard of Hessel Bottom and had no reason on earth to flatter us. There is our finding, arrived at by another road and by better men, three years after we set our names to it."
- "I am content to let the matter rest where the College and this committee have jointly put it. The remedy lies with the land and not with the station. Drain that bottom, sweeten it, keep the stock off it in the wet of the spring, and feed the coarse hay off it with a mixed ration and not by itself, and the Hollow will test with the rest of us. Go on as they are going and abuse the man who reads their figures for them, and they will be grumbling in twenty years' time as they were grumbling when we began, and no better off. Respectfully, NYLE GRIGG, Larrow Green."
- **THE REPORT AS PRINTED IN 1908** (quoted in full): *"REPORT OF THE COMMITTEE APPOINTED TO INQUIRE INTO THE COMPLAINTS OF THE PATRONS OF THE OSTREY HOLLOW STATION. To the President and Directors of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Your committee, appointed in January last, respectfully reports as follows. The complaint before us is of some years' standing and is in substance this: that the milk delivered at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station is returned by the test at a figure below that of milk delivered elsewhere in this valley, and that the patrons of that station are in consequence paid less for the same labor and the same cows than their neighbors are paid. Since payment by test came in among us and the pound of butter fat took the place of the pound of milk as the measure of a man's month, a difference of this kind is no longer a matter of opinion. It is a matter of money, and it comes out of the same pocket every month, and your committee has approached it in that light and not as a quarrel. Hessel Bottom, where six farms cut hay and turn out stock, is a wet meadow lying along the creek under Ostrey Hollow. Its ground is springy the year round and is under water in part every spring. Its herbage is sedge, rush, and the coarse sour grasses that come in on such land, and its hay is cut late and heavy and is fed as the main dependence of those farms from November to April. There is no better winter keep to be had there. We say this without reproach to any man on that bottom; the land is what it is, and they took it as their fathers left it to them. Your committee visited each of the six farms, some of them three and four times. We drew our samples ourselves and we sealed them ourselves. We saw the herds milked, we saw the cans filled, and we saw them hauled to the station and weighed in at the intake, and we stood by while the test was made and saw nothing whatever in the doing of it to remark upon. We tried the station scales against a known weight and we tried the patrons' own scales against the station's. We inquired into the water supply at each barn and satisfied ourselves upon it. Your committee further observed the general condition of the stock upon that bottom, as men who have kept cattle all their lives are entitled to observe it. The herds are thin in the flank at the end of the winter feeding and slow to come to their flesh in the spring. Their coats are rough later in the season than the coats of hill herds. They do well enough in flow, for the wet land is generous of milk, but it is our opinion, formed on the ground and not out of a book, that what such land gives in quantity it withholds in quality, and that a cow cannot put into her pail what her keep has never put into her. We report that we found no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter, and we wish that finding to be printed in the same type as the rest. We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March. The milk is whole and the scales are honest. It is our opinion that the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station. NYLE GRIGG, chairman, and the committee."*
- Closing line: "That report was received and accepted, and the question was closed by it for eleven years — until this season, and the men who have reopened it have brought nothing new before us but their impatience."

Frick and Rundle both say the patrons themselves set a figure in 1925, but in different units and amounts.
73. **Duration of the valley's silence**: Rundle six years; Vira/Roy/Keddie never again.
74. **Rosalie "now" in 1939** — as before.
75. **Rundle's retraction** — as before; Sill (1934), five years earlier, had the same station-gain fact from the cold-room side and drew no conclusion from it.
76. **Rundle's Article VII authorship and motive** — as before; Frick: "I say nothing against the rule. But it makes the test the whole of the bargain."
77. **Rundle's geography/logistics** — as before.
78. **Rundle's dating vs other documents** — as before.
79. **"Fifty years"** — as before.
80. **Board composition and venue**: Frick — three arbitrators, Frick the only one who "kept no cow"; three sessions — Ashlin 17 March, then two at the station's weigh room; Effie — "17 March to 4 May 1925," "having had before it the books and appliances of the station"; Nym Item 7 — minute book 17 March–4 May; Rundle — "sat at Ashlin." Nobody else mentions sessions at the station.
81. **Keddie's private suspicion** (Frick's corridor passage, "not in the minutes") vs Keddie's own statement and unsent letter, which accuse nobody and say "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are"; Frick himself: "it has been said since that he went to his grave accusing nobody, which is true, and that he suspected nobody, which is not." Only Frick reports this.
82. **Frick's exhibit list ("Four books and nothing else")**: test book; **"the receiving book of the said station"** (a station receiving book — an object no other source names; distinct from Sill's Larrow Green receiving book, Nym Item 16); Brant weigh book; Strawn's 1923 notebook. No crate, no invoice, no 1908 report as an exhibit (though "somebody read the report… into the record"), no ledgers/station-gain line (though Teague "was asked about the station's account"), no Larrow Green receiving/churn books (Sill's "heavy" cream was not before the board). Effie: "the crate — never mentioned in the hearing at all."
83. **Sill's tenure**: came 1893, forty-one years, retired 1934, aged 64; Effie names him buttermaker without dates; creamery built 1891 — who made butter 1891–93 is unstated. Nym Item 16 (Larrow Green receiving and churn books, water-damaged) = Sill's books, "my hand on every page of it from ninety-three."
84. **Sill's "heavy" cream from 1893**: he says the Hollow's cream came in heavy "for as long as I have been in this room" and his hand is in the book "from ninety-three" — but the station only opened in May 1896.
85. **Sill's 1902 anecdote** — "a man in here shouting that his milk tested under his brother-in-law's down the creek… the same feed and the same water" (Roy: the 1902 complainants included Emmet Brant; the story's known brothers-in-law are Brant and Keddie, but Keddie was the tester, not a patron "down the creek" — unresolved).
86. **Sill's own outfit and the chipped piece** (1896–97) vs Nym Item 5: two of the nine surviving 1897 pipettes "chipped at the tip" — different glass, different chips.
87. **Sill's 3.85 for the Hollow** — a fourth "true" Hollow figure: 3.80 (Keddie 1911/1924, Strawn 1924, Ivy, Alder Corners), 3.85 (Sill), 3.90 (Selby 1924), 3.905 (Vaught §V).
88. **Sill blames water, exonerates the cows and (implicitly) the tester**; Teague/Vira/Rundle-valley blame the tester; Selby/Sill blame water; Grigg/1908/Emmet-drawer blame hay; Ivy/Vaught/Strawn blame glass; Roy "it was not the weighing."
89. **Grigg's Bulletin 214 figure**: "depress the butter fat test by two tenths in the winter feeding" vs Ivy/Vaught quoting the bulletin: "Five hundredths in winter; nothing at all in summer" (Ivy: "Four times too small"; Vaught: "some six times"). Grigg's 0.2 would exactly cover the 0.19 gap — but only in winter, while Roy/Keddie say the gap was the same in July and February.
90. **Grigg's reprint and the circular are the same year (1919)**: Grigg says the question was "reopened this season by men who were boys when we made it" and they "have brought nothing new before us but their impatience"; Frick (1925) says Selby "had circulated a warning… some years before"; order of reprint vs circular within 1919 unstated. Grigg's "abuse the man who reads their figures for them" shows the Hollow was blaming Keddie by 1919 (Strawn 1916: "his patrons blame him for their figures").
91. **Grigg's committee visited the station and stood by the test; tried the station scales "against a known weight"; tried the patrons' own scales against the station's; inquired into each barn's water supply** — the fullest 1908 text; Nym's Item 11 quotes only the closing paragraph. The report says "we saw them hauled to the station and weighed in at the intake" — yet "nobody ever came up that road" (Conflict 57).
92. **Grigg's "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter… printed in the same type as the rest"** vs the valley's later "settled opinion" (Rundle) and Teague's insinuation.
93. **Grigg's "twenty years' time" prophecy (1919 → 1939)** — Rundle's 1939 pamphlet still records the grumbling; Grigg's "eleven years" closure = 1908–1919.
94. **Frick 1946 vs Teague 1948**: Frick describes Teague's board testimony as insinuation without charge; Teague's own memoir presents it as plain ("The remedy is two men or a lock") and calls Frick "as fair a chairman as ever a hearing had."
95. **Frick's statement summary matches Keddie's Source 3 statement nearly word for word** (thirty-one times a week, twenty-nine seasons, "never taken a pound of cream").
96. **Frick's age/career**: 74 in 1946; "forty years" of titles and boundaries (so practicing from c. 1906); two homicides.
97. **Sill's "nine families"** agrees with Vira, Roy, Keddie; his "somebody up that hill road was helping the cans along" = Selby's theory; Selby: "I do not know a name."
98. **Sill's premiums "in three states"** and "not one man in that office ever came down here" — parallel to the Hollow's "nobody came up."
99. **Frick: "the skim milk handed back"; Rundle: "the skim went home to the calves and the pigs the same morning."** Consistent.
100. **Sources by date now**: 1908 report (in Source 15) → 1919 Grigg letter → 1925 Keddie → 1926 Selby → 1934 Sill → 1936 Strawn → 1939 Rundle → 1941 Effie → 1946 Frick → 1948 Teague → 1955/58 Roy → 1958 Rosalie, Vaught → 1959 Ivy, Rundle's footnote → 1972 Nym → 1979 Vira.
