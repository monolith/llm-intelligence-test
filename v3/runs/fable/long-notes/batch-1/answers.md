# Batch 1 — Answer Sheet (from the retention notes alone)

Method used throughout: a quoted document outranks a memory; arithmetic that closes outranks a round figure; a same-day diary entry outranks recollection; sources are counted by independence, not by number (two people repeating a father's figure are one source). Where the sources do not decide, I say so.

## Section A — Reconstruction (stories 1–3 of eight, in the order they occurred)

### 1. The Rule That Made the Test the Master (1889–1893)

**Who.** The farmers of the Larrow Valley, Ordell County; Gideon Vose, their first president; Effie Loomis, later secretary; Hobart Sill, buttermaker.

**What happened.** In 1889 the farmers met in the schoolhouse at Larrow Green — cold weather, a horse got loose — and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (Rundle; Effie; Falke ¶3). Gideon Vose, "heavy, quiet, obstinate," was elected president and served to 1911; he wrote the by-laws himself at his kitchen table (Rundle; Duncan). In 1891 the Association built its creamery on Larrow Creek below the mill, set up a churn and began paying cash monthly; before that "every man's arithmetic was his own" (Effie). Sill came to the churn in 1893 and stayed forty-one years.

The by-laws carried Article VII, quoted identically by Effie, Rundle and the pamphlet Nym accessioned: "Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." Effie called it "the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business"; Falke: "Under payment by weight nothing that follows… would have cost any man a dollar." Rundle: Gideon "put that in for convenience and it was convenient for thirty years." The by-laws also provided for arbitration (Frick) — the clause the 1925 board sat under.

**When.** Organized 1889; creamery 1891. Article VII's year cannot be settled: Effie says 1892, Rundle 1891 ("men left over it"), Grigg says payment by test "came in with the 1893 meeting." The printed pamphlet (Nym Item 1) is undated, carrying "only the words 'adopted at the annual meeting'" (Falke), and the 1889–92 minute book, the one record that would fix it, was lost in the 1938 office removal (Effie; Falke). Three memories, no document: 1891–1893, most likely 1892 on the secretary's authority, unproven.

**Quantities.** Gideon president twenty-two years; Larrow Green's own patrons averaged 3.78 (Sill; Falke), "an ordinary figure for this county." Also in 1889: Ansel Keddie (born 1861) married Orra Brant (born 1866), Emmet Brant's sister — the tie that later put the tester at the biggest patron's table — and Marcus Ammon founded Ammon & Sons at Tarnet, the house that would sell the glass.

**Documents.** By-laws pamphlet (Nym 1); Effie Loomis, "Notes toward a history" (1941); Rundle, "Fifty Years" (1939), sections I–II; Grigg's 1919 letter; Falke ¶3; Frick (1946); the lost 1889–92 minute book.

### 2. The Station Opens and the Short Glass Comes Up (1895–1898)

**Who.** Ansel Keddie, tester and separator man; Hobart Sill; Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, Tarnet (Marcus Ammon); Emmet and Orra Brant.

**What happened.** In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the hill road, to serve Hessel Bottom (Falke ¶4). Ansel Keddie — born 1861, a native of the hollow — went up in '95 to ready it (Vira; Hazel) and was tester and separator man from the opening, 4 May 1896, the date on the test book's first leaf. The outfit had not come; Sill lent glass from his own bench, and the leaf reads "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie" (Keddie's later "Ashlin creamery" is a slip — the leaf, Nym, Sill, Kyle and Orra say Larrow Green). Milk was weighed, sampled, tested thirty-one times a week and separated; cream went down daily by wagon, skim back to the patron. The 1896 average on Sill's glass was 3.80. The first season's intake — about 900,000 lb (Sill) or 1,100,000 (Kyle, hearsay) — was carried on the creamery's books, never with the station papers, so Nym found no figure.

On 14 April 1897 Ammon & Sons invoiced the outfit: 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20; 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at .22 — 31.68; acid measure .90; four-bottle hand tester 18.00; freight 2.15; total $77.93 (Nym Item 2; Junia's facsimile; Junia's prose and the Chronicle transpose it to $77.39; six dozen pipettes, not Vaught's and Cleve's five). It went into use 1 May 1897 — Orra's same-day entry, "the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green," with Nym, Junia and the Chronicle, outweighs Vaught's and Kyle's 1898 and Hazel's 1899. Sill's glass went back, one piece chipped.

The lot was short: pipettes blown 17.6 c.c. delivered 16.72, one part in twenty light (Ivy's 1958 sheet). Junia's explanation: a reground mold, the lot "consistently wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind cannot find"; the works books burned in 1931. From inside nobody could see it — the pipette is "the one instrument… the plant cannot check against anything else" (Vaught).

**When and quantities.** 1898, the first full year on the new glass: average 3.61, and "3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year" to 1924 (Keddie's summary leaf). The 1898 station account (Teague's line, ruled from 1905): fat credited 57,760 lb; received at Larrow Green 60,800; "station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual" — the loss already in the ledgers under a name. Emmet Brant began his weigh book 1 January 1897 (Nym; Frick; Duncan — Roy's 1898 and Hazel's 1900 rejected); Orra's diary began 4 April 1897.

**Documents.** Test book (first leaf; 1922 summary leaf); the invoice; Orra's diary (1897); Sill (1934); Kyle (1953); Junia, chapter IX (1949); Teague's ledgers (Nym 6); Emmet's weigh book; Ivy's burette sheet; Falke ¶4.

### 3. The Trouble at the Head of the Valley: Complaint, Committee and the Accidental Experiment (1902–1911)

**Who.** The Hessel Bottom patrons, Emmet Brant chief among them; Nyle Grigg's committee; Ansel Keddie; the State Agricultural College at Farland.

**What happened.** From its first full year the station's milk tested low — 3.61 against Larrow Green's 3.78, and 0.19 short of the truth (Ivy; Hazel; Falke) — the same in February as in July. The first complaint of record came in 1902 (Falke; Roy; Rundle; Grigg): talk at the store "that our milk up here runs poorer than theirs down the valley" (Orra, March 1902 — Ansel: "it is my figures they will look at"). It was renewed at every annual meeting for a quarter of a century.

Hessel Bottom: 160 acres of wet meadow under the hill; six farms (Roy; Grigg's report; Kepp; Hazel; Chronicle — Effie's seven rejected, she never went up the valley) holding nine families; Emmet Brant the largest patron every year, sixty-odd acres and thirty-eight cows.

In January 1908 the Association named a committee of three under Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green. Orra saw "four men up the road in Grigg's sleigh"; they went to Emmet's first, "being the biggest," and "wanted to see the barns and not the station." They sampled the bottom's herds January–March (the report; Orra's entries — not "every month of that year," Grigg's 1919 inflation), visited each farm three and four times, sealed samples, tried the scales, and reported: herds "thin in the flank," coats rough; "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter"; "the milk is whole and the scales are honest… the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and… the remedy lies with the land and not with the station." Received and accepted, it "closed the question for eleven years." Emmet read it twice and put it in a drawer: "the bottom fed his father and it will feed him."

The College tested the hay in 1911: Bulletin No. 214's own summary (quoted by Falke, Ivy, Vaught) found bog hay "depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture" — not the "two tenths" Grigg and Kepp remembered. Five hundredths in winter cannot carry nineteen hundredths in July.

The station tested it too, by accident. On 8 May 1911 the pipette broke (Orra: "A. home cross about a broken measure. Rain all day"); a borrowed measure, up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon, served nine weeks, to 10 July (Keddie's entries; Ivy; Roy; Vaught; Falke — Teague's and Kepp's "six weeks of June and July" wrong), and the tests stood at 3.80. Then: "Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." Strawn's June visit: "Nothing here to note." Ansel said at supper "it was something in the glass" (Duncan) but wrote only the question mark, and kept on fourteen years.

**Documents.** The 1908 report (Nym 11; reprinted 1919 with Grigg's letter); Bulletin 214 (Nym 12); the test book's 1911 entries; Orra's diary (1902, 1908, 1911); Roy (1955); Kepp (1926); Strawn's 1911 entry.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** Derivation: Ansel and Orra's son was Duncan, born 1894 (Duncan's own notes; Orra's diary has him five in 1899 and seventeen in 1911); Ivy, born 1931, is Duncan's daughter ("Duncan's girl" — Roy; Hazel; Duncan: "which makes her my father's granddaughter"). The Chronicle confirms the link without knowing it: Ivy "grew up in this town, where her father taught school for many years" — Duncan taught at Ashlin from 1911 — and gives her age as twenty-seven in October 1958, which is 1931. Cleve: "the granddaughter came back." What points elsewhere: the Chronicle's front page (3 October 1958) calls her Ansel's "great-niece," and Falke's 1959 report repeats it in ¶2. Both are second-hand — the Chronicle was written "in a day and a half from a college press release and two telephone calls," and Falke evidently took it from the newspaper or the same release; Falke then verified and withdrew it in his appended note ("the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine"). Those are the two printings Duncan complained of. One retracted error and one press-release error do not stand against the family's own genealogist, the Brant cousins, and the arithmetic of the birth years.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins.** Derivation: Emmet Brant (1854–1929) and Orra Brant Keddie (1866–1948) were brother and sister. Emmet's son Roy (born c. 1888) and Orra's son Duncan (born 1894) were therefore first cousins — "the children of a brother and a sister, which is the whole definition" (Duncan; Roy). Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are the children of first cousins: second cousins. So say Roy, Hazel, Duncan and Rundle's 1959 footnote — the family on both sides. Vira and Cleve, the two outsiders taped in 1977–79, call Ivy Hazel's "first cousin once removed"; Hazel names and rejects exactly that ("my niece or my cousin once removed… she is not"). Once-removed would require Hazel and Ivy to stand a generation apart, and they do not. What the sources leave open in the intervening generations: (i) whether Orra and Emmet were full siblings (Roy: "same mother and same father, whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother") or half siblings by their father's second wife (Hazel) — the twelve-year gap fits either, Duncan and Orra say only "brother/sister," and if Hazel is right the two women are half second cousins; (ii) Hazel's own birth year (only "small" in 1919, born c. 1910–15) and Roy's (c. 1888, from "sixty-seven" in 1955); (iii) the names of Roy's wife and Duncan's wife, given nowhere; (iv) Orra's birth year, 1866 by her diary against Duncan's 1868 — resolved for 1866, since it alone makes her "eighty-two" in 1948 as Duncan himself says.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose.** Selby himself says so ("My father… kept a herd of Jerseys at the home place… In 1910 that herd tested 4.56"), as do Effie ("son of the late president"), Duncan, and Kyle, who sat outside Selby's door for twenty-five years ("the son, whatever is said at Ashlin… the nephew story… is nonsense"). Vira and Rundle say nephew; Rundle warns that his pamphlet "is not a record. It is what the valley remembers, tidied," and Vira repeats valley talk. A man's own word about his father, backed by the office, outranks that. It matters because the Voses authored the rule and then ran the office that enforced it. Gideon organized the Association, was president 1889–1911 and wrote Article VII — "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" — the clause under which a short pipette cost the Hollow money. His son managed the Association 1913–1938 (Selby's "thirteen years this spring, since 1913"; Strawn's 1913 "New manager"; Kyle's 1911 is a year or two early, like his other dates). As manager Selby told Teague in 1916 to let the station-gain account alone; signed and sent the 1919 "water in the cans" circular that split the valley and the Brant–Keddie family; was the manager Strawn told to order new glass on 12 June 1923 and treated it as "housekeeping"; argued the water theory to the 1925 board; ran the office that let Keddie go while the board still sat; and in 1926 declared the 1924 rise the proof of water. The Vose Jerseys at 4.56 were the benchmark the Hollow's 3.61 was measured against. Father and son held the presidency and the management for forty-nine years; "the family always thought the valley belonged to them a little" (Vira). The son/nephew error also marks Vira and Rundle as unreliable on family matters — relevant to B4.

**B4. No relationship between the Voses and the Keddies (or Brants) is established; the best reading is none.** What is claimed: Vira (1979) — Orra Keddie and "old Mrs. Vose" were first cousins; Cleve (1977) — "cousins of some sort… My mother had it from her mother… I could not draw you the line on paper"; Duncan reports "two women in this town" who say Orra and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins. All three are hearsay carried by talk, none can trace a line, and they may descend from one rumour. Against them: Duncan (1968), Orra's son, the only Keddie to address it — "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses… It is talk… My mother would have told me," Orra being "the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection"; he names a likely root, "the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either." Orra's diary, thirty-three years, never names a Vose; Kyle, who corrected the nephew story, mentions no tie; Roy and Hazel, setting out the family "so that it can be copied," put no Vose in it. No marriage, no document, no birth record connects the families, and the pages of Orra's diary covering her family before 1889 were not preserved, so nothing could settle it. Verdict: not proven and, on the weight of the nearest witnesses, probably nonexistent — the tester was not the manager's cousin.

## Section C — Math

### C1

**(a) Proportion.** From Ivy's sheet of 9 March 1958: nine pipettes, stems blown 17.6 c.c., delivered against a calibrated burette (mean of five runs each): 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Sum = 150.48; ÷ 9 = **16.72 c.c.** Short 17.60 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c. Ratio 16.72 ÷ 17.60 = **0.95**. The test bottle's neck is ruled for a 17.6 c.c. charge, so a 16.72 c.c. charge reads 0.95 of the true fat: **the tests were low by 5 per cent — one part in twenty** (equivalently, the true figure was 1/0.95 = 5.26 per cent above the recorded one).
Conflict resolved: Vaught §III and the Chronicle give a mean of 16.27 (ratio 0.9244). I reject 16.27 because (1) Ivy's sheet is the primary record and its nine readings average 16.72 exactly, none lying near 16.27; (2) Nym accessioned the crate with "mean 16.72"; (3) 16.27 is the same digit transposition the Chronicle and Junia make with $77.93 → $77.39; (4) 0.95 closes the story's arithmetic — 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80, the figure the borrowed measure gave in 1911 and the new glass gave in 1924 (Strawn; Keddie), and the Alder Corners summers on the same herds ran 3.78–3.82 — whereas Vaught's own 0.9244 yields 3.905, which he concedes is "not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80"; (5) 5 per cent of delivered fat is 5¼ per cent of credited fat, Teague's "as usual" station gain (3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 0.0526; 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 0.050).

**(b) True average and shortfall.** Recorded station average over the affected years: **3.61** (Keddie's summary leaf, "3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year"; Ivy; Nym; Vaught; Falke — Teague's and Frick's 3.16 is a transposition). True average = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. Recorded tests were short by 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 points** (5 per cent of 3.80). Roy's 0.17 is 3.78 − 3.61, the comparison with Larrow Green's different herds, not with the truth; Kepp's and Selby's 0.21 rests on no measurement.

**(c) Pounds not credited.** Full delivery years on the short glass: the outfit was in use 1 May 1897 to June 1923, so 1897 and 1923 are part years and the full years are **1898–1922, twenty-five years**. Milk received: 1,600,000 lb a year × 25 = **40,000,000 lb** (Effie; Ivy; Nym Item 6; Vaught; Kyle, who compiled the figure for the 1925 board). Teague's and Rundle's 44,000,000 is rejected: the office's own compiled total is 40,000,000, and Teague's own 1898 entry checks against 1,600,000 × 3.61% = 57,760 lb.
Credited: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb. True: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb. Not credited: 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = **76,000 lb**. Cross-checks: 1,444,000 × (1/0.95 − 1) = 76,000; Teague's station gain 3,040 lb × 25 years = 76,000 — the ledgers had carried the missing fat all along as "station gain." (Frick's and Cleve's 67,000 lb is the patrons' own 1925 petition reckoning; Vaught's 118,000 lb follows from the transposed 16.27. Adding the two part years would add roughly 3,000–3,500 lb, but the question asks for full years.)

**(d) Money and fraction received.** Butterfat averaged **25 cents a pound** over the period (Effie; Ivy; Nym; Kyle: "twenty-five cents the pound, taking the fat years with the lean," compiled for the board; Roy "about twenty-five"). 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000**. (At Sill's and Kepp's remembered 27 cents it would be $20,520; the office's compiled average is preferred. Rundle's and the Chronicle's $19,600 is the January 1925 petition figure on 44,000,000 lb, not the corrected loss.) The patrons received the award of **$3,800** — the sum in the board's own award text, quoted by Effie and in the minute book (Nym Item 7), and given by Frick who chaired, Teague, Ivy, Selby, Kyle, Falke and the Chronicle; Vira's and Cleve's $3,500 is what two fathers told two children. $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = **0.20, one fifth**. Nothing else was paid; the award was "without admission," and Keddie got nothing.

### C2 — Emmet Brant, 1910

The largest patron was Emmet Brant (Effie: "largest patron every year"; Duncan; Hazel; Orra: "being the biggest"). His weigh-book leaf, quoted by Roy, Nym Item 4 and Hazel: "Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42."

**(a) Credited:** 200,000 lb × 3.42 ÷ 100 = **6,840 lb** of butterfat.

**(b) Owed and not credited:** true test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60 (Ivy: "at 3.42 short 0.18"). True fat = 200,000 × 3.60 ÷ 100 = 7,200 lb. Shortfall = 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb** (check: 6,840 × 1/19 = 360; 200,000 × 0.18 ÷ 100 = 360).

**(c) Worth that year:** butterfat was **24 cents** in 1910 (Roy: "Butterfat was twenty-four cents that year" — the only source for the 1910 price; 25 cents is the twenty-five-year average). 360 lb × $0.24 = **$86.40**. (At the 25-cent average it would be $90.00; at 24 cents, the year's own price, $86.40 is the answer.)
