## Section A — Reconstruction (the last two of the eight stories)

### Story 1 — "Four Books and a Crate": the glass condemned and the man condemned (12 June 1923 – 4 May 1925)

**Who.** Ansel Keddie, tester and separator man at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station since it opened on 4 May 1896; the Hessel Bottom patrons (six farms, nine families), chief among them Emmet Brant, the largest patron and Keddie's brother-in-law; Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector; Selby Vose, manager; Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper; Bertram Kyle, clerk; Alonzo Frick, attorney of Ashlin, chairman of a board of three.

**What happened.** On 12 June 1923, on an ordinary circuit visit, Strawn ran the station's milk pipettes against the burette issued to him in 1921 and wrote: "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot… told the manager to order new." The Association ordered from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet on 28 June (six dozen pipettes, six dozen bottles); Ammon replied on 2 July, regretting the trouble and declining to say anything of the 1897 lot; new glass was in use in July ("new glass at the station and new pails" — Orra), and in October Strawn found it delivered its mark. The condemned crate went up a twenty-foot ladder into the loft over the intake room. In the office it was "a broken window" (Kyle); Strawn drew no inference; nobody connected it with twenty-six years of low tests. In 1924, the same man testing the same herds, the station's average stood at 3.80 — the 1896 figure and the figure of the nine borrowed-measure weeks of 1911 — against 3.61 every year since 1898.

The patrons read the rise as a confession. In January 1925 they petitioned (Emmet's name on it), reckoning their loss at 67,000 lb of butterfat, about $19,600. The board sat on 17 March at Ashlin, where Keddie gave his nine-sentence statement ("thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, and I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are"), and twice in April in the station's weigh room, the crate directly overhead. Before it were four books only: the test book (4 May 1896 – 31 December 1924), the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book (lent and wanted back), and Strawn's 1923 notebook. Kyle got out the office's totals: 40,000,000 lb over twenty-five years, 1,600,000 a year, butterfat 25 cents. Three explanations were argued — water (Vose), cream theft (Teague, obliquely: "two men or a lock"), wet hay (the 1908 report read in) — and none accepted. On 30 April, by letter, the Association put Keddie out. On 4 May the board found "no cause established" and awarded $3,800 "without admission," charged to the station account. Keddie went home, built his cow shed, and turned carpenter at a quarter the pay; Emmet never entered his house again; he died in February 1934 uncleared.

**Documents.** Strawn's 1923 notebook (Frick's Exhibit 4; Nym Item 9); the order book and Ammon's letter of 2 July 1923 (Item 10); the test book (Exhibit 1; Item 3); the receiving book (Exhibit 2); the weigh book (Exhibit 3; Item 4); the petition; the board's minute book and award (Item 7); Keddie's statement (Item 8) and unsent letter; the dismissal letter (Orra's diary, 30 April 1925); Kyle's four-book list; Vose's 1926 address; Frick's 1946 recollections.

### Story 2 — "Up the Ladder": the crate measured and the name cleared (February 1958 – 1964)

**Who.** Ivy Keddie, born 1931, Ansel's granddaughter (Duncan's daughter), graduate student in dairy husbandry at the State Agricultural College, Farland; Professor Leland Vaught; Roy Brant, Emmet's son and keeper of the weigh book; Rosalie Cudd of the Alder Corners Cheese Company; the *Ashlin Chronicle*; Orin Falke, county agent; Halbert Nym of the Historical Rooms.

**What happened.** In February 1958 Ivy climbed the ladder into the loft over the old station's intake room and found the Ammon & Sons crate under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff: nine pipettes unbroken (two chipped at the tip) of the six dozen bought in 1897. On 9 March at Farland she ran each five times against a calibrated burette: 16.70 to 16.74 c.c., mean 16.72 against 17.6 blown on the stem — short 0.88 c.c., 95 hundredths of the mark. 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80; the gap of 0.19 was one-twentieth of the true test. She then laid side by side what nobody had: the test book from the Ashlin office; Roy's weigh book, lent and returned (1910: 200,000 lb at 3.42, truly 3.60); Rosalie's table of the four Hessel Bottom men's summers at Alder Corners, 3.78–3.82 for sixteen seasons; Strawn's 1923 volume from the Farland library; and the annual ledgers, whose "station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual" summed over 1898–1922 to 76,000 lb — five per cent of the fat delivered — worth $19,000 at 25 cents. The $3,800 award had been a fifth of it. Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958, Vaught and Keddie) concluded: "No milk was watered… no cream was stolen… the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty." On 3 October the *Chronicle* ran "Old Ordell Wrong Righted," carrying the press release's errors (great-niece, eleven pipettes, $77.39, $19,600) and an accusation against Ammon & Sons that Junia Ammon's 1949 history had already answered: a reground mold, an accident nobody caught. In 1959 Ivy read "What the Crate Held"; the test book and crate went to the Ordell County Historical Rooms; Rundle footnoted his pamphlet; Falke's report for the county board first allowed the wet hay, then, on reading Bulletin 214's "not more than five hundredths in winter," withdrew it, corrected "great-niece" to "granddaughter," and found no action lay. The weigh book followed in 1963; Orra's diary was printed in 1964 ("cleared in 1958, twenty-four years after his death"); the Alder Corners book went to Vessey County in 1970. Thirty-five years from glass to cause, thirty-three from dismissal; the outfit had cost $77.93.

**Documents.** Ivy's burette sheet (Nym Item 14); the crate (Item 5); the invoice of 14 April 1897 (Item 2); Bulletins 471 (Item 15) and 214 (Item 12); Rosalie's letter and table (Item 13); the Strawn photostat (Item 9); the *Chronicle*, 3 October 1958; Falke's report and note (1959); Rundle's 1959 footnote; Roy's 1958 postscript; Duncan's 1968 notes; Orra's diary (1964).

## Section F — Theory

### F1. What produced the shortfall

**The theory.** The Babcock test reads butterfat directly off a bottle neck graduated for a fixed charge of milk, 17.6 c.c., drawn with a pipette blown to that mark. On 1 May 1897 the Ostrey Hollow station replaced the true glass it had borrowed from Larrow Green with six dozen Ammon & Sons pipettes from a lot whose mold had been reground: they delivered 16.72 c.c., 95 hundredths of the mark, every one within 0.04 c.c. of the others. Five per cent less milk in the bottle is five per cent less fat in the neck, so every reading was 0.95 of the truth. That fixes the size: 3.80 × 0.95 = 3.61 — a gap of 0.19, one part in twenty, and proportional, so it scaled with the fat (Brant's 3.42 was a 3.60 herd; Vose's 4.56 Jerseys would have read 4.33) and did not move with the season (the same 0.19 in July and February — Roy, Hazel, Falke). The complement shows in the office: the cream reaching Larrow Green carried the fat the milk really had, so the ledger booked a "station gain" of 5¼ per cent of the credited fat every year, "as usual" — exactly 0.05 ÷ 0.95 — 76,000 lb over 1898–1922.

It began when it did because 1 May 1897 is the day the short glass went into use: 1896, on Sill's borrowed outfit, averaged 3.80; 1898, the first full year on the Ammon glass, 3.61, and within three hundredths of that every year after. It ended when it did because on 12 June 1923 Strawn ran the pipettes against the burette issued in 1921 (he had skipped the check in 1922), condemned the lot, and new glass that delivered its mark was in use from July; 1924 read 3.80. Nothing at the station or on the farms had to change because nothing there was wrong: the same man tested in the same way in front of the inspector, the scales were true, the lactometer never found water, the herds and hay were what they had always been. The only variable was the instrument, and the record contains a controlled proof of it: from 8 May to 10 July 1911 a borrowed measure stood in for a broken one and the tests read 3.80; the day the station's own glass came back they fell to 3.61. Independent confirmation: the same four Hessel Bottom men's milk, tested on Jerome Cudd's own glass at Alder Corners every summer 1915–1930, ran 3.78–3.82.

**The three competing explanations and what defeats each.**

1. *Water in the cans* — Selby Vose (the 1919 circular; his 1926 address), echoed by Hobart Sill. Defeated by Strawn's lactometer at every visit, 1908–1936: "no added water, as always here"; by Rosalie Cudd's cheese ("Cheese is a witness"; never docked one of them); by the flatness of the gap — nine families would have had to water at exactly one part in twenty, every month for twenty-six years, and all stop in July 1923 (Sill's own objection: "nine families all got honest in one summer"); by the 1911 weeks, when the same cans read 3.80; by the ledgers — watered milk delivers no surplus fat, yet the cream arrived heavy; and by chronology — the rise (1923–24) preceded the "looking" (the January 1925 petition), not the reverse.
2. *Cream theft by the tester, low tests to cover it* — Wilbur Teague at the hearing ("two men or a lock"), the valley's settled opinion (Rundle 1939), Vira, Cleve's father. Defeated by the station-gain line itself: stolen cream would reach the Green light, and it reached the Green heavy every year (Rundle's own retraction; Sill: "Always more. Never less"); by the nine weeks of 1911 — a man skimming cream does not raise his own figures for nine weeks when handed a borrowed pipette (Falke ¶8); by the fact that the figures came right in 1923–24 while Keddie was still testing, not after he was put out; by the inspector, who watched him work; and by the Alder Corners figures, which show the milk really was 3.80.
3. *The wet hay of Hessel Bottom* — Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee, Dr. Kepp's 1926 paper, Falke's first draft (withdrawn). Defeated by Bulletin No. 214 (the college's 1911 feeding trial): "not more than five hundredths in winter, and nothing on summer pasture" — "four times too small and the wrong shape"; by the gap's indifference to season; by the same cows on the same hay testing 3.78–3.82 over the ridge; by 1911, when hay and herds were unchanged and the figures rose; by 1923–24, when the gap vanished though the bottom was never drained; and by the error scaling with fat (a hill herd at 4.56 would have lost 0.23), the signature of an instrument, not a ration. Kepp's rebuttal of 1911 ("six weeks of June and July") misdates a span that began 8 May in a wet spring.

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?** No. Effie: the crate "was never mentioned in the hearing at all." Frick: "Four books and nothing else" — the test book, the receiving book, Brant's weigh book, and Strawn's 1923 notebook; the board saw the scales tried and "the sampling done and the test made," necessarily with the 1923 replacement glass. Kyle carried the same four books in and out and says no one "laid the second beside the fourth" (the receiving book beside the notebook that recorded the condemnation). Two of the three sittings sat in the weigh room with the crate twenty feet above them (Duncan; Cleve). The board therefore had the written record of the condemnation before it as Exhibit 4 and never looked at, or asked for, the glass itself.

**What the tester believed.** The record is settled; his mind is not. What he wrote: a question mark against the day his own measure came back (10 July 1911), let stand for fourteen years, and to the board, "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are." What others say he said: Frick (1946) — in the corridor at Ashlin he "had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the room"; Duncan (1968) — in the summer of 1911 he said twice "it was something in the glass" and "never dared put it in the book"; Ivy (1958) — "he never knew"; Orra's contemporary diary — "it is my figures they will look at" (1902), "cross about a broken measure" (8 May 1911), and in 1934 "a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name," naming no cause. These cannot be reconciled from the sources: Frick and Duncan each report direct speech, decades apart, pointing in opposite directions, and Ivy was three when he died. The documentary trace favors the glass — the question mark sits at the return of his own pipette, not beside any patron's can — and Frick's remark may be what a sleepless man told a lawyer to explain why he would not accuse his neighbors; but that is inference. Answer: he put a question mark on the glass and would not name a cause; whether he privately blamed the glass or the patrons cannot be determined.

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

In 1889 the Larrow Valley farmers formed a cooperative creamery whose by-law paid each patron on the butterfat test of the station where he delivered. Their Ostrey Hollow skimming station opened in May 1896; on 1 May 1897 it took into use six dozen Ammon & Sons pipettes that delivered 16.72 c.c. instead of 17.6. For twenty-six years every test there read one part in twenty low — 3.61 for 3.80 — while the cream reached Larrow Green heavy, booked yearly as "station gain, as usual." Water, theft, and wet hay were blamed; a 1908 committee, a 1919 circular, and a 1925 arbitration that dismissed the tester Ansel Keddie never looked at the glass, condemned in 1923 and left in the loft. In 1958 his granddaughter measured it. Test book, weigh book, ledgers, cheese-factory book, inspector's notebook, invoice, committee report, bulletin — each held one piece, and no keeper of one ever saw the rest.
