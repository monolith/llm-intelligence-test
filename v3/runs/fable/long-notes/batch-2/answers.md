# Batch 2 — Answer Sheet (from the retention notes only)

Working assumption for Section A: the eight stories run in the order the events occurred — (1) the founding and Article VII, (2) the station's opening and the Ammon glass, (3) the low tests, the weigh book and the station-gain line — then the three below — then (7) the 1923 condemnation and the 1925 arbitration and dismissal, (8) the 1958 crate and Bulletin 471. Where sources conflict I say which I kept and why.

## Section A — Reconstruction (stories 4, 5, 6)

### 1. The Committee in Grigg's Sleigh (1902–1911): the wet-hay verdict

**Who.** The Hessel Bottom patrons — six farms on 160 acres of wet meadow under Ostrey Hollow, nine families, Emmet Brant the largest — and the Association's committee of three under Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green, no patron of the station ("bought hay off two of them"); later Dr. Roland Kepp of Ashlin and the College at Farland.

**What.** From 1902 the patrons said aloud what their pay slips showed: milk paid on a test about 0.19 under the valley's (3.61 at the station against 3.78 at Larrow Green), and the same gap in July as in February. Orra's diary, March 1902: "Talk at the store that our milk up here runs poorer than theirs down the valley"; Ansel: "it is my figures they will look at." The Association appointed the committee in January 1908. Orra saw "four men up the road today in Grigg's sleigh" (a driver, or an unrecorded fourth); they went to Emmet's first, "being the biggest," ate his dinner, and "wanted to see the barns and not the station," which Ansel remarked on and let alone. They sampled the herds through January, February and March — the report's own words, confirmed by the diary; Grigg's 1919 "every month of that year" is memory and I reject it — visited each farm three and four times, tried the scales, found the stock thin in the flank and rough in the coat, found "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter," and laid the cause to "the wet hay of that bottom… the remedy lies with the land and not with the station." The report was received and accepted and "closed the question for eleven years." Emmet read it twice and put it in the drawer: "the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not"; "the bottom fed his father and it will feed him."

The theory was then tested and failed. In 1911 the College's feeding trial, Bulletin No. 214, found bog hay depressed the test "by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture" (Falke quotes the summary entire; Grigg's and Kepp's "two tenths in the winter feeding" is secondhand and rejected). Five hundredths in winter cannot carry nineteen hundredths in July. Kepp's 1926 Farm Club paper restated the hay case with soil chemistry and a 0.21 gap while admitting he had never read a test; Falke in 1959 first allowed the hay as "a real if partial factor" and withdrew it on reading 214.

**Quantities.** Six farms, 160 acres; gap 0.19 (3.61 v 3.78); sampling January–March 1908; hay effect ≤0.05 winter, 0 summer.

**Documents.** The 1908 report (Nym Item 11; full text in Grigg's 1919 reprint with covering letter); Bulletin No. 214 (Item 12); Orra's diary, January and March 1908; Roy Brant's account; Kepp's 1926 paper; Falke's ¶9 and appended note.

### 2. Nine Weeks on a Borrowed Measure (8 May – 10 July 1911)

**Who.** Ansel Keddie, tester; Emmet Brant and his weigh book; Merle Strawn, the state inspector; Duncan Keddie, seventeen and still at home; Orra.

**What.** On the morning of 8 May 1911 the station's own pipette broke. Orra that night: "A. home cross about a broken measure. Rain all day, the low piece under water again" — a wet spring, not the summer flush Kepp later invoked. A borrowed measure was used for nine weeks (63 days). Where it came from cannot be settled: Larrow Green by the cream wagon (Keddie, Ivy), "from town" (Teague), the state man's own (Roy), "elsewhere" (Falke). Through those weeks the station's tests stood at 3.80 instead of 3.61 — up by exactly the standing gap of 0.19 — with the same man, herds, pasture, scales, acid and bottles. Strawn came in June, mid-interval, and wrote "Nothing here to note." On 10 July Keddie entered: "Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." He let the question mark stand fourteen years. Teague's and Kepp's "six weeks" are rejected: the two dated entries in the book are 63 days apart and Orra's diary fixes the start. Emmet entered the rise and fall in his weigh book "and there it sat for near on fifty years." Teague booked "a small charge for replacement." Duncan (1968) remembers his father saying twice that summer, at supper and by the door, "it was something in the glass," and never daring to write it; the printed diary has no such entry, so that memory stands uncorroborated. The same year Bulletin 214 came out; in October Duncan left to teach at Ashlin. Nobody read the interval as evidence until 1958: Ivy called it "an experiment… nobody knew it was one," Vaught said it "ran itself… and lay unread for forty-seven years," and Falke that it disposes of the theft theory — "the same man made the tests in that interval, under no different supervision, and the figures rose."

**Quantities.** 8 May–10 July = nine weeks; 3.61 → 3.80 → 3.61; rise 0.19 = the whole deficiency.

**Documents.** The test-book entries of 8 May and 10 July 1911 and the 1922 summary leaf; Keddie's 1925 unsent letter; the Brant weigh book; Strawn's June 1911 entry; Orra's diary; Teague's chapter; Kepp's 1926 objection; Bulletin 471 §IV; Falke ¶6 and ¶8.

### 3. Over the Ridge, and the Water Letter (1915–1922)

**Who.** Jerome Cudd's Alder Corners Cheese Company in Vessey County (built 1913, opened 1914); four of the six Hessel Bottom farmers; Selby Vose, manager from 1913 (Gideon's son, not nephew); Bertram Kyle, the clerk; Ansel, Orra and Emmet; Nyle Grigg; Strawn.

**What.** From 1915, in the summer flush — about 1 June to mid-September, when Redlow Ridge's four miles of switchback were open — four Bottom men hauled their milk over to Cudd's factory and brought it back to the Hollow the rest of the year: sixteen seasons, 1915–1930 (Ivy's "fourteen" is rejected against Rosalie's table and Roy). Cudd tested on his own bench, glass and book and never compared a figure with anyone. His summer averages for the four ran 3.78 to 3.82, 3.80 across the table; 1919: "3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge." A cheesemaker "knows watered milk inside a day"; he never docked one of them. So the same cows on the same hay gave 3.80 on another man's glass and 3.61 on the station's — the 0.19 again, "the only honest arithmetic anybody in that valley ever did without meaning to." At home they were said to have "gone over to the enemy"; the Brants did not go.

In 1919, the question "reopened by men who were boys when we made it," Grigg reprinted the 1908 report with a covering letter, and the office answered with a circular about water in the cans: Kyle typed it, Selby signed it, "it went out over his name and no one else's." The bundle came to the station; Ansel would neither mail it nor leave it on the platform but carried it to eleven doors (nine families by the patrons' daughters; Orra, who counted, says eleven) and stood while each man read it, home at eleven with his hat on. Two days later Emmet stood in the Keddie yard: he had hauled whole milk since the day the station opened — why carry the paper if he did not believe it? "He has not been in this house since, and he is my brother." Ten years' silence followed, to Emmet's death in 1929; Orra walked to Larrow Green rather than pass his lane. The letters were "answered by none of them, which in that valley is a shout"; Hazel's mother put hers in the stove, Vira's father in the desk drawer. Strawn told the manager he had never found water, and never did. Keddie's summary leaf of 1922: 3.61 or within three hundredths every year since 1898, save the nine weeks.

**Quantities.** Four men; sixteen seasons; 3.80 average over the ridge; gap 0.19; nine (or eleven) families circularized; ten years' silence.

**Documents.** Rosalie Cudd's 1958 letter with the sixteen-season table and Jerome's 1919 entry (the Alder Corners book, Vessey County Historical Society from 1970; Nym Item 13); the circular (in the Toland desk drawer; unaccessioned); Kyle's 1953 account; Grigg's 1919 letter; Strawn's 1917 and 1919 entries; Orra's June 1919 entries; Roy's, Hazel's and Vira's accounts; Selby's 1926 defense ("It named nobody").

## Section C — Math

### C3. Butterfat credited, received, and the difference (1898–1922)

**Inputs and how they are settled.** The full delivery years are 1898–1922: 25 years, 40,000,000 lb of milk, 1,600,000 lb a year (Effie, Ivy, Nym, Vaught; Kyle compiled the figure for the 1925 board). Teague's and Rundle's 44,000,000 is rejected by arithmetic: Teague's own typical entry (57,760 lb credited at 3.61 ⇒ 1,600,000 lb of milk) times 25 gives 40,000,000, not 44,000,000. The station average is 3.61 (Keddie's summary leaf; Ivy; Nym; Vaught; Falke); Teague's and Frick's 3.16 is a digit transposition and does not reproduce Teague's own 57,760. The fat actually delivered corresponds to 3.80 (the 1911 borrowed-measure weeks, the 1924 figure on new glass in Strawn's book and Keddie's, and 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95, 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80).

**(a) Credited to patrons:** 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb** (check: 57,760 × 25 = 1,444,000).

**(b) Reached the creamery in the station's cream:** 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lb** (check: 60,800 × 25 = 1,520,000; Nym Item 6 and Vaught §VII give the difference as 76,000 lb, which is 1,520,000 − 1,444,000).

**(c) The difference:** 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = **76,000 lb**.
- As a percentage of the fat **credited** to the patrons: 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.26%** — Teague's "five and a quarter per cent, as usual."
- As a percentage of the fat **received at Larrow Green**: 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5.00%** exactly — one part in twenty, the pipettes' own shortfall.
Teague's chapter puts the 5¼% "of the fat that was actually in the cream"; that is the wrong base. Nym has it right: "five and a quarter percent of the credited fat is five percent of the fat delivered." (At 25 cents the pound, 76,000 lb is the $19,000 of Ivy and Nym.)

### C4. The station's testing outfit

**(a) Invoice total: $77.93** (14 April 1897, Ammon & Sons, Tarnet). Settled by the itemization, which is quoted identically by Nym (Item 2), Junia's facsimile and Ivy: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93. The Chronicle's and Junia's prose "seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents" ($77.39) is a transposition and does not sum.

**(b) Pipettes: six dozen = 72**, at 35 cents — 72 × 0.35 = 25.20, the line on the invoice. Vaught's and Cleve's "five dozen" is rejected: 60 × 0.35 = 21.00, not 25.20, and Cleve counted from hearsay ("they tell me now").
**Each was short by 0.88 c.c.**: blown 17.6 c.c., delivered a mean of 16.72 c.c. (Ivy's sheet of 9 March 1958: nine pipettes, 16.70–16.74, mean 16.72; Nym Item 5 independently records mean 16.72). 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c., i.e. the pipettes delivered 0.95 of the mark; individual shortfalls ran 0.86–0.90 c.c., "in the same proportion, one to another" (Falke). Vaught's and the Chronicle's 16.27 c.c. (short 1.33) is rejected: it is another digit swap of 16.72, it yields 0.9244 and a 3.905 "true" test that Vaught himself calls "not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80," whereas 0.95 reproduces 3.80 from 3.61 exactly and matches 1911 and 1924.

### C5. Duration, delivery years, and the first season

**(a) How long the faulty glass was in use.** From **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923**: 26 years and 42 days (9,537 days), i.e. 26 years and six weeks. Subtracting the nine weeks of 1911 (63 days) when a borrowed measure stood in, the short pipettes were actually in the tester's hand about 9,474 days — 26 years less three weeks. Start date settled by the invoice of 14 April 1897, Nym, Junia, the Chronicle and above all Orra's same-day entry "May 1, 1897. A. says the station's own glass came up"; Vaught's and Kyle's 1 May 1898 (recollection and inference from correspondence) and Hazel's 1899 are rejected. End date settled by Strawn's own dated entry of 12 June 1923 and the paper trail it started (order 28 June, Ammon's reply 2 July); Selby's and Rundle's 12 July, Hazel's 1921 and Kyle's 1922 are rejected. One caveat the sources leave open: what glass was used between 12 June and the new lot's arrival in July is nowhere stated (Falke says the crate lay in the loft "since the day it was condemned"; Ivy says it was carried up in June).

**The money calculation runs over 25 full delivery years, 1898–1922.** The two numbers differ because the loss is computed from annual ledger totals (Teague's station-gain line, 1,600,000 lb a year × 25 = 40,000,000 lb), and only whole years are counted: 1897 is dropped because the short glass was in use only from 1 May ("1897, part year" on Keddie's summary leaf, the first four months being on Sill's borrowed glass), and 1923 is dropped because the glass was condemned on 12 June and new glass was in use from July, making it a mixed year with no station average recorded. 1896 was entirely on borrowed glass (3.80) and is outside both spans. So roughly thirteen and a half months of use (May–December 1897 and January–June 1923) are not in the 76,000 lb / $19,000 — the calculation understates, if anything. (The nine weeks of 1911 stay inside a counted year; Keddie's summary notes them "in their place.")

**(b) Milk received in the first season (4 May–31 December 1896): cannot be determined from the sources.** Nym records that the station papers hold no 1896 intake figure, and Kyle explains why: the first season's milk "was weighed at the Hollow and carried on the creamery's own books, and the sheets for it were never put with the station papers." Only two figures exist, both hearsay and 200,000 lb apart: Sill's "about nine hundred thousand pounds" (he weighed the cream at the Green, not the milk at the Hollow) and Kyle's "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds… what I understood at the time from men who were there. It is not in any book I ever ruled" (Kyle was not yet in the office in 1896). They are not independent documents, and neither is a document at all. What the sources do establish: a part season on borrowed glass averaging 3.80, somewhere between 0.9 and 1.1 million lb; eight months at the later full-year rate of 1,600,000 lb a year would be about 1,067,000 lb, but that is a projection of later years onto a station's first summer, not evidence.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran from 8 May 1911, when the station's own pipette broke, to 10 July 1911, when it was back in use: nine weeks, 63 days — fixed by the two dated entries in Keddie's book, by Orra's diary entry of 8 May, and confirmed by Roy, Ivy, Vaught and Falke; Teague's and Kepp's "six weeks" are memory and Kepp's "June and July" mislocates the start. The one variable was the measuring glass: a borrowed pipette in place of the station's own. Held constant were the tester (Keddie, "under no different supervision" — Strawn visited in June and noted nothing), the patrons and their cans, the herds, the hay and pasture, the scales, the acid, the test bottles, the bookkeeping. The result: the station's tests rose from 3.61 to 3.80 for the nine weeks and fell back to 3.61 on the day the own glass returned — a rise of exactly the standing 0.19. What that establishes, which no calculation from the 1958 measurements can, is causation in the field and in 1911: change the glass alone and the whole deficiency disappears, so the hay (same hay, and a wet May — "the low piece under water again"), watered milk (the patrons would have had to stop and restart on the days a pipette broke and was replaced) and cream theft (the same man tested and his own figures rose) are all excluded at once; and it dates the fault to at least 1911, fourteen years after purchase and twelve before condemnation — the glass was short from the start, not worn (against Selby's "Apparatus wears"). The 1958 arithmetic shows the short pipettes *could* account for a 5% shortfall; the 1911 weeks show the shortfall *did* vanish when only the glass was changed. What the interval does not establish: which of the two measures was the true one — by itself it shows only that two pipettes disagreed by one part in twenty, and it took the 1923 burette, the 1958 measurement and the outside controls (Larrow Green 3.78, Alder Corners 3.80, the 1924 figure on new glass) to fix the station's glass as the short one; it does not give the shortfall in cubic centimeters; it says nothing about the bottles or the rest of the outfit; it does not show the fault was constant across all twenty-six years (though the flat 3.61 suggests it); it does not settle where the borrowed measure came from; and it is one unreplicated nine-week run, whose only replication is the 1923–24 glass change.

**D2.** Three reasons, each resting on a different record, a different place and different narrators. (1) *The cream arrived heavy, not light.* Every year the Association's own ledger carried the "station gain": fat credited to the patrons on the station test 57,760 lb, fat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream 60,800 lb, gain 3,040 lb, "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" — 76,000 lb over twenty-five years. Sill weighed that cream in the cold room from 1893: "Always more. Never less." Stolen cream would have reached the Green light; it reached the Green heavy, as Rundle saw in 1939 and Kyle in 1953. Narrators: Teague, Sill, Kyle, Rundle. (2) *The nine weeks of 1911.* With a borrowed pipette the same man's tests rose 0.19 and fell back the day his own glass returned; a thief does not stop and restart with a broken pipette. Narrators: Keddie's book, Roy (from the weigh book), Falke. (3) *The new glass of 1923.* Keddie tested the whole 1924 season on the replacement lot and the average stood at 3.80 (Strawn's 1924 entry; Keddie's letter; even Selby admits the 1924 rise), a year before he was dismissed; and when the alleged thief was removed in 1925 the figure did not rise again but "remained there" (Falke). The rise came with the glass, not with the man's departure — Vira's memory that the figures came up after the dismissal is contradicted by Strawn's dated 1924 entry. Narrators: Strawn, Keddie, Selby, Falke. A fourth, from over the ridge (Rosalie Cudd, Roy, Hazel): the same herds tested 3.78–3.82 on Cudd's independent glass for sixteen summers, so the milk really was 3.80 milk and the whole 0.19 was in the station's reading. These are independent because each would have to be explained away by a different mechanism: (1) is a weight taken at the receiving end by other men with other instruments; (2) is the test book at the station in one year; (3) is the inspector's notebook and an adverse witness in another year; (4) is another county's book. All of (1), (2) and (3) would hold if the glass had never been measured in 1958 — (1) most plainly: it was in the ledgers from 1898 (or from Teague's arrival in 1905), read out at every annual meeting, weighed by Sill and stated by him in 1934, and drawn out by Rundle in 1939. As to the bookkeeper: no. Teague invented the line in 1905, wrote it for forty years, called it "furniture," raised the station account with Vose once in 1916 and "was told to let it alone" ("I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience"), urged the theft explanation to the board in 1925 ("Cream is portable… The remedy is two men or a lock"), and in 1948 printed the entry and the accusation in the same chapter without noticing that the sign of his own figure refutes it — getting the base wrong (5¼% "of the fat actually in the cream") and the total wrong (44,000,000 lb) besides. Kyle: "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years." What exactly he "put to Mr. Vose" in 1916 is not quoted, so I cannot say he never noticed the line was odd; I can say he never treated it as an answer to the charge he made.

**D3.** Take "know" in two senses. That the station's tests ran low — yes, beyond doubt, before 12 June 1923: the patrons had complained at every annual meeting since 1902; the 1908 committee had been appointed to explain it; Teague put the station account to him in 1916 and he told him to let it alone; in 1919 he signed a circular, typed by Kyle "with no doubt at all in it about what was meant," warning the Hollow patrons about water in the cans — a document that makes no sense unless he knew the tests were low and believed fat was missing from the milk; Strawn told him that year he had never found water, and kept telling him. His 1926 address confirms the state of his mind: "Water was going into cans at the head of that valley and it stopped when the Association began to look." That the *tests* were low — that the deficiency lay in the station's own reading and not in the milk — no, the sources say he did not know it, and show how he failed to. The proof was on the sheet he signed every year from 1913 (1911 by Kyle, but Effie, Strawn's "New manager" entry and Selby's own "since 1913" outvote him): a station gain of 5¼% "as usual," fat arriving that had never been credited. Kyle, at the desk outside his door, watched him "take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign… He read the totals and he never read the lines above them." When Teague raised the account he chose not to look. When Strawn condemned the glass he read it as "a piece of housekeeping that appears in our own order book with a date and a price against it," and in 1926, with the 1924 rise in front of him, still credited it to water stopping rather than to the new glass ordered "inside the month." So: he knew the figures were low and had a theory that blamed the patrons; he had signed, ten times over, the line that showed the loss was the station's, and by the only eyewitness never read it. Whether that is ignorance or avoidance the sources do not decide — "told to let it alone" cuts both ways — but on the plain question he knew the tests ran low and did not know why, and the reason sat under his thumb.
