# Answer sheet — part 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

**C1 (a) Proportion.** The nine surviving pipettes from the crate were blown 17.6 c.c. and delivered (mean of five runs each) 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Sum = 150.48; ÷ 9 = 16.72 c.c. Ratio 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly (17.6 × 0.95 = 16.72). The bottle neck is ruled for a 17.6 charge, so a 95 percent charge produces a fat column 95 percent as long: every test read 95 percent of the truth, i.e. low by one part in twenty (5 percent). Stated the other way, true = recorded ÷ 0.95, so the truth was 5.26 percent above the recorded figure. Vaught's 16.27 c.c. (ratio 0.9244) is a transposition; it contradicts the sheet, the 3.61→3.80 arithmetic and his own conclusion paragraph.

**C1 (b) True average.** Recorded average 3.61 (summary leaf). 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80. Shortfall 3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19 of a point. Cross-checks: 1896 on borrowed glass 3.80; the 1911 nine weeks 3.80; 1924 on new glass 3.80; Alder Corners 3.78–3.82.

**C1 (c) Pounds not credited.** 25 full delivery years (1898–1922), 40,000,000 lb of milk. Credited: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb. Delivered: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb. Difference: 76,000 lb of butterfat. Ledger cross-check: 3,040 lb station gain × 25 years = 76,000 lb.

**C1 (d) Money and fraction.** 76,000 lb × $0.25 = $19,000. Patrons received the award of $3,800. 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = 0.20 — one fifth (20 percent).

**C2 — Emmet Brant, 1910.** Delivered 200,000 lb, paid on 3.42. (a) Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = 6,840 lb. (b) True test 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60; delivered 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb; owed and not credited 7,200 − 6,840 = 360 lb (= 5 percent of 7,200; matches Ivy's "recorded at 3.42 was short 0.18": 0.18 × 2,000 = 360). (c) Butterfat in 1910 was 24 cents (Roy Brant's book): 360 × $0.24 = $86.40. (At the 25-cent period average it would be $90.00; the question asks for that year, so $86.40.)

**C3 — the 25 full years.** (a) Credited to patrons: 1,444,000 lb (57,760 × 25). (b) Reached Larrow Green in station cream: 1,520,000 lb (60,800 × 25). (c) Difference 76,000 lb = 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = 5.00 percent of the fat actually delivered, or 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 5.26 percent (the ledger's "five and a quarter") of the fat credited. The ledger reckons against the credited figure; the glass explains the 5 percent of the delivered figure. Teague's and Vaught's "five and a quarter percent of the fat actually in the cream" mixes the two.

**C4.** (a) Invoice of 14 April 1897: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = $77.93 (not $77.39). (b) 6 dozen = 72 pipettes (72 × $0.35 = $25.20 confirms the count; five dozen would be $21.00). Each short by 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c. on the mean; the nine measured individually ran 0.86 to 0.90 short. The 0.88 is measured on nine survivors and inferred to the lot; Ammon's account of a consistently wrong lot supports the inference, and the flat 3.61 for 26 years is consistent with it.

**C5 (a).** Faulty glass in use from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923: 26 years, 1 month and 11 days (about 26.1 years), less the nine weeks of 8 May–10 July 1911 when a borrowed measure was used. The money calculation runs over 25 full delivery years, 1898 through 1922. They differ because the ledger is annual and the glass changed in mid-year at both ends: 1897 (May–December, "part year" on the summary leaf) and 1923 (January–12 June) cannot be split out of annual totals, so the 25-year figure drops about thirteen months of short tests, and slightly overcounts the nine true weeks of 1911. The 76,000 lb is therefore a floor, not the exact total. **C5 (b).** Cannot be determined from the sources. The station column of the ledgers begins with 1897; the 1896 intake (4 May to year-end) was carried on the creamery's books and the sheets were never put with the station papers (Kyle); the curator states flatly that no figure for 1896 exists anywhere. Sill recalls "about nine hundred thousand pounds" thirty-eight years later; Kyle "a good eleven hundred thousand," from men who were there, "not in any book I ever ruled." Two unrecorded recollections that disagree by 200,000 lb are not a figure.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval: 8 May to 10 July 1911, sixty-three days, nine weeks (Teague's and Kepp's six weeks are wrong; the test book carries both dates and Orra's diary carries the first). The variable: the measuring pipette — the station's own broke and a borrowed one was used. Held constant: patrons, herds, pasture, season (the interval straddles May–July, not a feed change), acid, bottles, hand tester, the tester himself and his habits, supervision (none). Result: the station average rose from 3.61 to 3.80 the day the borrowed measure came in, held there, and fell to 3.61 the day the station's own returned — recorded contemporaneously by a man who did not understand it and wrote a question mark. What it establishes that a calculation cannot: that the gap lived in the measure and not in the cows, the ground, the cans or the man, because those did not change and the figure did; a calculation from the 1958 measurement shows only that the glass could produce a 0.19 gap, the experiment shows that it did, at that station, with those herds, in real time. It also fixes the size of the effect, 0.19, forty-seven years before anyone had a burette. What it does not establish: the shortfall in cubic centimeters (that needed the 1958 burette); by itself, that the same fault ran from 1897 to 1923 (that rests on the summary leaf's 3.61 within 0.03 every year, the 1896 and 1924 readings of 3.80 on other glass, and the Alder Corners figures); where the borrowed measure came from or that it was itself exactly true (its 3.80 is corroborated externally, which makes it likely); how the lot came to be short; that feed has no effect at all (only that it is not the 0.19); and anything to anyone at the time, since nobody read it.

**D2.** Reasons the tester was not stealing cream, each from a different keeper of a different record: (1) The Association's own ledger, in the accuser's hand: every year more fat arrived at Larrow Green in the station's cream than the station's tests credited — 60,800 against 57,760, "as usual." Theft takes fat out of the cream before it leaves; stolen cream arrives light. This cream arrived heavy. (Teague, Nym, Kyle, Rundle's press note.) (2) The buttermaker's receiving book: Hobart Sill weighed the Hollow's cans twice, on the platform and in the cold room, from 1893 on, and found them heavy on "page after page." A physical weighing on the creamery floor, not an office figure. (3) The tester's own book for 1911: the same man, alone, on the same routine, produced 3.80 for nine weeks on a borrowed measure and 3.61 the day his own returned — his hand did not change, the glass did (Keddie; Falke's paragraph 8). (4) The chronology of the rise: the tests went to 3.80 from July 1923 and the whole of 1924 while Keddie was still the only man in the building, ten months before his dismissal (Strawn's 1924 entry; Vose's own address; Sill's "one summer along in the twenties"). The valley's story that "the figures went right when the man left" has the order backwards. (5) An outside witness: Jerome Cudd's book in another county, kept by a man who never saw an Ordell figure, shows the same herds at 3.80 — exactly 3.61 ÷ 0.95 — which a thief at the Hollow could not have arranged. (6) The 1958 measurement: the glass alone accounts for the whole gap, and a proportional, scatter-free 5 percent for twenty-six years is the signature of an instrument, not of a man helping himself. These are independent because they rest on different documents kept by people with different, sometimes opposed interests — the accuser's ledger, the buttermaker's scale, the accused's book, the inspector's notebook, a cheesemaker's book in another county, and a laboratory sheet — and each would stand if the others were lost. Reasons 1 through 5 hold with the crate never opened; reason 1 alone was sufficient and was in the office from the start. Did the bookkeeper ever question his own entries? No. Teague says the evenness "stops him looking" and the line became "furniture"; Kyle says he "never asked anybody anything in forty years." Teague's one recorded act is that he "put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone" — uncorroborated, content unstated, and never repeated. In 1925 and again in 1948 he reproduced the entry and, in the same breath, argued theft, without noticing that the entry refutes it. A schoolteacher saw the contradiction on one reading of the ledgers in 1939.

**D3.** Yes — in the sense the question asks, and the record is explicit. That the station's tests ran below the valley's was the complaint from 1902 onward and the office's standing knowledge; Vose's 1926 address says the milk "for a generation ... tested below the milk brought to Larrow Green," and he sent the June 1919 circular precisely because, as he put it, a manager "who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing" is idle. He also had before him, each spring from 1913, a signed sheet stating that the station's cream delivered five and a quarter percent more fat than its tests credited, and in 1916 his bookkeeper raised the account with him and was told to let it alone. So he knew the tests were low and he had, in his own hand's signature and in a direct warning, the evidence that the milk was not. What the sources do not establish is that he understood the sheet: Kyle watched him for a thousand signatures — "he read the totals and he never read the lines above them" — and his 1926 speech, which still blames water and dates the rise after a petition that came eighteen months later, shows a man who never absorbed what he signed. Knowledge of the low tests: certain. Knowledge that they were wrong: available to him, put to him once, and refused.

## Section F — Theory

**F1.** The cause was a short lot of glass. On 14 April 1897 Ammon & Sons of Tarnet sold the Association six dozen milk pipettes blown 17.6 c.c. for the Ostrey Hollow station; the lot delivered 16.72 c.c., 0.88 c.c. short, and it went into use on 1 May 1897. A butterfat test reads the length of a fat column in a bottle neck ruled for a 17.6 charge; with a 16.72 charge the column, and therefore the reading, is 16.72/17.6 = 95 percent of the truth, for every patron, every day. That is why the error had the size it had (one part in twenty, 0.19 of a point on 3.80 milk, 0.18 on 3.42 milk, 0.24 on 4.56 milk); why it was precise and never scattered (the same glass delivered the same short charge for a careful man and a careless one); why it was invisible from inside the plant (the pipette is the standard the plant measures with, and cannot be caught by measuring with itself); and why it preserved every herd's rank while putting the whole station wrong against the world. It began when the borrowed Larrow Green glass, which read 3.80 in 1896, was replaced by the short lot (the summary leaf: 1897 "part year," 3.61 from 1898). It ended on 12 June 1923 when Strawn, two seasons after the state issued burettes, ran the pipettes and condemned the crate; new glass in July 1923 gave 3.80 in 1924. Nothing on the farms or in the station had to change at either end because the milk was 3.80 all along: the fat went down the valley regardless, weighed heavy by Sill, and was booked every year as "station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual" — 76,000 lb, $19,000, over 1898–1922. The 1911 nine weeks were the same theory run as an experiment: one pipette exchanged, 3.80; exchanged back, 3.61.

Three competing explanations. (1) **Water in the cans** — Selby Vose (circular 1919, the board 1925, address 1926), Sill in his own way. Defeated by Strawn's lactometer at every visit, never any added water, and his telling Vose so in 1919; by the Brant book's two agreeing scales; by cheese — a cheesemaker sees watered milk in the vat the next morning and Cudd never saw it in sixteen summers; by the ledger, since watered milk sends less fat to the creamery and this cream arrived with more; by the chronology, the tests rising in July 1923, eighteen months before any petition; and by 1911, when the patrons did not change and the figure did. (2) **Theft of cream by the tester, covered by low tests** — Teague (1925, 1948), the valley (Rundle's Chapter IV, Vira, Oram's father). Defeated by the station-gain line in Teague's own hand (stolen cream arrives light; this arrived heavy every year); Sill's double weighing; 1911 (same man, figures rose); 1924 at 3.80 with Keddie still alone in the room; the proportional, scatter-free error; and the burette. (3) **Wet hay of Hessel Bottom** — the 1908 Grigg committee, Grigg again 1919, Kepp 1926, Falke briefly 1959. Defeated by Bulletin 214 (≤0.05 in winter, nothing measurable on pasture, against a gap of 0.19 in July and February alike — four times too small and the wrong shape); by Alder Corners, where the same meadow's summer milk ran 3.80; by 1911 and 1924, when nothing was done to the land; and by Falke's own withdrawal once he read the bulletin's figures.

Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware? No. The exhibits were four books; the crate was never mentioned in any session (Loomis took every word); the appliances the board saw tried in the weigh room were the new glass of 1923; the crate lay in the loft twenty feet above two of the three sessions. The board did have Strawn's 1923 notebook, which recorded the condemnation, and drew nothing from it.

What the tester believed. On the record, nothing: "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are," and a question mark he let stand fourteen years, because he would not write what he could not prove. Privately, the nearest witness — his son, seventeen in 1911 — heard him say twice that summer that it was "something in the glass," which fits the question mark, his 1902 remark to Orra that "it is my figures they will look at," and his letter's insistence that he knew what came into the room. Frick's recollection of a corridor remark that Keddie blamed the patrons is a single, uncorroborated memory of 1946 and sits against Keddie's own written words; I weigh it below Duncan's account but note the sources do not settle a private belief beyond doubt. Best answer: he suspected the glass from 1911 and would neither assert nor record it.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** In 1897 a Larrow Valley creamery cooperative bought six dozen milk pipettes for its Ostrey Hollow skimming station. The lot was short — 16.72 c.c. against a 17.6 mark — so for twenty-six years every butterfat test there read 95 percent of the truth: 3.61 instead of 3.80. Because the by-law made the station's own test govern pay, the Hessel Bottom farmers were underpaid 76,000 lb of fat, about $19,000, while the missing fat reached the creamery anyway and was booked yearly as "station gain, as usual." The valley blamed wet hay (1908), watered milk (1919) and a thieving tester; a 1925 arbitration paid $3,800 without finding a cause, and the tester, Ansel Keddie, was dismissed. An inspector had condemned the glass in 1923 without drawing the inference. In 1958 Keddie's granddaughter measured nine survivors from the crate. It took eight records because each — test book, ledger, receiving book, weigh book, inspector's notebook, cheese-factory book, invoice, bulletin — held one piece, and nobody laid them side by side.
