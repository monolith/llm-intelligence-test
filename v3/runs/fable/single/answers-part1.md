# Answer sheet — part 1 (Section A, stories 1–4)

Conventions: r01–r24 are the retellings in the order given. Where a figure was contested I use the resolved value and name the losing claim in brackets. Full contradiction handling is in Section E (part 3).

## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1 — The rule that made the test the master: the Association, Article VII, and the manager's water theory (1889–1926)

In 1889 the farmers of the Larrow Valley met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose was elected first president and held the office until 1911 (twenty-two years); he died in 1917. The main creamery was built on Larrow Creek at Larrow Green in 1891. Gideon drafted the by-laws himself. Article VII reads: "Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." The last clause is the hinge of everything that followed: the station's own figure, with no appeal. The year of adoption cannot be determined: Rundle (r12) says 1891, Loomis (r02) 1892, Grigg (r15) 1893; the printed pamphlet (Nym, Item 1) carries only "adopted at the annual meeting," and the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move — Loomis herself says nothing about the first four annual meetings can be spoken to from the record.

The officers of the long middle period: Effie Loomis, secretary 1901–1940, kept the minutes; Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945, the ledgers; Hobart Sill, buttermaker 1893–1934, the churn and receiving books; Selby Vose, manager 1913–1938 — Gideon's son, not nephew (Selby's own 1926 address says "my father, who was your first president"; Loomis, Duncan Keddie and Kyle agree; Vira Toland and Rundle's "nephew" rejected; Kyle's start year of 1911 rejected against Loomis, Vose himself and Strawn's 1913 note of a "new manager"). Bertram Kyle sat at the outer desk. The office was one room over a harness shop at Ashlin (Teague, Frick, Oram, Duncan; the header to r23 saying Larrow Green is an outlier). Loomis's own verdict: none of them ever stood in the Ostrey Hollow station on a working morning.

From 1902 the Hollow's patrons complained that their milk tested under the rest of the valley's. The office's answer was water. In June 1919 Vose signed a circular, typed by Kyle, to the station's nine patron families, warning that added water would be looked for; the tester carried it round by hand. The state inspector, Merle Strawn, had run the lactometer at that station at every visit and never found added water, and told Vose so in 1919. Vose argued water before the 1925 board and defended the circular in his 1926 address, claiming the tests rose "when the looking began." His own address dates the petition to January 1925, while the rise came in July 1923 with the new glass; the 1924 average was 3.80, not his 3.90. Rosalie Cudd's point completes the refutation: watered milk cannot be hidden in a cheese vat, and in sixteen summers the Bottom men's milk never gave one.

Documents: by-laws pamphlet (Item 1); Loomis's minutes and 1941 notes; Vose's 1926 address; Strawn's circuit books (1912–1934 lactometer entries); Kyle's 1953 account.

### Story 2 — Ansel Keddie, the test book, and the nine weeks of 1911 (1895–1934)

Ansel Keddie, born 1861, married Orra Brant in 1889. He went up to the station in 1895 to get it ready and opened it on 4 May 1896 on glass that Hobart Sill made up from his own bench at Larrow Green (first leaf: "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes"; Keddie's later letter says "the Ashlin creamery," but his own contemporaneous leaf and Sill's account outrank a slip of memory). He was tester and separator man, alone in the building, for twenty-nine seasons, 1896 through 1924. The day began at half past five; every can was weighed; a ten-day composite sample per patron was warmed and mixed; the charge drawn to the mark; four bottles in the hand tester; the fat read with dividers. The book had five columns — date, patron, pounds, test, fat — with errors struck and initialed and never overwritten. Strawn in 1907: "works slow and clean."

His summary leaf of 1922: 1896, 3.80; 1897, part year; 1898, 3.61, and 3.61 or within three hundredths every year since, except nine weeks of 1911. On 8 May 1911 he wrote "Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." The tests rose to 3.80 and held. On 10 July: "Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." He let the question mark stand fourteen years. His son Duncan, seventeen that summer, heard him say twice at supper that it was "something in the glass"; he would not write what he could not prove. The interval was nine weeks (8 May to 10 July is 63 days; Orra's diary of 8 May records the break); Teague's and Kepp's "six weeks" are rejected. Where the borrowed measure came from cannot be determined (Ivy says Larrow Green; Roy says the state man left it in June, impossible for a May start; Strawn's June entry is silent).

In 1924, on the new glass, the year averaged 3.80 in Keddie's own hand, while he was still tester. His statement to the board on 17 March 1925 was nine sentences: tested "thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons," "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are," never took a pound of cream. He was dismissed on 30 April 1925, four days before the award; he came home at his ordinary hour, hung up his apron and ate ham. He kept four or five cows and did carpentry, and died at home in February 1934, nine years under the imputation. Orra died in 1948, ten years before the clearing.

Documents: the test book (first leaf, the 1911 entries, the 1922 summary leaf; impounded March 1925, Association office 1925–1959, Historical Rooms from 1959 — Nym's header "kept by E. Brant" is a curator's slip); his statement (Item 8); the unsent 1925 letter; Orra's diary; Duncan's 1968 notes; Strawn's 1907, 1916 and 1924 entries; Frick's 1946 recollection ("an honest man was ruined by a figure nobody could explain").

### Story 3 — "Five and a quarter per cent, as usual": the ledger's station-gain line (1897–1925, read at last 1939–1958)

Every year the Association's station account carried one entry, in this form for 1898: "Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account." The arithmetic: 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26 percent of the fat credited; 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.00 percent of the fat delivered. Teague (r05) and Vaught (r11) both say the gain was five and a quarter percent "of the fat actually in the cream"; the entry's own numbers say otherwise, and Ivy Keddie and Nym state it correctly.

Over the twenty-five full delivery years 1898–1922 the station received 40,000,000 lb of milk, 1,600,000 a year (Loomis from Teague's own annual sheets, added twice; Kyle, who got the figure out for the arbitration; Nym; Bulletin 471). Teague's and Rundle's 44,000,000 are rejected: 1,444,000 lb credited ÷ 3.61 percent = 40,000,000. Fat credited 1,444,000 lb; fat received at Larrow Green 1,520,000 lb; the gain 76,000 lb (3,040 × 25). At the ledger average of 25 cents a pound (Loomis, Kyle, Roy Brant's own book; Sill's and Kepp's 27 cents rejected), $19,000.

Teague calls the line "my own device," begun when he came in 1905, yet quotes the 1898 entry as being "in the same hand for as long as the hand was mine," and Nym has the station column running from 1897; Sill says the office sheet showed the Hollow's cream heavy from his earliest years. Who originated the line cannot be settled; it predates 1905. Sill weighed every Hollow can twice, at the platform and again in the cold room, from 1893 on, and found more fat than the sheet said had been bought — always more, never less — and thought nothing of it ("so does everybody's"). Kyle copied "as usual" for twenty-five years: "a figure with a name on it has been explained." Vose signed the annual sheet every spring, thumb on the bottom figure. Teague put the account to Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone; then, in 1925 and again in 1948, he argued that the tester was taking cream — a theory his own line refutes, since stolen cream reaches the creamery light and this cream reached it heavy every year. Rundle saw that on one look at the ledgers in 1939 and printed a retraction while his pamphlet was in press. The 1925 award of $3,800 was charged to the same station account, "and it balanced." Bulletin 471 (1958): the 76,000 lb "stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

Documents: annual ledgers (Item 6); Teague's 1948 chapter; Sill's receiving and churn books (Item 16) and his 1934 remarks; Kyle's 1953 account; Rundle's 1939 press note; Loomis §7.

### Story 4 — Hessel Bottom, the Brants, and the weigh book (1897–1963)

Hessel Bottom is 160 acres of wet meadow under Ostrey Hollow. Six farms had rights on it (Roy Brant, Hazel Brant, the 1908 report, Kepp, the Chronicle; Loomis's "seven" rejected). Nine patron families hauled to the station (Vira Toland's family received one of the nine circulars; Keddie's letter, Roy, Hazel and Sill say nine; Orra's diary "eleven" rejected). Strawn's 1909 note that the station took from the Bottom farms "and three places on the hill" is one way nine is made up; Hazel's "a farm can hold more than one family" is another; the sources do not settle the composition.

Emmet Brant (1854–1929) had sixty-odd acres of the meadow and thirty-eight cows in a good year — the station's largest patron in every recorded year. From 1 January 1897 (the 1925 exhibit list, Nym's provenance, Duncan; Roy's 1898 and Hazel's 1900 rejected) he entered every can twice: his own barn scale, then the station's figure. The two columns agreed for a lifetime, barring a slopped pound — whatever was wrong at that station, it was not the weighing. His leaf for 1910: "Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42." Butterfat that year was 24 cents. Corrected, 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60: credited 6,840 lb, delivered 7,200, 360 lb unpaid on one farm in one year.

The Bottom's milk ran seventeen hundredths under Larrow Green's 3.78 (Roy) and nineteen hundredths under its own truth of 3.80 — and, as Roy and Hazel insisted, the same in July as in February, which no feed effect does. The first to say so aloud, in 1902, were laughed at; Emmet was one. He read the 1908 hay report twice and put it in the drawer: the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not. The June 1919 water circular went to nine kitchens; two days later Emmet stood in the Keddie yard and asked his brother-in-law why he had carried it himself, and the two never spoke again until Emmet died in spring 1929. His name was on the January 1925 petition. From 1915 four of the six Bottom men hauled over the ridge in summer; the Brants, with the biggest herd, did not.

Family: Orra Keddie was Emmet's full sister (Roy, Emmet's son, rebuts the stepmother story explicitly; Hazel's "half sister" rejected). Duncan Keddie and Roy Brant were first cousins; Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant second cousins. The book went to Roy in 1929, was lent — not left — to Ivy in 1958 and returned, to Hazel at Roy's death in 1962, and to the Historical Rooms in 1963 (Nym's Item 4; its header "kept by A. Keddie" is a slip). It was Exhibit 3 before the 1925 board.

Documents: the Brant weigh book (1910 leaf); Roy's 1955 account and 1958 postscript; Hazel's 1981 tape; Vira Toland's 1979 tape; Orra's diary (1908, June 1919, January 1925, spring 1929); Duncan's notes; the 1908 report's description of the Bottom.
