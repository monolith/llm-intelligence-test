# Answer sheet — part 2 (Section A, stories 5–8; Section B)

### Story 5 — The hay theory: the 1908 committee, Bulletin No. 214, and the long life of a wrong answer (1908–1959)

By the winter of 1907–08 the Hollow's grumbling had reached the annual meeting and the board named a committee of three under Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green, a Larrow Green patron with no stake in the station's figures. Orra Keddie's diary has them arriving in Grigg's sleigh in January 1908, going first to Emmet Brant's, wanting the barns and not the station, and gone by March. Their report (Nym, Item 11) says they sampled the six Hessel Bottom herds through January, February and March 1908, drew and sealed their own samples, tried the station's scales and the patrons' scales, watched the test made "and saw nothing whatever in the doing of it to remark upon," found "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter," and concluded that "the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station." The report was accepted and closed the question for eleven years. In his 1919 covering letter, reprinting it when the complaint was reopened, Grigg says the committee sampled "the whole of 1908 — every month of that year"; his own report, printed beneath, and the diary say winter only.

The science the theory leaned on cut against it. The State Agricultural College at Farland ran a feeding trial and published Bulletin No. 214 in 1911: "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Grigg (1919) and Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S. (1926 paper to the Farm Club), both cite the bulletin as "two tenths" in winter; the bulletin's summary paragraph, transcribed identically by Ivy Keddie, Nym, Vaught and Falke, says five hundredths. Kepp added a field estimate of "a fifth of a point" for every wet bottom in the county, put the station's gap at 0.21, fat at 27 cents, and dismissed the 1911 interval as "six weeks of June and July" — it was nine weeks from 8 May, and the figures fell the day the station's own glass came back, mid-July, not with any change of pasture.

What defeats the theory: the gap was nineteen hundredths flat in every month — the bulletin's worst case, 0.05 in winter and nil in summer, is four times too small and the wrong shape (Ivy: "It is four times too small and it is the wrong shape"); the 1911 interval moved the figure without touching hay, herds or season; the same meadow's milk tested 3.80 at Alder Corners in the flush; the 1924 average went to 3.80 with nothing done to the land. County agent Falke, in 1959, first allowed the hay as "a real if partial factor," then obtained Bulletin 214 and withdrew the concession: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July." The 1908 committee's other findings — milk whole, scales honest, nobody dishonest — were right.

Documents: 1908 report (Item 11) and Grigg's 1919 reprint; Bulletin No. 214 (Item 12); Kepp's 1926 paper and discussion; Orra's diary (January and March 1908); Falke's 1959 report and note; Roy and Hazel Brant on the year-round flatness.

### Story 6 — Over the ridge: the Alder Corners Cheese Company and the four Hessel Bottom men (1913–1970)

Jerome Cudd built the Alder Corners Cheese Company on the flat below the church at Alder Corners, Vessey County, over Redlow Ridge from Ostrey Hollow — built in 1913 by his daughter's account, opened for business in 1914 by Rundle's and Roy Brant's; the two are reconcilable and I do not treat them as a conflict. He made cheese there until he died in March 1941, between the setting and the cutting of a vat; his daughter Rosalie Cudd (daughter, on her own word and Duncan's and Rundle's; Ivy Keddie's and the Chronicle's "niece" rejected) took the factory that spring and still had it in 1958. The ridge road, four miles of switchback, shuts from November to April or May; nobody from Ordell County ever hauled to Alder Corners in winter.

From 1915 four of the six Hessel Bottom farmers hauled their milk over the ridge in the summer flush — about the first of June to mid-September — and to their own station the rest of the year: cheese paid better than butter in flush, Cudd bought everything he could get, and the ridge was a shorter pull while it was open. It was resented at home as going over to the enemy. They came for sixteen seasons, 1915 through 1930. Cudd tested on his own bench with his own glass and never compared a figure with anyone; "it never entered his head." His season averages for the four men together, copied straight off the leaves by Rosalie in 1958, run 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.80, 3.80, 3.82, 3.79, 3.78 (1915–1922) and 3.81, 3.80, 3.79, 3.82, 3.78, 3.80, 3.81, 3.79 (1923–1930) — between 3.78 and 3.82 every summer, wet years and dry, about 3.80. His entry for 1919: "The four Hessel Bottom men, summer of 1919: 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge." In sixteen summers he never docked or complained of one of them; a cheesemaker knows watered milk the next morning because the curd will not knit.

The significance: 3.80 is exactly 3.61 ÷ 0.95. The same milk off the same wet ground tested nineteen hundredths higher on independent glass in another county, in the very months when the hay theory allowed no effect at all. The comparison is valid for the faulty-glass summers, 1915–1922 (eight full seasons) and the first weeks of 1923; from July 1923 the station itself read 3.80. Ivy's talk speaks of "fourteen seasons" of overlap; the sources give no station-side seasonal series to check that count against, and it is not needed for the point.

Provenance: the test book passed from Jerome to Rosalie in 1941 and to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970; it has never left Vessey County. The figures reached Ordell County only as Rosalie's 1958 copies (Nym, Item 13, correspondence file, 1919 season). Ivy Keddie never saw the book itself.

Documents: Rosalie Cudd's 1958 letter with its sixteen-season table; the Alder Corners test book (via copies); Roy Brant on the hauling; Rundle §V; Duncan's provenance note and 1970 addition.

### Story 7 — The arbitration at Ashlin and the station weigh room (January–May 1925)

In January 1925 the patrons of the Ostrey Hollow station petitioned the Association to be made whole for a quarter-century of low tests; Emmet Brant's name was on the paper. The by-laws provided for arbitration and the officers did not resist it. A board of three was constituted under Alonzo Frick, attorney, of Ashlin — chairman, he supposed, because he kept no cow. It sat three times between 17 March and 4 May 1925: the first session at the Association's office at Ashlin on 17 March, the second and third, at Frick's insistence, in the station's own weigh room, with the men standing outside in the road and the cream hauler's son on the platform in the rain. Every appliance then in use was tried before the board — scales, sampling, a test made twice the same way — on the glass installed in July 1923.

Three explanations were argued. Water: the manager, Selby Vose, standing by his 1919 circular, "without heat and without proof." Theft: the bookkeeper, Wilbur Teague, describing how a station account would look if one man were helping himself, then observing that he charged nobody; his own ledger line, showing the cream arriving heavy, was in the building and nobody laid it beside the accusation. Hay: the 1908 report read into the record, with the advantage of blaming nobody in the room. Keddie's nine-sentence statement was made on 17 March. Four exhibits and nothing else: the station test book (4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924), the station receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book (lent, and wanted back), and the state inspector's circuit notebook for 1923 — the volume containing the condemnation of the pipettes. Nobody laid the second beside the fourth. The condemned crate was twenty feet above two of the three sessions and was not mentioned once in any session (Loomis, who took every word). The "books and appliances" of the award's phrase were the four books and the new glass.

On 30 April 1925 the Association dismissed Keddie, while the board was still sitting. On 4 May the board "finds no cause established" and awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800, without admission, charged to the station account — recorded by Loomis, entered by Teague, "and it balanced." Vira Toland's and Cleve Oram's $3,500 are rejected against the minute-book text, Frick, Teague, Vose and Falke. The award was one fifth of the $19,000 the ledgers show unpaid. What the patrons themselves claimed cannot be determined: Frick gives 67,000 lb of fat, Rundle $19,600 "to the dollar," and the two do not reconcile at any price the sources give. Frick, in 1946: "an award without a finding is an unsatisfactory thing and every member of that board knew it"; he believed Keddie then and later, and thought ill of the Association for the dismissal.

Documents: the arbitration minute book (Item 7) and award text; Keddie's statement (Item 8); Frick's exhibit list; Loomis §6 and §8; Teague's chapter; Vose's 1926 address; Kyle's list of the four books; Orra's diary (17 March, April, 30 April 1925); Duncan and Oram on the sessions under the crate.

### Story 8 — What the crate held: Ammon & Sons' glass, Strawn's burette, and Ivy Keddie's measurement (1897–1959)

On 14 April 1897 Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet, invoiced the Association for the Ostrey Hollow station's own outfit: six dozen milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at 35 cents ($25.20); twelve dozen 8 percent test bottles at 22 cents ($31.68); an acid measure (90 cents); a four-bottle hand tester ($18.00); freight ($2.15); total $77.93 (the lines add; the Chronicle's and Junia Ammon's "$77.39" and Vaught's and Oram's "five dozen" rejected). It went into use on 1 May 1897 — Orra's diary of that day, the office file, the invoice date, Bulletin 471's own conclusion and Keddie's "1897, part year"; Vaught's and Kyle's "1 May 1898" rejected. The lot was short: Junia Ammon's belief, written in 1949, is that a reground mold ran the lot consistently wrong and a consistency check cannot catch consistency; the works books burned in 1931, so the cause on the glass-house side cannot be determined, and the Chronicle's charge of a knowing fraud is unsupported.

Merle Strawn rode the Ordell circuit from 1907 to 1936, tested the station's scales and lactometer every visit, and never found water. The state issued burettes to field men in spring 1921; he skipped the station's glass in 1922 for want of daylight, and on 12 June 1923 ran the pipettes: "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them." The Association ordered on 28 June; Ammon replied 2 July, entered six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles, and said its books of the nineties could tell nothing. New glass was in use from July 1923 and checked true in October; the 1924 average stood at 3.80. The burette program was discontinued in 1926. The crate went into the loft over the intake room. Vose's and Rundle's "12 July" is rejected: the order of 28 June postdates the condemnation.

In February 1958 Ivy Keddie, born 1931, Ansel's granddaughter, a graduate student at Farland, climbed the ladder and found the crate under a tarpaulin, stenciled AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET, with nine unbroken pipettes (the Chronicle's and Rundle's "eleven" rejected against the measurement sheet's nine values and the curator's count). On 9 March 1958 at Farland she measured them against a calibrated burette, five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 — mean 16.72 c.c. against 17.6 blown, short 0.88 c.c., 95 hundredths of the mark. Bulletin No. 471, October 1958, under Professor Leland Vaught and Ivy Keddie, concluded: no milk watered, no cream stolen; from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 the tests were low by one part in twenty because the pipettes were short by one part in twenty; the difference, 76,000 lb of butterfat, stands in the ledgers as station gain. Vaught's own method section carries a transposed 16.27 c.c. and its false consequences (0.9244, 3.905, 118,000 lb), which his conclusion paragraph and the sheet contradict. The Ashlin Chronicle ran it on 3 October 1958; Ivy read "What the Crate Held" to the Historical Society in 1959; Falke reported to the county board the same year. Thirty-five years from condemnation to cause; thirty-three from dismissal; Keddie twenty-four years dead. The crate and the test book went to the Historical Rooms in 1959.

Documents: the invoice (Item 2; Ammon facsimile); Ammon's letter of 2 July 1923 (Item 10); Strawn's 1921, 1922, 12 June 1923, October 1923, 1924 and 1926 entries (Item 9 photostat); the measurement sheet (Item 14); Bulletin No. 471 (Item 15); the crate itself (Item 5); Ivy's 1959 talk; Junia Ammon's 1949 chapter; the Chronicle; Falke's report.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie and Ansel Keddie.** Granddaughter. Ansel (b. 1861) and Orra had one son in the sources, Duncan (b. 1894), who taught school at Ashlin; Duncan states plainly that "my daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter," and Ivy opens her 1959 talk with "Ansel Keddie ... was my grandfather. My father, Duncan Keddie ... was his son." Oram calls her "the granddaughter." Two sources say great-niece: the Ashlin Chronicle (assembled from a college press release) and Falke's report at page two — and Falke, in his closing note, says he has verified that she is the granddaughter and the error was his. The Chronicle even records that her father taught school in Ashlin for many years, which identifies Duncan. The contrary value is one press-release error carried twice, and one of its carriers retracted it.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant.** Second cousins. Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie (née Brant) were brother and sister; Emmet's son Roy and Orra's son Duncan were therefore first cousins (both Roy and Duncan say so in the same words); Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are the children of first cousins, which is second cousins. Rundle's 1959 footnote agrees. Vira Toland and Cleve Oram say "first cousin once removed"; that would require one of the two to be a generation nearer Emmet and Orra than she is, and neither is — the age gap (Ivy born 1931; Hazel old enough to remember the 1919 circular) is what invites the mistake. Left open by the sources: whether Emmet and Orra were full siblings (Roy: same mother and father) or half siblings by Emmet's father's second wife (Hazel) — it does not change the degree of cousinship; Roy's mother and Duncan's wife are never named; Hazel's birth year is never given; and Roy's own birth year is only inferable (sixty-seven in the winter of 1955, so about 1888).

**B3. Selby Vose's father.** Gideon Vose — the Association's first president (1889–1911) and the man who drafted the by-laws, including Article VII. Selby says so himself in his 1926 address ("my father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law"), and Loomis ("son of the late president"), Duncan Keddie and Kyle confirm; Vira Toland's and Rundle's "nephew" is village talk. It matters twice. First, the rule the father wrote — pay on the station's own test, and nothing else shall govern — is what turned a short pipette into money: under payment by weight no one would have lost a dollar. Second, the son ran the office for twenty-five years (1913–1938) under that rule, signed every annual sheet that carried "station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual," was told by his bookkeeper in 1916 to look at the account and told him to let it alone, sent the 1919 water circular, argued water before the board, and in 1926 was still explaining the figures by the men. The family that wrote the rule owned the office that never went up the road and looked — Loomis's "the family always thought the valley belonged to them a little," said less kindly.

**B4. The Vose family and the Keddie family.** No relationship is established. Two narrators assert one: Vira Toland ("Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins. Everybody knew that") and Cleve Oram ("cousins of some sort ... my mother had it from her mother ... I could not draw you the line on paper"). Both are hearsay of hearsay. Against them stands Duncan Keddie, Orra's son, writing specifically to put the family right: there was "no connection whatever" with the Voses; people confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Gideon either; his mother "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it." Any cousinship would have run through the Brants, and neither Roy nor Hazel Brant, who set out the family carefully, mentions one. A negative cannot be proved from these papers, so strictly the sources cannot settle it; but the affirmative claim rests on talk alone and is denied by the only witness in a position to know.
