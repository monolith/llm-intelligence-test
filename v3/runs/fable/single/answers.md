## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1 — The rule that made the test the master: the Association, Article VII, and the manager's water theory (1889–1926)

In 1889 the farmers of the Larrow Valley met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose was elected first president and held the office until 1911 (twenty-two years); he died in 1917. The main creamery was built on Larrow Creek at Larrow Green in 1891. Gideon drafted the by-laws himself. Article VII reads: "Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." The last clause is the hinge of everything that followed: the station's own figure, with no appeal. The year of adoption cannot be determined: Rundle (r12) says 1891, Loomis (r02) 1892, Grigg (r15) 1893; the printed pamphlet (Nym, Item 1) carries only "adopted at the annual meeting," and the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move — Loomis herself says nothing about the first four annual meetings can be spoken to from the record.

The officers of the long middle period: Effie Loomis, secretary 1901–1940, kept the minutes; Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945, the ledgers; Hobart Sill, buttermaker 1893–1934, the churn and receiving books; Selby Vose, manager 1913–1938 — Gideon's son, not nephew (Selby's own 1926 address says "my father, who was your first president"; Loomis, Duncan Keddie and Kyle agree; Vira Toland and Rundle's "nephew" rejected; Kyle's start year of 1911 rejected against Loomis, Vose himself and Strawn's 1913 note of a "new manager"). Bertram Kyle sat at the outer desk. The office was one room over a harness shop at Ashlin (Teague, Frick, Oram, Duncan; the header to r23 saying Larrow Green is an outlier). Loomis's own verdict: none of them ever stood in the Ostrey Hollow station on a working morning.

From 1902 the Hollow's patrons complained that their milk tested under the rest of the valley's. The office's answer was water. In June 1919 Vose signed a circular, typed by Kyle, to the station's nine patron families, warning that added water would be looked for; the tester carried it round by hand. The state inspector, Merle Strawn, had run the lactometer at that station at every visit and never found added water, and told Vose so in 1919. Vose argued water before the 1925 board and defended the circular in his 1926 address, claiming the tests rose "when the looking began." His own address dates the petition to January 1925, while the rise came in July 1923 with the new glass; the 1924 average was 3.80, not his 3.90. Rosalie Cudd's point completes the refutation: watered milk cannot be hidden in a cheese vat, and in sixteen summers the Bottom men's milk never gave one.

Documents: by-laws pamphlet (Item 1); Loomis's minutes and 1941 notes; Vose's 1926 address; Strawn's circuit books (1912–1934 lactometer entries); Kyle's 1953 account.

### Story 2 — Ansel Keddie, the test book, and the nine weeks of 1911 (1895–1934)

Ansel Keddie, born 1861, married Orra Brant in 1889. He went up to the station in 1895 to get it ready and opened it on 4 May 1896 on glass that Hobart Sill made up from his own bench at Larrow Green (first leaf: "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes"; Keddie's later letter says "the Ashlin creamery," but his own contemporaneous leaf and Sill's account outrank a slip of memory). He was tester and separator man, alone in the building, for twenty-nine seasons, 1896 through 1924. The day began at half past five; every can was weighed; a ten-day composite sample per patron was warmed and mixed; the charge drawn to the mark; four bottles in the hand tester; the fat read with dividers. The book had five columns — date, patron, pounds, test, fat — with errors struck and initialed and never overwritten. Strawn in 1907: "works slow and clean."

His summary leaf of 1922: 1896, 3.80; 1897, part year; 1898, 3.61, and 3.61 or within three hundredths every year since, except nine weeks of 1911. On 8 May 1911 he wrote "Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." The tests rose to 3.80 and held. On 10 July: "Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." He let the question mark stand fourteen years. His son Duncan, seventeen that summer, heard him say twice at supper that it was "something in the glass"; he would not write what he could not prove. The interval was nine weeks (8 May to 10 July is 63 days; Orra's diary of 8 May records the break); Teague's and Kepp's "six weeks" are rejected. Where the borrowed measure came from cannot be determined (Ivy says Larrow Green; Roy says the state man left it in June, impossible for a May start; Strawn's June entry is silent).

In 1924, on the new glass, the year averaged 3.80 in Keddie's own hand, while he was still tester. His statement to the board on 17 March 1925 was nine sentences: tested "thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons," "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are," never took a pound of cream. He was dismissed on 30 April 1925, four days before the award; he came home at his ordinary hour, hung up his apron and ate ham. He kept four or five cows and did carpentry, and died at home in February 1934, nine years under the imputation. Orra died in 1948, ten years before the clearing.

Documents: the test book (first leaf, the 1911 entries, the 1922 summary leaf; impounded March 1925, Association office 1925–1959, Historical Rooms from 1959 — Nym's header "kept by E. Brant" is a curator's slip); his statement (Item 8); the unsent 1925 letter; Orra's diary; Duncan's 1968 notes; Strawn's 1907, 1916 and 1924 entries; Frick's 1946 recollection ("an honest man was ruined by a figure nobody could explain").

### Story 3 — "Five and a quarter per cent, as usual": the ledger's station-gain line (1897–1925, read at last 1939–1958)

Every year the Association's station account carried one entry, in this form for 1898: "Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account." The arithmetic: 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26 percent of the fat credited; 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.00 percent of the fat delivered. Teague (r05) and Vaught (r11) both say the gain was five and a quarter percent "of the fat actually in the cream"; the entry's own numbers say otherwise, and Ivy Keddie and Nym state it correctly.

Over the twenty-five full delivery years 1898–1922 the station received 40,000,000 lb of milk, 1,600,000 a year (Loomis from Teague's own annual sheets, added twice; Kyle, who got the figure out for the arbitration; Nym; Bulletin 471). Teague's and Rundle's 44,000,000 are rejected: 1,444,000 lb credited ÷ 3.61 percent = 40,000,000. Fat credited 1,444,000 lb; fat received at Larrow Green 1,520,000 lb; the gain 76,000 lb (3,040 × 25). At the ledger average of 25 cents a pound (Loomis, Kyle, Roy Brant's own book; Sill's and Kepp's 27 cents rejected), $19,000.

Teague calls the line "my own device," begun when he came in 1905, yet quotes the 1898 entry as being "in the same hand for as long as the hand was mine," and Nym has the station column running from 1897; Sill says the office sheet showed the Hollow's cream heavy from his earliest years. Who originated the line cannot be settled; it predates 1905. Sill weighed every Hollow can twice, at the platform and again in the cold room, from 1893 on, and found more fat than the sheet said had been bought — always more, never less — and thought nothing of it ("so does everybody's"). Kyle copied "as usual" for twenty-five years: "a figure with a name on it has been explained." Vose signed the annual sheet every spring, thumb on the bottom figure. Teague put the account to Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone; then, in 1925 and again in 1948, he argued that the tester was taking cream — a theory his own line refutes, since stolen cream reaches the creamery light and this cream reached it heavy every year. Rundle saw that on one look at the ledgers in 1939 and printed a retraction while his pamphlet was in press. The 1925 award of $3,800 was charged to the same station account, "and it balanced." Bulletin 471 (1958): the 76,000 lb "stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

Documents: annual ledgers (Item 6); Teague's 1948 chapter; Sill's receiving and churn books (Item 16) and his 1934 remarks; Kyle's 1953 account; Rundle's 1939 press note; Loomis §7.

### Story 4 — Hessel Bottom, the Brants, and the weigh book (1897–1963)

Hessel Bottom is 160 acres of wet meadow under Ostrey Hollow. Six farms had rights on it (Roy Brant, Hazel Brant, the 1908 report, Kepp, the Chronicle; Loomis's "seven" rejected). Nine patron families hauled to the station (Vira Toland's family received one of the nine circulars; Keddie's letter, Roy, Hazel and Sill say nine; Orra's diary "eleven" rejected). Strawn's 1909 note that the station took from the Bottom farms "and three places on the hill" is one way nine is made up; Hazel's "a farm can hold more than one family" is another; the sources do not settle the composition.

Emmet Brant (1854–1929) had sixty-odd acres of the meadow and thirty-eight cows in a good year — the station's largest patron in every recorded year. From 1 January 1897 (the 1925 exhibit list, Nym's provenance, Duncan; Roy's 1898 and Hazel's 1900 rejected) he entered every can twice: his own barn scale, then the station's figure. The two columns agreed for a lifetime, barring a slopped pound — whatever was wrong at that station, it was not the weighing. His leaf for 1910: "Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42." Butterfat that year was 24 cents. Corrected, 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60: credited 6,840 lb, delivered 7,200, 360 lb unpaid on one farm in one year.

The Bottom's milk ran seventeen hundredths under Larrow Green's 3.78 (Roy) and nineteen hundredths under its own truth of 3.80 — and, as Roy and Hazel insisted, the same in July as in February, which no feed effect does. The first to say so aloud, in 1902, were laughed at; Emmet was one. He read the 1908 hay report twice and put it in the drawer: the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not. The June 1919 water circular went to nine kitchens; two days later Emmet stood in the Keddie yard and asked his brother-in-law why he had carried it himself, and the two never spoke again until Emmet died in spring 1929. His name was on the January 1925 petition. From 1915 four of the six Bottom men hauled over the ridge in summer; the Brants, with the biggest herd, did not.

Family: Orra Keddie was Emmet's full sister (Roy, Emmet's son, rebuts the stepmother story explicitly; Hazel's "half sister" rejected). Duncan Keddie and Roy Brant were first cousins; Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant second cousins. The book went to Roy in 1929, was lent — not left — to Ivy in 1958 and returned, to Hazel at Roy's death in 1962, and to the Historical Rooms in 1963 (Nym's Item 4; its header "kept by A. Keddie" is a slip). It was Exhibit 3 before the 1925 board.

Documents: the Brant weigh book (1910 leaf); Roy's 1955 account and 1958 postscript; Hazel's 1981 tape; Vira Toland's 1979 tape; Orra's diary (1908, June 1919, January 1925, spring 1929); Duncan's notes; the 1908 report's description of the Bottom.

### Story 5 — The hay theory: the 1908 committee, Bulletin No. 214, and the long life of a wrong answer (1908–1959)

By the winter of 1907–08 the Hollow's grumbling had reached the annual meeting and the board named a committee of three under Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green, a Larrow Green patron with no stake in the station's figures. Orra Keddie's diary has them arriving in Grigg's sleigh in January 1908, going first to Emmet Brant's, wanting the barns and not the station, and gone by March. Their report (Nym, Item 11) says they sampled the six Hessel Bottom herds through January, February and March 1908, drew and sealed their own samples, tried the station's scales and the patrons' scales, watched the test made "and saw nothing whatever in the doing of it to remark upon," found "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter," and concluded that "the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station." The report was accepted and closed the question for eleven years. In his 1919 covering letter, reprinting it when the complaint was reopened, Grigg says the committee sampled "the whole of 1908 — every month of that year"; his own report, printed beneath, and the diary say winter only.

The science the theory leaned on cut against it. The State Agricultural College at Farland ran a feeding trial and published Bulletin No. 214 in 1911: "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Grigg (1919) and Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S. (1926 paper to the Farm Club), both cite the bulletin as "two tenths" in winter; the bulletin's summary paragraph, transcribed identically by Ivy Keddie, Nym, Vaught and Falke, says five hundredths. Kepp added a field estimate of "a fifth of a point" for every wet bottom in the county, put the station's gap at 0.21, fat at 27 cents, and dismissed the 1911 interval as "six weeks of June and July" — it was nine weeks from 8 May, and the figures fell the day the station's own glass came back, mid-July, not with any change of pasture.

What defeats the theory: the gap was nineteen hundredths flat in every month — the bulletin's worst case, 0.05 in winter and nil in summer, is four times too small and the wrong shape (Ivy: "It is four times too small and it is the wrong shape"); the 1911 interval moved the figure without touching hay, herds or season; the same meadow's milk tested 3.80 at Alder Corners in the flush; the 1924 average went to 3.80 with nothing done to the land. County agent Falke, in 1959, first allowed the hay as "a real if partial factor," then obtained Bulletin 214 and withdrew the concession: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July." The 1908 committee's other findings — milk whole, scales honest, nobody dishonest — were right.

Documents: 1908 report (Item 11) and Grigg's 1919 reprint; Bulletin No. 214 (Item 12); Kepp's 1926 paper and discussion; Orra's diary (January and March 1908); Falke's 1959 report and note; Roy and Hazel Brant on the year-round flatness.

### Story 6 — Over the ridge: the Alder Corners Cheese Company and the four Hessel Bottom men (1913–1970)

Jerome Cudd built the Alder Corners Cheese Company on the flat below the church at Alder Corners, Vessey County, over Redlow Ridge from Ostrey Hollow — built in 1913 by his daughter's account, opened for business in 1914 by Rundle's and Roy Brant's; the two are reconcilable and I do not treat them as a conflict. He made cheese there until he died in March 1941, between the setting and the cutting of a vat; his daughter Rosalie Cudd (daughter, on her own word and Duncan's and Rundle's; Ivy Keddie's and the Chronicle's "niece" rejected) took the factory that spring and still had it in 1958. The ridge road, four miles of switchback, shuts from November to April or May; nobody from Ordell County ever hauled to Alder Corners in winter.

From 1915 four of the six Hessel Bottom farmers hauled their milk over the ridge in the summer flush — about the first of June to mid-September — and to their own station the rest of the year: cheese paid better than butter in flush, Cudd bought everything he could get, and the ridge was a shorter pull while it was open. It was resented at home as going over to the enemy. They came for sixteen seasons, 1915 through 1930. Cudd tested on his own bench with his own glass and never compared a figure with anyone; "it never entered his head." His season averages for the four men together, copied straight off the leaves by Rosalie in 1958, run 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.80, 3.80, 3.82, 3.79, 3.78 (1915–1922) and 3.81, 3.80, 3.79, 3.82, 3.78, 3.80, 3.81, 3.79 (1923–1930) — between 3.78 and 3.82 every summer, wet years and dry, about 3.80. His entry for 1919: "The four Hessel Bottom men, summer of 1919: 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge." In sixteen summers he never docked or complained of one of them; a cheesemaker knows watered milk the next morning because the curd will not knit.

The significance: 3.80 is exactly 3.61 ÷ 0.95. The same milk off the same wet ground tested nineteen hundredths higher on independent glass in another county, in the very months when the hay theory allowed no effect at all. The comparison is valid for the faulty-glass summers, 1915–1922 (eight full seasons) and the first weeks of 1923; from July 1923 the station itself read 3.80. Ivy's talk speaks of "fourteen seasons" of overlap; the sources give no station-side seasonal series to check that count against, and it is not needed for the point.

Provenance: the test book passed from Jerome to Rosalie in 1941 and to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970; it has never left Vessey County. The figures reached Ordell County only as Rosalie's 1958 copies (Nym, Item 13, correspondence file, 1919 season). Ivy Keddie never saw the book itself.

Documents: Rosalie Cudd's 1958 letter with its sixteen-season table; the Alder Corners test book (via copies); Roy Brant on the hauling; Rundle §V; Duncan's provenance note and 1970 addition.

### Story 7 — The arbitration at Ashlin and the station weigh room (January–May 1925)

In January 1925 the patrons of the Ostrey Hollow station petitioned the Association to be made whole for a quarter-century of low tests; Emmet Brant's name was on the paper. The by-laws provided for arbitration and the officers did not resist it. A board of three was constituted under Alonzo Frick, attorney, of Ashlin — chairman, he supposed, because he kept no cow. It sat three times between 17 March and 4 May 1925: the first session at the Association's office at Ashlin on 17 March, the second and third, at Frick's insistence, in the station's own weigh room, with the men standing outside in the road and the cream hauler's son on the platform in the rain. Every appliance then in use was tried before the board — scales, sampling, a test made twice the same way — on the glass installed in July 1923.

Three explanations were argued. Water: the manager, Selby Vose, standing by his 1919 circular, "without heat and without proof." Theft: the bookkeeper, Wilbur Teague, describing how a station account would look if one man were helping himself, then observing that he charged nobody; his own ledger line, showing the cream arriving heavy, was in the building and nobody laid it beside the accusation. Hay: the 1908 report read into the record, with the advantage of blaming nobody in the room. Keddie's nine-sentence statement was made on 17 March. Four exhibits and nothing else: the station test book (4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924), the station receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book (lent, and wanted back), and the state inspector's circuit notebook for 1923 — the volume containing the condemnation of the pipettes. Nobody laid the second beside the fourth. The condemned crate was twenty feet above two of the three sessions and was not mentioned once in any session (Loomis, who took every word). The "books and appliances" of the award's phrase were the four books and the new glass.

On 30 April 1925 the Association dismissed Keddie, while the board was still sitting. On 4 May the board "finds no cause established" and awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800, without admission, charged to the station account — recorded by Loomis, entered by Teague, "and it balanced." Vira Toland's and Cleve Oram's $3,500 are rejected against the minute-book text, Frick, Teague, Vose and Falke. The award was one fifth of the $19,000 the ledgers show unpaid. What the patrons themselves claimed cannot be determined: Frick gives 67,000 lb of fat, Rundle $19,600 "to the dollar," and the two do not reconcile at any price the sources give. Frick, in 1946: "an award without a finding is an unsatisfactory thing and every member of that board knew it"; he believed Keddie then and later, and thought ill of the Association for the dismissal.

Documents: the arbitration minute book (Item 7) and award text; Keddie's statement (Item 8); Frick's exhibit list; Loomis §6 and §8; Teague's chapter; Vose's 1926 address; Kyle's list of the four books; Orra's diary (17 March, April, 30 April 1925); Duncan and Oram on the sessions under the crate.

### Story 8 — What the crate held: Ammon & Sons' glass, Strawn's burette, and Ivy Keddie's measurement (1897–1959)

On 14 April 1897 Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet, invoiced the Association for the Ostrey Hollow station's own outfit: six dozen milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at 35 cents ($25.20); twelve dozen 8 percent test bottles at 22 cents ($31.68); an acid measure (90 cents); a four-bottle hand tester ($18.00); freight ($2.15); total $77.93 (the lines add; the Chronicle's and Junia Ammon's "$77.39" and Vaught's and Oram's "five dozen" rejected). It went into use on 1 May 1897 — Orra's diary of that day, the office file, the invoice date, Bulletin 471's own conclusion and Keddie's "1897, part year"; Vaught's and Kyle's "1 May 1898" rejected. The lot was short: Junia Ammon's belief, written in 1949, is that a reground mold ran the lot consistently wrong and a consistency check cannot catch consistency; the works books burned in 1931, so the cause on the glass-house side cannot be determined, and the Chronicle's charge of a knowing fraud is unsupported.

Merle Strawn rode the Ordell circuit from 1907 to 1936, tested the station's scales and lactometer every visit, and never found water. The state issued burettes to field men in spring 1921; he skipped the station's glass in 1922 for want of daylight, and on 12 June 1923 ran the pipettes: "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them." The Association ordered on 28 June; Ammon replied 2 July, entered six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles, and said its books of the nineties could tell nothing. New glass was in use from July 1923 and checked true in October; the 1924 average stood at 3.80. The burette program was discontinued in 1926. The crate went into the loft over the intake room. Vose's and Rundle's "12 July" is rejected: the order of 28 June postdates the condemnation.

In February 1958 Ivy Keddie, born 1931, Ansel's granddaughter, a graduate student at Farland, climbed the ladder and found the crate under a tarpaulin, stenciled AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET, with nine unbroken pipettes (the Chronicle's and Rundle's "eleven" rejected against the measurement sheet's nine values and the curator's count). On 9 March 1958 at Farland she measured them against a calibrated burette, five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 — mean 16.72 c.c. against 17.6 blown, short 0.88 c.c., 95 hundredths of the mark. Bulletin No. 471, October 1958, under Professor Leland Vaught and Ivy Keddie, concluded: no milk watered, no cream stolen; from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 the tests were low by one part in twenty because the pipettes were short by one part in twenty; the difference, 76,000 lb of butterfat, stands in the ledgers as station gain. Vaught's own method section carries a transposed 16.27 c.c. and its false consequences (0.9244, 3.905, 118,000 lb), which his conclusion paragraph and the sheet contradict. The Ashlin Chronicle ran it on 3 October 1958; Ivy read "What the Crate Held" to the Historical Society in 1959; Falke reported to the county board the same year. Thirty-five years from condemnation to cause; thirty-three from dismissal; Keddie twenty-four years dead. The crate and the test book went to the Historical Rooms in 1959.

Documents: the invoice (Item 2; Ammon facsimile); Ammon's letter of 2 July 1923 (Item 10); Strawn's 1921, 1922, 12 June 1923, October 1923, 1924 and 1926 entries (Item 9 photostat); the measurement sheet (Item 14); Bulletin No. 471 (Item 15); the crate itself (Item 5); Ivy's 1959 talk; Junia Ammon's 1949 chapter; the Chronicle; Falke's report.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie and Ansel Keddie.** Granddaughter. Ansel (b. 1861) and Orra had one son in the sources, Duncan (b. 1894), who taught school at Ashlin; Duncan states plainly that "my daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter," and Ivy opens her 1959 talk with "Ansel Keddie ... was my grandfather. My father, Duncan Keddie ... was his son." Oram calls her "the granddaughter." Two sources say great-niece: the Ashlin Chronicle (assembled from a college press release) and Falke's report at page two — and Falke, in his closing note, says he has verified that she is the granddaughter and the error was his. The Chronicle even records that her father taught school in Ashlin for many years, which identifies Duncan. The contrary value is one press-release error carried twice, and one of its carriers retracted it.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant.** Second cousins. Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie (née Brant) were brother and sister; Emmet's son Roy and Orra's son Duncan were therefore first cousins (both Roy and Duncan say so in the same words); Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are the children of first cousins, which is second cousins. Rundle's 1959 footnote agrees. Vira Toland and Cleve Oram say "first cousin once removed"; that would require one of the two to be a generation nearer Emmet and Orra than she is, and neither is — the age gap (Ivy born 1931; Hazel old enough to remember the 1919 circular) is what invites the mistake. Left open by the sources: whether Emmet and Orra were full siblings (Roy: same mother and father) or half siblings by Emmet's father's second wife (Hazel) — it does not change the degree of cousinship; Roy's mother and Duncan's wife are never named; Hazel's birth year is never given; and Roy's own birth year is only inferable (sixty-seven in the winter of 1955, so about 1888).

**B3. Selby Vose's father.** Gideon Vose — the Association's first president (1889–1911) and the man who drafted the by-laws, including Article VII. Selby says so himself in his 1926 address ("my father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law"), and Loomis ("son of the late president"), Duncan Keddie and Kyle confirm; Vira Toland's and Rundle's "nephew" is village talk. It matters twice. First, the rule the father wrote — pay on the station's own test, and nothing else shall govern — is what turned a short pipette into money: under payment by weight no one would have lost a dollar. Second, the son ran the office for twenty-five years (1913–1938) under that rule, signed every annual sheet that carried "station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual," was told by his bookkeeper in 1916 to look at the account and told him to let it alone, sent the 1919 water circular, argued water before the board, and in 1926 was still explaining the figures by the men. The family that wrote the rule owned the office that never went up the road and looked — Loomis's "the family always thought the valley belonged to them a little," said less kindly.

**B4. The Vose family and the Keddie family.** No relationship is established. Two narrators assert one: Vira Toland ("Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins. Everybody knew that") and Cleve Oram ("cousins of some sort ... my mother had it from her mother ... I could not draw you the line on paper"). Both are hearsay of hearsay. Against them stands Duncan Keddie, Orra's son, writing specifically to put the family right: there was "no connection whatever" with the Voses; people confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Gideon either; his mother "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it." Any cousinship would have run through the Brants, and neither Roy nor Hazel Brant, who set out the family carefully, mentions one. A negative cannot be proved from these papers, so strictly the sources cannot settle it; but the affirmative claim rests on talk alone and is denied by the only witness in a position to know.

## Section C — Math

**C1 (a) Proportion.** The nine surviving pipettes from the crate were blown 17.6 c.c. and delivered (mean of five runs each) 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Sum = 150.48; ÷ 9 = 16.72 c.c. Ratio 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly (17.6 × 0.95 = 16.72). The bottle neck is ruled for a 17.6 charge, so a 95 percent charge produces a fat column 95 percent as long: every test read 95 percent of the truth, i.e. low by one part in twenty (5 percent). Stated the other way, true = recorded ÷ 0.95, so the truth was 5.26 percent above the recorded figure. Vaught's 16.27 c.c. (ratio 0.9244) is a transposition; it contradicts the sheet, the 3.61→3.80 arithmetic and his own conclusion paragraph.

**C1 (b) True average.** Recorded average 3.61 (summary leaf). 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80. Shortfall 3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19 of a point. Cross-checks: 1896 on borrowed glass 3.80; the 1911 nine weeks 3.80; 1924 on new glass 3.80; Alder Corners 3.78–3.82.

**C1 (c) Pounds not credited.** 25 full delivery years (1898–1922), 40,000,000 lb of milk. Credited: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb. Delivered: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb. Difference: 76,000 lb of butterfat. Ledger cross-check: 3,040 lb station gain × 25 years = 76,000 lb.

**C1 (d) Money and fraction.** 76,000 lb × $0.25 = $19,000. Patrons received the award of $3,800. 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = 0.20 — one fifth (20 percent).

**C2 — Emmet Brant, 1910.** Delivered 200,000 lb, paid on 3.42. (a) Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = 6,840 lb. (b) True test 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60; delivered 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb; owed and not credited 7,200 − 6,840 = 360 lb (= 5 percent of 7,200; matches Ivy's "recorded at 3.42 was short 0.18": 0.18 × 2,000 = 360). (c) Butterfat in 1910 was 24 cents (Roy Brant's book): 360 × $0.24 = $86.40. (At the 25-cent period average it would be $90.00; the question asks for that year, so $86.40.)

**C3 — the 25 full years.** (a) Credited to patrons: 1,444,000 lb (57,760 × 25). (b) Reached Larrow Green in station cream: 1,520,000 lb (60,800 × 25). (c) Difference 76,000 lb = 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = 5.00 percent of the fat actually delivered, or 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 5.26 percent (the ledger's "five and a quarter") of the fat credited. The ledger reckons against the credited figure; the glass explains the 5 percent of the delivered figure. Teague's and Vaught's "five and a quarter percent of the fat actually in the cream" mixes the two.

**C4.** (a) Invoice of 14 April 1897: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = $77.93 (not $77.39). (b) 6 dozen = 72 pipettes (72 × $0.35 = $25.20 confirms the count; five dozen would be $21.00). Each short by 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c. on the mean; the nine measured individually ran 0.86 to 0.90 short. The 0.88 is measured on nine survivors and inferred to the lot; Ammon's account of a consistently wrong lot supports the inference, and the flat 3.61 for 26 years is consistent with it.

**C5 (a).** Faulty glass in use from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923: 26 years, 1 month and 11 days (about 26.1 years), less the nine weeks of 8 May–10 July 1911 when a borrowed measure was used. The money calculation runs over 25 full delivery years, 1898 through 1922. They differ because the ledger is annual and the glass changed in mid-year at both ends: 1897 (May–December, "part year" on the summary leaf) and 1923 (January–12 June) cannot be split out of annual totals, so the 25-year figure drops about thirteen months of short tests, and slightly overcounts the nine true weeks of 1911. The 76,000 lb is therefore a floor, not the exact total. **C5 (b).** Cannot be determined from the sources. The station column of the ledgers begins with 1897; the 1896 intake (4 May to year-end) was carried on the creamery's books and the sheets were never put with the station papers (Kyle); the curator states flatly that no figure for 1896 exists anywhere. Sill recalls "about nine hundred thousand pounds" thirty-eight years later; Kyle "a good eleven hundred thousand," from men who were there, "not in any book I ever ruled." Two unrecorded recollections that disagree by 200,000 lb are not a figure.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval: 8 May to 10 July 1911, sixty-three days, nine weeks (Teague's and Kepp's six weeks are wrong; the test book carries both dates and Orra's diary carries the first). The variable: the measuring pipette — the station's own broke and a borrowed one was used. Held constant: patrons, herds, pasture, season (the interval straddles May–July, not a feed change), acid, bottles, hand tester, the tester himself and his habits, supervision (none). Result: the station average rose from 3.61 to 3.80 the day the borrowed measure came in, held there, and fell to 3.61 the day the station's own returned — recorded contemporaneously by a man who did not understand it and wrote a question mark. What it establishes that a calculation cannot: that the gap lived in the measure and not in the cows, the ground, the cans or the man, because those did not change and the figure did; a calculation from the 1958 measurement shows only that the glass could produce a 0.19 gap, the experiment shows that it did, at that station, with those herds, in real time. It also fixes the size of the effect, 0.19, forty-seven years before anyone had a burette. What it does not establish: the shortfall in cubic centimeters (that needed the 1958 burette); by itself, that the same fault ran from 1897 to 1923 (that rests on the summary leaf's 3.61 within 0.03 every year, the 1896 and 1924 readings of 3.80 on other glass, and the Alder Corners figures); where the borrowed measure came from or that it was itself exactly true (its 3.80 is corroborated externally, which makes it likely); how the lot came to be short; that feed has no effect at all (only that it is not the 0.19); and anything to anyone at the time, since nobody read it.

**D2.** Reasons the tester was not stealing cream, each from a different keeper of a different record: (1) The Association's own ledger, in the accuser's hand: every year more fat arrived at Larrow Green in the station's cream than the station's tests credited — 60,800 against 57,760, "as usual." Theft takes fat out of the cream before it leaves; stolen cream arrives light. This cream arrived heavy. (Teague, Nym, Kyle, Rundle's press note.) (2) The buttermaker's receiving book: Hobart Sill weighed the Hollow's cans twice, on the platform and in the cold room, from 1893 on, and found them heavy on "page after page." A physical weighing on the creamery floor, not an office figure. (3) The tester's own book for 1911: the same man, alone, on the same routine, produced 3.80 for nine weeks on a borrowed measure and 3.61 the day his own returned — his hand did not change, the glass did (Keddie; Falke's paragraph 8). (4) The chronology of the rise: the tests went to 3.80 from July 1923 and the whole of 1924 while Keddie was still the only man in the building, ten months before his dismissal (Strawn's 1924 entry; Vose's own address; Sill's "one summer along in the twenties"). The valley's story that "the figures went right when the man left" has the order backwards. (5) An outside witness: Jerome Cudd's book in another county, kept by a man who never saw an Ordell figure, shows the same herds at 3.80 — exactly 3.61 ÷ 0.95 — which a thief at the Hollow could not have arranged. (6) The 1958 measurement: the glass alone accounts for the whole gap, and a proportional, scatter-free 5 percent for twenty-six years is the signature of an instrument, not of a man helping himself. These are independent because they rest on different documents kept by people with different, sometimes opposed interests — the accuser's ledger, the buttermaker's scale, the accused's book, the inspector's notebook, a cheesemaker's book in another county, and a laboratory sheet — and each would stand if the others were lost. Reasons 1 through 5 hold with the crate never opened; reason 1 alone was sufficient and was in the office from the start. Did the bookkeeper ever question his own entries? No. Teague says the evenness "stops him looking" and the line became "furniture"; Kyle says he "never asked anybody anything in forty years." Teague's one recorded act is that he "put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone" — uncorroborated, content unstated, and never repeated. In 1925 and again in 1948 he reproduced the entry and, in the same breath, argued theft, without noticing that the entry refutes it. A schoolteacher saw the contradiction on one reading of the ledgers in 1939.

**D3.** Yes — in the sense the question asks, and the record is explicit. That the station's tests ran below the valley's was the complaint from 1902 onward and the office's standing knowledge; Vose's 1926 address says the milk "for a generation ... tested below the milk brought to Larrow Green," and he sent the June 1919 circular precisely because, as he put it, a manager "who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing" is idle. He also had before him, each spring from 1913, a signed sheet stating that the station's cream delivered five and a quarter percent more fat than its tests credited, and in 1916 his bookkeeper raised the account with him and was told to let it alone. So he knew the tests were low and he had, in his own hand's signature and in a direct warning, the evidence that the milk was not. What the sources do not establish is that he understood the sheet: Kyle watched him for a thousand signatures — "he read the totals and he never read the lines above them" — and his 1926 speech, which still blames water and dates the rise after a petition that came eighteen months later, shows a man who never absorbed what he signed. Knowledge of the low tests: certain. Knowledge that they were wrong: available to him, put to him once, and refused.

## Section F — Theory

**F1.** The cause was a short lot of glass. On 14 April 1897 Ammon & Sons of Tarnet sold the Association six dozen milk pipettes blown 17.6 c.c. for the Ostrey Hollow station; the lot delivered 16.72 c.c., 0.88 c.c. short, and it went into use on 1 May 1897. A butterfat test reads the length of a fat column in a bottle neck ruled for a 17.6 charge; with a 16.72 charge the column, and therefore the reading, is 16.72/17.6 = 95 percent of the truth, for every patron, every day. That is why the error had the size it had (one part in twenty, 0.19 of a point on 3.80 milk, 0.18 on 3.42 milk, 0.24 on 4.56 milk); why it was precise and never scattered (the same glass delivered the same short charge for a careful man and a careless one); why it was invisible from inside the plant (the pipette is the standard the plant measures with, and cannot be caught by measuring with itself); and why it preserved every herd's rank while putting the whole station wrong against the world. It began when the borrowed Larrow Green glass, which read 3.80 in 1896, was replaced by the short lot (the summary leaf: 1897 "part year," 3.61 from 1898). It ended on 12 June 1923 when Strawn, two seasons after the state issued burettes, ran the pipettes and condemned the crate; new glass in July 1923 gave 3.80 in 1924. Nothing on the farms or in the station had to change at either end because the milk was 3.80 all along: the fat went down the valley regardless, weighed heavy by Sill, and was booked every year as "station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual" — 76,000 lb, $19,000, over 1898–1922. The 1911 nine weeks were the same theory run as an experiment: one pipette exchanged, 3.80; exchanged back, 3.61.

Three competing explanations. (1) **Water in the cans** — Selby Vose (circular 1919, the board 1925, address 1926), Sill in his own way. Defeated by Strawn's lactometer at every visit, never any added water, and his telling Vose so in 1919; by the Brant book's two agreeing scales; by cheese — a cheesemaker sees watered milk in the vat the next morning and Cudd never saw it in sixteen summers; by the ledger, since watered milk sends less fat to the creamery and this cream arrived with more; by the chronology, the tests rising in July 1923, eighteen months before any petition; and by 1911, when the patrons did not change and the figure did. (2) **Theft of cream by the tester, covered by low tests** — Teague (1925, 1948), the valley (Rundle's Chapter IV, Vira, Oram's father). Defeated by the station-gain line in Teague's own hand (stolen cream arrives light; this arrived heavy every year); Sill's double weighing; 1911 (same man, figures rose); 1924 at 3.80 with Keddie still alone in the room; the proportional, scatter-free error; and the burette. (3) **Wet hay of Hessel Bottom** — the 1908 Grigg committee, Grigg again 1919, Kepp 1926, Falke briefly 1959. Defeated by Bulletin 214 (≤0.05 in winter, nothing measurable on pasture, against a gap of 0.19 in July and February alike — four times too small and the wrong shape); by Alder Corners, where the same meadow's summer milk ran 3.80; by 1911 and 1924, when nothing was done to the land; and by Falke's own withdrawal once he read the bulletin's figures.

Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware? No. The exhibits were four books; the crate was never mentioned in any session (Loomis took every word); the appliances the board saw tried in the weigh room were the new glass of 1923; the crate lay in the loft twenty feet above two of the three sessions. The board did have Strawn's 1923 notebook, which recorded the condemnation, and drew nothing from it.

What the tester believed. On the record, nothing: "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are," and a question mark he let stand fourteen years, because he would not write what he could not prove. Privately, the nearest witness — his son, seventeen in 1911 — heard him say twice that summer that it was "something in the glass," which fits the question mark, his 1902 remark to Orra that "it is my figures they will look at," and his letter's insistence that he knew what came into the room. Frick's recollection of a corridor remark that Keddie blamed the patrons is a single, uncorroborated memory of 1946 and sits against Keddie's own written words; I weigh it below Duncan's account but note the sources do not settle a private belief beyond doubt. Best answer: he suspected the glass from 1911 and would neither assert nor record it.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** In 1897 a Larrow Valley creamery cooperative bought six dozen milk pipettes for its Ostrey Hollow skimming station. The lot was short — 16.72 c.c. against a 17.6 mark — so for twenty-six years every butterfat test there read 95 percent of the truth: 3.61 instead of 3.80. Because the by-law made the station's own test govern pay, the Hessel Bottom farmers were underpaid 76,000 lb of fat, about $19,000, while the missing fat reached the creamery anyway and was booked yearly as "station gain, as usual." The valley blamed wet hay (1908), watered milk (1919) and a thieving tester; a 1925 arbitration paid $3,800 without finding a cause, and the tester, Ansel Keddie, was dismissed. An inspector had condemned the glass in 1923 without drawing the inference. In 1958 Keddie's granddaughter measured nine survivors from the crate. It took eight records because each — test book, ledger, receiving book, weigh book, inspector's notebook, cheese-factory book, invoice, bulletin — held one piece, and nobody laid them side by side.
