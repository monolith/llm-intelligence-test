## 0. The affair in one paragraph (orientation for the fresh reader)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (organized 1889 at Larrow Green; creamery at Larrow Green on Larrow Creek built 1891; office at Ashlin; skimming station at Ostrey Hollow seven miles up the valley, opened 4 May 1896) paid patrons on butterfat by test, and by Article VII the station's own test governed. The Ostrey Hollow station's tests ran about 0.19 of a point below the truth from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 because its milk-test pipettes (six dozen bought from Ammon & Sons, Tarnet, invoice 14 April 1897) delivered 16.72 c.c. instead of the 17.6 c.c. they were blown for — 95 hundredths of the mark, so every test read one part in twenty low. Over 25 delivery years (1898–1922) that was 76,000 lb of butterfat, worth $19,000 at 25 cents/lb, which appeared in the Association's ledgers every year as "station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual." Meanwhile the valley blamed wet hay (1908 committee), the tester Ansel Keddie (rumor of cream-stealing), or the patrons (1919 "water in the cans" circular from manager Selby Vose). State inspector Merle Strawn condemned the pipettes on 12 June 1923; new glass from July 1923; tests rose (1924 average 3.80). Patrons petitioned January 1925; board of arbitration under attorney Alonzo Frick sat 17 March–4 May 1925; "finds no cause established" yet awarded $3,800 without admission; Keddie was put out 30 April 1925. The crate of condemned pipettes was never mentioned at the hearing. In February 1958 Ivy Keddie (Ansel's granddaughter, State Agricultural College at Farland) found the crate, measured nine surviving pipettes on 9 March 1958, and Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958, Professor Vaught) cleared everyone: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there."

New in retellings 9–16: the Brant weigh book's own keeper (Roy Brant) shows the WEIGHING always agreed and puts the gap at 0.17; Rosalie Cudd's Alder Corners letter supplies sixteen summer seasons (1915–1930) of the four Hessel Bottom men at 3.78–3.82 (~3.80); Vaught's own bulletin intro (as retold) garbles the numbers (16.27, five dozen, 1898, 3.905, 118,000 lb) against its own quoted conclusion; Dell Rundle's 1939 jubilee pamphlet records the valley's cream-theft folklore and then refutes it in a press note from the station-gain entry ("It reached the Green heavy"); Frick's 1946 recollections give the board of three, sessions 2–3 held in the station's weigh room, the three explanations with authors (Vose=water, Teague=cream-theft insinuation, the 1908 report=hay), Keddie's private corridor opinion that the patrons were at fault, a four-book exhibit list, and the patrons' own claim of 67,000 lb; buttermaker Hobart Sill (1934) testifies the Hollow's cream came in HEAVY every year against the sheets, that HIS own glass was the borrowed 1896 outfit, and that 1896 took in ~900,000 lb; Nyle Grigg's 1919 reprint shows his cover letter inflating the 1908 sampling to a full year and misquoting Bulletin 214 as "two tenths"; and vet Roland Kepp (1926) repeats the same "two tenths" misquote, claims a 0.21 gap, and dismisses the 1911 interval as "six weeks of summer figures."

## 1. Retelling 01 — Vira Toland, "What the Hollow Said," tape recorded at Ostrey Hollow, 1979

**Narrator / setting.** Vira Toland, daughter of a Hessel Bottom patron. Taped at her kitchen table by a young man from the county who asked three questions in an hour and let her have the rest; transcribed as spoken, her doublings-back left in.

**Her life.** Born 1912 at the top end of Ostrey Hollow in the house her father's father put up. Except two years down at Ashlin when first married ("I would not count those two years anyhow"), never lived anywhere else. Her father hauled milk to the station like everybody up there. Nine families hauled to that station; in and out of each other's kitchens all year; she could name every one but "you want the trouble, they always want the trouble."

**The station as she saw it.** Stood at the very top of the Hollow where the road bends then goes to nothing but the ridge. Low building, whitewashed, cold as a spring house inside even in August; smelled of milk, steam and hot iron; you could hear the engine before the last turn. Ansel Keddie ran it. "He went up there in ninety-five, the year before they opened it, to get it ready, and he tested that first summer too," and was still there when she was big enough to ride the wagon with her father.

**Keddie's book.** Everybody knew about it; no secret. Ruled with his own straightedge, every line the same height, figures like print. He wrote in it standing at a slope desk by the window. If a man asked what his milk had done, he did not hunt; turned to the day and read it off. "He tested a thousand cans a week and never a blot in that book." Her father said "if the rest of the world kept its accounts the way Ansel Keddie kept his there would be no lawyers in Ashlin." Vira: "There are still lawyers in Ashlin."

**1919, the letter.** "That is the year everything went sour up here"; she was seven; remembers it like a smell. A letter from the Association, printed, over the manager's signature: "it said there was water in the cans coming down from Ostrey Hollow. That is what it said. Water in the cans." Went to nine families; her father one of the nine. He read it at the table with his hat still on (which he never did); her mother stood at the stove with her back to him and did not turn round; he read it twice; put it in the drawer of the desk in the front room; still there when he died; "I believe my brother has it yet." Some kept theirs till they died; one woman had hers pinned inside the lid of her trunk. "You can tell people a thing like that is only business and they will still keep the paper forty years to prove what was said of them." It rained that day; she remembers the wet on her father's shoulders and cannot think of one without the other "in sixty years."

**The Voses.** Manager who signed: Selby Vose, down at Larrow Green. "He was old Gideon Vose's NEPHEW" (Vira's claim — conflicts with Effie and Selby himself, who say son; but Rundle also says nephew). Old Gideon started the whole Association; the family "always thought the valley belonged to them a little." "Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins. Everybody knew that" and nobody wrote it down. So the letter was "somebody's cousin saying it."

**The Keddie–Brant split.** Orra was Ansel Keddie's wife; Orra was Emmet Brant's sister. Emmet Brant: biggest man on the Bottom, most cows, most hay ground, hauled more milk to the station than anybody; the letter cost him the most. "The man that tested the milk had married the sister of the man that got the worst of it." Before 1919 the two families ate Sunday dinners together (two houses in one kitchen, men at the fence after); after 1919 they never spoke — not at the store, not at a funeral. Orra would walk down to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane. Both long dead, lived within sight of each other's chimney smoke.

**Tests went up.** "All at once, one summer, the figures came up and stayed up, and nobody ever said why." Not the station, not the Association. Her father had done nothing different; none of them had. "The queerest part... that the thing corrected itself and no man stood up and gave a reason for it."

**The hearing.** "The winter and spring after." At Ashlin, in an office, lawyers and Association officers, the men going down in wagons; it went on and on. At the end they put Ansel Keddie out — an old man. "They paid the patrons THIRTY-FIVE HUNDRED DOLLARS and called it settled" (Vira's figure — all other sources say $3,800), split nine ways; her father's share "did not buy a hay rake."

**Her verdict.** "He was taking the cream. They caught him at it and they put him out, and that is why the numbers went right the summer after." (Note her own chronology puts the rise BEFORE the hearing.) A man alone with the separator running and the door shut, nobody in the valley can add, nobody came up that road and looked; not wicked, just unwatched. Her father never said it aloud; called Keddie "an honest man who had got into something."

**Aftermath.** Keddie "died in the thirties," still under it; the valley looked at him a certain way in church; Orra had it too and outlived him a good while.

**1958 visitor.** A young woman came in 1958 "from the college away east," asking after all this; poked about the old station building (shut for years; they kept fence wire and salt in it); sat at Vira's table; careful, wrote everything down. "She was Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed, so she was of the family both sides in a manner of speaking." Vira told her about the letter in the drawer. (This is evidently Ivy Keddie — R04 — but Vira never names her, and Ivy's college is Farland, the State Agricultural College. Roy and Rundle say Ivy and Hazel are second cousins, not first-once-removed.)

## 2. Retelling 02 — Effie Loomis, "Notes toward a history of the Association," 1941

**Narrator.** Secretary of the Association 1901–1940; kept the minute book in her own hand throughout. Wrote these numbered sections in 1941, the year after she laid down the minute book, for the Association's own files; minutes and recollection kept apart. She was fourteen in 1889 (so born about 1875).

**§1 Organizing.** 1889: farmers of the Larrow Valley met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose elected first president; held it until 1911 — twenty-two years, longest ever. The 1889–1892 minute book was lost when the office was moved in 1938; for the first twelve years she is only a reader of papers, "and a poorer reader than some," because those are the papers lost.

**§2 Creamery.** Main creamery built at Larrow Green in 1891, on Larrow Creek, "the same building yet." Before it, members sold milk where they could; no common test, no common price. "It began as a plain remedy for a plain grievance."

**§3 By-laws, Article VII.** Gideon Vose drafted the by-laws himself; not a lawyer. Quoted entire:
> *Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*
Adopted at the annual meeting of 1892, the year after the creamery opened. Before it a man was paid on can weight; after, on fat found by test at the place he delivered. She calls it a just rule. The last clause — "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" — is "the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business": the station's test and no appeal from it, "written in 1892 by a man who died in 1917 and could not be asked about it afterward."

**§4 Officers in her time.** Effie Loomis secretary 1901–1940. Wilbur Teague bookkeeper from 1905, kept the ledgers. Hobart Sill buttermaker at Larrow Green, had the churn book and cream receiving book. Selby Vose, SON of the late president, manager 1913–1938. "Not one of us ever went up the valley and stood in the Ostrey Hollow station on a working morning to watch the work being done. I include myself first in that."

**§5 Station.** Skimming station seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green, at the head of the Hollow; took in the milk of the farms up there, separated it, sent cream down, gave skim back. Best milk came off Hessel Bottom, the wet meadow below the hamlet. SEVEN farms had rights on Hessel Bottom (cut hay and pastured) — conflicts with six per Roy/Grigg/Kepp; the largest was Emmet Brant's, the station's largest patron in every recorded year.

**§6 Petition and board.** January 1925 the Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned: their milk had never tested as neighbors' did and they had been paid accordingly a long time; asked to be made whole. Board of arbitration sat at Ashlin under Alonzo Frick, attorney of that place, 17 March to 4 May 1925. Three explanations argued; none accepted; "they are in the minutes and the minutes are in the safe." She recorded the award on the last day:
> *The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account.*
"Finds no cause established, and then an award of thirty-eight hundred dollars" — not her place to ask how both halves could be true.

**§7 Quantities (Teague's ledgers).** Over the twenty-five years of the claim the station received forty million pounds of milk = average 1,600,000 lb/year; butterfat price averaged 25 cents/lb. Figures are Mr. Teague's from the annual sheets; she added the columns twice.

**§8 Where the record stops.** (1) The 1938 office move lost the 1889–1892 minute book — carried out with the rest, never arrived; anyone telling what the first four annual meetings resolved "is telling you a story he had from his father." (2) The crate: never mentioned in the hearing at all — "I took every word down and it is not there." The crate of measures the state's inspector condemned at the station in June 1923, set aside, new ones ordered. Not a word in any of the three sessions, by patrons, officers or board; the award's "books and appliances of the station" is a form of words, not a list. "The interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying."

## 3. Retelling 03 — Ansel Keddie, statement to the board (17 March 1925) and unsent letter (spring 1925)

**Frame.** Two papers: (I) what he said standing before the board at Ashlin on 17 March 1925 — nine sentences, taken down as given; (II) a letter written at Ostrey Hollow later that spring, never sent, found among his papers.

**I. Statement (paraphrased closely; nine sentences).** Kept the test book in his own hand since the station opened; not a figure in it he did not put there. Set down date, patron, pounds, test and fat; errors lined through with his letters against the line; never wrote a figure over a figure. Same order, same hour every working day of every season. Heard what patrons and officers say; "Neither of them has come up that road on a working morning and stood at my elbow and watched the work done." Asked to account for figures; the figures are what he read and wrote. "I am an old man and I have no other trade." "I have tested as I was taught and as the state man saw me do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, and I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are." "I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself."

**II. Letter.** Written because in that room "a man is allowed to answer and not allowed to explain," and somebody should know how the work was done.
- Day began at half past five; wagons up the Hollow in the dark most of the year; intake room open, lamps lit, scale swinging free before the first wagon. Every can on the scale, weight called and written before the milk went anywhere. Scale tested by the state's man and true; in 29 seasons never had a patron dispute a weight and be right.
- Sampling: a sample from each patron's milk as weighed into his own jar, numbered to him; the jar made up over ten days; on testing morning set in warm water till blood-warm through, poured vessel to vessel until the risen cream was carried down. "A sample that is not warmed and mixed is worth nothing and a boy could tell you so."
- The charge: milk drawn up in the measure to the mark on the stem, tip wiped, blown out into the bottle carrying the patron's number; acid poured down the side; bottle turned till dark. Four bottles in the hand tester, weighted against each other, cover down, crank at the taught speed, no faster; fat rises in the neck as a clear column, read in per cent against the rulings with a pair of dividers, written down. "It is only a trade that must be done the same way every time, which is a harder thing than it sounds."
- "The glass I began on in ninety-six was borrowed off the ASHLIN creamery, and we used it until our own outfit came up the valley in a crate the spring following." (Conflicts with his own first leaf and R07: borrowed of Larrow Green — and with Sill R14, who says the outfit was his own glass from Larrow Green.)
- Book in FIVE columns: date, patron, pounds, test, fat. Errors crossed with initials; never wrote over "because a figure written over another figure is a figure no man can swear to afterward." Twenty-nine seasons, 1896 through 1924. "I did not test a pound in the season of 1925."
- First leaf quoted:
> *Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie.*
- 1922 back leaf of averages, quoted:
> *Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80.*
- The nine weeks of 1911: measure broke 8 May; made shift with one borrowed; from that morning tests came up and stood at 3.80; 10 July own measure in use again and tests down the same day. Entries quoted:
> *May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure.*
> *July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.*
"That question mark is mine and I have let it stand for fourteen years."
- The gap was not seasonal: there in July as in February, flush of grass and dead of winter on dry hay, wet year and dry. In 1924 the station average was 3.80, written with the same care.
- Complaint history: began 1902, when it was first said at Larrow Green that Ostrey Hollow milk did not test with the rest. 1908 a committee came up, sampled the herds through the winter, reported the wet hay of the Bottom thinned the cream and the remedy lay with the land. 1919 a circular from the Association to nine families "saying there was water in their cans." He carries no charge against any signer; "nine kitchens in this Hollow have had that letter in a drawer ever since."
- "My wife Orra is Emmet Brant's sister." Emmet brought more milk than any man on the Bottom; every pound weighed and every test made by Keddie himself. "He and I have not spoken since the circular."
- "I was put out on the thirtieth of April. They found no cause established and they put me out on the last day of the month, and both of those things are true at once." (Note: 30 April 1925 precedes the award of 4 May 1925.) Ends: "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are."

## 4. Retelling 04 — Ivy Keddie, "What the Crate Held," talk to the Ordell County Historical Society, Ashlin, 1959

**Narrator.** Ivy Keddie; Ansel Keddie was her grandfather; her father Duncan Keddie, Ansel's son, taught school at Ashlin. Read from typescript with the crate on a table and the borrowed books open; papers read from originals. "It depends on nine pieces of glass and a burette."

**Five sets of records, never in one room before 1958.**
1. The station test book — in the Association's office since 1925; came to "these Rooms" (Ordell County Historical Rooms) in 1959.
2. The Brant weigh book, kept at Hessel Bottom from 1897 onward; lent by Roy Brant, Emmet Brant's son, who has kept it up since his father died.
3. The Alder Corners Cheese Company's test figures, from over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County, copied out for her by Rosalie Cudd, who runs that factory and is Jerome Cudd's NIECE (Ivy's claim — Rosalie herself, Rundle, and Nym's provenance all make her Jerome's DAUGHTER); the book has never left Vessey County and Ivy never saw it.
4. Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks, state dairy inspector for the county "from 1908" (Strawn himself says 1907), in her college's library at Farland since 1947 — eleven years unquestioned.
5. The Association's own annual ledgers, present that night.

**The crate.** February 1958 (she says "last year") she went up the Hollow to the station building, used for storage since it closed. A loft over what was the intake room, reached by a ladder; under a tarpaulin at the back a wooden crate stenciled on the end AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET. Carried up there June 1923; still there. Inside, packed in straw, the station's condemned milk-test pipettes. Six dozen bought new for the station in 1897 on an invoice of $77.93 for the whole outfit (pipettes, bottles, acid measure, hand tester, freight). Nine unbroken.

**Measurement, 9 March (1958), at Farland, against a calibrated burette.** Sheet quoted:
> *Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for.*

**The arithmetic.** A test draws a measured charge into a bottle whose neck is ruled for that charge; a short charge gives a proportionally short reading. 16.72 / 17.6 = 0.95 exactly. Every test read 95% of the truth. Grandfather's 1922 summary leaf quoted again (identical text to R03). 3.61 / 0.95 = 3.80; recorded 3.61, true 3.80, gap 0.19 = 5% of 3.80. The gap is proportional, not fixed: "It lost a twentieth of whatever was there."

**Money.** Twenty-five full delivery years 1898–1922: 40,000,000 lb milk, average 1,600,000/yr. At 3.61%: 1,444,000 lb fat credited. At 3.80%: 1,520,000 lb delivered. Difference 76,000 lb. Butterfat averaged 25 cents/lb → $19,000 unpaid. The 1925 award of $3,800 "is a fifth of it."

**Two percentages.** 76,000 is 5% of fat delivered (1,520,000) and 5¼% of fat credited (1,444,000). The Association's ledger line uses the second (a bookkeeper reckons surplus against the figure already entered). Neither is an error, "but five percent is the one that is also the glass."

**Fell hardest on the best milk.** A herd recorded at 4.56 was short 0.24; a herd recorded at 3.42 was short 0.18. (4.56 = Selby Vose's Jerseys in 1910 per R08; 3.42 = the Brant weigh book's 1910 leaf per R07/R09.)

**1911 as an accidental experiment.** Station's measure broke 8 May 1911; substitute used until 10 July. "The measure they borrowed in 1911 came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon." Same patrons, herds, pasture, season, acid, bottles, man at the crank — one object changed — average went to 3.80 and back to 3.61 the day the station's own returned. "No explanation of this valley's twenty-six-year anomaly survives those nine weeks except the one I have given you."

**Wet hay refuted.** The State Agricultural College tested it in 1911, Bulletin No. 214:
> *Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture.*
Five hundredths in winter, nothing in summer, against a flat 0.19 gap every month — "four times too small and it is the wrong shape." Over the ridge the Alder Corners factory tested the milk of four Hessel Bottom men in their summer flush; where the two books overlap, "fourteen seasons of them," the same milk ran about 0.19 higher at Alder Corners than at home. (Rosalie's table gives SIXTEEN seasons, 1915–1930.)

**Bulletin No. 471**, issued "last October" (October 1958), last paragraph, "Professor Vaught's wording and mine together":
> *No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain.*

**Timeline she closes with.** Glass condemned 1923, cause found 1958: 35 years. Grandfather dismissed 1925: 33 years. He died in 1934, dead 24 years when the clearing sheet was read. "I have no remedy to propose. I have only the crate, and it is on the table."

## 5. Retelling 05 — Wilbur Teague, "The Books of the Association," Chapter Nine, 1948

**Narrator.** Bookkeeper of the Association 1905–1945 (forty years). Unpublished memoir written at Ashlin in the winter of 1948, three years after giving up the desk. "I never carried a column that would not prove."

**The office at Ashlin.** One room over a harness shop; cold at the north end, never cold enough at the south; ledgers on a shelf a taller predecessor built; sloped desk with a brass rail across the foot; station sheets came out of their envelope in March on stiffer paper than the creamery used.

**The Association on paper.** Three things: creamery at Larrow Green, office at Ashlin, skimming station seven miles up the valley at Ostrey Hollow. Mr. Selby Vose manager over the whole; Miss Effie Loomis secretary with the minutes; Teague at the ledger. The station was the smallest and "made the most noise." Patrons hauled up to the station instead of seven miles down; weighed, sampled, tested; paid on pounds and test; station separated, sent cream down to Larrow Green, skim back to the farmer for hogs and calves.

**The station-gain line.** One side: fat credited to patrons on the station's own test. Other side: fat actually received at Larrow Green in the station's cream, weighed and tested into the vats. Never the same; the second always larger; the difference carried to the general account "since the Association had received it." The line was HIS OWN DEVICE, begun when he came in 1905 — not "some old dodge of the founders"; "a difference that will not be entered somewhere will come back and bite you at audit." Entry quoted complete:
> *Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account.*
"That is the entry for 1898, and there is one like it for every year after, in the same terms and in the same hand for as long as the hand was mine." He says the gain ran at "five and a quarter percent of the fat that was actually in the cream" (arithmetically wrong: 3,040/60,800 = 5.0%; 3,040/57,760 = 5.26% — the 5¼ is of the CREDITED fat, as Ivy and Nym say). "As usual" got into the entry and stayed. "A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture."

**His two large numbers.** The station took in FORTY-FOUR million pounds of milk "over the years the account ran" (others: 40 million for 1898–1922; Rundle also uses 44M). The average test of all Ostrey Hollow patrons over the whole period was 3.16 (others: 3.61 — looks like a digit transposition; Frick repeats 3.16). Larrow Green did better; Ostrey Hollow men were sore about it for 25 years.

**1911.** The station's measure broke in spring 1911; "they made shift with a borrowed one for SIX WEEKS before their own came up from town" (Keddie/Ivy: nine weeks, 8 May–10 July; Kepp also says six weeks); entered as a small charge for replacement. People later asked him about that year "as though a bookkeeper kept the weather."

**His evidence to the 1925 board, repeated.** "Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons." One man weighed, drew the sample, made the test, wrote the figure the pay slip was made from, and ran the separator; no second man in the building from spring to fall. He does not say what anyone did on any day; says a business so arranged "will sooner or later have a little taken from it"; "The remedy is two men or a lock, and the Association had neither." (Frick R13 identifies this as the second of the three explanations — the cream-theft insinuation, put "in the way an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it.")

**The board.** Patrons petitioned in the winter "when a farmer has time to be angry"; board at Ashlin under Mr. Alonzo Frick, "as fair a chairman as ever a hearing had"; Teague gave evidence. Board found nothing established against anybody; awarded $3,800 without admission, charged to the station account; he entered it and it balanced.

**Two things more.** (1) Mr. Vose knew what the account was doing: he signed the annual sheet every year, laid before him each spring. (2) Teague did not sit silent forty years: he put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone; he let it alone — "the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience." The figures are in the ledgers at Ashlin, in ink, dated.

## 6. Retelling 06 — Merle Strawn, "Extracts from twenty-eight circuit books," with covering note, 1936

**Narrator.** State dairy inspector (retired), copied out in spring 1936 at the request of the state dairy office from the pocket books carried on the Ordell circuit; "I have copied what is in them and put in nothing that is not."

**Covering note.** Took the Ordell circuit in 1907; laid it down in 1936. Fifty-odd plants in four counties (creameries, cheese factories, skimming stations, tiny three-day butter places); each seen at least once a year, doubtful ones oftener. By train, livery, or in spring neither. Learns plants by smells: sour-sweet receiving platform in July, lime and steam of a wash room, "the flat cold nothing of a well-run cold room." Four things in the same order every time: (1) scales against his sealed weights, intake and cream scales, light and heavy; (2) lactometer on the day's milk, patron by patron where any question, for added water; (3) boiler, water supply, cold room; (4) stand at the tester's elbow and watch a test through from sample to reading, not letting him hurry. "I put down what I found. I did not put down what I thought about it."

**Entries (Ostrey Hollow unless noted).**
- 1907: first round. Station "up seven miles from Larrow Green, opened before my time." Scales true. Cold room fair. Tester keeps his book in his own hand, "better than most." Watched two tests; "works slow and clean and reads his own figure without being coaxed to it."
- 1909, fall: scales true; boiler in order, safety tried. Station takes from the farms of the wet bottom below it and from three places on the hill. Milk sweet, cans clean; one man cautioned about a rusted seam.
- 1911, June, en route across county: scales true; watched a test through; "Nothing here to note." (This visit falls inside the 8 May–10 July borrowed-measure window; he noticed nothing.)
- 1912: scales true; lactometer no added water; cream shipped down the same day it is separated. Creek out over the flat road; went round by the ridge.
- 1913: Larrow Green creamery, two days, churn room and receiving books; scales true. "New manager at the Association this year and he came out with me to the platform, which the last one never did."
- 1915: Ostrey Hollow and plants down the county line; scales true; wash room floor holding water at the drain, ordered graded.
- 1916: scales true; no added water. "The tester complains that his patrons blame him for their figures. I told him that is the trade and he said he knew it."
- 1917: cheese factory in the next county, then Larrow Green; scales true; short-handed; two testers doing the work of three, "which is where mistakes live."

## 8. Retelling 08 — Selby Vose, manager's annual address to the Association, 1926

**Frame.** Printed in the annual report for the year. "Thirteen years this spring, since 1913." Never had a year he liked less to report on, nor was gladder to have behind him. "Plain speech is the only thing that has ever done this valley any good."

**Ordinary business.** Season opened wet and stayed wet into June; hauling heavy on horses and men. Creamery at Larrow Green ran through the flush without a day lost; butter held its place with the trade; churn room clean; ice went into the house early and lasted; no complaint of any man on the payroll. The Ostrey Hollow station, seven miles up, ran its season out; cream came down daily and sweet.

**The other matter.** January 1925 the Ostrey Hollow patrons laid a petition; a board of arbitration sat at Ashlin under Mr. Alonzo Frick, "an able and impartial man"; heard everybody at length, at cost in time in the spring. "The board found no cause established. It found no cause established against the station, against the office, or against any officer or servant of this Association" — "the only sentence in this business that was arrived at by men under oath with the books in front of them." Having found no cause, the board nevertheless awarded $3,800 without admission, charged to the station account; he supported it then and now: "a generous settlement of an unproved claim"; the Association paid rather than let members go on believing they were used badly by neighbors; "we are not a business house; we are the farmers of this valley in company together."

**His stated cause.** "Water was going into cans at the head of that valley and it stopped when the Association began to look. The rise of 1924 is the measure of it." For a generation the milk tested below Larrow Green's and every year they were told it was the wet hay, or the tester, or the office, or the weather. Then the Association's attention came to rest on the station — petition, hearing, men questioned before neighbors — "and in the very next season the tests at that station rose by TWENTY-ONE HUNDREDTHS and the average for 1924 stood at 3.90, which is as good a figure as any station in this county will show you." (Keddie's book and Strawn both give 1924 as 3.80; 3.61 + 0.21 = 3.82, not 3.90; and the 1924 rise precedes the January 1925 petition he says triggered it. Kepp in the same year 1926 uses 0.21 as the GAP.)

**On the state man.** Told kindly that the state man's visit explains it; "It does not." He came through every year or two, tried the scales, found them true, "and he condemned the station's testing apparatus on the TWELFTH OF JULY, 1923, and told us to order new" (all other sources: 12 June 1923; Rundle repeats July). "We ordered new inside the month and had it in use before the flush was over. Apparatus wears." A station in daily use since 1896 wears its fittings out twice over. "I decline to build a mystery on a piece of housekeeping that appears in our own order book with a date and a price against it."

**No names.** Says it without naming a name; does not know one and would not use it. "A little water in a can is not a felony in a man's own mind. It is a way of making a hard year come out even." No prosecution wished, none has been or will be.

**Defends the 1919 circular.** Written "because it was my duty to write it." It "warned the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, in temperate language, that added water would be looked for and would be dealt with. It named nobody. It accused nobody. It said that this Association pays for butterfat and cannot pay for creek water, and I would write every word of it again tomorrow." (Vira and Keddie both describe it as asserting there WAS water in the cans; Roy and Rundle describe it as a warning against adding water — closer to Vose's version.) A manager who sees figures go one way for twenty years and says nothing "is being idle at their expense."

**His father's herd.** "My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law under which every patron in this Association is paid on the test of the station where he delivers" — so Selby is Gideon's SON — kept a herd of Jerseys at the home place; Selby kept them after him. In 1910 that herd tested 4.56. Brought forward to refute the trade's claim that the Larrow Valley is poor milk country with sour land and indifferent cattle: "There is no better milk in the state than the milk this valley can produce when it comes to the platform as it left the cow, and the proof of it stood in my own barn."

**Closing.** "Bring it as it left the cow." Wash the cans, cool the milk, keep the dog out of the milk house. Do that and the Ostrey Hollow tests will stay where they went in 1924 and the business will be "dead and buried." Thanks for thirteen years of patience; asks for one more. (Per Effie he served until 1938.)

## 9. Retelling 09 — Roy Brant, "an account for the county farm bureau's local-history collection," 1955 (postscript 1958)

**Narrator / frame.** Roy Brant, farmer at Hessel Bottom, Emmet Brant's son. Set down in longhand at Hessel Bottom in the winter of 1955, aged sixty-seven and still milking (so born about 1888); a postscript added three years afterward (1958). The bureau asked for "something on the old days"; he chose to write about his father's book instead — "the old days are gone and the book is on the shelf behind my chair, and a man does better to talk about what he can put his hand on."

**Emmet Brant.** Born 1854, died 1929. From "the first of January, 1898" (CONFLICT: Nym's provenance and Frick's exhibit list say begun 1 January 1897; Ivy says "from 1897 onward") until the week before he died, he wrote down every can of milk that left the barn; Roy has kept it up since, in the same book until it was filled and then in another, in the same way — "because he showed me how and because I never found a reason to stop."

**The Bottom.** Hessel Bottom is 160 acres of wet meadow lying under the hill at Ostrey Hollow. SIX farms had rights on it, cut hay off it and pastured it (CONFLICT: Effie says seven; Grigg's 1908 report and Kepp also say six). The Brant farm was the largest of the six — sixty-odd acres of the meadow, and thirty-eight cows in a good year, "which for this valley was a herd." Water stands on the low end into May; a soft place by the alders has taken a horse to the hock in his time and his father's. The hay is coarse, cuts late; "a cow will eat it and do well enough, whatever has been said about that hay by men who never fed it"; Roy has cut the meadow with a scythe and with a mower behind two horses; "it never failed to grow something."

**The book (physical).** A plain ledger, ruled by hand. Two columns for each delivery day: first column, the weight of every can off Emmet's own barn scale before the wagon went down; second column, the figure the station gave him back for that same milk. "That is all it is." No comment anywhere but three or four lines in thirty years, no opinion at all. Front cover gone; back held by a strip of harness leather Emmet tacked on himself; the first forty leaves stained brown at one corner where it lay in a leaky window a winter he was sick.

**The two columns agreed.** The central point, said "slowly": day after day, month after month, for the whole run of years, Emmet's weight and the station's weight came out the same, barring a pound or two on days a can got slopped on the hill. "Two scales, his and the station's, kept by two men who by the end were not speaking to one another, and the scales never fell out at all. Whatever was wrong at that station — and something was wrong at that station for a very long time — it was not the weighing."

**The 1910 leaf** (the one he is always asked for), in Emmet's hand:
> *Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42.*
He wrote "even" and underlined it, "which for him was carrying on." Butterfat that year was twenty-four cents; over the whole stretch the complaint covers, one year with another, about twenty-five cents.

**How the book was kept.** Emmet wrote at the kitchen table after the second milking, in the lamp, with his hat still on; Roy's mother would not have the table cleared till he was done. "It took him no time at all, and it is the only reason there is anything left to argue about now."

**The trouble.** Hollow milk ran SEVENTEEN hundredths of a point (0.17) under what the same class of milk was making down at Larrow Green (CONFLICT: 0.19 consensus; 0.21 Kepp). "Seventeen hundredths is nothing to look at on one pay slip. Set the slips in a row for a lifetime of farming and it is a farm." First said out loud in 1902; the sayers were laughed at; Emmet was one of them. Roy's key observation: "it was seventeen hundredths in July and it was seventeen hundredths in February. It did not come and go with the season. It did not care whether the cows were on the meadow or on hay in the barn. Nobody would take account of that, then or since, though it was in front of them."

**1908.** The Association appointed a committee "with Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green at the head of it"; sampled the Bottom herds through the winter; reported wet hay thins the cream, remedy with the land not the station. Emmet read the report twice, put it in the drawer, and said "the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not."

**1911.** Nine weeks in the summer when the station's own measure was out of use, having been broken. "The state man left his own measure that June and that is what they used" (CONFLICT on the substitute's source: Ivy — borrowed measure came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon; Teague — "their own came up from town"; Keddie — just "a borrowed measure"; also the break was 8 May, before June). Through those nine weeks the tests came up — all of them, every farm on the Bottom — then the station's own came back and the figures went down to where they had been. Emmet entered the rise and the fall, could make nothing of it; Roy could make nothing of it either; "there it sat for near on fifty years."

**1919.** The manager sent a paper round to the Hollow patrons "warning us against putting water in the milk" (phrased as a warning — Vose's characterization, vs Vira/Keddie's "said there was water"). Nine families got it and "read it for what it was." Emmet never forgave it. He and Ansel Keddie were brothers-in-law, and from 1919 they did not speak — "not from any quarrel over the water, but because my father held that the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not." (New motive for the Keddie–Brant split; cf. Vira's account.)

**Alder Corners.** From 1915, four of the Bottom men hauled their milk over Redlow Ridge to the cheese factory in the summer flush and back to the Hollow the rest of the year. The factory over there had OPENED IN 1914 (Rosalie says her father BUILT it in 1913). They went on that way for SIXTEEN seasons. Reasons: the price, the flush market, a shorter haul while the road was open. "It was talked about here as though they had gone over to the enemy, which shows you what a valley is." Roy did NOT go over: "the biggest herd is the one that cannot afford to be talked about, and I have thought since that this was a poor reason and cost us money."

**The family.** Orra Keddie was Emmet's sister — FULL brother and sister, same mother and same father, "whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it" (a stepmother rumor existed). So Ansel Keddie, keeper of the station's test book, was married into the family with more to lose by that station than any other on the meadow — "I have never known what to do with that fact except set it down." Duncan Keddie, schoolteacher at Ashlin, was Ansel and Orra's boy; Duncan and Roy are FIRST cousins. Roy's daughter is HAZEL; Duncan's daughter is IVY; Hazel and Ivy are SECOND cousins (CONFLICT: Vira says "first cousin once removed"; Rundle's 1959 footnote agrees with second cousins) — "both of them have had to have it explained to them by me more than once."

**The book's future.** Roy is 67; "the book has outlived two of the three men who are in it" (Emmet d. 1929, Ansel d. 1934; Roy the survivor). It goes to Hazel when he is done — "all the arrangement I have made and all I intend to make." (Matches Nym's provenance: to Hazel Brant 1962, to the Rooms 1963.)

**Postscript, 1958.** "Duncan's girl Ivy came out to the place this year" and asked to borrow the book for work at the college at Farland. He let her have it. "She is careful with paper." It comes back when she is through; after him it is Hazel's.

## 10. Retelling 10 — Rosalie Cudd, cheesemaker at Alder Corners, letter to Ivy Keddie, 1958

**Frame.** A long reply from the Alder Corners Cheese Company, Vessey County, to "a graduate student at Farland" (Ivy Keddie) who had written to ask what four Ordell County farmers' milk tested on the far side of Redlow Ridge. Ivy's letter came Tuesday; Rosalie read it twice — once standing in the make room with wet hands, once properly after supper. "In thirty-odd years nobody from over the ridge has ever asked me a plain question about our books, and here is a young woman from the college who asks one in four lines and apologizes for troubling me."

**The Cudds and the factory.** "My father, Jerome Cudd, built this factory in nineteen thirteen" (Roy and Rundle say it OPENED in 1914 — possibly built 1913/opened 1914), on the flat below the church, because there was spring water on the lot and a hard road out to the east. He made cheese there until he died in 1941 — in March, "between the setting and the cutting of a vat, which he would have arranged that way if he had been asked." Rosalie took the factory over that same spring — "seventeen years now" (→1958) — and has changed the boiler and the roof and nothing else. She was a girl of twelve when the first vat was set (born ~1901), drying hoops before she was tall enough to see into one. NOTE: she is Jerome's DAUGHTER — Ivy in R04 called her his "niece" (and Nym's Item 13 provenance "founder to his daughter in 1941" supports daughter).

**The ridge.** Four miles of switchback, cut for a lumber road and never much improved. Shuts in November, does not open before April, some years May; the drifts sit in the cut below the top and stay after everything else is bare. "So no man from your valley ever hauled to me in winter, whatever the price was. It was not possible."

**The four men.** From 1915, four men off Hessel Bottom hauled to her in the summer flush — about the first of June to the middle of September — and the rest of the year to the Ostrey Hollow station. Plain reasons: cheese paid better than butter in a flush month; she was buying hard in flush ("a cheese factory can use everything you can carry it"); her gate was a shorter pull for those four farms while the road was open. They came for SIXTEEN seasons; the last summer was 1930. (CONFLICT: Ivy says "fourteen seasons" of overlap; Nym Item 13 holds figures "for the season of 1919" only.) Color: they started before daylight to have cans on her platform by seven; horses steaming, tarps wet with the cloud that sits on the ridge top till the sun is well up. One brought his boy, who used to sit on the scale platform and be weighed for a joke; "that boy is a man in the state road department now and does not remember me."

**Jerome's testing.** He tested himself, on his own bench, in the little room off the intake, with his own glassware bought new, washed in the evening, kept in a rack he made; wrote his own book. He never compared a figure of his with anybody else's — "it never entered his head... He is not keeping a check on another man's arithmetic seven miles off over a mountain."

**His 1919 entry, copied exactly:**
> *The four Hessel Bottom men, summer of 1919: 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge.*
He had heard the Ordell talk (wet hay thins the cream; drain your land before you complain) and thought it nonsense, writing his opinions "in his own book where nobody would ever read it." His line: "the Ordell people had a theory about that bottom the way some families have a ghost: it explained things, and it cost nothing, and nobody was ever going to test it."

**Her testimony.** Told "a man from your county who came in here once in a temper": that milk was good milk. Season after season the four came in at about three-eighty and never gave an hour's trouble in the vat. The season averages of the four men taken together, copied straight off the leaves, nothing smoothed, no season thrown out; the men separately are within a few hundredths of each other and always were, "which is what you would expect of four herds off one piece of ground."

**On water, flatly.** A cheesemaker knows watered milk inside a day, without a test: the curd comes soft and will not knit; slack, weak body; yield down; "you can stand at the vat and see your money going out with the whey." It cannot be hidden from the man who makes the cheese — "the cheese tells him, and it tells him the next morning, not next year. Butter is more forgiving that way. Cheese is a witness." In sixteen summers Jerome never made one complaint of those four men, never docked one of them a cent, never said a word about their milk "except that he wished he had more of it in July."

**Distance from the quarrel.** The valley's trouble came to her "the way weather comes, in pieces and late, and always with somebody's name attached." She paid no attention — not her county, not her patrons in September, a make to get out. "I have listened to forty years of your valley explaining that meadow to itself, and I never once heard anybody go up there and look." She never saw the station's figures and wouldn't know how to get them. Invites Ivy over in summer — dinner, and her father's book, "a nicer object than anything I have said about it here."

## 11. Retelling 11 — Leland Vaught, introduction and method of Bulletin No. 471, October 1958

**Frame.** Leland Vaught (first name new here), professor of dairy husbandry, State Agricultural College at Farland; the opening and method sections of Bulletin No. 471, written for extension agents and county workers, closing with the bulletin's own concluding paragraph reproduced entire. Signed L. VAUGHT.

**§I Why written.** The station has been asked, "at intervals for fifty years," why one Ordell County skimming station's butterfat tests ran below its own creamery's seven miles down the same valley. Answered at various times "by reference to the land and by reference to the men. It is answered here by reference to a piece of glass." The simplicity is itself part of the history: "Nobody in that valley lacked the intelligence to find it. What they lacked was any occasion to suspect the one article in the plant that nobody in the trade is in the habit of suspecting." Carry-away sentence (bolded): **the milk pipette is the one instrument in the butterfat test which the plant cannot check against anything else, and it is the instrument on which every figure in the plant depends.**

**§II The instrument.** The test's four operations: measured charge drawn into a pipette and delivered into a test bottle; sulfuric acid dissolves everything but the fat; bottle spun in a hand tester; liberated fat rises into the graduated neck and is read DIRECTLY in percent. The operator weighs nothing; he is entitled to read a column-length as a percentage "for one reason only: the charge of milk that went into that bottle is a fixed and known quantity" — the maker rules the neck for that quantity and no other. Standard charge in the state and on this glassware: **17.6 c.c.** If the pipette delivers less, the reading is "low in the exact proportion by which the charge was short." Three consequences, set out because "in fifty years of this history nobody appears to have held all three in mind at once":
1. **Proportional, not additive.** Removes a fixed fraction, not fixed points; rich milk loses more absolutely; ratios between tests on the same short glass are undisturbed; the station shows a perfectly orderly, internally consistent set of figures; its herds rank correctly against one another; "Only against the outside world is it wrong."
2. **Precise.** A leaking pipe or slipping scale gives scattered error, and scatter attracts attention. "A pipette that delivers 16.27 c.c. where it is blown 17.6 delivers 16.27 c.c. every time, for a quarter of a century, in January and in July, for a careful operator and a careless one. It has the appearance of excellent work." (NOTE the 16.27 — see the conflict below.)
3. **Invisible from inside the plant.** Scales checkable with test weights; tester's speed countable; acid checkable for strength; bottle graduation checkable with mercury; "But the pipette is not checked against anything, because in the ordinary practice of the trade *the pipette is the standard*... An instrument that is itself the measure cannot be caught by measuring with it. It can be caught only by a burette brought in from outside, and burettes reached the state's field men very late in this history."

**§III Material examined.** Of the lot bought for the station in 1897 — "**five dozen** pipettes were purchased" (CONFLICT: the Ammon invoice, Ivy, and Nym all say SIX dozen) — **nine** recovered from the condemned crate, unbroken and legible; stems blown 17.6 c.c.; delivered against a calibrated burette, five runs each; "the mean delivery of the nine is **16.27 c.c.**" (CONFLICT: Ivy's measurement sheet and Nym Item 5 say 16.72 — the retelling's body looks like a digit transposition of 16.72, propagated through all its arithmetic). "The station's own glass went into use on **the first of May, 1898**" (CONFLICT: 1 May 1897 per the office file, Ivy, and the bulletin's own conclusion) "and remained in use until the state inspector condemned the lot in June of 1923." Method set out for repetition: fill to mark with water at room temperature, wipe the tip, deliver in the ordinary working manner — same interval of drainage, same touch of tip to the receiver wall — into a burette of certified graduation; five deliveries per pipette, averaged; "the whole of it occupies an afternoon and requires no apparatus a creamery does not own, save the burette. There is no reason why every station in this state should not be checked once in this way, and several reasons in this history why it should be."

**§IV The 1911 interval, "worth more than the arithmetic."** For nine weeks in summer 1911 the station's own pipette was broken and a borrowed measure used. Same patrons, herds, pasture, season, acid, bottles, tester and habits — "**One variable changed.**" The average rose from 3.61 to 3.80, held for the nine weeks, and returned to 3.61 the day the station's own measure came back — "recorded contemporaneously, in the operator's hand, by a man who did not know what he was recording and set a question mark against it." "I have spent my working life urging students to prefer an experiment to a calculation. Here is one that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven years" (1911+47=1958). It disposes of every explanation looking to herds, ground, or men, "for the sufficient reason that none of those was altered in those nine weeks, and the figures were."

**§V Magnitude (the retelling's aberrant arithmetic).** Ratio of delivered to marked capacity: 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = **0.9244**. Recorded average 3.61 → true average 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = **3.905**, shortfall **0.295** of a point. Applied to the 40,000,000 lb over the 25 full delivery years → **118,000 lb** of butterfat delivered and not credited. He himself concedes: "this figure is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the glassware." (ALL of this conflicts with Ivy's 0.95 / 3.80 / 0.19 / 76,000 lb — and with the bulletin's own §VII conclusion.) Also: the ledgers' "station gain" is "five and a quarter percent of the butterfat actually delivered" (repeats Teague's wrong denominator; Ivy/Nym: 5¼% of credited = 5% of delivered).

**§VI Feed.** Bulletin No. 214 (1911) quoted identically (bog hay depressed the test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, nothing measurable on summer pasture). "The discrepancy under examination is present in every month of the year and is some SIX times the largest effect the trial could produce" (6 × 0.05 = 0.30, fitting his 0.295; Ivy's version: the 0.19 gap is about four times 0.05 — "four times too small and it is the wrong shape").

**§VII Conclusion reproduced entire** — identical text to R04/Nym Item 15: no milk watered, no cream stolen; from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 tests low by ONE PART IN TWENTY because the pipettes were short by one part in twenty; 76,000 lb of butterfat credited year by year as station gain. — The body of this retelling (16.27, five dozen, 1898, 0.9244, 3.905, 0.295, 118,000, "six times") is thus internally inconsistent with its own quoted conclusion (0.95, one-in-twenty, 76,000, 1897) and with every other source.

## 12. Retelling 12 — Dell Rundle, "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years," jubilee pamphlet, 1939 (reprinted 1959)

**Frame.** Printed at Larrow Green for the Association's fiftieth year; compiled from recollections of old members and "two winters of asking questions at the store"; reissued 1959 with ONE footnote, closing note unaltered. Rundle taught the school at the Green a good many years; "What follows is not a record. It is what the valley remembers, tidied, and where the valley is certain of a thing I have written it down as certain."

**I. The men of 1889.** 1889: farmers met at Larrow Green (in the schoolhouse, cold weather; it went on very late; somebody's horse got loose in the middle) and organized the Association. They were tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking whatever the merchant chose to allow. Gideon Vose elected first president — "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the law and thought better of it" — wrote the by-laws himself at his own kitchen table "in a hand that the Association's clerks were still complaining about thirty years later." "His NEPHEW Selby Vose, afterward manager of the Association, used to say that his uncle wrote by-laws the way other men build stone wall — slowly, and to last." (Nephew — sides with Vira, against Effie and Selby's own 1926 "my father.") 1891: creamery built at Larrow Green "on the creek below the mill"; churn set up; the valley began to be paid in cash once a month, "which had never happened to it before." "There are men still living who can tell you what the first check did in their mother's kitchen, and they will tell you whether you ask or not."

**II. The rule.** Article VII printed whole (identical text). "Article VII dates from 1891, the year the churn was set up" (CONFLICT: Effie says adopted 1892; Grigg says "came in with the 1893 meeting"; the printed pamphlet itself carries no year per Nym). Thought a hard rule at the time; men LEFT the Association over it. A just rule: "the pound of milk is a liar where the pound of butterfat is not." But the last clause — the test of the station where he delivers shall govern; not the creamery's test, "not any test but the one made at his own door" — Gideon put in "for convenience and it was convenient for thirty years, and it is the sentence on which the whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned."

**III. The station.** The Hollow seven miles up; in the nineties the pull down was three hours in mud, impossible in March. Station opened 1896: weighed, tested, separated on the spot; cream down by wagon; skim home the same morning. Building plain, boarded and battened, intake room at the road end, "a loft over it that I believe has never been cleared out since it was put up"; behind it the creek, "which in a wet April comes up to the sill and has twice come over it."

**IV. The trouble.** From 1902 the Hollow men complained; not believed. 1908: committee up under Nyle Grigg; sampled Hessel Bottom herds through the winter; wet-hay finding; the Hollow did not accept it. 1919: the manager "circulated a warning to the Hollow patrons against adding water to their milk, and after that the two ends of this valley did not speak civilly to one another for six years" (→ to 1925). Rundle had children of both ends in one schoolroom: "it came down to them entire, without a word of it being explained, the way a debt does."
- The folklore, as printed: "It is the settled opinion of the valley that cream left that station by the back door, and the figures went right when the man who kept it did not. I set that down as I have heard it set down a hundred times, by men who were there and men who were not, and I have never heard it contradicted at the store."
- "The state inspector condemned the station's glassware on the TWELFTH OF JULY, 1923" (repeats Vose's July date; Strawn's own entry is 12 June) "for reasons of his own that had nothing to do with the quarrel"; new glass put in.
- January 1925: petition. "The station took in, over the years complained of, FORTY-FOUR million pounds of milk" (matches Teague's 44M; vs 40M consensus). "The patrons' loss upon it was NINETEEN THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED DOLLARS, and their petition set the figure out to the dollar" ($19,600 — new figure; Ivy computes $19,000; Frick reports the patrons' reckoning as 67,000 lb of fat). Board under Alonzo Frick sat at Ashlin that spring; heard everybody; "found nothing established, it made an award to the patrons without admitting anything, and it sent this valley home to live with itself, which the valley has done, after a fashion, ever since."

**V. Over the ridge.** 1914: the Alder Corners Cheese Company OPENED over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County, by Jerome Cudd; in the summers a few Hessel Bottom men hauled over the mountain to him rather than down to their own station; "That was resented here." "It is Jerome Cudd's DAUGHTER, Rosalie Cudd, who makes the cheese at Alder Corners now."
- **Footnote added to the 1959 reprint:** "The Hollow matter has since been taken up again at the college at Farland by Miss Ivy Keddie, who is SECOND COUSIN to Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom. ELEVEN pipettes out of the station's old condemned crate were found still to be in existence. I am too old to rewrite a pamphlet and I refer the reader to the college. — D.R." (Eleven — CONFLICT: nine per Ivy, Nym, Vaught. Second cousin — agrees with Roy, against Vira.)

**VI. Fifty years.** Fifty Junes of cream coming down in the early cool; fifty Januaries over ice with the horses roughshod; a check every month in kitchens that had known none; "a creamery still standing that was built by men with nothing but each other and a piece of paper they had written themselves."

**NOTE ADDED WHILE IN PRESS (1939).** While the pamphlet was in type Rundle was allowed to look at the Association's old ledgers "on another errand altogether." The station account carries every year the entry (quoted identically to Teague's 1898 form: credited 57,760 lb; received 60,800 lb; station gain 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual; carried to the general account). Then the retraction: "I must go back on Chapter IV. Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green LIGHT. It reached the Green HEAVY, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand. I leave it as written and correct it here." — Rundle thus refuted the cream-theft folklore in print in 1939, nineteen years before Ivy, from the ledger alone; the belief persisted anyway (Vira still holds it in 1979).

## 13. Retelling 13 — Alonzo Frick, "Recollections of the Ordell Arbitration," Ordell County Bar Association yearbook, 1946

**Frame.** The chairman of the 1925 board, aged seventy-four in 1946 (born ~1872); forty years of titles and boundaries and two homicides, and "none of them has stayed with me as the Ostrey Hollow matter has." An arbitration under the by-laws — "no appeal from it, no reported decision, and nothing whatever to cite it for." Includes the exhibit list reprinted from his own file.

**The complaint as he frames it.** Payment on fat by test; the test of the station where the man delivers governs. "I say nothing against the rule. But it makes the test the whole of the bargain, and it leaves a dissatisfied man with no remedy on earth except to quarrel with a figure." The patrons "had been paid, year upon year, on an average test of 3.16" (repeats Teague's transposed 3.16; the book says 3.61). "Three and sixteen is not a scandalous figure standing by itself. It becomes one when the man over the hill is paid on a better figure for milk out of cows no different from yours; when the difference holds steady for a quarter of a century; and when nobody in the office can tell you the cause of it."

**The board.** January 1925 petition; the officers did not resist arbitration. A board of THREE constituted; Frick named chairman, "I have always supposed because I was the one of the three who kept no cow."

**The three sessions.** First at the Association's office at Ashlin, 17 March 1925. THE SECOND AND THIRD HE ASKED TO HAVE AT OSTREY HOLLOW ITSELF, in the station's own weigh room, "so that the board might see the plant it was to pass upon instead of hearing it described by men with an interest in the description. That was my doing and I am still content with it." (Effie/Teague/Vose describe the board sitting at Ashlin.) "Every physical appliance of that station was before us and we satisfied ourselves upon it. We saw the scales tried. We saw the sampling done and the test made" — though by spring 1925 the station was on the NEW glass (post-July 1923), so the demonstration could not exhibit the fault; and per Effie the crate of condemned measures was never mentioned in any session. "I did not understand the test then and do not understand it now; I am a lawyer and the thing is chemistry. But I could see that it was done the same way twice, and that the men who did it were not hiding their hands."

**The three explanations, WITH AUTHORS** (they "did not sit easily together and nobody seemed to mind that they did not"):
1. **Water — Selby Vose's.** He had circulated the warning years before and stood by it: the Hollow's milk came in thinned; "a little water in a can is the oldest trick in the trade"; a station cannot test what is not there. "He put it without heat and without proof."
2. **Cream-theft — Wilbur Teague's**, "in the way an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it": asked about the station's account, he "answered at length about how such an account would look if a man were helping himself, and then observed that he made no charge against anybody. There is no cross-examination that reaches a witness of that kind, and I did not attempt one."
3. **The hay.** "Somebody read the report of the 1908 committee into the record," and the bog meadow was before the board "the better part of an afternoon — the wet ground, the coarse hay off it, the cows that eat it all winter. It is an old opinion in that valley and it had the advantage of blaming nobody in the room."

**Keddie.** Tested since the station opened in 1896; book in his own hand every season. His 17 March statement summarized (consistent with R03: tested as taught and as the state man saw, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons; cannot tell why the figures are what they are; never took a pound of cream). "I believed him then and I believe him now. His denial was accurate and it did him no good at all, and the reason it did him no good is that a man who cannot account for a figure is presumed by everybody in the room to be the figure's author."
- **NEW, not in the minutes:** in the corridor at Ashlin, Keddie told Frick privately "that he had always thought the PATRONS were at fault and would not say so in the room. He was not a bitter man about it. He had lived among them thirty years and would not stand up and accuse his neighbors, and I record it here because it has been said since that he went to his grave accusing nobody, which is true, and that he suspected nobody, which is not." (So Keddie privately leaned toward the water explanation — and was as wrong as everyone else.)

**Dismissal and award.** "On the thirtieth of April, four days before we made our award, the Association dismissed him. There was no finding against him from us and there never was one; the board had not concluded and did not conclude that he had done anything whatever. He was simply let go while the board was still sitting, and I have never been able to think well of the Association for it." 4 May 1925: no cause established; $3,800 to the Ostrey Hollow patrons, without admission, charged to the station account. "The patrons' own reckoning of what they had lost was SIXTY-SEVEN THOUSAND POUNDS of butter fat over the years complained of, and nothing was ever brought forward to disturb that figure" (vs Ivy's computed 76,000 lb; vs Rundle's $19,600 petition figure). "We were not able to say who owed it or why, and we said so. An award without a finding is an unsatisfactory thing and every member of that board knew it."

**The exhibit list, reprinted exactly:**
> EXHIBITS BEFORE THE BOARD OF ARBITRATION, OSTREY HOLLOW MATTER, 1925
> 1. The test book of the Ostrey Hollow station, 4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924, in the hand of Ansel Keddie.
> 2. The receiving book of the said station.
> 3. The private weigh book of Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom, begun 1 January 1897.
> 4. The circuit notebook of the state dairy inspector for the year 1923.
"Four books and nothing else. It is a short list for a matter of that size, and I have looked at it many times since." (Exhibit 2 — a station receiving book — is a record not in Nym's catalogue. Exhibit 3's start date 1 Jan 1897 vs Roy's "first of January, 1898." Exhibit 4 means Strawn's 12 June 1923 condemnation entry was physically in evidence before the board — yet per Effie the crate/condemnation was never spoken of in any session.)

**Reflection.** "A board of arbitration can only decide between the accounts that are put to it. Three were put to us; each of them was urged by a man of standing; each of them would have been believed by half the valley; and not one of them was proved. I have wondered since whether the answer was in that room and simply was not in any of the three stories, and whether a lawyer who understood chemistry might have found it. I did not understand chemistry. I understood that an honest man was ruined by a figure nobody could explain, and that we paid his neighbors money without being able to tell them what they were being paid for."

## 14. Retelling 14 — Hobart Sill, buttermaker (ret.), remarks recorded at the Larrow Green creamery on the day he left it, 1934

**Frame.** Taken down as spoken; "a man who made the Association's butter for forty-one years and had no patience with anybody who did not." Came in 1893 (41 years to 1934) — "there was no floor in this room but plank, and we cooled with creek ice out of the pond." Aged sixty-four (born ~1870). "I never sent out a tub of butter I would not eat myself, which is more than the office can say about anything it ever sent out."

**The cream ran HEAVY.** The cream wagon came down from Ostrey Hollow every day but Sunday, seven miles of hill road, warm in July no matter how early they started; set off at the platform, weighed, sampled, into the cold room. "That cream ran heavy. Every year it ran heavy. There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there... Always more. Never less." He weighed it himself, over again, when it came into the cold room — "I have never in my life taken another man's figure for what was going into my churn" — the receiving book "ruled and signed before I put my coat on," his hand on every page from '93, "the Hollow's cream coming in heavy on page after page after page." He told men in that room a hundred times; "none of them ever thought it was worth anything." His own dismissal of it: "Cream is not milk and it will not stand still and be counted... So it ran heavy. So does everybody's. I never made anything of it, and I will say plain, nobody ever asked me to." (Physical, annual eyewitness of the station gain at the receiving end — the same fact as the ledger line and Rundle's press note.)

**His figures.** Larrow Green's own patrons "ran 3.78 taking one year with another right through those years" — "ordinary milk, ordinary county, ordinary cows" (fits Nym Item 16: the Green ran "a little under two tenths" better than the station's 3.61). His claim for the Hollow: "The Hollow milk was really a 3.85 and no better... Wet ground up there, but good cows, and I never had a word to say against the cows. A 3.85 and no better, and if the figures they were paid on came out under that, then somebody up that hill road was helping the cans along, and I said so at the time and I say it now." (True figure 3.80 — his 3.85 overstates; a water believer: "You cannot tell me nine families all got honest in one summer. There was water in those cans and I said so at the time.")

**On the change.** "One summer, along in the twenties, those figures came up and they stayed up, and nothing on this earth changed but the season... Something got stopped. Something got said to somebody. I never knew what and I never asked, because the day a buttermaker starts asking about other men's cans is the day he stops making butter."

**1902 and 1919.** 1902 is the first he remembers of the complaints — "a man in here shouting that his milk tested under his BROTHER-IN-LAW'S down the creek and it was the same feed and the same water and what did we mean by it." The 1919 circular — "warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons about water in the milk, plain as that. I thought well of him for it. It was the only plain thing that office ever did, and of course they hated him for it up the Hollow, and of course it changed nothing, because a paper never emptied a can."

**On offices.** "The Association has a paper for everything and butter for nothing." Men in collars told him his churn ran slow "when they could not have told you which end of a cow gets up first." His butter took premiums in three states; not one office man ever came down to see it made; "every one of them could tell you to the half cent what the fat was worth. TWENTY-SEVEN CENTS. That is what fat was through those years, take one year with another, twenty-seven cents the pound" (vs the 25¢ consensus; Kepp also says 27¢; Roy: 24¢ in 1910, ~25¢ overall).

**THE 1896 OPENING GLASS WAS HIS.** When the station opened — "that was ninety-six, in May, and there was a crowd went up for it" — it had no outfit of its own: "It was ordered and it was not come, and they were ready to start taking milk, and there was talk of holding off the whole season. And I said no, they can have mine. I went to my own bench in this room and I made up an outfit for them out of my own glass, my own, that I had used and knew, and it went up the valley on the wagon and they opened on it. Their own came the spring after and they sent mine back to me, and one piece of it was chipped and I never said a word about that either." (Confirms Keddie's first leaf "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green"; contradicts Keddie's letter's "borrowed off the Ashlin creamery." Also explains why 1896's average was the TRUE 3.80: the borrowed year ran on Sill's good glass; the short Ammon glass arrived spring 1897.)

**The 1896 season's intake.** "That first season they took in about NINE HUNDRED THOUSAND pounds up there. May to the end of the year, that is, so it was not even a whole one." (Nym's queries say no figure for 1896 exists in the ledgers or anywhere — Sill supplies one from memory.) "It was the biggest thing that ever happened to Ostrey Hollow, and I was glad enough to have had a hand in it."

**Closing.** He went up to the Hollow only twice in his life; took the cream off the platform and made it into good butter — "as good as any, and better than most, and there was never a season it was not." "Put in about the butter. Do not put in that I said anything against the cows up there, because I did not. The cows were never the trouble. Put in that Hobart Sill made the butter and it was good butter and he stood at the churn until the day they gave him a chair."

## 15. Retelling 15 — Nyle Grigg, covering letter of 1919 to the reprinted 1908 committee report

**Frame.** A farmer of standing at Larrow Green sends the eleven-year-old report back into circulation "the Ostrey Hollow question having been reopened this season by men who were boys when we made it," adding a covering letter claiming the intervening science proved him right. Committee of THREE ("we three were named"), appointed by the board in the winter (report: "appointed in January last" — January 1908) when the board "could not let it alone any longer without seeming to have an interest in letting it alone." "I have never in my life been given a duty I liked less."

**Covering letter (1919) points.**
- From about 1902 grumbling out of Ostrey Hollow; "It was not one man and it was not one season. It went on and it grew," into annual-meeting talk and every crossroads store between the Green and Ashlin, taking "the shape such things always take, which is that somebody must be doing it on purpose."
- "Payment by test came in with the 1893 MEETING, as I have always understood it" — a THIRD dating of Article VII (Rundle 1891, Effie 1892, Grigg 1893, pamphlet undated per Nym) — "and from that year a difference of this kind stopped being a matter of opinion and became a matter of money."
- His standing: knew the Bottom men since he was young; bought hay off two of them; NOT a patron of the station — "I haul to Larrow Green and always have"; chosen for disinterest; "I have been abused for the finding by men on both sides of it, which I take to be a fair sign that the finding was an honest one."
- **Memory inflation (internal contradiction):** the letter claims "We sampled those herds through THE WHOLE OF 1908 — every month of that year, in the barn and on the pasture, out of the night's milk and the morning's, and we did not let a man know which day we were coming" — while his own report, printed below it, says the sampling was through January, February and March (as Effie/Nym also record).
- **Bulletin 214 misquote:** the College's feeding trial "found the bog hay to depress the butter fat test by TWO TENTHS in the winter feeding. Two tenths, gentlemen, out of a College that had never heard of Hessel Bottom and had no reason on earth to flatter us. There is our finding, arrived at by another road and by better men, three years after we set our names to it" (1908+3=1911 — dating consistent; the FIGURE is wrong: the bulletin says NOT MORE THAN FIVE HUNDREDTHS in winter and nothing in summer, which refutes rather than vindicates the hay theory).
- His closing prescription: "Drain that bottom, sweeten it, keep the stock off it in the wet of the spring, and feed the coarse hay off it with a mixed ration and not by itself, and the Hollow will test with the rest of us. Go on as they are going and abuse the man who reads their figures for them, and they will be grumbling in twenty years' time as they were grumbling when we began, and no better off."

**The 1908 report as printed.**
- The complaint restated: Hollow milk returned by test below milk delivered elsewhere; patrons paid less "for the same labor and the same cows." "Since payment by test came in among us and the pound of butter fat took the place of the pound of milk as the measure of a man's month, a difference of this kind is no longer a matter of opinion. It is a matter of money, and it comes out of the same pocket every month, and your committee has approached it in that light and not as a quarrel."
- Hessel Bottom described: "where SIX farms cut hay and turn out stock" (six — with Roy and Kepp, vs Effie's seven); wet meadow along the creek under Ostrey Hollow; ground springy year round, part under water every spring; herbage sedge, rush, and coarse sour grasses; hay cut late and heavy, fed as the main dependence November to April; "There is no better winter keep to be had there. We say this without reproach to any man on that bottom; the land is what it is, and they took it as their fathers left it to them."
- Method: visited each of the six farms, some three and four times; drew and sealed their own samples; saw the herds milked, the cans filled, hauled to the station and weighed in; "stood by while the test was made and saw nothing whatever in the doing of it to remark upon"; tried the station scales against a known weight AND the patrons' own scales against the station's; inquired into the water supply at each barn and were satisfied.
- Stock observations: herds "thin in the flank at the end of the winter feeding and slow to come to their flesh in the spring"; coats rough later in the season than hill herds'; do well in flow ("the wet land is generous of milk"); "it is our opinion, formed on the ground and not out of a book, that what such land gives in quantity it withholds in quality, and that a cow cannot put into her pail what her keep has never put into her."
- **"We report that we found no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter, and we wish that finding to be printed in the same type as the rest."** (A line not quoted in earlier sources.)
- Final paragraph = the familiar text (sampled through January, February and March; milk whole; scales honest; wet hay thins the cream; remedy with the land and not the station). Signed NYLE GRIGG, chairman, and the committee.
- Post-report line: the report was received and accepted "and the question was closed by it for eleven years — until this season, and the men who have reopened it have brought nothing new before us but their impatience."

## 16. Retelling 16 — Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S., "The bog meadows and the milk," paper before the Ordell County Farm Club, 1926

**Frame.** A veterinary surgeon of Ashlin, thirty years' practice, reads a paper bringing "the agricultural literature to bear on an old local grievance"; the floor discussion appended as taken down by the club's secretary. His habit with an ailing herd: "ask first what the cattle are standing on... the answer to nine cases in ten is under the animal's feet, and... the tenth is in the water she drinks." The question "has been dealt with hitherto by opinion, by grumbling, and by the naming of names. It deserves better."

**The land (his soil case).** A wet bottom is "a distinct kind of ground with a distinct kind of chemistry, and the water is the least part of it": without drainage the year's growth lies sour; acid soil, poor in lime and the phosphates; sweet grasses crowded out within a few seasons by sedge, rush, and coarse tussock grasses. That herbage carries bulk and water but not lime or phosphorus in any quantity; cut late because such ground cannot be got onto early, "and late cutting takes out what little was in it."

**The cow.** "A cow in milk is a manufactory working on a narrow margin"; short of minerals she "robs her own frame to make good, and she goes on robbing it until she cannot." Wet-meadow herds: thin over the loin out of winter feeding, rough in the coat, slow to condition in spring, "ready subjects for every disorder of the digestion in the fall," breed later, worse feet. "They will milk, gentlemen. Wet land is generous of flow and no man on a bog meadow ever complained that his pail was light. But what is in the pail is another matter, and the fat is the first thing to suffer, because the fat is the dearest thing the cow makes and it is the first economy she practices when she is pressed."

**His numbers.** Field observation of thirty years: "The wet bottoms of this county yield a milk poorer in fat by A FIFTH OF A POINT [0.20] and every practical man knows it," hill herds against bottom herds, farm for farm, independent of breed, man, or barn. Hessel Bottom: "the completest example of wet meadow in this county"; SIX farms cut hay and pasture stock on it; springs come out at the foot of the slope and run year round; part under water every spring, "not fit to be walked on until well into May"; sedge hay "the winter dependence and very nearly the whole of it." The station gap "as it has been given to me and as I have satisfied myself, is TWENTY-ONE HUNDREDTHS of a point — 0.21" (vs Roy's 0.17, the true 0.19; same number Vose used in 1926 for the 1924 RISE). The patrons' complaint "is perfectly just and I have never doubted it." Praises the 1908 committee (herds sound, milk whole, scales honest, cause in the hay). "It is settled already, in the literature, for anybody who will read."

**Bulletin 214 misquote (same as Grigg's).** "The College fed the bog hay under control and measured what it did, and it found the butter fat test depressed by TWO TENTHS in the winter feeding. That is the whole case. Two tenths from the College, laid alongside the fifth of a point I have found in the field and the twenty-one hundredths that station has been complaining of, and the three agree so nearly that I do not know what more a reasonable man requires." (The bulletin actually says ≤0.05 winter / nothing summer — it REFUTES the theory. The "two tenths" error is identical to Grigg's 1919 letter — apparently propagated from Grigg's reprint rather than from the bulletin itself. Kepp's "agreement" of 0.20 / 0.20 / 0.21 is an agreement among a misquote, his own impression, and an inflated gap.)

**The 1911 objection dismissed.** "It has been urged upon me, by a gentleman who I know will forgive my noticing it here" (someone was pressing the 1911 argument in 1926, long before Ivy), that in summer 1911 "the testing at that station was broken off for SIX WEEKS, their own apparatus having failed them and a borrowed one being used, and that the figures stood higher during those six weeks than before or after" (six weeks — with Teague, vs the dated nine weeks 8 May–10 July). His reply: "Six weeks in the flush of the season, gentlemen. Six weeks of June and July, when every herd in the county is at its best and the stock are off the wet ground and up on the aftermath. I am astonished that anybody should found an argument on six weeks of summer figures. I could produce you six weeks in any year that would prove anything you liked." (Ignores that the gap was year-round — Roy: 0.17 in July AND February — and that the rise and fall were pinned to the exact days the measure changed.)

**The remedy.** "Not a mystery and not cheap": drain the bottom, open the old ditches and cut new, lime and lime again, sow it down when it will carry a seeding, keep the stock off in the wet of the spring ("a hoof does more harm in a day than a season will repair"), never feed that hay alone — mixed ration and a mineral supplement "as the College directs." "Fat has been worth TWENTY-SEVEN CENTS the pound through these years" (with Sill's 27¢; vs 25¢ consensus), and a fifth of a point off every hundredweight "will pay for a great deal of tile."

**Discussion from the floor.** A member: might the difference be in the cows rather than the land — the bottom farms being poorer men with poorer stock? Kepp: considered and rejected; "some of the best-bred cattle in the county stand on wet land, and some very indifferent cattle on the hills test well"; a man may improve his herd twenty years and never get his fat up while his ground is sour. A member: might the trouble lie in the station rather than the bottom, "as had been said in the valley for years"? Kepp: "he had no interest in that quarrel and no competence in it; that he had never in his life read a test and would not know how; but that when a cause is found in the soil, in the herbage, in the condition of the stock, in the College's trial, and in every wet bottom in the county alike, it is not good sense to go looking for a second cause in a building." The chairman thanked him; the paper to be printed for the members.

50. Keddie's private suspicion: corridor remark to Frick — he "had always thought the patrons were at fault" — vs the public record of accusing nobody. (He privately credited the water story; the truth blamed no one.)
51. The station's receiving book (Frick's Exhibit 2) exists in no other source — not among Nym's Items 1–16.
52. Grigg's 1919 covering letter vs his own 1908 report: sampling "through the whole of 1908 — every month... barn and pasture... night's milk and the morning's... unannounced" vs the report's "through January, February and March" (Effie/Nym agree with Jan–Mar).
53. Bulletin 214 misquoted as "two tenths in the winter feeding" by BOTH Grigg (1919) and Kepp (1926); the bulletin says not more than five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer. The identical error suggests Kepp took the figure from Grigg's reprint, not the bulletin. Kepp then claims three-way "agreement" (0.20 field / "0.20" College / 0.21 gap).
54. Kepp dismisses the 1911 interval as "six weeks of summer figures" (also wrong length) when the record shows a year-round gap (Roy: 0.17 in July AND February; Keddie: July as February) and a rise/fall pinned to the days the measure changed; he also has the stock "off the wet ground" in June–July although the episode began 8 May.
55. Fat price: 25¢ period average (Effie, Ivy, Nym; Roy ~25¢, 24¢ in 1910) vs 27¢ "through those years" (Sill 1934, Kepp 1926).
56. Sill's Hollow estimate "really a 3.85 and no better" vs the demonstrated true 3.80 — his inference (water) was wrong but his premise (the paid figures were too low) was right; he had daily physical proof (heavy cream) and "nobody ever asked me to" make anything of it.
57. Sill's memory of the change: "one summer, along in the twenties" the figures came up — consistent with the July 1923 glass change; he attributes it to watering having "got stopped."
58. Sill's 1902 memory — a man shouting his milk tested under his BROTHER-IN-LAW's down the creek — resonates oddly with the valley's brother-in-law pair (Keddie/Brant), though those two were both at the Hollow end.
59. Sill's account resolves the 1896-glass question (his own outfit, of Larrow Green) and adds that one piece came back chipped; Keddie's "Ashlin creamery" version stands isolated and is contradicted by Keddie's own first leaf.
60. 1896 intake: officially nonexistent (Nym's queries) vs Sill's ~900,000 lb May–December — also note 900,000 in ~8 months vs the later 1,600,000/yr average.
61. Roy's "the book has outlived two of the three men who are in it" — Emmet (d. 1929) and Ansel (d. 1934), Roy surviving; Keddie is "in" the book as the station column.
62. The stepmother rumor: Roy insists Orra and Emmet were full siblings "whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it" — a rumor recorded nowhere else.
63. Rundle 1939 (nineteen years before Bulletin 471) already proved from the ledger that cream theft was impossible ("It reached the Green heavy") — printed as an in-press correction while leaving the folklore paragraph standing; the correction changed nothing in the valley (Vira 1979).
64. Rundle's pamphlet repeats Vose's wrong July date and Teague's 44M; "what the valley remembers, tidied."
65. Alder Corners chronology: built 1913 (Rosalie) / opened 1914 (Roy, Rundle); four men hauled summers 1915–1930 (sixteen seasons; June 1–mid-September; ridge closed Nov–Apr/May, so winter hauling impossible).
66. Rosalie's table (3.78–3.82, ~3.80, sixteen seasons) exactly matches the true average from the pipette arithmetic — independent confirmation from a tester who never saw the station's figures and never compared ("it never entered his head").
67. Jerome Cudd's cheese-vat argument against water ("cheese is a witness"; watered milk shows in the curd the next morning): in sixteen summers no complaint, no docking — direct rebuttal of the water thesis for at least the four Alder Corners men.
68. Frick believed Keddie ("I believed him then and I believe him now") while Teague — a fellow officer — insinuated theft; Vira's father privately thought Keddie honest too ("an honest man who had got into something") while Vira concluded theft.
69. Vaught's "fifty years" of being asked (→ from ~1908) vs complaints from 1902; his "forty-seven years" for 1911→1958 is exact.
70. The 1911 argument was already circulating by 1926 — "urged upon me by a gentleman" (Kepp) — identity unknown (Emmet Brant kept both 1911 entries; Keddie kept his question mark).
71. The 1919 reopening: Grigg's letter says the question was "reopened this season by men who were boys when we made it" — i.e., patron agitation preceded and presumably provoked Vose's circular the same year; the report had "closed" the question for eleven years (1908–1919).
72. Kepp's floor answer — "it is not good sense to go looking for a second cause in a building" — inverts the truth (the cause was in the building); likewise his "I could produce you six weeks in any year that would prove anything you liked" dismisses the one clean natural experiment.
73. Chain of scale-corroboration: Emmet's two agreeing columns (Roy), the committee's scale checks (1908), Strawn's "scales true" every visit, Keddie's undisputed weights — ALL sources agree the weighing was honest; the fault was confined to the volumetric glass, which nobody could check from inside (Vaught's three consequences).
74. Documents-in-a-drawer motif: the 1919 circular in nine drawers (one in a trunk lid), Emmet's copy of the 1908 report "in the drawer," Keddie's unsent letter among his papers, Jerome's opinions "in his own book where nobody would ever read it," Frick's exhibit list "looked at many times since" — the record repeatedly existed and went unread (Vaught: the 1911 experiment "lay unread for forty-seven years").
