## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1 — The Rule That Made a Figure the Whole Bargain

**Who:** the founding farmers of the Larrow Valley; Gideon Vose. **What:** In 1889 the valley's farmers, tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking what the merchant allowed, met in the schoolhouse at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose — "once thought of the law and thought better of it" — was elected first president and held the office twenty-two years, to 1911; he died in 1917. In 1891 the Association built its creamery at Larrow Green on Larrow Creek, and the valley began to be paid in cash monthly, which had never happened to it before. Vose drafted the by-laws himself at his kitchen table. Article VII, quoted identically by three sources: every patron paid on the pounds of butterfat found by test, not on the weight of his milk — "and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." The adoption year cannot be fixed: the printed pamphlet carries no date on its face (Nym, Item 1), Effie Loomis says 1892, Rundle 1891, Grigg "the 1893 meeting" — and the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move, so the county agent Falke rightly declined to date it; so do I. Men left the Association over the rule. It was a just rule — "the pound of milk is a liar where the pound of butterfat is not" — but its last clause allowed no appeal from a station's own test. The test became the whole of the bargain, leaving a dissatisfied man "no remedy on earth except to quarrel with a figure" (Frick), written by a man who died before he could be asked about it. Under payment by weight, nothing that followed at Ostrey Hollow would have cost anyone a dollar (Falke). **Documents:** the by-laws pamphlet (Nym Item 1); Effie's 1941 notes; Rundle's 1939 jubilee pamphlet; Grigg's 1919 covering letter; Falke's 1959 report; Frick's 1946 recollections.

### Story 2 — Ansel Keddie's Twenty-Nine Seasons

**Who:** Ansel Keddie (1861–1934), tester and separator man; his wife Orra; their son Duncan. **What:** Keddie went up in 1895 to ready the new skimming station, opened it 4 May 1896, and made every test through 1924 — twenty-nine seasons, thirty-one tests a week, alone in the building. He married Orra Brant in 1889 (born 1866: her diary's preface, her own "I am sixty-four" in August 1930, and Duncan's "eighty-two when she died in 1948" outvote Duncan's stated "1868"). Their son Duncan (b. 1894) taught school at Ashlin from 1911. Keddie's method was the point of him: every can weighed and the weight called before the milk moved; composite samples warmed blood-warm and mixed; the charge drawn to the mark; the fat column read with dividers; a book with every error lined through and initialed, never a figure written over a figure. The state inspector: "works slow and clean and reads his own figure without being coaxed to it." His averages ran 3.80 in 1896 (on borrowed true glass) and 3.61 within 0.03 from 1898 on — and he could not say why: "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are." In the summer of 1911 he came home saying, twice, that it was something in the glass (Duncan), but would not write what he could not prove — only a question mark he let stand fourteen years. He bore the 1902 talk and the 1908 committee; in 1919 he hand-carried the manager's water circular to every family himself — "I would not have another man carry it" — and it cost him his brother-in-law. Before the 1925 board he made a nine-sentence statement; privately, in the Ashlin corridor, he told Frick he had always thought the patrons at fault. Dismissed 30 April 1925, four days before the award, he came up the road at his ordinary hour, hung the apron behind the shed door, and ate his supper. He lived nine years as a carpenter — better at it than the other trade, at a quarter of the pay — died February 1934, and was cleared twenty-four years later. **Documents:** his statement and unsent letter (r03); Orra's diary (r24); Duncan's notes (r18); Frick (r13); Strawn's extracts (r06); Vira's tape (r01).

### Story 3 — The Brants of Hessel Bottom and the Weigh Book

**Who:** Emmet Brant (1854–1929), his son Roy, Roy's daughter Hazel. **What:** Emmet farmed the largest of the SIX farms on Hessel Bottom (the 1908 report's own count, with Roy, Kepp, and Hazel; Effie's "seven" is the odd figure out) — sixty-odd of its 160 wet acres, thirty-eight cows — and hauled more milk to the station than any man. From 1 January 1897 (Frick's exhibit list and Nym's provenance are documentary; Roy's "1898" and Hazel's "1900" are memory) he wrote every delivery in a ledger: his own barn scale's weight beside the figure the station gave back. The two columns agreed for thirty years — proof, kept unknowingly, that whatever was wrong at that station, it was not the weighing. The 1910 leaf: 200,000 lb even, "even" underlined, paid on a test of 3.42. Emmet was among the first to say aloud, in 1902, that the tests ran low, and was laughed at; he read the 1908 hay report twice and put it in the drawer ("the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not"); he entered the 1911 rise and fall and could make nothing of it. The 1919 circular ended relations with his brother-in-law Ansel — not over the water itself, Roy insists, but because the keeper of the test book had carried the letter and not stood up against it; Orra's diary records Emmet in the yard asking Ansel why he had carried it. Ten years of silence, ended only by Emmet's death in 1929. Roy (b. ~1888) kept the book on identically, refused the Alder Corners haul ("the biggest herd cannot afford to be talked about" — "a poor reason and cost us money"), lent — lent, not left — the book to Ivy in 1958, and died in 1962; Hazel gave it to the Historical Rooms in 1963. Roy insists Emmet and Orra were full siblings; Hazel's "half sister by his father's second wife" preserves exactly the stepmother rumor Roy wrote to kill. **Documents:** the weigh book (Nym Item 4; Frick's Exhibit 3); Roy's 1955 account (r09); Hazel's 1981 tape (r17); Duncan's notes (r18); Orra's diary (r24).

### Story 4 — The Short Glass

**Who:** Ammon & Sons of Tarnet; the station's testing outfit; inspector Merle Strawn. **What:** On 14 April 1897 Ammon & Sons invoiced the Association $77.93 for the Ostrey Hollow outfit: six dozen milk-test pipettes blown 17.6 c.c. at 35 cents, twelve dozen 8-per-cent test bottles, an acid measure, a four-bottle hand tester, freight. (The invoice survives in two identical transcriptions; the itemized sum proves $77.93 against the "$77.39" carried by both Junia Ammon's prose and the Chronicle.) The lot was blown short — mean delivery 16.72 c.c., 95 hundredths of the mark. Junia Ammon's explanation: a reground mold, uncatchable because the lot was consistent — "consistently wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind cannot find"; the works records burned in 1931. The glass went into use 1 May 1897 (Orra's diary that very day: the own glass came up, "done borrowing of Larrow Green"), replacing the true outfit buttermaker Hobart Sill had made up from his own Larrow Green bench for the 1896 opening — which is why 1896 alone reads 3.80. The pipette is the one instrument a plant cannot check against anything else: it IS the standard; scales, tester speed, acid, and bottle necks are all checkable, so the error was proportional, precise, and invisible from inside (Vaught). It flashed into view once: on 8 May 1911 the measure broke; a borrowed one served nine weeks; every farm's tests rose to 3.80 and fell back on 10 July, the day the own glass returned; Keddie's question mark stood unread forty-seven years. Burettes reached the state's field men only in 1921; Strawn skipped the check in 1922, ran it on 12 June 1923, and condemned the whole crate on the spot — "Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them." Replacement ordered 28 June; Ammon's reply 2 July; new glass in use in July, verified true in October; 1924 tested 3.80. The condemned crate went up the loft ladder and sat under a tarpaulin thirty-five years, its six dozen whittled to nine survivors by men climbing up after wire (Oram). **Documents:** the invoice (Nym Item 2); Junia Ammon's chapter (r20); Strawn's 1923 entry (r06; Item 9); the Ammon letter (Item 10); Keddie's 1911 entries (r03); Oram's tape (r21).
