## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### Story 5 — The Three Wrong Answers

**Who:** the valley's theorists — Nyle Grigg, Dr. Roland Kepp, Selby Vose, Wilbur Teague — against the Hollow's patrons. **What:** From 1902 the grievance grew — "it was not one man and it was not one season" — and the valley answered it three ways, all wrong, all aimed at men or land. HAY: a committee of three under Grigg (a Larrow Green hauler, chosen for disinterest) sampled the six Hessel Bottom herds January–March 1908, found "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter," and blamed the wet bog hay. The College's own controlled trial, Bulletin 214 (1911), found bog hay depressed the test by at most five hundredths of a point in winter and nothing measurable in summer — a refutation. Grigg's 1919 reprint nevertheless inflated the sampling to "the whole of 1908" against his own printed report and misquoted the bulletin as "two tenths"; Kepp's 1926 paper repeated the identical misquote (evidently from Grigg's reprint), claimed a 0.21 gap, and waved off the 1911 interval as "six weeks of summer figures." Defeated by the gap's flatness (0.19 in July as in February — a feed effect is seasonal), by Bulletin 214's real figures, and by the same cows testing ~3.80 at Alder Corners. WATER: manager Vose's theory. His 1919 circular warned nine families about added water (typed by clerk Kyle "in a civil form of words but with no doubt at all in it about what was meant"); he stood by it before the 1925 board "without heat and without proof," and in 1926 credited the 1924 rise to the Association's scrutiny. Defeated by inspector Strawn's lactometer, which found no added water on any visit 1907–1934 ("as always here" — and he told Vose so in 1919); by cheesemaker Jerome Cudd, to whom watered milk shows in the curd "the next morning, not next year," and who never once docked the four Hollow men in sixteen summers; and by the rise arriving with the new glass of July 1923, not with the January 1925 petition. THEFT: the valley's settled folklore that cream left the station by the back door, insinuated at the hearing by bookkeeper Teague ("cream is portable... the remedy is two men or a lock"). Defeated by the Association's own ledgers — the station's cream reached Larrow Green HEAVY every year; stolen cream arrives light. Rundle printed that refutation in 1939; Vira was still telling the theft story in 1979. **Documents:** the 1908 report with Grigg's letter (r15); Kepp's paper (r16); Vose's address (r08); Strawn's extracts (r06); Rosalie Cudd's letter (r10); Bulletin 214 (Nym Item 12).

### Story 6 — The Ledger in Plain Sight

**Who:** Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945; Hobart Sill, buttermaker 1893–1934; Bertram Kyle, clerk; Dell Rundle. **What:** Every year from 1898 the station account carried one entry in one form — 1898's, verbatim: fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb; fat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream, 60,800 lb; "Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account." Teague claims he devised the line when he came in 1905, yet quotes the 1898 entry in that exact form — the line, or his memory, is older than he says. Over the twenty-five full years the gain accumulated to 76,000 lb of butterfat, worth $19,000: the exact measure of what the glass withheld, entered annually in ink. Five and a quarter per cent of the fat CREDITED is five per cent of the fat DELIVERED — and five per cent is the glass. Nobody read it. Teague put the anomaly to Vose once, in 1916, was told to let it alone, and did — "the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience." Vose signed the sheet each spring with his thumb on the bottom figure; "he read the totals and he never read the lines above them" (Kyle, who copied the line for twenty-five years and testifies it never crossed his mind to wonder: "a figure with a name on it has been explained"). At the receiving end Sill reweighed the Hollow's cream himself every day from 1893 and told the room a hundred times that it ran heavy — always more fat in the cans than the papers said was bought — "and nobody ever asked me to" make anything of it. Rundle, shown the ledgers in 1939, saw it at once: "Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year" — and printed the retraction of his own theft chapter while the pamphlet was in press, nineteen years before the bulletin. The figures were read aloud yearly to fifty farmers with their hats on their knees. "It was not a secret. It was worse than a secret. It was in plain sight with a name on it." **Documents:** the annual ledgers (Nym Item 6); Teague's memoir (r05); Sill's remarks (r14); Kyle's account (r23); Rundle's in-press note (r12).

### Story 7 — The Arbitration of 1925

**Who:** the Ostrey Hollow patrons; the board of three under attorney Alonzo Frick; the Association's officers; Ansel Keddie. **What:** In January 1925 the patrons — Emmet Brant's name on the paper — petitioned to be made whole. Their own reckoning was 67,000 lb of fat (Frick); Rundle gives the petition's dollar figure as $19,600; the true shortfall, computed in 1958, was 76,000 lb / $19,000 — the three figures never reconcile. The officers did not resist arbitration. The board of three sat under Frick ("the one of the three who kept no cow") from 17 March to 4 May 1925: the first session at the Ashlin office, the second and third — at Frick's insistence — in the station's own weigh room, which Oram (standing outside in the rain), Orra's diary, Kyle, and Duncan all corroborate against the Ashlin-only accounts. Before it were four exhibits, listed and carried by Kyle: Keddie's test book, the station's receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book, and Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook — the last containing the condemnation entry. The board watched the scales tried and a test made, but on the new glass of 1923, incapable of exhibiting the old fault; and the condemned crate, twenty feet overhead in the loft, was never mentioned in any session (Effie, who took every word down). Three explanations were argued — Vose's water, Teague's theft-by-insinuation, the 1908 hay report read into the record — "and not one of them was proved." Keddie's nine sentences: he had tested as taught, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, and could not tell why the figures were what they were; Frick believed him then and ever after. On 30 April, while the board still sat, the Association dismissed him — "there was no finding against him from us and there never was one." On 4 May the award, verbatim: "finds no cause established... awards... thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account" — one-fifth of the loss, split nine ways. "An award without a finding is an unsatisfactory thing and every member of that board knew it." **Documents:** the minute book (Nym Item 7); Frick's recollections (r13); Effie §6 (r02); Kyle (r23); Oram (r21); Orra's diary (r24); Vose's address (r08).

### Story 8 — The Ladder and the Burette

**Who:** Ivy Keddie (b. 1931), Ansel's granddaughter; Professor Leland Vaught; Rosalie Cudd. **What:** In February 1958 Ivy — Duncan's daughter, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland — climbed the ladder into the station loft and found the crate, stenciled AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET, under a tarpaulin where it had sat since 1923. She brought five records into one room for the first time: the test book, the Brant weigh book (borrowed from Roy — lent, not left), the Alder Corners figures (Rosalie's letter with its sixteen summer seasons, 1915–1930, of the four Hessel Bottom men, every season between 3.78 and 3.82), Strawn's circuit notebooks (in Farland's library since 1947, unasked-for), and the Association's ledgers. On 9 March 1958 she measured the nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette: mean delivery 16.72 c.c. against stems blown 17.6 — 95 hundredths of the mark. The arithmetic closed every gap at once: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80, matching 1896, the nine weeks of 1911, 1924, Alder Corners, and the ledger's five per cent. Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958, Vaught with Ivy) concluded: no milk watered, no cream stolen; the tests low one part in twenty from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 because the pipettes were short one part in twenty; the 76,000 lb standing credited year by year as station gain. The news traveled with fresh errors — the Chronicle's "great-niece," "eleven pipettes," "16.27 c.c.," "$19,600," and an accusation against Ammon & Sons the record does not support — while county agent Falke's sober 1959 report corrected itself twice in print. Ivy's 1959 talk to the Historical Society put the crate on the table: "The glass was short and Ansel Keddie was not... It would have been a kindness if somebody in this county had thought to climb a ladder." Keddie had been dead twenty-four years, dismissed thirty-three; Orra died in 1948 without seeing it. By 1972 the records stood catalogued at the Ordell County Historical Rooms. **Documents:** Ivy's talk and measurement sheet (r04; Nym Item 14); Bulletin 471 (r11; Item 15); Rosalie's letter (r10); the Chronicle (r19); Falke's report (r22); Duncan's notes (r18); Nym's catalogue (r07).

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie and Ansel Keddie: granddaughter.** The chain: Ansel and Orra's son was Duncan (Ansel's letter names "my wife Orra"; Duncan's notes open "My father was Ansel Keddie"; Orra's diary tracks Duncan from age five in 1899), and Ivy is Duncan's daughter (Duncan: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931"; Roy's 1958 postscript: "Duncan's girl Ivy"). Ivy herself: "Ansel Keddie was my grandfather." The contrary claim — "great-niece" — appears in the Chronicle and in Falke's report, but Falke verified and corrected it in print, and the Chronicle was assembled in a day and a half from a press release; the newspaper's own "Miss Keddie is twenty-seven" in October 1958 actually corroborates the 1931 birth. First-person statement plus consistent family record settles it.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant: second cousins.** Derivation: Emmet Brant and Orra were brother and sister; their sons Roy and Duncan were first cousins ("the children of a brother and a sister, which is the whole definition"); Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy, being children of first cousins, are second cousins. Roy, Hazel, and Duncan each derive it identically, and Rundle's 1959 footnote agrees. Vira and Oram both say "first cousin once removed" — valley shorthand, outranked by the family's own counting. What the sources leave open: whether Emmet and Orra shared both parents. Roy is emphatic they were full siblings, "same mother and same father," written expressly against a stepmother story; Hazel (1981) says half sister "by his father's second wife"; Duncan says plainly "brother and sister." The cousin degree is unaffected either way. On balance the two witnesses a generation closer (Emmet's son and Orra's son) outweigh Hazel, but the half-or-full question cannot be closed absolutely. Also open-then-resolvable: Orra's birth year — 1866 by her diary's preface, her own "I am sixty-four" in August 1930, and Duncan's "eighty-two when she died in 1948," against Duncan's stated "eighteen sixty-eight"; arithmetic favors 1866.

**B3. Selby Vose's father: Gideon Vose.** Selby's own printed 1926 address says "my father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law"; Effie ("son of the late president"), Duncan, and Kyle ("the nephew story... is nonsense") concur. Vira and Rundle say nephew — memory against the man's own printed word, and the first person wins. Why it matters: Gideon wrote Article VII, the no-appeal rule that made the station's test the whole bargain, and his son then managed the Association for twenty-five years — signed every annual sheet carrying the station gain, wrote the 1919 water circular, argued the water theory before the board, and dismissed the 1923 condemnation as housekeeping. The family that wrote the rule administered the rule's consequences, and to the Hollow the accusing letter read as the establishment closing ranks (Vira: "somebody's cousin saying it").

**B4. Vose family and Keddie family: no relationship established — probably none.** The claim that Orra Keddie and old Mrs. (Gideon) Vose were first cousins comes from Vira ("Everybody knew that" — "and nobody wrote it down") and Oram ("cousins of some sort... I could not draw you the line on paper"), both explicitly hearsay out of the same valley rumor pool — effectively one source, not two. Against it stands Duncan, Orra's own son, whose mother "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection" and never once put a Vose in it: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses." He also identifies the likely source of the confusion — the unrelated Ashlin Voses. A negative cannot be proven outright, but the closest possible witness against an unwritten rumor decides the balance: the sources establish only that the rumor existed, and the best-supported answer is that there was no kinship.
