## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1 — The Rule That Made a Figure the Whole Bargain

**Who:** the founding farmers of the Larrow Valley; Gideon Vose. **What:** In 1889 the valley's farmers, tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking what the merchant allowed, met in the schoolhouse at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose — "once thought of the law and thought better of it" — was elected first president and held the office twenty-two years, to 1911; he died in 1917. In 1891 the Association built its creamery at Larrow Green on Larrow Creek, and the valley began to be paid in cash monthly, which had never happened to it before. Vose drafted the by-laws himself at his kitchen table. Article VII, quoted identically by three sources: every patron paid on the pounds of butterfat found by test, not on the weight of his milk — "and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." The adoption year cannot be fixed: the printed pamphlet carries no date on its face (Nym, Item 1), Effie Loomis says 1892, Rundle 1891, Grigg "the 1893 meeting" — and the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move, so the county agent Falke rightly declined to date it; so do I. Men left the Association over the rule. It was a just rule — "the pound of milk is a liar where the pound of butterfat is not" — but its last clause allowed no appeal from a station's own test. The test became the whole of the bargain, leaving a dissatisfied man "no remedy on earth except to quarrel with a figure" (Frick), written by a man who died before he could be asked about it. Under payment by weight, nothing that followed at Ostrey Hollow would have cost anyone a dollar (Falke). **Documents:** the by-laws pamphlet (Nym Item 1); Effie's 1941 notes; Rundle's 1939 jubilee pamphlet; Grigg's 1919 covering letter; Falke's 1959 report; Frick's 1946 recollections.

### Story 2 — Ansel Keddie's Twenty-Nine Seasons

**Who:** Ansel Keddie (1861–1934), tester and separator man; his wife Orra; their son Duncan. **What:** Keddie went up in 1895 to ready the new skimming station, opened it 4 May 1896, and made every test through 1924 — twenty-nine seasons, thirty-one tests a week, alone in the building. He married Orra Brant in 1889 (born 1866: her diary's preface, her own "I am sixty-four" in August 1930, and Duncan's "eighty-two when she died in 1948" outvote Duncan's stated "1868"). Their son Duncan (b. 1894) taught school at Ashlin from 1911. Keddie's method was the point of him: every can weighed and the weight called before the milk moved; composite samples warmed blood-warm and mixed; the charge drawn to the mark; the fat column read with dividers; a book with every error lined through and initialed, never a figure written over a figure. The state inspector: "works slow and clean and reads his own figure without being coaxed to it." His averages ran 3.80 in 1896 (on borrowed true glass) and 3.61 within 0.03 from 1898 on — and he could not say why: "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are." In the summer of 1911 he came home saying, twice, that it was something in the glass (Duncan), but would not write what he could not prove — only a question mark he let stand fourteen years. He bore the 1902 talk and the 1908 committee; in 1919 he hand-carried the manager's water circular to every family himself — "I would not have another man carry it" — and it cost him his brother-in-law. Before the 1925 board he made a nine-sentence statement; privately, in the Ashlin corridor, he told Frick he had always thought the patrons at fault. Dismissed 30 April 1925, four days before the award, he came up the road at his ordinary hour, hung the apron behind the shed door, and ate his supper. He lived nine years as a carpenter — better at it than the other trade, at a quarter of the pay — died February 1934, and was cleared twenty-four years later. **Documents:** his statement and unsent letter (r03); Orra's diary (r24); Duncan's notes (r18); Frick (r13); Strawn's extracts (r06); Vira's tape (r01).

### Story 3 — The Brants of Hessel Bottom and the Weigh Book

**Who:** Emmet Brant (1854–1929), his son Roy, Roy's daughter Hazel. **What:** Emmet farmed the largest of the SIX farms on Hessel Bottom (the 1908 report's own count, with Roy, Kepp, and Hazel; Effie's "seven" is the odd figure out) — sixty-odd of its 160 wet acres, thirty-eight cows — and hauled more milk to the station than any man. From 1 January 1897 (Frick's exhibit list and Nym's provenance are documentary; Roy's "1898" and Hazel's "1900" are memory) he wrote every delivery in a ledger: his own barn scale's weight beside the figure the station gave back. The two columns agreed for thirty years — proof, kept unknowingly, that whatever was wrong at that station, it was not the weighing. The 1910 leaf: 200,000 lb even, "even" underlined, paid on a test of 3.42. Emmet was among the first to say aloud, in 1902, that the tests ran low, and was laughed at; he read the 1908 hay report twice and put it in the drawer ("the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not"); he entered the 1911 rise and fall and could make nothing of it. The 1919 circular ended relations with his brother-in-law Ansel — not over the water itself, Roy insists, but because the keeper of the test book had carried the letter and not stood up against it; Orra's diary records Emmet in the yard asking Ansel why he had carried it. Ten years of silence, ended only by Emmet's death in 1929. Roy (b. ~1888) kept the book on identically, refused the Alder Corners haul ("the biggest herd cannot afford to be talked about" — "a poor reason and cost us money"), lent — lent, not left — the book to Ivy in 1958, and died in 1962; Hazel gave it to the Historical Rooms in 1963. Roy insists Emmet and Orra were full siblings; Hazel's "half sister by his father's second wife" preserves exactly the stepmother rumor Roy wrote to kill. **Documents:** the weigh book (Nym Item 4; Frick's Exhibit 3); Roy's 1955 account (r09); Hazel's 1981 tape (r17); Duncan's notes (r18); Orra's diary (r24).

### Story 4 — The Short Glass

**Who:** Ammon & Sons of Tarnet; the station's testing outfit; inspector Merle Strawn. **What:** On 14 April 1897 Ammon & Sons invoiced the Association $77.93 for the Ostrey Hollow outfit: six dozen milk-test pipettes blown 17.6 c.c. at 35 cents, twelve dozen 8-per-cent test bottles, an acid measure, a four-bottle hand tester, freight. (The invoice survives in two identical transcriptions; the itemized sum proves $77.93 against the "$77.39" carried by both Junia Ammon's prose and the Chronicle.) The lot was blown short — mean delivery 16.72 c.c., 95 hundredths of the mark. Junia Ammon's explanation: a reground mold, uncatchable because the lot was consistent — "consistently wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind cannot find"; the works records burned in 1931. The glass went into use 1 May 1897 (Orra's diary that very day: the own glass came up, "done borrowing of Larrow Green"), replacing the true outfit buttermaker Hobart Sill had made up from his own Larrow Green bench for the 1896 opening — which is why 1896 alone reads 3.80. The pipette is the one instrument a plant cannot check against anything else: it IS the standard; scales, tester speed, acid, and bottle necks are all checkable, so the error was proportional, precise, and invisible from inside (Vaught). It flashed into view once: on 8 May 1911 the measure broke; a borrowed one served nine weeks; every farm's tests rose to 3.80 and fell back on 10 July, the day the own glass returned; Keddie's question mark stood unread forty-seven years. Burettes reached the state's field men only in 1921; Strawn skipped the check in 1922, ran it on 12 June 1923, and condemned the whole crate on the spot — "Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them." Replacement ordered 28 June; Ammon's reply 2 July; new glass in use in July, verified true in October; 1924 tested 3.80. The condemned crate went up the loft ladder and sat under a tarpaulin thirty-five years, its six dozen whittled to nine survivors by men climbing up after wire (Oram). **Documents:** the invoice (Nym Item 2); Junia Ammon's chapter (r20); Strawn's 1923 entry (r06; Item 9); the Ammon letter (Item 10); Keddie's 1911 entries (r03); Oram's tape (r21).

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### Story 5 — The Three Wrong Answers

**Who:** the valley's theorists — Nyle Grigg, Dr. Roland Kepp, Selby Vose, Wilbur Teague — against the Hollow's patrons. **What:** From 1902 the grievance grew — "it was not one man and it was not one season" — and the valley answered it three ways, all wrong, all aimed at men or land. HAY: a committee of three under Grigg (a Larrow Green hauler, chosen for disinterest) sampled the six Hessel Bottom herds January–March 1908, found "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter," and blamed the wet bog hay. The College's own controlled trial, Bulletin 214 (1911), found bog hay depressed the test by at most five hundredths of a point in winter and nothing measurable in summer — a refutation. Grigg's 1919 reprint nevertheless inflated the sampling to "the whole of 1908" against his own printed report and misquoted the bulletin as "two tenths"; Kepp's 1926 paper repeated the identical misquote (evidently from Grigg's reprint), claimed a 0.21 gap, and waved off the 1911 interval as "six weeks of summer figures." Defeated by the gap's flatness (0.19 in July as in February — a feed effect is seasonal), by Bulletin 214's real figures, and by the same cows testing ~3.80 at Alder Corners. WATER: manager Vose's theory. His 1919 circular warned nine families about added water (typed by clerk Kyle "in a civil form of words but with no doubt at all in it about what was meant"); he stood by it before the 1925 board "without heat and without proof," and in 1926 credited the 1924 rise to the Association's scrutiny. Defeated by inspector Strawn's lactometer, which found no added water on any visit 1907–1934 ("as always here" — and he told Vose so in 1919); by cheesemaker Jerome Cudd, to whom watered milk shows in the curd "the next morning, not next year," and who never once docked the four Hollow men in sixteen summers; and by the rise arriving with the new glass of July 1923, not with the January 1925 petition. THEFT: the valley's settled folklore that cream left the station by the back door, insinuated at the hearing by bookkeeper Teague ("cream is portable... the remedy is two men or a lock"). Defeated by the Association's own ledgers — the station's cream reached Larrow Green HEAVY every year; stolen cream arrives light. Rundle printed that refutation in 1939; Vira was still telling the theft story in 1979. **Documents:** the 1908 report with Grigg's letter (r15); Kepp's paper (r16); Vose's address (r08); Strawn's extracts (r06); Rosalie Cudd's letter (r10); Bulletin 214 (Nym Item 12).

### Story 6 — The Ledger in Plain Sight

**Who:** Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945; Hobart Sill, buttermaker 1893–1934; Bertram Kyle, clerk; Dell Rundle. **What:** Every year from 1898 the station account carried one entry in one form — 1898's, verbatim: fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb; fat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream, 60,800 lb; "Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account." Teague claims he devised the line when he came in 1905, yet quotes the 1898 entry in that exact form — the line, or his memory, is older than he says. Over the twenty-five full years the gain accumulated to 76,000 lb of butterfat, worth $19,000: the exact measure of what the glass withheld, entered annually in ink. Five and a quarter per cent of the fat CREDITED is five per cent of the fat DELIVERED — and five per cent is the glass. Nobody read it. Teague put the anomaly to Vose once, in 1916, was told to let it alone, and did — "the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience." Vose signed the sheet each spring with his thumb on the bottom figure; "he read the totals and he never read the lines above them" (Kyle, who copied the line for twenty-five years and testifies it never crossed his mind to wonder: "a figure with a name on it has been explained"). At the receiving end Sill reweighed the Hollow's cream himself every day from 1893 and told the room a hundred times that it ran heavy — always more fat in the cans than the papers said was bought — "and nobody ever asked me to" make anything of it. Rundle, shown the ledgers in 1939, saw it at once: "Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year" — and printed the retraction of his own theft chapter while the pamphlet was in press, nineteen years before the bulletin. The figures were read aloud yearly to fifty farmers with their hats on their knees. "It was not a secret. It was worse than a secret. It was in plain sight with a name on it." **Documents:** the annual ledgers (Nym Item 6); Teague's memoir (r05); Sill's remarks (r14); Kyle's account (r23); Rundle's in-press note (r12).

### Story 7 — The Arbitration of 1925

**Who:** the Ostrey Hollow patrons; the board of three under attorney Alonzo Frick; the Association's officers; Ansel Keddie. **What:** In January 1925 the patrons — Emmet Brant's name on the paper — petitioned to be made whole. Their own reckoning was 67,000 lb of fat (Frick); Rundle gives the petition's dollar figure as $19,600; the true shortfall, computed in 1958, was 76,000 lb / $19,000 — the three figures never reconcile. The officers did not resist arbitration. The board of three sat under Frick ("the one of the three who kept no cow") from 17 March to 4 May 1925: the first session at the Ashlin office, the second and third — at Frick's insistence — in the station's own weigh room, which Oram (standing outside in the rain), Orra's diary, Kyle, and Duncan all corroborate against the Ashlin-only accounts. Before it were four exhibits, listed and carried by Kyle: Keddie's test book, the station's receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book, and Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook — the last containing the condemnation entry. The board watched the scales tried and a test made, but on the new glass of 1923, incapable of exhibiting the old fault; and the condemned crate, twenty feet overhead in the loft, was never mentioned in any session (Effie, who took every word down). Three explanations were argued — Vose's water, Teague's theft-by-insinuation, the 1908 hay report read into the record — "and not one of them was proved." Keddie's nine sentences: he had tested as taught, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, and could not tell why the figures were what they were; Frick believed him then and ever after. On 30 April, while the board still sat, the Association dismissed him — "there was no finding against him from us and there never was one." On 4 May the award, verbatim: "finds no cause established... awards... thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account" — one-fifth of the loss, split nine ways. "An award without a finding is an unsatisfactory thing and every member of that board knew it." **Documents:** the minute book (Nym Item 7); Frick's recollections (r13); Effie §6 (r02); Kyle (r23); Oram (r21); Orra's diary (r24); Vose's address (r08).

### Story 8 — The Ladder and the Burette

**Who:** Ivy Keddie (b. 1931), Ansel's granddaughter; Professor Leland Vaught; Rosalie Cudd. **What:** In February 1958 Ivy — Duncan's daughter, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland — climbed the ladder into the station loft and found the crate, stenciled AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET, under a tarpaulin where it had sat since 1923. She brought five records into one room for the first time: the test book, the Brant weigh book (borrowed from Roy — lent, not left), the Alder Corners figures (Rosalie's letter with its sixteen summer seasons, 1915–1930, of the four Hessel Bottom men, every season between 3.78 and 3.82), Strawn's circuit notebooks (in Farland's library since 1947, unasked-for), and the Association's ledgers. On 9 March 1958 she measured the nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette: mean delivery 16.72 c.c. against stems blown 17.6 — 95 hundredths of the mark. The arithmetic closed every gap at once: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80, matching 1896, the nine weeks of 1911, 1924, Alder Corners, and the ledger's five per cent. Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958, Vaught with Ivy) concluded: no milk watered, no cream stolen; the tests low one part in twenty from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 because the pipettes were short one part in twenty; the 76,000 lb standing credited year by year as station gain. The news traveled with fresh errors — the Chronicle's "great-niece," "eleven pipettes," "16.27 c.c.," "$19,600," and an accusation against Ammon & Sons the record does not support — while county agent Falke's sober 1959 report corrected itself twice in print. Ivy's 1959 talk to the Historical Society put the crate on the table: "The glass was short and Ansel Keddie was not... It would have been a kindness if somebody in this county had thought to climb a ladder." Keddie had been dead twenty-four years, dismissed thirty-three; Orra died in 1948 without seeing it. By 1972 the records stood catalogued at the Ordell County Historical Rooms. **Documents:** Ivy's talk and measurement sheet (r04; Nym Item 14); Bulletin 471 (r11; Item 15); Rosalie's letter (r10); the Chronicle (r19); Falke's report (r22); Duncan's notes (r18); Nym's catalogue (r07).

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie and Ansel Keddie: granddaughter.** The chain: Ansel and Orra's son was Duncan (Ansel's letter names "my wife Orra"; Duncan's notes open "My father was Ansel Keddie"; Orra's diary tracks Duncan from age five in 1899), and Ivy is Duncan's daughter (Duncan: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931"; Roy's 1958 postscript: "Duncan's girl Ivy"). Ivy herself: "Ansel Keddie was my grandfather." The contrary claim — "great-niece" — appears in the Chronicle and in Falke's report, but Falke verified and corrected it in print, and the Chronicle was assembled in a day and a half from a press release; the newspaper's own "Miss Keddie is twenty-seven" in October 1958 actually corroborates the 1931 birth. First-person statement plus consistent family record settles it.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant: second cousins.** Derivation: Emmet Brant and Orra were brother and sister; their sons Roy and Duncan were first cousins ("the children of a brother and a sister, which is the whole definition"); Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy, being children of first cousins, are second cousins. Roy, Hazel, and Duncan each derive it identically, and Rundle's 1959 footnote agrees. Vira and Oram both say "first cousin once removed" — valley shorthand, outranked by the family's own counting. What the sources leave open: whether Emmet and Orra shared both parents. Roy is emphatic they were full siblings, "same mother and same father," written expressly against a stepmother story; Hazel (1981) says half sister "by his father's second wife"; Duncan says plainly "brother and sister." The cousin degree is unaffected either way. On balance the two witnesses a generation closer (Emmet's son and Orra's son) outweigh Hazel, but the half-or-full question cannot be closed absolutely. Also open-then-resolvable: Orra's birth year — 1866 by her diary's preface, her own "I am sixty-four" in August 1930, and Duncan's "eighty-two when she died in 1948," against Duncan's stated "eighteen sixty-eight"; arithmetic favors 1866.

**B3. Selby Vose's father: Gideon Vose.** Selby's own printed 1926 address says "my father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law"; Effie ("son of the late president"), Duncan, and Kyle ("the nephew story... is nonsense") concur. Vira and Rundle say nephew — memory against the man's own printed word, and the first person wins. Why it matters: Gideon wrote Article VII, the no-appeal rule that made the station's test the whole bargain, and his son then managed the Association for twenty-five years — signed every annual sheet carrying the station gain, wrote the 1919 water circular, argued the water theory before the board, and dismissed the 1923 condemnation as housekeeping. The family that wrote the rule administered the rule's consequences, and to the Hollow the accusing letter read as the establishment closing ranks (Vira: "somebody's cousin saying it").

**B4. Vose family and Keddie family: no relationship established — probably none.** The claim that Orra Keddie and old Mrs. (Gideon) Vose were first cousins comes from Vira ("Everybody knew that" — "and nobody wrote it down") and Oram ("cousins of some sort... I could not draw you the line on paper"), both explicitly hearsay out of the same valley rumor pool — effectively one source, not two. Against it stands Duncan, Orra's own son, whose mother "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection" and never once put a Vose in it: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses." He also identifies the likely source of the confusion — the unrelated Ashlin Voses. A negative cannot be proven outright, but the closest possible witness against an unwritten rumor decides the balance: the sources establish only that the rumor existed, and the best-supported answer is that there was no kinship.

## Section C — Math

**C1(a). One part in twenty low — tests read 95% of the truth.** The nine surviving pipettes, blown 17.6 c.c. on the stem, delivered (mean of five runs each) 16.70–16.74, mean **16.72 c.c.** (Ivy's 9 March 1958 measurement sheet, quoted verbatim in r04 and Nym Item 14). 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95**. The test bottle's neck is graduated for the full 17.6 charge and the reading is proportional to the charge, so every test read 95% of the true figure — 5% low. The "16.27" in Vaught's introduction and the Chronicle is a digit transposition: it contradicts the itemized sheet (whose nine listed values average 16.72) and the bulletin's own quoted conclusion, "one part in twenty."

**C1(b). True average 3.80; recorded tests short 0.19 of a point.** Recorded average 3.61 (Keddie's 1922 averages leaf, held within 0.03 from 1898). True: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**; short **0.19**. Independent corroborations of 3.80: 1896 on the borrowed true glass; the nine weeks of 1911; 1924 on the new glass (the book, and Strawn's "their book gives the year at 3.80"); Alder Corners' sixteen summers at 3.78–3.82.

**C1(c). 76,000 lb.** Twenty-five full delivery years, 1898–1922: 40,000,000 lb of milk (Teague's annual sheets; Effie added the columns twice; Kyle got the figure out for the arbitration). Credited: 40,000,000 × 3.61% = **1,444,000 lb**. Actually delivered: 40,000,000 × 3.80% = **1,520,000 lb**. Not credited: **76,000 lb** — the same total the ledger accumulated year by year as station gain. (The patrons' 67,000 was their own unaudited reckoning; 118,000 follows from the 16.27 error.)

**C1(d). $19,000; the patrons received one-fifth.** 76,000 lb × $0.25 (the period-average price per Effie, Ivy, and Kyle, who was set to find it; the 27¢ of Sill and Kepp is recollection against the ledger) = **$19,000**. The award was $3,800 = **20%, exactly one-fifth**.

**C2. Emmet Brant, 1910** (weigh-book leaf: 200,000 lb even, paid on 3.42):
(a) Credited: 200,000 × 3.42% = **6,840 lb**.
(b) True test 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60; true fat 200,000 × 3.60% = 7,200 lb; owed and not credited: **360 lb** (matches Ivy's "a herd recorded at 3.42 was short 0.18": 0.18 × 200,000/100 = 360).
(c) Butterfat was 24 cents in 1910 (Roy gives the year-specific price): 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40**. (At the 25¢ period average it would be $90; the year's own price is the better figure.)

**C3.** (a) Credited to the station's patrons over 1898–1922: **1,444,000 lb** (25 × the typical annual 57,760). (b) Received at Larrow Green in the station's cream: **1,520,000 lb** (25 × 60,800; also 1,444,000 + 76,000). (c) The difference, 76,000 lb, is **5.26% ("five and a quarter per cent") of the fat CREDITED** (76,000 ÷ 1,444,000) and **5.00% of the fat DELIVERED/received** (76,000 ÷ 1,520,000). The ledger's "five and a quarter" is a percentage of the credited figure — Teague ("of the fat that was actually in the cream") and Vaught's introduction ("of the butterfat actually delivered") both misstate the denominator; Ivy and Nym state it correctly, and the five per cent is the number that is also the glass.

**C4.** (a) Invoice total **$77.93** — proven by its own itemization: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93 (transcribed identically by Nym Item 2 and Junia Ammon's facsimile). The "$77.39" in Junia's prose and the Chronicle fails the addition. (b) **Six dozen = 72 pipettes** (72 × $0.35 = $25.20, confirming the line; "five dozen" — Vaught's introduction, Oram — fails the same arithmetic). Each short by 17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88 c.c.** — strictly the mean of the nine measurable survivors, individually 0.86–0.90 short.

**C5(a).** The faulty glass was in use from **1 May 1897** (Orra's contemporaneous diary entry of that day; the office file per Nym Item 2; Keddie's "1897, part year"; the bulletin's conclusion — against the "1 May 1898" of Vaught's introduction and Kyle) **until condemned 12 June 1923: twenty-six years and about six weeks.** The money calculation runs over **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922**. The numbers differ because the endpoints are part-years — 1897 was on the short glass only from May, 1923 only until June (new glass from July) — and the ledger's full-year figures exist only for 1898–1922, so the computation uses complete years only.

**C5(b). Cannot be determined.** No figure for the 1896 part-season (4 May–December) exists in the ledgers or anywhere in the record — the station column begins with 1897 (Nym's queries). Two conflicting recollections exist: Sill's "about nine hundred thousand pounds" and Kyle's hearsay "a good eleven hundred thousand." Both are memory, they differ by 200,000 lb, and neither has documentary support. The sources establish only a range of recollection (~0.9–1.1 million lb), which is not a figure.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 interval as experiment.** When: **8 May 1911** (Keddie's dated entry; Orra's same-day diary, "A. home cross about a broken measure") **to 10 July 1911 — nine weeks** (the dated book entries beat the "six weeks" remembered by Teague and Kepp). Variable: the single measuring pipette — a borrowed measure of true capacity in place of the short one (its source is unsettled: Larrow Green per Ivy, the state man per Roy). Held constant: patrons, herds, ground, feed, acid, bottles, tester, scales, method, supervision — everything but the one instrument. Result: every farm's tests rose at once to 3.80, held for the nine weeks, and returned to 3.61 the day the station's own measure came back; Keddie set a question mark against it. What it establishes that calculation cannot: **causation by intervention**. Dividing 3.61 by 0.95 in 1958 shows only that the glass hypothesis is consistent with the record; the interval shows the instrument actually moving the figures — onset and reversal pinned to the swap — while men, cows, land, and water all stood still, which rules out every explanation that requires any of them to have changed. What it does NOT establish: the mechanism or magnitude of the defect (that the pipettes delivered 16.72 against 17.6 — only the 1958 burette measurement showed that); that the borrowed measure was itself accurate (the match to the independently corroborated 3.80 supports it, but nothing in 1911 could verify it); that the same defect operated across the whole 1897–1923 span (completed only by the measurement and the flat annual station gain); and nothing about fault for the short lot.

**D2. Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream.**
1. **The receiving end:** the station's cream arrived at Larrow Green HEAVY every year — more fat weighed in than had been credited — recorded annually in the station-gain ledger line and physically re-verified daily by Sill at his own scale. Stolen cream arrives light. Rundle drew exactly this inference from the ledgers, in print, in 1939.
2. **The 1911 interval:** the same man, alone, unsupervised, with identical opportunity, ran a borrowed measure for nine weeks and the figures ROSE, then fell when his own glass returned. A thief writing low figures to mask skimming is unaffected by which pipette he holds.
3. **The timing of the correction:** the tests rose in 1924 — while Keddie was still at the bench, a full year before his dismissal — immediately after the July 1923 glass replacement (his book; Strawn's "tests improved... 3.80"). Had theft been the cause, the figures would have righted only after the thief left on 30 April 1925.
They are independent because they live in different documents kept by different hands at different ends of the process: the creamery's ledgers and receiving books; the station test book's internal record; the calendar across Strawn's notebooks and the board record. **All three hold without the 1958 measurement** — name one: the heavy-cream ledger, which had been refuting the accusation annually since 1898. **Did the bookkeeper ever question the entries that refuted his own accusation? No.** Teague wrote the line for forty years and called a figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year "furniture"; he raised the gain with Vose once, in 1916, was told to let it alone, and did ("the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience"); at the 1925 hearing he insinuated theft without ever setting the insinuation beside his own entries — which showed the Association receiving more fat than it paid for. Kyle: "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years."

**D3. Did the manager know the tests ran low?** Split the question. That the station's tests RAN LOW — yes, beyond doubt: the gap against Larrow Green was the valley's standing complaint from 1902; he signed the annual sheet carrying the station-gain line every year ("Mr. Vose knew what the account was doing" — Teague); Teague put the anomaly to him directly in 1916 and was told to let it alone; his own 1919 circular and 1926 address are premised on twenty years of low figures. That the tests were FALSE — that the milk contained more fat than the station read — no: he never drew that inference. He attributed the gap to added water before the board and still in 1926, after Strawn had told him in 1919 that no water had ever been found; Kyle testifies he read totals, not lines — "put his thumb on the bottom figure and sign" — and the one line proving the fat was arriving unpaid-for was the line he signed. So: knowledge of the discrepancy from at latest 1916; no knowledge that the readings themselves were wrong — he held the opposite theory to the end. The record supports willful incuriosity, not concealment.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. The theory.** In April 1897 Ammon & Sons of Tarnet shipped the Ostrey Hollow station six dozen milk-test pipettes from a lot blown short — most plausibly off a reground mold (Junia Ammon), a fault no factory check could catch because the lot was consistent. Each delivered about 16.72 c.c. against a 17.6 c.c. mark: 95% of the charge. The butterfat test reads the fat column in a bottle neck graduated for the full 17.6 charge, so every reading was 95% of the truth. That fixes the **size**: a deficit of exactly one part in twenty — 0.19 of a point on the Hollow's true 3.80 — proportional, not additive (rich milk lost more absolutely: 4.56 read ~4.33, 3.60 read 3.42), perfectly steady in every month and year, and internally consistent, so the station's herds ranked correctly against each other and the books looked like excellent work. It **began 1 May 1897** because that is the day the short outfit replaced the accurate glass Sill had lent for the 1896 season (hence 1896's true 3.80). It **ended in July 1923** because the state had issued burettes to its field men in 1921, and on 12 June 1923 Strawn finally ran the pipettes against his and condemned the lot; true replacement glass went into use within the month (1924: 3.80). **Nothing at the station or on the farms had to change at either end** because neither men nor cows nor land was ever the cause: the pipette is the one instrument that is its own standard, checkable only from outside — and the same 5% surfaced annually in the ledger as "station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual."

**The three competing explanations, and what defeats each.**
1. **Added water** — Selby Vose (the 1919 circular; before the 1925 board; the 1926 address); privately shared by Sill and even by Keddie. Defeated by: Strawn's lactometer, negative at every visit 1907–1934 ("as always here"), told to Vose in 1919; Jerome Cudd's cheese vats, where watered milk shows "the next morning, not next year," clean for sixteen summers of the same men's milk; the 1911 and 1923 corrections arriving with glass changes while the patrons did nothing different; and no watering scheme produces an exactly proportional, year-round-constant 5%.
2. **Cream theft by the tester** — the valley's folklore (Vira, still in 1979), insinuated at the hearing by Teague. Defeated by: the station's cream reaching Larrow Green HEAVY every year (Sill's daily reweighing; the station-gain line; Rundle's 1939 printed retraction — stolen cream arrives light); the 1911 rise under the same unsupervised man; and the 1924 correction with Keddie still at the bench, a year before his removal.
3. **The wet hay of Hessel Bottom** — the 1908 Grigg committee, endorsed by Kepp in 1926. Defeated by: Bulletin 214's controlled trial (at most 0.05 in winter, nothing in summer — a quarter the size and the wrong shape; the "two tenths" cited by Grigg and Kepp is a misquote); the gap's flatness across seasons (0.19 in July as in February — feed effects are seasonal); the same cows testing ~3.80 at Alder Corners in the summer flush; and 1911/1923 — the land did not change when the figures did.

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware? No.** It inspected the plant — Frick moved sessions two and three into the station weigh room and the board watched the scales tried and a test made, but on the new glass of 1923, which could exhibit nothing. Strawn's 1923 notebook, containing the condemnation entry, was physically before the board as Exhibit 4 — yet by Effie, who took down every word of all three sessions, the condemned crate was never once mentioned, and it sat in the loft directly over the board's heads (Duncan: "two of the three sessions of that arbitration sat in the room underneath it"). The evidence was in the room; the inference was never drawn.

**What did the tester himself believe?** Publicly, nothing — "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are"; he accused no one. Privately, two attested layers: in the summer of 1911 he came home saying twice that "it was something in the glass" (Duncan), but would not write what he could not prove — only the question mark he let stand; and by 1925 he told Frick in the Ashlin corridor that "he had always thought the patrons were at fault," i.e., the water theory. Both are well-sourced: he glimpsed the true cause once, could not hold it against the trade's whole practice, and settled into suspecting his neighbors — as wrong, in the end, as everyone else, and as unwilling to say so aloud.

## Section G — Summary

**G1 (148 words).** From 1897 to 1923 the Ostrey Hollow skimming station of a Larrow Valley dairy cooperative read every butterfat test one part in twenty low: its milk pipettes, one short-blown lot, delivered 16.72 c.c. against a 17.6 mark, and the station's own test governed pay. Six farms lost 76,000 pounds of butterfat — about $19,000. The valley blamed wet hay, watered milk, and a thieving tester; Ansel Keddie was dismissed in 1925, four days before an arbitration board found "no cause established" and paid $3,800. The truth lay in plain sight, scattered: a ledger's annual "station gain," nine weeks of 1911 when a borrowed measure made the figures right, a patron's private weigh book, a cheese factory's book over the ridge. Each record had a different keeper; none was ever laid beside another. In 1958 Keddie's granddaughter measured the surviving glass; Bulletin 471 cleared everyone — twenty-four years after his death.
