# Answers — Part 3

## Section C — Math

### C1

**(a) The proportion.** Ivy Keddie's signed measurement sheet of 9 March 1958: nine pipettes from
the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown **17.6 c.c.**, delivered against a calibrated burette, mean of
five runs each — 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. **Mean 16.72 c.c.**

> 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95 exactly**

Every test made on that glass read **95 per cent of the truth** — low by **one part in twenty**.

Why this follows from the glassware: the test bottle's neck is ruled to read percent for one
charge of milk and no other. Deliver 95 per cent of the charge and 95 per cent of the fat is
liberated, a column 95 per cent as long stands in the neck, and the neck — which knows nothing of
the substitution — is read off in the ordinary way. The error is proportional, not additive.

*(Rejecting 16.27 c.c.: see E1 item 2. It is a transposition, it appears only in Vaught's method
section and in the newspaper that copied his press release, it contradicts Vaught's own concluding
paragraph, and Vaught himself flags that it will not reconcile with the 1924 result.)*

**(b) The true average and the shortfall.**

> Recorded average (test book summary leaf, 1898 onward): **3.61**
> True average = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**
> Shortfall = 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 of a point**

Two independent confirmations that 3.80 is the true figure, neither of which uses the glass: the
1896 season on Larrow Green's borrowed glass averaged 3.80; the nine weeks of 1911 on a borrowed
measure stood at 3.80; and the year after the glass was replaced, 1924, the station's book gave
3.80. A third, from outside the county: the same four Hessel Bottom herds ran 3.78–3.82 (about
3.80) at Alder Corners for sixteen summers.

Note the shape of 0.19: it is not a fixed quantity but **5 per cent of 3.80**. The station did not
lose a set number of points; it lost a twentieth of whatever was there. So the error fell hardest
on the best milk — a herd recorded at 4.56 lost 0.24, a herd recorded at 3.42 lost 0.18 — and no
man could know that his good cows cost him more than his poor ones.

**(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited.** Over the **twenty-five full delivery years 1898–1922**,
the ledgers give the station's intake as **40,000,000 lb** of milk (an average of 1,600,000 lb a
year).

> Credited: 40,000,000 × 3.61% = **1,444,000 lb**
> Delivered: 40,000,000 × 3.80% = **1,520,000 lb**
> **Difference = 76,000 lb of butterfat**

**(d) Money, and what the patrons got.** Butterfat over that period averaged **25 cents a pound**
(Teague's annual sheets, extracted for the arbitration by Kyle and quoted independently by Loomis
and by Ivy).

> 76,000 lb × $0.25 = **$19,000**
> Award actually paid: **$3,800**
> 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **0.20 — exactly one fifth**

The patrons received **20 per cent** of what they were owed, thirty-three years before anyone could
say what they had been paid for.

### C2 — Emmet Brant, 1910

From his own weigh book: *"Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even,
by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42."*

**(a) Credited:**
> 200,000 × 3.42% = **6,840 lb of butterfat**

**(b) Owed and not credited:**
> True test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60
> Owed = 200,000 × 3.60% = 7,200 lb
> Not credited = 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb**
> (check: 6,840 × (1/0.95 − 1) = 6,840 × 0.05263 = 360 ✔)

**(c) Value that year.** Roy Brant, writing beside the leaf itself, gives the price of butterfat in
1910 as **24 cents**:
> 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40**

At the 25-cent period average it would be $90.00. I use 24¢ because the question asks for that
year and the 24¢ figure is attached to that year by the man holding the book; the 25¢ figure is
explicitly a multi-year average, "taking the fat years with the lean."

*(Sill's 27¢ and Kepp's 27¢ are impressions, not extractions from the sheets, and are rejected —
see E1 item 10.)*

### C3 — The two sides of the ledger

**(a) Credited to the station's patrons:** **1,444,000 lb** (40,000,000 × 3.61%).

**(b) Reached the main creamery in the station's cream:** **1,520,000 lb** (40,000,000 × 3.80%) —
this is the figure the ledgers recorded as *"butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream,"*
weighed and tested into the vats there, and re-weighed independently by Sill into his cold room.

**(c) The difference expressed as a percentage — and of what.** The difference is **76,000 lb**,
and it is two different percentages depending on the denominator, both correct, neither
interchangeable:

> 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5.00 % of the fat actually delivered** (and received)
> 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.263 % ≈ 5¼ % of the fat credited to the patrons**

**The ledger's "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" is of the credited figure** — a bookkeeper
naturally reckons a surplus against the figure he has already entered. Five per cent is the one
that is also the glass: 1 part in 20 short delivery gives exactly 5 per cent of the true quantity
missing.

Cross-check on the sample year, 1898: credited 57,760, received 60,800, gain 3,040.
> 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.263 % (credited) — matches the ledger's own wording
> 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.00 % (delivered) — matches the glass
> 57,760 ÷ 0.95 = 60,800 exactly
> and 60,800 ÷ 3.80% = 1,600,000 lb of milk — exactly the stated annual average

Every figure in this history closes on itself once 0.95 is used and 3.61 is the recorded average.

*Two narrators get the denominator wrong.* Teague calls the gain *"five and a quarter percent of
the fat that was actually in the cream"* (that is 5.00 %), and Vaught writes *"the gain is five and
a quarter percent of the butterfat actually delivered."* Nym, the curator, is the only narrator who
states the relation correctly: *"five and a quarter percent of the credited fat is five percent of
the fat delivered, and each is right of its own denominator and of no other."*

*One caveat I should flag:* the ledger's "received" side is fat measured after separation and a
seven-mile haul, and it exactly equals the true delivered fat with no loss shown. Either losses
were negligible and absorbed elsewhere, or the two sides were reconciled in a way the sources do
not describe. Nothing turns on it — the *sign* of the difference is what defeats the theft theory
— but the perfect closure is an assumption, not a demonstration.

### C4 — The invoice

**(a) Total: $77.93.** Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, Tarnet, 14 April 1897, sold to the Larrow Valley
Co-op. Creamery Assn. for the Ostrey Hollow station:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 | 25.20 |
| 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at .22 | 31.68 |
| 1 acid measure | .90 |
| 1 hand tester, 4 bottle | 18.00 |
| Freight | 2.15 |
| **Total** | **77.93** |

The column adds: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93 ✔ (and 72 × .35 = 25.20 ✔; 144 × .22
= 31.68 ✔). Two narrators render it in prose as "seventy-seven dollars and **thirty-nine** cents" —
Junia Ammon, in the same chapter in which she reprints the invoice reading 77.93, and the *Ashlin
Chronicle*. The transposed reading is defeated by the invoice's own arithmetic.

**(b) Pipettes bought and how short each was.**
> **72 pipettes** (six dozen), at 35 cents apiece.
> Each short **0.88 c.c.** (17.6 − 16.72), i.e. 5 per cent of its mark.

Two qualifications. The 0.88 is a **mean** of the nine survivors, whose individual deliveries ran
16.70–16.74, so the per-pipette shortfall ran about 0.86–0.90 c.c.; the tight spread is itself the
point (a consistent lot, consistently wrong). And we are inferring the whole lot of 72 from 9
survivors — a fair inference, since the lot was blown to one reground mold and the survivors agree
to four hundredths, but an inference.

*Rejecting "five dozen" (Vaught's method section, and Oram's memory of the crate): the invoice is
primary and is transcribed independently by the curator and by the supplier's own historian.*

### C5

**(a) How long the glass was in use vs. how many years the money runs over.**

> **In use: 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 — twenty-six years and about six weeks.**
> **Money calculation: twenty-five full delivery years, 1898 through 1922.**

They are different numbers for three reasons. (i) 1897 is a **part year** — the station's own glass
went in on 1 May, and the test book's summary leaf marks 1897 exactly that way: *"1897, part
year."* (ii) 1923 is likewise a part year, ending 12 June, with new glass from July. (iii) 1896 is
excluded altogether: from 4 May 1896 the station ran on glass borrowed from Larrow Green, and its
average was the true 3.80 — no loss at all. So the ledgers' annual columns give twenty-five clean
whole years, 1898–1922, and the two part-years at each end are real losses that simply are not in
the $19,000.

The $19,000 is therefore a floor, not a total. Bulletin 471's own conclusion gives the *span of the
fault* as 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 while the *sum* is computed over 1898–1922, which is not an
inconsistency but is easily mistaken for one.

**(b) The station's first season intake — cannot be determined from the sources.**

The sources give two figures and one flat statement that no record exists:

- Hobart Sill, who lent the outfit the station opened on: *"about nine hundred thousand pounds,"*
  May to the end of 1896.
- Bertram Kyle, the office clerk: *"a good eleven hundred thousand pounds, which is what I
  understood at the time from men who were there. It is not in any book I ever ruled."*
- Halbert Nym, curator, in his notes and queries: *"The station's intake for its first season, 4
  May to the end of 1896, is not in the ledgers and is nowhere else; the station column begins with
  1897, and inquirers wanting a figure for 1896 must be told there is none."*

Kyle explains why there is no record: that first season the milk was carried on the creamery's own
books, before there was any station account, and those sheets were never put with the station
papers. Both surviving figures are recollections of a number neither man wrote down; they differ
by 200,000 lb, roughly 22 per cent; and the only narrator who went looking says the record does
not exist. **The honest answer is that it is not determinable — the sources establish only that it
was somewhere near a million pounds for a part-season of about eight months, against a later
full-year average of 1,600,000 lb.**

## Section D — Logic

### D1 — The 1911 interval as an experiment

**When and how long.** From **8 May 1911**, when the station's own milk pipette broke, to **10 July
1911**, when it came back into service — **nine weeks**. Dated twice in Keddie's own test book
(*"May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure."* / *"July 10. Our own measure in
use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."*) and corroborated the same day in
Orra Keddie's diary (*"A. home cross about a broken measure"*).

**The variable:** the milk pipette — the one measuring instrument in the test — replaced by a
substitute and then restored.

**Held constant:** the same patrons and the same herds; the same wet ground and the same pasture;
the same season, running across it rather than within one weather; the same acid, the same test
bottles, the same four-bottle hand tester; the same operator, at the same hour, in the same order,
turning the crank at the same speed; the same scales; the same pay rule.

**The result:** the station's average test rose from 3.61 to 3.80, held there nine weeks, and
returned to 3.61 **on the day** the station's own measure came back. Roy Brant confirms
independently that every farm on the Bottom rose and fell together.

**What it establishes that a calculation cannot.** It is a physical intervention with a reversal on
withdrawal — an A-B-A design — and therefore carries **causation**, which no amount of arithmetic
on the ledgers can supply. It excludes, at a stroke and without appeal, every explanation located
in the cows, the ground, the hay, the weather, the men, the honesty of the patrons, or the
accounting: none of those was altered in those nine weeks, and the figures were. Ivy Keddie's
burette in 1958 supplies the *magnitude*; only 1911 supplies the *mechanism-class*. Vaught was
right to ask readers to weigh it above anything computed in his bulletin.

**What it does not establish.** (i) It does not say *which* glass was at fault — on the interval
alone, the substitute could have been long rather than the station's short; only the 1958
calibration settles that direction. (ii) It does not quantify the loss: nine summer weeks cannot
fix a twenty-five-year annual figure, and Kepp's objection is formally correct even though his
conclusion is wrong (he also got the length wrong, calling it six weeks, and never addressed that
the deficit he was explaining stood flat in February as well as July). (iii) It does not prove the
error was constant across twenty-six years — that comes from the summary leaf's "3.61 or within
three hundredths of it every year." (iv) It does not tell us where the substitute came from: Ivy
says Larrow Green with the cream wagon, Roy says the state man left his own that June. Strawn's own
1911 entry records the visit and mentions no loan, which weighs against Roy, but the point is not
settled.

### D2 — Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream

**Reason 1 — the cream arrived heavy, not light.** The Association's ledgers recorded, every year,
more butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream than the station's own books said
had been bought from the patrons: 60,800 lb against 57,760 lb in 1898, and one like it every year
after. Theft removes fat. The books record a surplus, in ink, annually. Dell Rundle reasoned it out
from the ledgers in 1939 and retracted his own chapter in a note while the pamphlet was in type.

**Reason 2 — the buttermaker's independent weighing.** Hobart Sill re-weighed every can of Hollow
cream into his own cold room because he never took another man's figure for what went into his
churn, and found *"more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there. Always
more. Never less,"* recorded in his own hand in the receiving book from 1893.

**Reason 3 — the nine weeks of 1911.** The same man, alone in the same building, with no additional
supervision, produced figures that rose for nine weeks and fell the day one piece of glass changed.
A thief does not steal only in the weeks his own pipette happens to be broken.

**Reason 4 — the milk that never entered the building.** From 1915, four Hessel Bottom men hauled to
Alder Corners over Redlow Ridge in the summer flush. That milk tested 3.78–3.82 there in the very
summers the same herds tested 3.61 at home. No cream can be stolen from milk that never came
through the station door; the deficiency followed the *station*, not the *milk*.

**Reason 5 — the weights.** Emmet Brant weighed every can on his own scale before the wagon left
the barn and entered the station's figure beside it for thirty years. The two columns agreed to a
pound or two, and those were days a can got slopped on the hill — two scales kept by two men who by
the end were not speaking. Nothing was taken at the intake.

**Why these are independent.** They rest on five different bodies of evidence, held by five people
with different interests and no opportunity to coordinate: the Association's own ledgers (kept by
the man who *made* the accusation); a buttermaker's separate physical re-weighing at the far end of
the haul; the tester's contemporaneous test book; a cheese factory in another county whose owner
never compared a figure with anybody and did not know the quarrel existed; and the private book of
the patron with the most to gain from proving theft. Reason 1 and Reason 2 measure the same
quantity but by different hands in different books, so they are not fully independent of each
other; each is independent of 3, 4 and 5.

**Which survive without the 1958 measurement:** all of them. Not one requires a pipette to be
calibrated. Reasons 1 and 2 were available from 1898; Reason 3 from 1911; Reason 5 continuously;
Reason 4 from 1915. The case against theft was complete and lying in the open for a quarter of a
century before anybody found a ladder.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question the entries that refute his own accusation? No.** Wilbur Teague
wrote those entries himself, every year, in his own hand, with the words *"as usual"* worn into the
wording. He raised the station account with Vose once, in **1916**, was told to let it alone, and
did — *"I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience."* In 1925 he put the theft
theory to the board in the manner Frick describes: asked about the account, he answered at length
about how such an account would look if a man were helping himself, then observed that he made no
charge against anybody. And in 1948, writing his memoir, he quotes the refuting entry in full and
is *still* saying "cream is portable" — and even misreads his own line, calling the gain "five and
a quarter percent of the fat that was actually in the cream," which is 5.00 per cent, not 5.25.
He never once asked what the surplus was surplus *to*. As he says himself: a figure that comes in
at five and a quarter every year is furniture.

### D3 — What Selby Vose knew before 1923

**He certainly knew the tests ran low. There is no evidence he ever knew what the station-gain line
meant, and specific eyewitness evidence that he never read it.** Both halves need saying.

That he knew the tests ran low is not in dispute and he says it himself in 1926: *"For a generation
the milk brought to that station tested below the milk brought to Larrow Green."* He had it from
the complaints running from 1902, from the 1908 committee report, and from writing the 1919
circular — which he justified on exactly that ground: *"A manager who sees his figures going one
way for twenty years and says nothing to the men who make them is not being kind to them."* That
is a confession of twenty years' knowledge, offered as a virtue.

He was also warned once about the account itself. Teague put it to him in **1916** and was told to
let it alone. So from 1916 Vose knew that his bookkeeper thought the station account wanted
explaining, and chose not to have it explained. That is the most serious fact against him and it
comes from the only man in the room.

But on whether he understood the gain, the two eyewitnesses split. Teague: *"Mr. Vose knew what
that account was doing. He signed the sheet every year... and a man who signs a sheet has had the
sheet in front of him."* That is an inference from a signature, and it is Teague's self-defense.
Kyle, who sat outside the door for twenty-five years and watched him do it a thousand times:
*"He read the totals and he never read the lines above them. He was not a man who read. He would
take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign."* Kyle liked him and says so,
which removes the motive to slander; and his account is direct observation of the specific act,
where Teague's is a deduction from the existence of a signature. I take Kyle's.

His behavior afterward supports incuriosity rather than concealment. A man concealing a known
shortfall does not, in 1926, stand up in front of the whole membership and *volunteer* a theory
about the station's figures, invite anyone who disagrees to come and stand where he is standing,
and pin his case on a rise he has got wrong in both size and direction of causation. He also
dismissed the 1923 condemnation as ordinary wear — *"I decline to build a mystery on a piece of
housekeeping"* — which is exactly what a man says who has never grasped that the pipette is the
instrument everything else depends on.

**Verdict:** knowledge that the tests were low, from at least 1902 and by his own admission for
twenty years; knowledge that the account carried an unexplained annual surplus, put to him
directly in 1916 and suppressed; no established knowledge that the station was short-measuring.
His fault was the manager's fault Loomis confessed for all four officers — *"not one of us ever
went up the valley and stood in the Ostrey Hollow station on a working morning to watch the work
being done"* — compounded by having been told once and having said let it alone.

## Section E — Contradictions

### Between narrators

**1. The award: $3,800 vs $3,500.** $3,800 — Loomis (transcribing the minute she wrote), Nym (Item
7, transcribed and checked twice), Frick (chairman), Vose, Teague, Falke. $3,500 — Vira Toland,
Cleve Oram. **Resolution: $3,800.** A transcribed award text appearing identically in three
independently held documents outranks two village memories. *(Same wrong value in two narrators —
see below.)*

**2. Mean pipette delivery: 16.72 vs 16.27 c.c.** 16.72 — Ivy Keddie's signed measurement sheet,
quoted in her own talk and independently transcribed by Nym as Item 14, with all nine individual
readings shown (16.70–16.74, which average to 16.72). 16.27 — Vaught's Bulletin 471 §III and §V,
and the *Ashlin Chronicle*. **Resolution: 16.72,** on three grounds: the primary signed sheet with
its component readings; arithmetic (16.72/17.6 = 0.95 exactly → 3.61 → 3.80, which matches 1896,
1911, 1924 and Alder Corners, and matches the ledger's own 5¼%/5%); and Vaught's own bulletin
conclusion says "one part in twenty" and "seventy-six thousand pounds," which 16.27 cannot produce.
*(Same wrong value in two narrators.)*

**3. Pipettes purchased: six dozen vs five dozen.** Six dozen — the 1897 invoice, transcribed by
Nym and reproduced by Ammon's own historian. Five dozen — Vaught §III, Oram. **Resolution: 72,**
on the invoice, whose line arithmetic (72 × .35 = 25.20) closes. *(Same wrong value in two
narrators.)*

**4. Surviving pipettes: nine vs eleven.** Nine — Ivy's sheet (nine individual readings), Nym Item
5, Falke, Oram. Eleven — the *Chronicle*, and Rundle's 1959 reprint footnote. **Resolution: nine.**
The sheet lists them individually; Rundle's footnote is dated to the reprint and refers the reader
to the college, i.e. he took it from the same press coverage. *(Same wrong value in two narrators
— but not two sources.)*

**5. Faulty glass into use: 1 May 1897 vs 1 May 1898.** 1897 — Orra's diary entry of that day
(contemporaneous), the 14 April 1897 invoice, Nym's office file, and Bulletin 471's own concluding
paragraph. 1898 — Vaught §III, Kyle. **Resolution: 1897.** *(Same wrong value in two narrators.)*

**6. Condemnation date: 12 June 1923 vs 12 July 1923 vs 1921.** 12 June — Strawn's dated field
entry, quoted by him and photostatted by Nym; Falke; Bulletin 471's conclusion. 12 July — Vose,
Rundle. 1921 — Hazel. **Resolution: 12 June 1923.** A dated contemporaneous field book is the
strongest class of evidence here. *(Same wrong value in two narrators — Vose and Rundle, and
probably not independent: Rundle compiled at Larrow Green where Vose's address was printed in the
annual report.)*

**7. Recorded station average: 3.61 vs 3.16.** 3.61 — the test book's own summary leaf, quoted
identically by Keddie, Ivy and Nym; Falke. 3.16 — Teague, Frick. **Resolution: 3.61.** A
transposition. Arithmetic kills 3.16 outright: 3.16 % of 40,000,000 is 1,264,000 lb, which cannot
stand in a 5¼ % relation to 1,520,000 lb received. *(Same wrong value in two narrators, and **not**
independent: Frick heard the figure at the hearing where Teague was the witness on the account.)*

**8. Milk received: 40,000,000 vs 44,000,000 lb.** 40M — Loomis (from Teague's own annual sheets,
columns added twice), Kyle (who extracted it for the arbitration), Nym (from the ledgers), Ivy. 44M
— Teague, Rundle. **Resolution: 40,000,000.** Three independent readings of the ledgers beat one
memory of them, and the sample entry closes on it: 60,800 lb fat ÷ 3.80 % = 1,600,000 lb of milk,
× 25 years = 40,000,000 exactly. The irony is that Teague's own sheets are the source of the figure
that contradicts Teague. *(Same wrong value in two narrators.)*

**9. Butterfat price: 25¢ vs 27¢, and 24¢ in 1910.** 25¢ over the period — Teague's sheets via
Loomis, Kyle (who was set to find it for the arbitration and did), Ivy, and Roy Brant ("about
twenty-five cents"). 27¢ — Sill, Kepp. 24¢ in 1910 — Roy Brant. **Resolution: 25¢ as the period
average, 24¢ for 1910;** these two are not in conflict (different scopes). Sill's and Kepp's 27¢ is
impression, not extraction. *(Same wrong value in two narrators; Kepp plausibly picked it up in the
same county talk.)*

**10. Farms on Hessel Bottom: six vs seven.** Six — the 1908 committee report (which visited each
farm), Kepp, Roy Brant, Hazel Brant. Seven — Loomis. **Resolution: six.** The office was counting
from paper; four sources counted from the ground.

**11. Families receiving the 1919 circular: nine vs eleven.** Nine — Ansel Keddie (who carried them
to the doors himself), Vira Toland, Roy Brant, Hazel Brant. Eleven — Orra's diary, contemporaneous.
**Resolution: nine, but not with full confidence.** Contemporaneity normally wins; here the man who
physically delivered them says nine, and three separate recipient households say nine. Flagged.

**12. The gap: 0.19 vs 0.17 vs 0.21 vs "a fifth of a point."** **Resolution: 0.19,** which is
*derived* (3.80 − 3.61) rather than remembered, and confirmed by Hazel and Falke. Roy's 0.17 is
measuring the wrong pair — it is the station against Larrow Green's 3.78, not against the truth.
Vose's 0.21 is his own arithmetic on a wrong 1924 average, and does not even reconcile with his own
numbers (3.61 + 0.21 ≠ 3.90). Kepp's "fifth of a point" is a county-wide field impression, not this
station.

**13. The 1924 station average: 3.80 vs 3.90 vs 3.85.** 3.80 — Keddie's own book, Strawn's 1924
entry (*"their book gives the year at 3.80"*), and Alder Corners' independent 3.80. 3.90 — Vose.
3.85 — Falke (and Sill's estimate of the true milk). **Resolution: 3.80.**

**14. Length of the 1911 interval: nine weeks vs six weeks.** Nine — the two dated book entries, 8
May to 10 July, plus Orra's diary; Falke. Six — Teague, Kepp. **Resolution: nine weeks,** by the
calendar from two dated entries. *(Same wrong value in two narrators; Kepp says the objection was
"urged upon me by a gentleman," so he may be repeating Teague or Teague's circle — possibly not
independent.)*

**15. Source of the 1911 borrowed measure: Larrow Green with the cream wagon (Ivy) vs the state man
left his own (Roy Brant).** **Cannot be settled.** Strawn's 1911 entry records the June visit and
mentions no loan, which weighs against Roy; Ivy had the records in front of her; but neither is
decisive, and nothing in the outcome turns on it.

**16. Where the crate was found: the station loft vs the Ashlin office.** Loft — Ivy (the finder),
Hazel, Oram, Duncan, Falke, the *Chronicle*. Ashlin office — Nym, Item 5. **Resolution: the loft
over the intake room.** A curatorial slip against six accounts including the finder's own and an
eyewitness who had put his hand on the crate a hundred times.

**17. Emmet Brant's weigh book begun: 1 Jan 1897 vs 1898 vs 1900.** 1897 — Nym's provenance,
Frick's exhibit list, Duncan's book history. 1898 — Roy. 1900 — Hazel. **Resolution: 1 January
1897,** on two documentary lists plus Duncan; Hazel disclaims dates at the outset and Roy is
recalling.

**18. Orra and Emmet: full siblings (Roy) vs half-siblings (Hazel).** **Cannot be settled.** See B2.

**19. Selby Vose: son (Vose himself, Loomis, Duncan, Kyle) vs nephew (Vira, Rundle).**
**Resolution: son.**

**20. Rosalie Cudd: daughter (Rosalie herself, Rundle, Duncan, Nym's provenance) vs niece (Ivy, the
Chronicle).** **Resolution: daughter.** *(Same wrong value in two narrators — and **not**
independent: the Chronicle worked from the college press release, i.e. from Ivy and Vaught.)*

**21. Ivy Keddie: granddaughter vs great-niece vs "Hazel's first cousin once removed."**
**Resolution: granddaughter; second cousin to Hazel.** See B1, B2. *(Same wrong value in two
narrators twice over: "great-niece" in the Chronicle and Falke's page two — not independent, Falke
says the error is his and corrects it; "first cousin once removed" in Vira and Oram — one piece of
village lore, not two sources.)*

**22. The Keddie–Vose kinship: asserted (Vira, Oram) vs denied (Duncan).** **Cannot be settled
documentarily; the balance favors no kinship.** See B4.

**23. Alder Corners founded: 1913 (Rosalie) vs 1914 (Roy, Rundle).** Probably built 1913 and in
operation 1914; Rosalie is first-hand (she was twelve when the first vat was set). **Not
materially settled, and immaterial.**

**24. Strawn's circuit began 1907 (his own covering note and first entry) vs 1908 (Ivy; Falke's
"from 1908 forward").** **Resolution: 1907.**

**25. Where the Association's office was: Ashlin (Teague — "one room over a harness shop" — Loomis,
the 1938 move, the hearing's first session) vs Larrow Green (Kyle's own heading).** **Resolution:
Ashlin** on three officers; possibly Kyle describes an earlier location, but the sources do not
say.

**26. Strawn's notebooks were lent from the Farland library (Ivy, Duncan, Nym) vs from the state
dairy office (the Chronicle's acknowledgments).** **Resolution: Farland library,** where they had
sat since 1947.

**27. The Alder Corners overlap: Ivy says "fourteen seasons" where the two books overlap; the Alder
Corners run is 1915–1930 and the faulty glass ended 12 June 1923, giving nine affected summers.**
**Cannot be reconciled from the sources** — Ivy may be counting overlap with the station book
generally rather than with the faulty period, but she does not say.

**28. Not a contradiction, though it looks like one:** Loomis says the crate was never mentioned at
the hearing, while Frick's exhibit list includes Strawn's 1923 notebook containing the condemnation
entry. Both are true, and together they are the hinge of the whole history: the fact was in the
room, in an exhibit, unread. Kyle confirms — nobody laid the second book beside the fourth.

## Section F — Theory

### F1 — What actually happened

**The theory.** Under Article VII every patron was paid on butterfat found by test, and *the test of
the station where he delivers shall govern* — final, with no re-test and no appeal. The Ostrey
Hollow station opened 4 May 1896 on glass borrowed from Larrow Green (Hobart Sill's own bench
outfit) and recorded 3.80. On **1 May 1897** it began using its own outfit, bought from Ammon &
Sons of Tarnet on 14 April 1897: six dozen milk pipettes blown and marked **17.6 c.c.** which in
fact delivered a mean of **16.72 c.c.** — 95 per cent of their mark. From that day the station's
recorded average fell to 3.61 and stayed there, within three hundredths, for twenty-six years.

*Why that size, and why constant.* A butterfat test reads a column of fat in a bottle neck ruled for
one charge and no other. Put in 95 per cent of the charge and 95 per cent of the fat is liberated
and 95 per cent of the column stands in the neck, and the operator reads it off in the ordinary
way. The error is therefore **proportional, not additive** — a twentieth of whatever was there,
which is why it was 0.19 against a true 3.80, why it took 0.24 from a herd recorded at 4.56 and
0.18 from one recorded at 3.42, and why every herd at that station still ranked correctly against
every other. It is also **precise**: a scale that slips or a pipe that leaks scatters, and scatter
attracts attention; glass blown short delivers the same short quantity every time, in January and
July, for a careful operator and a careless one, and looks like excellent work.

*Why it was invisible.* The pipette is the one instrument in the plant that cannot be checked
against anything, because in the practice of the trade **the pipette is the standard** — it is what
the plant measures with. Scales go against test weights, acid against strength, bottle graduations
against mercury, the crank against a count. An instrument that is itself the measure cannot be
caught by measuring with it; it can be caught only by a burette brought in from outside.

*Why it began when it did.* Because the true glass went out and the short glass came in, on 1 May
1897 — the one day nothing else changed either.

*Why it ended when it did.* Because a burette came from outside. The state began issuing burettes
to its field men in **1921**; Merle Strawn skipped the Hollow's glass in 1922 for want of daylight,
and on **12 June 1923** ran their pipettes against it, found they would not deliver their mark, and
condemned the crate. New glass was in use from July, and the station's book gave 1924 at 3.80.

*Why nothing had to change at the station or on the farms for either boundary.* Because nothing at
the station or on the farms was ever wrong. Same cows, same wet hay, same six farms, same tester,
same hour, same order, same acid, same bottles, same crank speed, same honest scales, same whole
milk. Only the measure changed, twice — and both times the figures moved with it and nothing else
did.

*The cause behind the cause.* Junia Ammon's own account: a mold had been reground, the lot ran
short, and a boy checking the first, tenth and fiftieth piece of a lot found them consistent —
consistently wrong, the one fault that kind of check cannot find. Not fraud; an accident with a
perfect disguise.

*The cost.* Over the twenty-five full delivery years 1898–1922: 40,000,000 lb of milk, 1,444,000 lb
of fat credited against 1,520,000 lb delivered, **76,000 lb** short, worth **$19,000** at 25¢. It
was not hidden. It stood in the Association's own ledgers every year as *"station gain, 3,040 lb,
five and a quarter per cent, as usual,"* and was read out at the annual meeting. As Kyle put it:
*"It was not a secret. It was worse than a secret. It was in plain sight with a name on it."*

### The three competing explanations

**1. Watered milk.** Advanced by **Selby Vose**, manager — circulated to nine families in 1919,
urged before the board in 1925, restated in his 1926 annual address; echoed by Hobart Sill and by
much of the valley. *Defeated by:* Merle Strawn's lactometer at every single visit from 1907 to
1936, which never once found added water at that station — and he told Vose so in 1919, before the
circular's ink was dry. By Rosalie Cudd: watered milk cannot be concealed from a cheesemaker,
because the curd comes slack and will not knit and the yield drops the next morning, and in sixteen
summers her father never docked one of those four men a cent. By the fact that the same men's milk
tested 3.80 over the ridge in the summers it tested 3.61 at home. By shape: water follows a man's
need and a hard year, and would not sit at exactly one part in twenty for twenty-six years, flat in
February and July. And by Vose's own chronology, which puts the cause (petition, hearing, 1925)
after the effect (the rise of 1924).

**2. The tester taking cream.** Advanced at the hearing by **Wilbur Teague**, bookkeeper, in the
form of an insinuation he then disclaimed; held by the valley at large (Vira Toland states it
flatly; Rundle wrote it as settled opinion; Oram's father believed it to his death). *Defeated by:*
the Association's own ledgers, which show the cream arriving at Larrow Green **heavy** every year —
more fat received than the station said it had bought. Theft removes fat. Dell Rundle worked this
out from the ledgers in 1939 and retracted his own chapter in a note while the pamphlet was in
type. Independently, Sill re-weighed every can into his cold room and found the same surplus, in
his own hand, page after page. Also defeated by the 1911 interval (same lone man, figures up, then
down with the glass) and by Emmet Brant's weigh book (two scales agreeing for thirty years between
two men who were not speaking).

**3. The wet hay of Hessel Bottom.** Advanced by the **1908 committee under Nyle Grigg**, revived by
Grigg's 1919 reprint, elaborated by **Dr. Roland Kepp** in 1926, and conceded as a partial factor by
Falke in 1959 before he withdrew it. *Defeated by:* Bulletin No. 214 (1911) — *"Bog hay fed as the
whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per
cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture"* — four times too small
and the wrong shape against 0.19 standing flat in every month. By Alder Corners: sixteen summers of
that same wet-ground milk at 3.78–3.82. And by the discovery that both its champions were quoting
the College as saying "two tenths" — a fourfold misquotation nobody checked against the printed
bulletin until Falke wrote to the Farland library in 1959.

### Subsidiary questions

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware? No.** The award recites *"having had
before it the books and appliances of the station"* — which Loomis, who took down every word,
identifies as a form of words and not a list, adding that the crate *"was never mentioned in the
hearing at all... not a word of it was spoken in any of the three sessions, by the patrons, by the
officers, or by the board."* What the board examined was the *working* apparatus: it saw the scales
tried and a test made through, on the **new** glass that had been in use since July 1923. The
exhibits were four books, one of them Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook containing the condemnation
entry — so the fact was on the table, in evidence, and unread; Kyle, who made the list and checked
it twice, could not say that any man laid the second book beside the fourth. And the crate itself
lay in the loft over the intake room, twenty feet up a nailed ladder, directly above the room in
which two of the three sittings were held.

**What did the tester himself believe?** In public, nothing he could prove. To the board: *"I
cannot tell you why the figures are what they are."* In his unsent letter: *"I carry no charge
against any man,"* and of the 1911 anomaly, *"That question mark is mine and I have let it stand
for fourteen years. I could not answer it then and I cannot answer it now, and I will not put
anything in its place that I do not know."* In private, he had the answer in 1911 and could not
stand it up: Duncan Keddie remembers his father coming home that summer saying **it was something
in the glass** — twice, at supper and again by the door — and never daring to write it, having
nothing but a suspicion against the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every
station in the county. Frick recalls him saying in a corridor at Ashlin that he thought the patrons
were at fault; that is a single recollection twenty-one years later and sits badly against
everything in the man's own hand, and I do not treat it as settled. **The defensible answer: from
1911 he suspected the glass, could not prove it, and would not write down what he could not prove —
"the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had."**

## Section G — Summary (150 words max)

For twenty-six years the milk of Ostrey Hollow tested one part in twenty low, because pipettes
bought new in 1897 delivered 16.72 cubic centimetres where they were marked 17.6. Nothing else was
wrong — not the cows, the wet hay, the scales, or the tester. Payment ran on the station's own test,
final and unappealable, so 76,000 pounds of butterfat — $19,000 — went uncredited and sat in the
Association's ledgers under the name "station gain," read aloud yearly. The valley blamed water,
then theft, then the land; dismissed Ansel Keddie in 1925 and paid $3,800. An inspector condemned
the glass in 1923 without looking back, and his notebook lay unread on the hearing table. In 1958
Keddie's granddaughter climbed a loft ladder, measured nine surviving pipettes, and ended it.

Eight records were needed because no single one held both the figure and its cause.

*(149 words.)*
