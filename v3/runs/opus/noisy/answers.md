# Answer sheet — Ostrey Hollow / Larrow Valley (r01–r24)
## Part 1 of 3 — Section A, stories 1–4

*Method note applying to all of Section A: where sources conflict I have preferred, in this order, (1) a document transcribed identically by two or more independent narrators, (2) a contemporaneous record over a later recollection — including when the recollector is the man who wrote the record, (3) arithmetic that closes over arithmetic that does not, (4) a narrator's own correction of himself, (5) the count of genuinely **independent** sources rather than the count of narrators. Facts I could not settle are marked as unsettled inside the story.*

### Story 1 — The By-Law That Made a Test Into Money (1889–1896)

**Who.** The farmers of the Larrow Valley, Ordell County; Gideon Vose, their first president; Effie Loomis, secretary 1901–1940; Ansel Keddie, tester.

**What and when.** The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was organized in 1889 at Larrow Green, in the schoolhouse, in cold weather, at a meeting that ran very late and during which somebody's horse got loose. The motive was plain: the members had been hauling butter to Ashlin and taking what the merchant allowed, and they had worked out that the way to be paid properly for a thing is to own the place that buys it. The creamery was built in 1891 on Larrow Creek below the mill, and the valley began to be paid in cash once a month, which had never happened to it before.

Then came the article that governs everything afterward: **Article VII**, drafted by Gideon Vose at his own kitchen table, quoted word-for-word and identically by three independent narrators (Loomis, the curator Nym, and Rundle):

> *Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*

The load-bearing half is the last clause, which nobody quotes: **the station's test, and no appeal from it.** Frick, the lawyer, put it exactly — the rule "makes the test the whole of the bargain, and it leaves a dissatisfied man with no remedy on earth except to quarrel with a figure." Falke's 1959 county report calls it the hinge: under payment by weight, nothing that followed would have cost any man a dollar.

**The year cannot be fixed.** Loomis says 1892, Rundle 1891, Grigg 1893 ("as I have always understood it"). The printed pamphlet carries no year on its face, and the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move. **1892 is best supported** — Loomis was working from the record while it existed and ties the date to a stated fact — but no document proves it, and Falke, writing officially, declined to state a year. I decline too, beyond the lean.

**The station.** Built through the winter of 1895–96 seven miles up the valley at Ostrey Hollow, so patrons need not pull three hours down through March mud. Keddie went up in 1895 to get it ready. It opened **4 May 1896**: milk weighed in, sampled, tested for butterfat, separated; cream hauled down to Larrow Green, skim home to the calves and pigs the same morning. It opened **on borrowed glass** — its own outfit had been ordered and had not come, there was talk of holding off the whole season, and Hobart Sill, the buttermaker, made up an outfit out of his own glass at his own bench and sent it up on the wagon. That first season the station averaged **3.80** — the only true figure it recorded for twenty-eight years. The station closed in 1946 and the building was afterwards used for hay tools.

**Documents.** The by-laws pamphlet (gray wrappers, undated); Loomis's *Notes toward a history* (1941); Rundle's jubilee pamphlet (1939); the test book's first leaf — *"Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie."*; Falke's report to the county board (1959).

### Story 2 — The Short Lot from Tarnet (1897–1923)

**Who.** Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet — Marcus Ammon, founder; Junia Ammon, the firm's historian. The Association as customer. Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector, at the far end.

**What and when.** On **14 April 1897** the firm billed the Association for the Ostrey Hollow station's own outfit. The invoice survives in three transcriptions — the curator's, Ivy Keddie's, and the seller's own copy reprinted in the firm's 1949 history — and all three read identically: 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20; 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at .22 — 31.68; 1 acid measure — .90; 1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00; freight — 2.15; **total 77.93**.

The glass went into use on **1 May 1897**. That date is fixed contemporaneously by Orra Keddie's diary for the day — *"A. says the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green"* — and by Bulletin 471's conclusion and the firm's own "customer of twenty-six years' standing" in 1923. Two later narrators (Vaught, and the clerk Kyle) say 1 May 1898; both are wrong. Sill's borrowed glass went back down the valley, one piece chipped.

1897 stands in the test book as "part year," with no average. From **1898 the station's average was 3.61** and stayed within three hundredths of it every year for twenty-six years, save nine weeks in 1911.

**Why the lot was short cannot be determined.** The firm's works books — what glass, what gauge, what man, what day — **burned in 1931**, and only the office side survives. Junia Ammon's reconstruction, offered as belief and not record, is that a mold had been reground and the lot ran short by accident: a boy checks the first of a lot and the tenth and the fiftieth and finds them consistent, "because they are consistent — they are consistently wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind cannot find." The Ashlin Chronicle in 1958 asserted instead that a cheap short lot was knowingly put off on the Association, treating the firm's 1923 silence as proof. **Nothing in the sources decides between accident and fraud**, and the newspaper's inference is not evidence.

**The end of it.** On **12 June 1923** Strawn ran the station's pipettes against a burette and condemned the whole crate: *"These measures will not deliver their mark."* The Association ordered new on **28 June**; the firm's reply of **2 July** is in its letter book — *"Your order of the 28th ultimo... is entered and will go forward this month. We regret the trouble. Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name."* New glass was in use in July; Strawn ran it against the burette in October — *"They deliver their mark."* 1924, the first full year on true glass, gives **3.80**.

The condemned crate was carried up a ladder into the loft over the intake room and lay there under a tarpaulin for thirty-five years.

**Documents.** The 1897 invoice; Orra Keddie's diary, 1 May 1897; Strawn's circuit book, 12 June and October 1923 (photostat at the Historical Rooms); the Ammon letter book, 2 July 1923; Junia Ammon's chapter IX (1949).

### Story 3 — Nine Weeks in 1911: The Experiment Nobody Knew He Was Running

**Who.** Ansel Keddie, at the bench; Emmet Brant and the five other Hessel Bottom farms, in their own books; Merle Strawn, passing through in June; and, fifteen years later, Dr. Roland Kepp trying to argue it away.

**What and when.** Two entries in the station test book, in Keddie's hand:

> *May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure.*
> *July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.*

Between those dates — **8 May to 10 July 1911, nine weeks** — the station's average test stood at **3.80**. On the day its own measure came back it returned to **3.61**. Keddie set a question mark against it and let the question mark stand fourteen years, refusing in 1925 to put anything in its place that he did not know.

**The rise is recorded twice, independently.** Roy Brant: "Through those nine weeks our tests came up, all of us, every farm on the Bottom; and then the station's own came back into use and the figures went down again." His father entered both the rise and the fall in the private weigh book and could make nothing of either. Orra Keddie's diary for **8 May 1911** carries the third, domestic corroboration: *"A. home cross about a broken measure."*

**Quantities that matter.** Nine weeks; 3.61 → 3.80 → 3.61; a rise of nineteen hundredths, which is precisely the ratio 16.72/17.6 applied to 3.80. The interval sits in the middle of an unbroken twenty-six-year run at 3.61.

**Disputes inside the strand.** (i) *Duration* — Teague and Kepp both say six weeks; the two dated entries give nine, and Ivy Keddie, Strawn, Roy Brant and Vaught all say nine. Nine. (ii) *Where the borrowed measure came from* — Teague says "up from town," Ivy Keddie says up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon, Roy Brant says the state man left his own measure that June. **Unresolved**: Strawn was at the station in June 1911 and wrote only "Watched a test through. Nothing here to note," with no mention of lending anything — which is itself the case's smallest and sharpest irony, since he was watching a test made on the good glass.

**Kepp's 1926 attack, the only direct assault on the interval, fails on four checkable points:** he shrinks it to six weeks; he calls it "June and July" when it began 8 May, before the flush; his flush argument is self-refuting, since flush season occurred in 1910 and 1912 as well and the average never moved; and the figures fell back on 10 July, mid-flush, with the herds still on the aftermath.

**What it is worth.** Vaught, who computed a great deal and got all of it wrong, asked readers to weigh this above anything computed in his bulletin: one variable changed — the patrons, herds, pasture, season, acid, bottles, tester and the tester's habits all unaltered — and the figures moved and moved back. "Here is one that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven years."

**Documents.** The station test book, the two 1911 entries; the Brant weigh book; Orra Keddie's diary; Strawn's circuit book, June 1911; Kepp's 1926 paper; Bulletin No. 471, §IV.

### Story 4 — The Wet Meadow on Trial: Grigg, Bulletin 214, and Dr. Kepp (1902–1959)

**Who.** Nyle Grigg, farmer of Larrow Green and chairman of the 1908 investigating committee — not a station patron and chosen for that reason; the six farms of Hessel Bottom; Dr. Roland Kepp, veterinary surgeon of Ashlin; Orin Falke, county agent.

**What and when.** The complaints begin in **1902**: it was first said down at Larrow Green that Ostrey Hollow milk did not test with the rest. Emmet Brant was among the first to say it out loud and was laughed at. Orra Keddie's diary, March 1902: "A. came home and would not eat. I said it is not your cows and not your hands, and he said no, but it is my figures they will look at."

A committee was appointed in January 1908 — four men up the road in Grigg's sleigh, to Emmet Brant's first, being the biggest. They visited **each of the six farms**, some three and four times, drew and sealed their own samples, watched the milking and the weighing and the testing, tried the station scales against a known weight and the patrons' scales against the station's, and inquired into the water at every barn. They found **"no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter,"** and asked that the clearance be printed in the same type as the rest. Their conclusion: the wet hay of that bottom thins the cream, and the remedy lies with the land and not with the station. **They sampled January, February and March only** — winter, and no summer month at all.

In **1911** the State Agricultural College published Bulletin No. 214, transcribed identically by four independent narrators: *"Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."*

**Grigg's 1919 covering letter, reprinting his own report, contradicts the page beneath his signature twice:** he remembers sampling "the whole of 1908 — every month of that year... in the barn and on the pasture" (January–March is not pasture season), and he gives Bulletin 214 as "two tenths in the winter feeding" — a fourfold inflation with the fatal summer clause suppressed.

**Kepp, 1926,** gave the theory its best form before the Ordell County Farm Club: undrained ground goes sour, acid and poor in lime and phosphates; sedge and rush crowd out the sweet grasses; a cow in milk is a manufactory on a narrow margin and "the fat is the first thing to suffer, because the fat is the dearest thing the cow makes." His remedy — drain, lime, keep stock off in the wet of spring, never feed that hay alone — is nearly Grigg's word for word. He repeats the "two tenths," puts the gap at 0.21, and, asked from the floor whether the trouble might lie in the station rather than the bottom, replied that he had never in his life read a test and would not know how, and that it is not good sense to look for a second cause in a building.

**Why it fails, in four ways.** *Magnitude* — 0.05 at most, against a flat 0.19; four times too small. *Shape* — the hay effect is winter-only and unmeasurable in summer; the gap "was seventeen hundredths in July and it was seventeen hundredths in February" (Roy Brant), "nineteen hundredths, flat as a floor" (Hazel Brant). *Sampling* — winter only, generalized to the year. *External control* — the Alder Corners table (Story 5). Falke conceded the hay in his 1959 report, then wrote to Farland for Bulletin 214, read it, and withdrew the concession in an appended note: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July."

**Documents.** The 1908 committee report, full text in Grigg's 1919 reprint and fair copy at the Rooms; Grigg's covering letter; Bulletin No. 214; Kepp's paper with the floor discussion; Falke's report and its appended note; Orra Keddie's diary, 1902 and 1908.

### Story 5 — The Circular of 1919 and the Two Kitchens

**Who.** Selby Vose, manager of the Association 1913–1938; Bertram Kyle, the clerk who typed it; Ansel Keddie, who carried it; Emmet Brant, his brother-in-law; the hauling families of Ostrey Hollow; Merle Strawn; Jerome and Rosalie Cudd, over Redlow Ridge.

**What and when.** In **June 1919** a printed circular went out over the manager's signature warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons that added water would be looked for and would be dealt with. Kyle typed it and remembers typing it, and thought at the time it was a hard thing to send to men who were bringing their milk in every morning of their lives. It named nobody and accused everybody.

**Ansel Keddie delivered it himself.** Orra Keddie's diary: he would not put them in the mail nor leave them on the platform; he took them round himself, stood at the door while each man read it, and came home at eleven at night with his hat still on.

**How many families?** Vira Toland, Roy Brant, Rundle and Hazel Brant all say **nine**; Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary says **eleven**. I cannot settle it. Nine has the arithmetic behind it — six farms on the Bottom plus the "three places on the hill" Strawn recorded in 1909 — and two recipients among its sources; eleven has contemporaneity and the wife of the man who walked to every door. The likeliest reconciliation, and it is a guess, is that nine patron families were on the Association's list and Ansel knocked on eleven doors, a farm being able to hold more than one household.

**The consequence.** Two days later Emmet Brant came up, would not come in, stood in the yard and asked Ansel why he had carried the paper himself if he did not believe it. Ansel said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. **They never spoke again — ten years, ended only by Emmet's death in 1929.** Before it the two households had eaten Sunday dinners together for thirty years. Roy Brant's later account gives the grievance in summary form (his father held that the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not); Orra's is contemporaneous, specific, and preferred where the two differ. Ansel's own words: "That is the plainest fact in this letter and it is the one I find I have the least to say about."

Across the valley the letters were kept. Vira Toland's father read his with his hat still on, put it in the desk drawer, and it was there when he died; one woman pinned hers inside the lid of her trunk. "The two ends of this valley did not speak civilly to one another for six years."

**Why the theory was false, and known to be.** Strawn's 1919 entry: *"I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so."* He tested with the lactometer at every visit across twenty-nine seasons and never once found added water; his 1923 entry says "no added water, **as always here**." The 1908 committee had already found "the milk is whole." Vose sent the circular anyway, urged water before the board in 1925 "without heat and without proof," and was still defending it in print in 1926.

**The independent assay.** From **1915 to 1930 — sixteen summers — four of the six Hessel Bottom men hauled over Redlow Ridge to the Alder Corners Cheese Company** in Vessey County during the flush, roughly 1 June to mid-September, and came back down to Ostrey Hollow the rest of the year (the ridge shuts in November and does not open before April; nobody hauled it in winter). Rosalie Cudd's appended table gives all sixteen season averages: every one between **3.78 and 3.82**, none smoothed, none thrown out. Her father's book entry for the summer of **1919 itself** — the very season of the circular — reads: *"The four Hessel Bottom men, summer of 1919: 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge."* And her own argument, which no test can improve on: watered milk cannot be hidden from a cheesemaker, because the curd comes soft and will not knit and the yield is down the next morning. "Butter is more forgiving that way. **Cheese is a witness.**" In sixteen summers her father never made one complaint of those four men and never docked one of them a cent.

**Documents.** The circular (never transcribed in full); Orra Keddie's diary, June 1919; Strawn's circuit books; Jerome Cudd's book entry; Rosalie Cudd's sixteen-season table (1958 letter); Vose's printed annual address (1926); Kyle's recollection (1953).

### Story 6 — Station Gain: The Answer Written in Ink Every Year in the Association's Own Books

**Who.** Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945; Bertram Kyle, outer-desk clerk for twenty-five years; Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green 1893–1934; Selby Vose; Dell Rundle; and, on the other side, Cleve Oram's father, who drove the cream wagon.

**What and when.** Teague invented the station-gain line in 1905. The specimen entry for **1898** is transcribed identically by three independent narrators (Teague, the curator, and Rundle's in-press note):

> *Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account.*

"There is one like it for every year after, in the same terms and in the same hand." Kyle copied that line, or the like of it, more times than he can count, and never once wondered at it: **"A figure with a name on it has been explained, and you rule under it and go on to the next."**

**The fat was never missing.** It arrived at Larrow Green, every year, heavy. Hobart Sill weighed it in himself — the platform man weighed it and Sill weighed it again into the cold room, because he never in his life took another man's figure for what was going into his churn — for **forty-one years**: "There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there. Always more. Never less... page after page after page, in my own writing." His explanation was "that is cream for you," and his own summary of the failure is the case's motto in miniature: **"I never made anything of it, and I will say plain, nobody ever asked me to."**

**One man asked, once.** Teague put the station-gain question to Vose in **1916** and was told to let it alone, and let it alone: "I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience." He insists Vose knew: "He signed the sheet every year. I laid the annual sheet before him each spring and he put his name at the foot of it." Kyle, from the outer desk, corroborates and qualifies it: Vose "read the totals and he never read the lines above them... He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign. I watched him do it a thousand times."

**And one man worked it out.** In 1939, while his jubilee pamphlet was being set in type, Dell Rundle was allowed to look at the old ledgers on an unrelated errand, saw the station-gain line, and printed a retraction of his own theft chapter: *"Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand."* A schoolteacher with no stake refuted the valley's twenty-year certainty nineteen years before the measurement — and nobody noticed; the valley was still telling Vira Toland the theft story in 1979.

**Against all this** stands one honest dissent: Cleve Oram's father, who hauled that cream for better than twenty years and always said there was less coming off the platform than went onto it. He said it in the village and never under oath, and he was not asked. It is an impression; the receiving book is a measurement, taken twice a day for forty-one years, and it goes the other way.

**The pattern.** Teague on the ledger — "a figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture. It stops him looking." Sill on the platform — "so does everybody's." Vaught in the laboratory — "it has the appearance of excellent work." Three men, three vantage points, one mechanism: **consistency reads as correctness.**

**Documents.** The Association's general ledger and station accounts, 1898 onward; the Larrow Green receiving and churn books (water-damaged at the foot, at the Rooms as Item 16); Rundle's *Note added while in press* (1939); Teague's memoir chapter (1948); Kyle's account (1953).

### Story 7 — The Board of Arbitration and the Ruin of Ansel Keddie (1925–1934)

**Who.** Alonzo Frick, attorney of Ashlin and chairman, chosen "because I was the one of the three who kept no cow"; Ansel Keddie; Selby Vose; Wilbur Teague; Effie Loomis, taking every word down; Bertram Kyle, who carried the books in.

**What and when.** The Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned in **January 1925**, Emmet Brant's name on the paper. The board convened at Ashlin on **17 March 1925**; the **second and third sessions were held at the station itself, in the weigh room**, at Frick's request, so the board could see the plant — confirmed independently by Frick, by Orra Keddie's diary, and by Cleve Oram, who stood outside on the platform in the rain and could hear the voices but not the words.

Keddie gave a **nine-sentence statement, standing**: he had kept every figure himself, never written a figure over a figure, made the tests in the same order at the same hour of every working day, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons; "Neither of them has come up that road on a working morning and stood at my elbow and watched the work done... I am an old man and I have no other trade... I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station."

**Four exhibits, and no more** — the list confirmed from two independent directions, Frick's own file and Kyle, who made it and checked it off twice: (1) the station test book, 4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924, in Keddie's hand; (2) the station's receiving book; (3) Emmet Brant's private weigh book, lent and wanted back; (4) **the state dairy inspector's circuit notebook for the year 1923** — the book containing the entry of 12 June 1923 condemning the whole crate. Kyle: "I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked."

**Three explanations were urged and none proved:** water, by the manager Vose; cream theft, obliquely by the bookkeeper Teague, in the way an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it, ending with the observation that he made no charge against anybody; and the hay, by somebody reading the 1908 report into the record. Frick: "the three did not sit easily together and nobody seemed to mind that they did not."

**The crate was never mentioned in any of the three sessions.** Loomis took down every word and it is not there.

**The outcome.** Keddie was put out on **30 April 1925**, four days before the award, while the board was still sitting. Orra's diary: "Twenty-nine seasons and a letter. He came up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm and hung it behind the shed door and asked what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it." On **4 May** the board rose and Loomis entered the award: *"finds no cause established... awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account."* Frick: "An award without a finding is an unsatisfactory thing and every member of that board knew it... an honest man was ruined by a figure nobody could explain, and we paid his neighbors money without being able to tell them what they were being paid for."

**Afterwards.** Keddie did not test a pound in 1925. He kept four or five cows on ground too small for them and turned carpenter — the cow shed, the Loomis gate, half the doors in the hollow, "a carpenter at sixty-six," at a quarter of the pay, whistling at work, which he never did when he was paid. He **died in February 1934**, nine years under it. There were more at the burying than his widow looked for, and men shook her hand who had not spoken to him in nine years, and every one of them said what a hand he was with a door, and not one said the other thing. He had told her once: **"a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."**

**Documents.** The award (identical transcriptions by Loomis and the curator); Keddie's statement and his unsent letter of spring 1925; Frick's exhibit list (1946); Kyle's account (1953); Orra Keddie's diary, January–May 1925 and the loose leaf of February 1934.

### Story 8 — The Granddaughter, the Ladder, and Bulletin No. 471 (1958–1970)

**Who.** Ivy Keddie, born 1931, Duncan's daughter and Ansel's granddaughter, graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland; Professor Leland Vaught; Roy and Hazel Brant; Rosalie Cudd; Halbert Nym, curator; Orin Falke; the *Ashlin Chronicle*.

**What and when.** In **February 1958** Ivy Keddie went up the Hollow to the station building, climbed the ladder into the loft over the intake room, and found under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff the wooden crate stenciled AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET. It had been carried up there in June 1923 and it was there still. (The curator's accession note places the find "in the Association office at Ashlin" and is wrong; Ivy's own account, Strawn's 1928 note that the loft had been "full since before the war," Rundle's description of the building, and Cleve Oram, who had put his hand on the crate himself, all put it in the loft.)

On **9 March 1958** she measured the **nine surviving pipettes** against a calibrated burette at Farland, five deliveries each, and signed the sheet:

> *Nine pipettes... stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for.*

She then brought together **five sets of records that had never been in one room**: the station test book; the Brant weigh book, **lent** by Roy Brant and returned (Hazel is emphatic on the word: "he lent it. He did not leave it to her"); Rosalie Cudd's sixteen Alder Corners seasons, copied out and posted on a four-line request; Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook, found in the college library where his books had lain unasked-for since 1947; and the Association's ledgers.

**Bulletin No. 471, October 1958**, by Vaught and Keddie, closes: *"No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."* Vaught's own introduction contradicts that conclusion in every quantity (16.27 c.c., 0.9244, 3.905, 0.295, 118,000 lb, five dozen, 1 May 1898), notices that his figure "is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80" — and publishes anyway.

**Reception.** The *Ashlin Chronicle*'s front page of 3 October 1958 got the ladder and the glass right and nearly every particular wrong. Falke's 1959 county report is the model of the opposite habit: it conceded the hay and then withdrew the concession on reading Bulletin 214, and corrected "great-niece" to granddaughter in the same note. Ivy's 1959 talk to the Historical Society, with the crate on the table: "nothing I am going to show you tonight depends in the smallest degree on my opinion of my grandfather. **It depends on nine pieces of glass and a burette.**"

**Where everything went.** Test book and crate to the Ordell County Historical Rooms, 1959; the Brant weigh book to Hazel on Roy's death in 1962 and to the Rooms in 1963; Strawn's notebooks at Farland; the Alder Corners book to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970. Duncan Keddie set the family straight on paper in 1968.

**The arithmetic of the delay.** Glass condemned 1923, cause found 1958 — thirty-five years. Grandfather dismissed 1925 — thirty-three. Dead 1934 — twenty-four years dead when the sheet was read that cleared him.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** The line is Ansel + Orra → Duncan (born 1894, schoolteacher at Ashlin) → Ivy (born 1931). Duncan states it himself in the 1968 family notes and says why he is stating it: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise." It is corroborated by Ivy's own talk, by the curator, by Roy Brant and by Rosalie Cudd. Pointing elsewhere: the *Ashlin Chronicle* (1958) and Orin Falke's county report both printed **great-niece**. These are not two sources but one — both worked from the college press release in the same autumn — and Falke, on checking, corrected himself in an appended note: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." A source that corrects itself against an outside check outranks the same source uncorrected, and outranks a weekly that never checked.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins.** Derivation: Orra Keddie and Emmet Brant were sister and brother; their children, Duncan Keddie and Roy Brant, are therefore first cousins; the children of first cousins — Ivy and Hazel — are second cousins. The derivation is performed independently and in the same terms by both families: by Duncan in 1968 ("Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece, which people say because it is shorter") and by Hazel in 1981 ("People are forever telling me she is my niece or my cousin once removed... It is not complicated; it is only that nobody wants to count"). Rundle's 1959 footnote says second cousins too. Against: Vira Toland and Cleve Oram both say "first cousin once removed" — village usage from outside either household, and the two are not independent of the valley's common talk. **What the sources leave open about the intervening generations:** whether Orra and Emmet were **full or half siblings**. Roy Brant insists full and specifically forestalls the alternative ("whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it"); Duncan and Orra herself say only "my brother" / "Emmet Brant's sister"; Hazel says half-sister by her great-grandfather's second wife. I take full siblings as better supported — Roy names and rejects Hazel's version in advance, and Orra's diary has her sitting in "that kitchen where I was a girl" and her husband naming her mother's hollyhocks — but it is not settled, and **it does not change the degree**: the children of half-siblings are still first cousins and their children still second cousins.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose**, the Association's organizer, its first president 1889–1911, and the author of the by-laws including Article VII. Four sources say son: Vose himself in his printed annual address ("My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law"), Effie Loomis's minute-based note, Duncan Keddie's family notes, and Bertram Kyle, who sat outside his door for twenty-five years and calls the nephew story nonsense. Three say nephew — Vira Toland, Rundle and Cleve Oram — all repeating valley talk, and none in a position to know. A man's own printed words to his membership are unanswerable on the point. **Why it matters:** the by-law the father wrote made the station's own test the whole of a man's pay with no appeal anywhere on earth, and the son administered it — he sent the 1919 water circular and defended it in print, told Teague in 1916 to let the station-gain question alone, signed the annual sheet carrying that gain line every year with his thumb on the bottom figure, and urged water before the board in 1925 without proof. The rule and its enforcement stayed in one family for fifty years. There is a smaller irony: Selby inherited his father's Jersey herd, which tested 4.56 in 1910 — the richest documented milk in the valley, and therefore the milk the proportional error robbed hardest, by 0.24 of a point. The persistence of the "nephew" version is itself evidence of something: valley memory drifting away from a father-son succession that looked like the family owning the place.

**B4. The relationship between the Voses and the Keddies cannot be determined.** Vira Toland says old Mrs. Vose was first cousin to Orra Keddie — "everybody knew that and nobody wrote it down anywhere" — and makes it carry weight, since it turned the 1919 circular from the Association speaking into "somebody's cousin saying it." Cleve Oram says the Keddies and Voses were "cousins of some sort," which he had from his mother, who had it from hers, and which he could not draw on paper. Against them, Duncan Keddie — Orra's son — denies it flatly, attributes the confusion to the unrelated Ashlin Voses, and gives a reason worth something: his mother could not pass a headstone without giving the connection and never once put a Vose in it. No document touches the question, and the 1889–1892 minute book that might have helped was lost in 1938. Two pieces of hearsay circulating in one small valley are close to one source, not two; but Duncan is an interested party denying a tie that would have made the water circular a letter from kin. **Verdict: not established.** What the sources do establish is that the belief was current in the valley for sixty years and that the one narrator inside the household rejected it.

## Section C — Math

### C1

**(a) Proportion.** The pipettes were blown on the stem at **17.6 c.c.** and delivered a measured mean of **16.72 c.c.** (nine pipettes, five deliveries each, against a calibrated burette, 9 March 1958).

> 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95 exactly** — the glass delivered 95 % of its mark, i.e. it was short by **one part in twenty**.

Why this becomes the test error: the test bottle's neck is ruled to read *percent fat* for a charge of 17.6 c.c. **and no other quantity**. A charge 5 % short liberates 5 % less fat, stands 5 % shorter in the graduated neck, and is read off in the ordinary way. So **every test at that station read 0.95 of the truth.**

*Rejecting the rival figure:* Vaught's introduction and the *Ashlin Chronicle* both give 16.27 c.c. (ratio 0.9244). These are one source, not two — the newspaper worked from the college press release. 16.27 is a digit transposition of 16.72, it is refuted by the measurement sheet transcribed identically by Ivy Keddie and the curator, and it fails arithmetically: 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.905, which cannot be squared with the 3.80 the station actually recorded in 1924 on good glass — an inconsistency Vaught himself flags and prints anyway.

**(b) True average and the shortfall in points.**
> Recorded average **3.61**; 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80** true. Short by **0.19 of a point** (nineteen hundredths).

Crucial qualification: **0.19 is not a fixed quantity, it is 5 % of whatever was there.** A herd recorded at 4.56 (Vose's Jerseys, 1910) was short 0.24; a herd recorded at 3.42 (Brant, 1910) was short 0.18. The richer the milk, the more was taken. (Roy Brant's independently computed 0.17 is not an error: he compared the station against Larrow Green's own patrons at 3.78, and 3.78 − 3.61 = 0.17 exactly.)

**(c) Butterfat not credited.**
> Station intake, 25 full delivery years: **40,000,000 lb of milk** (avg. 1,600,000 lb/yr).
> Credited at 3.61 %: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**.
> Actually delivered, at 3.80 %: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lb**.
> Difference = **76,000 lb of butterfat.**

Corroboration: Bulletin 471's own concluding paragraph states 76,000 lb, and Effie Loomis published the 40,000,000 lb / 25-cent figures in 1941 — seventeen years before the solution — from Teague's annual sheets, columns added twice, and Kyle says he personally got the same figures out for the arbitration in 1925.

**(d) Money.**
> 76,000 lb × **$0.25** (the average butterfat price over the claim years) = **$19,000**.
> The patrons received **$3,800** — **exactly one fifth (20 %)**.

*Rejected:* 67,000 lb (Frick, echoed by Oram) is the patrons' own pre-solution reckoning, not a computed result; $19,600 (Rundle, echoed by the Chronicle) is the petition's claim figure, and does not even reconcile with 67,000 lb at any price in evidence; 118,000 lb (Vaught) follows mechanically from 16.27.

### C2 — Emmet Brant, 1910

From the weigh-book leaf, transcribed identically by the curator and by Roy Brant, and recited from memory by Hazel Brant: *"Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42."* Butterfat that year: **24 cents**.

**(a) Credited:** 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb of butterfat.**
**(b) Owed and not credited:** true test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = **3.60**; true fat = 200,000 × 0.0360 = **7,200 lb**; shortfall = 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb** (= 5 % of 7,200; = 0.18 of a point on 200,000 lb).
**(c) Worth:** 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40** in that one year, to the station's largest patron.

### C3 — Over the same 25 full delivery years

**(a) Credited to the patrons on station test:** **1,444,000 lb.**
**(b) Actually reached Larrow Green in the station's cream:** **1,520,000 lb.**
**(c) The difference, 76,000 lb, expressed as a percentage — of what:**
> 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5.00 % of the butterfat actually delivered/received.**
> 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.263 % of the butterfat credited** — the ledger's "five and a quarter per cent."

Both are correct **of their own denominator and of no other.** The Association's books use the second because a bookkeeper reckons a surplus against the figure he has already entered. The 1898 specimen entry proves the ratio at year scale: 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.00 %; 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.263 %. **5.00 % is the one that is also the glass** (1/20 short). Vaught attaches "five and a quarter per cent" to the *delivered* figure, which is the wrong denominator.

### C4 — The invoice

**(a) Total: $77.93.** Verified by addition of the printed line items: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = **77.93**. Both Junia Ammon and the *Chronicle* write "seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents" in prose — Junia directly beneath her own reproduction of the invoice reading 77.93. Arithmetic settles it; the transposition is theirs.

**(b) Pipettes bought: six dozen = 72**, at $0.35 each ($25.20 ✓). Each was short **0.88 c.c.** (17.6 − 16.72). Vaught and Cleve Oram both say five dozen; the invoice exists in three transcriptions, including the seller's own copy from its files, and 60 × 0.35 = $21.00, which does not produce the printed $25.20. Only nine of the 72 survived to be measured; two of those nine are chipped at the tip.

### C5

**(a) Two different periods, and why.**
> **In use: 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 — 26 years and about six weeks.**
> **Money calculation: 25 full delivery years, 1898 through 1922.**

They differ because the two end years are not clean. **1897** is a part year — the glass entered service on 1 May, and the test book's summary leaf accordingly records "1897, part year" with no average at all, so no annual intake or average can be applied to it. **1923** is likewise split: bad glass to 12 June, new glass from July, so it belongs to neither regime. Ivy Keddie therefore ran the arithmetic over the twenty-five whole years that were entirely on the faulty glass at a stable 3.61. (A third, smaller reason the "in use" span is not 26 years of *uniform* error: the nine weeks of 1911 were run on a borrowed measure.)

**(b) First-season intake: cannot be determined from the sources.** The curator states flatly that the station column in the records begins with 1897 and that inquirers wanting a figure for 1896 "must be told there is none" — the first season's milk was carried on the creamery's own books and those sheets were never put with the station papers (Kyle confirms this independently). Two men supply memories and they do not agree: Hobart Sill says "about nine hundred thousand pounds," Bertram Kyle "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds," each explicitly from recollection or from what he "understood at the time from men who were there." Both narrators run high on their other round numbers (Sill: 27 cents for 25, "3.85"; Kyle: no independent check). A 200,000 lb spread between two hearsay figures with no book behind either is not a determination. **Declined.**

## Section D — Logic

### D1 — The 1911 interval as an experiment

**When and how long.** **8 May to 10 July 1911 — nine weeks.** Fixed by two dated entries in the operator's own hand ("Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." / "Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ?"), and corroborated contemporaneously by Orra Keddie's diary for 8 May. Teague and Kepp both say six weeks; two dated document entries beat two recollections, and four other narrators say nine.

**Variable.** One: the measuring pipette. The station's own broke and a borrowed measure was substituted, then withdrawn.

**Held constant.** The patrons, the herds, the ground, the season, the feed, the acid, the test bottles, the hand tester, the operator and his habits and speed, the sampling routine, the scales, the by-law and the pay basis. Strawn even watched a test through in June and wrote "Nothing here to note."

**Result.** The station average rose from 3.61 to **3.80** and held there for nine weeks, then fell back to 3.61 **on the day** the station's own measure returned. Recorded twice independently: in the tester's book (with a question mark) and in Emmet Brant's private weigh book, where the rise and the fall appear on the patrons' side — "all of us, every farm on the Bottom."

**What it establishes that calculation cannot.** It is an intervention, not an inference. It removes and restores the suspected cause under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and it therefore disposes at a stroke of every explanation that appeals to the land, the hay, the herds, the season or the men, for the sufficient reason that none of those was altered in those nine weeks and the figures were. A calculation from the 1958 measurement can only tell you what short glass *would* have done; the nine weeks show what removing it *did* — in 1911, recorded by a man who did not know what he was recording. Vaught, who computed a great deal, asked readers to weigh it above anything computed in his bulletin.

**What it does not establish.** (i) It does not say *why* the glass was short — mold, wear, manufacture — nor which pieces. (ii) It does not fix the size of the error over twenty-six years; nine weeks of one summer are not a long-run mean, and the 3.80 figure only coincides with the true rate because other evidence (1896, Alder Corners) independently anchors it. (iii) It does not prove the borrowed measure was itself accurate — only that the two measures differed; the inference that the borrowed one was true is drawn from elsewhere. (iv) It does not date the beginning or the end of the fault. (v) By itself it does not refute theft — a thief could in principle have paused — though it makes the theory absurd, since the pause would have to coincide exactly with the breaking and mending of his own pipette; the ledger evidence closes that gap. (vi) It says nothing about whose fault the purchase was. **And note what the interval also proves about the *source* of the borrowed measure: nothing.** Three narrators give three origins (up from town; up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon; left by the state man in June) and the point is unresolved.

### D2 — Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream

**Reason 1 — the fat was never missing (documentary, Ashlin).** The Association's own ledger records, every year from 1898, more butterfat arriving at Larrow Green in the station's cream than was credited to the patrons at the station: 60,800 lb received against 57,760 lb credited, station gain 3,040 lb, "five and a quarter per cent, as usual." Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green **light**. It reached the Green **heavy**.

**Reason 2 — the cream was physically weighed, twice a day, for forty-one years (physical, Larrow Green).** Hobart Sill weighed it onto the platform and again into the cold room, because he never took another man's figure for what went into his churn, and recorded it in his own receiving book: "There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there. Always more. Never less... page after page after page, in my own writing."

**Reason 3 — the nine weeks of 1911 (contemporaneous, at the station and on the farms).** Same man, same bench, same supervision, no change of any kind except a broken pipette — and the figures rose, then fell back the day his own measure returned.

**Reason 4 — the outside assay (independent, another county).** For sixteen summers, 1915–1930, four Hessel Bottom men's milk tested 3.78–3.82 at the Alder Corners Cheese Company over Redlow Ridge, where Keddie never touched it. The same milk, the same ground, the same years, ~0.19 higher than at home.

**Why these are independent.** They are four different kinds of record, kept by four different people with four different interests, in three different buildings and two counties: an accounting entry made in the Association's office by the bookkeeper who later advanced the accusation; a physical double weighing made by a buttermaker who believed the patrons were watering their cans and was hostile to the Hollow; a pair of dated entries in the tester's own book plus a matching pair in a patron's private weigh book; and a cheesemaker's season averages in a factory that had no stake in Ordell County's quarrel at all. None depends on any other, and **none of them depends on the 1958 measurement.** Reasons 1, 2 and 3 were all in existence, in ink, before 1912; reason 4 by 1930. Dell Rundle proved the point from reason 1 alone in 1939 and printed the retraction of his own theft chapter.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question the entries?** Once, and not afterwards. Teague put the station-gain question to Selby Vose in **1916**, was told to let it alone, and let it alone: "I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience." He wrote the same entry for forty years with the words "as usual" in it, and then in 1925 he was the man who put the cream-theft explanation to the board of arbitration — obliquely, answering at length about how such an account would look if a man were helping himself, and then observing that he made no charge against anybody. **He advanced the accusation his own ledger line refuted, and never once put the two together.** Kyle's verdict from the outer desk: "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years."

### D3 — Did the manager know the tests ran low before 1923?

**Yes, beyond any doubt, that the tests ran low — and that is a different question from whether he knew why.**

Evidence he knew the tests ran low: the complaints were made openly from 1902 and renewed at annual meetings in every decade; he was manager from 1913 and the Ostrey Hollow question was the standing grievance of his managership; in **1916** Teague laid the station-gain discrepancy before him personally and was told to let it alone — which requires that he understood there was something to let alone; in **1919** he printed and signed a circular whose entire premise was that the Hollow's figures were low and that added water explained it; Strawn told him to his face that same year that he had tested for water at every visit and never found any; he urged the water theory before the board in 1925; and he defended the whole record in print in 1926. A manager who circulates a warning about the cause of a discrepancy has necessarily noticed the discrepancy.

**Whether he knew the ledger was recording the answer is a separate matter, and the sources point the other way.** Teague insists "He signed the sheet every year... a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him. What he made of it he never told me." But Bertram Kyle, who sat a foot from the door for twenty-five years, is the better witness on the physical act: Vose "read the totals and he never read the lines above them. He was not a man who read. He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign. I watched him do it a thousand times."

**My answer:** he knew the tests ran low, and he had been told in 1916 that the station's account carried a persistent unexplained gain and had chosen not to pursue it — which is culpable inattention at minimum. **Whether he ever grasped that the gain line was the missing fat cannot be determined**, and Kyle's eyewitness account of how he handled paper is the strongest evidence that he did not. Teague's "he had the sheet in front of him" is an inference from a signature, not knowledge of a state of mind, and Teague was not a disinterested reporter — he had been rebuffed by the man he is describing.

### E1(ii) — Cases where the sources cannot settle it

- **How many families received the 1919 circular: nine or eleven.** Nine (Vira Toland, Roy Brant, Rundle, Hazel Brant, and the arithmetic six-Bottom-farms-plus-three-hill-places) against eleven in Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary, written by the wife of the man who carried them to every door. Contemporaneity pulls one way, independent recipients and arithmetic the other. **Unsettled.** Best guess: nine on the Association's list, eleven doors knocked on.
- **What Ansel Keddie believed the cause was.** His own papers say he did not know and would not guess. Frick reports a private corridor remark in 1925 that he had always thought the patrons at fault. Duncan says he came home in the summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass and never dared write it. Orra's diary for 8 May 1911 records only that he was cross about a broken measure. **Unsettled.**
- **Where the borrowed measure of 1911 came from.** Three narrators, three origins. Strawn's own June 1911 entry mentions no loan. **Unsettled.**
- **Whether the short lot was an accident or knowingly sold.** The works books that would answer it burned in 1931. The only account of cause is the seller's own reconstruction, offered as belief. The *Chronicle*'s contrary assertion is inference from silence. **Unsettled.**
- **The Vose–Keddie kinship.** See B4. **Unsettled.**
- **The year Article VII was adopted.** 1891 / 1892 / 1893, the pamphlet undated, the minute book lost in 1938. 1892 best supported; **not settled by any document**, and Falke, writing officially, declined to state it.
- **The station's 1896 intake.** ~900,000 (Sill) vs ~1,100,000 (Kyle), no book behind either. **Unsettled.**

### E1(iii) — Two narrators carrying the same wrong value, and what breaks each tie

1. **$3,500 award** — Vira Toland (1979) and Cleve Oram (1977). *Not independent:* both are Hollow-side oral memories of the same valley figure at half a century's distance. **Broken by** the award text transcribed identically twice, plus the chairman, the manager, the bookkeeper and the clerk.
2. **67,000 lb** — Frick (1946) and Oram (1977). *Not independent:* Frick identifies it as "the patrons' own reckoning," i.e. the claim circulating in the valley, which is where Oram got it. **Broken by** arithmetic and Bulletin 471's 76,000.
3. **$19,600** — Rundle (1939) and the *Chronicle* (1958). Rundle's is the petition's own figure and predates the solution; the *Chronicle* wrongly attributes it to the 1958 bulletin. **Broken by** 76,000 × $0.25 = $19,000, and by the bulletin's own text.
4. **12 July 1923** — Vose (1926) and Rundle (1939). *Not independent:* Vose's printed address was exactly the kind of source Rundle compiled from. **Broken by** Strawn's dated entry and, decisively, by the 28 June order acknowledged in Ammon's 2 July letter.
5. **Eleven pipettes** — Rundle's 1959 footnote and the *Chronicle*. **Broken by** the signed measurement sheet and by Oram, a local witness who handled the crate and says nine.
6. **Five dozen pipettes** — Vaught and Oram. Probably independent errors. **Broken by** the invoice in three transcriptions and by $25.20 ÷ $0.35 = 72.
7. **16.27 c.c.** — Vaught and the *Chronicle*. *Not independent:* the paper worked from the college press release. **Broken by** the measurement sheet and by 16.27's incompatibility with the station's own 3.80 in 1924 — which Vaught himself notices in print.
8. **$77.39** — Junia Ammon and the *Chronicle*. **Broken by** adding the five line items on the invoice each of them reproduces or claims to have seen.
9. **"Six weeks" for 1911** — Teague and Kepp. Possibly one source: Teague used the figure in Ashlin, where Kepp practised, and Kepp names an unidentified gentleman who had pressed the 1911 argument on him. **Broken by** the two dated book entries.
10. **1 May 1898 for the glass** — Vaught and Kyle. Independent (a bulletin and a clerk). **Broken by** Orra Keddie's diary of 1 May 1897 and the April 1897 invoice.
11. **3.85** — Sill (as a guess at the true rate) and Falke (as the post-1924 average). **Broken by** Keddie's book and Strawn's book, both 3.80.
12. **"Nephew"** — Vira Toland, Rundle and Oram; three narrators, one valley rumour. **Broken by** Vose's own "My father."
13. **"First cousin once removed"** — Vira Toland and Oram; again one valley usage. **Broken by** two explicit derivations from inside both families.
14. **"Great-niece"** — the *Chronicle* and Falke. *Not independent:* one press release, one autumn. **Broken by** Falke's own verified correction and Duncan's family notes.
15. **44 million lb** — Teague and Rundle; **3.16** — Teague and Frick. *Not independent:* Teague was the man everyone asked for a number, and both figures are his. **Broken by** the annual sheets (Loomis, Kyle) and the test book's summary leaf. Note the diagnostic pattern: **the documents Teague transcribes are perfect; only his recollected round numbers fail** — and both of them became the valley's official figures.
16. **"Two tenths" for Bulletin 214** — Grigg and Kepp. **Broken by** four identical transcriptions of the bulletin's actual sentence, one fetched from the college library by a man with no stake.

### E1(iv) — Narrators who contradict themselves on the page

- **Junia Ammon (r20):** "The house sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900" — printed a page above an invoice she reproduces entire, dated 14 April 1897, sold to a customer in Ordell County, and a page above her own "a customer of twenty-six years' standing" in 1923. And she prints "Total — 77.93" and then writes "Seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents."
- **Leland Vaught (r11):** every quantity in his introduction and method contradicts the bulletin's concluding paragraph, which he reproduces entire in the same document — and he half-notices, writing that his 118,000 lb "is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the glassware," and publishing anyway. **A reader who trusts his body over his own last paragraph gets the case wrong.**
- **Nyle Grigg (r15):** his 1919 covering letter says the committee sampled "the whole of 1908 — every month of that year... in the barn and on the pasture"; the report reprinted directly beneath his signature says "through January, February and March" — which is also not pasture season in that county. A scrupulous, disinterested man misremembered his own evidence into the shape of his conclusion.
- **Halbert Nym (r07):** Items 3 and 4 carry each other's keepers; his own Item 8 note ("in the hand of Item 3") refutes his Item 3 header; and his Item 3 column list drops the fat column Keddie describes.
- **Selby Vose (r08):** claims the 1924 tests rose "by twenty-one hundredths" to an average of "3.90" — 3.61 + 0.21 = 3.82.
- **Hazel Brant (r17):** opens by disclaiming years altogether, then supplies four (1900, 1899, 1921, 1926), all wrong; and reconciles "nine families, six farms" with "a farm can hold more than one family," when the actual reconciliation is six Bottom farms plus three places on the hill.
- **Cleve Oram (r21):** says five dozen measures "went into that crate to begin with — that was the whole outfit," then explains the nine survivors by loft breakage, when the crate as condemned in 1923 already held only what had survived twenty-six years of daily use.
- **Duncan Keddie (r18):** gives his mother's birth as 1868 and her death in 1948 "aged eighty-two."
- **Orin Falke (r22)** — the honourable version: paragraph 9 concedes the bog hay as a real partial factor; the appended note withdraws it on reading Bulletin 214, and corrects "great-niece" as well. This is self-correction rather than self-contradiction, and it is the single best piece of epistemic conduct in the corpus.

## Section F — Theory

### F1 — What actually produced the shortfall

**The theory in one statement.** Every butterfat test made at Ostrey Hollow between 1 May 1897 and 12 June 1923 was low by exactly one part in twenty, because the pipettes the station drew its charge with delivered 16.72 c.c. against a mark blown at 17.6 c.c. Nothing else was wrong: not the land, not the cows, not the men, not the scales, not the acid, not the operator's method.

**Why that size, and why exactly.** The butterfat test does not weigh anything. A measured charge of milk is drawn to a mark and delivered into a bottle whose graduated neck is ruled to read *percent fat for that charge and no other*. Every mark on the neck means "this much fat, out of the standard charge." If the charge is short by 5 %, then 5 % less fat is liberated, a 5 % shorter column stands in the neck, and the neck — which knows nothing of the substitution — is read off in the ordinary way. **The reading is not merely low; it is low in the exact proportion by which the charge was short.** 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95. Hence 3.61 recorded where 3.80 was true, and hence the error was *proportional, not additive*: the 4.56 herd lost 0.24 of a point and the 3.42 herd 0.18, and no man could know his good cows cost him more than his poor ones.

**Why it began when it did.** On 1 May 1897 the station stopped using the glass Hobart Sill had made up at his own bench and lent them, and started using its own new outfit from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet. 1896, on Sill's borrowed glass, had averaged 3.80 — the true rate. 1897 is a part year. From 1898 the average is 3.61 and stays there. **The anomaly begins precisely at the changeover of the glass**, and Orra Keddie's diary dates that changeover to the day.

**Why it ended when it did.** On 12 June 1923 Merle Strawn ran the station's pipettes against a burette — an instrument the state had only begun issuing to field men in 1921 — and condemned the whole crate. New glass was in service in July; Strawn checked it in October and it delivered its mark; 1924, the first full year on true glass, reads 3.80.

**Why nothing at the station or on the farms had to change for either.** Because the fault was in the instrument that measures, not in anything measured. Three properties made it invisible. It was **proportional**, so the ranking of herds inside the station stayed perfectly orderly and only the comparison with the outside world was wrong. It was **precise** — a scattered error attracts attention, but a pipette that is short every time, in January and in July, for a careful operator and a careless one, "has the appearance of excellent work." And it was **unfalsifiable from inside the plant**: the scales can be checked against test weights, the acid for strength, the bottle's graduation with mercury, the operator's speed by counting — but the pipette is not checked against anything, because in the ordinary practice of the trade **the pipette is the standard**. An instrument that is itself the measure cannot be caught by measuring with it. It can be caught only by a burette brought in from outside, and burettes reached the state's field men very late in this history.

### The three competing explanations, their advocates, and what defeats each

**1. The bog hay of Hessel Bottom thins the cream.** Advanced by the 1908 investigating committee under **Nyle Grigg**, restated by Grigg in his 1919 covering letter, given its most sophisticated form by **Dr. Roland Kepp** in 1926, and conceded (then withdrawn) by county agent **Orin Falke** in 1959. *Defeated by:* (a) **magnitude** — the College's own Bulletin No. 214 found bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the test "by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture"; the gap was 0.19, four times too large, and both Grigg and Kepp quote the bulletin as "two tenths," a fourfold inflation with the summer clause suppressed; (b) **shape** — the gap stood flat in every month of every year, "seventeen hundredths in July and seventeen hundredths in February"; a feed effect cannot be seasonless; (c) **the committee's own record** — it sampled January, February and March 1908 only, and generalized to the year, and Grigg eleven years later remembered doing the very thing that would have tested him; (d) **the external control** — the same milk off the same wet ground tested 3.78–3.82 for sixteen summers at Alder Corners; (e) **the 1911 nine weeks**, in which the hay did not change and the figures did.

**2. The Ostrey Hollow patrons watered their milk.** Advanced by **Selby Vose**, manager — the 1919 circular, his argument before the board in 1925, and his printed defence in 1926. *Defeated by:* (a) Strawn tested with the lactometer at every visit for twenty-nine seasons, never once found added water, and told the manager so in 1919 — four years before Vose was still asserting it; (b) the 1908 committee found "the milk is whole and the scales are honest" and "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter"; (c) Rosalie Cudd's decisive physical argument — watered milk cannot be concealed from a cheesemaker, because the curd comes soft and will not knit and the yield falls the next morning; in sixteen summers her father made no complaint and docked no man a cent; (d) the summer of 1919 itself, when the four accused men's milk tested 3.79–3.82 over the ridge; (e) Bulletin 471: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow."

**3. The tester stole cream at the station.** Advanced obliquely before the board by the bookkeeper **Wilbur Teague**, believed by the valley at large (Vira Toland; the hauler Oram's father), printed and then retracted by **Dell Rundle**. *Defeated by:* (a) the fat was never missing — it reached Larrow Green **heavy**, every year, recorded in the Association's own ledger as "station gain"; (b) Sill physically weighed the excess in twice a day for forty-one years; (c) the nine weeks of 1911; (d) the Alder Corners figures; (e) Rundle's own 1939 retraction from the ledger alone; (f) Bulletin 471: "no cream was stolen there."

### Subsidiary 1 — Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?

**No — and it is worse than that.** The crate had lain in the loft over the intake room since June 1923. Two of the board's three sessions were held in the weigh room directly underneath it, twenty feet below the trap. Effie Loomis, who took down every word of all three sessions, records that **the crate was never mentioned at all** — not by the patrons, not by the officers, not by the board — and that the award's phrase about "the books and appliances of the station" was a form of words the board used and not a list of anything; the appliances then in the room were the *new* glass. Worse still, the written record of the condemnation **was an exhibit**: of only four documents before the board, exhibit 4 was the state dairy inspector's circuit notebook for 1923, which contains the entry "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them." This is confirmed independently from two directions — Frick's exhibit list reprinted from his own file, and Bertram Kyle, who made the list, checked it off twice, and carried the books in and out: "I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked." Frick spent the rest of his life wondering "whether the answer was in that room and simply was not in any of the three stories, and whether a lawyer who understood chemistry might have found it. I did not understand chemistry." It was in that room, in exhibit 4, in the inspector's own hand.

### Subsidiary 2 — What did the tester himself believe the cause was?

**Cannot be determined; three incompatible accounts and no document that settles it.** (i) **His own papers** — the nine-sentence statement and the unsent letter of 1925 — say he could not tell them why the figures were what they were, and that he would not put anything in the place of the question mark he set in 1911 "that I do not know." (ii) **Frick** reports a private, unminuted corridor remark at Ashlin: Keddie told him he had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the room — and Frick is careful to distinguish *accusing*, which Keddie never did, from *suspecting*, which he says he did. (iii) **Duncan Keddie**, writing in 1968, says his father came home in the summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, said it twice, at supper and again by the door, and never dared put it in the book — "he had, after all, nothing to hold in his hand but a suspicion about a piece of glass, and against it stood the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county." (iv) **Orra Keddie's diary for 8 May 1911**, the only contemporaneous record of that day, says merely "A. home cross about a broken measure," and records no suspicion of any kind then or later.

Duncan is a first-hand household witness but is recalling at fifty-seven years and had every reason to want his father to have seen it; Frick is second-hand at twenty-one years and reports a single sentence; Keddie's own papers are documents but are also a public statement made under accusation. **The only thing safely established is what he wrote: a question mark, which he let stand for fourteen years and would not replace with a guess.** That is a fact about his conduct, not about his belief, and it is where the evidence stops.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** A cooperative creamery in the Larrow Valley paid every farmer on a butterfat test, and its by-law made the test at his own station final, with no appeal. At the Ostrey Hollow station the milk read 3.61 where it was truly 3.80 — for twenty-six years, because the pipettes bought in 1897 delivered 16.72 c.c. against a 17.6 c.c. mark, exactly one part in twenty short. The valley blamed the wet hay, then the farmers' water, then the tester, and ruined him in 1925. It took eight records to see it: the by-law, the invoice, nine weeks in 1911 when a borrowed measure was used, the committee reports, the circular, the ledger's own "station gain," the arbitration, and the granddaughter who climbed a ladder in 1958. Each held one piece. Nobody had ever laid them side by side.
