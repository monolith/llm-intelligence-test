## Section C — Math

### C1

**(a) Proportion.** The pipettes were blown on the stem at **17.6 c.c.** and delivered a measured mean of **16.72 c.c.** (nine pipettes, five deliveries each, against a calibrated burette, 9 March 1958).

> 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95 exactly** — the glass delivered 95 % of its mark, i.e. it was short by **one part in twenty**.

Why this becomes the test error: the test bottle's neck is ruled to read *percent fat* for a charge of 17.6 c.c. **and no other quantity**. A charge 5 % short liberates 5 % less fat, stands 5 % shorter in the graduated neck, and is read off in the ordinary way. So **every test at that station read 0.95 of the truth.**

*Rejecting the rival figure:* Vaught's introduction and the *Ashlin Chronicle* both give 16.27 c.c. (ratio 0.9244). These are one source, not two — the newspaper worked from the college press release. 16.27 is a digit transposition of 16.72, it is refuted by the measurement sheet transcribed identically by Ivy Keddie and the curator, and it fails arithmetically: 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.905, which cannot be squared with the 3.80 the station actually recorded in 1924 on good glass — an inconsistency Vaught himself flags and prints anyway.

**(b) True average and the shortfall in points.**
> Recorded average **3.61**; 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80** true. Short by **0.19 of a point** (nineteen hundredths).

Crucial qualification: **0.19 is not a fixed quantity, it is 5 % of whatever was there.** A herd recorded at 4.56 (Vose's Jerseys, 1910) was short 0.24; a herd recorded at 3.42 (Brant, 1910) was short 0.18. The richer the milk, the more was taken. (Roy Brant's independently computed 0.17 is not an error: he compared the station against Larrow Green's own patrons at 3.78, and 3.78 − 3.61 = 0.17 exactly.)

**(c) Butterfat not credited.**
> Station intake, 25 full delivery years: **40,000,000 lb of milk** (avg. 1,600,000 lb/yr).
> Credited at 3.61 %: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**.
> Actually delivered, at 3.80 %: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lb**.
> Difference = **76,000 lb of butterfat.**

Corroboration: Bulletin 471's own concluding paragraph states 76,000 lb, and Effie Loomis published the 40,000,000 lb / 25-cent figures in 1941 — seventeen years before the solution — from Teague's annual sheets, columns added twice, and Kyle says he personally got the same figures out for the arbitration in 1925.

**(d) Money.**
> 76,000 lb × **$0.25** (the average butterfat price over the claim years) = **$19,000**.
> The patrons received **$3,800** — **exactly one fifth (20 %)**.

*Rejected:* 67,000 lb (Frick, echoed by Oram) is the patrons' own pre-solution reckoning, not a computed result; $19,600 (Rundle, echoed by the Chronicle) is the petition's claim figure, and does not even reconcile with 67,000 lb at any price in evidence; 118,000 lb (Vaught) follows mechanically from 16.27.

### C2 — Emmet Brant, 1910

From the weigh-book leaf, transcribed identically by the curator and by Roy Brant, and recited from memory by Hazel Brant: *"Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42."* Butterfat that year: **24 cents**.

**(a) Credited:** 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb of butterfat.**
**(b) Owed and not credited:** true test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = **3.60**; true fat = 200,000 × 0.0360 = **7,200 lb**; shortfall = 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb** (= 5 % of 7,200; = 0.18 of a point on 200,000 lb).
**(c) Worth:** 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40** in that one year, to the station's largest patron.

### C3 — Over the same 25 full delivery years

**(a) Credited to the patrons on station test:** **1,444,000 lb.**
**(b) Actually reached Larrow Green in the station's cream:** **1,520,000 lb.**
**(c) The difference, 76,000 lb, expressed as a percentage — of what:**
> 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5.00 % of the butterfat actually delivered/received.**
> 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.263 % of the butterfat credited** — the ledger's "five and a quarter per cent."

Both are correct **of their own denominator and of no other.** The Association's books use the second because a bookkeeper reckons a surplus against the figure he has already entered. The 1898 specimen entry proves the ratio at year scale: 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.00 %; 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.263 %. **5.00 % is the one that is also the glass** (1/20 short). Vaught attaches "five and a quarter per cent" to the *delivered* figure, which is the wrong denominator.

### C4 — The invoice

**(a) Total: $77.93.** Verified by addition of the printed line items: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = **77.93**. Both Junia Ammon and the *Chronicle* write "seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents" in prose — Junia directly beneath her own reproduction of the invoice reading 77.93. Arithmetic settles it; the transposition is theirs.

**(b) Pipettes bought: six dozen = 72**, at $0.35 each ($25.20 ✓). Each was short **0.88 c.c.** (17.6 − 16.72). Vaught and Cleve Oram both say five dozen; the invoice exists in three transcriptions, including the seller's own copy from its files, and 60 × 0.35 = $21.00, which does not produce the printed $25.20. Only nine of the 72 survived to be measured; two of those nine are chipped at the tip.

### C5

**(a) Two different periods, and why.**
> **In use: 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 — 26 years and about six weeks.**
> **Money calculation: 25 full delivery years, 1898 through 1922.**

They differ because the two end years are not clean. **1897** is a part year — the glass entered service on 1 May, and the test book's summary leaf accordingly records "1897, part year" with no average at all, so no annual intake or average can be applied to it. **1923** is likewise split: bad glass to 12 June, new glass from July, so it belongs to neither regime. Ivy Keddie therefore ran the arithmetic over the twenty-five whole years that were entirely on the faulty glass at a stable 3.61. (A third, smaller reason the "in use" span is not 26 years of *uniform* error: the nine weeks of 1911 were run on a borrowed measure.)

**(b) First-season intake: cannot be determined from the sources.** The curator states flatly that the station column in the records begins with 1897 and that inquirers wanting a figure for 1896 "must be told there is none" — the first season's milk was carried on the creamery's own books and those sheets were never put with the station papers (Kyle confirms this independently). Two men supply memories and they do not agree: Hobart Sill says "about nine hundred thousand pounds," Bertram Kyle "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds," each explicitly from recollection or from what he "understood at the time from men who were there." Both narrators run high on their other round numbers (Sill: 27 cents for 25, "3.85"; Kyle: no independent check). A 200,000 lb spread between two hearsay figures with no book behind either is not a determination. **Declined.**

## Section D — Logic

### D1 — The 1911 interval as an experiment

**When and how long.** **8 May to 10 July 1911 — nine weeks.** Fixed by two dated entries in the operator's own hand ("Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." / "Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ?"), and corroborated contemporaneously by Orra Keddie's diary for 8 May. Teague and Kepp both say six weeks; two dated document entries beat two recollections, and four other narrators say nine.

**Variable.** One: the measuring pipette. The station's own broke and a borrowed measure was substituted, then withdrawn.

**Held constant.** The patrons, the herds, the ground, the season, the feed, the acid, the test bottles, the hand tester, the operator and his habits and speed, the sampling routine, the scales, the by-law and the pay basis. Strawn even watched a test through in June and wrote "Nothing here to note."

**Result.** The station average rose from 3.61 to **3.80** and held there for nine weeks, then fell back to 3.61 **on the day** the station's own measure returned. Recorded twice independently: in the tester's book (with a question mark) and in Emmet Brant's private weigh book, where the rise and the fall appear on the patrons' side — "all of us, every farm on the Bottom."

**What it establishes that calculation cannot.** It is an intervention, not an inference. It removes and restores the suspected cause under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and it therefore disposes at a stroke of every explanation that appeals to the land, the hay, the herds, the season or the men, for the sufficient reason that none of those was altered in those nine weeks and the figures were. A calculation from the 1958 measurement can only tell you what short glass *would* have done; the nine weeks show what removing it *did* — in 1911, recorded by a man who did not know what he was recording. Vaught, who computed a great deal, asked readers to weigh it above anything computed in his bulletin.

**What it does not establish.** (i) It does not say *why* the glass was short — mold, wear, manufacture — nor which pieces. (ii) It does not fix the size of the error over twenty-six years; nine weeks of one summer are not a long-run mean, and the 3.80 figure only coincides with the true rate because other evidence (1896, Alder Corners) independently anchors it. (iii) It does not prove the borrowed measure was itself accurate — only that the two measures differed; the inference that the borrowed one was true is drawn from elsewhere. (iv) It does not date the beginning or the end of the fault. (v) By itself it does not refute theft — a thief could in principle have paused — though it makes the theory absurd, since the pause would have to coincide exactly with the breaking and mending of his own pipette; the ledger evidence closes that gap. (vi) It says nothing about whose fault the purchase was. **And note what the interval also proves about the *source* of the borrowed measure: nothing.** Three narrators give three origins (up from town; up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon; left by the state man in June) and the point is unresolved.

### D2 — Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream

**Reason 1 — the fat was never missing (documentary, Ashlin).** The Association's own ledger records, every year from 1898, more butterfat arriving at Larrow Green in the station's cream than was credited to the patrons at the station: 60,800 lb received against 57,760 lb credited, station gain 3,040 lb, "five and a quarter per cent, as usual." Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green **light**. It reached the Green **heavy**.

**Reason 2 — the cream was physically weighed, twice a day, for forty-one years (physical, Larrow Green).** Hobart Sill weighed it onto the platform and again into the cold room, because he never took another man's figure for what went into his churn, and recorded it in his own receiving book: "There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there. Always more. Never less... page after page after page, in my own writing."

**Reason 3 — the nine weeks of 1911 (contemporaneous, at the station and on the farms).** Same man, same bench, same supervision, no change of any kind except a broken pipette — and the figures rose, then fell back the day his own measure returned.

**Reason 4 — the outside assay (independent, another county).** For sixteen summers, 1915–1930, four Hessel Bottom men's milk tested 3.78–3.82 at the Alder Corners Cheese Company over Redlow Ridge, where Keddie never touched it. The same milk, the same ground, the same years, ~0.19 higher than at home.

**Why these are independent.** They are four different kinds of record, kept by four different people with four different interests, in three different buildings and two counties: an accounting entry made in the Association's office by the bookkeeper who later advanced the accusation; a physical double weighing made by a buttermaker who believed the patrons were watering their cans and was hostile to the Hollow; a pair of dated entries in the tester's own book plus a matching pair in a patron's private weigh book; and a cheesemaker's season averages in a factory that had no stake in Ordell County's quarrel at all. None depends on any other, and **none of them depends on the 1958 measurement.** Reasons 1, 2 and 3 were all in existence, in ink, before 1912; reason 4 by 1930. Dell Rundle proved the point from reason 1 alone in 1939 and printed the retraction of his own theft chapter.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question the entries?** Once, and not afterwards. Teague put the station-gain question to Selby Vose in **1916**, was told to let it alone, and let it alone: "I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience." He wrote the same entry for forty years with the words "as usual" in it, and then in 1925 he was the man who put the cream-theft explanation to the board of arbitration — obliquely, answering at length about how such an account would look if a man were helping himself, and then observing that he made no charge against anybody. **He advanced the accusation his own ledger line refuted, and never once put the two together.** Kyle's verdict from the outer desk: "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years."

### D3 — Did the manager know the tests ran low before 1923?

**Yes, beyond any doubt, that the tests ran low — and that is a different question from whether he knew why.**

Evidence he knew the tests ran low: the complaints were made openly from 1902 and renewed at annual meetings in every decade; he was manager from 1913 and the Ostrey Hollow question was the standing grievance of his managership; in **1916** Teague laid the station-gain discrepancy before him personally and was told to let it alone — which requires that he understood there was something to let alone; in **1919** he printed and signed a circular whose entire premise was that the Hollow's figures were low and that added water explained it; Strawn told him to his face that same year that he had tested for water at every visit and never found any; he urged the water theory before the board in 1925; and he defended the whole record in print in 1926. A manager who circulates a warning about the cause of a discrepancy has necessarily noticed the discrepancy.

**Whether he knew the ledger was recording the answer is a separate matter, and the sources point the other way.** Teague insists "He signed the sheet every year... a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him. What he made of it he never told me." But Bertram Kyle, who sat a foot from the door for twenty-five years, is the better witness on the physical act: Vose "read the totals and he never read the lines above them. He was not a man who read. He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign. I watched him do it a thousand times."

**My answer:** he knew the tests ran low, and he had been told in 1916 that the station's account carried a persistent unexplained gain and had chosen not to pursue it — which is culpable inattention at minimum. **Whether he ever grasped that the gain line was the missing fat cannot be determined**, and Kyle's eyewitness account of how he handled paper is the strongest evidence that he did not. Teague's "he had the sheet in front of him" is an inference from a signature, not knowledge of a state of mind, and Teague was not a disinterested reporter — he had been rebuffed by the man he is describing.

### E1(ii) — Cases where the sources cannot settle it

- **How many families received the 1919 circular: nine or eleven.** Nine (Vira Toland, Roy Brant, Rundle, Hazel Brant, and the arithmetic six-Bottom-farms-plus-three-hill-places) against eleven in Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary, written by the wife of the man who carried them to every door. Contemporaneity pulls one way, independent recipients and arithmetic the other. **Unsettled.** Best guess: nine on the Association's list, eleven doors knocked on.
- **What Ansel Keddie believed the cause was.** His own papers say he did not know and would not guess. Frick reports a private corridor remark in 1925 that he had always thought the patrons at fault. Duncan says he came home in the summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass and never dared write it. Orra's diary for 8 May 1911 records only that he was cross about a broken measure. **Unsettled.**
- **Where the borrowed measure of 1911 came from.** Three narrators, three origins. Strawn's own June 1911 entry mentions no loan. **Unsettled.**
- **Whether the short lot was an accident or knowingly sold.** The works books that would answer it burned in 1931. The only account of cause is the seller's own reconstruction, offered as belief. The *Chronicle*'s contrary assertion is inference from silence. **Unsettled.**
- **The Vose–Keddie kinship.** See B4. **Unsettled.**
- **The year Article VII was adopted.** 1891 / 1892 / 1893, the pamphlet undated, the minute book lost in 1938. 1892 best supported; **not settled by any document**, and Falke, writing officially, declined to state it.
- **The station's 1896 intake.** ~900,000 (Sill) vs ~1,100,000 (Kyle), no book behind either. **Unsettled.**

### E1(iii) — Two narrators carrying the same wrong value, and what breaks each tie

1. **$3,500 award** — Vira Toland (1979) and Cleve Oram (1977). *Not independent:* both are Hollow-side oral memories of the same valley figure at half a century's distance. **Broken by** the award text transcribed identically twice, plus the chairman, the manager, the bookkeeper and the clerk.
2. **67,000 lb** — Frick (1946) and Oram (1977). *Not independent:* Frick identifies it as "the patrons' own reckoning," i.e. the claim circulating in the valley, which is where Oram got it. **Broken by** arithmetic and Bulletin 471's 76,000.
3. **$19,600** — Rundle (1939) and the *Chronicle* (1958). Rundle's is the petition's own figure and predates the solution; the *Chronicle* wrongly attributes it to the 1958 bulletin. **Broken by** 76,000 × $0.25 = $19,000, and by the bulletin's own text.
4. **12 July 1923** — Vose (1926) and Rundle (1939). *Not independent:* Vose's printed address was exactly the kind of source Rundle compiled from. **Broken by** Strawn's dated entry and, decisively, by the 28 June order acknowledged in Ammon's 2 July letter.
5. **Eleven pipettes** — Rundle's 1959 footnote and the *Chronicle*. **Broken by** the signed measurement sheet and by Oram, a local witness who handled the crate and says nine.
6. **Five dozen pipettes** — Vaught and Oram. Probably independent errors. **Broken by** the invoice in three transcriptions and by $25.20 ÷ $0.35 = 72.
7. **16.27 c.c.** — Vaught and the *Chronicle*. *Not independent:* the paper worked from the college press release. **Broken by** the measurement sheet and by 16.27's incompatibility with the station's own 3.80 in 1924 — which Vaught himself notices in print.
8. **$77.39** — Junia Ammon and the *Chronicle*. **Broken by** adding the five line items on the invoice each of them reproduces or claims to have seen.
9. **"Six weeks" for 1911** — Teague and Kepp. Possibly one source: Teague used the figure in Ashlin, where Kepp practised, and Kepp names an unidentified gentleman who had pressed the 1911 argument on him. **Broken by** the two dated book entries.
10. **1 May 1898 for the glass** — Vaught and Kyle. Independent (a bulletin and a clerk). **Broken by** Orra Keddie's diary of 1 May 1897 and the April 1897 invoice.
11. **3.85** — Sill (as a guess at the true rate) and Falke (as the post-1924 average). **Broken by** Keddie's book and Strawn's book, both 3.80.
12. **"Nephew"** — Vira Toland, Rundle and Oram; three narrators, one valley rumour. **Broken by** Vose's own "My father."
13. **"First cousin once removed"** — Vira Toland and Oram; again one valley usage. **Broken by** two explicit derivations from inside both families.
14. **"Great-niece"** — the *Chronicle* and Falke. *Not independent:* one press release, one autumn. **Broken by** Falke's own verified correction and Duncan's family notes.
15. **44 million lb** — Teague and Rundle; **3.16** — Teague and Frick. *Not independent:* Teague was the man everyone asked for a number, and both figures are his. **Broken by** the annual sheets (Loomis, Kyle) and the test book's summary leaf. Note the diagnostic pattern: **the documents Teague transcribes are perfect; only his recollected round numbers fail** — and both of them became the valley's official figures.
16. **"Two tenths" for Bulletin 214** — Grigg and Kepp. **Broken by** four identical transcriptions of the bulletin's actual sentence, one fetched from the college library by a man with no stake.

### E1(iv) — Narrators who contradict themselves on the page

- **Junia Ammon (r20):** "The house sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900" — printed a page above an invoice she reproduces entire, dated 14 April 1897, sold to a customer in Ordell County, and a page above her own "a customer of twenty-six years' standing" in 1923. And she prints "Total — 77.93" and then writes "Seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents."
- **Leland Vaught (r11):** every quantity in his introduction and method contradicts the bulletin's concluding paragraph, which he reproduces entire in the same document — and he half-notices, writing that his 118,000 lb "is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the glassware," and publishing anyway. **A reader who trusts his body over his own last paragraph gets the case wrong.**
- **Nyle Grigg (r15):** his 1919 covering letter says the committee sampled "the whole of 1908 — every month of that year... in the barn and on the pasture"; the report reprinted directly beneath his signature says "through January, February and March" — which is also not pasture season in that county. A scrupulous, disinterested man misremembered his own evidence into the shape of his conclusion.
- **Halbert Nym (r07):** Items 3 and 4 carry each other's keepers; his own Item 8 note ("in the hand of Item 3") refutes his Item 3 header; and his Item 3 column list drops the fat column Keddie describes.
- **Selby Vose (r08):** claims the 1924 tests rose "by twenty-one hundredths" to an average of "3.90" — 3.61 + 0.21 = 3.82.
- **Hazel Brant (r17):** opens by disclaiming years altogether, then supplies four (1900, 1899, 1921, 1926), all wrong; and reconciles "nine families, six farms" with "a farm can hold more than one family," when the actual reconciliation is six Bottom farms plus three places on the hill.
- **Cleve Oram (r21):** says five dozen measures "went into that crate to begin with — that was the whole outfit," then explains the nine survivors by loft breakage, when the crate as condemned in 1923 already held only what had survived twenty-six years of daily use.
- **Duncan Keddie (r18):** gives his mother's birth as 1868 and her death in 1948 "aged eighty-two."
- **Orin Falke (r22)** — the honourable version: paragraph 9 concedes the bog hay as a real partial factor; the appended note withdraws it on reading Bulletin 214, and corrects "great-niece" as well. This is self-correction rather than self-contradiction, and it is the single best piece of epistemic conduct in the corpus.

## Section F — Theory

### F1 — What actually produced the shortfall

**The theory in one statement.** Every butterfat test made at Ostrey Hollow between 1 May 1897 and 12 June 1923 was low by exactly one part in twenty, because the pipettes the station drew its charge with delivered 16.72 c.c. against a mark blown at 17.6 c.c. Nothing else was wrong: not the land, not the cows, not the men, not the scales, not the acid, not the operator's method.

**Why that size, and why exactly.** The butterfat test does not weigh anything. A measured charge of milk is drawn to a mark and delivered into a bottle whose graduated neck is ruled to read *percent fat for that charge and no other*. Every mark on the neck means "this much fat, out of the standard charge." If the charge is short by 5 %, then 5 % less fat is liberated, a 5 % shorter column stands in the neck, and the neck — which knows nothing of the substitution — is read off in the ordinary way. **The reading is not merely low; it is low in the exact proportion by which the charge was short.** 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95. Hence 3.61 recorded where 3.80 was true, and hence the error was *proportional, not additive*: the 4.56 herd lost 0.24 of a point and the 3.42 herd 0.18, and no man could know his good cows cost him more than his poor ones.

**Why it began when it did.** On 1 May 1897 the station stopped using the glass Hobart Sill had made up at his own bench and lent them, and started using its own new outfit from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet. 1896, on Sill's borrowed glass, had averaged 3.80 — the true rate. 1897 is a part year. From 1898 the average is 3.61 and stays there. **The anomaly begins precisely at the changeover of the glass**, and Orra Keddie's diary dates that changeover to the day.

**Why it ended when it did.** On 12 June 1923 Merle Strawn ran the station's pipettes against a burette — an instrument the state had only begun issuing to field men in 1921 — and condemned the whole crate. New glass was in service in July; Strawn checked it in October and it delivered its mark; 1924, the first full year on true glass, reads 3.80.

**Why nothing at the station or on the farms had to change for either.** Because the fault was in the instrument that measures, not in anything measured. Three properties made it invisible. It was **proportional**, so the ranking of herds inside the station stayed perfectly orderly and only the comparison with the outside world was wrong. It was **precise** — a scattered error attracts attention, but a pipette that is short every time, in January and in July, for a careful operator and a careless one, "has the appearance of excellent work." And it was **unfalsifiable from inside the plant**: the scales can be checked against test weights, the acid for strength, the bottle's graduation with mercury, the operator's speed by counting — but the pipette is not checked against anything, because in the ordinary practice of the trade **the pipette is the standard**. An instrument that is itself the measure cannot be caught by measuring with it. It can be caught only by a burette brought in from outside, and burettes reached the state's field men very late in this history.

### The three competing explanations, their advocates, and what defeats each

**1. The bog hay of Hessel Bottom thins the cream.** Advanced by the 1908 investigating committee under **Nyle Grigg**, restated by Grigg in his 1919 covering letter, given its most sophisticated form by **Dr. Roland Kepp** in 1926, and conceded (then withdrawn) by county agent **Orin Falke** in 1959. *Defeated by:* (a) **magnitude** — the College's own Bulletin No. 214 found bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the test "by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture"; the gap was 0.19, four times too large, and both Grigg and Kepp quote the bulletin as "two tenths," a fourfold inflation with the summer clause suppressed; (b) **shape** — the gap stood flat in every month of every year, "seventeen hundredths in July and seventeen hundredths in February"; a feed effect cannot be seasonless; (c) **the committee's own record** — it sampled January, February and March 1908 only, and generalized to the year, and Grigg eleven years later remembered doing the very thing that would have tested him; (d) **the external control** — the same milk off the same wet ground tested 3.78–3.82 for sixteen summers at Alder Corners; (e) **the 1911 nine weeks**, in which the hay did not change and the figures did.

**2. The Ostrey Hollow patrons watered their milk.** Advanced by **Selby Vose**, manager — the 1919 circular, his argument before the board in 1925, and his printed defence in 1926. *Defeated by:* (a) Strawn tested with the lactometer at every visit for twenty-nine seasons, never once found added water, and told the manager so in 1919 — four years before Vose was still asserting it; (b) the 1908 committee found "the milk is whole and the scales are honest" and "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter"; (c) Rosalie Cudd's decisive physical argument — watered milk cannot be concealed from a cheesemaker, because the curd comes soft and will not knit and the yield falls the next morning; in sixteen summers her father made no complaint and docked no man a cent; (d) the summer of 1919 itself, when the four accused men's milk tested 3.79–3.82 over the ridge; (e) Bulletin 471: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow."

**3. The tester stole cream at the station.** Advanced obliquely before the board by the bookkeeper **Wilbur Teague**, believed by the valley at large (Vira Toland; the hauler Oram's father), printed and then retracted by **Dell Rundle**. *Defeated by:* (a) the fat was never missing — it reached Larrow Green **heavy**, every year, recorded in the Association's own ledger as "station gain"; (b) Sill physically weighed the excess in twice a day for forty-one years; (c) the nine weeks of 1911; (d) the Alder Corners figures; (e) Rundle's own 1939 retraction from the ledger alone; (f) Bulletin 471: "no cream was stolen there."

### Subsidiary 1 — Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?

**No — and it is worse than that.** The crate had lain in the loft over the intake room since June 1923. Two of the board's three sessions were held in the weigh room directly underneath it, twenty feet below the trap. Effie Loomis, who took down every word of all three sessions, records that **the crate was never mentioned at all** — not by the patrons, not by the officers, not by the board — and that the award's phrase about "the books and appliances of the station" was a form of words the board used and not a list of anything; the appliances then in the room were the *new* glass. Worse still, the written record of the condemnation **was an exhibit**: of only four documents before the board, exhibit 4 was the state dairy inspector's circuit notebook for 1923, which contains the entry "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them." This is confirmed independently from two directions — Frick's exhibit list reprinted from his own file, and Bertram Kyle, who made the list, checked it off twice, and carried the books in and out: "I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked." Frick spent the rest of his life wondering "whether the answer was in that room and simply was not in any of the three stories, and whether a lawyer who understood chemistry might have found it. I did not understand chemistry." It was in that room, in exhibit 4, in the inspector's own hand.

### Subsidiary 2 — What did the tester himself believe the cause was?

**Cannot be determined; three incompatible accounts and no document that settles it.** (i) **His own papers** — the nine-sentence statement and the unsent letter of 1925 — say he could not tell them why the figures were what they were, and that he would not put anything in the place of the question mark he set in 1911 "that I do not know." (ii) **Frick** reports a private, unminuted corridor remark at Ashlin: Keddie told him he had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the room — and Frick is careful to distinguish *accusing*, which Keddie never did, from *suspecting*, which he says he did. (iii) **Duncan Keddie**, writing in 1968, says his father came home in the summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, said it twice, at supper and again by the door, and never dared put it in the book — "he had, after all, nothing to hold in his hand but a suspicion about a piece of glass, and against it stood the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county." (iv) **Orra Keddie's diary for 8 May 1911**, the only contemporaneous record of that day, says merely "A. home cross about a broken measure," and records no suspicion of any kind then or later.

Duncan is a first-hand household witness but is recalling at fifty-seven years and had every reason to want his father to have seen it; Frick is second-hand at twenty-one years and reports a single sentence; Keddie's own papers are documents but are also a public statement made under accusation. **The only thing safely established is what he wrote: a question mark, which he let stand for fourteen years and would not replace with a guess.** That is a fact about his conduct, not about his belief, and it is where the evidence stops.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** A cooperative creamery in the Larrow Valley paid every farmer on a butterfat test, and its by-law made the test at his own station final, with no appeal. At the Ostrey Hollow station the milk read 3.61 where it was truly 3.80 — for twenty-six years, because the pipettes bought in 1897 delivered 16.72 c.c. against a 17.6 c.c. mark, exactly one part in twenty short. The valley blamed the wet hay, then the farmers' water, then the tester, and ruined him in 1925. It took eight records to see it: the by-law, the invoice, nine weeks in 1911 when a borrowed measure was used, the committee reports, the circular, the ledger's own "station gain," the arbitration, and the granddaughter who climbed a ladder in 1958. Each held one piece. Nobody had ever laid them side by side.
