# Retention notes after retellings 1–8 (Ostrey Hollow / Larrow Valley case)

*Written for a fresh reader who has seen none of the source documents. Everything below comes from retellings r01–r08 only. Distractor documents (road commission, playhouse story, drugstore daybook, buoy story, court column, combinatorics paper, sanitarium ledger, ferry API spec) are deliberately excluded — they are unrelated noise.*

---

## 0. THE CASE IN ONE PARAGRAPH (my synthesis, so the thread isn't lost)

A cooperative creamery association in the Larrow Valley ran a skimming station seven miles up the valley at Ostrey Hollow. For roughly a generation the milk tested there came out lower than milk tested at the main creamery. Patrons were paid on butterfat test, so a low test meant low pay. Suspicion fell first on the land (wet bog hay), then on the patrons (watering their cans), then on the tester (stealing cream). A 1925 arbitration found "no cause established" but paid $3,800 anyway; the tester, Ansel Keddie, was dismissed. In 1958 his granddaughter Ivy Keddie measured nine surviving pipettes from a crate condemned by the state inspector in June 1923 and found they delivered 16.72 c.c. where they were blown for 17.6 c.c. — exactly 0.95 of the mark. The glass, not any man, was the cause. Every test at that station read 95% of the truth for 26 years.

## 2. PEOPLE AND RELATIONSHIPS (complete)

### The Keddie family
- **Ansel Keddie** — tester at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station. Went up there in **1895**, the year before it opened, to get it ready (r01); tested that first summer too. Began the test book **4 May 1896**. Tested **twenty-nine seasons, 1896 through 1924**; "did not test a pound in the season of 1925." Put out (dismissed) **30 April 1925** — "the last day of the month." **Died in the thirties** (r01: "He died in the thirties and he was still under it when he died"); r04 gives it precisely: **died 1934**, and had been dead **24 years** when the 1958 measurement sheet cleared him. Self-description: "I am an old man and I have no other trade."
- **Orra Keddie** — Ansel's wife. **Emmet Brant's sister** (stated by both r01 and r03). Also, per r01, **first cousin to old Mrs. Vose** — "everybody knew that and nobody wrote it down anywhere." Outlived Ansel "a good while" and carried the valley's suspicion the whole time. Would walk down to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane.
- **Duncan Keddie** — Ansel's son, Ivy's father; **taught school at Ashlin** (r04).
- **Ivy Keddie** — Ansel's granddaughter. Of the **State Agricultural College at Farland** (r07 Item 5). Found the crate **February 1958**; measured the pipettes **9 March 1958**; delivered the 1959 talk; co-authored the wording of **Bulletin No. 471** with **Professor Vaught**.

### The Brant family
- **Emmet Brant** — "the biggest man on the Bottom," most cows, most hay ground, hauled more milk to the station than anybody (r01); "the station's largest patron in every year of which we have a record" (r02). **Orra Keddie's brother**, hence Ansel Keddie's brother-in-law. The 1919 letter "cost him the most." Before 1919 the Brant and Keddie households ate Sunday dinners together, "the two houses in one kitchen and the men out at the fence after." **After 1919 they never spoke again** — "not at the store, not at a funeral" — and both died without speaking. Ansel's own words: "He and I have not spoken since the circular. That is the plainest fact in this letter and it is the one I find I have the least to say about."
- **Roy Brant** — Emmet Brant's **son**; kept up the Hessel Bottom weigh book himself after his father died; lent it to Ivy Keddie (r04). Per r07 Item 4 provenance, the book passed **to Roy Brant in 1929**, lent to Ivy Keddie in 1958 and returned.
- **Hazel Brant** — r01: the 1958 college researcher was "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed, so she was of the family both sides in a manner of speaking." r07 Item 4: the weigh book went **to Miss Hazel Brant in 1962**, then to the Rooms in 1963. (Note: Vira Toland's "young woman from the college away east in **1958**" is plainly Ivy Keddie, though Vira misplaces the college as "away east" — Farland is the State Agricultural College — and describes her poking about the old station building.)

### The Vose family
- **Gideon Vose** — **first president** of the Association, elected at the organizing meeting in **1889**, held the office **until 1911 — twenty-two years**, longer than any other man. Drafted the by-laws himself; "he was not a lawyer and they read as a farmer wrote them, which is to their credit." **Died 1917** ("written in 1892 by a man who died in 1917 and could not be asked about it afterward" — r02). Kept a herd of **Jerseys** at the home place.
- **Selby Vose** — Gideon's **son** (r02, r08: "My father, who was your first president"). Note r01 says Selby was "old Gideon Vose's **nephew**" — a conflict; see §8. Made **manager in 1913**, served **until 1938** (r02). Signed the 1919 circular. Signed the annual station sheet every year. Inherited and kept his father's Jersey herd; in **1910 that herd tested 4.56**.
- **Old Mrs. Vose** — first cousin to Orra Keddie (r01). The kinship that made the 1919 letter, in Vira's phrase, "not only the Association saying it... it was somebody's cousin saying it, and that is a different thing altogether in a place this size."

### Association staff
- **Effie Loomis** — secretary **1901–1940**, kept the minute book in her own hand throughout. **Fourteen years old in 1889**, therefore born about 1875. Recorded the 1925 award personally on the last day.
- **Wilbur Teague** — bookkeeper, came in **1905**, kept ledgers to **1945** ("forty years by the calendar and a good deal more than that by the wrist"). Invented the station-gain line in 1905. Office at Ashlin: one room over a harness shop, "cold at the north end and never cold enough at the south"; sloped desk with a brass rail across the foot to keep the sheets from sliding; remembers the stiffer paper of the station sheets in March.
- **Hobart Sill** — **buttermaker** at Larrow Green; kept the **churn book and the cream receiving book** (r02).
- Effie Loomis's indictment of all four (herself first): "not one of us ever went up the valley and stood in the Ostrey Hollow station on a working morning to watch the work being done."

### Others
- **Alonzo Frick** — **attorney of Ashlin**; chairman of the 1925 board of arbitration. r05: "as fair a chairman as ever a hearing had." r08: "an able and impartial man."
- **Merle Strawn** — state dairy inspector, Ordell circuit, took it **1907**, laid it down **1936**.
- **Jerome Cudd** — founder of the **Alder Corners Cheese Company**, over **Redlow Ridge** in **Vessey County**.
- **Rosalie Cudd** — **Jerome Cudd's niece**; runs the Alder Corners factory; copied out its test figures for Ivy Keddie in 1958. (r07 Item 13: the Alder Corners book passed "from its founder to his daughter in 1941 and to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970" — a **daughter**, not a niece; see §8.)
- **Professor Vaught** — of the State Agricultural College; the closing paragraph of Bulletin No. 471 is "Professor Vaught's wording and mine together" (Ivy Keddie).
- **Halbert Nym** — curator, 1972.
- **Vira Toland** — narrator of r01; born **1912** at the top end of Ostrey Hollow, in the house her father's father built; lived there always except **two years at Ashlin** when first married, "and I would not count those two years anyhow." Seven years old in 1919. Her father hauled milk to the station; the 1919 letter went to him. She has a **brother** who she believes still has the letter.

## 3. THE INSTITUTION

- **Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association**, organized **1889** at **Larrow Green** by the farmers of the Larrow Valley. Gideon Vose first president.
- **Main creamery built at Larrow Green in 1891**, on **Larrow Creek**; "it is the same building yet" (1941).
- Before the creamery, "the members sold their milk where they could get a price for it and there was no common test and no common price, and every man's arithmetic was his own."
- Three things on paper in Teague's time: **creamery at Larrow Green, office at Ashlin, skimming station at Ostrey Hollow** (seven miles up the valley).
- **County: Ordell County.** Ashlin is the town with the lawyers, the office over the harness shop, and the Historical Rooms. Vessey County is over Redlow Ridge (Alder Corners). Tarnet is where the supply firm Ammon & Sons is.
- Skimming station function: patrons haul milk up rather than drive seven miles down; milk weighed in; a sample taken from each man's can and tested for butterfat; paid on **pounds × test**; the station separates the milk, **sends the cream down the valley to Larrow Green** and **gives the skim back to the farmer** for hogs and calves.
- **Hessel Bottom** — the wet meadow below the hamlet, source of the best milk. **Seven farms had rights on Hessel Bottom**; they cut hay and pastured on it; the largest of the seven was **Emmet Brant's**.
- The station "took from the farms of the wet bottom below it and from **three places on the hill**" (r06, 1909).
- Station building description (r01): at the very top of the Hollow where the road bends and then goes to nothing but the ridge; low, whitewashed, "cold as a spring house inside even in August," smelled of milk and steam and hot iron; you could hear the engine before you came round the last turn.
- After closing it was used for **storage — fence wire and salt** (r01), "odds and ends" in the loft (r06 1928).

## 4. ARTICLE VII — THE GOVERNING BY-LAW

Adopted at the annual meeting of **1892**, the year after the creamery opened. Drafted by Gideon Vose. Quoted **identically** by Effie Loomis (r02 §3) and by the curator (r07 Item 1):

> *Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*

- Effie Loomis's gloss: before it, a man was paid for what his cans weighed — "a hundred pounds of milk was a hundred pounds of milk whosever cow it came out of." After it, he was paid for the fat found by a test made where he delivered. She calls the rule just: "it pays a man for what he actually brings and not for the water that is naturally in it."
- **The hinge**: the last clause, "which is the one nobody quotes" — *the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.* "Not the test of the creamery, not the average of the valley, not a test made over again anywhere else. The station's test, and **no appeal from it**." Effie: "That clause is the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business."
- She notes it "has been misquoted at me across this desk for forty years."
- r07 Item 1: printed pamphlet, **gray wrappers**; **the printed copies carry no year on their face, only the words "adopted at the annual meeting."**

## 6. THE DOCUMENTS, QUOTED

### 6.1 Article VII (r02 §3; r07 Item 1)
> *Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*

### 6.2 The Ammon & Sons invoice, 14 April 1897 (r07 Item 2) — one leaf, folded in three
> *Sold to the Larrow Valley Co-op. Creamery Assn., for the Ostrey Hollow station:*
> *6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20*
> *12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at .22 — 31.68*
> *1 acid measure — .90*
> *1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00*
> *Freight — 2.15*
> *Total — 77.93*

(Six dozen = 72 pipettes bought new. Ivy Keddie in r04 gives the same invoice total as "seventy-seven dollars and ninety-three cents for the whole outfit — pipettes, bottles, acid measure, hand tester and freight together." The firm is **Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, Tarnet**; the crate is stenciled on the end **AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET**.)

### 6.3 First leaf of the station test book (r03, r07 Item 3)
> *Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie.*

### 6.4 The 1922 summary leaf (quoted **identically** in r03, r04 and r07 — three independent transcriptions agreeing)
> *Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80.*

### 6.5 The two 1911 entries in the test book (r03)
> *May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure.*

> *July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.*

Keddie: "That question mark is mine and I have let it stand for fourteen years. I could not answer it then and I cannot answer it now, and I will not put anything in its place that I do not know."

### 6.6 The 1908 committee report (r07 Item 11, fair copy, signed by the chairman)
> *We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March. The milk is whole and the scales are honest. It is our opinion that the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station.*

Curator's note: **the sampling was of January, February and March 1908 only, as the report states** — i.e. winter only, no summer sampling.

### 6.7 Bulletin No. 214 (1911), State Agricultural College (r04, r07 Item 12)
> *Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture.*

### 6.8 Strawn's circuit-book entry, 12 June 1923 (r06; photostat = r07 Item 9)
> *Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk pipettes against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them.*

Strawn's own framing line before it: "**I had carried the burette two seasons before this check.**"

### 6.9 The ledger station-gain entry, 1898 (r05; r07 Item 6 — identical wording in both)
> *Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account.*

Teague: "there is one like it for every year after, in the same terms and in the same hand for as long as the hand was mine."

### 6.10 The award of the board of arbitration, 4 May 1925 (r02 §6; r07 Item 7 — identical)
> *The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account.*

Effie Loomis: "I have written many things into that book with more pleasure. *Finds no cause established*, and then an award of thirty-eight hundred dollars: I took it down as it was given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once."

### 6.11 Ivy Keddie's measurement sheet, 9 March 1958 (r04; r07 Item 14 — identical; typed leaf, figures entered by hand, signed)
> *Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for.*

(The nine individual means, in the order given: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71.)

### 6.12 Bulletin No. 471, October 1958, closing paragraph (r04; r07 Item 15) — Professor Vaught's wording and Ivy Keddie's together
> *No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain.*

### 6.13 Keddie's statement to the board, 17 March 1925 (r03 §I — nine sentences, taken down as given, delivered standing)
> I have kept the test book of this station in my own hand since the station opened, and there is not a figure in it that I did not put there. I have set down the date, the patron, the pounds, the test and the fat, and where I have made an error I have drawn a line through it and put my letters against the line, and I have never written a figure over a figure. I have made the tests in the same order and at the same hour of every working day of every season. I have heard what the patrons say of me and I have heard what the officers say of me. Neither of them has come up that road on a working morning and stood at my elbow and watched the work done. I am asked to account for figures, and the figures are what I read and what I wrote and nothing besides. I am an old man and I have no other trade. I have tested as I was taught and as the state man saw me do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, and I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are. I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself.

### 6.14 Brant farm weigh-book leaf for 1910 (r07 Item 4)
> *Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42.*

### 6.15 The 1919 circular
Never transcribed in full anywhere. What is known of its content: printed, over the manager's signature (Selby Vose), sent to **nine families** of Ostrey Hollow, saying **there was water in the cans coming down from Ostrey Hollow**. Vose's own account (r08): "It warned the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, in temperate language, that added water would be looked for and would be dealt with. It named nobody. It accused nobody. It said that this Association pays for butterfat and cannot pay for creek water, and I would write every word of it again tomorrow."

## 7. THE NUMBERS

### 7.1 The physical fault
- Pipettes **blown on the stem at 17.6 c.c.**; measured mean delivery **16.72 c.c.**; **short 0.88 c.c.**
- 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95 exactly**. Every test read **95 percent of the truth**.
- Recorded average **3.61**; 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80** true. **Gap = 0.19 of a point (nineteen hundredths).**
- Ivy Keddie's crucial insight: **nineteen hundredths is not a fixed quantity.** "Nineteen hundredths is five percent of three point eight. The station did not lose a set number of points. **It lost a twentieth of whatever was there.**"
- **Because the error was proportional it fell hardest on the best milk.** A herd recorded at **4.56 was short 0.24** of a point. A herd recorded at **3.42 was short 0.18**. "The richer the milk, the more was taken from it, and no man had any way of knowing that his good cows cost him more than his poor ones." (Note: 4.56 is exactly Vose's Jersey herd figure for 1910, and 3.42 is exactly the Brant weigh-book figure for 1910 — the two worked examples are the two documented herds.)

### 7.2 The money (Ivy Keddie's chain, r04; corroborated by r07 Item 6 curatorial note)
- **25 full delivery years, 1898 to 1922.**
- Station intake: **40,000,000 lb of milk**, average **1,600,000 lb a year**.
- At recorded 3.61%: patrons credited with **1,444,000 lb of fat**.
- At true 3.80%: they had delivered **1,520,000 lb**.
- Difference: **76,000 lb of butterfat**.
- Butterfat averaged **25 cents a pound** over those years → **$19,000**.
- The 1925 award was **$3,800** — **exactly one fifth of it**.

### 7.3 The two percentages (the deliberate arithmetic trap, addressed in both r04 and r07)
- 76,000 is **five percent** of the fat **actually delivered** (1,520,000).
- 76,000 is **five and a quarter percent** of the fat **credited** (1,444,000).
- Both are right; each is right **of its own denominator and of no other**.
- The Association's ledger line uses the second, "because a bookkeeper naturally reckons a surplus against the figure he has already entered."
- **"But five percent is the one that is also the glass."** (i.e. 1/20 short glass → 5% of the true quantity)
- The specimen 1898 entry proves the ratio: 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26% (of credited); 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.00% (of received).

### 7.4 Effie Loomis's independent figures (r02 §7, from Teague's annual sheets)
- "For the twenty-five years over which the patrons laid their claim, the ledgers show that the Ostrey Hollow station received **forty million pounds of milk**, which is an average of **one million six hundred thousand pounds in a year**. Over the same period the price paid for butterfat averaged **twenty-five cents a pound**."
- She disclaims the cows and vouches only for the copying: "they are Mr. Teague's, out of the annual sheets, and I have added the columns twice."
- **These agree exactly with Ivy Keddie's figures — independent corroboration written 18 years before the solution.**

### 7.5 Other quantities
- Invoice total **$77.93**; six dozen (72) pipettes at $.35 = $25.20; twelve dozen test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at $.22 = $31.68; acid measure $.90; hand tester (4 bottle) $18.00; freight $2.15.
- **Nine pipettes survived unbroken** in the crate; **two of the nine chipped at the tip**.
- Keddie tested **thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons**; samples composited **over ten days**; **four bottles in the hand tester**.
- Vira Toland (r01): Keddie "tested **a thousand cans a week** and never a blot in that book."
- Nine families received the 1919 circular; **seven farms had rights on Hessel Bottom**.
- Strawn's circuit: **fifty-odd plants in four counties**; **twenty-eight pocket books, one to the year, oilcloth covers, pocket size**.
- 1910 Brant delivery: **200,000 lb even**, paid on 3.42.
- Vose's 1924 claim: tests "rose by twenty-one hundredths and the average for 1924 stood at **3.90**."
- Larrow Green creamery's own average (r07 Item 16) runs "a little under two tenths of a point better" than the station's 3.61.

## 8. CONFLICTS, ODDITIES AND ERRORS — the heart of the test

### 8.1 The award figure: $3,500 vs $3,800
- **r01 (Vira Toland, 1979)**: "they paid the patrons **thirty-five hundred dollars** and called it settled. Thirty-five hundred dollars, split up nine ways over all those years — my father's share of it did not buy a hay rake."
- **Every documentary source** — r02 (secretary who recorded it), r04, r05 (bookkeeper who entered it), r07 (minute book transcription), r08 (manager) — says **$3,800**.
- **Verdict:** Vira misremembers by $300 after 54 years. The $3,800 is right.

### 8.2 The year of the hearing: 1919-adjacent vs 1925
- **r01** places the hearing "the winter and spring after" the tests went up, immediately following the 1919 letter in her telling — she compresses 1919 and 1925 into one adjacent sequence, having been seven at the time.
- **All other sources**: the petition was **January 1925** and the board sat **17 March – 4 May 1925**.
- **Verdict:** Vira's sequence is right in shape (the tests rose, then there was a hearing) but her chronology collapses six years. The rise was 1924; the hearing 1925.

### 8.3 The date the glass was condemned: 12 June vs 12 July 1923
- **Strawn's own contemporaneous field book (r06), the photostat (r07 Item 9), Bulletin No. 471, and r07 Item 5**: **12 June 1923**.
- **Selby Vose (r08, 1926)**: "he... condemned the station's testing apparatus on **the twelfth of July, 1923**."
- **Verdict:** Vose is wrong by a month. Note the direction of the error — moving it to July makes the replacement look faster relative to the flush and makes the condemnation look more incidental. Also note the Association's own order was placed **28 June**, which is impossible if the condemnation was 12 July.

### 8.4 Selby Vose: son or nephew of Gideon?
- **r01 (Vira)**: "He was old Gideon Vose's **nephew**."
- **r02 (Effie Loomis)**: "**Selby Vose, son of the late president**, was made manager in 1913."
- **r08 (Selby Vose himself)**: "**My father**, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law..."
- **Verdict:** son. Vira is wrong.

### 8.5 Teague's totals disagree with everyone's
- **Teague (r05)**: "that station took in **forty-four million pounds** of milk" and "the average test of all the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, taken over the whole period... was **3.16**."
- **Everyone else** (Effie Loomis from Teague's own sheets; Ivy Keddie; the curator): **40,000,000 lb** and **3.61**.
- **Note the oddity:** Teague is the bookkeeper — the man whose ledgers everyone else is quoting — yet writing from memory in 1948 he gives a milk total 10% high and an average test 0.45 low. His 3.16 does not correspond to any figure in the case (it is neither the recorded 3.61 nor the true 3.80). His own specimen entry (57,760 / 60,800 / 3,040) is quoted verbatim and correctly by the curator, so his *documents* are sound; his recollected round numbers are not. **He explicitly says the figures "are asked for" and gives them over "the years the account ran," a longer and vaguer span than the 25 years 1898–1922 used by the others**, which may partly explain the 44 million.
- **Also**: Teague says of the 1911 break that "they made shift with a borrowed one **for six weeks**." The test book says **8 May to 10 July = nine weeks**. Every other source says nine weeks.
- **Also**: Teague says the borrowed measure came "up from town"; Ivy Keddie says "**The measure they borrowed in 1911 came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon.**" (Consistent in spirit; Larrow Green is down-valley from the station, so "up from town" and "up from Larrow Green" agree.)

### 8.6 The curator's catalogue errors (r07) — at least three, and they matter
1. **Item 3, "Station test book, kept by E. Brant."** The book's first leaf is signed **A. Keddie** and every other source says Ansel Keddie kept it in his own hand for twenty-nine seasons. The header is wrong. (**E. Brant** = Emmet Brant, the largest patron — the two names have been transposed between Items 3 and 4.)
2. **Item 4, "Farm weigh book, kept by A. Keddie."** This is the **Hessel Bottom** book, kept by one household from 1 January 1897, passing to **Roy Brant** in 1929 and **Hazel Brant** in 1962 — i.e. the **Brant** weigh book, exactly as r04 describes it ("The Brant weigh book, kept at Hessel Bottom from 1897 onward, was lent to me by Roy Brant, who is Emmet Brant's son"). The header is wrong. **The two attributions in Items 3 and 4 are swapped.**
3. **Item 5, crate provenance**: the curator says the crate "was found **in the Association office at Ashlin**, in February 1958, by Miss Ivy Keddie." Ivy Keddie's own account (r04) is explicit and circumstantial: "In February of last year I went **up the Hollow to the station building**... There is a **loft over what was the intake room**, reached by a ladder, and **under a tarpaulin at the back of it** there was a wooden crate..." Corroborated by Strawn's 1928 entry about the loft being "full of odds and ends" and "full since before the war," and by Ivy's "It had been carried up there in June of 1923 and it was there still." **The curator has the wrong building.**
4. **Item 13** says the Alder Corners test book "passed from its founder to his **daughter** in 1941"; r04 says Rosalie Cudd, who runs the factory, is **Jerome Cudd's niece**. Possible but unresolved conflict (a daughter in 1941 and a niece running it in 1958 are not strictly incompatible — but the natural reading conflicts).
5. **Item 3's column list** ("columns for date, patron, pounds and test" — four) conflicts with Keddie's own "**The book I kept in five columns: the date, the patron, the pounds, the test, the fat**" and his statement ("the date, the patron, the pounds, the test and the fat"). The curator drops the fat column.

### 8.7 The crate was never mentioned at the hearing — Effie Loomis's central observation
r02 §8: "The second is the crate. **The crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all.** I took every word down and it is not there. I mean the crate of measures that the state's inspector condemned up at the station in June of 1923, which was set aside and new ones ordered in its place. **Not a word of it was spoken in any of the three sessions**, by the patrons, by the officers, or by the board, and the award's phrase about '**the books and appliances of the station**' is a form of words the board used and not a list of anything. Forty years of taking down what men say has taught me that the interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying."

This is the pivot of the whole case: the physical evidence sat condemned in a loft twenty-one months before the hearing, and nobody at the hearing mentioned it.

### 8.8 "Finds no cause established" + $3,800 — the contradiction everyone notices
- Effie Loomis: "it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once."
- Ansel Keddie: "They found no cause established and they put me out on the last day of the month, and **both of those things are true at once**, and I have given up trying to hold them in my head together."
- Selby Vose spins it the other way: "a **generous settlement of an unproved claim**... There is not a business house in Ordell County that would have done it. We did it because we are not a business house."
- Ivy Keddie: "$3,800... **That is a fifth of it.**"

### 8.9 The 1924 rise — four incompatible explanations
| Explainer | Cause claimed |
|---|---|
| **Vira Toland (r01)** | Keddie was taking the cream; they caught him and put him out, "and that is why the numbers went right the summer after" |
| **Selby Vose (r08)** | Patrons were watering their cans and stopped when the Association began to look: "The tests came up when the looking began" |
| **Wilbur Teague (r05)** | Doesn't claim the rise directly but argues one man alone with a separator will eventually take a little |
| **Ivy Keddie (r04)** | The condemned glass was replaced in July 1923; the new pipettes deliver their mark; 1924 is the first full year on true glass |

**The decisive chronology against Vira and Vose:** the tests rose in **1924**. Keddie was not dismissed until **April 1925** — after the rise, not before. The petition was not filed until **January 1925**. The new glass went in **July 1923**. Only the glass precedes the rise. Vira's causal order is backwards, and Vose's "the tests came up when the looking began" is contradicted by his own dating: the looking (petition, hearing) came after the rise.

Vose also asserts "the tests at that station rose by twenty-one hundredths and the average for 1924 stood at **3.90**." Everyone else — Keddie's own book, Strawn's field book — gives **3.80** for 1924, a rise of nineteen hundredths from 3.61. **Vose has inflated both the level and the rise by a tenth of a point.** (3.61 + 0.21 = 3.82, which is not 3.90 either — his own arithmetic does not close.)

### 8.10 The 1911 natural experiment — the strongest single piece of evidence
Ivy Keddie's framing: "For nine weeks in 1911 there was an experiment at that station, and nobody knew it was one... Same patrons, same herds, same pasture, same season, same acid, same bottles, same man at the crank — **one object changed** — and the average went to 3.80 and came back to 3.61 the day the station's own measure returned. **No explanation of this valley's twenty-six-year anomaly survives those nine weeks except the one I have given you.**"

Note that Keddie recorded this anomaly himself in 1911, flagged it with a question mark, let the question mark stand fourteen years, and still could not explain it in 1925 — **the answer was in his own book the whole time**.

Note also Strawn visited in **June 1911** — during the borrowed-measure window — and wrote "Watched a test through. Nothing here to note." He would have been watching a test made with the *good* borrowed glass.

### 8.11 The bog-hay theory — why it fails, in three ways (r04)
1. **Magnitude:** Bulletin 214 gives at most 0.05 in winter; the gap was 0.19 flat. "**It is four times too small.**"
2. **Shape:** the hay effect is winter-only and unmeasurable on summer pasture; the gap "stood flat in every month of every year, **July and February alike**." "**It is the wrong shape.**" Corroborated by Keddie: "They did not come and go with the season. The gap was there in July as it was there in February, in the flush of the grass and in the dead of the winter on dry hay, and it was the same in a wet year as in a dry one."
3. **External control:** over the ridge, the **Alder Corners factory** in Vessey County tested the milk of **four Hessel Bottom men** in their summer flush; where the two books overlap — **fourteen seasons** — "that same milk off that same wet ground ran about **nineteen hundredths higher at Alder Corners than at home**." The same milk, tested elsewhere, tested 0.19 higher. (r07 Item 13 dates the copied Alder Corners figures to the **season of 1919** specifically.)
4. Additional weakness: the 1908 committee sampled **only January, February and March** — winter only — and generalized to the whole year.

### 8.12 The water theory — why it fails
- **Merle Strawn tested for added water with a lactometer on every single visit across 29 years and never found any.** His 1919 entry: "The manager of the Association has sent a circular round to the patrons of this station about water in the cans. **I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so.**" The 1923 entry says "Lactometer, no added water, **as always here**."
- Vose sent the circular anyway in 1919 and was still defending the theory in 1926, three years after being told by the state inspector that there was no water.
- The 1908 committee also found "The milk is whole and the scales are honest."
- Bulletin No. 471: "**No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow.**"
- Logical point (implicit): watering dilutes everything, and the shortfall was strictly proportional and identical across wet and dry years, rich and poor herds.

### 8.13 The cream-theft theory — why it fails
- **Bulletin No. 471: "no cream was stolen there."**
- The missing fat is **not missing**. It arrived at Larrow Green. Teague's own ledger line records it arriving, year after year: 60,800 lb received against 57,760 lb credited. **Nobody stole it; the Association banked it as "station gain."** This is the quiet horror of the case — the difference was in the Association's own books, in ink, dated, every year, described as a gain.
- The theft theory also cannot explain the 1911 nine weeks (why would a thief stop for exactly the period his own pipette was broken?) or the proportionality (why would a thief take exactly 5% of whatever was there, from every patron, every day, for 26 years, and never more in a flush year?).
- Keddie's own denial: "I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself."
- Vira Toland's contrary certainty: "**He was taking the cream. They caught him at it and they put him out**... it is not that he is a wicked man, it is that **nobody ever came up that road and looked**." Her father's softer version: "Ansel Keddie was an honest man who had got into something, which is a nice way of saying it and I never saw the difference."

### 8.14 The structural failure — everyone names it, nobody acted
- **Effie Loomis (r02 §4)**: of herself, Teague, Sill and Vose — "**not one of us ever went up the valley and stood in the Ostrey Hollow station on a working morning to watch the work being done. I include myself first in that.**"
- **Ansel Keddie (r03, statement)**: "**Neither of them has come up that road on a working morning and stood at my elbow and watched the work done.**"
- **Vira Toland (r01)**: "**nobody ever came up that road and looked.**"
- Three narrators, three decades apart, on three sides of the quarrel, produce the same sentence.
- **Teague's version of the failure** (r05): "**Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons.** One man weighed the milk, one man drew the sample, one man made the test, one man wrote the figure that the pay slip was made from, and one man ran the separator. There was no second man in that building from the spring to the fall... **The remedy is two men or a lock, and the Association had neither.**" — a correct diagnosis of a control weakness that happened to be the wrong explanation of this particular loss.
- **Teague's evenness insight** (r05): the gain "ran at five and a quarter percent... so evenly that the words '**as usual**' got into the entry and stayed there, and a man may as well be honest about what evenness does to a bookkeeper: **it stops him looking. A figure that jumps about will keep you awake. A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture.**"
- **Teague's 1916 escalation and retreat**: "I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone. I let it alone. **I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience**, and a bookkeeper who takes his employer's business up a second time after being told once has generally begun to look for another employer." He also insists **Vose knew**: "**He signed the sheet every year.** I laid the annual sheet before him each spring and he put his name at the foot of it, and a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him. What he made of it he never told me."
- **Strawn's own missed chance**: he had the burette from **1921**, **skipped the check in 1922** ("the day being short and the road home being what it was, and I have put that down because it is what happened"), and caught it in **June 1923**. Had he run the glass in 1921 the case ends two years earlier. His honesty in recording the omission is itself notable. And the **burette program was discontinued in 1926** — the instrument that solved a twenty-six-year fraud-that-wasn't was withdrawn three years after it worked.

### 8.15 Smaller oddities worth keeping
- **The station's first season (4 May – end of 1896) has no intake figure anywhere.** r07 notes and queries: "the station column begins with **1897**, and inquirers wanting a figure for 1896 must be told **there is none**."
- **1896 tested 3.80** — the true rate — because the station was using **glass borrowed from Larrow Green** until 1 May 1897. The drop to 3.61 in 1898 coincides exactly with the arrival of the station's own bad glass. (1897 is "part year" — the transition year — and so no average is given. This is a beautiful, quiet confirmation buried in the summary leaf: **the anomaly begins precisely when the Ammon & Sons glass goes into service.**)
- **Bulletin 471's window is 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923** — exactly from the day the station's own glass went into use to the day it was condemned.
- **The lost minute book**: the **1889–1892** minute book was lost in the **1938 office move**. Effie Loomis: "It was not thrown out; it was carried out with the rest and it did not arrive, and I have looked in every place a book can be in this building... **anybody who tells you what was resolved at the first four annual meetings is telling you a story he had from his father.**" Consequence: **Article VII's adoption year (1892) rests on her word, not on a surviving minute** — and the printed by-laws pamphlet carries **no year on its face** (r07 Item 1).
- **Three explanations were argued before the 1925 board**, and Effie Loomis declined to rehearse them: "None of the three was accepted and I shall not rehearse them here; they are in the minutes and the minutes are in the safe." (Presumably: bog hay, watering, and theft — but she does not say.)
- **Five sets of records had never been in one room before 1958** (r04): the station test book, the Brant weigh book, the Alder Corners figures, Strawn's circuit notebooks, and the Association's annual ledgers. Ivy Keddie has **never seen** the Alder Corners book itself — the figures were copied for her.
- **Strawn's four-step routine at every plant, always in the same order** "so that I would not have to remember which of them I had done": (1) scales against sealed weights, intake and cream, loaded light and loaded heavy; (2) lactometer on the day's milk, patron by patron where there was any question, for added water; (3) boiler, water supply and cold room — pressure, gauge, cased well, whether the room would hold overnight; (4) "**the one most men grudged me**" — stand at the tester's elbow and watch a test through from sample to reading, "and I did not let him hurry it." **His routine had no step for checking the glass until the burette arrived in 1921.**
- **Strawn's creed**: "The state pays a field man to weigh and to look, and every time in my life I have seen a field man paid for an opinion instead, the opinion has outlived the weighing and done harm after the man was dead." And: "**I never had a theory about a plant and I do not recommend having one. A theory is a thing a man carries out to the platform with him and it gets in the way of the weights.**"
- **The letters kept for forty years** (r01): Vira's father read the 1919 letter "with his hat still on, which he never did"; her mother stood at the stove with her back to him and did not turn around; he read it twice, put it in the desk drawer in the front room, and it was still there when he died. One woman kept hers **pinned inside the lid of her trunk**. Vira: "You can tell people a thing like that is only business and they will still keep the paper forty years to prove what was said of them."
- **Vira's closing image**: "It rained the day of that letter, I remember the wet on my father's shoulders when he came in, and I have never in sixty years been able to think about the one without the other."
- **Ivy Keddie's disclaimer and close**: "nothing I am going to show you tonight depends in the smallest degree on my opinion of my grandfather. **It depends on nine pieces of glass and a burette.**" And: "**I have no remedy to propose. I have only the crate, and it is on the table, and you may look into it on your way out.**"
- **Vose's peroration**: "Bring it as it left the cow. Wash the cans, cool the milk, keep the dog out of the milk house, and bring it as it left the cow." He asks for "one more" year of patience after thirteen. (He in fact served to 1938 — twelve more.)
- **Vose's Jersey argument**: he cites the 1910 herd test of 4.56 not to boast but to refute the trade's claim "that the Larrow Valley is poor milk country, that our land is sour and our cattle are indifferent." Irony: **4.56 is the exact figure Ivy Keddie uses to show that the richest milk was robbed hardest** (short 0.24 of a point).
- **Keddie's method, in his own words** (r03, the fullest technical account in the case): day began at **half past five**; wagons came up in the dark most of the year; intake room open, lamps lit, scale swinging free before the first wagon. Every can weighed, the weight called and written **before the milk went anywhere else**. Scale tested by the state's man and true — "I have never in twenty-nine seasons had a patron dispute a weight with me and be right." A sample taken from each patron's milk **as it was weighed**, into **his own jar, numbered to him and to nobody else**; the jar **made up over ten days** before testing. On testing morning the jar warmed in water **until blood-warm right through**, then poured vessel to vessel and poured again "until the cream that had risen was carried down through the whole of it. **A sample that is not warmed and mixed is worth nothing and a boy could tell you so.**" Then the charge: milk drawn up in the measure **to the mark on the stem**, the tip wiped, the charge blown out into the bottle carrying the patron's number. Then the acid **poured down the side**, the bottle turned until the milk was dark. **Four bottles in the hand tester, weighted against each other**, cover down, crank turned "at the speed I was taught to turn it and no faster, and no man ever stood beside me and made me hurry it." Then the fat stands up in the neck **in a clear column**, read off in per cent **against the rulings, with a pair of dividers**, and written down. "That is the whole of it. **It is not a hard trade. It is only a trade that must be done the same way every time, which is a harder thing than it sounds.**"
- **The fatal detail inside that method**: the charge is drawn "to the mark on the stem" and the bottle neck "is ruled to read percent for that charge and no other" (Ivy Keddie). Keddie's technique was flawless; the mark itself was a lie. He did everything right with a wrong instrument, which is why watching him work — as Strawn did repeatedly — revealed nothing.
- **Error-correction discipline**: "where I have made an error I have drawn a line through it and put my letters against the line, and **I have never written a figure over a figure**... because a figure written over another figure is a figure no man can swear to afterward."
- **The station book's provenance** (r07 Item 3): kept at the station from 4 May 1896; **impounded by the board of arbitration in March 1925**; in the Association's office at Ashlin 1925–1959; received at the Rooms 1959.
- **Item 8**: Keddie's signed statement to the board, one leaf, ruled, both sides, "**in the hand of Item 3**" — i.e. the curator's own physical evidence that the test book and the statement are the same hand, which quietly refutes his own "kept by E. Brant" header.
- **Item 16**: the Larrow Green receiving and churn books are **water-damaged at the foot**.
- **Curator's outstanding tasks**: "Item 5 wants a case with a lock. Accession numbers still to be cut for Items 13 and 16."
- **Effie Loomis on the Association's worth**: "it began as a plain remedy for a plain grievance, and I have never heard anybody in this valley say we should have been better without it."
- **Vira on the Vose family**: "I think the family always thought the valley belonged to them a little."
- **Vira's opening refusal**: "I could name you every one of them, but you don't want names, you want the trouble, **they always want the trouble**."
- **The nine hauling families** of Ostrey Hollow are the same nine that received the 1919 circular and the same nine among whom the $3,800 (Vira: $3,500) was divided.

## 9. WHAT A FRESH READER SHOULD CARRY FORWARD

1. **The answer is the glass**: pipettes short by exactly 1 part in 20, in service 1 May 1897 – 12 June 1923.
2. **The three false theories** (bog hay, watered milk, stolen cream) each have a specific, documented refutation: Bulletin 214 plus the Alder Corners cross-check; Strawn's lactometer on every visit; the ledger's own station-gain line showing the fat arrived.
3. **The 1911 nine weeks** (8 May – 10 July) are the natural experiment that settles it, and were recorded, flagged with a question mark, and ignored for 47 years.
4. **Every witness who was in a position to look, didn't** — and three of them say so in nearly identical words.
5. **The unreliable narrators and their specific errors**: Vira Toland ($3,500 for $3,800; nephew for son; hearing compressed to 1919; certain of theft). Selby Vose (12 July for 12 June; 3.90 and "twenty-one hundredths" for 3.80 and nineteen; causal order backwards). Wilbur Teague (44 million for 40; 3.16 for 3.61; six weeks for nine). Halbert Nym (Items 3 and 4 attributions swapped; crate found at Ashlin instead of the station loft; four columns for five).
6. **The most reliable sources**: Strawn's contemporaneous field books; the transcribed documents themselves (which agree word-for-word across r02/r03/r04/r05/r07); Effie Loomis on what the record does and does not contain.
7. **Two percentages, both correct**: 5% of fat delivered = 5.25% of fat credited = 76,000 lb = $19,000. The award was $3,800, one fifth.
