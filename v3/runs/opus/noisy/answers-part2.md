### Story 5 — The Circular of 1919 and the Two Kitchens

**Who.** Selby Vose, manager of the Association 1913–1938; Bertram Kyle, the clerk who typed it; Ansel Keddie, who carried it; Emmet Brant, his brother-in-law; the hauling families of Ostrey Hollow; Merle Strawn; Jerome and Rosalie Cudd, over Redlow Ridge.

**What and when.** In **June 1919** a printed circular went out over the manager's signature warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons that added water would be looked for and would be dealt with. Kyle typed it and remembers typing it, and thought at the time it was a hard thing to send to men who were bringing their milk in every morning of their lives. It named nobody and accused everybody.

**Ansel Keddie delivered it himself.** Orra Keddie's diary: he would not put them in the mail nor leave them on the platform; he took them round himself, stood at the door while each man read it, and came home at eleven at night with his hat still on.

**How many families?** Vira Toland, Roy Brant, Rundle and Hazel Brant all say **nine**; Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary says **eleven**. I cannot settle it. Nine has the arithmetic behind it — six farms on the Bottom plus the "three places on the hill" Strawn recorded in 1909 — and two recipients among its sources; eleven has contemporaneity and the wife of the man who walked to every door. The likeliest reconciliation, and it is a guess, is that nine patron families were on the Association's list and Ansel knocked on eleven doors, a farm being able to hold more than one household.

**The consequence.** Two days later Emmet Brant came up, would not come in, stood in the yard and asked Ansel why he had carried the paper himself if he did not believe it. Ansel said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. **They never spoke again — ten years, ended only by Emmet's death in 1929.** Before it the two households had eaten Sunday dinners together for thirty years. Roy Brant's later account gives the grievance in summary form (his father held that the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not); Orra's is contemporaneous, specific, and preferred where the two differ. Ansel's own words: "That is the plainest fact in this letter and it is the one I find I have the least to say about."

Across the valley the letters were kept. Vira Toland's father read his with his hat still on, put it in the desk drawer, and it was there when he died; one woman pinned hers inside the lid of her trunk. "The two ends of this valley did not speak civilly to one another for six years."

**Why the theory was false, and known to be.** Strawn's 1919 entry: *"I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so."* He tested with the lactometer at every visit across twenty-nine seasons and never once found added water; his 1923 entry says "no added water, **as always here**." The 1908 committee had already found "the milk is whole." Vose sent the circular anyway, urged water before the board in 1925 "without heat and without proof," and was still defending it in print in 1926.

**The independent assay.** From **1915 to 1930 — sixteen summers — four of the six Hessel Bottom men hauled over Redlow Ridge to the Alder Corners Cheese Company** in Vessey County during the flush, roughly 1 June to mid-September, and came back down to Ostrey Hollow the rest of the year (the ridge shuts in November and does not open before April; nobody hauled it in winter). Rosalie Cudd's appended table gives all sixteen season averages: every one between **3.78 and 3.82**, none smoothed, none thrown out. Her father's book entry for the summer of **1919 itself** — the very season of the circular — reads: *"The four Hessel Bottom men, summer of 1919: 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge."* And her own argument, which no test can improve on: watered milk cannot be hidden from a cheesemaker, because the curd comes soft and will not knit and the yield is down the next morning. "Butter is more forgiving that way. **Cheese is a witness.**" In sixteen summers her father never made one complaint of those four men and never docked one of them a cent.

**Documents.** The circular (never transcribed in full); Orra Keddie's diary, June 1919; Strawn's circuit books; Jerome Cudd's book entry; Rosalie Cudd's sixteen-season table (1958 letter); Vose's printed annual address (1926); Kyle's recollection (1953).

### Story 6 — Station Gain: The Answer Written in Ink Every Year in the Association's Own Books

**Who.** Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945; Bertram Kyle, outer-desk clerk for twenty-five years; Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green 1893–1934; Selby Vose; Dell Rundle; and, on the other side, Cleve Oram's father, who drove the cream wagon.

**What and when.** Teague invented the station-gain line in 1905. The specimen entry for **1898** is transcribed identically by three independent narrators (Teague, the curator, and Rundle's in-press note):

> *Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account.*

"There is one like it for every year after, in the same terms and in the same hand." Kyle copied that line, or the like of it, more times than he can count, and never once wondered at it: **"A figure with a name on it has been explained, and you rule under it and go on to the next."**

**The fat was never missing.** It arrived at Larrow Green, every year, heavy. Hobart Sill weighed it in himself — the platform man weighed it and Sill weighed it again into the cold room, because he never in his life took another man's figure for what was going into his churn — for **forty-one years**: "There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there. Always more. Never less... page after page after page, in my own writing." His explanation was "that is cream for you," and his own summary of the failure is the case's motto in miniature: **"I never made anything of it, and I will say plain, nobody ever asked me to."**

**One man asked, once.** Teague put the station-gain question to Vose in **1916** and was told to let it alone, and let it alone: "I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience." He insists Vose knew: "He signed the sheet every year. I laid the annual sheet before him each spring and he put his name at the foot of it." Kyle, from the outer desk, corroborates and qualifies it: Vose "read the totals and he never read the lines above them... He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign. I watched him do it a thousand times."

**And one man worked it out.** In 1939, while his jubilee pamphlet was being set in type, Dell Rundle was allowed to look at the old ledgers on an unrelated errand, saw the station-gain line, and printed a retraction of his own theft chapter: *"Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand."* A schoolteacher with no stake refuted the valley's twenty-year certainty nineteen years before the measurement — and nobody noticed; the valley was still telling Vira Toland the theft story in 1979.

**Against all this** stands one honest dissent: Cleve Oram's father, who hauled that cream for better than twenty years and always said there was less coming off the platform than went onto it. He said it in the village and never under oath, and he was not asked. It is an impression; the receiving book is a measurement, taken twice a day for forty-one years, and it goes the other way.

**The pattern.** Teague on the ledger — "a figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture. It stops him looking." Sill on the platform — "so does everybody's." Vaught in the laboratory — "it has the appearance of excellent work." Three men, three vantage points, one mechanism: **consistency reads as correctness.**

**Documents.** The Association's general ledger and station accounts, 1898 onward; the Larrow Green receiving and churn books (water-damaged at the foot, at the Rooms as Item 16); Rundle's *Note added while in press* (1939); Teague's memoir chapter (1948); Kyle's account (1953).

### Story 7 — The Board of Arbitration and the Ruin of Ansel Keddie (1925–1934)

**Who.** Alonzo Frick, attorney of Ashlin and chairman, chosen "because I was the one of the three who kept no cow"; Ansel Keddie; Selby Vose; Wilbur Teague; Effie Loomis, taking every word down; Bertram Kyle, who carried the books in.

**What and when.** The Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned in **January 1925**, Emmet Brant's name on the paper. The board convened at Ashlin on **17 March 1925**; the **second and third sessions were held at the station itself, in the weigh room**, at Frick's request, so the board could see the plant — confirmed independently by Frick, by Orra Keddie's diary, and by Cleve Oram, who stood outside on the platform in the rain and could hear the voices but not the words.

Keddie gave a **nine-sentence statement, standing**: he had kept every figure himself, never written a figure over a figure, made the tests in the same order at the same hour of every working day, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons; "Neither of them has come up that road on a working morning and stood at my elbow and watched the work done... I am an old man and I have no other trade... I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station."

**Four exhibits, and no more** — the list confirmed from two independent directions, Frick's own file and Kyle, who made it and checked it off twice: (1) the station test book, 4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924, in Keddie's hand; (2) the station's receiving book; (3) Emmet Brant's private weigh book, lent and wanted back; (4) **the state dairy inspector's circuit notebook for the year 1923** — the book containing the entry of 12 June 1923 condemning the whole crate. Kyle: "I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked."

**Three explanations were urged and none proved:** water, by the manager Vose; cream theft, obliquely by the bookkeeper Teague, in the way an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it, ending with the observation that he made no charge against anybody; and the hay, by somebody reading the 1908 report into the record. Frick: "the three did not sit easily together and nobody seemed to mind that they did not."

**The crate was never mentioned in any of the three sessions.** Loomis took down every word and it is not there.

**The outcome.** Keddie was put out on **30 April 1925**, four days before the award, while the board was still sitting. Orra's diary: "Twenty-nine seasons and a letter. He came up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm and hung it behind the shed door and asked what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it." On **4 May** the board rose and Loomis entered the award: *"finds no cause established... awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account."* Frick: "An award without a finding is an unsatisfactory thing and every member of that board knew it... an honest man was ruined by a figure nobody could explain, and we paid his neighbors money without being able to tell them what they were being paid for."

**Afterwards.** Keddie did not test a pound in 1925. He kept four or five cows on ground too small for them and turned carpenter — the cow shed, the Loomis gate, half the doors in the hollow, "a carpenter at sixty-six," at a quarter of the pay, whistling at work, which he never did when he was paid. He **died in February 1934**, nine years under it. There were more at the burying than his widow looked for, and men shook her hand who had not spoken to him in nine years, and every one of them said what a hand he was with a door, and not one said the other thing. He had told her once: **"a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."**

**Documents.** The award (identical transcriptions by Loomis and the curator); Keddie's statement and his unsent letter of spring 1925; Frick's exhibit list (1946); Kyle's account (1953); Orra Keddie's diary, January–May 1925 and the loose leaf of February 1934.

### Story 8 — The Granddaughter, the Ladder, and Bulletin No. 471 (1958–1970)

**Who.** Ivy Keddie, born 1931, Duncan's daughter and Ansel's granddaughter, graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland; Professor Leland Vaught; Roy and Hazel Brant; Rosalie Cudd; Halbert Nym, curator; Orin Falke; the *Ashlin Chronicle*.

**What and when.** In **February 1958** Ivy Keddie went up the Hollow to the station building, climbed the ladder into the loft over the intake room, and found under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff the wooden crate stenciled AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET. It had been carried up there in June 1923 and it was there still. (The curator's accession note places the find "in the Association office at Ashlin" and is wrong; Ivy's own account, Strawn's 1928 note that the loft had been "full since before the war," Rundle's description of the building, and Cleve Oram, who had put his hand on the crate himself, all put it in the loft.)

On **9 March 1958** she measured the **nine surviving pipettes** against a calibrated burette at Farland, five deliveries each, and signed the sheet:

> *Nine pipettes... stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for.*

She then brought together **five sets of records that had never been in one room**: the station test book; the Brant weigh book, **lent** by Roy Brant and returned (Hazel is emphatic on the word: "he lent it. He did not leave it to her"); Rosalie Cudd's sixteen Alder Corners seasons, copied out and posted on a four-line request; Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook, found in the college library where his books had lain unasked-for since 1947; and the Association's ledgers.

**Bulletin No. 471, October 1958**, by Vaught and Keddie, closes: *"No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."* Vaught's own introduction contradicts that conclusion in every quantity (16.27 c.c., 0.9244, 3.905, 0.295, 118,000 lb, five dozen, 1 May 1898), notices that his figure "is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80" — and publishes anyway.

**Reception.** The *Ashlin Chronicle*'s front page of 3 October 1958 got the ladder and the glass right and nearly every particular wrong. Falke's 1959 county report is the model of the opposite habit: it conceded the hay and then withdrew the concession on reading Bulletin 214, and corrected "great-niece" to granddaughter in the same note. Ivy's 1959 talk to the Historical Society, with the crate on the table: "nothing I am going to show you tonight depends in the smallest degree on my opinion of my grandfather. **It depends on nine pieces of glass and a burette.**"

**Where everything went.** Test book and crate to the Ordell County Historical Rooms, 1959; the Brant weigh book to Hazel on Roy's death in 1962 and to the Rooms in 1963; Strawn's notebooks at Farland; the Alder Corners book to the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970. Duncan Keddie set the family straight on paper in 1968.

**The arithmetic of the delay.** Glass condemned 1923, cause found 1958 — thirty-five years. Grandfather dismissed 1925 — thirty-three. Dead 1934 — twenty-four years dead when the sheet was read that cleared him.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** The line is Ansel + Orra → Duncan (born 1894, schoolteacher at Ashlin) → Ivy (born 1931). Duncan states it himself in the 1968 family notes and says why he is stating it: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise." It is corroborated by Ivy's own talk, by the curator, by Roy Brant and by Rosalie Cudd. Pointing elsewhere: the *Ashlin Chronicle* (1958) and Orin Falke's county report both printed **great-niece**. These are not two sources but one — both worked from the college press release in the same autumn — and Falke, on checking, corrected himself in an appended note: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." A source that corrects itself against an outside check outranks the same source uncorrected, and outranks a weekly that never checked.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins.** Derivation: Orra Keddie and Emmet Brant were sister and brother; their children, Duncan Keddie and Roy Brant, are therefore first cousins; the children of first cousins — Ivy and Hazel — are second cousins. The derivation is performed independently and in the same terms by both families: by Duncan in 1968 ("Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece, which people say because it is shorter") and by Hazel in 1981 ("People are forever telling me she is my niece or my cousin once removed... It is not complicated; it is only that nobody wants to count"). Rundle's 1959 footnote says second cousins too. Against: Vira Toland and Cleve Oram both say "first cousin once removed" — village usage from outside either household, and the two are not independent of the valley's common talk. **What the sources leave open about the intervening generations:** whether Orra and Emmet were **full or half siblings**. Roy Brant insists full and specifically forestalls the alternative ("whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it"); Duncan and Orra herself say only "my brother" / "Emmet Brant's sister"; Hazel says half-sister by her great-grandfather's second wife. I take full siblings as better supported — Roy names and rejects Hazel's version in advance, and Orra's diary has her sitting in "that kitchen where I was a girl" and her husband naming her mother's hollyhocks — but it is not settled, and **it does not change the degree**: the children of half-siblings are still first cousins and their children still second cousins.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose**, the Association's organizer, its first president 1889–1911, and the author of the by-laws including Article VII. Four sources say son: Vose himself in his printed annual address ("My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law"), Effie Loomis's minute-based note, Duncan Keddie's family notes, and Bertram Kyle, who sat outside his door for twenty-five years and calls the nephew story nonsense. Three say nephew — Vira Toland, Rundle and Cleve Oram — all repeating valley talk, and none in a position to know. A man's own printed words to his membership are unanswerable on the point. **Why it matters:** the by-law the father wrote made the station's own test the whole of a man's pay with no appeal anywhere on earth, and the son administered it — he sent the 1919 water circular and defended it in print, told Teague in 1916 to let the station-gain question alone, signed the annual sheet carrying that gain line every year with his thumb on the bottom figure, and urged water before the board in 1925 without proof. The rule and its enforcement stayed in one family for fifty years. There is a smaller irony: Selby inherited his father's Jersey herd, which tested 4.56 in 1910 — the richest documented milk in the valley, and therefore the milk the proportional error robbed hardest, by 0.24 of a point. The persistence of the "nephew" version is itself evidence of something: valley memory drifting away from a father-son succession that looked like the family owning the place.

**B4. The relationship between the Voses and the Keddies cannot be determined.** Vira Toland says old Mrs. Vose was first cousin to Orra Keddie — "everybody knew that and nobody wrote it down anywhere" — and makes it carry weight, since it turned the 1919 circular from the Association speaking into "somebody's cousin saying it." Cleve Oram says the Keddies and Voses were "cousins of some sort," which he had from his mother, who had it from hers, and which he could not draw on paper. Against them, Duncan Keddie — Orra's son — denies it flatly, attributes the confusion to the unrelated Ashlin Voses, and gives a reason worth something: his mother could not pass a headstone without giving the connection and never once put a Vose in it. No document touches the question, and the 1889–1892 minute book that might have helped was lost in 1938. Two pieces of hearsay circulating in one small valley are close to one source, not two; but Duncan is an interested party denying a tie that would have made the water circular a letter from kin. **Verdict: not established.** What the sources do establish is that the belief was current in the valley for sixty years and that the one narrator inside the household rejected it.
