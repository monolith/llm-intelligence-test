# Retention notes after retellings 1–16 (Ostrey Hollow / Larrow Valley case)

*Written for a fresh reader who has seen none of the source documents. Everything below comes from retellings r01–r16 only. Distractor documents (road commission, playhouse story, drugstore daybook, buoy story, court column, combinatorics paper, sanitarium ledger, ferry API spec, Pemberton country-practice casebook, transit-shelter recording, Antiphon travel sketch, Corbin Junction telegraph copy-book, floor-safe story, quasi-absolute-summability paper, Purlwright plugin API, laundromat recording) are deliberately excluded — they are unrelated noise.*

## 0. THE CASE IN ONE PARAGRAPH (my synthesis, so the thread isn't lost)

A cooperative creamery association in the Larrow Valley ran a skimming station seven miles up the valley at Ostrey Hollow. For roughly a generation the milk tested there came out lower than milk tested at the main creamery. Patrons were paid on butterfat test, so a low test meant low pay. Suspicion fell first on the land (wet bog hay), then on the patrons (watering their cans), then on the tester (stealing cream). A 1925 arbitration found "no cause established" but paid $3,800 anyway; the tester, Ansel Keddie, was dismissed. In 1958 his granddaughter Ivy Keddie measured nine surviving pipettes from a crate condemned by the state inspector in June 1923 and found they delivered 16.72 c.c. where they were blown for 17.6 c.c. — exactly 0.95 of the mark. The glass, not any man, was the cause. Every test at that station read 95% of the truth for 26 years.

## 2. PEOPLE AND RELATIONSHIPS (complete)

### The Keddie family
- **Ansel Keddie** — tester at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station. Went up there in **1895**, the year before it opened, to get it ready (r01); tested that first summer too. (r13 says he "had tested at that station since it opened in 1896" — a small conflict with r01's 1895.) Began the test book **4 May 1896**. Tested **twenty-nine seasons, 1896 through 1924**; "did not test a pound in the season of 1925." Put out (dismissed) **30 April 1925** — "the last day of the month," and per r13 **four days before the board made its award**. **Died in the thirties** (r01: "He died in the thirties and he was still under it when he died"); r04 gives it precisely: **died 1934**, and had been dead **24 years** when the 1958 measurement sheet cleared him. Self-description: "I am an old man and I have no other trade."
- **Orra Keddie** — Ansel's wife. **Emmet Brant's sister** (stated by r01, r03, and r09). r09 is emphatic: "They were full brother and sister, same mother and same father, whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it." Also, per r01, **first cousin to old Mrs. Vose** — "everybody knew that and nobody wrote it down anywhere." Outlived Ansel "a good while" and carried the valley's suspicion the whole time. Would walk down to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane.
- **Duncan Keddie** — Ansel's son, Ivy's father; **taught school at Ashlin** (r04, r09). Per r09, **first cousin to Roy Brant**.
- **Ivy Keddie** — Ansel's granddaughter, Duncan's daughter. Of the **State Agricultural College at Farland** (r07 Item 5); a **graduate student** there (r10's heading). Found the crate **February 1958**; measured the pipettes **9 March 1958**; delivered the 1959 talk; co-authored the wording of **Bulletin No. 471** with **Professor Vaught**. Per r09 and r12 footnote, **second cousin to Hazel Brant**. Wrote to Rosalie Cudd in 1958 (four lines, apologizing for the trouble); borrowed the Brant weigh book from Roy Brant in 1958 ("She is careful with paper"). r10: "here is a young woman from the college who asks one [plain question] in four lines and apologizes for troubling me."

### The Brant family
- **Emmet Brant** — "the biggest man on the Bottom," most cows, most hay ground, hauled more milk to the station than anybody (r01); "the station's largest patron in every year of which we have a record" (r02). **Born 1854, died 1929** (r09). **Orra Keddie's brother**, hence Ansel Keddie's brother-in-law. Kept the farm weigh book from **1 January 1898 until the week before he died** (r09) — note r07 Item 4 and r13's exhibit list both date the book's start to **1 January 1897**; see §8.17. Sixty-odd acres of the meadow; **thirty-eight cows in a good year**. One of the first men to complain out loud in **1902**, and was laughed at (r09). The 1919 letter "cost him the most." Before 1919 the Brant and Keddie households ate Sunday dinners together, "the two houses in one kitchen and the men out at the fence after." **After 1919 they never spoke again** — "not at the store, not at a funeral" — and both died without speaking. Ansel's own words: "He and I have not spoken since the circular. That is the plainest fact in this letter and it is the one I find I have the least to say about." **Roy Brant supplies the reason (r09), which nobody else gives**: "not from any quarrel over the water, but because my father held that the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not." He read the 1908 committee report twice, put it in the drawer, and said "that the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not." He wrote at the kitchen table after the second milking, in the lamp, **with his hat still on**, and his wife would not have the table cleared till he was done.
- **Roy Brant** — Emmet Brant's **son**; narrator of r09; born about **1888** (67 in 1955). Kept up the Hessel Bottom weigh book himself after his father died, "in the same book until it was filled and then in another, in the same way"; lent it to Ivy Keddie (r04, r09 postscript). Per r07 Item 4 provenance, the book passed **to Roy Brant in 1929**, lent to Ivy Keddie in 1958 and returned. First cousin to Duncan Keddie. Did **not** haul over Redlow Ridge — "We had the biggest herd on the meadow, and the biggest herd is the one that cannot afford to be talked about, and I have thought since that this was a poor reason and cost us money."
- **Hazel Brant** — **Roy Brant's daughter** (r09); second cousin to Ivy Keddie. r01: the 1958 college researcher was "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed, so she was of the family both sides in a manner of speaking" — r09 and r12's footnote both say **second cousins**, so Vira's degree is wrong. r07 Item 4: the weigh book went **to Miss Hazel Brant in 1962**, then to the Rooms in 1963 — which matches Roy's stated intention exactly: "It goes to Hazel when I am done with it. That is all the arrangement I have made and all I intend to make." (Vira Toland's "young woman from the college away east in **1958**" is plainly Ivy Keddie, though Vira misplaces the college as "away east" — Farland is the State Agricultural College — and describes her poking about the old station building.)

### The Vose family
- **Gideon Vose** — **first president** of the Association, elected at the organizing meeting in **1889**, held the office **until 1911 — twenty-two years**, longer than any other man. Drafted the by-laws himself; "he was not a lawyer and they read as a farmer wrote them, which is to their credit." r12 adds: "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the law and thought better of it," who wrote the by-laws **at his own kitchen table**, "in a hand that the Association's clerks were still complaining about thirty years later." **Died 1917** ("written in 1892 by a man who died in 1917 and could not be asked about it afterward" — r02). Kept a herd of **Jerseys** at the home place.
- **Selby Vose** — Gideon's **son** (r02, r08: "My father, who was your first president"). Note r01 says Selby was "old Gideon Vose's **nephew**" — and **r12 independently says the same** ("His nephew Selby Vose, afterward manager of the Association, used to say that his uncle wrote by-laws the way other men build stone wall — slowly, and to last"). Two sources now say nephew; see §8.4. Made **manager in 1913**, served **until 1938** (r02). Signed the 1919 circular. Signed the annual station sheet every year. Inherited and kept his father's Jersey herd; in **1910 that herd tested 4.56**. Urged the **water** explanation before the 1925 board, "without heat and without proof" (r13).
- **Old Mrs. Vose** — first cousin to Orra Keddie (r01). The kinship that made the 1919 letter, in Vira's phrase, "not only the Association saying it... it was somebody's cousin saying it, and that is a different thing altogether in a place this size."

### Association staff
- **Effie Loomis** — secretary **1901–1940**, kept the minute book in her own hand throughout. **Fourteen years old in 1889**, therefore born about 1875. Recorded the 1925 award personally on the last day.
- **Wilbur Teague** — bookkeeper, came in **1905**, kept ledgers to **1945** ("forty years by the calendar and a good deal more than that by the wrist"). Invented the station-gain line in 1905. Office at Ashlin: one room over a harness shop, "cold at the north end and never cold enough at the south"; sloped desk with a brass rail across the foot to keep the sheets from sliding; remembers the stiffer paper of the station sheets in March. Per r13, he is the man who put the **cream-theft** explanation to the board — obliquely: "in the way an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it — he was asked about the station's account and answered at length about how such an account would look if a man were helping himself, and then observed that he made no charge against anybody. There is no cross-examination that reaches a witness of that kind, and I did not attempt one."
- **Hobart Sill** — **buttermaker** at Larrow Green; kept the **churn book and the cream receiving book** (r02). Narrator of r14. Came **1893**, left **1934**, **forty-one years**, aged **sixty-four** at the end. "there was no floor in this room but plank, and we cooled with creek ice out of the pond." Made butter that "took premiums in three states." Never went up to the station "but twice in my life."
- Effie Loomis's indictment of all four (herself first): "not one of us ever went up the valley and stood in the Ostrey Hollow station on a working morning to watch the work being done."

## 3. THE INSTITUTION

- **Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association**, organized **1889** at **Larrow Green** by the farmers of the Larrow Valley. Gideon Vose first president. r12 adds the scene: they met **in the schoolhouse, in cold weather**, "and the older men who were there remembered afterward chiefly that it went on very late and that somebody's horse got loose in the middle of it." Motive: "They were tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking whatever the merchant chose to allow them for it, and they had worked out — the way farmers do, slowly and then all at once — that the way to be paid properly for a thing is to own the place that buys it."
- **Main creamery built at Larrow Green in 1891**, on **Larrow Creek**; "it is the same building yet" (1941). r12: "on the creek below the mill, and the churn was set up in it, and the valley began to be paid in cash once a month, which had never happened to it before."
- Before the creamery, "the members sold their milk where they could get a price for it and there was no common test and no common price, and every man's arithmetic was his own."
- Three things on paper in Teague's time: **creamery at Larrow Green, office at Ashlin, skimming station at Ostrey Hollow** (seven miles up the valley).
- **County: Ordell County.** Ashlin is the town with the lawyers, the office over the harness shop, and the Historical Rooms. Vessey County is over Redlow Ridge (Alder Corners). Tarnet is where the supply firm Ammon & Sons is. Other Ordell places named: Larrow Green (creamery, school, store), Ostrey Hollow (hamlet + station), Hessel Bottom (the wet meadow).
- Skimming station function: patrons haul milk up rather than drive seven miles down; milk weighed in; a sample taken from each man's can and tested for butterfat; paid on **pounds × test**; the station separates the milk, **sends the cream down the valley to Larrow Green** and **gives the skim back to the farmer** for hogs and calves. r12: "the milk was weighed and tested and separated on the spot, the cream came down to the Green by wagon, and the skim went home to the calves and the pigs the same morning."
- Why the station existed (r12): "The Hollow is seven miles up from the Green, and in the nineties the pull down was three hours in mud and could not be done at all in March."
- **Hessel Bottom** — the wet meadow below the hamlet, source of the best milk. **r02 says seven farms had rights on Hessel Bottom; r09, r15 and r16 all say six.** See §8.19. They cut hay and pastured on it; the largest was **Emmet Brant's**. r09 gives its size: **a hundred and sixty acres of wet meadow lying under the hill at Ostrey Hollow**; the Brant share **sixty-odd acres**. "The water stands on the low end into May, and there is a soft place by the alders that has taken a horse to the hock in my time and my father's. The hay off it is coarse, and it cuts late, and a cow will eat it and do well enough, whatever has been said about that hay by men who never fed it... it never failed to grow something."
- The station "took from the farms of the wet bottom below it and from **three places on the hill**" (r06, 1909).
- Station building description (r01): at the very top of the Hollow where the road bends and then goes to nothing but the ridge; low, whitewashed, "cold as a spring house inside even in August," smelled of milk and steam and hot iron; you could hear the engine before you came round the last turn. r12 adds: "a plain one, boarded and battened, with the intake room at the road end and **a loft over it that I believe has never been cleared out since it was put up**; and behind it the creek, which in a wet April comes up to the sill and has twice come over it."
- After closing it was used for **storage — fence wire and salt** (r01), "odds and ends" in the loft (r06 1928).
- **Alder Corners Cheese Company**, Vessey County — on the flat below the church; spring water on the lot; hard road out to the east. Rosalie changed only the boiler and the roof in seventeen years.
- **Redlow Ridge** (r10, the fullest description): "Four miles of switchback, cut for a lumber road and never much improved since. It shuts in November and it does not open before April, some years May, and there is no arguing with it — the drifts sit in the cut below the top and stay there after everything else is bare. **So no man from your valley ever hauled to me in winter, whatever the price was. It was not possible.**" The haulers "started before daylight to have the cans on my platform by seven, because milk that has been four miles down a switchback in a wagon on a July morning is not improving while it stands, and they came in with the horses steaming and the tarps wet with the cloud that sits on the top of the ridge till the sun is well up."

## 4. ARTICLE VII — THE GOVERNING BY-LAW

Adopted at the annual meeting of **1892**, the year after the creamery opened (r02). Drafted by Gideon Vose. Quoted **identically** by Effie Loomis (r02 §3), by the curator (r07 Item 1), and again word-for-word by Dell Rundle (r12 §II) — three independent transcriptions:

> *Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*

- Effie Loomis's gloss: before it, a man was paid for what his cans weighed — "a hundred pounds of milk was a hundred pounds of milk whosever cow it came out of." After it, he was paid for the fat found by a test made where he delivered. She calls the rule just: "it pays a man for what he actually brings and not for the water that is naturally in it."
- **The hinge**: the last clause, "which is the one nobody quotes" — *the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.* "Not the test of the creamery, not the average of the valley, not a test made over again anywhere else. The station's test, and **no appeal from it**." Effie: "That clause is the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business."
- She notes it "has been misquoted at me across this desk for forty years."
- **r12's gloss** on the same clause: "But mark the last clause, because the valley has had fifty years to learn what it means. *The test of the station where he delivers shall govern.* Not the test of the creamery. Not any test but the one made at his own door. **Gideon Vose put that in for convenience and it was convenient for thirty years, and it is the sentence on which the whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned.**" Rundle also: "It was thought a hard rule at the time and there were men who left the Association over it. It is a just rule. A man who keeps good cows and feeds them ought not to be paid the same as the man who keeps poor ones, and **the pound of milk is a liar where the pound of butterfat is not**."
- **r13's lawyer's gloss** (Frick): "I say nothing against the rule. But it makes the test the whole of the bargain, and **it leaves a dissatisfied man with no remedy on earth except to quarrel with a figure**." He also notes the arbitration was "under the by-laws of a creamery association, **with no appeal from it, no reported decision, and nothing whatever to cite it for**."
- r07 Item 1: printed pamphlet, **gray wrappers**; **the printed copies carry no year on their face, only the words "adopted at the annual meeting."**
- **DATING CONFLICT — now three-way**: r02 says **1892**; **r12 says "Article VII dates from 1891, the year the churn was set up"**; **r15 (Grigg) says "Payment by test came in with the 1893 meeting, as I have always understood it"** — and hedges it himself with "as I have always understood it." The 1889–1892 minute book was lost in 1938 and the pamphlet is undated, so **no surviving document fixes the year**. Effie Loomis (the secretary, working from the record she had) is the best authority; Rundle is a folklore compiler; Grigg is explicitly going on memory. See §8.20.

## 7. THE NUMBERS

### 7.1 The physical fault
- Pipettes **blown on the stem at 17.6 c.c.**; measured mean delivery **16.72 c.c.**; **short 0.88 c.c.**
- 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95 exactly**. Every test read **95 percent of the truth**.
- Recorded average **3.61**; 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80** true. **Gap = 0.19 of a point (nineteen hundredths).**
- Ivy Keddie's crucial insight: **nineteen hundredths is not a fixed quantity.** "Nineteen hundredths is five percent of three point eight. The station did not lose a set number of points. **It lost a twentieth of whatever was there.**"
- **Because the error was proportional it fell hardest on the best milk.** A herd recorded at **4.56 was short 0.24** of a point. A herd recorded at **3.42 was short 0.18**. "The richer the milk, the more was taken from it, and no man had any way of knowing that his good cows cost him more than his poor ones." (4.56 is exactly Vose's Jersey herd figure for 1910, and 3.42 is exactly the Brant weigh-book figure for 1910 — the two worked examples are the two documented herds.)
- **Vaught's mechanism (r11 §II), the clearest statement of why it worked**: the test is four operations — a measured charge drawn into a pipette and delivered into a test bottle; sulfuric acid dissolves everything but the fat; the bottle is spun in a hand tester; the fat rises into the graduated neck and is read **directly** in percent. "The operator weighs nothing. He reads the length of a column of fat in a narrow neck and calls it a percentage, and he is entitled to do so for one reason only: **the charge of milk that went into that bottle is a fixed and known quantity. The maker rules the neck for that quantity and no other.** Every mark on it means 'this much fat, out of the standard charge.'" The standard charge in the state is **17.6 c.c.** "Consider then what follows if the pipette delivers less than its mark. Less milk goes into the bottle; less fat is liberated; a shorter column stands in the neck; and the neck, which knows nothing of the substitution, is read off in the ordinary way. **The reading is not merely low. It is low in the exact proportion by which the charge was short.**"

### 7.1b Vaught's three consequences (r11 §II) — the best analysis in the whole corpus
> **First, the error is proportional and not additive.** It does not remove a fixed number of points from every test. It removes a fixed *fraction*. Rich milk therefore loses more than poor milk, absolutely, and **the ratio between any two tests made on the same short glass is undisturbed. A station using such glass will show a perfectly orderly, internally consistent set of figures. Its herds will rank correctly against one another. Only against the outside world is it wrong.**

> **Second, the error is precise.** A leaking pipe or a slipping scale gives a scattered error, and scatter attracts attention. A pipette that delivers [short] every time, for a quarter of a century, in January and in July, for a careful operator and a careless one — **it has the appearance of excellent work.**

> **Third — and this is the reason the thing ran so long — the error is invisible from inside the plant.** Every other element of the test can be verified against a standard. The scales can be checked with test weights. The tester's speed can be counted. The acid can be checked for strength. The bottle's graduation can be checked with mercury. **But the pipette is not checked against anything, because in the ordinary practice of the trade the pipette is the standard. It is what the plant measures with. An instrument that is itself the measure cannot be caught by measuring with it. It can be caught only by a burette brought in from outside**, and burettes reached the state's field men very late in this history.

Vaught's one-line summary for the extension worker who reads no further: "**the milk pipette is the one instrument in the butterfat test which the plant cannot check against anything else, and it is the instrument on which every figure in the plant depends.**"

Vaught's opening: "This station has been asked, at intervals for fifty years, why the butterfat tests at one skimming station in Ordell County ran below those of its own creamery seven miles down the same valley. The question has been answered at various times by reference to the land and by reference to the men. **It is answered here by reference to a piece of glass.** ... Nobody in that valley lacked the intelligence to find it. **What they lacked was any occasion to suspect the one article in the plant that nobody in the trade is in the habit of suspecting.**"

### 7.1c Vaught's repeatable method (r11 §III)
"The pipette is filled to its mark with water at room temperature, the tip is wiped, and the contents are delivered in the ordinary working manner — the same interval of drainage, the same touch of the tip to the wall of the receiver — into a burette of certified graduation. Five deliveries are made from each pipette and the readings averaged. **The whole of it occupies an afternoon and requires no apparatus a creamery does not own, save the burette. There is no reason why every station in this state should not be checked once in this way, and several reasons in this history why it should be.**"

### 7.2 The money (Ivy Keddie's chain, r04; corroborated by r07 Item 6 curatorial note)
- **25 full delivery years, 1898 to 1922.**
- Station intake: **40,000,000 lb of milk**, average **1,600,000 lb a year**.
- At recorded 3.61%: patrons credited with **1,444,000 lb of fat**.
- At true 3.80%: they had delivered **1,520,000 lb**.
- Difference: **76,000 lb of butterfat**.
- Butterfat averaged **25 cents a pound** over those years → **$19,000**.
- The 1925 award was **$3,800** — **exactly one fifth of it**.

### 7.3 The two percentages (the deliberate arithmetic trap, addressed in r04 and r07, and repeated by r11)
- 76,000 is **five percent** of the fat **actually delivered** (1,520,000).
- 76,000 is **five and a quarter percent** of the fat **credited** (1,444,000).
- Both are right; each is right **of its own denominator and of no other**.
- The Association's ledger line uses the second, "because a bookkeeper naturally reckons a surplus against the figure he has already entered."
- **"But five percent is the one that is also the glass."** (i.e. 1/20 short glass → 5% of the true quantity)
- The specimen 1898 entry proves the ratio: 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26% (of credited); 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.00% (of received).
- **r11 §V restates the ledger side correctly even while its own arithmetic is wrong**: "Its ledgers carried, annually, a credit for fat received at the creamery in excess of fat credited to patrons at the station, under the heading 'station gain.' The gain is five and a quarter percent of the butterfat actually delivered." — note Vaught attaches 5.25% to the **delivered** figure, which is the wrong denominator; 5.25% is of the **credited** figure.

### 7.4 Effie Loomis's independent figures (r02 §7, from Teague's annual sheets)

Over the same period the price paid for butterfat averaged **twenty-five cents a pound**."
- She disclaims the cows and vouches only for the copying: "they are Mr. Teague's, out of the annual sheets, and I have added the columns twice."
- **These agree exactly with Ivy Keddie's figures — independent corroboration written 18 years before the solution.**

### 7.5 The butterfat price — three figures
- **25 cents a pound**, average over the 25 claim years — r02 (from the annual sheets) and r04. **This is the load-bearing figure** ($19,000 = 76,000 × $0.25).
- **24 cents** in **1910** specifically — r09, from the Brant book. Consistent (a single year, not an average).
- **27 cents** "through these years, take one year with another" — **r14 (Sill)** and **r16 (Kepp)**, independently. Both are speaking loosely from memory rather than from a sheet; Kepp may simply have taken the figure from local talk. **Two cents high against the secretary's added-twice columns.**

### 7.6 The size of the gap — five different figures for the same thing
| Figure | Source | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **0.19** (nineteen hundredths) | r03, r04, r06, and the arithmetic (3.80 − 3.61) | **The correct one** — recorded 3.61 vs true 3.80 |

**Roy Brant's 0.17 is the most quietly impressive number in the whole case**: it is not an error, it is a *different and correctly-computed comparison*, and it corroborates Sill's 3.78 from a completely different direction.

## 8. CONFLICTS, ODDITIES AND ERRORS — the heart of the test

### 8.1 The award figure: $3,500 vs $3,800
- **r01 (Vira Toland, 1979)**: "they paid the patrons **thirty-five hundred dollars** and called it settled. Thirty-five hundred dollars, split up nine ways over all those years — my father's share of it did not buy a hay rake."
- **Every documentary source** — r02 (secretary who recorded it), r04, r05 (bookkeeper who entered it), r07 (minute book transcription), r08 (manager), **r13 (the chairman who made the award)** — says **$3,800**.
- **Verdict:** Vira misremembers by $300 after 54 years. The $3,800 is right, now confirmed by the chairman himself.

### 8.2 The year of the hearing: 1919-adjacent vs 1925
- **r01** places the hearing "the winter and spring after" the tests went up, immediately following the 1919 letter in her telling — she compresses 1919 and 1925 into one adjacent sequence, having been seven at the time.
- **All other sources**: the petition was **January 1925** and the board sat **17 March – 4 May 1925**.
- **Verdict:** Vira's sequence is right in shape (the tests rose, then there was a hearing) but her chronology collapses six years. The rise was 1924; the hearing 1925.

### 8.3 The date the glass was condemned: 12 June vs 12 July 1923
- **Strawn's own contemporaneous field book (r06), the photostat (r07 Item 9), Bulletin No. 471 (quoted in r04, r07 and r11), and r07 Item 5**: **12 June 1923**.
- **Selby Vose (r08, 1926)**: "he... condemned the station's testing apparatus on **the twelfth of July, 1923**."
- **r12 (Rundle, 1939) repeats the July date**: "The state inspector condemned the station's glassware on the twelfth of July, 1923, for reasons of his own that had nothing to do with the quarrel." **This is almost certainly Vose's error propagating into the valley's folk history** — Rundle compiled from recollection and the Association's people, and Vose's printed annual address of 1926 was exactly the kind of source available to him.
- **Verdict:** 12 June. Note the direction of the error — moving it to July makes the replacement look faster relative to the flush and makes the condemnation look more incidental. Also note the Association's own order was placed **28 June**, which is impossible if the condemnation was 12 July. Rundle's added phrase "for reasons of his own that had nothing to do with the quarrel" shows how completely the condemnation had been filed away as irrelevant.

### 8.4 Selby Vose: son or nephew of Gideon?
- **r01 (Vira)**: "He was old Gideon Vose's **nephew**."
- **r12 (Rundle)**: "**His nephew Selby Vose**, afterward manager of the Association, used to say that his uncle wrote by-laws the way other men build stone wall."
- **r02 (Effie Loomis)**: "**Selby Vose, son of the late president**, was made manager in 1913."
- **r08 (Selby Vose himself)**: "**My father**, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law..."
- **Verdict: son.** Two secondary sources now say nephew, but the man's own printed words to the membership and the secretary's minute-based note both say son, and Vose's own address is unanswerable on the point. Rundle is a folklore compiler and Vira heard it in the valley — the "nephew" version is a persistent local error, which is itself interesting: **it shows valley memory drifting in a consistent direction, away from a father-son succession that would look like nepotism.**

### 8.5 Teague's totals disagree with everyone's — and now Frick and Rundle repeat one of them
- **Teague (r05)**: "that station took in **forty-four million pounds** of milk" and "the average test of all the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, taken over the whole period... was **3.16**."
- **Everyone else** (Effie Loomis from Teague's own sheets; Ivy Keddie; the curator): **40,000,000 lb** and **3.61**.
- **r12 (Rundle, 1939) repeats the 44 million**: "The station took in, over the years complained of, forty-four million pounds of milk."
- **r13 (Frick, 1946) repeats the 3.16**: "The patrons of that station had been paid, year upon year, on an average test of **3.16**... Three and sixteen is not a scandalous figure standing by itself."
- **This is important:** Teague's two erroneous figures each turn up in a later independent source. The likeliest explanation is that **Teague was the source for both** — he was the Association's bookkeeper, the man everyone asked for a number, and he had been giving out 44 million and 3.16 for years. Frick heard 3.16 in the hearing room in 1925 (Teague testified); Rundle heard 44 million at the office or the store. **Teague's memory errors became the valley's official figures.** Note that the *documents* Teague transcribes are perfect (the 57,760 / 60,800 / 3,040 entry is verbatim correct in r05, r07 and r12) — it is only his recollected round numbers that fail.
- **Note the oddity:** Teague is the bookkeeper — the man whose ledgers everyone else is quoting — yet writing from memory in 1948 he gives a milk total 10% high and an average test 0.45 low. His 3.16 corresponds to no figure in the case (neither the recorded 3.61 nor the true 3.80). **He explicitly says the figures "are asked for" and gives them over "the years the account ran," a longer and vaguer span than the 25 years 1898–1922 used by the others**, which may partly explain the 44 million.
- **Also**: Teague says of the 1911 break that "they made shift with a borrowed one **for six weeks**." The test book says **8 May to 10 July = nine weeks**. r04, r06, r09 and r11 all say nine weeks. **r16 (Kepp) also says six weeks, twice** — another Teague error in circulation, or independent slippage.
- **Also**: Teague says the borrowed measure came "up from town"; Ivy Keddie says "**The measure they borrowed in 1911 came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon**"; **r09 (Roy Brant) says something different again: "The state man left his own measure that June and that is what they used."** Three accounts of the borrowed measure's origin. Roy Brant's is the odd one out and is checkable against Strawn's own field book — Strawn was at the station in **June 1911** and wrote only "Watched a test through. Nothing here to note," with no mention of leaving a measure. Strawn also did not have a burette until 1921, but a burette is not a pipette; he could have carried a spare measure. **Unresolved.**

### 8.6 The curator's catalogue errors (r07) — at least five, and they matter
1. **Item 3, "Station test book, kept by E. Brant."** The book's first leaf is signed **A. Keddie**; every other source says Ansel Keddie kept it in his own hand for twenty-nine seasons; **and Frick's 1925 exhibit list (r13) describes it as "The test book of the Ostrey Hollow station, 4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924, in the hand of Ansel Keddie."** The header is wrong. (**E. Brant** = Emmet Brant, the largest patron — the two names have been transposed between Items 3 and 4.)
2. **Item 4, "Farm weigh book, kept by A. Keddie."** This is the **Hessel Bottom** book, kept by one household from 1 January 1897, passing to **Roy Brant** in 1929 and **Hazel Brant** in 1962 — i.e. the **Brant** weigh book, exactly as r04 describes it, as **r09 describes it at first hand** ("My father was Emmet Brant... he wrote down every can of milk that went out of this barn, and I have kept it up since"), and as **Frick's exhibit 3 describes it** ("The private weigh book of Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom"). The header is wrong. **The two attributions in Items 3 and 4 are swapped, and this is now confirmed from three directions.**
3. **Item 5, crate provenance**: the curator says the crate "was found **in the Association office at Ashlin**, in February 1958, by Miss Ivy Keddie." Ivy Keddie's own account (r04) is explicit and circumstantial: "In February of last year I went **up the Hollow to the station building**... There is a **loft over what was the intake room**, reached by a ladder, and **under a tarpaulin at the back of it** there was a wooden crate..." Corroborated by Strawn's 1928 entry about the loft being "full of odds and ends" and "full since before the war," by Ivy's "It had been carried up there in June of 1923 and it was there still," **and now by r12's independent description of the building: "with the intake room at the road end and a loft over it that I believe has never been cleared out since it was put up."** **The curator has the wrong building.**
4. **Item 13** says the Alder Corners test book "passed from its founder to his **daughter** in 1941"; **r04 says Rosalie Cudd is Jerome Cudd's niece**. **NOW RESOLVED: r10 is Rosalie Cudd's own letter and she says "My father, Jerome Cudd" — and r12 independently says "It is Jerome Cudd's daughter, Rosalie Cudd, who makes the cheese at Alder Corners now." The curator was right and Ivy Keddie was wrong.** A small but real error by the case's heroine, and worth remembering: she had never met Rosalie Cudd or seen the Alder Corners book — the figures were copied and posted to her.
5. **Item 3's column list** ("columns for date, patron, pounds and test" — four) conflicts with Keddie's own "**The book I kept in five columns: the date, the patron, the pounds, the test, the fat**" and his statement ("the date, the patron, the pounds, the test and the fat"). The curator drops the fat column.

### 8.7 The crate was never mentioned at the hearing — Effie Loomis's central observation
r02 §8: "The second is the crate. **The crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all.** I took every word down and it is not there. I mean the crate of measures that the state's inspector condemned up at the station in June of 1923, which was set aside and new ones ordered in its place. **Not a word of it was spoken in any of the three sessions**, by the patrons, by the officers, or by the board, and the award's phrase about '**the books and appliances of the station**' is a form of words the board used and not a list of anything. Forty years of taking down what men say has taught me that the interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying."

This is the pivot of the whole case: the physical evidence sat condemned in a loft twenty-one months before the hearing, and nobody at the hearing mentioned it. **And see §8.23 — it is now much worse than that.**

### 8.8 "Finds no cause established" + $3,800 — the contradiction everyone notices
- Effie Loomis: "it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once."
- Ansel Keddie: "They found no cause established and they put me out on the last day of the month, and **both of those things are true at once**, and I have given up trying to hold them in my head together."
- Selby Vose spins it the other way: "a **generous settlement of an unproved claim**... There is not a business house in Ordell County that would have done it. We did it because we are not a business house."
- Ivy Keddie: "$3,800... **That is a fifth of it.**"
- **Frick, the chairman, agrees it was indefensible** (r13): "We were not able to say who owed it or why, and we said so. **An award without a finding is an unsatisfactory thing and every member of that board knew it.**" And: "we paid his neighbors money without being able to tell them what they were being paid for."

### 8.9 The 1924 rise — the competing explanations
| Explainer | Cause claimed |
|---|---|
| **Vira Toland (r01)** | Keddie was taking the cream; they caught him and put him out, "and that is why the numbers went right the summer after" |
| **Selby Vose (r08)** | Patrons were watering their cans and stopped when the Association began to look: "The tests came up when the looking began" |
| **Wilbur Teague (r05)** | Doesn't claim the rise directly but argues one man alone with a separator will eventually take a little |
| **Hobart Sill (r14)** | "one summer, along in the twenties, those figures came up and they stayed up, and nothing on this earth changed but the season, and men do not suddenly start pouring better milk out of the same cows in the same barns. **Something got stopped. Something got said to somebody.** I never knew what and I never asked" |
| **Dell Rundle (r12)** | Records the valley's version: "the figures went right when the man who kept it did not" — then retracts the theft premise in his in-press note |
| **Ivy Keddie (r04)** | The condemned glass was replaced in July 1923; the new pipettes deliver their mark; 1924 is the first full year on true glass |

**The decisive chronology against Vira, Vose, Sill and the valley:** the tests rose in **1924**. Keddie was not dismissed until **April 1925** — after the rise, not before. The petition was not filed until **January 1925**. The new glass went in **July 1923**. Only the glass precedes the rise. Vira's causal order is backwards; Vose's "the tests came up when the looking began" is contradicted by his own dating; **Sill's "something got said to somebody" is the correct instinct with the wrong object — something did get said, by Strawn, to the manager, on 12 June 1923, and what got stopped was the short glass.**

Vose also asserts "the tests at that station rose by twenty-one hundredths and the average for 1924 stood at **3.90**." Everyone else — Keddie's own book, Strawn's field book — gives **3.80** for 1924, a rise of nineteen hundredths from 3.61. **Vose has inflated both the level and the rise by a tenth of a point.** (3.61 + 0.21 = 3.82, which is not 3.90 either — his own arithmetic does not close.)

### 8.10 The 1911 natural experiment — the strongest single piece of evidence
Ivy Keddie's framing: "For nine weeks in 1911 there was an experiment at that station, and nobody knew it was one... Same patrons, same herds, same pasture, same season, same acid, same bottles, same man at the crank — **one object changed** — and the average went to 3.80 and came back to 3.61 the day the station's own measure returned. **No explanation of this valley's twenty-six-year anomaly survives those nine weeks except the one I have given you.**"

**Vaught's version (r11 §IV), which he rates above his own arithmetic:** "I would ask the reader to weigh what follows above anything computed in this bulletin... The patrons were the same patrons; the herds were the same herds; the pasture, the season, the acid, the bottles, the tester and the tester's habits were all unaltered. **One variable changed.** The station's average test rose from 3.61 to 3.80 and held there for the nine weeks, and returned to 3.61 on the day the station's own measure came back into service. This is recorded contemporaneously, in the operator's hand, **by a man who did not know what he was recording and set a question mark against it.** I have spent my working life urging students to prefer an experiment to a calculation. **Here is one that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven years.** It disposes of every explanation that looks to the herds, to the ground or to the men, for the sufficient reason that none of those was altered in those nine weeks, and the figures were."

**Roy Brant's independent corroboration (r09) — new and valuable**: "Through those nine weeks our tests came up, **all of us, every farm on the Bottom**; and then the station's own came back into use and the figures went down again to where they had been. My father noticed the rise and he noticed the fall and he entered both, and he could make nothing of it, and I could make nothing of it either, and there it sat for near on fifty years." **The rise is in the Brant private weigh book too, from the patrons' side — a second, independent record of the same nine weeks.**

Note that Keddie recorded this anomaly himself in 1911, flagged it with a question mark, let the question mark stand fourteen years, and still could not explain it in 1925 — **the answer was in his own book the whole time**. Note also Strawn visited in **June 1911** — during the borrowed-measure window — and wrote "Watched a test through. Nothing here to note." He would have been watching a test made with the *good* borrowed glass.

**Kepp's dismissal of it (r16)** is the only direct attempt anyone makes to argue the interval away, and it fails on facts: see §8.22.

### 8.11 The bog-hay theory — why it fails, in four ways
1. **Magnitude:** Bulletin 214 gives at most 0.05 in winter; the gap was 0.19 flat. "**It is four times too small**" (r04). Vaught (r11 §VI) puts the same point with different arithmetic off his own erroneous figures: "Five hundredths in winter; nothing at all in summer. The discrepancy under examination is present in every month of the year and is **some six times** the largest effect the trial could produce under conditions harsher than any farmer would impose." (Four times against 0.19; six times against his own 0.295.)
2. **Shape:** the hay effect is winter-only and unmeasurable on summer pasture; the gap "stood flat in every month of every year, **July and February alike**." "**It is the wrong shape.**" Corroborated by Keddie: "They did not come and go with the season. The gap was there in July as it was there in February, in the flush of the grass and in the dead of the winter on dry hay, and it was the same in a wet year as in a dry one." **And now independently by Roy Brant (r09)**, who makes it the one thing he asks the reader to notice: "**it was seventeen hundredths in July and it was seventeen hundredths in February. It did not come and go with the season. It did not care whether the cows were on the meadow or on hay in the barn. Nobody would take account of that, then or since, though it was in front of them.**"
3. **External control:** over the ridge, the **Alder Corners factory** in Vessey County tested the milk of **four Hessel Bottom men** in their summer flush; where the two books overlap — **fourteen seasons** (r04's count) — "that same milk off that same wet ground ran about **nineteen hundredths higher at Alder Corners than at home**." **Now fully documented by r10's sixteen-season table, 1915–1930, all between 3.78 and 3.82.** (Note: r10 says sixteen seasons of hauling; r04 says fourteen seasons of *overlap* — consistent, since the station's affected run ends in 1923 and the two books can only be compared where both are on the same footing. But see §8.25.)
4. **The 1908 committee sampled only January, February and March** — winter only — and generalized to the whole year. **Grigg later claimed in 1919 they had sampled every month; his own reprinted report contradicts him.**
5. **New, from r10 — the cheesemaker's flat testimony:** "That milk was good milk. Season after season those four men came in at about three-eighty, and not one of them ever gave me an hour's trouble in the vat... they sit between 3.78 and 3.82, year in, year out, **wet years and dry ones, whether the meadow had been under water in May or hard as a road**." And Jerome Cudd's own 1919 book note: "**Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge.**"
6. **New, from r09 — the man who fed the hay:** "The hay off it is coarse, and it cuts late, and a cow will eat it and do well enough, **whatever has been said about that hay by men who never fed it**."

### 8.12 The water theory — why it fails
- **Merle Strawn tested for added water with a lactometer on every single visit across 29 years and never found any.** His 1919 entry: "The manager of the Association has sent a circular round to the patrons of this station about water in the cans. **I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so.**" The 1923 entry says "Lactometer, no added water, **as always here**."
- Vose sent the circular anyway in 1919 and was still defending the theory in 1926, three years after being told by the state inspector that there was no water — **and still urged it before the board in 1925, "without heat and without proof"** (r13).
- The 1908 committee also found "The milk is whole and the scales are honest" and "**no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter**."
- Bulletin No. 471: "**No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow.**"
- Logical point (implicit): watering dilutes everything, and the shortfall was strictly proportional and identical across wet and dry years, rich and poor herds.
- **NEW AND DECISIVE — the cheesemaker's argument (r10):** "A cheesemaker knows watered milk inside a day. He does not need a test to know it, though the test will tell him too. **The curd comes soft and it will not knit; you get a slack, weak body and your yield is down, and you can stand at the vat and see your money going out with the whey.** It cannot be hidden from the man who makes the cheese, because the cheese tells him, and it tells him the next morning, not next year. **Butter is more forgiving that way. Cheese is a witness.** In sixteen summers my father never made one complaint of those four men, never docked one of them a cent, and never — not once that I heard, and I was in the room — said a word about their milk except that he wished he had more of it in July." **Sixteen summers of independent physical assay by a process that cannot be fooled.**
- **NEW — the 1919 sequencing point:** the Alder Corners figures for 1919 (3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82) are from **the very summer the water circular went out**. In the same season Vose was accusing them of watering, their milk over the ridge was testing 3.80.

### 8.13 The cream-theft theory — why it fails
- **Bulletin No. 471: "no cream was stolen there."**
- The missing fat is **not missing**. It arrived at Larrow Green. Teague's own ledger line records it arriving, year after year: 60,800 lb received against 57,760 lb credited. **Nobody stole it; the Association banked it as "station gain."** This is the quiet horror of the case — the difference was in the Association's own books, in ink, dated, every year, described as a gain.
- **NEW AND ENORMOUS — Hobart Sill weighed it in (r14).** "That cream ran heavy. **Every year it ran heavy. There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there.** The office would send down its sheet and the sheet would say so many pounds of fat bought at the station, and the fat that came into this room off that wagon was more than the sheet said. **Always more. Never less.**" And he weighed it himself: "The platform man weighed it and I weighed it over again when it came into the cold room, because I have never in my life taken another man's figure for what was going into my churn... **you will find the Hollow's cream coming in heavy on page after page after page, in my own writing, for as long as I have been in this room.**" **So the discrepancy was independently physically measured, twice a day, by the buttermaker, and recorded in his own hand in the receiving book, for forty-one years.** His explanation: "Well, that is cream for you. Cream is not milk and it will not stand still and be counted... So it ran heavy. So does everybody's. **I never made anything of it, and I will say plain, nobody ever asked me to.**"
- **NEW — Dell Rundle reasoned it out in 1939 (r12, note added in press):** "Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. **It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand.**" A schoolteacher, on one afternoon's accidental sight of the ledgers, refuted the valley's twenty-year certainty — and printed the refutation in the pamphlet's own back pages, where it evidently changed nobody's mind (the valley was still telling Vira Toland the theft story in 1979, forty years later).
- The theft theory also cannot explain the 1911 nine weeks (why would a thief stop for exactly the period his own pipette was broken?) or the proportionality (why would a thief take exactly 5% of whatever was there, from every patron, every day, for 26 years, and never more in a flush year?).
- Keddie's own denial: "I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself."
- **Frick believed him** (r13): "I believed him then and I believe him now. **His denial was accurate and it did him no good at all, and the reason it did him no good is that a man who cannot account for a figure is presumed by everybody in the room to be the figure's author.**"
- Vira Toland's contrary certainty: "**He was taking the cream. They caught him at it and they put him out**... it is not that he is a wicked man, it is that **nobody ever came up that road and looked**." Her father's softer version: "Ansel Keddie was an honest man who had got into something, which is a nice way of saying it and I never saw the difference."
- **Rundle on the valley's consensus (r12), before his retraction:** "It is the settled opinion of the valley that cream left that station by the back door, and the figures went right when the man who kept it did not. I set that down as I have heard it set down a hundred times, by men who were there and men who were not, and **I have never heard it contradicted at the store.**"
- **Roy Brant, the largest loser, never accuses anybody at all** — the most striking omission in r09. He says only "something was wrong at that station for a very long time — **it was not the weighing**."

### 8.14 The structural failure — everyone names it, nobody acted
- **Effie Loomis (r02 §4)**: of herself, Teague, Sill and Vose — "**not one of us ever went up the valley and stood in the Ostrey Hollow station on a working morning to watch the work being done. I include myself first in that.**"
- **Ansel Keddie (r03, statement)**: "**Neither of them has come up that road on a working morning and stood at my elbow and watched the work done.**"
- **Vira Toland (r01)**: "**nobody ever came up that road and looked.**"
- **Rosalie Cudd (r10), from over the ridge and outside the county:** "If I sound short about Ordell it is only that I have listened to forty years of your valley explaining that meadow to itself, and **I never once heard anybody go up there and look.**"
- **Hobart Sill (r14), inverting it:** "**not one man in that office ever came down here to see how it was done**, and every one of them could tell you to the half cent what the fat was worth." (And he himself "never went up there but twice in my life.")
- **Five narrators, across sixty years, on four sides of the quarrel and one of them in another county, produce the same sentence.**
- **Teague's version of the failure** (r05): "**Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons.** One man weighed the milk, one man drew the sample, one man made the test, one man wrote the figure that the pay slip was made from, and one man ran the separator. There was no second man in that building from the spring to the fall... **The remedy is two men or a lock, and the Association had neither.**" — a correct diagnosis of a control weakness that happened to be the wrong explanation of this particular loss.
- **Teague's evenness insight** (r05): the gain "ran at five and a quarter percent... so evenly that the words '**as usual**' got into the entry and stayed there, and a man may as well be honest about what evenness does to a bookkeeper: **it stops him looking. A figure that jumps about will keep you awake. A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture.**" **Sill independently supplies the same failure in the physical domain: heavy cream every year for forty-one years, "so does everybody's," never questioned.** **And Vaught supplies the theoretical version: "It has the appearance of excellent work."** Three men, three vantage points, one mechanism — *consistency reads as correctness.*
- **Teague's 1916 escalation and retreat**: "I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone. I let it alone. **I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience**, and a bookkeeper who takes his employer's business up a second time after being told once has generally begun to look for another employer." He also insists **Vose knew**: "**He signed the sheet every year.** I laid the annual sheet before him each spring and he put his name at the foot of it, and a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him. What he made of it he never told me."
- **Sill's version of never being asked**: "I never made anything of it, and I will say plain, **nobody ever asked me to**." And on the 1924 rise: "I never knew what and I never asked, because **the day a buttermaker starts asking about other men's cans is the day he stops making butter.**"
- **Strawn's own missed chance**: he had the burette from **1921**, **skipped the check in 1922** ("the day being short and the road home being what it was, and I have put that down because it is what happened"), and caught it in **June 1923**. Had he run the glass in 1921 the case ends two years earlier. His honesty in recording the omission is itself notable. And the **burette program was discontinued in 1926** — the instrument that solved a twenty-six-year fraud-that-wasn't was withdrawn three years after it worked. **Vaught (r11) generalizes the point: "It can be caught only by a burette brought in from outside, and burettes reached the state's field men very late in this history."**
- **Frick's version (r13), the sharpest formulation of the human cost:** "I understood that **an honest man was ruined by a figure nobody could explain**, and that we paid his neighbors money without being able to tell them what they were being paid for." And: "I have wondered since whether the answer was in that room and simply was not in any of the three stories, and **whether a lawyer who understood chemistry might have found it. I did not understand chemistry.**"

### 8.15 Smaller oddities worth keeping
- **The station's first season (4 May – end of 1896) has no intake figure anywhere** — r07 notes and queries: "the station column begins with **1897**, and inquirers wanting a figure for 1896 must be told **there is none**." **BUT r14 (Sill) supplies one from memory: "That first season they took in about nine hundred thousand pounds up there. May to the end of the year, that is, so it was not even a whole one."** An approximate recollection, not a record, and Sill's other round numbers (27 cents, 3.85) run high — but it is the only 1896 figure in existence.
- **1896 tested 3.80** — the true rate — because the station was using **glass borrowed from Larrow Green** until 1 May 1897. **We now know from r14 exactly whose glass and why**: the station's own outfit had been ordered and had not arrived, they were ready to take milk, "there was talk of holding off the whole season," and Sill made up an outfit "out of my own glass, my own, that I had used and knew," and it went up on the wagon. The drop to 3.61 in 1898 coincides exactly with the arrival of the station's own bad glass. (1897 is "part year" — the transition year — and so no average is given. **The anomaly begins precisely when the Ammon & Sons glass goes into service.**) **The whole twenty-six-year injustice therefore dates from the moment they stopped using the buttermaker's honest borrowed glass and started using their own new purchase — and Sill, who lent the good glass, spent forty-one years blaming the patrons.**
- **The chipped piece**: Sill's borrowed glass came back to him in 1897 with "one piece of it chipped and I never said a word about that either." (Note two of the nine surviving Ammon pipettes are also chipped at the tip — unrelated, but the echo is worth keeping.)
- **Bulletin 471's window is 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923** — exactly from the day the station's own glass went into use to the day it was condemned.
- **The lost minute book**: the **1889–1892** minute book was lost in the **1938 office move**. Effie Loomis: "It was not thrown out; it was carried out with the rest and it did not arrive, and I have looked in every place a book can be in this building... **anybody who tells you what was resolved at the first four annual meetings is telling you a story he had from his father.**" Consequence: **Article VII's adoption year rests on her word, not on a surviving minute** — and the printed by-laws pamphlet carries **no year on its face** (r07 Item 1). **This is exactly why r12 and r15 can date it to 1891 and 1893 without anyone being able to correct them.**
- **Three explanations were argued before the 1925 board**, and Effie Loomis declined to rehearse them: "None of the three was accepted and I shall not rehearse them here; they are in the minutes and the minutes are in the safe." **r13 (Frick) now names all three and attributes each**: (1) **water**, put by the manager **Selby Vose**; (2) **cream theft**, put obliquely by the bookkeeper **Wilbur Teague**; (3) **the hay**, introduced by somebody reading the 1908 committee report into the record. Frick: "it is worth setting down who put each of them, because **the three did not sit easily together and nobody seemed to mind that they did not**." And: "each of them was urged by a man of standing; each of them would have been believed by half the valley; and not one of them was proved."
- **Five sets of records had never been in one room before 1958** (r04): the station test book, the Brant weigh book, the Alder Corners figures, Strawn's circuit notebooks, and the Association's annual ledgers. Ivy Keddie has **never seen** the Alder Corners book itself — the figures were copied for her. **r10 confirms this and offers to show her the book: "Come over in the summer and see the place. The road is open by then, mostly, and I will give you your dinner and show you my father's book, which is a nicer object than anything I have said about it here."**
- **Strawn's four-step routine at every plant, always in the same order** "so that I would not have to remember which of them I had done": (1) scales against sealed weights, intake and cream, loaded light and loaded heavy; (2) lactometer on the day's milk, patron by patron where there was any question, for added water; (3) boiler, water supply and cold room — pressure, gauge, cased well, whether the room would hold overnight; (4) "**the one most men grudged me**" — stand at the tester's elbow and watch a test through from sample to reading, "and I did not let him hurry it." **His routine had no step for checking the glass until the burette arrived in 1921.**
- **Strawn's creed**: "The state pays a field man to weigh and to look, and every time in my life I have seen a field man paid for an opinion instead, the opinion has outlived the weighing and done harm after the man was dead." And: "**I never had a theory about a plant and I do not recommend having one. A theory is a thing a man carries out to the platform with him and it gets in the way of the weights.**"
- **The letters kept for forty years** (r01): Vira's father read the 1919 letter "with his hat still on, which he never did"; her mother stood at the stove with her back to him and did not turn around; he read it twice, put it in the desk drawer in the front room, and it was still there when he died. One woman kept hers **pinned inside the lid of her trunk**. Vira: "You can tell people a thing like that is only business and they will still keep the paper forty years to prove what was said of them." (**Note the echo: Emmet Brant also wrote "with his hat still on," per r09 — in that valley the hat staying on marks a man doing something out of the ordinary run.**)
- **Vira's closing image**: "It rained the day of that letter, I remember the wet on my father's shoulders when he came in, and I have never in sixty years been able to think about the one without the other."
- **Ivy Keddie's disclaimer and close**: "nothing I am going to show you tonight depends in the smallest degree on my opinion of my grandfather. **It depends on nine pieces of glass and a burette.**" And: "**I have no remedy to propose. I have only the crate, and it is on the table, and you may look into it on your way out.**"
- **Vose's peroration**: "Bring it as it left the cow. Wash the cans, cool the milk, keep the dog out of the milk house, and bring it as it left the cow." He asks for "one more" year of patience after thirteen. (He in fact served to 1938 — twelve more.)
- **Vose's Jersey argument**: he cites the 1910 herd test of 4.56 not to boast but to refute the trade's claim "that the Larrow Valley is poor milk country, that our land is sour and our cattle are indifferent." Irony: **4.56 is the exact figure Ivy Keddie uses to show that the richest milk was robbed hardest** (short 0.24 of a point).
- **Keddie's method, in his own words** (r03, the fullest technical account in the case): day began at **half past five**; wagons came up in the dark most of the year; intake room open, lamps lit, scale swinging free before the first wagon. Every can weighed, the weight called and written **before the milk went anywhere else**. Scale tested by the state's man and true — "I have never in twenty-nine seasons had a patron dispute a weight with me and be right." A sample taken from each patron's milk **as it was weighed**, into **his own jar, numbered to him and to nobody else**; the jar **made up over ten days** before testing. On testing morning the jar warmed in water **until blood-warm right through**, then poured vessel to vessel and poured again "until the cream that had risen was carried down through the whole of it. **A sample that is not warmed and mixed is worth nothing and a boy could tell you so.**" Then the charge: milk drawn up in the measure **to the mark on the stem**, the tip wiped, the charge blown out into the bottle carrying the patron's number. Then the acid **poured down the side**, the bottle turned until the milk was dark. **Four bottles in the hand tester, weighted against each other**, cover down, crank turned "at the speed I was taught to turn it and no faster, and no man ever stood beside me and made me hurry it." Then the fat stands up in the neck **in a clear column**, read off in per cent **against the rulings, with a pair of dividers**, and written down. "That is the whole of it. **It is not a hard trade. It is only a trade that must be done the same way every time, which is a harder thing than it sounds.**"
- **The fatal detail inside that method**: the charge is drawn "to the mark on the stem" and the bottle neck "is ruled to read percent for that charge and no other" (Ivy Keddie; Vaught's §II is the full exposition). Keddie's technique was flawless; the mark itself was a lie. He did everything right with a wrong instrument, which is why watching him work — as Strawn did repeatedly, as the 1908 committee did, and as **the whole board of arbitration did in the station's own weigh room in 1925** — revealed nothing. **Frick: "I could see that it was done the same way twice, and that the men who did it were not hiding their hands."**
- **Error-correction discipline**: "where I have made an error I have drawn a line through it and put my letters against the line, and **I have never written a figure over a figure**... because a figure written over another figure is a figure no man can swear to afterward."
- **The station book's provenance** (r07 Item 3): kept at the station from 4 May 1896; **impounded by the board of arbitration in March 1925**; in the Association's office at Ashlin 1925–1959; received at the Rooms 1959. **Frick's exhibit list gives its terminal date: 31 December 1924.**
- **Item 8**: Keddie's signed statement to the board, one leaf, ruled, both sides, "**in the hand of Item 3**" — i.e. the curator's own physical evidence that the test book and the statement are the same hand, which quietly refutes his own "kept by E. Brant" header.
- **Item 16**: the Larrow Green receiving and churn books are **water-damaged at the foot**. **These are Sill's books — the ones with "the Hollow's cream coming in heavy on page after page after page, in my own writing."**
- **Curator's outstanding tasks**: "Item 5 wants a case with a lock. Accession numbers still to be cut for Items 13 and 16."
- **Effie Loomis on the Association's worth**: "it began as a plain remedy for a plain grievance, and I have never heard anybody in this valley say we should have been better without it." **Rundle's peroration is the same sentiment at length** (r12 §VI): "fifty Junes of cream coming down that road in the early cool, fifty Januaries of it going down over ice with the horses roughshod, a check every month in kitchens that had known none, and a creamery still standing that was built by men with nothing but each other and a piece of paper they had written themselves. **Whatever this valley did wrong at the head of it, it did this right, and it did it for half a century, and the men of 1889 would think we had done well.**"
- **Vira on the Vose family**: "I think the family always thought the valley belonged to them a little."
- **Vira's opening refusal**: "I could name you every one of them, but you don't want names, you want the trouble, **they always want the trouble**."
- **The nine hauling families** of Ostrey Hollow are the same nine that received the 1919 circular and the same nine among whom the $3,800 (Vira: $3,500) was divided.
- **Rundle on how the quarrel propagated to the next generation** (r12): "I have had the children of both ends in one schoolroom through those years and I can tell you that **it came down to them entire, without a word of it being explained, the way a debt does.**"
- **Roy Brant on the book's physical state** (r09): "It is a ledger, a plain one, ruled by hand. Two columns for each day of delivery... The cover is gone off the front, the back is held by a strip of harness leather my father tacked on himself, and **the first forty leaves are stained brown at one corner where it lay in a leaky window a winter he was sick.**" And: "There is no comment in it anywhere but three or four lines in thirty years, and no opinion at all."
- **Roy Brant's closing on the book**: "I am sixty-seven and the book has outlived two of the three men who are in it." (The three men: Emmet Brant, Ansel Keddie, and Roy himself — Emmet and Ansel both dead by 1955.)
- **Roy Brant on the cost of the flatness**: "Seventeen hundredths is nothing to look at on one pay slip. **Set the slips in a row for a lifetime of farming and it is a farm.**"
- **Sill's parting instructions to the man taking him down**: "Put in about the butter. **Do not put in that I said anything against the cows up there, because I did not. The cows were never the trouble.** Put in that Hobart Sill made the butter and it was good butter and he stood at the churn until the day they gave him a chair."
- **Sill on the office**: "That is the offices for you. Papers. **The Association has a paper for everything and butter for nothing.** I have had men come in here in a collar and tell me my churn was running slow when they could not have told you which end of a cow gets up first."
- **Kepp's soil mechanism (r16)**, the most detailed statement of the losing theory: a wet bottom is "a distinct kind of ground with a distinct kind of chemistry, and the water is the least part of it. Where drainage is wanting, the year's growth does not rot down as it does on a hill field; it lies sour, and the soil that results is **an acid soil, poor in lime, and poor in the phosphates**. On such ground the sweet grasses will not hold. They are crowded out within a few seasons by sedge and rush and the coarse tussock grasses... That herbage carries bulk and it carries water. What it does not carry, in any quantity worth the name, is **lime or phosphorus**, and it is cut late because such ground cannot be got onto early, and late cutting takes out what little was in it." And on the cow: "**A cow in milk is a manufactory working on a narrow margin**... If her ration is short of the minerals, she does not simply give less; **she robs her own frame to make good, and she goes on robbing it until she cannot.**" Signs: thin over the loin, rough coat, slow to condition, digestive trouble in the fall, breed later, worse feet. "They will milk, gentlemen. Wet land is generous of flow and no man on a bog meadow ever complained that his pail was light. But what is in the pail is another matter, and **the fat is the first thing to suffer, because the fat is the dearest thing the cow makes and it is the first economy she practices when she is pressed.**"
- **Kepp's remedy**: "Drain the bottom; open the old ditches and cut new; lime the ground and lime it again; sow it down when it will carry a seeding; keep the stock off it in the wet of the spring, **when a hoof does more harm in a day than a season will repair**; and never feed that hay alone. Feed it with a mixed ration and a mineral supplement, as the College directs. Fat has been worth twenty-seven cents the pound through these years, and a fifth of a point taken off every hundredweight a farm ships, year in and year out, will pay for a great deal of tile." (**Grigg's 1919 remedy is nearly word for word the same**: "Drain that bottom, sweeten it, keep the stock off it in the wet of the spring, and feed the coarse hay off it with a mixed ration and not by itself, and the Hollow will test with the rest of us.")
- **Kepp's floor discussion (r16)** — two questions, both answered away:
  - *A member* asked whether the difference might be in the **cows** rather than the land, the bottom farms being poorer men with poorer stock. *Kepp*: considered and rejected; "some of the best-bred cattle in the county stand on wet land, and some very indifferent cattle on the hills test well; and a man may improve his herd for twenty years and never get his fat up while his ground is sour."
  - *A member* asked whether **the trouble might lie in the station rather than the bottom**, as had been said in the valley for years. *Kepp*: "he had no interest in that quarrel and no competence in it; that **he had never in his life read a test and would not know how**; but that when a cause is found in the soil, in the herbage, in the condition of the stock, in the College's trial, and in every wet bottom in the county alike, **it is not good sense to go looking for a second cause in a building.**" — **The correct answer was put to him from the floor in 1926 and he waved it off with an appeal to parsimony while admitting he could not read a test.**

### 8.16 Vaught's Bulletin No. 471 introduction contradicts its own conclusion — r11's internal collapse (MAJOR)
The bulletin's concluding paragraph, which Vaught reproduces entire at the end of his own introduction, says **one part in twenty**, **76,000 lb**, and **from the first of May, 1897**. His introduction and method sections give a completely different and mutually consistent *wrong* set:

| Quantity | r11's figure | Correct figure (r04, r07 measurement sheet, r07 Item 2) |
|---|---|---|
| Pipettes purchased | **five dozen** (60) | **six dozen** (72), per the Ammon & Sons invoice |
| Mean delivery of the nine | **16.27 c.c.** | **16.72 c.c.** (digits transposed) |
| Ratio delivered/marked | **0.9244** | **0.95** exactly |
| Shortfall | (implied 1.33 c.c.) | **0.88 c.c.** |
| True average | **3.905** | **3.80** |
| Gap | **0.295** | **0.19** |
| Fat not credited | **118,000 lb** | **76,000 lb** |
| Glass in use from | **first of May, 1898** | **first of May, 1897** |
| Error as a fraction | (implied ~1 part in 13) | **one part in twenty** |

**16.27 vs 16.72 is a transposition of two digits, and every other wrong number in r11 follows mechanically from it.** The 1898 date is a separate, independent error.

**Vaught half-notices.** After computing 118,000 lb he writes: "**It should be said that this figure is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the glassware.**" That is exactly right and exactly damning — his own 3.905 cannot be squared with the 3.80 the station actually recorded in 1924 on good glass. He flags the inconsistency and publishes anyway.

**He also mis-assigns the ledger percentage**: "The gain is five and a quarter percent of the butterfat actually delivered." 5.25% is of the fat **credited**; 5.00% is of the fat **delivered**. This is precisely the denominator trap r04 and r07 spend paragraphs untangling.

**And his bog-hay ratio changes with his bad arithmetic**: "some six times the largest effect the trial could produce" (0.295 ÷ 0.05 ≈ 6) where Ivy Keddie says "four times too small" (0.19 ÷ 0.05 ≈ 4).

**Verdict:** The mechanism, the reasoning, the method and the 1911 argument in r11 are all sound and are the best exposition in the corpus. **Every quantity in it is wrong.** The correct figures are on the measurement sheet of 9 March 1958 (§6.11), which two independent transcriptions (r04, r07 Item 14) reproduce identically, and in the bulletin's own conclusion, which three independent transcriptions (r04, r07 Item 15, r11 itself) reproduce identically. **A reader who trusts r11's body over r11's own last paragraph gets the case wrong.**

### 8.17 The Brant weigh book's start date: 1 January 1897 vs 1 January 1898
- **r07 Item 4**: "kept by one household from **1 January 1897**."
- **r13, Frick's 1925 exhibit list**: "The private weigh book of Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom, **begun 1 January 1897**."
- **r09, Roy Brant, the son who inherited it and has it on the shelf behind his chair**: "From the first of January, **1898**, until the week before he died, he wrote down every can of milk that went out of this barn."
- **Verdict: unresolved, but 1897 has the weight.** Two documentary records (a 1925 exhibit list made with the book in the room, and a 1972 accession note made with the book in hand) against one son's recollection in 1955. **But note what 1898 would mean if Roy were right**: the book would begin exactly when the bad glass's effect first shows in a full year — a suspiciously tidy coincidence, and probably why the wrong year is memorable. **Keep both.**

### 8.18 The Alder Corners founding date: 1913 vs 1914
- **r10, Rosalie Cudd**: "My father, Jerome Cudd, built this factory in **nineteen thirteen**." She adds she "was a girl of twelve when the first vat was set."
- **r09, Roy Brant**: "The factory over there had opened in **1914**."
- **r12, Dell Rundle**: "in **1914** the Alder Corners Cheese Company was opened over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County, by Jerome Cudd."
- **Verdict: probably both true and not a conflict at all** — built in 1913, opened for business in 1914 is a perfectly ordinary sequence for a country factory, and Rosalie says "built," the others say "opened." **Or Rosalie is off by one on her father's own building. Low stakes either way; nothing in the case turns on it.** Note the hauling began in **1915** on all accounts.

### 8.19 Hessel Bottom: six farms or seven?
- **r02 (Effie Loomis)**: **seven farms** had rights on Hessel Bottom.
- **r09 (Roy Brant, who farms it)**: "**Six farms** had rights on it and cut hay off it and pastured it. Ours was the largest of the six."
- **r15 (the 1908 report itself, printed)**: "Hessel Bottom, where **six farms** cut hay and turn out stock"; and "Your committee visited each of the **six farms**."
- **r16 (Kepp)**: "**Six farms** cut hay and pasture stock upon it."
- **Verdict: six.** Three sources including a contemporaneous printed committee report and the largest farmer on the meadow. Effie Loomis's seven is the outlier. **Note this does not disturb the "nine families" who got the 1919 circular** — those are the Ostrey Hollow hauling families generally, not only the Bottom farms; the station also took "from three places on the hill" (r06). Six on the Bottom plus three on the hill = **nine**, which fits exactly.

### 8.20 Article VII's date: 1891 vs 1892 vs 1893
- **r02 (Effie Loomis, secretary)**: adopted at the annual meeting of **1892**, "the year after the creamery opened"; written by a man who died in 1917.
- **r12 (Rundle)**: "Article VII dates from **1891**, the year the churn was set up."
- **r15 (Grigg)**: "Payment by test came in with the **1893** meeting, **as I have always understood it**."
- **r07 Item 1**: the printed pamphlet **carries no year on its face**, only "adopted at the annual meeting."
- **The 1889–1892 minute book was lost in 1938.** Effie Loomis: "anybody who tells you what was resolved at the first four annual meetings is telling you a story he had from his father."
- **Verdict: 1892 is best supported** (Loomis was working from the record while it existed, and 1892 is the only date attached to a specific stated fact — Gideon Vose's authorship, and "the year after the creamery opened"). But **no surviving document proves it**, and this is a case where the record's own gap is the point: the by-law that governed every pay slip for fifty years cannot be dated from a document.

### 8.21 Grigg's covering letter contradicts the report printed directly beneath it (MAJOR)
Two flat contradictions inside one 1919 document:

1. **The sampling period.**
   - **Grigg's letter**: "We sampled those herds **through the whole of 1908 — every month of that year**, in the barn and on the pasture, out of the night's milk and the morning's, and we did not let a man know which day we were coming."
   - **The report, reprinted immediately below over his own signature**: "We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom **through January, February and March**."
   - **The curator (r07 Item 11) independently confirms**: "the sampling was of January, February and March 1908 only, as the report states."
   - **This matters enormously.** The single strongest objection to the bog-hay theory is that the gap was flat in summer as in winter, and the committee never sampled a summer month. **Grigg, eleven years later, remembers having done the very thing that would have refuted him.** It is not obvious whether this is self-serving or ordinary memory drift; his tone is transparently sincere, and he volunteers his own disinterest at length. **But the reprint he sponsored refutes him on the same page.**

2. **Bulletin No. 214's finding.**
   - **Grigg**: "That trial found the bog hay to depress the butter fat test by **two tenths in the winter feeding**. Two tenths, gentlemen, out of a College that had never heard of Hessel Bottom and had no reason on earth to flatter us."
   - **The bulletin's actual words** (three identical transcriptions in r04, r07 Item 12, r11): "**not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture.**"
   - **A fourfold inflation, plus complete suppression of the "nothing measurable in summer" clause** — which is the half of the sentence that destroys his case.
   - **Note the timing**: Grigg's letter is dated 1919 and says the bulletin appeared "three years after we set our names to it" — 1908 + 3 = 1911, which is right. So he has had eight years with the document and still gets it fourfold wrong.

3. **A third, smaller one**: Grigg says the 1908 committee's sampling ran "in the barn and **on the pasture**." January–March in that county is not pasture season.

**Verdict:** Grigg is honest and wrong. His disinterest is real (he was not a station patron and had no money in it), which is exactly what makes the case instructive: **a scrupulous man, reprinting his own evidence, misremembered it into the shape of his conclusion, and nobody in the valley checked the page beneath his signature.**

### 8.22 Kepp's dismissal of the 1911 interval — the one direct assault on the decisive evidence, and it fails (MAJOR)
> "It has been urged upon me, by a gentleman who I know will forgive my noticing it here, that in the summer of 1911 the testing at that station was broken off for **six weeks**, their own apparatus having failed them and a borrowed one being used, and that the figures stood higher during those six weeks than before or after. **Six weeks in the flush of the season, gentlemen. Six weeks of June and July, when every herd in the county is at its best and the stock are off the wet ground and up on the aftermath. I am astonished that anybody should found an argument on six weeks of summer figures. I could produce you six weeks in any year that would prove anything you liked.**"

**Four things wrong with this, all checkable:**
1. **It was nine weeks, not six** — 8 May to 10 July (r03's two book entries; r04, r06, r11 all say nine). Kepp shrinks it, then argues the shrunken version is too small to matter.
2. **It was 8 May to 10 July, not "June and July"** — it starts in early May, before the flush.
3. **The flush argument is self-refuting.** If flush season raised the figures, the station's average would rise every summer of every year. It never did: the summary leaf says **3.61 "or within three hundredths of it every year since," save the nine weeks of 1911**. The flush was there in 1910 and 1912 too. **The only thing that distinguished those nine weeks from every other summer in twenty-six years was the borrowed measure.**
4. **The figures fell back on the exact day the station's own measure returned** — 10 July, mid-flush, with the herds still on the aftermath. Kepp's mechanism predicts no such thing.

**And the deeper failure**: Kepp's whole paper argues that the gap is caused by seasonal wet-ground feeding, while simultaneously his own case rests on a gap that is present "in every month of the year." He never notices the tension. **He is the most rigorous-sounding advocate of the wrong answer, and he is undone by a fact he restates himself.**

Note the "gentleman who I know will forgive my noticing it here" — someone in Ordell County was making the 1911 argument publicly in 1926, **thirty-two years before Ivy Keddie**. We are not told who. (**Candidates: Wilbur Teague, who also uses the "six weeks" figure and was in Ashlin where Kepp practiced; or Strawn, who was still on the circuit — though Strawn refuses to hold theories. Teague's "six weeks" matching Kepp's "six weeks" is suggestive.**)

### 8.23 Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook WAS an exhibit before the board (MAJOR — the case's worst irony)
**Frick's exhibit list (r13), reprinted from his own file:**
> 4. The circuit notebook of the state dairy inspector **for the year 1923**.

**That notebook contains the entry of 12 June 1923:** "Ran their milk pipettes against the burette. **These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them**, and told the manager to order new."

So: **the written record of the condemnation was physically in the room, one of only four documents before the board, and Effie Loomis — who took down every word — records that the crate was never mentioned in any of the three sessions.** The board had the answer in its hands as an exhibit and nobody read the page. Frick spent the rest of his life wondering "whether the answer was in that room and simply was not in any of the three stories" — **it was in that room, in exhibit 4, in the inspector's own hand.**

Compounding it: the board held its **second and third sessions in the station's own weigh room**, where they "satisfied ourselves upon" every physical appliance and watched a test made — **on the new glass**, since the old crate had been condemned twenty-one months earlier and was by then in the loft. Frick: "Every physical appliance of that station was before us and we satisfied ourselves upon it."

This supersedes and sharpens §8.7. The failure was not that the evidence was absent; it was that **nobody knew what question to ask of the document in front of them.** Frick's own diagnosis is exactly right: "**I did not understand chemistry.**"

### 8.24 The number of surviving pipettes: nine vs eleven
- **r04 (Ivy Keddie), r07 Item 14, the measurement sheet, r11 (Vaught)**: **nine**, of which **two chipped at the tip**.
- **r12's 1959 footnote (Rundle)**: "**Eleven pipettes** out of the station's old condemned crate were found still to be in existence."
- **Verdict: nine.** Rundle is an aged schoolteacher adding a footnote from hearsay about work done at a college he refers the reader to. **Possibly he counted the two chipped ones separately from a report of "nine measured," or simply misheard.**

### 8.25 The Alder Corners overlap: fourteen seasons or sixteen?
- **r04 (Ivy Keddie)**: "where the two books overlap — **fourteen seasons** — that same milk off that same wet ground ran about nineteen hundredths higher at Alder Corners than at home."
- **r10 (Rosalie Cudd)**: the men hauled **sixteen seasons**, 1915–1930, and she supplies all sixteen figures.
- **Reconciliation**: the station's bad glass came out in **July 1923**. Seasons 1915–1923 inclusive is nine; 1924–1930 is seven. Neither split gives fourteen. **But** the comparison Ivy is making is between Alder Corners and *the station's affected figures*, and she also uses only the seasons for which she had station data for those specific four men. **The likeliest reading: Rosalie sent sixteen seasons; Ivy could match fourteen of them against the station book.** Unresolved but harmless — every one of the sixteen sits at 3.78–3.82 and the conclusion is the same whichever subset you take.
- **Also note r07 Item 13 dates the copied Alder Corners figures to "the season of 1919" specifically** — that is the *book entry* Jerome Cudd wrote at the end of summer 1919 (§6.16), not the extent of what Rosalie sent. **r10 shows she sent the whole run.**

### 8.26 Whose glass opened the station — and the quiet vindication of Hobart Sill
Everyone knew the first season's glass was "borrowed of Larrow Green" (the test book's own first leaf). **Nobody until r14 said whose.** Sill made it up from his own bench, out of glass he "had used and knew." And that glass produced **3.80 — the true figure** — in 1896, the only clean year in the station's history until 1924.

**The irony is total**: the man whose honest glass gave the only accurate reading the station ever made in its first twenty-eight years spent forty-one years insisting the patrons were watering their cans, weighed the excess fat coming down the hill twice a day with his own hands, wrote it in his own book "on page after page after page," and said "**that is cream for you**."

### 8.27 What each late source got right that nobody else did
- **r09 (Roy Brant)** — the two-column weigh book proves **the weighing was honest on both sides**: "Two scales, his and the station's, kept by two men who by the end were not speaking to one another, and **the scales never fell out at all.** Whatever was wrong at that station — and something was wrong at that station for a very long time — **it was not the weighing.**" This eliminates an entire class of explanation (short weight at intake) that nobody else even tests. It also gives the reason for the Brant–Keddie silence, and a second independent record of the 1911 rise.
- **r10 (Rosalie Cudd)** — sixteen seasons of external assay at ~3.80, and the cheese-as-witness argument against watering.
- **r11 (Vaught)** — the mechanism, and why an instrument that *is* the standard cannot be caught from inside.
- **r12 (Rundle)** — the theft theory refuted from the ledger in 1939, in print, by a man with no stake, nineteen years early.
- **r13 (Frick)** — the exhibit list (which puts the condemnation record in the hearing room), the attribution of each of the three theories to a named man, and Keddie's private corridor admission.
- **r14 (Sill)** — whose glass opened the station; the cream running heavy every year for forty-one years, weighed twice daily; Larrow Green's own average of 3.78; the 1896 intake figure.
- **r15 (Grigg)** — the full 1908 report, including its explicit clearance of everybody of dishonesty; and a documented case of a witness misremembering his own evidence into his conclusion.
- **r16 (Kepp)** — the fullest statement of the losing theory, the only direct attack on the 1911 interval, and the floor question that put the right answer to him in 1926.

### 8.28 Keddie's private suspicion — r13's disclosure, in tension with r03
**Frick (r13):** "I add one thing which is not in the minutes. **In the corridor at Ashlin, Keddie told me privately that he had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the room.** He was not a bitter man about it. He had lived among them thirty years and would not stand up and accuse his neighbors, and I record it here because **it has been said since that he went to his grave accusing nobody, which is true, and that he suspected nobody, which is not.**"

**Against this: r03, Keddie's own unsent letter and his statement**, in which he refuses to speculate at all, says flatly "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are," and lets a question mark stand fourteen years rather than "put anything in its place that I do not know."

**Reconciliation:** not necessarily a contradiction. Frick is careful — he distinguishes *accusing* (which Keddie never did, and Frick affirms) from *suspecting* (which Frick says he did, privately, once, in a corridor). It squares with the human situation: the tester whose figures were low, believing the patrons must be at fault, because that was the only account left that did not make him a thief. **But it is a single unminuted recollection at twenty-one years' distance, and it is the one place where the case's most scrupulous witness is reported saying something his own papers would not.** Also poignant: **his suspicion of the patrons and their suspicion of him were both wrong, and both about people innocent in exactly the same way.**

## 9. WHAT A FRESH READER SHOULD CARRY FORWARD

1. **The answer is the glass**: pipettes short by exactly 1 part in 20 (delivering 16.72 c.c. where blown 17.6), in service **1 May 1897 – 12 June 1923**. Recorded 3.61 = true 3.80. 76,000 lb of butterfat = $19,000 at 25c.
2. **The three false theories** (bog hay, watered milk, stolen cream) each have specific, documented refutations: Bulletin 214's five hundredths plus the Alder Corners sixteen-season cross-check at 3.78–3.82; Strawn's lactometer on every visit for 29 years plus the cheesemaker's "cheese is a witness"; and the ledger's own station-gain line — corroborated physically by Hobart Sill weighing the cream in heavy every year for forty-one years.
3. **The 1911 nine weeks** (8 May – 10 July) are the natural experiment that settles it, recorded independently in Keddie's test book (with a question mark) **and in the Brant private weigh book**, flagged and ignored for 47 years. Kepp's 1926 attempt to argue it away is the only direct attack on it and fails on four checkable points.
4. **Every witness who was in a position to look, didn't** — and now **five** of them say so in nearly identical words (Loomis, Keddie, Vira Toland, Rosalie Cudd, Sill inverted).
5. **The evidence was in the arbitration room.** Frick's exhibit 4 was the state inspector's 1923 circuit notebook, containing the condemnation entry, and nobody mentioned the crate in any of the three sessions.
6. **The unreliable narrators and their specific errors:**
   - **Vira Toland** — $3,500 for $3,800; nephew for son; hearing compressed to 1919; certain of theft; "first cousin once removed" for second cousin.
   - **Selby Vose** — 12 July for 12 June; 3.90 and "twenty-one hundredths" for 3.80 and nineteen; causal order backwards.
   - **Wilbur Teague** — 44 million for 40; 3.16 for 3.61; six weeks for nine. **And both his bad numbers propagate: 3.16 into Frick, 44 million into Rundle.**
   - **Halbert Nym** — Items 3 and 4 attributions swapped; crate found at Ashlin instead of the station loft; four columns for five. **(But he was right about Rosalie Cudd being a daughter — Ivy Keddie was wrong.)**
   - **Leland Vaught** — **16.27 for 16.72 and every figure that follows from it** (0.9244, 3.905, 0.295, 118,000 lb); five dozen for six; 1 May 1898 for 1897; 5.25% attached to the wrong denominator. His prose is the best in the corpus and his arithmetic is unusable. He flags the inconsistency himself and publishes anyway.
   - **Dell Rundle** — Article VII to 1891; 12 July for 12 June; 44 million; loss of $19,600 "set out to the dollar"; eleven pipettes for nine; nephew for son. **But he retracted the theft theory correctly and in print in 1939.**
   - **Alonzo Frick** — 3.16 for 3.61; 67,000 lb for 76,000; Keddie testing "since it opened in 1896" (r01 says 1895). **His exhibit list and his account of the three theories are gold.**
   - **Hobart Sill** — 27 cents for 25; "a 3.85 and no better" (close, but a guess); still blames water in 1934 while holding the disproof in his own receiving book.
   - **Nyle Grigg** — claims twelve months' sampling where his own reprinted report says three; Bulletin 214 as "two tenths" for five hundredths; payment by test from "the 1893 meeting."
   - **Roland Kepp** — Bulletin 214 as "two tenths"; the gap as 0.21; six weeks for nine; "June and July" for May–July; the flush argument that his own data refute.
   - **Roy Brant** — weigh book begun 1898 (documents say 1897); "the state man left his own measure" in 1911 (unique to him). **His 0.17 is not an error — it is a correctly computed different comparison, against Larrow Green's own 3.78.**
7. **The most reliable sources**: Strawn's contemporaneous field books; **the transcribed documents themselves**, which agree word-for-word across r02/r03/r04/r05/r07/r11/r12/r13 wherever two or more sources quote the same paper; Effie Loomis on what the record does and does not contain; the Alder Corners table; and the Brant weigh book's two agreeing columns. **In every single conflict in this case, the document beats the recollection — including when the recollector is the man who wrote the document.**
8. **Two percentages, both correct**: 5% of fat delivered = 5.25% of fat credited = 76,000 lb = $19,000. The award was $3,800, one fifth.
9. **The governing pattern of the whole case**: *consistency reads as correctness*. Teague ("a figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture"), Sill ("so it ran heavy — so does everybody's"), and Vaught ("it has the appearance of excellent work") each state it independently, from the ledger, the platform, and the laboratory. The error was invisible **because** it never varied.
