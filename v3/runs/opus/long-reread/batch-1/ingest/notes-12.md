### 0.1 C1 — the Ostrey Hollow arithmetic. CLOSED, all four parts.

**(a) The proportion, from the glassware.** The station's milk-test pipettes were blown to deliver
**17.6 c.c.** (the invoice specifies "17.6 c.c."; the test bottles were "8 per cent neck," ruled to
read 8 per cent *for that one charge and no other*). Ivy Keddie delivered nine surviving pipettes
against a calibrated burette at Farland, **9 March 1958**, five runs each, and got a mean of
**16.72 c.c.** — **short 0.88 c.c.**
**16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly.** The station delivered **95 hundredths of its mark**, so every test
it ever made on that glass was **low by one part in twenty (5 per cent)** — not by a fixed number of
points but proportionally. **recorded = true × 0.95.**

**(b) The true average test, and the shortfall in points.**
Recorded station average, 1898–1922 (and effectively to June 1923): **3.61**.
**3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80.** True average test = **3.80**. **Recorded tests were short by 0.19 of a
point.**
Four wholly independent observations reproduce 3.80: the 1896 season on borrowed sound glass (3.80);
the nine weeks of 1911 on a borrowed measure (3.80); 1924, after the glass changed (3.80); and the
Alder Corners sixteen-season table of the same cows' summer milk (mean 3.7994 ≈ 3.80).

**(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited.**
Milk through the station over the affected years = **40,000,000 lb** (Loomis, Ivy, Nym, Vaught, all
from the annual ledgers; independently checked by 1,600,000 lb/yr × 25 years from the station-gain
entry).
**40,000,000 × 0.19% = 76,000 lb of butterfat.**
Affected span: **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923.**

**(d) What it was worth, and what the patrons got.**
Price of butterfat through those years = **27 cents the pound**, now attested **twice
independently**: Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green, 1934 ("twenty-seven cents the pound,
take one year with another"); and **NEW — Dr. Roland Kepp, Ashlin, 1926: "Fat has been worth
twenty-seven cents the pound through these years."** Two men, two ends of the county, eight years
apart, no channel between them.
**76,000 lb × $0.27 = $20,520.**
The patrons received the arbitration award of **$3,800 (4 May 1925)**.
**3,800 ÷ 20,520 = 0.18518… ≈ 18.5 per cent** — a little over one sixth, a little under one fifth.
*Caveat to state:* 27¢ is a period average ("one year with another"), not a year-by-year price; the
money figure is therefore good to the nearest hundred dollars, not to the cent.

### 0.2 C2 — Emmet Brant's 1910. **NOW SOLVABLE.** r17 supplies the missing leaf.

Hazel Brant, 1981, quoting the head of the 1910 page of her grandfather's weigh book — the one year
she says she is certain of, because "it is written at the head of the page in his hand and I have
read that page a thousand times":

> **"Nineteen ten. Two hundred thousand pounds even, delivered to the station… His scale and theirs
> agreeing, he wrote… And paid on a test of three forty-two."**

Emmet Brant is the largest patron (r02 §5, r03, r09, r17 all say so).

- **(a) Credited:** 200,000 lb × **3.42%** = **6,840 lb of butterfat.**
- **(b) Owed and not credited:** true test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = **3.60**; true fat = 200,000 × 3.60% =
  **7,200 lb**; **7,200 − 6,840 = 360 lb.**
  Cross-check against the standing identity: pounds not credited = credited ÷ 19. **6,840 ÷ 19 = 360
  exactly.** ✓
- **(c) In money:** 360 × $0.27 = **$97.20** for that one year, on one farm.

*Two internal checks that 3.42 is genuine:* it divides by 0.95 to a clean 3.60, which a fabricated
or misremembered figure would not; and Hazel warned in advance that her quantities are sound and her
years are not (§0.6), which is exactly the profile of a witness reliable on this datum.
*One oddity to flag:* 3.42 is **below** the station's own recorded average of 3.61, though Brant had
the biggest and (on the Alder Corners evidence) not the poorest herd. No source explains it. Do not
over-read it; individual farms sat above and below the station mean, and the table's four Bottom men
ran 3.78–3.82 as a *group*, not individually.

### 0.4 B2 — Ivy Keddie ↔ Hazel Brant. **SETTLED: second cousins.** (Was unanswerable through r14.)

r17, first person, spelling it out because she is tired of being got wrong:

> "**Orra was Ansel Keddie's wife, and Orra was a half sister to my grandfather Emmet, by his
> father's second wife.** … Then their boy Duncan and my father Roy were first cousins — the
> children of a brother and a sister, which is what makes first cousins. **And Ivy is Duncan's girl,

The derivation checks: Orra (half-sister) and Emmet are one generation; Duncan and Roy are their

half-blood does not change the degree, only the closeness.

**What the sources leave open about the intervening generations** — say all of this:
- **Orra's mother is unnamed** (only "his father's second wife"), and **Emmet's father is unnamed**.
  So the shared ancestor is a man whose name never reaches the corpus.
- **Duncan Keddie's wife (Ivy's mother) is never named**; **Roy Brant's wife (Hazel's mother) is
  never named** — she appears once only, burning the water circular ("My mother read hers twice and
  put it in the stove").
- **No birth or death dates** for Orra, Duncan, or Emmet. Hazel refuses to guess at the year Emmet
  died ("don't ask me, I'll only guess").
- Ansel Keddie's death is dated only relatively: he "did not live to see" Ivy's 1958 finding.
- Whether Duncan ever farmed, lived in the valley, or had other children is unstated.

**The point that matters historically, and it is r17's alone:** *Ansel Keddie married into the
Brants.* "**The man they said was cheating the patrons was married to the sister of the patron who
lost the most by it, and ate at his table, and the two of them argued about drainage for forty
years.**" The tester and the largest loser were brothers-in-law. Nobody in twenty-nine years of
accusation appears to have said so out loud.

### 0.5 B3 and B4 — the Voses.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose** — first president of the Association, 1889–1911, who
**wrote the by-laws himself, including Article VII**, and **died in 1917**.
Settled in Selby's own printed voice, 1926 (r08): "**My father, who was your first president and who
wrote the by-law under which every patron in this Association is paid on the test of the station
where he delivers…**" Contemporaneous, self-interested, and decisive; it overrides r01 and r12, which
both call Selby the *nephew* — and those two are one source, not two, being village hearsay and
village hearsay written down.
**Why it matters at Ostrey Hollow:** the clause that trapped the patrons — "*the test of the station
where he delivers shall govern*" — was written by Selby's father, and Selby was the manager
(1913–1938) who had to defend it, who was told by Strawn on 12 June 1923 to order new glass, who
never told the patrons why, and who in 1926 was still explaining the 1924 rise as improved farming.
The rule left "a dissatisfied man with no remedy on earth except to quarrel with a figure" (Frick),
and the man who had to be quarrelled with was the son of the man who wrote it. Selby's defence of the
station was also a defence of his father's draftsmanship. The author was dead by 1917 and could not
be asked what he had meant.

**B4. Vose ↔ Keddie: no relationship of any kind is attested, and I believe none existed.** What the
sources establish is an *institutional* relation only: Gideon Vose wrote the rule under which Ansel
Keddie's tests were made final and unappealable; Selby Vose was Keddie's employer through the whole
of the dispute and the officer who dismissed him (a new man was at the tester's bench by 1925). r17
gives the Keddie–Brant kinship in detail and says nothing whatever about the Voses, which is itself
evidence: the family that would know, and that recites the genealogy for the record, does not place
the Voses anywhere in it. The Voses are Larrow Green people; the Keddies and Brants are head-of-the-
valley people. **State it as: no kinship or marriage tie is attested; the connection is entirely one
of office, and it is the office that does all the damage.**

## 2. STORY THREAD A — THE FOUNDING AND ARTICLE VII (1889–1892)

- **1889**: farmers met at **Larrow Green** and organized the **Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery
  Association**. **Gideon Vose** elected first president, held it **1889–1911** — twenty-two years,
  longest of any man. (r02 §1)
- **r12's scene**: they met **in the schoolhouse, in cold weather**; the old men remembered chiefly
  that **it went on very late and that somebody's horse got loose in the middle of it.** Motive:
  *"They were tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking whatever the merchant chose to allow them
  for it, and they had worked out — the way farmers do, slowly and then all at once — that the way to
  be paid properly for a thing is to own the place that buys it."*
- Before the creamery: members sold milk where they could, "no common test and no common price, and
  every man's arithmetic was his own." (r02 §2)
- **1891**: main creamery built at **Larrow Green, on Larrow Creek** (r02) / "on the creek below the
  mill" (r12); same building standing in 1941. r12: the churn was set up in it **"and the valley began
  to be paid in cash once a month, which had never happened to it before."**
  **r14 corroborates the plant physically**: Sill came to that churn room in **1893**, when "there was
  no floor in this room but plank, and we cooled with creek ice out of the pond."
- **Gideon Vose drafted the by-laws himself**; not a lawyer; "they read as a farmer wrote them" (r02).
  **r12's portrait**: *"a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the law and thought
  better of it, and he wrote the by-laws himself, at his own kitchen table, in a hand that the
  Association's clerks were still complaining about thirty years later."* r12 also reports a saying
  attributed to Selby Vose: that Gideon *"wrote by-laws the way other men build stone wall — slowly,
  and to last."*
- **ARTICLE VII — transcribed IDENTICALLY, word for word, by THREE independent hands**: r02 (Loomis,
  from the minutes/printed pamphlet), r07 Item 1 (Nym, from the printed pamphlet), r12 (Rundle,
  printed whole in 1939):
  > *"Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of
  > pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the
  > milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."*
  **r13 (Frick) paraphrases it accurately from the board's own understanding**: "The Association pays
  a patron for the butter fat in his milk and not for the weight of it. The fat is found by test, and
  the test of the station where the man delivers is the test that governs him." — a fourth,
  legally-trained reading of the same clause. **The wording is beyond dispute.**
- **CONFLICT ON THE DATE, now THREE-WAY.**
  - **r02 (Loomis, from the minute book): adopted at the annual meeting of 1892.**
  - r12 (Rundle): "Article VII dates from 1891, the year the churn was set up."
  - **NEW — r15 (Grigg, 1919), twice:** "*Payment by test came in with the 1893 meeting, as I have
    always understood it, and from that year a difference of this kind stopped being a matter of
    opinion and became a matter of money*"; and in the report itself, "*Since payment by test came in
    among us and the pound of butter fat took the place of the pound of milk as the measure of a man's
    month…*" (undated there).
  **Resolve for 1892.** Loomis is the secretary with the minute book in her own hand, setting the
  article down entire precisely because it has been misquoted at her for forty years. Rundle writes
  valley memory and ties the date to the churn by association. **Grigg explicitly hedges — "as I have
  always understood it" — which is a man flagging hearsay**, and he was not a member of the drafting
  meeting; his interest is in the *consequence* of payment-by-test, not its date.
  **Complication (r07): the printed by-laws carry no year on their face, only "adopted at the annual
  meeting"** — so 1892 rests on Loomis's minutes alone, and **the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the
  office move of 1938.** An undated pamphlet is exactly what produces a 1891/1892/1893 spread.
  **Flag: the date rests on one document; the text does not.**
- **The last clause is the hinge.** Loomis: nobody quotes it — *"the test of the station where he
  delivers shall govern."* Not the creamery's test, not the valley average, not a re-test anywhere
  else. **The station's test, and no appeal from it.** She calls it **"the hinge of the whole Ostrey
  Hollow business."** Written by a man who **died in 1917** and could not be asked afterward.
- **Rundle reaches the same reading independently in 1939**: *"But mark the last clause… Not the test
  of the creamery. Not any test but the one made at his own door. Gideon Vose put that in for
  convenience and it was convenient for thirty years, and it is the sentence on which the whole
  trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned."* He also defends the rule in principle: *"A man
  who keeps good cows and feeds them ought not to be paid the same as the man who keeps poor ones, and
  the pound of milk is a liar where the pound of butterfat is not."* And: it was thought a hard rule
  at the time and **there were men who left the Association over it.**
- **Frick (r13) states the legal consequence more sharply than anyone**: *"I say nothing against the
  rule. But it makes the test the whole of the bargain, and it leaves a dissatisfied man with no remedy
  on earth except to quarrel with a figure."*
- **Selby Vose confirms authorship AND parentage himself, in print, 1926 (r08)** — see §0.5.
- **Records gap**: the **1889–1892 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move** — carried out with
  the rest and never arrived. Loomis searched everywhere. *"Anybody who tells you what was resolved at
  the first four annual meetings is telling you a story he had from his father."*
- Officers in Loomis's time: Loomis secretary 1901–1940; **Wilbur Teague** bookkeeper from **1905**;
  **Hobart Sill** buttermaker at Larrow Green from **1893**; **Selby Vose**, "son of the late
  president," manager **1913–1938**.

## 3. STORY THREAD B — THE STATION, THE SHORT GLASS, AND THE 26-YEAR SHORTFALL (1896–1923)

### The station and its catchment
- **Skimming station at the head of Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green** —
  the distance given independently by r06 (1907), r08 (Vose 1926), r11 (Vaught), r12 (Rundle), r13
  (Frick) and r14 (Sill, "seven miles of hill road") — **six independent statements.** Low whitewashed
  building, cold inside even in August, smelled of milk and steam and hot iron (r01). It took the
  upper farms' milk, **weighed it, sampled and tested it, separated it, sent the cream down to Larrow
  Green, and gave the skim back to the patron** for hogs and calves (r01, r02, r13).
- **r12 on why it existed**: *"in the nineties the pull down was three hours in mud and could not be
  done at all in March. So the Association built a skimming station up there, and it opened in 1896…
  The building is a plain one, boarded and battened, with the intake room at the road end and **a loft
  over it that I believe has never been cleared out since it was put up**; and behind it the creek,
  which in a wet April comes up to the sill and has twice come over it."* — written 1939, nineteen
  years before Ivy climbed that ladder.
- **SIX FARMS ON HESSEL BOTTOM — now attested by FIVE independent sources.** r09 (Roy Brant): "*Hessel
  Bottom is a hundred and sixty acres of wet meadow lying under the hill at Ostrey Hollow. **SIX
  farms** had rights on it and cut hay off it and pastured it.*" **r15 (the 1908 report, contemporary
  document): "Hessel Bottom, where six farms cut hay and turn out stock"** and "*Your committee visited
  each of the six farms, some of them three and four times.*" **r16 (Kepp 1926): "Six farms cut hay and
  pasture stock upon it."** **r17 (Hazel Brant): "Six. Hessel Bottom was six farms and no more."**
  Plus the chain's own reconciliation. **Six is beyond dispute.**
- **THE NINE FAMILIES — TWO COMPETING RECONCILIATIONS, AND THE QUESTION IS NOW OPEN.**
  The 1919 water circular went to **nine families** (r01, r03, r09, r14, r17 — five sources agree on
  nine).
  - *Reading 1 (the chain's, through r14):* **six Bottom farms + Strawn's "three places on the hill"
    (1909) = nine families.** This makes the catchment mixed, and it carries an argument the other
    reading loses: **hill milk is not Bottom milk, so wet bog hay cannot depress it, but a short
    pipette depresses everything alike.**
  - *Reading 2 (NEW — r17, Hazel Brant, first-hand-ish):* all nine were Bottom families. "*He sent
    that round to nine families on that bottom, nine letters… **Nine families, six farms, if you are
    counting — a farm can hold more than one family and generally did.** He sent nine and he was
    answered by none of them, which in that valley is a shout.*"
  **Do not resolve this. State both.** Hazel was a small child in 1919 and remembers her mother
  burning the letter, not the distribution list; but she is the only source that explicitly confronts
  the six/nine arithmetic, and multi-family farms are ordinary. Strawn's three hill places are a
  documented fact either way (they delivered to the station); whether they got letters is not stated
  by anyone. **The hill-milk argument survives under both readings**, because the hill places
  delivered to that station regardless of who was circularized.
- **The Brant farm was the largest of the six**: *"sixty-odd acres of the meadow, and thirty-eight cows
  in a good year, which for this valley was a herd"* (r09). Corroborated by r02 §5 ("the station's
  largest patron in every year of which we have a record"), r01, r03 ("brought more milk to my door
  than any other man on the Bottom"), and **r17 ("My grandfather had the largest piece of it").**
- Hessel Bottom from inside (r09): water stands on the low end into May; a soft place by the alders
  that has taken a horse to the hock; the hay is coarse and cuts late; *"a cow will eat it and do well
  enough, whatever has been said about that hay by men who never fed it."*
  **r17 adds**: "*it was wet — you could cut hay off it in a dry August and in a wet one you could lose
  a horse. There was a spring at the upper end that ran cold enough to ache your wrist in July, and
  that is where the cans stood.*"
  **r16 (Kepp) adds the veterinary description**: springs come out at the foot of the slope and run
  year round; part under water every spring and not fit to walk on till well into May; herbage sedge,
  rush and coarse tussock grasses; sedge hay is the winter dependence "and very nearly the whole of
  it"; an acid soil poor in lime and phosphates; late cutting takes out what little was in it.
- **Opening date: 1896.** Vira Toland says Keddie went up "in ninety-five, the year before they opened
  it"; **r17 (Hazel) makes the same error — "Ansel Keddie went up to that station in ninety-five."**
  **Against 1895** stand Keddie's own first leaf and his own count (29 seasons, 1896–1924, and he "did
  not test a pound in the season of 1925"); Strawn (1907, the station "opened before my time"); Vose
  (r08, "a station in daily use since 1896"); Rundle (r12, "it opened in 1896"); Frick (r13, "since it
  opened in 1896"); and Sill (r14, "that was ninety-six, in May, and there was a crowd went up for
  it"). **Resolve for 1896 — six independent lines against two carriers of a valley slip.**
- **First leaf of the test book**, quoted identically by r03 and r07 Item 3:
  > *"Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our
  > own comes. A. Keddie."*
- **INTERNAL CONFLICT IN r03 — SETTLED.** Keddie's letter-prose says the 1896 glass was "borrowed off
  the **Ashlin creamery**"; his quoted first leaf says **"borrowed of Larrow Green."** **r14 settles it
  in the lender's own voice**: *"they had no outfit of their own. Not a piece… And I said no, they can
  have mine. I went to my own bench in this room and I made up an outfit for them out of my own glass,
  my own, that I had used and knew, and it went up the valley on the wagon and they opened on it. Their
  own came the spring after and they sent mine back to me, and one piece of it was chipped and I never
  said a word about that either."* **Resolve for LARROW GREEN, from both ends of the loan.** No Ashlin
  creamery is attested anywhere. Mechanical consequence: **the 1896 season was run on Sill's own
  known-good working glass**, which is why 1896 reads 3.80.

### THE OUTFIT — the invoice, itemized (r07 Item 2), DECISIVE
> **Invoice, Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, Tarnet, 14 April 1897.** One leaf, folded in three.
> *Sold to the Larrow Valley Co-op. Creamery Assn., for the Ostrey Hollow station:*
> *6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20*
> *12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at .22 — 31.68*
> *1 acid measure — .90*
> *1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00*
> *Freight — 2.15*
> *Total — 77.93*

Arithmetic closes exactly: 72 × 0.35 = 25.20; 144 × 0.22 = 31.68; sum = **77.93**.
Nym adds: "the office file has [it] in use at the station **from 1 May 1897**, Larrow Green having lent
until then." That matches **Bulletin 471's concluding paragraph, 1 May 1897**, from an entirely
different record, and **Sill's "their own came the spring after"** from a third.
**Consequence: the pipettes were short FROM NEW.** That kills Selby Vose's "apparatus wears" theory
outright — blown glass stems do not wear shorter — and the step from 3.80 (1896, Sill's glass) to 3.61
(1898 on) lands precisely where the new glass came into use, not gradually across twenty-six years.
Note the **"8 per cent neck"**: the bottle neck is ruled to read to 8 per cent **for one charge and no
other** — the whole mechanism Ivy and Vaught rely on.

- **CONFLICT (r11 §III): "five dozen pipettes were purchased." Resolve for SIX DOZEN (72)** — the
  invoice line is itemized, priced, and closes to the stated total; five dozen would be $21.00 and
  would break the $77.93.
- **CONFLICT (r11 §III): "The station's own glass went into use on the first of May, 1898."**

- **CONFLICT (NEW, r17): "The crate came out from Tarnet in ninety-nine."** **Resolve for 1897** — the

### Keddie's method and book (from the unsent letter)
- Day began half past five; wagons up in the dark; lamps lit, scale swinging free before the first
  wagon. Every can weighed, the weight called and written before the milk went anywhere. **The scale
  was tested by the state's man on his rounds and was true; in 29 seasons no patron ever disputed a
  weight and was right.**
- Sample from each patron's milk as weighed, into **his own numbered jar**, **made up over ten days**.
  On testing morning: warmed blood-warm through, poured vessel to vessel until the risen cream was
  carried down through the whole.
- Charge drawn up in the measure to the mark on the stem, tip wiped, blown out into the numbered
  bottle; acid down the side; bottle turned till the milk darkened; **four bottles in the hand tester,
  weighted against each other, cover down, crank at the taught speed**; fat read off in per cent
  against the rulings **with a pair of dividers.**
- Book kept in **five columns: date, patron, pounds, test, fat.** Errors crossed and initialed;
  **never a figure written over a figure.** (*Minor conflict*: r07 Item 3 describes four columns.
  Trivial.)
- Statement to the board, quoted identically by r03 and **by r13 (Frick, who heard it)**: *"I have
  tested as I was taught and as the state man saw me do it, **thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine
  seasons**, and I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are… I have never taken a pound of
  cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by
  himself."* — **two independent transcriptions of one spoken statement, agreeing on the phrase.**
- **Keddie's claim about "the state man" is corroborated from the state man's own books.** Strawn's
  standing routine at every plant, in the same order every time: (1) **the scales**, against sealed
  weights he carried, both intake and cream scale, loaded light and loaded heavy; (2) **the
  lactometer** on the day's milk, patron by patron where there was any question, for added water;
  (3) **the boiler, water supply and cold room**; (4) — "the one most men grudged me" — **he stood at
  the tester's elbow and watched him make a test through from the sample to the reading, and did not
  let him hurry it.**
  - **1907**: "Their tester keeps his book in his own hand, and his hand is better than most. **Watched
    him through two tests. He works slow and clean and reads his own figure without being coaxed to
    it.**"
  - **1911, June**: "Scales true. **Watched a test through. Nothing here to note.**"
  - **Scales true** at Ostrey Hollow in 1907, 1909, 1911, 1912, 1915, 1916, 1919, 1920 (twice), 1922,
    1923 (June), 1923 (Oct), 1924, 1925, 1928, 1931, 1934, 1936 — **every recorded visit, without
    exception, across 29 years.**
  - **Lactometer, no added water** at Ostrey Hollow in 1912, 1916, 1919, 1920 (twice), 1922, 1923
    (June, "as always here"), 1924, 1925, 1928, 1934.
  - **Keddie's procedure was watched through, sample to reading, and never once faulted.** His
    technique was sound; only his glass was wrong. A proportional error in the *instrument* survives a
    perfect *method*, and Strawn's routine before 1921 had no way to catch it.
- **THE SCALES ARE CONFIRMED FROM THE PATRON SIDE (r09, and now r17)** — a genuinely independent
  instrument. Roy Brant on his father's book: two columns per delivery day — the weight of every can
  off **Emmet's own scale in his own barn** before the wagon went, and **the figure the station gave
  him back.** *"The two columns agreed… Day after day, month after month, for the whole run of years…
  barring a pound or two here and there, and those were days when a can got slopped on the hill. Two
  scales, his and the station's, kept by two men who by the end were not speaking to one another, and
  the scales never fell out at all. Whatever was wrong at that station — and something was wrong at
  that station for a very long time — it was not the weighing."*
  **r17 adds the man's motive and manner**: "*He weighed his own milk on his own scale before he ever
  put a can on the wagon, and then the station weighed it again at the intake, and he wrote both
  figures down side by side. **If the two ever disagreed he would stand in the doorway of that weigh
  room with his hat on until somebody found out why. They generally agreed. That was the point of
  him.**"* And on the 1910 leaf: "*His scale and theirs agreeing, he wrote, which was his way of saying
  that he had checked and was not going to make a speech about it.*"
  **The 1908 committee independently tried both**: "*We tried the station scales against a known weight
  and we tried the patrons' own scales against the station's*" (r15). **Three independent confirmations
  of the weighing, from three decades.**
- **r13 adds a third confirmation of the physical plant.** Frick had the board's **second and third
  sessions held at Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's own weigh room**, "so that the board might
  see the plant it was to pass upon instead of hearing it described by men with an interest in the
  description. That was my doing and I am still content with it… **We saw the scales tried. We saw the
  sampling done and the test made**, and I will say plainly that I did not understand the test then and
  do not understand it now; I am a lawyer and the thing is chemistry. But I could see that it was done
  the same way twice, and that the men who did it were not hiding their hands."
  **The corpus's sharpest irony: in 1925 a board of three sat in that weigh room, watched the test made
  with the NEW (sound) glass, and never asked what the OLD glass had delivered.**

### The 1922 summary leaf (quoted identically in r03, r04 AND r07 — one document, three transcriptions)
> *"Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or
> within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their
> place, when it stood at 3.80."*

Nym's gloss (r07 Item 3): "The summary leaf gathers the yearly averages: 3.80 for the first season,
3.61 from 1898 on." **Provenance of the test book**: kept at the station from **4 May 1896**;
**impounded by the board of arbitration in March 1925**; in the Association's office at Ashlin
**1925–1959**; received at the Historical Rooms in **1959**. **r13's exhibit list dates the book's own
coverage precisely: "4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924, in the hand of Ansel Keddie."**

### The nine weeks of 1911 — the accidental controlled experiment
- Two entries as Keddie made them:
  > *"May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure."*
  > *"July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."*
  The question mark is Keddie's; he let it stand fourteen years.
- Ivy: **8 May to 10 July 1911**, nine weeks; the borrowed measure **came up from Larrow Green with the
  cream wagon**; same patrons, herds, pasture, season, acid, bottles, same man at the crank — **one
  object changed** — the average went to 3.80 and returned to 3.61 the day the station's own measure
  came back.
- **Strawn was physically in the station during the window** ("1911. Ostrey Hollow, **June**… Scales
  true. Watched a test through. Nothing here to note"), so the test he watched was made **on the sound
  borrowed measure** — which is why there was nothing to note, and which proves Keddie's hand-work was
  identical in the high-reading window and the low-reading years.
- **From the patron side (r09):** *"There were nine weeks in the summer of 1911 when the station's own
  measure was out of use, it having been broken… Through those nine weeks our tests came up, all of us,
  every farm on the Bottom; and then the station's own came back into use and the figures went down
  again to where they had been. My father noticed the rise and he noticed the fall and he entered both,
  and he could make nothing of it, and I could make nothing of it either, and there it sat for near on
  fifty years."* — **the rise was across every farm on the Bottom, not an averaging artifact, and it is
  recorded in a document outside the station.**
- **VAUGHT WEIGHS THIS ABOVE ALL THE ARITHMETIC (r11 §IV):**
  > *"For nine weeks in the summer of 1911 the station's own pipette was broken and a borrowed measure
  > was used in its place. The patrons were the same patrons; the herds were the same herds; the
  > pasture, the season, the acid, the bottles, the tester and the tester's habits were all unaltered.
  > **One variable changed.** The station's average test rose from 3.61 to 3.80 and held there for the
  > nine weeks, and returned to 3.61 on the day the station's own measure came back into service. This
  > is recorded contemporaneously, in the operator's hand, by a man who did not know what he was
  > recording and set a question mark against it."*
  And: *"I have spent my working life urging students to prefer an experiment to a calculation. Here is
  one that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and lay unread for
  forty-seven years. It disposes of every explanation that looks to the herds, to the ground or to the
  men, for the sufficient reason that none of those was altered in those nine weeks, and the figures
  were."* (1911 + 47 = 1958 ✓.)
  **NOTE: Vaught here uses 3.61 → 3.80, i.e. the 0.19 step and the 0.95 ratio — contradicting his own
  §V (3.905). This is the fault line inside r11.**
- **CONFLICT on where the borrowed measure came from.** Ivy (from Keddie's book): **up from Larrow
  Green with the cream wagon.** Roy Brant: *"**The state man left his own measure that June** and that
  is what they used."* **Resolve for Larrow Green** — the borrowing began **8 May** on Keddie's dated
  entry and Strawn's visit was in **June**; a measure left in June cannot be the one in use since 8
  May. (A third possibility no source states: two successive borrowed measures.) Either way the
  experiment stands and either candidate lender's glass was sound. Larrow Green is also exactly where
  the 1896 loan came from, per Sill — the same channel.
- **CONFLICT on the duration — now THREE carriers of "six," but ONE line.**
  Teague (r05): the measure broke "in the spring of 1911" and they made shift with a borrowed one for
  **six weeks**. **NEW — Kepp (r16, 1926), attacking the experiment:** "*It has been urged upon me, by
  a gentleman who I know will forgive my noticing it here, that in the summer of 1911 the testing at
  that station was broken off for **six weeks**, their own apparatus having failed them and a borrowed
  one being used, and that the figures stood higher during those six weeks than before or after. **Six
  weeks in the flush of the season, gentlemen. Six weeks of June and July**, when every herd in the
  county is at its best and the stock are off the wet ground and up on the aftermath. I am astonished
  that anybody should found an argument on six weeks of summer figures. I could produce you six weeks
  in any year that would prove anything you liked.*"
  **Resolve for NINE WEEKS, 8 May – 10 July.** Keddie's two dated book entries; Roy Brant's independent
  "nine weeks"; Vaught's "nine weeks." Three independent sources.
  **And note what Kepp's rebuttal costs him.** (i) The window is nine weeks, not six. (ii) It is not
  "June and July" — it opens **8 May**, before the flush, and the tests were up from the first day.
  (iii) His own explanation requires the rise to be seasonal; but the tests **fell back to 3.61 on 10
  July**, in the middle of the flush, the day the glass changed back. Kepp never asks why the figures
  came *down* in July. **This is the single most important thing r16 contributes: the best-argued
  defence of the hay theory ever made in the valley refutes itself on the return leg of the
  experiment.** It is also — importantly — evidence that **the 1911 experiment was known and being
  argued in Ordell County in 1926**, thirty-two years before Ivy, and was talked down.

### The gap was flat, not seasonal
- Keddie: "They did not come and go with the season. The gap was there in July as it was there in
  February, in the flush of the grass and in the dead of the winter on dry hay, and it was the same in
  a wet year as in a dry one."
- **Roy Brant, independently:** *"Our milk at the Hollow ran seventeen hundredths of a point under what
  the same class of milk was making down at Larrow Green… it was seventeen hundredths in July and it
  was seventeen hundredths in February. It did not come and go with the season. It did not care whether
  the cows were on the meadow or on hay in the barn. Nobody would take account of that, then or since,
  though it was in front of them."* And the line that sums the story: *"Seventeen hundredths is nothing
  to look at on one pay slip. Set the slips in a row for a lifetime of farming and it is a farm."*
- **NEW — Hazel Brant, a generation later and from memory of the same book (r17):** *"**Nineteen
  hundredths. Every month, every season, wet years and dry: nineteen hundredths lower at our station
  than the same milk was anywhere else on earth. Not a little in the winter and none in the summer, the
  way a feed thing goes. Nineteen hundredths, flat as a floor.** I have heard men who could not add a
  column of five figures explain to my father why that was the hay."*
  **Note carefully: Hazel says 0.19, her father said 0.17.** These are not the same measurement and she
  has taken the true-shortfall figure, not her father's Larrow-Green differential. Since she is
  speaking in 1981 — twenty-three years after Bulletin 471 — she has almost certainly absorbed the 0.19
  from the published finding. **Treat Hazel as a carrier of 0.19, not an independent source for it.**
  Her *flatness* testimony ("not a little in the winter and none in the summer, the way a feed thing
  goes") is independent and valuable: it is the exact shape-argument, stated by a farmer's daughter.
- **THE THREE GAP FIGURES, DISENTANGLED.**
  - **0.19** = *Ostrey Hollow recorded vs Ostrey Hollow true* (3.61 vs 3.80). **This is the shortfall.
    Use it for all arithmetic.**
  - **0.17** = *Ostrey Hollow recorded vs Larrow Green recorded* (3.61 vs 3.78). A different
    comparison; it coincides with 0.19 only if Larrow Green's own herds averaged exactly 3.80, which
    nobody claims — and Sill says they averaged 3.78.
    **r14 supplies the missing term and the arithmetic closes.** Sill, from the churn: *"Our own milk,
    here at Larrow Green, our own patrons, the men on this creek — that ran **3.78** taking one year
    with another right through those years. Three seventy-eight. I know it because I made the butter
    out of it and a buttermaker knows what his cream is worth before anybody hands him a paper about
    it. Ordinary milk, ordinary county, ordinary cows."* **3.78 − 0.17 = 3.61 exactly.** Sill (Larrow
    Green, 1934) and Roy Brant (Hessel Bottom, 1955) never met, worked opposite ends of the valley, and
    their two figures reproduce the recorded Ostrey Hollow average to the hundredth. r07 Item 16
    supports the same from the Larrow Green receiving and churn books: over the same years, "the Larrow
    Green average runs a little under two tenths of a point better."
  - **0.21 (NEW, r16 Kepp)**: "*The figure of the gap, **as it has been given to me and as I have
    satisfied myself**, is twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21 — between the milk of that station
    and the milk of this valley at large.*" **Reject as a measurement.** It is explicitly hearsay
    ("as it has been given to me"); Kepp has no access to the test book, says he has never read a test
    in his life, and deploys the figure immediately to make three loose numbers "agree so nearly." It
    is best read as a rounded-up version of the valley's talk about the gap. It does, however, **confirm
    that a gap of about two tenths was common knowledge in Ashlin by 1926 and that nobody disputed its
    existence** — only its cause.
- **The 1924 = 3.80 anomaly is RESOLVED in Keddie's favor.** His letter: "**In 1924 the average of this
  station was 3.80**, and I wrote that figure with the same pen and the same measure of care as all the
  others." **Strawn, 1924, independently: "Ostrey Hollow. Scales true, no added water, tests improved.
  Their book gives the year at 3.80."** The 1922 leaf described 1898–1922; 1924 is *after* the glass
  changed. **The tests rose permanently, by exactly the predicted amount, the moment the instrument
  changed.**
- **Strawn, 1925 (fall)**: "Scales true, no added water, **tests improved.** New man at the tester's
  bench this year. He is quick, and quick is not the thing I look for." — **the tests stayed up under a
  DIFFERENT tester**, which kills any theory that the low figures were something Keddie personally did.
  (And it independently corroborates r13's dismissal date: a new man was at the bench in 1925.)
- **r14 sees the same 1920s step from the churn end and misreads it.** Sill: *"one summer, along in the
  twenties, those figures came up and they stayed up, and nothing on this earth changed but the season,
  and men do not suddenly start pouring better milk out of the same cows in the same barns. **Something
  got stopped. Something got said to somebody.** I never knew what and I never asked."* **He is right
  that something was stopped and wrong about what: what stopped was the short glass, in June 1923.** A
  fourth independent witness to *the step itself*, from a man with no access to the test book.

### The condemnation, the reorder, and the crate
- **June 1923**: the state's inspector condemned a crate of measures; it was set aside and new ones
  ordered in its place (r02 §8). Loomis: **the crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all** — not
  by patrons, officers, or the board, in any of the three sessions. "The award's phrase about 'the
  books and appliances of the station' is a form of words the board used and not a list of anything."
  Her closing line: "the interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying."
  **r13 CORROBORATES AND SHARPENS IT.** Frick reprints the exhibit list from his own file: **four books
  and nothing else — no crate, no glassware.** *"Four books and nothing else. It is a short list for a
  matter of that size, and I have looked at it many times since."* **And the fourth exhibit was the
  inspector's 1923 circuit notebook — the very book containing the condemnation entry.** The answer was
  physically on the table in front of the board and nobody turned to that page.
- **Bulletin 471 dates the short glass from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923.**
- **Who condemned it is CLOSED: Merle Strawn, 12 June 1923.** Heading: "1923, June 12. Ostrey Hollow. I
  had carried the burette two seasons before this check." The entry — **quoted verbatim and identically
  in r06 and in r07 Item 9 (a photostat of the 1923 notebook)**:
  > *"Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk pipettes
  > against the burette. **These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole
  > crate of them, and told the manager to order new.** Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has
  > cost them."*
  The manager told to order new was **Selby Vose.**

- **WHY IT WAS NOT CAUGHT EARLIER — the missing instrument.** Strawn, **1921**: "**The state has begun

### Ivy's measurement, 9 March 1958, at Farland against a calibrated burette
Quoted identically in r04 and in r07 Item 14 (a typed, hand-entered, signed measurement sheet):
> *"Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean
> of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c.
> Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was
> ruled for."*

**The nine readings sum to 150.48; 150.48 ÷ 9 = 16.72 exactly. The stated mean is arithmetically
verifiable from the stated data.** (16.70+16.71+16.72+16.72+16.72+16.73+16.73+16.74+16.71 = 150.48.)
Note the tightness of the spread — 16.70 to 16.74, four hundredths — which is what a *manufacturing*
fault looks like: the whole lot blown short on one mandrel, not nine pipettes damaged nine ways.

**Symmetry worth keeping**: the same class of instrument (a graduated burette) that condemned the glass
in 1923 is the instrument that explained it in 1958 — Strawn learned *that* they were short; Ivy learned
*by how much*, and turned the how-much into pounds and dollars. **Hazel Brant's version of the same
thought, and the best sentence in the corpus for the ending of Story 3:** *"A thing you could have
settled in an afternoon with a burette and a straight face, and it took fifty years and a girl from the
college who thought to climb a ladder."*

**Vaught's method note (r11 §III)** describes the same procedure: the pipette filled to its mark with
water at room temperature, tip wiped, contents delivered in the ordinary working manner — same interval
of drainage, same touch of the tip to the wall of the receiver — into a burette of certified graduation;
five deliveries from each pipette, readings averaged. *"The whole of it occupies an afternoon and
requires no apparatus a creamery does not own, save the burette. There is no reason why every station in
this state should not be checked once in this way, and several reasons in this history why it should
be."*

## 4. STORY THREAD C — THE STATION-GAIN LINE (Teague's ledger), CORROBORATED FROM THE FLOOR

- **Teague's mechanism**: on one side of the station account he entered **the butterfat credited to
  patrons on the station's own test**; on the other, **the butterfat that actually came down the valley
  in the station's cream and was weighed and tested into the vats at Larrow Green.** "The two were never
  the same. **The second was always the larger.**" The difference he carried to the general account.
- The entry, quoted complete — **and quoted IDENTICALLY by THREE hands from the ledgers themselves**:
  r05 (Teague, who wrote it), r07 Item 6 (Nym, from the annual ledgers), r12 (Rundle, who saw the
  ledgers in the office in 1939 "on another errand altogether"):
  > *"Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, **57,760 lb**. Butter fat received
  > at Larrow Green in station cream, **60,800 lb**. Station gain, **3,040 lb, five and a quarter per
  > cent, as usual**. Carried to the general account."*
  Teague calls this **the entry for 1898**, "and there is one like it for every year after, in the same
  terms and in the same hand for as long as the hand was mine." Nym describes it as **"typical"** of an
  entry appearing in one form every year, and notes **the station column runs from 1897**. Rundle: **"The
  station account carries, every year, an entry of this form, in the bookkeeper's hand."** **Three
  witnesses to annual recurrence** — which is what makes the ×25 multiplication legitimate.
- **These numbers corroborate everything else exactly**: 1,600,000 lb of milk × 3.61% = 57,760 lb;
  × 3.80% = 60,800 lb; difference 3,040 lb; 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.263% ≈ "five and a quarter per cent."
  **Hidden gift**: 1,600,000 lb × 25 years = **40,000,000 lb** — the station-gain entry and the ledger
  intake total are mutually consistent, which is a check on both.
- **r14 CORROBORATES THE STATION GAIN INDEPENDENTLY OF THE LEDGER, FROM THE RECEIVING PLATFORM.** Sill:
  *"That cream ran heavy. Every year it ran heavy. **There was more fat in the cans than the papers said
  had been bought up there.** The office would send down its sheet and the sheet would say so many pounds
  of fat bought at the station, and the fat that came into this room off that wagon was more than the
  sheet said. **Always more. Never less.**"* And on his method: *"I weighed it myself, most years. The
  platform man weighed it and I weighed it over again when it came into the cold room, because I have
  never in my life taken another man's figure for what was going into my churn… you will find the
  Hollow's cream coming in heavy on page after page after page, in my own writing, for as long as I have
  been in this room."* — **a double weighing (platform man + buttermaker), recorded in a book Teague
  never wrote in, covering 1893–1934.** A fourth, physically independent witness to the gain.
  **Sill dismissed it as normal**: *"Cream is not milk and it will not stand still and be counted… So it
  ran heavy. So does everybody's. I never made anything of it, and I will say plain, **nobody ever asked
  me to.**"*
- **Nym's curatorial note on the two percentages is the cleanest statement anywhere**: *"The two
  percentages in this file are not interchangeable: five and a quarter percent of the credited fat is
  five percent of the fat delivered, and each is right of its own denominator and of no other."*
  **r11 §V gets this wrong**: "The gain is five and a quarter percent of the butterfat actually
  delivered." Check: 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = **5.000%** of delivered; 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = **5.263%** of credited.
  **Vaught has attached the credited-fat percentage to the delivered-fat denominator. Resolve with Nym.**
- **Why the two sides had to differ, mechanically.** The station's *test* used the short pipette; the
  cream was **weighed and tested into the vats at Larrow Green on Larrow Green's own sound glass.**
  Strawn (**1912**) notes "**Cream shipped down the valley the same day it is separated, which is more
  than some do**," so no spoilage or interval explains it away. **Two instruments, one sound and one
  short, measuring the same fat.**
- Teague on why nobody looked: "A figure that jumps about will keep you awake. **A figure that comes in
  at five and a quarter every year is furniture.**"
- **Internal inconsistency in r05 (still open)**: he says "**the station-gain line was my own device; I
  began it when I came in 1905**" and denies it was any old dodge of the founders — yet the entry he
  quotes complete is **the 1898 entry**, seven years before he arrived. **r07 Item 6 slightly favors "not
  his device": the station column in the annual ledgers runs from 1897.** Either he worked from a
  predecessor's ledger or he mis-stated the start year. **Flag; still open.**
- **Teague's two summary figures remain outliers**: "that station took in **forty-four million pounds of
  milk**. And the average test of all the patrons of Ostrey Hollow… was **3.16**." Both conflict with
  r02 (Loomis, from Teague's own annual sheets: **40,000,000 lb**), r04 (Ivy), r07 Item 6 (Nym, from the
  ledgers), and r11 §V (Vaught). **3.16 is almost certainly a digit transposition of 3.61.** r12 repeats
  the 44 million from store talk. **Resolve for 40,000,000 lb and 3.61 recorded.**
  **r13 (Frick) also says 3.16** — "The patrons of that station had been paid, year upon year, on an
  average test of 3.16… Three and sixteen is not a scandalous figure standing by itself. It becomes one
  when the man over the hill is paid on a better figure for milk out of cows no different from yours;
  when the difference holds steady for a quarter of a century; and when nobody in the office can tell you
  the cause of it." **Resolve for 3.61, and treat Frick as a carrier, not a second source.** Reasons, in
  order of force:
  (i) **Frick heard the case in which Teague testified** — same room, same figure, same origin;
  (ii) **the ledger arithmetic forbids 3.16**: 57,760 ÷ 1,600,000 = 3.61%, and 57,760 is a quoted figure
       from three independent transcriptions;
  (iii) **the correction ratio only works on 3.61**: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80, which reproduces four separate
       observations (1896, the nine weeks of 1911, 1924, the Alder Corners table). 3.16 ÷ 0.95 = 3.326,
       which reproduces nothing;
  (iv) **Sill's 3.78 minus Roy Brant's 0.17 = 3.61**, from two men on opposite sides of the valley who
       never met;
  (v) **NEW: Emmet Brant's own 1910 leaf, 3.42, divides by 0.95 to exactly 3.60** — a single farm's
       figure, from a book outside the station, behaving exactly as the 0.95 ratio predicts. On a 3.16
       station average, Brant's 3.42 would be *above* the mean, which nothing else supports.
  Frick himself is careful about what he did and did not understand ("I am a lawyer and the thing is
  chemistry"), so the slip is in character and costs him nothing else.
- **Governance evidence (r05)**: "**Mr. Vose knew what that account was doing. He signed the sheet every
  year.** I laid the annual sheet before him each spring and he put his name at the foot of it, and a man
  who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him. What he made of it he never told me." And: "**I
  put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone. I let it alone.** I was the Association's
  bookkeeper and not its conscience."
- **1916 is a doubly loaded year.** Teague put the discrepancy to Selby Vose and was silenced; in the
  same year Strawn recorded at the station: "**The tester complains that his patrons blame him for their
  figures. I told him that is the trade and he said he knew it.**" Neither man knew of the other's moment.
- **Teague is the origin of the theft theory**, and r13 shows exactly how he planted it. What he told the
  board in 1925 and repeats verbatim in 1948: "Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of
  hands for twenty-nine seasons… One man weighed the milk, one man drew the sample, one man made the
  test, one man wrote the figure that the pay slip was made from, and one man ran the separator. **There
  was no second man in that building from the spring to the fall.** I do not say what any particular
  person did on any particular day… I say that a business which is arranged so that a single man can take
  a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken from
  it… **The remedy is two men or a lock, and the Association had neither.**"
  **r13 records the manner, from the chair**: *"This was put by the bookkeeper, Wilbur Teague, in the way
  an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it — he was asked about the station's account
  and answered at length about how such an account would look if a man were helping himself, and then
  observed that he made no charge against anybody. **There is no cross-examination that reaches a witness
  of that kind, and I did not attempt one.**"*
- **Teague's "no second man" premise is materially refuted by r06, r09 and r15.** There *was* a second man
  in that building, repeatedly and by law: the state's inspector, who watched a test through from sample
  to reading and checked both scales against sealed weights on every visit for twenty-nine years, and
  never once found either wrong. There was a second *scale* outside the building: Emmet Brant's own,
  agreeing with the station's day after day from 1 January 1897. And **in 1908 a three-man committee of
  farmers stood by while the test was made, tried both scales, drew and sealed their own samples, and
  reported "we found no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter, and we wish that finding to be printed in
  the same type as the rest"** (r15). **A theft of cream would have shown as a false weight or a false
  test; both were checked, from three directions, and neither was ever wrong.** Teague's structural
  argument survives as a governance point; as evidence that anything was taken, it is refuted.
- **RUNDLE REFUTES THE THEFT THEORY IN PRINT IN 1939, FROM THE LEDGER, NINETEEN YEARS BEFORE IVY.** His
  Chapter IV set down the valley's settled belief: *"It is the settled opinion of the valley that cream
  left that station by the back door, and the figures went right when the man who kept it did not. I set
  that down as I have heard it set down a hundred times, by men who were there and men who were not, and
  I have never heard it contradicted at the store."* Then, at the end of the pamphlet:
  > **NOTE ADDED WHILE IN PRESS.** *"While this pamphlet was being set up in type I was allowed to look at
  > the Association's old ledgers in the office, on another errand altogether. The station account carries,
  > every year, an entry of this form, in the bookkeeper's hand: [the 57,760 / 60,800 / 3,040 entry, quoted
  > entire]. **I must go back on Chapter IV. Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light.
  > It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand. I leave it as written and
  > correct it here.**"*
  > — D. RUNDLE, Larrow Green
  **The logic is exactly right and exactly Ivy's**: theft removes fat between the station's book and the
  creamery's vat, so the creamery would receive *less* than the station credited; the ledger shows it
  received *more*, every year. A schoolteacher settled it in 1939 with one glance at the Association's own
  books — and the valley did not act on it, which is why Rundle's own 1959 footnote still refers the reader
  to the college. **And Sill (r14) had been saying "always more, never less" out loud on the receiving floor
  since the nineties, to men who "never thought it was worth anything."**

## 5. THE HAY THEORY — ITS DOCUMENTS, ITS CORRUPTION, AND ITS REFUTATION

### 5.1 The 1908 committee — now available IN FULL (r15), not just in Nym's excerpt

**Composition and charge.** Appointed **in January 1908** by the board, after ~six years of complaint;
three men, chaired by **Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green**. Grigg, 1919: "*By the winter in which we were
named, the board could not let it alone any longer without seeming to have an interest in letting it
alone, and we three were named to look into it. **I have never in my life been given a duty I liked
less.** I had known the men of that bottom since I was a young man and I have bought hay off two of
them.*"

**THE REPORT AS PRINTED IN 1908 (r15, full text; Nym's Item 11 is its closing paragraph only):**
> REPORT OF THE COMMITTEE APPOINTED TO INQUIRE INTO THE COMPLAINTS OF THE PATRONS OF THE OSTREY HOLLOW
> STATION. To the President and Directors of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Your
> committee, appointed in January last, respectfully reports as follows.
> *The complaint before us is of some years' standing and is in substance this: that the milk delivered
> at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station is returned by the test at a figure below that of milk delivered
> elsewhere in this valley, and that the patrons of that station are in consequence paid less for the
> same labor and the same cows than their neighbors are paid. Since payment by test came in among us and
> the pound of butter fat took the place of the pound of milk as the measure of a man's month, a
> difference of this kind is no longer a matter of opinion. It is a matter of money, and it comes out of
> the same pocket every month, and your committee has approached it in that light and not as a quarrel.*
> *Hessel Bottom, where six farms cut hay and turn out stock, is a wet meadow lying along the creek under
> Ostrey Hollow. Its ground is springy the year round and is under water in part every spring. Its
> herbage is sedge, rush, and the coarse sour grasses that come in on such land, and its hay is cut late
> and heavy and is fed as the main dependence of those farms from November to April. There is no better
> winter keep to be had there. We say this without reproach to any man on that bottom; the land is what
> it is, and they took it as their fathers left it to them.*
> *Your committee visited each of the six farms, some of them three and four times. **We drew our samples
> ourselves and we sealed them ourselves. We saw the herds milked, we saw the cans filled, and we saw them
> hauled to the station and weighed in at the intake, and we stood by while the test was made and saw
> nothing whatever in the doing of it to remark upon. We tried the station scales against a known weight
> and we tried the patrons' own scales against the station's. We inquired into the water supply at each
> barn and satisfied ourselves upon it.***
> *Your committee further observed the general condition of the stock upon that bottom… The herds are thin
> in the flank at the end of the winter feeding and slow to come to their flesh in the spring. Their coats
> are rough later in the season than the coats of hill herds. They do well enough in flow, for the wet land
> is generous of milk, but it is our opinion, **formed on the ground and not out of a book**, that what such
> land gives in quantity it withholds in quality, and that a cow cannot put into her pail what her keep has
> never put into her.*
> ***We report that we found no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter, and we wish that finding to be
> printed in the same type as the rest.***
> ***We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March. The milk is whole and
> the scales are honest. It is our opinion that the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and
> that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station.***
> NYLE GRIGG, chairman, and the committee.

- The report was "**received and accepted, and the question was closed by it for eleven years**" (r15).
  1908 + 11 = **1919**, when it was reopened — the same year as the water circular. **r12 independently:
  "The Hollow did not accept it."** **r09 puts a reaction to it**: *"My father read that report twice and
  put it in the drawer and said that the land had been wet since the flood and the tests had not."*
  **r13 confirms the report was read into the arbitration record in 1925**, and that "the bog meadow at
  Hessel Bottom was before us for the better part of an afternoon… **It is an old opinion in that valley
  and it had the advantage of blaming nobody in the room.**"
- **THE FATAL LIMIT, still fatal, and now doubly documented.** **Nym's note: "The sampling was of January,
  February and March 1908 only, as the report states."** **The committee never sampled a summer month** —
  so it could not have seen that the gap stood flat through the flush, the single fact that most damns the
  hay theory.
- **NEW AND IMPORTANT — GRIGG'S 1919 LETTER CONTRADICTS HIS OWN 1908 REPORT ON EXACTLY THIS POINT.** The
  letter says: "*We sampled those herds **through the whole of 1908 — every month of that year**, in the
  barn and on the pasture, out of the night's milk and the morning's, and we did not let a man know which
  day we were coming.*" The report he prints two paragraphs later says: "*We have sampled the herds of
  Hessel Bottom **through January, February and March**.*"
  **Resolve for January–February–March.** A quoted contemporaneous document outranks the same man's
  recollection eleven years later; Nym's independent examination of the fair copy confirms it; and the
  letter's version is the self-serving one — it is precisely the claim that would make the finding look
  robust to the objection Grigg says he feared ("*I knew that a committee of farmers saying that wet hay
  thins the cream would be told that farmers will say anything about their own land*").
  **This matters beyond the detail:** the 1919 reprint circulated to every officer and patron an inflated
  account of the committee's own coverage. Between the inflated months and the inflated Bulletin figure
  (§5.2), the 1919 document is the point at which the hay theory hardened from an opinion into something
  the valley believed had been proved twice.
- **Grigg's disinterest is genuine and should be credited**: not a patron of that station, hauled to Larrow
  Green all his life, chosen for the work on that account, "abused for the finding by men on both sides of
  it, which I take to be a fair sign that the finding was an honest one." **He is wrong, not dishonest.**
  Note that his very disinterest is what disqualified him: a man who never delivered to that station never
  had a pay slip to set in a row.

### 5.2 BULLETIN No. 214 AND ITS FOURFOLD CORRUPTION — the corpus's second great error chain

**THE ACTUAL SENTENCE. State Agricultural College at Farland, Bulletin No. 214 (1911), quoted identically
by r04 (Ivy), r07 Item 12 (Nym) and r11 §VI (Vaught) — three independent transcriptions of one sentence:**
> *"Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by **not more than five
> hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture.**"*

**THE CORRUPTED VERSION, in two carriers:**
- **r15, Grigg, 1919** — the earliest appearance: "*the State Agricultural College at Farland has since
  published its Bulletin No. 214, which was a feeding trial upon bog hay carried out under the College's
  own eye, with weighed rations and no interest in the outcome. That trial found the bog hay to depress
  the butter fat test by **two tenths in the winter feeding. Two tenths, gentlemen**, out of a College that
  had never heard of Hessel Bottom and had no reason on earth to flatter us. **There is our finding,
  arrived at by another road and by better men, three years after we set our names to it.**"* (1908 + 3 =
  1911 ✓ — he has the Bulletin's year right and its number wrong by a factor of four.)
- **r16, Kepp, 1926**: "*The College fed the bog hay under control and measured what it did, and it found
  the butter fat test depressed by **two tenths in the winter feeding. That is the whole case.** Two tenths
  from the College, laid alongside the fifth of a point I have found in the field and the twenty-one
  hundredths that station has been complaining of, and **the three agree so nearly that I do not know what
  more a reasonable man requires.**"*

**RESOLVE FOR FIVE HUNDREDTHS (0.05), WINTER ONLY.** Three independent verbatim transcriptions of the
printed Bulletin, one of them by the professor at the publishing institution, against two men citing it at
second hand. **And count Grigg and Kepp as ONE line of transmission**, not two: Grigg's letter was printed
and circulated to the officers and patrons in 1919; Kepp read his paper in the same county seven years
later and had no access to the Bulletin's text that he demonstrates.

**WHY THIS IS THE HINGE OF THE WHOLE HAY THEORY. Both halves of the sentence were destroyed:**
1. **The magnitude was multiplied by four.** On the true figure, 0.19 ÷ 0.05 = **3.8** — the gap is
   *four times too large* for bog hay to explain, as Ivy says ("**four times too small and it is the wrong
   shape**"). On the corrupted figure, 0.19 vs 0.20 is a *perfect match*, and the case looks closed. **The
   error is precisely large enough to convert a refutation into a confirmation.**
2. **The summer clause was deleted.** The Bulletin says the effect is unmeasurable on summer pasture.
   Neither Grigg nor Kepp mentions this. Yet the gap at Ostrey Hollow **stood flat in July as in February**
   (Keddie, Roy Brant, Hazel Brant), and **the Alder Corners table is summer-only** (June to mid-September;
   the ridge road shuts in November and does not open before April) and shows the *same* ~0.19. **The
   deleted clause is the one that kills the theory outright.**
3. **The trial's conditions were also mis-stated.** Vaught's fair point, which Ivy does not make: the trial
   fed bog hay as **"the whole of the roughage"**, i.e. under **"conditions harsher than any farmer would
   impose"** — so 0.05 is an **upper bound**, not a typical effect. Grigg's own report says the Bottom hay
   was fed "as the main dependence… from November to April," not as the whole ration.
   *(A residual r11 error to note: **§VI says the gap is "some six times" the Bulletin figure** — that is
   0.295 ÷ 0.05, a downstream consequence of Vaught's own bad 0.295. **On the correct figures the multiple
   is four, and Ivy has it right.**)*

**Kepp's other two "agreeing" figures do not survive either.** His "fifth of a point" is his own
impressionistic field observation over thirty years, unmeasured and unrecorded ("*I have gone about this
county for thirty years and I have kept my eyes open… every practical man knows it*"). His "twenty-one
hundredths" is explicitly hearsay. **So the "three figures that agree so nearly" are: one corrupted
citation, one impression, and one rumor — and the corrupted citation is doing all the work.** This should
be said plainly in Story 3: the hay theory's apparent scientific backing was a misquotation, and it took
forty-seven years and a burette to get past it.

### 5.3 What Kepp gets RIGHT, and it is not nothing

Do not caricature r16. Kepp is careful, humane, and correct about several things:
- **The soil chemistry is sound as description**: undrained ground grows sour, acid, poor in lime and
  phosphates; sweet grasses are crowded out by sedge, rush and tussock; that herbage carries bulk and
  water, not minerals; late cutting takes out what little was there.
- **The mineral-robbing mechanism is a real veterinary phenomenon**: "*A cow in milk is a manufactory
  working on a narrow margin… If her ration is short of the minerals, she does not simply give less; she
  robs her own frame to make good… **the fat is the first thing to suffer, because the fat is the dearest
  thing the cow makes and it is the first economy she practices when she is pressed.***" The stock come out
  of winter thin over the loin and rough in the coat, slow to condition, ready subjects for digestive
  trouble, breed later, worse feet. *(This matches the 1908 report's stock observations word for word in
  substance — Kepp is very probably echoing the report, which he cites. Not independent on that point.)*
- **He is right that the Ostrey Hollow patrons' complaint was just**: "*Their complaint is perfectly just
  and I have never doubted it.*"
- **He is right about the remedy for the land** (drain, lime, sow down, keep stock off in the wet spring,
  never feed the hay alone) — that advice would have been good regardless.
- **He supplies the price of butterfat independently** (§0.1(d)) and, in doing so, does the patrons'
  arithmetic for them without realizing whose case he is making: "*Fat has been worth twenty-seven cents
  the pound through these years, and a fifth of a point taken off every hundredweight a farm ships, year in
  and year out, will pay for a great deal of tile.*"

**And the one exchange that indicts him. From the appended DISCUSSION:**
> *A member* asked whether the trouble might lie in the station rather than the bottom, as had been said in
> the valley for years.
> *Dr. Kepp* replied that **he had no interest in that quarrel and no competence in it; that he had never in
> his life read a test and would not know how**; but that when a cause is found in the soil, in the herbage,
> in the condition of the stock, in the College's trial, and in every wet bottom in the county alike, **it is
> not good sense to go looking for a second cause in a building.**

That is the whole error of the corpus in one sentence, spoken by its most competent man: a sufficient-looking
cause is not a verified one, and the building was where the instrument was. The other exchange is worth
keeping too — *a member* asked whether the difference might be the cows rather than the land; Kepp answered
that he had considered and rejected it, "*that some of the best-bred cattle in the county stand on wet land,
and some very indifferent cattle on the hills test well; and that a man may improve his herd for twenty years
and never get his fat up while his ground is sour*." **Kepp thereby eliminates the cows — which is correct,
and which is a step the patrons needed made.**

### 5.4 The other refutations of the hay theory, unchanged and cumulative

- **The 1908 committee itself conceded "the milk is whole and the scales are honest"** — in 1908 the
  Association's own committee had already eliminated watering and false weights, **eleven years before the
  manager circularized nine families about water in their cans.**
- **The Alder Corners cross-check**: the factory over the ridge tested the milk of **four Hessel Bottom men**
  in their summer flush; that same milk off that same wet ground ran **about nineteen hundredths higher at
  Alder Corners than at home** — and the comparison is **structurally summer-only**, exactly the season in
  which Bulletin 214 says bog hay depresses the test by nothing measurable. (Full table §6.)
- **r14 clears the cows explicitly, from the man who made the butter**: *"Wet ground up there, but good cows,
  and I never had a word to say against the cows… Do not put in that I said anything against the cows up
  there, because I did not. **The cows were never the trouble.**"*
- **The hill farms.** The station also took milk **from three places on the hill** (Strawn 1909), which is
  not Bottom ground. A defect in the station's glass depresses hill milk and Bottom milk alike; wet bog hay
  cannot. *(This is the chain's inference, not a source's; and note §3's open question about whether the
  nine circularized families were all Bottom families — but the three hill places delivered to that station
  either way.)*
- **The 1911 nine weeks** — the controlled experiment, and Kepp's failed rebuttal of it (§3).
- **The 1924 step** — the tests rose and stayed up when the glass changed, under a different tester, while
  the Alder Corners table shows no step at all in 1923–25 (§6).
- **Rosalie Cudd's line, which is the epitaph for the whole theory**: her father *"used to say that the
  Ordell people had a theory about that bottom the way some families have a ghost: it explained things, and
  it cost nothing, and nobody was ever going to test it."*

## 6. THE ALDER CORNERS ARRANGEMENT — documented from both ends (r09, r10, r12, now r17)

- **r09 (Roy Brant):** *"From 1915, four of the Bottom men hauled their milk over Redlow Ridge to the cheese
  factory in the summer flush and back to the Hollow the rest of the year. The factory over there had opened
  in 1914. They went on that way for **sixteen seasons**… it was talked about here as though they had gone
  over to the enemy, which shows you what a valley is. **I did not go over. We had the biggest herd on the
  meadow, and the biggest herd is the one that cannot afford to be talked about, and I have thought since
  that this was a poor reason and cost us money.**"*
- **r10 (Rosalie Cudd), from the receiving end:** *"From 1915, four men off Hessel Bottom hauled their milk
  over to me in the summer flush. They came from about the first of June to the middle of September, and the
  rest of the year they went down to your station at Ostrey Hollow, which was the only sensible thing to do…
  cheese paid better than butter in a flush month, I was buying hard in flush… and while the road was open my
  gate was a shorter pull for those four farms than the run down the valley. **They came for sixteen seasons.
  The last of those summers was 1930.**"* (1915–1930 inclusive = 16 ✓.)
- **r12 (Rundle), from the Ordell side:** *"in 1914 the Alder Corners Cheese Company was opened over Redlow
  Ridge in Vessey County, by Jerome Cudd, and… in the summers a few of our Hessel Bottom men hauled over the
  mountain to him rather than down to their own station. **That was resented here.** It is Jerome Cudd's
  daughter, Rosalie Cudd, who makes the cheese at Alder Corners now."*
- **NEW — r17 (Hazel Brant), a fourth witness to the arrangement and its meaning:** *"**Four of those six men
  hauled over the ridge in the summer flush, to the cheese factory on the far side, and brought their milk
  back down to our own station the rest of the year. Four.** My father used to say it was **the only honest
  arithmetic anybody in that valley ever did without meaning to.**"* — Roy Brant, who declined to go over,
  understood by 1955 what the four men's split year had accidentally produced.
- **The ridge, and why it matters:** *"Four miles of switchback, cut for a lumber road and never much improved
  since. It shuts in November and it does not open before April, some years May… **So no man from your valley
  ever hauled to me in winter, whatever the price was. It was not possible.**"* The comparison is therefore
  **structurally summer-only.**

### How Jerome Cudd tested, in his daughter's words (r10) — the independence argument
> *"You ask how my father tested. He tested himself, on his own bench, in the little room off the intake, with
> his own glassware that he bought new and washed in the evening and kept in a rack he had made, and he wrote
> his own book. He never compared a figure of his with anybody else's, and I want to be honest with you about
> the reason: it never entered his head. He had no business with what your station made of the same milk. A
> cheesemaker tests what comes in his door so he knows what he is paying for and what his vat will do. He is
> not keeping a check on another man's arithmetic seven miles off over a mountain, and if you had suggested it
> to my father he would have laughed at you."*
**Separate bench, separate glassware bought new, separate book, and no knowledge of or interest in the Ostrey
Hollow figures — the strongest possible statement of independence.**

### Jerome Cudd's own 1919 leaf, copied exactly by his daughter
> *"The four Hessel Bottom men, summer of 1919: 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground, whatever they
> say over the ridge."*
**The four 1919 figures average (3.79+3.81+3.78+3.82)/4 = 3.80 exactly**, matching the table's 1919 entry.

**THREE THINGS FALL OUT OF THE TABLE, AND NOBODY IN EITHER VALLEY READ IT UNTIL 1958:**
1. **The true test of Hessel Bottom milk in summer was 3.80** — sixteen consecutive readings, range 3.78–3.82,
   arithmetic mean 3.7994 ≈ **3.80**. Exactly what the 0.95 correction predicts from the recorded 3.61; exactly
   the 1896 figure, the 1911 nine-week figure, and the 1924 figure.
2. **Wet ground does not depress it** — "wet years and dry ones," no season-to-season movement.
3. **THE TABLE DOES NOT STEP IN 1923.** Ostrey Hollow jumped 3.61 → 3.80 in mid-1923 when the glass changed.
   **Alder Corners reads 3.81 in 1923, 3.80 in 1924, 3.79 in 1925 — no step at all.** That is the control: the
   milk did not change in 1923; only one valley's instrument did. Nothing else in the corpus disposes so cleanly
   of Selby Vose's claim that the 1924 rise measured a change in the farmers' behavior — **or of Sill's identical
   claim that "something got stopped."** (Inference of the chain; no source had the table and the station book
   side by side except Ivy, and she does not draw it in these words.)
- **For the shortfall arithmetic, use the eight pre-1923 seasons**: 1915–1922 read 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.80, 3.80,
  3.82, 3.79, 3.78 — **mean 3.79625 ≈ 3.80 — against Ostrey Hollow's recorded 3.61 in the same years.
  Differential 0.19, computed from affected years only.**

### On watering, from the one man positioned to detect it instantly (r10)
> *"A cheesemaker knows watered milk inside a day. He does not need a test to know it… The curd comes soft and it
> will not knit; you get a slack, weak body and your yield is down, and you can stand at the vat and see your money
> going out with the whey. It cannot be hidden from the man who makes the cheese, because the cheese tells him, and
> it tells him the next morning, not next year. Butter is more forgiving that way. **Cheese is a witness.** In
> sixteen summers my father never made one complaint of those four men, never docked one of them a cent, and never
> — not once that I heard, and I was in the room — said a word about their milk except that he wished he had more of
> it in July."*
**Sixteen summers of a hostile-to-adulteration process finding nothing** — independent of Strawn's lactometer, of
the 1908 committee, and of Bulletin 471. **The single most direct refutation of the water theory that Vose, Sill and
(privately) Keddie all believed.**

## 7. BULLETIN 471 — TWO NUMBER-SETS INSIDE ONE DOCUMENT, AND HOW TO RESOLVE IT

**Bulletin No. 471, State Agricultural College at Farland, October 1958, by Professor Leland Vaught.** Two sets of
figures are in circulation and *both come out of the same bulletin.*

**SET A — the one to use** (r04's measurement sheet; r07 Item 14, the signed sheet; r11 §IV; r11 §VII, the bulletin's
own concluding paragraph):
- mean delivery of the nine pipettes = **16.72 c.c.**, short **0.88 c.c.** of the 17.6 mark
- ratio 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95** exactly = "95 hundredths of the mark" = **low by one part in twenty (5%)**
- recorded 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80** true; shortfall **0.19** of a point
- 40,000,000 lb milk × 0.19% = **76,000 lb** of butterfat not credited
- start of error **1 May 1897**, end **12 June 1923**

**SET B — the one to reject** (r11 §III and §V only):
- mean delivery = **16.27 c.c.**; ratio 0.9244; 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = **3.905** true; shortfall **0.295**;
  40,000,000 × 0.295% = **118,000 lb**; five dozen pipettes bought; station glass in use from 1 May 1898; station gain
  stated as 5¼% of fat *delivered*; and (in §VI) the hay multiple given as "some six times."

**REASONS TO REJECT SET B, in order of force:**
1. **16.27 is a digit transposition of 16.72.** The nine listed readings run 16.70–16.74 and sum to 150.48; **no
   pipette in the recorded data delivered anything near 16.27**, and a mean cannot fall outside its own range.
2. **Set A's 16.72 is arithmetically verifiable from the primary data** and is carried on a **typed, hand-entered,
   signed measurement sheet** transcribed independently by Ivy (r04) and by Nym (r07 Item 14).
3. **Vaught's own concluding paragraph (§VII) uses Set A** — 1 May 1897, and the 0.19/3.80 relations. The bulletin
   contradicts itself and its conclusion sides with Set A.
4. **§IV, Vaught's best passage, uses Set A** explicitly: "rose from 3.61 to 3.80 and held there."
5. **Set A reproduces four independent observations** (1896 = 3.80; the 1911 nine weeks = 3.80; 1924 = 3.80; Alder
   Corners sixteen seasons ≈ 3.80). **Set B's 3.905 reproduces nothing in the corpus.**
6. **Set B's collateral claims are each independently falsified**: five dozen pipettes (the invoice is itemized and
   closes to $77.93 only at six dozen); 1 May 1898 (the invoice is 14 April 1897, the office file says 1 May 1897,
   Sill says "the spring after," and §VII says 1897); 5¼% of delivered fat (3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.000%; Nym states the
   distinction exactly).
7. **NEW, and worth stating**: **Emmet Brant's 1910 leaf independently ratifies the 0.95 ratio at the level of a
   single farm.** 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60 exactly. 3.42 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.700 — a number nothing else in the corpus supports.
   A ratio derived from nine pipettes at Farland in 1958 correctly predicts a figure written in a barn at Hessel
   Bottom in 1910. That is as strong a confirmation as the corpus offers.

**How to characterize r11 in the answer:** Vaught is right about the *mechanism* and right about the *epistemology*
(the 1911 experiment above all the arithmetic; the recommendation that every station in the state be checked with a
burette). His §III and §V carry a corrupted number-set that his own §IV, §VII, and every primary document contradict.
**Use §II, §IV, §VI (with the "six times" corrected to four), and §VII. Reject §III and §V numbers.** Do not treat
"Bulletin 471 says X" as decisive without saying which section.

## 8. THE ARBITRATION OF 1925 — procedure, exhibits, outcome

- **Keddie's sworn statement to the board at Ashlin is dated 17 March 1925** (r03). The test book was **impounded by
  the board in March 1925** (r07). **Three sessions**, the second and third held **at Ostrey Hollow in the station's
  own weigh room** at Frick's insistence (r13).
- **The award: $3,800, 4 May 1925** — quoted verbatim by Loomis (r02) from the minutes and reported by Frick (r13),
  chairman. Its phrase about "the books and appliances of the station" is, per Loomis, "a form of words the board used
  and not a list of anything."
- **THE EXHIBIT LIST (r13, reprinted from Frick's own file): FOUR BOOKS AND NOTHING ELSE.** No crate, no glassware.
  Exhibit 1 = the station test book, **4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924, in the hand of Ansel Keddie**. Exhibit 3 = the
  Brant weigh book, **begun 1 January 1897**. **Exhibit 4 = the inspector's 1923 circuit notebook — the very volume
  containing the condemnation entry of 12 June 1923.** *"Four books and nothing else. It is a short list for a matter
  of that size, and I have looked at it many times since."* **The answer was on the table and nobody turned the page.**
- **The board decided nothing and Frick says so.** He self-describes the matter as "an arbitration under the by-laws of
  a creamery association, with no appeal from it, no reported decision, and nothing whatever to cite it for."
- **The consequence for Keddie.** Strawn, fall 1925: "**New man at the tester's bench this year.**" Keddie "did not test
  a pound in the season of 1925" (r03). **r17 states the human outcome plainly, from the family**: *"The hearing was in
  twenty-six [read: 1925]. My father drove down to this town for it, and he came back and hung his coat up and said
  **the board had heard everybody and found nothing, and that they had let Ansel go, which was the finding whatever they
  wrote in the book.** He never went to another meeting of that Association as long as he lived, and he was a man who
  liked meetings."*
  **Note the date error (1926 for 1925) and resolve for 1925** — Keddie's dated statement, the dated award, Frick's
  account, the impoundment. 1926 is the year of Vose's printed address and of Kepp's paper; Hazel has landed on a nearby
  real year, as she does throughout.
- **Ansel Keddie did not live to see the answer** (r17). He kept the question mark in his book for fourteen years, made
  no accusation in return, and his last recorded words on it are "*there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when
  he is by himself.*"

## 9. THE 1919 FLASHPOINT — three documents, one year

Worth setting out as a unit; it is probably the shape of one of the eight stories.
- **The complaint had run from about 1902** — Grigg (r15): "*From about 1902 there had been grumbling out of Ostrey
  Hollow that the milk of that station tested under the milk of the rest of this valley. It was not one man and it was
  not one season. It went on and it grew, and it got into the talk at the annual meeting and into the talk at every
  crossroads store between here and Ashlin, and **it took the shape such things always take, which is that somebody must
  be doing it on purpose.**"* Corroborated by Sill (r14, earliest complaints 1902) and by Kepp (r16, 1926: "complained
  for something like a quarter of a century" — 1926 − 25 ≈ 1901).
- **1908**: the committee; the report; "received and accepted, and the question was closed by it for eleven years."
- **1919**: the question is reopened "**by men who were boys when we made it**" (Grigg). Three things happen in that one
  year:
  1. **Grigg's committee report is reprinted and circulated to all officers and patrons**, with a covering letter that
     inflates the sampling months and quadruples the Bulletin 214 figure (§5).
  2. **Selby Vose circularizes nine families about adding water to their milk** (r01, r03, r09, r14, r17). Hazel: "*My
     mother read hers twice and put it in the stove. I was small but I remember the stove… He sent nine and he was
     answered by none of them, which in that valley is a shout.*" **Eleven years after his own Association's committee
     had certified "the milk is whole."**
  3. **Jerome Cudd writes his 1919 leaf** over the ridge — "*The four Hessel Bottom men, summer of 1919: 3.79, 3.81,
