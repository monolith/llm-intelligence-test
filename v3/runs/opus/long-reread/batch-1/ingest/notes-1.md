## 1. THE NARRATORS AND WHAT THEY ARE WORTH

- **r01 — Vira Toland**, born 1912 at the top end of Ostrey Hollow, daughter of a Hessel Bottom patron. Taped at her kitchen table, 1979, by "a young man from the county." Childhood memory, sixty years on, no documents in hand. She was 7 in 1919. Lived two years at Ashlin when first married; otherwise always in the Hollow. Warm, vivid, and repeatedly wrong on dates and figures.
- **r02 — Effie Loomis**, secretary of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association 1901–1940, "Notes toward a history of the Association," written 1941, the year after she laid down the minute book. Deliberately separates "from the minutes" from "from my own recollection." Quotes Article VII and the 1925 award verbatim. **Highest documentary authority for Association proceedings.** Born 1875 (was 14 in 1889).
- **r03 — Ansel Keddie**, tester at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station. Two papers: (I) his nine-sentence sworn statement standing before the board of arbitration at Ashlin, **17 March 1925**; (II) an unsent letter written at Ostrey Hollow, spring 1925, found among his papers. Quotes his own test-book leaves verbatim. The quoted book leaves outrank the letter's prose recollections.
- **r04 — Ivy Keddie**, "What the Crate Held," a talk read from typescript to the **Ordell County Historical Society, Ashlin, 1959**, with the crate on the table and the borrowed books open beside it; quoted papers read from originals. Ansel's granddaughter. **This is the solving document.** Physical measurement + five independent record sets.
- **r05 — Wilbur Teague**, bookkeeper of the Association **1905–1945**, Chapter Nine of an unpublished memoir written at Ashlin, winter 1948, three years after retiring. Quotes his own 1898 ledger entry verbatim. Reliable on the ledger line; unreliable on the two summary totals he gives from memory (see conflicts).

## 2. STORY THREAD A — THE FOUNDING OF THE ASSOCIATION AND ARTICLE VII (1889–1892)

- **1889**: farmers of the Larrow Valley met at **Larrow Green** and organized the **Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association**. **Gideon Vose** elected first president, held the office **1889–1911** — twenty-two years, longest of any man. (r02 §1)
- Before the creamery, members sold milk where they could, "no common test and no common price, and every man's arithmetic was his own." (r02 §2)
- **1891**: main creamery built at **Larrow Green, on Larrow Creek**; same building still standing in 1941. (r02 §2)
- **Gideon Vose drafted the by-laws himself**; not a lawyer; "they read as a farmer wrote them."
- **Article VII adopted at the annual meeting of 1892**, the year after the creamery opened. Effie Loomis sets it down entire because it has been misquoted at her across the desk for forty years:
  > *"Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."*
- Loomis's gloss: before it, a man was paid on can weight; after it, on the fat found by a test made where he delivered. **The last clause is the one nobody quotes — "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." Not the creamery's test, not the valley average, not a re-test anywhere else. The station's test, and no appeal from it.** She calls that clause "the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business." Written 1892 by a man who **died in 1917** and could not be asked about it afterward.
- **Records gap**: the **1889–1892 minute book was lost in the office move of 1938**. Not thrown out — carried out with the rest and never arrived. Loomis searched every place a book can be in the building. "Anybody who tells you what was resolved at the first four annual meetings is telling you a story he had from his father."
- Officers in Loomis's time: Loomis secretary 1901–1940 (minute book in her own hand throughout); **Wilbur Teague** bookkeeper from **1905**; **Hobart Sill** buttermaker at Larrow Green (kept the churn book and the cream receiving book); **Selby Vose**, "son of the late president," made **manager in 1913, served until 1938**. Loomis's confession: "not one of us ever went up the valley and stood in the Ostrey Hollow station on a working morning to watch the work being done. I include myself first in that."
- Office at Ashlin: one room over a harness shop, cold at the north end (r05).

## 3. STORY THREAD B — THE STATION, THE SHORT GLASS, AND THE TWENTY-SIX-YEAR SHORTFALL (1896–1923)

### The station
- **Skimming station at the head of Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green.** Low whitewashed building, cold inside even in August, smelled of milk and steam and hot iron; engine audible before the last turn (r01). Took in the milk of the upper farms, weighed it, sampled and tested it, separated it, sent the **cream down to Larrow Green** and **gave the skim back to the patron** for hogs and calves (r02 §5, r05).
- **Nine families hauled to that station** (r01, r03 — the 1919 circular went to nine families). **Seven farms had rights on Hessel Bottom**, the wet meadow below the hamlet; the best milk came off the Bottom. Not a contradiction: nine station patrons, seven with Bottom rights.
- **Emmet Brant** was the largest of the seven Hessel Bottom farms and **"the station's largest patron in every year of which we have a record"** (r02 §5). Most cows, most hay ground (r01).
- **Ansel Keddie** ran/tested it. Vira Toland: he "went up there in ninety-five, the year before they opened it, to get it ready, and he tested that first summer too." **Keddie's own first leaf and his own count contradict the 1895 testing**: 29 seasons, 1896 through 1924, and he "did not test a pound in the season of 1925."
- First leaf of the test book:
  > *"Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie."*
- **Internal conflict inside r03**: the letter's prose says "The glass I began on in ninety-six was borrowed off the **Ashlin creamery**"; the quoted first leaf says **"borrowed of Larrow Green."** Resolve for Larrow Green — quoted contemporaneous document over 29-year-old recollection; and no Ashlin creamery is attested anywhere (Ashlin held the office over a harness shop and the courts/lawyers).
- **The station's own outfit came up the valley in a crate the spring following (1897).**

### Keddie's method and book (from the unsent letter)
- Day began half past five; wagons up in the dark; intake room open, lamps lit, scale swinging free before the first wagon. Every can weighed and the weight called and written before the milk went anywhere. **The scale was tested by the state's man on his rounds and was true; in 29 seasons no patron ever disputed a weight and was right.**
- Sample taken from each patron's milk as it was weighed, into **his own numbered jar**, **made up over ten days** before testing. On testing morning: warmed in water to blood-warm through, poured vessel to vessel until the risen cream was carried down through the whole.
- Charge drawn up in the measure to the mark on the stem, tip wiped, blown out into the numbered bottle; acid poured down the side; bottle turned till the milk darkened; **four bottles in the hand tester, weighted against each other, cover down, crank turned at the taught speed**; fat read off in per cent against the rulings **with a pair of dividers**.
- Book kept in **five columns: date, patron, pounds, test, fat.** Errors crossed and initialed; **never a figure written over a figure.**
- Statement to the board: "I have tested as I was taught and as the state man saw me do it, **thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons**, and I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are… I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself."

### The 1922 summary leaf (quoted identically in r03 and r04 — same document, one source)
> *"Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80."*

### The nine weeks of 1911 — the accidental controlled experiment
- Two entries as Keddie made them:
  > *"May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure."*
  > *"July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."*
- The question mark is Keddie's; he let it stand fourteen years and would not put anything in its place he did not know.
- Ivy: **8 May to 10 July 1911**, nine weeks; the borrowed measure **came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon**; same patrons, herds, pasture, season, acid, bottles, same man at the crank — **one object changed** — average went to 3.80 and returned to 3.61 the day the station's own measure came back.
- **Conflict**: Teague (r05) says the measure broke "in the spring of 1911" and they made shift with a borrowed one for **six weeks** before **their own came up from town**. Resolve for **nine weeks, borrowed from Larrow Green** — Keddie's dated book entries are a contemporaneous document; Teague is recalling 37 years later and recorded it only as a small replacement charge.

### The gap was flat, not seasonal
- Keddie: "They did not come and go with the season. The gap was there in July as it was there in February, in the flush of the grass and in the dead of the winter on dry hay, and it was the same in a wet year as in a dry one."
- **Anomaly to flag**: Keddie's letter also says "**In 1924 the average of this station was 3.80**, and I wrote that figure with the same pen and the same measure of care as all the others." This contradicts his own 1922 summary leaf (3.61 ±0.03 every year since 1898 save the 1911 nine weeks) and contradicts the physical explanation, since the short glass was in use until June 1923 — but 1924 is *after* the condemnation, so a 1924 average of 3.80 is exactly what the pipette theory predicts. Read that way it is not an error at all but corroboration: the tests rose permanently once the glass was replaced. Treat cautiously.

### The condemnation and the crate
- **June 1923**: the **state's inspector condemned a crate of measures** at the station; it was set aside and **new ones ordered in its place** (r02 §8). Loomis: **the crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all** — not by patrons, officers, or the board, in any of the three sessions. "The award's phrase about 'the books and appliances of the station' is a form of words the board used and not a list of anything." Her closing line: "the interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying."
- **Vaught's bulletin dates the short glass from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923** — 12 June 1923 is presumably the condemnation date.
- Ivy, February 1958: went up the Hollow to the station building (used for storage since it closed; fence wire and salt kept in it). Loft over the old intake room, reached by a ladder; under a tarpaulin at the back, a wooden crate **stenciled on the end "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET."** Carried up there **June 1923** and there still.
- Inside, packed in straw: the station's **condemned milk-test pipettes**. **Six dozen (72) bought new for the station in 1897**, on an invoice totalling **$77.93** for the whole outfit — pipettes, bottles, acid measure, hand tester and freight together. **Nine were unbroken.**

### Ivy's measurement, 9 March [1958], at Farland against a calibrated burette
> *"Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for."*

### The five record sets Ivy assembled (no two ever in one room before 1958)
1. **The station test book** — in the Association's office since 1925; came to the Historical Society Rooms in 1959.
2. **The Brant weigh book**, kept at Hessel Bottom **from 1897 onward**, lent by **Roy Brant, Emmet Brant's son**, who has kept it up himself since his father died.
3. **The Alder Corners Cheese Company's test figures**, from over **Redlow Ridge in Vessey County**, copied out for her by **Rosalie Cudd**, who runs that factory and is **Jerome Cudd's niece**. The book has never left Vessey County; Ivy never saw it. (Second-hand transcript — note the dependency.)
4. **Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks**, kept by the **state's dairy inspector for this county from 1908**; in Ivy's own college library at **Farland since 1947** — eleven years in which nobody put a question to them.
5. **The Association's own annual ledgers.**

### The refutation of the "wet hay" explanation
- **1908**: a committee came up and sampled the herds through the winter and reported that **the wet hay of the Bottom thinned the cream, and that the remedy lay with the land** (r03).
- **State Agricultural College, Bulletin No. 214 (1911)**, quoted by Ivy:
  > *"Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."*
- Ivy: five hundredths in winter, nothing in summer, against a gap of nineteen hundredths standing flat in every month of every year — "**four times too small and it is the wrong shape.**"
- **Alder Corners cross-check**: the factory over the ridge tested the milk of **four Hessel Bottom men** in their summer flush; over **fourteen overlapping seasons** that same milk off that same wet ground ran **about nineteen hundredths higher at Alder Corners than at home.**

### The vindication
- **Bulletin No. 471**, issued **October 1958**, last paragraph, Professor **Vaught**'s wording and Ivy's together:
  > *"No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."*
- Ivy's tally: **glass condemned 1923, cause found 1958 — thirty-five years.** Grandfather dismissed 1925, cleared 1958 — **thirty-three years.** He **died in 1934** and had been **dead twenty-four years** when the sheet was read that clears him. "I have no remedy to propose. I have only the crate, and it is on the table."

## 4. STORY THREAD C — THE STATION-GAIN LINE (Teague's ledger, 1898/1905 onward)

- Teague's account of the mechanism: on one side of the station account he entered **the butterfat credited to patrons on the station's own test** (what the station's books said it had bought); on the other, **the butterfat that actually came down the valley in the station's cream and was weighed and tested into the vats at Larrow Green** (what they had in hand). "The two were never the same. **The second was always the larger.**" The difference he carried to the general account of the Association.
- The entry, quoted complete:
  > *"Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, **57,760 lb**. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, **60,800 lb**. Station gain, **3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual**. Carried to the general account."*
  **This is the entry for 1898**, "and there is one like it for every year after, in the same terms and in the same hand for as long as the hand was mine."
- **These numbers corroborate everything else exactly**: 1,600,000 lb of milk × 3.61% = 57,760 lb; × 3.80% = 60,800 lb; difference 3,040 lb; 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.263% ≈ "five and a quarter per cent."
- Teague on why nobody looked: "A figure that jumps about will keep you awake. **A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture.**"
- **Internal inconsistency in r05**: he says "**the station-gain line was my own device; I began it when I came in 1905**" and denies it was any old dodge of the founders — yet the entry he quotes complete is **the 1898 entry**, seven years before he arrived. Either he was working from a predecessor's ledger, or he mis-stated the start year, or the "1898" label is his slip. Unresolved; flag.
- **Teague's two summary figures are the outliers in the whole corpus**: "Over the years the account ran, that station took in **forty-four million pounds of milk**. And the average test of all the patrons of Ostrey Hollow… was **3.16**." Both conflict with r02 (Loomis, from Teague's own annual sheets: **40,000,000 lb**, average 1,600,000/yr) and r04 (Ivy: 40,000,000 lb, recorded 3.61). **3.16 is almost certainly a digit transposition of 3.61.** The 44 vs 40 million may be a longer span ("the years the account ran," 1898–1945, vs the 25 claim years) or simple error. **Resolve for 40,000,000 lb and 3.61 recorded** — Loomis copied from Teague's own annual sheets and added the columns twice; Ivy independently reached the same figures from the ledgers; Teague was writing from memory at 1948.
- **Governance evidence**: "**Mr. Vose knew what that account was doing. He signed the sheet every year.** I laid the annual sheet before him each spring and he put his name at the foot of it, and a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him. What he made of it he never told me." And: "**I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone. I let it alone.** I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience."
- **Teague is the origin of the theft theory.** What he told the board in 1925 and repeats verbatim in 1948: "Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons… One man weighed the milk, one man drew the sample, one man made the test, one man wrote the figure that the pay slip was made from, and one man ran the separator. **There was no second man in that building from the spring to the fall.** I do not say what any particular person did on any particular day… I say that a business which is arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken from it… **The remedy is two men or a lock, and the Association had neither.**" — Note he never accuses Keddie; the valley converted his structural point into a personal charge.

## 5. STORY THREAD D — THE 1919 CIRCULAR AND THE KEDDIE–BRANT RUPTURE

- **1902**: complaints began — "it was first said down at Larrow Green that Ostrey Hollow milk did not test with the rest." (r03)
- **1908**: the committee and the wet-hay report (above).
- **1919**: a printed circular went out from the Association, **over the manager's signature — Selby Vose**, to **nine families** at Ostrey Hollow, saying **there was water in the cans** coming down from Ostrey Hollow. (r01; r03 confirms: "In 1919 a circular went out from the Association to nine families up here saying there was water in their cans.")
- Vira Toland's scene: it rained that day; her father read it at the table **with his hat still on, which he never did**; her mother stood at the stove with her back turned the whole time; he read it twice, then put it in the drawer of the desk in the front room, **where it still was when he died**; her brother may have it yet. Some kept theirs till they died; **one woman kept hers pinned inside the lid of her trunk.** "You can tell people a thing like that is only business and they will still keep the paper forty years to prove what was said of them."
- Keddie on the circular: "I carry no charge against any man who put his name to that paper. I only say that I stood in that room every morning of those years and I know what came into it, and that **nine kitchens in this Hollow have had that letter in a drawer ever since**."
- **The rupture**: **Orra Keddie was Ansel Keddie's wife and Emmet Brant's sister.** So the man who tested the milk had married the sister of the man the letter cost most. Before 1919 the two families ate Sunday dinners together, "the two houses in one kitchen and the men out at the fence after." **After 1919 they did not speak — not at the store, not at a funeral. Orra would walk down to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane.** They lived within sight of each other's chimney smoke for years and never said good morning.
- Keddie's own line: "My wife Orra is Emmet Brant's sister. Emmet brought more milk to my door than any other man on the Bottom, and every pound of it I weighed myself and every test of it I made myself and wrote in the book. **He and I have not spoken since the circular.** That is the plainest fact in this letter and it is the one I find I have the least to say about."
- Orra outlived Ansel "a good while" and carried the stigma the whole time (r01).

## 6. STORY THREAD E — THE PETITION, THE ARBITRATION, AND THE DISMISSAL (1925)

- **January 1925**: the patrons of Ostrey Hollow petitioned the Association. Complaint: **their milk had never tested as their neighbors' milk tested and they had been paid accordingly for a very long time**; they asked to be made whole. (r02 §6). Teague: "The patrons petitioned in the winter, when a farmer has time to be angry."
- **The board of arbitration sat at Ashlin under Alonzo Frick, attorney, of that place, from 17 March to 4 May 1925.** (r02 §6) Teague calls Frick "as fair a chairman as ever a hearing had."
- **Three explanations were argued before it. None of the three was accepted.** Loomis declines to rehearse them; "they are in the minutes and the minutes are in the safe." **This is a live gap — the three explanations are not named in any source read so far.**
- The award, recorded by Loomis herself on the last day:
  > *"The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account."*
- Loomis: "*Finds no cause established*, and then an award of thirty-eight hundred dollars: I took it down as it was given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once."
- Keddie on the same paradox: "**I was put out on the thirtieth of April.** They found no cause established and they put me out on the last day of the month, and both of those things are true at once, and I have given up trying to hold them in my head together."
- **Chronology oddity worth flagging**: the board sat until **4 May 1925** and the award was recorded on the last day, but **Keddie was put out on 30 April 1925** — four days *before* the award. The dismissal preceded the finding.
- Teague was called and gave his evidence; the award was charged to the station account and he entered it "and it balanced."
- **The crate of condemned glass, sitting in the loft since June 1923, was never mentioned in any of the three sessions.**

### Conflicts on this thread
- **Award amount: $3,500 (r01, Vira, from memory: "they paid the patrons thirty-five hundred dollars and called it settled… split up nine ways over all those years — my father's share of it did not buy a hay rake") vs $3,800 (r02 quoted award, r04, r05).** Resolve for **$3,800** — Loomis transcribed the award from the board's own words into the minute book and quotes it; Teague entered it in the ledger; Ivy uses it in arithmetic that closes exactly against $19,000 (a fifth). Vira is a single, non-independent memory.
- **Date/order of the hearing: Vira places it "the winter and spring after" 1919 — i.e. 1919/20.** Wrong by six years; the hearing is fixed at 1925 by three independent sources (minutes, Keddie's dated statement, Teague).
- **Cause of the tests rising: Vira says "the tests went up… all at once, one summer… and nobody ever said why," and reads it as the correction that followed Keddie's removal.** The chronology is inverted: the glass was condemned **12 June 1923** and replaced, so the tests rose in mid-1923 — **two years before the 1925 hearing and Keddie's dismissal.** Vira's causal story ("He was taking the cream. They caught him at it and they put him out, and that is why the numbers went right the summer after") is therefore refuted on its own timeline as well as by Bulletin 471.
- Vira's own father would not say it out loud: he "said Ansel Keddie was an honest man who had got into something, which is a nice way of saying it and I never saw the difference."

## 7. RELATIONSHIP EVIDENCE (for B1–B4)

**B1 — Ivy Keddie to Ansel Keddie.** Ivy states it in her own first paragraph: "**Ansel Keddie, who kept the test book at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station, was my grandfather. My father, Duncan Keddie, who taught school at Ashlin, was his son.**" She says it once and then sets it aside because nothing in her demonstration depends on her opinion of him — "It depends on nine pieces of glass and a burette." **The thing pointing elsewhere**: Vira Toland describes the researcher who came in 1958 as "**Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed**, so she was **of the family both sides** in a manner of speaking." That is not a contradiction — it is a second, maternal-line description of the same woman. Vira never names her.

**B2 — Ivy Keddie to Hazel Brant.** Derivation from what is on the page: Ansel married **Orra**, who was **Emmet Brant's sister**. Their son **Duncan** is therefore Emmet Brant's nephew. Ivy is Duncan's daughter, so Emmet Brant's great-niece; **Roy Brant** (Emmet's son, keeper of the Brant weigh book) is Duncan's first cousin and Ivy's first cousin once removed. Vira's independent testimony puts Ivy at **first cousin once removed to Hazel Brant** — which fits exactly if **Hazel is of Roy's generation, i.e. a child of Emmet Brant** (Duncan's first cousin, Ivy's first cousin once removed). **What the sources leave open**: Hazel Brant is never introduced, dated, or given a parent in anything read so far. Whether she is Emmet's daughter, a daughter of another Brant sibling, or Roy's own child (which would make her Ivy's second cousin, not first cousin once removed) is not established. Also open: whether Orra and Emmet had other siblings, and Orra's own parents. **Flag for later readers: watch for Hazel Brant's parentage.**

**B3 — Selby Vose's father.** **Direct conflict.**
- r01 (Vira, hearsay): "The manager that signed it was Selby Vose, down at Larrow Green. He was **old Gideon Vose's nephew**."
- r02 (Loomis, from the minutes): "**Selby Vose, son of the late president**, was made manager in 1913 and served until 1938." The late president at the time of Loomis's writing is Gideon Vose (president 1889–1911, died 1917).
Resolve for **son** — Loomis names officers from the minute book she kept in her own hand; Vira is a child's village recollection sixty years on, and is demonstrably wrong on the award figure and the hearing date. **Why it matters at Ostrey Hollow**: the man who wrote **Article VII**, whose last clause made the station's own test unappealable, was Gideon Vose; his son Selby was the manager who (a) **signed the annual station-gain sheet every spring** for twenty-five years (Teague), (b) was **told of the discrepancy by Teague in 1916 and said to let it alone**, and (c) **signed the 1919 circular accusing nine families of watering their cans.** The rule that trapped the patrons and the officer who declined to look behind it were father and son. Vira's independent sense of it: "the family always thought the valley belonged to them a little."

**B4 — Vose and Keddie.** One claim only: r01, Vira Toland — "**Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins. Everybody knew that.**" Presented as common knowledge that "nobody wrote down anywhere." Her point is that the 1919 letter was not merely the Association speaking but "somebody's cousin saying it, and that is a different thing altogether in a place this size." **Uncorroborated by any document or any other narrator so far, and offered by the least reliable narrator; but the sources also offer nothing against it.** It would make Selby Vose's mother a first cousin of Ansel Keddie's wife — i.e. Selby a first cousin once removed of Orra, and connected to the Brants by marriage as well. **Flag: needs corroboration; note that "old Mrs. Vose" is unnamed and undated, and could denote Gideon's wife or his mother.**

## 8. ARITHMETIC FOR SECTION C

### C1(a) — the proportion, from the glassware
Pipettes were **blown to deliver 17.6 c.c.**; measured mean delivery **16.72 c.c.**; short **0.88 c.c.**
**16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly.** Every test made with that glass **read 95 per cent of the truth** — low by **one part in twenty (5 %)** measured against the truth. Ivy's mechanism: a butterfat bottle's neck is ruled to read percent **for one charge and no other**; a short charge gives a short reading **in the same proportion**.
The nine individual means: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 (five runs each). Spread 16.70–16.74 — tight; no outlier to argue about.
**The error is proportional, not a fixed offset.** Ivy is emphatic: "The station did not lose a set number of points. **It lost a twentieth of whatever was there.**" Worked examples she gives: a herd **recorded at 4.56 was short 0.24** of a point (true 4.80); a herd **recorded at 3.42 was short 0.18** (true 3.60). "**Because the error was a proportion, it fell hardest on the best milk.** The richer the milk, the more was taken from it, and no man had any way of knowing that his good cows cost him more than his poor ones." — This is why Emmet Brant, with the best and the most milk, lost most.

### C1(b) — the true average test and the shortfall in points
Recorded average **3.61**. **3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80.** True average test **3.80 per cent**.
**Short by 0.19 percentage points** ("nineteen hundredths"). Note 0.19 is **5 % of 3.80** and **5.26 % (five and a quarter per cent) of 3.61** — both figures circulate and both are right so long as you say what they are percentages of.
Independent corroboration of 3.80: (i) the 1896 season on **borrowed Larrow Green glass** averaged **3.80**; (ii) the **nine weeks of 1911** on a borrowed measure stood at **3.80**; (iii) **1924**, after replacement, averaged **3.80**; (iv) Teague's 1898 ledger line has 60,800 lb actually received on 1,600,000 lb of milk = **3.80 %**; (v) the Alder Corners overlap ran **~0.19 higher** than at home over fourteen seasons.

### C1(c) — pounds of butterfat not credited
Basis (r04, corroborated by r02): **twenty-five full delivery years, 1898 to 1922**; the station took in **40,000,000 lb of milk**, an average of **1,600,000 lb a year**.
- Credited at 3.61 %: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**
- Truly delivered at 3.80 %: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lb**
- **Difference: 76,000 lb of butterfat.**
Cross-check per year: 1,600,000 × 0.0019 = 3,040 lb/yr × 25 = 76,000 lb. Matches Teague's 1898 "station gain, 3,040 lb" exactly.
**Two caveats to state, because the question says "over the full delivery years affected":**
1. **Vaught's bulletin dates the defect from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923** — i.e. part of 1897 and part of 1923 lie outside the 25 full years used for the 76,000 lb. Ivy calls it "**this valley's twenty-six-year anomaly**." The 76,000 lb therefore **understates** the whole; the 1897 and 1923 partial years are unquantified in any source read so far. (1896 is not affected — borrowed Larrow Green glass, 3.80.)
2. **The nine weeks of 1911 were tested with a sound borrowed measure**, so no shortfall accrued in them; Ivy's 76,000 lb does not appear to deduct them. A pro-rata deduction would be ~526 lb, but 8 May–10 July is the flush, so the true deduction is larger than pro-rata. Neither correction is computed anywhere in the sources; the 76,000 lb figure is the one the bulletin and the ledgers both carry.

### C1(d) — what it was worth, and what the patrons got
Butterfat over those years averaged **25 cents a pound** (r02 §7, from Teague's annual sheets, columns added twice; repeated by r04).
**76,000 lb × $0.25 = $19,000.**
The board awarded **$3,800**. **$3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20 — exactly one fifth (20 %).** Ivy: "That is a fifth of it."
Vira's memory of "thirty-five hundred dollars, split up nine ways over all those years — my father's share of it did not buy a hay rake" is the wrong figure but the right sense of scale.
Where the missing fat went: Vaught — "**stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain**." Teague carried it "to the general account of the Association where it belonged, since the Association had received it."

### C2 — Emmet Brant's 1910
**The largest patron is Emmet Brant** — "the station's largest patron in every year of which we have a record" (r02 §5), biggest man on the Bottom, most cows and hay ground, hauled more milk than anybody (r01), "brought more milk to my door than any other man on the Bottom" (r03).
**The pounds of milk Emmet Brant delivered in 1910 have NOT appeared in any source read in this segment.** The record that would hold it is named but not quoted: **the Brant weigh book, kept at Hessel Bottom from 1897 onward, lent to Ivy by Roy Brant.** **Next reader: watch for Brant's 1910 weigh-book figure, and for a 1910-specific butterfat price.**
The method, ready to apply once the poundage M is known:
- (a) credited fat 1910 = **M × 0.0361**
- (b) owed and not credited = **M × 0.0019** (equivalently, credited ÷ 19, since 0.0019/0.0361 = 1/19; or true fat × 0.05)
- (c) value = (b) × the butterfat price. **$0.25/lb is the 25-year average**, not necessarily the 1910 price; use a 1910 figure if one appears, and say which you used.
Sanity anchor: the whole station averaged 1,600,000 lb/yr across nine patrons; Brant was the largest of them, so a figure of roughly 300,000–500,000 lb would be plausible — **do not use that guess as an answer.**
