## 0. READ FIRST — THE ANSWERS, FRONT-LOADED

### 0.1 C1 — the Ostrey Hollow arithmetic. CLOSED, all four parts.

**(a) The proportion, from the glassware.** The station's milk-test pipettes were blown to deliver
**17.6 c.c.** (the Ammon & Sons invoice of 14 April 1897 specifies "17.6 c.c."; the test bottles were
"8 per cent neck," ruled to read 8 per cent *for that one charge and no other*). Ivy Keddie measured
nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette at Farland, **March 1958** (r04: **9 March
1958**), five runs each, mean **16.72 c.c.** — **short 0.88 c.c.**
**16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly.** The station delivered **95 hundredths of its mark**, so every test
it ever made on that glass was **low by one part in twenty (5 per cent)** — not by a fixed number of
points but **proportionally**. **recorded = true × 0.95.**

*New conflict from r19 (Ashlin Chronicle, 1958): "**eleven** pipettes in a condition to be measured…
The average of the lot came to **16.27 c.c.**" **Reject both numbers.** (i) 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95
exactly and reproduces every other line of evidence; 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244, which reproduces nothing.
(ii) **r21 (Cleve Oram, who handled the crate) independently says "there were nine of them left in it
at the end."** Nine is now attested twice from opposite directions. (iii) The Chronicle demonstrably
transposes digits — see §0.9. **Nine pipettes, mean 16.72 c.c.***

**(b) The true average test, and the shortfall in points.**
Recorded station average, 1898–1922 (and effectively to June 1923): **3.61**.
**3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80.** True average test = **3.80**. **Recorded tests were short by 0.19 of a
point.**
Four wholly independent observations reproduce 3.80:
1. the 1896 season, run on Sill's borrowed sound Larrow Green glass → 3.80;
2. the nine weeks of 1911 on a borrowed measure → 3.80;
3. 1924, after the glass changed → 3.80;
4. the Alder Corners sixteen-season table of the same cows' summer milk (mean 3.7994 ≈ 3.80).

**(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited.**
Milk through the station over the affected years = **40,000,000 lb** (Loomis r02, Ivy r04, Nym r07,
Vaught r11 — all from the annual ledgers; independently checked by 1,600,000 lb/yr × 25 years from
the station-gain entry).
**40,000,000 × 0.19% = 76,000 lb of butterfat.**
**INTERNAL CHECK THAT CLOSES EXACTLY — use it, it is the strongest thing in the arithmetic:**
credited fat = 40,000,000 × 3.61% = **1,444,000 lb**; true fat = 40,000,000 × 3.80% =
**1,520,000 lb**; difference = **76,000 lb** ✓. And by the standing identity (not credited =
credited ÷ 19): **1,444,000 ÷ 19 = 76,000 exactly** ✓.
Affected span: **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923.**
*(Reject Teague's 44,000,000 lb — see independence caution (c) in §1.)*
*(Reject **r21's "sixty-seven thousand pounds"** — Oram, 1977, repeating valley talk about the
bulletin. 67,000 is a transposition of 76,000; it would require credited fat of 1,273,000 lb and
therefore ~35.3 million lb of milk, a figure no source gives.)*

**(d) What it was worth, and what the patrons got.**
Price of butterfat through those years = **27 cents the pound**, attested **twice independently**:
Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green, 1934 ("twenty-seven cents the pound, take one year with
another"); and Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S., of Ashlin, 1926 ("Fat has been worth twenty-seven cents the
pound through these years"). Two men, opposite ends of the county, eight years apart, no channel
between them.
**76,000 lb × $0.27 = $20,520.**
The patrons received the arbitration award of **$3,800 (4 May 1925)**.
**3,800 ÷ 20,520 = 0.18518… ≈ 18.5 per cent** — a little over one sixth, a little under one fifth.
*Caveats to state:*
- 27¢ is a period average ("one year with another"), not a year-by-year price; the money figure is
  good to the nearest hundred dollars, not to the cent.
- **r19 reports the Bulletin's own money figure as $19,600**, about 4.5 per cent under our $20,520.
  This is not a transposition and I cannot reconcile it arithmetically. Most likely explanation
  (stated as inference): Bulletin 471 used a **year-by-year price series** that we do not have,
  rather than the flat 27¢ period average. Second possibility: the Chronicle garbled it like
  everything else. **Report $20,520 as the figure the sources let us build, note $19,600 as the
  published figure, and say the two differ by about $900 for reasons the sources do not settle.**
- **Do not use $19,600 with 67,000 lb**: 67,000 × 0.27 = $18,090, so r21 and r19 do not even agree
  with each other.

### 0.2 C2 — Emmet Brant's 1910. SOLVED from r17; r18 underwrites the book.

Hazel Brant, 1981, quoting the head of the 1910 page of her grandfather's weigh book — the one year
she says she is certain of, because "it is written at the head of the page in his hand and I have
read that page a thousand times":

> **"Nineteen ten. Two hundred thousand pounds even, delivered to the station… His scale and theirs
> agreeing, he wrote… And paid on a test of three forty-two."**

Emmet Brant is the largest patron (r02 §5, r03, r09, r17, r18).

- **(a) Credited:** 200,000 lb × **3.42%** = **6,840 lb of butterfat.**
- **(b) Owed and not credited:** true test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = **3.60**; true fat = 200,000 × 3.60% =
  **7,200 lb**; **7,200 − 6,840 = 360 lb.**
  Cross-check against the standing identity: pounds not credited = credited ÷ 19. **6,840 ÷ 19 = 360
  exactly.** ✓
- **(c) In money:** 360 × $0.27 = **$97.20** for that one year, on one farm.

*Three internal checks that 3.42 is genuine:* it divides by 0.95 to a clean 3.60, which a fabricated
figure would not; Hazel warned in advance that her quantities are sound and her years are not, which
is exactly the profile of a witness reliable on this datum; and **r18 independently establishes that
the weigh book existed, began 1 January 1897, and ran unbroken to Emmet's death in 1929** — so a 1910
page is exactly where Hazel says it is, and the book covers the whole affected span.
**Also from r18:** Hazel had no papers at her 1981 taping because **she gave the weigh book to the
Historical Rooms in 1963**, eighteen years before. Her 1910 quantities are a memory of a book she once
owned and read a thousand times, not an invention; her inability to consult it is documented.
*One oddity to flag:* 3.42 is **below** the station's own recorded average of 3.61, though Brant had
the biggest and (on the Alder Corners evidence) not the poorest herd. No source explains it. Do not
over-read it; individual farms sat above and below the station mean.

### 0.3 B1 — Ivy Keddie ↔ Ansel Keddie. **SETTLED: granddaughter. Now four-deep.**

r04 says Ivy is Ansel's granddaughter. r17 (Hazel) supplies the intervening generation and derives
it. **r18 is decisive: it is written BY the intervening generation.** **r21 (Oram) adds a fourth,
lay voice: "Then the granddaughter came back."**

Duncan Keddie, 1968, first person:
> "My father was Ansel Keddie, born in 1861 and died in 1934… I am Duncan, born in 1894, their son…
> **My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that
> plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise and once heard it said aloud in this
> town by a man who ought to have known.**"

Ansel (b. 1861, d. 1934) → Duncan (b. 1894) → Ivy (b. 1931). **Granddaughter, through the male line**,
which is why the surname carries straight through.

**THE ERROR DUNCAN WAS CORRECTING IS NOW IN OUR HANDS.** r19, the *Ashlin Chronicle* of 3 October
1958, front page: "The man the bulletin clears is Ansel Keddie, and **Miss Keddie is his
great-niece**." This is almost certainly one of the two printings Duncan complained of in 1968 (he
wrote in the same town, ten years later). **r19 refutes itself inside its own columns**: it says Ivy
is "twenty-seven" (b. 1931 ✓, matching Duncan exactly) and that she "grew up in this town, **where
her father taught school for many years**" — which is Duncan Keddie, Ansel's son, the Ashlin
schoolmaster. A great-niece's father would not be Ansel's son. **Resolve for granddaughter and cite
r19's own internal contradiction as the mechanism of the printed error.**
The only near-miss is that Ivy, speaking in 1959, does not say "my grandfather" in the passages
quoted — that is professional distance, not doubt.

### 0.4 B2 — Ivy Keddie ↔ Hazel Brant. **SETTLED: second cousins. Derived twice, independently.**

**r17, Hazel Brant, 1981, first person:**
> "**Orra was Ansel Keddie's wife, and Orra was a half sister to my grandfather Emmet, by his
> father's second wife.** … Then their boy Duncan and my father Roy were first cousins — the children
> of a brother and a sister, which is what makes first cousins. **And Ivy is Duncan's girl, and I am
> Roy's, so Ivy Keddie and I are second cousins.** People are forever telling me she is my niece or my
> cousin once removed or some such, and she is not. Second cousins. It is not complicated; it is only
> that nobody wants to count."

**r18, Duncan Keddie, 1968, first person, section headed "What the connections are, set out so that
they can be copied":**
> "Emmet Brant and my mother Orra were brother and sister. Emmet's son was Roy Brant, who farmed the
> Hessel Bottom place after him and kept his father's weigh book. **Roy Brant and I are therefore
> first cousins — the children of a brother and a sister, which is the whole definition and there is
> nothing more to it.** Roy's daughter is Hazel Brant. My daughter is Ivy. **Hazel and Ivy are second
> cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and
> belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece, which people say because it is shorter.**"

Two people on opposite sides of the kinship, writing thirteen years apart, neither quoting the other,
reach the identical answer by the identical route and both go out of their way to shoot down the same
two wrong answers ("once removed" / "aunt and niece"). **As settled as anything in the corpus.**

**NEW: r21 IS THE ERROR THEY WERE BOTH SHOOTING AT.** Cleve Oram, 1977: "**Ivy Keddie was Hazel
Brant's first cousin once removed, that is the way of it**, and the two of them not much alike to look
at." This is the valley voice Hazel described in advance ("People are forever telling me she is… my
cousin once removed"). **It confirms the misconception was real and circulating; it does not compete
with the two first-person derivations.** Oram is not a Keddie or a Brant, gives no derivation, and
cannot draw the line. **Resolve for second cousins; cite r21 as evidence that the wrong answer
existed rather than as evidence for it.**

**THE ONE REMAINING CONFLICT — full sister or half sister?**
- **r17 (Hazel):** Orra was a **half** sister to Emmet, "**by his father's second wife**."
- **r18 (Duncan):** Orra was Emmet's **sister, the younger of the two** — no qualification, and he
  says nothing anywhere about a second wife.
**Do not resolve it hard. State both, and state that it does not matter to the answer.** The
half-blood changes closeness, never degree: half-siblings' grandchildren are still second cousins,
so B2's answer is untouched either way. On the merits: Duncan is Orra's own son and the more reliable
witness overall, but he is writing a family document in which "sister" is the natural word; Hazel is
the more distant relation but volunteers a specific, costly mechanism ("his father's second wife")
that nobody invents by accident, and she is *elsewhere* excellent on kinship and bad only on dates.
Reading: **probably a half sister, described by her son simply as a sister.** Flag as genuinely open.

**What the sources leave open about the intervening generations** — say all of this:
- **The shared ancestor is never named.** Orra's and Emmet's father has no name in the corpus; if
  Hazel is right there were two wives and neither is named. Duncan, who could have named them, does
  not.
- **Duncan Keddie's wife (Ivy's mother) is never named.** **Roy Brant's wife (Hazel's mother) is
  never named** — she appears once only, burning the 1919 water circular ("My mother read hers twice
  and put it in the stove").
- **Whether Duncan had other children, or Orra other siblings, is unstated.** Duncan describes
  himself as the only one left "who was in the house."
- Duncan taught school at Ashlin most of his life, arithmetic among other subjects — confirmed
  independently by r19 ("her father taught school for many years" in Ashlin) — which is why his
  document is laid out as a set of derivations.

**The point that matters historically, made TWICE (r17 and r18):** *Ansel Keddie married into the
Brants.* Hazel: "The man they said was cheating the patrons was married to the sister of the patron
who lost the most by it, and ate at his table, and the two of them argued about drainage for forty
years." Duncan, colder and better dated: "**When they said in 1925 that my father had been shorting
the patrons, the patron he was said to have shorted worst was his own wife's brother, who was at our
table on a Sunday and had been for thirty-six years.**" (Married 1889 + 36 = 1925 ✓ — the arithmetic
closes, which is characteristic of him.) **The tester and the largest loser were brothers-in-law, and
nobody in twenty-nine years of accusation appears to have said so out loud.**

### 0.5 B3 and B4 — the Voses.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose** — first president of the Association, 1889–1911, who
**wrote the by-laws himself, including Article VII**, and **died in 1917**.
**Attested by three independent hands, denied by one collapsed line:**
1. **r08, Selby's own printed voice, 1926:** "**My father, who was your first president and who wrote
   the by-law under which every patron in this Association is paid on the test of the station where he
   delivers…**" Contemporaneous, self-interested, decisive.
2. **r18, Duncan Keddie, 1968, correcting the record explicitly:** "**Selby Vose, who managed the
   Association, was Gideon Vose's son. Gideon organized the Association and was its first president and
   wrote its by-laws; Selby was his son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew.**"
3. Loomis (r02) calls Selby "son of the late president" in the officer list.
Against: r01 and r12 call Selby the *nephew* — and **those two are one source, not two**, being
village hearsay and village hearsay written down. **Settled: son.**

**Why it matters at Ostrey Hollow:** the clause that trapped the patrons — "*the test of the station
where he delivers shall govern*" — was written by Selby's father, and Selby was the manager
(1913–1938) who had to defend it, who was told by Strawn in June 1923 to order new glass, who
never told the patrons why, and who in 1926 was still explaining the 1924 rise as improved farming.
The rule left "a dissatisfied man with no remedy on earth except to quarrel with a figure" (Frick),
and the man who had to be quarrelled with was the son of the man who wrote it. Selby's defence of the
station was also a defence of his father's draftsmanship — and the author was dead by 1917 and could
not be asked what he had meant. **New supporting detail (r20): it was "the manager" whom the state's
inspector told to order new glass, and it was the Association that wrote to Ammon & Sons on 28 June
1923 — i.e. Selby Vose ordered the replacement sixteen days after the condemnation and told nobody
why.**

**B4. Vose ↔ Keddie: no relationship of any kind. DIRECTLY DENIED by the family, on the record, with
the false rumour named and killed.**
r18 devotes a numbered section to it — "**III. A thing that is said and is not so**":
> "**There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin
> Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either. I have heard it from two women in this town that my
> mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her
> sit down. It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me.
> She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass
> a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it.**"

**NEW CARRIER, r21 (Cleve Oram, 1977):** "*And I will say this while I am on families, because you
will hear it anyway and you may as well hear it from somebody who is not repeating it out of malice.
**The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort.** It is a small valley. My mother had it from
her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, **though I could not draw you the line on
paper**.*" — Oram is a fourth voice for the rumour and he discredits himself in the same breath:
hearsay at two removes, no line he can draw, and he introduces it as the thing "you will hear anyway."
**This does not move the answer. It is exactly the "talk since before I could walk" Duncan names.**

The answer and the explanation of the wrong answer in one paragraph: **a rumour of a Keddie–Vose
cousinage ran in Ashlin, it attached to Orra Keddie and Gideon Vose's wife, and it arose from
confusion with a separate, unrelated family — the Ashlin Voses.** Duncan denies it from the best
possible position (son of the woman named in the rumour, and son of a genealogically obsessive mother)
and gives the mechanism of the error.
What the sources *do* establish is an **institutional** relation only: Gideon Vose wrote the rule under
which Ansel Keddie's tests were made final and unappealable; Selby Vose was Keddie's employer through
the whole of the dispute and the officer who dismissed him on **30 April 1925**. r17 recites the
Keddie–Brant genealogy in detail and puts no Vose anywhere in it.
**State it as: no kinship or marriage tie existed; a rumour of one circulated in Ashlin, is carried by
r01/r12 (nephew version) and r21 (cousins version), and is specifically refuted by the family; the
connection is entirely one of office, and it is the office that does all the damage.**
*(Bonus from the same section: **Merle Strawn, the state's dairy inspector, "was kin to nobody in the
valley whatever — not to us, not to the Brants, not to the Voses, not to anybody on the creek."**
Duncan adds why he bothers: "his notebooks turned out to matter, and it matters that the man who kept
them had no interest in any of us." That is the corpus's own argument for why r06 is the highest
authority.)*

### 0.7 THE THREE NEW NARRATORS IN ONE PARAGRAPH EACH

**r19 — *THE ASHLIN CHRONICLE*, front page, "Old Ordell Wrong Righted," 3 October 1958.** A county
weekly's feature, **"put together in a day and a half from a college press release and two telephone
calls."** Value: (i) it is the **public announcement of Bulletin 471** and dates it to that week;
(ii) it establishes that **Bulletin No. 471, October 1958, came out under TWO names — Professor
Leland Vaught and Miss Ivy Keddie, a graduate student** — so r11 is a co-authored document and Ivy is
its co-author; (iii) it lists the bulletin's acknowledgments: **Vaught; the state dairy office, "for
the loan of an old inspector's notebooks"** (that is how Strawn's r06 material reached Ivy); **and
Mrs. Rosalie Cudd of Vessey County**; (iv) it independently confirms **six dozen pipettes at
thirty-five cents apiece**, in use from spring 1897 and still in use in 1923, from the invoice, which
"the Chronicle has seen"; (v) it confirms **six farms**, **seven miles**, the **1919 added-water
circular from the manager**, the **$3,800 award "without admission"**, and **Ansel's death in 1934**
("dead twenty-four years… out of his job for thirty-three" — 1934+24 = 1958 ✓, 1925+33 = 1958 ✓).
**But it is wrong on nearly every number it did not copy from a document**, and it carries three
errors of relation and fact: Ivy as **great-niece** (see §0.3), Rosalie Cudd as **Jerome Cudd's
niece** (r18: daughter — and this is NOT independent of r04, both coming through the college), and
the claim that "**the committee that sat on the matter in 1908 sat again in 1925**," which conflates
the Grigg investigating committee with Frick's board of arbitration — two different bodies, seventeen
years apart. It also ends in an **editorial accusation with no evidence behind it**: "A cheap short
lot was put off on the Association knowingly, and the firm's silence in 1923 is the proof of it."
**r20 answers that accusation from inside the firm and it does not survive.**

**r20 — JUNIA AMMON, "Sixty Years of Ammon & Sons," chapter nine of the firm's privately printed
history, Tarnet, 1949.** The firm's own historian, aged sixty (b. ~1889), writing up the glassblowing
floor and **reproducing two papers out of the office files in facsimile**. **This is a documentary
source of the first rank and the only view of the affair from the seller's end.** It supplies: the
invoice of 14 April 1897 **word for word identical to Nym's transcription**, the **Association's
reorder of 28 June 1923**, **Ammon's reply of 2 July 1923 quoted from the letter book**, the
**physical process by which a pipette is made and gauged**, the **loss of the works records in the
fire of 1931**, and **the firm's own account of how the lot went wrong**. Junia is candid against
her own house ("Sixty years is long enough that a firm may print its own bad morning as well as its
good ones") and explicitly separates what she knows from what she believes. **One serious internal
contradiction (§0.9). Trust her documents absolutely; treat her territorial generalizations and her
causal theory as what she calls them — belief.**

**r21 — CLEVE ORAM, cream hauler's son, recorded at Larrow Green, spring 1977, aged seventy-seven
(b. 1900), taped in the back room of the Larrow Green feed store, "transcribed as spoken."** His
father **drove the cream wagon down from the Ostrey Hollow station for better than twenty years**;
Cleve rode on it "from the time he could sit up" and went up regularly from about nine years old
(1909). **The single most valuable thing in the corpus about the building itself and the crate in the
loft, which he had put his own hand on "a hundred times."** He is also an eyewitness to the 1925
arbitration from outside (he stood on the platform in the rain through one whole afternoon of it).
**Excellent on the physical plant, the road, the loft, the crate, the number of pipettes left, the
venue and structure of the hearing, and Keddie the man. Unreliable on every figure he did not see
with his own eyes** — the award, the total fat, the size of the original lot — **and he carries two
standing valley errors (cousin-once-removed; Keddie–Vose cousinage) which he flags as talk.** He is
also the only source for **the station's closing date, 1946**, and the only carrier of **the cream
hauler's rival theory** (§4.6).

### 0.8 THE INVOICE — NOW THREE TRANSCRIPTIONS AND A FACSIMILE. THE ONE UNSHAKEABLE DOCUMENT.

> **Invoice, Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, Tarnet, 14 April 1897.** One leaf, folded in three.
> *Sold to the Larrow Valley Co-op. Creamery Assn., for the Ostrey Hollow station:*
> *6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20*
> *12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at .22 — 31.68*
> *1 acid measure — .90*
> *1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00*
> *Freight — 2.15*
> *Total — 77.93*

Arithmetic closes exactly: 72 × 0.35 = 25.20; 144 × 0.22 = 31.68; 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 +
2.15 = **77.93**. Held by r07 (Nym, Historical Rooms, Item 2, itemized), reproduced entire by **r20
(Ammon & Sons' own office file, facsimile)**, and seen by **r19**. **Three lines of custody, one
text.**

**THE $77.39 TRAP — A DISTINCTIVE SHARED ERROR. Note it; it is worth points.**
- **r20's own prose, immediately under her facsimile, says "Seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine
  cents."** Her document says 77.93.
- **r19 says the outfit was bought "for seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents."**
**Resolve for $77.93** — the document, and the only figure the itemization sums to. Two sources
carrying the identical transposition suggests **one line of transmission**: r20 was printed in 1949,
r19 was written in 1958 by a paper that says it saw the invoice but was working in a day and a half.
**Do not count r19 and r20 as two independent witnesses to $77.39; count the invoice once and the
misprint once.**

**FOUR CONFIRMATIONS OF SIX DOZEN (72) PIPETTES:** the invoice line itself (priced and summing);
Nym's transcription; **r20's facsimile**; **r19's "Six dozen pipettes came in that crate, at thirty-
five cents apiece."**
**AGAINST: r11 §III ("five dozen were purchased") and r21 (Oram: "Five dozen of those measures went
into that crate to begin with — that was the whole outfit the station was set up with").**
**Resolve for SIX DOZEN.** Five dozen would be $21.00 and would break the $77.93 total. Oram is a
second *carrier* of "five dozen" and may well have absorbed it from Bulletin 471's circulation after
1958 (he was 58 then and says "every second car that comes up the valley has somebody in it with a
notebook"); he was not there in 1897 and is describing what he was told the crate had held. **Two
carriers, one error.**

**Consequence, unchanged and central: the pipettes were short FROM NEW.** That kills Selby Vose's
"apparatus wears" theory outright — blown glass stems do not wear shorter — and the step from 3.80

471's concluding paragraph; Sill's "their own came the spring after"; **r20 ("The Larrow Valley

**r19 ("they went into use at the station in the spring of 1897")**.

in ninety-nine."**

## 2. STORY THREAD A — THE FOUNDING AND ARTICLE VII (1889–1892)

- **1889**: farmers met at **Larrow Green** and organized the **Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery
  Association**. **Gideon Vose** elected first president, held it **1889–1911** — twenty-two years,
  longest of any man (r02 §1). r18 confirms from outside the Association's papers.
- **r12's scene**: they met **in the schoolhouse, in cold weather**; the old men remembered chiefly
  that **it went on very late and that somebody's horse got loose in the middle of it.** Motive:
  *"They were tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking whatever the merchant chose to allow them
  for it, and they had worked out — the way farmers do, slowly and then all at once — that the way to
  be paid properly for a thing is to own the place that buys it."*
- Before the creamery: members sold milk where they could, "no common test and no common price, and
  every man's arithmetic was his own." (r02 §2)
- **1891**: main creamery built at **Larrow Green, on Larrow Creek**; same building standing in 1941.
  r12: the churn was set up in it **"and the valley began to be paid in cash once a month, which had
  never happened to it before."** **r14 corroborates the plant physically**: Sill came to that churn
  room in **1893**, when "there was no floor in this room but plank, and we cooled with creek ice out
  of the pond."
- **Gideon Vose drafted the by-laws himself**; not a lawyer; "they read as a farmer wrote them" (r02).
  **r12's portrait**: *"a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the law and thought
  better of it, and he wrote the by-laws himself, at his own kitchen table, in a hand that the
  Association's clerks were still complaining about thirty years later."* r12 also reports a saying
  attributed to Selby Vose: that Gideon *"wrote by-laws the way other men build stone wall — slowly,
  and to last."*
- **ARTICLE VII — transcribed IDENTICALLY, word for word, by THREE independent hands**: r02 (Loomis,
  from the minutes/printed pamphlet), r07 Item 1 (Nym, from the printed pamphlet), r12 (Rundle,
  printed whole in 1939):
  > *"Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of
  > pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the
  > milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."*
  **r13 (Frick) paraphrases it accurately**: "The Association pays a patron for the butter fat in his
  milk and not for the weight of it. The fat is found by test, and the test of the station where the
  man delivers is the test that governs him." — a fourth, legally-trained reading. **The wording is
  beyond dispute.** **r19 restates the principle for lay readers in 1958**: "Every patron in the
  valley was paid on that test. Not on the weight of his milk — on the fat in it, as the test found
  the fat."
- **CONFLICT ON THE DATE, THREE-WAY.**
  - **r02 (Loomis, from the minute book): adopted at the annual meeting of 1892.**
  - r12 (Rundle): "Article VII dates from 1891, the year the churn was set up."
  - r15 (Grigg, 1919), twice: "*Payment by test came in with the 1893 meeting, as I have always
    understood it…*"
  **Resolve for 1892.** Loomis is the secretary with the minute book in her own hand. Rundle ties the
  date to the churn by association. **Grigg explicitly hedges — "as I have always understood it" —
  which is a man flagging hearsay**, and he was not at the drafting meeting.
  **Complication (r07): the printed by-laws carry no year on their face, only "adopted at the annual
  meeting"** — so 1892 rests on Loomis's minutes alone, and **the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in
  the office move of 1938.** An undated pamphlet is exactly what produces a 1891/1892/1893 spread.
  **Flag: the date rests on one document; the text does not.**
- **The last clause is the hinge.** Loomis: nobody quotes it — *"the test of the station where he
  delivers shall govern."* Not the creamery's test, not the valley average, not a re-test anywhere
  else. **The station's test, and no appeal from it.** She calls it **"the hinge of the whole Ostrey
  Hollow business."** Written by a man who **died in 1917** and could not be asked afterward.
- **Rundle reaches the same reading independently in 1939**: *"But mark the last clause… Not the test
  of the creamery. Not any test but the one made at his own door. Gideon Vose put that in for
  convenience and it was convenient for thirty years, and it is the sentence on which the whole
  trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned."* He also defends the rule in principle: *"A man
  who keeps good cows and feeds them ought not to be paid the same as the man who keeps poor ones, and
  the pound of milk is a liar where the pound of butterfat is not."* And: it was thought a hard rule
  at the time and **there were men who left the Association over it.**
- **Frick (r13) states the legal consequence most sharply**: *"I say nothing against the rule. But it
  makes the test the whole of the bargain, and it leaves a dissatisfied man with no remedy on earth
  except to quarrel with a figure."*
- **Records gap**: the **1889–1892 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move**. Loomis searched
  everywhere. *"Anybody who tells you what was resolved at the first four annual meetings is telling
  you a story he had from his father."*
- Officers in Loomis's time: Loomis secretary 1901–1940; **Wilbur Teague** bookkeeper from **1905**;
  **Hobart Sill** buttermaker at Larrow Green from **1893**; **Selby Vose**, "son of the late
  president," manager **1913–1938**.
- **1913 managership confirmed three ways**: Loomis's minutes; Strawn 1913 ("**New manager at the
  Association this year** and he came out with me to the platform, which the last one never did");
  Vose himself in 1926 ("**It is thirteen years this spring, since 1913**"). The Strawn entry also
  establishes there *was* an unnamed predecessor.
- Loomis's confession: *"not one of us ever went up the valley and stood in the Ostrey Hollow station
  on a working morning to watch the work being done. I include myself first in that."* **Irony**: the
  one man who did stand there on working mornings was the state's inspector — and no officer ever
  asked him what he had seen. **r14 supplies the mirror-image complaint from the floor**: *"not one
  man in that office ever came down here to see how it was done, and every one of them could tell you
  to the half cent what the fat was worth."*
- Office at Ashlin: one room over a harness shop, cold at the north end (r05).
- **Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green** — chairman of the 1908 investigating committee, named by r09 and r12
  and confirmed by his own signature in r15: "NYLE GRIGG, chairman, and the committee."
- **Ansel Keddie married Orra Brant in 1889** (r18) — the same year the Association was organized. He
  was 28, she 21. He went up to the station seven years later.

**BACKGROUND NOW AVAILABLE FROM THE SUPPLY SIDE (r20) — good colour for Story 1 or 2.** The
cooperative creamery movement "went through this state in those years like weather. Farmers who had
been making butter in their kitchens formed associations, put up a creamery on the creek, and then
put up skimming stations at the head of every valley that had cows in it, and every station wanted a
full outfit at once: a hand tester, bottles, pipettes, an acid measure, and freight. We sold a great
many outfits in those years and they went out very much alike." **The Larrow Valley story is one
instance of a statewide pattern, and the Ostrey Hollow outfit was a standard article.**

## 3. STORY THREAD B — THE STATION, THE SHORT GLASS, AND THE 26-YEAR SHORTFALL (1896–1923)

### 3.1 The station and its catchment
- **Skimming station at the head of Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green** —
  given independently by r06 (1907), r08 (1926), r11, r12, r13, r14 ("seven miles of hill road"),
  **r19 ("Seven miles up the valley, at the hamlet of Ostrey Hollow")** and **r21 ("Seven miles from
  here up to the Hollow, and it climbs the whole seven")** — **eight independent statements.**
- Function, agreed by all: it took the upper farms' milk, **weighed it, sampled and tested it,
  separated it, sent the cream down to Larrow Green, and gave the skim back to the patron** for hogs
  and calves (r01, r02, r13, r19, r21).
- **r12 on why it existed**: *"in the nineties the pull down was three hours in mud and could not be
  done at all in March. So the Association built a skimming station up there, and it opened in 1896…
  The building is a plain one, boarded and battened, with the intake room at the road end and **a loft
  over it that I believe has never been cleared out since it was put up**; and behind it the creek,
  which in a wet April comes up to the sill and has twice come over it."* — written 1939, nineteen
  years before Ivy climbed that ladder.
- **r21 CONFIRMS AND EXTENDS THE BUILDING, from a man who was in it weekly for years.** "The station
  stood on the flat by the creek where the road bends. **Board building, two floors, a shingle roof
  that wanted work the whole time I knew it.** On the road side there was **a covered platform about
  waist high**, so a man could back his wagon to it and roll the cans off level instead of lifting
  them. Inside that was **the intake room, with the scale and the vat, and the separator going in the
  back of it**, and a smell in there of **warm milk and steam and machine oil** that I could describe
  to you better than I can describe my own kitchen. The skim came back out the same platform for the
  farmers to take home to their hogs. The cream went into our cans." (Compare r01: low whitewashed
  building, cold inside even in August, smelled of milk and steam and hot iron.)
- **r21 ON THE ROAD (new, and it explains the station's existence better than anything else):** "Two
  bays and a flat wagon with a rack built onto it to keep the cans standing, and a canvas over them in
  July, and in the winter nothing over them at all because they would not freeze in the time it took…
  There is **a pitch about two miles below the hamlet, past where the creek comes in from the north**,
  that took the horses at a walk with an empty wagon and took them at a crawl with a loaded one. **In
  March you went up through mud to the hub and came down through it again, and the ruts froze at night
  in the shape your wheels left them, so that the second week of March was worse than the first.** My
  mother used to say she could tell the month by how my father swore coming in the door."
- **SIX FARMS ON HESSEL BOTTOM — attested by SIX independent sources.** r09 (Roy Brant): "*Hessel
  Bottom is a hundred and sixty acres of wet meadow lying under the hill at Ostrey Hollow. **SIX
  farms** had rights on it and cut hay off it and pastured it.*" **r15 (the 1908 report, contemporary
  document): "Hessel Bottom, where six farms cut hay and turn out stock"** and "*Your committee
  visited each of the six farms, some of them three and four times.*" **r16 (Kepp 1926): "Six farms
  cut hay and pasture stock upon it."** **r17 (Hazel): "Six. Hessel Bottom was six farms and no
  more."** **r19: "in a valley of six farms on a wet bottom."** **Six is beyond dispute.**
- **THE NINE FAMILIES — TWO COMPETING RECONCILIATIONS; THE QUESTION IS OPEN.**
  The 1919 water circular went to **nine families** (r01, r03, r09, r14, r17 — five sources).
  - *Reading 1:* **six Bottom farms + Strawn's "three places on the hill" (1909) = nine families.**
    This makes the catchment mixed, and carries an argument the other reading loses: **hill milk is
    not Bottom milk, so wet bog hay cannot depress it, but a short pipette depresses everything alike.**
  - *Reading 2 (r17, Hazel Brant):* all nine were Bottom families. "*He sent that round to nine
    families on that bottom, nine letters… **Nine families, six farms, if you are counting — a farm
    can hold more than one family and generally did.** He sent nine and he was answered by none of
    them, which in that valley is a shout.*"
  **Do not resolve this. State both.** Hazel was a small child in 1919; but she is the only source
  that explicitly confronts the six/nine arithmetic, and multi-family farms are ordinary. Strawn's
  three hill places delivered to the station either way. **The hill-milk argument survives under both
  readings.**
- **The Brant farm was the largest of the six**: *"sixty-odd acres of the meadow, and thirty-eight
  cows in a good year, which for this valley was a herd"* (r09). Corroborated by r02 §5 ("the
  station's largest patron in every year of which we have a record"), r01, r03 ("brought more milk to
  my door than any other man on the Bottom"), r17 ("My grandfather had the largest piece of it"), and
  **r18 ("Emmet farmed the largest place on Hessel Bottom and hauled the most milk into the station of
  any man on the creek"). Five independent statements; the largest-patron identification is closed.**
- Hessel Bottom from inside (r09): water stands on the low end into May; a soft place by the alders
  that has taken a horse to the hock; the hay is coarse and cuts late; *"a cow will eat it and do well
  enough, whatever has been said about that hay by men who never fed it."*
  **r17 adds**: "*it was wet — you could cut hay off it in a dry August and in a wet one you could lose
  a horse. There was a spring at the upper end that ran cold enough to ache your wrist in July, and
  that is where the cans stood.*"
  **r16 (Kepp) adds the veterinary description**: springs come out at the foot of the slope and run
  year round; part under water every spring and not fit to walk on till well into May; herbage sedge,
  rush and coarse tussock grasses; sedge hay is the winter dependence "and very nearly the whole of
  it"; an acid soil poor in lime and phosphates; late cutting takes out what little was in it.
- **Opening date: 1896.** Vira Toland says Keddie went up "in ninety-five, the year before they opened
  it"; **r17 (Hazel) makes the same error.** **Against 1895** stand Keddie's own first leaf and his own
  count (29 seasons, 1896–1924, and he "did not test a pound in the season of 1925"); Strawn (1907,
  the station "opened before my time"); Vose (r08, "a station in daily use since 1896"); Rundle (r12,
  "it opened in 1896"); Frick (r13, "since it opened in 1896"); Sill (r14, "that was ninety-six, in
  May, and there was a crowd went up for it"); and **r18, twice** ("from the year it opened"; "My
  father kept it in his own hand from 1896 until he left"). **Resolve for 1896 — seven independent
  lines against two carriers of a valley slip.** *(r21 is consistent without dating it: Keddie "had
  been the tester there from before I was born," Oram b. 1900.)*
- **First leaf of the test book**, quoted identically by r03 and r07 Item 3:
  > *"Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our
  > own comes. A. Keddie."*
- **INTERNAL CONFLICT IN r03 — SETTLED.** Keddie's letter-prose says the 1896 glass was "borrowed off
  the **Ashlin creamery**"; his quoted first leaf says **"borrowed of Larrow Green."** **r14 settles
  it in the lender's own voice**: *"they had no outfit of their own. Not a piece… And I said no, they
  can have mine. I went to my own bench in this room and I made up an outfit for them out of my own
  glass, my own, that I had used and knew, and it went up the valley on the wagon and they opened on
  it. Their own came the spring after and they sent mine back to me, and one piece of it was chipped
  and I never said a word about that either."* **Resolve for LARROW GREEN, from both ends of the
  loan.** No Ashlin creamery is attested anywhere. Mechanical consequence: **the 1896 season was run
  on Sill's own known-good working glass**, which is why 1896 reads 3.80.

### 3.2 THE OUTFIT AND ITS ORIGIN — see §0.8 for the invoice, custody and the $77.39 trap.

**NEW AND IMPORTANT — HOW A PIPETTE WAS MADE, AND HOW THE LOT WENT WRONG (r20).** This is the
mechanism behind the whole affair and no other source has it.
> "A milk test pipette is a simple thing and it is not an easy thing. A man gathers his glass, blows
> the bulb, draws the stem down, and works to a gauge that hangs on a nail at his elbow. He is making
> a vessel that must **deliver** a stated quantity of milk — **not hold it, *deliver* it, which is a
> different measurement and a harder one, because milk is not water and clings to the wall of the
> glass as it runs out.** The mark goes on the stem while the glass is still warm. Then the piece goes
> into the lehr to cool slowly, and comes out the next morning, and is gauged, and is either packed or
> thrown into the cullet barrel under the bench."
Packing was **by the dozen, in straw, in a wooden case with the customer's name in blue pencil on the
end**. Two books were kept: the **order book** (everything that went out) and the **works book** (what
glass, what gauge, what man, what day). **The lots were numbered and the numbers were entered.**
Mr. Marcus Ammon's motto, lettered and hung in the packing room: **"a creamery buys arithmetic from us
and not glassware."**

**JUNIA AMMON'S OWN EXPLANATION — flag it as her belief, not a document:**
> "I have thought about that lot for a long while, and here is what I believe happened, and I set it
> down because nobody else is going to. **The mold had been reground and the lot ran short. It was an
> accident and nobody in the works caught it.** A gauge sits on a nail and gets knocked off the nail
> and hung back on it; a mold comes back from the shop a hair different and looks exactly as it looked
> before; **a boy checks the first of a lot and the tenth and the fiftieth and finds them consistent,
> because they are consistent — they are consistently wrong, which is the one fault a check of that
> kind cannot find.** That is how it goes in a glass house, and any man who has stood on a blowing
> floor will tell you the same."
**This is the best available account of the origin of the error, and it is structurally the same
argument as the whole Ostrey Hollow case: a systematic error is invisible to any check that only tests
for consistency.** Note it is offered as belief ("here is what I believe happened"), fifty-two years
after the fact, by someone with an interest in the accident reading; note also that **it exonerates
the firm of intent, which is exactly what r19 accused it of.** Weigh: r19's accusation rests on
nothing but the 1923 letter; r20's account rests on trade knowledge plus the same letter; **neither
can be tested, because the works records burned in 1931.** **The honest answer is that the sources do
not establish whether the short lot was accident or fraud, and that the balance of evidence favours
accident** (a house that knowingly shipped short glass would not have priced and itemized the invoice
so plainly, and the same house supplied "half the creameries in the state" without any other such
story attaching to it).

**AMMON & SONS, THE FIRM (r20).** Founded by **Marcus Ammon** "on a rented floor with two men and a
secondhand annealing oven"; by Junia's childhood it "filled four stories and a basement on the street
behind the railroad yard at Tarnet, with the sidings close enough that a case of glass went from our
packing room to a freight car without touching a street." **"At our height we supplied half the
creameries in the state"** — the glass, not the churns and vats, which came from works at the coast.
Business logic: "A creamery orders a churn once in twenty years. **A creamery orders glass forever,
because glass breaks**, and that is why a house like ours could live sixty years on a trade nobody
outside it has ever heard of." Territory in the first years "ran along the two railroad lines and
hardly a mile off them… **The creamery trade of the nineties was won a depot at a time.**"
**Still in business in 1923 and in 1949.** *(See §0.9(1) for the contradiction about Ordell County.)*

### 3.3 Keddie's method and book (from the unsent letter)
- Day began half past five; wagons up in the dark; lamps lit, scale swinging free before the first
  wagon. Every can weighed, the weight called and written before the milk went anywhere. **The scale
  was tested by the state's man on his rounds and was true; in 29 seasons no patron ever disputed a
  weight and was right.**
- Sample from each patron's milk as weighed, into **his own numbered jar**, **made up over ten days**.
  On testing morning: warmed blood-warm through, poured vessel to vessel until the risen cream was
  carried down through the whole.
- Charge drawn up in the measure to the mark on the stem, tip wiped, blown out into the numbered
  bottle; acid down the side; bottle turned till the milk darkened; **four bottles in the hand tester,
  weighted against each other, cover down, crank at the taught speed**; fat read off in per cent
  against the rulings **with a pair of dividers.**
- Book kept in **five columns: date, patron, pounds, test, fat.** Errors crossed and initialed;
  **never a figure written over a figure.** (*Minor conflict*: r07 Item 3 describes four columns.)
- Statement to the board, quoted identically by r03 and **by r13 (Frick, who heard it)**: *"I have
  tested as I was taught and as the state man saw me do it, **thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine
  seasons**, and I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are… I have never taken a pound of
  cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by
  himself."* — **two independent transcriptions of one spoken statement.**
- **r18 on the man:** "**He was a careful man in small things to a degree that other people found
  trying, and it was the making of the record he left and, I have sometimes thought, the unmaking of
  him.**" And: "**He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove, and I have thought
  since that this was the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had.**"
- **r21 gives us Keddie in the body, the only physical portrait we have:** "**A long dry man, very
  quiet, and he wore a canvas apron with the strap over one shoulder, and he had a way of holding a
  bottle up to the window with his arm straight out that I can see yet.** I was not frightened of him
  and most of the boys were."
- **Keddie's claim about "the state man" is corroborated from the state man's own books.** Strawn's
  standing routine at every plant, in the same order every time: (1) **the scales**, against sealed
  weights he carried, both intake and cream scale, loaded light and loaded heavy; (2) **the
  lactometer** on the day's milk, patron by patron where there was any question, for added water;
  (3) **the boiler, water supply and cold room**; (4) — "the one most men grudged me" — **he stood at
  the tester's elbow and watched him make a test through from the sample to the reading, and did not
  let him hurry it.**
  - **1907**: "Their tester keeps his book in his own hand, and his hand is better than most. **Watched
    him through two tests. He works slow and clean and reads his own figure without being coaxed to
    it.**"
  - **1911, June**: "Scales true. **Watched a test through. Nothing here to note.**"
  - **Scales true** at Ostrey Hollow in 1907, 1909, 1911, 1912, 1915, 1916, 1919, 1920 (twice), 1922,
    1923 (June), 1923 (Oct), 1924, 1925, 1928, 1931, 1934, 1936 — **every recorded visit, without
    exception, across 29 years.**
  - **Lactometer, no added water** at Ostrey Hollow in 1912, 1916, 1919, 1920 (twice), 1922, 1923
    (June, "as always here"), 1924, 1925, 1928, 1934.
  - **Keddie's procedure was watched through, sample to reading, and never once faulted.** His
    technique was sound; only his glass was wrong. **A proportional error in the *instrument* survives
    a perfect *method*, and Strawn's routine before 1921 had no way to catch it.**
- **THE SCALES ARE CONFIRMED FROM THE PATRON SIDE (r09, r17)** — a genuinely independent instrument.
  Roy Brant on his father's book: two columns per delivery day — the weight of every can off **Emmet's
  own scale in his own barn** before the wagon went, and **the figure the station gave him back.**
  *"The two columns agreed… Day after day, month after month, for the whole run of years… barring a
  pound or two here and there, and those were days when a can got slopped on the hill. Two scales, his
  and the station's, kept by two men who by the end were not speaking to one another, and the scales
  never fell out at all. Whatever was wrong at that station — and something was wrong at that station
  for a very long time — it was not the weighing."*
  **r17 adds the man's motive and manner**: "*He weighed his own milk on his own scale before he ever
  put a can on the wagon, and then the station weighed it again at the intake, and he wrote both
  figures down side by side. **If the two ever disagreed he would stand in the doorway of that weigh
  room with his hat on until somebody found out why. They generally agreed. That was the point of
  him.**"*
  **The 1908 committee independently tried both**: "*We tried the station scales against a known
  weight and we tried the patrons' own scales against the station's*" (r15). **Three independent
  confirmations of the weighing, from three decades.** **r19 sums it for the public: "The scales were
  true, the acid was right, the cows were ordinary, and a piece of glass out of a crate was wrong."**

### 3.4 The 1922 summary leaf (quoted identically in r03, r04 AND r07 — one document, three hands)
> *"Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or
> within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their
> place, when it stood at 3.80."*

Nym's gloss (r07 Item 3): "The summary leaf gathers the yearly averages: 3.80 for the first season,
3.61 from 1898 on." **r13's exhibit list dates the book's own coverage: "4 May 1896 to 31 December
1924, in the hand of Ansel Keddie."**

### 3.5 The nine weeks of 1911 — the accidental controlled experiment
- Two entries as Keddie made them:
  > *"May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure."*
  > *"July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."*
  The question mark is Keddie's; he let it stand fourteen years.
- Ivy: **8 May to 10 July 1911**, nine weeks; the borrowed measure **came up from Larrow Green with
  the cream wagon**; same patrons, herds, pasture, season, acid, bottles, same man at the crank —
  **one object changed** — the average went to 3.80 and returned to 3.61 the day the station's own
  measure came back.
- **THE BIGGEST SINGLE ADDITION r18 MADE.** Duncan Keddie:
  > "**He came home that summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it
  > in the book.** I was seventeen and I remember the kitchen and I remember that he said it twice, at
  > supper and again by the door, which was not his way. **What he wrote in the book was a question
  > mark.** He was a man who would not put down what he could not prove… He had, after all, nothing to
  > hold in his hand but a suspicion about a piece of glass, and against it stood the outfit he had been
  > taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county. **So he kept on. He kept on for
  > another fourteen years, and wrote down every figure the glass gave him, and the figures were what
  > convicted him.**"
  **Ansel Keddie identified the true cause in the summer of 1911 — forty-seven years before it was
  proved — said it aloud twice in his own kitchen, and would not write it down because he could not
  prove it.** The "?" on the 10 July leaf is not bafflement; it is a suppressed hypothesis. Arithmetic
  checks: Duncan b. 1894 → 17 in summer 1911 ✓; 1911 + 14 = 1925, the year of the dismissal ✓.
  **Single source, family memory, 57 years after the event — but exactly congruent with the documented
  question mark, with Keddie's documented refusal to write what he could not prove, and with his 1925
  sworn "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are." Report it as what it is: the son's
  testimony, uncorroborated, and decisive if true.**
- **Strawn was physically in the station during the window** ("1911. Ostrey Hollow, **June**… Scales
  true. Watched a test through. Nothing here to note"), so the test he watched was made **on the sound
  borrowed measure** — which is why there was nothing to note, and which proves Keddie's hand-work was
  identical in the high-reading window and the low-reading years.
- **From the patron side (r09):** *"There were nine weeks in the summer of 1911 when the station's own
  measure was out of use, it having been broken… Through those nine weeks our tests came up, all of
  us, every farm on the Bottom; and then the station's own came back into use and the figures went
  down again to where they had been. My father noticed the rise and he noticed the fall and he entered
  both, and he could make nothing of it, and I could make nothing of it either, and there it sat for
  near on fifty years."* — **the rise was across every farm on the Bottom, not an averaging artifact,
  and it is recorded in a document outside the station.**
- **VAUGHT WEIGHS THIS ABOVE ALL THE ARITHMETIC (r11 §IV):**
  > *"For nine weeks in the summer of 1911 the station's own pipette was broken and a borrowed measure
  > was used in its place. The patrons were the same patrons; the herds were the same herds; the
  > pasture, the season, the acid, the bottles, the tester and the tester's habits were all unaltered.
  > **One variable changed.** The station's average test rose from 3.61 to 3.80 and held there for the
  > nine weeks, and returned to 3.61 on the day the station's own measure came back into service. This
  > is recorded contemporaneously, in the operator's hand, by a man who did not know what he was
  > recording and set a question mark against it."*
  And: *"I have spent my working life urging students to prefer an experiment to a calculation. Here is
  one that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and lay unread for
  forty-seven years. It disposes of every explanation that looks to the herds, to the ground or to the
  men, for the sufficient reason that none of those was altered in those nine weeks, and the figures
  were."* (1911 + 47 = 1958 ✓.)
  **NOTE: Vaught here uses 3.61 → 3.80, i.e. the 0.19 step and the 0.95 ratio — contradicting his own
  §V (3.905). This is the fault line inside r11.**
  **And note the irony r18 creates: Vaught says the operator "did not know what he was recording." On
  his son's testimony, he knew exactly, and did not dare write it.**
- **CONFLICT on where the borrowed measure came from.** Ivy (from Keddie's book): **up from Larrow
  Green with the cream wagon.** Roy Brant: *"**The state man left his own measure that June** and that
  is what they used."* **Resolve for Larrow Green** — the borrowing began **8 May** on Keddie's dated
  entry and Strawn's visit was in **June**; a measure left in June cannot be the one in use since 8
  May. (A third possibility no source states: two successive borrowed measures.) Either way the
  experiment stands and either candidate lender's glass was sound. Larrow Green is also exactly where
  the 1896 loan came from, per Sill — the same channel, and **r21 confirms the channel physically: the
  cream wagon ran down from the Hollow to Larrow Green and back, every working day, for over twenty
  years.**
- **CONFLICT on the duration — three carriers of "six," but ONE line.**
  Teague (r05): the measure broke "in the spring of 1911" and they made shift with a borrowed one for
  **six weeks**. Kepp (r16, 1926), attacking the experiment: "*It has been urged upon me, by a
  gentleman who I know will forgive my noticing it here, that in the summer of 1911 the testing at
  that station was broken off for **six weeks**… **Six weeks in the flush of the season, gentlemen.
  Six weeks of June and July**, when every herd in the county is at its best and the stock are off the
  wet ground and up on the aftermath. I am astonished that anybody should found an argument on six
  weeks of summer figures. I could produce you six weeks in any year that would prove anything you
  liked.*"
  **Resolve for NINE WEEKS, 8 May – 10 July.** Keddie's two dated book entries; Roy Brant's independent
  "nine weeks"; Vaught's "nine weeks." Three independent sources.
  **And note what Kepp's rebuttal costs him.** (i) The window is nine weeks, not six. (ii) It is not
  "June and July" — it opens **8 May**, before the flush, and the tests were up from the first day.
  (iii) His own explanation requires the rise to be seasonal; but the tests **fell back to 3.61 on 10
  July**, in the middle of the flush, the day the glass changed back. Kepp never asks why the figures
  came *down* in July. **The best-argued defence of the hay theory ever made in the valley refutes
  itself on the return leg of the experiment.** It is also evidence that **the 1911 experiment was
  known and being argued in Ordell County in 1926**, thirty-two years before Ivy, and was talked down.

### 3.6 The gap was flat, not seasonal
- Keddie: "They did not come and go with the season. The gap was there in July as it was there in
  February, in the flush of the grass and in the dead of the winter on dry hay, and it was the same in
  a wet year as in a dry one."
- **Roy Brant, independently:** *"Our milk at the Hollow ran seventeen hundredths of a point under what
  the same class of milk was making down at Larrow Green… it was seventeen hundredths in July and it
  was seventeen hundredths in February. It did not come and go with the season. It did not care whether
  the cows were on the meadow or on hay in the barn. Nobody would take account of that, then or since,
  though it was in front of them."* And the line that sums the story: *"Seventeen hundredths is nothing
  to look at on one pay slip. **Set the slips in a row for a lifetime of farming and it is a farm.**"*
- **Hazel Brant, a generation later and from memory of the same book (r17):** *"**Nineteen hundredths.
  Every month, every season, wet years and dry: nineteen hundredths lower at our station than the same
  milk was anywhere else on earth. Not a little in the winter and none in the summer, the way a feed
  thing goes. Nineteen hundredths, flat as a floor.** I have heard men who could not add a column of
  five figures explain to my father why that was the hay."*
  **Note: Hazel says 0.19, her father said 0.17.** Different measurements; she has taken the
  true-shortfall figure, not her father's Larrow-Green differential. Speaking in 1981, twenty-three
  years after Bulletin 471, she has almost certainly absorbed 0.19 from the published finding. **Treat
  Hazel as a carrier of 0.19, not an independent source for it.** Her *flatness* testimony is
  independent and valuable.
- **THE THREE GAP FIGURES, DISENTANGLED.**
  - **0.19** = *Ostrey Hollow recorded vs Ostrey Hollow true* (3.61 vs 3.80). **This is the shortfall.
    Use it for all arithmetic.**
  - **0.17** = *Ostrey Hollow recorded vs Larrow Green recorded* (3.61 vs 3.78). A different
    comparison; it coincides with 0.19 only if Larrow Green's own herds averaged exactly 3.80, which
    nobody claims — and Sill says they averaged 3.78.
    **r14 supplies the missing term and the arithmetic closes.** Sill, from the churn: *"Our own milk,
    here at Larrow Green, our own patrons, the men on this creek — that ran **3.78** taking one year
    with another right through those years. Three seventy-eight. I know it because I made the butter
    out of it and a buttermaker knows what his cream is worth before anybody hands him a paper about
    it. Ordinary milk, ordinary county, ordinary cows."* **3.78 − 0.17 = 3.61 exactly.** Sill (Larrow
    Green, 1934) and Roy Brant (Hessel Bottom, 1955) never met, worked opposite ends of the valley, and
    their two figures reproduce the recorded Ostrey Hollow average to the hundredth. r07 Item 16
    supports the same from the Larrow Green receiving and churn books.
  - **0.20 ("two tenths")** = the corrupted Bulletin 214 misquote, carried by r15 and r16 — **one
    error, two carriers**, refuted by three verbatim transcriptions of the Bulletin's actual sentence.
    *(This third item is reconstructed from §1 caution (f); notes-16 was cut mid-sentence here.)*
- **r19's lay statement of the whole mechanism, worth quoting in Section A:** "*A short measure of milk
  goes into a bottle whose neck is marked for a full one, and the fat reads low in the same proportion,
  and it reads low every single time, for as long as you go on using that glass. Nobody watered
  anything. Nobody stole anything.*"
- **r19's statement of the social failure, likewise:** "*Everybody had a theory. **The theories were
  about the men, because the theories in this county always are.** The Ostrey Hollow farmers thought
  they were being cheated and said so at every annual meeting for a quarter of a century. The officers
  of the Association thought the Ostrey Hollow farmers were up to something, and said so rather more
  quietly. **Nobody, in all those years of it, appears to have thought the trouble might be
  furniture.**"

## 4. STORY THREADS C–H — WHAT SURVIVES [much of this is recon; flag thin spots]

### 4.1 The complaints and the 1908 committee [recon + r15]
- Complaints date from **~1902** (r14, r15). r19: the Hollow farmers "said so at every annual meeting
  for a quarter of a century."
- **The investigating committee of 1908**, **Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green chairman**, signed "NYLE
  GRIGG, chairman, and the committee." Grigg was **not a patron of that station** and says he was
  chosen for that reason and "has been abused for the finding by men on both sides of it."
- The committee **"visited each of the six farms, some of them three and four times,"** and **"tried
  the station scales against a known weight and… the patrons' own scales against the station's."**
  It found the weighing sound. **It did not test the glass.**
- The report was **reprinted in 1919 with Grigg's covering letter** addressed "To the officers and
  patrons" — i.e. the 1908 finding was pushed back into circulation eleven years later, at the moment
  the quarrel reopened.
- **Grigg's letter contradicts his own report on the sampling months. Trust the report.**
- **r19 wrongly says this committee "sat again in 1925."** It did not; the 1925 body was Frick's board
  of arbitration.

### 4.2 The 1919 water circular
- **The manager (Selby Vose) circulated a warning to the Ostrey Hollow patrons about added water**
  (r19, explicit: "The manager of the Association had circulated a warning to the Ostrey Hollow
  patrons about added water as early as 1919").
- It went to **nine families** (r01, r03, r09, r14, r17). See §3.1 for the six/nine question.
- **Answered by none of them** — "which in that valley is a shout" (r17). Hazel's mother "read hers
  twice and put it in the stove."
- **Strawn's lactometer readings show no added water at Ostrey Hollow in 1919 or in any year he
  tested** (1912, 1916, 1919, 1920 twice, 1922, 1923 "as always here", 1924, 1925, 1928, 1934). **The
  accusation was tested by the state and failed, and the Association appears never to have looked at
  the inspector's book.**
- 1919 is also the year the 1908 report was reprinted (r15) — the quarrel reopened on two fronts at
  once.

### 4.3 The wet-hay theory (Kepp, r16, 1926)
- The fullest and best-argued defence of the station: acid soil poor in lime and phosphates, sedge
  hay, late cutting, ground under water into May. Kepp had thirty years' practice, no interest in the
  Association, and **no competence in testing** ("I have never in my life read a test and would not
  know how").
- **Refuted three ways:** the gap was flat across seasons and years (§3.6); the same cows' summer milk
  at Alder Corners averaged 3.7994 (r10); and the 1911 window changed nothing but the glass. **And
  Kepp's own rebuttal of 1911 collapses on the return leg** (§3.5).
- Kepp is nonetheless **a second independent attestation of the 27¢ butterfat price** and of the six
  Hessel Bottom farms.

### 4.4 The condemnation of 1923 — **NOW DATED DECISIVELY, 12 JUNE 1923**
- Strawn's own circuit book records the condemnation on his June 1923 visit and his order to the
  manager to get new glass. **r06 gives 12 June 1923.**
- **r08 (Vose 1926) and r12 (Rundle 1939) give 12 July 1923 — one line, two carriers** (Rundle had
  Vose's printed address to draw on).
- **NEW AND DECISIVE (r20): "The Association wrote to us on the twenty-eighth of June" [1923].** An
  order dated **28 June 1923** cannot follow a condemnation of **12 July 1923**. **The 12 July date is
  impossible. Resolve for 12 June 1923.** This is the cleanest conflict-resolution in the corpus: a
  third party's dated business letter kills a date carried by two Association voices.
- **The reply, quoted from Ammon & Sons' letter book, 2 July 1923:**
  > *"Your order of the 28th ultimo for six dozen milk pipettes and six dozen test bottles is entered
  > and will go forward this month. We regret the trouble. Our books of the nineties are not such that
  > we can tell you anything about the lot you name."*
  (Note the reorder was **six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles** — half the 1897 quantity of
  bottles. The order was shipped within the month.)
- **What Strawn did (r20, from the receiving end):** "an inspector for the state went into that
  station with a burette and put their pipettes to it, and **the glass out of our crate would not
  deliver its mark. He condemned the lot — the whole crate of them — and told the manager to order
  new.**" **r19 agrees:** "he ran their measures against a burette, found they would not deliver what
  was marked on them, and told the manager to order new ones. **He wrote down that he had condemned
  them. He wrote down nothing about what they might have been doing for the twenty-six years
  before.**"
- **The condemned glass was crated and put in the loft** and left there. **r21 is the eyewitness:**
  "It was the station's old glass, **put up there when the state man made them throw it out.** Bottles,
  and the little glass measures they drew the milk up with for the test."
- **Nobody told the patrons.** The tests rose to 3.80 in 1924 and Vose explained the rise in 1926 as
  improved farming.
- The affected span therefore runs **1 May 1897 – 12 June 1923**, twenty-six years and six weeks; the
  station's recorded average was 3.61 "effectively to June 1923," and 1924 reads 3.80.

### 4.5 The 1925 arbitration, the dismissal, the award
- **Three sessions.** **Session 1 at Ashlin, in the Cooperative's office** — r21: "because that is
  where the lawyer was and lawyers do not travel." **Sessions 2 and 3 at Ostrey Hollow itself, in the
  station's weigh room (= the intake room, with the scale in it).**
  Confirmed three ways: **r13 (Frick, chairman): "That was my doing and I am still content with it…
  We saw the scales tried. We saw the sampling done and the test made, and I will say plainly that I
  did not understand the test then and do not understand it now; I am a lawyer and the thing is
  chemistry. But I could see that it was done the same way twice, and that the men who did it were not
  hiding their hands."** **r18: "Twenty feet of ladder, thirty-five years, and two of the three
  sessions of that arbitration sat in the room underneath it."** **r21 (outside on the platform in the
  rain with about a dozen others through one whole afternoon): "there were three sittings of it
  altogether and two of them were held up at the station itself, in the weigh room… because the books
  and the scales and the machinery were all up there and it was easier to carry the men to the
  machinery than the machinery to the men… You could hear the voices but not the words. **And the
  crate was up the ladder over their heads the entire time. Twenty feet, if that.** I have thought
  about that since, I will not pretend I thought about it then."**
  **THE CENTRAL IRONY OF THE WHOLE AFFAIR: in 1925 a board of three sat in that weigh room, watched a
  test made with the NEW (sound) glass, and never asked what the OLD glass — in a crate over their
  heads — had delivered.**
- **Keddie's sworn statement to the board, Ashlin, 17 March 1925** (r03 I, and r13 quoting from
  memory of hearing it) — see §3.3.
- **Ansel Keddie dismissed 30 April 1925** (r18), by Selby Vose. He had been tester since 1896, "29
  seasons," and "did not test a pound in the season of 1925."
- **Award: $3,800, 4 May 1925, "without admission"** — quoted verbatim by r02 from the minutes;
  independently reported by r19 ("thirty-eight hundred dollars, 'without admission,' **which is
  lawyer's language for we are paying you and we are not saying why**"). **Reject r21's $3,500**
  (§0.9(4)).
- **Keddie got nothing** and was not asked back (r19). Note the sequence: **dismissed 30 April, award
  4 May** — the man was removed four days before the money was paid.
- **r13's exhibit list from his own file** dates the test book "4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924, in the
  hand of Ansel Keddie."
- **Frick, forty years at the bar and "two homicides," says none of it stayed with him as this did.**

### 4.6 Ansel Keddie after 1925, and the cream hauler's rival theory (r21)
- **"Keddie went home after twenty-five and stayed home. He kept four or five cows on a piece of
  ground that was not big enough for them, and he did carpentry — good carpentry, he hung a barn door
  for us and it swung true for thirty years — and he died in the thirties. I went to the funeral. It
  was well attended and nobody said much, which tells you something."** (r18 gives the year: **1934**;
  r19 confirms 1934.)
- **THE HAULER'S ACCUSATION — new, and it must be reported and then weighed.** Cleve Oram:
  > "Now I will tell you what was said, and you can do what you want with it. **My father hauled that
  > cream and he always said there was less of it coming off that platform than went onto it.** He said
  > it to me on the wagon seat more than once, and he said it in this village to men who are dead now.
  > **He never said it to Keddie's face and he never said it under oath, because he was not asked**,
  > but he believed it and he believed it to the end. They say now it was the glass. I have no quarrel
  > with the college. I am only telling you what the man who drove the wagon thought, and **he was the
  > one man in the valley who saw both ends of that cream every day of his life.**"
- **How to handle it.** (i) It is hearsay-once-removed of an unsworn belief, never tested, and Oram
  himself declines to endorse it. (ii) It is **inconsistent with the station-gain entry** (r12's and
  r14's verbatim transcriptions, and Sill's testimony from the receiving floor): the creamery
  consistently received **more** fat than the station's books credited — which is precisely what a
  short pipette produces and precisely the opposite of cream disappearing between platform and churn.
  (iii) It is inconsistent with Strawn's scales-true findings at both intake and cream scale on every
  visit for twenty-nine years. (iv) But it is **exactly the kind of thing that made the valley suspect
  a man rather than an instrument**, and it is worth quoting for that: the one witness positioned to
  see both ends of the cream spent twenty years telling people the milk was going somewhere. **Report
  it as evidence about the valley's beliefs, not as evidence about the cream.**

### 4.7 1958: the crate, the measurement, Bulletin 471
- **Winter/February 1958**: Ivy Keddie, then twenty-seven, of the State Agricultural College at
  Farland, **went up the ladder into the loft over the old station's intake room and brought the crate
  down herself** (r19: "Last February"; r21: "That was the winter of fifty-eight, and she went up that
  ladder and brought the crate down herself"). The crate had sat under dust and a tarpaulin **since
  1923 — thirty-five years** (r18 and r21 both give thirty-five).
- **The ladder**: "a ladder nailed to the wall by the door, not a stair, a ladder, and the trap at the
  top of it was a piece of plank with a rope on it" (r21). Also in the loft: "some sacks, a broken
  churn, wire, the kind of thing a building collects."
- **Why only nine pipettes survived (r21, and this is the answer to a question no other source
  addresses):** "**a thing in a loft for thirty-five years gets broken by everybody who goes up after
  wire.** Nobody ever gave it a thought. It was a crate under a tarp. I would not have been able to
  tell you what was in it if I had not been up there after a hay fork with my own hands."
- **March 1958 (r04: 9 March)**: Ivy measured the survivors against a calibrated burette in a college
  laboratory, five runs each. **Every one was blown on the stem 17.6 c.c.; not one delivered it; mean
  16.72 c.c.** (see §0.1 for the r19 variant and why it is rejected).
- **Bulletin No. 471, October 1958, State Agricultural College at Farland, by Professor Leland Vaught
  and Miss Ivy Keddie.** Acknowledgments: Vaught; **the state dairy office, for the loan of an old
  inspector's notebooks** (Strawn's); and **Mrs. Rosalie Cudd of Vessey County**, "of the cheese
  company over the ridge… who went into her family's records on a stranger's say-so."
- **Published in the county on 3 October 1958** (r19), thirty-three years after the dismissal and
  twenty-four after Ansel's death.
- **Ivy's own words, to the Chronicle by telephone from Farland, Thursday evening [2 October 1958]:**
  > "**The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not.** He tested the milk in front of the state's own
  > man and he tested it the way he was taught, and the reason his figures would not come right was in
  > a crate over his head the entire time. **I would like it printed that he never knew. It would have
  > been a kindness if somebody in this county had thought to climb a ladder.**"
  *(Note the tension with r18: on Duncan's testimony Ansel **did** know, in 1911, and would not write
  it. Either Ivy did not know what her father knew, or she is being merciful in print. Worth flagging;
  the sources do not settle which.)*
- **1959**: Ivy gave the talk "What the Crate Held" to the Ordell County Historical Society at Ashlin,
  with the crate on the table (r04).
- **Cost accounting for Section A:** the outfit that did the damage cost **$77.93**; the damage was
  **76,000 lb of butterfat, ≈ $20,520** (published as $19,600); the patrons received **$3,800**.
  r19's line: the invoice "is a tidy document in a clerk's copperplate, ruled and totaled, and it is
  **one of the more expensive pieces of paper this county has ever had in it.**"

### 4.8 The end of the station (r21, sole source)
- **"The station closed in forty-six."** The milk went down the valley in a truck by then and nobody
  was skimming anything at Ostrey Hollow.
- The building was afterward used for hay tools — "mowers, a side rake, that sort of thing, out of the
  weather."
- Last time Oram passed it (before 1977): "the platform was gone and the roof was going. **The ladder
  was still on the wall. I looked.**"
