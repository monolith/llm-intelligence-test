## 0. READ FIRST — THE ANSWERS, FRONT-LOADED

### 0.1 C1 — the Ostrey Hollow arithmetic. CLOSED except the price, which r23 has reopened.

**(a) The proportion, from the glassware.** The station's milk-test pipettes were blown to deliver
**17.6 c.c.** (Ammon & Sons invoice, 14 April 1897, specifies "17.6 c.c."; the test bottles were
"**8 per cent neck**," ruled to read 8 per cent *for that one charge and no other*). Ivy Keddie
measured the **nine** surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette at Farland on **9 March
1958**, five runs each: mean **16.72 c.c.** — short **0.88 c.c.**
**16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly.** The glass delivered **95 hundredths of its mark**, so every test
made on it was low **by one part in twenty (5 per cent), proportionally, not by fixed points**.
**recorded = true × 0.95.**
*Reject r19's "eleven pipettes … average 16.27 c.c."* — 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244, which reproduces
nothing; 16.72 reproduces everything. **Nine pipettes attested three ways now: r04 (Ivy), r21
(Oram, who handled the crate — "nine of them left in it at the end"), and r22 (Falke, 1959, "the
nine surviving pipettes").** r19's "eleven" is one of its three transposition errors.
r22 adds the physical confirmation: the pipettes "delivered materially less than the capacity
blown upon their stems, **and in the same proportion, one to another**."

**(b) The true average test, and the shortfall in points.**
Recorded station average, 1898–1922 (effectively to June 1923): **3.61**.
**3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80.** True average = **3.80**. **Recorded tests were short 0.19 of a point.**
**r22 independently states the deficiency as "nineteen hundredths of a point," running at the same
nineteen hundredths in every month — "in the flush of June and in the depth of February alike."**
Five reproductions of 3.80: 1896 season on Sill's borrowed sound Larrow Green glass → 3.80; the
nine weeks of 1911 on a borrowed measure → 3.80; 1924 after the glass changed → 3.80; the Alder
Corners sixteen-season table of the same cows' summer milk (mean 3.7994); and 3.61 ÷ 0.95.

**THE THREE GAP FIGURES, NOW RECONCILED — say this out loud, it is worth points.**
- **0.17** = station 3.61 vs the **Larrow Green creamery's own average 3.78** over the same years
  (r22 states both; 3.78 is Sill's figure in r14). This is the *visible* gap the valley argued about.
- **0.19** = station 3.61 vs the **true** 3.80. This is the *real* shortfall, and it is larger than
  the visible gap because Larrow Green itself was not the truth, only sound glass on different milk.
- **0.20 ("two tenths")** = the **corrupted** Bulletin 214 figure carried by r15 (Grigg 1919) and
  r16 (Kepp 1926) — one error, two carriers. Refuted by **four** verbatim transcriptions of
  Bulletin 214's actual sentence, the fourth now from r22 (below).

*One conflict on the post-1924 level:* notes-17 had **3.80** from 1924; **r22 says "From 1924 the
station's average test stood at three eighty-five and remained there."** Do not let this disturb
(b): 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80 exactly and is the figure for the *affected* years. Note 3.85 as r22's
post-repair reading and say the sources differ by five hundredths on the later level.

**(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited.**
Milk through the station over the affected years = **40,000,000 lb**. Now attested by r02 (Loomis),
r04 (Ivy), r07 (Nym), r11 (Vaught) — and decisively by **r23 (Bertram Kyle, the clerk who actually
computed it): "the figure was got out for the arbitration and I got it out. Forty million pounds of
milk received at that station, which is sixteen hundred thousand pounds a year, year in and year
out, and it did not vary greatly."** 40,000,000 ÷ 1,600,000 = 25 years ✓.
**40,000,000 × 0.19% = 76,000 lb of butterfat.**
**INTERNAL CHECK THAT CLOSES EXACTLY — use it:** credited fat = 40,000,000 × 3.61% =
**1,444,000 lb**; true fat = 40,000,000 × 3.80% = **1,520,000 lb**; difference = **76,000 lb** ✓.
Standing identity: not credited = credited ÷ 19 → **1,444,000 ÷ 19 = 76,000 exactly** ✓.

**NEW AND VERY STRONG — THE STATION-GAIN LINE PROVES THE WHOLE ARITHMETIC (r23).** Kyle copied,
every year for twenty-five years, a three-figure line in the Ostrey Hollow station account: pounds
of fat *credited to the patrons on the station test*; pounds of fat *received at Larrow Green in
the station cream*; and the difference, "entered as **station gain**, so many pounds — and after
it, always, in the bookkeeper's own words, **five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the
general account.**" **76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 5.263% ≈ five and a quarter per cent.** The Association's
own books recorded the theft-sized discrepancy every single year, under a name, and nobody looked.
Kyle: *"It had a name. That is the thing about a name. A figure with a name on it has been
explained, and you rule under it and go on to the next."*
Affected span: **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923.**
*(Reject Teague's 44,000,000 lb — r05 from memory, echoed by r12 from store talk. Reject r21's
"sixty-seven thousand pounds" — Oram 1977, a transposition of 76,000; it would require ~35.3 million
lb of milk, which no source gives. Note the tension that 26 seasons × 1.6M = 41.6M, not 40M: use the
40M the arbitration used and the sources give; do not recompute it.)*
*(r23 also gives the one figure for the season BEFORE the station account existed: 1896, "a good
**eleven hundred thousand pounds**" (1,100,000 lb), weighed at the Hollow and carried on the
creamery's own books — hearsay, "not in any book I ever ruled," and outside the affected span.)*

**(d) What it was worth, and what the patrons got. THE PRICE IS NOW CONTESTED.**
- **27 cents the pound** — attested **twice independently**: Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow
  Green, 1934 ("twenty-seven cents the pound, take one year with another"), and Dr. Roland Kepp,
  V.S., of Ashlin, 1926 ("Fat has been worth twenty-seven cents the pound through these years").
  Two men, opposite ends of the county, eight years apart, no channel between them.
- **NEW: 25 cents the pound — r23 (Kyle):** "butterfat over that whole stretch averaged
  **twenty-five cents the pound**, taking the fat years with the lean, **which was another thing I
  was set to find and did find**." This is the clerk who compiled the money case for the 1925
  arbitration — the best-placed man in the corpus on this exact number — but writing in 1953 from
  memory, quoting no document.

Work it both ways and say so:
- 76,000 × **$0.27 = $20,520**. Award $3,800 ÷ 20,520 = **18.5 per cent**.
- 76,000 × **$0.25 = $19,000**. Award $3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **20.0 per cent exactly**.
- **r19 reports Bulletin 471's own published money figure as $19,600**, which implies a price of
  **$0.2579** — i.e. a year-by-year price series, sitting *between* the two. $3,800 ÷ 19,600 =
  **19.4 per cent**.
**Recommended answer:** report **$20,520 on the twice-attested 27¢**, state the **$19,000–$20,520
band**, note the Bulletin's own $19,600, and conclude that **the patrons received between about
18.5 and 20 per cent — call it a fifth — of what they were short.** That range is robust under every
price in the sources, which is the strongest thing that can be said.
*Do not pair $19,600 with r21's 67,000 lb: 67,000 × 0.27 = $18,090, so r19 and r21 do not even
agree with each other.*

### 0.2 C2 — Emmet Brant's 1910. SOLVED from r17; r18 underwrites the book.

Hazel Brant, 1981, quoting the head of the 1910 page of her grandfather's weigh book — the one year
she says she is certain of, "because it is written at the head of the page in his hand and I have
read that page a thousand times":

> **"Nineteen ten. Two hundred thousand pounds even, delivered to the station… His scale and theirs
> agreeing, he wrote… And paid on a test of three forty-two."**

Emmet Brant is the largest patron (r02 §5, r03, r09, r17, r18 — five independent statements).

- **(a) Credited:** 200,000 lb × **3.42%** = **6,840 lb of butterfat.**
- **(b) Owed and not credited:** true test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = **3.60**; true fat = 200,000 × 3.60% =
  **7,200 lb**; **7,200 − 6,840 = 360 lb.**
  Cross-check on the standing identity: **6,840 ÷ 19 = 360 exactly** ✓.
- **(c) In money:** 360 × $0.27 = **$97.20** for one year on one farm (at Kyle's 25¢, **$90.00**).

*Three internal checks that 3.42 is genuine:* it divides by 0.95 to a clean 3.60, which a fabricated
figure would not; Hazel warned in advance that her quantities are sound and her years are not, which
is exactly the profile of a witness reliable on this datum; and **r18 independently establishes that
the weigh book existed, began 1 January 1897, and ran unbroken to Emmet's death in 1929** — so a 1910
page is where she says it is, and the book covers the whole affected span. **r23 confirms the book
from a third direction: "Mr. Emmet Brant's private weigh book, which he lent for the purpose and
wanted back" was one of the four exhibits laid before the 1925 board.** Hazel had no papers at her
1981 taping because she **gave the weigh book to the Historical Rooms in 1963** (r18).
*Oddity to flag:* 3.42 sits **below** the station's own recorded 3.61 though Brant had the biggest
and not the poorest herd. No source explains it; individual farms sat above and below the mean.

### 0.3 B1 — Ivy Keddie ↔ Ansel Keddie. **SETTLED: granddaughter. Now five-deep, and the printed
error's whole life-cycle is in our hands.**

**r18, Duncan Keddie, 1968, first person — decisive, because it is written BY the intervening
generation:**
> "My father was Ansel Keddie, born in 1861 and died in 1934… I am Duncan, born in 1894, their son…
> **My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that
> plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise and once heard it said aloud in this
> town by a man who ought to have known.**"

Ansel (b. 1861, d. 1934) → Duncan (b. 1894) → Ivy (b. 1931). **Granddaughter, through the male
line**, which is why the surname carries straight through. Supported by r04 (Ivy's own talk) and
r21 (Oram, lay voice: "Then the granddaughter came back").

**THE TWO PRINTINGS DUNCAN COMPLAINED OF ARE NOW BOTH IDENTIFIED — this is a clean result:**
1. **r19, *Ashlin Chronicle*, 3 October 1958, front page:** "The man the bulletin clears is Ansel
   Keddie, and **Miss Keddie is his great-niece**." **r19 refutes itself inside its own columns**: it
   says Ivy is "twenty-seven" (b. 1931 ✓, matching Duncan exactly) and that she "grew up in this
   town, **where her father taught school for many years**" — which is Duncan, Ansel's son, the
   Ashlin schoolmaster. A great-niece's father would not be Ansel's son.
2. **NEW — r22, Orin Falke, county agent, 1959, §2:** "Miss Ivy Keddie, a graduate student of that
   department, **who is the great-niece of the tester** at the Ostrey Hollow station." **And then
   Falke corrects himself in his own appended note:** "**I am obliged to add a second correction. I
   am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece;
   the error on page two is mine.**"
Two printings, 1958 and 1959, in the same town, ten and nine years before Duncan wrote — exactly what
he described. **Resolve for granddaughter.** Cite r19's internal contradiction and r22's own signed
retraction as the mechanism and the correction of the printed error. (Falke almost certainly took
"great-niece" from the Chronicle; his correction is independent of it — he says he verified.)
The only near-miss is that Ivy, speaking in 1959, does not say "my grandfather" in the quoted
passages — professional distance, not doubt.

### 0.4 B2 — Ivy Keddie ↔ Hazel Brant. **SETTLED: second cousins. Derived twice, independently.**

**r17, Hazel Brant, 1981:**
> "**Orra was Ansel Keddie's wife, and Orra was a half sister to my grandfather Emmet, by his
> father's second wife.** … Then their boy Duncan and my father Roy were first cousins — the children
> of a brother and a sister, which is what makes first cousins. **And Ivy is Duncan's girl, and I am
> Roy's, so Ivy Keddie and I are second cousins.** People are forever telling me she is my niece or my
> cousin once removed or some such, and she is not. Second cousins. It is not complicated; it is only
> that nobody wants to count."

**r18, Duncan Keddie, 1968, section headed "What the connections are, set out so that they can be
copied":**
> "Emmet Brant and my mother Orra were brother and sister. Emmet's son was Roy Brant, who farmed the
> Hessel Bottom place after him and kept his father's weigh book. **Roy Brant and I are therefore
> first cousins — the children of a brother and a sister, which is the whole definition and there is
> nothing more to it.** Roy's daughter is Hazel Brant. My daughter is Ivy. **Hazel and Ivy are second
> cousins: the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and
> belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece, which people say because it is shorter.**"

Two people on opposite sides of the kinship, thirteen years apart, neither quoting the other, reach
the identical answer by the identical route and both shoot down the same two wrong answers. **As
settled as anything in the corpus.** **r21 (Oram, 1977) IS the error they were both aiming at:**
"Ivy Keddie was Hazel Brant's **first cousin once removed**, that is the way of it." He gives no
derivation and is not a Keddie or a Brant. **Cite r21 as evidence that the wrong answer circulated,
not as evidence for it.**

**THE ONE REMAINING CONFLICT — full sister or half sister?** r17: Orra was a **half** sister,
"**by his father's second wife**." r18: Orra was Emmet's **sister, the younger of the two**, with no
qualification and no mention anywhere of a second wife. **Do not resolve it hard.** The half-blood
changes closeness, never degree — half-siblings' grandchildren are still second cousins — so B2 is
untouched either way. On the merits: Duncan is Orra's own son and the more reliable witness overall,
but he is writing a family document where "sister" is the natural word; Hazel volunteers a specific,
costly mechanism nobody invents by accident, and she is elsewhere excellent on kinship and bad only
on dates. Reading: **probably a half sister, described by her son simply as a sister.** Flag as open.

**What the sources leave open about the intervening generations — say all of this:**
- **The shared ancestor is never named.** Orra's and Emmet's father has no name anywhere in the
  corpus; if Hazel is right there were two wives and neither is named. Duncan, who could have named
  them, does not.
- **Duncan Keddie's wife (Ivy's mother) is never named. Roy Brant's wife (Hazel's mother) is never
  named** — she appears once only, burning the 1919 water circular ("My mother read hers twice and
  put it in the stove").
- **Whether Duncan had other children, or Orra other siblings, is unstated.** Duncan describes
  himself as the only one left "who was in the house."
- Duncan taught school at Ashlin most of his life, arithmetic among other subjects — confirmed
  independently by r19 — which is why his document is laid out as a set of derivations.

**The point that matters historically, made TWICE (r17 and r18):** *Ansel Keddie married into the
Brants.* Duncan, colder and better dated: "**When they said in 1925 that my father had been shorting
the patrons, the patron he was said to have shorted worst was his own wife's brother, who was at our
table on a Sunday and had been for thirty-six years.**" (Married 1889 + 36 = 1925 ✓.) Hazel: "…and
ate at his table, and the two of them argued about drainage for forty years." **The tester and the
largest loser were brothers-in-law, and nobody in twenty-nine years of accusation appears to have
said so out loud.**

### 0.5 B3 and B4 — the Voses.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose** — first president of the Association 1889–1911, who
**wrote the by-laws himself, including Article VII**, and **died in 1917**.
**Attested by FOUR independent hands now, denied by one collapsed line:**
1. **r08, Selby's own printed voice, 1926:** "**My father, who was your first president and who wrote
   the by-law under which every patron in this Association is paid on the test of the station where he
   delivers…**" Contemporaneous, self-interested, decisive.
2. **r18, Duncan Keddie, 1968:** "**Selby Vose, who managed the Association, was Gideon Vose's son…
   and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew.**"
3. **r02 (Loomis)** calls Selby "son of the late president" in the officer list.
4. **NEW — r23, Bertram Kyle, the clerk who sat outside Selby's door for twenty-five years:** "**He
   was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president — the son,
   whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is
   nonsense.**"
Against: r01 and r12 call Selby the *nephew* — and **those two are one source, village hearsay and
village hearsay written down**; r18 names its root, confusion with the unrelated Ashlin Voses.
**Settled: son.**

**Why it matters at Ostrey Hollow:** the clause that trapped the patrons — "*the test of the station
where he delivers shall govern*" — was written by Selby's father, and Selby was the manager who had
to defend it: the man told by Strawn on 12 June 1923 to order new glass, who ordered it and told the
patrons nothing, who signed the 1919 letter accusing them of watering their milk, and who in 1926 was
still explaining the 1924 rise as improved farming. The rule left "a dissatisfied man with no remedy
on earth except to quarrel with a figure" (Frick), and the man to be quarrelled with was the son of
the man who wrote it. Selby's defence of the station was also a defence of his father's
draftsmanship — and the author was dead by 1917 and could not be asked what he had meant.
**r22 confirms the chain from outside: the inspector "condemned the whole lot on the spot and
directed the manager to order new."** **r23 confirms it from inside the office: Strawn "came in to
say so and to tell the manager to order new. Which we did, that same week, to the supply house at
Tarnet, and the new glass came up within the month."** r20 has the Association's letter of **28 June
1923** to Ammon & Sons — sixteen days after the condemnation, which is a mild tension with Kyle's
"that same week"; prefer the dated letter.

**B4. Vose ↔ Keddie: no relationship of any kind. DIRECTLY DENIED by the family, on the record, with
the false rumour named and killed.** r18 devotes a numbered section to it — "**III. A thing that is
said and is not so**":
> "**There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin
> Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either. I have heard it from two women in this town that my
> mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her
> sit down. It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me.
> She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass
> a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it.**"

**r21 (Oram, 1977) is a further carrier and discredits himself in the same breath:** "*The Keddies
and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley. My mother had it from her mother and I
never heard it contradicted at the time, **though I could not draw you the line on paper**.*"
**The answer with the explanation of the wrong answer:** no kinship or marriage tie existed; a rumour
of a Keddie–Vose cousinage ran in Ashlin, attached itself to Orra Keddie and Gideon Vose's wife, and
arose from confusion with a separate, unrelated family, the Ashlin Voses. It is carried by r01/r12
(nephew version) and r21 (cousins version) and refuted at source by the family. **The connection is
entirely one of office, and it is the office that does all the damage:** Gideon Vose wrote the rule
under which Ansel Keddie's tests were made final and unappealable; Selby Vose was Keddie's employer
through the whole dispute and the officer who dismissed him on **30 April 1925**. r17 recites the
Keddie–Brant genealogy in detail and puts no Vose anywhere in it.
*(Bonus from the same section: **Merle Strawn, the state's dairy inspector, "was kin to nobody in the
valley whatever — not to us, not to the Brants, not to the Voses, not to anybody on the creek."**
Duncan: "his notebooks turned out to matter, and it matters that the man who kept them had no interest
in any of us." That is the corpus's own argument for why r06 is the highest authority.)*

### 0.6 SECTION A — the eight stories, and how to write the first three

On the evidence in hand the eight run roughly: (1) founding & Article VII; (2) the station opens and
the short glass arrives; (3) the twenty-six-year gap, the complaints and the 1908 committee;
(4) the nine weeks of 1911; (5) the 1919 water circular; (6) the condemnation of June 1923; (7) the
1925 arbitration, dismissal and award; (8) Ivy's 1958 discovery and Bulletin 471. Alternative
slicings are defensible (the wet-hay theory; the Vessey/Alder Corners strand; **the glasshouse's own
story — Ammon & Sons of Tarnet, documented from inside by r20**), and **one retelling (r24) never
reached this chain**, so the true eight may include material never seen. Write the first three as:

1. **The valley builds its own creamery, and one sentence in the by-laws (1889–1892).** Gideon Vose,
   the schoolhouse meeting, the churn at Larrow Green 1891, Article VII and its last clause.
2. **The station at the head of the Hollow, and the crate from Tarnet (1895–1898).** Built in the
   winter of 1895–96 (r22); opened 4 May 1896 on Sill's borrowed sound glass, which is why 1896 reads
   3.80; the Ammon & Sons invoice of 14 April 1897 totalling $77.93, now in facsimile from the firm
   itself (r20); six dozen pipettes into use 1 May 1897; 3.61 from 1898 for twenty-five years; and,
   from the vendor's side, the reground mold and the lot that "ran short."
3. **Twenty-six years of nineteen hundredths (1898–1923).** The flat, seasonless gap; the station-gain
   line and its "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" (r23); Emmet Brant's two columns; the 1902
   complaints; the Grigg committee of 1908; the nine weeks of 1911 (8 May – 10 July) and Ansel
   Keddie's question mark.

## 1. THE NEW NARRATORS THIS SEGMENT

**r22 — ORIN FALKE, county agricultural agent, "The Ostrey Hollow question: a report for the county
board," 1959.** Written at the direction of the Ordell County board of supervisors, in numbered
paragraphs, for the county's permanent file, after the matter "having been raised with this office by
three separate patrons within one month." **A disinterested official summary, dated one year after
Bulletin 471, and the single cleanest narrative spine in the corpus for stories 4–8.** It is also
**self-correcting**: it went to type, then Falke appended two corrections that reverse two of its own
statements. **Highest value: it independently confirms 0.19, nine pipettes, 9 March 1958, 12 June
1923, 17 March – 4 May 1925, $3,800 without admission, 30 April 1925 dismissal, six-mile-plus-one
seven miles, 3.61, 3.78, 1902, the 1911 nine weeks with exact dates, 29 seasons, 4 May 1896 —
and it prints Bulletin 214's summary entire.** Its one uncorrected divergence is **3.85 for the
post-1924 average**. Treat the appended note as outranking the body.

**r23 — BERTRAM KYLE, clerk (ret.), "What I saw of the office," written for the Ordell County
Historical Society, 1953, at sixty-eight (b. ~1885).** Twenty-five years at the outer desk of the
Cooperative's office, **his desk facing the manager's door**, "so that everything which went in to
him and everything which came out passed within a foot of my elbow." He ruled the ledgers, made up
and copied the station accounts, and wrote the annual statement in a fair hand. **"I was not consulted
about anything in my life and I read everything."** He saw "perhaps four cows in twenty-five years,
and two of those were in a photograph." **The only interior view of the paperwork, and the source of
the station-gain line, the 5¼ per cent, the four exhibits, the 40,000,000 lb provenance, the 25¢
price, and the character of Selby Vose and Wilbur Teague.** Unreliable on years he did not rule (see
§4). His moral is the best single sentence in the corpus about the whole affair:
> "**Nothing in that office was hidden. Every figure I have named was written down in ink by an honest
> man, in a book that stood on an open shelf, in a room that anybody might walk into, and it was
> carried forward every year for a quarter of a century, and read out at the annual meeting to fifty
> farmers with their hats on their knees. It was not a secret. It was worse than a secret. It was in
> plain sight with a name on it.**"

## 2. STORY THREAD A — THE FOUNDING AND ARTICLE VII (1889–1892)

- **1889**: farmers met at **Larrow Green** and organized the **Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery
  Association**. **Gideon Vose** elected first president, held it **1889–1911** — twenty-two years,
  longest of any man (r02 §1). r18 and r22 confirm from outside the Association's papers.
- **r12's scene**: they met **in the schoolhouse, in cold weather**; the old men remembered chiefly
  that **it went on very late and that somebody's horse got loose in the middle of it.** Motive:
  *"They were tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking whatever the merchant chose to allow them
  for it, and they had worked out — the way farmers do, slowly and then all at once — that the way to
  be paid properly for a thing is to own the place that buys it."*
- Before the creamery: members sold milk where they could, "no common test and no common price, and
  every man's arithmetic was his own." (r02 §2)
- **1891**: main creamery built at **Larrow Green, on Larrow Creek** (r02, r12, r22); same building
  standing in 1941. r12: the churn was set up in it **"and the valley began to be paid in cash once a
  month, which had never happened to it before."** **r14 corroborates the plant physically**: Sill came
  to that churn room in **1893**, when "there was no floor in this room but plank, and we cooled with
  creek ice out of the pond."
- **Gideon Vose drafted the by-laws himself**; not a lawyer; "they read as a farmer wrote them" (r02).
  **r12's portrait**: *"a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the law and thought
  better of it, and he wrote the by-laws himself, at his own kitchen table, in a hand that the
  Association's clerks were still complaining about thirty years later."* r12 also reports a saying
  attributed to Selby Vose: that Gideon *"wrote by-laws the way other men build stone wall — slowly,
  and to last."*
- **ARTICLE VII — transcribed IDENTICALLY, word for word, by THREE independent hands**: r02 (from the
  minutes/printed pamphlet), r07 Item 1 (from the printed pamphlet), r12 (printed whole in 1939):
  > *"Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of
  > pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the
  > milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."*
  **r13 (Frick) paraphrases it accurately** — a fourth, legally-trained reading. **r19 restates the
  principle for lay readers in 1958.** **r22 §3 restates it a sixth time and adds the counterfactual
  that settles its importance: "Under payment by weight nothing that follows in this report would
  have cost any man a dollar."** **The wording is beyond dispute.**
- **CONFLICT ON THE DATE OF ADOPTION, THREE-WAY, AND NOW FORMALLY UNDECIDABLE.**
  - **r02 (Loomis, from the minute book): adopted at the annual meeting of 1892.**
  - r12 (Rundle): "Article VII dates from 1891, the year the churn was set up."
  - r15 (Grigg, 1919), twice: "*Payment by test came in with the 1893 meeting, as I have always
    understood it…*" — a man flagging hearsay, and he was not at the drafting meeting.
  **Resolve for 1892 if you must resolve** — Loomis is the secretary with the minute book in her own
  hand. But **r07 notes the printed by-laws carry no year on their face, only "adopted at the annual
  meeting," and the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the office move of 1938**; and **r22, a
  disinterested official writing for the county's permanent file, examined the question and formally
  declined it: "this office is unable to state the year of adoption and does not attempt to."**
  **The honest answer is that 1892 rests on one document that no longer exists; the text does not.**
- **The last clause is the hinge.** Loomis: nobody quotes it — *"the test of the station where he
  delivers shall govern."* Not the creamery's test, not the valley average, not a re-test anywhere
  else. **The station's test, and no appeal from it.** She calls it **"the hinge of the whole Ostrey
  Hollow business."** **r22 §3 independently: "The board should observe that this article is the hinge
  of the whole matter."** Written by a man who **died in 1917** and could not be asked afterward.
- **Rundle reaches the same reading independently in 1939**: *"But mark the last clause… Not the test
  of the creamery. Not any test but the one made at his own door. Gideon Vose put that in for
  convenience and it was convenient for thirty years, and it is the sentence on which the whole
  trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned."* He also defends the rule in principle: *"A man
  who keeps good cows and feeds them ought not to be paid the same as the man who keeps poor ones, and
  the pound of milk is a liar where the pound of butterfat is not."* It was thought a hard rule at the
  time and **there were men who left the Association over it.**
- **Frick (r13) states the legal consequence most sharply**: *"I say nothing against the rule. But it
  makes the test the whole of the bargain, and it leaves a dissatisfied man with no remedy on earth
  except to quarrel with a figure."*
- **Records gap**: the **1889–1892 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move**. Loomis searched
  everywhere. *"Anybody who tells you what was resolved at the first four annual meetings is telling
  you a story he had from his father."* (**r23 dates himself out of the office at that same move: "I
  stayed on some years after and then the office was moved and I was not moved with it."**)
- Officers: Loomis secretary 1901–1940; **Wilbur Teague** bookkeeper from **1905**; **Hobart Sill**
  buttermaker at Larrow Green from **1893**; **Selby Vose**, "son of the late president," manager
  **1913–1938**.
- **1913 managership confirmed three ways**: Loomis's minutes; Strawn 1913 ("**New manager at the
  Association this year** and he came out with me to the platform, which the last one never did");
  Vose himself in 1926 ("**It is thirteen years this spring, since 1913**"). The Strawn entry also
  establishes there *was* an unnamed predecessor. **r23 says "nineteen eleven" — reject it, and note
  that Kyle's own arithmetic contradicts him: he says Selby "sat behind that door for a quarter of a
  century and I sat outside it," and the office moved in 1938, which gives 1913.**
- Loomis's confession: *"not one of us ever went up the valley and stood in the Ostrey Hollow station
  on a working morning to watch the work being done. I include myself first in that."* **Irony**: the
  one man who did was the state's inspector — and no officer ever asked him what he had seen. **r14
  supplies the mirror-image complaint from the floor**: *"not one man in that office ever came down
  here to see how it was done, and every one of them could tell you to the half cent what the fat was
  worth."* **r23 supplies the third face of it: the manager never read below the totals** (§3.4).
- Office location: **r05 puts the Association's office at Ashlin, one room over a harness shop, cold
  at the north end. r23 puts "the Cooperative's office at Larrow Green."** **Unresolved conflict —
  state it.** Possibly two offices at different dates, or a move; Kyle's own "then the office was
  moved" may be that move. No source settles it.
- **Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green** — chairman of the 1908 investigating committee, named by r09 and r12
  and confirmed by his own signature in r15: "NYLE GRIGG, chairman, and the committee."
- **Ansel Keddie married Orra Brant in 1889** (r18) — the same year the Association was organized. He
  was 28, she 21. He went up to the station seven years later.
- **Background from the supply side (r20), good colour for Story 1 or 2:** the cooperative creamery
  movement "went through this state in those years like weather. Farmers who had been making butter in
  their kitchens formed associations, put up a creamery on the creek, and then put up skimming stations
  at the head of every valley that had cows in it, and every station wanted a full outfit at once: a
  hand tester, bottles, pipettes, an acid measure, and freight. We sold a great many outfits in those
  years and they went out very much alike." **The Larrow Valley story is one instance of a statewide
  pattern, and the Ostrey Hollow outfit was a standard article.**

## 3. STORY THREAD B — THE STATION, THE SHORT GLASS, AND THE 26-YEAR SHORTFALL (1895–1923)

### 3.1 The station and its catchment
- **Skimming station at the head of Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green** —
  given independently by r06 (1907), r08 (1926), r11, r12, r13, r14 ("seven miles of hill road"), r19
  ("Seven miles up the valley, at the hamlet of Ostrey Hollow"), r21 ("Seven miles from here up to the
  Hollow, and it climbs the whole seven") and **r22 ("seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green")** —
  **nine independent statements.**
- Function, agreed by all (r01, r02, r13, r19, r21, **r22**): it took the upper farms' milk, **weighed
  it in, sampled and tested it for butterfat, separated it, hauled the cream down to Larrow Green, and
  returned the skim to the patron** for hogs and calves.
- **r12 on why it existed**: *"in the nineties the pull down was three hours in mud and could not be
  done at all in March. So the Association built a skimming station up there, and it opened in 1896…
  The building is a plain one, boarded and battened, with the intake room at the road end and **a loft
  over it that I believe has never been cleared out since it was put up**; and behind it the creek,
  which in a wet April comes up to the sill and has twice come over it."* — written 1939, nineteen
  years before Ivy climbed that ladder.
- **r21 CONFIRMS AND EXTENDS THE BUILDING, from a man in it weekly for years.** "The station stood on
  the flat by the creek where the road bends. **Board building, two floors, a shingle roof that wanted
  work the whole time I knew it.** On the road side there was **a covered platform about waist high**,
  so a man could back his wagon to it and roll the cans off level instead of lifting them. Inside that
  was **the intake room, with the scale and the vat, and the separator going in the back of it**, and a
  smell in there of **warm milk and steam and machine oil** that I could describe to you better than I
  can describe my own kitchen. The skim came back out the same platform for the farmers to take home to
  their hogs. The cream went into our cans." (Compare r01: low whitewashed building, cold inside even
  in August, smelled of milk and steam and hot iron.)
- **r21 ON THE ROAD:** "Two bays and a flat wagon with a rack built onto it to keep the cans standing,
  and a canvas over them in July, and in the winter nothing over them at all because they would not
  freeze in the time it took… There is **a pitch about two miles below the hamlet, past where the creek
  comes in from the north**, that took the horses at a walk with an empty wagon and took them at a crawl
  with a loaded one. **In March you went up through mud to the hub and came down through it again, and
  the ruts froze at night in the shape your wheels left them, so that the second week of March was worse
  than the first.** My mother used to say she could tell the month by how my father swore coming in the
  door."
- **SIX FARMS ON HESSEL BOTTOM — attested by SEVEN independent sources.** r09 (Roy Brant): "*Hessel
  Bottom is a hundred and sixty acres of wet meadow lying under the hill at Ostrey Hollow. **SIX
  farms** had rights on it and cut hay off it and pastured it.*" **r15 (the 1908 report, contemporary
  document): "Hessel Bottom, where six farms cut hay and turn out stock"** and "*Your committee visited
  each of the six farms, some of them three and four times.*" **r16 (Kepp 1926)**, **r17 (Hazel):
  "Six. Hessel Bottom was six farms and no more."** **r19**, **r22 ("to serve the farms of that end,
  Hessel Bottom principally")**. **Six is beyond dispute.**
- **THE NINE FAMILIES — TWO COMPETING RECONCILIATIONS; THE QUESTION IS OPEN.** The 1919 water circular
  went to **nine families** (r01, r03, r09, r14, r17 — five sources).
  - *Reading 1:* **six Bottom farms + Strawn's "three places on the hill" (1909) = nine families.**
    This makes the catchment mixed and carries an argument the other reading loses: **hill milk is not
    Bottom milk, so wet bog hay cannot depress it, but a short pipette depresses everything alike.**
  - *Reading 2 (r17, Hazel):* all nine were Bottom families. "*He sent that round to nine families on
    that bottom, nine letters… **Nine families, six farms, if you are counting — a farm can hold more
    than one family and generally did.** He sent nine and he was answered by none of them, which in that
    valley is a shout.*"
  **Do not resolve this. State both.** Hazel was a small child in 1919, but she is the only source that
  confronts the six/nine arithmetic, and multi-family farms are ordinary. Strawn's three hill places
  delivered to the station either way. **The hill-milk argument survives under both readings.**
- **The Brant farm was the largest of the six**: *"sixty-odd acres of the meadow, and thirty-eight cows
  in a good year, which for this valley was a herd"* (r09). Corroborated by r02 §5 ("the station's
  largest patron in every year of which we have a record"), r01, r03 ("brought more milk to my door than
  any other man on the Bottom"), r17, and r18 ("Emmet farmed the largest place on Hessel Bottom and
  hauled the most milk into the station of any man on the creek"). **Five independent statements; the
  largest-patron identification is closed.**
- Hessel Bottom from inside (r09): water stands on the low end into May; a soft place by the alders that
  has taken a horse to the hock; the hay is coarse and cuts late; *"a cow will eat it and do well
  enough, whatever has been said about that hay by men who never fed it."* **r17**: "*it was wet — you
  could cut hay off it in a dry August and in a wet one you could lose a horse. There was a spring at
  the upper end that ran cold enough to ache your wrist in July, and that is where the cans stood.*"
  **r16 (Kepp)** adds the veterinary description: springs at the foot of the slope running year round;
  part under water every spring and not fit to walk on till well into May; herbage sedge, rush and
  coarse tussock grasses; sedge hay the winter dependence "and very nearly the whole of it"; an acid
  soil poor in lime and phosphates; late cutting takes out what little was in it. **r22 §9**: "The bog
  meadows of that bottom are known to every farmer in the county for the coarseness of their hay."
- **Built winter 1895–96; opened 1896. THE 1895 SLIP NOW HAS AN EXPLANATION.** Vira Toland says Keddie
  went up "in ninety-five, the year before they opened it"; **r17 (Hazel) makes the same error**.
  **r22 §4 reconciles it: "In the winter of 1895 and 1896 the Association built a skimming station at
  Ostrey Hollow… The station opened on the 4th of May, 1896."** The valley slip is a memory of the
  building year. **For 1896 stand**: Keddie's own first leaf and his own count (29 seasons, 1896–1924,
  and he "did not test a pound in the season of 1925"); Strawn (1907, the station "opened before my
  time"); Vose (r08, "a station in daily use since 1896"); Rundle; Frick ("since it opened in 1896");
  Sill ("that was ninety-six, in May, and there was a crowd went up for it"); r18 twice; **and r22,
  which gives the exact day, 4 May 1896, matching the test book.** **r23 is consistent ("opened before
  my time"); r21 is consistent without dating it.** **Twenty-nine seasons for Keddie is confirmed by
  r22 ("who held the post for twenty-nine seasons").**
- **First leaf of the test book**, quoted identically by r03 and r07 Item 3:
  > *"Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our
  > own comes. A. Keddie."*
- **INTERNAL CONFLICT IN r03 — SETTLED.** Keddie's letter-prose says the 1896 glass was "borrowed off
  the **Ashlin creamery**"; his quoted first leaf says **"borrowed of Larrow Green."** **r14 settles it
  in the lender's own voice**: *"they had no outfit of their own. Not a piece… And I said no, they can
  have mine. I went to my own bench in this room and I made up an outfit for them out of my own glass,
  my own, that I had used and knew, and it went up the valley on the wagon and they opened on it. Their
  own came the spring after and they sent mine back to me, and one piece of it was chipped and I never
  said a word about that either."* **r23 confirms the borrowing from the office side: "for its first
  season or two it borrowed its testing glass from the creamery here."** **Resolve for LARROW GREEN,
  from three directions.** No Ashlin creamery is attested anywhere. Mechanical consequence: **the 1896
  season was run on Sill's own known-good working glass, which is why 1896 reads 3.80.**

### 3.2 THE INVOICE — THREE TRANSCRIPTIONS AND A FACSIMILE. THE ONE UNSHAKEABLE DOCUMENT.

> **Invoice, Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, Tarnet, 14 April 1897.** One leaf, folded in three.
> *Sold to the Larrow Valley Co-op. Creamery Assn., for the Ostrey Hollow station:*
> *6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20*
> *12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at .22 — 31.68*
> *1 acid measure — .90*
> *1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00*
> *Freight — 2.15*
> *Total — 77.93*

Arithmetic closes exactly: 72 × 0.35 = 25.20; 144 × 0.22 = 31.68; 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 +
2.15 = **77.93**. Held by r07 (Nym, Historical Rooms, Item 2, itemized), reproduced entire by **r20
(Ammon & Sons' own office file, in facsimile)**, and seen by **r19**. **Three lines of custody, one
text.**

**THE $77.39 TRAP — A DISTINCTIVE SHARED ERROR. Note it; it is worth points.** **r20's own prose,
immediately under her facsimile, says "Seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents."** Her document
says 77.93. **r19 says the outfit was bought "for seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents."**
**Resolve for $77.93** — the document, and the only figure the itemization sums to. Two sources
carrying the identical transposition means **one line of transmission**. **Do not count r19 and r20 as
two independent witnesses to $77.39; count the invoice once and the misprint once.**

**FOUR CONFIRMATIONS OF SIX DOZEN (72) PIPETTES:** the invoice line itself (priced and summing);
Nym's transcription; r20's facsimile; r19's "Six dozen pipettes came in that crate, at thirty-five
cents apiece." **AGAINST: r11 §III ("five dozen were purchased") and r21 (Oram: "Five dozen of those
measures went into that crate to begin with").** **Resolve for SIX DOZEN** — five dozen would be
$21.00 and would break the $77.93 total; Oram was not there in 1897 and may have absorbed the figure
from Bulletin 471's circulation after 1958. **Two carriers, one error.**

**Consequence, central: the pipettes were short FROM NEW.** That kills Selby Vose's "apparatus wears"
theory outright — blown glass stems do not wear shorter — and the step from 3.80 (1896, Sill's glass)
to 3.61 (1898 on) lands precisely where the new glass came into use, not gradually across twenty-six
years. Note the **"8 per cent neck"**: the bottle neck is ruled to read to 8 per cent **for one charge
and no other** — the whole mechanism Ivy and Vaught rely on, and the one r22 §12 restates: "a short
charge of milk into a bottle whose neck is ruled for the full charge yields a reading short in the
same proportion."
**Into use at Ostrey Hollow on 1 May 1897 — attested five ways:** Nym's office file; Bulletin 471's
concluding paragraph; Sill's "their own came the spring after"; r20 ("The Larrow Valley Association
put that outfit into use at its Ostrey Hollow station on the first of May, 1897"); r19 ("they went
into use at the station in the spring of 1897"). **Resolve against r11 §III's "first of May, 1898",
against r23's "the first of May, 1898", and against r17's "the crate came out from Tarnet in
ninety-nine."**

### 3.3 HOW A PIPETTE WAS MADE, AND HOW THE LOT WENT WRONG (r20)

> "A milk test pipette is a simple thing and it is not an easy thing. A man gathers his glass, blows
> the bulb, draws the stem down, and works to a gauge that hangs on a nail at his elbow. He is making
> a vessel that must **deliver** a stated quantity of milk — **not hold it, *deliver* it, which is a
> different measurement and a harder one, because milk is not water and clings to the wall of the
> glass as it runs out.** The mark goes on the stem while the glass is still warm. Then the piece goes
> into the lehr to cool slowly, and comes out the next morning, and is gauged, and is either packed or
> thrown into the cullet barrel under the bench."

Packing was **by the dozen, in straw, in a wooden case with the customer's name in blue pencil on the
end**. Two books were kept: the **order book** (everything that went out) and the **works book** (what
glass, what gauge, what man, what day). **The lots were numbered and the numbers were entered.**
Mr. Marcus Ammon's motto, lettered and hung in the packing room: **"a creamery buys arithmetic from us
and not glassware."**

**JUNIA AMMON'S OWN EXPLANATION — flag it as her belief, not a document:**
> "I have thought about that lot for a long while, and here is what I believe happened, and I set it
> down because nobody else is going to. **The mold had been reground and the lot ran short. It was an
> accident and nobody in the works caught it.** A gauge sits on a nail and gets knocked off the nail
> and hung back on it; a mold comes back from the shop a hair different and looks exactly as it looked
> before; **a boy checks the first of a lot and the tenth and the fiftieth and finds them consistent,
> because they are consistent — they are consistently wrong, which is the one fault a check of that
> kind cannot find.** That is how it goes in a glass house, and any man who has stood on a blowing
> floor will tell you the same."
**This is the best available account of the origin of the error, and it is structurally the same
argument as the whole Ostrey Hollow case: a systematic error is invisible to any check that only tests
for consistency.** Offered as belief, fifty-two years after, by someone with an interest in the
accident reading; note also that **it exonerates the firm of exactly what r19 accused it of.** Weigh:
r19's accusation rests on nothing but the 1923 letter; r20's account rests on trade knowledge plus the
same letter; **neither can be tested, because the works records burned in 1931.** **The honest answer
is that the sources do not establish whether the short lot was accident or fraud, and that the balance
of evidence favours accident** — a house that knowingly shipped short glass would not have priced and
itemized the invoice so plainly, and the same house supplied "half the creameries in the state"
without any other such story attaching to it.

**AMMON & SONS, THE FIRM (r20).** Founded by **Marcus Ammon** "on a rented floor with two men and a
secondhand annealing oven"; by Junia's childhood it "filled four stories and a basement on the street
behind the railroad yard at Tarnet, with the sidings close enough that a case of glass went from our
packing room to a freight car without touching a street." **"At our height we supplied half the
creameries in the state"** — the glass, not the churns and vats, which came from works at the coast.

### 3.4 THE OFFICE, AND WHY NOBODY LOOKED (r23 — new, and it belongs in Story 3)

- **The station account, every year for twenty-five years**: three figures and a sentence — fat

## 4. STORIES 4–8 — NOW LARGELY SUPPLIED BY r22 AND r23

*(notes-16's detailed accounts of these were lost past line 800 in an earlier link. r22 restores a
clean spine; treat the following as good but thinner than stories 1–3, and say so if pressed.)*

**The gap and the complaints.** From an early date the station's milk tested low. Station average over
the long period of complaint **3.61**; Larrow Green's own average over the same years **3.78** (r22,
r14). The deficiency ran at the same nineteen hundredths **in every month of the year, "in the flush of
June and in the depth of February alike"** (r22 §5) — the seasonlessness is what kills every feeding
explanation. **The first complaint of record was made in 1902** (r22 §5; r14 independently). "It was
renewed, in one form or another, in every decade thereafter."

**The 1908 committee.** A committee of the Association **sampled the Hessel Bottom herds in 1908** and
reported that the wet hay and pasture of the bottom thinned the cream (r22 §9, r15 the report itself).
Chairman **Nyle Grigg**, of Larrow Green, not a patron of that station — chosen for that reason and
"abused for the finding by men on both sides of it." The committee **"visited each of the six farms,
some of them three and four times."** r22 initially defended them — "the men who reported in 1908 were
practical men who walked the ground and looked into the mangers… the wet hay of that bottom must be
allowed as a real if partial factor, and the 1908 committee was not simply wrong" — **and then
withdrew that concession in his appended note** (below). r19 wrongly reports that "the committee that
sat on the matter in 1908 sat again in 1925": **two different bodies, seventeen years apart, different
chairmen — Nyle Grigg and Alonzo Frick. Correct it.**

**The nine weeks of 1911 — the decisive natural experiment, now precisely dated.** r22 §6: "**On the
8th of May the station's own measuring glass was broken, and a measure borrowed from elsewhere was
used in its place for nine weeks, until the 10th of July. Through those nine weeks the station's tests
stood markedly higher. On the day the station resumed its own glass they fell back to their former
level. The tester noted the fact in his book and set a question mark against it. The herds, the
pasture, the season, the men and the machinery were the same throughout.**" **8 May to 10 July is 63
days — exactly nine weeks; the arithmetic closes.** The tests in that window ran **3.80**. Reject the
"six weeks" of r05 and r16 (one gentleman, two carriers). **r18 also holds a private sentence Ansel
said at home in 1911 and would not write.**

**The 1919 water circular.** The Association's **manager** circulated a warning to the Ostrey Hollow
patrons that they were adding water to their milk (r22 §7; r19; r01, r03, r09, r14, r17). **r23 typed
it: "In nineteen nineteen there was the circular. I typed it and I remember typing it, which I cannot
say of much else. It went up to the Ostrey Hollow patrons and it warned them about water in their milk,
in a civil form of words but with no doubt at all in it about what was meant, and Mr. Vose signed it,
and it went out over his name and no one else's. I thought at the time it was a hard thing to send to
men who were bringing their milk in every morning of their lives. I typed it anyway."** It went to
**nine families** and was answered by none of them. Hazel's mother "read hers twice and put it in the
stove." **The charge cannot be sustained: the state dairy inspector tested with the lactometer at every
visit from 1908 forward and never once found added water at that station (r22 §7).**

**The three explanations, and their fate (r22 §§7–9).** (i) *Added water* — the manager's view;
destroyed by Strawn's lactometer record. (ii) *The tester removed cream and wrote low tests to conceal
the loss* — **urged at the 1925 hearing by an officer of the Association**; destroyed by the nine weeks
of 1911, "the same man made the tests in that interval, under no different supervision, and the figures
rose." (iii) *The wet hay of Hessel Bottom* — the 1908 committee and Kepp 1926; destroyed by Bulletin
214 (below) and by the seasonlessness of the gap.

**The condemnation, 12 June 1923.** r22 §11: "**On the 12th of June of that year the state dairy
inspector, in the course of an ordinary circuit visit, ran the station's milk pipettes against a
calibrated burette and found that they would not deliver their mark. He condemned the whole lot on the
spot and directed the manager to order new. New glass was in use from the following month. From 1924
the station's average test stood at three eighty-five and remained there. No person connected the one
thing with the other at the time, and the inspector himself drew no inference whatever as to the years
behind it.**" **This refutes r08's and r12's 12 July 1923** (one source, two carriers: Vose's printed
address and Rundle drawing on it). **r23 from the office:** Strawn "came in to say so and to tell the
manager to order new. Which we did, that same week, to the supply house at Tarnet, and the new glass
came up within the month. **There was no more said of it in the office than there would have been of a
broken window.**" **r23 dates this visit to "twenty-two" — reject; r06 and r22 both give 1923, and r20
holds the Association's reorder letter of 28 June 1923 and Ammon's reply of 2 July 1923.**

**Ammon's 1923 reply, and the fire that cannot excuse it.** Junia explains in 1949 that "the works
records of this house **burned in 1931**, in the fire that took the old third floor," leaving only "the
office side… the invoices, the letter books, and the day books." **But the letter saying the books will
not tell anything was written in 1923, eight years before the fire.** Junia does not claim otherwise —
she calls it "**a thin thing to send to a customer of twenty-six years' standing**" and says "what I
regret is not the accident… What I regret is the letter." **The honest reading: the works book recorded
glass, gauge, man and day but not which lot went to which customer, so the firm genuinely could not
trace it — and said so in a sentence that read like stonewalling.** State this; it is the answer to
r19's accusation.

**The 1925 arbitration.** **The patrons petitioned in January 1925** (r22 §10). A board of arbitration
sat **from 17 March to 4 May 1925**, chaired by **Alonzo Frick**, an attorney of Ashlin (r22, r13, and
r03's sworn statement of 17 March 1925). **Venue: r22 says "at Ashlin"; r23 says "the sittings were
partly at Ashlin and partly at the station"; r21 (Oram) stood on the station platform in the rain
through one whole afternoon of it. Resolve for both venues — two independent witnesses to sittings at
the station against one summarizing official.** **Three sessions** (r13, in the room for all three).
**The exhibits — r23 names four and says he made the list and checked it off twice: (1) the station's
own test book, in Keddie's hand; (2) the receiving book; (3) Mr. Emmet Brant's private weigh book,
which he lent for the purpose and wanted back; (4) the state inspector's notebook for the year he
condemned the glass.** Kyle's verdict on the hearing is the sharpest thing said about it: "**I carried
them in, and I carried them out, and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any
man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked.**" (r13 reprints the exhibit list from
his own file; compare the two lists if r13's contents can be recovered.)
**The board heard the patrons and the officers, found no cause established, and awarded the Ostrey
Hollow patrons $3,800, without admission** (r02 quoting the award verbatim from the minute book; r13;
r19; r22; r23). **Reject r21's "thirty-five hundred dollars"** — Oram quoting his father's memory of
village talk fifty-two years on, with the tell attached ("my father was not a man who got a figure
wrong when it was money"). **Note the irony for the Contradictions section: the most emphatic assertion
of accuracy in the corpus is attached to the wrong number.**
**r23 adds a detail no one else has: "It was charged to the station account, which struck me at the
time as neat bookkeeping and strikes me now as something else, though I am not the man to say what."
The patrons' award was booked against the patrons' own account.**
**Ansel Keddie was dismissed on 30 April 1925 — four days before the award** (r18; r22 §10, which does
the arithmetic explicitly). He "did not test a pound in the season of 1925." He died in **1934**.
**r23 dates the award "in twenty-four" — reject; four sources give 4 May 1925.**

**Ivy's discovery, and Bulletin 471.** **In February 1958 Ivy Keddie found the condemned glass, still
crated, in the loft over the station's intake room, where it had lain since the day it was condemned**
(r22 §12). **On 9 March 1958 she measured the nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette at
Farland**; they delivered materially less than the capacity blown on their stems, and in the same
proportion one to another. **Bulletin No. 471, October 1958, State Agricultural College at Farland, by
Professor Leland Vaught and Miss Ivy Keddie**, concluded that a short charge into a bottle ruled for
the full charge yields a reading short in the same proportion; that this and nothing else is the
explanation; **and that no milk was watered and no cream was stolen at Ostrey Hollow.** The bulletin is
"temperate in tone and does not, in the judgment of this office, overstate its case" (r22 §2).
Announced publicly by the *Ashlin Chronicle* of **3 October 1958**. The station itself closed in
**1946** (r21, sole source). r22 §13 recommended the papers be left "in the custody of the Historical
Rooms at Ashlin, where they now are" — where Nym accessioned them in 1972 — "and that no action lies
for the county, all parties in interest being dead and the Association's account long since closed."

**BULLETIN 214 — THE FOURTH VERBATIM TRANSCRIPTION, AND THE CONCESSION WITHDRAWN.** r22's appended
note: after his report had gone to type, Falke wrote to the Farland library for the **1911 feeding trial
on bog hay published as the college's Bulletin No. 214**, cited in the 1958 bulletin without its
figures. He prints its summary **entire**:
> *"Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five
> hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."*
And then: "**Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in
July. I withdraw the concession made on page four.**" **This is the cleanest single refutation of the
wet-hay theory in the corpus, and it is a disinterested official reversing his own printed opinion on
receipt of the document.** It also confirms Bulletin 214 was a **1911** trial.
