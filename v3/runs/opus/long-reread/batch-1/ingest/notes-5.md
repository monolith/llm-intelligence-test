## 1. THE NARRATORS AND WHAT THEY ARE WORTH

- **r01 — Vira Toland**, born 1912 at the top end of Ostrey Hollow, daughter of a Hessel Bottom patron. Taped
  at her kitchen table, **1979**, by "a young man from the county." Childhood memory, sixty years on, no
  documents in hand. She was 7 in 1919. Lived two years at Ashlin when first married; otherwise always in the
  Hollow. Warm, vivid, and repeatedly wrong on dates and figures.
- **r02 — Effie Loomis**, secretary of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association **1901–1940**,
  "Notes toward a history of the Association," written **1941**, the year after she laid down the minute book.
  Deliberately separates "from the minutes" from "from my own recollection." Quotes Article VII and the 1925
  award verbatim. **Highest documentary authority for Association proceedings.** Born 1875 (was 14 in 1889).
- **r03 — Ansel Keddie**, tester at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station. Two papers: (I) his nine-sentence
  sworn statement standing before the board of arbitration at Ashlin, **17 March 1925**; (II) an unsent letter
  written at Ostrey Hollow, **spring 1925**, found among his papers. Quotes his own test-book leaves verbatim.
  **The quoted book leaves outrank the letter's prose recollections.**
- **r04 — Ivy Keddie**, "What the Crate Held," a talk read from typescript to the **Ordell County Historical
  Society, Ashlin, 1959**, with the crate on the table and the borrowed books open beside it; quoted papers read
  from originals. Ansel's granddaughter. **This is the solving document.** Physical measurement + five
  independent record sets.
- **r05 — Wilbur Teague**, bookkeeper of the Association **1905–1945**, Chapter Nine of an unpublished memoir
  written at Ashlin, **winter 1948**, three years after retiring. Quotes his own 1898 ledger entry verbatim.
  Reliable on the ledger line; unreliable on the two summary totals he gives from memory (see conflicts).
- **r06 — Merle Strawn**, state dairy inspector (retired), **"Extracts from twenty-eight circuit books," with a
  covering note, 1936.** *"Copied out this spring at the request of the state dairy office, from the pocket
  books I carried on the Ordell circuit. I have copied what is in them and put in nothing that is not."* Field
  notes made **on the day, on the spot, by a disinterested state officer** — the only contemporaneous outside
  record in the corpus, and the only source that is neither a party to the dispute nor writing from memory.
  **On matters he personally weighed, tested, or ordered, r06 is the highest authority available**, above even
  Loomis's minutes (which record what men said in a room; Strawn records what an instrument read). He refuses
  to give opinions or a "general estimation" of any plant. His Larrow content is wrapped in unrelated generated
  filler; none of that bears on anything.

## 2. STORY THREAD A — THE FOUNDING OF THE ASSOCIATION AND ARTICLE VII (1889–1892)

- **1889**: farmers of the Larrow Valley met at **Larrow Green** and organized the **Larrow Valley Cooperative
  Creamery Association**. **Gideon Vose** elected first president, held the office **1889–1911** — twenty-two
  years, longest of any man. (r02 §1)
- Before the creamery, members sold milk where they could, "no common test and no common price, and every man's
  arithmetic was his own." (r02 §2)
- **1891**: main creamery built at **Larrow Green, on Larrow Creek**; same building still standing in 1941.
- **Gideon Vose drafted the by-laws himself**; not a lawyer; "they read as a farmer wrote them."
- **Article VII adopted at the annual meeting of 1892**, the year after the creamery opened. Effie Loomis sets
  it down entire because it has been misquoted at her across the desk for forty years:
  > *"Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of
  > butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test
  > of the station where he delivers shall govern."*
- Loomis's gloss: before it, a man was paid on can weight; after it, on the fat found by a test made where he
  delivered. **The last clause is the one nobody quotes — "the test of the station where he delivers shall
  govern." Not the creamery's test, not the valley average, not a re-test anywhere else. The station's test,
  and no appeal from it.** She calls that clause **"the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business."** Written
  1892 by a man who **died in 1917** and could not be asked about it afterward.
- **Records gap**: the **1889–1892 minute book was lost in the office move of 1938**. Not thrown out — carried
  out with the rest and never arrived. Loomis searched every place a book can be in the building. "Anybody who
  tells you what was resolved at the first four annual meetings is telling you a story he had from his father."
- Officers in Loomis's time: Loomis secretary 1901–1940 (minute book in her own hand throughout); **Wilbur
  Teague** bookkeeper from **1905**; **Hobart Sill** buttermaker at Larrow Green (kept the churn book and the
  cream receiving book); **Selby Vose**, "son of the late president," made **manager in 1913, served until
  1938**. Loomis's confession: *"not one of us ever went up the valley and stood in the Ostrey Hollow station
  on a working morning to watch the work being done. I include myself first in that."*
- **Independent confirmation of the 1913 managership (r06).** Strawn, **1913**: "Larrow Green creamery,
  two days, the churn room and the receiving books. Scales true. **New manager at the Association this year**
  and he came out with me to the platform, which the last one never did." A contemporaneous outside note fixing
  Selby Vose's appointment to 1913 exactly as the minutes have it. Also a character note: the new manager walked
  the platform with the inspector; his predecessor never did.
- **Sharp irony against Loomis's confession**: the one man who *did* stand in the Ostrey Hollow station on
  working mornings, year after year, watching the work being done, was the state's inspector — and no officer
  of the Association ever asked him what he had seen.
- Office at Ashlin: one room over a harness shop, cold at the north end (r05).

## 3. STORY THREAD B — THE STATION, THE SHORT GLASS, AND THE 26-YEAR SHORTFALL (1896–1923)

### The station
- **Skimming station at the head of Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green** (distance
  independently confirmed by r06, 1907: "up seven miles from Larrow Green"). Low whitewashed building, cold
  inside even in August, smelled of milk and steam and hot iron; engine audible before the last turn (r01).
  Took in the milk of the upper farms, weighed it, sampled and tested it, separated it, sent the **cream down to
  Larrow Green** and **gave the skim back to the patron** for hogs and calves (r02 §5, r05).
- **Nine families hauled to that station** (r01; r03 — the 1919 circular went to nine families). **Seven farms
  had rights on Hessel Bottom**, the wet meadow below the hamlet; the best milk came off the Bottom.
- **The catchment described by an outsider (r06).** Strawn, **1909**: "The station takes from **the farms of the
  wet bottom below it and from three places on the hill.**" **Minor unresolved tension:** seven Bottom farms +
  three hill places = ten, against nine families on the circular. Possible reconciliations (none stated in any
  source): a Bottom *rights*-holder who did not haul to the station; two farms in one family; or six Bottom
  haulers of seven rights-holders. Treat "nine families / seven Bottom farms" as the working figures, and note
  the hill farms exist and are not on the Bottom — which matters, because the wet-hay theory could never have
  explained a gap that fell on hill milk too.
- **Emmet Brant** was the largest of the seven Hessel Bottom farms and **"the station's largest patron in every
  year of which we have a record"** (r02 §5). Most cows, most hay ground (r01).
- **Ansel Keddie** ran/tested it. Vira Toland: he "went up there in ninety-five, the year before they opened
  it, to get it ready, and he tested that first summer too." **Keddie's own first leaf and his own count
  contradict the 1895 testing**: 29 seasons, 1896 through 1924, and he "did not test a pound in the season of
  1925." Strawn (1907) confirms the station "opened before my time," consistent with 1896.
- First leaf of the test book:
  > *"Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own
  > comes. A. Keddie."*
- **Internal conflict inside r03**: the letter's prose says "The glass I began on in ninety-six was borrowed off
  the **Ashlin creamery**"; the quoted first leaf says **"borrowed of Larrow Green."** Resolve for **Larrow
  Green** — quoted contemporaneous document over 29-year-old recollection; and no Ashlin creamery is attested
  anywhere (Ashlin held the office over a harness shop, and the courts/lawyers).
- **The station's own outfit came up the valley in a crate the spring following (1897).**

### Keddie's method and book (from the unsent letter)
- Day began half past five; wagons up in the dark; intake room open, lamps lit, scale swinging free before the
  first wagon. Every can weighed and the weight called and written before the milk went anywhere. **The scale
  was tested by the state's man on his rounds and was true; in 29 seasons no patron ever disputed a weight and
  was right.**
- Sample taken from each patron's milk as it was weighed, into **his own numbered jar**, **made up over ten
  days** before testing. On testing morning: warmed in water to blood-warm through, poured vessel to vessel
  until the risen cream was carried down through the whole.
- Charge drawn up in the measure to the mark on the stem, tip wiped, blown out into the numbered bottle; acid
  poured down the side; bottle turned till the milk darkened; **four bottles in the hand tester, weighted
  against each other, cover down, crank turned at the taught speed**; fat read off in per cent against the
  rulings **with a pair of dividers**.
- Book kept in **five columns: date, patron, pounds, test, fat.** Errors crossed and initialed; **never a figure
  written over a figure.**
- Statement to the board: "I have tested as I was taught and as the state man saw me do it, **thirty-one times
  a week for twenty-nine seasons**, and I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are… I have never taken
  a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by
  himself."
- **Keddie's claim about "the state man" is corroborated from the state man's own books, and it is the strongest
  single vindication of his method.** Strawn's standing routine at every plant, in the same order every time:
  (1) **the scales**, against sealed weights he carried — both the intake scale and the cream scale, loaded
  light and loaded heavy; (2) **the lactometer** on the day's milk, patron by patron where there was any
  question, for added water; (3) **the boiler, water supply and cold room**; (4) — "the one most men grudged
  me" — **he stood at the tester's elbow and watched him make a test through from the sample to the reading,
  and did not let him hurry it.**
  - **1907**: "Their tester keeps his book in his own hand, and his hand is better than most. **Watched him
    through two tests. He works slow and clean and reads his own figure without being coaxed to it.**"
  - **1911, June**: "Scales true. **Watched a test through. Nothing here to note.**"
  - **Scales true** recorded at Ostrey Hollow in 1907, 1909, 1911, 1912, 1915, 1916, 1919, 1920 (twice),
    1922, 1923 (June), 1923 (Oct), 1924, 1925, 1928, 1931, 1934, 1936 — i.e. **every recorded visit, without
    exception, across twenty-nine years.**
  - **Lactometer, no added water** recorded at Ostrey Hollow in 1912, 1916, 1919, 1920 (twice), 1922, 1923
    (June, "as always here"), 1924, 1925, 1928, 1934.
  - **Keddie's procedure was watched through, from sample to reading, by the state's inspector, and never once
    faulted.** The man's technique was sound; only his glass was wrong. This is the crucial separation: a
    proportional error in the *instrument* survives a perfect *method*, and Strawn's routine before 1921 had
    no way to catch it (see 1921 below).

### The 1922 summary leaf (quoted identically in r03 and r04 — same document, ONE source)
> *"Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within
> three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it
> stood at 3.80."*

### The nine weeks of 1911 — the accidental controlled experiment
- Two entries as Keddie made them:
  > *"May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure."*
  > *"July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."*
- The question mark is Keddie's; he let it stand fourteen years and would not put anything in its place he did
  not know.
- Ivy: **8 May to 10 July 1911**, nine weeks; the borrowed measure **came up from Larrow Green with the cream
  wagon**; same patrons, herds, pasture, season, acid, bottles, same man at the crank — **one object changed** —
  average went to 3.80 and returned to 3.61 the day the station's own measure came back.
- **Strawn was physically in the station during the window.** "**1911.** Ostrey Hollow, **June**, on my way over
  to the other side of the county. Scales true. Watched a test through. Nothing here to note." 8 May–10 July
  1911 brackets June, so the test Strawn watched through in June 1911 was made **on the sound borrowed
  measure** — which is exactly why there was nothing to note. It also independently confirms that Keddie's
  hand-work was identical in the high-reading window and the low-reading years: same man, same method, watched
  by the state; only the glass differed.
- **Conflict**: Teague (r05) says the measure broke "in the spring of 1911" and they made shift with a borrowed
  one for **six weeks** before **their own came up from town**. Resolve for **nine weeks, borrowed from Larrow
  Green** — Keddie's dated book entries are a contemporaneous document; Teague is recalling 37 years later and
  recorded it only as a small replacement charge.

### The gap was flat, not seasonal
- Keddie: "They did not come and go with the season. The gap was there in July as it was there in February, in
  the flush of the grass and in the dead of the winter on dry hay, and it was the same in a wet year as in a dry
  one."
- **The 1924 = 3.80 anomaly is RESOLVED, and resolved in Keddie's favor.** Keddie's letter says "**In 1924
  the average of this station was 3.80**, and I wrote that figure with the same pen and the same measure of
  care as all the others." Segment 1 flagged this as apparently contradicting his own 1922 summary leaf.
  **r06 independently confirms the figure**: Strawn, **1924**: "Ostrey Hollow. Scales true, no added water,
  **tests improved. Their book gives the year at 3.80.**" Two independent records (Keddie's letter; Strawn's
  field book, copied 1936) give 3.80 for 1924. It is not an error and not a contradiction: the 1922 leaf
  described 1898–1922, and 1924 is *after* the glass was replaced. **The tests rose permanently, by exactly the
  predicted amount, the moment the instrument changed.** This is the single cleanest confirmation of the
  pipette theory in the corpus, and it was sitting in the state's own book from 1924.
- Strawn, **1925** (fall): "Scales true, no added water, **tests improved.** New man at the tester's bench this
  year. He is quick, and quick is not the thing I look for." — the tests stayed up under a *different* tester,
  which kills any residual theory that the low figures were something Keddie personally did.

### The condemnation and the crate
- **June 1923**: the **state's inspector condemned a crate of measures** at the station; it was set aside and
  **new ones ordered in its place** (r02 §8). Loomis: **the crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all** —
  not by patrons, officers, or the board, in any of the three sessions. "The award's phrase about 'the books and
  appliances of the station' is a form of words the board used and not a list of anything." Her closing line:
  "the interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying."
- **Vaught's bulletin dates the short glass from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923.**
- **Who condemned the crate is CLOSED. It was Merle Strawn, with his book entry quoted verbatim, dated to the
  day.** Strawn's heading: "**1923, June 12.** Ostrey Hollow. I had carried the burette two seasons before this
  check." Then the entry itself:
  > *"Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk pipettes against
  > the burette. **These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and
  > told the manager to order new.** Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them."*
  The date **12 June 1923** matches Bulletin 471's end-date exactly; "the whole crate of them" matches the crate
  Ivy found in the loft; "told the manager to order new" matches r02 §8's "new ones ordered in its place." The
  manager told to order new glass was **Selby Vose**.
  - Note Strawn's own closing clause — "**I did not weigh what it has cost them**" — the field man declining, on
    principle, to compute the consequence. The consequence went uncomputed for thirty-five years.
- **WHY IT WAS NOT CAUGHT EARLIER — the missing mechanism of the whole story.** Strawn, **1921**: "**The state
  has begun issuing burettes to the field men this year**, graduated, in a wooden case, for checking the
  delivery of measures and pipettes at the plants. Mine came in the spring with a printed sheet of directions.
  **It is a slow business to run a plant's glass against it and most plants have never had it done.**" Before
  1921 no field officer in the state carried an instrument capable of detecting a short pipette. The error was
  undetectable-in-practice for its first twenty-four years, not overlooked.
- **The near miss.** Strawn, **1922**: "Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. Boiler in order.
  **I did not run their glass against the burette this year, the day being short and the road home being what
  it was, and I have put that down because it is what happened.**" One short afternoon in 1922 cost the patrons
  an additional year of the shortfall — and Strawn recorded his own omission rather than hide it, which is a
  mark of the book's reliability.
- **The replacement is dated, and the new glass was verified.** Strawn, **1923, October**: "Ostrey Hollow. **New
  glass in use since July.** Ran the new pipettes against the burette. **They deliver their mark.** Scales
  true." So: condemned 12 June 1923; **new glass in use from July 1923**; new glass checked and correct by
  October 1923. (No source says what was used in the few weeks between mid-June and July.)
- **The burette program was short-lived.** Strawn, **1926**: "Word from the state office that the burette
  program is **discontinued from this year**. The instruments are to be kept by the men who have them but no
  more are to be issued and no returns are wanted. Mine is in its case in my grip and has been used twice
  since." The state issued the detecting instrument for **five years (1921–1925)** and then stopped. The Ostrey
  Hollow crate was caught inside a five-year window that had opened two years earlier and would close three
  years later.
- **The crate in the loft, seen by the inspector in 1928.** Strawn, **1928**: "Ostrey Hollow. Scales true.
  Lactometer, no added water. The station is quieter than it was. **The loft over the intake room is full of
  odds and ends and I told them a loft over a milk room is a place for nothing but air, and was told in return
  that it had been full since before the war.**" That is the loft where Ivy found the crate thirty years later.
  The station-hand's "full since before the war" is loose and wrong as to the crate (which went up in June 1923,
  after the war) — an offhand remark, not Strawn's own claim; it shows how quickly the crate had become part of
  the furniture.
- Ivy, **February 1958**: went up the Hollow to the station building (used for storage since it closed; fence
  wire and salt kept in it). Loft over the old intake room, reached by a ladder; under a tarpaulin at the back,
  a wooden crate **stenciled on the end "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET."** Carried up there **June 1923** and
  there still.
- Inside, packed in straw: the station's **condemned milk-test pipettes**. **Six dozen (72) bought new for the
  station in 1897**, on an invoice totalling **$77.93** for the whole outfit — pipettes, bottles, acid measure,
  hand tester and freight together. **Nine were unbroken.**

### Ivy's measurement, 9 March [1958], at Farland against a calibrated burette
> *"Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean of five
> runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c.
> That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for."*

Note the pleasing symmetry: **the same class of instrument (a graduated burette) that condemned the glass in
1923 is the instrument that explained it in 1958** — Strawn ran the pipettes against a burette and learned
*that* they were short; Ivy ran the survivors against a burette and learned *by how much*, and turned the
"how much" into pounds and dollars.

### The five record sets Ivy assembled (no two ever in one room before 1958)
1. **The station test book** — in the Association's office since 1925; came to the Historical Society Rooms in 1959.
2. **The Brant weigh book**, kept at Hessel Bottom **from 1897 onward**, lent by **Roy Brant, Emmet Brant's
   son**, who has kept it up himself since his father died.
3. **The Alder Corners Cheese Company's test figures**, from over **Redlow Ridge in Vessey County**, copied out
   for her by **Rosalie Cudd**, who runs that factory and is **Jerome Cudd's niece**. The book has never left
   Vessey County; **Ivy never saw it** — second-hand transcript; note the dependency.
4. **Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks**, kept by the **state's dairy inspector for this county from 1908**
   (Ivy's dating); in Ivy's own college library at **Farland since 1947** — eleven years in which nobody put a
   question to them.
5. **The Association's own annual ledgers.**

- **Dating conflict on Strawn's tenure. Ivy (r04) says "from 1908." Strawn says 1907.** His covering note:
  "**I took the Ordell circuit in nineteen hundred and seven** and I have laid it down this year [1936]."
  His first extract is headed "**1907.** First round of the new circuit." **Resolve for 1907** — a man's own
  first-person statement about his own appointment, written into his own books at the time and copied out under
  the state's instruction, outranks a later researcher's summary description. Corroborating arithmetic: he
  says the books number **twenty-eight, one to the year**; 1907 through 1936 inclusive is thirty years, 1908
  through 1936 is twenty-nine — neither is exactly 28, so the count does not settle it by itself (he may not
  have kept a book for every year, or may count differently); but 1907 is his own word, twice.
- **Provenance chain for the notebooks, with a gap.** Strawn's closing note (1936): "**The books themselves —
  twenty-eight of them, one to the year, oilcloth covers, pocket size — go to the state dairy office with this
  copy, as the rules require of a man laying down a circuit.**" Ivy has them at Farland from 1947. Nothing in
  either source explains the transfer from the state dairy office to a college library eleven years later. Not
  a contradiction — plausible and unremarkable — but unstated.

### The refutation of the "wet hay" explanation
- **1908**: a committee came up and sampled the herds through the winter and reported that **the wet hay of the
  Bottom thinned the cream, and that the remedy lay with the land** (r03).
- **State Agricultural College, Bulletin No. 214 (1911)**, quoted by Ivy:
  > *"Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of
  > one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."*
- Ivy: five hundredths in winter, nothing in summer, against a gap of nineteen hundredths standing flat in
  every month of every year — "**four times too small and it is the wrong shape.**"
- **Alder Corners cross-check**: the factory over the ridge tested the milk of **four Hessel Bottom men** in
  their summer flush; over **fourteen overlapping seasons** that same milk off that same wet ground ran **about
  nineteen hundredths higher at Alder Corners than at home.**
- **Supporting point from r06**: the station also took milk **from three places on the hill** (1909), which is
  not Bottom ground at all. A defect in the station's glass depresses hill milk and Bottom milk alike; wet bog
  hay cannot. No source draws this inference explicitly — flag it as inference.

### The vindication
- **Bulletin No. 471**, issued **October 1958**, last paragraph, Professor **Vaught**'s wording and Ivy's
  together:
  > *"No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the
  > twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by
  > one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the
  > Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."*
- Ivy's tally: **glass condemned 1923, cause found 1958 — thirty-five years.** Grandfather dismissed 1925,
  cleared 1958 — **thirty-three years.** He **died in 1934** and had been **dead twenty-four years** when the
  sheet was read that clears him. "I have no remedy to propose. I have only the crate, and it is on the table."
- **Keddie's death year is quietly corroborated.** Strawn, **1934**: "Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer,
  no added water. A separator bearing run hot and shut down two days, since repaired. **The tester's book is
  kept up, though not in the hand it used to be kept in.**" Consistent with r04's precise 1934 and against
  r01's vague "in the thirties." (Keddie had in fact left the bench in 1925, so the 1934 remark is about the
  book's continuing keeping, not about his death — but the elegiac note is there.)

## 4. STORY THREAD C — THE STATION-GAIN LINE (Teague's ledger, 1898/1905 onward)

- Teague's mechanism: on one side of the station account he entered **the butterfat credited to patrons on the
  station's own test** (what the station's books said it had bought); on the other, **the butterfat that
  actually came down the valley in the station's cream and was weighed and tested into the vats at Larrow
  Green** (what they had in hand). "The two were never the same. **The second was always the larger.**" The
  difference he carried to the general account of the Association.
- The entry, quoted complete:
  > *"Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, **57,760 lb**. Butter fat received at
  > Larrow Green in station cream, **60,800 lb**. Station gain, **3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as
  > usual**. Carried to the general account."*
  **This is the entry for 1898**, "and there is one like it for every year after, in the same terms and in the
  same hand for as long as the hand was mine."
- **These numbers corroborate everything else exactly**: 1,600,000 lb of milk × 3.61% = 57,760 lb;
  × 3.80% = 60,800 lb; difference 3,040 lb; 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.263% ≈ "five and a quarter per cent."
- **Why the two sides of Teague's account had to differ, stated mechanically.** The station's *test* was made
  with the short pipette; the cream shipped down the valley was **weighed and tested into the vats at Larrow
  Green**, on Larrow Green's own (sound) glass. Strawn (**1912**) notes "**Cream shipped down the valley the
  same day it is separated, which is more than some do**," so there was no spoilage or interval to explain the
  difference away. Two instruments, one sound and one short, measuring the same fat: the gap was structural and
  permanent, and it landed in the Association's general account every year.
- Teague on why nobody looked: "A figure that jumps about will keep you awake. **A figure that comes in at five
  and a quarter every year is furniture.**"
- **Internal inconsistency in r05 (unresolved)**: he says "**the station-gain line was my own device; I began it
  when I came in 1905**" and denies it was any old dodge of the founders — yet the entry he quotes complete is
  **the 1898 entry**, seven years before he arrived. Either he was working from a predecessor's ledger, or he
  mis-stated the start year, or the "1898" label is his slip. **Flag; still open.**
- **Teague's two summary figures are the outliers in the whole corpus**: "Over the years the account ran, that
  station took in **forty-four million pounds of milk**. And the average test of all the patrons of Ostrey
  Hollow… was **3.16**." Both conflict with r02 (Loomis, from Teague's own annual sheets: **40,000,000 lb**,
  average 1,600,000/yr) and r04 (Ivy: 40,000,000 lb, recorded 3.61). **3.16 is almost certainly a digit

  and found neither. **Teague's structural argument survives as a governance point; as evidence that anything
  was actually taken, it is refuted.**

## 5. STORY THREAD D — THE 1919 CIRCULAR AND THE KEDDIE–BRANT RUPTURE

- **1902**: complaints began — "it was first said down at Larrow Green that Ostrey Hollow milk did not test with
  the rest." (r03)
- **1908**: the committee and the wet-hay report (above).
- **1919**: a printed circular went out from the Association, **over the manager's signature — Selby Vose**, to
  **nine families** at Ostrey Hollow, saying **there was water in the cans** coming down from Ostrey Hollow.
  (r01; r03 confirms: "In 1919 a circular went out from the Association to nine families up here saying there
  was water in their cans.")
- **THE DECISIVE DOCUMENT ON THE WATERING CHARGE, AND IT IS CONTEMPORANEOUS.** Strawn, **1919**: "Ostrey Hollow.
  Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. **The manager of the Association has sent a circular round to the
  patrons of this station about water in the cans. I have tested for water at this station on every visit I
  have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so.**"
  - This is the state's own officer, on the ground, in the year of the circular, recording that (a) he had
    tested for added water at Ostrey Hollow on **every visit**, (b) he had **never found any**, and (c) he
    **told the manager so** — i.e. told **Selby Vose**, the man whose signature was on the paper.
  - It converts the 1919 circular from an honest mistake into something worse: the accusation was **known to be
    unsupported by the state's own testing at the time it was made or immediately after it was made**, and no
    source records any retraction. Nine kitchens kept the paper for forty years for a charge the inspector had
    already refuted to the officer who signed it.
  - It also independently confirms the circular's existence, its year, its subject ("water in the cans"), and
    that it came from **the manager** — the only outside confirmation of the circular in the corpus, since no
    source reproduces its text.
  - Corroborating the "no water" finding across the whole span, Strawn records "lactometer, no added water" at
    Ostrey Hollow in 1912, 1916, 1919, 1920 (spring and fall), 1922, 1923 (twice — "as always here"), 1924,
    1925, 1928 and 1934. Vaught's 1958 conclusion — "**No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow**" — was
    demonstrable in 1919 and provable from a book that reached a library in 1947.
- Vira Toland's scene: it rained that day; her father read it at the table **with his hat still on, which he
  never did**; her mother stood at the stove with her back turned the whole time; he read it twice, then put it
  in the drawer of the desk in the front room, **where it still was when he died**; her brother may have it yet.
  Some kept theirs till they died; **one woman kept hers pinned inside the lid of her trunk.** "You can tell
  people a thing like that is only business and they will still keep the paper forty years to prove what was
  said of them."
- Keddie on the circular: "I carry no charge against any man who put his name to that paper. I only say that I

- Orra outlived Ansel "a good while" and carried the stigma the whole time (r01).

## 6. STORY THREAD E — THE PETITION, THE ARBITRATION, AND THE DISMISSAL (1925)

- **January 1925**: the patrons of Ostrey Hollow petitioned the Association. Complaint: **their milk had never
  tested as their neighbors' milk tested and they had been paid accordingly for a very long time**; they asked
  to be made whole. (r02 §6). Teague: "The patrons petitioned in the winter, when a farmer has time to be
  angry."
- **The board of arbitration sat at Ashlin under Alonzo Frick, attorney, of that place, from 17 March to 4 May
  1925.** (r02 §6) Teague calls Frick "as fair a chairman as ever a hearing had."
- **Three explanations were argued before it. None of the three was accepted.** Loomis declines to rehearse
  them; "they are in the minutes and the minutes are in the safe." **STILL A LIVE GAP — the three explanations
  are named nowhere in r01–r06.** Presumptively the wet hay, and watering/theft; the third is unknown.
- The award, recorded by Loomis herself on the last day:
  > *"The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of
  > the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of
  > thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account."*
- Loomis: "*Finds no cause established*, and then an award of thirty-eight hundred dollars: I took it down as it
  was given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once."
- Keddie on the same paradox: "**I was put out on the thirtieth of April.** They found no cause established and
  they put me out on the last day of the month, and both of those things are true at once, and I have given up
  trying to hold them in my head together."
- **Chronology oddity**: the board sat until **4 May 1925** and the award was recorded on the last day, but
  **Keddie was put out on 30 April 1925** — four days *before* the award. The dismissal preceded the finding.
- Teague was called and gave his evidence; the award was charged to the station account and he entered it "and
  it balanced."
- **The crate of condemned glass, sitting in the loft since June 1923, was never mentioned in any of the three
  sessions.**
- **A second, worse silence at the 1925 hearing.** Nothing in r06 records Strawn being called, consulted, or
  asked for his books, and his 1925 extract mentions only a routine fall visit and the new tester. So the board
  sat for seven weeks at Ashlin on the question of why Ostrey Hollow milk tested low, while **the state's
  inspector for the county — who had condemned the station's entire crate of pipettes twenty-one months
  earlier, who had told the manager in 1919 there was no water in the cans, and whose dated book said both in
  plain words — was on his circuit and was never asked.** The board "had before it the books and appliances of
  the station" and did not look at the appliances that had been condemned, nor send for the man who condemned
  them. Both halves of the missing evidence — the crate in the loft and the inspector in the field — existed,
  were within seven miles and a day's drive, and were not produced.
- **Strawn's own explanation of the silence, in his closing note (1936):** "I have been asked once or twice,
  **by men who ought to know better**, to say in a general way how a certain plant stood in my estimation over
  the years, and I have always answered that **I do not keep an estimation, I keep a book, and the book is open
  to anybody the state sends for it.** I never had a theory about a plant and I do not recommend having one. A
  theory is a thing a man carries out to the platform with him and it gets in the way of the weights." His
  discipline is the reason his record is trustworthy — and, read against 1925, the reason it sat unused: he
  would give facts to anyone who sent for the book, and offer nothing to anyone who didn't. Also from the
  covering note: "The state pays a field man to weigh and to look, and **every time in my life I have seen a
  field man paid for an opinion instead, the opinion has outlived the weighing and done harm after the man was
  dead.**" (Which is exactly what happened to Teague's opinion and Keddie's name.)

## 8. ARITHMETIC FOR SECTION C

### C1(a) — the proportion, from the glassware
Pipettes were **blown to deliver 17.6 c.c.**; measured mean delivery **16.72 c.c.**; short **0.88 c.c.**
**16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly.** Every test made with that glass **read 95 per cent of the truth** — low by
**one part in twenty (5 %)** measured against the truth. Ivy's mechanism: a butterfat bottle's neck is ruled to
read percent **for one charge and no other**; a short charge gives a short reading **in the same proportion**.
The nine individual means: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 (five runs each).
Spread 16.70–16.74 — tight; no outlier to argue about.
**The error is proportional, not a fixed offset.** Ivy: "The station did not lose a set number of points. **It
lost a twentieth of whatever was there.**" Worked examples she gives: a herd **recorded at 4.56 was short
0.24** of a point (true 4.80); a herd **recorded at 3.42 was short 0.18** (true 3.60). "**Because the error was
a proportion, it fell hardest on the best milk.** The richer the milk, the more was taken from it, and no man
had any way of knowing that his good cows cost him more than his poor ones." — This is why Emmet Brant, with the
best and the most milk, lost most.
**Corroboration of the defect (not of the size)**: Strawn's 12 June 1923 entry, "**These measures will not
deliver their mark**," is a second, independent, contemporaneous finding that the glass under-delivered — from a
different burette, a different operator, and thirty-five years earlier. He did not quantify it ("I did not weigh
what it has cost them"); Ivy did.

### C1(b) — the true average test and the shortfall in points
Recorded average **3.61**. **3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80.** True average test **3.80 per cent**.
**Short by 0.19 percentage points** ("nineteen hundredths"). Note 0.19 is **5 % of 3.80** and **5.26 % (five and
a quarter per cent) of 3.61** — both figures circulate and both are right so long as you say what they are
percentages of.
Corroboration of 3.80, **six** strands, of which the last is outside the family entirely:
(i) the **1896 season on borrowed Larrow Green glass** averaged **3.80**;
(ii) the **nine weeks of 1911** on a borrowed measure stood at **3.80**;
(iii) **1924**, after replacement, averaged **3.80** (Keddie's letter);
(iv) Teague's 1898 ledger line: 60,800 lb actually received on 1,600,000 lb of milk = **3.80 %**;
(v) the **Alder Corners** overlap ran **~0.19 higher** than at home over fourteen seasons;
(vi) **Merle Strawn, 1924, from the station's book read on the spot: "Their book gives the year at 3.80."**
This is the independent confirmation of (iii): the same figure, from the same book, read and recorded by a state
officer who had no stake in it, thirty-four years before Ivy's bulletin. **Note for independence-counting:
(iii) and (vi) are the same underlying 1924 station average, so they are one fact with two independent
witnesses, not two facts.** Likewise (i) and (ii) both come from Keddie's book — but they are separate events
within it.

### C1(c) — pounds of butterfat not credited
Basis (r04, corroborated by r02): **twenty-five full delivery years, 1898 to 1922**; the station took in
**40,000,000 lb of milk**, an average of **1,600,000 lb a year**.
- Credited at 3.61 %: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**
- Truly delivered at 3.80 %: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lb**
- **Difference: 76,000 lb of butterfat.**
Cross-check per year: 1,600,000 × 0.0019 = 3,040 lb/yr × 25 = 76,000 lb. Matches Teague's 1898 "station gain,
3,040 lb" exactly.
**Caveats to state, because the question says "over the full delivery years affected":**
1. **Vaught's bulletin dates the defect from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923** — i.e. part of 1897 and part of 1923
   lie outside the 25 full years used for the 76,000 lb. Ivy calls it "**this valley's twenty-six-year
   anomaly**." The 76,000 lb therefore **understates** the whole; the 1897 and 1923 partial years are
   unquantified in any source. (1896 is not affected — borrowed Larrow Green glass, 3.80.) **r06 pins the
   1923 partial year at both ends — short glass condemned 12 June 1923, new glass "in use since July" —** so
   the 1923 affected stretch is roughly the season's start through mid-June, a substantial part of the flush,
   not a trivial remainder. Still unquantified in pounds by any source.
2. **The nine weeks of 1911 were tested with a sound borrowed measure**, so no shortfall accrued in them; Ivy's
   76,000 lb does not appear to deduct them. A pro-rata deduction would be ~526 lb, but 8 May–10 July is the
   flush, so the true deduction is larger than pro-rata. Neither correction is computed anywhere in the sources;
   the 76,000 lb figure is the one the bulletin and the ledgers both carry.

### C1(d) — what it was worth, and what the patrons got
Butterfat over those years averaged **25 cents a pound** (r02 §7, from Teague's annual sheets, columns added
twice; repeated by r04).
**76,000 lb × $0.25 = $19,000.**
The board awarded **$3,800**. **$3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20 — exactly one fifth (20 %).** Ivy: "That is a fifth of
it."
Vira's memory of "thirty-five hundred dollars, split up nine ways over all those years — my father's share of
it did not buy a hay rake" is the wrong figure but the right sense of scale.
Where the missing fat went: Vaught — "**stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as
station gain**." Teague carried it "to the general account of the Association where it belonged, since the
Association had received it."

### C2 — Emmet Brant's 1910
**The largest patron is Emmet Brant** — "the station's largest patron in every year of which we have a record"
(r02 §5), biggest man on the Bottom, most cows and hay ground (r01), "brought more milk to my door than any
other man on the Bottom" (r03).
**The pounds of milk Emmet Brant delivered in 1910 have STILL NOT appeared in any source read (r01–r06), and
nothing in Segments 4 or 5 supplied it either.** The record that would hold it is named but not quoted: **the
Brant weigh book, kept at Hessel Bottom from 1897 onward, lent to Ivy by Roy Brant.** **r06 supplies no
poundages for any patron in any year — Strawn records scales, lactometer, boiler, cold room and tests, never
volumes.**
**Next reader: watch for Brant's 1910 weigh-book figure, and for a 1910-specific butterfat price.**
Method, ready to apply once the poundage M is known:
- (a) credited fat 1910 = **M × 0.0361**
- (b) owed and not credited = **M × 0.0019** (equivalently, credited ÷ 19, since 0.0019/0.0361 = 1/19; or true
  fat × 0.05)
- (c) value = (b) × the butterfat price. **$0.25/lb is the 25-year average**, not necessarily the 1910 price;
  use a 1910 figure if one appears, and say which you used.
Sanity anchor only: the whole station averaged 1,600,000 lb/yr across nine patrons; Brant was the largest, so
roughly 300,000–500,000 lb would be plausible — **do not use that guess as an answer.** Note also that 1910
sits inside the affected period and outside the 1911 sound-glass window, so the full 5 % applies for the whole
year.
