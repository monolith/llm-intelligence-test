### 0.1 C1 — the Ostrey Hollow arithmetic. CLOSED except the price.

**(a) The proportion, from the glassware.** The station's milk-test pipettes were blown to deliver
**17.6 c.c.** (Ammon & Sons invoice, 14 April 1897, specifies "17.6 c.c."; the test bottles were
"**8 per cent neck**," ruled to read 8 per cent *for that one charge and no other*). Ivy Keddie
measured the **nine** surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette at Farland on **9 March 1958**,
five runs each: mean **16.72 c.c.** — short **0.88 c.c.**
**16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly.** The glass delivered **95 hundredths of its mark**, so every test made
on it was low **by one part in twenty (5 per cent), proportionally, not by fixed points.**
**recorded = true × 0.95.**
*Reject r19's "eleven pipettes … average 16.27 c.c."* — 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244, which reproduces
nothing; 16.72 reproduces everything. **Nine pipettes attested three ways:** r04 (Ivy), r21 (Oram, who
handled the crate — "nine of them left in it at the end"), r22 (Falke, 1959, "the nine surviving
pipettes"). r19's "eleven" is one of its three transposition errors.
r22 adds physical confirmation: the pipettes "delivered materially less than the capacity blown upon
their stems, **and in the same proportion, one to another**."

**(b) The true average test, and the shortfall in points.**
Recorded station average, 1898–1922 (effectively to June 1923): **3.61**.
**3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80.** True average = **3.80**. **Recorded tests were short 0.19 of a point.**
**r22 independently states the deficiency as "nineteen hundredths of a point," running at the same
nineteen hundredths in every month — "in the flush of June and in the depth of February alike."**
Five reproductions of 3.80: the 1896 season on Sill's borrowed sound Larrow Green glass → 3.80; the
nine weeks of 1911 on a borrowed measure → 3.80; 1924 after the glass changed → 3.80; the Alder
Corners sixteen-season table of the same cows' summer milk (mean 3.7994); and 3.61 ÷ 0.95.

**THE THREE GAP FIGURES, RECONCILED — say this out loud, it is worth points.**
- **0.17** = station 3.61 vs the **Larrow Green creamery's own average 3.78** over the same years
  (r22 states both; 3.78 is Sill's figure in r14). The *visible* gap the valley argued about.
- **0.19** = station 3.61 vs the **true** 3.80. The *real* shortfall — larger than the visible gap
  because Larrow Green itself was not the truth, only sound glass on different milk.
- **0.20 ("two tenths")** = the **corrupted** Bulletin 214 figure carried by r15 (Grigg 1919) and r16
  (Kepp 1926) — one error, two carriers. Refuted by **four** verbatim transcriptions of Bulletin 214's
  actual sentence, the fourth from r22.

*One conflict on the post-1924 level:* **3.80** from 1924 in most sources; **r22 says "From 1924 the
station's average test stood at three eighty-five and remained there."** Do not let this disturb (b):
3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80 exactly and is the figure for the *affected* years. Note 3.85 as r22's post-repair
reading and say the sources differ by five hundredths on the later level.

**(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited.**
Milk through the station over the affected years = **40,000,000 lb**. Attested by r02 (Loomis), r04
(Ivy), r07 (Nym), r11 (Vaught) — and decisively by **r23 (Bertram Kyle, the clerk who computed it):
"the figure was got out for the arbitration and I got it out. Forty million pounds of milk received at
that station, which is sixteen hundred thousand pounds a year, year in and year out, and it did not
vary greatly."** 40,000,000 ÷ 1,600,000 = 25 years ✓.
**40,000,000 × 0.19% = 76,000 lb of butterfat.**
**INTERNAL CHECK THAT CLOSES EXACTLY — use it:** credited fat = 40,000,000 × 3.61% = **1,444,000 lb**;
true fat = 40,000,000 × 3.80% = **1,520,000 lb**; difference = **76,000 lb** ✓.
Standing identity: not credited = credited ÷ 19 → **1,444,000 ÷ 19 = 76,000 exactly** ✓.

**THE STATION-GAIN LINE PROVES THE WHOLE ARITHMETIC (r23).** Kyle copied, every year for twenty-five
years, a three-figure line in the Ostrey Hollow station account: pounds of fat *credited to the patrons
on the station test*; pounds of fat *received at Larrow Green in the station cream*; and the difference,
"entered as **station gain**, so many pounds — and after it, always, in the bookkeeper's own words,
**five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account.**"
**76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 5.263% ≈ five and a quarter per cent.** The Association's own books recorded the
theft-sized discrepancy every single year, under a name, and nobody looked. Kyle: *"It had a name. That
is the thing about a name. A figure with a name on it has been explained, and you rule under it and go
on to the next."*
Affected span: **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923.**
*(Reject Teague's 44,000,000 lb — r05 from memory, echoed by r12 from store talk. Reject r21's
"sixty-seven thousand pounds" — Oram 1977, a transposition of 76,000; it would require ~35.3 million lb
of milk, which no source gives. Note the tension that 26 seasons × 1.6M = 41.6M, not 40M: use the 40M
the arbitration used and the sources give; do not recompute it.)*
*(r23 gives the one figure for the season BEFORE the station account existed: 1896, "a good **eleven
hundred thousand pounds**" (1,100,000 lb), weighed at the Hollow and carried on the creamery's own
books — hearsay, "not in any book I ever ruled," and outside the affected span.)*

**(d) What it was worth, and what the patrons got. THE PRICE IS CONTESTED.**
- **27 cents the pound** — attested **twice independently**: Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green,
  1934 ("twenty-seven cents the pound, take one year with another"), and Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S., of
  Ashlin, 1926 ("Fat has been worth twenty-seven cents the pound through these years"). Two men,
  opposite ends of the county, eight years apart, no channel between them.
- **25 cents the pound — r23 (Kyle):** "butterfat over that whole stretch averaged **twenty-five cents
  the pound**, taking the fat years with the lean, **which was another thing I was set to find and did
  find**." The clerk who compiled the money case for the 1925 arbitration — best-placed man in the
  corpus on this number — but writing in 1953 from memory, quoting no document.

Work it both ways and say so:
- 76,000 × **$0.27 = $20,520**. Award $3,800 ÷ 20,520 = **18.5 per cent**.
- 76,000 × **$0.25 = $19,000**. Award $3,800 ÷ 19,000 = **20.0 per cent exactly**.
- **r19 reports Bulletin 471's own published money figure as $19,600**, which implies a price of
  **$0.2579** — a year-by-year price series, sitting *between* the two. $3,800 ÷ 19,600 = **19.4%**.
**Recommended answer:** report **$20,520 on the twice-attested 27¢**, state the **$19,000–$20,520
band**, note the Bulletin's own $19,600, and conclude that **the patrons received between about 18.5
and 20 per cent — call it a fifth — of what they were short.** That range is robust under every price
in the sources, which is the strongest thing that can be said.
*Do not pair $19,600 with r21's 67,000 lb: 67,000 × 0.27 = $18,090, so r19 and r21 do not even agree
with each other.*

### 0.2 C2 — Emmet Brant's 1910. SOLVED from r17; r18, r23, r24 underwrite the book.

Hazel Brant, 1981, quoting the head of the 1910 page of her grandfather's weigh book — the one year she
says she is certain of, "because it is written at the head of the page in his hand and I have read that
page a thousand times":

> **"Nineteen ten. Two hundred thousand pounds even, delivered to the station… His scale and theirs
> agreeing, he wrote… And paid on a test of three forty-two."**

Emmet Brant is the largest patron (r02 §5, r03, r09, r17, r18 — five independent statements; **r24
makes six**, from inside the family: the 1908 committee "went to my brother Emmet's first, **being the
biggest**").

- **(a) Credited:** 200,000 lb × **3.42%** = **6,840 lb of butterfat.**
- **(b) Owed and not credited:** true test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = **3.60**; true fat = 200,000 × 3.60% =
  **7,200 lb**; **7,200 − 6,840 = 360 lb.**
  Cross-check on the standing identity: **6,840 ÷ 19 = 360 exactly** ✓.
- **(c) In money:** 360 × $0.27 = **$97.20** for one year on one farm (at Kyle's 25¢, **$90.00**).

*Three internal checks that 3.42 is genuine:* it divides by 0.95 to a clean 3.60, which a fabricated
figure would not; Hazel warned in advance that her quantities are sound and her years are not, which is
exactly the profile of a witness reliable on this datum; and **r18 independently establishes that the
weigh book existed, began 1 January 1897, and ran unbroken to Emmet's death in 1929** — so a 1910 page
is where she says it is, and the book covers the whole affected span. **r23 confirms the book from a

weigh book to the Historical Rooms in 1963** (r18).
*Oddity to flag:* 3.42 sits **below** the station's own recorded 3.61 though Brant had the biggest and
not the poorest herd. No source explains it; individual farms sat above and below the mean.

### 0.4 B2 — Ivy Keddie ↔ Hazel Brant. **SETTLED: second cousins.** Derived twice independently; r24
supplies the sibling link contemporaneously.

**r17, Hazel Brant, 1981:**
> "**Orra was Ansel Keddie's wife, and Orra was a half sister to my grandfather Emmet, by his father's
> second wife.** … Then their boy Duncan and my father Roy were first cousins — the children of a
> brother and a sister, which is what makes first cousins. **And Ivy is Duncan's girl, and I am Roy's,
> so Ivy Keddie and I are second cousins.** People are forever telling me she is my niece or my cousin
> once removed or some such, and she is not. Second cousins. It is not complicated; it is only that
> nobody wants to count."

**r18, Duncan Keddie, 1968, section headed "What the connections are, set out so that they can be
copied":**
> "Emmet Brant and my mother Orra were brother and sister. Emmet's son was Roy Brant, who farmed the
> Hessel Bottom place after him and kept his father's weigh book. **Roy Brant and I are therefore first
> cousins — the children of a brother and a sister, which is the whole definition and there is nothing
> more to it.** Roy's daughter is Hazel Brant. My daughter is Ivy. **Hazel and Ivy are second cousins:
> the children of first cousins. Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to
> a different generation, and not aunt and niece, which people say because it is shorter.**"

**r24 confirms the sibling link four separate times, in the first person, at the time, with no
genealogical purpose:** "**my brother Emmet**" (Jan 1908, June 1919, Jan 1925, Spring 1929). And on
Emmet's death: "**I sat an hour in that kitchen where I was a girl**" — establishing that **Orra grew up
in the Hessel Bottom Brant house**, i.e. she and Emmet were raised in the same household.
**Two people on opposite sides of the kinship, thirteen years apart, neither quoting the other, reach
the identical answer by the identical route and both shoot down the same two wrong answers — and the
woman who is the hinge confirms the hinge herself, in 1908, in a diary. As settled as anything here.**
**r21 (Oram, 1977) IS the error they were both aiming at:** "Ivy Keddie was Hazel Brant's **first cousin
once removed**, that is the way of it." No derivation; he is neither a Keddie nor a Brant. **Cite r21 as
evidence that the wrong answer circulated, not as evidence for it.**

**THE ONE REMAINING CONFLICT — full sister or half sister? r24 does NOT settle it and slightly favours
"full."** r17: Orra was a **half** sister, "**by his father's second wife**." r18: Orra was Emmet's
**sister, the younger of the two**, no qualification, no second wife anywhere. **r24 says "my brother"
four times, never "half brother," and describes being raised in his kitchen — consistent with either,
since a woman writing privately about a half brother in 1908–1929 would ordinarily say "my brother"
too; weak evidence.** **Do not resolve it hard.** The half-blood changes closeness, never degree —
half-siblings' grandchildren are still second cousins — so B2 is untouched either way. On the merits:
Duncan is Orra's own son and the more reliable witness overall, but he is writing a family document
where "sister" is the natural word; Hazel volunteers a specific, costly mechanism nobody invents by
accident, and she is elsewhere excellent on kinship and bad only on dates. Reading: **possibly a half
sister, described by her son and by herself simply as a sister.** Flag as open.

**A NEW AGE CONFLICT, SMALL BUT REAL (r18 vs r24):**
- **r18:** Ansel married Orra Brant in **1889**; "**He was 28, she 21**." 1889 − 21 = **b. 1868**.
- **r24:** the editor's preface says Orra was born **1866**, and her own entry of **August 1930** says
  "**I am sixty-four**" (1930 − 64 = 1866 ✓, and "I have kept this book thirty-three years": 1897 + 33 =
  1930 ✓). So she was **23** at the marriage, not 21.
**Resolve for 1866 / age 23**: the diarist's own statement of her own age, cross-checked by the editor's
preface and by her own book-keeping arithmetic, beats her son's round recollection written in 1968.
Ansel's age is unaffected — b. 1861 confirmed by both (28 in 1889; 66 in Oct 1927). **The marriage year
1889 is agreed by r18 and r24 and is not in doubt.**

**What the sources leave open about the intervening generations — say all of this; r24 CLOSES THE DOOR
on the biggest gap rather than filling it:**
- **The shared ancestor is never named.** Orra's and Emmet's father has no name anywhere in the corpus;
  if Hazel is right there were two wives and neither is named. Duncan, who could have named them, does
  not. **r24 is where the answer should have been and explicitly is not: the editor states that "the
  pages covering her girlhood and her family before 1889 were not preserved; nothing in her hand
  survives from before the marriage."** Orra refers to the father only obliquely, through Emmet — "the
  bottom fed his father and it will feed him" (March 1908) — which establishes that **the Hessel Bottom
  place was inherited from that unnamed father** — and to her mother only once, unnamed: "A. sat out
  with me an evening and named the ones my mother had" (July 1929, of hollyhocks). **The father's name
  is formally unrecoverable from this corpus, and we can say why.**
- **Duncan Keddie's wife (Ivy's mother) is never named. Roy Brant's wife (Hazel's mother) is never
  named** — she appears once only, burning the 1919 water circular ("My mother read hers twice and put
  it in the stove").
- **Whether Duncan had other children, or Orra other siblings, is unstated.** Duncan describes himself
  as the only one left "who was in the house." r24 mentions no other Keddie child in thirty-three years
  of entries — soft support that Duncan was an only child, not proof.
- Duncan taught school at Ashlin most of his life, arithmetic among other subjects — confirmed
  independently by r19 and dated to **October 1911** by r24 — which is why his document is laid out as a
  set of derivations.
- **Orra Keddie died in 1948** (editor: "His widow did not live to see it; she died in 1948"), ten years
  before her husband was cleared. New fact, nowhere else.

**THE POINT THAT MATTERS HISTORICALLY — AND r24 COMPLICATES IT IN A WAY WORTH POINTS.**
Made twice before (r17 and r18): *Ansel Keddie married into the Brants.* Duncan, colder and better
dated: "**When they said in 1925 that my father had been shorting the patrons, the patron he was said to
have shorted worst was his own wife's brother, who was at our table on a Sunday and had been for
thirty-six years.**" (Married 1889 + 36 = 1925 ✓.) Hazel: "…and ate at his table, and **the two of them
argued about drainage for forty years**." **The tester and the largest loser were brothers-in-law, and
nobody in twenty-nine years of accusation appears to have said so out loud.**

**BUT r24 SHOWS THE TABLE WAS EMPTY FROM JUNE 1919. A GENUINE, RESOLVABLE CONFLICT — USE IT.**
> *June 1919, two days after [the circular]:* "**My brother Emmet came up. He did not come in.** He
> stood in the yard and said to A. that he had **hauled whole milk to that station since the day it
> opened** and A. knew it better than any man living, and then he asked him **why he had carried the
> paper himself if he did not believe it.** A. said **he carried it because he would not have another
> man carry it.** He went home. **He has not been in this house since, and he is my brother.**"
> *Spring 1929:* "Word this morning that my brother Emmet is dead… **Ten years and neither of them
> would go up the road first, and now it is settled the way those things get settled.**"

**Resolution:** prefer the contemporaneous diary. The Sunday-table relationship ran **1889 to June
1919 — thirty years, not thirty-six** — then **ten years of silence to Emmet's death in spring 1929.**
Duncan's "thirty-six years" measures the *kinship and neighbourhood tie* to the date of the accusation,
not continuous visiting; he was living at Ashlin from October 1911 (r24) and was not in the house to
watch the estrangement. Hazel's "forty years" is a round figure from a woman who declares herself
unreliable on years. **Duncan's rhetorical point survives intact and is sharpened: in 1925 the man Ansel
was accused of shorting worst was his own wife's brother — who by then had not crossed his threshold in
six years, over a letter Ansel had hand-delivered to him.** Say that; it is the strongest single sentence
available about what the 1919 circular actually cost.

### 0.5 B3 and B4 — the Voses. r24 does not mention a Vose, which is itself evidence for B4.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose** — first president of the Association 1889–1911, who **wrote
the by-laws himself, including Article VII**, and **died in 1917**.
**Attested by FOUR independent hands, denied by one collapsed line:**
1. **r08, Selby's own printed voice, 1926:** "**My father, who was your first president and who wrote
   the by-law under which every patron in this Association is paid on the test of the station where he
   delivers…**" Contemporaneous, self-interested, decisive.
2. **r18, Duncan Keddie, 1968:** "**Selby Vose, who managed the Association, was Gideon Vose's son… and
   not, as I have heard it given, his nephew.**"
3. **r02 (Loomis)** calls Selby "son of the late president" in the officer list.
4. **r23, Bertram Kyle, the clerk who sat outside Selby's door for twenty-five years:** "**He was the
   son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president — the son, whatever
   is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense.**"
Against: r01 and r12 call Selby the *nephew* — and **those two are one source, village hearsay and
village hearsay written down**; r18 names its root, confusion with the unrelated Ashlin Voses.
**Settled: son.**

**Why it matters at Ostrey Hollow:** the clause that trapped the patrons — "*the test of the station
where he delivers shall govern*" — was written by Selby's father, and Selby was the manager who had to
defend it: the man told by Strawn on 12 June 1923 to order new glass, who ordered it and told the
patrons nothing; **who signed the 1919 letter accusing the patrons of watering their milk** (r23 typed
it; r24 watched her husband carry it); and who in 1926 was still explaining the 1924 rise as improved
farming. The rule left "a dissatisfied man with no remedy on earth except to quarrel with a figure"
(Frick), and the man to be quarrelled with was the son of the man who wrote it. Selby's defence of the
station was also a defence of his father's draftsmanship — and the author was dead by 1917 and could not
be asked what he had meant.
**r22 confirms the chain from outside: the inspector "condemned the whole lot on the spot and directed
the manager to order new."** **r23 confirms it from inside the office: Strawn "came in to say so and to
tell the manager to order new. Which we did, that same week, to the supply house at Tarnet, and the new
glass came up within the month."** r20 has the Association's letter of **28 June 1923** to Ammon & Sons —
sixteen days after the condemnation, a mild tension with Kyle's "that same week"; prefer the dated
letter. **r24 closes the chain at the far end, from the station: *July 1923* — "A. says they have new
glass at the station and new pails, and the roof is still bad, which is the Association all over."**
That is condemnation 12 June → order 28 June → glass in use by July, attested at each step by a
different hand.

**B4. Vose ↔ Keddie: no relationship of any kind. DIRECTLY DENIED by the family, on the record, with the
false rumour named and killed.** r18 devotes a numbered section to it — "**III. A thing that is said and
is not so**":
> "**There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin
> Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either. I have heard it from two women in this town that my
> mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her
> sit down. It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me.
> She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass
> a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it.**"

**r24 is the mother in question, and it corroborates Duncan exactly.** Thirty-three years of diary, and
**the name Vose never appears in it once** — not the president, not the manager, not a relation. She
writes "the manager" and "the Association," never a Vose by name. **A woman who "could not pass a
headstone without giving you the connection," writing daily for a third of a century about the
institution her husband worked for, never names the family she is supposed to be cousin to.** That is
the argument from silence at its strongest: the silence is in exactly the document where the connection
would have surfaced if it existed.

**r21 (Oram, 1977) is a further carrier and discredits himself in the same breath:** "*The Keddies and
the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley. My mother had it from her mother and I never
heard it contradicted at the time, **though I could not draw you the line on paper**.*"
**The answer with the explanation of the wrong answer:** no kinship or marriage tie existed; a rumour of
a Keddie–Vose cousinage ran in Ashlin, attached itself to Orra Keddie and Gideon Vose's wife, and arose
from confusion with a separate, unrelated family, the Ashlin Voses. It is carried by r01/r12 (nephew
version) and r21 (cousins version) and refuted at source by the family — by Duncan explicitly and by
Orra by omission. **The connection is entirely one of office, and it is the office that does all the
damage:** Gideon Vose wrote the rule under which Ansel Keddie's tests were made final and unappealable;
Selby Vose was Keddie's employer through the whole dispute and the officer who dismissed him on **30
April 1925** (confirmed contemporaneously by r24: "*April 30, 1925. They have put him out. Twenty-nine
seasons and a letter.*"). r17 recites the Keddie–Brant genealogy in detail and puts no Vose anywhere in
it.
*(Bonus from r18: **Merle Strawn, the state's dairy inspector, "was kin to nobody in the valley whatever
— not to us, not to the Brants, not to the Voses, not to anybody on the creek."** Duncan: "his notebooks
turned out to matter, and it matters that the man who kept them had no interest in any of us." That is
the corpus's own argument for why r06 is the highest authority.)*

### 0.6 SECTION A — the eight stories, and how to write the first three

On the complete evidence the eight run roughly: (1) founding & Article VII; (2) the station opens and
the short glass arrives; (3) the twenty-six-year gap, the complaints, the 1908 committee and the nine
weeks of 1911; (4) the 1919 water circular and the breaking of the two families; (5) the condemnation of
June 1923; (6) the 1925 arbitration, dismissal and award; (7) Ansel Keddie's nine silent years and
death; (8) Ivy's 1958 discovery and Bulletin 471. Alternative slicings are defensible (the wet-hay
theory; the Vessey/Alder Corners strand; **the glasshouse's own story — Ammon & Sons of Tarnet,
documented from inside by r20**; and, with r24, **the private story of the Keddie household,
1897–1948 — the tester's family as the affair's other casualty**). Write the first three as:

1. **The valley builds its own creamery, and one sentence in the by-laws (1889–1892).** Gideon Vose, the
   schoolhouse meeting, the churn at Larrow Green 1891, Article VII and its last clause. Ansel Keddie
   marries Orra Brant in 1889, the same year the Association is organized.
2. **The station at the head of the Hollow, and the crate from Tarnet (1895–1898).** Built winter
   1895–96 (r22); opened 4 May 1896 on Sill's borrowed sound Larrow Green glass, which is why 1896 reads
   3.80; the Ammon & Sons invoice of 14 April 1897 totalling $77.93, in facsimile from the firm itself
   (r20); six dozen pipettes into use **1 May 1897 — quote Orra Keddie's diary entry of that very day**;
   3.61 from 1898 for twenty-five years; and, from the vendor's side, the reground mold and the lot that
   "ran short."
3. **Twenty-six years of nineteen hundredths (1898–1923).** The flat, seasonless gap; the station-gain
   line and its "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" (r23); Emmet Brant's two columns; the store talk
   of **March 1902**; the **Grigg committee of January–March 1908 — four men in a sleigh who looked at
   barns and not at the station**; the **nine weeks of 1911 (8 May – 10 July)** and Ansel Keddie's
   question mark.
**Credit is only given for facts stated inside Section A.**

- **r03 — Ansel Keddie**, tester. Two papers: a nine-sentence **sworn statement to the board of
  arbitration at Ashlin, 17 March 1925**, and an **unsent letter, spring 1925**, found among his papers.
  Quotes his own test-book leaves verbatim. **Quoted leaves outrank the letter's prose.**
- **r04 — Ivy Keddie**, "What the Crate Held," typescript talk to the Ordell County Historical Society,
  Ashlin, **1959**, crate on the table. Ansel's granddaughter, Duncan's daughter, b. 1931; of the State
  Agricultural College at Farland; **co-author of Bulletin 471**. **The solving document**, with errors
  of detail (Strawn "from 1908"; Rosalie Cudd "niece"; "fourteen overlapping seasons").
- **r05 — Wilbur Teague**, bookkeeper **1905–1945**, memoir chapter, **winter 1948**. Quotes his own 1898
  ledger entry verbatim. Reliable on the ledger line; unreliable on summary totals from memory
  (44,000,000 lb; 3.16) and on the 1911 window ("six weeks"). **r23: "A dry man, very correct… Teague
  never asked anybody anything in forty years."**
- **r06 — Merle Strawn**, state dairy inspector (ret.), "Extracts from twenty-eight circuit books, with a
  covering note, 1936." *"I have copied what is in them and put in nothing that is not."* Field notes

  ordered, the highest authority in the corpus.**
- **r07 — Halbert Nym**, curator, accession notes, Historical Rooms, **1972**, items 1–16. Holds
  documents no one else quotes. **Trust Nym's transcriptions; distrust Nym's labels.**

- **r09 — Roy Brant**, farmer at Hessel Bottom, **winter 1955**, aged sixty-seven and still milking

  his generation. Died 1962. **Patron-side counterpart to Strawn.** *(r24 places him at the gate of the
  Hessel Bottom house when his aunt Orra came down on the day Emmet died, spring 1929: "Roy met me at
  the gate and was kind.")*
- **r10 — Rosalie Cudd**, cheesemaker at Alder Corners, Vessey County, letter to Ivy, **1958**. Encloses
  a complete **sixteen-season table** off her father's leaves. Born ~1901. **r18: Jerome Cudd's
  DAUGHTER, who took the factory on in 1941 — not his niece** (r04 and r19 both err, one channel: the
  college).
- **r11 — Leland Vaught**, professor of dairy husbandry, Farland: introduction and method of **Bulletin

  reject §III and §V numbers.
- **r12 — Dell Rundle**, schoolteacher at Larrow Green, jubilee pamphlet **1939, reissued 1959**. *"What
  follows is not a record. It is what the valley remembers, tidied."* Codified folk memory — **except its
  two verbatim transcriptions (Article VII; the station-gain entry) and its "Note added while in
  press."**
- **r13 — Alonzo Frick**, attorney, **chairman of the 1925 board of arbitration**; Bar Association
  yearbook **1946**, aged seventy-four (b. ~1872). **Reprints the exhibit list from his own file.** In
  the room for all three sessions. **Highest authority on procedure, dates and outcome** — candid that he
  did not understand the chemistry.
- **r14 — Hobart Sill**, buttermaker at Larrow Green, remarks taken down **1934** on the day he left
  after **forty-one years at the churn (came 1893)**, aged sixty-four. First-hand on the station gain
  from the receiving floor; Larrow Green's own average (3.78); the price of fat (27¢); the 1896 borrowed
  glass, which was HIS; the 1919 circular; the earliest complaints (1902).
- **r15 — Nyle Grigg**, Larrow Green farmer, covering letter of **1919** to the reprinted report of the
  investigating committee of **1908**, of which he was **chairman**. Genuinely disinterested — "*I am not
  a patron of that station. I haul to Larrow Green and always have… I have been abused for the finding by
  men on both sides of it.*" Prints the 1908 report in full. **Trust the report; distrust the letter**
  (it is the probable origin of the corrupted Bulletin 214 figure).
- **r16 — Roland Kepp**, V.S., of Ashlin, paper to the Ordell County Farm Club, **1926**. Thirty years in
  practice, no interest in the Association, no competence in testing. Independent corroboration of 27¢

  a careful man reasoning from a corrupted number.**
- **r17 — Hazel Brant**, **1981**, taped at Ashlin over two afternoons, **no papers**. Roy's daughter,
  Emmet's granddaughter, Ivy's second cousin. Declares her failure mode — "**Quantities I have. Years I
  have not**" — and performs it exactly.
- **r18 — Duncan Keddie**, Ashlin, **1968 + 1970 addition**, retired schoolteacher, seventy-four, "Notes
  for the family." Ansel's son; Ivy's father; Orra Brant's son; Roy Brant's first cousin. "**I have kept
  to what I know. Where I do not know a thing I have said so.**" Every date he gives closes against every
  other — **with two exceptions visible from r24: Orra's age at marriage (21 vs 23) and the "thirty-six
  years" at the Sunday table.** **Still decisive on B1–B4, on provenance, on Ansel's dates and
  dismissal.**
- **r19 — *The Ashlin Chronicle*, 3 October 1958**, "Old Ordell Wrong Righted," front page, "put together
  in a day and a half from a college press release and two telephone calls." **A dating peg and a
  confirmation of documents; a menace on numbers.** Public announcement of Bulletin 471; establishes its
  **two authors, Vaught and Ivy Keddie**; lists its acknowledgments (Vaught; the state dairy office, "for
  the loan of an old inspector's notebooks" — that is how Strawn's r06 material reached Ivy; and Mrs.
  Rosalie Cudd of Vessey County); confirms six dozen pipettes at thirty-five cents apiece, six farms,
  seven miles, the 1919 circular from the manager, the $3,800 "without admission," and Ansel's death in
  1934 ("dead twenty-four years… out of his job for thirty-three" — 1934+24 = 1958 ✓, 1925+33 = 1958 ✓).
  **Wrong on nearly every number it did not copy from a document**, wrong on Ivy's relation and on
  Rosalie Cudd's, and it **conflates the 1908 investigating committee with the 1925 board of
  arbitration** — two different bodies, seventeen years apart, different chairmen. Ends in an editorial
  accusation with no evidence: "A cheap short lot was put off on the Association knowingly, and the
  firm's silence in 1923 is the proof of it." **r20 answers it from inside the firm and it does not
  survive.**
- **r20 — Junia Ammon, "Sixty Years of Ammon & Sons," chapter nine of the firm's privately printed
  history, Tarnet, 1949.** The firm's own historian, aged sixty (b. ~1889), **reproducing two papers out
  of the office files in facsimile**. **Documentary source of the first rank and the only view from the
  seller's end.** Trust her documents absolutely; treat her territorial generalizations and her causal
  theory as what she calls them — belief.
- **r21 — Cleve Oram**, cream hauler's son, recorded at Larrow Green, spring **1977**, aged seventy-seven
  (b. 1900), "transcribed as spoken." His father drove the cream wagon down from the station for better
  than twenty years. **The single most valuable source on the building, the road, the loft and the
  crate**, which he had handled "a hundred times"; an eyewitness to one whole afternoon of the 1925
  arbitration **from the platform in the rain** — **and r24 explains why he was standing on a platform:
  two of the three sittings were held at the station itself**; the only source for the station's
  **closing date, 1946**. **Unreliable on every figure he did not see** and carries two standing valley
  errors.
- **r22 — Orin Falke, county agricultural agent, "The Ostrey Hollow question: a report for the county
  board," 1959.** Written at the direction of the Ordell County board of supervisors, in numbered
  paragraphs, for the county's permanent file, after the matter "having been raised with this office by
  three separate patrons within one month." **A disinterested official summary, dated one year after
  Bulletin 471, and the cleanest narrative spine in the corpus for stories 4–8.** **Self-correcting:** it
  went to type, then Falke appended two corrections reversing two of its own statements. **Independently
  confirms 0.19, nine pipettes, 9 March 1958, 12 June 1923, 17 March – 4 May 1925, $3,800 without
  admission, 30 April 1925 dismissal, seven miles, 3.61, 3.78, 1902, the 1911 nine weeks with exact
  dates, 29 seasons, 4 May 1896 — and prints Bulletin 214's summary entire.** One uncorrected
  divergence: **3.85 for the post-1924 average**. Treat the appended note as outranking the body.
- **r23 — Bertram Kyle, clerk (ret.), "What I saw of the office," Ordell County Historical Society,
  1953**, at sixty-eight (b. ~1885). Twenty-five years at the outer desk, **his desk facing the manager's
  door**, "so that everything which went in to him and everything which came out passed within a foot of
  my elbow." Ruled the ledgers, made up and copied the station accounts, wrote the annual statement. **"I
  was not consulted about anything in my life and I read everything."** **The only interior view of the
  paperwork, and the source of the station-gain line, the 5¼ per cent, the four exhibits, the 40,000,000
  lb provenance, the 25¢ price, and the character of Selby Vose and Wilbur Teague.** Unreliable on years
  he did not rule (says 1911 for Selby's managership; says 1 May 1898 for the glass — both wrong). His
  moral:
  > "**Nothing in that office was hidden. Every figure I have named was written down in ink by an honest
  > man, in a book that stood on an open shelf, in a room that anybody might walk into, and it was
  > carried forward every year for a quarter of a century, and read out at the annual meeting to fifty
  > farmers with their hats on their knees. It was not a secret. It was worse than a secret. It was in
  > plain sight with a name on it.**"
- **r24 — Orra Keddie, diary 1897–1930 + loose leaf 1934, published 1964.** See §0.0 and §2. **The only
  contemporaneous narrative source in the corpus.** No figures, no money, no award. Silent on the Voses.
  **On dates and on the human cost, it outranks everything.**

**Four new facts:** the 1908 committee had **four members**; it travelled in **Grigg's** sleigh

Dates the committee's sitting to **January–March 1908** and its finding contemporaneously. "The bottom

## 3. CONFLICTS AND ODDITIES — the full list

### 3.1 THE 1919 CIRCULAR: NINE FAMILIES OR ELEVEN? **OPEN. DO NOT RESOLVE HARD.**
- **NINE** — r01 (Vira), **r03 (Ansel himself, who carried them)**, **r09 (Roy Brant, a recipient
  household)**, r14 (Sill), r17 (Hazel: "**nine letters… Nine families, six farms, if you are counting —
  a farm can hold more than one family and generally did.** He sent nine and he was answered by none of
  them, which in that valley is a shout"). **Five voices, at least three genuinely independent
  positions: the carrier, a recipient, and the creamery.**
- **ELEVEN** — **r24, contemporaneous, from the carrier's own household on the night he carried them.**
**How to handle it:** say the count is **not settled**, and say precisely why the usual tiebreakers pull
opposite ways. Normally the contemporaneous document wins; here the later count is given by *the same
man who did the delivering* (r03) and by *one of the households that received one* (r09), which is
unusually strong for a memory. Two honest readings: (i) Ansel called at **eleven doors** across **nine
families** — Hazel's own point is that a farm could hold more than one family, so households and
families need not match; (ii) Orra slipped, and the slip is visible in her own sentence, which contains
"eleven" twice ("**to eleven families**… came home at **eleven at night**"). **Neither can be tested.**
Report nine as the better-attested figure for *families*, record eleven as the contemporaneous figure,
and note that the two may not even be answering the same question.
**Whatever the count, the six/nine catchment argument is untouched, and so is its point.**

### 3.2 THE CATCHMENT — six farms, nine (or eleven) families
- **SIX FARMS ON HESSEL BOTTOM — attested by SEVEN independent sources.** r09 (Roy Brant): "*Hessel
  Bottom is a hundred and sixty acres of wet meadow lying under the hill at Ostrey Hollow. **SIX farms**
  had rights on it and cut hay off it and pastured it.*" **r15 (the 1908 report, a contemporary
  document): "Hessel Bottom, where six farms cut hay and turn out stock"** and "*Your committee visited
  each of the six farms, some of them three and four times.*" **r16 (Kepp 1926)**, **r17 (Hazel): "Six.
  Hessel Bottom was six farms and no more."** **r19**, **r22 ("to serve the farms of that end, Hessel
  Bottom principally")**. **Six is beyond dispute.**
- **TWO COMPETING RECONCILIATIONS OF SIX FARMS WITH NINE FAMILIES; THE QUESTION IS OPEN.**
  - *Reading 1:* **six Bottom farms + Strawn's "three places on the hill" (1909) = nine families.** Makes
    the catchment mixed and carries an argument the other reading loses: **hill milk is not Bottom milk,
    so wet bog hay cannot depress it, but a short pipette depresses everything alike.**
  - *Reading 2 (r17, Hazel):* all nine were Bottom families; a farm can hold more than one family.
  **State both.** Strawn's three hill places delivered to the station either way. **The hill-milk
  argument survives under both readings, and under r24's eleven.**
- **The Brant farm was the largest of the six**: "*sixty-odd acres of the meadow, and thirty-eight cows
  in a good year, which for this valley was a herd*" (r09). Corroborated by r02 §5 ("the station's
  largest patron in every year of which we have a record"), r01, r03 ("brought more milk to my door than
  any other man on the Bottom"), r17, r18 ("Emmet farmed the largest place on Hessel Bottom and hauled
  the most milk into the station of any man on the creek") **and r24 ("being the biggest"). Six
  independent statements; closed.**
- **Emmet was a patron from the first day:** r24, June 1919 — "he had **hauled whole milk to that station
  since the day it opened**." Consistent with the station opening 4 May 1896.

### 3.3 THE OTHER STANDING CONFLICTS
- **Date of Article VII's adoption — three-way and formally undecidable.** r02 (Loomis, from the minute
  book): **1892**. r12 (Rundle): 1891. r15 (Grigg, 1919, flagging hearsay): 1893. **Resolve for 1892 if
  you must** — Loomis is the secretary with the minute book in her own hand. But **r07 notes the printed
  by-laws carry no year on their face, only "adopted at the annual meeting," the 1889–1892 minute book
  was lost in the office move of 1938**, and **r22, writing for the county's permanent file, examined the
  question and formally declined it: "this office is unable to state the year of adoption and does not
  attempt to."** **The honest answer: 1892 rests on one document that no longer exists; the text does
  not.**
- **Where the Association's office was.** **r05 puts it at Ashlin, one room over a harness shop, cold at
  the north end. r23 puts "the Cooperative's office at Larrow Green."** **Unresolved — state it.**
  Possibly two offices at different dates, or a move; Kyle's own "then the office was moved" (1938) may
  be that move. No source settles it. *(r24 is silent; Orra never went to the office.)*
- **Selby Vose's managership: 1913.** Confirmed three ways — Loomis's minutes; Strawn 1913 ("**New
  manager at the Association this year** and he came out with me to the platform, which the last one
  never did"); Vose himself in 1926 ("**It is thirteen years this spring, since 1913**"). Establishes an
  unnamed predecessor. **r23 says "nineteen eleven" — reject it; Kyle's own arithmetic contradicts him**
  (Selby "sat behind that door for a quarter of a century and I sat outside it," and the office moved in
  1938 → 1913).
- **The condemnation date: 12 June 1923** (r06 Strawn's field book, r22). Reject **12 July 1923** — r08
  and r12, one line of transmission.
- **Accident or fraud at the glassworks — NOT ESTABLISHED.** r19 accuses the firm; r20 (Junia Ammon)
  offers the mold-regrind accident as **her belief**. **Neither can be tested, because the works records
  burned in 1931.** Balance favours accident: a house that knowingly shipped short glass would not have
  priced and itemized the invoice so plainly, and the same house supplied "half the creameries in the
  state" with no other such story attaching to it.
- **Post-1924 station average: 3.80 vs r22's 3.85.** Five hundredths apart; does not affect C1(b).
- **r24's internal oddity:** the diary records the *dismissal* (30 April 1925) but **not the award**, and
  the 1926 leaves are torn out. So the household's own record goes silent at exactly the two moments the
  public record speaks. Worth one sentence.

## 4. STORY THREAD A — THE FOUNDING AND ARTICLE VII (1889–1892)

- **1889**: farmers met at **Larrow Green** and organized the **Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery
  Association**. **Gideon Vose** elected first president, held it **1889–1911** — twenty-two years,
  longest of any man (r02 §1). r18 and r22 confirm from outside the Association's papers. Gideon Vose
  **died in 1917**.
- **r12's scene**: they met **in the schoolhouse, in cold weather**; the old men remembered chiefly that
  **it went on very late and that somebody's horse got loose in the middle of it.**
- **The churn at Larrow Green was set up in 1891** (used in the Story-2 plan above).
- **Article VII** — quoted verbatim by two sources (r02 from the minute book, r12 in transcription) —
  provides that **every patron is paid on the test of the station where he delivers, and "the test of
  the station where he delivers shall govern."** Gideon Vose **wrote the by-laws himself**. The last
  clause is the one that trapped the patrons: it made the station test final and unappealable, so a
  patron who thought himself shorted had, in Frick's phrase, **"no remedy on earth except to quarrel
  with a figure."**
- **Ansel Keddie married Orra Brant in 1889**, the same year the Association was organized — the two
  founding facts of the whole affair happen in the same year and should be paired in Story 1.
- Adoption year of Article VII: **1892 (r02) / 1891 (r12) / 1893 (r15)** — see §3.3; r22 formally
  declines to state it; the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move.
