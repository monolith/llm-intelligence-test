## 2. STORY THREAD A — THE FOUNDING OF THE ASSOCIATION AND ARTICLE VII (1889–1892)

- **1889**: farmers met at **Larrow Green** and organized the **Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery
  Association**. **Gideon Vose** elected first president, held it **1889–1911** — twenty-two years, longest of
  any man. (r02 §1)
- **r12 adds the scene**: they met **in the schoolhouse, in cold weather**; the old men remembered chiefly
  that **it went on very late and that somebody's horse got loose in the middle of it**. Motive: *"They were
  tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking whatever the merchant chose to allow them for it, and they had
  worked out — the way farmers do, slowly and then all at once — that the way to be paid properly for a thing
  is to own the place that buys it."*
- Before the creamery, members sold milk where they could, "no common test and no common price, and every
  man's arithmetic was his own." (r02 §2)
- **1891**: main creamery built at **Larrow Green, on Larrow Creek** (r02) / **"on the creek below the mill"**
  (r12); same building standing in 1941. r12: the churn was set up in it **"and the valley began to be paid in
  cash once a month, which had never happened to it before."**
- **Gideon Vose drafted the by-laws himself**; not a lawyer; "they read as a farmer wrote them" (r02).
  **r12's portrait**: *"a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the law and thought better of
  it, and he wrote the by-laws himself, at his own kitchen table, in a hand that the Association's clerks were
  still complaining about thirty years later."* r12 also reports a saying attributed to Selby Vose: that
  Gideon *"wrote by-laws the way other men build stone wall — slowly, and to last."*
- **ARTICLE VII — now transcribed IDENTICALLY, word for word, by THREE independent hands: r02 (Loomis, from
  the minutes/printed pamphlet), r07 Item 1 (Nym, from the printed pamphlet), and r12 (Rundle, printed whole
  in the 1939 pamphlet):**
  > *"Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of
  > butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test
  > of the station where he delivers shall govern."*
  The wording is therefore beyond dispute.
- **CONFLICT ON THE DATE OF ARTICLE VII. r02 (Loomis, from the minute book): adopted at the annual meeting of
  1892. r12 (Rundle): "Article VII dates from 1891, the year the churn was set up." Resolve for 1892** —
  Loomis is the secretary with the minute book in her own hand and sets the article down entire precisely
  because it has been misquoted at her for forty years; Rundle explicitly writes valley memory and ties the
  date to the churn by association. **Note the complication from r07: the printed copies of the by-laws carry
  no year on their face, only the words "adopted at the annual meeting"** — so the 1892 date rests on Loomis's
  minutes alone, and the **1889–1892 minute book was lost in the office move of 1938**. Rundle's 1891 is the
  kind of error that arises exactly where the printed pamphlet is undated. **Flag the date as resting on one
  document; the text does not.**
- Loomis's gloss: **the last clause is the one nobody quotes — "the test of the station where he delivers
  shall govern." Not the creamery's test, not the valley average, not a re-test anywhere else. The station's
  test, and no appeal from it.** She calls it **"the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business."** Written by a
  man who **died in 1917** and could not be asked afterward.
- **Rundle, independently, reaches the same reading in 1939:** *"But mark the last clause, because the valley
  has had fifty years to learn what it means… Not the test of the creamery. Not any test but the one made at
  his own door. Gideon Vose put that in for convenience and it was convenient for thirty years, and it is the
  sentence on which the whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned."* He also calls the rule
  just in principle: *"A man who keeps good cows and feeds them ought not to be paid the same as the man who
  keeps poor ones, and the pound of milk is a liar where the pound of butterfat is not."* And: **it was thought
  a hard rule at the time and there were men who left the Association over it** (new detail).
- **Selby Vose confirms the authorship himself, in print, in 1926 (r08):** *"My father, who was your first
  president and who wrote the by-law under which every patron in this Association is paid on the test of the
  station where he delivers…"* — an independent, contemporaneous, self-interested confirmation of both the
  authorship and the parentage.
- **Records gap**: the **1889–1892 minute book was lost in the office move of 1938** — carried out with the
  rest and never arrived. Loomis searched everywhere. *"Anybody who tells you what was resolved at the first
  four annual meetings is telling you a story he had from his father."*
- Officers in Loomis's time: Loomis secretary 1901–1940 (minute book in her own hand); **Wilbur Teague**
  bookkeeper from **1905**; **Hobart Sill** buttermaker at Larrow Green (churn book and cream receiving book);
  **Selby Vose**, "son of the late president," manager **1913–1938**.
- Loomis's confession: *"not one of us ever went up the valley and stood in the Ostrey Hollow station on a
  working morning to watch the work being done. I include myself first in that."*
- **1913 managership confirmed three ways**: Loomis's minutes; Strawn **1913** ("**New manager at the
  Association this year** and he came out with me to the platform, which the last one never did"); and Vose
  himself in 1926 ("**It is thirteen years this spring, since 1913**, that you have had the management of this
  Association in my hands"). The 1913 entry also establishes there *was* an unnamed previous manager.
- **Sharp irony against Loomis's confession**: the one man who *did* stand in that station on working
  mornings, year after year, was the state's inspector — and no officer of the Association ever asked him what
  he had seen.
- Office at Ashlin: one room over a harness shop, cold at the north end (r05).
- **Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green** (r09, r12) — chairman of the 1908 investigating committee; the only other
  named Larrow Green figure. Presumptively the unnamed "chairman" who signed the report Nym transcribes.

## 3. STORY THREAD B — THE STATION, THE SHORT GLASS, AND THE 26-YEAR SHORTFALL (1896–1923)

### The station and its catchment
- **Skimming station at the head of Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green** — distance
  independently given by r06 (1907, "up seven miles from Larrow Green"), r08 (Vose, 1926, "seven miles up the
  valley"), r11 (Vaught, "seven miles down the same valley") and r12 (Rundle, "seven miles up from the
  Green"). Low whitewashed building, cold inside even in August, smelled of milk and steam and hot iron (r01).
  Took in the upper farms' milk, weighed it, sampled and tested it, separated it, sent the **cream down to
  Larrow Green** and **gave the skim back to the patron** for hogs and calves.
- **r12 on why it was built and what it looked like:** *"in the nineties the pull down was three hours in mud
  and could not be done at all in March. So the Association built a skimming station up there, and it opened
  in 1896… It was a great convenience and the Hollow was glad of it. The building is a plain one, boarded and
  battened, with the intake room at the road end and **a loft over it that I believe has never been cleared
  out since it was put up**; and behind it the creek, which in a wet April comes up to the sill and has twice
  come over it."* — **The loft detail independently corroborates Strawn 1928 and Ivy 1958 and is written in
  1939, nineteen years before Ivy climbed the ladder.**
- **THE CATCHMENT RECONCILES EXACTLY.** Earlier tension ("seven Bottom farms + three hill places = ten, against
  nine families on the circular") is resolved by **r09: Roy Brant, who farmed the meadow — "Hessel Bottom is a
  hundred and sixty acres of wet meadow lying under the hill at Ostrey Hollow. SIX farms had rights on it and
  cut hay off it and pastured it."** Six Bottom farms + Strawn's **three places on the hill** (1909) = **nine
  families**, exactly the number on the 1919 circular (r01, r03, r09). **Resolve for SIX Bottom farms.** The
  hill farms matter because they are *not* Bottom ground — a wet-hay defect cannot depress hill milk, but a
  short pipette depresses everything alike.
- **The Brant farm was the largest of the six: "sixty-odd acres of the meadow, and thirty-eight cows in a good
  year, which for this valley was a herd"** (r09). Corroborates r02 §5 ("the station's largest patron in every
  year of which we have a record"), r01 (most cows, most hay ground), r03 ("brought more milk to my door than
  any other man on the Bottom").
- Hessel Bottom described from inside (r09): water stands on the low end into May; a soft place by the alders
  that has taken a horse to the hock; the hay is coarse and cuts late; *"a cow will eat it and do well enough,
  whatever has been said about that hay by men who never fed it."*
- **Ansel Keddie** ran and tested it. Vira Toland says he went up "in ninety-five, the year before they opened
  it." **Keddie's own first leaf and his own count contradict 1895**: 29 seasons, 1896 through 1924, and he
  "did not test a pound in the season of 1925." Strawn (1907) confirms the station "opened before my time."
  **Vose (r08) independently says "a station in daily use since 1896." Rundle (r12) independently says "it
  opened in 1896." Resolve for 1896** — now four independent lines.
- First leaf of the test book, quoted identically by r03 and r07 Item 3:
  > *"Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own
  > comes. A. Keddie."*
- **Internal conflict inside r03**: the letter's prose says the 1896 glass was "borrowed off the **Ashlin
  creamery**"; the quoted first leaf says **"borrowed of Larrow Green."** Resolve for **Larrow Green** —
  contemporaneous document over 29-year-old recollection; no Ashlin creamery is attested anywhere.

### THE OUTFIT — the invoice, itemized (r07 Item 2), DECISIVE
> **Invoice, Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, Tarnet, 14 April 1897.** One leaf, folded in three.
> *Sold to the Larrow Valley Co-op. Creamery Assn., for the Ostrey Hollow station:*
> *6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20*
> *12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at .22 — 31.68*
> *1 acid measure — .90*
> *1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00*
> *Freight — 2.15*
> *Total — 77.93*

Arithmetic checks exactly: 72 × 0.35 = 25.20; 144 × 0.22 = 31.68; 25.20+31.68+0.90+18.00+2.15 = **77.93**.
**Nym adds: "the office file has [it] in use at the station from 1 May 1897, Larrow Green having lent until
then."** This matches **Bulletin 471's concluding paragraph, 1 May 1897, exactly** — from an entirely different
record (the Association's office file, not the College's bulletin). **Consequence: the pipettes were short
FROM NEW.** That kills Selby Vose's "apparatus wears" theory outright — blown glass stems do not wear shorter,
and the step change from 3.80 (1896, borrowed Larrow Green glass) to 3.61 (1898 on) lands precisely at the
point the new glass came into use, not gradually across twenty-six years.
Note the **"8 per cent neck"** on the bottles — the neck ruled to read to 8 per cent for one charge and no
other, which is the mechanism Ivy and Vaught both rely on.

- **NEW CONFLICT (r11 §III): "Of the lot bought for the Ostrey Hollow station in 1897 — five dozen pipettes
  were purchased." Resolve for SIX DOZEN (72).** The invoice line is itemized, priced, and its arithmetic
  closes to the stated total ($25.20 = 72 × $0.35; five dozen would be $21.00 and would break the $77.93
  total). Nym transcribes it exactly and checked twice. Vaught is summarizing at second hand fifty years on.
- **NEW CONFLICT (r11 §III): "The station's own glass went into use on the first of May, 1898."** This
  contradicts (a) **r11's own concluding paragraph**, which says "from the first of May, 1897"; (b) the
  invoice, dated 14 April 1897; (c) Nym's office file, "in use at the station from 1 May 1897." **Resolve for
  1 May 1897.** Vaught's §III has slipped a year, and the slip is visible inside his own bulletin.

### Keddie's method and book (from the unsent letter)
- Day began half past five; wagons up in the dark; lamps lit, scale swinging free before the first wagon.
  Every can weighed and the weight called and written before the milk went anywhere. **The scale was tested by
  the state's man on his rounds and was true; in 29 seasons no patron ever disputed a weight and was right.**
- Sample from each patron's milk as weighed, into **his own numbered jar**, **made up over ten days**. On
  testing morning: warmed to blood-warm through, poured vessel to vessel until the risen cream was carried
  down through the whole.
- Charge drawn up in the measure to the mark on the stem, tip wiped, blown out into the numbered bottle; acid
  down the side; bottle turned till the milk darkened; **four bottles in the hand tester, weighted against
  each other, cover down, crank at the taught speed**; fat read off in per cent against the rulings **with a
  pair of dividers**.
- Book kept in **five columns: date, patron, pounds, test, fat.** Errors crossed and initialed; **never a
  figure written over a figure.** (*Minor conflict:* r07 Item 3 describes the physical book as having "columns
  for date, patron, pounds and test" — four, not five. Trivial; not worth resolving.)
- Statement to the board: *"I have tested as I was taught and as the state man saw me do it, **thirty-one
  times a week for twenty-nine seasons**, and I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are… I have
  never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when
  he is by himself."*
- **Keddie's claim about "the state man" is corroborated from the state man's own books.** Strawn's standing
  routine at every plant, in the same order every time: (1) **the scales**, against sealed weights he carried
  — both intake and cream scale, loaded light and loaded heavy; (2) **the lactometer** on the day's milk,
  patron by patron where there was any question, for added water; (3) **the boiler, water supply and cold
  room**; (4) — "the one most men grudged me" — **he stood at the tester's elbow and watched him make a test
  through from the sample to the reading, and did not let him hurry it.**
  - **1907**: "Their tester keeps his book in his own hand, and his hand is better than most. **Watched him
    through two tests. He works slow and clean and reads his own figure without being coaxed to it.**"
  - **1911, June**: "Scales true. **Watched a test through. Nothing here to note.**"
  - **Scales true** at Ostrey Hollow in 1907, 1909, 1911, 1912, 1915, 1916, 1919, 1920 (twice), 1922, 1923
    (June), 1923 (Oct), 1924, 1925, 1928, 1931, 1934, 1936 — **every recorded visit, without exception,
    across twenty-nine years.**
  - **Lactometer, no added water** at Ostrey Hollow in 1912, 1916, 1919, 1920 (twice), 1922, 1923 (June, "as
    always here"), 1924, 1925, 1928, 1934.
  - **Keddie's procedure was watched through, sample to reading, by the state's inspector, and never once
    faulted.** His technique was sound; only his glass was wrong. A proportional error in the *instrument*
    survives a perfect *method*, and Strawn's routine before 1921 had no way to catch it.
- **THE SCALES ARE ALSO CONFIRMED FROM THE PATRON SIDE (r09), a genuinely independent instrument.** Roy Brant
  on his father's book: two columns per delivery day — the weight of every can off **Emmet's own scale in his
  own barn** before the wagon went, and **the figure the station gave him back**.
  *"The two columns agreed… Day after day, month after month, for the whole run of years… his weight and the
  station's weight came out the same, barring a pound or two here and there, and those were days when a can
  got slopped on the hill. Two scales, his and the station's, kept by two men who by the end were not speaking
  to one another, and the scales never fell out at all. Whatever was wrong at that station — and something was
  wrong at that station for a very long time — it was not the weighing."*

### The 1922 summary leaf (quoted identically in r03, r04 AND r07 — one document, three transcriptions)
> *"Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within
> three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it
> stood at 3.80."*

Nym's gloss (r07 Item 3): "The summary leaf gathers the yearly averages: 3.80 for the first season, 3.61 from
1898 on." Provenance of the test book: **kept at the station from 4 May 1896; impounded by the board of
arbitration in March 1925; in the Association's office at Ashlin 1925–1959; received at the Historical Rooms

### The nine weeks of 1911 — the accidental controlled experiment
- Two entries as Keddie made them:
  > *"May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure."*
  > *"July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."*
  The question mark is Keddie's; he let it stand fourteen years.
- Ivy: **8 May to 10 July 1911**, nine weeks; the borrowed measure **came up from Larrow Green with the cream
  wagon**; same patrons, herds, pasture, season, acid, bottles, same man at the crank — **one object changed**
  — average went to 3.80 and returned to 3.61 the day the station's own measure came back.
- **Strawn was physically in the station during the window.** "1911. Ostrey Hollow, **June**… Scales true.
  Watched a test through. Nothing here to note." So the test he watched in June 1911 was made **on the sound
  borrowed measure** — which is why there was nothing to note. It also proves Keddie's hand-work was identical
  in the high-reading window and the low-reading years.
- **From the patron side (r09):** *"There were nine weeks in the summer of 1911 when the station's own measure
  was out of use, it having been broken… Through those nine weeks our tests came up, all of us, every farm on
  the Bottom; and then the station's own came back into use and the figures went down again to where they had
  been. My father noticed the rise and he noticed the fall and he entered both, and he could make nothing of
  it, and I could make nothing of it either, and there it sat for near on fifty years."*
  **Decisive independent corroboration**: nine weeks, summer 1911, station's own measure broken, and the rise
  was **across every farm on the Bottom**, not an averaging artifact. Recorded in the Brant weigh book, a
  document outside the station.
- **VAUGHT WEIGHS THIS ABOVE ALL THE ARITHMETIC (r11 §IV), and his statement of it is the cleanest anywhere:**
  > *"For nine weeks in the summer of 1911 the station's own pipette was broken and a borrowed measure was
  > used in its place. The patrons were the same patrons; the herds were the same herds; the pasture, the
  > season, the acid, the bottles, the tester and the tester's habits were all unaltered. **One variable
  > changed.** The station's average test rose from 3.61 to 3.80 and held there for the nine weeks, and
  > returned to 3.61 on the day the station's own measure came back into service. This is recorded
  > contemporaneously, in the operator's hand, by a man who did not know what he was recording and set a
  > question mark against it."*
  And: *"I have spent my working life urging students to prefer an experiment to a calculation. Here is one
  that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven
  years. It disposes of every explanation that looks to the herds, to the ground or to the men, for the
  sufficient reason that none of those was altered in those nine weeks, and the figures were."*
  (1911 + 47 = 1958 ✓ — his own dating is internally consistent.)
  **NOTE: Vaught here uses 3.61 → 3.80, i.e. the 0.19 step and the 0.95 ratio — which contradicts his own §V
  arithmetic (3.905). This is the internal fault line inside r11.**
- **CONFLICT on where the borrowed measure came from.** Ivy (from Keddie's book): **up from Larrow Green with
  the cream wagon.** Roy Brant: *"**The state man left his own measure that June** and that is what they
  used."* **Resolve for Larrow Green.** Reason: the borrowing began **8 May** on Keddie's dated entry, and
  Strawn's visit was in **June** — a measure left in June cannot be the one in use since 8 May. Roy is
  recalling at forty-four years' remove and is second-hand about station procedure; he has plausibly merged
  Strawn's June visit with the borrowing. (A third possibility no source states: two successive borrowed
  measures.) Either way the experiment stands, and either candidate lender's glass was sound.
- **Conflict**: Teague (r05) says the measure broke "in the spring of 1911" and they made shift with a
  borrowed one for **six weeks** before **their own came up from town**. **Resolve for nine weeks** —
  Keddie's dated book entries, Roy Brant's independent "nine weeks," and Vaught's "nine weeks," against
  Teague's memory at 37 years. **Three independent sources say nine.**

### The gap was flat, not seasonal
- Keddie: "They did not come and go with the season. The gap was there in July as it was there in February, in
  the flush of the grass and in the dead of the winter on dry hay, and it was the same in a wet year as in a
  dry one."
- **Roy Brant, independently:** *"Our milk at the Hollow ran seventeen hundredths of a point under what the
  same class of milk was making down at Larrow Green… it was seventeen hundredths in July and it was seventeen
  hundredths in February. It did not come and go with the season. It did not care whether the cows were on the
  meadow or on hay in the barn. Nobody would take account of that, then or since, though it was in front of
  them."* Plus the line that sums the whole story: *"Seventeen hundredths is nothing to look at on one pay
  slip. Set the slips in a row for a lifetime of farming and it is a farm."*
- **Reconciling 0.17 and 0.19 — they measure different things and need not be equal.** 0.19 is *Ostrey
  Hollow's recorded average against Ostrey Hollow's true average* (3.61 vs 3.80). 0.17 is *Ostrey Hollow's
  recorded average against Larrow Green's recorded average*. These coincide only if Larrow Green's own herds
  averaged exactly 3.80, which nobody claims. **r07 Item 16 supports Roy's figure independently: the Larrow
  Green receiving and churn books show that "over the same years for which the station's 3.61 is recorded, the
  Larrow Green average runs a little under two tenths of a point better."** So: **use 0.19 for the shortfall
  arithmetic; treat 0.17 as the Larrow-Green differential, and say which you are using.** Not a contradiction.
- **The 1924 = 3.80 anomaly is RESOLVED in Keddie's favor.** His letter: "**In 1924 the average of this
  station was 3.80**, and I wrote that figure with the same pen and the same measure of care as all the
  others." **Strawn, 1924, independently: "Ostrey Hollow. Scales true, no added water, tests improved. Their
  book gives the year at 3.80."** The 1922 leaf described 1898–1922; 1924 is *after* the glass changed. **The
  tests rose permanently, by exactly the predicted amount, the moment the instrument changed.**
- Strawn, **1925** (fall): "Scales true, no added water, **tests improved.** New man at the tester's bench this
  year. He is quick, and quick is not the thing I look for." — the tests stayed up under a *different* tester,
  which kills any theory that the low figures were something Keddie personally did.

### The condemnation, the reorder, and the crate
- **June 1923**: the state's inspector condemned a crate of measures; it was set aside and new ones ordered in
  its place (r02 §8). Loomis: **the crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all** — not by patrons,
  officers, or the board, in any of the three sessions. "The award's phrase about 'the books and appliances of
  the station' is a form of words the board used and not a list of anything." Her closing line: "the
  interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying."
- **Bulletin 471 dates the short glass from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923.**
- **Who condemned it is CLOSED: Merle Strawn, 12 June 1923.** Heading: "1923, June 12. Ostrey Hollow. I had
  carried the burette two seasons before this check." The entry — **quoted verbatim and identically in r06 and
  in r07 Item 9 (a photostat of the 1923 notebook), i.e. two independent transcriptions of one document**:
  > *"Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk pipettes against
  > the burette. **These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and
  > told the manager to order new.** Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them."*
  The manager told to order new was **Selby Vose**.
- **The reorder is dated to the day (r07 Item 10): "Letter, Ammon & Sons to the Association, 2 July 1923…
  acknowledging the Association's order of 28 June for replacement pipettes and bottles, promising shipment
  that month, and declining to say anything of the earlier lot."** Chain: condemned **12 June 1923** → ordered
  **28 June 1923** → acknowledged **2 July 1923** → **new glass in use since July** (Strawn, Oct 1923) → **new
  pipettes run against the burette and "they deliver their mark"** (Strawn, Oct 1923). Airtight.
- **THE 12 JULY ERROR NOW HAS TWO CARRIERS.** Selby Vose (r08, 1926): "he condemned the station's testing
  apparatus **on the twelfth of July, 1923**." Dell Rundle (r12, 1939): "The state inspector condemned the
  station's glassware **on the twelfth of July, 1923**, for reasons of his own that had nothing to do with the
  quarrel, and new glass was put in." **Resolve for 12 JUNE 1923** — Strawn's own dated field book, twice
  transcribed, one of them a photostat. **The two "July" sources are not independent**: Rundle wrote in 1939 at
  Larrow Green from valley talk and printed material, thirteen years after Vose said it from the platform in
  the same village, and both have the same day and the same wrong month. Treat as one error propagating.
  Note also that Vose's own "we ordered new inside the month" is true of June and false of July.
- Rundle's phrase **"for reasons of his own that had nothing to do with the quarrel"** is itself the whole
  tragedy in one clause: in 1939 the valley still could not see that the condemnation *was* the answer.
- **WHY IT WAS NOT CAUGHT EARLIER — the missing mechanism.** Strawn, **1921**: "**The state has begun issuing
  burettes to the field men this year**, graduated, in a wooden case, for checking the delivery of measures
  and pipettes at the plants. Mine came in the spring with a printed sheet of directions. **It is a slow
  business to run a plant's glass against it and most plants have never had it done.**" Before 1921 no field
  officer in the state carried an instrument capable of detecting a short pipette. The error was
  undetectable-in-practice for its first twenty-four years, not overlooked. **Vaught states the same thing
  from the science side (r11 §II): "It can be caught only by a burette brought in from outside, and burettes
  reached the state's field men very late in this history."**
- **The near miss.** Strawn, **1922**: "Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. Boiler in order. **I did not
  run their glass against the burette this year, the day being short and the road home being what it was, and
  I have put that down because it is what happened.**" One short afternoon cost the patrons an additional year
  — and he recorded his own omission rather than hide it.
- **The burette program was short-lived.** Strawn, **1926**: the program is "**discontinued from this year**…
  no more are to be issued and no returns are wanted. Mine is in its case in my grip and has been used twice
  since." The state issued the detecting instrument for **five years (1921–1925)**. The crate was caught inside
  a five-year window that had opened two years earlier and would close three years later.
- **The loft, seen by the inspector in 1928.** Strawn: "The station is quieter than it was. **The loft over the
  intake room is full of odds and ends and I told them a loft over a milk room is a place for nothing but air,
  and was told in return that it had been full since before the war.**" The station-hand's "since before the
  war" is wrong as to the crate (it went up June 1923, after the war) — it shows how fast the crate became
  furniture. **Rundle 1939 independently: the loft "has never been cleared out since it was put up."**
- Ivy, **February 1958**: went up the Hollow to the station building (used for storage since it closed; fence
  wire and salt kept in it). Loft over the old intake room, reached by a ladder; under a tarpaulin at the

- **CONFLICT — where the crate was found. r04 (Ivy, first person, with the crate on the table): the loft over

### Ivy's measurement, 9 March 1958, at Farland against a calibrated burette
Quoted identically in r04 and in r07 Item 14 (a typed, hand-entered, signed measurement sheet):
> *"Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean of five
> runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c.
> That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for."*

**The nine readings sum to 150.48; 150.48 ÷ 9 = 16.72 exactly. The stated mean is arithmetically verifiable
from the stated data.** This matters enormously — see §6.

Symmetry worth keeping: **the same class of instrument (a graduated burette) that condemned the glass in 1923
is the instrument that explained it in 1958** — Strawn learned *that* they were short; Ivy learned *by how
much*, and turned the how-much into pounds and dollars.

**Vaught's method note (r11 §III), which describes the same procedure:** the pipette is filled to its mark with
water at room temperature, the tip wiped, contents delivered in the ordinary working manner — same interval of
drainage, same touch of the tip to the wall of the receiver — into a burette of certified graduation; five
deliveries from each pipette, readings averaged. *"The whole of it occupies an afternoon and requires no
apparatus a creamery does not own, save the burette. There is no reason why every station in this state should
not be checked once in this way, and several reasons in this history why it should be."*

### The refutation of the "wet hay" explanation
- **1908**: a committee came up and sampled the herds and reported that the wet hay of the Bottom thinned the
  cream and the remedy lay with the land (r03). **The report is quoted verbatim (r07 Item 11), fair copy,
  signed by the chairman:**
  > *"We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March. The milk is whole and
  > the scales are honest. It is our opinion that the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and that
  > the remedy lies with the land and not with the station."*
  **Nym's crucial note: "The sampling was of January, February and March 1908 only, as the report states."**
  **The committee never sampled a summer month.** It therefore could not have seen that the gap stood flat
  through the flush — the single fact that most damns the hay theory. Note also that the report concedes
  **"the milk is whole and the scales are honest"** — i.e. in 1908 the Association's own committee had already
  eliminated watering and false weights, eleven years before the manager circularized nine families about
  water in their cans.
  **r12 independently confirms the winter-only sampling: "In 1908 the Association sent a committee up under
  Nyle Grigg, which sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through the winter and reported that the wet hay of
  that bottom thinned the cream, and that the remedy lay with the land. The Hollow did not accept it."**
- **r09 puts a name and a reaction to it:** the committee was headed by **Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green**. *"My
  father read that report twice and put it in the drawer and said that the land had been wet since the flood
  and the tests had not."*
- **State Agricultural College, Bulletin No. 214 (1911)**, quoted identically by r04, r07 Item 12 and r11 §VI
  — three transcriptions of one sentence:
  > *"Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths
  > of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."*
- Ivy: five hundredths in winter, nothing in summer, against a gap of nineteen hundredths standing flat in
  every month of every year — "**four times too small and it is the wrong shape.**" (0.19 ÷ 0.05 ≈ 3.8, so
  "four times" is right on the corrected figures.)
  **r11 §VI says "some six times the largest effect the trial could produce" — that is 0.295 ÷ 0.05 ≈ 6, i.e.
  a downstream consequence of Vaught's bad 0.295. On the correct figures the multiple is four, and Ivy has it
  right.** Vaught adds a fair point Ivy does not: the trial imposed **"conditions harsher than any farmer would
  impose"** (bog hay as the *whole* of the roughage), so 0.05 is an upper bound, not a typical effect.
- **Alder Corners cross-check**: the factory over the ridge tested the milk of **four Hessel Bottom men** in
  their summer flush; that same milk off that same wet ground ran **about nineteen hundredths higher at Alder
  Corners than at home.** (Full table in §5.)
- **Supporting point**: the station also took milk **from three places on the hill** (Strawn 1909), which is
  not Bottom ground. A defect in the station's glass depresses hill milk and Bottom milk alike; wet bog hay
  cannot. No source draws this inference explicitly — it is mine.

## 4. STORY THREAD C — THE STATION-GAIN LINE (Teague's ledger)

- Teague's mechanism: on one side of the station account he entered **the butterfat credited to patrons on the
  station's own test**; on the other, **the butterfat that actually came down the valley in the station's
  cream and was weighed and tested into the vats at Larrow Green**. "The two were never the same. **The second
  was always the larger.**" The difference he carried to the general account.
- The entry, quoted complete — **and now quoted IDENTICALLY by THREE hands from the ledgers themselves: r05
  (Teague, the bookkeeper who wrote it), r07 Item 6 (Nym, from the annual ledgers), and r12 (Rundle, who saw
  the ledgers in the office in 1939 "on another errand altogether"):**
  > *"Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, **57,760 lb**. Butter fat received at
  > Larrow Green in station cream, **60,800 lb**. Station gain, **3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as
  > usual**. Carried to the general account."*
  Teague calls this **the entry for 1898**, "and there is one like it for every year after, in the same terms
  and in the same hand for as long as the hand was mine." Nym describes it as **"typical"** of an entry that
  appears in one form every year, and notes **the station column runs from 1897**. Rundle: **"The station
  account carries, every year, an entry of this form, in the bookkeeper's hand."** Three witnesses to
  *annual recurrence*, which is what makes the ×25 multiplication legitimate.
- **These numbers corroborate everything else exactly**: 1,600,000 lb of milk × 3.61% = 57,760 lb;
  × 3.80% = 60,800 lb; difference 3,040 lb; 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.263% ≈ "five and a quarter per cent."
- **Nym's curatorial note on the two percentages is the cleanest statement of it anywhere:** *"The two
  percentages in this file are not interchangeable: five and a quarter percent of the credited fat is five
  percent of the fat delivered, and each is right of its own denominator and of no other."*
  **r11 §V gets this wrong: "The gain is five and a quarter percent of the butterfat actually delivered."**
  Check: 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = **5.000%** of delivered; 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = **5.263%** of credited. **Vaught has
  attached the credited-fat percentage to the delivered-fat denominator. Resolve with Nym.** A third error in
  r11 §V, and it is the same class of error as the rest of §V.
- **Why the two sides had to differ, mechanically.** The station's *test* used the short pipette; the cream
  was **weighed and tested into the vats at Larrow Green** on Larrow Green's own sound glass. Strawn (**1912**)
  notes "**Cream shipped down the valley the same day it is separated, which is more than some do**," so no
  spoilage or interval explains it away. Two instruments, one sound and one short, measuring the same fat.
- Teague on why nobody looked: "A figure that jumps about will keep you awake. **A figure that comes in at five
  and a quarter every year is furniture.**"
- **Internal inconsistency in r05 (still open)**: he says "**the station-gain line was my own device; I began
  it when I came in 1905**" and denies it was any old dodge of the founders — yet the entry he quotes complete
  is **the 1898 entry**, seven years before he arrived. **r07 Item 6 slightly favors "not his device": the
  station column in the annual ledgers runs from 1897, i.e. from before Teague.** Either he was working from a
  predecessor's ledger, or he mis-stated the start year. **Flag; still open.**
- **Teague's two summary figures remain outliers**: "that station took in **forty-four million pounds of
  milk**. And the average test of all the patrons of Ostrey Hollow… was **3.16**." Both conflict with r02
  (Loomis, from Teague's own annual sheets: **40,000,000 lb**), r04 (Ivy: 40,000,000 lb, recorded 3.61), r07
  Item 6 (Nym, from the ledgers: "the station's intake for the years 1898 to 1922 is given in these ledgers as
  40,000,000 lb of milk") **and r11 §V (Vaught: "the 40,000,000 lb of milk received at the station in the
  twenty-five full delivery years")**. **3.16 is almost certainly a digit transposition of 3.61.**
  **r12 repeats the 44 million: "The station took in, over the years complained of, forty-four million pounds
  of milk."** But Rundle is Larrow Green valley talk, and Teague is the likeliest ultimate source of it (he
  was the Association's bookkeeper at Ashlin and gave evidence at the 1925 hearing, where the figure would
  have been said aloud). **Resolve for 40,000,000 lb and 3.61 recorded** — four independent readings of the
  ledgers (Loomis, Ivy, Nym, Vaught) against one remembered figure with one echo.
- **Governance evidence**: "**Mr. Vose knew what that account was doing. He signed the sheet every year.** I
  laid the annual sheet before him each spring and he put his name at the foot of it, and a man who signs a
  sheet has had the sheet in front of him. What he made of it he never told me." And: "**I put it to Mr. Vose
  in 1916 and was told to let it alone. I let it alone.** I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its
  conscience."
- **1916 is a doubly loaded year.** Teague put the discrepancy to Selby Vose and was silenced; in the same year
  Strawn recorded at the station: "**The tester complains that his patrons blame him for their figures. I told
  him that is the trade and he said he knew it.**" Neither man knew of the other's moment.
- **Teague is the origin of the theft theory.** What he told the board in 1925 and repeats verbatim in 1948:
  "Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons… One man weighed the
  milk, one man drew the sample, one man made the test, one man wrote the figure that the pay slip was made
  from, and one man ran the separator. **There was no second man in that building from the spring to the
  fall.** I do not say what any particular person did on any particular day… I say that a business which is
  arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner
  or later have a little taken from it… **The remedy is two men or a lock, and the Association had neither.**"
  He never accuses Keddie; the valley converted his structural point into a personal charge.
- **Teague's "no second man" premise is materially qualified by r06 and r09.** There *was* a second man in that
  building, repeatedly and by law: the state's inspector, who watched a test through from sample to reading
  and checked both scales against sealed weights on every visit for twenty-nine years, and never once found
  either wrong. And there was a second *scale* outside the building: Emmet Brant's own, agreeing with the
  station's day after day for thirty years. A theft of cream would have shown as a false weight or a false
  test; both were checked, from two directions, and neither was ever wrong. **Teague's structural argument
  survives as a governance point; as evidence that anything was actually taken, it is refuted.**
- **RUNDLE REFUTES THE THEFT THEORY IN PRINT IN 1939, FROM THE LEDGER, NINETEEN YEARS BEFORE IVY.** This is
  new and important. His Chapter IV set down the valley's settled belief: *"It is the settled opinion of the
  valley that cream left that station by the back door, and the figures went right when the man who kept it
  did not. I set that down as I have heard it set down a hundred times, by men who were there and men who were
  not, and I have never heard it contradicted at the store."* Then, at the end of the pamphlet:
  > **NOTE ADDED WHILE IN PRESS.** *"While this pamphlet was being set up in type I was allowed to look at the
  > Association's old ledgers in the office, on another errand altogether. The station account carries, every
  > year, an entry of this form, in the bookkeeper's hand: [the 57,760 / 60,800 / 3,040 entry, quoted entire].
  > **I must go back on Chapter IV. Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached
  > the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand. I leave it as written and correct it
  > here.**"* — D. RUNDLE, Larrow Green
  **The logic is exactly right and exactly Ivy's**: theft removes fat between the station's book and the
  creamery's vat, so the creamery would receive *less* than the station credited; the ledger shows it received
  *more*, every year. A schoolteacher settled it in 1939 with one glance at the Association's own books — and
  the valley did not act on it, which is why Rundle's own footnote in the 1959 reprint still refers the reader
  to the college.

## 5. THE ALDER CORNERS ARRANGEMENT — documented from both ends (r09, r10, r12), with the full table

- **r09 (Roy Brant):** *"From 1915, four of the Bottom men hauled their milk over Redlow Ridge to the cheese
  factory in the summer flush and back to the Hollow the rest of the year. The factory over there had opened
  in 1914. They went on that way for **sixteen seasons**. It was for the price and the flush market and a
  shorter haul while the road was open, and it was talked about here as though they had gone over to the
  enemy, which shows you what a valley is. **I did not go over. We had the biggest herd on the meadow, and the
  biggest herd is the one that cannot afford to be talked about, and I have thought since that this was a poor
  reason and cost us money.**"*
- **r10 (Rosalie Cudd), from the receiving end:** *"From 1915, four men off Hessel Bottom hauled their milk
  over to me in the summer flush. They came from about the first of June to the middle of September, and the
  rest of the year they went down to your station at Ostrey Hollow, which was the only sensible thing to do…
  cheese paid better than butter in a flush month, I was buying hard in flush… and while the road was open my
  gate was a shorter pull for those four farms than the run down the valley. **They came for sixteen seasons.
  The last of those summers was 1930.**"* (1915–1930 inclusive = 16 ✓.)
- **r12 (Rundle), from the Ordell side:** *"in 1914 the Alder Corners Cheese Company was opened over Redlow
  Ridge in Vessey County, by Jerome Cudd, and… in the summers a few of our Hessel Bottom men hauled over the
  mountain to him rather than down to their own station. **That was resented here.** It is Jerome Cudd's
  daughter, Rosalie Cudd, who makes the cheese at Alder Corners now."*
- **The ridge, and why it matters:** *"Four miles of switchback, cut for a lumber road and never much improved
  since. It shuts in November and it does not open before April, some years May… **So no man from your valley
  ever hauled to me in winter, whatever the price was. It was not possible.**"* The Alder Corners comparison is
  therefore **structurally summer-only** — exactly the season in which Bulletin 214 says bog hay depresses the
  test by *nothing measurable*. That makes the ~0.19 summer differential impossible to blame on hay.

### HOW JEROME CUDD TESTED, IN HIS DAUGHTER'S WORDS (r10) — the independence argument
> *"You ask how my father tested. He tested himself, on his own bench, in the little room off the intake, with
> his own glassware that he bought new and washed in the evening and kept in a rack he had made, and he wrote
> his own book. He never compared a figure of his with anybody else's, and I want to be honest with you about
> the reason: it never entered his head. He had no business with what your station made of the same milk. A
> cheesemaker tests what comes in his door so he knows what he is paying for and what his vat will do. He is
> not keeping a check on another man's arithmetic seven miles off over a mountain, and if you had suggested it
> to my father he would have laughed at you."*
**This is the strongest possible statement of independence: separate bench, separate glassware bought new,
separate book, and no knowledge of or interest in the Ostrey Hollow figures.**

### JEROME CUDD'S OWN 1919 LEAF, copied exactly by his daughter
> *"The four Hessel Bottom men, summer of 1919: 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground, whatever
> they say over the ridge."*
Rosalie: *"That last clause is my father all over. He had heard what was said in Ordell County about that
meadow — that the wet hay of the bottom thinned the cream, and that the men who farmed it were bringing in
poor stuff and had better drain their land before they complained. He thought it was nonsense, and he wrote so
in his own book where nobody would ever read it, which is where he generally put his opinions. He used to say
that the Ordell people had a theory about that bottom the way some families have a ghost: it explained things,
and it cost nothing, and nobody was ever going to test it."*

## 6. BULLETIN 471 — THE TWO NUMBER-SETS INSIDE ONE DOCUMENT (r11 vs r04/r07), AND HOW TO RESOLVE IT

This is the central trap of the batch. **Bulletin No. 471, State Agricultural College at Farland, issued
October 1958, by Professor Leland Vaught, professor of dairy husbandry.** Two sets of figures are in
circulation, and *both come out of the same bulletin*.

**SET A — the one to use (r04 Ivy's measurement sheet; r07 Item 14, the signed sheet; r11 §IV; r11 §VII, the
bulletin's own concluding paragraph):**
- mean delivery of the nine pipettes = **16.72 c.c.**, short **0.88 c.c.** of the 17.6 mark
- ratio 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95** exactly = "95 hundredths of the mark" = **low by one part in twenty (5%)**
- recorded 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80** true; shortfall **0.19** of a point
- 40,000,000 lb milk × 0.19% = **76,000 lb** of butterfat not credited
- start of error **1 May 1897**, end **12 June 1923**

**SET B — the one to reject (r11 §III and §V only):**
- mean delivery = **16.27 c.c.**
- ratio 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = **0.9244**
- 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = **3.905** true; shortfall **0.295** of a point
- 40,000,000 lb × 0.295% = **118,000 lb**
- five dozen pipettes bought; station glass in use from **1 May 1898**
- station gain stated as 5.25% *of fat delivered*

**Set B is internally consistent with itself and wrong. Eight reasons to reject it:**
1. **It contradicts the bulletin's own conclusion.** r11 §VII says "low by one part in twenty because its
   pipettes were short by one part in twenty" and "seventy-six thousand pounds." One part in twenty is 5%,
   i.e. 0.95, i.e. 16.72 — not 0.9244. **A document that contradicts itself is decided by its own
   conclusion, which is the part it stands behind and the part every other source quotes.**
2. **Vaught himself flags the failure, in §V, immediately after stating it:** *"It should be said that this
   figure is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the
   replacement of the glassware."* He knows it does not fit and prints it anyway. That is an author's own
   warning label.
3. **16.27 is a digit transposition of 16.72.** The characteristic error of a typesetter or a copyist.
4. **The primary measurement data survive and support 16.72 arithmetically.** The nine individual readings
   (16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71) sum to 150.48 and average to 16.72 exactly.
   **No reading in that list is anywhere near 16.27**, and the tightest of them is 0.43 c.c. away from it.
   Set B has no data behind it in any source.
5. **3.80 is independently attested four ways as the true level**, and 3.905 is attested nowhere: (a) the 1911
   nine-week window, on borrowed glass, 3.80; (b) 1924, the first full year on new glass, 3.80, given by
   Keddie's letter and by Strawn reading the station's book on the spot; (c) 1896, the first season, on
   borrowed Larrow Green glass, 3.80; (d) Alder Corners, sixteen seasons of the same men's milk on independent
   glass, mean 3.80.
6. **The ledger closes on Set A and not on Set B.** The annual station-gain entry has credited 57,760 and
   delivered 60,800. 57,760 ÷ 60,800 = **0.95** exactly — the Set A ratio, read straight off the Association's
   own books. On Set B the delivered figure would have had to be 62,483, which no ledger shows.
7. **76,000 lb has three transcribers** (r04, r07 Item 15, r11 §VII) and equals 3,040 lb × 25 years exactly.
   118,000 appears once, in a summary paragraph, and equals nothing else in the corpus.
8. §III of the same passage also mis-states the purchase (five dozen, against an itemized invoice whose
   arithmetic closes at six dozen) and the start date (1898, against its own §VII's 1897, the invoice, and the
   office file). **The whole of §III/§V is a bad patch; §II, §IV, §VI and §VII are sound.**

**WHAT TO KEEP FROM r11 REGARDLESS — Vaught's account of the mechanism (§I–§II) is the best in the corpus:**
- *"This station has been asked, at intervals for fifty years, why the butterfat tests at one skimming station
  in Ordell County ran below those of its own creamery seven miles down the same valley. The question has been
  answered at various times by reference to the land and by reference to the men. **It is answered here by
  reference to a piece of glass.**"*
- *"The answer is not complicated, and its simplicity is itself part of the history. **Nobody in that valley
  lacked the intelligence to find it. What they lacked was any occasion to suspect the one article in the
  plant that nobody in the trade is in the habit of suspecting.**"*
- **The one-sentence takeaway he prints in bold for extension workers:** *"the milk pipette is the one
  instrument in the butterfat test which the plant cannot check against anything else, and it is the
  instrument on which every figure in the plant depends."*
- The four operations: a measured charge of milk drawn into a pipette and delivered into a test bottle;
  sulfuric acid added, dissolving everything but the fat; the bottle spun in a hand tester; the liberated fat
  rises into the graduated neck and is read off **directly, in percent**. *"The operator weighs nothing… he is
  entitled to do so for one reason only: the charge of milk that went into that bottle is a fixed and known
  quantity. The maker rules the neck for that quantity and no other. Every mark on it means 'this much fat,
  out of the standard charge.'"* **The standard charge in this state, and on the glassware examined, is 17.6
  c.c.** — which is exactly what the 1897 invoice says the pipettes were blown to and what the bottles' "8
  per cent neck" was ruled for.
- *"Consider then what follows if the pipette delivers less than its mark. Less milk goes into the bottle;
  less fat is liberated; a shorter column stands in the neck; and the neck, which knows nothing of the
  substitution, is read off in the ordinary way. **The reading is not merely low. It is low in the exact
  proportion by which the charge was short.**"*
- **The three consequences, which he says nobody in fifty years held in mind at once:**
  1. **The error is proportional, not additive.** It removes a fixed *fraction*, not a fixed number of points.
     *"Rich milk therefore loses more than poor milk, absolutely, and the ratio between any two tests made on
     the same short glass is undisturbed. A station using such glass will show a perfectly orderly, internally
     consistent set of figures. Its herds will rank correctly against one another. **Only against the outside
     world is it wrong.**"* — This is why nobody at the station could see it, and why the Alder Corners and
     Larrow Green comparisons are the only things that could have caught it.
  2. **The error is precise.** *"A leaking pipe or a slipping scale gives a scattered error, and scatter
     attracts attention. A pipette that delivers [short] where it is blown 17.6 delivers [the same amount]
     every time, for a quarter of a century, in January and in July, for a careful operator and a careless
     one. **It has the appearance of excellent work.**"* — This is the formal explanation of Keddie's flat,
     seasonless gap and of why Strawn kept finding nothing to note.
  3. **The error is invisible from inside the plant.** *"Every other element of the test can be verified
     against a standard. The scales can be checked with test weights. The tester's speed can be counted. The
     acid can be checked for strength. The bottle's graduation can be checked with mercury. But the pipette is
     not checked against anything, because in the ordinary practice of the trade **the pipette is the
     standard**. It is what the plant measures with. **An instrument that is itself the measure cannot be
     caught by measuring with it.** It can be caught only by a burette brought in from outside, and burettes
     reached the state's field men very late in this history."*

**THE CONCLUDING PARAGRAPH OF BULLETIN 471, quoted identically by r04, r07 Item 15 and r11 §VII — three
transcriptions, one document:**
> *"No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the
> twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short
> by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in
> the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."*
> — L. VAUGHT

## 7. STORY THREAD D — THE 1919 CIRCULAR AND THE KEDDIE–BRANT RUPTURE

- **1902**: complaints began — "it was first said down at Larrow Green that Ostrey Hollow milk did not test
  with the rest" (r03). **r09 gives the other side and a name: "The first men to say so out loud said it in
  1902, and they were laughed at, and one of them was my father [Emmet Brant]."** **r12 confirms the year and
  the direction of the complaint: "From 1902 the men of the Hollow complained that their milk tested lower at
  their own station than the same class of milk tested at the Green. They were not believed."** Three sources,
  one year.
- **1908**: the Grigg committee and the wet-hay report (above), sampling January–March only.
- **1919**: a printed circular went from the Association, **over the manager's signature — Selby Vose** — to
  **nine families** at Ostrey Hollow, about **water in the cans.** Confirmed by r01, r03, r06, r09, r12 and by
  Vose himself in r08.
- **THE DECISIVE DOCUMENT ON THE WATERING CHARGE, CONTEMPORANEOUS.** Strawn, **1919**: "Ostrey Hollow. Scales
  true. Lactometer, no added water. **The manager of the Association has sent a circular round to the patrons
  of this station about water in the cans. I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made
  to it and I have never found any, and I told him so.**"
  - The state's own officer, on the ground, in the year of the circular, recording that (a) he had tested for
    added water at Ostrey Hollow on **every visit**, (b) he had **never found any**, and (c) he **told the
    manager so** — i.e. told **Selby Vose**, whose signature was on the paper.
  - It converts the 1919 circular from an honest mistake into something worse: the accusation was **known to
    be unsupported by the state's own testing at the time it was made**, and no source records any retraction.
  - The Association's *own* 1908 committee had already reported "the milk is whole."
  - And over the ridge, Jerome Cudd was taking the same four men's milk every summer and never once docked a
    cent (r10).
- **THE TEXTS, WHICH DO NOT MATCH.** No source reproduces the circular. Four characterizations:
  - r01/r03: it said **there was water in their cans** — an accusation.
  - r09 (Roy Brant): "the manager sent a paper round to the Hollow patrons **warning us against putting water
    in the milk. Nine families at the head of the valley got that paper and nine families read it for what it
    was.**"
  - r12 (Rundle, 1939, neutral village register): "**the manager circulated a warning to the Hollow patrons
    against adding water to their milk, and after that the two ends of this valley did not speak civilly to
    one another for six years.**" (1919 + 6 = 1925, the arbitration — a nice independent bracket on the feud.)
  - r08 (Vose, 1926, defending it): "**It warned the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, in temperate language, that
    added water would be looked for and would be dealt with. It named nobody. It accused nobody. It said that
    this Association pays for butterfat and cannot pay for creek water, and I would write every word of it
    again tomorrow. A manager who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing to the men
    who make them is not being kind to them; he is being idle at their expense.**"
  **Resolution: both are true of the same paper.** A warning addressed to exactly nine named-by-address
  families that added water "would be looked for and would be dealt with" names nobody and accuses everybody
  who received it. Roy Brant's "nine families read it for what it was" is the honest reconciliation. **Vose's
  claim that it "accused nobody" is a description of the wording, not of the act.**
- Vira Toland's scene: it rained that day; her father read it at the table **with his hat still on, which he
  never did**; her mother stood at the stove with her back turned; he read it twice, then put it in the drawer
  of the desk in the front room, **where it still was when he died**; her brother may have it yet. Some kept
  theirs till they died; **one woman kept hers pinned inside the lid of her trunk.** "You can tell people a
  thing like that is only business and they will still keep the paper forty years to prove what was said of
  them." (Unnoticed echo: Emmet Brant also wrote **with his hat still on**, at the kitchen table after the
  second milking, in the lamp, and his wife would not have the table cleared till he was done.)
- **Rundle on how far it reached:** *"I have had the children of both ends in one schoolroom through those
  years and I can tell you that it came down to them entire, without a word of it being explained, the way a
  debt does."*
- Keddie on the circular: "I carry no charge against any man who put his name to that paper. I only say that I
  stood in that room every morning of those years and I know what came into it, and that **nine kitchens in
  this Hollow have had that letter in a drawer ever since**."
- **THE RUPTURE.** **Orra Keddie was Ansel Keddie's wife and Emmet Brant's sister.** The man who tested the
  milk had married the sister of the man the letter cost most. Before 1919 the two families ate Sunday dinners
  together, "the two houses in one kitchen and the men out at the fence after." **After 1919 they did not
  speak — not at the store, not at a funeral. Orra would walk down to Larrow Green rather than pass her
  brother's lane.** They lived within sight of each other's chimney smoke and never said good morning.
  - Keddie: "My wife Orra is Emmet Brant's sister. Emmet brought more milk to my door than any other man on the
    Bottom, and every pound of it I weighed myself and every test of it I made myself and wrote in the book.
    **He and I have not spoken since the circular.** That is the plainest fact in this letter and it is the one
    I find I have the least to say about."
  - **Roy Brant supplies the reason, first-hand from his father's side:** *"He and Ansel Keddie were
    brothers-in-law, and from 1919 they did not speak — **not from any quarrel over the water, but because my
    father held that the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did
    not.**"* **The rupture was not about the accusation but about Keddie's silence in the face of it.** Keddie
    himself never states a cause; Roy does, and he is the only source who does. Treat as the best available
    account of Emmet Brant's motive, and note that Keddie's own letter — "I carry no charge against any man
    who put his name to that paper" — is consistent with a man who had declined to fight it.
- Orra outlived Ansel "a good while" and carried the stigma the whole time (r01).

## 8. STORY THREAD E — THE PETITION, THE ARBITRATION, AND THE DISMISSAL (1925), AND VOSE'S 1926 ADDRESS

- **January 1925**: the patrons of Ostrey Hollow petitioned the Association: **their milk had never tested as
  their neighbors' milk tested and they had been paid accordingly for a very long time**; they asked to be
  made whole (r02 §6). Teague: "The patrons petitioned in the winter, when a farmer has time to be angry."
  **Vose confirms the month and year in 1926**; **Rundle confirms it in 1939: "In January of 1925 the Hollow
  patrons petitioned the Association."**
- **NEW AND IMPORTANT — THE PETITION'S OWN DOLLAR FIGURE (r12):** *"The station took in, over the years
  complained of, forty-four million pounds of milk. **The patrons' loss upon it was nineteen thousand six
  hundred dollars, and their petition set the figure out to the dollar.**"* This is the **claimed** loss, from
  the patrons' side, in January 1925 — the only place in the corpus where the petition's own arithmetic
  surfaces. See §9(d) for how it sits against the corrected $19,000, and note that Rundle's milk figure in the
  same sentence (44 million) is the rejected one, so the $19,600 may well rest on the same bad intake number.
  **Record it as the patrons' claim, not as the truth.**
- **The board of arbitration sat at Ashlin under Alonzo Frick, attorney, of that place, from 17 March to 4 May
  1925** (r02 §6; r07 Item 7 confirms the dates from the minute book's own title; r12 confirms "at Ashlin that
  spring under Alonzo Frick, attorney, of that town"). Teague calls Frick "as fair a chairman as ever a
  hearing had"; Vose calls him "an able and impartial man."
- **Three explanations were argued before it. None of the three was accepted.** Loomis declines to rehearse
  them; "they are in the minutes and the minutes are in the safe." **STILL A LIVE GAP — the three explanations
  are named nowhere in r01–r12.** Presumptively the wet hay, and watering/theft; the third is unknown. (r08's
  "we were told it was the wet hay, or the tester, or the office, or the weather" lists four candidate
  explanations circulating in the valley, but Vose is not describing the board's three.)
- The award, recorded by Loomis on the last day — **transcribed identically by Nym from the minute book (r07
  Item 7), two independent transcriptions:**
  > *"The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of
  > the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of
  > thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account."*
  **Rundle's 1939 paraphrase matches in substance: "It heard everybody. It found nothing established, it made
  an award to the patrons without admitting anything, and it sent this valley home to live with itself, which
  the valley has done, after a fashion, ever since."**
- Loomis: "*Finds no cause established*, and then an award of thirty-eight hundred dollars: I took it down as
  it was given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once."
- Keddie on the same paradox: "**I was put out on the thirtieth of April.** They found no cause established and
  they put me out on the last day of the month, and both of those things are true at once, and I have given up
  trying to hold them in my head together."
- **Chronology oddity**: the board sat until **4 May 1925** and the award was recorded on the last day, but
  **Keddie was put out on 30 April 1925** — four days *before* the award. The dismissal preceded the finding.
  Note also that **Vose in 1926 says the board "found no cause established against the station, against the
  office, or against any officer or servant of this Association"** — while the servant it exonerated had
  already been dismissed.
- **r08 gives the Association's official reading of the award, and it is a startling one:** *"It was, I say to
  you frankly, a generous settlement of an unproved claim. This Association paid thirty-eight hundred dollars
  rather than let a body of its own members go on believing they had been used badly by their neighbors. There
  is not a business house in Ordell County that would have done it. We did it because we are not a business
  house; we are the farmers of this valley in company together, and a company that will not spend money to
  keep peace among its own members has not understood what it is for."* Set against **$19,000 truly owed**
  (and against the patrons' own claim of $19,600), this is the key moral inversion in the whole record: **the
  Association believed itself generous while paying a fifth.**
- Teague was called and gave his evidence; the award was charged to the station account and he entered it "and
  it balanced."
- **The crate of condemned glass, in the loft since June 1923, was never mentioned in any of the three
  sessions.** The station's *test book* was impounded in March 1925 (r07 Item 3), so the board had the book
  and not the glass.
- **A second, worse silence.** Nothing in r06 records Strawn being called, consulted, or asked for his books;
  his 1925 extract mentions only a routine fall visit and the new tester. The board sat seven weeks on the
  question of why Ostrey Hollow milk tested low, while **the state's inspector for the county — who had
  condemned the station's entire crate of pipettes twenty-one months earlier, who had told the manager in 1919
  there was no water in the cans, and whose dated book said both in plain words — was on his circuit and was
  never asked.** The board "had before it the books and appliances of the station" and did not look at the
  appliances that had been condemned, nor send for the man who condemned them. **Vose's 1926 claim that the
  board "heard everybody who wished to be heard" is true only in the narrowest sense, and his claim that its
  sentence is "the only sentence in this business that was arrived at by men under oath with the books in
  front of them" is false as to the appliances.** (Rundle's "It heard everybody" reproduces the same
  comfortable formula in 1939.)
- **Strawn's own explanation of the silence (1936 covering note):** "I have been asked once or twice, **by men
  who ought to know better**, to say in a general way how a certain plant stood in my estimation over the
  years, and I have always answered that **I do not keep an estimation, I keep a book, and the book is open to
  anybody the state sends for it.** I never had a theory about a plant and I do not recommend having one. A
  theory is a thing a man carries out to the platform with him and it gets in the way of the weights." And:
  "The state pays a field man to weigh and to look, and **every time in my life I have seen a field man paid
  for an opinion instead, the opinion has outlived the weighing and done harm after the man was dead.**"
  (Which is exactly what happened to Teague's opinion and Keddie's name.)

### Conflicts on this thread
- **Award amount: $3,500 (r01, Vira, from memory: "they paid the patrons thirty-five hundred dollars and
  called it settled… split up nine ways over all those years — my father's share of it did not buy a hay
  rake") vs $3,800 (r02 quoted award; r04; r05; r07 Item 7 quoted award; r08 Vose twice). Resolve for
  $3,800** — two independent transcriptions of the award document plus the manager's printed address plus the
  bookkeeper's entry, against one child's memory. Vira is a single non-independent source, demonstrably wrong
  elsewhere. (r12 gives no figure for the award.)
- **Date/order of the hearing: Vira places it "the winter and spring after" 1919 — i.e. 1919/20.** Wrong by six
  years; the hearing is fixed at 1925 by the minutes, Keddie's dated statement, Teague, Nym's minute-book
  title, Vose's address, Rundle's pamphlet, and r06's "new man at the tester's bench in 1925."
- **Cause of the tests rising.** Vira: "the tests went up… all at once, one summer… and nobody ever said why,"
  read as the correction that followed Keddie's removal ("He was taking the cream. They caught him at it and
  they put him out, and that is why the numbers went right the summer after"). **Refuted on its own timeline**:
  the glass was condemned **12 June 1923**, ordered **28 June**, and new glass was in use **from July 1923** —
  the tests rose in mid-1923, **two years before the 1925 hearing and Keddie's dismissal.** Her memory of *a
  summer when the numbers went right* is accurate; her attribution is six years and one causal arrow wrong.
  **Rundle's 1939 pamphlet shows the same folk-syllogism in its finished form — "the figures went right when
  the man who kept it did not" — which is how Vira came to have it.** Rundle then refutes it himself.
- Vira's own father would not say it out loud: he "said Ansel Keddie was an honest man who had got into
  something, which is a nice way of saying it and I never saw the difference."

### 8A. THE MANAGER'S 1926 ADDRESS (r08) — what it claims, and where it fails
The Association's official position, printed, one year after the arbitration. The clearest evidence of *why*
the truth stayed buried for another thirty-two years.
- **He revives and doubles down on the watering theory.** *"Water was going into cans at the head of that
  valley and it stopped when the Association began to look. The rise of 1924 is the measure of it… Nothing was
  done to those cows. Nothing was done to that bottom land. Nobody changed their feed and nobody changed their
  pasture. The tests came up when the looking began, and any man who wants a different explanation of that is
  welcome to stand where I am standing and give us one."* And: *"A little water in a can is not a felony in a
  man's own mind. It is a way of making a hard year come out even, and the man who does it tells himself he
  will stop when the price improves. I have no wish to see anybody prosecuted, and nobody has been or will
  be."* He says it "without naming a name, because I do not know a name and I would not use it if I did."
- **Refuted comprehensively.** Strawn's lactometer found no added water at Ostrey Hollow on every visit,
  including 1912, 1916, 1919, 1920 (twice), 1922, 1923 ("as always here"), **1924 and 1925** — the very years
  Vose says the watering "stopped"; the Association's own 1908 committee reported "the milk is whole"; Strawn
  told Vose personally in 1919; Jerome Cudd took the same milk sixteen summers and never docked a cent; and
  Bulletin 471 (1958): "**No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow.**"
- **ERROR 1 — the date of the condemnation.** Vose: "he condemned the station's testing apparatus **on the
  twelfth of July, 1923**, and told us to order new. **We ordered new inside the month** and had it in use
  before the flush was over." True date **12 June 1923**. He has the day right and the month wrong — **and his
  own "inside the month" is only true of June**, since the order went on **28 June**. Internally inconsistent.
  (This is the error Rundle repeats in 1939.)
- **ERROR 2 — the 1924 average, wrong twice over.** Vose: "**the tests at that station rose by twenty-one
  hundredths and the average for 1924 stood at 3.90.**" But (i) 3.61 + 0.21 = 3.82, not 3.90; and 3.90 − 3.61
  = 0.29, not 0.21 — **his two figures do not agree with each other**; and (ii) the true 1924 average is
  **3.80**, given independently by Keddie's letter and by Strawn reading the station's own book on the spot in
  1924. **Resolve for 3.80, a rise of 0.19.** Vose is inflating the level and understating the step to make the
  rise look like a behavioral correction rather than an instrument correction.
- **ERROR 3 — the causal chronology is impossible on his own facts.** He argues the tests rose because "the
  looking began" — the petition (January 1925) and the hearing (March–May 1925). But **the 1924 average
  predates both.** And the glass changed in mid-1923, before even that. Every event he offers as cause comes
  after the effect. (And Alder Corners, on independent glass, shows no step in 1923–24 at all.)
- **He was offered the correct explanation in 1926 and publicly refused it.** *"I am told, and I have been told
  it kindly and by men who mean well, that the state man's visit of that period explains the whole thing. It
  does not… **Apparatus wears.** A station in daily use since 1896 will wear its fittings out twice over in
  that time, and it is the state man's business to say so before they fail him, and ours to replace them when
  he does. **I decline to build a mystery on a piece of housekeeping that appears in our own order book with a
  date and a price against it.**"* Three things follow. (a) By 1926 someone in the valley had already
  identified the apparatus as the cause; **the correct answer was on the table thirty-two years before Ivy
  proved it, and the manager killed it from the platform.** (b) "Apparatus wears" is refuted by the 1897
  invoice: the pipettes were blown to 17.6 c.c. and short from new, in use from 1 May 1897 — the same date
  Bulletin 471 gives for the start of the error. Glass stems do not shorten with use; and Vaught's mechanism
  (§6) shows the error was fixed and precise, not progressive. (c) **There exists an Association order book
  with a date and a price for the replacement glass**, which no source quotes; the price is unknown.
- **The Jersey herd — a real 1910 figure, and a trap.** *"My father… kept a herd of Jerseys at the home place,
  as some of you remember, and I kept them after him. **In 1910 that herd tested 4.56.** I do not bring that
  forward to boast of the cows… I bring it forward because it settles a thing that gets said about this county
  in the trade below us — that the Larrow Valley is poor milk country… That is not true. There is no better
  milk in the state than the milk this valley can produce when it comes to the platform as it left the cow."*
  - **4.56 is exactly Ivy's first worked example** ("a herd recorded at 4.56 was short 0.24 of a point, true
    4.80"), just as **3.42 is exactly Emmet Brant's 1910 test**. So Ivy's two illustrations are both real 1910
    figures from named herds. (Check on Set A: 4.56 ÷ 0.95 = 4.80 exactly, short 0.24 ✓ — Ivy's example only
    works on the 0.95 ratio, which is a further check on §6.)
  - **But the Vose herd was at the home place, i.e. delivering to Larrow Green, not to Ostrey Hollow** — on
    sound glass. If so, **4.56 was true, not depressed, and Ivy's use of it (if she applied the correction to
    it) is an error**; more likely she used it counterfactually — "a herd recorded at 4.56 *would have been*
    short 0.24" — to make Vaught's point that a proportional error falls hardest on the best milk. **No source
    states which. Flag.** Either way the irony stands: the manager's boast that his own Jerseys made 4.56
    while the Hollow made 3.42 compares a sound instrument with a short one, and he offered it as proof the
    Hollow's problem was the men.
- His closing ask: *"Bring it as it left the cow. Wash the cans, cool the milk, keep the dog out of the milk
  house, and bring it as it left the cow. Do that, and the Ostrey Hollow tests will stay where they went in
  1924, and this business will be dead and buried where it belongs."*
- Ordinary business of 1926, for colour: the season opened wet and stayed wet into June; hauling heavy on the
  horses and heavier on the men; the Larrow Green creamery ran through the flush without a day lost; the ice
  went into the house early and lasted; the Ostrey Hollow station "ran its season out and its cream came down
  to us daily and sweet."

- **Bulletin No. 471**, issued **October 1958**, concluding paragraph as quoted in §6.
- Ivy's tally: **glass condemned 1923, cause found 1958 — thirty-five years.** Grandfather dismissed 1925,
  cleared 1958 — **thirty-three years.** He **died in 1934** and had been **dead twenty-four years** when the
  sheet was read that clears him. "I have no remedy to propose. I have only the crate, and it is on the table."
- **Keddie's death year quietly corroborated.** Strawn, **1934**: "A separator bearing run hot and shut down
  two days, since repaired. **The tester's book is kept up, though not in the hand it used to be kept in.**"
  Consistent with r04's precise 1934 and against r01's vague "in the thirties." (Keddie had left the bench in
  1925, so the remark is about the book, not his death — but the elegiac note is there.)
- **Rundle's 1959 footnote closes the circle from the valley's side:** *"Added to the 1959 reprint. The Hollow
  matter has since been taken up again at the college at Farland by Miss Ivy Keddie, who is second cousin to
  Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom. Eleven pipettes out of the station's old condemned crate were found still to
  be in existence. I am too old to rewrite a pamphlet and I refer the reader to the college. — D.R."*
- **Rundle's peroration (1939), which is the valley's own best defence of itself:** *"Set against the quarrel,
  put the rest: fifty Junes of cream coming down that road in the early cool, fifty Januaries of it going down
  over ice with the horses roughshod, a check every month in kitchens that had known none, and a creamery
  still standing that was built by men with nothing but each other and a piece of paper they had written
  themselves. Whatever this valley did wrong at the head of it, it did this right, and it did it for half a
  century, and the men of 1889 would think we had done well."*

## 9. THE ARITHMETIC — C1 AND C2 WORKED (re-derived here; see the GAP note at the top)

### C1(a) — the proportion, and how the glassware gives it
The pipettes were **blown to 17.6 c.c.** (stamped on the stems; confirmed by the 1897 invoice line "6 doz.
milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c."). Ivy's nine survivors, delivered against a calibrated burette, five runs each,
gave a **mean of 16.72 c.c.** (readings 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71; sum
150.48; 150.48 ÷ 9 = 16.72).
> **16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly.**
So each charge delivered **95 hundredths of the mark**, i.e. was **short by 0.88 c.c., one part in twenty**.
Because the bottle's neck was ruled to read percent *for a 17.6 c.c. charge and no other*, every reading came
out at **0.95 of the truth**: the tests were **low by 5 per cent — proportionally, not by a fixed number of
points**. Correction rule: **true = recorded ÷ 0.95** (equivalently, recorded × 1.0526316).
*Cross-check without the glassware at all:* the Association's own ledger has credited 57,760 and delivered
60,800; **57,760 ÷ 60,800 = 0.95**. Two entirely separate routes to the same ratio.
*Reject 16.27 / 0.9244 / "short by 1.33" — see §6.*

### C1(b) — the true average, and the points short
Recorded station average over the affected years: **3.61** (the 1922 summary leaf; "3.61 or within three
hundredths of it every year since [1898]"; Nym's gloss; Loomis; Ivy; Vaught).
> **3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80 exactly.**
> **Shortfall = 3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19 of a point (nineteen hundredths).**
Independently confirmed at 3.80 four ways: 1896 first season on borrowed Larrow Green glass; the nine weeks of
1911 on a borrowed measure; 1924, the first full year on new glass (Keddie's letter; Strawn reading the book);
and the Alder Corners sixteen-season mean of the same men's milk on independent glass.
*(Distinguish from Roy Brant's 0.17, which is Ostrey Hollow recorded against Larrow Green recorded, a different
comparison — see §3.)*

### C1(c) — pounds of butterfat not credited, over the full delivery years affected
Intake: **40,000,000 lb of milk** over the **twenty-five full delivery years, 1898–1922** (Nym, from the
ledgers: "the station's intake for the years 1898 to 1922 is given in these ledgers as 40,000,000 lb"; Vaught:
"the 40,000,000 lb of milk received at the station in the twenty-five full delivery years"; Loomis; Ivy).
> **40,000,000 × 0.19% = 40,000,000 × 0.0019 = 76,000 lb of butterfat.**
**Independent check from the ledger, which is exact:** the station-gain entry runs **3,040 lb a year**, "and
there is one like it for every year after" (Teague), "every year" (Nym), "every year" (Rundle) —
> **3,040 × 25 = 76,000 lb.** ✓
And the per-year figures close too: 1,600,000 lb of milk × 3.61% = 57,760 lb credited; × 3.80% = 60,800 lb
delivered; difference 3,040 lb; and 1,600,000 × 25 = 40,000,000 ✓.
**76,000 lb is also the figure printed in Bulletin 471's own concluding paragraph**, quoted identically by
three sources. Three independent routes, one answer.
*Boundary note:* the error ran **1 May 1897 – 12 June 1923**, so 1897 and 1923 are part-years lying outside the
25 full years. The question asks for "the full delivery years affected," which is 1898–1922 = 25 years =
76,000 lb. The two part-years would add roughly a further year's worth between them (order 3,000 lb) but **no
source quantifies them and they should not be invented.**

### C1(d) — the money, and the fraction the patrons got
**Butterfat price: 25 cents a pound.** *(This is the price the chain has been carrying, and every figure closes
on it. I flag honestly that the sentence citing it sat in the part of the predecessor's notes past my read
limit — the index line said r09 supplies the 1910 butterfat price — so the citation, though not the number, is
second-hand to me. Every consumer of these notes should say so.)*
> **76,000 lb × $0.25 = $19,000.**
> **The award was $3,800** (quoted award document, two transcriptions; Teague's entry; Vose twice).
> **$3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20 exactly — the patrons received ONE FIFTH, 20 per cent, of what they were owed.**
The remaining $15,200 stayed where it had always been: on the Association's general account, where it had been
carried year by year as "station gain."
**Against the patrons' own claim:** Rundle records that the January 1925 petition "set the figure out to the
dollar" at **$19,600**. $3,800 ÷ $19,600 = 19.4 per cent. **Note the two figures are close but not equal, and
that Rundle's own sentence pairs $19,600 with the rejected 44-million-pound intake, so the petition's
arithmetic may rest on the wrong intake number.** Candidate reconciliations (none stated by any source, all
speculative, do not assert): the patrons may have included the 1897 and 1923 part-years; or used a slightly
different price; or worked from the 44-million figure with a smaller assumed differential. **What is
established: the corrected loss is $19,000 at 25c; the patrons themselves asked for $19,600; they were given
$3,800; and Selby Vose called that "a generous settlement of an unproved claim."**

### C2 — the largest patron's year of 1910 — WHAT IS ESTABLISHED AND WHAT IS MISSING
**The largest patron is Emmet Brant** — "the station's largest patron in every year of which we have a record"
(r02 §5); "sixty-odd acres of the meadow, and thirty-eight cows in a good year, which for this valley was a
herd" (r09); most cows and most hay ground (r01); "brought more milk to my door than any other man on the
Bottom" (r03). His deliveries are recorded twice over: in the station test book and in **his own weigh book**,
kept at Hessel Bottom, later Roy Brant's, lent to Ivy in 1958 and returned, to Hazel Brant in 1962 (r07 Item 4).
**His recorded 1910 test was 3.42** — one of Ivy's two worked examples, and the figure is real, from a named
herd, in the same year as Selby Vose's 4.56.
> **True test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60 exactly.**
> **Points not credited = 3.60 − 3.42 = 0.18 of a point.**
> (Note the pattern: the proportional error takes 0.18 from a 3.60 herd and 0.24 from a 4.80 herd — Vaught's
> "rich milk loses more, absolutely." Emmet Brant's own deficit was *smaller* per hundredweight than the
> manager's would have been, which is a fact the manager never had to face.)
**The one input I cannot supply is his 1910 milk poundage.** The predecessor's index said r09 closes it; the
sentence carrying it fell past my read limit and it appears nowhere in r10, r11 or r12. **Do not guess it.**
Once it is recovered, call it **M** lb of milk and the three answers fall out in one line each:
- **(a) pounds credited = M × 3.42% = 0.0342 M**
- **(b) pounds owed and not credited = M × 0.18% = 0.0018 M** (equivalently, credited × 1/19 = credited ×
  0.0526316; equivalently, credited ÷ 0.95 − credited)
- **(c) money = 0.0018 M × $0.25 = $0.00045 M** (equivalently, the pounds in (b) × 25 cents)
**Sanity bracket only, NOT an answer and NOT from any source:** a 38-cow herd of the period would run in the
low hundreds of thousands of pounds a year, which would put (a) in the thousands of pounds, (b) in the
hundreds, and (c) in the tens of dollars. **If the poundage cannot be recovered, the honest answer to C2 is
that (a), (b) and (c) cannot be determined from the sources in hand, while stating the 3.42 recorded, the 3.60
true, the 0.18-point deficit, the 25c price and the formulas above** — that is most of the credit and none of
the fabrication.

## 10. RELATIONSHIP EVIDENCE (for B1–B4)

**B1 — Ivy Keddie to Ansel Keddie. CLOSED: granddaughter (son's daughter).**
Ivy states it in her own first paragraph: "**Ansel Keddie, who kept the test book at the Ostrey Hollow
skimming station, was my grandfather. My father, Duncan Keddie, who taught school at Ashlin, was his son.**"
She says it once and sets it aside — "It depends on nine pieces of glass and a burette."
**Independently confirmed by r09:** "**Duncan Keddie, who taught school at Ashlin, was Ansel and Orra's boy**,
and he and I are first cousins," and the 1958 postscript "**Duncan's girl Ivy** came out to the place this
year." **The thing pointing elsewhere**: Vira Toland describes the researcher who came in 1958 as "**Hazel
Brant's first cousin once removed**, so she was **of the family both sides** in a manner of speaking." That is
not a contradiction of B1 — it is a maternal-line description of the same woman — but it is **wrong on its own
terms** (see B2). Vira never names her.
*Note the small pleasing consistency across three generations of Keddies: Ansel keeps a book in his own hand
and is faulted by nobody who watched him; Duncan teaches school at Ashlin; Ivy measures nine pipettes against
a burette and signs the sheet.*

**B2 — Ivy Keddie to Hazel Brant. CLOSED: SECOND COUSINS.**
**r09, from the man who is father to one of them and first cousin to the other's father:** *"Orra Keddie was
my father's sister. **They were full brother and sister, same mother and same father**, whatever you may hear
from people who like a story with a stepmother in it. …Duncan Keddie… was Ansel and Orra's boy, and **he and I
are first cousins**. **My daughter Hazel and his daughter Ivy are second cousins**, and I mention it because
both of them have had to have it explained to them by me more than once."*
Derivation: Emmet Brant and Orra (Brant) Keddie were **full siblings**. Orra married Ansel Keddie; their son
is **Duncan**. Emmet's son is **Roy**. Duncan and Roy are **first cousins**. Duncan's daughter **Ivy** and
Roy's daughter **Hazel** are **second cousins**. Ivy is therefore Emmet Brant's **great-niece**; Roy Brant is
Ivy's **first cousin once removed**.
**NOW INDEPENDENTLY CONFIRMED BY r12's 1959 footnote: "Miss Ivy Keddie, who is second cousin to Hazel Brant
of Hessel Bottom."** Rundle is a Larrow Green schoolteacher with no stake in the Brant–Keddie line, writing
twenty years after his pamphlet and one year after Ivy's work; he is not repeating Roy Brant so far as
anything shows. **Two independent sources for second cousins.**
**Resolve against Vira's "first cousin once removed."** Roy is inside the relation on both sides and says
explicitly that he has had to explain it to both women more than once — i.e. he is correcting exactly the
confusion Vira reproduces; and Rundle, outside the family, has it right. Vira's phrase is a villager's
approximation — and note that "first cousin once removed" is precisely the relation between **Roy** and
**Ivy**, so Vira has plausibly slid one generation.
**Independently consistent with the weigh book's descent (r07 Item 4): to Roy Brant in 1929, lent to Ivy
Keddie in 1958 and returned, to Miss Hazel Brant in 1962** — matching r09's "It goes to Hazel when I am done
with it… after me it is Hazel's." (So Roy relinquished it, presumably by death, between 1958 and 1962.)
**What the sources still leave open on the intervening generations:** Orra and Emmet's parents are never named
and no dates are given for them; whether Orra and Emmet had **other** siblings is never stated (Roy says only
that those two were full brother and sister); Orra's own birth and death dates are unknown ("outlived Ansel a
good while"); Duncan's dates are never given, nor his wife, nor Ivy's mother; Roy's wife and Hazel's mother
are never named; Hazel's dates are never given; and Emmet Brant's own death date is nowhere stated (the weigh
book passes to Roy in 1929, which is suggestive but not stated as the reason). **And note the loose end Roy
raises and dismisses without explaining: "whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother
in it" — some version circulating in the valley made Orra and Emmet half-siblings. Roy denies it flatly and
no source supports it.**

**B3 — Selby Vose's father. CLOSED: Gideon Vose's SON.**
- r01 (Vira, hearsay): "The manager that signed it was Selby Vose, down at Larrow Green. He was **old Gideon
  Vose's nephew**."
- **r12 (Rundle, 1939, valley memory tidied): "His nephew Selby Vose, afterward manager of the Association,
  used to say that his uncle wrote by-laws the way other men build stone wall."** — the same claim, twice over
  in one sentence ("nephew," "his uncle").
- r02 (Loomis, from the minutes): "**Selby Vose, son of the late president**, was made manager in 1913 and
  served until 1938." The late president at Loomis's writing is Gideon Vose (president 1889–1911, died 1917).
- **r08 (Selby Vose himself, printed, 1926): "My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law
  under which every patron in this Association is paid on the test of the station where he delivers, kept a
  herd of Jerseys at the home place… and I kept them after him."**
**Resolve for SON.** The man's own printed statement about his own father, delivered from the platform to the
members in the room who knew the family, plus the secretary's minute-book entry, against **two non-independent
village recollections**: Vira's is a child's memory sixty years on, demonstrably wrong on the award figure and
the hearing date; Rundle's is the same Larrow Green talk written down, in a pamphlet whose author says in his
own preface that it is "not a record… it is what the valley remembers, tidied." That the two agree adds
nothing — they are one channel. r06 independently confirms the 1913 date of the managership without touching
parentage. **Note the specific irony: the erroneous "nephew" version comes attached in r12 to a saying
attributed to Selby himself, and Selby's own printed words in r08 say "my father."**

**Why it matters at Ostrey Hollow**: the man who wrote **Article VII**, whose last clause made the station's
own test unappealable, was Gideon Vose; his son Selby was the manager who (a) **signed the annual station-gain
sheet every spring** for twenty-five years (Teague), (b) was **told of the discrepancy by Teague in 1916 and
said to let it alone**, (c) **signed the 1919 circular** about water in the cans, (d) **was told to his face by
the state's inspector in 1919 that there was no added water at that station and never had been**, (e) **was
the officer Strawn "told to order new" glass on 12 June 1923** and who signed off the reorder of 28 June, so
he knew the station's measures had been condemned twenty-one months before the arbitration at which the crate
was never mentioned, and (f) **in 1926, from the platform, publicly rejected the apparatus explanation when it
was offered to him and re-asserted the watering charge instead.** The rule that trapped the patrons and the
officer who declined to look behind it were father and son. Vira's independent sense of it: "the family always
thought the valley belonged to them a little." Selby also kept his father's Jersey herd at the home place —
i.e. delivering to Larrow Green on sound glass, which is the herd that tested 4.56 in 1910 while the Hollow's
largest patron was recorded at 3.42.

**B4 — the Vose family and the Keddie family.**
*(The predecessor's treatment of B4 fell past my read limit; what follows is assembled from the retained
material and should be checked against anything the earlier chain preserved.)*
**No blood or marriage tie between the Voses and the Keddies is stated or implied anywhere in r01–r12.** The
two families are connected only by office and by consequence:
- **Employer and servant.** Gideon Vose's Association hired Ansel Keddie to run and test the Ostrey Hollow
  station from 4 May 1896; Selby Vose was Keddie's manager from 1913 until Keddie was put out on 30 April 1925.
- **Rule-maker and rule-victim.** Gideon Vose wrote Article VII; its last clause ("the test of the station
  where he delivers shall govern") made Keddie's own reading final and unappealable, which is what turned a
  short pipette into twenty-six years of unrecoverable loss and made Keddie personally the face of it.
- **Accuser and accused, at one remove.** Selby Vose signed the 1919 circular that the Hollow read as an
  accusation; Keddie stood in the room the circular was about, said afterward he carried no charge against any
  man who signed it, and lost his brother-in-law over having said nothing at the time.
- **The two 1910 herds.** Vose's Jerseys at the home place, on Larrow Green's sound glass, recorded 4.56; the
  Hollow's largest patron, on the short glass, recorded 3.42. The manager used the contrast as proof the
  valley could make good milk and the trouble was the men.
- **Asymmetry of outcome.** Keddie was dismissed four days before a board found no cause established, died in
  1934, and was cleared in 1958, twenty-four years dead. Selby Vose served as manager until 1938 and was
  exonerated in the same sentence that cleared the man he had already dismissed.
**Kinship between the two families: none in evidence — and note that Ivy Keddie, who ended the matter, was
connected by blood to the *Brants* (B2), not to the Voses.** If a question asks whether the families were
related, "no relationship is established by the sources, and none is claimed by any of them" is the correct
answer, not a guess.

## 11. WORKING PARTITION OF THE EIGHT STORIES (hypothesis, for Section A)

Section A asks for **the first three of the eight, in the order they occurred.** No source lists the eight.
My working partition, offered so the next reader has something to argue with rather than a blank page:
1. **The founding and Article VII (1889–1892)** — Gideon Vose, the schoolhouse meeting, the 1891 creamery, the
   by-law and its last clause, the lost 1889–92 minute book.

## 12. STANDING OPEN QUESTIONS (carry forward)

1. **Emmet Brant's 1910 milk poundage at Ostrey Hollow** — needed for C2(a)–(c). Said to be in r09. Not
   recoverable from what this segment holds. **Highest-priority missing number.**
2. **The citation for the 25c/lb butterfat price** — the number closes every calculation and is carried by the
   chain, but the sentence naming its source fell outside my read span. Believed to be r09.
3. **The board's three explanations** are named nowhere in r01–r12. Loomis will not rehearse them ("they are
   in the minutes and the minutes are in the safe"). Two are presumptively wet hay and watering/theft; the
   third is unknown. **Do not invent it.**
4. **Ivy's "fourteen overlapping seasons"** against sixteen — unexplained by any source.
5. **Teague's "my own device… I began it when I came in 1905"** against the 1898 entry he quotes and Nym's
   station column running from 1897.
6. **Whether Ivy applied the 0.95 correction to Vose's 4.56 herd** (which was on sound glass at Larrow Green)
   or used it counterfactually. No source says.
7. **The price paid for the replacement glass in 1923** — Vose says it "appears in our own order book with a
   date and a price against it"; no source quotes the order book.
8. **The 1889–1892 minute book** (lost in the 1938 office move) and therefore any first-hand record of the
   first four annual meetings, including the adoption of Article VII.
9. **The text of the 1919 circular** — no source reproduces it; four characterizations, no document.
10. **The $19,600 in the patrons' petition** against the corrected $19,000 — the gap of $600 is unexplained,
    and Rundle pairs the claim with the rejected 44-million intake.
11. **The stepmother rumor** about Orra and Emmet Brant — raised and denied by Roy, unsupported elsewhere.
12. **The identity of the manager before Selby Vose** (Strawn's 1913 "the last one never did" proves there was
    one; nobody names him).
