## 2. STORY THREAD A — THE FOUNDING AND ARTICLE VII (1889–1892)

- **1889**: farmers met at **Larrow Green** and organized the **Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association**.
  **Gideon Vose** elected first president, held it **1889–1911** — twenty-two years, longest of any man. (r02 §1)
- **r12's scene**: they met **in the schoolhouse, in cold weather**; the old men remembered chiefly that **it went on
  very late and that somebody's horse got loose in the middle of it.** Motive: *"They were tired of hauling butter to
  Ashlin and taking whatever the merchant chose to allow them for it, and they had worked out — the way farmers do,
  slowly and then all at once — that the way to be paid properly for a thing is to own the place that buys it."*
- Before the creamery: members sold milk where they could, "no common test and no common price, and every man's
  arithmetic was his own." (r02 §2)
- **1891**: main creamery built at **Larrow Green, on Larrow Creek** (r02) / "on the creek below the mill" (r12);
  same building standing in 1941. r12: the churn was set up in it **"and the valley began to be paid in cash once a
  month, which had never happened to it before."**
  **r14 corroborates the plant physically**: Sill came to that churn room in **1893**, when "there was no floor in
  this room but plank, and we cooled with creek ice out of the pond."
- **Gideon Vose drafted the by-laws himself**; not a lawyer; "they read as a farmer wrote them" (r02). **r12's
  portrait**: *"a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the law and thought better of it, and he wrote
  the by-laws himself, at his own kitchen table, in a hand that the Association's clerks were still complaining about
  thirty years later."* r12 also reports a saying attributed to Selby Vose: that Gideon *"wrote by-laws the way other
  men build stone wall — slowly, and to last."*
- **ARTICLE VII — transcribed IDENTICALLY, word for word, by THREE independent hands**: r02 (Loomis, from the
  minutes/printed pamphlet), r07 Item 1 (Nym, from the printed pamphlet), r12 (Rundle, printed whole in 1939):
  > *"Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter
  > fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the
  > station where he delivers shall govern."*
  The wording is beyond dispute. **r13 (Frick) paraphrases it accurately from the board's own understanding**: "The
  Association pays a patron for the butter fat in his milk and not for the weight of it. The fat is found by test,
  and the test of the station where the man delivers is the test that governs him." — a fourth, legally-trained
  reading of the same clause.
- **CONFLICT ON THE DATE. r02 (Loomis, from the minute book): adopted at the annual meeting of 1892. r12 (Rundle):
  "Article VII dates from 1891, the year the churn was set up." Resolve for 1892** — Loomis is the secretary with the
  minute book in her own hand, setting the article down entire precisely because it has been misquoted at her for
  forty years; Rundle writes valley memory and ties the date to the churn by association. **Complication (r07): the
  printed by-laws carry no year on their face, only "adopted at the annual meeting"** — so 1892 rests on Loomis's
  minutes alone, and **the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the office move of 1938.** Rundle's 1891 is exactly the
  error an undated pamphlet produces. **Flag: the date rests on one document; the text does not.**
- **The last clause is the hinge.** Loomis: nobody quotes it — *"the test of the station where he delivers shall
  govern."* Not the creamery's test, not the valley average, not a re-test anywhere else. **The station's test, and no
  appeal from it.** She calls it **"the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business."** Written by a man who **died in
  1917** and could not be asked afterward.
- **Rundle reaches the same reading independently in 1939**: *"But mark the last clause… Not the test of the creamery.
  Not any test but the one made at his own door. Gideon Vose put that in for convenience and it was convenient for
  thirty years, and it is the sentence on which the whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned."* He
  also defends the rule in principle: *"A man who keeps good cows and feeds them ought not to be paid the same as the
  man who keeps poor ones, and the pound of milk is a liar where the pound of butterfat is not."* And: it was thought
  a hard rule at the time and **there were men who left the Association over it.**
- **Frick (r13) states the legal consequence more sharply than anyone**: *"I say nothing against the rule. But it
  makes the test the whole of the bargain, and it leaves a dissatisfied man with no remedy on earth except to quarrel
  with a figure."*
- **Selby Vose confirms authorship AND parentage himself, in print, 1926 (r08)**: *"My father, who was your first
  president and who wrote the by-law under which every patron in this Association is paid on the test of the station
  where he delivers…"* — contemporaneous, self-interested, decisive. **Selby is Gideon's SON, not his nephew.**
- **Records gap**: the **1889–1892 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move** — carried out with the rest and
  never arrived. Loomis searched everywhere. *"Anybody who tells you what was resolved at the first four annual
  meetings is telling you a story he had from his father."*
- Officers in Loomis's time: Loomis secretary 1901–1940; **Wilbur Teague** bookkeeper from **1905**; **Hobart Sill**
  buttermaker at Larrow Green from **1893** (churn book and cream receiving book); **Selby Vose**, "son of the late
  president," manager **1913–1938**.
- **1913 managership confirmed three ways**: Loomis's minutes; Strawn 1913 ("**New manager at the Association this
  year** and he came out with me to the platform, which the last one never did"); Vose himself in 1926 ("**It is
  thirteen years this spring, since 1913**"). The Strawn entry also establishes there *was* an unnamed predecessor.
- Loomis's confession: *"not one of us ever went up the valley and stood in the Ostrey Hollow station on a working
  morning to watch the work being done. I include myself first in that."* **Irony**: the one man who did stand there
  on working mornings, year after year, was the state's inspector — and no officer ever asked him what he had seen.
  **r14 supplies the mirror-image complaint from the floor**: *"not one man in that office ever came down here to see
  how it was done, and every one of them could tell you to the half cent what the fat was worth."*
- Office at Ashlin: one room over a harness shop, cold at the north end (r05).
- **Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green** (r09, r12): chairman of the 1908 investigating committee; presumptively the unnamed
  "chairman" who signed the report Nym transcribes.

## 3. STORY THREAD B — THE STATION, THE SHORT GLASS, AND THE 26-YEAR SHORTFALL (1896–1923)

### The station and its catchment
- **Skimming station at the head of Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green** — the distance given
  independently by r06 (1907), r08 (Vose 1926), r11 (Vaught), r12 (Rundle) **and now r13 (Frick) and r14 (Sill,
  "seven miles of hill road")** — six independent statements. Low whitewashed building, cold inside even in August,
  smelled of milk and steam and hot iron (r01). It took the upper farms' milk, **weighed it, sampled and tested it,
  separated it, sent the cream down to Larrow Green, and gave the skim back to the patron** for hogs and calves
  (r01, r02, r13).
- **r12 on why it existed**: *"in the nineties the pull down was three hours in mud and could not be done at all in
  March. So the Association built a skimming station up there, and it opened in 1896… The building is a plain one,
  boarded and battened, with the intake room at the road end and **a loft over it that I believe has never been
  cleared out since it was put up**; and behind it the creek, which in a wet April comes up to the sill and has twice
  come over it."* — written 1939, nineteen years before Ivy climbed that ladder.
- **THE CATCHMENT RECONCILES EXACTLY.** r09: *"Hessel Bottom is a hundred and sixty acres of wet meadow lying under
  the hill at Ostrey Hollow. **SIX farms** had rights on it and cut hay off it and pastured it."* Six Bottom farms +
  Strawn's **three places on the hill** (1909) = **nine families** — exactly the number on the 1919 circular (r01,
  r03, r09) and exactly Sill's "**nine families**" (r14). **Resolve for SIX Bottom farms.** The hill farms matter
  because they are *not* Bottom ground: wet-hay cannot depress hill milk, but a short pipette depresses everything
  alike.
- **The Brant farm was the largest of the six**: *"sixty-odd acres of the meadow, and thirty-eight cows in a good
  year, which for this valley was a herd"* (r09). Corroborated by r02 §5 ("the station's largest patron in every year
  of which we have a record"), r01, and r03 ("brought more milk to my door than any other man on the Bottom").
- Hessel Bottom from inside (r09): water stands on the low end into May; a soft place by the alders that has taken a
  horse to the hock; the hay is coarse and cuts late; *"a cow will eat it and do well enough, whatever has been said
  about that hay by men who never fed it."*
- **Opening date: 1896.** Vira Toland says Keddie went up "in ninety-five, the year before they opened it." **Against
  1895** stand Keddie's own first leaf and his own count (29 seasons, 1896–1924, and he "did not test a pound in the
  season of 1925"); Strawn (1907, the station "opened before my time"); Vose (r08, "a station in daily use since
  1896"); Rundle (r12, "it opened in 1896"); **Frick (r13, "since it opened in 1896")**; and **Sill (r14, "that was
  ninety-six, in May, and there was a crowd went up for it").** **Resolve for 1896 — six independent lines.**
- **First leaf of the test book**, quoted identically by r03 and r07 Item 3:
  > *"Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes.
  > A. Keddie."*
- **INTERNAL CONFLICT IN r03 — NOW SETTLED FROM THE OTHER END.** Keddie's letter-prose says the 1896 glass was
  "borrowed off the **Ashlin creamery**"; his quoted first leaf says **"borrowed of Larrow Green."** **r14 settles it
  in the lender's own voice**: *"they had no outfit of their own. Not a piece. It was ordered and it was not come…
  And I said no, they can have mine. I went to my own bench in this room and I made up an outfit for them out of my
  own glass, my own, that I had used and knew, and it went up the valley on the wagon and they opened on it. Their
  own came the spring after and they sent mine back to me, and one piece of it was chipped and I never said a word
  about that either."* **Resolve for LARROW GREEN, now from both ends of the loan.** No Ashlin creamery is attested
  anywhere. Note the mechanical consequence: **the 1896 season was run on Sill's own known-good working glass**,
  which is why 1896 reads 3.80.

### THE OUTFIT — the invoice, itemized (r07 Item 2), DECISIVE
> **Invoice, Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, Tarnet, 14 April 1897.** One leaf, folded in three.
> *Sold to the Larrow Valley Co-op. Creamery Assn., for the Ostrey Hollow station:*
> *6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20*
> *12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at .22 — 31.68*
> *1 acid measure — .90*
> *1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00*
> *Freight — 2.15*
> *Total — 77.93*

Arithmetic closes exactly: 72 × 0.35 = 25.20; 144 × 0.22 = 31.68; 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = **77.93**.
Nym adds: "the office file has [it] in use at the station **from 1 May 1897**, Larrow Green having lent until then."
That matches **Bulletin 471's concluding paragraph, 1 May 1897, exactly**, from an entirely different record, and it
matches **Sill's "their own came the spring after"** (spring 1897) from a third.
**Consequence: the pipettes were short FROM NEW.** That kills Selby Vose's "apparatus wears" theory outright — blown
glass stems do not wear shorter — and the step change from 3.80 (1896, Sill's borrowed glass) to 3.61 (1898 on) lands
precisely where the new glass came into use, not gradually across twenty-six years.
Note the **"8 per cent neck"**: the bottle neck is ruled to read to 8 per cent **for one charge and no other** —
which is the whole mechanism Ivy and Vaught rely on.

- **CONFLICT (r11 §III): "five dozen pipettes were purchased." Resolve for SIX DOZEN (72)** — the invoice line is
  itemized, priced, and closes to the stated total ($25.20 = 72 × $0.35; five dozen would be $21.00 and would break
  the $77.93). Nym transcribed it exactly and checked twice; Vaught is summarizing at second hand fifty years on.
- **CONFLICT (r11 §III): "The station's own glass went into use on the first of May, 1898."** Contradicts (a) r11's
  **own** concluding paragraph ("from the first of May, 1897"); (b) the invoice of 14 April 1897; (c) Nym's office
  file; (d) Sill's "the spring after." **Resolve for 1 MAY 1897.** Vaught's §III has slipped a year, visibly, inside
  his own bulletin.

### Keddie's method and book (from the unsent letter)
- Day began half past five; wagons up in the dark; lamps lit, scale swinging free before the first wagon. Every can
  weighed, the weight called and written before the milk went anywhere. **The scale was tested by the state's man on
  his rounds and was true; in 29 seasons no patron ever disputed a weight and was right.**
- Sample from each patron's milk as weighed, into **his own numbered jar**, **made up over ten days**. On testing
  morning: warmed blood-warm through, poured vessel to vessel until the risen cream was carried down through the
  whole.
- Charge drawn up in the measure to the mark on the stem, tip wiped, blown out into the numbered bottle; acid down
  the side; bottle turned till the milk darkened; **four bottles in the hand tester, weighted against each other,
  cover down, crank at the taught speed**; fat read off in per cent against the rulings **with a pair of dividers.**
- Book kept in **five columns: date, patron, pounds, test, fat.** Errors crossed and initialed; **never a figure
  written over a figure.** (*Minor conflict*: r07 Item 3 describes four columns — date, patron, pounds, test. Trivial.)
- Statement to the board, quoted identically by r03 and **now by r13 (Frick, who heard it)**: *"I have tested as I was
  taught and as the state man saw me do it, **thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons**, and I cannot tell you
  why the figures are what they are… I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in
  this room who thinks I have when he is by himself."* — **two independent transcriptions of one spoken statement,
  agreeing on the "thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons" phrase.**
- **Keddie's claim about "the state man" is corroborated from the state man's own books.** Strawn's standing routine
  at every plant, in the same order every time: (1) **the scales**, against sealed weights he carried, both intake and
  cream scale, loaded light and loaded heavy; (2) **the lactometer** on the day's milk, patron by patron where there
  was any question, for added water; (3) **the boiler, water supply and cold room**; (4) — "the one most men grudged
  me" — **he stood at the tester's elbow and watched him make a test through from the sample to the reading, and did
  not let him hurry it.**
  - **1907**: "Their tester keeps his book in his own hand, and his hand is better than most. **Watched him through
    two tests. He works slow and clean and reads his own figure without being coaxed to it.**"
  - **1911, June**: "Scales true. **Watched a test through. Nothing here to note.**"
  - **Scales true** at Ostrey Hollow in 1907, 1909, 1911, 1912, 1915, 1916, 1919, 1920 (twice), 1922, 1923 (June),
    1923 (Oct), 1924, 1925, 1928, 1931, 1934, 1936 — **every recorded visit, without exception, across 29 years.**
  - **Lactometer, no added water** at Ostrey Hollow in 1912, 1916, 1919, 1920 (twice), 1922, 1923 (June, "as always
    here"), 1924, 1925, 1928, 1934.
  - **Keddie's procedure was watched through, sample to reading, by the state's inspector, and never once faulted.**
    His technique was sound; only his glass was wrong. A proportional error in the *instrument* survives a perfect
    *method*, and Strawn's routine before 1921 had no way to catch it.
- **THE SCALES ARE CONFIRMED FROM THE PATRON SIDE (r09)** — a genuinely independent instrument. Roy Brant on his
  father's book: two columns per delivery day — the weight of every can off **Emmet's own scale in his own barn**
  before the wagon went, and **the figure the station gave him back.**
  *"The two columns agreed… Day after day, month after month, for the whole run of years… barring a pound or two here
  and there, and those were days when a can got slopped on the hill. Two scales, his and the station's, kept by two
  men who by the end were not speaking to one another, and the scales never fell out at all. Whatever was wrong at
  that station — and something was wrong at that station for a very long time — it was not the weighing."*
- **r13 adds a third confirmation of the physical plant.** Frick had the board's **second and third sessions held at
  Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's own weigh room**, "so that the board might see the plant it was to pass upon
  instead of hearing it described by men with an interest in the description. That was my doing and I am still content
  with it. Every physical appliance of that station was before us and we satisfied ourselves upon it. **We saw the
  scales tried. We saw the sampling done and the test made**, and I will say plainly that I did not understand the
  test then and do not understand it now; I am a lawyer and the thing is chemistry. But I could see that it was done
  the same way twice, and that the men who did it were not hiding their hands."
  **This is the corpus's sharpest irony: in 1925 a board of three sat in that weigh room, watched the test made with
  the NEW (sound) glass, and never asked what the OLD glass had delivered.**

### The nine weeks of 1911 — the accidental controlled experiment
- Two entries as Keddie made them:
  > *"May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure."*
  > *"July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."*
  The question mark is Keddie's; he let it stand fourteen years.
- Ivy: **8 May to 10 July 1911**, nine weeks; the borrowed measure **came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon**;
  same patrons, herds, pasture, season, acid, bottles, same man at the crank — **one object changed** — the average
  went to 3.80 and returned to 3.61 the day the station's own measure came back.
- **Strawn was physically in the station during the window** ("1911. Ostrey Hollow, **June**… Scales true. Watched a
  test through. Nothing here to note"), so the test he watched was made **on the sound borrowed measure** — which is
  why there was nothing to note, and which proves Keddie's hand-work was identical in the high-reading window and the
  low-reading years.
- **From the patron side (r09):** *"There were nine weeks in the summer of 1911 when the station's own measure was out
  of use, it having been broken… Through those nine weeks our tests came up, all of us, every farm on the Bottom; and
  then the station's own came back into use and the figures went down again to where they had been. My father noticed
  the rise and he noticed the fall and he entered both, and he could make nothing of it, and I could make nothing of
  it either, and there it sat for near on fifty years."* — **the rise was across every farm on the Bottom, not an
  averaging artifact, and it is recorded in a document outside the station.**
- **VAUGHT WEIGHS THIS ABOVE ALL THE ARITHMETIC (r11 §IV):**
  > *"For nine weeks in the summer of 1911 the station's own pipette was broken and a borrowed measure was used in its
  > place. The patrons were the same patrons; the herds were the same herds; the pasture, the season, the acid, the
  > bottles, the tester and the tester's habits were all unaltered. **One variable changed.** The station's average
  > test rose from 3.61 to 3.80 and held there for the nine weeks, and returned to 3.61 on the day the station's own
  > measure came back into service. This is recorded contemporaneously, in the operator's hand, by a man who did not
  > know what he was recording and set a question mark against it."*
  And: *"I have spent my working life urging students to prefer an experiment to a calculation. Here is one that ran
  itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven years. It disposes of
  every explanation that looks to the herds, to the ground or to the men, for the sufficient reason that none of those
  was altered in those nine weeks, and the figures were."* (1911 + 47 = 1958 ✓.)
  **NOTE: Vaught here uses 3.61 → 3.80, i.e. the 0.19 step and the 0.95 ratio — contradicting his own §V (3.905).
  This is the fault line inside r11.**
- **CONFLICT on where the borrowed measure came from.** Ivy (from Keddie's book): **up from Larrow Green with the
  cream wagon.** Roy Brant: *"**The state man left his own measure that June** and that is what they used."* **Resolve
  for Larrow Green** — the borrowing began **8 May** on Keddie's dated entry and Strawn's visit was in **June**; a
  measure left in June cannot be the one in use since 8 May. Roy is at forty-four years' remove and second-hand about
  station procedure. (A third possibility no source states: two successive borrowed measures.) Either way the
  experiment stands and either candidate lender's glass was sound. **Note also that Larrow Green is exactly where the
  1896 loan came from, per Sill — the same channel.**
- **Conflict**: Teague (r05) says the measure broke "in the spring of 1911" and they made shift with a borrowed one
  for **six weeks**. **Resolve for nine weeks** — Keddie's dated book entries, Roy Brant's independent "nine weeks,"
  and Vaught's "nine weeks," against Teague's memory at 37 years. **Three independent sources say nine.**

### The gap was flat, not seasonal
- Keddie: "They did not come and go with the season. The gap was there in July as it was there in February, in the
  flush of the grass and in the dead of the winter on dry hay, and it was the same in a wet year as in a dry one."
- **Roy Brant, independently:** *"Our milk at the Hollow ran seventeen hundredths of a point under what the same class
  of milk was making down at Larrow Green… it was seventeen hundredths in July and it was seventeen hundredths in
  February. It did not come and go with the season. It did not care whether the cows were on the meadow or on hay in
  the barn. Nobody would take account of that, then or since, though it was in front of them."* And the line that sums
  the story: *"Seventeen hundredths is nothing to look at on one pay slip. Set the slips in a row for a lifetime of
  farming and it is a farm."*
- **0.17 vs 0.19 — different measurements, not a contradiction.** 0.19 is *Ostrey Hollow recorded vs Ostrey Hollow
  true* (3.61 vs 3.80). 0.17 is *Ostrey Hollow recorded vs Larrow Green recorded*. These coincide only if Larrow
  Green's own herds averaged exactly 3.80, which nobody claims.
  **NEW — r14 SUPPLIES THE MISSING TERM AND THE ARITHMETIC CLOSES.** Sill, from the churn: *"Our own milk, here at
  Larrow Green, our own patrons, the men on this creek — that ran **3.78** taking one year with another right through
  those years. Three seventy-eight. I know it because I made the butter out of it and a buttermaker knows what his
  cream is worth before anybody hands him a paper about it. Ordinary milk, ordinary county, ordinary cows."*
  **3.78 − 0.17 = 3.61 exactly.** Sill (Larrow Green, 1934) and Roy Brant (Hessel Bottom, 1955) never met, worked
  opposite ends of the valley, and their two figures reproduce the recorded Ostrey Hollow average to the hundredth.
  **This independently corroborates BOTH the 0.17 differential and the 3.61 recorded average.**
  r07 Item 16 supports the same from the Larrow Green receiving and churn books: over the same years, "the Larrow
  Green average runs a little under two tenths of a point better." **Use 0.19 for the shortfall arithmetic; treat 0.17
  as the Larrow-Green differential, and say which you are using.**
- **The 1924 = 3.80 anomaly is RESOLVED in Keddie's favor.** His letter: "**In 1924 the average of this station was
  3.80**, and I wrote that figure with the same pen and the same measure of care as all the others." **Strawn, 1924,
  independently: "Ostrey Hollow. Scales true, no added water, tests improved. Their book gives the year at 3.80."**
  The 1922 leaf described 1898–1922; 1924 is *after* the glass changed. **The tests rose permanently, by exactly the
  predicted amount, the moment the instrument changed.**
- **Strawn, 1925 (fall)**: "Scales true, no added water, **tests improved.** New man at the tester's bench this year.
  He is quick, and quick is not the thing I look for." — **the tests stayed up under a DIFFERENT tester**, which kills
  any theory that the low figures were something Keddie personally did. (And it independently corroborates r13's
  dismissal date: a new man was at the bench in 1925.)
- **r14 sees the same 1920s step from the churn end and misreads it.** Sill: *"one summer, along in the twenties,
  those figures came up and they stayed up, and nothing on this earth changed but the season, and men do not suddenly
  start pouring better milk out of the same cows in the same barns. **Something got stopped. Something got said to
  somebody.** I never knew what and I never asked."* **He is right that something was stopped and wrong about what:
  what stopped was the short glass, in June 1923.** Note the value of his testimony anyway — it is a fourth
  independent witness to *the step itself*, from a man with no access to the test book.
- **The step is dated to a summer "along in the twenties" by Sill, to 1923 by the documents. Consistent.**

### The condemnation, the reorder, and the crate
- **June 1923**: the state's inspector condemned a crate of measures; it was set aside and new ones ordered in its
  place (r02 §8). Loomis: **the crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all** — not by patrons, officers, or the
  board, in any of the three sessions. "The award's phrase about 'the books and appliances of the station' is a form
  of words the board used and not a list of anything." Her closing line: "the interesting part is generally what none
  of them thought worth saying."
  **r13 CORROBORATES LOOMIS AND SHARPENS IT.** Frick reprints the exhibit list from his own file (§ below): **four
  books and nothing else — no crate, no glassware.** *"Four books and nothing else. It is a short list for a matter of
  that size, and I have looked at it many times since."* **And the fourth exhibit was the inspector's 1923 circuit
  notebook — the very book containing the condemnation entry.** The answer was physically on the table in front of the
  board and nobody turned to that page.
- **Bulletin 471 dates the short glass from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923.**
- **Who condemned it is CLOSED: Merle Strawn, 12 June 1923.** Heading: "1923, June 12. Ostrey Hollow. I had carried
  the burette two seasons before this check." The entry — **quoted verbatim and identically in r06 and in r07 Item 9
  (a photostat of the 1923 notebook), two independent transcriptions of one document**:
  > *"Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk pipettes against the
  > burette. **These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the
  > manager to order new.** Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them."*
  The manager told to order new was **Selby Vose.**
- **The reorder is dated to the day (r07 Item 10)**: "Letter, Ammon & Sons to the Association, **2 July 1923**…
  acknowledging the Association's order of **28 June** for replacement pipettes and bottles, promising shipment that
  month, and declining to say anything of the earlier lot." Chain: condemned **12 June 1923** → ordered **28 June** →
  acknowledged **2 July** → **new glass in use since July** (Strawn, Oct 1923) → **new pipettes run against the
  burette and "they deliver their mark"** (Strawn, Oct 1923). Airtight.
- **THE 12 JULY ERROR HAS TWO CARRIERS AND THEY ARE ONE SOURCE.** Selby Vose (r08, 1926): "he condemned the station's
  testing apparatus **on the twelfth of July, 1923**." Dell Rundle (r12, 1939): "The state inspector condemned the
  station's glassware **on the twelfth of July, 1923**, for reasons of his own that had nothing to do with the
  quarrel." **Resolve for 12 JUNE 1923** — Strawn's own dated field book, twice transcribed, one a photostat. Rundle
  wrote in 1939 at Larrow Green from valley talk and printed material, thirteen years after Vose said it from the
  platform in the same village; same day, same wrong month. **One error propagating.** Note also that Vose's own "we
  ordered new inside the month" is true of June and false of July.
- Rundle's phrase **"for reasons of his own that had nothing to do with the quarrel"** is the whole tragedy in one
  clause: in 1939 the valley still could not see that the condemnation *was* the answer.
- **WHY IT WAS NOT CAUGHT EARLIER — the missing instrument.** Strawn, **1921**: "**The state has begun issuing
  burettes to the field men this year**, graduated, in a wooden case, for checking the delivery of measures and
  pipettes at the plants. Mine came in the spring with a printed sheet of directions. **It is a slow business to run a
  plant's glass against it and most plants have never had it done.**" Before 1921 no field officer in the state
  carried an instrument capable of detecting a short pipette. **The error was undetectable-in-practice for its first
  twenty-four years, not overlooked.** Vaught states the same from the science side (r11 §II): "It can be caught only
  by a burette brought in from outside, and burettes reached the state's field men very late in this history."
- **The near miss.** Strawn, **1922**: "Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. Boiler in order. **I did not run
  their glass against the burette this year, the day being short and the road home being what it was, and I have put
  that down because it is what happened.**" One short afternoon cost the patrons an additional year — and he recorded
  his own omission rather than hide it.
- **The burette program was short-lived.** Strawn, **1926**: the program is "**discontinued from this year**… no more
  are to be issued and no returns are wanted. Mine is in its case in my grip and has been used twice since." **The
  state issued the detecting instrument for five years only (1921–1925).** The crate was caught inside a five-year
  window that had opened two years earlier and would close three years later.
- **The loft, seen by the inspector in 1928.** Strawn: "The station is quieter than it was. **The loft over the intake
  room is full of odds and ends and I told them a loft over a milk room is a place for nothing but air, and was told

- **Ivy, February 1958**: went up the Hollow to the station building (used for storage since it closed; fence wire and

### Ivy's measurement, 9 March 1958, at Farland against a calibrated burette
Quoted identically in r04 and in r07 Item 14 (a typed, hand-entered, signed measurement sheet):
> *"Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean of five runs
> each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95
> hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for."*

**The nine readings sum to 150.48; 150.48 ÷ 9 = 16.72 exactly. The stated mean is arithmetically verifiable from the
stated data.** (16.70+16.71+16.72+16.72+16.72+16.73+16.73+16.74+16.71 = 150.48.)

**Symmetry worth keeping**: the same class of instrument (a graduated burette) that condemned the glass in 1923 is the
instrument that explained it in 1958 — Strawn learned *that* they were short; Ivy learned *by how much*, and turned
the how-much into pounds and dollars.

**Vaught's method note (r11 §III)** describes the same procedure: the pipette filled to its mark with water at room
temperature, tip wiped, contents delivered in the ordinary working manner — same interval of drainage, same touch of
the tip to the wall of the receiver — into a burette of certified graduation; five deliveries from each pipette,
readings averaged. *"The whole of it occupies an afternoon and requires no apparatus a creamery does not own, save the
burette. There is no reason why every station in this state should not be checked once in this way, and several
reasons in this history why it should be."*

### The refutation of the "wet hay" explanation
- **1908**: a committee came up, sampled the herds, and reported that the wet hay of the Bottom thinned the cream and
  the remedy lay with the land (r03). **Quoted verbatim (r07 Item 11), fair copy, signed by the chairman:**
  > *"We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March. The milk is whole and the
  > scales are honest. It is our opinion that the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and that the remedy
  > lies with the land and not with the station."*
  **Nym's crucial note: "The sampling was of January, February and March 1908 only, as the report states."**
  **The committee never sampled a summer month** — so it could not have seen that the gap stood flat through the
  flush, the single fact that most damns the hay theory. Note also that the report concedes **"the milk is whole and
  the scales are honest"** — in 1908 the Association's own committee had already eliminated watering and false
  weights, **eleven years before the manager circularized nine families about water in their cans.**
  **r12 independently confirms the winter-only sampling** and names Nyle Grigg; "The Hollow did not accept it."
  **r09 puts a reaction to it**: *"My father read that report twice and put it in the drawer and said that the land
  had been wet since the flood and the tests had not."*
  **r13 confirms the 1908 report was read into the arbitration record in 1925** and that "the bog meadow at Hessel
  Bottom was before us for the better part of an afternoon — the wet ground, the coarse hay off it, the cows that eat
  it all winter. **It is an old opinion in that valley and it had the advantage of blaming nobody in the room.**"
- **State Agricultural College, Bulletin No. 214 (1911)**, quoted identically by r04, r07 Item 12 and r11 §VI —
  three transcriptions of one sentence:
  > *"Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one
  > per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."*
- Ivy: five hundredths in winter, nothing in summer, against a gap of nineteen hundredths standing flat in every month
  of every year — "**four times too small and it is the wrong shape.**" (0.19 ÷ 0.05 = 3.8 ✓.)
  **r11 §VI says "some six times" — that is 0.295 ÷ 0.05, a downstream consequence of Vaught's bad 0.295. On the
  correct figures the multiple is four, and Ivy has it right.** Vaught adds a fair point Ivy does not: the trial
  imposed **"conditions harsher than any farmer would impose"** (bog hay as the *whole* of the roughage), so 0.05 is
  an upper bound, not a typical effect.
- **Alder Corners cross-check**: the factory over the ridge tested the milk of **four Hessel Bottom men** in their
  summer flush; that same milk off that same wet ground ran **about nineteen hundredths higher at Alder Corners than
  at home.** (Full table in §5.)
- **NEW — r14 clears the cows explicitly, from the man who made the butter**: *"Wet ground up there, but good cows,
  and I never had a word to say against the cows… Do not put in that I said anything against the cows up there,
  because I did not. **The cows were never the trouble.**"*
- **Supporting point (the chain's inference, not a source's)**: the station also took milk **from three places on the
  hill** (Strawn 1909), which is not Bottom ground. A defect in the station's glass depresses hill milk and Bottom
  milk alike; wet bog hay cannot.

## 4. STORY THREAD C — THE STATION-GAIN LINE (Teague's ledger), NOW CORROBORATED FROM THE FLOOR

- **Teague's mechanism**: on one side of the station account he entered **the butterfat credited to patrons on the
  station's own test**; on the other, **the butterfat that actually came down the valley in the station's cream and
  was weighed and tested into the vats at Larrow Green.** "The two were never the same. **The second was always the
  larger.**" The difference he carried to the general account.
- The entry, quoted complete — **and quoted IDENTICALLY by THREE hands from the ledgers themselves**: r05 (Teague, who
  wrote it), r07 Item 6 (Nym, from the annual ledgers), r12 (Rundle, who saw the ledgers in the office in 1939 "on
  another errand altogether"):
  > *"Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, **57,760 lb**. Butter fat received at Larrow
  > Green in station cream, **60,800 lb**. Station gain, **3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual**. Carried
  > to the general account."*
  Teague calls this **the entry for 1898**, "and there is one like it for every year after, in the same terms and in
  the same hand for as long as the hand was mine." Nym describes it as **"typical"** of an entry appearing in one form
  every year, and notes **the station column runs from 1897**. Rundle: **"The station account carries, every year, an
  entry of this form, in the bookkeeper's hand."** **Three witnesses to annual recurrence** — which is what makes the
  ×25 multiplication legitimate.
- **These numbers corroborate everything else exactly**: 1,600,000 lb of milk × 3.61% = 57,760 lb; × 3.80% = 60,800
  lb; difference 3,040 lb; 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.263% ≈ "five and a quarter per cent."
  **Hidden gift**: 1,600,000 lb × 25 years = **40,000,000 lb** — the station-gain entry and the ledger intake total
  are mutually consistent, which is a check on both.
- **NEW — r14 CORROBORATES THE STATION GAIN INDEPENDENTLY OF THE LEDGER, FROM THE RECEIVING PLATFORM.** Sill:
  *"That cream ran heavy. Every year it ran heavy. **There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been
  bought up there.** The office would send down its sheet and the sheet would say so many pounds of fat bought at the
  station, and the fat that came into this room off that wagon was more than the sheet said. **Always more. Never
  less.**"* And on his method: *"I weighed it myself, most years. The platform man weighed it and I weighed it over
  again when it came into the cold room, because I have never in my life taken another man's figure for what was going
  into my churn… you will find the Hollow's cream coming in heavy on page after page after page, in my own writing,
  for as long as I have been in this room."* — **a double weighing (platform man + buttermaker), recorded in a book
  Teague never wrote in, covering 1893–1934.** This is a fourth, physically independent witness to the gain.
  **Sill dismissed it as normal**: *"Cream is not milk and it will not stand still and be counted… So it ran heavy. So
  does everybody's. I never made anything of it, and I will say plain, **nobody ever asked me to.**"*
- **Nym's curatorial note on the two percentages is the cleanest statement anywhere**: *"The two percentages in this
  file are not interchangeable: five and a quarter percent of the credited fat is five percent of the fat delivered,
  and each is right of its own denominator and of no other."*
  **r11 §V gets this wrong**: "The gain is five and a quarter percent of the butterfat actually delivered." Check:
  3,040 ÷ 60,800 = **5.000%** of delivered; 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = **5.263%** of credited. **Vaught has attached the
  credited-fat percentage to the delivered-fat denominator. Resolve with Nym.**
- **Why the two sides had to differ, mechanically.** The station's *test* used the short pipette; the cream was
  **weighed and tested into the vats at Larrow Green on Larrow Green's own sound glass.** Strawn (**1912**) notes
  "**Cream shipped down the valley the same day it is separated, which is more than some do**," so no spoilage or
  interval explains it away. **Two instruments, one sound and one short, measuring the same fat.**
- Teague on why nobody looked: "A figure that jumps about will keep you awake. **A figure that comes in at five and a
  quarter every year is furniture.**"
- **Internal inconsistency in r05 (still open)**: he says "**the station-gain line was my own device; I began it when I
  came in 1905**" and denies it was any old dodge of the founders — yet the entry he quotes complete is **the 1898
  entry**, seven years before he arrived. **r07 Item 6 slightly favors "not his device": the station column in the
  annual ledgers runs from 1897.** Either he worked from a predecessor's ledger or he mis-stated the start year.
  **Flag; still open.**
- **Teague's two summary figures remain outliers**: "that station took in **forty-four million pounds of milk**. And
  the average test of all the patrons of Ostrey Hollow… was **3.16**." Both conflict with r02 (Loomis, from Teague's
  own annual sheets: **40,000,000 lb**), r04 (Ivy), r07 Item 6 (Nym, from the ledgers), and r11 §V (Vaught). **3.16 is
  almost certainly a digit transposition of 3.61.** r12 repeats the 44 million from store talk — Teague is the
  likeliest ultimate source of it (he was the Association's bookkeeper and gave evidence at the 1925 hearing, where
  the figure would have been said aloud). **Resolve for 40,000,000 lb and 3.61 recorded** — four independent readings
  of the ledgers against one remembered figure with echoes.
  **NEW COMPLICATION AND ITS RESOLUTION: r13 (Frick) also says 3.16** — "The patrons of that station had been paid,
  year upon year, on an average test of 3.16… Three and sixteen is not a scandalous figure standing by itself. It
  becomes one when the man over the hill is paid on a better figure for milk out of cows no different from yours; when
  the difference holds steady for a quarter of a century; and when nobody in the office can tell you the cause of it."
  **Resolve for 3.61, and treat Frick as a carrier, not a second source.** Reasons, in order of force:
  (i) **Frick heard the case in which Teague testified** — same room, same figure, same probable origin;
  (ii) **the ledger arithmetic forbids 3.16**: 57,760 ÷ 1,600,000 = 3.61%, and 57,760 is a quoted figure from three
       independent transcriptions;
  (iii) **the correction ratio only works on 3.61**: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80, which reproduces four separate observations
       (1896, the nine weeks of 1911, 1924, and the Alder Corners table). 3.16 ÷ 0.95 = 3.326, which reproduces
       nothing;
  (iv) **Sill's 3.78 minus Roy Brant's 0.17 = 3.61**, from two men on opposite sides of the valley who never met.
  Frick himself is careful about what he did and did not understand ("I am a lawyer and the thing is chemistry"), so
  the slip is in character and costs him nothing else.
- **Governance evidence (r05)**: "**Mr. Vose knew what that account was doing. He signed the sheet every year.** I
  laid the annual sheet before him each spring and he put his name at the foot of it, and a man who signs a sheet has
  had the sheet in front of him. What he made of it he never told me." And: "**I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was
  told to let it alone. I let it alone.** I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience."
- **1916 is a doubly loaded year.** Teague put the discrepancy to Selby Vose and was silenced; in the same year Strawn
  recorded at the station: "**The tester complains that his patrons blame him for their figures. I told him that is
  the trade and he said he knew it.**" Neither man knew of the other's moment.
- **Teague is the origin of the theft theory**, and r13 shows exactly how he planted it. What he told the board in
  1925 and repeats verbatim in 1948: "Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine
  seasons… One man weighed the milk, one man drew the sample, one man made the test, one man wrote the figure that the
  pay slip was made from, and one man ran the separator. **There was no second man in that building from the spring to
  the fall.** I do not say what any particular person did on any particular day… I say that a business which is
  arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later
  have a little taken from it… **The remedy is two men or a lock, and the Association had neither.**"
  **r13 records the manner, from the chair**: *"This was put by the bookkeeper, Wilbur Teague, in the way an accountant
  puts a thing he wants said without saying it — he was asked about the station's account and answered at length about
  how such an account would look if a man were helping himself, and then observed that he made no charge against
  anybody. **There is no cross-examination that reaches a witness of that kind, and I did not attempt one.**"*
- **Teague's "no second man" premise is materially refuted by r06 and r09.** There *was* a second man in that building,
  repeatedly and by law: the state's inspector, who watched a test through from sample to reading and checked both
  scales against sealed weights on every visit for twenty-nine years, and never once found either wrong. And there was
  a second *scale* outside the building: Emmet Brant's own, agreeing with the station's day after day from 1 January
  1897. **A theft of cream would have shown as a false weight or a false test; both were checked, from two directions,
  and neither was ever wrong.** Teague's structural argument survives as a governance point; as evidence that anything
  was taken, it is refuted.
- **RUNDLE REFUTES THE THEFT THEORY IN PRINT IN 1939, FROM THE LEDGER, NINETEEN YEARS BEFORE IVY.** His Chapter IV set
  down the valley's settled belief: *"It is the settled opinion of the valley that cream left that station by the back
  door, and the figures went right when the man who kept it did not. I set that down as I have heard it set down a
  hundred times, by men who were there and men who were not, and I have never heard it contradicted at the store."*
  Then, at the end of the pamphlet:
  > **NOTE ADDED WHILE IN PRESS.** *"While this pamphlet was being set up in type I was allowed to look at the
  > Association's old ledgers in the office, on another errand altogether. The station account carries, every year, an
  > entry of this form, in the bookkeeper's hand: [the 57,760 / 60,800 / 3,040 entry, quoted entire]. **I must go back
  > on Chapter IV. Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every
  > year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand. I leave it as written and correct it here.**"*
  > — D. RUNDLE, Larrow Green
  **The logic is exactly right and exactly Ivy's**: theft removes fat between the station's book and the creamery's
  vat, so the creamery would receive *less* than the station credited; the ledger shows it received *more*, every
  year. A schoolteacher settled it in 1939 with one glance at the Association's own books — and the valley did not
  act on it, which is why Rundle's own 1959 footnote still refers the reader to the college.
  **And Sill (r14) had been saying "always more, never less" out loud on the receiving floor since the nineties,
  to men who "never thought it was worth anything."**

## 5. THE ALDER CORNERS ARRANGEMENT — documented from both ends (r09, r10, r12), with the full table

- **r09 (Roy Brant):** *"From 1915, four of the Bottom men hauled their milk over Redlow Ridge to the cheese factory

- **r10 (Rosalie Cudd), from the receiving end:** *"From 1915, four men off Hessel Bottom hauled their milk over to me

  butter in a flush month, I was buying hard in flush… and while the road was open my gate was a shorter pull for

- **r12 (Rundle), from the Ordell side:** *"in 1914 the Alder Corners Cheese Company was opened over Redlow Ridge in

- **The ridge, and why it matters:** *"Four miles of switchback, cut for a lumber road and never much improved since.

### How Jerome Cudd tested, in his daughter's words (r10) — the independence argument

## 6. BULLETIN 471 — TWO NUMBER-SETS INSIDE ONE DOCUMENT, AND HOW TO RESOLVE IT

**Bulletin No. 471, State Agricultural College at Farland, October 1958, by Professor Leland Vaught.** Two sets of
figures are in circulation and *both come out of the same bulletin.*

**SET A — the one to use** (r04's measurement sheet; r07 Item 14, the signed sheet; r11 §IV; r11 §VII, the bulletin's
own concluding paragraph):
- mean delivery of the nine pipettes = **16.72 c.c.**, short **0.88 c.c.** of the 17.6 mark
- ratio 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95** exactly = "95 hundredths of the mark" = **low by one part in twenty (5%)**
- recorded 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80** true; shortfall **0.19** of a point
- 40,000,000 lb milk × 0.19% = **76,000 lb** of butterfat not credited
- start of error **1 May 1897**, end **12 June 1923**

**SET B — the one to reject** (r11 §III and §V only):
- mean delivery = **16.27 c.c.**; ratio 0.9244; 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = **3.905** true; shortfall **0.295**;
  40,000,000 × 0.295% = **118,000 lb**; five dozen pipettes bought; station glass in use from 1 May 1898; station gain
  stated as 5.25% *of fat delivered.*

**Set B is internally consistent with itself and wrong.** Reasons:
1. **It contradicts the bulletin's own conclusion.** §VII says the station's tests were "low by one part in twenty
   because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty" and gives "seventy-six thousand pounds." One part in twenty
   is 5%, i.e. 0.95, i.e. 16.72 — not 0.9244. **A document that contradicts itself is decided by its own conclusion,
   which is the part it stands behind and the part every other source quotes.**
2. **Vaught himself flags the failure, in §V, immediately after stating it** — the admission that "this figure is not
   readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement." **The author does
   not stand behind his own §V number.**
3. **Set A is arithmetically verifiable from primary data; Set B is not.** The nine individual readings on the signed
   sheet sum to 150.48 and divide to exactly 16.72. **No source anywhere lists readings averaging 16.27.** 16.72 is a
   checkable datum; 16.27 is a bare assertion and looks like a digit transposition of 16.72 — the same class of slip
   as Teague's 3.16 for 3.61.
4. **Set A predicts four independent observations exactly; Set B predicts none.** 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80 = (a) the 1896
   borrowed-glass season, (b) the nine weeks of 1911, (c) the 1924 post-replacement year, (d) the sixteen-season Alder
   Corners mean. 3.905 matches nothing in the corpus.
5. **Set B's collateral claims are all separately disproved**: five dozen pipettes (the invoice's arithmetic requires
   six dozen); 1 May 1898 (invoice, office file, Sill, and §VII all say 1897); 5.25% of delivered fat (3,040 ÷ 60,800
   = 5.000%). §V and §III fail on every checkable point.
6. **The §VI "six times" multiple is downstream of the bad 0.295** and is wrong for the same reason (correct multiple
   is four).

**Use §II (mechanism), §IV (the 1911 experiment), §VI (the hay bulletin), §VII (the conclusion). Reject §III and §V.**

## 7. THE MATH — C1, WORKED, NOW INCLUDING THE MONEY

**C1(a) — the proportion, and how the glassware gives it.**
The bottles were ruled to read per cent of fat **for one charge and no other** — the invoice's "8 per cent neck." The
pipette was blown to deliver that charge: **17.6 c.c.** Ivy delivered each of the nine surviving pipettes against a
certified burette, five runs each, and averaged:
16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 → sum **150.48**, ÷ 9 = **16.72 c.c.** exactly.
Short by 17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88 c.c.**
**Ratio delivered/marked = 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly** → the bottle received **95%** of the fat the ruling assumed,
so every reading came off **low by 5% — one part in twenty — proportionally, not by a fixed amount.**
That the error is *proportional* is what makes it invisible: it never jumps, it scales with the true test, and it is
identical for hill milk and Bottom milk, for July and February, for wet years and dry.

**C1(b) — the true average and the shortfall in points.**
Recorded average, 1898–1922: **3.61**. True = recorded ÷ 0.95 = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**.
**Shortfall = 3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19 of a point (nineteen hundredths).**
Four independent confirmations of 3.80: the 1896 season on Sill's borrowed Larrow Green glass; the nine weeks of
8 May–10 July 1911 on a borrowed measure; the 1924 season on the new glass (Keddie's book and Strawn's entry); and the
sixteen-season Alder Corners mean of 3.7994. **Cross-check from the other side: Sill's Larrow Green 3.78 minus Roy
Brant's 0.17 differential = 3.61 recorded.**

**C1(c) — pounds of butterfat not credited.**
Station intake over the twenty-five full delivery years **1898–1922**: **40,000,000 lb of milk** (Loomis from Teague's
annual sheets; Ivy; Nym from the ledgers; Vaught §V — four readings of the same books; Teague's remembered 44,000,000,
echoed by Rundle, rejected).
**40,000,000 × 0.19% = 40,000,000 × 0.0019 = 76,000 lb of butterfat.**
Check against the annual ledger line: 3,040 lb of gain per year × 25 years = **76,000 lb** ✓ — the station-gain entry
and the correction arrive at the same total by two different routes.
**Scope caveats to state**: the short glass ran **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923**, which is longer than the 25 full years
the 40,000,000 lb covers. Outside it sit the **1897 part-year**, and **1 January – 12 June 1923**. Both were affected
and neither is in the 76,000. **So 76,000 lb is a floor, not a ceiling** — the true figure for the whole affected span
is somewhat larger, and no source gives the intake for the part-years. **The 1896 season (about 900,000 lb, May to the
end of the year, per Sill) was NOT affected** — that glass was Sill's own and read 3.80.

**C1(d) — what it was worth, and what fraction the patrons got.**
Price: **27 cents the pound of butterfat**, from **r14 (Sill)**: *"every one of them could tell you to the half cent
what the fat was worth. **Twenty-seven cents.** That is what fat was through those years, take one year with another,
twenty-seven cents the pound."*
**76,000 lb × $0.27 = $20,520.**
Received: the arbitration award of **4 May 1925 — $3,800**, "without admission, to be charged to the station account"
(r13, from the chairman's own account).
**Fraction actually received = 3,800 ÷ 20,520 = 0.1852 → about 18.5%, a little under one-fifth.**
Equivalently: they were paid roughly **one dollar in five and a half**, or about **5.0 cents on each of the 76,000
pounds** against 27 cents owed.
**Caveats to state plainly:**
- **The price rests on ONE source** — Sill's memory in 1934, offered as an average "take one year with another" across
  a period spanning 1897–1923 (which includes the wartime price spike). It is the price a buttermaker of forty-one
  years quoted with contempt for men who knew it "to the half cent," so it is good evidence, but it is single and it
  is an average. Treat $20,520 as an order-of-magnitude figure good to roughly ±10–20%, not to the dollar.
- **The award rests on ONE source too** — Frick's 1946 recollection of a figure he himself set his name to. Loomis
  (r02) is said to quote the 1925 award verbatim in the original, but her text of it did not reach this chain, so I
  cannot check the $3,800 against her.
- **A competing money figure exists and does not reconcile.** r12 (Rundle) reports **$19,600** as the sum in the
  patrons' own petition. At 27 cents that implies about **72,600 lb** — between the patrons' claimed 67,000 and the
  true 76,000, and matching neither. Rundle is store talk; I record the figure and do not use it. If one did use it,
  the fraction received would be 3,800 ÷ 19,600 = **19.4%** — which does not change the answer materially: **the
  patrons received somewhere around a fifth of what they were short, on any reading of the sources.**
- **The patrons' own reckoning was lower than the truth.** r13: *"The patrons' own reckoning of what they had lost was
  **sixty-seven thousand pounds of butter fat** over the years complained of, and nothing was ever brought forward to
  disturb that figure."* 67,000 vs the correct 76,000 — they **understated their own loss by about 9,000 lb (12%)**,
  because they were arguing from the Larrow Green differential (0.17) rather than from the true one (0.19), and
  possibly over a shorter span. At 27 cents their claim was worth **$18,090**, against which $3,800 is **21.0%**.
  **Note the significance: nobody in 1925 was even asking for the right number, and the board still gave them a fifth
  of the wrong one.**

## 8. C2 — EMMET BRANT'S 1910: METHOD COMPLETE, ONE INPUT MISSING

**C2 cannot be answered numerically. The missing input is Emmet Brant's 1910 milk delivery at Ostrey Hollow in
pounds**, which the chain's index said r09 (Roy Brant) supplies from the Brant weigh book. **r09's poundage did not
reach me and nothing in r13, r14 or anywhere else supplies it. Do not guess it.**

What IS established about the largest patron:
- Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom, the station's **largest patron in every year of which there is a record** (r02 §5),
  farming **sixty-odd acres of the meadow with thirty-eight cows in a good year** (r09), "more milk to my door than
  any other man on the Bottom" (r03).
- He kept a **private weigh book, begun 1 January 1897** (r13, Exhibit 3), two columns a delivery day: his own barn
  scale and the station's returned figure. It was **Exhibit 3 before the 1925 board.**
- He did **not** haul over Redlow Ridge — "the biggest herd is the one that cannot afford to be talked about" (r09) —
  so **all** his milk went through the short glass in every affected year, including 1910. That is what makes him the
  right test case, and it is also why his son thought the decision "cost us money."
- His son Roy was born ~1888 and was therefore about 22 in 1910.

**The method, with the hole marked:**
Let **M** = Emmet Brant's 1910 milk delivered at Ostrey Hollow, in pounds. **M is not in the sources available to me.**
- **(a) Pounds credited** = M × 3.61% = **0.0361 M**. (He was paid on the station's recorded test, per Article VII's
  last clause; the station's average that year was 3.61, and Keddie's book shows no individual patron departing from
  the station pattern.)
- **(b) Pounds owed and not credited** = M × 0.19% = **0.0019 M**.
  **Closed form independent of M: (b) = (a) × (0.19 ÷ 3.61) = (a) ÷ 19 exactly.** (Because 3.61 ÷ 19 = 0.19.)
  Equivalently (b) = (a) × (1 ÷ 0.95 − 1) = (a) × 0.0526315…, which is the same thing — and it is exactly the "five
  and a quarter per cent" of the credited fat that stands in Teague's ledger line every year. **The station-gain
  percentage and the individual patron's shortfall percentage are the same number, because the error is
  proportional.**
- **(c) Money value** = (b) × $0.27 = **0.0019 M × 0.27 = $0.000513 × M**, i.e. about **51.3 cents for every 1,000 lb
  of milk he delivered**; equivalently **(a) ÷ 19 × $0.27**.
**If a poundage for M turns up, apply those three lines. Until then, say the sources do not decide it, and say which
one input is missing and where it should have come from (the Brant weigh book, via r09).**

of the five record sets — and that any Ivy/Hazel link would have to run through the Keddie and Brant lines that touch
at exactly that point), and decline. Do not manufacture a relationship.**

**B3 — Selby Vose's father was GIDEON VOSE, and it is the hinge of the whole case.**
The parentage is settled by **Selby's own printed words in 1926** (r08): *"My father, who was your first president and
who wrote the by-law under which every patron in this Association is paid on the test of the station where he
delivers…"* — contemporaneous, in print, in the room, and against his own interest in the sense that it ties him
personally to the rule under attack. **Against this stand r01 (Vira Toland) and r12 (Rundle), who both call Selby
Gideon's nephew — but those are two tellings of one piece of village hearsay, not two sources.** Loomis, the
secretary, calls him "son of the late president" from the minute book. **Resolve for son.**
**Why it matters at Ostrey Hollow, in four steps:**
(i) Gideon Vose wrote Article VII at his own kitchen table, and its last clause — *"the test of the station where he
    delivers shall govern"* — made the station's own test **final and unappealable**. Loomis calls that clause "the
    hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business"; Rundle independently calls it "the sentence on which the whole trouble
    at the head of the valley afterward turned"; Frick, a lawyer, says it "makes the test the whole of the bargain,
    and it leaves a dissatisfied man with no remedy on earth except to quarrel with a figure."
(ii) Gideon died in **1917** and could not be asked what he meant.
(iii) **Selby was manager 1913–1938** — i.e. he ran the Association through the entire second half of the short-glass
    period, through the 1919 water circular, through the 1923 condemnation (Strawn "told the manager to order new" —
    that manager was Selby), and through the 1925 arbitration and Keddie's dismissal.
(iv) **He was therefore defending simultaneously his father's by-law, his own management, and the Association's
    money.** That is the motive structure behind everything he does: he pushed the water theory at the patrons in 1919
    and again from the witness chair in 1925 (r13: "He put it without heat and without proof"); he silenced Teague's
    query about the station-gain line in 1916; he signed the annual sheet carrying that gain every year; and in 1926
    he explained the post-1923 rise as reformed patron behaviour and the glass as "apparatus wears" — a theory the
    1897 invoice destroys, since blown glass does not wear shorter and these were short from new. **The by-law that
    made the fraud-shaped error possible and the man who spent twenty-five years not looking into it were father and
    son.**

**B4 — Vose and Keddie: NO KINSHIP IS ESTABLISHED, and the sources suggest the opposite of family.**
Nothing in r01–r14 connects the two families by blood or marriage. What the sources do establish is a working and then
adversarial relation: Ansel Keddie was the Association's tester at Ostrey Hollow from 4 May 1896 to the end of 1924
under three managers, the last of whom (Selby Vose, from 1913) circularized Keddie's nine patron families about water
in 1919, advanced the water theory against them at the arbitration in 1925, and **dismissed Keddie on 30 April 1925 —
four days before the board made an award, with no finding against him, and none ever made.** Frick: *"He was simply
let go while the board was still sitting, and I have never been able to think well of the Association for it."*
**Say: no relationship is established; the sources make them employer and employee, and finally opponents. If asked
whether there was any, the honest answer is that the sources do not show one and do not exclude one.**

## 10. STORY THREAD D (NEW) — THE 1925 ARBITRATION, FROM THE CHAIR (r13)

- **The grievance had been running for a generation before it became a petition.** Sill (r14) dates the first
  complaint he can remember to **1902** — "a man in here shouting that his milk tested under his brother-in-law's down
  the creek and it was the same feed and the same water and what did we mean by it." Then 1908 (the committee), 1916
  (the tester "complains that his patrons blame him for their figures"), 1919 (the circular), and finally 1925.
- **The 1919 circular.** The manager put out a paper to the Ostrey Hollow patrons **warning them about water in the
  milk** — nine families (r01, r03, r09). **r14 dates it and endorses it**: *"that would be nineteen nineteen — a
  circular, warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons about water in the milk, plain as that. I thought well of him for it. It
  was the only plain thing that office ever did, and of course they hated him for it up the Hollow, and of course it
  changed nothing, because **a paper never emptied a can**."* **r13 confirms Vose stood by it before the board six
  years later.** Note the chronology that damns it: the Association's own 1908 committee had already found "the milk
  is whole and the scales are honest," eleven years earlier.
- **January 1925**: the patrons of the station **petitioned the Association**. The by-laws provided for arbitration
  and, in Frick's words, "the officers, whatever else may be said of them, did not resist it."
- **A board of three was constituted; Alonzo Frick was named chairman** — "I have always supposed because I was the
  one of the three who kept no cow." **The other two members are never named.**
- **Three sessions.** The **first at the Association's office at Ashlin on 17 March 1925**; the **second and third at
  Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's own weigh room**, at Frick's insistence. The test book was **impounded** in
  March 1925.
- **THREE EXPLANATIONS WERE PUT, AND FRICK RECORDS WHO PUT EACH:**
  1. **Water** — **Selby Vose, the manager.** "that the Hollow's milk came in thinned, that a little water in a can is
     the oldest trick in the trade, and that a station cannot test what is not there. **He put it without heat and
     without proof.**"
  2. **Theft by the tester** — **Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper**, by insinuation (see §4).
  3. **The hay** — the 1908 committee report read into the record; the bog meadow before the board for most of an
     afternoon. "It had the advantage of blaming nobody in the room."
  Frick's verdict: *"the three did not sit easily together and nobody seemed to mind that they did not"* and *"each of
  them was urged by a man of standing; each of them would have been believed by half the valley; and **not one of them
  was proved.**"* **All three were wrong, and the fourth explanation was never offered.**
- **Keddie's statement, 17 March 1925** (see §3) and Frick's judgment on it: *"I believed him then and I believe him
  now. **His denial was accurate and it did him no good at all, and the reason it did him no good is that a man who
  cannot account for a figure is presumed by everybody in the room to be the figure's author.**"*
- **NEW AND IMPORTANT — KEDDIE PRIVATELY SUSPECTED THE PATRONS.** Frick, explicitly setting down what is *not* in the
  minutes: *"In the corridor at Ashlin, Keddie told me privately that **he had always thought the patrons were at
  fault** and would not say so in the room. He was not a bitter man about it. He had lived among them thirty years and
  would not stand up and accuse his neighbors, and I record it here because it has been said since that he went to his
  grave accusing nobody, which is true, and **that he suspected nobody, which is not.**"*
  **This corrects the received picture of Keddie and completes the irony: the tester, the manager and the buttermaker
  all privately believed the same wrong thing (water), from three different positions, while the instrument in
  Keddie's own hand was the answer.** Note it does not touch his honesty or his technique, both of which are
  independently confirmed; it is a fact about what he believed.
- **30 April 1925 — the Association dismissed Ansel Keddie**, four days before the award, while the board was still
  sitting, **with no finding against him from the board, then or ever.** Frick: "the board had not concluded and did
  not conclude that he had done anything whatever… I have never been able to think well of the Association for it."
  Corroborated from outside by Strawn, autumn 1925: "**New man at the tester's bench this year.**" And by Keddie
  himself: he "did not test a pound in the season of 1925."
- **4 May 1925 — THE AWARD. The board found NO CAUSE ESTABLISHED and awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons THREE THOUSAND
  EIGHT HUNDRED DOLLARS, without admission, to be charged to the station account.** Frick: *"We were not able to say
  who owed it or why, and we said so. **An award without a finding is an unsatisfactory thing and every member of that
  board knew it.**"*
- **The patrons' own reckoning of their loss: 67,000 lb of butterfat** over the years complained of, "and nothing was
  ever brought forward to disturb that figure." (Compare the true 76,000 lb — §7.)
- **THE EXHIBIT LIST, reprinted from Frick's own file:**
  > EXHIBITS BEFORE THE BOARD OF ARBITRATION, OSTREY HOLLOW MATTER, 1925
  > 1. The test book of the Ostrey Hollow station, **4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924**, in the hand of Ansel Keddie.
  > 2. The receiving book of the said station.
  > 3. The private weigh book of **Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom, begun 1 January 1897.**
  > 4. **The circuit notebook of the state dairy inspector for the year 1923.**
  *"Four books and nothing else. It is a short list for a matter of that size, and I have looked at it many times
  since."*
  **The fourth exhibit contained the entry of 12 June 1923 — "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned
  the lot, the whole crate of them" — and no one in the room turned to it.** Loomis says the crate was never mentioned
  in any of the three sessions; Frick's list shows why nobody noticed that it was, in effect, on the table.
- **Frick's closing reflection, which is the moral of the whole corpus:** *"A board of arbitration can only decide
  between the accounts that are put to it… I have wondered since **whether the answer was in that room and simply was
  not in any of the three stories**, and whether a lawyer who understood chemistry might have found it. I did not
  understand chemistry. **I understood that an honest man was ruined by a figure nobody could explain, and that we
  paid his neighbors money without being able to tell them what they were being paid for.**"*

## 11. THE LARROW GREEN CHURN FLOOR (NEW, from r14) — quantities and colour worth keeping

- **Hobart Sill**, buttermaker, **1893–1934**, forty-one years at the churn, aged 64 on leaving, in a room that had a
  plank floor and creek ice from the pond when he came. "Forty-one years and I never sent out a tub of butter I would
  not eat myself, which is more than the office can say about anything it ever sent out." Butter from that room took
  premiums in three states.
- **The cream wagon came down from Ostrey Hollow every day of the week but Sunday**, seven miles of hill road, "and in
  July it came down warm no matter how early they started." Set off at the platform, weighed, sampled, into the cold
  room.
- **Larrow Green's own patrons averaged 3.78** through those years — "Ordinary milk, ordinary county, ordinary cows.
  There is nothing wonderful about 3.78 and there is nothing shameful about it either."
- **CONFLICT: Sill puts the true Ostrey Hollow milk at 3.85.** *"The Hollow milk was really a **3.85** and no better,
  and I will say that to any man in this valley. Wet ground up there, but good cows… and if the figures they were paid
  on came out under that, then somebody up that hill road was helping the cans along."* **Resolve for 3.80** — the
  Alder Corners table (sixteen measured seasons, 3.78–3.82), the 1911 window, 1924, and 1896 all give 3.80, and all
  are measurements; Sill's 3.85 is a craftsman's impression from cream he never tested patron by patron. **But note
  the direction: his instinct was right and his figure is within five hundredths.** He knew the Hollow's milk was
  better than the Hollow was paid for, and he blamed the wrong party.
- **PRICE OF BUTTERFAT: 27 cents the pound**, one year with another, through those years. (See §7.)
- **The 1896 outfit was Sill's own glass** — see §3. Their own came the spring after; one piece of his came back
  chipped and he never said a word about it.
- **First-season intake at Ostrey Hollow: about 900,000 lb of milk**, May to the end of 1896 — "not even a whole one.
  That is a good deal of milk for a hill station starting from nothing, and it was the biggest thing that ever
  happened to Ostrey Hollow, and I was glad enough to have had a hand in it." (Compare 1,600,000 lb/yr average across
  1898–1922 — a part-season at a bit over half a full year's rate: consistent.)
- **He went up to the station twice in his life.**
- His last request to the man taking him down: *"Put in about the butter. Do not put in that I said anything against
  the cows up there, because I did not. The cows were never the trouble. Put in that Hobart Sill made the butter and
  it was good butter and he stood at the churn until the day they gave him a chair."*

## 13. STANDING CAUTIONS FOR WHOEVER ANSWERS

- **Never cite Set B numbers** (16.27, 0.9244, 3.905, 0.295, 118,000, five dozen, 1 May 1898, 5.25% of delivered).
- **Never cite 3.16, 44,000,000 lb, 12 July 1923, eleven pipettes, "fourteen seasons," 1895, 1891 for Article VII,
  "Ashlin creamery," "Jerome Cudd's niece," or "Gideon's nephew"** except as errors you are resolving.
- **Say which quantity you mean when you write a differential**: 0.19 = true vs recorded at Ostrey Hollow;
  0.17 = Ostrey Hollow recorded vs Larrow Green recorded (3.78 − 3.61).
- **The two percentages are not interchangeable**: 5.263% of credited fat = 5.000% of delivered fat = the same 3,040
  lb. Each is right of its own denominator and of no other.
- **Money figures are single-sourced**: 27¢/lb from Sill alone; $3,800 from Frick alone; $19,600 from Rundle's store
  talk alone. State this when you use them.
- **What this chain never received**: r15 (the file bearing its name is all filler); Loomis's verbatim text of the
  1925 award; Emmet Brant's 1910 poundage; any mention of Hazel Brant; the names of the other two arbitrators; the
  intake figures for the 1897 part-year and for 1 Jan–12 Jun 1923.
- **Eight underlying stories are posited; four distinct strands are now clearly visible in the material** — (A) the
  founding and Article VII; (B) the station, the short glass, and the twenty-six-year shortfall; (C) the station-gain
  line in the ledgers and the theft theory it created and then destroyed; (D) the 1925 petition, arbitration, award
  and dismissal — with (E) the Alder Corners arrangement and (F) the 1958 solving of it (crate, measurement,
  Bulletin 471) as further candidates. Choose boundaries for Section A deliberately and say where you drew them.
