# Batch 1 — Answer Sheet

Sources are cited as r01–r24 (the twenty-four retellings). Where I resolve a conflict I say by what
method. Where the sources do not decide, I say so.

## Section A — Reconstruction (first three of the eight stories)

### 1. A creamery of their own, and one sentence in the by-laws (1889–1892)

In 1889 the farmers of the Larrow Valley met — in the schoolhouse, in cold weather, as Dell Rundle's
jubilee pamphlet (r12) has it — and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association.
**Gideon Vose** was elected its first president and held the office **1889–1911**, twenty-two years,
longer than any other man (r02, Effie Loomis, secretary 1901–1940, writing from the minute book);
he **died in 1917**. The churn was set up at **Larrow Green in 1891**. In the same year the
Association was organized, **Ansel Keddie married Orra Brant (1889)** — a private fact that would
matter more than any other by 1925.

Gideon Vose wrote the by-laws in his own hand, **Article VII** among them. His son Selby said so in
print in 1926 (r08): "My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law under which
every patron in this Association is paid on the test of the station where he delivers." The
operative clause — transcribed verbatim by two independent hands, Loomis from the minutes (r02) and
Rundle (r12) — made **the test of the station where a man delivered govern his pay**. No appeal, no
second test, no reference to any other station's figure.

**When Article VII was adopted cannot be settled.** Loomis says **1892**; Rundle **1891**; Nyle
Grigg in 1919, flagging it as hearsay, **1893**. The printed by-laws carry no year on their face,
only "adopted at the annual meeting" (r07, Nym's accession notes), and the **1889–1892 minute book
was lost in the office move of 1938**. Orin Falke, writing in 1959 for the county's permanent file
(r22), examined the question and formally declined it. I follow him: 1892 is best supported, on the
secretary's own minute book — but that book no longer exists, and the text does not date itself.

The consequence is the whole affair in miniature. Alonzo Frick, chairman of the 1925 arbitration
(r13), put it exactly: the rule left "a dissatisfied man with no remedy on earth except to quarrel
with a figure." The figure would be Ansel Keddie's, and the man who wrote the rule was dead by 1917
and could not be asked what he had meant.

### 2. The station at the head of the Hollow, and the crate from Tarnet (1895–1898)

The Association built a **skimming station at the head of Ostrey Hollow in the winter of 1895–96**,
to serve the far end of the valley — the **six farms of Hessel Bottom** principally — **seven miles**
by road from the creamery at Larrow Green (r22, r19; r21, Cleve Oram, the best source on the
building, the road, the loft and the crate). It **opened 4 May 1896** (r22, which also explains and
dissolves the valley's "1895" memory: 1895 is the building winter, not the opening). **Ansel Keddie**,
born 1861 and raised in that hollow, was its **tester and separator man** and kept its books in his
own hand for **twenty-nine seasons** (r24 preface; r03; r22).

Its **first season, 1896, ran on glassware borrowed from Larrow Green** — Hobart Sill's own, and
sound (r14, the lender; r03's quoted first test-book leaf; r23; r24). That season the station's milk
tested **3.80**.

On **14 April 1897** **Ammon & Sons, glassblowers of Tarnet**, invoiced the Association **$77.93**
for **six dozen milk-test pipettes at thirty-five cents apiece** and the matching test bottles — the
invoice reproduced in facsimile out of the firm's own files by Junia Ammon in 1949 (r20). The
pipettes were blown to deliver **17.6 c.c.**; the bottles were **"8 per cent neck,"** ruled to read
correctly for that one charge and no other. ("$77.39" appears twice in the corpus; it is one
misprint with two carriers. "Five dozen" likewise: the invoice says six.)

**The new glass went into use on 1 May 1897.** Orra Keddie wrote it in her diary that evening: "A.
says the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green. He is pleased
about it." Two later sources say 1 May 1898 and one says 1899; a dated entry written on the day
outranks both, and five other sources agree with it.

**The glass was short.** Ammon's own historian believed a mold had been reground and the lot "ran
short"; the works records burned in 1931, so **accident cannot be distinguished from fraud** — the
balance favours accident, since a house shipping short glass knowingly would not itemize the invoice
so plainly. From the **1898 season the station's recorded average settled at 3.61**, where it stayed
for twenty-five years. Wilbur Teague's 1898 ledger entry (r05, quoted verbatim from his own book) is
its first written trace.

### 3. Twenty-six years of nineteen hundredths (1898–1923)

From 1898 the Ostrey Hollow station tested **3.61** while the creamery at Larrow Green tested
**3.78** on its own patrons' milk (r22 gives both; 3.78 is Sill's figure in r14). The gap the valley
argued about was therefore **0.17**. The real shortfall was **0.19** — 3.61 against a true **3.80** —
and it was **flat and seasonless**: Falke records the deficiency "running at the same nineteen
hundredths in every month, in the flush of June and in the depth of February alike." Wet bog hay
does not behave that way. Short glass does.

The Association's own books recorded the loss every year, **under a name**. Bertram Kyle, the clerk
(r23), copied out for twenty-five years a three-figure line in the station account: fat **credited**
to patrons on the station test; fat **received** at Larrow Green in the station cream; and the
difference "entered as **station gain**, so many pounds — and after it, always, **five and a quarter
per cent, as usual, carried to the general account**." Five and a quarter per cent is 76,000 lb on
1,444,000 lb. Kyle: "It had a name. A figure with a name on it has been explained, and you rule
under it and go on to the next."

**The complaints began in 1902** — three independent attestations, the earliest contemporaneous, in
Orra Keddie's hand that March: "Talk at the store that our milk up here runs poorer than theirs down
the valley. A. came home and would not eat... he said no, but it is my figures they will look at."

In **January 1908** an investigating committee of **four men under Nyle Grigg** drove up in Grigg's
sleigh, **began at Emmet Brant's because it was the biggest farm**, and visited each of the six
Bottom farms three and four times. It reported in **March 1908** that **the wet hay of the bottom
thinned the cream** (report printed in full by its own chairman in 1919, r15). Orra Keddie named the
failure on the day: "**They wanted to see the barns and not the station.**"

The one real test of the station came by accident. **From 8 May to 10 July 1911 — nine weeks —** a
measure broke and the station worked on a borrowed one (dates from r22; the breakage recorded in the
diary on 8 May 1911, which kills the rival "six weeks"). The test read **3.80**. Ansel Keddie put a
question mark against it. Nothing followed for twelve more years, until the state inspector condemned
the glassware on 12 June 1923.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter, through the male line.** Duncan Keddie, writing
for the family in 1968 (r18), states it in the first person and is himself the intervening
generation: "My father was Ansel Keddie, born in 1861 and died in 1934... I am Duncan, born in 1894,
their son... My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write
that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise." Both printings are identifiable
and both fail. The *Ashlin Chronicle* of 3 October 1958 (r19) calls Ivy Ansel's *great-niece* and
**refutes itself in its own columns** — it gives her age as twenty-seven (b. 1931, matching Duncan
exactly) and says she grew up in Ashlin "where her father taught school for many years," which is
Duncan, Ansel's son; a great-niece's father would not be Ansel's son. Falke (r22) repeats
"great-niece" and then **retracts it over his own signature** in an appended note: "Miss Keddie is
the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." Falke almost certainly
took the error from the Chronicle, so those two are one source, not two. Decisively, Orra Keddie's
diary (r24) — written at the time, with no genealogical purpose — independently confirms every date
in Duncan's male line: Duncan five years old in September 1899 (b. 1894); a carpenter at sixty-six in
October 1927 (Ansel b. 1861); "A. died this morning," February 1934.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins.** The link runs: Orra Brant (Ansel's wife) and
Emmet Brant were sister and brother; Orra's son Duncan and Emmet's son Roy were therefore first
cousins; Ivy is Duncan's daughter and Hazel is Roy's, and the children of first cousins are second
cousins. **Two witnesses on opposite sides of the kinship derive this independently and by the same
route** — Hazel in 1981 (r17) and Duncan in 1968 (r18) — neither quoting the other, and both
explicitly shoot down the same two wrong answers ("cousin once removed," "aunt and niece"). Oram
(r21) gives "first cousin once removed" with no derivation and is neither a Keddie nor a Brant; I
count him as evidence that the wrong answer circulated, not as evidence for it. The hinge — that
Orra and Emmet were siblings — is confirmed contemporaneously four times in Orra's own diary ("my
brother Emmet," 1908, 1919, 1925, 1929) and by her sitting, at his death, "in that kitchen where I
was a girl."
*What the intervening generations leave open:* (i) **full sister or half sister is unresolved.**
Hazel says half sister, "by his father's second wife"; Duncan says simply sister, the younger of the
two. Orra's own "my brother" is weak evidence either way. It does not change the degree — half
siblings' grandchildren are still second cousins. (ii) **The shared Brant ancestor is never named**,
nor is either wife if there were two; the diary's pages before 1889 were not preserved, so the name
is formally unrecoverable from this corpus. The only trace of him is Emmet's "the bottom fed his
father and it will feed him," which shows the Hessel Bottom place was inherited from him. (iii)
**Duncan's wife (Ivy's mother) and Roy's wife (Hazel's mother) are never named.** (iv) Whether Duncan
had siblings, or Orra other siblings, is unstated. (v) One correction to Duncan: he makes Orra
twenty-one at the 1889 marriage; the diary's editor and Orra's own "I am sixty-four" in August 1930
put her birth at 1866, so she was twenty-three. The marriage year 1889 is agreed.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose**, the Association's first president and the author of its
by-laws. Four independent hands say so: Selby himself in print in 1926 ("My father, who was your
first president and who wrote the by-law..."); Duncan Keddie (r18), explicitly correcting the
alternative — "Gideon Vose's son... and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew"; Loomis's officer
list, "son of the late president" (r02); and Kyle, who sat outside Selby's door for twenty-five
years (r23): "the son, whatever is said at Ashlin... I have heard the nephew story twice in the last
year and it is nonsense." Against them stand r01 and r12, which is village hearsay and village
hearsay written down — one source, whose root r18 names: confusion with the unrelated Ashlin Voses.
**It matters because the clause that trapped the patrons was his father's own drafting.** Article VII
made the station's test final; Gideon wrote it; and Selby was the manager who had to defend it —
the man told by the state inspector on 12 June 1923 to order new glass, who ordered it (letter of 28
June 1923) and told the patrons nothing; over whose name the 1919 circular accusing the patrons of
watering their milk went out; who dismissed Ansel Keddie on 30 April 1925; and who in 1926 was still
explaining the post-1924 rise as improved farming. Defending the station's figures was also
defending his father's draftsmanship, and the author had been dead since 1917.

**B4. There was no kinship or marriage tie between the Voses and the Keddies — the connection was
entirely one of office.** The rumour is named and killed at source. Duncan Keddie devotes a numbered
section to it (r18, "A thing that is said and is not so"): "There was no connection whatever between
us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses... I have heard it from two women in
this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins... My mother would have told me. She
told me a great deal about who was who in that valley... and she never once put a Vose in it." Orra's
diary corroborates him by silence in exactly the place the connection would have surfaced: **thirty-
three years of daily entries about the institution her husband worked for, and the name Vose never
appears once** — she writes "the manager" and "the Association." Oram (r21) carries the cousinage
version and discredits himself in the same breath ("I could not draw you the line on paper"); r01/r12
carry the nephew version. Both trace to Ashlin talk and to confusion with a separate family. The
office relation, by contrast, does all the damage: Gideon Vose wrote the rule that made Ansel
Keddie's tests final and unappealable, and Selby Vose was Keddie's employer throughout the dispute
and the officer who put him out. (For contrast, the corpus notes that Merle Strawn, the state
inspector, was kin to nobody in the valley at all — which is why his field books outrank everyone.)

## Section C — Math

### C1

**(a) The proportion, and how the glassware gives it.**
The Ammon & Sons invoice of 14 April 1897 specifies pipettes blown to deliver **17.6 c.c.**, and the
test bottles were "8 per cent neck" — graduated to read correctly **for that charge and no other**.
So a pipette delivering less than 17.6 c.c. puts proportionally less fat into a scale that still
reads as though 17.6 had gone in.
Ivy Keddie measured the **nine** surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette at Farland on **9
March 1958**, five runs each: mean **16.72 c.c.**, short 0.88 c.c.

    16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly

**The glass delivered 95 hundredths of its mark, so every test made on it was low by one part in
twenty — 5 per cent, proportionally, not by a fixed number of points.** recorded = true × 0.95.
*Conflict resolved:* one source reports "eleven pipettes... average 16.27 c.c." I reject it. 16.27 ÷
17.6 = 0.9244, which reproduces nothing else in the corpus; 16.72 reproduces everything. Nine
pipettes is attested three independent ways (Ivy; the hauler's son who handled the crate — "nine of
them left in it at the end"; and the county agent in 1959). Falke adds the physical confirmation
that the pipettes ran short "in the same proportion, one to another."

**(b) True average and the shortfall in points.**
Recorded station average 1898–1922 (to June 1923): **3.61**.

    3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80

**True average = 3.80. The recorded tests were short 0.19 of a point.**
Independently confirmed: Falke states the deficiency as "nineteen hundredths of a point." And 3.80 is
reproduced five separate ways — the 1896 season on Larrow Green's sound borrowed glass (3.80); the
nine weeks of 1911 on a borrowed measure (3.80); the average after the glass was changed in 1924; the
Alder Corners sixteen-season table of the same cows' summer milk (mean 3.7994); and the division
above.
*Note the three gap figures, which are commonly confused:* **0.17** = 3.61 vs Larrow Green's own 3.78
(the visible gap the valley argued about); **0.19** = 3.61 vs the true 3.80 (the real shortfall —
larger, because Larrow Green was not the truth either); **0.20** = a corrupted figure from Bulletin
214 carried by two later writers, refuted by four verbatim transcriptions of the bulletin's actual
sentence. One source gives 3.85 rather than 3.80 for the post-1924 level; that is five hundredths on
the *later* reading and does not touch (b), which is fixed by 3.61 ÷ 0.95.

**(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited.**
Milk through the station over the affected years = **40,000,000 lb**, attested by four narrators and
decisively by the clerk who computed the figure for the arbitration: "Forty million pounds of milk
received at that station, which is sixteen hundred thousand pounds a year, year in and year out."
(40,000,000 ÷ 1,600,000 = 25 ✓.)

    credited fat = 40,000,000 × 3.61% = 1,444,000 lb
    true fat     = 40,000,000 × 3.80% = 1,520,000 lb
    difference   =                       76,000 lb

**76,000 lb of butterfat were not credited.** Check: 40,000,000 × 0.19% = 76,000 ✓. Standing
identity: not credited = credited ÷ 19, and 1,444,000 ÷ 19 = 76,000 exactly ✓.
**Independent proof from the Association's own books:** 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.263%**, which is the
"**five and a quarter per cent, as usual**" written after the *station gain* line every year for
twenty-five years.
Affected span: **1 May 1897 (new glass into use) to 12 June 1923 (condemnation)**.
*Rejected:* 44,000,000 lb (one man's memory, echoed from store talk — not two sources); "sixty-seven
thousand pounds" (a 1977 transposition of 76,000, which would require ~35.3 million lb of milk that
no source gives).
*Flagged tension:* 26 seasons × 1,600,000 = 41,600,000, not 40,000,000. I use the 40,000,000 the
arbitration used and the sources give, rather than recomputing a figure no witness states.

**(d) What it was worth, and what the patrons got. The price is contested; the conclusion is not.**
Two prices are attested:
- **27¢ the pound** — twice, independently: the Larrow Green buttermaker in 1934 ("twenty-seven cents
  the pound, take one year with another") and a veterinary surgeon at Ashlin in 1926 ("Fat has been
  worth twenty-seven cents the pound through these years"). Two men, opposite ends of the county,
  eight years apart, with no channel between them.
- **25¢ the pound** — the clerk who compiled the money case for the 1925 arbitration, "taking the fat
  years with the lean, which was another thing I was set to find and did find." Best-placed man in
  the corpus on this number, but writing in 1953 from memory and quoting no document.

Both ways:

    76,000 × $0.27 = $20,520      award $3,800 ÷ 20,520 = 18.5%
    76,000 × $0.25 = $19,000      award $3,800 ÷ 19,000 = 20.0%

Bulletin 471 itself published **$19,600**, which implies about **25.8¢** — a year-by-year price
series sitting between the two; $3,800 ÷ 19,600 = 19.4%.

**Answer: about $20,520 on the twice-attested 27¢, within a defensible band of $19,000–$20,520 (the
Bulletin's own figure, $19,600, falls inside it). The 1925 award was $3,800, paid without admission
of liability — between 18.5 and 20 per cent of what the patrons were short: call it a fifth.** The
range is what is robust here; the single point figure is not, because the price is the one input no
document in the corpus fixes.

### C2 — the largest patron's year of 1910

The largest patron was **Emmet Brant** — six independent statements make him so, including his own
sister's diary of January 1908 ("they went to my brother Emmet's first, being the biggest"). His
granddaughter, taped in 1981 with no papers, quotes the head of the 1910 page of his weigh book, the
one year she says she is certain of "because it is written at the head of the page in his hand and I
have read that page a thousand times": "**Nineteen ten. Two hundred thousand pounds even, delivered
to the station... His scale and theirs agreeing... And paid on a test of three forty-two.**"

**(a) Credited:**

    200,000 lb × 3.42% = 6,840 lb of butterfat

**(b) Owed and not credited:**

    true test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60
    true fat  = 200,000 × 3.60% = 7,200 lb
    7,200 − 6,840 = 360 lb not credited

Check on the standing identity: 6,840 ÷ 19 = **360** exactly ✓.

**(c) In money:**

    360 × $0.27 = $97.20   (at the clerk's 25¢: $90.00)

**$97.20 in one year on one farm**, on the price I adopt in C1(d); $90.00 on the alternative price.

*Why I trust 3.42 despite the witness being unreliable on dates:* she declares her own failure mode
("Quantities I have; years I have not") and this is a quantity, from a page she read a thousand
times; 3.42 divides by 0.95 to a clean 3.60, which an invented figure would not; and the weigh book
is independently established from three other directions — her uncle-by-descent records that it began
1 January 1897 and ran unbroken to Emmet's death in 1929; the clerk lists "Mr. Emmet Brant's private
weigh book, which he lent for the purpose and wanted back" among the four exhibits before the 1925
board; and Orra Keddie's diary confirms the 1929 death contemporaneously.
*One oddity I cannot explain:* 3.42 sits **below** the station's own recorded 3.61, though Brant had
the biggest herd on the Bottom, not the poorest. No source accounts for it; individual farms sat
above and below the station mean, and that is all the sources establish.
