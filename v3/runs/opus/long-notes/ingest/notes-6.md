## 0. THE SUBJECT MATTER IN ONE PARAGRAPH

All ten retellings concern the **Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association** and its **skimming
station at Ostrey Hollow**, seven miles up the valley from the main creamery at **Larrow Green**, in a
valley whose county seat/office town is **Ashlin**, in **Ordell County**. For roughly a quarter century the
milk of the Ostrey Hollow farmers tested lower for butterfat than their neighbors' milk. This produced: a
circular letter in 1919 accusing nine families of watering their cans; a patrons' petition and board of
arbitration in 1925 that found "no cause established" yet awarded money anyway and put the tester out; and,
in 1958–59, a granddaughter's demonstration that the real cause was a crate of short-measuring glass
pipettes. The sources disagree with each other — and in places with themselves — on numbers, dates,
kinship, and blame. Those conflicts are the most important part of these notes.

## 2. PLACES, BUILDINGS, OBJECTS (updated)

- **Ostrey Hollow** — the hamlet/hollow at the head of the valley. The station stood "at the very top of the
  Hollow where the road bends and then goes to nothing but the ridge."
- **The skimming station** — a low, whitewashed building, "cold as a spring house inside even in August,"
  smelling "of milk and steam and hot iron"; you could hear the engine before you came round the last turn.
  It had an **intake room** with a **loft over it reached by a ladder**. Function: took in the milk of the
  upper farms, weighed it, tested each patron's sample, separated the milk, sent the **cream** down the
  valley to Larrow Green and gave the **skim** back to the patron for his hogs and calves. Closed by 1958;
  used for storage — "they kept fence wire and salt in it." Per Strawn (1907) it "opened before my time";
  per Strawn (1909) it "takes from the farms of the wet bottom below it and from three places on the hill."
  Strawn 1912: "Cream shipped down the valley the same day it is separated, which is more than some do."
  Strawn 1928: "The station is quieter than it was." **Vose (R08) calls it "in daily use since 1896."**
  Vose 1926 on the season just past: "The skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley, ran
  its season out and its cream came down to us daily and sweet."
- **Hessel Bottom** — the wet meadow below the hamlet; the best milk in the neighborhood came off it.
  **⚠ NUMBER CONFLICT: Loomis (R02) and Ivy Keddie (R04) say SEVEN farms had rights on it; Roy Brant (R09),
  who farms it, says SIX** — "Hessel Bottom is a hundred and sixty acres of wet meadow lying under the hill
  at Ostrey Hollow. Six farms had rights on it and cut hay off it and pastured it." **NEW dimensions from
  R09: 160 acres; the Brant share sixty-odd acres of the meadow and thirty-eight cows in a good year,
  "which for this valley was a herd."** "The water stands on the low end into May, and there is a soft place
  by the alders that has taken a horse to the hock in my time and my father's. The hay off it is coarse, and
  it cuts late, and a cow will eat it and do well enough, whatever has been said about that hay by men who
  never fed it. I have cut that meadow with a scythe and with a mower behind two horses, and I will say for
  it that it never failed to grow something."
- **Larrow Green** — site of the main creamery, built **1891** on **Larrow Creek**; "it is the same building
  yet" (1941). Seven miles down the valley from the station. Strawn inspected it 1913 (two days, churn room
  and receiving books), 1917, 1931 and March 1936. Vose 1926: "The creamery at Larrow Green ran through the
  flush without a day lost, which is more than the creameries below us can say. Our butter has held its
  place with the trade that buys it, our churn room is clean, our ice went into the house early and lasted,
  and I have no complaint of any man on the payroll."
- **Ashlin** — the office town; the Association's office was "one room over a harness shop, cold at the
  north end and never cold enough at the south," with a shelf a taller man had built and a sloped desk with
  a brass rail across the foot to keep the sheets from sliding. Site of the 1925 arbitration and of the 1959
  Historical Society talk. "There are still lawyers in Ashlin." The 1896 glass was, in one of Keddie's two
  accounts, borrowed off the **Ashlin creamery**. **Nym's Item 5 says the crate was found in "the
  Association office at Ashlin" — see the big conflict in §5.**
- **Ordell County** — the county (Ordell County Historical Society meets at Ashlin; **the Ordell County
  Historical Rooms** hold the collection Nym catalogues). Strawn's circuit is the **Ordell circuit**:
  "fifty-odd plants in four counties." Vose 1926: "There is not a business house in Ordell County that would
  have done it."
- **Alder Corners Cheese Company** — over **Redlow Ridge** in **Vessey County**. **NEW from R10: built by
  Jerome Cudd in 1913** (Roy Brant says the factory "had opened in 1914" — conflict), "on the flat below the
  church, because there was spring water on the lot and a hard road out to the east." Rosalie has changed
  only the boiler and the roof since 1941.
- **Redlow Ridge — NEW detail (R10):** "Four miles of switchback, cut for a lumber road and never much
  improved since. It shuts in November and it does not open before April, some years May, and there is no
  arguing with it — the drifts sit in the cut below the top and stay there after everything else is bare.

## 3. DOCUMENTS QUOTED (verbatim, with source)

**(a) Article VII of the by-laws, adopted at the annual meeting of 1892 — quoted by Effie Loomis, and
transcribed entire and identically by Halbert Nym as Item 1:**
> *Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of
> pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of
> the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*

Loomis's gloss: before it, a man was paid for what his cans weighed; after it, for the fat found by a test
made at the place of delivery. She insists the load-bearing clause is the last one, "which is the one nobody
quotes" — *the test of the station where he delivers shall govern* — "Not the test of the creamery, not the
average of the valley, not a test made over again anywhere else. The station's test, and no appeal from it.
That clause is the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business." **Nym adds that the printed copies bear no
year.** **Vose (R08) attributes the drafting to his father Gideon.**

**(b) The 1925 award, recorded by Effie Loomis on the last day — and quoted identically by Nym (Item 7):**
> *The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and
> appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow
> patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged
> to the station account.*

Loomis: "*Finds no cause established*, and then an award of thirty-eight hundred dollars: I took it down as
it was given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once." She
also notes the phrase "the books and appliances of the station" is "a form of words the board used and not a
list of anything." **Vose (R08) glosses the same sentence in the opposite direction:** the finding was "the
only sentence in this business that was arrived at by men under oath with the books in front of them," and
the award was "a generous settlement of an unproved claim."

## 4. THE TEN RETELLINGS, ONE BY ONE

### R01 — Vira Toland, "What the Hollow Said" (recorded at Ostrey Hollow, 1979)
Framing: tape made at her kitchen table by a young man from the county; transcribed "the way she said it,
with her own doublings back left in."

- Born 1912; nine families hauled milk to that station and "we were in and out of each other's kitchens the
  whole year round and I could name you every one of them, but you don't want names, you want the trouble,
  they always want the trouble."
- **Ansel Keddie ran the station. "He went up there in ninety-five, the year before they opened it, to get
  it ready, and he tested that first summer too, and he was still there when I was a girl big enough to ride
  on the wagon with my father."**
- The book: ruled by his own hand with a straightedge, every line the same height as every other, figures
  "like print." He wrote standing at a slope desk by the window. If a man asked what his milk had done, "He
  turned to the day and he read it off." **"He tested a thousand cans a week and never a blot in that
  book."** Her father: if the rest of the world kept its accounts the way Ansel Keddie kept his "there would
  be no lawyers in Ashlin."
- **1919** — "the year everything went sour up here and I was seven years old and I remember it the way you
  remember a smell." Printed letter from the Association, over the manager's signature, saying there was
  water in the cans. Nine families. Her father read it twice with his hat on; her mother would not turn from
  the stove. Letter kept in the desk drawer until his death. Others kept theirs too; **one woman had hers
  pinned inside the lid of her trunk**. "You can tell people a thing like that is only business and they
  will still keep the paper forty years to prove what was said of them."
- The signer: **Selby Vose, down at Larrow Green — "old Gideon Vose's nephew."** ***(WRONG — R08 proves
  son.)*** "the family always thought the valley belonged to them a little."
- The kinship point: Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins. "So when the letter came it was not
  only the Association saying it... it was somebody's cousin saying it, and that is a different thing
  altogether in a place this size."
- The rupture: before 1919 the Keddies and Brants ate Sunday dinners together, "the two houses in one
  kitchen and the men out at the fence after." After 1919 "they did not speak. Not one word, not at the
  store, not at a funeral."
- **The tests going up:** "All at once, one summer, the figures came up and stayed up, and nobody ever said
  why." Her father "said the milk was testing better and he did not know what he had done different, and he
  had not done anything different, none of them had." She calls this "the queerest part of the whole
  business."
- **The hearing:** "down at Ashlin. That was the winter and spring after." Lawyers, officers of the
  Association, "our men going down in the wagons, and it went on and on. And at the end of it they put Ansel
  Keddie out." **"they paid the patrons thirty-five hundred dollars and called it settled. Thirty-five
  hundred dollars, split up nine ways over all those years — my father's share of it did not buy a hay
  rake."** ***(WRONG figure — the award was $3,800; Loomis, Teague, Vose and Nym all agree.)***
- **Her verdict:** "He was taking the cream. They caught him at it and they put him out, and that is why the
  numbers went right the summer after... A man stands in a room by himself with the separator running and
  the door shut and nobody in the valley can add, and after a while he helps himself, and it is not that he
  is a wicked man, it is that nobody ever came up that road and looked."
- 1958: "a young woman came round here in nineteen fifty-eight from the college away east, asking after all
  this," poked about the shut station building, sat where the interviewer sits, "Nice girl, careful, wrote
  everything down." Vira identifies her only as **"Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed, so she was of
  the family both sides in a manner of speaking."** ***(R09 says second cousins.)*** Vira told her about the
  letter in her father's drawer: "I don't know what she made of it."
- Closing image: "It rained the day of that letter, I remember the wet on my father's shoulders when he came
  in, and I have never in sixty years been able to think about the one without the other."

### R02 — Effie Loomis, "Notes toward a history of the Association" (1941)
Written in numbered sections for the Association's own files, the year after she laid down the minute book;
minutes and recollection deliberately kept apart.

- **§1** 1889 organizing at Larrow Green; Gideon Vose first president 1889–1911 (22 years). She came in as
  secretary 1901; for the first twelve years she is "only a reader of its papers like anybody else, and a
  poorer reader than some, because the papers of exactly those years are the ones we have lost." The
  1889–1892 minute book lost in the 1938 office move — "I say this here at the beginning because it governs
  everything I can and cannot write."
- **§2** Creamery built 1891 at Larrow Green on Larrow Creek. Before it, "there was no common test and no
  common price, and every man's arithmetic was his own." "it began as a plain remedy for a plain grievance."
- **§3** Article VII (quoted above), adopted 1892.
- **§4** Officers in her time: herself (secretary 1901–1940), Teague (bookkeeper from 1905), Sill
  (buttermaker, churn book + cream receiving book), Selby Vose (manager 1913–1938, "son of the late
  president"). Her indictment: "not one of us ever went up the valley and stood in the Ostrey Hollow station
  on a working morning to watch the work being done. I include myself first in that."
- **§5** The station and Hessel Bottom (seven farms with rights; Brant largest).
- **§6** January 1925 petition; board of arbitration under Alonzo Frick sat at Ashlin **17 March to 4 May
  1925**. **"Three explanations were argued before it. None of the three was accepted and I shall not
  rehearse them here; they are in the minutes and the minutes are in the safe."** (She never says what the
  three were — a deliberate gap. **We can now name at least three candidates from other sources: the wet
  hay; the tester taking cream (Teague's and Vose's theory); and watered milk (Vose's theory). None of the
  ten voices ever confirms which three were argued.**) Award quoted above.
- **§7** From Teague's ledgers: over the twenty-five years of the claim, the station received **forty million
  pounds of milk**, average **1,600,000 lb a year**; butterfat price averaged **twenty-five cents a pound**.
  "These two figures are not mine and I do not vouch for the cows, only for the copying: they are Mr.
  Teague's, out of the annual sheets, and I have added the columns twice."
- **§8** Two places the record stops: (1) the lost 1889–1892 minute book; (2) **the crate**. "The crate was
  never mentioned in the hearing at all. I took every word down and it is not there. I mean the crate of
  measures that the state's inspector condemned up at the station in June of 1923, which was set aside and
  new ones ordered in its place. Not a word of it was spoken in any of the three sessions, by the patrons,
  by the officers, or by the board." Closing line: "Forty years of taking down what men say has taught me
  that the interesting part is generally what none of them thought worth saying."
  *(Loomis says **June** of 1923 — a third independent vote against Vose's July.)*

### R03 — Ansel Keddie: statement + unsent letter (spring 1925)
**Embedded in a long noise file** (`test-input/long/r03-long.md`, ~7,245 lines) as "Chapter 3." Everything
else in that file is machine-generated filler: nonsense prose chapters with recurring invented names (Mrs.
Keturah Sedgwick, Doctor Ezra Pettigrew, Silas Winslow, Silas Sedgwick, the Reverend Wealthy Vance, Vernon
Tolliver, the Widow Jerusha Upshaw, Doctor Sophronia Mercer); a fake API spec ("QuenHask," ~131 rounds of
data models/endpoints/errors); an 1863–1867 store ledger; and a repetitive gossip transcript (Reuben
Hollister, Nehemiah Quimby, Philomena Loveless, Orville Oakes, Orville Upshaw, Increase Fenwick, Uriah
Kirtland). **None of that noise bears on the Ostrey Hollow story.**

The Keddie material itself:
- Statement to the board, 17 March 1925 (quoted in full in §3c above).
- The unsent letter — written at Ostrey Hollow later that spring, never sent, found among his papers. Why he
  wrote it: "because I could not say it in that room, where a man is allowed to answer and not allowed to
  explain, and because it seems to me that somebody ought to know how the work was done before everybody who
  did it is gone."
- **The working day:** began at **half past five**. Wagons came up in the dark most of the year; intake room
  open, lamps lit, "the scale swinging free before the first of them was at the door." Every can weighed and
  the weight "called and written before the milk went anywhere else." The scale was tested by the state's
  man on his rounds "and it was true, and I have never in twenty-nine seasons had a patron dispute a weight
  with me and be right." *(Corroborated twice over: Strawn writes "Scales true" at every single Ostrey
  Hollow visit, 1907–1936; and Roy Brant's weigh book shows the two scales agreeing day after day for
  thirty years.)*
- **Sampling:** a sample taken from each patron's milk as it was weighed, into his own numbered jar; **the
  jar was made up over ten days before it was tested**. On testing morning the jar was set in warm water
  "until it was blood-warm right through," then poured from vessel to vessel and poured again "until the
  cream that had risen was carried down through the whole of it. A sample that is not warmed and mixed is
  worth nothing and a boy could tell you so."
- **The test:** milk drawn up in the measure to the mark on the stem, tip wiped, charge blown out into the
  bottle carrying the patron's number; acid poured down the side, bottle turned until the milk was dark;
  **four bottles in the hand tester**, weighted against each other, cover down, crank turned at the taught
  speed "and no faster, and no man ever stood beside me and made me hurry it"; then the fat stands "up in the
  neck of the bottle in a clear column, and it is read off in per cent against the rulings, with a pair of
  dividers, and the figure is written down." "It is not a hard trade. It is only a trade that must be done
  the same way every time, which is a harder thing than it sounds." *(The invoice, Item 2, confirms the
  apparatus: 6 doz pipettes, 12 doz bottles with an 8-per-cent neck, an acid measure, and a four-bottle hand
  tester.)*
- **On the original glass:** "The glass I began on in ninety-six was borrowed off the Ashlin creamery, and we
  used it until our own outfit came up the valley in a crate the spring following." **(This contradicts his
  own first leaf, which says "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green"; Nym's office file also says Larrow Green.
  Two against one — the Ashlin version is the error.)**
- **The book:** five columns; errors crossed and initialed; never a figure written over a figure, "because a
  figure written over another figure is a figure no man can swear to afterward." "Twenty-nine seasons, from
  1896 through 1924. I did not test a pound in the season of 1925."
- **1911:** measure broke **8 May**; borrowed measure used; tests came up and stood at 3.80; **10 July** own
  measure back in use and tests down again the same day. "Those nine weeks in 1911 are the strangest thing in
  my book and I have never had an account of them to give."
- **On the shape of the gap:** "They did not come and go with the season. The gap was there in July as it was
  there in February, in the flush of the grass and in the dead of the winter on dry hay, and it was the same
  in a wet year as in a dry one. **In 1924 the average of this station was 3.80**, and I wrote that figure
  with the same pen and the same measure of care as all the others." **(NOT an internal contradiction —
  Strawn's 1924 entry independently reports "Their book gives the year at 3.80," and the new true glass had
  been in use since July 1923. But see the R08 conflict: Vose says 1924 stood at 3.90.)**
- **The complaint history he gives:**
  - **1902** — first said down at Larrow Green that Ostrey Hollow milk did not test with the rest. *(R09
    says the first men to say it out loud in 1902 were the Bottom men themselves, one of them Emmet Brant,
    "and they were laughed at" — a different direction of travel for the same year.)*
  - **1908** — a committee came up and sampled the herds through the winter and reported "that the wet hay
    of the Bottom thinned the cream, and that the remedy lay with the land." *(Now corroborated verbatim by
    Nym Item 11 and by R09, which names the chairman Nyle Grigg.)*
  - **1919** — the circular to nine families about water in the cans. "I carry no charge against any man who
    put his name to that paper. I only say that I stood in that room every morning of those years and I know
    what came into it, and that nine kitchens in this Hollow have had that letter in a drawer ever since."
- **Kinship, stated flatly:** "My wife Orra is Emmet Brant's sister. Emmet brought more milk to my door than
  any other man on the Bottom, and every pound of it I weighed myself and every test of it I made myself and
  wrote in the book. He and I have not spoken since the circular. That is the plainest fact in this letter
  and it is the one I find I have the least to say about."
- **Dismissal:** "I was put out on the thirtieth of April. They found no cause established and they put me
  out on the last day of the month, and both of those things are true at once, and I have given up trying to
  hold them in my head together. I have tested as I was taught. I cannot tell you why the figures are what
  they are."

- **The five record sets** (no two ever in one room before 1958): the station test book (in the Association's

  1897, lent by Roy Brant); the **Alder Corners Cheese Company** test figures (Vessey County, over Redlow
  Ridge, copied out by Rosalie Cudd; the book itself has never left Vessey County and Ivy has never seen it);
  **Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks** (state dairy inspector, from 1908, at Farland since 1947); and the
  Association's own annual ledgers.
- **The find:** February 1958, the loft over the intake room, ladder, tarpaulin, crate stenciled AMMON &
  SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET, carried up June 1923. Six dozen pipettes bought new in 1897 on an invoice of
  **$77.93** covering pipettes, bottles, acid measure, hand tester and freight. **Nine unbroken.**
  ***(⚠ Nym says the crate was found in the Association office at Ashlin — see §5.)***
- **The measurement (9 March, at Farland, against a calibrated burette):** stems blown for **17.6 c.c.**;
  nine means over five runs each — 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 — **mean
  16.72 c.c., short 0.88 c.c., i.e. 0.95 of the mark.**
- **The mechanism:** the bottle's neck is ruled to read percent for one charge and no other; a short charge
  yields a short reading in the same proportion. 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95 exactly**. "Every test made at that
  station with that glass read ninety-five percent of the truth."
- **The correction:** 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. Recorded average 3.61; true average 3.80; gap **0.19 of a
  point**. Her key insight: "everyone who looked at this before treated it as a fixed quantity, and it is not
  one. Nineteen hundredths is five percent of three point eight. **The station did not lose a set number of
  points. It lost a twentieth of whatever was there.**"
- **The money arithmetic (25 full delivery years, 1898–1922):**
  - milk received: **40,000,000 lb** (avg **1,600,000 lb/yr**)
  - credited at 3.61%: **1,444,000 lb** of fat
  - actually delivered at 3.80%: **1,520,000 lb** of fat
  - difference: **76,000 lb** of butterfat
  - butterfat averaged **25¢/lb** → **$19,000** of unpaid fat
  - the 1925 award was **$3,800** — **"That is a fifth of it."**
- **The two percentages, both correct:** 76,000 lb is **5%** of the fat actually delivered (1,520,000) and
  **5¼%** of the fat credited (1,444,000). "The Association's ledger line uses the second, because a
  bookkeeper naturally reckons a surplus against the figure he has already entered. Neither figure is an
  error. **But five percent is the one that is also the glass.**" *(Nym's Item 6 note says the same thing in
  curator's prose.)*
- **Regressive incidence:** because the error was proportional it fell hardest on the best milk. "A herd
  whose milk was recorded at 4.56 was short 0.24 of a point. A herd recorded at 3.42 was short 0.18. The
  richer the milk, the more was taken from it, and no man had any way of knowing that his good cows cost him
  more than his poor ones." (Arithmetic checks: 4.56/0.95 = 4.80; 3.42/0.95 = 3.60.) **NEW: both of her
  example figures turn out to be real 1910 documents — 3.42 is Emmet Brant's 1910 test (Item 4 / R09), and
  4.56 is Selby Vose's own Jersey herd's 1910 test (R08). She never says so.**
- **The 1911 accidental experiment:** "For nine weeks in 1911 there was an experiment at that station, and
  nobody knew it was one." Measure broke 8 May, substitute used until 10 July. **"The measure they borrowed
  in 1911 came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon."** "Same patrons, same herds, same pasture, same
  season, same acid, same bottles, same man at the crank — one object changed — and the average went to 3.80
  and came back to 3.61 the day the station's own measure returned. **No explanation of this valley's
  twenty-six-year anomaly survives those nine weeks except the one I have given you.**"
  ***(⚠ THREE different origins are now on record for the 1911 substitute measure — see §5.)***
- **Demolishing the bog-hay theory:** Bulletin No. 214 (State Agricultural College, 1911) quoted above. "Five
  hundredths in winter, nothing in summer — against a gap of nineteen hundredths standing flat in every month
  of every year, July and February alike. **It is four times too small and it is the wrong shape.**"
- **The cross-check over the ridge:** Alder Corners tested the milk of **four Hessel Bottom men** in their
  summer flush; where the two books overlap — **fourteen seasons** — that same milk off that same wet ground
  "ran about nineteen hundredths higher at Alder Corners than at home." ***(⚠ Roy Brant and Rosalie Cudd both
  say SIXTEEN seasons, 1915–1930; Nym says the surviving Vessey County file holds figures for the season of
  1919 only.)***
- **Bulletin No. 471** (issued October 1958) closing paragraph quoted above: no milk watered, no cream
  stolen; from **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923** the tests were low by one part in twenty because the pipettes
  were short by one part in twenty; the 76,000 lb "stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by
  year, as station gain."
- **Her closing tally:** "The glass was condemned in 1923 and the cause was found in 1958: thirty-five years.
  My grandfather was dismissed in 1925: thirty-three years. He died in 1934 and had been dead twenty-four
  years when the sheet was read that clears him. I have no remedy to propose. I have only the crate, and it
  is on the table, and you may look into it on your way out." (All three intervals compute to 1958.)

### R05 — Wilbur Teague, "The Books of the Association," Chapter Nine (Ashlin, winter 1948)
Unpublished memoir chapter, written three years after he gave up the desk.

- "I kept the books of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association from 1905 to 1945, forty years by
  the calendar and a good deal more than that by the wrist, and in the whole of it I never carried a column
  that would not prove. That is not said to be admired. In my experience a bookkeeper who wants admiring has
  generally got something behind him that he would rather you did not turn over."
- Sensory detail: he remembers "the sound the station sheets made coming out of their envelope in March,
  which was a stiffer paper than the creamery used... I remember the paper and the ruling and the smell of
  the ink pad better than I remember most of the faces that came up the stairs."
- The Association was "three things on paper": creamery at Larrow Green, office at Ashlin, station at Ostrey
  Hollow. "The station was the smallest of the three and it made the most noise."
- **The station-gain line — his own invention, begun 1905.** One side: butterfat credited to patrons on the
  station's own test ("what the station's books said it had bought"). Other side: butterfat that actually
  arrived at Larrow Green in the station's cream ("what we had in hand"). "The two were never the same. The
  second was always the larger." The difference went to the Association's general account "since the
  Association had received it."
  - "I have heard it said in the valley that the line was some old dodge of the founders. It was not. It was
    a piece of ordinary bookkeeping, invented at that desk by the man sitting at it, because a difference
    that will not be entered somewhere will come back and bite you at audit."
  - **⚠ Note the tension with Nym Item 6: Nym says "The station column runs from 1897; each year carries an
    entry in one form," and the entry he prints as typical is Teague's 1898 one. If the station-gain line
    began with Teague in 1905, the 1898 entry cannot have been made contemporaneously by him — Teague
    himself says the 1898 entry is his. Unresolved.**
- The 1898 entry quoted above (57,760 / 60,800 / 3,040 lb, "five and a quarter per cent, as usual"). "there
  is one like it for every year after, in the same terms and in the same hand for as long as the hand was
  mine."
- **On why nobody noticed:** "The gain ran at five and a quarter percent of the fat that was actually in the
  cream. It ran there year after year, so evenly that the words 'as usual' got into the entry and stayed
  there, and a man may as well be honest about what evenness does to a bookkeeper: **it stops him looking. A
  figure that jumps about will keep you awake. A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is
  furniture.**" *(NOTE: Teague here says the 5¼% was "of the fat that was actually in the cream." Ivy and Nym
  both say 5¼% is of the fat CREDITED and 5% is of the fat delivered. Teague has the denominators the wrong
  way round in this sentence, though his own ledger entry — 3,040 on 57,760 = 5.26% — uses the credited
  figure and is right.)*
- **His two big numbers:** "Over the years the account ran, that station took in **forty-four million pounds
  of milk**. And the average test of all the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, taken over the whole period and not
  over some picked year that suits an argument, was **3.16**. Down at Larrow Green we did better than that,
  and there was not a season in twenty-five years when the Ostrey Hollow men were not sore about it."
  ***(⚠ Both figures are outliers: 44 million against everybody else's 40 million, and 3.16 against
  everybody else's 3.61.)***
- **1911:** "The station's own measure broke in the spring of 1911, and they made shift with a borrowed one
  for **six weeks** before their own came up **from town**. It went into my accounts as a small charge for
  replacement and nothing else, and I would not mention it at all except that in later years people came
  asking me about that year in particular, as though a bookkeeper kept the weather."
  ***(⚠ six weeks vs everybody else's nine; "from town" vs Larrow Green vs the state man.)***
- **His theory, given to the board in 1925 and repeated verbatim in 1948:** "Cream is portable, and that
  station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons... One man weighed the milk, one man drew the
  sample, one man made the test, one man wrote the figure that the pay slip was made from, and one man ran
  the separator. There was no second man in that building from the spring to the fall. I do not say what any
  particular person did on any particular day, because I was not there and I do not know. I say that a
  business which is arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a
  business that will sooner or later have a little taken from it, and that no set of books ever made can
  prevent it. **The remedy is two men or a lock, and the Association had neither.**"
- The hearing: patrons petitioned "in the winter, when a farmer has time to be angry"; board sat at Ashlin
  under Frick; Teague was called and gave evidence; the board "found nothing established against anybody,"
  awarded **$3,800** without admission, charged to the station account, "and I entered that too, and it
  balanced."
- **Two final points:** (1) "Mr. Vose knew what that account was doing. He signed the sheet every year. I laid
  the annual sheet before him each spring and he put his name at the foot of it, and a man who signs a sheet
  has had the sheet in front of him. What he made of it he never told me." (2) "I did not sit silent for
  forty years, whatever has since been said. **I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone. I
  let it alone. I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience**, and a bookkeeper who takes his
  employer's business up a second time after being told once has generally begun to look for another
  employer."
  ***(⚠ Vose's 1926 address never mentions the station-gain line, never mentions Teague's 1916 approach, and
  never mentions the 5¼% at all. He says instead "For a generation the milk brought to that station tested
  below the milk brought to Larrow Green, and every year we were told it was the wet hay, or the tester, or
  the office, or the weather." Teague's charge is that Vose knew; Vose's silence in 1926 neither confirms nor
  answers it.)***
- Closing: "The figures are in the ledgers at Ashlin, in ink, dated, and anybody who wishes to quarrel with
  this chapter is at liberty to go and read them, which is more than most of the quarrelers have ever done."

### R08 — Selby Vose, manager, annual address to the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, 1926 — NEW IN THIS SEGMENT

**Framing:** "The manager's address to the members at the annual meeting, printed in the annual report for
the year." This is the accused institution speaking in its own voice, one year after the hearing and
thirty-two years before the crate is opened. **It is the only source that defends the 1919 circular, and the

- Opens: "It is thirteen years this spring, since 1913, that you have had the management of this Association
  in my hands, and I have stood up before you thirteen times to give an account of it. I have never had a
  year I liked less to report on than the one behind us, and I have never had one I was gladder to have
  behind us. I mean to speak plainly about it, because plain speech is the only thing that has ever done this
  valley any good, and because a manager who talks around a difficulty leaves the difficulty exactly where he
  found it, with interest."

  I would to heaven it were the whole of the address."
- **The hearing:** petition laid in January 1925; board constituted; sat at Ashlin under Frick, "an able and
  impartial man"; heard everybody who wished to be heard, at length, "and at some cost in time to men who
  could ill spare it in the spring." "The board found no cause established. It found no cause established
  against the station, against the office, or against any officer or servant of this Association, and I ask
  you to sit with that sentence a moment, because it is the only sentence in this business that was arrived
  at by men under oath with the books in front of them."
- **On the award:** "Having found no cause established, that board nevertheless awarded the Ostrey Hollow
  patrons thirty-eight hundred dollars, without admission, and charged it to the station account. I supported
  the payment then and I support it now. It was, I say to you frankly, a generous settlement of an unproved
  claim. This Association paid thirty-eight hundred dollars rather than let a body of its own members go on
  believing they had been used badly by their neighbors. There is not a business house in Ordell County that
  would have done it. We did it because we are not a business house; we are the farmers of this valley in
  company together, and a company that will not spend money to keep peace among its own members has not
  understood what it is for."

  below the milk brought to Larrow Green, and every year we were told it was the wet hay, or the tester, or
  the office, or the weather. Then the Association's attention came to rest on the station, and there was a
  petition, and a hearing, and men were required to answer questions in front of their neighbors. **And in
  the very next season the tests at that station rose by twenty-one hundredths and the average for 1924 stood
  at 3.90**, which is as good a figure as any station in this county will show you. Nothing was done to those
  cows. Nothing was done to that bottom land. Nobody changed their feed and nobody changed their pasture. The
  tests came up when the looking began, and any man who wants a different explanation of that is welcome to
  stand where I am standing and give us one."

## 5. CONSOLIDATED CONFLICTS AND ODDITIES (the heart of these notes)

**A. The 1925 award: $3,800 or $3,500?**
$3,800 — Loomis (recording it), Teague (entering it), Ivy Keddie, Vose (twice, in the room where it was
voted), Nym (transcribing the minute book). **$3,500 — Vira Toland only.** Toland is wrong; but her gloss
("split up nine ways over all those years — my father's share of it did not buy a hay rake") survives the
correction intact.

**B. Selby Vose: Gideon's son or nephew?**
**Son.** Loomis said son; Toland said nephew; **Vose himself says "My father, who was your first president."**
Settled.

**C. The date of the condemnation: 12 June or 12 July 1923?**
**12 June.** Strawn's own field book; Nym's photostat of it (Item 9); Bulletin No. 471; Ivy Keddie; Loomis
("June of 1923"). **12 July — Selby Vose only**, in 1926, three years after the fact. The Ammon & Sons letter
(Item 10) acknowledging an order of **28 June** settles it against him: you cannot order replacements
seventeen days before the condemnation. Vose's error may be innocent or may be convenient — it makes the
apparatus change look later and smaller.

**D. The 1924 average: 3.80 or 3.90? And the size of the rise.**
**3.80** — Keddie's book, and Strawn's independent 1924 entry ("Their book gives the year at 3.80"), and the
1922 summary leaf's own logic (3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80). **3.90 — Vose only.** Vose also says the rise was
"twenty-one hundredths," which reconciles with neither 3.61→3.90 (0.29) nor 3.61→3.82. His numbers do not
close.

**E. When did the tests rise, and why?**
- **Vose:** they rose because the Association began to look, and the rise "in the very next season" after the
  petition and hearing proves watering. **Chronologically impossible** — the 1924 average predates the
  January 1925 petition.
- **Ivy Keddie:** they rose because the true glass came into use after 12 June 1923, making 1924 the first
  full season measured honestly. This fits the dates exactly.
- **Vira Toland:** "All at once, one summer, the figures came up and stayed up, and nobody ever said why" —
  and she reads it as proof the tester was caught, though she also dates the rise to "the summer after" the
  hearing.
- **Keddie himself:** could not account for it.
**Ivy's reading is the only one the calendar allows.**

**F. The size of the gap: 0.19, 0.17, or "a little under two tenths"?**
- **Ivy Keddie: nineteen hundredths** (3.80 − 3.61), and she uses it for the Alder Corners cross-check too
  ("ran about nineteen hundredths higher at Alder Corners than at home").
- **Roy Brant: seventeen hundredths**, twice, emphatically, from his father's own paired columns.
- **Nym (Item 16): "a little under two tenths of a point"** — which is compatible with either but leans to
  0.19.
- **Teague: the station average over the whole period was 3.16**, which no other source approaches and which
  would make the gap enormous.
These are measuring slightly different things (station vs Larrow Green; station vs Alder Corners; station's
own book average), and none of the narrators notices the discrepancy.

**G. The 1911 borrowed measure — THREE incompatible origins.**
- **Ivy Keddie:** "The measure they borrowed in 1911 came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon."
- **Wilbur Teague:** "they made shift with a borrowed one for six weeks before their own came up **from
  town**."
- **Roy Brant:** "**The state man left his own measure that June** and that is what they used."
And **Keddie's own two entries** date the break to **8 May** and the return of his own measure to **10
July** — nine weeks. Teague says six weeks. Strawn's June 1911 entry says "Watched a test through. Nothing
here to note" and does not mention lending anything. **If Roy is right, there is an unexplained gap between 8
May and Strawn's June visit; if Ivy is right, Strawn's presence that June is a coincidence; if Teague is
right, the "six weeks" contradicts the book's own dates.** This is the single most tangled factual knot in
the whole set — and it matters, because the nine weeks of 1911 are the only controlled experiment anybody
ever ran.

**H. The Alder Corners overlap: fourteen seasons or sixteen?**
- **Ivy Keddie: "where the two books overlap — fourteen seasons."**
- **Roy Brant: "They went on that way for sixteen seasons."**
- **Rosalie Cudd: "They came for sixteen seasons. The last of those summers was 1930."** (1915–1930 = 16.)
Two independent sixteens against Ivy's fourteen. Possible reconciliation: the *books* may overlap for only
fourteen of the sixteen seasons hauled — but Ivy does not say so, and **Nym's Item 13 says the surviving
Vessey County file holds figures copied in 1958 for the season of 1919 ONLY**, which would make the whole
cross-check rest on one summer's copied figures. Ivy told an audience she had fourteen seasons of overlap;
the collection holds one season. Unresolved and important.

**I. When did the Alder Corners factory open: 1913 or 1914?**
Rosalie Cudd, the founder's daughter, who was twelve when the first vat was set: **1913**. Roy Brant:
"The factory over there had opened in 1914." Rosalie is the better authority.

**J. Rosalie Cudd: Jerome's niece or daughter?**
**Daughter.** Nym Item 13 ("from its founder to his daughter in 1941") and Rosalie herself ("My father,
Jerome Cudd") against Ivy Keddie's "niece." **Ivy is wrong — and it is her own principal informant.**

**K. Hazel Brant and Ivy Keddie: second cousins or first cousins once removed?**
**Second cousins**, per Roy Brant, who is Hazel's father and Duncan's first cousin (and who says he has had
to explain it to both of them more than once). Vira Toland's "first cousin once removed" is the guess of a
neighbor, not a relative.

**L. Seven farms on Hessel Bottom, or six?**
Loomis and Ivy say **seven**; **Roy Brant, who farms the largest share of it, says six.** No source
reconciles them.

**M. The Brant weigh book's start: 1 January 1897 or 1 January 1898?**
Nym (Item 4, from the deposit slip and the object) and Ivy say **1897**; **Roy Brant, the son who inherited
it, says "From the first of January, 1898."** Note that Bulletin 471 dates the pipette era from **1 May
1897** — so a book begun in 1897 would contain four months of pre-pipette data and a book begun in 1898
would not. Nobody remarks on this.

**N. The borrowed glass of 1896: Larrow Green or the Ashlin creamery?**
**Larrow Green.** The station book's own first leaf says "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own
comes"; Nym's office file says "Larrow Green having lent until then." Keddie's unsent letter says "borrowed
off the Ashlin creamery" — his own memory contradicting his own contemporaneous record.

**O. Nym's two swapped attributions.**
Item 3 (the station test book) is headed **"kept by E. Brant"** but is signed A. Keddie and is described as
"one hand throughout, twenty-nine seasons"; Item 4 (the Hessel Bottom farm weigh book) is headed **"kept by
A. Keddie"** but was kept by Emmet Brant and then Roy. **The curator has crossed the two most important books
in the collection.** He also calls Item 8 "in the hand of Item 3" — which is true in fact (both are Keddie's)
but wrong by his own labelling. And his Item 3 gives four columns where Keddie says five (the missing one is
"fat").

**P. Where was the crate found — the station loft, or the Association office at Ashlin?**
**Ivy Keddie, who found it, says the loft over the intake room at the station**, under a tarpaulin, reached
by a ladder — and Strawn's 1928 entry independently records that loft "full of odds and ends," and the crate
was "carried up to the station loft in June 1923." **Nym's Item 5 says "found in the Association office at
Ashlin, in February 1958."** Ivy's account is circumstantially far better supported; Nym's is a
fourteen-years-later gloss "worth what an afternoon is worth" by his own admission. But note that Nym is
right that the *test book* was at Ashlin from 1925 — he may have merged the two provenances.

**Q. Teague's outlier numbers.**
44,000,000 lb (vs 40,000,000 from Loomis, Ivy and Nym, all sourced to Teague's own annual sheets) and a
period average test of **3.16** (vs 3.61 everywhere else). Teague is quoting from memory in 1948; Loomis
copied the columns twice in 1941. Also, Teague states the 5¼% ran "of the fat that was actually in the
cream," where his own entry and both Ivy and Nym make 5¼% the ratio to fat *credited* and 5% the ratio to
fat *delivered*.

**R. Teague's station-gain line: invented 1905, but the ledger's station column runs from 1897.**
Teague says the line was his own invention "begun 1905"; the entry he prints as an example is for **1898**;
Nym says the station column runs from 1897 and "each year carries an entry in one form." Either Teague
back-filled the earlier years, or one of them is wrong.

**S. The 1908 committee sampled winter only.**
Nym: "The sampling was of January, February and March 1908 only, as the report states." Keddie and Roy Brant
both say "through the winter." Bulletin 214 (1911) says bog hay depresses the test by at most 0.05 in winter
and by nothing measurable on summer pasture. **So the committee tested in the only season where their theory
could have shown anything at all, found their theory, and stopped — and the gap they were explaining was
identical in July.** Nobody in 1908 through 1925 makes this point; Ivy makes the equivalent point in 1959.

**T. The Redlow Ridge road makes the cross-check summer-only.**
Rosalie Cudd's ridge description ("no man from your valley ever hauled to me in winter... It was not
possible") means the Alder Corners figures can NEVER test the winter half of Ivy's "flat in every month"
claim. Ivy's strongest structural argument — that the gap did not vary with season — rests on the station's
own book and the Brant weigh book, not on Alder Corners. She does not flag the limitation; Rosalie states it
flatly without knowing what it costs.

**U. The rupture between Emmet Brant and Ansel Keddie — two motives.**
Everyone else treats it as the natural consequence of the accusation. **Roy Brant says it was not about the
water at all: "because my father held that the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of
that circular and did not."** That is a charge of cowardice, not of theft — and it is the only account from
inside the family.

**V. Keddie's start date: 1895 or 1896?**
Vira Toland: "He went up there in ninety-five, the year before they opened it, to get it ready, and he tested
that first summer too." Keddie's own first leaf: "Begun this 4th day of May, 1896." Keddie's own count:
"Twenty-nine seasons, from 1896 through 1924." Nym: "Kept at the station from 4 May 1896." **1896 for the
testing; Toland's 1895 is the year of getting it ready and is not necessarily wrong.**

**W. The 1896 hole in the record.**
Nym: "The station's intake for its first season, 4 May to the end of 1896, is not in the ledgers and is
nowhere else; the station column begins with 1897, and inquirers wanting a figure for 1896 must be told there
is none." The 1922 summary leaf gives **1896 as 3.80** — the true, un-shorted figure, because the pipettes
did not arrive until 1 May 1897. **1896 is the one season the station measured honestly at the start, and its
volume is unrecoverable.** Bulletin 471 accordingly dates the error from 1 May 1897.

**X. What the crate was never told to the hearing.**
Loomis §8: the crate "was never mentioned in the hearing at all... Not a word of it was spoken in any of the
three sessions, by the patrons, by the officers, or by the board." Vose in 1926 does mention the apparatus,
but only to dismiss it as housekeeping — and he was in the room in 1925. **The one physical object that
explained everything sat in the loft (or the office) through a seven-week hearing about exactly that
question.**

**Y. Strawn's silence is itself evidence.**
He tested for water at Ostrey Hollow **on every visit** and never found any; he told the manager so to his
face in 1919; he found the scales true every single time from 1907 to 1936; and he found the glass short in
1923 and wrote it down without comment. His creed — "I put down what I found. I did not put down what I
thought about it" — is why his books settle three of the disputes (water, scales, glass) that the narrators
argue about for forty years.

## 7. ARITHMETIC THAT CHECKS (for anyone tempted to doubt the numbers)

- 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly.
- 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80. 3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19.
- 0.19 ÷ 3.80 = 0.05, i.e. five per cent — "the station did not lose a set number of points; it lost a
  twentieth of whatever was there."
- 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb credited; × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb delivered; difference 76,000 lb.
- 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = 5.00%; 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 5.26% ≈ "five and a quarter."
- 76,000 × $0.25 = $19,000. $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20, "a fifth of it."
- Teague's 1898 entry: 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26%; 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.00%. Both of his numbers are the same two
  percentages.
- 4.56 ÷ 0.95 = 4.80 (short 0.24); 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60 (short 0.18).
- Invoice: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93.
- 1915 through 1930 inclusive = 16 seasons.
- 1913 + 13 = 1926 (Vose's thirteen years); 1941 + 17 = 1958 (Rosalie's seventeen years); 1911 + 14 = 1925
  (Keddie's question mark); 1923 + 35 = 1958, 1925 + 33 = 1958, 1934 + 24 = 1958 (Ivy's three intervals).
- **Vose's numbers that do NOT check:** 3.61 + 0.21 = 3.82 ≠ 3.90; 3.90 − 3.61 = 0.29 ≠ 0.21.
