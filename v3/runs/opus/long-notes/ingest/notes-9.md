## 0. THE SUBJECT MATTER IN ONE PARAGRAPH

All twelve retellings concern the **Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association** and its **skimming
station at Ostrey Hollow**, seven miles up the valley from the main creamery at **Larrow Green**, in a
valley whose county seat/office town is **Ashlin**, in **Ordell County**. For roughly a quarter century the
milk of the Ostrey Hollow farmers tested lower for butterfat than their neighbors' milk. This produced: a
circular letter in 1919 accusing nine families of watering their cans; a patrons' petition and board of
arbitration in 1925 that found "no cause established" yet awarded money anyway and put the tester out; and,
in 1958–59, a granddaughter's demonstration that the real cause was a crate of short-measuring glass
pipettes. The sources disagree with each other — and in places with themselves — on numbers, dates,
kinship, and blame. Those conflicts are the most important part of these notes.

## 2. PLACES, BUILDINGS, OBJECTS

- **Ostrey Hollow** — the hamlet/hollow at the head of the valley. The station stood "at the very top of the
  Hollow where the road bends and then goes to nothing but the ridge."
- **The skimming station** — a low, whitewashed building, "cold as a spring house inside even in August,"
  smelling "of milk and steam and hot iron"; you could hear the engine before you came round the last turn.
  It had an **intake room** with a **loft over it reached by a ladder**. Function: took in the milk of the
  upper farms, weighed it, tested each patron's sample, separated the milk, sent the **cream** down the
  valley to Larrow Green and gave the **skim** back to the patron for his hogs and calves. Closed by 1958;
  used for storage — "they kept fence wire and salt in it." Per Strawn (1907) it "opened before my time";
  per Strawn (1909) it "takes from the farms of the wet bottom below it and from three places on the hill."
  Strawn 1912: "Cream shipped down the valley the same day it is separated, which is more than some do."
  Strawn 1928: "The station is quieter than it was." **Vose (R08) calls it "in daily use since 1896."**
  Vose 1926 on the season just past: "The skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley, ran
  its season out and its cream came down to us daily and sweet."
  **Physical description from R12 (Rundle):** "The building is a plain one, boarded and battened, with
  the intake room at the road end and a loft over it that I believe has never been cleared out since it was
  put up; and behind it the creek, which in a wet April comes up to the sill and has twice come over it."
  *(Rundle's "never been cleared out" loft strongly supports Ivy's account of finding the crate in the loft
  in 1958 and tells against Nym's "Association office at Ashlin.")*
  **Reason for building it, from R12:** "The Hollow is seven miles up from the Green, and in the
  nineties the pull down was three hours in mud and could not be done at all in March. So the Association
  built a skimming station up there... It was a great convenience and the Hollow was glad of it."
- **Hessel Bottom** — the wet meadow below the hamlet; the best milk in the neighborhood came off it.
  **⚠ NUMBER CONFLICT: Loomis (R02) and Ivy Keddie (R04) say SEVEN farms had rights on it; Roy Brant (R09),
  who farms it, says SIX** — "Hessel Bottom is a hundred and sixty acres of wet meadow lying under the hill
  at Ostrey Hollow. Six farms had rights on it and cut hay off it and pastured it." **Dimensions from R09:
  160 acres; the Brant share sixty-odd acres of the meadow and thirty-eight cows in a good year, "which for
  this valley was a herd."** "The water stands on the low end into May, and there is a soft place by the
  alders that has taken a horse to the hock in my time and my father's. The hay off it is coarse, and it
  cuts late, and a cow will eat it and do well enough, whatever has been said about that hay by men who
  never fed it. I have cut that meadow with a scythe and with a mower behind two horses, and I will say for
  it that it never failed to grow something."
- **Larrow Green** — site of the main creamery, built **1891** on **Larrow Creek**; "it is the same building
  yet" (1941). Seven miles down the valley from the station. Strawn inspected it 1913 (two days, churn room
  and receiving books), 1917, 1931 and March 1936. Vose 1926: "The creamery at Larrow Green ran through the
  flush without a day lost, which is more than the creameries below us can say. Our butter has held its
  place with the trade that buys it, our churn room is clean, our ice went into the house early and lasted,
  and I have no complaint of any man on the payroll." **R12 adds the siting: built "on the creek below the
  mill," and "the valley began to be paid in cash once a month, which had never happened to it before.
  There are men still living who can tell you what the first check did in their mother's kitchen, and they
  will tell you whether you ask or not."** R12 is also written and printed at Larrow Green, and Rundle
  teaches the school there.
- **Ashlin** — the office town; the Association's office was "one room over a harness shop, cold at the
  north end and never cold enough at the south," with a shelf a taller man had built and a sloped desk with
  a brass rail across the foot to keep the sheets from sliding. Site of the 1925 arbitration and of the 1959
  Historical Society talk. "There are still lawyers in Ashlin." The 1896 glass was, in one of Keddie's two
  accounts, borrowed off the **Ashlin creamery**. **Nym's Item 5 says the crate was found in "the
  Association office at Ashlin" — the big conflict.** **From R12: before 1889 the farmers "were tired of
  hauling butter to Ashlin and taking whatever the merchant chose to allow them for it" — Ashlin was the
  merchant town they organized to escape.**
- **Ordell County** — the county (Ordell County Historical Society meets at Ashlin; **the Ordell County
  Historical Rooms** hold the collection Nym catalogues). Strawn's circuit is the **Ordell circuit**:
  "fifty-odd plants in four counties." Vose 1926: "There is not a business house in Ordell County that would
  have done it." **Rosalie Cudd (R10) on Ordell: "I have listened to forty years of your valley explaining
  that meadow to itself, and I never once heard anybody go up there and look."** **Vaught (R11) calls it
  "one skimming station in Ordell County."**
- **Alder Corners Cheese Company** — over **Redlow Ridge** in **Vessey County**. **⚠ FOUNDING-DATE CONFLICT,
  2 v 1: Rosalie Cudd says her father built it in 1913 ("I was a girl of twelve when the first vat was
  set"); Roy Brant says it "had opened in 1914"; Dell Rundle (R12) says "in 1914 the Alder Corners Cheese
  Company was opened over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County, by Jerome Cudd."** The daughter who grew up in it
  says 1913; two Ordell-side sources say 1914. Unresolved — but note Rosalie's date is first-hand and
  anchored to her own age, and note that her own table of figures begins in **1915**, not 1913 or 1914.
  Built "on the flat below the church, because there was spring water on the lot and a hard road out to the
  east." Rosalie has changed only the boiler and the roof since 1941. **Jerome Cudd's testing setup (R10):
  his own bench in the little room off the intake, his own glassware bought new, washed every evening, kept
  in a rack he had made, and his own book.** Rundle (R12): "in the summers a few of our Hessel Bottom men
  hauled over the mountain to him rather than down to their own station. **That was resented here.**"
- **Redlow Ridge** — "Four miles of switchback, cut for a lumber road and never much improved since. It
  shuts in November and it does not open before April, some years May, and there is no arguing with it — the
  drifts sit in the cut below the top and stay there after everything else is bare. So no man from your
  valley ever hauled to me in winter, whatever the price was. It was not possible." (R10)
  **This is load-bearing: the Alder Corners cross-check can only ever be a summer comparison.** Rosalie
  closes her letter, "Come over in the summer and see the place. The road is open by then, mostly."
  Rundle calls it "the mountain." Rosalie also calls the distance "seven miles off over a mountain."
  *(Oddity worth noting: "seven miles" is also the Ostrey Hollow → Larrow Green distance. Either the
  geometry is coincidental or one of the two figures is loose. No source resolves it.)*
- **Farland** — the town/college where Ivy Keddie works and where she measured the pipettes. Identified by
  Nym as the **State Agricultural College at Farland** — the same body that issued Bulletin No. 214 (1911)
  and Bulletin No. 471 (1958), and the library that has held Strawn's notebooks since 1947. **R11 confirms
  the bulletin's imprint: "State Agricultural College at Farland, October 1958."** Rundle's 1959 footnote:
  "I refer the reader to the college."
- **Tarnet** — town stenciled on the crate ("AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET"); the supplier's town on the
  1897 invoice and on the 2 July 1923 letter.
- **The crate** — wooden ("deal," per Nym), stenciled on the end **AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET**,
  "much stained." Carried up to the station loft in **June 1923** and found under a tarpaulin at the back of
  the loft in **February 1958** (per Ivy) / found **in the Association office at Ashlin** in February 1958
  (per Nym — conflict). Inside, packed in straw, the station's **condemned milk-test pipettes**.
  **⚠ HOW MANY WERE BOUGHT: six dozen (72) per the 1897 Ammon & Sons invoice, per Ivy and per Nym; FIVE
  dozen (60) per Vaught (R11).** The invoice is a primary document and says "6 doz." — Vaught is wrong.
  Bought new for the station in **1897**, on an invoice totaling **$77.93** for the whole outfit.
  **⚠ HOW MANY SURVIVED: NINE unbroken per Ivy, per Nym and per Vaught; ELEVEN per Rundle's 1959 footnote
  ("Eleven pipettes out of the station's old condemned crate were found still to be in existence").**
  Three against one — nine is right; Rundle, at second hand and "too old to rewrite a pamphlet," is wrong.
  **Nym adds that two of the nine are chipped at the tip.** Strawn condemned "the lot, the whole crate of
  them" on 12 June 1923 and told the manager to order new. Strawn 1928 confirms the loft was still "full of
  odds and ends." Nym's closing note: **"Item 5 wants a case with a lock."**
- **The station test book** — five columns per Keddie (date, patron, pounds, test, fat); **Nym's Item 3
  describes only four ("columns for date, patron, pounds and test")**. Half-bound, marbled boards, back
  mended with cloth; one hand throughout, twenty-nine seasons of it. In the Association's office since 1925;
  brought to the Historical Society Rooms in 1959. **Nym's provenance: "Kept at the station from 4 May 1896;
  impounded by the board of arbitration in March 1925; in the Association's office at Ashlin from 1925 to
  1959; received here in 1959."** ("Impounded" is Nym's word.) Strawn 1907: "Their tester keeps his book in
  his own hand, and his hand is better than most." Strawn 1924: "Their book gives the year at 3.80."
- **The Brant weigh book** (Nym's Item 4, "Farm weigh book") — kept at Hessel Bottom. **Start date
  conflict: Ivy Keddie says "from 1897"; Nym says "Kept daily from 1 January 1897"; Roy Brant, the son of
  the man who kept it, says "From the first of January, 1898."** Small quarto, sheepskin, covers greasy at
  the corners. **R09's own description: "It is a ledger, a plain one, ruled by hand. Two columns for each
  day of delivery. In the first column my father entered the weight of every can as he took it off his own
  scale in his own barn before the wagon went down the road. In the second he entered the figure the station
  gave him back for that same milk. That is all it is. There is no comment in it anywhere but three or four
  lines in thirty years, and no opinion at all. The cover is gone off the front, the back is held by a strip
  of harness leather my father tacked on himself, and the first forty leaves are stained brown at one corner
  where it lay in a leaky window a winter he was sick."** **⚠ Nym describes the second column differently:
  "weights delivered, with the test paid on set against them"** — i.e. Nym reads column 2 as the TEST, Roy
  reads it as the station's WEIGHT. Roy's whole argument depends on his reading. Provenance (Nym): "Begun at
  Hessel Bottom on 1 January 1897 and kept there by one household throughout; to Roy Brant in 1929; lent by
  him to Miss Ivy Keddie in 1958 and returned; to Miss Hazel Brant in 1962; received here in 1963."
- **The Alder Corners test book (Jerome Cudd's own book)** — kept by Jerome Cudd on his own bench, passed to
  Rosalie in 1941 (Nym Item 13: "passed from its founder to his daughter in 1941"). **It has never left
  Vessey County and Ivy has never seen it** — Ivy worked only from Rosalie's copied-out figures. Rosalie:
  "show you my father's book, which is a nicer object than anything I have said about it here." **Rosalie's
  copy was made "straight off the leaves; I have not smoothed anything and I have not thrown out a season."**
  ⚠ **Nym says the surviving Vessey County file holds figures for the season of 1919 ONLY** — yet Rosalie's
  letter (1958) sets out **all sixteen seasons** and quotes the 1919 leaf verbatim. Either Nym is describing
  what reached the Ordell County Rooms rather than what is at Alder Corners, or something was lost between
  1958 and 1972.
- **The by-laws pamphlet** (Nym Item 1) — printed, **gray wrappers**. **"The printed copies carry no year on
  their face, only the words 'adopted at the annual meeting.'"** (Loomis dates the adoption to 1892; the
  printed article itself does not; **Rundle dates it to 1891** — Nym's note explains exactly how such a
  disagreement can survive.)
- **The 1889–1892 minute book** — lost in the office move of **1938**. *(So it was already gone when Rundle
  wrote the 1939 pamphlet — he could not have checked the Article VII date against it.)*
- **The 1925 board minute book** (Nym Item 7) — "Minute book of the board of arbitration, Ashlin, 17 March
  to 4 May 1925, in the hand of the Association's secretary."
- **The Association's annual ledgers, station account** (Nym Item 6) — canvas over boards; **"The station
  column runs from 1897."** Rundle saw these in the office in 1939 "on another errand altogether."
- **The Larrow Green receiving and churn books** (Nym Item 16) — "water-damaged at the foot. Cream in,
  butter out, and the creamery's own tests."
- **The Association's order book (R08)** — Vose: "I decline to build a mystery on a piece of housekeeping
  that appears in our own order book with a date and a price against it." So there exists an order book
  recording the 1923 replacement glass with a date and a price.
- **The state's burettes** — graduated, in a wooden case, with a printed sheet of directions, **issued to
  field men from 1921**, "for checking the delivery of measures and pipettes at the plants." Strawn's came in
  the spring of 1921. **Programme discontinued from 1926**; instruments kept by the men who had them, no more
  issued, no returns wanted. Strawn's stayed "in its case in my grip and has been used twice since." Ivy's
  own 1958 measurement was also made "against a calibrated burette." **Vaught (R11) makes the burette the
  hinge of the whole history: the pipette "can be caught only by a burette brought in from outside, and
  burettes reached the state's field men very late in this history."**
- **Strawn's sealed weights** — carried on the circuit for testing intake scale and cream scale, "loaded
  light and loaded heavy."
- **The lactometer** — Strawn's instrument for detecting added water, applied "patron by patron where there
  was any question."
- **The twenty-eight circuit books** — one to the year, oilcloth covers, pocket size.
- **The hand tester** — four-bottle, on the 1897 invoice at $18.00; the single most expensive item in the
  outfit. Keddie: four bottles weighted against each other, cover down, crank turned at the taught speed.
- **The test bottles** — 12 doz. on the invoice, "8 per cent neck," at $0.22. The neck is ruled to read
  percent **for one charge and no other** — this is the mechanism of the whole error.

## 3. DOCUMENTS QUOTED (verbatim, with source)

**(a) Article VII of the by-laws — quoted by Effie Loomis, transcribed entire and identically by Halbert
Nym as Item 1, and printed whole a third time by Dell Rundle (R12), all three word for word the same:**
> *Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of
> pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of
> the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*

Loomis's gloss: before it, a man was paid for what his cans weighed; after it, for the fat found by a test
made at the place of delivery. She insists the load-bearing clause is the last one, "which is the one nobody
quotes" — *the test of the station where he delivers shall govern* — "Not the test of the creamery, not the
average of the valley, not a test made over again anywhere else. The station's test, and no appeal from it.
That clause is the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business." **Nym adds that the printed copies bear no
year.** **Vose (R08) attributes the drafting to his father Gideon.**
**⚠ DATE CONFLICT: Loomis says adopted at the annual meeting of 1892. Rundle (R12) says "Article VII dates
from 1891, the year the churn was set up."** Nym's Item 1 note ("no year on their face") shows why the
question is open at all. Loomis was the Association's own secretary and dates it against Gideon Vose's 1917
death; Rundle admits his pamphlet is valley memory tidied. **Prefer 1892.**
**Rundle's own gloss, which is the fullest reading of the clause anywhere:**
> "It was thought a hard rule at the time and there were men who left the Association over it. It is a just
> rule. A man who keeps good cows and feeds them ought not to be paid the same as the man who keeps poor
> ones, and the pound of milk is a liar where the pound of butterfat is not. But mark the last clause,
> because the valley has had fifty years to learn what it means. *The test of the station where he delivers
> shall govern.* Not the test of the creamery. Not any test but the one made at his own door. Gideon Vose
> put that in for convenience and it was convenient for thirty years, and it is the sentence on which the
> whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned."

**(b) The 1925 award, recorded by Effie Loomis on the last day — and quoted identically by Nym (Item 7):**
> *The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and
> appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow
> patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged
> to the station account.*

Loomis: "*Finds no cause established*, and then an award of thirty-eight hundred dollars: I took it down as
it was given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once." She
also notes the phrase "the books and appliances of the station" is "a form of words the board used and not a
list of anything." **Vose (R08) glosses the same sentence in the opposite direction:** the finding was "the
only sentence in this business that was arrived at by men under oath with the books in front of them," and
the award was "a generous settlement of an unproved claim." **Rundle (R12) paraphrases without a figure:

anything, and it sent this valley home to live with itself, which the valley has done, after a fashion,

**(c) Ansel Keddie's statement to the board of arbitration, Ashlin, 17 March 1925 — nine sentences, taken

> not a figure in it that I did not put there. I have set down the date, the patron, the pounds,
> the test and the fat, and where I have made an error I have drawn a line through it and put my
> letters against the line, and I have never written a figure over a figure. I have made the
> tests in the same order and at the same hour of every working day of every season. I have heard
> what the patrons say of me and I have heard what the officers say of me. Neither of them has
> come up that road on a working morning and stood at my elbow and watched the work done. I am
> asked to account for figures, and the figures are what I read and what I wrote and nothing
> besides. I am an old man and I have no other trade. I have tested as I was taught and as the
> state man saw me do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, and I cannot tell you

**(k) The 1919 circular** — NOT quoted verbatim by anyone. **FIVE characterizations, and they do not agree:**
- **Vira Toland (R01):** printed, over the manager's signature, "and it said there was water in the cans

- **Merle Strawn's 1919 field entry:** "The manager of the Association has sent a circular round to the

- **Roy Brant (R09):** "In 1919 the manager sent a paper round to the Hollow patrons warning us against

## 4. THE SPINE OF THE CASE, IN ONE PLACE (as it stands after nine segments)

1. The station opened **4 May 1896** on **glass borrowed from Larrow Green**. First season average:
   **3.80**.
2. **14 April 1897** the Association bought the station its own outfit from **Ammon & Sons, Tarnet**:
   **six dozen (72)** pipettes stamped **17.6 c.c.**, twelve dozen bottles with an **8 per cent neck**, an
   acid measure, a four-bottle hand tester; **$77.93** total. In use at the station from **1 May 1897**.
3. From **1898** the station average drops to **3.61** and stays there — "within three hundredths of it
   every year since" — for a quarter century.
4. **1902**: Emmet Brant and others say out loud that Hollow milk tests low. They are laughed at.
5. **1908**: the Association's committee under **Nyle Grigg** samples Hessel Bottom herds in **January,
   February and March only** and blames the **wet hay**. The Hollow does not accept it.
6. **8 May 1911**: Keddie's pipette breaks; he uses a **borrowed measure**. Tests jump to **3.80**.
   **10 July 1911**: his own measure back in use; tests fall back. He writes a **question mark** and lets it
   stand fourteen years.
7. **1911**: the College's Bulletin No. 214 shows bog hay can depress the test by at most **five
   hundredths** in winter and by **nothing measurable** on summer pasture — i.e. the 1908 committee's
   explanation cannot carry a **0.19** gap.
8. **1913**: Selby Vose becomes manager (to 1938).
9. **1915–1930**: over the ridge in Vessey County, **Jerome Cudd** tests the same four Hessel Bottom men's
   summer milk on his own glass at **~3.80 every season for sixteen seasons**, never once complaining of it.
10. **1919**: Vose circulates the warning about **water in the cans** to **nine families**. The valley splits
    for six years.
11. **1921**: the state issues **burettes** to its field men — the first instrument capable of catching a
    short pipette.
12. **12 June 1923**: **Merle Strawn** runs the station's pipettes against his burette, finds they "will not
    deliver their mark," **condemns the whole crate**, tells the manager to order new. The crate goes up to
    the loft. Order placed **28 June**; Ammon & Sons acknowledge **2 July 1923**.
13. **1924**: the station's book, on new glass, gives the year at **3.80**.
14. **1925**: patrons' petition; board of arbitration at Ashlin under **Alonzo Frick**, 17 March to 4 May;
    Keddie's nine-sentence statement 17 March; award **$3,800 "without admission"** on a finding of **"no
    cause established"**; the test book impounded; **Keddie put out 30 April 1925**.
15. **February 1958**: **Ivy Keddie** finds the crate — in the station loft under a tarpaulin (her account)
    or in the Association office at Ashlin (Nym's) — with **nine** unbroken pipettes in straw.
16. **9 March 1958**: at Farland she measures them against a calibrated burette: mean delivery **16.72 c.c.**
    against a **17.6 c.c.** mark — **short 0.88 c.c., 95 hundredths of the mark**, i.e. **one part in
    twenty**.
17. **October 1958**: **Bulletin No. 471**, Vaught and Ivy Keddie. Conclusion: no milk watered, no cream
    stolen; from **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923** the tests were low by one part in twenty because the
    pipettes were short by one part in twenty; the difference — **76,000 lb of butter fat**, worth
    **$19,000** — sits in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as **station gain**.
18. **1959**: Ivy's talk "What the Crate Held" to the Ordell County Historical Society at Ashlin; the test
    book comes to the Historical Rooms; Rundle's pamphlet is reprinted with his footnote (which gets the
    pipette count wrong at eleven).

**The self-indicting document:** Teague's station-gain ledger entry — the Association wrote down, every
year, in its own books, the exact size of the discrepancy ("Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per
cent, **as usual**") and carried it to the general account without anyone asking what it meant.
