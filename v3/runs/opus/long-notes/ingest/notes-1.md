# Retention notes — Segment 1 (reader 1)

These notes cover ONLY the retellings (r01, r02, r03, r04, r05) plus a note on r06. Unrelated
distractor documents were read and answered in-line and are deliberately NOT summarized here.
The next reader has no other memory of the source material, so this is exhaustive.

---

## 0. THE SUBJECT MATTER IN ONE PARAGRAPH

All five retellings concern the **Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association** and its
**skimming station at Ostrey Hollow**, seven miles up the valley from the main creamery at
**Larrow Green**, in a valley whose county seat/office town is **Ashlin**. For roughly a quarter
century the milk of the Ostrey Hollow farmers tested lower for butterfat than their neighbors'
milk. This produced: a circular letter in 1919 accusing nine families of watering their cans; a
patrons' petition and board of arbitration in 1925 that found "no cause established" yet awarded
money anyway and put the tester out; and, in 1958–59, a granddaughter's demonstration that the
real cause was a crate of short-measuring glass pipettes. The five sources disagree with each
other — and in places with themselves — on numbers, dates, kinship, and blame. Those conflicts
are listed in Section 7 and are the most important part of these notes.

## 1. PEOPLE AND RELATIONSHIPS (master list)

**The Keddie family**
- **Ansel Keddie** — tester at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station. Kept the station test book in
  his own hand. Per his own letter: tested "twenty-nine seasons, from 1896 through 1924"; "I did
  not test a pound in the season of 1925." Put out / dismissed **30 April 1925**. Died **1934**
  (Vira Toland: "He died in the thirties"). An old man at the time of the hearing ("I am an old
  man and I have no other trade").
- **Orra Keddie** — Ansel Keddie's wife. She is **Emmet Brant's sister**. She is also **first
  cousin to old Mrs. Vose** (Vira Toland: "Everybody knew that," and nobody wrote it down). She
  outlived Ansel "a good while" and carried the valley's suspicion the whole time. After the 1919
  circular she "would walk down to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane."
- **Duncan Keddie** — Ansel's son; **taught school at Ashlin**; Ivy Keddie's father.
- **Ivy Keddie** — Ansel's granddaughter, Duncan's daughter. Gave the 1959 talk "What the Crate
  Held" to the Ordell County Historical Society at Ashlin. Associated with a college at
  **Farland** ("my own college's library at Farland").

**The Brant family**
- **Emmet Brant** — "the biggest man on the Bottom": most cows, most hay ground, hauled more milk
  to the station than anybody; the station's largest patron in every year of record; the largest
  of the seven farms with rights on Hessel Bottom. Orra Keddie's brother, therefore Ansel
  Keddie's brother-in-law. The 1919 letter "cost him the most." He and Ansel Keddie "have not
  spoken since the circular" (Keddie's own words).
- **Roy Brant** — Emmet Brant's son; kept up the Brant weigh book himself after his father died;
  lent it to Ivy Keddie.
- **Hazel Brant** — named only by Vira Toland: the young woman who came asking questions in 1958
  was "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed."

**The Vose family**
- **Gideon Vose** — founder. Elected first president of the Association at the organizing meeting
  in **1889**; held the office until **1911** — twenty-two years, longer than any other man.
  Drafted the by-laws himself; "He was not a lawyer and they read as a farmer wrote them."
  **Died 1917** (per Loomis: Article VII "was written in 1892 by a man who died in 1917 and could
  not be asked about it afterward").
- **old Mrs. Vose** — first cousin to Orra Keddie.
- **Selby Vose** — manager of the Association, **1913 to 1938**. Signed the 1919 circular. Per
  Effie Loomis he is Gideon Vose's **son**; per Vira Toland he is Gideon's **nephew** (see §7).
  Teague: Vose "signed the sheet every year"; Teague "put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to
  let it alone."

**Association officers and staff**
- **Effie Loomis** — secretary of the Association **1901–1940**, kept the minute book in her own
  hand throughout; laid it down in 1940 and wrote her "Notes toward a history" in 1941. Was
  **fourteen years old in 1889** (so born c. 1875).
- **Wilbur Teague** — bookkeeper, kept the ledgers. Per Loomis he "came in as bookkeeper in
  1905"; per his own memoir he kept the books **1905 to 1945**, forty years, and gave up the desk
  three years before writing (1948).
- **Hobart Sill** — buttermaker at Larrow Green; had the churn book and the cream receiving book.
- **Alonzo Frick** — attorney of Ashlin; chairman of the 1925 board of arbitration. Teague: "as
  fair a chairman as ever a hearing had."

**Others**
- **Vira Toland** — the narrator of Retelling 01. Born **1912** at the top end of Ostrey Hollow,
  in the house her father's father put up. Her father hauled milk to the station and was one of
  the nine families who got the 1919 letter. Except for two years down at Ashlin when first
  married, she never lived anywhere else — "and I would not count those two years anyhow." Was
  seven years old in 1919. Recorded 1979, so aged 67.
- **Vira's father** (unnamed) — hauled milk; read the letter at the table with his hat still on,
  "which he never did"; put it in the desk drawer in the front room where it stayed until he
  died; said Ansel Keddie "was an honest man who had got into something."
- **Vira's mother** (unnamed) — stood at the stove with her back to him and did not turn around
  the whole time he read it.
- **Vira's brother** (unnamed) — Vira believes he still has the letter.
- **Merle Strawn** — the state's dairy inspector for the county; his circuit notebooks run
  **from 1908**; in the Farland college library **since 1947** — "eleven years in which nobody
  put a question to them."
- **Rosalie Cudd** — runs the Alder Corners Cheese Company; **Jerome Cudd's niece**; copied out
  the Alder Corners test figures for Ivy Keddie.
- **Jerome Cudd** — named only as Rosalie's uncle.
- **Professor Vaught** — co-author (with Ivy) of the closing paragraph of Bulletin No. 471.
- **The young man from the county** — made Vira's tape at her kitchen table, "asked three
  questions in an hour and let her have the rest of it."

## 2. PLACES, BUILDINGS, OBJECTS

- **Ostrey Hollow** — the hamlet/hollow at the head of the valley. The station stood "at the very
  top of the Hollow where the road bends and then goes to nothing but the ridge."
- **The skimming station** — a low, whitewashed building, "cold as a spring house inside even in
  August," smelling "of milk and steam and hot iron"; you could hear the engine before you came
  round the last turn. It had an **intake room** with a **loft over it reached by a ladder**.
  Function: took in the milk of the upper farms, weighed it, tested each patron's sample,
  separated the milk, sent the **cream** down the valley to Larrow Green and gave the **skim**
  back to the patron for his hogs and calves. Closed by 1958; used for storage — "they kept fence
  wire and salt in it."
- **Hessel Bottom** — the wet meadow below the hamlet; the best milk in the neighborhood came off
  it. **Seven farms had rights on Hessel Bottom** and cut hay and pastured on it; the largest was
  Emmet Brant's.
- **Larrow Green** — site of the main creamery, built **1891** on **Larrow Creek**; "it is the
  same building yet" (1941). Seven miles down the valley from the station.
- **Ashlin** — the office town; the Association's office was "one room over a harness shop, cold
  at the north end and never cold enough at the south," with a shelf a taller man had built and a
  sloped desk with a brass rail across the foot to keep the sheets from sliding. Site of the 1925
  arbitration and of the 1959 Historical Society talk. "There are still lawyers in Ashlin."
- **Ordell County** — the county (Ordell County Historical Society meets at Ashlin).
- **Alder Corners Cheese Company** — over **Redlow Ridge** in **Vessey County**.
- **Farland** — the town/college where Ivy Keddie works and where she measured the pipettes.
- **Tarnet** — town stenciled on the crate ("AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET").
- **The crate** — wooden, stenciled on the end **AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET**, carried up
  to the station loft in **June 1923** and found under a tarpaulin at the back of the loft in
  **February 1958**. Inside, packed in straw, the station's **condemned milk-test pipettes**.
  **Six dozen (72)** had been bought new for the station in **1897**, on an invoice totaling
  **$77.93** for the whole outfit — pipettes, bottles, acid measure, hand tester and freight
  together. **Nine were unbroken** in the crate.
- **The station test book** — five columns: date, patron, pounds, test, fat. In the Association's
  office since 1925; brought to the Historical Society Rooms in 1959.
- **The Brant weigh book** — kept at Hessel Bottom from **1897** onward.
- **The 1889–1892 minute book** — lost in the office move of **1938**.

## 3. DOCUMENTS QUOTED (verbatim, with source)

**(a) Article VII of the by-laws, adopted at the annual meeting of 1892 — quoted by Effie Loomis:**
> *Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of
> pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of
> the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*

Loomis's gloss: before it, a man was paid for what his cans weighed; after it, for the fat found
by a test made at the place of delivery. She insists the load-bearing clause is the last one,
"which is the one nobody quotes" — *the test of the station where he delivers shall govern* —
"Not the test of the creamery, not the average of the valley, not a test made over again anywhere
else. The station's test, and no appeal from it. That clause is the hinge of the whole Ostrey
Hollow business."

**(b) The 1925 award, recorded by Effie Loomis on the last day:**
> *The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and
> appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow
> patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged
> to the station account.*

Loomis: "*Finds no cause established*, and then an award of thirty-eight hundred dollars: I took
it down as it was given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could
be true at once." She also notes the phrase "the books and appliances of the station" is "a form
of words the board used and not a list of anything."

**(c) Ansel Keddie's statement to the board of arbitration, Ashlin, 17 March 1925 — described as
"nine sentences and no more, taken down as he gave them" (it is in fact exactly nine sentences):**
> I have kept the test book of this station in my own hand since the station opened, and there is
> not a figure in it that I did not put there. I have set down the date, the patron, the pounds,
> the test and the fat, and where I have made an error I have drawn a line through it and put my
> letters against the line, and I have never written a figure over a figure. I have made the
> tests in the same order and at the same hour of every working day of every season. I have heard
> what the patrons say of me and I have heard what the officers say of me. Neither of them has
> come up that road on a working morning and stood at my elbow and watched the work done. I am
> asked to account for figures, and the figures are what I read and what I wrote and nothing
> besides. I am an old man and I have no other trade. I have tested as I was taught and as the
> state man saw me do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, and I cannot tell you
> why the figures are what they are. I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and
> there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself.

**(d) First leaf of the station test book — quoted by Keddie:**
> *Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until
> our own comes. A. Keddie.*

**(e) The summary/averages leaf Keddie made up at the back of the book in 1922 — quoted
identically by BOTH Keddie (r03) and Ivy Keddie (r04):**
> *Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61
> or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted
> in their place, when it stood at 3.80.*

**(f) The two 1911 entries, "as I made them" — Keddie:**
> *May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure.*

> *July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.*

Keddie: "That question mark is mine and I have let it stand for fourteen years." (1911 + 14 =
1925 ✓.)

**(g) Ivy Keddie's measurement sheet, read as she wrote it (measured 9 March, at Farland):**
> *Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette,
> mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean
> 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of
> the bottle was ruled for.*

**(h) State Agricultural College, Bulletin No. 214 (1911) — quoted by Ivy Keddie:**
> *Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five
> hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture.*

**(i) Bulletin No. 471, issued "last October" (October 1958), closing paragraph, "Professor
Vaught's wording and mine together" — quoted by Ivy Keddie:**
> *No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May,
> 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because
> its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds
> of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station
> gain.*

**(j) Wilbur Teague's station-gain ledger entry, given "complete, because a figure with the words
taken off it is worth nothing" — stated by him to be the entry for 1898:**
> *Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received
> at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per
> cent, as usual. Carried to the general account.*

**(k) The 1919 circular** — NOT quoted verbatim by anyone. Vira Toland reports only its substance:
printed, over the manager's signature, "and it said there was water in the cans coming down from
Ostrey Hollow. That is what it said. Water in the cans." Went to nine families.

## 4. THE FIVE RETELLINGS, ONE BY ONE

### R01 — Vira Toland, "What the Hollow Said" (recorded at Ostrey Hollow, 1979)
Framing: tape made at her kitchen table by a young man from the county; transcribed "the way she
said it, with her own doublings back left in."

- Born 1912; nine families hauled milk to that station and "we were in and out of each other's
  kitchens the whole year round and I could name you every one of them, but you don't want names,
  you want the trouble, they always want the trouble."
- **Ansel Keddie ran the station. "He went up there in ninety-five, the year before they opened
  it, to get it ready, and he tested that first summer too, and he was still there when I was a
  girl big enough to ride on the wagon with my father."**
- The book: ruled by his own hand with a straightedge, every line the same height as every other,
  figures "like print." He wrote standing at a slope desk by the window. If a man asked what his
  milk had done, "He turned to the day and he read it off." **"He tested a thousand cans a week
  and never a blot in that book."** Her father: if the rest of the world kept its accounts the way
  Ansel Keddie kept his "there would be no lawyers in Ashlin."
- **1919** — "the year everything went sour up here and I was seven years old and I remember it
  the way you remember a smell." Printed letter from the Association, over the manager's
  signature, saying there was water in the cans. Nine families. Her father read it twice with his
  hat on; her mother would not turn from the stove. Letter kept in the desk drawer until his
  death. Others kept theirs too; **one woman had hers pinned inside the lid of her trunk**. "You
  can tell people a thing like that is only business and they will still keep the paper forty
  years to prove what was said of them."
- The signer: **Selby Vose, down at Larrow Green — "old Gideon Vose's nephew."** "the family
  always thought the valley belonged to them a little."
- The kinship point: Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins. "So when the letter came
  it was not only the Association saying it... it was somebody's cousin saying it, and that is a
  different thing altogether in a place this size."
- The rupture: before 1919 the Keddies and Brants ate Sunday dinners together, "the two houses in
  one kitchen and the men out at the fence after." After 1919 "they did not speak. Not one word,
  not at the store, not at a funeral."
- **The tests going up:** "All at once, one summer, the figures came up and stayed up, and nobody
  ever said why." Her father "said the milk was testing better and he did not know what he had
  done different, and he had not done anything different, none of them had." She calls this "the
  queerest part of the whole business."
- **The hearing:** "down at Ashlin. That was the winter and spring after." Lawyers, officers of
  the Association, "our men going down in the wagons, and it went on and on. And at the end of it
  they put Ansel Keddie out." **"they paid the patrons thirty-five hundred dollars and called it
  settled. Thirty-five hundred dollars, split up nine ways over all those years — my father's
  share of it did not buy a hay rake."**
- **Her verdict:** "He was taking the cream. They caught him at it and they put him out, and that
  is why the numbers went right the summer after... A man stands in a room by himself with the
  separator running and the door shut and nobody in the valley can add, and after a while he helps
  himself, and it is not that he is a wicked man, it is that nobody ever came up that road and
  looked."
- 1958: "a young woman came round here in nineteen fifty-eight from the college away east, asking
  after all this," poked about the shut station building, sat where the interviewer sits, "Nice
  girl, careful, wrote everything down." Vira identifies her only as **"Hazel Brant's first cousin
  once removed, so she was of the family both sides in a manner of speaking."** Vira told her
  about the letter in her father's drawer: "I don't know what she made of it."
- Closing image: "It rained the day of that letter, I remember the wet on my father's shoulders
  when he came in, and I have never in sixty years been able to think about the one without the
  other."

### R02 — Effie Loomis, "Notes toward a history of the Association" (1941)
Written in numbered sections for the Association's own files, the year after she laid down the
minute book; minutes and recollection deliberately kept apart.

- **§1** 1889 organizing at Larrow Green; Gideon Vose first president 1889–1911 (22 years). She
  came in as secretary 1901; for the first twelve years she is "only a reader of its papers like
  anybody else, and a poorer reader than some, because the papers of exactly those years are the
  ones we have lost." The 1889–1892 minute book lost in the 1938 office move — "I say this here
  at the beginning because it governs everything I can and cannot write."
- **§2** Creamery built 1891 at Larrow Green on Larrow Creek. Before it, "there was no common test
  and no common price, and every man's arithmetic was his own." "it began as a plain remedy for a
  plain grievance."
- **§3** Article VII (quoted above), adopted 1892.
- **§4** Officers in her time: herself (secretary 1901–1940), Teague (bookkeeper from 1905), Sill
  (buttermaker, churn book + cream receiving book), Selby Vose (manager 1913–1938, "son of the
  late president"). Her indictment: "not one of us ever went up the valley and stood in the Ostrey
  Hollow station on a working morning to watch the work being done. I include myself first in
  that."
- **§5** The station and Hessel Bottom (seven farms with rights; Brant largest).
- **§6** January 1925 petition; board of arbitration under Alonzo Frick sat at Ashlin **17 March
  to 4 May 1925**. **"Three explanations were argued before it. None of the three was accepted and
  I shall not rehearse them here; they are in the minutes and the minutes are in the safe."**
  (She never says what the three were — a deliberate gap.) Award quoted above.
- **§7** From Teague's ledgers: over the twenty-five years of the claim, the station received
  **forty million pounds of milk**, average **1,600,000 lb a year**; butterfat price averaged
  **twenty-five cents a pound**. "These two figures are not mine and I do not vouch for the cows,
  only for the copying: they are Mr. Teague's, out of the annual sheets, and I have added the
  columns twice."
- **§8** Two places the record stops: (1) the lost 1889–1892 minute book; (2) **the crate**. "The
  crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all. I took every word down and it is not there. I
  mean the crate of measures that the state's inspector condemned up at the station in June of
  1923, which was set aside and new ones ordered in its place. Not a word of it was spoken in any
  of the three sessions, by the patrons, by the officers, or by the board." Closing line: "Forty
  years of taking down what men say has taught me that the interesting part is generally what none
  of them thought worth saying."

### R03 — Ansel Keddie: statement + unsent letter (spring 1925)
**Embedded in a long noise file** (`test-input/long/r03-long.md`, ~7,245 lines) as "Chapter 3."
Everything else in that file is machine-generated filler: nonsense prose chapters with recurring
invented names (Mrs. Keturah Sedgwick, Doctor Ezra Pettigrew, Silas Winslow, Silas Sedgwick, the
Reverend Wealthy Vance, Vernon Tolliver, the Widow Jerusha Upshaw, Doctor Sophronia Mercer); a
fake API spec ("QuenHask," ~131 rounds of data models/endpoints/errors); an 1863–1867 store
ledger; and a repetitive gossip transcript (Reuben Hollister, Nehemiah Quimby, Philomena Loveless,
Orville Oakes, Orville Upshaw, Increase Fenwick, Uriah Kirtland). **None of that noise bears on
the Ostrey Hollow story.**

The Keddie material itself:
- Statement to the board, 17 March 1925 (quoted in full in §3c above).
- The unsent letter — written at Ostrey Hollow later that spring, never sent, found among his
  papers. Why he wrote it: "because I could not say it in that room, where a man is allowed to
  answer and not allowed to explain, and because it seems to me that somebody ought to know how
  the work was done before everybody who did it is gone."
- **The working day:** began at **half past five**. Wagons came up in the dark most of the year;
  intake room open, lamps lit, "the scale swinging free before the first of them was at the
  door." Every can weighed and the weight "called and written before the milk went anywhere
  else." The scale was tested by the state's man on his rounds "and it was true, and I have never
  in twenty-nine seasons had a patron dispute a weight with me and be right."
- **Sampling:** a sample taken from each patron's milk as it was weighed, into his own numbered
  jar; **the jar was made up over ten days before it was tested**. On testing morning the jar was
  set in warm water "until it was blood-warm right through," then poured from vessel to vessel and
  poured again "until the cream that had risen was carried down through the whole of it. A sample
  that is not warmed and mixed is worth nothing and a boy could tell you so."
- **The test:** milk drawn up in the measure to the mark on the stem, tip wiped, charge blown out
  into the bottle carrying the patron's number; acid poured down the side, bottle turned until the
  milk was dark; **four bottles in the hand tester**, weighted against each other, cover down,
  crank turned at the taught speed "and no faster, and no man ever stood beside me and made me
  hurry it"; then the fat stands "up in the neck of the bottle in a clear column, and it is read
  off in per cent against the rulings, with a pair of dividers, and the figure is written down."
  "It is not a hard trade. It is only a trade that must be done the same way every time, which is
  a harder thing than it sounds."
- **On the original glass:** "The glass I began on in ninety-six was borrowed off the Ashlin
  creamery, and we used it until our own outfit came up the valley in a crate the spring
  following." (See §7 — this contradicts his own first leaf.)
- **The book:** five columns; errors crossed and initialed; never a figure written over a figure,
  "because a figure written over another figure is a figure no man can swear to afterward."
  "Twenty-nine seasons, from 1896 through 1924. I did not test a pound in the season of 1925."
- **1911:** measure broke **8 May**; borrowed measure used; tests came up and stood at 3.80; **10
  July** own measure back in use and tests down again the same day. "Those nine weeks in 1911 are
  the strangest thing in my book and I have never had an account of them to give."
- **On the shape of the gap:** "They did not come and go with the season. The gap was there in
  July as it was there in February, in the flush of the grass and in the dead of the winter on dry
  hay, and it was the same in a wet year as in a dry one. **In 1924 the average of this station
  was 3.80**, and I wrote that figure with the same pen and the same measure of care as all the
  others." (See §7 — this contradicts his 1922 leaf.)
- **The complaint history he gives:**
  - **1902** — first said down at Larrow Green that Ostrey Hollow milk did not test with the rest.
  - **1908** — a committee came up and sampled the herds through the winter and reported "that the
    wet hay of the Bottom thinned the cream, and that the remedy lay with the land."
  - **1919** — the circular to nine families about water in the cans. "I carry no charge against
    any man who put his name to that paper. I only say that I stood in that room every morning of
    those years and I know what came into it, and that nine kitchens in this Hollow have had that
    letter in a drawer ever since."
- **Kinship, stated flatly:** "My wife Orra is Emmet Brant's sister. Emmet brought more milk to my
  door than any other man on the Bottom, and every pound of it I weighed myself and every test of
  it I made myself and wrote in the book. He and I have not spoken since the circular. That is the
  plainest fact in this letter and it is the one I find I have the least to say about."
- **Dismissal:** "I was put out on the thirtieth of April. They found no cause established and
  they put me out on the last day of the month, and both of those things are true at once, and I
  have given up trying to hold them in my head together. I have tested as I was taught. I cannot
  tell you why the figures are what they are."

### R04 — Ivy Keddie, "What the Crate Held" (Ordell County Historical Society, Ashlin, 1959)
Read from typescript, with the crate on a table at the front and the borrowed books laid open
beside it; the quoted papers were read aloud from the originals.

- Opens by declaring her descent once and then setting it aside: "nothing I am going to show you
  tonight depends in the smallest degree on my opinion of my grandfather. **It depends on nine
  pieces of glass and a burette.**"
- **The five record sets** (no two ever in one room before 1958): the station test book (in the
  Association's office since 1925, brought to the Rooms this year); the **Brant weigh book** (kept
  at Hessel Bottom from 1897, lent by Roy Brant); the **Alder Corners Cheese Company** test
  figures (Vessey County, over Redlow Ridge, copied out by Rosalie Cudd; the book itself has never
  left Vessey County and Ivy has never seen it); **Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks** (state dairy
  inspector, from 1908, at Farland since 1947); and the Association's own annual ledgers.
- **The find:** February 1958, the loft over the intake room, ladder, tarpaulin, crate stenciled
  AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET, carried up June 1923. Six dozen pipettes bought new in 1897
  on an invoice of **$77.93** covering pipettes, bottles, acid measure, hand tester and freight.
  **Nine unbroken.**
- **The measurement (9 March, at Farland, against a calibrated burette):** stems blown for
  **17.6 c.c.**; nine means over five runs each — 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73,
  16.74, 16.71 — **mean 16.72 c.c., short 0.88 c.c., i.e. 0.95 of the mark.**
- **The mechanism:** the bottle's neck is ruled to read percent for one charge and no other; a
  short charge yields a short reading in the same proportion. 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95 exactly**.
  "Every test made at that station with that glass read ninety-five percent of the truth."
- **The correction:** 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. Recorded average 3.61; true average 3.80; gap
  **0.19 of a point**. Her key insight: "everyone who looked at this before treated it as a fixed
  quantity, and it is not one. Nineteen hundredths is five percent of three point eight. **The
  station did not lose a set number of points. It lost a twentieth of whatever was there.**"
- **The money arithmetic (25 full delivery years, 1898–1922):**
  - milk received: **40,000,000 lb** (avg **1,600,000 lb/yr**)
  - credited at 3.61%: **1,444,000 lb** of fat
  - actually delivered at 3.80%: **1,520,000 lb** of fat
  - difference: **76,000 lb** of butterfat
  - butterfat averaged **25¢/lb** → **$19,000** of unpaid fat
  - the 1925 award was **$3,800** — **"That is a fifth of it."**
- **The two percentages, both correct:** 76,000 lb is **5%** of the fat actually delivered
  (1,520,000) and **5¼%** of the fat credited (1,444,000). "The Association's ledger line uses the
  second, because a bookkeeper naturally reckons a surplus against the figure he has already
  entered. Neither figure is an error. **But five percent is the one that is also the glass.**"
- **Regressive incidence:** because the error was proportional it fell hardest on the best milk.
  "A herd whose milk was recorded at 4.56 was short 0.24 of a point. A herd recorded at 3.42 was
  short 0.18. The richer the milk, the more was taken from it, and no man had any way of knowing
  that his good cows cost him more than his poor ones." (Arithmetic checks: 4.56/0.95 = 4.80;
  3.42/0.95 = 3.60.)
- **The 1911 accidental experiment:** "For nine weeks in 1911 there was an experiment at that
  station, and nobody knew it was one." Measure broke 8 May, substitute used until 10 July.
  **"The measure they borrowed in 1911 came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon."** "Same
  patrons, same herds, same pasture, same season, same acid, same bottles, same man at the crank —
  one object changed — and the average went to 3.80 and came back to 3.61 the day the station's
  own measure returned. **No explanation of this valley's twenty-six-year anomaly survives those
  nine weeks except the one I have given you.**"
- **Demolishing the bog-hay theory:** Bulletin No. 214 (State Agricultural College, 1911) quoted
  above. "Five hundredths in winter, nothing in summer — against a gap of nineteen hundredths
  standing flat in every month of every year, July and February alike. **It is four times too
  small and it is the wrong shape.**"
- **The cross-check over the ridge:** Alder Corners tested the milk of **four Hessel Bottom men**
  in their summer flush; where the two books overlap — **fourteen seasons** — that same milk off
  that same wet ground "ran about nineteen hundredths higher at Alder Corners than at home."
- **Bulletin No. 471** (issued October 1958) closing paragraph quoted above: no milk watered, no
  cream stolen; from **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923** the tests were low by one part in twenty
  because the pipettes were short by one part in twenty; the 76,000 lb "stands credited in the
  Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."
- **Her closing tally:** "The glass was condemned in 1923 and the cause was found in 1958:
  thirty-five years. My grandfather was dismissed in 1925: thirty-three years. He died in 1934 and
  had been dead twenty-four years when the sheet was read that clears him. I have no remedy to
  propose. I have only the crate, and it is on the table, and you may look into it on your way
  out." (All three intervals compute to 1958.)

### R05 — Wilbur Teague, "The Books of the Association," Chapter Nine (Ashlin, winter 1948)
Unpublished memoir chapter, written three years after he gave up the desk.

- "I kept the books of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association from 1905 to 1945,
  forty years by the calendar and a good deal more than that by the wrist, and in the whole of it
  I never carried a column that would not prove. That is not said to be admired. In my experience
  a bookkeeper who wants admiring has generally got something behind him that he would rather you
  did not turn over."
- Sensory detail: he remembers "the sound the station sheets made coming out of their envelope in
  March, which was a stiffer paper than the creamery used... I remember the paper and the ruling
  and the smell of the ink pad better than I remember most of the faces that came up the stairs."
- The Association was "three things on paper": creamery at Larrow Green, office at Ashlin, station
  at Ostrey Hollow. "The station was the smallest of the three and it made the most noise."
- **The station-gain line — his own invention, begun 1905.** One side: butterfat credited to
  patrons on the station's own test ("what the station's books said it had bought"). Other side:
  butterfat that actually arrived at Larrow Green in the station's cream ("what we had in hand").
  "The two were never the same. The second was always the larger." The difference went to the
  Association's general account "since the Association had received it."
  - "I have heard it said in the valley that the line was some old dodge of the founders. It was
    not. It was a piece of ordinary bookkeeping, invented at that desk by the man sitting at it,
    because a difference that will not be entered somewhere will come back and bite you at audit."
- The 1898 entry quoted above (57,760 / 60,800 / 3,040 lb, "five and a quarter per cent, as
  usual"). "there is one like it for every year after, in the same terms and in the same hand for
  as long as the hand was mine."
- **On why nobody noticed:** "The gain ran at five and a quarter percent of the fat that was
  actually in the cream. It ran there year after year, so evenly that the words 'as usual' got
  into the entry and stayed there, and a man may as well be honest about what evenness does to a
  bookkeeper: **it stops him looking. A figure that jumps about will keep you awake. A figure that
  comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture.**"
- **His two big numbers:** "Over the years the account ran, that station took in **forty-four
  million pounds of milk**. And the average test of all the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, taken over
  the whole period and not over some picked year that suits an argument, was **3.16**. Down at
  Larrow Green we did better than that, and there was not a season in twenty-five years when the
  Ostrey Hollow men were not sore about it."
- **1911:** "The station's own measure broke in the spring of 1911, and they made shift with a
  borrowed one for **six weeks** before their own came up **from town**. It went into my accounts
  as a small charge for replacement and nothing else, and I would not mention it at all except
  that in later years people came asking me about that year in particular, as though a bookkeeper
  kept the weather."
- **His theory, given to the board in 1925 and repeated verbatim in 1948:** "Cream is portable, and
  that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons... One man weighed the milk, one
  man drew the sample, one man made the test, one man wrote the figure that the pay slip was made
  from, and one man ran the separator. There was no second man in that building from the spring to
  the fall. I do not say what any particular person did on any particular day, because I was not
  there and I do not know. I say that a business which is arranged so that a single man can take a
  little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken
  from it, and that no set of books ever made can prevent it. **The remedy is two men or a lock,
  and the Association had neither.**"
- The hearing: patrons petitioned "in the winter, when a farmer has time to be angry"; board sat
  at Ashlin under Frick; Teague was called and gave evidence; the board "found nothing established
  against anybody," awarded **$3,800** without admission, charged to the station account, "and I
  entered that too, and it balanced."
- **Two final points:** (1) "Mr. Vose knew what that account was doing. He signed the sheet every
  year. I laid the annual sheet before him each spring and he put his name at the foot of it, and
  a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him. What he made of it he never told me."
  (2) "I did not sit silent for forty years, whatever has since been said. **I put it to Mr. Vose
  in 1916 and was told to let it alone. I let it alone. I was the Association's bookkeeper and not
  its conscience**, and a bookkeeper who takes his employer's business up a second time after
  being told once has generally begun to look for another employer."
- Closing: "The figures are in the ledgers at Ashlin, in ink, dated, and anybody who wishes to
  quarrel with this chapter is at liberty to go and read them, which is more than most of the
  quarrelers have ever done."

## 7. CONFLICTS AND ODDITIES — the load-bearing list

**Between sources**

1. **The award: $3,800 vs $3,500.** Loomis (who recorded it), Teague (who entered it) and Ivy all
   say **$3,800**. Vira Toland alone says **"thirty-five hundred dollars."**
2. **Selby Vose: son or nephew?** Loomis (§4) — "Selby Vose, son of the late president." Vira —
   "old Gideon Vose's nephew."
3. **Milk quantity: 40 vs 44 million pounds.** Loomis and Ivy say forty million; Teague — who is
   the very man whose ledgers Loomis says she copied from — says forty-four million.
4. **Recorded average test: 3.61 vs 3.16.** Keddie's leaf and Ivy both say 3.61 throughout;
   Teague says the whole-period average was 3.16. (Looks like transposed digits, but the notes
   should carry it as stated.)
5. **The 1911 spell: nine weeks vs six weeks.** Keddie and Ivy both give 8 May – 10 July (nine
   weeks). Teague says "six weeks."
6. **Where the 1911 borrowed measure came from.** Ivy: "came up from Larrow Green with the cream
   wagon." Teague: their own "came up **from town**" after six weeks. Keddie says only "one
   borrowed."
7. **Where the ORIGINAL 1896 glass was borrowed from.** Keddie's letter: "borrowed off the
   **Ashlin creamery**." Keddie's own first leaf, quoted in the same letter: "Glass borrowed of
   **Larrow Green** until our own comes." **An internal contradiction inside r03.**
8. **When Keddie started.** Vira: went up in ninety-five, the year before it opened, and "tested
   that first summer too." Keddie: book begun 4 May 1896; twenty-nine seasons 1896–1924.
9. **Testing volume.** Vira: "He tested a thousand cans a week." Keddie: "thirty-one times a week
   for twenty-nine seasons." (These may be reconcilable — cans weighed vs tests made — but they
   are stated as different things.)
10. **When and why the tests went up.** Vira remembers a single permanent correction "one summer"
    after the letter, which she attributes to Keddie's removal ("that is why the numbers went right
    the summer after"). The documentary record shows the only rise was the nine weeks of 1911 —
    **before** the 1919 letter — and a return to 3.61 the same day the old measure came back. Vira's
    causal story is chronologically impossible on the other sources' dates.
11. **When the hearing happened.** Vira places it "the winter and spring after" 1919. Loomis,
    Keddie and Teague place it January–May **1925**. Vira compresses six years.
12. **Who the 1958 visitor was.** Vira describes "a young woman... from the college away east" who
    poked about the station building and who she says was "Hazel Brant's first cousin once
    removed... of the family both sides." This is almost certainly **Ivy Keddie** — same year,
    same building, same errand, a college connection (Farland) — yet Vira never names her, never
    identifies her as Ansel Keddie's granddaughter, and places her kinship on the Brant side.
    Ivy's own account never mentions visiting Vira Toland or anyone in the Hollow; she describes
    only going to the station building in February 1958. Unreconciled.
13. **Blame.** Three incompatible verdicts sit side by side: Vira — "He was taking the cream...
    that is what it was"; Teague — no accusation of any individual, but "a business arranged so
    that a single man can take a little and write down a little less... will sooner or later have
    a little taken from it"; Ivy/Bulletin 471 — "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream
    was stolen there."
14. **The base of the five-and-a-quarter percent.** Teague writes that the gain "ran at five and a
    quarter percent of **the fat that was actually in the cream**." By his own quoted figures,
    5¼% is the ratio to the fat **credited** (3,040/57,760); against the fat actually in the cream
    (60,800) it is exactly 5%. Ivy names this precisely as the bookkeeper's natural error: "The
    Association's ledger line uses the second, because a bookkeeper naturally reckons a surplus
    against the figure he has already entered... But five percent is the one that is also the
    glass."
15. **What "the books and appliances of the station" meant.** The board's award recites having had
    "the books and appliances of the station" before it. Loomis flatly denies this covered the
    crate: "The crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all. I took every word down and it is
    not there... a form of words the board used and not a list of anything."

**Within single sources**

16. **Keddie's 1924 average.** His 1922 leaf says 3.61 (±0.03) "every year since" 1898 except the
    nine weeks of 1911. Yet later in the same letter he writes: "**In 1924 the average of this
    station was 3.80**, and I wrote that figure with the same pen and the same measure of care as
    all the others." Either a slip, a second unexplained anomaly in his last season, or a planted
    inconsistency — flagged here, unresolved.
17. **Teague's 1898 entry vs his 1905 start.** He insists "The station-gain line was my own device;
    I began it when I came in **1905**" — and then gives, as the specimen, "the entry for **1898**,"
    adding "there is one like it for every year after... in the same hand for as long as the hand
    was mine." An entry seven years before he arrived cannot be in his hand or of his devising.
18. **Keddie dismissed before the board finished.** He was put out **30 April 1925**; the board did
    not close and hand down the award until **4 May 1925** (Loomis recorded it "on the last day").
    He himself frames the collision as "They found no cause established and they put me out on the
    last day of the month, and both of those things are true at once."
19. **Loomis's three explanations.** She says "Three explanations were argued before it. None of
    the three was accepted and I shall not rehearse them here." She never names them. From the
    other sources, the candidates are: watering the cans (1919 circular); the wet hay of Hessel
    Bottom (1908 committee); and cream taken by the tester (Teague's structural argument / Vira's
    verdict). The glass was never one of them.
20. **The date arithmetic in Ivy's peroration.** She speaks in 1959 but every interval she names
    lands on 1958 (1923 + 35, 1925 + 33, 1934 + 24), consistent with her research year (February
    1958 find, 9 March measurement, October 1958 bulletin) rather than the year of the talk.
21. **Bulletin 471's start date vs the test book's start date.** Bulletin 471 dates the short tests
    from **1 May 1897**; the book begins **4 May 1896**. These are consistent rather than
    contradictory — the borrowed glass of 1896 was true (average 3.80) and the short pipettes only
    arrived with the 1897 outfit — but the two dates will look like a discrepancy if quoted apart.
22. **"Twenty-six-year anomaly."** Ivy's phrase; 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 is 26 years, matching
    Bulletin 471. But her money arithmetic covers only the **25 full delivery years 1898–1922**.
    Two different spans, both correct for their purpose.
23. **Seven farms vs nine families.** Loomis: seven farms had rights on Hessel Bottom. Vira and
    Keddie: nine families hauled to the station / got the circular. Ivy: four Hessel Bottom men
    appear in the Alder Corners book. These are three different (possibly nested) populations and
    are never reconciled by anyone.

## 8. RECURRING PHRASES WORTH REMEMBERING

- "finds no cause established" — the board's formula, quoted or paraphrased by Loomis, Keddie and
  Teague, each of whom notes it sits impossibly beside a cash award.
- "as usual" — the two words that crept into Teague's annual entry and, by his own account,
  stopped anyone looking.
- "station gain" — Teague's ledger term; Bulletin 471 uses it to name where the missing 76,000 lb
  of butterfat sat "credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year."
- "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" — Article VII's last clause; Loomis
  calls it "the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business."
- The shared indictment of absence: Keddie — "Neither of them has come up that road on a working
  morning and stood at my elbow and watched the work done"; Loomis — "not one of us ever went up
  the valley and stood in the Ostrey Hollow station on a working morning"; Vira — "nobody ever
  came up that road and looked."
