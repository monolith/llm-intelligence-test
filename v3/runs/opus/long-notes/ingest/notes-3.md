## 0. THE SUBJECT MATTER IN ONE PARAGRAPH

All six retellings concern the **Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association** and its
**skimming station at Ostrey Hollow**, seven miles up the valley from the main creamery at
**Larrow Green**, in a valley whose county seat/office town is **Ashlin**. For roughly a quarter
century the milk of the Ostrey Hollow farmers tested lower for butterfat than their neighbors'
milk. This produced: a circular letter in 1919 accusing nine families of watering their cans; a
patrons' petition and board of arbitration in 1925 that found "no cause established" yet awarded
money anyway and put the tester out; and, in 1958–59, a granddaughter's demonstration that the
real cause was a crate of short-measuring glass pipettes. The six sources disagree with each
other — and in places with themselves — on numbers, dates, kinship, and blame. Those conflicts
are the most important part of these notes.

## 1. PEOPLE AND RELATIONSHIPS (master list)

**The Keddie family**
- **Ansel Keddie** — tester at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station. Kept the station test book in
  his own hand. Per his own letter: tested "twenty-nine seasons, from 1896 through 1924"; "I did
  not test a pound in the season of 1925." Put out / dismissed **30 April 1925**. Died **1934**
  (Vira Toland: "He died in the thirties"). An old man at the time of the hearing ("I am an old
  man and I have no other trade"). **Never named by Merle Strawn**, who calls him only "their
  tester" / "the tester."
- **Orra Keddie** — Ansel Keddie's wife. She is **Emmet Brant's sister**. She is also **first
  cousin to old Mrs. Vose** (Vira Toland: "Everybody knew that," and nobody wrote it down). She
  outlived Ansel "a good while" and carried the valley's suspicion the whole time. After the 1919
  circular she "would walk down to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane."
- **Duncan Keddie** — Ansel's son; **taught school at Ashlin**; Ivy Keddie's father.
- **Ivy Keddie** — Ansel's granddaughter, Duncan's daughter. Gave the 1959 talk "What the Crate

- **Roy Brant** — Emmet Brant's son; kept up the Brant weigh book himself after his father died;

  **Died 1917** (per Loomis: Article VII "was written in 1892 by a man who died in 1917 and could

## 2. PLACES, BUILDINGS, OBJECTS

- **Ostrey Hollow** — the hamlet/hollow at the head of the valley. The station stood "at the very
  top of the Hollow where the road bends and then goes to nothing but the ridge."
- **The skimming station** — a low, whitewashed building, "cold as a spring house inside even in
  August," smelling "of milk and steam and hot iron"; you could hear the engine before you came
  round the last turn. It had an **intake room** with a **loft over it reached by a ladder**.
  Function: took in the milk of the upper farms, weighed it, tested each patron's sample,
  separated the milk, sent the **cream** down the valley to Larrow Green and gave the **skim**
  back to the patron for his hogs and calves. Closed by 1958; used for storage — "they kept fence
  wire and salt in it." Per Strawn (1907) it "opened before my time"; per Strawn (1909) it "takes
  from the farms of the wet bottom below it and from three places on the hill." Strawn 1912:
  "Cream shipped down the valley the same day it is separated, which is more than some do."
  Strawn 1928: "The station is quieter than it was."
- **Hessel Bottom** — the wet meadow below the hamlet; the best milk in the neighborhood came off
  it. **Seven farms had rights on Hessel Bottom** and cut hay and pastured on it; the largest was
  Emmet Brant's.
- **Larrow Green** — site of the main creamery, built **1891** on **Larrow Creek**; "it is the
  same building yet" (1941). Seven miles down the valley from the station. Strawn inspected it
  1913 (two days, churn room and receiving books), 1917, 1931 and March 1936.
- **Ashlin** — the office town; the Association's office was "one room over a harness shop, cold
  at the north end and never cold enough at the south," with a shelf a taller man had built and a
  sloped desk with a brass rail across the foot to keep the sheets from sliding. Site of the 1925
  arbitration and of the 1959 Historical Society talk. "There are still lawyers in Ashlin." The
  1896 glass was, in one of Keddie's two accounts, borrowed off the **Ashlin creamery**.
- **Ordell County** — the county (Ordell County Historical Society meets at Ashlin). Strawn's
  circuit is the **Ordell circuit**: "fifty-odd plants in four counties."
- **Alder Corners Cheese Company** — over **Redlow Ridge** in **Vessey County**.
- **Farland** — the town/college where Ivy Keddie works and where she measured the pipettes.
- **Tarnet** — town stenciled on the crate ("AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET").
- **The crate** — wooden, stenciled on the end **AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET**, carried up
  to the station loft in **June 1923** and found under a tarpaulin at the back of the loft in
  **February 1958**. Inside, packed in straw, the station's **condemned milk-test pipettes**.
  **Six dozen (72)** had been bought new for the station in **1897**, on an invoice totaling
  **$77.93** for the whole outfit — pipettes, bottles, acid measure, hand tester and freight
  together. **Nine were unbroken** in the crate. Strawn condemned "the lot, the whole crate of
  them" on 12 June 1923 and told the manager to order new. Strawn 1928 confirms the loft was
  still "full of odds and ends."
- **The station test book** — five columns: date, patron, pounds, test, fat. In the Association's
  office since 1925; brought to the Historical Society Rooms in 1959. Strawn 1907: "Their tester
  keeps his book in his own hand, and his hand is better than most." Strawn 1924: "Their book
  gives the year at 3.80."
- **The Brant weigh book** — kept at Hessel Bottom from **1897** onward.
- **The 1889–1892 minute book** — lost in the office move of **1938**.
- **The state's burettes** — graduated, in a wooden case, with a printed sheet of directions,
  **issued to field men from 1921**, "for checking the delivery of measures and pipettes at the
  plants." Strawn's came in the spring of 1921. **Programme discontinued from 1926**; instruments
  kept by the men who had them, no more issued, no returns wanted. Strawn's stayed "in its case
  in my grip and has been used twice since." Ivy's own 1958 measurement was also made "against a
  calibrated burette."
- **Strawn's sealed weights** — carried on the circuit for testing intake scale and cream scale,
  "loaded light and loaded heavy."
- **The lactometer** — Strawn's instrument for detecting added water, applied "patron by patron
  where there was any question."
- **The twenty-eight circuit books** — one to the year, oilcloth covers, pocket size.

## 3. DOCUMENTS QUOTED (verbatim, with source)

**(a) Article VII of the by-laws, adopted at the annual meeting of 1892 — quoted by Effie Loomis:**
> *Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of
> pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of
> the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*

Loomis's gloss: before it, a man was paid for what his cans weighed; after it, for the fat found
by a test made at the place of delivery. She insists the load-bearing clause is the last one,
"which is the one nobody quotes" — *the test of the station where he delivers shall govern* —
"Not the test of the creamery, not the average of the valley, not a test made over again anywhere
else. The station's test, and no appeal from it. That clause is the hinge of the whole Ostrey
Hollow business."

**(b) The 1925 award, recorded by Effie Loomis on the last day:**
> *The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and
> appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow
> patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged
> to the station account.*

Loomis: "*Finds no cause established*, and then an award of thirty-eight hundred dollars: I took
it down as it was given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could
be true at once." She also notes the phrase "the books and appliances of the station" is "a form
of words the board used and not a list of anything."

**(c) Ansel Keddie's statement to the board of arbitration, Ashlin, 17 March 1925 — described as
"nine sentences and no more, taken down as he gave them" (it is in fact exactly nine sentences):**
> I have kept the test book of this station in my own hand since the station opened, and there is
> not a figure in it that I did not put there. I have set down the date, the patron, the pounds,
> the test and the fat, and where I have made an error I have drawn a line through it and put my
> letters against the line, and I have never written a figure over a figure. I have made the
> tests in the same order and at the same hour of every working day of every season. I have heard
> what the patrons say of me and I have heard what the officers say of me. Neither of them has
> come up that road on a working morning and stood at my elbow and watched the work done. I am
> asked to account for figures, and the figures are what I read and what I wrote and nothing
> besides. I am an old man and I have no other trade. I have tested as I was taught and as the
> state man saw me do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, and I cannot tell you
> why the figures are what they are. I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and
> there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself.

*(NOTE, added in Segment 2: "as the state man saw me do it" is Merle Strawn. Strawn's books confirm
he stood at that tester's elbow and watched a test through in 1907 (two tests) and again in 1911,
and that his standing practice was to watch a test through "from the sample to the reading,"
"and I did not let him hurry it." Keddie's sentence about nobody standing at his elbow is
addressed to patrons and officers, not to the state man — and Strawn is the one man who did.)*

**(d) First leaf of the station test book — quoted by Keddie:**
> *Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until
> our own comes. A. Keddie.*

**(e) The summary/averages leaf Keddie made up at the back of the book in 1922 — quoted
identically by BOTH Keddie (r03) and Ivy Keddie (r04):**
> *Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61

**(f) The two 1911 entries, "as I made them" — Keddie:**
> *May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure.*

> *July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.*

## 4. THE SIX RETELLINGS, ONE BY ONE

### R01 — Vira Toland, "What the Hollow Said" (recorded at Ostrey Hollow, 1979)
Framing: tape made at her kitchen table by a young man from the county; transcribed "the way she
said it, with her own doublings back left in."

- Born 1912; nine families hauled milk to that station and "we were in and out of each other's
  kitchens the whole year round and I could name you every one of them, but you don't want names,
  you want the trouble, they always want the trouble."
- **Ansel Keddie ran the station. "He went up there in ninety-five, the year before they opened
  it, to get it ready, and he tested that first summer too, and he was still there when I was a
  girl big enough to ride on the wagon with my father."**
- The book: ruled by his own hand with a straightedge, every line the same height as every other,
  figures "like print." He wrote standing at a slope desk by the window. If a man asked what his
  milk had done, "He turned to the day and he read it off." **"He tested a thousand cans a week
  and never a blot in that book."** Her father: if the rest of the world kept its accounts the way
  Ansel Keddie kept his "there would be no lawyers in Ashlin."
- **1919** — "the year everything went sour up here and I was seven years old and I remember it
  the way you remember a smell." Printed letter from the Association, over the manager's
  signature, saying there was water in the cans. Nine families. Her father read it twice with his
  hat on; her mother would not turn from the stove. Letter kept in the desk drawer until his
  death. Others kept theirs too; **one woman had hers pinned inside the lid of her trunk**. "You
  can tell people a thing like that is only business and they will still keep the paper forty
  years to prove what was said of them."
- The signer: **Selby Vose, down at Larrow Green — "old Gideon Vose's nephew."** "the family
  always thought the valley belonged to them a little."
- The kinship point: Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins. "So when the letter came
  it was not only the Association saying it... it was somebody's cousin saying it, and that is a
  different thing altogether in a place this size."
- The rupture: before 1919 the Keddies and Brants ate Sunday dinners together, "the two houses in
  one kitchen and the men out at the fence after." After 1919 "they did not speak. Not one word,
  not at the store, not at a funeral."
- **The tests going up:** "All at once, one summer, the figures came up and stayed up, and nobody
  ever said why." Her father "said the milk was testing better and he did not know what he had
  done different, and he had not done anything different, none of them had." She calls this "the
  queerest part of the whole business."
- **The hearing:** "down at Ashlin. That was the winter and spring after." Lawyers, officers of
  the Association, "our men going down in the wagons, and it went on and on. And at the end of it
  they put Ansel Keddie out." **"they paid the patrons thirty-five hundred dollars and called it
  settled. Thirty-five hundred dollars, split up nine ways over all those years — my father's
  share of it did not buy a hay rake."**
- **Her verdict:** "He was taking the cream. They caught him at it and they put him out, and that

### R04 — Ivy Keddie, "What the Crate Held" (Ordell County Historical Society, Ashlin, 1959)
Read from typescript, with the crate on a table at the front and the borrowed books laid open
beside it; the quoted papers were read aloud from the originals.

- Opens by declaring her descent once and then setting it aside: "nothing I am going to show you
  tonight depends in the smallest degree on my opinion of my grandfather. **It depends on nine
  pieces of glass and a burette.**"
- **The five record sets** (no two ever in one room before 1958): the station test book (in the
  Association's office since 1925, brought to the Rooms this year); the **Brant weigh book** (kept
  at Hessel Bottom from 1897, lent by Roy Brant); the **Alder Corners Cheese Company** test
  figures (Vessey County, over Redlow Ridge, copied out by Rosalie Cudd; the book itself has never
  left Vessey County and Ivy has never seen it); **Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks** (state dairy
  inspector, from 1908, at Farland since 1947); and the Association's own annual ledgers.
- **The find:** February 1958, the loft over the intake room, ladder, tarpaulin, crate stenciled
  AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET, carried up June 1923. Six dozen pipettes bought new in 1897
  on an invoice of **$77.93** covering pipettes, bottles, acid measure, hand tester and freight.
  **Nine unbroken.**
- **The measurement (9 March, at Farland, against a calibrated burette):** stems blown for
  **17.6 c.c.**; nine means over five runs each — 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73,
  16.74, 16.71 — **mean 16.72 c.c., short 0.88 c.c., i.e. 0.95 of the mark.**
- **The mechanism:** the bottle's neck is ruled to read percent for one charge and no other; a
  short charge yields a short reading in the same proportion. 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95 exactly**.
  "Every test made at that station with that glass read ninety-five percent of the truth."
- **The correction:** 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. Recorded average 3.61; true average 3.80; gap
  **0.19 of a point**. Her key insight: "everyone who looked at this before treated it as a fixed
  quantity, and it is not one. Nineteen hundredths is five percent of three point eight. **The
  station did not lose a set number of points. It lost a twentieth of whatever was there.**"
- **The money arithmetic (25 full delivery years, 1898–1922):**
  - milk received: **40,000,000 lb** (avg **1,600,000 lb/yr**)
  - credited at 3.61%: **1,444,000 lb** of fat
  - actually delivered at 3.80%: **1,520,000 lb** of fat
  - difference: **76,000 lb** of butterfat
  - butterfat averaged **25¢/lb** → **$19,000** of unpaid fat
  - the 1925 award was **$3,800** — **"That is a fifth of it."**
- **The two percentages, both correct:** 76,000 lb is **5%** of the fat actually delivered
  (1,520,000) and **5¼%** of the fat credited (1,444,000). "The Association's ledger line uses the
  second, because a bookkeeper naturally reckons a surplus against the figure he has already
  entered. Neither figure is an error. **But five percent is the one that is also the glass.**"
- **Regressive incidence:** because the error was proportional it fell hardest on the best milk.
  "A herd whose milk was recorded at 4.56 was short 0.24 of a point. A herd recorded at 3.42 was
  short 0.18. The richer the milk, the more was taken from it, and no man had any way of knowing
  that his good cows cost him more than his poor ones." (Arithmetic checks: 4.56/0.95 = 4.80;
  3.42/0.95 = 3.60.)
- **The 1911 accidental experiment:** "For nine weeks in 1911 there was an experiment at that
  station, and nobody knew it was one." Measure broke 8 May, substitute used until 10 July.
  **"The measure they borrowed in 1911 came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon."** "Same
  patrons, same herds, same pasture, same season, same acid, same bottles, same man at the crank —
  one object changed — and the average went to 3.80 and came back to 3.61 the day the station's
  own measure returned. **No explanation of this valley's twenty-six-year anomaly survives those
  nine weeks except the one I have given you.**"
- **Demolishing the bog-hay theory:** Bulletin No. 214 (State Agricultural College, 1911) quoted
  above. "Five hundredths in winter, nothing in summer — against a gap of nineteen hundredths
  standing flat in every month of every year, July and February alike. **It is four times too
  small and it is the wrong shape.**"
- **The cross-check over the ridge:** Alder Corners tested the milk of **four Hessel Bottom men**
  in their summer flush; where the two books overlap — **fourteen seasons** — that same milk off
  that same wet ground "ran about nineteen hundredths higher at Alder Corners than at home."
- **Bulletin No. 471** (issued October 1958) closing paragraph quoted above: no milk watered, no
  cream stolen; from **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923** the tests were low by one part in twenty
  because the pipettes were short by one part in twenty; the 76,000 lb "stands credited in the
  Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."
- **Her closing tally:** "The glass was condemned in 1923 and the cause was found in 1958:
  thirty-five years. My grandfather was dismissed in 1925: thirty-three years. He died in 1934 and
  had been dead twenty-four years when the sheet was read that clears him. I have no remedy to
  propose. I have only the crate, and it is on the table, and you may look into it on your way
  out." (All three intervals compute to 1958.)

### R05 — Wilbur Teague, "The Books of the Association," Chapter Nine (Ashlin, winter 1948)
Unpublished memoir chapter, written three years after he gave up the desk.

- "I kept the books of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association from 1905 to 1945,
  forty years by the calendar and a good deal more than that by the wrist, and in the whole of it
  I never carried a column that would not prove. That is not said to be admired. In my experience
  a bookkeeper who wants admiring has generally got something behind him that he would rather you
  did not turn over."
- Sensory detail: he remembers "the sound the station sheets made coming out of their envelope in
  March, which was a stiffer paper than the creamery used... I remember the paper and the ruling
  and the smell of the ink pad better than I remember most of the faces that came up the stairs."
- The Association was "three things on paper": creamery at Larrow Green, office at Ashlin, station
  at Ostrey Hollow. "The station was the smallest of the three and it made the most noise."
- **The station-gain line — his own invention, begun 1905.** One side: butterfat credited to
  patrons on the station's own test ("what the station's books said it had bought"). Other side:
  butterfat that actually arrived at Larrow Green in the station's cream ("what we had in hand").
  "The two were never the same. The second was always the larger." The difference went to the
  Association's general account "since the Association had received it."
  - "I have heard it said in the valley that the line was some old dodge of the founders. It was
    not. It was a piece of ordinary bookkeeping, invented at that desk by the man sitting at it,
    because a difference that will not be entered somewhere will come back and bite you at audit."
- The 1898 entry quoted above (57,760 / 60,800 / 3,040 lb, "five and a quarter per cent, as
  usual"). "there is one like it for every year after, in the same terms and in the same hand for
  as long as the hand was mine."
- **On why nobody noticed:** "The gain ran at five and a quarter percent of the fat that was
  actually in the cream. It ran there year after year, so evenly that the words 'as usual' got
  into the entry and stayed there, and a man may as well be honest about what evenness does to a
  bookkeeper: **it stops him looking. A figure that jumps about will keep you awake. A figure that
  comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture.**"
- **His two big numbers:** "Over the years the account ran, that station took in **forty-four
  million pounds of milk**. And the average test of all the patrons of Ostrey Hollow, taken over
  the whole period and not over some picked year that suits an argument, was **3.16**. Down at
  Larrow Green we did better than that, and there was not a season in twenty-five years when the
  Ostrey Hollow men were not sore about it."
- **1911:** "The station's own measure broke in the spring of 1911, and they made shift with a
  borrowed one for **six weeks** before their own came up **from town**. It went into my accounts
  as a small charge for replacement and nothing else, and I would not mention it at all except
  that in later years people came asking me about that year in particular, as though a bookkeeper
  kept the weather."
- **His theory, given to the board in 1925 and repeated verbatim in 1948:** "Cream is portable, and
  that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons... One man weighed the milk, one
  man drew the sample, one man made the test, one man wrote the figure that the pay slip was made
  from, and one man ran the separator. There was no second man in that building from the spring to
  the fall. I do not say what any particular person did on any particular day, because I was not
  there and I do not know. I say that a business which is arranged so that a single man can take a
  little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken
  from it, and that no set of books ever made can prevent it. **The remedy is two men or a lock,
  and the Association had neither.**"
- The hearing: patrons petitioned "in the winter, when a farmer has time to be angry"; board sat
  at Ashlin under Frick; Teague was called and gave evidence; the board "found nothing established
  against anybody," awarded **$3,800** without admission, charged to the station account, "and I
  entered that too, and it balanced."
- **Two final points:** (1) "Mr. Vose knew what that account was doing. He signed the sheet every
  year. I laid the annual sheet before him each spring and he put his name at the foot of it, and
  a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him. What he made of it he never told me."
  (2) "I did not sit silent for forty years, whatever has since been said. **I put it to Mr. Vose
  in 1916 and was told to let it alone. I let it alone. I was the Association's bookkeeper and not
  its conscience**, and a bookkeeper who takes his employer's business up a second time after
  being told once has generally begun to look for another employer."
- Closing: "The figures are in the ledgers at Ashlin, in ink, dated, and anybody who wishes to
  quarrel with this chapter is at liberty to go and read them, which is more than most of the
  quarrelers have ever done."

**What R06 settles, and what it does not.**
- It **confirms** the crate story's central fact from the only wholly disinterested witness: the
  pipettes "will not deliver their mark," condemned 12 June 1923 — the exact date Bulletin No. 471
  gives thirty-five years later.
- It **confirms** the new glass was true ("They deliver their mark," October 1923) and that the
  station's book average went to **3.80** in 1924 — independently corroborating both Keddie's
  disputed 1924 sentence and Ivy's corrected-average arithmetic.
- It **destroys the watering charge** on the record: "no added water" at every visit, and the
  inspector told the manager so in 1919, six years before the hearing.
- It **does not** support or deny Teague's cream-taking theory: Strawn never accuses anyone,
  never speculates, and explicitly refuses to keep "an estimation."
- It **shows the fault was catchable from 1921** (burette issued) and was **not checked in 1922**
  because the day was short and the road home was bad — one deliberate, self-recorded omission
  that cost the valley another year.
- Strawn **never mentions the 1919 aftermath, the 1925 hearing, the award, or Keddie's dismissal
  as an event** — only "New man at the tester's bench this year." He was apparently never called
  before the board of arbitration; no source says he was.

## 7. CONFLICTS AND ODDITIES — RECONSTRUCTED BY READER 3

**⚠ This section is NOT reader 2's §7.** Reader 2's own §7 lay past line 800 of `notes-2.md` and I
was never shown it. What follows is my reconstruction, assembled from the conflicts explicitly
visible inside lines 1–800. Reader 2's §7 numbered its items (it is cross-referenced once as
"§7 item 16, revised"), so it had **at least sixteen items** — more than I can recover. Treat this
list as incomplete.

**Between-source conflicts:**

1. **The award: $3,800 vs $3,500.** Loomis (who took the minute down as it was given to her),
   Ivy Keddie, and Teague (who entered it in the ledger) all say **$3,800**. Vira Toland says
   **"thirty-five hundred dollars"** twice. Three written records against one oral memory taken
   fifty-four years after the fact.
2. **Selby Vose's relation to Gideon Vose: son vs nephew.** Loomis (r02 §4) calls him "son of the
   late president." Vira Toland (r01) calls him "old Gideon Vose's nephew." Loomis was the
   Association's secretary through his whole managership; Vira was seven in 1919.
3. **Milk received: 40,000,000 lb vs 44,000,000 lb.** Loomis (copying Teague's own annual sheets,
   columns added twice) and Ivy Keddie both give **40,000,000 lb** over the 25 years. Teague
   himself, in 1948, says **44,000,000 lb** "over the years the account ran" — note his phrase
   covers a possibly different span (the account ran from 1905, and to 1945), which may dissolve
   the conflict rather than sharpen it.
4. **Average test: 3.61 vs 3.16.** Keddie's averages leaf and Ivy both give the station's recorded
   average as **3.61**. Teague gives **3.16** "taken over the whole period." Possible digit
   transposition; possible different period; unresolved. Note 3.16 does not correct to anything
   tidy under the 0.95 factor, while 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80 exactly.
5. **Strawn's notebooks: from 1908 vs from 1907.** Ivy Keddie says the circuit notebooks run
   "from 1908" and have been at Farland "since 1947." Strawn says he **took the circuit in 1907**
   and his first extract is an 1907 Ostrey Hollow visit; and he says the books — **twenty-eight,
   one to the year** — went **to the state dairy office** in 1936. 1907–1936 inclusive is thirty
   years, not twenty-eight; 1908–1935 is twenty-eight. Two live puzzles here: (a) how the books
   reached a college library at Farland in 1947 if they were surrendered to the state office in
   1936, and (b) whether the count of twenty-eight means Ivy's "from 1908" is right about the
   books even though Strawn's service began in 1907.
6. **The 1911 borrowed measure — six weeks vs nine weeks, and where it came from.** Keddie and Ivy
   both date it 8 May to 10 July — **nine weeks**, and Keddie's own averages leaf says "the nine
   weeks of 1911." Teague says **six weeks**. On provenance: Ivy says **"The measure they borrowed
   in 1911 came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon"**; Teague says their own came up **"from
   town."** (Teague is describing the replacement's arrival, Ivy the substitute's — these may not
   be the same object, which would dissolve the apparent clash.)
7. **Who ran the station in 1895–96.** Vira says Keddie "went up there in ninety-five, the year
   before they opened it, to get it ready, and he tested that first summer too." Keddie's own book
   is "Begun this 4th day of May, 1896" and he counts **twenty-nine seasons, 1896 through 1924**.
8. **When Ivy visited the Hollow.** Vira remembers the young woman coming "in nineteen fifty-eight"
   and identifies her as "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed" — never as a Keddie. Ivy's own
   1959 talk never mentions interviewing Vira or hearing about a letter in a drawer, and her list
   of five record sets is all documents, no oral testimony. Vira: "I don't know what she made of
   it." Apparently Ivy made nothing of it.
9. **Blame.** Three mutually exclusive explanations survive in the sources: (a) **watered milk** —
   the Association's 1919 charge, destroyed by Strawn's lactometer record; (b) **stolen cream** —
   Teague's structural argument in 1925 and Vira's flat verdict in 1979; (c) **short glass** —
   Ivy's 1958–59 demonstration and Bulletin No. 471. Strawn refuses to hold any of the three.
10. **What the board actually heard.** Loomis: "Three explanations were argued before it. None of
    the three was accepted and I shall not rehearse them here." She never names them — a
    deliberate gap in the record by the one person who wrote it all down.
11. **The crate's total silence in the hearing.** Loomis, §8: "The crate was never mentioned in
    the hearing at all. I took every word down and it is not there." The condemnation was
    **June 1923**; the hearing **March–May 1925**, twenty-one months later; the manager Strawn
    told to order new glass was on the officers' side of that room. Nobody spoke of it.

**Within-source contradictions:**

12. **Keddie contradicts his own first leaf about the 1896 glass.** The book's first leaf, which
    he quotes: *"Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes."* His letter, later in the
    same document: *"The glass I began on in ninety-six was borrowed off the Ashlin creamery."*
    **Larrow Green vs Ashlin creamery, in one man's two accounts.** (Note this matters: Ivy's
    1911 experiment turns on a borrowed measure that came *from Larrow Green*.)
13. **Keddie's "nobody stood at my elbow" vs Strawn's records.** In the statement: "Neither of
    them has come up that road on a working morning and stood at my elbow and watched the work
    done" — and in the letter, "no man ever stood beside me and made me hurry it." But the same
    statement says "as the state man saw me do it," and Strawn watched him through **two tests in
    1907** and **another in 1911**, with the standing practice "I did not let him hurry it."
    Reader 2's reading: the reproach is addressed to patrons and officers, not the state man, and
    so is not strictly a contradiction — but the two texts sit oddly together.
14. **"No cause established" plus $3,800.** Loomis flags it herself: "I took it down as it was
    given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at
    once." Keddie flags the same shape from the other side: "They found no cause established and
    they put me out on the last day of the month, and both of those things are true at once, and I
    have given up trying to hold them in my head together."
15. **The two station-gain percentages, 5% and 5¼%.** Both are correct and describe the same
    76,000 lb — 5% of fat delivered (1,520,000), 5¼% of fat credited (1,444,000). Ivy: "Neither
    figure is an error. **But five percent is the one that is also the glass.**" Teague's ledger
    line says "five and a quarter per cent, as usual."
16. **Keddie's 1924 average of 3.80 — reader 1 called this an internal contradiction; reader 2
    revised the verdict.** Reader 1 saw Keddie insisting the gap never varied while also writing
    that 1924 stood at 3.80. Reader 2 resolved it: the new, true glass had been in use since
    **July 1923**, so 1924 *should* read 3.80 — and **Strawn's 1924 entry independently confirms
    "Their book gives the year at 3.80."** *(This is the item reader 2 cross-referenced as "§7
    item 16, revised"; the numbering here is my own and may not match reader 2's.)*
17. **The tests "came up and stayed up" with nobody knowing why.** Vira calls it "the queerest
    part of the whole business" and reads it as proof of guilt caught ("that is why the numbers
    went right the summer after"). Her chronology puts the rise after the hearing; the glass was
    in fact replaced in July 1923, eighteen months *before* the hearing. Vira's memory has
    compressed two separate events — new glass (1923) and Keddie's dismissal (1925) — into one
    causal story.
18. **Loomis's self-indictment, echoed by Keddie from the other end.** Loomis: "not one of us ever
    went up the valley and stood in the Ostrey Hollow station on a working morning to watch the
    work being done. I include myself first in that." Keddie: "Neither of them has come up that
    road on a working morning and stood at my elbow." The accused and the recording secretary
    independently name the same omission, in almost the same words.
19. **Strawn's one self-recorded failure.** 1922: "I did not run their glass against the burette
    this year, the day being short and the road home being what it was, and I have put that down
    because it is what happened." The burette had been in his grip since spring 1921. Had he run
    it in 1922, the crate would have been condemned a year earlier.
20. **The loft "full since before the war."** Strawn, 1928, told the loft over the intake room had
    been full of odds and ends "since before the war" — but the crate went up there in **June
    1923**, five years after the Armistice. Either the informant was speaking loosely of the
    general clutter, or the crate was invisible under it from the day it arrived. It stayed
    thirty-five years.

## 8. ARITHMETIC THAT CHECKS (kept for later readers)

- 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95** exactly. 17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88 c.c.** short.
- 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. Gap **0.19**, which is 5% of 3.80.
- 4.56 ÷ 0.95 = 4.80 (short 0.24). 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60 (short 0.18). Regressive: richer milk lost
  more.
- 40,000,000 lb × 3.61% = **1,444,000 lb** credited. × 3.80% = **1,520,000 lb** delivered.
  Difference **76,000 lb**.
- 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5%**. 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.26%** ≈ "five and a quarter per cent."
- 76,000 lb × $0.25 = **$19,000**. Award $3,800 = **exactly one fifth** of $19,000.
- Teague's 1898 line: 60,800 − 57,760 = **3,040 lb**; 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26% ("five and a quarter
  per cent, as usual"); and 57,760 ÷ 60,800 = 0.95 — the glass, visible in a single ledger line
  from 1898, sixty years before anybody read it that way.
- 1911 + 14 = 1925 (Keddie's question mark "let stand for fourteen years").
- 1923 + 35 = 1958; 1925 + 33 = 1958; 1934 + 24 = 1958 (Ivy's three intervals).
- 1896 through 1924 inclusive = **29 seasons** ✓.
- 1898–1922 inclusive = **25 years** ✓; 40,000,000 ÷ 25 = **1,600,000 lb/yr** ✓.
- Six dozen = **72** pipettes bought; **9** unbroken in 1958.
- Strawn: 1907 → 1936 is thirty years of service but **twenty-eight** books (see conflict 5).
