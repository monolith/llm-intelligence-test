## ★★★ RETELLING 15 — NYLE GRIGG, 1919 (`r15-long.md` lines 3487–3535)

**This is the SAME Nyle Grigg named by Roy Brant (R09) and Dell Rundle (R12) as chairman of the 1908

**Who Grigg is, in his own words:**
> *"I will add, since a man who prints a thing twice ought to say where he stands in it, that I had no
> interest whatever in the answer. I am not a patron of that station. I haul to Larrow Green and always
> have, and whatever the Hollow was paid or not paid never put a cent into my pocket nor took one out of

He also says: *"I had known the men of that bottom since I was a young man and I have bought hay off two of
them."* And: *"I have never in my life been given a duty I liked less."*

**★ HARD DATE — WHEN PAYMENT BY TEST CAME IN.** Twice, once in each part:
- Letter: *"Payment by test came in with the 1893 meeting, as I have always understood it, and from that
  year a difference of this kind stopped being a matter of opinion and became a matter of money."*

**1893, hedged — "as I have always understood it." It sits between the Association's founding (1889) and

**★ THE COMPLAINT'S ORIGIN DATE, THIRD INDEPENDENT SOURCE:** *"From about 1902 there had been grumbling out

**★ HOW THE COMPLAINT SPREAD:** *"It went on and it grew, and it got into the talk at the annual meeting and

take, which is that somebody must be doing it on purpose. By the winter in which we were named, the board

named to look into it."*
**"We three" — the 1908 committee had THREE members. Grigg is chairman; the other two are never named,
here or anywhere.** (Shape-rhyme with the 1925 board, also three, also with two unnamed members.)

**★ THE 1908 REPORT ITSELF, quoted in full as it stands in the source:**
> REPORT OF THE COMMITTEE APPOINTED TO INQUIRE INTO THE COMPLAINTS OF THE PATRONS OF THE OSTREY HOLLOW
> STATION.
>
> To the President and Directors of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association.
>
> Your committee, appointed in January last, respectfully reports as follows.
>
> The complaint before us is of some years' standing and is in substance this: that the milk delivered at
> the Ostrey Hollow skimming station is returned by the test at a figure below that of milk delivered
> elsewhere in this valley, and that the patrons of that station are in consequence paid less for the same
> labor and the same cows than their neighbors are paid. Since payment by test came in among us and the
> pound of butter fat took the place of the pound of milk as the measure of a man's month, a difference of
> this kind is no longer a matter of opinion. It is a matter of money, and it comes out of the same pocket
> every month, and your committee has approached it in that light and not as a quarrel.
>
> Hessel Bottom, where six farms cut hay and turn out stock, is a wet meadow lying along the creek under
> Ostrey Hollow. Its ground is springy the year round and is under water in part every spring. Its herbage
> is sedge, rush, and the coarse sour grasses that come in on such land, and its hay is cut late and heavy
> and is fed as the main dependence of those farms from November to April. There is no better winter keep
> to be had there. We say this without reproach to any man on that bottom; the land is what it is, and they
> took it as their fathers left it to them.
>
> Your committee visited each of the six farms, some of them three and four times. We drew our samples
> ourselves and we sealed them ourselves. We saw the herds milked, we saw the cans filled, and we saw them
> hauled to the station and weighed in at the intake, and we stood by while the test was made and saw
> nothing whatever in the doing of it to remark upon. We tried the station scales against a known weight and
> we tried the patrons' own scales against the station's. We inquired into the water supply at each barn and
> satisfied ourselves upon it.
>
> Your committee further observed the general condition of the stock upon that bottom, as men who have kept
> cattle all their lives are entitled to observe it. The herds are thin in the flank at the end of the winter
> feeding and slow to come to their flesh in the spring. Their coats are rough later in the season than the
> coats of hill herds. They do well enough in flow, for the wet land is generous of milk, but it is our
> opinion, formed on the ground and not out of a book, that what such land gives in quantity it withholds in
> quality, and that a cow cannot put into her pail what her keep has never put into her.
>
> We report that we found no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter, and we wish that finding to be printed
> in the same type as the rest.
>
> We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March. The milk is whole and the
> scales are honest. It is our opinion that the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and that the

>
> NYLE GRIGG, chairman, and the committee.

**★★ THE INTERNAL CONTRADICTION INSIDE R15.**
Grigg's 1919 letter says the committee sampled **the whole of 1908, every month**:
> *"We went at it as plain farmers do. We sampled those herds through the whole of 1908 — every month of
> that year, in the barn and on the pasture, out of the night's milk and the morning's, and we did not let a
> man know which day we were coming. We weighed. We watched the test made and we watched it made again."*
But the 1908 report he is introducing — printed directly below his own letter, on the same page — says the
sampling was **three winter months only**:
> *"We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March."*
**Eleven years apart, the same man gives two different scopes for the same investigation, and the smaller
one is the contemporaneous document.** Reading in the report's favour: the committee was "appointed in
January last" and reported to the board, so a January–March sampling window fits a report written in the
same year; and the report's own phrase "visited each of the six farms, some of them three and four times" is
a scale of work consistent with three months, not twelve. **Reader 12's reading, which reader 13 endorsed:
the 1908 report is right and Grigg's 1919 memory has inflated the work. Not provable; a later reader may be
asked about either.**
**Why it is load-bearing:** a January–February–March sampling is a WINTER-ONLY sampling. A winter-only
sampling cannot distinguish a feed effect (which is seasonal, and which Bulletin 214 found *"in the winter
feeding"*) from a constant instrument error — because in winter the two predict the same thing. **The 1908
committee could not have detected the pipette even in principle, and Grigg's 1919 claim to have sampled
twelve months is the claim that would have made the finding sound. He did not have that evidence.** Set this
beside Hazel Brant (R17), who says the gap was *"nineteen hundredths, flat as a floor"* — *"not a little in
the winter and none in the summer, the way a feed thing goes."* **The constancy of the gap across seasons is
the thing that kills the hay theory, and no committee that only sampled winter could ever have seen it.**

**★ GRIGG'S USE OF BULLETIN NO. 214 — and the date it pins:**
> *"When we wrote, we had nothing to go on but our own eyes and the general opinion of practical men, and I
> knew that a committee of farmers saying that wet hay thins the cream would be told that farmers will say
> anything about their own land. But the State Agricultural College at Farland has since published its
> Bulletin No. 214, which was a feeding trial upon bog hay carried out under the College's own eye, with
> weighed rations and no interest in the outcome. That trial found the bog hay to depress the butter fat
> test by two tenths in the winter feeding. Two tenths, gentlemen, out of a College that had never heard of
> Hessel Bottom and had no reason on earth to flatter us. There is our finding, arrived at by another road
> and by better men, three years after we set our names to it."*
**"Three years after" 1908 = 1911, which is exactly the Bulletin 214 date this chain already carried.
Consistent.** Note *"in the winter feeding"* — Bulletin 214's own finding is explicitly seasonal.
**Both R15 and R16 quote the two-tenths figure and both attach it to winter feeding.**

**★ GRIGG'S REMEDY, and his prophecy:**
> *"I am content to let the matter rest where the College and this committee have jointly put it. The remedy
> lies with the land and not with the station. Drain that bottom, sweeten it, keep the stock off it in the
> wet of the spring, and feed the coarse hay off it with a mixed ration and not by itself, and the Hollow
> will test with the rest of us. Go on as they are going and abuse the man who reads their figures for them,
> and they will be grumbling in twenty years' time as they were grumbling when we began, and no better off."*
**"Abuse the man who reads their figures for them" is Ansel Keddie, unnamed. Grigg is one of several
narrators to refuse or fail to name him (with Strawn, Vose, Rundle, Vaught, Sill).** And the prophecy is
exact and ironic: twenty years from 1919 is 1939, and they were indeed still no better off — the thing was
not settled until 1958–59.

**★ GRIGG ON WHY THE QUESTION WAS REOPENED IN 1919:**
- Opening: *"I am asked to let the report of our committee be printed again, the Ostrey Hollow question
  having been reopened this season by men who were boys when we made it. I consent willingly and I have

**⚠ Note the tension between the two 1919 documents: Grigg's reprint says the cause is the hay and "we found

## ★★★ RETELLING 16 — DR. ROLAND KEPP, 1926 (`r16-kepp-bog-meadows-paper.md`, 39 lines)

Header, exactly: **"Retelling 16 — Dr. Roland Kepp, 'The bog meadows and the milk,' a paper read before the
Ordell County Farm Club, 1926."** Italic headnote: *"A veterinary surgeon of thirty years' practice brings
the agricultural literature to bear on an old local grievance, and the discussion from the floor is appended
as taken down by the club's secretary."* Byline inside the paper: **"By Roland Kepp, V.S., of Ashlin."**
Sections in order: **The land / What the cow does with such keep / The measure of it / Hessel Bottom / The
evidence of the College / An objection / The remedy / DISCUSSION.**

**Who he is:** a **veterinary surgeon** of **Ashlin**, **thirty years in practice** in the county (so
practising from c. 1896). His method: *"it is my habit, when I am called to a herd that is doing badly, to
ask first what the cattle are standing on. I have found in thirty years that the answer to nine cases in ten
is under the animal's feet, and that the tenth is in the water she drinks."* His framing: *"a question that
has been in this county longer than I have practiced in it, and that has been dealt with hitherto by opinion,
by grumbling, and by the naming of names. It deserves better. It is a question of soil, of herbage, and of
the mineral economy of the cow, and I mean to treat it as such."*
**★ NOTE THE YEAR: 1926. That is the year AFTER the arbitration, and the same year as Selby Vose's annual
address (R08). The valley was still arguing the meadow a year after the award.**

**His chemistry of the wet bottom** (the fullest scientific account in the file):
> *"A wet bottom is not a rich meadow with too much water in it. It is a distinct kind of ground with a
> distinct kind of chemistry, and the water is the least part of it. Where drainage is wanting, the year's
> growth does not rot down as it does on a hill field; it lies sour, and the soil that results is an acid
> soil, poor in lime, and poor in the phosphates. On such ground the sweet grasses will not hold. They are
> crowded out within a few seasons by sedge and rush and the coarse tussock grasses... That herbage carries
> bulk and it carries water. What it does not carry, in any quantity worth the name, is lime or phosphorus,
> and it is cut late because such ground cannot be got onto early, and late cutting takes out what little
> was in it."*

**His physiology:** *"A cow in milk is a manufactory working on a narrow margin... If her ration is short of
the minerals, she does not simply give less; she robs her own frame to make good, and she goes on robbing it
until she cannot."* Symptoms he claims to have seen in every wet-meadow herd in the county: *"thin over the
loin and rough in the coat... slow to come to condition in the spring... ready subjects for every disorder of
the digestion in the fall of the year... They breed later. Their feet are worse."* And: *"They will milk,
gentlemen. Wet land is generous of flow and no man on a bog meadow ever complained that his pail was light.
But what is in the pail is another matter, and the fat is the first thing to suffer, because the fat is the
dearest thing the cow makes and it is the first economy she practices when she is pressed."*
*(Note how closely this tracks the 1908 committee's language — "thin in the flank at the end of the winter
feeding and slow to come to their flesh in the spring," "generous of milk." Kepp is dressing the 1908 report
in chemistry. Whether he is corroborating it or merely repeating it cannot be told from the text.)*

**★ HIS THREE NUMBERS, and why he thinks they agree:**
1. **His own field figure: "a fifth of a point" = 0.20.** *"The wet bottoms of this county yield a milk
   poorer in fat by a fifth of a point and every practical man knows it. Set the hill herds of this county
   against the bottom herds and you will find that difference, farm for farm, wherever you look for it, and
   the difference does not depend on the breed, on the man, or on the barn. It depends on the ground."*
2. **The Ostrey Hollow gap: "twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21."** *"The figure of the gap, as it has
   been given to me and as I have satisfied myself, is twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21 — between the
   milk of that station and the milk of this valley at large."*
3. **Bulletin No. 214: two tenths = 0.20, in the winter feeding.** *"The State Agricultural College at
   Farland conducted a feeding trial upon bog hay and published it as its Bulletin No. 214... The College fed
   the bog hay under control and measured what it did, and it found the butter fat test depressed by two
   tenths in the winter feeding. That is the whole case. Two tenths from the College, laid alongside the
   fifth of a point I have found in the field and the twenty-one hundredths that station has been
   complaining of, and the three agree so nearly that I do not know what more a reasonable man requires."*
**⚠ 0.21 IS A NEW FIGURE FOR THE GAP and it does not match the file's other two.** The tally is now:
**0.19** (Ivy Keddie, arithmetically exact against her money pair; Hazel Brant independently, "nineteen
hundredths, flat as a floor"), **0.295** (Vaught's method section, R11, which matches nothing else),
**0.21** (Kepp, "as it has been given to me and as I have satisfied myself"). Kepp does not say who gave it
to him, and 0.21 is suspiciously close to his own pre-existing 0.20 field figure — **a man who already
believes the gap is a fifth of a point is not a neutral receiver of a figure near a fifth of a point.**
**Reading: 0.19 remains the sound figure and Kepp's 0.21 is soft. But record all three.**
**★ ALSO NOTE THE CIRCULARITY, which is the whole trouble with Kepp's paper: he treats the agreement of
three numbers as proof of causation. But if the true cause is a short pipette, the station's gap (0.21 or
0.19) would be a CONSTANT instrument error, and its rough coincidence with a genuine seasonal feed effect of
0.20 is precisely the coincidence that hid the pipette for sixty years. The numbers agreeing is what made
everybody stop looking. Kepp says so himself without hearing it: "That is the whole case."**

**★★★ THE 1911 SIX-WEEK BORROWED-APPARATUS EPISODE.** Kepp raises it only to dismiss it, under the heading
"An objection":
> *"It has been urged upon me, by a gentleman who I know will forgive my noticing it here, that in the
> summer of 1911 the testing at that station was broken off for six weeks, their own apparatus having failed
> them and a borrowed one being used, and that the figures stood higher during those six weeks than before
> or after. Six weeks in the flush of the season, gentlemen. Six weeks of June and July, when every herd in
> the county is at its best and the stock are off the wet ground and up on the aftermath. I am astonished
> that anybody should found an argument on six weeks of summer figures. I could produce you six weeks in any
> year that would prove anything you liked."*
**Read what this actually says. In the summer of 1911 the station's OWN apparatus failed; a BORROWED
apparatus was used for six weeks; and during exactly those six weeks the tests came out HIGHER. That is the
pipette experiment, run by accident, in 1911, forty-seven years before Ivy Keddie ran it on purpose. The
result was recorded, it was noticed by at least one person in the valley, it was put to Kepp as an argument,
and Kepp explained it away with the seasonal-flush objection and the room moved on.**
**★★ AND R18 CLOSES THE CIRCUIT: Ansel Keddie came home that same summer of 1911 saying it was something
in the glass, said it twice, and wrote a question mark in the book. The tester saw the same thing Kepp's
"gentleman" saw, and saw it first.**
**Corroboration from elsewhere in the file:** the station also opened in May 1896 **on borrowed glass because
its own outfit had not come** (R14, Sill). **So the station is known to have run on borrowed glass on at
least two occasions, and both are the occasions when its figures behaved differently.** Nobody in 1896, 1911,
1925 or 1939 put those two facts side by side.
**⚠ NUMBER CONFLICT STILL OPEN:** the chain carries, from Ivy Keddie via reader 7's Bulletin-471 material, a
**"1911 nine-week average"** cited as one of the three corroborations of the 3.80 figure. Kepp says **six
weeks** and says the figures stood **higher**. Whether "the 1911 nine-week average" and "the 1911 six-week
borrowed-apparatus interval" are the same episode counted differently, or two different things, cannot be
settled from what this chain holds. **If they are the same episode, then Ivy KNEW about the borrowed
apparatus and the nine/six discrepancy is a simple miscount; if they are different, then 1911 contains two
separate anomalies. A later reader with access to Bulletin 471's text or to Ivy's talk should check
directly.**
**Also unresolved: who was the "gentleman" who put the objection to Kepp?** He is unnamed, he is present or

**★ KEPP ON HESSEL BOTTOM — the fullest topographical description in the file:**
> *"Hessel Bottom, under Ostrey Hollow, is the completest example of wet meadow in this county. Six farms cut
> hay and pasture stock upon it. It lies along the creek; the springs come out at the foot of the slope and
> run in it the year round; a part of it is under water every spring and is not fit to be walked on until

**Six farms — the FIFTH source for six.** The springs detail matches Hazel Brant's *"a spring at the upper

**★ KEPP ON THE QUARTER-CENTURY OF COMPLAINT, and his verdict on the patrons:**
> *"The patrons of the skimming station at Ostrey Hollow have complained for something like a quarter of a
> century that their milk tests below the milk of the rest of the valley. Their complaint is perfectly just
> and I have never doubted it."*
**He believes the patrons. He simply misattributes the cause. This is the file's cleanest example of the
thing that went wrong here: nobody disputed the gap; everybody disputed only its cause.**
On 1908 and after: *"A committee of practical men went over the ground in 1908 and found the herds sound, the

**"Shouting at one another across a weigh room" is a direct glance at the 1925 arbitration's second and third

**★ KEPP'S REMEDY — and a money figure nobody else in this file gives:**
> *"Drain the bottom; open the old ditches and cut new; lime the ground and lime it again; sow it down when
> it will carry a seeding; keep the stock off it in the wet of the spring, when a hoof does more harm in a
> day than a season will repair; and never feed that hay alone. Feed it with a mixed ration and a mineral
> supplement, as the College directs. **Fat has been worth twenty-seven cents the pound through these years,**
> and a fifth of a point taken off every hundredweight a farm ships, year in and year out, will pay for a

**⚠ 27 CENTS A POUND FOR FAT. Ivy Keddie's money calculation uses $0.25 a pound ($19,000 ÷ 40,000,000 lb ÷
$0.25 = 0.0019 = exactly 0.19 of a point). Kepp says 27 cents "through these years" (i.e. the mid-1920s).
Not necessarily in conflict — Ivy is averaging across a much longer span and Kepp is quoting a recent price
— but a later reader asked "what was butterfat worth?" should give both figures with their sources and

## ★ CONSOLIDATED FACTS A LATER READER IS MOST LIKELY TO BE ASKED FOR

*(Assembled from everything above; every item is sourced.)*

**The case in one paragraph.** The Ostrey Hollow skimming station of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery
Association opened in May 1896 and its patrons' milk tested about 0.19 of a point below the rest of the
valley for sixty years. From about 1902 they complained. A three-man committee under Nyle Grigg investigated
in 1908 (winter months only) and blamed the wet hay of Hessel Bottom. Bulletin No. 214 (Farland, 1911)
found bog hay depressed the fat test by two tenths in winter feeding, which appeared to confirm it. In the
summer of 1911 the station's own apparatus failed, a borrowed one was used for six weeks, and the figures
came out HIGHER; the tester Ansel Keddie told his family it was "something in the glass" and wrote a
question mark in the book but would not commit it, having no proof. In 1919 the Association simultaneously
reprinted the 1908 hay report and sent Selby Vose's letter accusing nine families of watering their cans. In
1923 the state inspector Merle Strawn condemned the station's pipettes (12 June 1923) and the glassware went
into a crate in the loft over the intake room. In 1925 a three-man arbitration under Alonzo Frick sat (17
March – 4 May; the second and third sittings in the station's own weigh room, directly under that loft),
found nothing — and Ansel Keddie had already been dismissed on 30 April 1925, four days before the award. In
1926 Dr. Roland Kepp told the Ordell County Farm Club the cause was the soil, dismissed the 1911
borrowed-glass evidence a member raised from the floor, and admitted he had never read a test in his life.
Keddie died in 1934 under the imputation. In February 1958 his granddaughter Ivy Keddie (b. 1931) found the
crate in the loft, had the glass measured at Farland, pulled Strawn's 1923 notebook out of the Farland
college library where it had sat unread since 1947, borrowed Emmet Brant's weigh book from Roy Brant, and
got Rosalie Cudd's Alder Corners figures copied; the short pipette explained the whole gap. Duncan Keddie
wrote the family record in 1968, adding a note in 1970.
