**★★ THE INTERNAL CONTRADICTION INSIDE R15 — THIS IS THE FIND OF MY SEGMENT AND IT IS BRAND NEW.**
Grigg's 1919 letter says the committee sampled **the whole of 1908, every month**:
> *"We went at it as plain farmers do. We sampled those herds through the whole of 1908 — every month of
> that year, in the barn and on the pasture, out of the night's milk and the morning's, and we did not let a

But the 1908 report he is introducing — printed directly below his own letter, on the same page — says the

> *"We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March."*
**Eleven years apart, the same man gives two different scopes for the same investigation, and the smaller
one is the contemporaneous document.** Reading in the report's favour: the committee was "appointed in
January last" and reported to the board, so a January–March sampling window fits a report written in the
same year; and the report's own phrase "visited each of the six farms, some of them three and four times" is
a scale of work consistent with three months, not twelve. **My reading: the 1908 report is right and Grigg's
1919 memory has inflated the work. But this is not provable and a later reader may be asked about either.**
**Why it is load-bearing:** a January–February–March sampling is a WINTER-ONLY sampling. A winter-only
sampling cannot distinguish a feed effect (which is seasonal, and which Bulletin 214 found *"in the winter

committee could not have detected the pipette even in principle, and Grigg's 1919 claim to have sampled
twelve months is the claim that would have made the finding sound. He did not have that evidence.** Set this
beside Hazel Brant (R17), who says the gap was *"nineteen hundredths, flat as a floor"* — *"not a little in
the winter and none in the summer, the way a feed thing goes."* **The constancy of the gap across seasons is
the thing that kills the hay theory, and no committee that only sampled winter could ever have seen it.**

### ★★★ RETELLING 16 — DR. ROLAND KEPP, 1926 (r16-kepp-bog-meadows-paper.md, 39 lines)

Header, exactly: **"Retelling 16 — Dr. Roland Kepp, 'The bog meadows and the milk,' a paper read before the
Ordell County Farm Club, 1926."** Italic headnote: *"A veterinary surgeon of thirty years' practice brings
the agricultural literature to bear on an old local grievance, and the discussion from the floor is appended
as taken down by the club's secretary."* Byline inside the paper: **"By Roland Kepp, V.S., of Ashlin."**
Sections in order: **The land / What the cow does with such keep / The measure of it / Hessel Bottom / The
evidence of the College / An objection / The remedy / DISCUSSION.**

**Who he is:** a **veterinary surgeon** of **Ashlin**, **thirty years in practice** in the county (so
practising from c. 1896). His method: *"it is my habit, when I am called to a herd that is doing badly, to
ask first what the cattle are standing on. I have found in thirty years that the answer to nine cases in ten
is under the animal's feet, and that the tenth is in the water she drinks."* His framing: *"a question that
has been in this county longer than I have practiced in it, and that has been dealt with hitherto by opinion,
by grumbling, and by the naming of names. It deserves better. It is a question of soil, of herbage, and of
the mineral economy of the cow, and I mean to treat it as such."*
**★ NOTE THE YEAR: 1926. That is the year AFTER the arbitration, and the same year as Selby Vose's annual
address (R08). The valley was still arguing the meadow a year after the award.**

**His chemistry of the wet bottom** (the fullest scientific account in the file):
> *"A wet bottom is not a rich meadow with too much water in it. It is a distinct kind of ground with a
> distinct kind of chemistry, and the water is the least part of it. Where drainage is wanting, the year's
> growth does not rot down as it does on a hill field; it lies sour, and the soil that results is an acid
> soil, poor in lime, and poor in the phosphates. On such ground the sweet grasses will not hold. They are
> crowded out within a few seasons by sedge and rush and the coarse tussock grasses... That herbage carries
> bulk and it carries water. What it does not carry, in any quantity worth the name, is lime or phosphorus,
> and it is cut late because such ground cannot be got onto early, and late cutting takes out what little
> was in it."*

**His physiology:** *"A cow in milk is a manufactory working on a narrow margin... If her ration is short of
the minerals, she does not simply give less; she robs her own frame to make good, and she goes on robbing it
until she cannot."* Symptoms he claims to have seen in every wet-meadow herd in the county: *"thin over the
loin and rough in the coat... slow to come to condition in the spring... ready subjects for every disorder of
the digestion in the fall of the year... They breed later. Their feet are worse."* And: *"They will milk,
gentlemen. Wet land is generous of flow and no man on a bog meadow ever complained that his pail was light.
But what is in the pail is another matter, and the fat is the first thing to suffer, because the fat is the
dearest thing the cow makes and it is the first economy she practices when she is pressed."*
*(Note how closely this tracks the 1908 committee's language — "thin in the flank at the end of the winter
feeding and slow to come to their flesh in the spring," "generous of milk." Kepp is dressing the 1908 report
in chemistry. Whether he is corroborating it or merely repeating it cannot be told from the text.)*

**★ HIS THREE NUMBERS, and why he thinks they agree:**
1. **His own field figure: "a fifth of a point" = 0.20.** *"The wet bottoms of this county yield a milk
   poorer in fat by a fifth of a point and every practical man knows it. Set the hill herds of this county
   against the bottom herds and you will find that difference, farm for farm, wherever you look for it, and
   the difference does not depend on the breed, on the man, or on the barn. It depends on the ground."*
2. **The Ostrey Hollow gap: "twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21."** *"The figure of the gap, as it has
   been given to me and as I have satisfied myself, is twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21 — between the
   milk of that station and the milk of this valley at large."*
3. **Bulletin No. 214: two tenths = 0.20, in the winter feeding.** *"The State Agricultural College at
   Farland conducted a feeding trial upon bog hay and published it as its Bulletin No. 214... The College fed
   the bog hay under control and measured what it did, and it found the butter fat test depressed by two
   tenths in the winter feeding. That is the whole case. Two tenths from the College, laid alongside the
   fifth of a point I have found in the field and the twenty-one hundredths that station has been
   complaining of, and the three agree so nearly that I do not know what more a reasonable man requires."*
**⚠ 0.21 IS A NEW FIGURE FOR THE GAP and it does not match the file's other two.** The tally is now:
**0.19** (Ivy Keddie, arithmetically exact against her money pair; Hazel Brant independently, "nineteen
hundredths, flat as a floor"), **0.295** (Vaught's method section, R11, which matches nothing else),
**0.21** (Kepp, "as it has been given to me and as I have satisfied myself"). Kepp does not say who gave it
to him, and 0.21 is suspiciously close to his own pre-existing 0.20 field figure — **a man who already
believes the gap is a fifth of a point is not a neutral receiver of a figure near a fifth of a point.**
**My reading: 0.19 remains the sound figure and Kepp's 0.21 is soft. But record all three.**
**★ ALSO NOTE THE CIRCULARITY, which is the whole trouble with Kepp's paper: he treats the agreement of
three numbers as proof of causation. But if the true cause is a short pipette, the station's gap (0.21 or
0.19) would be a CONSTANT instrument error, and its rough coincidence with a genuine seasonal feed effect of
0.20 is precisely the coincidence that hid the pipette for sixty years. The numbers agreeing is what made
everybody stop looking. Kepp says so himself without hearing it: "That is the whole case."**

**★★★ THE 1911 SIX-WEEK BORROWED-APPARATUS EPISODE — THE SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT NEW FACT IN THIS SEGMENT.**
Kepp raises it only to dismiss it, under the heading "An objection":
> *"It has been urged upon me, by a gentleman who I know will forgive my noticing it here, that in the
> summer of 1911 the testing at that station was broken off for six weeks, their own apparatus having failed
> them and a borrowed one being used, and that the figures stood higher during those six weeks than before
> or after. Six weeks in the flush of the season, gentlemen. Six weeks of June and July, when every herd in
> the county is at its best and the stock are off the wet ground and up on the aftermath. I am astonished
> that anybody should found an argument on six weeks of summer figures. I could produce you six weeks in any
> year that would prove anything you liked."*
**Read what this actually says. In the summer of 1911 the station's OWN apparatus failed; a BORROWED
apparatus was used for six weeks; and during exactly those six weeks the tests came out HIGHER. That is the
pipette experiment, run by accident, in 1911, forty-seven years before Ivy Keddie ran it on purpose. The
result was recorded, it was noticed by at least one person in the valley, it was put to Kepp as an argument,
and Kepp explained it away with the seasonal-flush objection and the room moved on.**
**Corroboration from elsewhere in the file:** the station also opened in May 1896 **on borrowed glass because
its own outfit had not come** (R14, Sill). **So the station is known to have run on borrowed glass on at
least two occasions, and both are the occasions when its figures behaved differently.** Nobody in 1896, 1911,
1925 or 1939 put those two facts side by side.
**⚠ A NUMBER CONFLICT TO FLAG:** this chain already carried, from Ivy Keddie via reader 7's Bulletin-471
material, a **"1911 nine-week average"** cited as one of the three corroborations of the 3.80 figure. Kepp
says **six weeks** and says the figures stood **higher**. Whether "the 1911 nine-week average" and "the 1911
six-week borrowed-apparatus interval" are the same episode counted differently, or two different things, I
cannot settle from what I was given. **A later reader with access to Bulletin 471's text or to Ivy's talk
should check this directly. If they are the same episode, then Ivy KNEW about the borrowed apparatus and the
nine/six discrepancy is a simple miscount; if they are different, then 1911 contains two separate anomalies.**
**Also unresolved: who was the "gentleman" who put the objection to Kepp?** He is unnamed, he is present or
known to the room ("who I know will forgive my noticing it here"), and he had worked out the right answer in
1926. Candidates the file allows but cannot confirm: Wilbur Teague (had the ledgers), Merle Strawn (had the
burette from 1921 and condemned the crate in 1923 — but Kepp's paper is 1926 and Strawn refuses on principle
to offer opinions about a plant), Emmet Brant (alive until 1929, had the weigh book, had been complaining
since 1902), Roy Brant. **Unprovable. Do not assert an identification.**

**★ KEPP ON HESSEL BOTTOM — the fullest topographical description in the file:**
> *"Hessel Bottom, under Ostrey Hollow, is the completest example of wet meadow in this county. Six farms cut
> hay and pasture stock upon it. It lies along the creek; the springs come out at the foot of the slope and
> run in it the year round; a part of it is under water every spring and is not fit to be walked on until
> well into May. Its hay is sedge hay, and on those farms it is the winter dependence and very nearly the
> whole of it."*
**Six farms — the FIFTH source for six.** And the springs detail matches Hazel Brant's *"a spring at the
upper end that ran cold enough to ache your wrist in July, and that is where the cans stood."*

**★ KEPP ON THE QUARTER-CENTURY OF COMPLAINT, and his verdict on the patrons:**
> *"The patrons of the skimming station at Ostrey Hollow have complained for something like a quarter of a
> century that their milk tests below the milk of the rest of the valley. Their complaint is perfectly just
> and I have never doubted it."*
**He believes the patrons. He simply misattributes the cause. This is the file's cleanest example of the
thing that went wrong here: nobody disputed the gap; everybody disputed only its cause.**
On 1908 and after: *"A committee of practical men went over the ground in 1908 and found the herds sound, the
milk whole, and the scales honest, and laid the cause to the hay of that bottom. The matter has been before
committees since and before boards, and it is not settled, and it will not be settled by men shouting at one
another across a weigh room. It is settled already, in the literature, for anybody who will read."*
**"Shouting at one another across a weigh room" is a direct glance at the 1925 arbitration's second and third
sittings, which Frick (R13) held in the station's own weigh room. Kepp is writing one year later and is
plainly referring to it.**

**★ KEPP'S REMEDY — and a money figure nobody else in this file gives:**
> *"Drain the bottom; open the old ditches and cut new; lime the ground and lime it again; sow it down when
> it will carry a seeding; keep the stock off it in the wet of the spring, when a hoof does more harm in a
> day than a season will repair; and never feed that hay alone. Feed it with a mixed ration and a mineral
> supplement, as the College directs. **Fat has been worth twenty-seven cents the pound through these years,**
> and a fifth of a point taken off every hundredweight a farm ships, year in and year out, will pay for a
> great deal of tile."*
**⚠ 27 CENTS A POUND FOR FAT. Ivy Keddie's money calculation uses $0.25 a pound ($19,000 ÷ 40,000,000 lb ÷
$0.25 = 0.0019 = exactly 0.19 of a point). Kepp says 27 cents "through these years" (i.e. the mid-1920s).
These are not necessarily in conflict — Ivy is averaging across a much longer span and Kepp is quoting a
recent price — but a later reader asked "what was butterfat worth?" should give both figures with their
sources and dates.**

**★ THE DISCUSSION FROM THE FLOOR (taken down by the club's secretary) — two questions, two answers, and the
second answer is the whole tragedy in four lines:**
- *"A member asked whether the difference might not be in the cows rather than the land, the bottom farms
  being poorer men with poorer stock."*
- *"Dr. Kepp replied that he had considered it and rejected it; that some of the best-bred cattle in the
  county stand on wet land, and some very indifferent cattle on the hills test well; and that a man may
  improve his herd for twenty years and never get his fat up while his ground is sour."*
- *"A member asked whether the trouble might lie in the station rather than the bottom, as had been said in
  the valley for years."*
- **⚠⚠ *"Dr. Kepp replied that he had no interest in that quarrel and no competence in it; that he had never
  in his life read a test and would not know how; but that when a cause is found in the soil, in the
  herbage, in the condition of the stock, in the College's trial, and in every wet bottom in the county
  alike, it is not good sense to go looking for a second cause in a building."***
- *"The chairman thanked Dr. Kepp and observed that the paper would be printed for the members."*
**Somebody in that room asked the right question, out loud, in 1926, and the expert answered it with an
argument from parsimony that was wrong. "It is not good sense to go looking for a second cause in a
building" — the second cause was in the building, in a crate, in the loft. And note the confession embedded
in the refusal: "he had never in his life read a test and would not know how." The man the county trusted to
settle a testing question could not read a test.**
**Set this beside Frick (R13): "I did not understand the test then and do not understand it now; I am a
lawyer and the thing is chemistry." TWO of the three outside authorities this valley called in — its
arbitration chairman and its veterinary expert — disclaimed competence in the one skill the question
required, and both nevertheless produced a conclusion. That is now a pattern and it should be named.**
**And a third instance: Vaught (R11), who DID have the competence, wrote "I have spent my working life urging
students to prefer an experiment to a calculation" — and his own method section then carried the errors.**

**★ THE 1910 BRANT LEAF: 200,000 lb even; test paid 3.42; scales agreeing.** This is the only single-year

**⚠ AN ARITHMETIC PROBLEM A LATER READER MAY BE ASKED ABOUT, so I record it plainly rather than resolving

## 0. THE SUBJECT MATTER IN ONE PARAGRAPH

All seventeen retellings concern the **Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association** and its **skimming
station at Ostrey Hollow**, seven miles up the valley from the main creamery at **Larrow Green**, in a
valley whose county seat/office town is **Ashlin**, in **Ordell County**. For roughly a quarter century the
milk of the Ostrey Hollow farmers tested lower for butterfat than their neighbors' milk. This produced: an
investigating committee in 1908 that blamed the wet hay; a circular letter in 1919 accusing nine families of
watering their cans (and, the same season, a reprint of the 1908 hay finding); a patrons' petition and board
of arbitration in 1925 that found "no cause established" yet awarded money anyway and put the tester out;
and, in 1958–59, a granddaughter's demonstration that the real cause was a crate of short-measuring glass
pipettes. The sources disagree with each other — and in places with themselves — on numbers, dates, kinship,
and blame. Those conflicts are the most important part of these notes.

- **Ostrey Hollow** — the hamlet/hollow at the head of the valley. The station stood "at the very top of the

- **The skimming station** — a low, whitewashed building, "cold as a spring house inside even in August,"
  smelling "of milk and steam and hot iron"; you could hear the engine before you came round the last turn.
  It had an **intake room** with a **loft over it reached by a ladder**, and — **per R13 — a WEIGH ROOM**,
  where the board of arbitration held its second and third sessions. Function: took in the milk of the upper
  farms, weighed it, tested each patron's sample, separated the milk, sent the **cream** down the valley to
  Larrow Green and gave the **skim** back to the patron for his hogs and calves. Closed by 1958; used for
  storage — "they kept fence wire and salt in it." Per Strawn (1907) it "opened before my time"; per Strawn
  (1909) it "takes from the farms of the wet bottom below it and from three places on the hill." Strawn 1912:
  "Cream shipped down the valley the same day it is separated, which is more than some do." Strawn 1928: "The
  station is quieter than it was." **Vose (R08) calls it "in daily use since 1896."** Vose 1926: "The
  skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley, ran its season out and its cream came down to
  us daily and sweet."
  **Physical description from R12 (Rundle):** "The building is a plain one, boarded and battened, with the
  intake room at the road end and a loft over it that I believe has never been cleared out since it was put

  **R14 (Sill): the cream wagon came down "every day of the week but Sunday, seven miles of hill road, and in

- **The 1889–1892 minute book** — lost in the office move of **1938**. *(So it was already gone when Rundle

  but Frick — the chairman — says the board sat three times and only the FIRST session was at Ashlin: "The
  second and the third I asked to have at Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's own weigh room, so that the
  board might see the plant it was to pass upon instead of hearing it described by men with an interest in
  the description. That was my doing and I am still content with it." Prefer Frick on venue.** Frick gives the
  first sitting as 17 March 1925 and the award as 4 May 1925; the second and third sittings fall between and
  are undated in any source.
- **The Association's annual ledgers, station account** (Nym Item 6) — canvas over boards; **"The station
  column runs from 1897."** Rundle saw these in the office in 1939 "on another errand altogether."
- **The Larrow Green receiving and churn books** (Nym Item 16) — "water-damaged at the foot. Cream in, butter
  out, and the creamery's own tests." **THESE ARE HOBART SILL'S BOOKS. R14: "You may go and look at the
  receiving book if they will let you at it, and you will find my hand on every page of it from ninety-three,
  and you will find the Hollow's cream coming in heavy on page after page after page, in my own writing, for
  as long as I have been in this room." This makes Nym's Item 16 the single most underexploited object in the
  entire file: a forty-one-year written record of the station gain in one man's hand, and nobody in 1925,
  1939 or 1958 ever opened it for that purpose.**
- **The Association's order book (R08)** — Vose: "I decline to build a mystery on a piece of housekeeping
  that appears in our own order book with a date and a price against it." So an order book records the 1923
  replacement glass with a date and a price.
- **⭐ THE 1908 COMMITTEE'S REPORT (Nym Item 11; text now in hand via R15)** — the fair copy is "signed by the
  chairman," unnamed there; **R15 gives the full text and the signature "NYLE GRIGG, chairman, and the
  committee."** **Reprinted in 1919 with Grigg's covering letter — a fact no other source records.** **Read
  into the 1925 record (R13).** **Read twice by Emmet Brant, who then put it in the drawer (R09).**
  **Commended by Kepp in 1926 as "a committee of practical men [who] went over the ground in 1908" (R16).**
  **This is now the most widely-circulated document in the whole affair, and its sampling window was three
  winter months.**
- **⭐ BULLETIN NO. 214 (State Agricultural College at Farland, 1911)** — a **feeding trial upon bog hay**,
  "carried out under the College's own eye, with weighed rations and no interest in the outcome" (R15). Found
  **the bog hay to depress the butterfat test by two tenths (0.20) IN THE WINTER FEEDING** (both R15 and R16,
  in near-identical words). **★ THE SEASONAL QUALIFIER IS THE KEY AND BOTH CITERS QUOTE IT WITHOUT HEARING
  IT: a winter-only effect cannot explain a year-round, season-flat 0.19 gap (R17). Bulletin 214 is real
  science correctly reported and wrongly applied.**
- **The state's burettes** — graduated, in a wooden case, with a printed sheet of directions, **issued to
  field men from 1921**, "for checking the delivery of measures and pipettes at the plants." Strawn's came in
  the spring of 1921. **Programme discontinued from 1926**; instruments kept by the men who had them, no more
  issued, no returns wanted. Strawn's stayed "in its case in my grip and has been used twice since." Ivy's
  1958 measurement was also made "against a calibrated burette." **Vaught (R11) makes the burette the hinge
  of the whole history.** **⭐ R17 gives the only eyewitness account of one in use: "some measuring instrument
  he carried in his coat."** **⭐ And Hazel's closing verdict names it: "A thing you could have settled in an
  afternoon with a burette and a straight face, and it took fifty years and a girl from the college who
  thought to climb a ladder."**
- **Strawn's sealed weights** — carried on the circuit for testing intake scale and cream scale, "loaded
  light and loaded heavy."
- **The lactometer** — Strawn's instrument for detecting added water, applied "patron by patron where there
  was any question."
- **The twenty-eight circuit books** — one to the year, oilcloth covers, pocket size.
- **The hand tester** — four-bottle, on the 1897 invoice at $18.00; the single most expensive item in the
  outfit. Keddie: four bottles weighted against each other, cover down, crank turned at the taught speed.
  *(Reader 11's notes were cut off at exactly this entry — line 800 — so anything he wrote after it about the
  pipettes themselves, the 1897 invoice, or the 2 July 1923 Ammon & Sons letter is lost to me. A later reader
  should go to `notes-11.md` past line 800 for it.)*
- **⭐ THE BORROWED APPARATUS (R14 for 1896; R16 for 1911)** — not one object but a recurring circumstance,
  and the file's hidden control experiment. **1896: the station opened in May on borrowed glass because its
  own outfit had not come** (one of Keddie's two accounts says it was borrowed off the Ashlin creamery).
  **Summer 1911: "their own apparatus having failed them and a borrowed one being used" for six weeks, and
  the figures "stood higher during those six weeks than before or after."** **Twice the station ran on glass
  that did not come out of the 1897 Tarnet crate, and both times the numbers moved. That is the experiment,
  run twice, by accident, and discarded twice.**
