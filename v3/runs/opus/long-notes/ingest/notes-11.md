## 0. THE SUBJECT MATTER IN ONE PARAGRAPH

All fourteen retellings concern the **Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association** and its **skimming
station at Ostrey Hollow**, seven miles up the valley from the main creamery at **Larrow Green**, in a
valley whose county seat/office town is **Ashlin**, in **Ordell County**. For roughly a quarter century the
milk of the Ostrey Hollow farmers tested lower for butterfat than their neighbors' milk. This produced: a
circular letter in 1919 accusing nine families of watering their cans; a patrons' petition and board of
arbitration in 1925 that found "no cause established" yet awarded money anyway and put the tester out; and,
in 1958–59, a granddaughter's demonstration that the real cause was a crate of short-measuring glass
pipettes. The sources disagree with each other — and in places with themselves — on numbers, dates,
kinship, and blame. Those conflicts are the most important part of these notes.

## 2. PLACES, BUILDINGS, OBJECTS

- **Ostrey Hollow** — the hamlet/hollow at the head of the valley. The station stood "at the very top of the
  Hollow where the road bends and then goes to nothing but the ridge."
- **The skimming station** — a low, whitewashed building, "cold as a spring house inside even in August,"
  smelling "of milk and steam and hot iron"; you could hear the engine before you came round the last turn.
  It had an **intake room** with a **loft over it reached by a ladder**, and — **per R13 — a WEIGH ROOM,
  which is where the board of arbitration held its second and third sessions.** Function: took in the milk
  of the upper farms, weighed it, tested each patron's sample, separated the milk, sent the **cream** down
  the valley to Larrow Green and gave the **skim** back to the patron for his hogs and calves. Closed by
  1958; used for storage — "they kept fence wire and salt in it." Per Strawn (1907) it "opened before my
  time"; per Strawn (1909) it "takes from the farms of the wet bottom below it and from three places on the
  hill." Strawn 1912: "Cream shipped down the valley the same day it is separated, which is more than some
  do." Strawn 1928: "The station is quieter than it was." **Vose (R08) calls it "in daily use since 1896."**
  Vose 1926 on the season just past: "The skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley, ran
  its season out and its cream came down to us daily and sweet."
  **Physical description from R12 (Rundle):** "The building is a plain one, boarded and battened, with
  the intake room at the road end and a loft over it that I believe has never been cleared out since it was
  put up; and behind it the creek, which in a wet April comes up to the sill and has twice come over it."
  *(Rundle's "never been cleared out" loft strongly supports Ivy's account of finding the crate in the loft
  in 1958 and tells against Nym's "Association office at Ashlin.")*
  **Reason for building it, from R12:** "The Hollow is seven miles up from the Green, and in the
  nineties the pull down was three hours in mud and could not be done at all in March. So the Association
  built a skimming station up there... It was a great convenience and the Hollow was glad of it."
  **⭐ NEW FROM R14 (Sill): the cream wagon came down "every day of the week but Sunday, seven miles of hill
  road, and in July it came down warm no matter how early they started." At Larrow Green it "was set off at
  the platform and it was weighed and it was sampled and it went into the cold room."**
  **⭐ NEW FROM R14: the station OPENED IN MAY 1896 with a crowd going up for it, and it opened on BORROWED
  GLASS BECAUSE ITS OWN OUTFIT HAD NOT COME.**
  **⭐ NEW FROM R13: the board of arbitration sat in the station's own weigh room twice in 1925 and saw the
  scales tried, the sampling done, and the test made. "Every physical appliance of that station was before
  us and we satisfied ourselves upon it."** *(Note the tension: Frick says every physical appliance was
  before the board, but his own exhibit list is FOUR BOOKS and no appliances. The pipettes were physically

## 3. DOCUMENTS QUOTED (verbatim, with source)

**(a) Article VII of the by-laws — quoted by Effie Loomis, transcribed entire and identically by Halbert
Nym as Item 1, and printed whole a third time by Dell Rundle (R12), all three word for word the same:**
> *Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of
> pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of
> the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*

Loomis's gloss: before it, a man was paid for what his cans weighed; after it, for the fat found by a test
made at the place of delivery. She insists the load-bearing clause is the last one, "which is the one nobody
quotes" — *the test of the station where he delivers shall govern* — "Not the test of the creamery, not the
average of the valley, not a test made over again anywhere else. The station's test, and no appeal from it.
That clause is the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business." **Nym adds that the printed copies bear no
year.** **Vose (R08) attributes the drafting to his father Gideon.**
**⚠ DATE CONFLICT: Loomis says adopted at the annual meeting of 1892. Rundle (R12) says "Article VII dates
from 1891, the year the churn was set up."** Nym's Item 1 note ("no year on their face") shows why the
question is open at all. Loomis was the Association's own secretary and dates it against Gideon Vose's 1917
death; Rundle admits his pamphlet is valley memory tidied. **Prefer 1892.**
**Rundle's own gloss, which is the fullest reading of the clause anywhere:**
> "It was thought a hard rule at the time and there were men who left the Association over it. It is a just
> rule. A man who keeps good cows and feeds them ought not to be paid the same as the man who keeps poor
> ones, and the pound of milk is a liar where the pound of butterfat is not. But mark the last clause,
> because the valley has had fifty years to learn what it means. *The test of the station where he delivers
> shall govern.* Not the test of the creamery. Not any test but the one made at his own door. Gideon Vose
> put that in for convenience and it was convenient for thirty years, and it is the sentence on which the
> whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned."

**⭐ (a-bis) FRICK'S RESTATEMENT OF THE SAME RULE (R13), which is the sharpest legal reading anyone gives:**
> "The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association pays a patron for the butter fat in his milk and not
> for the weight of it. The fat is found by test, and the test of the station where the man delivers is the
> test that governs him. I say nothing against the rule. **But it makes the test the whole of the bargain,
> and it leaves a dissatisfied man with no remedy on earth except to quarrel with a figure.**"

**(b) The 1925 award, recorded by Effie Loomis on the last day — and quoted identically by Nym (Item 7):**
> *The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and
> appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow
> patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged
> to the station account.*

Loomis: "*Finds no cause established*, and then an award of thirty-eight hundred dollars: I took it down as
it was given to me and it was not my place to ask how both halves of a sentence could be true at once." She
also notes the phrase "the books and appliances of the station" is "a form of words the board used and not a
list of anything." **Vose (R08) glosses the same sentence in the opposite direction:** the finding was "the
only sentence in this business that was arrived at by men under oath with the books in front of them," and
the award was "a generous settlement of an unproved claim." **Rundle (R12) paraphrases without a figure:
"It heard everybody. It found nothing established, it made an award to the patrons without admitting
anything, and it sent this valley home to live with itself, which the valley has done, after a fashion,
ever since."** *(Vira Toland alone says $3,500 — she is wrong; Loomis, Teague, Vose, Nym, Ivy and now Frick
all say $3,800, and Rundle declines to give a number at all.)*
**⭐ FRICK (R13), the chairman, restates it in his own words and confirms every element:** "On the fourth of
May, 1925, the board found no cause established and awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons thirty-eight hundred
dollars, without admission, to be charged to the station account." **And he settles Loomis's puzzle from the
inside: "An award without a finding is an unsatisfactory thing and every member of that board knew it."**
**⭐ FRICK ALSO CONFIRMS LOOMIS'S READING OF "the books and appliances of the station": his exhibit list is
FOUR BOOKS and no appliances at all. Loomis was right that the phrase was a form of words.**

**(c) Ansel Keddie's statement to the board of arbitration, Ashlin, 17 March 1925 — nine sentences, taken
down as he gave them. (Nym holds the original as Item 8: "One leaf, ruled, both sides, in the hand of Item
3, signed. Not transcribed." — note that Nym calls Item 3's hand "E. Brant's," which is his error):**
> I have kept the test book of this station in my own hand since the station opened, and there is
> not a figure in it that I did not put there. I have set down the date, the patron, the pounds,
> the test and the fat, and where I have made an error I have drawn a line through it and put my
> letters against the line, and I have never written a figure over a figure. I have made the
> tests in the same order and at the same hour of every working day of every season. I have heard
> what the patrons say of me and I have heard what the officers say of me. Neither of them has
> come up that road on a working morning and stood at my elbow and watched the work done. I am
> asked to account for figures, and the figures are what I read and what I wrote and nothing
> besides. I am an old man and I have no other trade. I have tested as I was taught and as the
> state man saw me do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, and I cannot tell you
> why the figures are what they are. I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and
> there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself.

*(NOTE from Segment 2: "as the state man saw me do it" is Merle Strawn. Strawn's books confirm he stood at
that tester's elbow and watched a test through in 1907 (two tests) and again in 1911, and that his standing
practice was to watch a test through "from the sample to the reading," "and I did not let him hurry it."
Keddie's sentence about nobody standing at his elbow is addressed to patrons and officers, not to the state
man — and Strawn is the one man who did.)*
**⭐ FRICK (R13) INDEPENDENTLY PARAPHRASES THE SAME STATEMENT, from the chair, twenty-one years later, and
gets it right in every particular: "He said he had tested as he was taught and as the state man had seen him
do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, that he could not tell us why the figures were what
they were, and that he had never taken a pound of cream out of that station." Two independent witnesses to
the same nine sentences, agreeing exactly. This is now among the best-attested documents in the file.**
**⚠ BUT NOTE THE LAST SENTENCE AGAINST R13's CORRIDOR SCENE: Keddie told the room "there is not a man in
this room who thinks I have when he is by himself" — and then told Frick, privately, in the corridor, that
he thought the patrons were at fault. Both men in that corridor were withholding what they believed.**

**(d) First leaf of the station test book — quoted by Keddie, and transcribed identically by Nym (Item 3):**
> *Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until
> our own comes. A. Keddie.*

**⭐⭐ R14 SETTLES THE BORROWED-GLASS QUESTION COMPLETELY, AND FROM THE LENDER'S SIDE.** Keddie gave two
accounts of where the 1896 glass came from — this first leaf says **Larrow Green**, and elsewhere he said it
was borrowed off the **Ashlin creamery**. The Ammon & Sons invoice gloss ("Larrow Green having lent until
then") already favoured Larrow Green. **Now Hobart Sill, the man who lent it, tells the whole story in his
own words:**
> "When they opened that station up at Ostrey Hollow — that was ninety-six, in May, and there was a crowd
> went up for it — they had no outfit of their own. Not a piece. It was ordered and it was not come, and
> they were ready to start taking milk, and there was talk of holding off the whole season. And I said no,
> they can have mine. I went to my own bench in this room and I made up an outfit for them out of my own
> glass, my own, that I had used and knew, and it went up the valley on the wagon and they opened on it.
> Their own came the spring after and they sent mine back to me, and one piece of it was chipped and I never
> said a word about that either."
**Larrow Green is right; the Ashlin-creamery version is wrong; and we now know the glass was Sill's personal
bench glass, "that I had used and knew" — i.e. known-good glass. That is exactly why the 1896 season ran at
3.80 and the seasons after it ran at 3.61.**

**(e) The summary/averages leaf Keddie made up at the back of the book in 1922 — quoted identically by
Keddie (r03), Ivy Keddie (r04) AND Nym (r07 Item 3):**
> *Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61
> or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted
> in their place, when it stood at 3.80.*

Nym's curatorial note: "The summary leaf gathers the yearly averages: 3.80 for the first season, 3.61 from
1898 on." *(Note what this leaf independently establishes: the station's FIRST season, 1896, on borrowed
Larrow Green glass, ran at 3.80 — the same figure as the 1911 borrowed measure, the 1924 new glass, and
Alder Corners. The 3.61 begins only in 1898, i.e. after the station's own Ammon & Sons pipettes came into
use on 1 May 1897. The whole case is on that leaf, and it was written in 1922 by a man who did not know it.)*
**⚠ FRICK (R13) GIVES THE STATION'S AVERAGE AS 3.16, NOT 3.61.** He writes: *"The patrons of that station
had been paid, year upon year, on an average test of 3.16... Three and sixteen is not a scandalous figure
standing by itself."* **This is a digit transposition of the same kind Vaught commits with 16.27/16.72. Six
sources give 3.61 (Keddie's own summary leaf, Ivy, Nym, and the arithmetic of the whole case). 3.16 appears
nowhere else. Frick is wrong — a lawyer writing from memory at seventy-four about a figure he says himself
he never understood. Record both; prefer 3.61.**

**(f) The two 1911 entries, "as I made them" — Keddie:**
> *May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure.*

**(w) THE EXHIBIT LIST BEFORE THE BOARD OF ARBITRATION, 1925 — reprinted by Alonzo Frick in 1946 "from my
own file, exactly as it was made up." This is the first complete inventory anyone has of what the 1925 board
actually had in front of it, and it is the single most damaging document in the file to the board's own
verdict:**
> EXHIBITS BEFORE THE BOARD OF ARBITRATION, OSTREY HOLLOW MATTER, 1925
>
> 1. The test book of the Ostrey Hollow station, 4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924, in the hand of Ansel
>    Keddie.
> 2. The receiving book of the said station.
> 3. The private weigh book of Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom, begun 1 January 1897.
> 4. The circuit notebook of the state dairy inspector for the year 1923.

Frick's own comment: **"Four books and nothing else. It is a short list for a matter of that size, and I
have looked at it many times since."**

**What this list proves, and it is a great deal:**
1. **The 1923 condemnation notebook was exhibit 4.** The sentence *"These measures will not deliver their
   mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them"* was physically before the board, and nobody connected
   it to the figures they were sitting there to explain.
2. **The test book was exhibit 1, and it carries the 1922 summary leaf** (document (e)) showing 3.80 in
   1896, 3.61 from 1898, and 3.80 again for the nine weeks of 1911. **Two of the four exhibits, taken
   together, contain the entire solution.**
3. **Nym's attribution error is refuted contemporaneously** — Frick's 1925 list puts the test book in Ansel
   Keddie's hand and the weigh book in Emmet Brant's, which is the correct way round.
4. **The Brant weigh book's start date is confirmed as 1 January 1897**, against Roy Brant's 1898.
5. **The award's phrase "the books and appliances of the station" was indeed a form of words** — no
   appliance was ever an exhibit, even though the board went up to the Hollow and watched a test made.
6. **A station receiving book existed as a separate object** and is otherwise unrecorded in this file.

## 7. THE STATE OF THE CASE, IN ONE PARAGRAPH, AS OF SEGMENT 11

The Ostrey Hollow skimming station opened in May 1896 on borrowed glass from the Larrow Green buttermaker's
own bench and tested 3.80 that first season. In May 1897 it began using six dozen new pipettes from Ammon &
Sons of Tarnet, blown to 17.6 c.c. but delivering only 16.72 — short by one part in twenty. From 1898 the
station's books read 3.61 instead of the true 3.80, and every patron at the head of the valley was underpaid
by five per cent of his butterfat for twenty-six years, because Article VII of the by-laws made the station's
own test final and unappealable. The missing fat did not vanish; it went down the hill in the cream cans and
was credited to the Association as "station gain," 3,040 lb in 1898 and about 76,000 lb in all, worth some
$19,000 — and the buttermaker who received it weighed it in with his own hands, wrote it in his own
receiving book on every page for forty-one years, and said so out loud a hundred times to men who thought it
was not worth anything. The valley produced three explanations and no evidence: water (the manager's),
theft (the bookkeeper's insinuation), and wet hay (the 1908 committee's). Twice the instrument itself gave
the answer away — for nine weeks in 1911 when a borrowed measure pushed the tests back to 3.80, and on
12 June 1923 when the state inspector ran the pipettes against a burette and condemned the whole crate — and
both times the valley recorded the fact and missed the meaning. In 1925 a board of arbitration sat three
times, twice in the station's own weigh room, with four books before it, two of which between them contained
the entire solution; it found no cause established, awarded $3,800 anyway, and watched the Association
dismiss the tester four days before the award with no finding against him. The tester died in 1934 believing
privately that his neighbors had been watering their cans; his neighbors died believing he had been robbing
them; the buttermaker retired that same year still saying there had been water in those cans. In February
1958 the tester's granddaughter found the condemned crate under a tarpaulin in the loft, measured nine
surviving pipettes against a burette, and printed the answer in October. **The evidence was never missing.
It was in the loft, in the ledgers, in the receiving book, in the field notebook, and on the summary leaf the
tester himself wrote in 1922 — and for sixty-two years nobody put any two of those things side by side.**
