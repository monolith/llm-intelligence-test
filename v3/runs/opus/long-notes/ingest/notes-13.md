**★★ THE INTERNAL CONTRADICTION INSIDE R15.**
Grigg's 1919 letter says the committee sampled **the whole of 1908, every month**:
> *"We went at it as plain farmers do. We sampled those herds through the whole of 1908 — every month of
> that year, in the barn and on the pasture, out of the night's milk and the morning's, and we did not let a
> man know which day we were coming. We weighed. We watched the test made and we watched it made again."*
But the 1908 report he is introducing — printed directly below his own letter, on the same page — says the
sampling was **three winter months only**:
> *"We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March."*
**Eleven years apart, the same man gives two different scopes for the same investigation, and the smaller
one is the contemporaneous document.** Reading in the report's favour: the committee was "appointed in
January last" and reported to the board, so a January–March sampling window fits a report written in the
same year; and the report's own phrase "visited each of the six farms, some of them three and four times" is
a scale of work consistent with three months, not twelve. **Reader 12's reading, which I endorse: the 1908
report is right and Grigg's 1919 memory has inflated the work. Not provable; a later reader may be asked
about either.**
**Why it is load-bearing:** a January–February–March sampling is a WINTER-ONLY sampling. A winter-only
sampling cannot distinguish a feed effect (which is seasonal, and which Bulletin 214 found *"in the winter
feeding"*) from a constant instrument error — because in winter the two predict the same thing. **The 1908
committee could not have detected the pipette even in principle, and Grigg's 1919 claim to have sampled
twelve months is the claim that would have made the finding sound. He did not have that evidence.** Set this
beside Hazel Brant (R17), who says the gap was *"nineteen hundredths, flat as a floor"* — *"not a little in
the winter and none in the summer, the way a feed thing goes."* **The constancy of the gap across seasons is
the thing that kills the hay theory, and no committee that only sampled winter could ever have seen it.**

**★ GRIGG'S USE OF BULLETIN NO. 214 — and the date it pins:**
> *"When we wrote, we had nothing to go on but our own eyes and the general opinion of practical men, and I
> knew that a committee of farmers saying that wet hay thins the cream would be told that farmers will say
> anything about their own land. But the State Agricultural College at Farland has since published its
> Bulletin No. 214, which was a feeding trial upon bog hay carried out under the College's own eye, with

> test by two tenths in the winter feeding. Two tenths, gentlemen, out of a College that had never heard of

> and by better men, three years after we set our names to it."*
**"Three years after" 1908 = 1911, which is exactly the Bulletin 214 date this chain already carried.
Consistent.** Note *"in the winter feeding"* — Bulletin 214's own finding is explicitly seasonal.
**Both R15 and R16 quote the two-tenths figure and both attach it to winter feeding.**

## ★★★ RETELLING 16 — DR. ROLAND KEPP, 1926 (`r16-kepp-bog-meadows-paper.md`, 39 lines)

Header, exactly: **"Retelling 16 — Dr. Roland Kepp, 'The bog meadows and the milk,' a paper read before the
Ordell County Farm Club, 1926."** Italic headnote: *"A veterinary surgeon of thirty years' practice brings
the agricultural literature to bear on an old local grievance, and the discussion from the floor is appended
as taken down by the club's secretary."* Byline inside the paper: **"By Roland Kepp, V.S., of Ashlin."**
Sections in order: **The land / What the cow does with such keep / The measure of it / Hessel Bottom / The
evidence of the College / An objection / The remedy / DISCUSSION.**

**Who he is:** a **veterinary surgeon** of **Ashlin**, **thirty years in practice** in the county (so
practising from c. 1896). His method: *"it is my habit, when I am called to a herd that is doing badly, to
ask first what the cattle are standing on. I have found in thirty years that the answer to nine cases in ten
is under the animal's feet, and that the tenth is in the water she drinks."* His framing: *"a question that
has been in this county longer than I have practiced in it, and that has been dealt with hitherto by opinion,
by grumbling, and by the naming of names. It deserves better. It is a question of soil, of herbage, and of
the mineral economy of the cow, and I mean to treat it as such."*
**★ NOTE THE YEAR: 1926. That is the year AFTER the arbitration, and the same year as Selby Vose's annual
address (R08). The valley was still arguing the meadow a year after the award.**

**His chemistry of the wet bottom** (the fullest scientific account in the file):
> *"A wet bottom is not a rich meadow with too much water in it. It is a distinct kind of ground with a
> distinct kind of chemistry, and the water is the least part of it. Where drainage is wanting, the year's
> growth does not rot down as it does on a hill field; it lies sour, and the soil that results is an acid
> soil, poor in lime, and poor in the phosphates. On such ground the sweet grasses will not hold. They are
> crowded out within a few seasons by sedge and rush and the coarse tussock grasses... That herbage carries
> bulk and it carries water. What it does not carry, in any quantity worth the name, is lime or phosphorus,
> and it is cut late because such ground cannot be got onto early, and late cutting takes out what little
> was in it."*

**His physiology:** *"A cow in milk is a manufactory working on a narrow margin... If her ration is short of
the minerals, she does not simply give less; she robs her own frame to make good, and she goes on robbing it
until she cannot."* Symptoms he claims to have seen in every wet-meadow herd in the county: *"thin over the
loin and rough in the coat... slow to come to condition in the spring... ready subjects for every disorder of
the digestion in the fall of the year... They breed later. Their feet are worse."* And: *"They will milk,
gentlemen. Wet land is generous of flow and no man on a bog meadow ever complained that his pail was light.
But what is in the pail is another matter, and the fat is the first thing to suffer, because the fat is the
dearest thing the cow makes and it is the first economy she practices when she is pressed."*
*(Note how closely this tracks the 1908 committee's language — "thin in the flank at the end of the winter
feeding and slow to come to their flesh in the spring," "generous of milk." Kepp is dressing the 1908 report
in chemistry. Whether he is corroborating it or merely repeating it cannot be told from the text.)*

**★ HIS THREE NUMBERS, and why he thinks they agree:**
1. **His own field figure: "a fifth of a point" = 0.20.** *"The wet bottoms of this county yield a milk
   poorer in fat by a fifth of a point and every practical man knows it. Set the hill herds of this county
   against the bottom herds and you will find that difference, farm for farm, wherever you look for it, and
   the difference does not depend on the breed, on the man, or on the barn. It depends on the ground."*
2. **The Ostrey Hollow gap: "twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21."** *"The figure of the gap, as it has
   been given to me and as I have satisfied myself, is twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21 — between the
   milk of that station and the milk of this valley at large."*
3. **Bulletin No. 214: two tenths = 0.20, in the winter feeding.** *"The State Agricultural College at
   Farland conducted a feeding trial upon bog hay and published it as its Bulletin No. 214... The College fed
   the bog hay under control and measured what it did, and it found the butter fat test depressed by two
   tenths in the winter feeding. That is the whole case. Two tenths from the College, laid alongside the
   fifth of a point I have found in the field and the twenty-one hundredths that station has been
   complaining of, and the three agree so nearly that I do not know what more a reasonable man requires."*
**⚠ 0.21 IS A NEW FIGURE FOR THE GAP and it does not match the file's other two.** The tally is now:
**0.19** (Ivy Keddie, arithmetically exact against her money pair; Hazel Brant independently, "nineteen
hundredths, flat as a floor"), **0.295** (Vaught's method section, R11, which matches nothing else),
**0.21** (Kepp, "as it has been given to me and as I have satisfied myself"). Kepp does not say who gave it
to him, and 0.21 is suspiciously close to his own pre-existing 0.20 field figure — **a man who already
believes the gap is a fifth of a point is not a neutral receiver of a figure near a fifth of a point.**
**Reading: 0.19 remains the sound figure and Kepp's 0.21 is soft. But record all three.**
**★ ALSO NOTE THE CIRCULARITY, which is the whole trouble with Kepp's paper: he treats the agreement of
three numbers as proof of causation. But if the true cause is a short pipette, the station's gap (0.21 or
0.19) would be a CONSTANT instrument error, and its rough coincidence with a genuine seasonal feed effect of
0.20 is precisely the coincidence that hid the pipette for sixty years. The numbers agreeing is what made
everybody stop looking. Kepp says so himself without hearing it: "That is the whole case."**

**★★★ THE 1911 SIX-WEEK BORROWED-APPARATUS EPISODE.** Kepp raises it only to dismiss it, under the heading

> summer of 1911 the testing at that station was broken off for six weeks, their own apparatus having failed
> them and a borrowed one being used, and that the figures stood higher during those six weeks than before
> or after. Six weeks in the flush of the season, gentlemen. Six weeks of June and July, when every herd in
> the county is at its best and the stock are off the wet ground and up on the aftermath. I am astonished
> that anybody should found an argument on six weeks of summer figures. I could produce you six weeks in any
> year that would prove anything you liked."*
**Read what this actually says. In the summer of 1911 the station's OWN apparatus failed; a BORROWED
apparatus was used for six weeks; and during exactly those six weeks the tests came out HIGHER. That is the

result was recorded, it was noticed by at least one person in the valley, it was put to Kepp as an argument,
and Kepp explained it away with the seasonal-flush objection and the room moved on.**

# ★ THE CASE, AS IT NOW STANDS — for a reader who must answer from these notes alone

**What happened.** The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was organized in **1889** by **Gideon
Vose**, its first president, who wrote its by-laws. **Payment by butterfat test came in with the 1893
meeting** (Grigg, hedged). The **Ostrey Hollow skimming station opened in May 1896** — on **borrowed glass**,
because its own outfit had not arrived (Sill, R14) — with **Ansel Keddie** (b. 1861) as tester and separator
man. Its own glassware, **six dozen (72) pipettes and the rest of the outfit, was bought in 1897 from Ammon &
Sons for $77.93** and came out from **Tarnet**; it went into use **1 May 1897**. **The pipettes were short.
They delivered 16.72 c.c. instead of 17.6, a ratio of about 0.95, and every test made at that station for
twenty-six years was low by about 0.19 of a point.**

**Who was hurt.** The **six farms of Hessel Bottom** — **nine families**, because a farm held more than one
household — under Ostrey Hollow, a wet sedge meadow along the creek where a horse could go in to the hock.
The largest was **Emmet Brant's**, who hauled the most milk of any man on the creek and therefore lost the
most. **Roughly 76,000 lb of butterfat, about $19,000, went unpaid over the life of the bad glass.**

**The complaint and the four failures to find it.**
1. **From about 1902** the Hollow patrons complained that their milk tested under the rest of the valley
   (three independent sources: Roy Brant, Hobart Sill, Nyle Grigg).
2. **1908:** a three-man committee under **Nyle Grigg** investigated, sampled **January–February–March only**
   — a winter-only window that cannot separate a seasonal feed effect from a constant instrument error —
   found no dishonesty, and **blamed the hay of the bottom.** The report was accepted and closed the question
   for eleven years.
3. **Summer 1911:** the station's own apparatus failed and a **borrowed** one was used for **six weeks**;
   **the figures stood higher.** **Ansel Keddie came home saying it was "something in the glass," said it
   twice, and wrote a question mark in the book** — and never wrote more, because he could not prove it.
   The same year, **Farland's Bulletin No. 214** found bog hay depressed the test by **two tenths in the
   winter feeding**, which everyone took as confirmation of the 1908 finding.
4. **1919:** the Association did two contradictory things in one season — **Selby Vose's water circular to
   the nine families**, accusing them of adding water, and a **reprint of the 1908 hay report** with Grigg's
   covering letter. Nobody was answered; one letter went in the Brant stove.
5. **12 June 1923:** **Merle Strawn**, the state dairy inspector (kin to nobody in the valley), tested the
   pipettes with a graduated **burette** he carried in his coat, **condemned the glass**, and ordered new.
   **The crate went up into the loft over the intake room and nobody asked what the condemned glass had done
   to twenty-six years of figures.**
6. **1925:** a three-man board of arbitration under the lawyer **Alonzo Frick** sat **17 March – 4 May**,
   impounded the test book, held two of its three sittings **in the station with the crate in the loft above
   its head**, and **found nothing**. **Ansel Keddie was dismissed 30 April 1925, four days before the
   award.** He never tested again; he died in **1934** with the imputation on him.
7. **1926:** **Dr. Roland Kepp**, V.S., of Ashlin, read a paper to the Ordell County Farm Club proving from
   soil chemistry that bog hay was the cause; from the floor a member asked whether the trouble might lie in
   the station rather than the bottom, **and Kepp — who had never in his life read a test — replied that "it
   is not good sense to go looking for a second cause in a building."**

**How it was found.** **Ivy Keddie** (b. 1931), Ansel's granddaughter, at the State Agricultural College at
Farland: **in 1958** she borrowed the Brant weigh book from Roy Brant (**lent, not bequeathed**), got copies
of the Alder Corners figures from **Rosalie Cudd**, pulled **Strawn's 1923 notebook** out of the college
library where it had sat unread since 1947, and **in February 1958 climbed twenty feet of ladder into the
station loft and found the crate under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff.** She took the glass east to Farland
and measured it. The result was published as **Bulletin No. 471**, whose front matter is Leland Vaught's
(R11) and whose closing paragraph Ivy and Nym quote — and **which contradicts itself numerically**.

**Why it took fifty years.** Because a genuine seasonal feed effect of **0.20** existed and roughly matched a
constant instrument error of **0.19**, so every number agreed with the wrong explanation; because the one
investigation sampled only winter, when the two explanations predict the same thing; because the outside
experts the valley trusted — a lawyer and a veterinary surgeon — both disclaimed competence in testing and
concluded anyway; and because the one man who had the competence and the suspicion **would not write down
what he could not prove**. **The decisive fact was always available and nobody stated it until Hazel Brant
in 1981: the gap was "nineteen hundredths, flat as a floor — not a little in the winter and none in the
summer, the way a feed thing goes." A constant is an instrument. A season is a feed.**
