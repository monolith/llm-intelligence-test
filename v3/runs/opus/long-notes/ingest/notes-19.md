### THE EDITOR'S PREFACE, quoted entire

### 1. ★★★★★ THE GLASS ARRIVED 1 MAY 1897. THE DATE IS NOW SETTLED BY THREE SOURCES AND KYLE IS WRONG.

> *May 1, 1897.* **"A. says the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green."**

- **This is a contemporaneous entry, written the day it happened, by a witness with no reason to lie about a
  date.** It matches R20's invoice-based "the first of May, 1897" and the Chronicle's "the spring of 1897."
- **⚠⚠ IT REFUTES KYLE'S "THE FIRST OF MAY, 1898" OUTRIGHT.** Reader 18 had already ruled: *"PREFER 1897 —
  it rests on a dated invoice (14 April 1897) reproduced in facsimile, plus Junia Ammon's prose, plus the
  Chronicle. Kyle's year is wrong, and Kyle warns the reader in his own document that his years are
  unreliable."* **R24 IS THE FOURTH INDEPENDENT SOURCE FOR 1897 AND THE ONLY CONTEMPORANEOUS ONE. THE
  QUESTION IS CLOSED. GIVE 1 MAY 1897. Record Kyle's 1898 as a memory slip of exactly one year — same day,
  same month.**
- **★★★ AND IT ADDS A FACT NOBODY ELSE IN THE CHAIN HAS: BEFORE 1 MAY 1897 THE OSTREY HOLLOW STATION WAS
  BORROWING GLASS FROM LARROW GREEN.** *"they are done borrowing of Larrow Green."* **THIS IS ENORMOUS AND
  NOBODY HAS PUT IT TOGETHER. Work the chronology:**
  - The station opened **4 May 1896**.
  - The invoice for its own outfit is dated **14 April 1897** and the glass came up **1 May 1897**.
  - **THEREFORE FOR ITS ENTIRE FIRST YEAR — MAY 1896 TO MAY 1897 — THE OSTREY HOLLOW STATION TESTED ON GLASS
    BORROWED FROM THE LARROW GREEN CREAMERY, AND ITS TESTS IN THAT YEAR WOULD HAVE BEEN CORRECT.**
  - **THE LOW TESTS THEREFORE CANNOT HAVE BEGUN BEFORE MAY 1897.** ⚠⚠ **THIS CONTRADICTS THE CHRONICLE
    (R19), WHICH SAYS "from very nearly the day the station opened, the milk of Ostrey Hollow tested lower
    than the milk of anywhere else in this county."** Two readings, and a later reader must not choose
    blindly:
    **(a)** The Chronicle is loose — "very nearly the day it opened" is a newspaperman's rounding for
    "within a year of opening," and one year out of twenty-nine rounds away. **This is the likelier
    reading** and it costs nothing.
    **(b)** The Chronicle is right and the borrowed Larrow Green glass was ALSO short — which would mean the
    contamination was wider than one crate and would revive Junia Ammon's "they went out very much alike"
    worry with a vengeance. **Nothing supports this and I do not assert it.**
    **BUT NOTE WHICH WAY THE 1911 EPISODE CUTS: in 1911 the station borrowed glass again and the tests went
    UP. If Larrow Green's glass had been short too, the 1911 tests would not have risen. THE 1911 EPISODE IS
    POSITIVE EVIDENCE THAT LARROW GREEN'S GLASS WAS SOUND, WHICH MAKES READING (a) RIGHT AND MEANS THE
    STATION'S FIRST YEAR WAS CLEAN.** Give this. It is a genuine new inference and it is well supported.
  - **★★★ AND IT MAKES THE 1911 EPISODE A REPEAT, NOT A NOVELTY.** Ansel had run this station on Larrow
    Green's glass before, in 1896–97, and knew what its milk read on borrowed glass. **When the 1911
    borrowing pushed the tests up, he was not comparing against nothing — he was comparing against his own
    first season. THAT IS WHY HE SAID IT WAS SOMETHING IN THE GLASS AND SAID IT TWICE. He had seen the
    station read high on Larrow Green's glass fourteen years earlier.** Nobody in the chain has made this
    connection. It converts Ansel's 1911 hypothesis from a lucky guess into a reasoned inference from his own
    experience, and it makes Kepp's 1926 dismissal of the borrowed-glass objection worse, not better.
  - **⚠ It also means the money computation may be one year long.** Ivy's $19,000 rests on 40,000,000 lb of
    milk at 1,600,000 lb/year — Kyle's figures — which is **exactly 25 years**, not 29. **So the money was
    computed over the twenty-five years of the complaint (1900–1925), NOT over Ansel's twenty-nine seasons,
    and NOT over the twenty-eight years the bad glass was actually in use (May 1897 – June 1923).** Nobody
    states this. **The arithmetic is internally consistent at 25 years; do not "fix" it to 28 or 29.**

**THE DISPUTE:** Vaught's method section in Bulletin 471 gives the average delivery of the condemned
pipettes as **16.27 c.c.** against a nominal **17.6 c.c.**, and gives a deficiency of **0.295** of a point.
The chain has long carried "16.27-for-16.72" as a **known transposition error**, i.e. the true average is
**16.72** and the true deficiency is **0.19**. The Chronicle (R19), working from the college press release,
**also prints 16.27** — so the two are not independent.

**THE PHYSICS (reader 17's, and it is sound):**
- 16.27 / 17.6 = 0.9244 → a **7.6% shortfall**. On milk testing about 3.8, that reads about **0.29 low** —
  which is Vaught's other disputed figure, **0.295**.
- 16.72 / 17.6 = 0.9500 → a **5.0% shortfall**. On milk testing about 3.8, that reads **0.19 low** — which is
  exactly the gap the file's soundest sources give.
- **THEREFORE THE TWO DISPUTED NUMBERS ARE NOT INDEPENDENT ERRORS — THEY ARE THE SAME ERROR PROPAGATING.
  If 16.72 is right, 0.19 is right and 0.295 is derived from the slip. If 16.27 is right, 0.295 is right and
  0.19 is wrong.**

**THE LOCK (reader 18's, from Kyle's 1953 memoir, and it is decisive because it was written before anybody

twenty-five years, a "station gain" of "five and a quarter per cent, as usual."**
- A pipette delivering **16.72** of a marked 17.6 produces a station gain of **0.05 / 0.95 = 5.26%** —
  **"five and a quarter per cent," to two decimal places.** ✓
- A pipette delivering **16.27** produces **0.0757 / 0.9243 = 8.19%**, which is nothing like it. ✗

**★★★ HOW A MILK-TEST PIPETTE IS MADE — the only technical account of the manufacture in the entire file,
and it explains exactly how the error was possible:**
> *"A milk test pipette is a simple thing and it is not an easy thing. A man gathers his glass, blows the
> bulb, draws the stem down, and **works to a gauge that hangs on a nail at his elbow**. He is making a
> vessel that must **deliver** a stated quantity of milk — **not hold it, *deliver* it, which is a different
> measurement and a harder one, because milk is not water and clings to the wall of the glass as it runs
> out.** The mark goes on the stem while the glass is still warm. Then the piece goes into the lehr to cool
> slowly, and comes out the next morning, and is gauged, and is either packed or thrown into the cullet
> barrel under the bench, and no man on that floor was ever thanked for what went into the barrel."*
**★★ "not hold it, DELIVER it" is the crux of the whole case in one clause. A pipette marked 17.6 c.c. is a
promise about what runs OUT, and milk clings. This is why a pipette can be visibly correct and functionally
short, and why nobody at the station could have caught it by looking.**
**★ New vocabulary the file did not have: the LEHR (the annealing oven the warm glass cools in), the CULLET
BARREL (rejects), the GAUGE ON A NAIL.**
