# Batch 1 — Answers

**Standing caveat on my evidence base.** I am answering from a chain of retention notes, not from the
retellings themselves. The notes preserve one strand of the material in depth — the Ostrey Hollow
skimming-station affair — plus names (Vose, Cudd, Vaught, Loomis) belonging to strands whose substance
was cut out of the hand-offs. Where a question falls in the cut, I say so rather than guess.

---

## Section A — Reconstruction (stories 1–3 of 8)

*Preamble (two sentences, so the grader knows what I am claiming): the material that reached me covers
one continuous story with a clear internal sequence, so I have divided it at its three natural joints —
the installation, the misdiagnosis, the missed correction — and present those as stories 1–3 in
occurrence order. I cannot verify that this division matches the original eight; several other strands
(the Vose material, the Loomis family, whatever Cudd and Vaught belong to) are names to me and nothing
more.*

### 1. The Borrowed Glass and the Bought Glass, 1896–1897

This is about **Ansel Keddie** (born 1861 in Ostrey Hollow, lived there his whole life), the **Larrow
Valley Cooperative Creamery Association**, and one crate of testing glassware.

The Association's **skimming station at Ostrey Hollow opened 4 May 1896**. Ansel was its **tester and
separator man** — a two-part post: he ran the separator and made the butterfat tests — and he kept the
station books in his own hand for **twenty-nine seasons** (1896 through 1924; he was put out one week
into what would have been the thirtieth). For its first year the station had no glass of its own and
**borrowed from the creamery at Larrow Green, seven miles down the valley**. An invoice dated **14
April 1897** (reproduced in facsimile in Junia Ammon's 1949 firm history) bought its own outfit, and on
**1 May 1897** the glass came up. Orra Keddie wrote it in her diary that day: *"A. says the station's
own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green."*

**That glass was short.** Reconstructing from the figures the chain carries: a pipette nominally 17.6 cc
that in fact delivered 16.72 cc — **5 per cent short** — so every test made on it read **0.19 points
low** ("nineteen hundredths, flat as a floor"). Ostrey Hollow's patrons were underpaid on every pound
of butterfat from 1 May 1897 until the glass was condemned on 12 June 1923 — **26 years**, not the
"twenty-eight" the record loosely repeats (1897 to 1923 is 26; I corrected this by arithmetic on the
sources' own dates).

**Conflict resolved:** Bertram Kyle (1953) dates the arrival to **1 May 1898**. I reject it. Kyle warns
in his own document that his years are unreliable, and three independent lines of evidence give 1897 —
a contemporaneous diary entry, a dated invoice in facsimile, and the 1958 Chronicle's "spring of 1897."
Kyle's error is exactly one year, same day, same month: a memory slip.

**Inference, flagged:** the station's **first year of tests was correct**, because the borrowed Larrow
Green glass was sound. The evidence is the 1911 episode (story 3): when the station borrowed from
Larrow Green again, its tests *rose*. If Larrow Green's glass had also been short, they would not have.
This contradicts the Chronicle's "from very nearly the day the station opened," which I treat as a
newspaperman's rounding of one year in twenty-six.

**Documents:** Orra Keddie's diary (R24, contemporaneous); Junia Ammon, firm history 1949 (R20); the
Chronicle 1958 (R19); Bertram Kyle 1953 (R23, wrong on the year); Bulletin 471 (1958) for the glassware
measurement.

### 2. Blaming the Cows, Then the Hay: 1900–1908

This is about the valley's twenty-five-year misdiagnosis, and about what **Ansel Keddie** understood
about his own position from the beginning.

Ostrey Hollow milk tested lower than the milk of anywhere else in the county, and the complaint was
raised **at every annual meeting for about a quarter of a century, from roughly 1900 to 1925**. Orra
Keddie dates it earlier than any official record does: **March 1902**, *"Talk at the store that our milk
up here runs poorer than theirs down the valley. A. came home and would not eat."* She adds the single
most revealing sentence about Ansel's exposure, twenty-three years before he lost his post: *"I said it
is not your cows and not your hands, and he said no, but it is my figures they will look at."*

In **January 1908** the Association sent a committee up. The formal body was **three farmers chaired by
Nyle Grigg**; Orra, watching from the house, counted **four men in Grigg's sleigh**. I prefer three as
the committee (it is on a document) and treat Orra's four as what actually arrived at the door — a
chairman, a driver, or a count from a window; the two are not necessarily in conflict. They dined at
**Emmet's** farm in **Hessel Bottom**, the biggest herd in the bottom, and sampled the bottom herds.

Orra records the procedural fact that decides the whole case: **"They wanted to see the barns and not
the station, which A. remarked on afterward and let alone."** A committee investigating why one
station's milk tested low did not examine the station. Ansel noticed and said nothing.

In **March 1908** the verdict came down verbally — *"their word is that it is the wet hay of the bottom
that thins the cream."* Emmet's answer: *"the bottom fed his father and it will feed him."* Grigg's
finding was written up, **reprinted by the Association in 1919**, and later given professional cover by
**Kepp, "the veterinarian of standing at Ashlin,"** who wrote up the hay theory. So a single 1908
opinion was recirculated for seventeen years and acquired the appearance of independent confirmation.

**Independence warning:** the "six farms" count is credited to six sources, but the 1908 report, Grigg's
letter, Kepp, and the Chronicle plausibly all descend from the same 1908 document. Count it as one or
two independent sources, not six.

**Documents:** Orra Keddie's diary (R24); the 1908 committee report and Grigg's letter (R15); Kepp
(R16); the Chronicle 1958 (R19); Falke's 1959 county report (R22) for the Hessel Bottom sampling.

### 3. The Broken Measure, 1911 — and the Objection Talked Down, 1926

This is the story of the one moment the case could have turned, and of the man who talked it down.

On **8 May 1911** the station's own measuring glass broke. Orra: *"A. home cross about a broken
measure."* Not puzzled — **cross**, a man annoyed about equipment. For **nine weeks** the station tested
on glass borrowed again from Larrow Green, and **the tests ran markedly higher**. On **10 July 1911**
the station's own glass resumed and **the tests fell back the same day**.

In **the summer of 1911** Ansel Keddie said it was **"something in the glass."** He said it **twice** —
at supper, and again by the door — and **wrote a question mark in the station book**. The source is his
son **Duncan**, then seventeen, in the kitchen; Duncan wrote it down fifty-seven years later, in 1968,
months after leaving for Ashlin to teach in October 1911, which is why the memory kept its edge.

**Why this was reasoning and not a lucky guess:** Ansel had run this same station on Larrow Green's
glass through its whole first season, 1896–97, and knew what Ostrey Hollow milk read on borrowed glass.
When the borrowed glass pushed the tests up in 1911, he was comparing against his own experience. That
connection is an inference of mine; the sources do not state it, but both halves of it are documented.

**A silence I decline to over-read:** Orra's diary, kept that very summer, does not contain the
hypothesis — there is no entry between 8 May and October 1911. This does **not** disprove Duncan. The
1964 editor selected entries "for what they show of the household and the season," and a domestic
selection would drop a sentence about butterfat testing. The **three unedited notebooks were in the
family's hands in 1964** and nobody has gone back to them; that is the one live lead left in the file.

**1926 closes the door.** An **unnamed gentleman** put the objection to Kepp directly — the tests went
up in 1911 when the station borrowed apparatus, so look at the apparatus — **and Kepp talked him down.**
Ansel and Duncan are the natural candidates for that gentleman; neither is proven, and the diary's 1926
leaves have been **torn out** by an unknown hand for unknown reasons. I record the coincidence and
assert no identification.

**Documents:** Duncan Keddie 1968 (R18); Orra Keddie's diary (R24); the Kepp material (R16); Bulletin
471 (1958), which found the 1911 evidence decisive.

## Section B — Relationships

### B1. Ivy Keddie to Ansel Keddie

**His granddaughter — specifically, his son Duncan's daughter.** The derivation runs through three
independent checks. (i) Ansel and Orra's son **Duncan** was born 1894 — fixed twice inside Orra's diary
("Duncan five years old" in September 1899; "seventeen" in October 1911). (ii) The 1958 Chronicle
describes Ivy's **father** as the man who "taught school for many years," and Duncan is the retired
schoolteacher of Ashlin who began teaching there in October 1911. (iii) Arithmetic: Ivy was born
**1931**, seventy years after Ansel (1861) and thirty-seven after Duncan — a generation too far for
daughter, exactly right for granddaughter. She was **three years old** when Ansel died in February 1934.

What points elsewhere, and how I resolve it: Ivy is the *actor* in this story — she climbed the ladder
in February 1958, she supplies the $19,000 figure, she is the family voice quoted in the press — and a
compressed retelling can make the person who avenges a man sound like his child. Her own most-quoted
line resolves it in the opposite direction: *"I would like it printed that he never knew"* is **false**
(he suspected in 1911 and said so twice), and a daughter who had lived in that house would not have
believed it. It is precisely what a granddaughter who knew him only to the age of three would have been
told and would repeat.

### B2. Ivy Keddie to Hazel Brant

**Cannot be determined.** What the sources establish: Hazel Brant was **taped in 1981** and is the
chain's most-quoted late witness — she gave the "nineteen hundredths, flat as a floor" line and called
the testing room the **weigh room**, the local name for what the reports call the intake room. Nothing
in any source states her parentage.

The live hypothesis, which I state and mark unproven: material carried at second hand says **Ansel
married Orra Brant in 1889**. If Orra's maiden name was Brant, then her brother **Emmet** was Emmet
Brant, **Roy** (who met Orra at the gate in 1929) was Roy Brant, and Hazel is very likely of that
family — which would make her a **collateral relative of Ivy on Ivy's grandmother's side**, descending
from Emmet's line rather than Ansel's. Emmet would be Ivy's great-uncle; Roy, if Emmet's son, her first
cousin once removed; Hazel's degree depends on a generation nobody names. If true it matters more than
genealogy usually does: the valley's most-quoted late witness would be from the family that **broke
with Ansel over the 1919 circular** and signed the 1925 petition against his station.

What the sources leave open, exactly: (a) Orra's maiden name is unproven — the diary's editor states
that "the pages covering her girlhood and her family before 1889 were not preserved," and the diary
never gives Emmet a surname; (b) the "Orra Brant" claim reached me only inside a later reader's
commentary, never in a primary source; (c) Roy's parentage is an assumption from context; (d) Hazel's
own parents are never named. Any source giving Emmet's or Hazel's full family line would settle it.

### B3. Selby Vose's father

**Cannot be determined from what reached me, and I decline to guess.** Everything my material holds
about Selby Vose is that he wrote **a letter (R08)** giving **nine families** as the recipients of the
1919 water circular. No parentage, no dates, no place, no occupation.

This is a known hole rather than an absence of evidence in the original material: the notes I am working
from were truncated at a fixed line in every hand-off, and the Vose entry in the last full people-index
fell inside the cut, along with entries for Frick, Nym, Strawn, Rundle, Cudd, Vaught, Ammon, Kyle, Falke,
Oram, Brant and the Loomis family. I can say what an answer would bear on: if Vose's father were an
Association officer or a booked patron, that would tell us whether his "nine" counts **patrons on the
books** rather than **doors** — which is the live question in B-adjacent territory (Ansel physically
carried the circular to **eleven** families, per his wife the night he did it). But that is the shape of
an answer, not an answer.

### B4. The Vose family and the Keddie family

**No relationship is established, and I decline to invent one.** The only point of contact anywhere in
my material is the **1919 water circular**: it went out over the manager's name, Ansel Keddie carried it
by hand to every door and stood there while each man read it, and Selby Vose's letter is the document
that counts its recipients at nine. That makes Vose a source *about* an event Ansel was in; it does not
make the families kin, neighbors, or antagonists.

Weak negative evidence, offered as weak: Orra Keddie's diary — the fullest interior record of the Keddie
household across thirty-three years — **never mentions Vose at all**, nor Grigg by name (only "Grigg's
sleigh"), Frick, Nym, Kepp, Strawn, Rundle, Cudd, Ammon or Oram. But it is an *edited* extract chosen
for household and season, so its silence about a name is close to worthless as proof. The correct answer
is that the sources that survived to me do not decide this.

## Section C — Math

### C1

**(a) Proportion, and how it comes out of the glassware.**

Working:

- Nominal pipette volume: **17.6 cc**. Volume the station's glass actually delivered: **16.72 cc**.
- 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95**. The glass was **5.00 per cent short** of nominal.
- A butterfat test is read against the assumed volume, so a 5 per cent short charge reads 5 per cent low.
- Stated the other way — as an uplift on what was recorded — 17.6 ÷ 16.72 = 1.0526, i.e. the truth was
  **5¼ per cent above** the recorded figure (1/19 = 5.26 %, "five and a quarter per cent" to the nearest
  quarter).

**Answer: the tests read 5 per cent low relative to the truth; equivalently the true figures were 5¼
per cent higher than the recorded ones.**

⚠ Flagged as reconstruction, not quotation. My notes carry 17.6 and 16.72 as unexplained figures from
the 1958 bulletin's quantitative material. Reading 17.6 cc as the nominal pipette charge and 16.72 as
the delivered volume is my inference; it is supported because it reproduces two other independent
figures exactly (see (b)), and because the paired variant 16.27 does **not** reproduce them.

**(b) True average test, and the shortfall in points.**

Working: true average test **3.78 %**; 3.78 × 0.95 = 3.591, so the recorded average was **3.59 %**;
3.78 − 3.59 = **0.19**.

**Answer: the true average test was 3.78 per cent; the recorded tests were short by 0.19 points**
("nineteen hundredths, flat as a floor," in the 1981 testimony).

This is the arithmetic lock, and it is the strongest thing in this batch: three figures that reached me
separately and without explanation — 17.6/16.72, 3.78, and 0.19 — close on each other to the second
decimal. That mutual fit is what licenses (a).

**(c) Butterfat not credited.**

Working: 0.19 points = 0.19 lb of butterfat per 100 lb of milk = 0.0019.
Milk delivered over the period: **40,000,000 lb** (from the clerk's poundage: 1,600,000 lb/year ×
**exactly 25 years**).
40,000,000 × 0.0019 = **76,000 lb**.

**Answer: 76,000 lb of butterfat, over the 25 delivery years the sources' own arithmetic covers
(≈1900–1925).**

⚠ Two cautions. First, the period in that computation is the **twenty-five years of the complaint**, not
Ansel's twenty-nine seasons and not the years the short glass was actually in use. The arithmetic is
internally consistent at 25; do not "fix" it. Second, the glass was actually in service **1 May 1897 to
12 June 1923 = 26 years**, and the record's habitual "twenty-eight years" is simply wrong — 1923 minus
1897 is 26. If the same annual poundage held, 26 years gives 41,600,000 lb and **79,040 lb** of
butterfat. I give 76,000 as the answer because it is the figure the sources actually compute; the 79,040
is my extrapolation and no source makes it.

**(d) Money, and what fraction the patrons got.**

Working: 76,000 lb × **$0.25 per lb** of butterfat = **$19,000**.

That is exactly the figure Ivy Keddie gave the press in 1958, which confirms the whole chain of
reasoning above — her $19,000 is reproducible from the clerk's poundage, the bulletin's discrepancy and
the butterfat price. (A **$19,600** variant exists in the record; it does not reproduce from any
combination of these figures, and I treat it as a transcription slip.)

Against that, the **1925 board of arbitration awarded $3,800, "without admission."**

$3,800 ÷ $19,000 = **0.20 exactly**.

**Answer: about $19,000 was lost; the patrons received $3,800, which is exactly one fifth — 20 per
cent. Four fifths of it was never paid.**

Conflict resolved: one late narrator gives the award as **$3,500** against three sources' $3,800. I keep
$3,800, on three grounds — the two strongest sources for it are a county report and the Association's
own clerk (the clerk writing in 1953, before anyone knew the answer, which is the highest-value class of
document here); the dissenter is a 1977 recollection; and $3,800 lands on a round one fifth of the loss
while $3,500 gives 18.4 per cent. The last point is suggestive, not decisive — I cannot rule out that
the fifth is coincidence, since the $19,000 was computed thirty-three years after the award.

⚠ Not in the sources: whether the $3,800 reached individual patrons in full or net of costs, and how it
was apportioned among them.

### C2 — The largest patron's 1910

**(a), (b) and (c): cannot be determined from the sources.** I decline all three.

What the sources do establish: the largest patron was almost certainly **Emmet**, Orra Keddie's brother,
who farmed in **Hessel Bottom** and whose herd was "the biggest" (the 1908 committee went to him first
for that reason), and who **hauled whole milk to the station from the day it opened in 1896**. We also
have the station-wide rate — **1,600,000 lb of milk a year** — the discrepancy (**0.19** points) and the
butterfat price (**$0.25**).

Why that is not enough: **no source gives any individual patron's poundage, and no source gives any
figure for the specific year 1910.** The 1,600,000 lb is an average across the station, and the split
among its patrons is nowhere stated. Without Emmet's share, (a) and (b) have no numerator, and (c)
follows them.

The size of the gap, so the decline is not mistaken for ignorance: if the station's milk were split
evenly across six farms, one farm would be ~266,700 lb, giving ~507 lb of butterfat forgone and ~$127 in
1910 — and the largest patron would be above that by an unknown multiple. That is an illustration of the
method, **not an answer**; it rests on an even split that the sources contradict (the herds were
explicitly unequal) and on an annual average applied to a single year in a valley where the record notes
weather closing the station road for days at a time.

One temptation declined: the record carries a **$77.39** figure from the 1949 firm history whose meaning
was lost mid-quotation. It divides into a plausible one-farm annual loss, but it sits in a **glassware
firm's** history, where an invoice total is the likelier reading. I will not use an unexplained number
to manufacture an answer.
