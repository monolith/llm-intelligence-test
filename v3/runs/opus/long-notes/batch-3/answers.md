# Section A — Reconstruction

## 1. The Board That Sat Under the Ladder (January – May 1925)

By 1925 the grievance was a quarter of a century old. It was already "talk at the store" in **March
1902** — Orra Keddie records her husband coming home and refusing to eat, saying *"it is not your cows
and not your hands… no, but it is my figures they will look at."* It had survived the 1908 hay verdict
and the 1919 circular accusing the patrons of watering their milk.

In **January 1925** the patrons put in a paper — a petition — against the Ostrey Hollow station.
Orra's entry names the signature that mattered: *"My brother Emmet's name is on it. I do not blame him
and I will not say so to A."* Emmet had hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened,
4 May 1896, and had not entered his sister's house since June 1919.

A **board of arbitration sat at Ashlin from 17 March to 4 May 1925** under **Alonzo Frick**. Three
independent records agree: Nym's minute book (Item 7), Falke's 1959 county report, and Orra, who
watched her husband go down on the opening morning — *"in his good coat, which he has had since Duncan
was married… He was up at three and did not sleep before that either. I ironed the coat twice for want
of anything I could do."* She never uses the word arbitration: *"There is a board of men to hear the
whole thing out."* Nobody explained the machinery to the tester's wife.

In **April 1925 the board came up the valley and held two of its sittings in the station's own weigh
room**, men standing outside in the road. This is in R24 alone. It matters because the crate of
pipettes condemned by the inspector **Merle Strawn on 12 June 1923** was in the loft directly over
their heads, and stayed there until February 1958. The board sat twice beneath the evidence and no
source records anyone climbing the ladder.

On **30 April 1925 the Association dismissed Ansel Keddie by letter** — *"Twenty-nine seasons and a
letter"* — four days before its own board reported. On **4 May** the award went to the patrons:
**$3,800, "without admission"** (Falke, Kyle, the Chronicle; Oram alone says $3,500). Ansel came up the
road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm, hung it behind the shed door and asked
what there was for supper. *"There was ham. He ate it."*

Quantities that matter: 29 seasons (1896–1924) ended by one letter; 4 days between letter and award;
2 sittings in the weigh room; $3,800; a 25-year complaint period behind it.

## 2. Nine Years, a Ladder, and Bulletin 471 (1925 – 1981)

The dismissal did not end in a verdict against him. It ended in **no verdict at all**, which is the
whole point of the story. The 1925 award was "without admission," the 1919 circular had been in "a
civil form of words," and nobody ever put a charge to Ansel Keddie's face. He told his wife once, and
only once, that **"a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."**

He became a carpenter. From **June 1925** he worked on the cow shed *"and whistles, which he never did
when he was paid."* By **October 1927** he was mending stairs, **the Loomis gate**, and half the doors
in the hollow — *"A carpenter at sixty-six"*, which fixes his birth year at **1861** by arithmetic —
*"and it pays a quarter of what the other did."* His wife's verdict, and it is not flattering to him:
*"He is better at it than he ever was at the other."*

**Spring 1929: Emmet died**, ending the ten-year estrangement without either man going up the road
first. **August 1930:** Orra closed the diary after 33 years. **February 1934:** Ansel died at home, in
the forenoon, Duncan coming up in the night. At the burying, *"men shook my hand who had not spoken to
him in nine years… every one of them said what a hand he was with a door. Not one of them said the
other thing."* **Orra died in 1948**, ten years short.

Then two documents written before anybody knew the answer — **Junia Ammon's firm history (1949)** and
**Bertram Kyle's memoir (1953)** — and then, in **February 1958**, Ansel's granddaughter **Ivy Keddie**,
aged 27, climbed the ladder into the Ostrey Hollow loft and brought down the condemned crate. Measured,
the glass read short by **nineteen hundredths of a point** — Hazel Brant's *"flat as a floor."*
**Bulletin 471** cleared his name in 1958, twenty-four years after his death. Ivy's figure for what it
cost the patrons was **$19,000**, and the arithmetic closes exactly: 1,600,000 lb of milk a year x
0.19 per cent x $0.25 a pound of butterfat = $760 a year; x 25 years = **$19,000**.

Every other document in the file is retrospect on that week: the **Chronicle (1958)**, **Falke (1959)**,
**Orra's diary published (1964)** — printed because the name had been cleared — **Duncan (1968)**,
**Oram (1977)**, **Hazel Brant taped (1981)**.

# Section E — Contradictions (E1)

### 1. When did the station's own glass arrive? 1 May 1897 or 1 May 1898?
- **Claims:** 1 May 1897 — Orra Keddie (R24, written the day: *"A. says the station's own glass came up
  and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green"*); Junia Ammon (R20, invoice dated 14 April 1897,
  reproduced in facsimile); the Chronicle (R19, "the spring of 1897"). **1 May 1898** — Bertram Kyle (R23).
- **Resolution: 1 May 1897.** Method, in order of weight: a **dated document** (the invoice) outranks a
  memoir; a **contemporaneous diary entry** outranks a memoir written fifty-six years later; and the
  dissenting narrator **warns the reader in his own text that his years are unreliable**. Kyle is wrong
  by exactly one year, same day, same month — the signature of a memory slip, not of a rival record.
- **⚠ But do not throw Kyle's date away**, because it is load-bearing for his own arithmetic. See item 9.

### 2. When did the low tests begin?
- **Claims:** the Chronicle (R19) — *"from very nearly the day the station opened"* (i.e. May 1896).
  R24 — the station borrowed Larrow Green's glass until 1 May 1897, so its first year's tests were made
  on other glass.
- **This is a contradiction inside the Chronicle itself**, which elsewhere dates the glass to spring 1897
  while dating the trouble to 1896.
- **Resolution: the tests went wrong on 1 May 1897; the Chronicle is rounding.** Method — **positive
  physical evidence beats a phrase.** In 1911 the station borrowed Larrow Green's glass again and the
  tests went **up**. If Larrow Green's glass had also been short, they would not have risen. So Larrow
  Green's glass was sound, so the first year (May 1896 – May 1897) was clean, so "very nearly the day it
  opened" is a newspaperman's compression of one year out of twenty-eight.

### 3. What was the 1925 award? $3,800 or $3,500?
- **Claims:** $3,800 — Falke (R22, county report), Kyle (R23, the Association's own clerk), the
  Chronicle (R19). **$3,500** — Cleve Oram (R21, 1977).
- **Resolution: $3,800.** Method — not majority alone. **Counting independence as the instructions ask:**
  Kyle was the Association's clerk writing from the Cooperative's office in 1953, before anyone knew the
  answer and with the books to hand; Falke wrote an official report to a county board in 1959. Those two
  are independent of each other and both sit closer to the record than Oram's 1977 recollection. The
  Chronicle may be downstream of one of them and I do not count it. So it is **two strong independent
  sources against one late memory**, and the round number $3,500 is the more likely rounding.
- **R24 is not a fourth source** — the editor flags that the award is not mentioned in the diary.

### 4. How many households got the 1919 water circular? Six, nine, or eleven?
- **Claims:** **six farms** — the 1908 report, Grigg's letter, Kepp, Hazel Brant, the Chronicle.
  **nine families** — Selby Vose's letter (R08). **eleven families** — Orra (R24), the same night, from
  the man who carried them.
- **Resolution: partly settled, partly not.** *Farms:* six. *Households:* **cannot be determined.**
  Method — first separate the **units**. "Farms," "families on the books," and "doors Ansel stood at" are
  three different counts and need not conflict. The cleanest reading, offered as a reading and not a
  fact, is **six farms, eleven households**, with Vose's nine being the patrons booked by the
  Association. But nothing decides between nine and eleven, and I decline to.
- **⚠ Independence warning on the "six":** six sources for six farms is probably **one** source counted
  six times — Grigg's 1908 finding, which the Association reprinted in 1919 and which everyone
  afterwards repeated. Six farms is the best-supported figure but should be held with the confidence of
  a single document, not of six witnesses.

### 5. Three men or four on the 1908 committee?
- **Claims:** a **three-man committee of farmers chaired by Nyle Grigg** (R15). **"Four men up the road
  today in Grigg's sleigh"** (R24).
- **Resolution: not a contradiction.** Method — a formal body on a document and a head-count from a
  window are different objects. Three committeemen plus Grigg (chairing, not counted among them), or
  plus a driver, reconciles them. **Prefer three as the body; record four as what arrived at the door.**

### 6. Intake room or weigh room?
- **Claims:** **intake room** — Ivy (R04), Rundle (R12), Duncan (R18), the Chronicle (R19), Falke (R22).
  **weigh room** — Hazel Brant (R17) and Orra (R24).
- **Resolution: settled, and it was never a factual dispute.** Cleve Oram (R21) states the two are the
  same room. R24 explains *why* it looked open: the people who lived in the hollow said weigh room, the
  people who wrote reports said intake room. **A register difference, not a disagreement.**

### 7. "He never knew."
- **Claims:** Ivy Keddie to the Chronicle, 1958 — *"I would like it printed that he never knew."*
  Duncan Keddie (R18, 1968) — in the summer of 1911 his father said it was **something in the glass**,
  said it **twice**, and wrote a **question mark in the station book**.
- **Resolution: Ivy is wrong.** Method — **proximity**. Duncan was seventeen and in the kitchen; Ivy was
  three years old when Ansel died in 1934 and had no access to 1911 except by report. Her sentence is a
  wish, and reads like one.
- **⚠ Two narrators, one value:** the Chronicle prints "he never knew," so it looks like two sources.
  **It is one** — the Chronicle is quoting Ivy. Duncan breaks the tie by being first-hand.
- **The diary's silence on 1911 does not corroborate Ivy.** R24 records the breakage of 8 May 1911
  ("A. home cross about a broken measure") and nothing between then and October 1911. An **edited**
  extract's silence is not evidence about the unedited notebook, and the preface says entries were
  chosen "for what they show of the household and the season" — a 1964 editor would drop a sentence
  about butterfat testing. Do not say the diary disproves Duncan.

### 8. Junia Ammon contradicts herself on the page.
- **Claims, both R20:** *"we sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900"* — and her own **invoice of
  14 April 1897 to a customer in Ordell County**, reproduced in facsimile in the same document.
- **Resolution: cannot be settled.** The cheapest reconciliation is that **Larrow Green and Ostrey Hollow
  are not in Ordell County** — in which case the 1897 sale was somewhere else and both statements stand.
  But **no source anywhere states which county Larrow Green is in.** Ordell County appears only as the
  county of the historical society Kyle wrote for, the board of supervisors Falke reported to, and that
  invoice. Method: I decline, and name the one fact that would settle it.

### 9. Bertram Kyle contradicts himself — and it is worth more than it looks.
- Kyle **warns that his years are unreliable** and then supplies a year (1 May 1898) that is wrong.
- **But his poundage is internally consistent with his own error.** He gives 40,000,000 lb total at
  1,600,000 lb a year — **exactly 25 years**. The chain reads that 25 years as the complaint period
  (about 1900–1925). **I offer a second reading, marked as my inference:** 1 May **1898** to the
  condemnation of 12 June **1923** is also exactly 25 years. Kyle may have computed the loss over the
  glass's working life *as he mis-dated it*.
- **Consequence, if so:** the glass actually went into service 1 May 1897, so the true exposure is
  **28 years**, and **Ivy's $19,000 inherits Kyle's one-year error and is a floor, not a total.** At the
  same rate ($760 a year — see F1) twenty-eight years is about **$21,280**.
- **⚠ I do not assert this.** Both readings give 25 years and I cannot see Kyle's text. Give $19,000 as
  the sourced figure and flag that it is computed over 25 years, not over the 28 the glass was in use.

### 10. $19,000 or $19,600?
- The chain carries both without resolution.
- **Resolution: $19,000.** Method — **arithmetic, not authority.** 1,600,000 lb x 0.19 per cent x $0.25
  x 25 years = $19,000 exactly, using four figures each independently attested (Kyle's poundage, Hazel
  Brant's 0.19, the $0.25 butterfat price, the 25-year span). **$19,600 falls out of nothing.** It is
  almost certainly a corruption. This is also, I believe, the reconstruction of the "arithmetic lock"
  that was lost from the chain.

### 11. 16.72 versus 16.27.
- The chain carries these as "a paired figure" with no surviving meaning. **They are digit
  transpositions of each other.** That pattern usually means **one true value and one copying error, not
  two facts.** **Which is right cannot be determined** — the material that defined them was lost. Same
  for **17.6**, which I can neither place nor interpret. I decline on all three rather than guess.

### 12. Length of service: "twenty-nine seasons" versus "a quarter of a century."
- **Not a contradiction; a category confusion, and it is the easiest trap in the file.** Ansel served
  **29 seasons** (1896 through 1924, put out one week before the 1925 season). The **quarter of a
  century** belongs to the *complaints* (about 1900–1925), and the **28 years** belongs to the *glass*
  (1 May 1897 – 12 June 1923). Three different spans, three different subjects. R24 confirms 29 twice
  (preface and the 30 April 1925 entry) and the arithmetic checks.

### 13. The same wrong value carried by more than one narrator: the wet-hay verdict.
- **Grigg's 1908 committee** found that the wet hay of the bottom thins the cream; **Kepp**, the
  veterinarian of standing at Ashlin, wrote the theory up; the **Association reprinted Grigg's finding in
  1919**; **Falke** was still reporting it in 1959. Four appearances.
- **What breaks the tie is not a counter-source — there isn't one — it is the 1911 episode.** The hay of
  Hessel Bottom did not change between 8 May and 10 July 1911, yet the tests rose for nine weeks on
  borrowed glass and fell back the same day the station's own glass returned. Feed cannot do that.
- **And the four appearances are one source.** Kepp wrote up Grigg; the Association reprinted Grigg;
  Falke reported what the county had been told. **Count it as one claim repeated, which is exactly how a
  wrong value survives twenty-five years.**

### 14. Contradictions I looked for and did not find.
- 4 May 1896 (station opens), 8 May 1911 (glass breaks), 12 June 1923 (condemnation), 17 March 1925
  (board opens), 30 April 1925 (dismissal), February 1934 (death), February 1958 (the loft): **no source
  in my notes disputes any of these**, and 1911, 1925 and 1934 each have a contemporaneous domestic
  record agreeing with an official one.

# Section F — Theory (F1)

## The theory

**The instrument was wrong and everything else was right.**

The Ostrey Hollow skimming station opened 4 May 1896 and, having no outfit of its own, **borrowed its
testing glass from the creamery at Larrow Green, seven miles down the valley.** Those tests were sound.
On an invoice dated 14 April 1897 the Association bought the station its own glass from Junia Ammon's
firm, and **on 1 May 1897 that glass came up the road**. It was mis-graduated. Every butterfat test made
on it thereafter read about **nineteen hundredths of a percentage point low** — Hazel Brant's *"flat as
a floor,"* and "flat" is the diagnostic word: the error did not wander with the season, the weather, the
herd or the man, because it was a property of the glass.

**Why that size.** A graduation error is *proportional*: the neck is short by a fixed fraction, so the
reading is low by a fixed fraction of the true test. At a herd average around 3.6–3.8 per cent butterfat
a shortfall of 0.19 points is about **five and a quarter per cent** of the reading (0.19 / 0.0525 =
3.62). **I note — as an inference I cannot verify — that "five and a quarter per cent" is the title of
the one section of Kyle's memoir the chain lost.** If that is what it meant, the size is fully explained:
5.25 per cent short glass, times a typical test, equals 0.19 points. **Marked as inference. The
alternative, that 5.25 per cent was a rate of interest or dividend, is equally available and I cannot
choose between them.**

**Why it began when it did.** Because that is the day the glass entered service, and not one day
earlier. The station's first year was clean, on Larrow Green's glass.

**Why it ended when it did.** On **12 June 1923** the inspector **Merle Strawn** condemned the station's
glass — for its own reasons, drawing, in Falke's words, *"no inference whatever"* — and in **July 1923**
new glass and new pails came up from the supply house at **Tarnet**. Orra records it in the same breath
as the bad roof. **The error stopped the day the object was replaced, and nobody noticed that it had
stopped**, because nobody had noticed it starting.

**Why nothing at the station or on the farms had to change.** Because nothing at the station or on the
farms was ever the cause. The milk, the cows, the hay, the pails, the separator and the tester's hands
were all constant across 1897 and across 1923, and the readings changed at both boundaries anyway. The
fault lived in a piece of graduated glass — what the Chronicle called **furniture**. That is also why
the **1911 nine weeks are the controlled experiment the case never knew it had run**: 8 May 1911 the
station's glass breaks, the station borrows Larrow Green's again, the tests go markedly **up** for nine
weeks with nothing else altered, and on 10 July 1911 the own glass returns and the tests fall back
**the same day**. Same cows, same hay, same man, different glass, different answer.

## The three explanations the sources advance, and what defeats each

**1. The cows — the milk of Ostrey Hollow is simply poorer.**
*Advanced by:* the valley itself, from at least March 1902 (*"talk at the store that our milk up here
runs poorer than theirs down the valley"* — R24), and adopted as the working premise of the 1908
committee, which came to *"look into the herds."*
*Defeated by:* the 1911 episode — the same herds tested higher for nine weeks on other glass. And by the
station's first year, 1896–97, when the same herds tested normally on Larrow Green's glass.

**2. The wet hay of the bottom thins the cream.**
*Advanced by:* **Nyle Grigg's three-man committee**, verdict delivered verbally in March 1908
(*"their word is that it is the wet hay of the bottom"*); written up by **Kepp**, the veterinarian of
standing at Ashlin; **reprinted by the Association in 1919**; still being reported by **Falke in 1959**.
*Defeated by:* the 1911 episode again — hay does not change for exactly nine weeks and change back on
10 July. And by the flatness of the error: a feed effect varies with the season and with wet years
against dry, and this discrepancy was constant at 0.19 for twenty-six years. And, procedurally, by
Orra's January 1908 line — **the committee investigating why one station's milk tested low never looked
at the station.** *"They wanted to see the barns and not the station, which A. remarked on afterward and
let alone."*

**3. Water in the milk — the patrons are adulterating.**
*Advanced by:* the Association's manager, in the **June 1919 circular** sent out over his name and
carried by hand to eleven doors by the tester.
*Defeated by:* the 1923 condemnation and the 1958 measurement of that same condemned glass, which
located the shortfall in the instrument; by uniformity — a fraud does not run at the same 0.19 across
every patron of one station for twenty-eight years while every neighbouring station reads true; and by
the 1911 reversal, which no watering scheme could produce or undo on schedule.

**The fourth explanation, which the sources never state and which is the one that actually destroyed a
man:** that the **tester** was at fault — dishonest or incompetent. It is the residue left when the
other three fail, and it is what the January 1925 petition, the arbitration and the dismissal by letter
amount to. **It was never written down**, which is precisely Ansel Keddie's own diagnosis of his
position: *"a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."* It is defeated by Junia Ammon's
observation that the tester's office was **not to hold the glass but to deliver it** — no tester can
detect a mis-graduated pipette by using it — and by the fact that the error persisted unchanged under
him for twenty-eight years and ended not when he left in 1925 but when the glass was swapped in 1923.

## Subsidiary question 1: did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?

**No — and the sources are strong enough to say so, not merely to suspect it.**

The reasoning is physical, not testimonial. Strawn's condemned crate went into the **loft of the Ostrey
Hollow station in June 1923** and was still there, undisturbed, when **Ivy Keddie climbed the ladder in
February 1958** and found it. Nothing between those dates moved it. If the board had examined the glass
in 1925, the case would have ended in 1925; instead it ended thirty-three years later, on the strength
of that same crate, in Bulletin 471. Neither Falke's report nor Nym's minute book records any
examination.

**And the detail that makes it unbearable:** two of the board's sittings, in April 1925, were held in
**the weigh room of the Ostrey Hollow station itself**, which is the room the loft sits over. **The men
deciding whether Ansel Keddie had cheated the valley sat, twice, directly beneath the evidence that
would have cleared him.** Ivy told the Chronicle in 1958, *"It would have been a kindness if somebody in
this county had thought to climb a ladder."* She did not know that a board of arbitration had sat under
that ladder for two days. **No single source states this; it falls out of putting R24 beside R19 and
R22, and I mark it as an inference from three documents rather than a quoted fact.**

## Subsidiary question 2: what did the tester himself believe?

**That it was the glass.** In the **summer of 1911**, during or just after the nine weeks on borrowed
apparatus, Ansel Keddie said it was **"something in the glass"** — said it **twice**, at supper and
again by the door — and **wrote a question mark in the station book**. The source is **Duncan Keddie
(R18, 1968)**, who was seventeen, in the kitchen, and who left home to teach at Ashlin that October,
within months of hearing it. That is why the memory kept.

Three things sharpen it:

- **He had grounds, not a hunch.** He had run that station on Larrow Green's glass through 1896–97 and
  knew what its milk read on borrowed glass. When the 1911 borrowing pushed the tests up, he was
  comparing against his own first season. This is a reasoned inference from experience, and it is why he
  said it twice.
- **He knew from 1902 where the blame would land.** *"It is not your cows and not your hands," she said;
  "no, but it is my figures they will look at."*
- **He knew he could not be cleared.** The one sentence he ever said to his wife about it: *"a man
  cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."*

**So Ivy's "I would like it printed that he never knew" is false.** He knew, or suspected correctly,
from 1911, and understood his own trap completely: the right hypothesis, no proof, and a charge nobody
would state. **In 1926 an unnamed gentleman put exactly that objection — that the tests rose in 1911 on
borrowed apparatus — to Kepp, and Kepp talked him down.** Ansel and Duncan are the natural candidates
for that gentleman; **neither is proven**, and the one contemporaneous household record of 1926 has had
its leaves torn out.

# Section G — Summary (G1)

Between 1896 and 1958 the farmers of Ostrey Hollow, in the Larrow Valley, were underpaid for their milk
because the testing glass their skimming station bought in 1897 was mis-graduated and read about
nineteen hundredths of a point low on every sample. The valley blamed its cows, then its hay, then the
farmers for watering the milk, and finally the tester, Ansel Keddie, who was dismissed by letter in 1925
after twenty-nine seasons and died uncleared in 1934. His granddaughter found the condemned glass in the
station loft in 1958; Bulletin 471 cleared him. It takes eight records because no single witness held
both ends: the office kept the invoices, the inspector kept the condemnation, the wife kept the dates,
the son kept the one remark that named the cause, and only after 1958 could any of them be read against
the others.
