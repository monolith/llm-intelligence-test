# Batch 2 — Answer Sheet

**Evidence position (one note, then I stop apologizing for it).** I am answering from the end of a
relay of retention notes, not from the retellings themselves. Full texts of R19 (the 1958
Chronicle), R20 (Junia Ammon's 1949 firm history), R22 (Falke's 1959 county report) and R23
(Bertram Kyle's 1953 memoir) were cut out of the hand-offs; only fragments quoted inside later
commentary survive, and Kyle's "five and a quarter per cent" section and the associated arithmetic
lock were lost entirely. Where a question turns on something that was in those lost pages, I say
so rather than manufacture it. R24 — Orra Keddie's diary, published 1964 — survives complete and
is the only contemporaneous domestic record; I lean on it hard, and say when I do.

## Section A — Reconstruction: stories 4, 5 and 6 of the eight

*Assumption, stated once: I take Batch 1's first three to have been (1) the founding of the Ostrey
Hollow skimming station and the arrival of its own glass, 1896–97; (2) the complaints and the 1908
herd inquiry; (3) the broken measure of 1911. What follows is what comes next, in the order it
happened.*

### 1. The Circular Over the Manager's Name — June 1919

By 1919 the blame had walked a long way. It had started with the cows (talk at the store as early
as March 1902 — "our milk up here runs poorer than theirs down the valley"), moved to the wet hay
of Hessel Bottom on the 1908 committee's verdict, and in June 1919 arrived at the farmers
themselves. A circular came up to the station for the patrons, over the **manager's name**, about
**water in the milk**. It was not Ansel Keddie's document; he was the deliveryman for an
accusation the office made. In the same year the Association reprinted Nyle Grigg's 1908 wet-hay
finding — two explanations pushed at once, neither of them the station.

Ansel would not put the circulars in the mail and would not leave them on the platform. He carried
them by hand to **eleven families**, stood at the door while each man read it, and came home at
eleven at night with his hat still on.

**The counts, resolved.** Three numbers circulate: **six farms** (1908 report twice, Grigg's
letter, Kepp, Hazel Brant, the Chronicle — but several of those are re-reporting one 1908
document, so call it two or three genuinely independent lines, all counting *farms*); **nine
families** (Selby Vose's letter, R08); **eleven families** (Orra Keddie, written the same night by
the wife of the man who carried them). I resolve it by asking what each number counts, not by
majority: six is the farm count and is solid; eleven is the number of doors Ansel physically stood
at, from the only eyewitness source; nine is most plausibly the patrons on the Association's
books. Six farms, eleven households, nine booked patrons is a shape that fits all three. I do not
claim it as proven.

**The cost.** Two days later Orra's brother **Emmet** — biggest herd in Hessel Bottom, who had
hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened on 4 May 1896 — came up, would not come
into the house, and stood in the yard: why had Ansel carried the paper himself if he did not
believe it? Ansel: "he carried it because he would not have another man carry it." Emmet never
entered the house again. The break ran **ten years**, to Emmet's death in spring 1929. He signed
the January 1925 petition against the station.

**Documents:** R24 (Orra, contemporaneous); R08 (Vose); R19 (Chronicle); R17 (Hazel Brant, 1981 —
possibly of Emmet's family, unproven).

### 2. A Broken Window: Strawn Condemns the Glass — 12 June 1923

On **12 June 1923** the inspector **Merle Strawn** condemned the Ostrey Hollow station's testing
glass. This is the moment the case could have ended twenty-six years after it began and
thirty-five years before it did, and nothing whatever happened.

Four separate silences, from four independent quarters, all on the same event: **the inspector
himself drew no inference whatever** (Falke, R22 ¶11); **the office** said no more of it "than
there would have been of a broken window" (Kyle, R23, writing in 1953, before anyone knew the
answer); **the tester's own kitchen** filed it as household news — Orra's entry of July 1923 says
"they have new glass at the station and new pails, and the roof is still bad, which is the
Association all over"; and **the Association** never connected it to the complaint it had been
fighting since about 1900. A replacement order went to the supply house at **Tarnet**; the new
glass and new pails were in by July 1923, which Orra records one month after the condemnation and
therefore independently confirms.

The condemned crate went up the ladder into the station **loft** and stayed there from 1923 until
**February 1958** — thirty-five years — where Ivy Keddie eventually found it.

**The quantities that matter.** The glass had then been in service since **1 May 1897**: **26
years and six weeks**, not the twenty-eight years the record loosely repeats. 1923 − 1897 = 26;
the "twenty-eight" is the 1897→1925 span, to the arbitration, and does not survive subtraction.
When the crate was finally measured, the pipettes ran short of the standard **17.6 cc**, delivering
about **16.72 cc** — five per cent light, which is the **0.19** of butterfat that Hazel Brant
called "nineteen hundredths, flat as a floor."

**Why it matters more than anything except 1911:** after 12 June 1923 the physical proof of Ansel
Keddie's innocence existed, in one crate, in one loft, in the building where he worked. Every
document written after that date was written in a county that already owned the answer.

**Documents:** R22 ¶11 (Falke); R23 (Kyle); R24 (Orra, July 1923); R04/R19 for the 1958 recovery.

### 3. The Board of Men — January to 4 May 1925

In **January 1925** the patrons put in a paper against the station — a petition, Emmet's name on
it. Orra: "I do not blame him and I will not say so to A."

A **board of arbitration** sat at Ashlin from **17 March to 4 May 1925**, presided over by **Alonzo
Frick**, minuted by **Nym** at Item 7 of his minute book. Ansel went down to Ashlin on the opening
day in his good coat — he had had it since Duncan was married — up at three and not asleep before
that; Orra ironed the coat twice for want of anything she could do. She never uses the word
arbitration and never names Frick: "There is a board of men to hear the whole thing out." Nobody
explained the machinery to the tester's wife.

**The fact only Orra has.** In **April 1925 two of the sittings were held at the station itself, in
the weigh room**, with the men standing outside in the road. The board therefore sat twice in the
room where the tests were made — directly beneath the loft holding Strawn's condemned crate. That
conjunction is not stated in any source; it falls out of setting R24 beside R22 and R19, and it is
the sharpest fact in the case. Ivy Keddie told the Chronicle in 1958 that "it would have been a
kindness if somebody in this county had thought to climb a ladder." She did not know a board of
arbitration had sat under that ladder for two days.

On **30 April 1925**, four days before the award, the Association **dismissed Ansel by letter**
after **twenty-nine seasons** (1896 through 1924 inclusive — the figure is confirmed twice inside
the diary and checks exactly against the station's 4 May 1896 opening). He came up the road at his
ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm, hung it behind the shed door, and asked what
there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it.

On **4 May 1925** the board awarded the patrons **$3,800, "without admission."**

**The award, resolved.** Three independent sources give $3,800 — Falke's 1959 county report, Kyle's
1953 memoir, the 1958 Chronicle. One, Cleve Oram in 1977, gives $3,500. Three to one, and the
dissenter is the latest and most distant; take **$3,800**. Orra's diary is *silent* on the award
(the editor flags the silence), so it is not a fourth vote either way.

**Documents:** R22 (Falke); R13 (Nym, Item 7); R23 (Kyle); R19 (Chronicle); R21 (Oram, dissenting);
R24 (Orra, contemporaneous, and sole source for the weigh-room sittings and the letter).

## Section C — Math

### C3. Butterfat credited vs butterfat delivered, over the 25 delivery years

**Inputs, with their provenance:**
- 40,000,000 lb of milk over the period, at 1,600,000 lb/year — Bertram Kyle, 1953 (a pre-answer
  document, which is why it is worth most).
- 0.19 — the size of the test discrepancy, in percentage points of butterfat ("nineteen hundredths,
  flat as a floor," Hazel Brant, R17).
- 3.78 — I take this to be the true average butterfat test of the Ostrey Hollow milk. **This is an
  inference.** The figure survives in my notes only as "a figure in the same quantitative cluster";
  I identify it as the true average because three otherwise unrelated numbers then close on each
  other (below).
- $0.25 per pound of butterfat, the price used in the money computation.

**(a) Credited to the patrons:** at 3.78 − 0.19 = **3.59%**

  40,000,000 × 0.0359 = **1,436,000 lb of butterfat credited**

**(b) Actually delivered in the station's cream to the main creamery:** at the true 3.78%

  40,000,000 × 0.0378 = **1,512,000 lb of butterfat**

  (I treat fat received at the station as fat shipped in the cream. No source gives a skimming
  loss; and the "station gain" line in D3 is precisely the Association's own statement that the
  creamery kept receiving fat the station had not credited.)

**(c) The difference:** 1,512,000 − 1,436,000 = **76,000 lb of butterfat**

  - As a percentage **of the fat actually delivered (b)**: 76,000 ÷ 1,512,000 = **5.03%**
  - As a percentage **of the fat credited to the patrons (a)**: 76,000 ÷ 1,436,000 = **5.29%**

  These are different numbers for the same shortfall, which is why the question asks. The honest
  one-line statement is: **the tests ran 5.0% low** — the patrons were credited with 95% of the fat
  they delivered. Said the other way, they were owed **5¼% more than they were paid**, and I note
  that Kyle's lost section was headed "five and a quarter per cent," which is the
  percentage-of-credited figure. That is a reconstruction from the section's title, not a quotation.

**Three-way check that the inference holds:**
1. 76,000 lb × $0.25 = **$19,000** — exactly Ivy Keddie's figure, arrived at independently.
2. 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly — a pipette five per cent light.
3. 3.78 × 0.05 = 0.189 ≈ **0.19** — Hazel Brant's discrepancy.

Volume, money and test all reduce to the same 5%. That is what convinces me 3.78 is the true test.
⚠ The chain also carries a **$19,600** variant of Ivy's figure, unresolved; it would need about
78,400 lb, and nothing I have explains where the extra 2,400 lb comes from.

### C4. The invoice for the station's own testing outfit

**(a) Total: probably $77.39 — not confirmed.** The invoice is dated **14 April 1897** and was
reproduced in facsimile in Junia Ammon's 1949 firm history. The figure **$77.39** comes from that
same document; the sentence containing it was cut off mid-quotation in the hand-off, and the
chain's own note is that "its meaning did not reach the chain." An invoice total is much the most
natural thing for a dollars-and-cents figure in a supply firm's history of that transaction to be,
and $77.39 is plausible for a Babcock outfit in 1897 — but I am reading a caption I cannot see.
**Treat as probable, not settled.**

**(b) How many pipettes: cannot be determined from the sources that reached me.** A pipette count
existed somewhere in the material — it is listed among the quantities R24 does *not* contain,
which means some other retelling did — but it was lost with the tails of R19/R20/R22/R23. I will
not invent one.

**By how much each was short: about 0.88 cc.** Standard Babcock milk pipette = **17.6 cc**; the
condemned pipettes measured **16.72 cc**; 17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88 cc**, i.e. 5.0% light. The record
also carries **16.27**, which I believe is a digit transposition of 16.72 by some later narrator:
16.27 would be 1.33 cc short, 7.6% light, and would produce a discrepancy of 0.29 rather than the
0.19 that three independent sources support. **Arithmetic decides it: 16.72 is right.**

### C5. Two spans, and the first season

**(a) How long the faulty glass was in use: 26 years and about six weeks.**

  1 May 1897 (the station's own glass arrives) to 12 June 1923 (Strawn condemns it).
  1923 − 1897 = 26; May to June adds six weeks. In delivery seasons: 1897 through 1922 complete,
  plus part of 1923.

  ⚠ The record repeatedly says "twenty-eight years" for this span. **It does not subtract.**
  Twenty-eight is the distance from 1897 to the 1925 arbitration, and I think that is where the
  figure came from. I correct it to 26.

**How many full delivery years the money runs over: 25.**

  Kyle's own figures force it: 40,000,000 lb ÷ 1,600,000 lb/year = **exactly 25 years**. That
  matches the Chronicle's "at every annual meeting for a quarter of a century," i.e. roughly
  1900–1925.

**Why they are different numbers.** They measure different things. Twenty-six years is a fact about
a piece of glass; twenty-five years is a fact about a *grievance*. The money was computed over the
span the patrons had been complaining — from about 1900, when the trouble became talk at the store,
to the 1925 award — not over the physical life of the instrument. The two windows are offset at
both ends: the calculation **omits** roughly 1897–1900, when the glass was already short and nobody
was yet objecting, and **includes** roughly mid-1923–1925, when the glass had already been
replaced and no loss was occurring. Those two errors run in opposite directions and roughly cancel,
which is presumably why nobody caught either. **The arithmetic is internally consistent at 25
years; do not "fix" it to 26.** (Note also that neither figure is Ansel's service, which was 29
seasons, 1896–1924. Three different spans, routinely confused.)

**(b) Pounds of milk received in the first season: cannot be determined from the sources.**

The only poundage figures anywhere in the material are Kyle's 40,000,000 lb total and his
1,600,000 lb/year, and the second of those is an average across the 25-year money window, not a
year-by-year series. The first season is additionally outside that window: the station opened
**4 May 1896**, so 1896 was a part-season, and February-ice-storm entries like 1921's ("the station
road impassable two days and the milk stood") show that annual receipts moved with the weather
anyway. All I can say with support is that the first season was a partial one and would therefore
have run **below** 1,600,000 lb. Anything more precise would be a fabrication.

## Section D — Logic

### D1. The nine weeks of 1911, read as an experiment

**When and how long.** The station's own measuring glass broke on **8 May 1911** — Orra records
Ansel coming home "cross about a broken measure" that day, which is a contemporaneous anchor
independent of everyone's later memory. The station ran on glass borrowed from Larrow Green until
its own glass resumed on **10 July 1911**: **nine weeks**.

**Variable and constants.** The only thing changed was the apparatus — the station's own measure
out, Larrow Green's in, and back again. Held constant: the same herds and the same cows, the same
patrons, the same wet hay of the same bottom, the same building, the same procedure, and above all
the same tester, Ansel Keddie, doing the work in his own hand throughout.

**Result.** During the nine weeks the tests ran **markedly higher**; when the station's own glass
came back on 10 July the tests **fell back the same day**. On/off/on, with a same-day return — the
cleanest structure a field experiment can have.

**What it establishes that a calculation cannot.** A ledger calculation can show only that a
discrepancy exists; it cannot say what caused it, and it can always be answered with "your cows,
your hay, your honesty." The swap is an *intervention*. It moves one thing and watches the number
follow, which locates the fault in the instrument and rules out every explanation that lives in the
herd, the feed or the men — in 1911, forty-seven years before anybody put a graduate to the glass.
It is also the reason Ansel's hypothesis was reasoning and not a lucky guess: he had run this
station on Larrow Green's glass once before, through its entire first year, May 1896 to 1 May 1897,
so he was comparing the borrowed reading against his own first season. That is why he said it was
something in the glass, said it twice, and put a question mark in the station book.

**What it does not establish.** (i) No quantity — nothing in the nine weeks says the glass was
0.19 low or 0.88 cc short; magnitude waited for 1958. (ii) It does not identify *which* article was
at fault — pipette, test bottle, or the reading of either. (iii) It does not prove the defect had
existed since 1897; strictly it proves a defect existed in May 1911. (iv) It cannot formally
exclude a coincident change in feed or season across those weeks — though the same-day fall-back on
10 July makes that reading very hard to hold. (v) It says nothing about anyone's intent, and it
proves nothing about the borrowed glass's own accuracy beyond the fact that it disagreed. (vi) One
station, one summer, no controls, no written protocol: it convinces, but it was never designed to
convince anyone, which is exactly why it convinced nobody in 1926.

### D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream

**Reason 1 — the direction of the discrepancy is wrong for theft (the ledgers).** Cream stolen at a
skimming station has to be missing from somewhere. It is not. The fat the creamery received in the
station's cream ran consistently *above* what the station had credited its patrons — a "station
gain," year after year, always the same sign, about five per cent. Theft produces a shortfall at
the receiving end; this produced a surplus. Whatever was happening at Ostrey Hollow, nothing was
leaving with anybody.

**Reason 2 — the 1911 swap (Duncan Keddie, R18, 1968, anchored by Orra's diary).** For nine weeks
the tester was the same man in the same room and only the glass changed, and the tests went up;
they fell again the day his own glass returned. A thief's arithmetic does not improve when you hand
him a different pipette.

**Reason 3 — an independent inspector condemned the instrument (Falke, R22 ¶11, on Strawn's 12
June 1923 condemnation).** A state or county inspector with no stake in the quarrel found the glass
defective — and, tellingly, "drew no inference whatever," which means he was not building a case
for anyone. The fault was documented as a property of the apparatus by a man who did not care whose
side it helped.

**Reason 4 — conduct (Orra Keddie, R24).** He hand-carried the water circular to eleven doors and
stood there while each man read it, rather than mail it. He never once was late in nearly thirty
years. He took the dismissal without a word and went to carpentry at a quarter of the pay. This is
character evidence and I rank it last, but it is not nothing.

**Why these are independent.** They come from different decades (1911, 1923, 1919–25, and the
ledger series running throughout), different observers (a son in a kitchen, a county inspector, a
farm wife, the Association's own books), and different *kinds* of evidence (an experiment, a
regulatory finding, an accounting identity, behavior). None of them derives from any other, and no
two of them descend from a common informant.

**Which survives if the glass had never been measured in 1958.** Reasons 1, 2 and 3 all do. Reason
1 is the strongest of these, because it needs no instrument at all — only the Association's own
sheets, which existed the whole time. Reason 2 is next, and it is the older of the two: the 1911
interval clears him on evidence that was complete forty-seven years before Ivy Keddie climbed the
ladder.

**Did the Association's bookkeeper ever question those ledger entries?** From what survives to me:
**no — nobody in the office ever put the question, before 1958.** I must be careful here, because
the surviving notes do not name a bookkeeper as the author of the accusation and the pages that
would settle it were lost; what I can say is that the office's own recorded response to the one
event that should have forced the question — the 1923 condemnation — was that there was "no more
said of it in the office than there would have been of a broken window," and that line is Bertram
Kyle's, the Association's clerk, writing in 1953. The man in the office who held the figures
recorded the office's indifference and did not himself draw the inference; his 1953 poundages and
his "five and a quarter per cent" are the very numbers that later cleared Ansel Keddie. He supplied
the refutation and never read it. **If the question assumes an accusation made specifically by that
bookkeeper, I cannot confirm the accusation from what reached me, and I decline to invent it.**

### D3. Did the manager know the tests ran low?

**Split the question, because the two halves have different answers.**

**Did he know the station's tests ran low? Yes, certainly, and it does not depend on the ledger at
all.** In June 1919 a circular went to the Ostrey Hollow patrons **over the manager's name**
alleging **water in the milk**. That accusation is only intelligible as an explanation for tests
that ran low; a manager who did not know the tests ran low has nothing to explain and no reason to
sign such a paper. Add that the complaint had been made at every annual meeting for about a quarter
of a century, that the office commissioned the 1908 inquiry into the Hessel Bottom herds, and that
the Association reprinted Grigg's wet-hay finding in 1919 — the office was actively supplying
theories for the low tests. It knew.

**Did he know the tests were low *because the station's glass was short*? Not established — and
probably not.** No source that reached me records the manager being told, being asked, or
answering. He is unnamed throughout. What can be said is that the information sufficient to catch
it passed under his signature every year: the "station gain" line is the arithmetic footprint of a
five per cent under-credit, it appeared with the same sign annually, and he signed it. That
demonstrates **opportunity and possession of the evidence, not knowledge of what it meant.**

**And there is a reason not to over-read the signature into guilt.** A station gain flatters the
Association's books — the creamery took delivery of fat it had not paid the patrons for. Nobody in
that office had any incentive to interrogate a line that ran in their favor, and the record shows a
body that reliably did not interrogate anything: the 1908 committee looked at the barns and not the
station, the 1923 inspector drew no inference whatever, the office filed a condemned testing outfit
as a broken window, and the roof was never mended before it fell in. Indifference explains the
signatures at least as well as knowledge does, and the sources do not let me choose between them.

**Verdict:** he knew the tests ran low, on his own signed circular of 1919. Whether he knew, before
12 June 1923, that the cause was the glass — **cannot be determined from the sources.** The
documents that might have decided it (Falke's full 1959 report and Kyle's full 1953 memoir,
including the "five and a quarter per cent" section) did not survive intact into this account.
