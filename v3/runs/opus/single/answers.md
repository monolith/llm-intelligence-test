# Answer sheet — Part 1

## Section A — Reconstruction (stories 1–4)

*My eight-story partition: (1) the Association and Article VII; (2) the station's opening and the
Ammon & Sons glass; (3) Ansel Keddie's twenty-nine seasons at the bench; (4) the complaint and the
hay verdict; (5) the station-gain line in the ledgers; (6) the two accidental experiments — 1911 and
Alder Corners; (7) the 1919 circular, the 1925 arbitration and the dismissal; (8) the 1923
condemnation, the 1958 discovery, and where the books went.*

---

### 1. The Rule That Made a Figure Into Money (1889–1892)

**Who.** The farmers of the Larrow Valley, Ordell County; Gideon Vose, their first president; Effie
Loomis, secretary 1901–1940, who is the record's own custodian and its most honest witness about
what the record cannot do.

**What happened.** In 1889 the farmers met at Larrow Green — in the schoolhouse, in cold weather,
late into the night — and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, because
they were tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking what the merchant allowed. Gideon Vose was
elected president and held the office twenty-two years, to 1911; he died in 1917. In 1891 they
built the creamery at Larrow Green on the creek, and the valley began to be paid in cash once a
month. Before it there was no common test and no common price; every man's arithmetic was his own.

Vose wrote the by-laws himself, at his kitchen table, not being a lawyer. The seventh article is
the hinge of everything that follows: *"Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers
according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not
according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."*
The rule is just — it pays a man for what he brings and not for the water naturally in it. But the
last clause is the one nobody quotes: not the creamery's test, not the valley's average, not a
test made over again anywhere else. The station's test, and no appeal from it. Under payment by
weight, nothing in the seven stories that follow would have cost any man a dollar.

**When.** Organized 1889; creamery 1891; Article VII adopted at an annual meeting whose year
**cannot be determined from the sources**: Loomis says 1892, Rundle 1891, Grigg 1893, the printed
pamphlet carries no year at all, and the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the office move of
1938 — carried out with the rest and never arriving. Loomis, the one witness with authority, says
plainly she was not in office until 1901 and that the papers of exactly those years are the lost
ones.

**Quantities.** Twenty-two years of Gideon Vose's presidency; a fifty-year Association; one
article, seven miles of road, and one clause of about twelve words that decided a quarter of a
century of pay.

**Documents.** The printed by-laws (undated on their face); Loomis's *Notes toward a history*; the
county agent's 1959 report, which declines to state the adoption year for exactly the right
reason.

---

### 2. Seventy-Seven Dollars' Worth of Wrong Glass (1895–1897)

**Who.** The Association; Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green from 1893; Ammon & Sons, Dairy
Supply, of Tarnet, and Junia Ammon, the firm's own historian.

**What happened.** In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station seven miles up
the valley at Ostrey Hollow, to serve the farms at the head of it and Hessel Bottom principally.
Ansel Keddie went up in 1895 to get it ready. It opened on 4 May 1896 — and its own testing outfit
had not come. Sill made one up out of his own glass at his own bench at Larrow Green and sent it up
the valley on the wagon, and the station opened on it. That first part-season the station's average
test was **3.80**.

On 14 April 1897 Ammon & Sons invoiced the Association for the station's own outfit: 6 doz. milk
test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at $.35 = $25.20; 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at $.22 =
$31.68; 1 acid measure, $.90; 1 hand tester, 4-bottle, $18.00; freight $2.15. **Total $77.93.**
The outfit went into use at the station on **1 May 1897**, and Sill's borrowed glass went back down
the valley with one piece chipped.

Every one of those seventy-two pipettes was blown on the stem at 17.6 c.c. and delivered about
**16.72** — short 0.88 c.c., exactly 95 hundredths of its mark. Junia Ammon's reconstruction, set
down in 1949 with the firm's own bad morning printed beside its good ones, is that the mold had
been reground and the lot ran short: a gauge is knocked off its nail and hung back on, a boy checks
the first piece and the tenth and the fiftieth and finds them consistent — consistently wrong,
which is the one fault that kind of check cannot catch. The works books that would have settled it
burned in 1931.

**When.** Station built 1895–96, opened 4 May 1896 on borrowed glass; own glass in use 1 May 1897.

**Quantities.** $77.93 for the whole outfit; 72 pipettes at 35 cents; 17.6 c.c. blown, 16.72
delivered; 0.88 c.c. short; a 5 per cent error that ran twenty-six years and cost $19,000.

**Documents.** The invoice of 14 April 1897 (surviving in two independent custodies — the
Association's office file and Ammon's own letter book); the office file's note that the outfit was
in use from 1 May 1897; the test book's first leaf, "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own
comes"; Ammon & Sons' letter of 2 July 1923.

*Correction made:* Vaught's method section and the clerk Kyle both date the station's own glass to
1 May 1898; the invoice, Orra Keddie's diary entry of 1 May 1897, and Bulletin 471's own opening
date defeat them. Vaught and Cleve Oram both say five dozen pipettes; the invoice line prices six
dozen at 35 cents to $25.20 and no other count sums. The *Chronicle* and Ammon's own prose both
say $77.39; her reproduced invoice adds to $77.93.

### 3. Twenty-Nine Seasons at the Slope Desk (1896–1925)

**Who.** Ansel Keddie, born 1861, tester and separator man at Ostrey Hollow from the day it opened;
his wife Orra, whose diary is the household's record; their son Duncan, schoolmaster at Ashlin.

**What happened.** Keddie worked alone. The day began at half past five; the wagons came up in the
dark most of the year and he had the intake room open, the lamps lit and the scale swinging free
before the first of them. Every can was weighed and the weight called and written before the milk
went anywhere. From each patron's milk he drew a sample into that patron's own numbered jar; the
jar was made up over ten days, then set in warm water until it was blood-warm through, and poured
from vessel to vessel until the risen cream was carried down through the whole of it. Then the
charge was drawn up in the measure to the mark, the tip wiped, the charge blown into the bottle
carrying the patron's number; acid down the side; four bottles in the hand tester, weighted against
each other; the crank turned at the speed he was taught and no faster; the fat read off in per cent
against the rulings with a pair of dividers.

He kept the book in five columns — date, patron, pounds, test, fat — in one hand for twenty-nine
seasons, 1896 through 1924, thirty-one tests a week. Where he made an error he struck it through
and set his initials against the line, and he never wrote a figure over a figure, because a figure
written over another is a figure no man can swear to afterward. The state's inspector stood at his
elbow through whole tests on visit after visit and had nothing to note.

The summary leaf he made up at the back in 1922, because he had been asked so often: *"Average test
of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three
hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when
it stood at 3.80."* That leaf is, unwittingly, the whole solution: 3.80 on borrowed glass, a broken
year when the glass changed, 3.61 forever after on his own.

**When.** 1896–1924 at the bench; he did not test a pound in 1925; dismissed 30 April 1925; kept
four or five cows and did carpentry until he died in 1934.

**Quantities.** 29 seasons; 31 tests a week; 3.80 / part year / 3.61; a gap of 0.19 of a point,
flat in July and February alike, wet years and dry.

**Documents.** The station test book (first leaf 4 May 1896, summary leaf 1922, the two 1911
entries and the question mark); his nine-sentence statement to the board of 17 March 1925; his
unsent letter of that spring; Orra Keddie's diary, 1897–1930.

*Correction made:* his unsent letter says the first glass was borrowed "off the Ashlin creamery";
his own first leaf of 1896 says Larrow Green, and Sill says he lent it — the contemporaneous
document beats the twenty-nine-year-old recollection. Teague and Frick both put the station average
at 3.16; the summary leaf says 3.61, the ledger arithmetic requires 3.61, and Frick heard his
figure from Teague, so the two are one source and not two.

### 4. The Hay That Was Blamed, and the Bulletin That Was Misquoted (1902–1926)

**Who.** The patrons of Ostrey Hollow, first among them Emmet Brant; Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green,
chairman of the 1908 investigating committee; Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S., of Ashlin.

**What happened.** From 1902 the men of the Hollow said out loud that their milk tested below the
same class of milk at Larrow Green. Emmet Brant was one of the first, and they were laughed at. The
complaint was renewed in every decade after. In January 1908 the Association named a committee under
Grigg — chosen because he hauled to Larrow Green and had no interest in the answer either way. They
sampled the Hessel Bottom herds through **January, February and March 1908**, visited each of the
six farms three and four times, drew and sealed their own samples, watched the milking and the
hauling and the testing, tried the station scales against a known weight and the patrons' scales
against the station's, and inquired into every barn's water. They reported that the milk was whole,
the scales honest, and no dishonesty of any kind in any quarter — and that *"the wet hay of that
bottom is what thins the cream, and the remedy lies with the land and not with the station."* The
report was accepted and closed the question for eleven years.

In 1919, with the complaint reopened, Grigg reprinted it with a covering letter claiming two things
his own report does not support: that they had sampled "every month of that year," and that the
State Agricultural College's Bulletin No. 214 (1911) had found bog hay to depress the test "by two
tenths in the winter feeding." In 1926 Dr. Kepp read a paper to the Ordell County Farm Club making
the same case with the same misquotation, adding his own field impression of "a fifth of a point"
and putting the station's gap at 0.21, and dismissing the 1911 interval as "six weeks of summer
figures."

**What Bulletin 214 actually says**, quoted verbatim by four separate sources: *"Bog hay fed as the
whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per
cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."*

**When.** Complaints from 1902; committee January–March 1908, reported 1908; Bulletin 214 published
1911; reprint and covering letter 1919; Kepp 1926; county agent Falke conceded the hay in 1959 and
withdrew the concession the same year on actually obtaining the bulletin.

**Quantities.** 0.05 of a point in winter and nothing in summer, against a standing gap of 0.19 in
every month — four times too small and the wrong shape. Six farms on 160 acres of wet meadow; three
committeemen; three months' sampling stretched in the retelling to twelve.

**Documents.** The 1908 report (fair copy, signed); Grigg's 1919 covering letter; Bulletin No. 214;
Kepp's paper and the discussion from the floor; the Alder Corners figures, which finish it.

*Correction made:* Grigg and Kepp both carry "two tenths." Four verbatim reproductions of the
bulletin's own summary paragraph break the tie, and neither man had read it — both cite it in
support of a conclusion already held.

### 5. The Answer in the Association's Own Ledger (1898–1939)

**Who.** Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945; Hobart Sill, buttermaker, who weighed the cream in;
Bertram Kyle, the outer-desk clerk who copied the line for twenty-five years; Selby Vose, manager
1913–1938, who signed the sheet every spring; Dell Rundle, schoolmaster, who read the ledgers by
accident in 1939.

**What happened.** The station bought milk on its own test and sent cream down the valley. Teague,
on coming in in 1905, set the two against each other in the station account: on one side the
butterfat credited to the patrons on the station's test, on the other the butterfat actually
weighed and tested into the vats at Larrow Green out of the station's cream. The second was always
larger. He entered the difference as "station gain" and carried it to the general account, because
a difference that will not be entered somewhere comes back at audit. The typical entry, in the same
terms and the same hand every year:

> *Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at
> Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as
> usual. Carried to the general account.*

That is one average year of the affair written out in three figures. The station's books said it had
bought 57,760 lb of fat; 60,800 lb came down the hill. The missing 3,040 lb had never gone anywhere:
it had never been credited in the first place, because the pipette read 95 per cent of the truth.

Nobody read it. Kyle copied it for a quarter of a century and says why: *"A figure with a name on
it has been explained, and you rule under it and go on to the next."* Teague says evenness stops a
bookkeeper looking — a figure that jumps keeps you awake; five and a quarter every year is
furniture — and the words "as usual" got into the entry and stayed. Sill, independently, weighed the
Hollow's cream over again himself into the cold room for forty-one years and told anyone who would
listen that it ran heavy, always more, never less, and nobody ever asked him about it. Teague put
the account to Vose once, in 1916, and was told to let it alone, and let it alone. Vose signed the
sheet annually; Kyle watched him put his thumb on the bottom figure and sign.

In 1939 Rundle, allowed to look at the ledgers on another errand while his jubilee pamphlet was in
type, printed a note going back on his own Chapter IV: cream stolen at the Hollow would have
reached the Green *light*; it reached the Green *heavy*, every year.

**When.** The line runs annually from 1898; Teague's device from 1905; the 1916 approach to Vose;
Rundle's retraction 1939; Bulletin 471's conclusion in 1958 points straight back at it.

**Quantities.** Over the twenty-five full delivery years 1898–1922: 40,000,000 lb of milk; 1,444,000
lb of fat credited; 1,520,000 lb received; **76,000 lb** of fat difference, worth **$19,000** at the
period's average of 25 cents. That is 5.00 per cent of the fat delivered and 5.26 ("five and a
quarter") per cent of the fat credited — both right, of different denominators.

**Documents.** The Association's annual ledgers, station account; the Larrow Green receiving and
churn books in Sill's hand from 1893; Teague's memoir; Kyle's account of the office; Rundle's note
added while in press.

*Correction made:* Teague's memoir says 44,000,000 lb and Rundle repeats it; three independent
readers of the same ledger (Loomis copying Teague's own annual sheets, Kyle who extracted the total
for the arbitration, and the curator reading the books in 1972) give 40,000,000, and 40,000,000 ÷
25 = 1,600,000 a year, which is exactly the specimen entry.

### 6. Two Experiments Nobody Knew They Were Running (1911; 1915–1930)

**Who.** Ansel Keddie, who ran the first without knowing it; Jerome Cudd of Alder Corners in Vessey
County, and his daughter Rosalie, who ran the second without meaning to; four Hessel Bottom men.

**What happened — the nine weeks.** On 8 May 1911 the station's own measure broke and a substitute
was used — borrowed, and brought up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon. From that morning the
station's average rose from 3.61 to 3.80 and stood there. On 10 July the station's own measure came
back into use and the tests fell the same day. Keddie entered both facts as they happened and set a
question mark against the second, and let the question mark stand fourteen years. Same patrons, same
herds, same pasture, same season, same acid, same bottles, same man at the same crank: one object
changed. His son says he came home that summer saying it was "something in the glass," said it twice
in one evening, and would not write down what he could not prove.

**What happened — over the ridge.** Jerome Cudd built a cheese factory at Alder Corners, on the far
side of Redlow Ridge in Vessey County, in 1913. From 1915, four of the six Hessel Bottom men hauled
to him in the summer flush — roughly 1 June to mid-September — and went back down to their own
station the rest of the year, because the ridge road is four miles of switchback that shuts in
November and does not open before April. They came for sixteen seasons; the last was 1930. Cudd
tested on his own bench with his own glass and never compared a figure with anybody, because it
never entered his head to. His note at the end of the summer of 1919: *"The four Hessel Bottom men,
summer of 1919: 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the
ridge."* The sixteen season averages sit between 3.78 and 3.82 and mean 3.80.

Rosalie Cudd adds the point that no ledger makes: watered milk cannot be hidden from a cheesemaker.
The curd comes soft and will not knit, the body is slack, the yield is down, and it tells him the
next morning. In sixteen summers her father never made one complaint of those four men and never
docked one of them a cent.

**When.** 8 May – 10 July 1911, nine weeks exactly; sixteen summers, 1915–1930.

**Quantities.** 3.61 → 3.80 → 3.61 across the nine weeks; 3.78–3.82 at Alder Corners, mean 3.80,
against the same men's 3.61 at home; a difference of about 0.19, present in the summer flush, when
the hay theory predicts none at all.

**Documents.** Keddie's two dated entries and the question mark; the Alder Corners test book (never
out of Vessey County; figures copied out for Ivy Keddie in 1958); Rosalie Cudd's letter of 1958 with
her table of sixteen seasons.

*Correction made:* Teague and Kepp both call the 1911 interval six weeks; 8 May to 10 July is
sixty-three days, nine weeks to the day. Roy Brant says the state inspector left his own measure
that June; the break was 8 May, six weeks before any June visit, and the inspector's own notebook
for 1911 records nothing lent — the borrowed measure came from Larrow Green.

### 7. The Circular, the Board, and the Man Put Out (1919–1934)

**Who.** Selby Vose, manager, son of Gideon; Ansel and Orra Keddie; Emmet Brant, the station's
largest patron and Orra's brother; Alonzo Frick, attorney of Ashlin, chairman of the board.

**What happened.** In June 1919, over the manager's signature, a circular went to the Ostrey Hollow
patrons warning them that added water would be looked for and dealt with. Kyle typed it; Vose signed
it and it went out over his name and no one else's; the state inspector, who had tested for added
water at that station on every visit and never found any, told him so. Keddie would not mail the
letters nor leave them on the platform. He carried them round himself, stood at each door while the
man read his, and came home at eleven at night with his hat still on. Two days later Emmet Brant
came up, would not come in, stood in the yard and asked why he had carried the paper himself if he
did not believe it. Keddie said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it.
Brother-in-law and brother-in-law did not speak again for ten years, until Emmet died in the spring
of 1929. Some families kept the letter in a drawer for forty years.

In January 1925 the patrons petitioned. A board of three was constituted under Frick — named
chairman, he supposed, because he was the one of the three who kept no cow. It sat three times: 17
March at the Association's office at Ashlin, and twice more, at Frick's own insistence, in the
station's own weigh room, so the board would see the plant instead of hearing it described. Three
explanations were argued: water, put by Vose without heat and without proof; theft by the tester,
insinuated by Teague in the manner of an accountant who wants a thing said without saying it; and
the hay, read in from the 1908 report. None was proved. Keddie made a nine-sentence statement: he
had tested as he was taught, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, he could not tell them
why the figures were what they were, and he had never taken a pound of cream out of that station.

On **30 April 1925** the Association dismissed him — four days before the board reported, with no
finding against him then or ever. On **4 May** the board found no cause established and awarded the
patrons **$3,800**, without admission, charged to the station account. He went home, kept four or
five cows on ground too small for them, hung barn doors that swung true for thirty years, and died
in 1934 with the imputation on him. At the burying, men who had not spoken to him in nine years said
what a hand he was with a door, and not one of them said the other thing.

**Quantities.** Nine families named in every later account (the diary says he called at eleven
doors); $3,800 awarded against a real loss of $19,000 — one fifth; the patrons' own 1925 reckoning
was 67,000 lb of fat; four exhibits before the board and nothing else.

**Documents.** The 1919 circular; the petition of January 1925; the board's minute book, 17 March –
4 May 1925, and its award; Keddie's statement; Frick's exhibit list; Orra Keddie's diary entries for
June 1919, March, April and June 1925.

*Correction made:* Toland and Oram both say $3,500; the award text, quoted twice from the minute
book, says $3,800. Toland and Rundle both call Selby Vose Gideon's nephew; Vose's own address says
"My father, who was your first president," and the secretary's minutes call him the son.

### 8. The Burette, the Loft, and Bulletin No. 471 (1921–1959)

**Who.** Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector on the Ordell circuit 1907–1936; Ivy Keddie, born 1931,
graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland and Ansel's granddaughter; Professor
Leland Vaught.

**What happened.** Strawn did four things at every plant, in the same order: scales against his
sealed weights; the lactometer for added water; boiler, water and cold room; and then he stood at
the tester's elbow through a whole test and did not let him hurry it. He never had a theory: *"I do
not keep an estimation, I keep a book."* In 1921 the state began issuing graduated burettes to field
men, in wooden cases, for checking the delivery of plant glassware; his came in the spring. In 1922
he did not run the Hollow's glass, the day being short and the road home what it was, and he wrote
down that he had not. On **12 June 1923** he did: *"Ran their milk pipettes against the burette.
These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the
manager to order new."* The Association ordered on 28 June; Ammon & Sons entered the order on 2
July; new glass was in use from July; in October he ran the new pipettes and they delivered their
mark; in 1924 he noted "their book gives the year at 3.80." He drew no inference at all about the
twenty-six years behind it, and the burette program was discontinued in 1926.

The condemned crate went up the ladder into the loft over the intake room and stayed there
thirty-five years, under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff, while two of the three sittings of the
1925 arbitration were held in the room twenty feet beneath it. In **February 1958** Ivy Keddie
climbed that ladder and brought it down. Nine pipettes were unbroken. On **9 March 1958**, at
Farland, she delivered each against a calibrated burette, five runs apiece: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72,
16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 — mean **16.72 c.c.** against 17.6 blown on the stems.
Short 0.88; ninety-five hundredths of the mark. She was the first person to bring five sets of
records into one room: the station test book, the Brant weigh book, the Alder Corners figures,
Strawn's circuit notebooks (at Farland since 1947, unasked-for eleven years), and the Association's
ledgers.

**Bulletin No. 471**, October 1958, by Vaught and Keddie, concludes: *"No milk was watered at Ostrey
Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the
station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in
twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the
Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."*

**Quantities.** 35 years from condemnation to explanation; 33 years from Keddie's dismissal; 24
years after his death; 9 pipettes; 16.72 against 17.6; 76,000 lb; $19,000.

**Documents.** Strawn's twenty-eight circuit notebooks and the photostat of the 1923 volume;
Keddie's measurement sheet of 9 March 1958; Bulletin No. 471; the *Ashlin Chronicle* of 3 October
1958; the county agent's 1959 report with its two printed corrections; the 1972 accession notes
recording where the five books and the crate finally came to rest.

*Correction made:* the *Chronicle* and Rundle's 1959 footnote both say eleven pipettes; the sheet
lists nine and the accession record counts nine. Vose and Rundle both date the condemnation 12 July
1923; Strawn's own field entry is 12 June. Vaught's method section gives the mean as 16.27 c.c. and
computes 118,000 lb from it, contradicting his own bulletin's "one part in twenty" and 76,000 lb —
he flags the irreconcilability himself and does not resolve it.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.**
The chain is stated by three people inside it and never broken: Ansel married Orra Brant in 1889;
their son was Duncan Keddie, who taught school at Ashlin; Duncan's daughter is Ivy, born 1931.
Duncan sets it out himself ("My daughter is Ivy... which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I
write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise"), and Ivy opens her own
1959 talk with "Ansel Keddie... was my grandfather. My father, Duncan Keddie... was his son." Two
sources point elsewhere: the *Ashlin Chronicle* of 3 October 1958 calls her his great-niece, and the
county agent's report of 1959 repeats it. Those two are not independent — Falke's paragraph 2 is
written off the college press notice the *Chronicle* also worked from — and Falke printed his own
correction on receipt of better information: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is
the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." Method: first-person
family testimony plus an explicit published retraction outrank a feature "put together in a day and
a half."

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins.**
Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie were brother and sister. Emmet's son Roy and Orra's son Duncan are
therefore first cousins — the children of a brother and a sister, which is the whole definition.
Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are the children of first cousins, which is second
cousins. Roy, Hazel and Duncan all derive it independently and all three complain about being
corrected; Rundle's 1959 footnote agrees. Vira Toland and Cleve Oram both say "first cousin once
removed"; both are repeating valley talk about a relationship they say they could not draw on paper,
and Hazel names that exact error and rejects it. What the sources leave open about the intervening
generation: **whether Orra was Emmet's full sister or his half sister.** Roy says full — "same
mother and same father, whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it."
Hazel, his own daughter, says half — "by his father's second wife." Duncan says only "brother and
sister," and "the younger of the two." No document survives to settle it: the diary's pages before
1889 were not preserved and nothing in Orra's hand exists from before her marriage. This
**cannot be determined from the sources**, and it does not change the count — half-siblings still
produce first cousins and then second cousins; it changes only whether the word "half" belongs in
front of them.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose**, organizer of the Association in 1889, its first
president for twenty-two years, and the author of its by-laws. Resolution: Selby says so himself
from the platform in 1926 ("My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law under
which every patron in this Association is paid on the test of the station where he delivers"); the
secretary's own history calls him "son of the late president"; and Duncan Keddie and the clerk Kyle
both name and reject the "nephew" story, which Toland and Rundle carry. Why it matters: Gideon wrote
Article VII, whose last clause — *the test of the station where he delivers shall govern* — made a
station's own reading final, with no appeal, no re-test, and no comparison with the creamery's
figure. That clause converted a defective piece of glass directly into money out of nine families'
pockets for twenty-six years; under payment by weight nothing would have been lost. Gideon died in
1917 and could not be asked what he meant by it. And the man defending the office through the whole
quarrel — who wrote the 1919 water circular, signed the station-gain sheets, and told his bookkeeper
in 1916 to let the account alone — was the rule-maker's son, which is why the Hollow experienced the
accusation as coming from a family that thought the valley half belonged to it.

**B4. No relationship between the Voses and the Keddies is established, and the best-placed witness
denies it flatly.** Vira Toland asserts that Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins —
"everybody knew that" — and makes it the emotional hinge of the 1919 letter: not merely the
Association accusing them but somebody's cousin. Cleve Oram says the families "were cousins of some
sort," adding that he had it from his mother who had it from hers and that he could not draw the
line on paper. Those two are not two sources; they are the same body of Ashlin and Larrow Green talk
reported twice, and both narrators label it as hearsay while reporting it. Duncan Keddie, writing
inside the family in 1968, denies it and explains it: "There was no connection whatever between us
and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon
either... My mother would have told me" — she being a woman who could not pass a headstone without
giving you the connection, and who never once put a Vose in it. No genealogy, no record, no third
party supports the claim. Method: a specific denial from inside the household, with a named
mechanism for the confusion, beats unsourced common knowledge; and I note that the claim's
persistence is explained by its usefulness — it makes the water letter a family betrayal. Strictly,
the sources cannot *prove* the absence of a remote connection; they establish only that nobody who
asserts it can trace it and the one person who would know says it is talk.

## Section C — Math

### C1.

**(a) The proportion.** The pipettes were blown on the stem at **17.6 c.c.** and delivered a mean of
**16.72 c.c.** (Ivy Keddie's sheet of 9 March 1958: nine pipettes, five runs each — 16.70, 16.71,
16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71; sum 150.48, ÷ 9 = 16.72 exactly).

    16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly.
    Short by 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c., i.e. 5% — "one part in twenty."

Why this transfers straight to the reading: the neck of a test bottle is ruled to read per cent for
one charge and no other. A 95% charge liberates 95% of the fat and stands as 95% of the column, and
the neck, which knows nothing of the substitution, is read off in the ordinary way. **Every test
made on that glass read 95 per cent of the truth.**

*Conflict resolved:* Vaught's method section gives the mean as 16.27 c.c. and the ratio as 0.9244,
and the *Chronicle* repeats 16.27. Two narrators, one wrong value. The tie breaks three ways: the
sheet lists nine individual readings, none below 16.70; the curator, handling the glass in 1972,
records a measured mean of 16.72; and Vaught's own concluding paragraph says "one part in twenty,"
which is 0.95, not 0.9244. 16.27 is a digit transposition of 16.72, and Vaught himself notes that
his figure "is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80" the year after the
glass was changed.

**(b) True average and shortfall.**

    Recorded average (test book summary leaf, 1898 onward): 3.61
    True = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80
    Shortfall = 3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19 of a point.

Two independent confirmations that 3.80 is the true figure, neither of them arithmetic: the station's
own first season on borrowed Larrow Green glass averaged 3.80, and the nine weeks of 1911 on a
borrowed measure stood at 3.80; and the first full year on the new glass, 1924, was 3.80.

Note the shortfall is a *fraction*, not a fixed number of points: 0.19 is 5% of 3.80. A herd recorded
at 4.56 lost 0.24; a herd recorded at 3.42 lost 0.18.

**(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited.**

    Milk received, 25 full delivery years 1898–1922:      40,000,000 lb
    Credited at 3.61%:   40,000,000 × 0.0361 =             1,444,000 lb
    Actually delivered at 3.80%: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 =     1,520,000 lb
    Not credited:        1,520,000 − 1,444,000 =              76,000 lb

**(d) Money, and what they got.**

    76,000 lb × $0.25/lb (period average) = $19,000
    Award actually paid, 4 May 1925       =  $3,800
    3,800 ÷ 19,000 = 0.20 → the patrons received exactly one fifth.

*Conflicts resolved:* Teague's memoir and Rundle's pamphlet both say 44,000,000 lb of milk; three
independent readers of the same ledger (Loomis copying Teague's own annual sheets, Kyle who
extracted the total for the arbitration, and the 1972 curator) give 40,000,000, and 40,000,000 ÷ 25
= 1,600,000 a year, which is exactly the specimen ledger entry (1,600,000 × 3.61% = 57,760;
× 3.80% = 60,800). Sill and Kepp both say fat was worth 27 cents; the ledger-derived 25 cents is
given by Loomis, Kyle, Ivy Keddie and Roy Brant. Vaught's 118,000 lb follows from his mistaken
16.27 and falls with it. The *Chronicle*'s $19,600 is attributed by it to the bulletin, which says
$19,000; Rundle attributes $19,600 to the patrons' 1925 petition, which is a different document and
a different claim.

### C2. Emmet Brant, 1910.

Source: the leaf of his weigh book — *"Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand
pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42."*

    (a) Credited:  200,000 × 3.42% = 200,000 × 0.0342 = 6,840 lb of butterfat.

    (b) True test: 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60
        True fat:  200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb
        Owed and not credited: 7,200 − 6,840 = 360 lb
        (check: 5% of 7,200 = 360 ✓; and 3.60 − 3.42 = 0.18, the figure Ivy Keddie
         gives for a herd recorded at 3.42 ✓)

    (c) 360 lb × $0.24 = $86.40 in that year's money.

Roy Brant states the 1910 price of butterfat as 24 cents; the 25-cent figure is the average over the
whole complaint period, not the price of 1910. At 25 cents the answer would be $90.00. I use $86.40
and flag the alternative, because the year-specific price comes from the same household that kept
the weigh book.

### C3. The other side of the account, same 25 years.

    (a) Credited to patrons on the station's test:                1,444,000 lb
    (b) Received at Larrow Green in the station's cream:           1,520,000 lb
    (c) Difference:                                                   76,000 lb

    76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = 0.0500  → 5.00% of the fat actually delivered
    76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 0.05263 → 5.26% ("five and a quarter per cent") of the fat credited

**Be exact about the denominator.** The Association's ledger line says "five and a quarter per
cent," and it is reckoning against the figure the bookkeeper had already entered — the *credited*
fat. The glass's own error is 5 per cent, and that is a percentage of the fat *delivered*. Both
numbers are correct of their own denominator and of no other; only the 5 per cent is also the glass.
The specimen year proves it: 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26%; 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.00%; and 57,760 ÷ 60,800 =
0.95, the pipette.

Teague states that the gain "ran at five and a quarter percent of the fat that was actually in the
cream," and Vaught repeats the same slip. Both are contradicted by their own figures; the curator's
note of 1972 states the relation correctly.

### C5.

**(a) Two different numbers, and why.**

*In use:* from **1 May 1897** to **12 June 1923** — the dates in Bulletin No. 471's own conclusion —
which is **26 years, 1 month and 12 days**; less the nine weeks of 8 May to 10 July 1911 when a
borrowed measure stood in, so about **25 years and 10½ months of actual service** spread over 26
years of occupancy.

*Money calculated:* **25 full delivery years, 1898 through 1922.**

They differ because the glass went in and came out in the middle of years. 1897 is a broken year —
the station tested on Larrow Green's borrowed glass until 1 May and on the short lot after; the test
book itself records 1897 as "part year" and gives it no average. 1923 is broken the other way — the
short lot to 12 June, the new Ammon lot from July. The 1896 season is broken and worse: it was
carried on the creamery's own books before there was a station account at all, so it is not in the
ledgers. What the ledgers state cleanly — 40,000,000 lb of milk and a recorded 3.61 — is stated for
the twenty-five complete years, and the money calculation can only run where the two agree. The
consequence is that the loss of the 1897 part-year and of five and a half months of 1923 is real and
is **not** in the $19,000.

**(b) The first season's intake: cannot be determined from the sources.**
What is established: the station opened 4 May 1896 and ran to the end of that year; and the figure
does not exist in writing. The 1972 accession notes are explicit — "The station's intake for its
first season, 4 May to the end of 1896, is not in the ledgers and is nowhere else; the station
column begins with 1897, and inquirers wanting a figure for 1896 must be told there is none." Kyle
confirms independently that the first season's milk was carried on the creamery's own books and the
sheets were never put with the station papers. The two figures offered are both recollections
without a document behind them and they disagree by more than twenty per cent: Sill says "about nine
hundred thousand pounds"; Kyle says "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds, which is what I
understood at the time from men who were there" — and Kyle was not there, the station having opened
before his time. Two unsupported recollections, one of them explicitly second-hand, cannot settle a
quantity.

## Section D — Logic

### D1. The nine weeks of 1911.

**When and how long.** The station's own pipette broke on **8 May 1911** and a borrowed measure was
used until **10 July 1911** — sixty-three days, **nine weeks exactly**. Teague and Kepp both call it
six weeks; the two dated entries in the test book settle it by arithmetic, and Ivy Keddie, Vaught,
Falke, Roy Brant and Hazel Brant all say nine.

**The design.** One variable changed: the measuring vessel. Held constant: the patrons, the herds,
the pasture and the season, the feed, the acid, the bottles, the hand tester, the scales, the
building, the supervision, and the man at the crank with his unchanged habits. **Result:** the
station's average rose from 3.61 to 3.80 on the first morning of the substitution, held there for
nine weeks, and fell back to 3.61 *on the day* the station's own measure returned. It is recorded
contemporaneously, in the operator's hand, by a man who did not know what he was recording and who
set a question mark against it and let it stand fourteen years.

**What it establishes that a calculation would not.** The 1958 measurement shows that nine pipettes
recovered from a crate were short and computes what that would do. The 1911 interval shows the
effect *actually appearing and disappearing in the field, on those farms, with that milk*, tied to
one object, forty-seven years before anybody had a theory to fit it to. It therefore eliminates
every explanation that looks to the herds, the ground, the feed, the season, the weather, the
patrons' honesty, or the operator's honesty or skill — because none of those was altered in those
nine weeks, and the figures were. As Vaught puts it, an experiment ran itself under conditions no
designed trial could have bettered, and lay unread.

**What it does not establish.**
- It does not, by itself, say *which* glass was true. Logically the borrowed measure might have run
  over. That is settled only by the 1958 burette work and by the 1924 figure on new glass.
- It does not fix the magnitude across the whole period. It shows a 0.19 step in one summer; that
  the same 0.19 held for twenty-five years comes from the summary leaf and the ledger, not from
  1911.
- It does not prove the nine surviving pipettes were the ones in service in 1911, nor that all
  seventy-two were equally short.
- It does not say *why* the glass was short. Ammon's reground-mold account is her own reconstruction
  from a burnt-out works record.
- It does not prove that no can of milk was ever watered and no cream ever went astray at that
  station on any occasion. It proves only that neither could be the cause of a standing,
  season-independent gap that switched off and on with a piece of glass.
- Kepp's dismissal of it — "six weeks in the flush of the season... I could produce you six weeks in
  any year that would prove anything you liked" — is the one objection the interval alone cannot
  answer. It is answered from outside: the summary leaf shows the gap flat in February as in July,
  and Alder Corners shows the same milk at 3.80 in the flush.

### D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream.

**Reason 1 — the direction of the ledger.** Cream stolen at the station would have arrived at
Larrow Green *light* against the fat the station's books said it had bought. It arrived **heavy**,
every year, by 3,040 lb in an average year and 76,000 lb over the period, entered by the Association's
own bookkeeper as "station gain." Rundle, who had printed the theft verdict as settled valley
opinion, read the ledgers while his pamphlet was in type and retracted the chapter in exactly these
words: "It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand."

**Reason 2 — the nine weeks of 1911.** The same man, the same room, the same separator, no different
supervision, nothing changed but a measure — and the figures rose to 3.80 and came back to 3.61 the
day the measure returned. Theft does not suspend itself for nine weeks and resume on a datable
morning.

**Reason 3 — the chronology of the correction.** The glass was condemned 12 June 1923, new glass was
in use from July, and the station's average for 1924 stood at 3.80 — recorded independently in the
state inspector's field book and in Keddie's own book. Keddie was still the tester through the whole
of that rise. He was not dismissed until 30 April 1925. If he had been the thief, the figures should
have corrected when he left; they corrected a season and a half before, while he stood at the same
bench.

**Why these are independent.** They come from three separate custodies, three record types and three
kinds of argument: (1) is an accounting stream generated at the *other end* of the valley, from cream
weighed into the churn room by Hobart Sill, who checked the platform man's figure himself for
forty-one years and had no dealings with the station's books; (2) is a contemporaneous field entry in
the station's own book, made blind; (3) turns on dates in an outside agency's notebook and needs no
quantity at all. A fourth, from Vaught, is structural: a pilferer produces scatter, and scatter
attracts attention, whereas this error was dead steady to three hundredths for a quarter of a century
in January and July alike — "It has the appearance of excellent work."

**Which survive without the 1958 measurement.** All three. Reasons 1, 2 and 3 were fully available in
1925 — the ledgers were on an open shelf, the 1911 entries were in Exhibit 1, and the 1924 average
was in Exhibit 1 and in Exhibit 4's parent series. Nothing about them required a burette.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question the entries that refute his own accusation?** Once, obliquely, and
never again. Teague invented the station-gain line himself in 1905, wrote "as usual" into it, and
carried it for forty years. He says: "I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone. I
let it alone. I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience." So he raised the *account*
with the manager one time and dropped it on being told to. He never questioned what it meant for the
charge he then insinuated to the board in 1925 — and in 1948, nine years after a schoolteacher had
retracted a theft chapter on the strength of that very line, Teague was still arguing in his memoir
that a one-man station "will sooner or later have a little taken from it," on the same page as the
entry proving the cream came down heavy. He also admits why: "A figure that comes in at five and a
quarter every year is furniture."

### D3. What the manager knew.

**He certainly knew the tests ran low, and had known for years.** That is not in dispute anywhere,
and he says it himself from the platform in 1926: "For a generation the milk brought to that station
tested below the milk brought to Larrow Green," and "A manager who sees his figures going one way
for twenty years and says nothing to the men who make them is not being kind to them; he is being
idle at their expense." The 1919 circular exists only because he had been watching those figures.
The clerk who typed it and sat outside his door confirms the traffic. And Teague put the station
account to him in 1916 — three years after he took office in 1913 — and was told to let it alone,
which is knowledge of the account as well as of the tests.

**What he understood by the "station gain" line cannot be determined.** He signed the annual sheet
carrying it every spring from 1913. Teague's position is the strict one: "a man who signs a sheet has
had the sheet in front of him. What he made of it he never told me." Against that, Kyle — who watched
him do it a thousand times — says he "read the totals and he never read the lines above them... take
a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign." So the sources establish that the
fact was physically in his hands annually for a decade and was verbally put to him once, and they do
not establish that he ever drew the inference the line permits — that the shortfall was in the
station's arithmetic and not in the patrons' cans. His 1926 address, in which he still insists on
water and calls the condemned glassware "a piece of housekeeping," is evidence that he had not drawn
it by then; it is not evidence about 1916. **Answer: yes as to low tests, certainly; no determination
as to the gain line.**

  document) says six, and Roy Brant, Hazel Brant, Kepp and the *Chronicle* agree. Loomis wrote from
  seven miles away and never went up.
- **Alder Corners founded 1913 (Rosalie) vs opened 1914 (Roy, Rundle).** Probably both: built in
  1913 on Rosalie's first-person account (she was twelve when the first vat was set), open for
  business as the county knew it in 1914. Not decisively settled; the disagreement does not bear on
  anything.

**Group 4 — conflicts the sources cannot settle. I decline these.**

- **The year Article VII was adopted.** 1892 (Loomis), 1891 (Rundle), 1893 (Grigg). The printed
  by-laws bear no year; the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the 1938 office move; Loomis, the only
  narrator with authority, disqualifies herself for those years. Falke declines it too, correctly.
  **Cannot be determined from the sources.**
- **The station's intake in its first season, 1896.** No ledger; Sill says ~900,000 lb, Kyle
  ~1,100,000 lb, and Kyle admits he had it from other men. **Cannot be determined.**
- **How many families received the 1919 circular.** Four later accounts say nine, including two
  recipients' households and the man who delivered them; Orra Keddie's diary, written within days,
  says he took them "round himself, to eleven families." The contemporaneous document says eleven
  doors; the recollections say nine letters. The likeliest reconciliation — eleven houses called at,
  nine of them addressed patrons, six farms among them since a farm held more than one family — is
  **my inference and is not stated anywhere.** Unsettled.
- **Whether Orra was Emmet's full or half sister.** Roy says full and names the rumour; Hazel, his
  daughter, says half by his father's second wife. No document; the diary's pre-1889 pages were not
  preserved. **Cannot be determined**, and it does not change the cousinships.
- **Where the 1911 borrowed measure came from.** Ivy says Larrow Green, up with the cream wagon; Roy
  Brant says the state man left his own that June. Roy is defeated as a *cause* by chronology — the
  break was 8 May and the rise began that morning, weeks before any June visit — and Strawn's 1911
  entry records nothing lent. Larrow Green is the better-supported answer but is not documented.
- **Ivy Keddie's "fourteen seasons" of overlap with the Alder Corners book.** The Alder Corners table
  runs sixteen seasons, 1915–1930; the faulty glass ended mid-1923; the station book ends 1924.
  No combination in the sources yields fourteen. Unverifiable.
- **What the tester privately believed.** See F1.
- **Whether the manager understood the station-gain line.** See D3.

## Section F — Theory

### F1. What actually produced the shortfall.

**The theory, in one causal chain.** A butterfat test reads a column of liberated fat in a bottle
whose neck is ruled in per cent **for one charge of milk and no other** — in this state, 17.6 cubic
centimetres. The operator weighs nothing; he reads a length and calls it a percentage, and he is
entitled to do so only because the charge is a known fixed quantity. The pipettes bought for the
Ostrey Hollow station from Ammon & Sons in April 1897 were blown and marked 17.6 c.c. and delivered
16.72. Less milk went into the bottle; less fat was liberated; a shorter column stood in the neck;
and the neck, which knows nothing of the substitution, was read off in the ordinary way. Every test
made on that glass therefore read **95 per cent of the truth**, and the men of Ostrey Hollow were
paid on it under a by-law that made their own station's test final and unappealable.

**Why that particular size.** Because the glass was short by that particular amount: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 =
0.95, one part in twenty. The error is a *proportion*, not a fixed deduction — which is why it
scaled with the milk (a herd recorded at 4.56 lost 0.24 of a point; one recorded at 3.42 lost 0.18),
why it fell hardest on the best milk, and why it produced a perfectly orderly set of figures in which
the station's herds ranked correctly against each other and only the outside world was wrong. On a
true herd average of 3.80 it came to 0.19 of a point: small enough to pass for soil, feed or weather,
far too small and far too steady to look like theft. Its very modesty is why it lasted.

**Why it began when it did.** On 1 May 1897 the station stopped using the outfit Hobart Sill had lent
it out of his own bench at Larrow Green — which was true — and began using the new lot. Hence the
test book's own three lines: 1896, 3.80; 1897, part year; 1898, 3.61, and 3.61 within three
hundredths ever after. The station's records contain their own before-and-after and nobody read them
as one.

**Why it ended when it did.** The state began issuing calibrated burettes to its field men in 1921.
On 12 June 1923 Merle Strawn ran the station's pipettes against his, found they would not deliver
their mark, condemned the whole crate and told the manager to order new. New glass was in use from
July 1923; the first full year on it, 1924, averaged 3.80.

**Why nothing had to change at the station or on the farms.** Because the fault was in a measuring
vessel and in nothing else. No cow, field, ration, can, scale, acid, centrifuge or habit was
implicated at either end, so none had to be altered to start the error or to stop it. The operator's
exceptional care made no difference: a short pipette delivers short for a careful man and a careless
one alike, which is exactly why the error looked like excellent work. And the reason it ran
twenty-six years is structural: every other element of the test can be checked against a standard —
scales against weights, the crank against a count, acid against a titration, the bottle's
graduations against mercury — but the pipette is not checked against anything, because in the
ordinary practice of the trade *the pipette is the standard*. An instrument that is itself the
measure cannot be caught by measuring with it. It can be caught only by a burette brought in from
outside, and burettes reached the state's field men very late in this history.

**The three competing explanations, and what defeats each.**

**(i) Water in the cans.** Advanced by Selby Vose, manager — circulated as a warning in June 1919,
pressed before the board in 1925, and defended from the platform in 1926; endorsed by Hobart Sill
("You cannot tell me nine families all got honest in one summer"). *Defeated by:* the state
inspector's lactometer, applied at that station on every visit from 1907 to 1936 and never once
finding added water — he told the manager so in 1919, in the same year as the circular; by Rosalie
Cudd's evidence that watered milk cannot be concealed from a cheesemaker, because the curd comes
soft and will not knit and the yield is down by the next morning, and that in sixteen summers her
father never docked those four Hessel Bottom men a cent; by the shape of the gap, identical in
February and July, whereas watering is a discretionary act that would scatter; by the ledgers, since
watered milk would have sent the cream down *light* and it came down heavy; and by Vose's own
chronology, since the tests rose in 1924, after the glass changed in July 1923 and a full year before
the hearing he credits with the reform.

**(ii) The tester was taking cream and writing low tests to cover the loss.** Insinuated to the board
by Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper, in the manner of a man who wants a thing said without saying it; it was
the settled opinion of the valley (Toland, Rundle's Chapter IV, and Cleve Oram's father, who drove
the wagon and always said less came off that platform than went onto it). *Defeated by:* the
Association's own station-gain line, written by Teague himself — the cream arrived at Larrow Green
**heavier** than the station's books said it had bought, by five and a quarter per cent of the
credited fat, every year for a quarter of a century, which is the wrong sign for theft; by Sill's
independent re-weighing of the same cream into the cold room for forty-one years, always more, never
less; by the nine weeks of 1911, when the same man at the same crank produced 3.80 and then 3.61
again the day a piece of glass came back; and by 1923–24, when the figures corrected themselves while
Keddie was still at the bench and a season and a half before he was dismissed. Rundle retracted his
own printed verdict on the first of these grounds alone.

**(iii) The wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream.** Advanced by the 1908 investigating
committee under Nyle Grigg, reasserted by Grigg in 1919, and pressed at length by Dr. Roland Kepp in
1926; conceded by the county agent in 1959 and withdrawn by him the same year. *Defeated by the very
authority its advocates cite:* Bulletin No. 214 (1911) found bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage
depressed the test "by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that
could be measured on summer pasture." Grigg and Kepp both quote it as "two tenths." The real figure
is four times too small and the wrong shape — a winter-only effect against a gap standing flat in
every month of the year. And it is defeated empirically by Alder Corners, where the same four men's
milk off that same wet ground ran 3.78 to 3.82 in the summer flush for sixteen consecutive seasons,
averaging 3.80, while their home station recorded them at 3.61.

**Subsidiary 1 — did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware? No.** The award recites
that the board had "before it the books and appliances of the station," but the secretary who took
down every word of all three sessions states flatly that the crate was never mentioned by anybody,
and that the award's phrase "is a form of words the board used and not a list of anything." Frick's
own exhibit list, reprinted from his file, is four books and nothing else: the station test book, the
station receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book, and the state inspector's circuit notebook
for 1923. What the board did examine was the apparatus then in daily use — the replacement lot,
running since July 1923 — and they watched a test made on it and satisfied themselves. The condemned
crate was in the loft over their heads, twenty feet up a ladder, through two of the three sittings.
The bitterest detail is that the answer was in the room in writing: Exhibit 4 was the notebook
containing the condemnation entry of 12 June 1923, and, as the clerk who carried the books in and out
says, no man in that room ever laid the second exhibit beside the fourth and looked.

**Subsidiary 2 — what did the tester himself believe?** On the record, that he did not know: he told
the board twice "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are," and in his own book he set a
question mark against the 1911 entry and let it stand fourteen years, because "I will not put
anything in its place that I do not know." Two private beliefs are reported, by two witnesses, and
they do not agree. His son Duncan, then seventeen and in the kitchen, says his father came home in
the summer of 1911 saying **it was something in the glass** — said it twice in one evening, which was
not his way — and never dared write it, having nothing to hold in his hand but a suspicion against
the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county. Frick says
that in the corridor at Ashlin in 1925 Keddie told him privately he had always thought **the patrons
were at fault** and would not say so in the room, having lived among them thirty years. Both are
single-witness and fourteen years apart, and by 1925 he was the accused man. My reading: he
suspected the glass in 1911, correctly, could not prove it, and thereafter fell back on the honest
"I cannot tell you," and the one line of documentary evidence in his own hand — the question mark —
is consistent with that and with nothing stronger. The Frick remark is real but late, and it cannot
be squared with the 1911 account except as a different thought at a different time.

## Section G — Summary

### G1. (147 words)

In 1889 the farmers of the Larrow Valley formed a cooperative creamery, and wrote a by-law paying
each man on the butterfat test made at the station where he delivered — final, with no appeal. Their
skimming station at Ostrey Hollow opened in 1896 on borrowed glass and averaged 3.80. In May 1897 it
began using pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. that delivered 16.72, and for twenty-six years every test there
read 95 per cent of the truth: 3.61 instead of 3.80. Nine families were suspected of watering milk;
their tester was suspected of stealing cream and dismissed in 1925; the wet hay was blamed. The
missing 76,000 lb, worth $19,000, sat in the Association's own ledgers all along, entered as
"station gain." A granddaughter measured the condemned glass in 1958. It took eight records because
each witness held one true fragment and no two fragments were ever in the same room.
