# Answers — Part 2: Section A (Stories 5–8) and Section B

## Story 5: The Water Letter and the Family Rift (1919)

In 1919, with the low-test complaint reopened, Association manager Selby Vose circulated a
printed letter to the Ostrey Hollow patrons warning against adding water to their milk — signed
over his own name, "naming nobody" and "accusing nobody" by his own later account, but understood
by everyone as an accusation. Most sources put the distribution at nine families (repeated across
several accounts, though most of these likely trace to the circular's own single distribution
list rather than independent counts); Orra Keddie's contemporaneous 1919 diary entry alone gives
eleven, delivered personally, door to door, by her husband, who "would not have another man carry
it." No family answered it — "which in that valley is a shout," in Hazel Brant's phrase. Reaction
split the valley: buttermaker Hobart Sill approved of it ("the only plain thing that office ever
did"); the Hollow "hated him for it."

The letter's most lasting effect was personal. Emmet Brant, Ansel Keddie's brother-in-law and the
station's largest patron, held that Keddie — as the man who kept the test book — "ought to have
stood up in front of that circular and did not." The two men, who had shared a family table and
argued amiably about drainage for decades, stopped speaking that year and never reconciled; Orra's
diary records her brother confronting Ansel in the yard days later and never entering the house
again. Dell Rundle's 1939 pamphlet frames the whole valley similarly, its "two ends" not speaking
"civilly to one another for six years" (1919–1925). The rift outlived Emmet, who died in 1929
still estranged from his brother-in-law.

**Quantities:** nine (majority) vs. eleven (Orra's diary) families; zero replies; six years of
valley estrangement (1919–1925).

**Documents:** r01, r08, r09, r12, r13, r14, r15, r17, r18, r24.

## Story 6: Over the Ridge — the Alder Corners Defection (1913/14–1930)

Jerome Cudd built a cheese factory at Alder Corners, Redlow Ridge, Vessey County (founding year
disputed: 1913 per Rosalie Cudd's own letter, 1914 per Roy Brant and Dell Rundle) — reached from
Ostrey Hollow by a four-mile switchback lumber road, closed by drifts each winter. From 1915, four
of Hessel Bottom's six farms began hauling their milk over the ridge each summer flush (roughly
June to mid-September) to sell to Cudd for cheese rather than to the home station, returning to
Ostrey Hollow the rest of the year — for sixteen consecutive seasons, the last being 1930. Reasons
given: better flush-season cheese prices and a shorter pull while the road was open; Roy Brant's
own larger family declined to go, a choice he calls, in hindsight, "a poor reason" that "cost us
money."

Cudd tested on his own bench with his own glassware, entirely independent of Ostrey Hollow's
testing chain, and kept no comparison with the home station's figures — he had no reason to. His
own 1919 book entry for the four defecting farmers reads 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82 — ordinary, good
tests — and he dismissed the Ordell wet-hay theory as folklore that "cost nothing" to believe.
Jerome died in March 1941; his daughter Rosalie took over the factory that same spring and ran it
for the rest of her working life (confirmed as "daughter," not "niece," by her own letter and by
Rundle's pamphlet, against a mistaken "niece" label used by the 1972 museum catalogue and the 1958
newspaper account).

In 1958, at Ivy Keddie's request, Rosalie copied out her father's full sixteen-season data table
for these same four farmers — averaging about 3.80 across all sixteen summers — becoming the
single clearest independent proof, entirely outside the Association's own books, that Ostrey
Hollow's low tests reflected the station and not the herds.

**Quantities:** 1913/1914 (founding, unsettled); four of six farms; sixteen seasons, 1915–1930;
~3.80 sixteen-season average.

**Documents:** r09, r10, r12.

## Story 7: The 1925 Board of Arbitration

The Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned the Association in January 1925 for restitution. A
three-man board chaired by Ashlin attorney Alonzo Frick ("because I was the one of the three who
kept no cow") sat three times: first at Ashlin on 17 March, then twice more at Ostrey Hollow
itself, in the station's own weigh room, at Frick's request, so the board could inspect the plant
directly. Three explanations were argued: added water (Selby Vose, "without heat and without
proof"), tester theft (Wilbur Teague, argued structurally rather than as a direct charge), and the
wet-hay theory (the 1908 report, read into the record). Ansel Keddie testified he had tested as he
was taught for twenty-nine seasons and could not account for the figures, though he privately told
Frick in the corridor — a detail absent from the official minutes — that he had always privately
suspected the patrons, but would not say so in the room.

Keddie was dismissed 30 April 1925, four days before the board's award, while it was still
sitting; Frick states flatly there was "no finding against him from us and there never was one."
On 4 May 1925 the board found no cause established against anyone and awarded the patrons $3,800
"without admission" — confirmed independently by the arbitration's own minute book, Vose's later
address, Teague's memoir, and Frick's own recollection, against a lone recollection of $3,500.
Only four documents were formally entered as exhibits: the station test book, the receiving book,
Emmet Brant's private weigh book, and the state inspector's 1923 circuit notebook. The crate of
condemned glassware, sitting in the loft directly above two of the three sittings, was never among
them, and nothing in any source suggests the board ever examined it.

**Quantities:** three sittings; four exhibits; $3,800 (majority) vs. $3,500 (minority); 30
April/4 May 1925.

**Documents:** r02, r03, r05, r06, r07, r08, r13, r21, r23, r24.

## Story 8: The Crate in the Loft — Discovery and Vindication (1958–1959)

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie — Ansel Keddie's granddaughter, a graduate student at the State
Agricultural College at Farland — found the station's condemned 1897 pipette crate, still under a
tarpaulin in the loft over the old intake room, thirty-five years after a state inspector had
condemned it for failing to deliver its marked 17.6 c.c. charge. On 9 March 1958 she measured the
nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette: they delivered, on average, 16.72 c.c. — 95%
of the mark, short by one part in twenty. Because the fat test reads directly against a fixed
assumed charge, a pipette short by 5% yields a fat reading short by exactly 5%, consistently, for
as long as it is used. Applied to the station's recorded average of 3.61, the true average worked
out to 3.80 — matching, almost exactly, the independent Alder Corners cross-check data Rosalie
Cudd supplied the same year.

The finding was published in October 1958 as State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 471, under
Professor Leland Vaught's name jointly with Ivy Keddie's, concluding that "no milk was watered...
and no cream was stolen," and that seventy-six thousand pounds of uncredited butterfat "stands
credited in the Association's own ledgers... as station gain." The Ashlin Chronicle covered the
story on its front page (introducing, and then not correcting, an error calling Ivy Keddie
Ansel's "great-niece"); county agent Orin Falke filed an official report the following year
clearing Ansel Keddie's name, twenty-four years after his death and thirty-three after his
dismissal, and explicitly corrected both the "great-niece" error and his own initial concession
to the wet-hay theory. Ivy delivered "What the Crate Held" to the Ordell County Historical
Society at Ashlin in 1959, with the crate itself on the table.

**Quantities:** 9 pipettes measured; 16.72 c.c. average delivery vs. 17.6 c.c. mark; 3.61 recorded
vs. 3.80 true average; 1958 (discovery/bulletin), 1959 (talk, official report).

**Documents:** r04, r07, r11, r17, r19, r22.

# Section B — Relationships

## B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie

Granddaughter. Derived from Duncan Keddie's own written genealogy (r18: Ivy, born 1931, "makes
her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it
printed otherwise"), corroborated by r04 (her own self-presentation) and r24's editorial note.
Two sources point elsewhere — the Ashlin Chronicle (r19) calls her "great-niece," and county agent
Falke's report (r22) makes the identical error before appending an explicit correction ("I am
informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the
error on page two is mine"). The pattern — one error, then a traceable correction citing
verification — suggests "great-niece" was a single garbled fact (likely from a rushed telephone
interview or press release) that spread from the newspaper into the county report, rather than a
genuine competing genealogical claim. Resolved: granddaughter, with high confidence.

## B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant

Second cousins — children of first cousins Duncan Keddie and Roy Brant, who were themselves the
children of siblings Orra Keddie and Emmet Brant. This is affirmed by four independent
family-insider sources: Roy Brant's own account (r09), Duncan Keddie's explicit genealogy, which
specifically rules out "cousins once removed" (r18), Hazel Brant's own statement, which
specifically rules out "niece" and "cousin once removed" as things people mistakenly say (r17),
and Dell Rundle's 1959 footnote (r12). Against this stand two non-family, less direct sources: the
unnamed 1958 visitor in r01, said to be "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed," and Cleve
Oram's 1977 recollection (r21), also "first cousin once removed" — an outsider's report, admittedly
secondhand. The generational chain itself (Orra/Emmet siblings → Duncan/Roy first cousins →
Ivy/Hazel second cousins) is fully and consistently traced by the family members themselves,
leaving nothing genuinely open about the intervening generations except the separate, unrelated
question of whether Orra and Emmet were full or half siblings (see E1) — which does not change the
cousin arithmetic either way.

## B3. Selby Vose's father, and why it matters

Gideon Vose — the Association's founding first president (1889–1911, d. 1917) and author of its
by-laws, including Article VII. This is affirmed by Selby's own 1926 address (a primary,
autobiographical source), by Effie Loomis's secretarial account (r02), and by two explicit,
unprompted refutations of a "nephew" rumor from family-adjacent insiders — Duncan Keddie (r18: "the
son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew") and Bertram Kyle (r23: "the son, whatever is
said at Ashlin... I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense") —
against a minority of two (Vira Toland, r01; Dell Rundle, r12) who call him "nephew." It matters
because it means the manager who presided over the entire complaint period (1913–1938), who
personally warned patrons about watering their milk in 1919 and defended that theory at the 1925
hearing and in his own 1926 address, was not a distant relative brought in from outside but the
direct son and institutional heir of the man who had designed the payment-by-test system that made
the low readings costly in the first place — giving him a personal as well as an institutional
stake in an explanation that did not implicate the equipment his father's system depended on.

## B4. Relationship between the Vose family and the Keddie family

None, according to the most authoritative source. Duncan Keddie's own genealogy notes (r18,
titled "A thing that is said and is not so") state flatly that "there was no connection whatever
between us and the Voses," attributing the persistent rumor — that Orra Keddie and Gideon Vose's
wife were cousins — to confusion with an unrelated family, the "Ashlin Voses." Duncan explicitly
notes that his mother, a woman who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection,"
never once claimed a Vose connection. This directly contradicts Vira Toland's claim (r01) that
Orra was "first cousin" to "old Mrs. Vose," and Cleve Oram's vaguer, explicitly hedged "cousins of
some sort... I could not draw you the line on paper" (r21), itself secondhand from his mother.
Resolved: no relationship, favoring the direct, explicitly self-correcting family-insider source
over two vaguer, non-family, hearsay accounts.
