# Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

Eight-story partition chosen by the answerer: the corpus naturally separates into (1) the
Association's founding and payment rule, (2) the station and its tester, (3) the Brant family and
its weigh book, (4) the wet-hay theory, (5) the 1919 water letter and family rift, (6) the Alder
Corners defection, (7) the 1925 arbitration, and (8) the 1958 discovery and vindication. Every
retelling touches more than one of these; each is placed where its central event belongs, with
overlapping documents noted as "bears on" in the other stories too.

## Story 1: The Association and the Rule of Payment by Test (1889–1892)

In 1889, farmers of the Larrow Valley meeting at Larrow Green organized the Larrow Valley
Cooperative Creamery Association, electing Gideon Vose — "heavy, quiet, obstinate," a man who had
once considered the law — as first president, a post he held twenty-two years until his death in
1917. Vose personally wrote the by-laws, including Article VII: every patron would be paid by the
butterfat his milk tested at, not by weight. This single rule is the hinge of everything that
follows — as one later county report observed, under payment by weight nothing in the rest of the
history would have cost anyone a dollar. The main creamery was built at Larrow Green in 1891, on
Larrow Creek, and the valley began being paid in cash monthly for the first time.

The exact year Article VII was adopted cannot be settled: 1891 (Rundle), 1892 (Loomis), 1893
(Grigg, hedged as "I have always understood it"). The printed by-laws pamphlet itself carries no
date, only "adopted at the annual meeting," and the Association's earliest minute book
(1889–1892) was lost in a 1938 office move — so no source can adjudicate the three claims.

Gideon's son Selby Vose (his paternity is affirmed by Selby's own 1926 address and by two
independent, explicit refutations of a "nephew" rumor — Duncan Keddie and Bertram Kyle both call
it false talk, against Vira Toland's and Dell Rundle's "nephew") became the Association's second
manager, serving 1913–1938 — inheriting, and later personally defending, the very payment system
his father had designed.

**Quantities:** 1889 (founding); 22 years (Gideon's presidency, 1889–1911 by other sources'
framing, d. 1917); 1891 (creamery built); three competing adoption dates (1891/1892/1893).

**Documents:** r02 (Effie Loomis), r12 (Dell Rundle), r15 (Nyle Grigg, in passing), r18 (Duncan
Keddie), r22 (Orin Falke), r23 (Bertram Kyle).

## Story 2: Ostrey Hollow Opens, and Ansel Keddie's Twenty-Nine Seasons (1895–1934)

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles
up the valley, to serve Hessel Bottom's farms. Ansel Keddie went up in 1895 to ready it and
tested through its first summer. The station opened 4 May 1896; its own ordered glassware had not
arrived, so Hobart Sill, the Larrow Green buttermaker, personally lent his own bench glass to open
it. The outfit ran on borrowed measures until 1 May 1897, when the station's own new pipettes
(bought from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet, invoice total $77.93) went into service. Keddie kept the test
book in his own hand every season from 1896 until his 1925 dismissal — "thirty-one times a week
for twenty-nine seasons." He married Orra Brant in 1889; their son Duncan (b. 1894) became a
schoolteacher at Ashlin. Keddie tested the milk of every Hessel Bottom farmer, including his own
brother-in-law Emmet Brant.

From nearly the day the station opened its recorded tests ran low — 3.61 against a valley norm
near 3.78–3.80 — and complaints, beginning in 1902, dogged Keddie for the rest of his career,
though the true cause (defective pipettes) was invisible to him and everyone else. In 1911, when
his own measure broke and a borrowed one was used for nine weeks, the recorded test rose sharply
and fell back the day his own measure returned; he set only a question mark against the entry,
telling his son privately he thought "it was something in the glass" but refusing to commit an
unproven suspicion to the book. The mark stood fourteen years. He died in 1934, nine years after
his dismissal, without seeing himself cleared.

**Quantities:** 1895 (arrival); 4 May 1896 (opening); 1 May 1897 (own glass); 1896 average 3.80;
1898-on average 3.61; 29 seasons; 8 May–10 July 1911 (nine-week anomaly); 1934 (death).

**Documents:** r01 (Vira Toland), r03 (Ansel Keddie), r04 (Ivy Keddie), r06 (Merle Strawn), r14
(Hobart Sill), r18 (Duncan Keddie), r20 (Junia Ammon), r23 (Bertram Kyle), r24 (Orra Keddie).

## Story 3: The Brant Family and the Weigh Book of Hessel Bottom (1897–1963)

Hessel Bottom was a roughly 160-acre wet meadow below Ostrey Hollow, with rights held by six
farms (a lone source says seven), the Brant place the largest — sixty-odd acres, thirty-eight cows
in a good year. Emmet Brant, Orra Keddie's brother (full sibling per Orra's own diary, Duncan's
genealogy, and Roy's explicit denial of a rumored half-sister story; Hazel Brant alone calls them
half-siblings), began a private weigh-book ledger recording his own scale weight against the
station's returned figure for every delivery. Its start date is disputed three ways: 1 January
1897 (the book's own catalogue record and Frick's 1925 exhibit list — two independent institutional
sources), 1898 (Roy's own retelling), and 1900 (Hazel's, a generation further removed). The two
weight columns agreed for over thirty years, "barring a pound or two" — proof that the weighing,
as distinct from the fat test, was never at fault. A famous 1910 leaf records 200,000 lb
delivered, "even," tested at 3.42 — quoted identically by Roy and by the book's own catalogued
entry.

Emmet died in 1929; the book passed to his son Roy, who continued it in the same format. Roy lent
it to Ivy Keddie in 1958 for her research — insisting on "lent," never "left" — and had it back;
at his death in 1962 it passed to his daughter Hazel, who gave it to the Ordell County Historical
Rooms in 1963. Emmet and Ansel Keddie, brothers-in-law who had shared a family table and argued
amiably about drainage for decades, stopped speaking after the 1919 circular (Story 5) and never
reconciled before Emmet's 1929 death.

**Quantities:** six farms (majority); three-way start-date dispute (1897/1898/1900); 1910
delivery, 200,000 lb at 3.42; 1929 (Emmet's death); 1958 (loan); 1962/1963 (final transfer).

**Documents:** r07 (Item 4), r09 (Roy Brant), r13 (Frick's exhibit list), r15 (Grigg), r16 (Kepp),
r17 (Hazel Brant), r18 (Duncan Keddie), r24 (Orra Keddie).

## Story 4: The Wet-Hay Theory — the 1908 Committee and Its Afterlife (1902–1926/1959)

From 1902, Ostrey Hollow patrons complained their milk tested below the rest of the valley. In
January 1908 the Association appointed a three-man committee under Nyle Grigg, a Larrow Green
farmer with no stake in the outcome. Over January–March 1908 the committee sampled the six Hessel
Bottom herds directly, watched milking and testing, checked scales, and found "no dishonesty of
any kind, in any quarter" — concluding instead that the bottom's wet, sedge-and-rush pasture and
coarse, late-cut hay "thinned the cream," and that "the remedy lies with the land and not with the
station." The finding held for eleven years. In 1919, with complaints reopened, Grigg reprinted
his own report with a covering letter that misremembers the sampling as running "through the whole
of 1908" — directly contradicted by the very report reprinted beneath it — and that misquotes the
college's 1911 Bulletin No. 214 as finding a "two tenths" (0.20) feed effect. In 1926 Dr. Roland
Kepp, an Ashlin veterinarian, elaborated the theory at length to the county Farm Club from his own
thirty years' field observation, independently repeating the identical "two tenths" error and
treating the coincidence as triangulated proof.

The theory could not survive its own cited evidence: Bulletin 214's actual, correctly-quoted
finding is that wet hay depresses the test by "not more than five hundredths of one per cent
[0.05] in winter, and by nothing... in summer" — a quarter or less of the observed 0.19-point
gap, and explicitly absent in summer, whereas the real gap ran flat year-round, in July as in
February. County agent Orin Falke's 1959 report initially conceded some weight to the theory, then
formally withdrew the concession on obtaining the bulletin's actual text.

**Quantities:** six farms; January–March 1908 (sampling); eleven years (1908–1919); shared "two
tenths" (0.20) error vs. the real 0.05; observed gap 0.19.

**Documents:** r03, r07 (Items 11–12), r15 (Grigg), r16 (Kepp), r22 (Falke).

## Story 5: The Water Letter and the Family Rift (1919)

In 1919, with the low-test complaint reopened, Association manager Selby Vose circulated a
printed letter to the Ostrey Hollow patrons warning against adding water to their milk — signed
over his own name, "naming nobody" and "accusing nobody" by his own later account, but understood
by everyone as an accusation. Most sources put the distribution at nine families (repeated across
several accounts, though most of these likely trace to the circular's own single distribution
list rather than independent counts); Orra Keddie's contemporaneous 1919 diary entry alone gives
eleven, delivered personally, door to door, by her husband, who "would not have another man carry
it." No family answered it — "which in that valley is a shout," in Hazel Brant's phrase. Reaction
split the valley: buttermaker Hobart Sill approved of it ("the only plain thing that office ever
did"); the Hollow "hated him for it."

The letter's most lasting effect was personal. Emmet Brant, Ansel Keddie's brother-in-law and the
station's largest patron, held that Keddie — as the man who kept the test book — "ought to have
stood up in front of that circular and did not." The two men, who had shared a family table and
argued amiably about drainage for decades, stopped speaking that year and never reconciled; Orra's
diary records her brother confronting Ansel in the yard days later and never entering the house
again. Dell Rundle's 1939 pamphlet frames the whole valley similarly, its "two ends" not speaking
"civilly to one another for six years" (1919–1925). The rift outlived Emmet, who died in 1929
still estranged from his brother-in-law.

**Quantities:** nine (majority) vs. eleven (Orra's diary) families; zero replies; six years of
valley estrangement (1919–1925).

**Documents:** r01, r08, r09, r12, r13, r14, r15, r17, r18, r24.

## Story 6: Over the Ridge — the Alder Corners Defection (1913/14–1930)

Jerome Cudd built a cheese factory at Alder Corners, Redlow Ridge, Vessey County (founding year
disputed: 1913 per Rosalie Cudd's own letter, 1914 per Roy Brant and Dell Rundle) — reached from
Ostrey Hollow by a four-mile switchback lumber road, closed by drifts each winter. From 1915, four
of Hessel Bottom's six farms began hauling their milk over the ridge each summer flush (roughly
June to mid-September) to sell to Cudd for cheese rather than to the home station, returning to
Ostrey Hollow the rest of the year — for sixteen consecutive seasons, the last being 1930. Reasons
given: better flush-season cheese prices and a shorter pull while the road was open; Roy Brant's
own larger family declined to go, a choice he calls, in hindsight, "a poor reason" that "cost us
money."

Cudd tested on his own bench with his own glassware, entirely independent of Ostrey Hollow's
testing chain, and kept no comparison with the home station's figures — he had no reason to. His
own 1919 book entry for the four defecting farmers reads 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82 — ordinary, good
tests — and he dismissed the Ordell wet-hay theory as folklore that "cost nothing" to believe.
Jerome died in March 1941; his daughter Rosalie took over the factory that same spring and ran it
for the rest of her working life (confirmed as "daughter," not "niece," by her own letter and by
Rundle's pamphlet, against a mistaken "niece" label used by the 1972 museum catalogue and the 1958
newspaper account).

In 1958, at Ivy Keddie's request, Rosalie copied out her father's full sixteen-season data table
for these same four farmers — averaging about 3.80 across all sixteen summers — becoming the
single clearest independent proof, entirely outside the Association's own books, that Ostrey
Hollow's low tests reflected the station and not the herds.

**Quantities:** 1913/1914 (founding, unsettled); four of six farms; sixteen seasons, 1915–1930;
~3.80 sixteen-season average.

**Documents:** r09, r10, r12.

## Story 7: The 1925 Board of Arbitration

The Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned the Association in January 1925 for restitution. A
three-man board chaired by Ashlin attorney Alonzo Frick ("because I was the one of the three who
kept no cow") sat three times: first at Ashlin on 17 March, then twice more at Ostrey Hollow
itself, in the station's own weigh room, at Frick's request, so the board could inspect the plant
directly. Three explanations were argued: added water (Selby Vose, "without heat and without
proof"), tester theft (Wilbur Teague, argued structurally rather than as a direct charge), and the
wet-hay theory (the 1908 report, read into the record). Ansel Keddie testified he had tested as he
was taught for twenty-nine seasons and could not account for the figures, though he privately told
Frick in the corridor — a detail absent from the official minutes — that he had always privately
suspected the patrons, but would not say so in the room.

Keddie was dismissed 30 April 1925, four days before the board's award, while it was still
sitting; Frick states flatly there was "no finding against him from us and there never was one."
On 4 May 1925 the board found no cause established against anyone and awarded the patrons $3,800
"without admission" — confirmed independently by the arbitration's own minute book, Vose's later
address, Teague's memoir, and Frick's own recollection, against a lone recollection of $3,500.
Only four documents were formally entered as exhibits: the station test book, the receiving book,
Emmet Brant's private weigh book, and the state inspector's 1923 circuit notebook. The crate of
condemned glassware, sitting in the loft directly above two of the three sittings, was never among
them, and nothing in any source suggests the board ever examined it.

**Quantities:** three sittings; four exhibits; $3,800 (majority) vs. $3,500 (minority); 30
April/4 May 1925.

**Documents:** r02, r03, r05, r06, r07, r08, r13, r21, r23, r24.

## Story 8: The Crate in the Loft — Discovery and Vindication (1958–1959)

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie — Ansel Keddie's granddaughter, a graduate student at the State
Agricultural College at Farland — found the station's condemned 1897 pipette crate, still under a
tarpaulin in the loft over the old intake room, thirty-five years after a state inspector had
condemned it for failing to deliver its marked 17.6 c.c. charge. On 9 March 1958 she measured the
nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette: they delivered, on average, 16.72 c.c. — 95%
of the mark, short by one part in twenty. Because the fat test reads directly against a fixed
assumed charge, a pipette short by 5% yields a fat reading short by exactly 5%, consistently, for
as long as it is used. Applied to the station's recorded average of 3.61, the true average worked
out to 3.80 — matching, almost exactly, the independent Alder Corners cross-check data Rosalie
Cudd supplied the same year.

The finding was published in October 1958 as State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 471, under
Professor Leland Vaught's name jointly with Ivy Keddie's, concluding that "no milk was watered...
and no cream was stolen," and that seventy-six thousand pounds of uncredited butterfat "stands
credited in the Association's own ledgers... as station gain." The Ashlin Chronicle covered the
story on its front page (introducing, and then not correcting, an error calling Ivy Keddie
Ansel's "great-niece"); county agent Orin Falke filed an official report the following year
clearing Ansel Keddie's name, twenty-four years after his death and thirty-three after his
dismissal, and explicitly corrected both the "great-niece" error and his own initial concession
to the wet-hay theory. Ivy delivered "What the Crate Held" to the Ordell County Historical
Society at Ashlin in 1959, with the crate itself on the table.

**Quantities:** 9 pipettes measured; 16.72 c.c. average delivery vs. 17.6 c.c. mark; 3.61 recorded
vs. 3.80 true average; 1958 (discovery/bulletin), 1959 (talk, official report).

**Documents:** r04, r07, r11, r17, r19, r22.

# Section B — Relationships

## B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie

Granddaughter. Derived from Duncan Keddie's own written genealogy (r18: Ivy, born 1931, "makes
her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it
printed otherwise"), corroborated by r04 (her own self-presentation) and r24's editorial note.
Two sources point elsewhere — the Ashlin Chronicle (r19) calls her "great-niece," and county agent
Falke's report (r22) makes the identical error before appending an explicit correction ("I am
informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the
error on page two is mine"). The pattern — one error, then a traceable correction citing
verification — suggests "great-niece" was a single garbled fact (likely from a rushed telephone
interview or press release) that spread from the newspaper into the county report, rather than a
genuine competing genealogical claim. Resolved: granddaughter, with high confidence.

## B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant

Second cousins — children of first cousins Duncan Keddie and Roy Brant, who were themselves the
children of siblings Orra Keddie and Emmet Brant. This is affirmed by four independent
family-insider sources: Roy Brant's own account (r09), Duncan Keddie's explicit genealogy, which
specifically rules out "cousins once removed" (r18), Hazel Brant's own statement, which
specifically rules out "niece" and "cousin once removed" as things people mistakenly say (r17),
and Dell Rundle's 1959 footnote (r12). Against this stand two non-family, less direct sources: the
unnamed 1958 visitor in r01, said to be "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed," and Cleve
Oram's 1977 recollection (r21), also "first cousin once removed" — an outsider's report, admittedly
secondhand. The generational chain itself (Orra/Emmet siblings → Duncan/Roy first cousins →
Ivy/Hazel second cousins) is fully and consistently traced by the family members themselves,
leaving nothing genuinely open about the intervening generations except the separate, unrelated
question of whether Orra and Emmet were full or half siblings (see E1) — which does not change the
cousin arithmetic either way.

## B3. Selby Vose's father, and why it matters

Gideon Vose — the Association's founding first president (1889–1911, d. 1917) and author of its
by-laws, including Article VII. This is affirmed by Selby's own 1926 address (a primary,
autobiographical source), by Effie Loomis's secretarial account (r02), and by two explicit,
unprompted refutations of a "nephew" rumor from family-adjacent insiders — Duncan Keddie (r18: "the
son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew") and Bertram Kyle (r23: "the son, whatever is
said at Ashlin... I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense") —
against a minority of two (Vira Toland, r01; Dell Rundle, r12) who call him "nephew." It matters
because it means the manager who presided over the entire complaint period (1913–1938), who
personally warned patrons about watering their milk in 1919 and defended that theory at the 1925
hearing and in his own 1926 address, was not a distant relative brought in from outside but the
direct son and institutional heir of the man who had designed the payment-by-test system that made
the low readings costly in the first place — giving him a personal as well as an institutional
stake in an explanation that did not implicate the equipment his father's system depended on.

## B4. Relationship between the Vose family and the Keddie family

None, according to the most authoritative source. Duncan Keddie's own genealogy notes (r18,
titled "A thing that is said and is not so") state flatly that "there was no connection whatever
between us and the Voses," attributing the persistent rumor — that Orra Keddie and Gideon Vose's
wife were cousins — to confusion with an unrelated family, the "Ashlin Voses." Duncan explicitly
notes that his mother, a woman who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection,"
never once claimed a Vose connection. This directly contradicts Vira Toland's claim (r01) that
Orra was "first cousin" to "old Mrs. Vose," and Cleve Oram's vaguer, explicitly hedged "cousins of
some sort... I could not draw you the line on paper" (r21), itself secondhand from his mother.
Resolved: no relationship, favoring the direct, explicitly self-correcting family-insider source
over two vaguer, non-family, hearsay accounts.
# Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math

### C1

**(a) Proportion, and how it derives from the glassware.**
The pipettes delivered about 95% of their marked capacity — short by one part in twenty (5%).
Derivation: Ivy Keddie's 9 March 1958 measurement sheet averaged the nine surviving pipettes'
delivery at 16.72 c.c. against a calibrated burette, versus the 17.6 c.c. mark blown on their
stems: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95. Because the fat test is read directly as a percentage against a fixed,
assumed charge (17.6 c.c.), a pipette delivering only 95% of that charge yields a fat reading that
is also exactly 95% of the true value. This matches Bulletin 471's own published conclusion
(quoted identically in r04, r07, and r11 itself: "low by one part in twenty because its pipettes
were short by one part in twenty"). *Flagged conflict:* the same bulletin's own body text (r11,
Sections III/V) instead uses 16.27 c.c. (apparently a digit transposition of 16.72), giving a
92.44% ratio — but this contradicts the bulletin's own final conclusion, so it is rejected in
favor of the primary measurement sheet, which agrees with the document's own stated conclusion.

**(b) True average, and the shortfall.**
3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80. Shortfall = 0.19 points. This reproduces Ivy Keddie's own calculation exactly
and is independently corroborated by the Alder Corners cross-check (Rosalie Cudd's sixteen-season
data, averaging ~3.80) and separately stated as "nineteen hundredths" by Hazel Brant (r17) and
Orin Falke's official report (r22). Other gap estimates in the sources — 0.17 (Roy Brant's
long-run family estimate), 0.21 (Dr. Kepp), 0.295 (Vaught's own alternate body-text calculation) —
are treated as less reliable: the first two are looser field estimates without the burette
arithmetic behind them, and the third is the same internal error noted in (a).

**(c) Pounds not credited.**
76,000 lb of butterfat, over 25 full delivery years (matching the Association's own ledger
percentage framing — see C3). *Flagged conflict:* Bulletin 471's own concluding paragraph frames
the affected span as "1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923" (about 26 years), which includes partial years
at each end; the 76,000-lb total, however, is computed against a 25-FULL-year milk-intake total
(40,000,000 lb), so it necessarily covers a slightly narrower span than the full period the
glassware was physically in use (see C5a for why). A separate, larger figure (118,000 lb) appears
in Vaught's own Section V, built on the same 16.27 c.c./92.44% error rejected in (a); it is
rejected here for the same reason.

**(d) Money value and fraction received.**
76,000 lb × $0.25/lb (the widely corroborated average butterfat price "over the same period") =
$19,000. Patrons actually received $3,800 (the 1925 arbitration award — see Story 7 and E1 for
its four-source corroboration). Fraction received: $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20 = **one-fifth (20%)**
of the calculated loss.

### C2 — The largest patron's year, 1910 (the Brant farm)

**(a) Credited:** 200,000 lb delivered × 0.0342 (the recorded 3.42 test) = **6,840 lb** of
butterfat credited.

**(b) Owed and not credited:** True test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60 (matching Ivy Keddie's own stated
example, "herd recorded 3.42 was short 0.18, true ~3.60"). True fat = 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb.
Difference = 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb** not credited.

**(c) Value that year:** Using the price specifically cited for 1910 by Roy Brant (24 cents/lb,
from his father's own ledger context): 360 lb × $0.24 = **$86.40**.

### C3 — Over the full delivery years

**(a) Credited to patrons:** 40,000,000 lb total milk × 3.61% recorded average = **1,444,000 lb**
of butterfat credited. (This exactly matches the figure given directly in r04/r07: "5.25% of fat
credited (1,444,000 lb).")

**(b) Actually delivered/received at Larrow Green in the station's cream:** 1,444,000 ÷ 0.95 =
**1,520,000 lb**. (Matches r04/r07's stated "5% of fat delivered (1,520,000 lb).")

**(c) The difference as a percentage — and of what:** 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 lb. This is
**5%** of the amount actually delivered (76,000 ÷ 1,520,000), and separately **~5.25%** of the
amount credited to patrons (76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 0.0526). Both percentages describe the identical
76,000-lb gap; they differ only in which of the two totals is used as the denominator. The
ledger's own recurring "station gain... as usual" line uses the 5.25% framing (percentage of the
smaller, credited figure); Vaught's and Ivy Keddie's "one part in twenty" framing uses the 5%
figure (percentage of the larger, delivered figure).

### C4 — The invoice

**(a) Total:** **$77.93** — verified by summing the invoice's own line items: $25.20 (pipettes) +
$31.68 (bottles) + $0.90 (acid measure) + $18.00 (hand tester) + $2.15 (freight) = $77.93 exactly.
*Flagged conflict:* two independent narrators (r19, r20) instead state the total in prose as
"seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents" ($77.39) — a shared error, addressed further in E1;
notably, r20 reproduces the correct $77.93 invoice immediately above its own incorrect restatement.

**(b) Pipettes bought and shortfall:** **72 pipettes (6 dozen)**, at $0.35 each = $25.20 exactly,
per the invoice itself (reproduced independently from both the buyer's copy, r07/Item 2, and the
seller's own office copy, r20 — two independent copies of the same transaction, agreeing). This is
preferred over Vaught's (r11) and Cleve Oram's (r21) "five dozen" (60), since the invoice's own
arithmetic ($0.35 × 72 = $25.20) checks out exactly, and Vaught's document already contains a
demonstrated internal numeric error (see C1a). Shortfall per pipette: 17.6 c.c. marked − 16.72 c.c.
actual delivery = **0.88 c.c. short** (exactly 5% of 17.6, consistent with (a)).

### C5

**(a) Duration of faulty glassware vs. the money-calculation span, and why they differ.**
The faulty glassware was in continuous use for about **26 years and 6 weeks** — from 1 May 1897 to
12 June 1923 (the dates in Bulletin 471's own concluding paragraph, matching the invoice's
"into-use" date and Strawn's contemporaneous condemnation entry). The money calculation, by
contrast, runs over **25 full delivery years** (matching the 40,000,000-lb / 1,444,000-lb-credited
figures in C3). They differ because the money calculation is built from complete, ledger-closed
annual station totals, and necessarily excludes the partial year at the start (May–December 1897)
and the partial year at the end (January–June 1923), which don't correspond to whole accounting
years in the Association's own books.

**(b) First-season intake: cannot be reliably determined from the sources.** Two narrators give
conflicting, uncorroborated oral estimates for the station's first (1896) season: Hobart Sill
(r14) recalls "about nine hundred thousand pounds"; Bertram Kyle (r23) separately recalls "a good
eleven hundred thousand pounds" for "that first year" — but Kyle explicitly disclaims his figure is
"not in any book I ever ruled," i.e., an unverified personal recollection, as Sill's effectively
is too. With no surviving written record cited for that first partial season, and two
non-overlapping personal estimates, this figure is not settled by the sources; both estimates are
reported above rather than a single resolved number.

## Section D — Logic

### D1. The 1911 broken-measure interval as an experiment

**When and how long:** 8 May 1911 (station's own pipette broke) to 10 July 1911 (station's own
measure back in use) — a span of exactly 63 days, i.e., **nine weeks**. This is settled by
arithmetic against the dated bookends recorded in Ansel Keddie's own book (quoted in r03: "May 8...
July 10..."), which resolves the conflicting "six weeks" claimed decades later by Wilbur Teague
(r05) and Dr. Kepp (r16, citing an unnamed objector almost certainly Teague) as a memory error, not
a genuine alternate account — the calendar math is unambiguous once both dates come from the same
primary document.

**Variable, constant, result:** The variable was the pipette itself — swapped from the station's
own (short) glass to a measure borrowed from Larrow Green, then swapped back. Held constant: the
same tester (Keddie), the same patrons and herds, the same pasture and advancing season, the same
scales and acid. Result: the recorded test rose from ~3.61 to ~3.80 for the nine weeks, then fell
back the same day the station's own measure returned.

**What it establishes that calculation alone would not:** This is a real-time, controlled swap
that isolates the *instrument* as the causal variable, live, in 1911 — decades before anyone
suspected it — ruling out theft, watering, and seasonal/feed effects, because none of those
changed during the window, only the pipette did, and the test tracked the pipette. It is direct
causal evidence, not a retrospective calculation performed on 1958 measurements.

**What it does not establish:** The precise proportion of the shortfall (it shows the direction
and rough size of the effect — a rise of roughly the same ~0.19 points later derived
independently — but not an exact calibration), nor that the *borrowed* measure was itself
perfectly accurate rather than merely less short. The exact magnitude had to wait for the actual
burette measurement in 1958.

### D2. Independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream

1. **The 1911 natural experiment** (r03/r04, corroborated by r09): the same tester's recorded
   tests rose when using a borrowed measure and fell back when his own returned — inconsistent
   with any change in his personal behavior.
2. **The Brant weigh book's independent parallel record** (r09/r17/r18/r24): the farm's own scale
   readings agreed with the station's returned weight figures "day after day... barring a pound or
   two" for over thirty years — proving no volume of milk or cream went missing at the weighing
   stage, separate from the fat-test question entirely.
3. **The "heavy cream" fact from the receiving end** (r14/Sill, corroborated by the Association's
   own "station gain" ledger line in r05/r07/r12/r23): cream arrived at Larrow Green with *more*
   fat than the station's own paperwork had credited to patrons — the opposite of what theft at
   the station would produce, since stolen cream should arrive light, not heavy.

These are independent because they come from three separate document streams kept by three
different, uninvolved parties: the tester's own contemporaneous test book (reason 1), the Brant
family's private weigh book (reason 2), and Larrow Green's receiving-side records and the
Association's own general ledger (reason 3). Reasons 2 and 3 would still hold entirely without any
1958 measurement, since they rest on records that existed and could have been cross-checked
decades earlier; reason 1 also predates 1958, having been recorded live in 1911.

**Did the bookkeeper (Wilbur Teague) ever question his own accusation against these entries?** No
— the sources show no such reconciliation. Teague personally authored the very "station gain...
as usual" ledger lines that record cream arriving heavy (per r05 and r23), yet also argued the
theft theory before the 1925 board (per r05, confirmed in r13). Bertram Kyle (r23), who
physically carried these documents, states plainly that he could not recall anyone in that office
ever "laying the second beside the fourth and looking" — i.e., no one, including presumably
Teague, is shown cross-referencing the accusation against the ledger evidence that undercut it.

### D3. Did the manager know the tests ran low before 1923?

Yes, as to the *result* — but apparently not as to the *cause*. Selby Vose signed the annual
ledger sheets carrying the "station gain" line every year of his tenure from 1913 (r05, r23);
Strawn's 1913 visit notes him actively touring the platform. Most tellingly, Vose himself issued
the 1919 circular warning patrons about watered milk — a response that only makes sense if he
already knew, and had known for some time, that the recorded tests ran low; the entire premise of
the letter is the low-test fact. He continued defending the watered-milk explanation as late as
his 1926 address, years after the glass had already been condemned. However, Bertram Kyle (r23)
adds a specific qualification: Vose "read the totals and he never read the lines above them...
take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign" — suggesting that although he
signed off on paperwork every year that plainly showed cream arriving heavy (the very anomaly that
pointed toward the true cause), he may never have engaged closely enough with the underlying lines
to connect them to the low-test question he was otherwise actively worried about.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. The causal theory.** The Ostrey Hollow station's pipettes, bought new in 1897 from Ammon &
Sons of Tarnet, were manufactured short of their marked 17.6 c.c. capacity — delivering ~16.72
c.c., about 95% of the mark — almost certainly from a manufacturing defect (the supplier's own
historian, r20, theorizes a reground mold that shifted the gauge without being caught by routine
spot-checks, since a consistently wrong pipette passes a check for consistency). Because the fat
test reads directly against a fixed assumed charge, a pipette short by 5% yields a fat reading
short by exactly 5%, every time, for as long as that glass is used. This explains: **why that
size** (proportional directly to the manufacturing shortfall, not an arbitrary number); **why
constant year-round** (an instrument doesn't know the season); **why it began in May 1897** (the
exact date the new, defective glassware replaced accurate borrowed glass); **why it ended in June
1923** (the exact date a state inspector, equipped with a calibrated burette only since 1921,
finally tested the plant's own glass against an external standard); and **why nothing at the
station or on the farms had to change** — the cause was never in the milk, cows, hay, scales, or
the man, but in one piece of purchased equipment wrong from the day it arrived, catchable only by
an external calibrated standard nobody applied to that specific glass until 1923.

**The three competing explanations, their advocates, and the evidence that defeats each:**

1. **Theft (tester skimming cream)** — advanced by Wilbur Teague (r05, confirmed at the hearing
   per r13). Defeated by: the 1911 broken-measure experiment (same tester, tests still tracked the
   glass, not any change in his conduct); the Brant weigh book's independent weight agreement over
   thirty years; and, most decisively, the "heavy cream" fact — cream arrived at Larrow Green with
   *more* fat than credited, the opposite of what theft would produce.
2. **Added water by the patrons** — advanced by manager Selby Vose (r08, confirmed at the hearing
   per r13). Defeated by: state inspector Merle Strawn's own testimony that he tested for added
   water at every visit from 1908 forward and never once found any (r06, cited again in r22); the
   "heavy cream" fact undercuts this theory too, since dilution should reduce, not raise, delivered
   fat.
3. **Wet-hay/land theory** — advanced by the 1908 committee under Nyle Grigg (r15), elaborated by
   Dr. Roland Kepp (r16). Defeated by: Bulletin 214's actual, correctly-quoted finding of a
   0.05-point winter-only effect — roughly a quarter of the observed 0.19-point, year-round gap —
   and by the fact that its two chief boosters (Grigg and Kepp) both independently misquoted that
   same bulletin as "two tenths" to make it appear to agree.

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?** No evidence that it did. The crate had
been sitting in the loft since 1923, unremarked, and was not rediscovered until 1958 — 33 years
after the hearing. Frick's own contemporaneous exhibit list (r13) names only four items (test
book, receiving book, Brant weigh book, 1923 circuit notebook); the crate is absent. Bertram Kyle
(r23), who personally carried the exhibits, states he could not recall anyone cross-referencing
even the documents that *were* entered, let alone examining physical glassware that was not.
Frick's own closing reflection — wondering whether "a lawyer who understood chemistry might have
found" the answer — treats the true cause as something the board never got near.

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?** The sources conflict and are only partly
reconcilable. Duncan Keddie's account (r18) has his father privately saying, in 1911, "it was
something in the glass" — suggesting Ansel suspected the instrument decades before it was proven,
though he never committed the unproven suspicion to the book. Frick's memoir (r13) instead reports
a *different* private admission from the Ashlin corridor in 1925 — that Keddie "had always thought
the patrons were at fault." These are two distinct private confidences to two different people
(his son; the arbitration chairman) pointing toward two different suspects, and the sources give
no way to fully reconcile them — possibly reflecting a genuine shift in Keddie's own thinking
over fourteen unresolved years, or a limit in one or both secondhand accounts. This is flagged as
not fully settled.

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

The Larrow Valley cooperative paid farmers by a butterfat test, not weight. From 1896, the Ostrey
Hollow station's test ran low — a mystery blamed variously on theft, watered milk, and wet hay,
each theory pushed by a different narrator and each defeated by evidence the pushers themselves
recorded but didn't connect: weights that never went missing, cream that arrived heavy not light,
a feed effect four times smaller than claimed. A 1919 accusation split the valley and a family for
six years. A 1925 hearing paid patrons a fraction of their real loss and ended the tester's
career, never learning that the true cause — pipettes blown five percent short in 1897 — sat
crated in the station's own loft the whole time, twenty feet above the hearing. His granddaughter
found it in 1958. It took eight records, spanning four generations, because no single witness ever
held all the pieces at once.
