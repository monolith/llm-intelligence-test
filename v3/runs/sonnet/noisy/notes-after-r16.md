## Master cast (people) and relationships

- **Ansel Keddie** — tester at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station from its start (kept the test book, "in his own hand," 1896–1924, "twenty-nine seasons," 1896–1924). Married to **Orra Keddie** (née Brant). Dismissed 30 April 1925. Died "in the thirties" (r01) / died 1934 (r04). Father of **Duncan Keddie**; grandfather of **Ivy Keddie** (r04 narrator).
- **Orra Keddie** — Ansel's wife; sister of **Emmet Brant**; first cousin of **old Mrs. Vose** (per r01). After the 1919 letter, she and her brother Emmet never spoke again; she would walk the long way to Larrow Green rather than pass his lane.
- **Emmet Brant** — biggest patron on Hessel Bottom (most cows, most hay ground, hauled the most milk); Orra's brother; father of **Roy Brant**. Station's largest patron every recorded year (r02).
- **Roy Brant** — Emmet's son; inherited/kept the Brant weigh book after his father died; lent it to Ivy Keddie in 1958; later gave items to Hazel Brant (1962).
- **Hazel Brant** — received the Brant weigh book from Roy Brant in 1962; also, a 1958-visiting researcher (unnamed young woman from r01, see below) was "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed."
- **Gideon Vose** — first president of the Association, 1889–1911 (22 years, "no other man has held it so long"); drafted the by-laws himself (not a lawyer); father of **Selby Vose**; died 1917; wrote Article VII, adopted 1892.
- **old Mrs. Vose** — Gideon's wife (implied); first cousin of Orra Keddie (per r01).
- **Selby Vose** — Gideon's nephew (per r01) OR Gideon's son (per r02, r08: "my father, who was your first president"). **Direct conflict**: r01 (Vira Toland) calls Selby "old Gideon Vose's nephew"; r02 (Effie Loomis) and r08 (Selby's own address) identify him as Gideon's son. Selby Vose became manager in 1913, served until 1938 (per r02); as of his 1926 address he says "thirteen years this spring, since 1913." He signed the 1919 circular about "water in the cans." He kept Jerseys at "the home place"; in 1910 that herd tested 4.56.
- **Vira Toland** — narrator of r01, patron's daughter, born 1912 at the top of Ostrey Hollow, in her father's father's house; married and lived two years at Ashlin, otherwise lived in the Hollow her whole life. Interviewed on tape at her kitchen table in 1979 by a young man from the county.
- Vira's unnamed **father** — hauled milk to the Ostrey Hollow station; one of nine families; received the 1919 letter, read it at the table with his hat on (unusual for him), read it twice, put it in the desk drawer where it stayed till he died; his share of the eventual settlement "did not buy a hay rake." Vira's unnamed **mother** stood at the stove with her back to him the whole time he read the letter.
- Unnamed young woman researcher (mentioned in r01) — came to Ostrey Hollow in 1958 from "the college away east," poked about the shut-up station building (used for fence wire and salt storage), asked after the old business, was "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed." - **Wilbur Teague** — bookkeeper, came in 1905, retired 1945 (40 years); narrator of r05, writing Chapter Nine of unpublished memoir "The Books of the Association" at Ashlin, winter 1948. Worked at a sloped desk with a brass rail, office one room over a harness shop at Ashlin.

## Places

- **Ostrey Hollow** — a valley/hollow, site of the skimming station, at the head of the Hollow, "seven miles up the valley" from Larrow Green (r02, r05, r08). Road bends at the top of the Hollow then goes to nothing but the ridge (r01).
- **Hessel Bottom** — the wet meadow below the hamlet at Ostrey Hollow; seven farms had rights on it (r02) — **but see NEW MATERIAL: r09, r15, r16 all say SIX farms — a numeric conflict, 6 (three sources) vs 7 (one source).** Cut hay and pastured there; produced the best milk brought to the station; wet hay was blamed (wrongly) for low tests.
- **Larrow Green** — site of the main creamery, built 1891 on Larrow Creek (r02); the Association's creamery; "same building yet" as of 1941.
- **Ashlin** — town with the Association's office (one room over a harness shop, per r05); site of the 1925 board of arbitration hearings; site of the Ordell County Historical Society (1959 talk) and Historical Rooms (1972 catalogue); Duncan Keddie taught school there.
- **Larrow Valley** — the valley containing the Association's territory ("Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association").
- **Vessey County / Redlow Ridge / Alder Corners** — location of the Alder Corners Cheese Company, "over the ridge" from Ostrey Hollow, used as an independent comparison dataset.
- **Farland** — site of the State Agricultural College, where Ivy Keddie worked/studied, where Merle Strawn's notebooks were housed from 1947, and where the pipette measurements were taken (9 March 1958).
- **Tarnet** — location of "Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply," the equipment supplier (invoice dated 14 April 1897, per r07/Item 2).

## r11 — Bulletin No. 471 introduction/method, Leland Vaught, Farland, October 1958

Professor Leland Vaught's own bulletin text (not a secondhand quotation) — introduction, method, and conclusion.

- **Purpose:** explains 50 years of unexplained low tests at "one skimming station in Ordell County" via "a piece of glass" — the milk pipette, "the one instrument in the butterfat test which the plant cannot check against anything else."
- **Mechanism (Section II):** the test's four operations — pipette draws a measured charge into a test bottle; acid dissolves all but fat; bottle spun in hand tester; fat column read directly as a percentage. The reading is only valid because the charge is a FIXED, KNOWN quantity (17.6 c.c. standard in this state). If the pipette delivers less than its mark, the reading is low IN EXACT PROPORTION to the shortfall.
- **Three consequences stressed:** (1) the error is proportional, not additive — richer milk loses more absolutely; herds still rank correctly against each other, only the outside comparison is wrong; (2) the error is precise/consistent — a short pipette delivers exactly the same wrong amount every time, "for a quarter of a century," giving "the appearance of excellent work"; (3) the error is invisible from inside the plant — every other component (scales, tester speed, acid strength, bottle graduation) can be checked against an external standard, but the pipette itself IS the standard, so it "cannot be caught by measuring with it," only by an external burette — and burettes reached field inspectors very late (1921, per r06).
- **Section III, material examined:** "Of the lot bought for the Ostrey Hollow station in 1897 — **five dozen** pipettes were purchased — **nine** were recovered... unbroken and legible." Stems blown 17.6 c.c. "delivered against a calibrated burette, five runs to each pipette," mean delivery of the nine = **16.27 c.c.** (CONFLICTS with r04/r07's "16.72 c.c." from Ivy Keddie's own measurement sheet). States "The station's own glass went into use on **the first of May, 1898**" (CONFLICTS with r07/Item 2's "1 May 1897," and self-conflicts with Section VII's own "from the first of May, 1897").
- **Method description:** pipette filled to mark with room-temp water, tip wiped, delivered "the same interval of drainage, the same touch of the tip," into a certified burette; five deliveries per pipette, averaged. "Occupies an afternoon," no special apparatus beyond the burette needed. Vaught recommends every station in the state be checked this way.
- **Section IV, the 1911 interval:** treated as the single most important piece of evidence — "one variable changed," all else held constant (patrons, herds, pasture, season, acid, bottles, tester and habits); test rose 3.61→3.80 for nine weeks, reverted the day the station's own measure returned; "recorded contemporaneously... by a man who did not know what he was recording and set a question mark against it." Vaught: "lay unread for forty-seven years."
- **Section V, Magnitude:** ratio 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = **0.9244**. Recorded average 3.61; "true" average = 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = **3.905**, shortfall **0.295**. Applied to 40,000,000 lb over 25 full years: **118,000 lb** of uncredited butterfat. Vaught himself notes: "this figure is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the glassware." Also cites the ledger's "station gain" as 5.25% of fat actually delivered — this percentage matches the previously known figure exactly, even though the absolute-pound figure (118,000) does not match the previously known 76,000 lb.
- **Section VI, feed question:** quotes Bulletin No. 214 verbatim (matching r04/r07's quotation exactly): "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." States the observed discrepancy is present year-round and "some six times the largest effect the [214] trial could produce under conditions harsher than any farmer would impose."
- **Section VII, Conclusion (quoted in full, matches r04/r07/Item 15 word for word):** "*No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain.*"
- **MAJOR INTERNAL CONFLICT WITHIN THIS SINGLE DOCUMENT:** The bulletin's own body (Section V) computes a shortfall ratio of 0.9244 (i.e., about 1 part in 13, not "one part in twenty" = 0.95) and a total of 118,000 lb — yet its own concluding paragraph (Section VII) states the shortfall was "one part in twenty" (i.e., ratio 0.95, matching the OTHER set of numbers used everywhere else in r04/r07 — 16.72 c.c., 95%, 76,000 lb) and "seventy-six thousand pounds." In other words: Section III/V's numbers (16.27 c.c., 0.9244, 3.905, 0.295, 118,000 lb) do NOT match Section VII's numbers (implicitly 16.72 c.c., 0.95, 76,000 lb, "one part in twenty") — even though both appear in the same bulletin under the same author's name. This is the single largest internal numeric inconsistency found in the entire set of sources.

## r12 — "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years," Dell Rundle, schoolteacher, 1939 (reprinted 1959)

Dell Rundle, schoolteacher at Larrow Green (not Ashlin — distinct from Duncan Keddie, who teaches at Ashlin), writes a jubilee pamphlet for the Association's 50th year, compiled from oral recollections and store-porch conversations.

- **I. The Men of 1889:** Gideon Vose elected first president; described as "heavy, quiet, obstinate," "had once thought of the law and thought better of it"; wrote the by-laws himself at his own kitchen table. **His NEPHEW Selby Vose** (matching r01, conflicting with r02/r08's "son") "used to say that his uncle wrote by-laws the way other men build stone wall." Creamery built 1891 "on the creek below the mill"; valley began being paid in cash monthly for the first time.
- **II.** Article VII quoted in full (matches other sources word for word). Rundle states "Article VII dates from 1891, the year the churn was set up" — a THIRD date for the by-law's adoption (alongside r02's 1892 and r15/Grigg's 1893).
- **III.** Station opened 1896 at Ostrey Hollow; "seven miles up from the Green"; "three hours in mud" pull, impassable in March. Building: "boarded and battened," intake room at road end, a loft above "never... cleared out"; creek behind has "twice come over" the sill in wet Aprils.
- **IV. The Trouble:** From 1902, complaints; not believed. 1908 committee under **Nyle Grigg** (name given here too), sampled through the winter, blamed wet hay. 1919 circular; "the two ends of this valley did not speak civilly to one another for six years" (i.e., 1919–1925). "It is the settled opinion of the valley that cream left that station by the back door" — Rundle initially records the theft theory as accepted local lore, saying he's "never heard it contradicted at the store." States the inspector condemned the glassware "on the twelfth of July, 1923" (matching r08, conflicting with r02/r06/r07's "12 June"). January 1925: patrons petitioned; **total intake "forty-four million pounds" (conflicting with the 40-million figure), patrons' loss set out "to the dollar" as $19,600.** Board of arbitration under Alonzo Frick that spring; "found nothing established," awarded compensation "without admitting anything" (amount not given in main text).
- **V. Over the Ridge:** States the Alder Corners Cheese Company was "opened in 1914" (conflicting with r10's "1913") by Jerome Cudd; some Hessel Bottom men hauled there in summer, "resented here." States "It is Jerome Cudd's daughter, Rosalie Cudd, who makes the cheese at Alder Corners now" — confirms DAUGHTER, corroborating r10, contradicting r04/r07's "niece."
- **Footnote [1], added to the 1959 reprint:** "The Hollow matter has since been taken up again at the college at Farland by Miss Ivy Keddie, who is second cousin to Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom." (Corroborates r09's family-tree claim exactly.) "**Eleven** pipettes out of the station's old condemned crate were found still to be in existence." (CONFLICTS with the "nine" figure used everywhere else — r04, r07, r11.) "I am too old to rewrite a pamphlet and I refer the reader to the college. — D.R."
- **VI. Fifty Years:** closing reflection — "fifty Junes of cream... fifty Januaries... a check every month... Whatever this valley did wrong at the head of it, it did this right."
- **"Note Added While In Press"** (crucial): Rundle, while the pamphlet was being typeset, was allowed to see the Association's old ledgers "on another errand altogether." He quotes the "station gain" entry (57,760 credited / 60,800 received / 3,040 gain / 5.25%, "as usual" — matching r05/r07/r11's wording exactly). Rundle then explicitly RETRACTS his own Chapter IV theft narrative: "I must go back on Chapter IV. Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand. I leave it as written and correct it here." — Rundle independently (and inadvertently) discovers the same key evidence Vaught's bulletin would later use, nineteen years before Bulletin 471, but without arriving at the pipette explanation himself — he only gets as far as ruling out theft.

## r13 — "Recollections of the Ordell Arbitration," Alonzo Frick, 1946

Alonzo Frick, attorney (age 74 in 1946), writes for the Ordell County Bar Association yearbook about chairing the 1925 board of arbitration.

- Frick has "tried titles and boundaries for forty years, and two homicides," yet none stayed with him like this arbitration — "no appeal from it, no reported decision, and nothing whatever to cite it for."
- States the Ostrey Hollow patrons "had been paid, year upon year, on an average test of **3.16**" (matches r05/Teague's conflicting figure, not the majority 3.61).
- Petition filed January 1925; board of three constituted, Frick named chairman "because I was the one of the three who kept no cow." **Board sat THREE times:** first session at Ashlin, 17 March 1925; the second and third Frick specifically arranged to hold AT OSTREY HOLLOW ITSELF, in the station's own weigh room, so the board could inspect the plant directly rather than "hearing it described by men with an interest in the description." Frick admits: "I am a lawyer and the thing is chemistry... I did not understand the test then and do not understand it now," but could see the test "was done the same way twice" and the operators "were not hiding their hands."
- **Three explanations presented to the board, each attributed to a specific person:**
  1. Water — Selby Vose (the manager), "without heat and without proof," standing by his earlier circular.
  2. Theft (tester skimming cream to cover the loss) — Wilbur Teague (the bookkeeper), argued indirectly/structurally, "in the way an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it," making "no charge against anybody."
  3. The hay/land theory — via the 1908 committee report being read into the record, occupying "the better part of an afternoon."
- **Ansel Keddie:** tested since 1896, statement to the board 17 March 1925 — "he had tested as he was taught... thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons," could not explain the figures, "had never taken a pound of cream out of that station." Frick: "I believed him then and I believe him now... a man who cannot account for a figure is presumed by everybody in the room to be the figure's author."
- **New, striking detail — a private admission NOT in the minutes:** In the Ashlin corridor, Keddie privately told Frick "that he had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the room." Frick adds: it has since been said that Keddie "went to his grave accusing nobody, which is true, and that he suspected nobody, which is not [true]" — i.e., Frick explicitly states the common later characterization of Keddie as suspecting no one is FALSE, based on this private conversation.
- **Dismissal and award:** Keddie dismissed 30 April 1925, FOUR DAYS before the award, while the board was "still sitting"; "There was no finding against him from us and there never was one... he was simply let go." Frick: "I have never been able to think well of the Association for it."
- **4 May 1925 award:** $3,800, "without admission," found "no cause established." **Patrons' own reckoning of loss: "sixty-seven thousand pounds of butter fat"** (yet another distinct total figure, alongside the Association's ledger 76,000 lb and Vaught's calculated 118,000 lb).
- **Exhibit list, reprinted from Frick's own file — only FOUR items (far fewer than the 1972 catalogue's 16):**
  1. The test book of the Ostrey Hollow station, 4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924, in the hand of Ansel Keddie.
  2. The receiving book of said station.
  3. The private weigh book of Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom, begun 1 January 1897.
  4. The circuit notebook of the state dairy inspector for the year 1923.
- Frick's closing reflection: three explanations were offered, "each... urged by a man of standing," "each... would have been believed by half the valley," "not one of them was proved." He wonders whether "a lawyer who understood chemistry might have found" the true answer — a poignant near-miss, since the pipette explanation existed in the physical evidence (the crate) all along but wasn't found until 1958.

## r14 — Remarks of Hobart Sill, buttermaker (ret.), Larrow Green, 1934

Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green for 41 years (1893–1934), gives oral remarks on his retirement day.

- Came 1893; "no floor in this room but plank," cooled with creek ice. Never sent out butter he wouldn't eat himself.
- **Key physical testimony:** the cream wagon from Ostrey Hollow came down six days a week (not Sunday), 7 miles, arriving warm in July. Sill re-weighed every can himself in the cold room (never trusting another man's figure) and recorded it in the receiving book, "my hand on every page of it from ninety-three." **The Hollow's cream consistently arrived "heavy"** — more butterfat in the cans than the station's own paperwork said had been bought. "Always more. Never less." (This is the same phenomenon underlying the "station gain" ledger entries and is the chief physical evidence against the theft theory, though Sill himself does not draw that conclusion.)
- Larrow Green's own local patrons tested "3.78 taking one year with another." Sill's own estimate of the Hollow's TRUE test: "**3.85** and no better" — "if the figures they were paid on came out under that, then somebody up that hill road was helping the cans along" — Sill personally endorses the WATER-ADULTERATION theory ("I said so at the time and I say it now"), an apparent inconsistency with his own "heavy cream" testimony (adding water to milk would not explain cream arriving with MORE fat than credited; if anything the "heavy" fact is evidence against both theft and, arguably, is not obviously explained by watering either — it is the instrument-error/pipette-shortfall explanation, per Vaught, that directly explains it). Sill does not appear to recognize this tension in his own account.
- Recalls the 1902 first complaint: "a man in here shouting that his milk tested under his brother-in-law's down the creek" (possibly referencing the Brant/Keddie brother-in-law relationship, though not explicitly named).
- 1919: "the manager put out a paper... a circular, warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons about water in the milk... I thought well of him for it. It was the only plain thing that office ever did... of course they hated him for it up the Hollow."
- Butterfat price: "twenty-seven cents the pound... take one year with another" (a third, higher price figure vs. the 24–25 cent figures elsewhere — corroborated independently by r16/Kepp).
- **New origin story for the station's 1896 opening-day glassware:** the station's own ordered outfit had NOT arrived when they were ready to open in May 1896; there was talk of holding off the whole season; Sill personally went to his own bench at Larrow Green and made up an outfit from his OWN glass, which went up the valley on the wagon; the station opened on Sill's borrowed glass; "their own came the spring after," and Sill's glass was returned to him, "one piece of it... chipped" — this corroborates and adds first-person detail to the test book's own first leaf ("Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes").
- States the station's FIRST SEASON (May–end of 1896, a partial year) took in "about nine hundred thousand pounds" of milk — "a good deal of milk for a hill station starting from nothing."
- Sill: "I never went up there but twice in my life." Retiring at 64 (implying birth ~1870). Closing instruction: "Put in that Hobart Sill made the butter and it was good butter... Do not put in that I said anything against the cows up there, because I did not. The cows were never the trouble."

## r15 — Covering letter and reprinted 1908 report, Nyle Grigg, 1919

Nyle Grigg, a Larrow Green farmer (not a Hessel Bottom patron; hauls to Larrow Green himself, no financial stake either way), reprints his own 1908 committee report in 1919 because the Ostrey Hollow complaint has been "reopened this season," and adds a covering letter.

- Grigg states he "consent[s] willingly" to reprinting and has "nothing in it to take back."
- Recaps: from 1902, grumbling that Hollow milk tested under the rest of the valley; "it was not one man and it was not one season." States "**Payment by test came in with the 1893 meeting**, as I have always understood it" (hedged, but a THIRD distinct date for the by-law's introduction, alongside r02's 1892 and r12's 1891).
- States the committee (three members, Grigg chairman, others unnamed) was appointed that winter (1907–08) and given a duty Grigg "never liked."
- **Grigg's own covering-letter recollection of the sampling's scope: "We sampled those herds through the whole of 1908 — every month of that year, in the barn and on the pasture, out of the night's milk and the morning's."** — THIS DIRECTLY CONTRADICTS the report he is himself reprinting immediately below, which states: "We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through **January, February and March**." This is a striking internal self-contradiction within a single document — Grigg's 1919 memory of the 1908 investigation's scope does not match the report's own 1908 text.
- Grigg states he has "no interest whatever in the answer," is not an Ostrey Hollow patron, has "been abused for the finding by men on both sides of it," which he takes as "a fair sign that the finding was an honest one."
- **Cites Bulletin No. 214 (published after the 1908 report) as vindication, but MISQUOTES its finding as: "the bog hay to depress the butter fat test by two tenths in the winter feeding."** (The bulletin's actual, verbatim-quoted-elsewhere finding is "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing... in summer" — Grigg's "two tenths" is four times the actual "five hundredths" figure.)
- Urges the remedy: "Drain that bottom, sweeten it, keep the stock off it... feed the coarse hay... with a mixed ration."
- **The reprinted 1908 report itself (full text):** Committee "appointed in January last" (i.e., January 1908). Complaint: Ostrey Hollow milk tests below the rest of the valley, "since payment by test came in among us." Describes Hessel Bottom: **SIX farms** cut hay/pasture there (matching r09/r16, conflicting with r02's "seven"); ground "springy the year round," under water "in part every spring"; herbage "sedge, rush, and the coarse sour grasses"; hay "cut late and heavy," fed November–April. Committee "visited each of the six farms, some of them three and four times," drew and sealed their own samples, watched milking/can-filling/hauling/weighing/testing, tried station scales against a known weight and against patrons' own scales, inquired into water supply at each barn. Observed herd body condition: "thin in the flank," "slow to come to their flesh," "coats... rough," concluding "what such land gives in quantity it withholds in quality." **Explicitly states: "We report that we found no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter, and we wish that finding to be printed in the same type as the rest."** Final sentence (matching the version already quoted in r03/r07/Item 11 exactly): "We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March. The milk is whole and the scales are honest. It is our opinion that the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and that the remedy lies with the station." [Note: r15's version says "lies with the land and not with the station" per the report's summary line at the very end of the document, consistent with all other quotations of this line.]
- Grigg's closing line: the report "was received and accepted, and the question was closed by it for eleven years — until this season, and the men who have reopened it have brought nothing new before us but their impatience."

## r16 — "The Bog Meadows and the Milk," Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S., 1926 (paper to Ordell County Farm Club, with floor discussion)

Dr. Roland Kepp, veterinary surgeon of Ashlin, 30 years' practice, reads a paper arguing the wet-hay/mineral-deficiency theory is scientifically settled.

- General theory: wet, poorly-drained, acid, lime-and-phosphate-poor bottomland produces coarse, late-cut, mineral-poor hay; cows on such feed "rob" their own bodies to maintain milk output, sacrificing butterfat first ("the dearest thing the cow makes") while milk VOLUME stays high or even increases ("Wet land is generous of flow"). Describes typical symptoms: thin over the loin, rough coat, slow spring recovery, digestive disorders in fall, later breeding, worse feet.
- Claims from his own 30 years' field observation: "wet bottoms of this county yield a milk poorer in fat by **a fifth of a point** [0.20]... farm for farm, wherever you look for it... depends on the ground" (not breed or management).
- **Hessel Bottom specifically:** SIX farms (matches r09/r15, conflicts with r02's seven). States the gap "as it has been given to me and as I have satisfied myself" is "**twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21**" between the station and "the milk of this valley at large" — yet another distinct numeric estimate of the gap (alongside 0.17 [r09], 0.19 [r04/Alder Corners cross-check], 0.295 [Vaught's own calc]).
- Cites the 1908 committee (not naming Grigg in the main text) as having found "the herds sound, the milk whole, and the scales honest."
- **Cites Bulletin No. 214, MISQUOTED exactly as in r15: "depressed by two tenths in the winter feeding."** Kepp explicitly triangulates: "Two tenths from the College, laid alongside the fifth of a point I have found in the field and the twenty-one hundredths that station has been complaining of, and the three agree so nearly that I do not know what more a reasonable man requires." (NOTE: this "triangulation" is logically suspect — the "two tenths" Bulletin 214 figure is itself a shared misquotation with r15, meaning it is not actually independent evidence; and 0.20/0.21/0.20 are only "agreeing" if one accepts the erroneous restatement of Bulletin 214, whose real, verbatim-quoted value elsewhere is 0.05 in winter and 0 in summer.)
- **Addresses the 1911 anomaly directly as "an objection... urged upon me, by a gentleman"** (unnamed, but almost certainly Wilbur Teague, matching Teague's structural-suspicion argument and his own "six weeks" figure): states the testing was "broken off for **six weeks**" (siding with Teague/r05 against the majority "nine weeks") in "June and July," dismisses it as unremarkable given it was flush season with the best pasture: "I could produce you six weeks in any year that would prove anything you liked."
- Remedy proposed: drain, lime, reseed, keep stock off wet spring ground, feed a mixed ration with mineral supplement per "the College."
- Cites butterfat price: "twenty-seven cents the pound through these years" — matches r14/Sill's figure exactly (both independent of the 24–25 cent cluster).
- **Floor discussion (appended, taken down by the club secretary):**
  - A member asks whether the difference might be in the COWS/poorer stock rather than the land. Kepp rejects this: some of the best-bred cattle stand on wet land; some indifferent cattle on hills test well; "a man may improve his herd for twenty years and never get his fat up while his ground is sour."
  - A member asks whether the trouble might lie in THE STATION rather than the bottom, "as had been said in the valley for years." Kepp explicitly declines competence: "he had never in his life read a test and would not know how"; argues by convergence of causes (soil, herbage, stock condition, the College trial, "every wet bottom in the county alike") that it is "not good sense to go looking for a second cause in a building" — i.e., Kepp dismisses the very explanation (pipette/instrument error) that Ivy Keddie and Vaught would establish 32 years later, in 1958.
  - Chairman thanks Kepp; paper to be printed for members.
