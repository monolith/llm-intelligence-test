## Section A — Reconstruction: the last two of the eight stories

I don't know where Batches 1–2 drew their story boundaries. Based on the internal chronology, the most natural eight-way division of the Ostrey Hollow saga runs: (1) founding of the Association, (2) the station's opening and its own glassware arriving, (3) early operation and first complaints, (4) the 1908 wet-hay investigation, (5) the 1911 broken-measure episode, (6) the 1919 circular and the Brant rift plus the 1923 glass condemnation, (7) the 1925 arbitration and dismissal, (8) the 1958 discovery, exoneration, and the scattering of the documents. If Batches 1–2 covered (1)–(6), these are (7) and (8) — the two that come last in time. Flagging this assumption explicitly since I can't verify it.

### Story 1: The 1925 Petition, Arbitration, and Dismissal

In January 1925, patrons of the Ostrey Hollow station — Emmet Brant's name among them, the tester's own brother-in-law — filed a formal petition against the station over decades of milk-fat readings running below the valley's other creamery. Rundle's pamphlet and the Ashlin Chronicle put the cumulative loss at 44 million pounds of milk worth $19,600; Frick's memoir separately cites a patrons'-own estimate of 67,000 pounds of butterfat (different units — not necessarily inconsistent, just uncorrelated in the sources).

The Association convened a three-member board of arbitration chaired by Ashlin attorney Alonzo Frick, chosen, he said, "because I was the one of the three who kept no cow." It sat from 17 March to 4 May 1925: one session at Ashlin, two more at Ostrey Hollow itself in the station's own weigh room, patrons standing outside in the road — confirmed independently by Frick, by Cleve Oram, and by tester Ansel Keddie's wife Orra in her diary (which records the 17 March start, "in his good coat").

Three theories were argued: manager Selby Vose's water theory; bookkeeper Wilbur Teague's cream-theft theory, raised obliquely despite his own ledgers arguing against it; and a revived wet-hay theory from Hessel Bottom's boggy meadow, first floated by an investigating committee in 1908. Four documents were entered as exhibits — the station's 1896–1924 test book, its receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book (begun 1897), and the state inspector's 1923 circuit notebook, which recorded that year's condemnation of the station's own measuring glass — but by both Frick's and clerk Bertram Kyle's account, no one in the room set the receiving book beside the inspector's notebook and compared them. The crate of condemned pipettes itself, sitting in the loft directly overhead during two of the sessions, was never produced or examined; it would not surface for another 33 years.

The board awarded the patrons $3,800 (best-attested figure — see Section E) and found no cause established against Keddie personally. Four days later, on 30 April 1925, the Association dismissed him anyway, ending 29 seasons. He walked home at his ordinary hour, hung his apron behind the shed door, and ate the ham waiting for supper — per Orra's diary. He died in 1934, still under the unresolved cloud.

### Story 2: The Crate, Bulletin 471, and the Scattering of the Books

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie — Ansel's granddaughter, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland — found a forgotten crate of milk-test pipettes in the Ostrey Hollow station's loft. It was the station's original outfit, bought new from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet in 1897 and put into service 1 May 1897, condemned by the state dairy inspector in June 1923 after 26 years' use, then stored rather than discarded. Nine pipettes survived (a couple of sources say eleven, but nine is independently corroborated by Cleve Oram's recollection and by county agent Orin Falke's 1959 report).

On 9 March 1958, Ivy measured the nine survivors against a calibrated burette at Farland. Each was marked to deliver a standard charge of 17.6 cubic centimeters — the charge a test bottle's graduated neck is scaled for — but the group averaged only 16.27 cc. A short charge run through glassware graduated for a full one reads a proportionally low fat percentage every time, in any season, regardless of who tests it or what the cows eat. That mechanical fact accounted for the station's entire 27-year deficiency: a long-run average of 3.61 against the comparable Larrow Green creamery's 3.78 — a gap of 0.19 percentage points, constant in every month of the year.

Ivy corresponded with Rosalie Cudd of the rival Alder Corners Cheese Company for comparison figures and borrowed Emmet Brant's old weigh book from his son Roy Brant to cross-check it against the station's own numbers. The college published the findings as Bulletin No. 471 in October 1958, co-authored by Ivy Keddie and Professor Leland Vaught, formally clearing Ansel Keddie's name — 24 years after his death and 33 years after his dismissal; his widow Orra had died in 1948 and never saw it. The Ashlin Chronicle ran the story on its front page 3 October 1958. Falke's 1959 county report independently reconfirmed the finding, while also correcting his own draft's repetition of a widespread error about Ivy's relationship to Ansel. Between 1959 and 1970 the surviving physical record — the crate, the station's test book, the Brant weigh book, and the Alder Corners test book — was distributed among three different local historical societies, closing out the paper trail the whole 1958 finding had depended on.

## Section E — Contradictions (E1)

### Direct contradictions between retellings

1. **Number of Hessel Bottom farms with grazing rights:** "seven" (Loomis, r02) vs. "six" (Roy Brant r09, Grigg's 1908 report r15, Kepp r16, Hazel Brant r17, the Chronicle r19). **Resolved: six** — five independent sources, including a primary 1908 committee report, against one.

2. **Article VII adoption date:** 1891 (Rundle r12) / 1892 (Loomis r02, curator Nym r07) / 1893 (Grigg r15). **Cannot be settled** — Falke's 1959 report explains why (the printed by-laws carry no date, and the founding-era minute book was lost in an office move) but does not resolve it.

3. **Date the Ammon & Sons outfit came from Tarnet:** 1897 (established: primary invoice dated 14 April 1897, plus Orra Keddie's diary entry dated exactly 1 May 1897) vs. "ninety-nine" [1899] (Hazel Brant, r17). **Resolved for 1897** — primary transactional document plus a same-day diary entry outweighs one secondhand recollection decades later; the conflict is otherwise unaddressed by any source.

4. **Ansel Keddie's private theory of the cause:** "something in the glass" (per son Duncan, witnessed 1911) vs. patrons/water at fault (per Frick's account of a 1925 corridor remark). **Cannot be fully settled** — both are secondhand reports of private statements at different times; treated in the notes as an unreconciled tension rather than a resolved question. See Section F.

5. **Keddie/Emmet Brant relationship in later years:** "argued about drainage for forty years" (Hazel Brant r17) vs. "stopped speaking after 1919" (Roy Brant r09, vividly corroborated by Orra's diary's 1919 yard-confrontation entry). **Leans toward the 1919 break** (two firsthand-family sources, one contemporaneous) but the notes flag this as a soft tension, not a hard contradiction — the two could coexist if the drainage arguments predated 1919.

6. **Orra Brant/Emmet Brant siblingship:** full siblings (Roy Brant r09 and Duncan Keddie r18, both firsthand) vs. half-siblings "by his father's second wife" (Hazel Brant r17, secondhand). **Resolved: full siblings** — two independent firsthand accounts against one secondhand one.

7. **Selby Vose's relation to Gideon Vose:** son (majority consensus) vs. nephew (Rundle r12, lone claim). **Resolved: son** — Duncan Keddie explicitly names and rebuts the nephew claim, and clerk Bertram Kyle (r23) independently calls it "nonsense."

8. **A Keddie–Vose family connection:** explicitly denied by Duncan Keddie (r18: "no connection whatever... two women in this town" started the rumor) vs. repeated as apparently credible by Cleve Oram (r21: "cousins of some sort... never heard it contradicted"). **Leans toward no connection** (Duncan is a direct family member giving a pointed denial; Oram's is inherited hearsay two generations removed), but preserved as an open conflict since no source definitively disproves it.

9. **Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel:** granddaughter (Roy Brant r09, r12's footnote, Hazel Brant r17, Duncan Keddie r18, and Falke's own corrected note in r22) vs. great-niece (the Chronicle r19, and the uncorrected main body of Falke's own r22 report). **Resolved: granddaughter** — Falke's report contains and then retracts the error itself after verifying with the family, which is about as strong a self-correction as the corpus offers, on top of a firsthand-family majority.

10. **Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant:** second cousins (Duncan Keddie r18, who explicitly rules out "cousins once removed" and "aunt and niece") vs. first cousin once removed (Cleve Oram r21). **Leans toward second cousins** — Duncan is Ivy's own father and states the relationship with unusual, deliberate precision; Oram is an outside oral-history narrator. Preserved as an open conflict per instructions.

11. **Rosalie Cudd's relation to Jerome Cudd:** daughter (Rosalie Cudd's own account r10, Duncan Keddie r18, general consensus) vs. niece (the Chronicle r19). **Resolved: daughter** — majority plus a firsthand source (Rosalie's own telling), against a single newspaper error.

No other source supports this, and the 1925 board is independently described elsewhere (Frick, Kyle) as a distinct three-person body formed for that purpose. **Resolved as a Chronicle error** — the claim is unsupported and implausible on its face (17 years apart, no shared membership stated anywhere else).

### Cases where two narrators carry the same wrong value

- **The Ammon & Sons invoice total.** The invoice itself, reproduced verbatim by Junia Ammon (r20), lists five line items — $25.20 + $31.68 + $0.90 + $18.00 + $2.15 — which sum to **$77.93**, and the invoice's own printed total says $77.93. But Junia Ammon's own restated total elsewhere in the same document says **$77.39**, and the Chronicle (r19) independently states $77.39 too. **What breaks the tie is arithmetic, not source-counting**: adding the five line items by hand gives $77.93, matching the primary document's own printed total. I flag one complication the instructions specifically warn about: r19 (1958) postdates r20's underlying 1897 invoice by decades but also postdates nothing about r20 itself (r20 was written in 1949, nine years before the Chronicle piece) — so it's possible the Chronicle simply drew the wrong figure from an existing company record rather than arriving at $77.39 independently. I can't rule that out, so I don't count this as two truly independent confirmations of $77.39 — only arithmetic settles it, and arithmetic says $77.93.

- **"Great-niece" instead of "granddaughter" for Ivy Keddie.** The Chronicle (r19, October 1958) and the main body of Falke's own report (r22, 1959) both state Ivy was Ansel's great-niece. Falke's report was written after the Chronicle piece and covers the same event, so I can't be confident these are independent errors rather than one error copied into the second document — another case the instructions flag. Regardless, the tie is broken by Falke's own appended correction, made after he "verified" with the family, plus the firsthand-family majority (Duncan Keddie, Roy Brant, Hazel Brant) — not by treating two appearances of "great-niece" as two independent votes.

### Cases where a narrator contradicts itself on the page

- **r03 (Ansel Keddie's own letter):** says the glass was "borrowed off the Ashlin creamery," while the station's own book's first-leaf entry (also part of r03's material) says "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green." Resolved toward Larrow Green — corroborated independently at least five times elsewhere, including Orra's diary.
- **r12 (Dell Rundle's 1939 pamphlet):** Chapter IV states the cream-theft narrative as settled fact, then a "NOTE ADDED WHILE IN PRESS" retracts it after Rundle saw the Association's actual ledgers — nineteen years ahead of Bulletin 471 reaching the same conclusion. Self-correction, not resolved by outside evidence but by the author's own second look.
- **r18 (Duncan Keddie's memoir):** states his mother Orra was born 1868 and died 1948 "at eighty-two" — an arithmetic mismatch (1948−1868=80, not 82) that r18 does not itself notice. - **r22 (Orin Falke's 1959 county report):** paragraph 9 partially credits the wet-hay theory as "a real if partial factor"; the report's own appended postscript, written after Falke actually obtained Bulletin No. 214's data, explicitly withdraws that concession with the arithmetic (a maximum 0.05-point winter effect and no summer effect cannot account for a constant 0.19-point deficiency). Also independently: the main body of r22 calls Ivy Keddie a "great-niece," and its own appended note corrects this to "granddaughter." Two self-corrections within the same document.

## Section F — Theory (F1)

**What actually happened.** The Ostrey Hollow station's original testing outfit, bought new from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet and put into service 1 May 1897, came from a lot of milk-test pipettes made from a mold that had been reground shortly before manufacture (per Junia Ammon's candid account, r20) — an accident that produced pipettes that consistently delivered less milk than their 17.6 cc mark, and did so *consistently*, which is exactly why no spot-check ever caught it: a sample of the lot checked against itself looks fine, because the whole lot is wrong in the same direction by the same amount. Every butterfat test run on that glass for the next 26 years used a short charge of milk in a bottle graduated for a full one, which reads a proportionally low fat percentage regardless of the milk's true fat content, the season, the cows, or the honesty of anyone involved. Measured in 1958, the nine surviving pipettes averaged 16.27 cc against their 17.6 cc mark — a real, physical shortfall, not a theory.

- **Why that size, roughly:** the station's long-run average (3.61) sat a constant 0.19 percentage points below the comparable Larrow Green creamery's average (3.78), in every month of the year alike. The sources assert, as a matter of stated fact (the Chronicle's summary and Falke's report both say so directly), that this constant gap is exactly what a fixed, proportional under-measure produces — it doesn't vary with season because the defect is in the glass, not in anything seasonal. I'll flag one thing the sources don't do: nobody in the material walks through the arithmetic connecting 16.27/17.6 (a ~7.6% volumetric shortfall) to the observed 0.19-point gap (roughly 5% of 3.78). A naive proportional calculation using those two headline numbers doesn't land on exactly the same percentage — plausibly because the nine pipettes measured in 1958 aren't necessarily representative of the full historical range of shortfall across the whole original set, or because thirty-five years of storage affected the glass. This is a minor unreconciled quantitative loose end even within the accepted explanation, not a fatal flaw in it.
- **Why it began when it did:** it began because the defective lot went into service on 1 May 1897, replacing the station's earlier borrowed glass (from Larrow Green/buttermaker Hobart Sill), which by all indications tested true.
- **Why it ended when it did:** the state dairy inspector tested the station's pipettes against a calibrated burette on 12 June 1923, found they under-delivered, condemned the lot on the spot, and new glass was in use within a month. From 1924 the station's average rose to 3.85 and stayed there.
- **Why nothing at the station or on the farms had to change either time:** because the cause was never behavior — not watering, not theft, not feed — it was a fixed manufacturing defect in a physical instrument. Replacing the instrument fixed the reading without anyone changing what they did.

**The three theories argued, who advanced each, and what defeats each:**

1. **Water added to the milk** — advanced by manager Selby Vose (formalized in his 1919 circular and defended in his 1926 annual address). Defeated by the state inspector's own lactometer testing record: per Falke's report, the inspector tested for added water at every visit from 1908 forward and never once found any.
2. **Cream theft** — advanced obliquely by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague before the 1925 board, and privately held (never stated under oath) by the cream hauler, father of narrator Cleve Oram. Defeated by the 1911 episode: when the station's own measuring instrument broke and a substitute was used for nine weeks (8 May–10 July), the recorded average rose to 3.80 and then reverted to 3.61 once the original equipment was back — the same tester, under the same supervision, with no change in anyone's opportunity to steal cream. A theft theory cannot explain why the number tracked the equipment.
3. **Wet hay from Hessel Bottom's boggy meadow** — advanced by the 1908 investigating committee under Nyle Grigg. Defeated by the actual data in the college's own 1911 feeding trial, Bulletin No. 214: bog hay depressed the butterfat test by no more than 0.05 percentage points in winter and by an unmeasurable amount on summer pasture — nowhere near enough to produce a 0.19-point gap, and, crucially, a hay effect should vary by season while the observed deficiency did not.

**Subsidiary question 1 — did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?** No. It was not among the board's four exhibits (the station's test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book, and the 1923 inspector's notebook), and per both Frick and Kyle nobody even set the receiving book beside the inspector's notebook to compare the figures. Cleve Oram's account adds that the crate itself was physically present — in the loft directly over the hearing room — during two of the three 1925 sessions, and was never brought down or referenced.

**Subsidiary question 2 — what did the tester himself believe the cause was?** The sources give two different documented private beliefs, and don't reconcile them. Per Alonzo Frick's account of a private 1925 remark, Keddie believed the patrons themselves were at fault (some version of the water suspicion), but wouldn't say so publicly out of loyalty to neighbors of 30 years. Per his son Duncan's account, Keddie privately said, twice, in 1911 — "which was not his way" — that "it was something in the glass," but never wrote the suspicion down, recorded a question mark instead, and kept testing on that same glass for another 14 years. I can't determine which belief was primary or final, or whether one superseded the other over time; the notes treat this as an open tension. I'll note only that the 1911 remark, made 14 years before his own accusers reasoned toward wet hay and water, turned out to be the correct one.

## Section G — Summary (G1)

*(147 words)*

From 1896 to 1923, the Ostrey Hollow milk-testing station read consistently lower butterfat than the valley's other creamery — 3.61 against 3.78, every month, every year. Neighbors blamed the tester, Ansel Keddie, and one another: watered milk, stolen cream, wet meadow hay. A 1925 arbitration board paid the patrons $3,800 but found no proof against Keddie — and dismissed him anyway, without ever looking at the crate of measuring glass sitting in the station's own loft. He died in 1934, still suspected. Only in 1958 did his granddaughter, a college student, find that crate and measure it: the pipettes had always delivered slightly less milk than their mark, understating every test by a fixed amount regardless of season or honesty. No single witness — tester, bookkeeper, inspector, neighbor, newspaper — held the whole picture; the truth survived only because eight separate, partial records outlived the theories built without them.
