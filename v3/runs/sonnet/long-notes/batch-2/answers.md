# Batch 2 Answers

**Note on method:** I do not have Batch 1's text and cannot see which three of the eight
underlying stories it covered or how it drew the boundaries between them. The retention notes
describe one continuous Ostrey Hollow milk-testing narrative, not eight separately-labeled
stories, so "eight stories" has to be a partition I infer. The material has a clear eight-beat
chronological spine: (1) founding of the Association and the by-laws, (2) the station's opening
and early testing outfit, (3) the 1908 wet-hay investigation, (4) the 1911 broken-measure episode,
(5) the 1919 water circular and the Brant–Keddie rift, (6) the 1923 glass condemnation, (7) the
1925 petition/arbitration/dismissal, (8) the 1958 discovery and exoneration. I've assumed Batch 1
covered (1)–(3) and reconstruct (4)–(6) below. This is inference, flagged as such, not something
the sources state directly.

## Section A — Reconstruction

### Story 1 (of this batch) — The Broken Measure: Nine Weeks in 1911

For years after the Ostrey Hollow skimming station opened, tester Ansel Keddie's recorded average
sat at about 3.61% butterfat — noticeably below the 3.78% the main Larrow Green creamery averaged
over the same span. On 8 May 1911 the station's own measuring glass broke. A substitute measure
was borrowed and used for roughly nine weeks, until repairs were finished around 10 July 1911.
During that interval — same tester, same patrons, same oversight — the recorded average rose to
about 3.80%, close to the 0.19-point deficiency the 1958 college bulletin later fixed as the
station's true shortfall. Once the station's own (defective) equipment went back into service, the
average fell straight back to 3.61%.

Keddie noticed. He told his teenage son Duncan, twice — unusual for him — that "it was something
in the glass." He never wrote the suspicion into his book, entering only a question mark against
the period, and said nothing further on record. He then tested another fourteen years, faithfully
recording the very figures later used against him.

**When:** 8 May – 10 July 1911 (about nine weeks).
**Quantities:** average moved 3.61% → ~3.80% → back to 3.61%.
**Documents:** Ansel Keddie's test book (question mark entered against the period); Duncan
Keddie's 1968 memoir (his father's private remark, witnessed at age 17); Orra Keddie's diary,
dated entry 8 May 1911 ("A. home cross about a broken measure") — a contemporaneous, independent
confirmation of the exact date; Orin Falke's 1959 county report, which gives the precise "8th of
May" to "10th of July" dates, confirms the question-mark detail, and later uses this episode to
argue against the cream-theft theory, since the same man, under the same supervision, produced
different figures depending only on which glass he used.

### Story 2 (of this batch) — The Circular and the Ten-Year Rift: 1919

In 1919 manager Selby Vose signed a printed circular sent to the Hessel Bottom patrons raising the
possibility of water in their milk — the "water theory" for the station's chronically low test.
Rather than mail the circulars or leave them on the platform, tester Ansel Keddie carried them
round himself, standing at each door while the man of the house read it, and came home near
eleven at night. Sources disagree on how many households: most say nine, but Orra Keddie's diary,
written that same month, says eleven — an unresolved numerical conflict, since her account is
contemporaneous but the "nine" figure is independently well established elsewhere.

Two days later, Orra's brother Emmet Brant — the largest patron on Hessel Bottom and Ansel's
brother-in-law of thirty years — came to the Keddie yard but would not step inside. He said he had
hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and that Ansel knew it better than any
man living, then asked why Ansel had carried the paper himself if he didn't believe its
accusation. Ansel answered that he carried it because he would not have another man carry it.
Emmet went home and, by every account, did not enter that house again until his death a decade
later, in 1929 — a rift of roughly ten years, corroborated independently by Orra's own diary from
both ends (the 1919 confrontation and a 1929 entry marking his death: "Ten years and neither of
them would go up the road first").

The same period brought an outside check on the station's honesty: from 1915, four Hessel Bottom
farmers began hauling their summer milk instead to the rival Alder Corners Cheese Company across
the ridge, an arrangement that ran sixteen seasons, through 1930.

**When:** 1919 (circular and rift begin); rift lasts to 1929.
**Quantities:** nine (established) vs. eleven (Orra's diary) families visited; ~10-year rift;
16 seasons of parallel Alder Corners hauling (1915–1930).
**Documents:** Bertram Kyle's clerk memoir (he typed the circular himself, names Vose as
signatory); Orra Keddie's diary, two dated 1919 entries plus the 1929 entry; Roy Brant's account
of the years of silence; Nyle Grigg's parallel 1919 covering letter; Rosalie Cudd's later table of
the sixteen Alder Corners seasons.

### Story 3 (of this batch) — The Condemned Glass: 1923

The Ostrey Hollow station's pipettes were assumed sound until 12 June 1923, when state dairy
inspector Merle Strawn, on an ordinary circuit visit, ran them against a calibrated burette and
found they would not deliver their marked charge. He condemned the whole lot on the spot. This
date is pinned precisely by Orin Falke's 1959 report, resolving an earlier ambiguity in other
sources between "June" and "12 July." The Association ordered replacement glass from its original
1897 supplier, Ammon & Sons of Tarnet, on 28 June 1923; the firm replied 2 July, apologizing for
the trouble but admitting its own 1890s records couldn't explain what had gone wrong with the
original batch. New glass was in service by July 1923 — independently confirmed within weeks by
Orra Keddie's diary that same month ("new glass at the station and new pails").

Nobody at the time understood *why* the old glass had been wrong — only that it no longer measured
true against a calibrated burette. The mechanism (pipettes very likely undersized from
manufacture, not merely worn) wasn't established until 1958. What the 1923 fix immediately
produced was a clear shift in the recorded numbers: from 1924 the station's average settled at
3.85%, well above the old 3.61%.

**When:** condemned 12 June 1923; order 28 June; supplier reply 2 July; new glass in service by
July 1923.
**Quantities:** post-fix average 3.85% from 1924 (vs. 3.61% before).
**Documents:** Orin Falke's 1959 report (exact condemnation date, 3.85% figure); Junia Ammon's
1949 company history, reproducing the order and reply letter verbatim; Orra Keddie's diary (dated
July 1923 entry); Merle Strawn's own 1923 circuit notebook (Exhibit 4 at the 1925 arbitration);
Bertram Kyle's clerk memoir, which gives a conflicting minority date of 1922 for the same
condemnation — a single uncorroborated source against several independently-dated ones, so I
weight it as an error.

## Section C — Math

### C3. Butterfat credited vs. butterfat actually delivered

The sources give two different totals for milk received "over the years of complaint": 44,000,000
lbs (Effie Loomis/1941, independently matching the 1958 Ashlin Chronicle) and 40,000,000 lbs
(Bertram Kyle's clerk memoir, which he also breaks into "sixteen hundred thousand pounds a year" —
i.e., 1,600,000 lbs/yr, and which is the only account precise enough to divide out a year-count:
40,000,000 ÷ 1,600,000 = 25 years exactly). I show the math both ways; the percentage in (c) turns
out to be essentially the same regardless of which total is used, since it's driven only by the
test-rate gap, not by volume.

Rates: station's own long-run average 3.61%; true average 3.80% (3.61% + the 0.19-point deficiency
the 1958 bulletin fixed, per Falke).

**Using Kyle's 40,000,000 lbs (also used for C5a below, for internal consistency):**
- (a) credited to patrons: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lbs butterfat**
- (b) actually reaching Larrow Green in the cream: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lbs butterfat**
- difference: 76,000 lbs

**Using Loomis/Chronicle's 44,000,000 lbs:**
- (a) 44,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,588,400 lbs
- (b) 44,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,672,000 lbs
- difference: 83,600 lbs

**(c) Percentage:** In both cases, difference ÷ (a) ≈ **5.26%**. I take the percentage *of the
pounds credited to patrons* (not of the pounds delivered, and not of the total milk) because
that's how Bertram Kyle's own description of the "station gain" ledger line frames it — a gain
recorded on top of what patrons had already been paid for. This computed 5.26% lines up closely
with the ledger's own stated figure, "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" (5.25%) — a real
cross-check between an arithmetic result and an independently-quoted document, with the small gap
explained by rounding in the input rates (3.61/3.78/0.19 are themselves given to only two decimal
places).

**Caveat:** the absolute pound figures in (a)/(b) depend on which of the two conflicting milk
totals (40M vs. 44M) is correct — the sources don't resolve that. The percentage in (c) is the
robust part of this answer.

### C4. The 1897 invoice

**(a) Total:** The itemized invoice (reproduced verbatim by Junia Ammon) reads: 6 doz. pipettes
$25.20 + 12 doz. bottles $31.68 + acid measure $0.90 + hand tester $18.00 + freight $2.15. Summed:
25.20 + 31.68 = 56.88; + 0.90 = 57.78; + 18.00 = 75.78; + 2.15 = **$77.93**. This matches the
invoice's own printed total exactly. I resolve the conflict with the other quoted figure — $77.39,
given both by Ammon's own restatement and by the 1958 Chronicle — in favor of $77.93, because it's
the only figure that the itemized line items actually sum to; $77.39 is very likely a shared
transcription slip (the Chronicle may have simply inherited it from the same secondary source as
Ammon's own prose, so these two agreeing accounts count as one line of evidence, not two).

**(b) Pipettes and shortfall:** The invoice bought **six dozen (72) pipettes**, each marked 17.6
c.c. — I resolve the "six dozen vs. five dozen" conflict (Cleve Oram's secondhand "five dozen") in
favor of six dozen because the invoice is the primary transactional document. The shortfall
figure available is from Ivy Keddie's 1958 burette measurement: average delivery 16.27 c.c. against
the 17.6 c.c. mark, i.e. **short by 1.33 c.c. on average**. Caveat: that 16.27 c.c. average was
measured on only the nine pipettes that survived to 1958 (out of the original 72), not on the
full original lot — so "1.33 c.c. short" is an average of the survivors, extrapolated to the whole
outfit; the sources give no way to check whether the original 72 were uniformly short by that
amount.

### C5. Duration of the fault vs. the money calculation

**(a)** The faulty glass was in service from 1 May 1897 (installation, per the invoice and
independently dated by Orra Keddie's diary) to 12 June 1923 (condemnation, per Falke) — a calendar
span of about **26 years**, minus the roughly nine-week gap in 1911 when a substitute measure was
used, so about 25 years 10 months of actual service.

The money/loss calculation, by contrast, works out to **25 full delivery years** — derived from
Kyle's own figures (40,000,000 lbs ÷ 1,600,000 lbs/yr = 25). These two numbers differ by about a
year. My best explanation: the glass's calendar service life (1897–1923) includes two partial
seasons at its edges — the 1897 season, since the outfit wasn't installed until 1 May, partway
into the delivery year, and the 1923 season, since the glass was condemned in mid-June, partway
through that year too. A "full delivery years" count for the money math would naturally exclude
those two partial seasons, landing about a year short of the full calendar span. I want to flag
this is my own reconciliation, not something any source states outright — no source gives an
explicit start/end date for "the years of complaint," and Kyle's own totals are themselves rounded
("forty million," matched suspiciously cleanly to "sixteen hundred thousand a year"), so treat the
25-year figure as an approximation the arithmetic implies, not a pinned date range.

**(b)** Per Bertram Kyle's recollection, the station's first-year (pre-ledger) intake was "a good
eleven hundred thousand pounds" — **approximately 1,100,000 lbs**. Kyle is explicit that this
figure is his own recollection, not from any ledger ("not in any book I ever ruled"), so treat it
as an approximate, single-source estimate rather than a documented figure.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The 1911 broken-measure episode (8 May–10 July, about nine weeks) is the closest thing in
this record to a controlled experiment. The variable that changed was the measuring equipment
itself: the station's own (later shown defective) glass was out of service, and a substitute/
borrowed measure was used instead. Everything else was held constant: same tester (Ansel Keddie),
same patrons and herds, same testing procedure, and — per Falke's report — no change in
supervision. The result was that the recorded average jumped from 3.61% to about 3.80% for the
duration of the substitution, then fell straight back to 3.61% the moment the station's own
equipment returned. What this establishes, that no after-the-fact calculation could: it's direct,
real-time evidence that the deficiency tracked the *instrument*, not the tester's behavior or the
patrons' milk — because if the cause had been human (theft, dishonesty, watering), swapping the
glass for nine weeks shouldn't have changed the number at all, and it did, both up and back down.
This is exactly the point Falke uses to reject the cream-theft theory. What the interval does
*not* establish: it doesn't tell us why the glass itself was wrong (that mechanism — a probably
undersized pipette mold — wasn't identified until the 1958 measurement), it doesn't tell us the
borrowed substitute was itself accurately calibrated (only that it read differently and, as later
evidence suggests, closer to true), and it's a single occurrence rather than a repeated trial, so
it can't rule out every possible coincidental confound in that particular nine-week window — though
the readings' immediate reversion afterward argues against any ordinary seasonal explanation.

**D2.** At least three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream: (1) the 1911
broken-measure episode itself (Falke's 1959 report, corroborated by Orra Keddie's diary and the
established timeline) — a natural experiment showing the deficiency followed the instrument, not
the man. (2) Hobart Sill's cross-weighing (his own 1934 retirement remarks) — as the buttermaker at
Larrow Green, he weighed the Hollow's cream every year and it always arrived "heavy," which is the
opposite of what theft en route or at the station would produce; notably Sill personally believed
the water theory his whole life, so this isn't a source with a motive to exonerate Keddie. (3) The
Association's own "station gain" ledger line (Wilbur Teague's device from 1905, described
independently by Kyle in 1953) — it shows the creamery consistently received *more* butterfat than
patrons were credited for, every year, which is direct bookkeeping proof that product wasn't
disappearing. (4) Emmet Brant's private weigh book (via Hazel Brant, 1981) shows his own
independent weighings "generally agreed" with the station's figures for the largest single patron.

These are independent because they come from different people, in different roles, writing at
different times, using different kinds of evidence: an official inspector's natural-experiment
argument; a buttermaker's personal cross-weighing testimony; an internal bookkeeping device; and a
patron's private parallel record. None derives from or repeats another.

The one that would still hold even if the glass had never been measured in 1958 is the "station
gain" ledger record: it's pure bookkeeping arithmetic, entirely independent of any pipette
calibration, and it directly contradicts the premise of theft on its own terms.

Did the bookkeeper ever question the entries that refute his own accusation? No — per the sources,
Wilbur Teague put the cream-theft theory to the 1925 board despite having personally kept the
"station gain" ledger line that was itself the strongest evidence against it. No source records him
ever reconciling or even acknowledging that contradiction; the notes describe it as an unresolved
irony in his own account.

**D3.** Yes and no, depending on what "knew" means. In the general, public sense, Selby Vose
clearly knew the station's tests ran low well before 1923 — that's the entire premise of the 1919
circular he signed, which accused patrons of watering their milk to explain the shortfall, and of
his 1926 address defending that same water theory. But in the specific sense of understanding what
his own ledger was telling him: Bertram Kyle, who worked at the outer desk for 25 years and watched
Vose sign these sheets, says explicitly that Vose "read the totals and never read the lines above
them" — taking a sheet, turning it, thumbing the bottom figure, and signing, "a thousand times."
The "station gain" line itself predates his tenure (Teague introduced it in 1905; Vose took office
in 1913), so the pattern had already been running for years before he ever signed anything. My
reading: he was aware of the symptom (low test, hence the water accusation) but the sources
indicate he did not attend to, or draw the implication from, the ledger data recording it —
he had the evidence in front of him and signed past it rather than read it.
