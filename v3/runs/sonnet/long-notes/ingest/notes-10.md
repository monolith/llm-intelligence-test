### THE CENTRAL STORY: Ostrey Hollow milk-testing station and the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association

#### Geography / entities
- **Larrow Valley** — the valley; contains **Larrow Green** (site of the main creamery, on Larrow Creek, built 1891) and, seven miles up-valley, **Ostrey Hollow**, at the head of which stood the **skimming station**.
- **Hessel Bottom** — a wet meadow below the Ostrey Hollow hamlet; the best milk in the Hollow came from here. Seven farms had grazing/haying rights on Hessel Bottom (per r02/Effie Loomis) — but Roy Brant (r09) says **six farms**; the largest was **Emmet Brant's** (per r02) — confirmed independently by Roy Brant (r09): the Brant farm was "the largest of the six — sixty-odd acres of the meadow, and thirty-eight cows in a good year."
- **Ashlin** — the county seat/town where hearings, the Association's office (later), the historical society, and legal business took place.
- **Farland** — site of a college with a library (where Ivy Keddie later worked/taught/studied, and where Merle Strawn's notebooks were held).
- **Redlow Ridge**, **Vessey County** — location of the **Alder Corners Cheese Company** (a rival/comparison creamery, used for cross-checking milk tests). Per r10 (Rosalie Cudd): the ridge is a 4-mile switchback road, cut for a lumber road, shuts in November and does not open before April (some years May); "no arguing with it."
- **Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association** — organized 1889 by valley farmers at Larrow Green. By-laws drafted by **Gideon Vose**, who was elected first president in 1889 and served until 1911 (22 years — longest of any president). Gideon Vose died in 1917.
- **Article VII** of the by-laws — quoted in full by Effie Loomis (r02), curator Halbert Nym (r07, Item 1), and a THIRD time by Dell Rundle (r12): *"Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."* Key point emphasized by Loomis and again by Rundle: "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" — no appeal from the station's own test, "the sentence on which the whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned" (Rundle). Adoption date: r02/r07 say **1892** ("adopted at the annual meeting," the year after the creamery opened); CONFLICT (r12/Rundle): states "Article VII dates from 1891, the year the churn was set up" — a one-year discrepancy against every prior source. Attorney Alonzo Frick's 1946 recollections (r13) also describe the "no appeal" rule as central and calls the whole affair "not litigation at all... an arbitration under the by-laws of a creamery association, with no appeal from it, no reported decision, and nothing whatever to cite it for."

#### People and relationships (the core family/social web)
- **Ansel Keddie** — tester at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station. Went up in 1895 to prepare it, opened/began testing 4 May 1896. Tested 29 seasons, 1896–1924 (did not test in 1925). Dismissed ("put out") on **30 April 1925**. Died in **1934**.
  - Internal inconsistency in Keddie's own letter (r03): "borrowed off the Ashlin creamery" vs. book's own first-leaf entry "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green" — Larrow Green corroborated independently at least three times elsewhere (r07 Item 2, r07 curatorial note); Ashlin claim remains sole outlier. NEW (r14/Sill, segment 10): a FOURTH/FIFTH corroboration of "Larrow Green" as the glass source — buttermaker Hobart Sill states HE PERSONALLY supplied his own glass/testing outfit from his own bench at Larrow Green when the station opened in May 1896 because the station's own ordered outfit had not yet arrived; his equipment went up the valley on the wagon and the station "opened on it." The station's own outfit "came the spring after" (spring 1897, consistent with the Ammon & Sons invoice dated 14 April 1897, in use from 1 May 1897 per r07's provenance note) and Sill's personal equipment was sent back to him, "and one piece of it was chipped and I never said a word about that either" — an interesting possible echo (unconfirmed connection) with the later "two chipped" pipettes noted among the 1958-recovered crate (r07), though this is Sill's own personal returned glass, not necessarily the same crate.
  - NEW (r13/Frick, segment 10): Keddie made a formal statement to the 1925 arbitration board on 17 March 1925, stating he had tested "as he was taught and as the state man had seen him do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons," that he could not explain the figures, and that he had never taken a pound of cream from the station. Frick (the board chairman) believed him. NEW MAJOR PSYCHOLOGICAL DETAIL: Frick recounts that privately, in the corridor at Ashlin, Keddie confided that he had always personally believed the PATRONS were at fault (i.e., privately suspected the water-theory / that patrons were watering their milk) but would not say so publicly because he had lived among them thirty years and would not accuse his neighbors. Frick explicitly corrects the record: "it has been said since that he went to his grave accusing nobody, which is true, and that he suspected nobody, which is not." This directly complicates/nuances the "innocent bystander who suspected no one" reading of Keddie found in other sources and is a wholly new piece of information not present in any earlier retelling.
- **Orra Keddie** (née Brant) — Ansel Keddie's wife; sister of **Emmet Brant**; first cousin of **old Mrs. Vose** (per r01). Per r09 (Roy Brant, her nephew): Orra and Emmet were full brother and sister, same mother and same father — Roy explicitly denies a "stepmother" story some people apparently tell (unconfirmed, unexplained anywhere else). After the 1919 circular, Orra and Emmet did not speak again.
- **Emmet Brant** — biggest patron on Hessel Bottom. Orra Keddie's brother. Per r09 (his son Roy): born 1854, died 1929. Kept the Brant farm weigh book "from the first of January, 1898" (Roy, firsthand) — conflicts with r07 Item 4's "1 January 1897." NEW (r13/Frick, segment 10): Frick's 1925 exhibit list (see below) describes Exhibit 3 as "The private weigh book of Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom, begun 1 January 1897" — this is now a THIRD data point on this recurring date conflict, and it sides with r07 Item 4's "1897" rather than Roy Brant's own firsthand "1898" claim, making Roy Brant's date the outlier against two independent sources (r07's curatorial provenance note and Frick's 1925 exhibit list, itself presumably drawn from contemporaneous documentation). Per r09: Emmet and Ansel Keddie did NOT speak from 1919 onward, not over the accusation itself but because "the man who kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not" (Roy Brant's framing).
- **Selby Vose** — per every source through segment 6 (r02, r05, r08, r09): **son** of Gideon Vose; became manager of the Association in 1913, served until 1938; signed the 1919 circular; gave his own 1926 annual address (r08) defending the circular and asserting a watered-milk theory. MAJOR CONFLICT (r12/Rundle): Rundle's 1939 pamphlet explicitly calls him "His [Gideon Vose's] nephew Selby Vose, afterward manager of the Association" — directly contradicting the son relationship established by every other source (notably r02/Loomis, a contemporary Association officer, and r09/Roy Brant, firsthand). This is the single most direct family-relationship conflict in the whole record. Rundle also quotes Selby Vose's own remark about his (per Rundle) uncle: "his uncle wrote by-laws the way other men build stone wall — slowly, and to last." NEW (r13/Frick, segment 10): confirms Selby Vose as "the manager" who put forward the water theory before the 1925 board — "He had circulated a warning to those patrons on the subject some years before and he stood by it before us... He put it without heat and without proof." NEW (r14/Sill, segment 10): confirms Selby Vose ("the manager") issued the 1919 circular ("a paper to them about it — that would be nineteen nineteen — a circular, warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons about water in the milk") and states Sill personally "thought well of him for it," calling it "the only plain thing that office ever did."
- **Gideon Vose** — founder/first president of the Association (1889–1911), drafted the by-laws at his own kitchen table in cramped handwriting "the Association's clerks were still complaining about... thirty years later" (r12/Rundle), died 1917. Per r12: "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the law and thought better of it." His widow is "old Mrs. Vose" (r01). Per r08: kept Jerseys testing 4.56 in 1910.
- **Effie Loomis** — secretary of the Association, 1901–1940; kept the minute book. Wrote "Notes toward a history of the Association" (r02) in 1941. Born ~1875.
- **Wilbur Teague** — bookkeeper of the Association, 1905–1945; kept the ledgers at Ashlin. Wrote memoir "The Books of the Association," Ch. 9, winter 1948 (r05). Invented the "station gain" ledger device in 1905. NEW/MAJOR ODDITY (r13/Frick, segment 10): before the 1925 arbitration board, Teague was the one who PUT FORWARD the cream-theft theory against Keddie — "in the way an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it — he was asked about the station's account and answered at length about how such an account would look if a man were helping himself, and then observed that he made no charge against anybody." Frick notes "There is no cross-examination that reaches a witness of that kind, and I did not attempt one." This is a striking tension/irony against Teague's own later-quoted "station gain" ledger entries (invented by Teague himself in 1905, quoted verbatim in r05/r07/r12) which are precisely the evidence that DISPROVES the theft theory (cream arriving at Larrow Green heavy, not light) — the same man who insinuated theft to the 1925 board apparently sat atop, in his own ledgers, the very proof that the theft could not have happened, decades before that evidence's exculpatory significance was recognized (by Rundle in 1939 and by Bulletin 471/Ivy Keddie in 1958). Flagged as a notable and unresolved irony in the record, not explained by any source.
- **Hobart Sill** — buttermaker at Larrow Green; kept churn/cream receiving books (r02). NEW (r14, segment 10, full retelling — see below): gives extensive firsthand testimony, retiring in 1934 after 41 years at the churn (started 1893). Personally cross-weighed the Hollow cream every year at Larrow Green and independently confirms — completely independently of Teague's ledger — that it always arrived "heavy" relative to the fat the station's paperwork said had been bought, "Always more. Never less." This is effectively a SIXTH independent corroboration of the core exculpatory "heavy cream" phenomenon (after Teague's ledger x3 quotations, Rundle's 1939 retraction, and Bulletin 471), now from an entirely separate first-hand physical-weighing source. Despite this, Sill personally believes the WATER theory ("There was water in those cans and I said so at the time") — an unresolved tension: his own testimony documents the phenomenon that (per Bulletin 471, decades later) actually disproves both theft AND, implicitly, undermines any simple story of intentional wrongdoing by the patrons, yet Sill never abandons his belief that patrons were adulterating their milk. Sill was 64 years old in 1934 (born ~1870).
- **Alonzo Frick** — attorney of Ashlin; chaired the 1925 board of arbitration (confirmed across r02, r03, r05, r08, and r12). NEW (r13, segment 10, full retelling — see below): Frick himself is revealed as the author of a 1946 memoir/article, written at age 74 (so born ~1872), for the yearbook of the Ordell County Bar Association. Extensive new firsthand detail below.
- **Vira Toland** — narrator of r01; born 1912 at the top of Ostrey Hollow; interviewed 1979.
- **Duncan Keddie** — Ansel Keddie's son; taught school at Ashlin; first cousin of Roy Brant (r09).
- **Ivy Keddie** — Ansel Keddie's granddaughter (Duncan's daughter); gave "What the Crate Held" talk in 1959 (r04), presenting 1958 findings. Per r09: second cousin of Hazel Brant — independently confirmed a second time in r12's 1959 footnote: "Miss Ivy Keddie, who is second cousin to Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom." Per r09 postscript: personally borrowed the Brant weigh book from Roy Brant in 1958 for Farland research. Wrote to Rosalie Cudd (r10) asking about the Hessel Bottom test figures.
- **Roy Brant** — Emmet Brant's son; kept the Brant weigh book after his father's death; lent it to Ivy Keddie in 1958 (confirmed firsthand in his own 1955 account plus 1958 postscript, r09, fully read as of segment 6). 67 in 1955, "still milking." Daughter Hazel Brant will inherit the book.
- **Rosalie Cudd** — runs the Alder Corners Cheese Company. Per r10 (her own 1958 letter) and r07 Item 13: Jerome Cudd's **daughter** (resolving the earlier "niece" conflict from segment-4 notes in favor of "daughter" — corroborated a THIRD time by r12/Rundle's 1939 pamphlet: "It is Jerome Cudd's daughter, Rosalie Cudd, who makes the cheese at Alder Corners now."). Was 12 in 1913 (born ~1901); took over the factory spring 1941 when her father died; had run it 17 years as of 1958.
- **Jerome Cudd** — founder of Alder Corners Cheese Company. Per r10: built the factory in 1913 "on the flat below the church"; per r09 and r12 (Rundle): the factory "opened" in **1914** (construction 1913 vs. opening 1914 — consistently distinguished across three sources). Died March 1941 "between the setting and the cutting of a vat." Considered the wet-hay theory "nonsense," comparing it to a family ghost story. His own 1919 test-book entry for the four Hessel Bottom men (3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82, average 3.80) independently corroborates the "true" test value.

NEW (r13/Frick, segment 10): Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook was formally entered as Exhibit 4 before the 1925 board (see exhibit list below) — this does not contradict the earlier-noted oddity that "no source records Strawn testifying at the 1925 hearing" (the notebook being admitted as a document is distinct from Strawn appearing as a witness; Frick's account of the hearing does not mention Strawn testifying in person, only the exhibit).
- **Professor Vaught (Leland Vaught)** — professor of dairy husbandry at Farland; co-authored Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958) with Ivy Keddie. r11 (his own introduction/method sections) contains a MAJOR internal numeric self-contradiction (fully detailed below).

- **Dell Rundle** — schoolteacher at Larrow Green; author of "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years," a jubilee pamphlet printed 1939 for the Association's 50th anniversary, reprinted 1959 with one added footnote. Wrote Chapter IV repeating the "settled opinion" theft narrative as fact, then — while the pamphlet was literally being typeset, upon being shown the Association's ledgers "on another errand altogether" — encountered the same "station gain" ledger entry Teague/Ivy Keddie later used, and RETRACTED his own theft narrative in a "NOTE ADDED WHILE IN PRESS," reasoning: "Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand." This self-correction happened in **1939**, nineteen years BEFORE Bulletin 471 (1958) reached the same exculpatory conclusion via the same evidence — a remarkable, independent, contemporaneous parallel discovery, in sharp contrast to Vira Toland (r01, 1979), who repeats the same disproven theft story decades AFTER the exoneration was published, with no such self-correction.

#### The central mystery/timeline of events
(Numbered items 1–48 from segment 6 carried forward verbatim in substance; segment-7 conflicts layered in; segment 8/9 added nothing; segment 10 additions/refinements appended as lettered sub-notes at the relevant items, plus two new end-of-list items for the 1934 and 1946 retellings themselves.)
1. **1889** — Association organized at Larrow Green; Gideon Vose first president. Per r12 (Rundle): they met in the schoolhouse in cold weather; "it went on very late and... somebody's horse got loose in the middle of it."
2. **1891** — Main creamery built at Larrow Green, "on the creek below the mill"; churn set up; valley "began to be paid in cash once a month, which had never happened to it before" (r12). NEW (r14/Sill): Sill says he came to Larrow Green in "Eighteen ninety-three," two years after the creamery/churn was set up — "there was no floor in this room but plank, and we cooled with creek ice out of the pond" — consistent with, and adding texture to, the 1891 founding date.
3. **1892** (per r02/r07) or **1891** (per r12/Rundle, CONFLICT) — Article VII adopted (pay by butterfat test, station's test governs, no appeal). Per r12: "there were men who left the Association over it."
4. **1895** — Ansel Keddie goes up to Ostrey Hollow to prepare the station.
5. **1896** (4 May) — Station opens. Per r12 (Rundle): "the pull down was three hours in mud and could not be done at all in March," hence the station's necessity; building "boarded and battened, with the intake room at the road end and a loft over it that I believe has never been cleared out since it was put up; and behind it the creek, which in a wet April comes up to the sill and has twice come over it." NEW (r14/Sill): confirms opening was in May 1896 ("that was ninety-six, in May, and there was a crowd went up for it") and adds that the station's OWN ordered equipment had not yet arrived when it opened — "It was ordered and it was not come, and they were ready to start taking milk, and there was talk of holding off the whole season" — so Sill personally supplied his own glass testing outfit from Larrow Green to open the station that first season (see Keddie entry above for the corroboration this provides). NEW numeric detail (r14/Sill): the station's first (partial) season, May 1896 to end of year, took in "about nine hundred thousand pounds" of milk — a new figure not previously recorded anywhere in the corpus.
6. **1897** — Station's own outfit arrives (per invoice, r07 Item 2, dated 14 April 1897; in use from 1 May 1897 per r07's provenance note). r11/Vaught's internally-conflicting claim of "1898" remains an outlier/self-contradiction. NEW (r14/Sill): confirms the station's own outfit "came the spring after" (i.e., spring 1897), consistent with this date; Sill's own personal glass was returned to him at that point with "one piece of it... chipped."
7. **1898** — Recorded average settles at 3.61. NEW CONFLICT (r13/Frick, 1946): Frick's recollection states the Hollow patrons "had been paid, year upon year, on an average test of 3.16" — a SECOND source (after the previously-noted "3.61% vs 3.16% average per Teague" conflict logged in segment 6) independently using the figure 3.16 rather than 3.61. NEW CONFLICT (r14/Sill, 1934): Sill states the TRUE (uncorrupted) value of Hollow milk "was really a 3.85 and no better" — this conflicts with Bulletin 471's later scientifically-derived true value of 3.80 (a 0.05-point discrepancy, Sill's being an impressionistic buttermaker's estimate rather than a calculated correction). Sill also gives Larrow Green's own (non-Hollow) patron milk average as "3.78 taking one year with another" — a wholly new baseline figure for the rest of the valley, not previously recorded.
8. **1902** — Complaints first arise; confirmed firsthand by Roy Brant (r09) and by r12 (Rundle): "From 1902 the men of the Hollow complained that their milk tested lower at their own station than the same class of milk tested at the Green. They were not believed." NEW (r14/Sill): confirms this date independently — "Nineteen two is the first I remember of it, a man in here shouting that his milk tested under his brother-in-law's down the creek and it was the same feed and the same water and what did we mean by it" — a fourth corroboration of the 1902 start date for complaints.
9. **1907** — Merle Strawn takes over the Ordell dairy-inspection circuit.
10. **1908** — Committee visits under **Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green** (named firsthand in r09's postscript, corroborated by r12/Rundle: "the Association sent a committee up under Nyle Grigg"), blames wet hay of Hessel Bottom. Sampling was winter months only. NEW (r13/Frick): confirms the 1908 committee report was read into the 1925 arbitration record — "Somebody read the report of the 1908 committee into the record, and the bog meadow at Hessel Bottom was before us for the better part of an afternoon."
11. **1909** — Strawn visits, all in order.
12. **8 May–10 July 1911** (nine weeks, per r03/r04/r09/r11 — sole outlier is r05/Teague's "six weeks") — station's own measure breaks; substitute used; recorded average rises to 3.80, then reverts to 3.61.
13. **1912–1918** — Strawn's routine annual visits, various minor notes (per r06); Selby Vose becomes manager 1913; Jerome Cudd builds Alder Corners factory 1913, opens 1914; from 1915, four Hessel Bottom farmers begin hauling summer milk to Alder Corners for 16 seasons (1915–1930, per r10's full table).
14. **1919** — Printed circular over manager Selby Vose's signature sent to nine families, alleging "water in the cans." Per r09: Emmet Brant and Ansel Keddie (brothers-in-law) stopped speaking, specifically over Keddie's perceived silence rather than the accusation itself. Per r06 (Strawn), contemporaneously rebuts water theory. Per r08 (Vose, 1926) defends the circular as measured. Per r10 (Jerome Cudd's 1919 book entry) independently shows the milk fine that exact year. Per r12/Rundle: "after that the two ends of this valley did not speak civilly to one another for six years" (1919+6=1925, matching the hearing year), and Rundle's vivid personal account of teaching children from both sides of the rift, who inherited the animosity "without a word of it being explained, the way a debt does." Per r12/Rundle presents the THEFT theory as "the settled opinion of the valley" as of 1939: "It is the settled opinion of the valley that cream left that station by the back door, and the figures went right when the man who kept it did not. I set that down as I have heard it set down a hundred times... and I have never heard it contradicted at the store." — Rundle later retracts this in his own "Note Added While in Press." NEW (r14/Sill, 1934): confirms the circular was issued in 1919 by "the manager," approves of it as "the only plain thing that office ever did," and adds his own theory of a later, unexplained turning point: "one summer, along in the twenties, those figures came up and they stayed up, and nothing on this earth changed but the season... Something got stopped. Something got said to somebody. I never knew what and I never asked." Sill interprets this shift (which the reader now knows, from Bulletin 471/r11, was actually the 1923 replacement of the station's condemned, undersized pipettes) as evidence that clandestine watering was somehow halted — a clear instance of a contemporary witness misreading the true mechanical cause of the correction as a moral/behavioral one. This is a new, poignant illustration of how the water-theory persisted even among people who had the corrected figures in front of them.
15. **1921** — State begins issuing graduated burettes to field men.
16. **1922** — Keddie's summary leaf of averages.
17. **June 1923** (precisely June 12, per Strawn's log, r07 photostat Item 9, and Bulletin 471; conflicting "July 12" per r08/Vose 1926 — and r12/Rundle ALSO gives "the twelfth of July, 1923" as the condemnation date, a second-ish source matching Vose's date rather than Strawn's contemporaneous log/photostat/Bulletin 471, though Rundle's pamphlet compiles valley hearsay rather than primary records) — station's measuring crate condemned; new glass ordered.
18. **January 1925** — Patrons formally petition. Per r12/Rundle: gives a precise total-milk and loss figure in the petition itself: "The station took in, over the years complained of, forty-four million pounds of milk. The patrons' loss upon it was nineteen thousand six hundred dollars, and their petition set the figure out to the dollar." — the **44 million lb** figure MATCHES Teague's r05 conflicting figure (vs. the more commonly cited 40 million from r02/r04/r07); the **$19,600** loss figure is close to but does NOT exactly match the previously-calculated ~$19,000 (76,000 lbs × $0.25/lb) — a further, more precise (but still not perfectly reconciled) numeric variant. NEW CONFLICT (r13/Frick): Frick states "The patrons' own reckoning of what they had lost was sixty-seven thousand pounds of butter fat over the years complained of, and nothing was ever brought forward to disturb that figure" — this is a THIRD distinct measure of the patrons' loss (after the ~76,000 lb calculated shortfall and the $19,600/~$19,000 dollar figures), now given in pounds of butterfat as "sixty-seven thousand," which does not match the previously-established 76,000 lb shortfall. Three different quantifications of the same underlying loss (76,000 lbs per the calculation cross-referenced against Bulletin 471; 67,000 lbs per Frick's recollection of the patrons' own reckoning; $19,600 or ~$19,000 in dollar terms) now sit unreconciled in the record.
19. **17 March – 4 May 1925** — Board of arbitration at Ashlin under Alonzo Frick; "found nothing established," awarded patrons a sum without admission. Per r02/r05/r08: **$3,800**. Per r01: conflicting **$3,500** (split nine ways). r12/Rundle gives NO dollar figure at all for the actual award — only reports "It found nothing established, it made an award to the patrons without admitting anything" — an omission, not a new numeric conflict, but notable that Rundle (writing closest in time, 1939, just 14 years after) doesn't specify the amount while others do. NEW MAJOR DETAIL (r13/Frick, segment 10, firsthand as chairman): the board sat **three times**: session 1 at the Association's office at Ashlin on **17 March 1925** (this is where Keddie gave his statement and the three explanations were heard); sessions 2 and 3 were, at Frick's own request, held **at Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's own weigh room**, "so that the board might see the plant it was to pass upon instead of hearing it described by men with an interest in the description." During these sessions the board watched the scales tried and the sampling/testing done, though Frick (a lawyer, not a chemist) admits "I did not understand the test then and do not understand it now." The award was made on **4 May 1925** and Frick CONFIRMS the **$3,800** figure ("thirty-eight hundred dollars"), agreeing with r02/r05/r08 against r01's $3,500. Frick states the board was a panel of THREE (not previously specified elsewhere as a numbered panel), and Frick was named chairman "because I was the one of the three who kept no cow" (a wry, previously-unrecorded detail; the identities of the other two board members are not given anywhere in the corpus).
20. **30 April 1925** — Ansel Keddie dismissed despite "no cause established." NEW (r13/Frick): confirms this date precisely — "four days before we made our award" (i.e., 4 May minus 4 days = 30 April) — and states plainly: "There was no finding against him from us and there never was one; the board had not concluded and did not conclude that he had done anything whatever. He was simply let go while the board was still sitting, and I have never been able to think well of the Association for it." This is the strongest and most explicit condemnation of the dismissal found anywhere in the corpus, from the man best positioned to judge it (the arbitration chairman himself).
21. **1926** — Selby Vose's defensive annual address (r08); burette-checking program discontinued statewide.
22. **1930** — Last summer of the 16-season Alder Corners hauling arrangement (per r10).
23. **1934** — Ansel Keddie dies, still under suspicion. NEW (segment 10): this is also the year Hobart Sill gave his own retirement remarks (r14) — see new item 23a below.
   - **23a. 1934** — Hobart Sill retires from the Larrow Green churn after 41 years (since 1893); his farewell remarks are recorded (r14). He was 64 years old. In these remarks he maintains his belief in the water theory even as he documents (via his own independent cream-weighing records) the very "heavy cream" phenomenon that, decades later, would help exonerate Keddie of any wrongdoing.
24. **1936** — Strawn's last inspection round; retires after 29 years (1907–1936).
25. **1938** — Selby Vose's manager term ends; Association office moves, 1889–1892 minute book lost.
26. **1939** — Dell Rundle's jubilee pamphlet published for the Association's 50th year. Contains the theft-narrative retraction described above.
27. **1940** — Effie Loomis retires.
28. **1941** — Effie Loomis writes her history (r02); Jerome Cudd dies, Rosalie Cudd takes over Alder Corners.
   - **28a. 1946** — Alonzo Frick, at age 74, writes his recollections of the arbitration for the Ordell County Bar Association yearbook (r13) — see full new retelling below. This is the LATEST-dated primary/firsthand-witness document in the corpus discovered so far except for Vira Toland's 1979 oral history and Ivy Keddie's 1959 talk / the 1959 pamphlet reprint.
29. **1945** — Wilbur Teague retires.
30. **1947** — Strawn's notebooks placed in Farland college library.
31. **1955 (winter)** — Roy Brant writes his account (r09), with 1958 postscript.
32. **1958 (Feb)** — Crate of nine (per most sources) unbroken pipettes found; per r07, two chipped.
