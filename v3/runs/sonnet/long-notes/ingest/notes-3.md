### THE CENTRAL STORY: Ostrey Hollow milk-testing station and the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association

#### Geography / entities
- **Larrow Valley** — the valley; contains **Larrow Green** (site of the main creamery, on Larrow Creek, built 1891) and, seven miles up-valley, **Ostrey Hollow**, at the head of which stood the **skimming station**.
- **Hessel Bottom** — a wet meadow below the Ostrey Hollow hamlet; the best milk in the Hollow came from here. Seven farms had grazing/haying rights on Hessel Bottom; the largest was **Emmet Brant's**.
- **Ashlin** — the county seat/town where hearings, the Association's office (later), the historical society, and legal business took place.
- **Farland** — site of a college with a library (where Ivy Keddie later worked/taught, and where Merle Strawn's notebooks were held).
- **Redlow Ridge**, **Vessey County** — location of the **Alder Corners Cheese Company** (a rival/comparison creamery, used for cross-checking milk tests).
- **Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association** — organized 1889 by valley farmers at Larrow Green. By-laws drafted by **Gideon Vose**, who was elected first president in 1889 and served until 1911 (22 years — longest of any president). Gideon Vose died in 1917.
- **Article VII** of the by-laws (adopted 1892, the year after the creamery opened), quoted in full by Effie Loomis (r02): *"Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."* Key point emphasized by Loomis: "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" — no appeal from the station's own test. This clause is called "the hinge of the whole Ostrey Hollow business."

#### People and relationships (the core family/social web)
- **Ansel Keddie** — tester at the Ostrey Hollow skimming station. Went up in 1895 to prepare it, opened/began testing 4 May 1896 (first leaf of his book, quoted twice, once in r03 and again in r04: *"Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie."*). Tested 29 seasons, 1896–1924 (did not test in 1925). Dismissed ("put out") on **30 April 1925**. Died in **1934**.
  - Note an internal inconsistency in Keddie's own letter (r03): the body text says the glass was "borrowed off the Ashlin creamery," but the book's own first-leaf entry (quoted in the same letter) says "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green." These two statements conflict with each other within the same document.
- **Orra Keddie** (née Brant) — Ansel Keddie's wife; sister of **Emmet Brant**; first cousin of **old Mrs. Vose** (per r01). After the 1919 circular (see below), Orra and her brother Emmet did not speak again — "not one word, not at the store, not at a funeral," per r01. She would walk the long way to avoid passing her brother's lane. She outlived Ansel by "a good while."
- **Emmet Brant** — biggest patron on Hessel Bottom (most cows, most hay ground, most milk hauled). Orra Keddie's brother. His son is **Roy Brant** (see r04), who kept up the Brant weigh book after his father died and lent it to Ivy Keddie in 1958.

Corroborated independently by r06 (Merle Strawn) — see below.

#### The central mystery/timeline of events
1. **1889** — Association organized at Larrow Green; Gideon Vose first president.
2. **1891** — Main creamery built at Larrow Green.
3. **1892** — Article VII adopted (pay by butterfat test, station's test governs, no appeal).
4. **1895** — Ansel Keddie goes up to Ostrey Hollow to prepare the station.
5. **1896** (4 May) — Station opens; Keddie begins testing, using glass borrowed (per the book's first leaf) "of Larrow Green" — or (per his own later letter text) "off the Ashlin creamery" [internal conflict, see above]. Recorded average test that year: 3.80.
6. **1897** — Station's own outfit (six dozen pipettes etc., invoice $77.93 total for pipettes, bottles, acid measure, hand tester, and freight — per r04) arrives; used from then on. Average this year: "part year" (incomplete data, per Keddie's 1922 summary leaf). Per r06 (Strawn), the state's burette-checking program did not exist until 1921, so no independent check of this glass's accuracy took place at delivery.
7. **1898** — Recorded average settles at 3.61 (and stays "within three hundredths of it" almost every year after, except the 1911 anomaly).
8. **1902** — Complaints first arise at Larrow Green that Ostrey Hollow milk doesn't test as well as the rest of the valley's.
9. **1907** — Per r06, Merle Strawn takes over the newly-formed Ordell dairy-inspection circuit this year (fifty-odd plants, four counties) and makes his first round; visits Ostrey Hollow, finds scales true, cold room fair, and praises the tester's (Keddie's) book-keeping hand and slow, careful testing method.
10. **1908** — A committee visits, samples herds through the winter, and reports that the wet hay of Hessel Bottom thins the cream, blaming the land itself (later shown to be false — see Bulletin No. 214 below).
11. **1909** — Per r06, Strawn visits Ostrey Hollow in fall: scales true, boiler in order (safety tried), milk sweet, cans clean; one man cautioned about a rusted seam.
12. **8 May 1911** — The station's own measuring device (pipette/measure) breaks. A substitute/borrowed measure is used.
    - Per r03 (Keddie) and r04 (Ivy Keddie): the borrowed measure came from Larrow Green, arriving with the cream wagon, used from 8 May to **10 July 1911** (about nine weeks). During this window the recorded average jumped to 3.80 and reverted to 3.61 the day the station's own measure returned.
    - Per r05 (Teague): the break is dated to "spring of 1911," and the borrowed measure was used for "six weeks" before the station's own came up from town — a shorter duration than the ~9 weeks given in r03/r04. **This is a numerical conflict between sources.**
    - Keddie's own book entries, quoted in r03: *"May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure."* and *"July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."* — the question mark is Keddie's own, left standing for 14 years (as of his 1925 letter) because he could not explain it.
    - Per r06, Merle Strawn's own inspection visit fell in **June 1911** — squarely inside this 9-week anomaly window per r03/r04 dating (or possibly just outside the shorter 6-week window per r05) — yet his 1911 log entry reads only: *"Ostrey Hollow, June, on my way over to the other side of the county. Scales true. Watched a test through. Nothing here to note."* He did not flag anything unusual, which is unsurprising since his checklist (scales, lactometer for water, boiler/cold room, watching a test performed) did not include calibrating the pipette/measure itself — no burette-checking method existed yet in 1911 (that began in 1921, see below).
13. **1912** — Per r06, Strawn visits: scales true, lactometer shows no added water, notes cream now shipped down the valley the same day it is separated ("which is more than some do").
14. **1913** — Selby Vose becomes Association manager (serves until 1938). Per r06, Strawn's 1913 visit is to Larrow Green creamery (not Ostrey Hollow) for two days on the churn room/receiving books; notes the new manager (i.e., Selby Vose) came out to the platform with him, unlike his predecessor.
15. **1915** — Per r06, Strawn visits Ostrey Hollow: wash room floor holding water at the drain, ordered it graded, with his general aphorism "A field man who lets a floor go will be shown a floor at every plant on his circuit inside of two years."
16. **1916** — Per r06, Strawn's Ostrey Hollow visit notes: scales true, no added water; "The tester complains that his patrons blame him for their figures. I told him that is the trade and he said he knew it." This is the same year (per r05/Teague) that Teague privately raised the shortfall issue with manager Selby Vose and was told to let it alone — so 1916 saw two independent people (the bookkeeper and, implicitly via Keddie's own complaint to the inspector, the tester himself) registering unease about the figures, with no institutional follow-up either time.
17. **1917** — Per r06, Strawn visits a cheese factory in the next county, then Larrow Green; notes it was "a bad year for help," plants short-handed, testers doing the work of three men — "which is where mistakes live." (Not a direct Ostrey Hollow visit note this year in the extracts given.)
18. **1919** — A printed circular, over manager Selby Vose's signature, is sent to nine families (the Ostrey Hollow patrons) stating there was "water in the cans" — i.e., accusing them of watering/diluting milk (or at least stating the tests showed as much). Per r01 (Vira Toland): her father received this letter at the table, read it twice with his hat still on, and kept it in a desk drawer until he died; his son (Vira's brother) reportedly still has it. Other families kept theirs for decades too — one woman had hers pinned in her trunk lid.
    - Note: r03 (Keddie's own letter) also references "In 1919 a circular went out from the Association to nine families up here saying there was water in their cans," confirming the 1919 date and the "nine families" detail, and states he bears no grudge against those who signed it.
    - **r06 (Strawn):** Strawn's own 1919 log entry directly and independently rebuts the circular's premise: *"Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. The manager of the Association has sent a circular round to the patrons of this station about water in the cans. I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so."* This is the single strongest piece of independent, contemporaneous, professional evidence in the whole documentary record that the "watered milk" accusation was baseless — recorded the same year the circular went out, by a state official with no stake in the outcome.
19. **1920** — Per r06, Strawn visits Ostrey Hollow twice (spring and fall): scales true both times, no added water both times; cold room short of ice in the fall, which he flagged and which "was seen to before I was out of the county."
20. **1921** — Per r06: the state begins issuing graduated burettes (in a wooden case, with printed directions) to field men specifically for checking the delivery accuracy of measures and pipettes at the plants. Strawn's own burette arrived that spring. He notes it is "a slow business" to run a plant's glass against it and "most plants have never had it done." This is the first point in the whole record that anyone had the *equipment* to catch the Ostrey Hollow pipette defect.
21. **1922** — Ansel Keddie writes a summary leaf of averages at the back of his test book (quoted identically in r03 and r04): *"Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80."*
    - Per r06, Strawn's own 1922 Ostrey Hollow visit notes: scales true, no added water, boiler in order — but *"I did not run their glass against the burette this year, the day being short and the road home being what it was, and I have put that down because it is what happened."* This is a near-miss: the pipette check that would eventually catch the defect (in 1923) was skipped in 1922 for logistical reasons, one year before the fault was finally caught.
22. **June 1923** — A crate of milk-test pipettes/measures at the Ostrey Hollow station is condemned by the state's inspector; new ones are ordered. This crate is set aside and stored (later found in the loft, under a tarpaulin, stenciled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET"). Per r02 (Effie Loomis), this crate/condemnation was **never mentioned at the 1925 hearing** at all — not by patrons, officers, or the board — despite the board's award referencing "the books and appliances of the station" as a generic phrase.
    - **r06 (Strawn) — precise date and firsthand account.** Strawn's log entry, dated **"1923, June 12,"** reads (quoted in full): *"Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk pipettes against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them."* He notes he "had carried the burette two seasons before this check" (i.e., since spring 1921, consistent with the burette program's start date above). This June 12, 1923 date matches **exactly** the end-date given later in Bulletin No. 471 ("the twelfth of June, 1923") — this is the FIRST time in the whole documentary record that this precise date is corroborated by an independent, contemporaneous witness (Strawn) rather than only by the retrospective 1958 bulletin.
    - Per r06, **"1923, October"**: Strawn returns to Ostrey Hollow; new glass has been in use since **July** (i.e., about a month after condemnation); he runs the new pipettes against the burette and confirms "They deliver their mark." Scales true.
23. **January 1925** — Patrons of Ostrey Hollow formally petition the Association, claiming their milk had never tested as well as their neighbors' and asking to be made whole.
24. **17 March 1925** — Ansel Keddie gives a nine-sentence statement to the board of arbitration at Ashlin (quoted in full in r03). He asserts he never took cream, tested consistently and correctly for 29 seasons, and cannot explain the figures.
25. **17 March – 4 May 1925** — Board of arbitration sits at Ashlin, chaired by attorney **Alonzo Frick**. Per r02: "Three explanations were argued before it. None of the three was accepted." Per r05 (Teague), Teague testified that the arrangement (one man alone handling weighing, sampling, testing, and running the separator, unsupervised) created opportunity for theft — but did NOT claim to know that theft occurred, and stated "the board found nothing established against anybody."
26. **30 April 1925** — Ansel Keddie is dismissed ("put out") — the last day of the month, despite the board finding "no cause established." Keddie's own letter (r03) juxtaposes these two facts without resolving the contradiction: "I was put out on the thirtieth of April. They found no cause established and they put me out on the last day of the month, and both of those things are true at once, and I have given up trying to hold them in my head together."
    - **r06 (Strawn):** Strawn's **1924** Ostrey Hollow entry (i.e., the year BEFORE Keddie's dismissal) already records the corrected figures: "Scales true, no added water, tests improved. Their book gives the year at 3.80." — confirming Keddie's own recorded average had already jumped to the "true" 3.80% figure a full year before the hearing and his dismissal, using the new (post-1923) pipettes, entirely under Keddie's own testing. This strongly undercuts any theory that Keddie personally caused or benefited from the earlier low readings, since the readings normalized immediately upon equipment replacement while he was still the tester.
27. **The award (1925)**: Per r02 and r05 (both consistent): the board awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons **$3,800**, "without admission," charged to the station account. Quoted award text (r02): *"The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account."*
    - This conflicts with r01, which gives the award as **$3,500**, split nine ways, and frames it as a settlement following a finding of guilt (theft), not a "no cause established" arbitration award.
28. **1925, fall** — Per r06, Strawn's Ostrey Hollow visit (after Keddie's April dismissal) notes: scales true, no added water, tests improved; "New man at the tester's bench this year. He is quick, and quick is not the thing I look for." This is Strawn's only oblique reference to the change of tester — he never names Keddie, never mentions the hearing, theft accusation, or dismissal at all, consistent with his stated professional philosophy of recording only what he weighed/tested/mended, never opinions.
29. **1926** — Per r06: the state discontinues the burette-checking program entirely this year. Instruments are to be kept by the men who already have them, but no more are issued and none are to be returned. Strawn notes his own burette, still in its case in his grip, "has been used twice since" (i.e., only twice more in his whole remaining career, through 1936).
30. **1928** — Per r06, Strawn's Ostrey Hollow visit: scales true, no added water; "The station is quieter than it was." He also notes the loft over the intake room (the same loft that would later be found, in 1958, to contain the condemned 1923 pipette crate) was "full of odds and ends," and he told them a loft over a milk room should hold nothing but air — he "was told in return that it had been full since before the war" (i.e., before WWI, so before 1917/1918). **Oddity/possible tension:** this implies other junk was already in that loft well before the crate itself was carried up there in June 1923, meaning the crate was added to a pre-existing accumulation of stored miscellany, not the loft's sole contents.
31. **1931** — Per r06: Strawn visits Larrow Green creamery and Ostrey Hollow in the same week; both in order; notes a bad ice year across the whole four-county circuit, with every plant short and complaining and two even buying ice from the railroad.
32. **1934** — Ansel Keddie dies, his name still under suspicion/cloud. Per r06, Strawn's 1934 Ostrey Hollow visit (the same year) notes: scales true, no added water; a separator bearing ran hot and the station shut down for two days (since repaired); "The tester's book is kept up, though not in the hand it used to be kept in" — an oblique, unstated acknowledgment that Keddie (or at least "the hand" associated with him) is gone from the operation, without ever naming him or referencing his death.
33. **1936, March** — Per r06: Strawn's LAST round on the circuit — Ostrey Hollow, Larrow Green, and the two factories over the county line, all four checked, scales true at all four, "Nothing to note anywhere and nothing left to say about it." This closes out his 29-year career on the Ordell circuit (1907–1936) on a note of studied blankness.
34. **1938** — Selby Vose's term as manager ends (per r02). Also the year the Association's office was moved and the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in the move (never recovered; per Effie Loomis in r02, "it was carried out with the rest and it did not arrive").
35. **1940** — Effie Loomis retires as secretary (served 1901–1940).
36. **1941** — Effie Loomis writes "Notes toward a history of the Association" (r02).
37. **1945** — Wilbur Teague retires as bookkeeper (served 1905–1945).
38. **1947** — Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks placed in Farland college library (per r04), where they sit unexamined for 11 years.
39. **1958 (February)** — Ivy Keddie finds the condemned pipette crate in the station loft (stenciled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET"), carried up there in June 1923; contains nine unbroken pipettes out of six dozen originally bought.
40. **1958 (9 March)** — Ivy Keddie measures the nine pipettes at Farland against a calibrated burette.
41. **1958 (October)** — Bulletin No. 471 issued (Ivy Keddie and Professor Vaught), exonerating Ansel Keddie and explaining the true cause: undersized pipettes, not theft or bad hay.
42. **1959** — Ivy Keddie delivers "What the Crate Held" to the Ordell County Historical Society at Ashlin (r04), with the crate itself displayed at the meeting.
43. **1979** — Vira Toland's oral history (r01) is recorded — twenty-one years AFTER the 1958/1959 exoneration, yet she still tells the older, disproven "theft" narrative as fact, apparently unaware of (or not crediting) Ivy Keddie's later findings. This is presented without her acknowledging Bulletin No. 471 at all.

### The scientific resolution (the key numeric/technical content, from r04)
- Ivy Keddie consulted **five sets of records**: (1) the station test book (in the Association's office since 1925, delivered to the Historical Society's "Rooms" the year of her talk); (2) the **Brant weigh book** (Hessel Bottom records from 1897 onward, lent by Roy Brant); (3) the **Alder Corners Cheese Company** test figures from Redlow Ridge, Vessey County (copied out by Rosalie Cudd; original book never left Vessey County; Ivy never saw it directly); (4) **Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks** (state dairy inspector's records from 1908, at Farland college library since 1947); (5) the Association's own annual ledgers.
  - **Note on dating conflict:** r04 states Strawn was inspector "from 1908," but r06 (Strawn's own covering note) states he "took the Ordell circuit in nineteen hundred and seven" and his first round entry is dated **1907**. This is a one-year discrepancy between Ivy Keddie's secondhand description of Strawn's tenure start and Strawn's own firsthand account.
- The crate: found February 1958 in the loft over the old intake room at the station, under a tarpaulin, stenciled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET." Carried up there in June 1923 (matching the condemnation event Effie Loomis mentioned, and now precisely dated to **June 12, 1923** by Strawn's own log — see above). Inside, packed in straw: the station's condemned milk-test pipettes — six dozen originally bought new in 1897 (invoice **$77.93** for the whole outfit: pipettes, bottles, acid measure, hand tester, and freight). Only **nine** were unbroken in the crate.
- On **9 March 1958**, Ivy Keddie measured these nine pipettes at Farland against a calibrated burette. Quoted measurement sheet: *"Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle was ruled for."*
- Math: the pipettes were supposed to deliver 17.6 c.c. but actually delivered a mean of 16.72 c.c. — 16.72/17.6 = 0.95 (95%). Every butterfat test made with this glass therefore read 95% of the true value.
- Applying this correction to Keddie's own 1922 summary leaf (recorded average 3.61%): 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80%** true average. The gap (0.19, i.e., "nineteen hundredths") is about 5% of the true 3.80 figure.
- Quantities over 25 full delivery years (1898–1922): station took in **40 million pounds** of milk (average **1,600,000 lbs/year**) — this figure matches Effie Loomis's account in r02 (which independently states the same "forty million pounds... average of one million six hundred thousand pounds in a year," attributed to bookkeeper Teague's annual sheets) but **conflicts with Wilbur Teague's own r05 account**, which states the station took in **44 million pounds** of milk over the years (a different, larger total — internal conflict between two sources, one of which is Teague's own memoir versus the "his figures" cited secondhand by Loomis).
- At the recorded 3.61% test: patrons were credited **1,444,000 lbs** of butterfat. At the true 3.80% test: they had actually delivered **1,520,000 lbs**. Difference: **76,000 lbs of butterfat**, unpaid.
- Butterfat averaged **$0.25/lb** over those years (matching Effie Loomis's r02 figure of "the price paid for butterfat averaged twenty-five cents a pound" for the 25-year claim period) → unpaid fat worth **$19,000**.
- The 1925 arbitration award of $3,800 was therefore "a fifth of" the true value owed ($3,800/$19,000 = 0.2).
- Two valid-but-different percentages exist depending on the denominator: 76,000 lbs is 5% of the actual fat delivered (1,520,000) and 5.25% of the fat credited (1,444,000). Ivy Keddie notes the Association's bookkeeping (per r05, Teague's "station gain" line) used the second denominator ("a bookkeeper naturally reckons a surplus against the figure he has already entered").
- **Key connection**: this 5.25% figure is EXACTLY the same percentage Wilbur Teague's ledger recorded every year as "station gain" (see r05 below) — meaning Teague's own books had been recording physical evidence of the shortfall for decades ("as usual") without anyone identifying its cause.
- Because the test error was proportional (a fixed percentage), richer milk lost more in absolute terms: a herd recorded at 4.56% test was short 0.24 points; one recorded at 3.42% was short only 0.18 points.
- The 1911 anomaly is explained: the substitute/borrowed measure (which, unlike the station's own faulty pipettes, was accurate) was in use 8 May–10 July 1911, causing tests to jump to the true 3.80% and drop back to the biased 3.61% the moment the faulty station measure returned — "one object changed" and the whole average moved, which Ivy Keddie says is the only surviving explanation for "this valley's twenty-six-year anomaly."
- The **wet-hay theory is explicitly refuted**. State Agricultural College tested the claim in 1911 and published **Bulletin No. 214**, quoted: *"Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."* This is "four times too small" and "the wrong shape" compared to the observed year-round 0.19-point gap (present in July as much as February).
- Cross-check: the Alder Corners factory (over the ridge) tested milk from four Hessel Bottom farmers during their summer flush; across 14 overlapping seasons, the SAME milk from the SAME wet ground tested about 0.19 points HIGHER at Alder Corners than at home — proving the milk itself wasn't deficient, only the Ostrey Hollow station's measurement.
- **Bulletin No. 471**, issued October 1958 (Ivy Keddie and Professor Vaught), final exoneration, quoted in full: *"No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."*
  - Note: this bulletin dates the faulty-pipette period as "the first of May, 1897" to "the twelfth of June, 1923" — i.e., starting when the station's OWN outfit arrived in 1897 (not the 1896 borrowed glass) and ending when the crate was condemned in June 1923. ### Wilbur Teague's "station-gain" ledger line (r05) — the bookkeeping trace of the same phenomenon
- Teague invented this bookkeeping device himself in 1905: one column entered the butterfat credited to patrons on the station's OWN test; the other entered the butterfat actually received and tested at Larrow Green from the station's cream. The second figure was ALWAYS larger. The difference was labeled "station gain" and credited to the Association's general account (i.e., the Association benefited financially from the shortfall, though unknowingly, per the later exoneration).
- Quoted sample entry, for 1898: *"Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account."*
- This "five and a quarter per cent" (5.25%) gain recurred so consistently every year that the phrase "as usual" became part of the standard ledger entry — precisely matching the 5.25% figure Ivy Keddie later calculates independently from the pipette measurements (76,000/1,444,000 = 5.25%). Teague's ledgers were unknowingly recording the exact signature of the equipment defect for decades.
- Teague's testimony to the 1925 board argued that the station's staffing arrangement (one man alone doing everything, unsupervised, for 29 seasons) created opportunity for theft, but he explicitly says he does NOT know what, if anything, any particular person did, and confirms "the board found nothing established against anybody."
- Teague states Selby Vose signed the annual station sheet every year (so had seen the figures) and that Teague raised concerns with Vose privately in 1916 but was told to let it alone, and did.
