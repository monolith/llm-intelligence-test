# Batch 1 Answers

General method note: where sources conflict I resolved using, in rough order of weight: (1) a document that self-corrects after checking (outranks everything, including its own earlier claim), (2) a primary/contemporaneous document (an invoice, a diary entry written the same day) over a later memory, (3) internal arithmetic consistency, (4) a firsthand/insider account over a secondhand one, (5) independent corroboration — counting sources that could share one origin (e.g. a newspaper quoting an existing pamphlet) as one, not two. I say explicitly which of these I used each time.

## Section A — Reconstruction

*Note on scope:* the retention notes available to me document one large, continuously-retold history — the Ostrey Hollow milk-testing affair — carried across 24 retellings (r01–r24). I have treated the eight "underlying stories" as eight distinguishable episodes within that one history (the notes explicitly say several narrators "ran separate strands together," which is exactly what I've tried to pull apart). The three below are the first three in calendar order.

### 1. The Founding of the Association and the Opening of the Ostrey Hollow Station (1889–1897)

In 1889, farmers of Larrow Valley organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association at Larrow Green. Gideon Vose, described as "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the law and thought better of it," drafted the by-laws at his own kitchen table and served as first president for 22 years (1889–1911; he died in 1917). The central rule, Article VII, provided that every patron be paid according to the butterfat test of his milk, not its weight — the single clause that made everything that followed matter. Its adoption date is disputed three ways (1891, 1892, 1893) among the sources that quote it, and even a careful 1959 county investigation (Orin Falke) could not settle it: the printed by-laws carry no date, and the Association's own founding-era minute book was lost in a later office move. The main creamery and churn were built at Larrow Green in 1891. Seven miles up-valley, at the head of Ostrey Hollow, milk from the rich Hessel Bottom meadow — grazed and hayed by six farms (the majority-supported figure over one source's "seven"), the largest belonging to Emmet Brant — needed its own testing point, since it couldn't easily be hauled whole to Larrow Green. Ansel Keddie went up in 1895 to prepare the station; it was built that winter and opened 4 May 1896 (independently confirmed by Falke's report). Having no equipment of its own yet, the station borrowed testing glass, most sources say from Larrow Green itself (one accepted from buttermaker Hobart Sill personally). This stopgap lasted about a year. Quantities that matter: Article VII's wording; the unresolved 1891/1892/1893 adoption dispute; six Hessel Bottom farms. Documents: Article VII (quoted verbatim by three sources); Falke's 1959 report; Orra Keddie's diary.

### 2. Ammon & Sons and the Flaw in the Glass (1897)

In spring 1897 the Association bought its own testing outfit — six dozen pipettes marked to deliver 17.6 c.c., twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, and a four-bottle hand tester — from Ammon & Sons, a dairy supply house in Tarnet founded by Marcus Ammon ("a creamery buys arithmetic from us and not glassware"). The invoice, dated 14 April 1897, totaled $77.93 (the firm's own 1949 retelling gives a conflicting $77.39). The outfit went into use at Ostrey Hollow on 1 May 1897 — fixed independently by both the invoice and, remarkably, by Orra Keddie's diary entry written that same day: "the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green. He is pleased about it." Unknown to anyone, the pipette mold had been reground before this lot was cast, and the new pipettes delivered short: an average of 16.27 c.c. against their marked 17.6 c.c. — a defect no ordinary spot-check could catch, because it was consistently, if wrongly, the same every time. This single silent manufacturing error would understate every butterfat test at the station for the next 26 years and would not be identified until 1958. Ammon & Sons never suspected it: when the Association wrote in 1923 asking what had gone wrong, the firm's only reply was that its 1890s books could not say. Junia Ammon, writing the company's own history in 1949 after the truth was known, gave a plausible, unevasive account of how such a defect could pass every check, and regretted only the unhelpful 1923 letter, not the original accident. Quantities: $77.93 invoice total; six dozen pipettes marked 17.6 c.c.; 1 May 1897. Documents: the 1897 invoice and the 1923 reply letter (both quoted in full by Junia Ammon); Orra Keddie's diary.

### 3. Rising Complaints and the 1908 Wet-Hay Committee (1898–1908)

By 1898 the station's recorded average butterfat test had settled at 3.61 — measurably lower than the Larrow Green creamery's own long-run average of 3.78, though no one yet knew this was a real, fixed gap rather than ordinary variation. Store talk that the Hollow's milk "ran poorer" first surfaced in 1902; Orra Keddie's diary records her husband Ansel coming home that March, refusing to eat, and telling her "it is not your cows and not your hands... but it is my figures they will look at" — then saying nothing more about it, a silence he kept for the rest of his working life. The complaints eventually reached the Association's leadership, which in January 1908 sent a four-man committee up the valley by sleigh under Larrow Green farmer Nyle Grigg — an outing Orra's diary independently confirms ("Four men up the road today in Grigg's sleigh, come to look into the herds"). The committee visited Emmet Brant's farm first, as the largest on the Bottom, and was given dinner there; per Orra, the men "wanted to see the barns and not the station" — they examined cows and feed, not equipment. In March 1908 the committee reported its conclusion: the wet hay of the low Hessel Bottom meadow was thinning the cream. Emmet Brant, whose family had farmed the bottom for generations, dismissed it flatly: "the bottom fed his father and it will feed him." The wet-hay verdict became the Association's standing explanation for decades, and would not be checked against real feeding-trial figures until much later. Quantities: 3.61 (Ostrey Hollow) vs. 3.78 (Larrow Green); four-man committee, Jan–Mar 1908. Documents: the 1908 committee report; Orra Keddie's diary; Falke's 1959 report, which later tested and rejected this theory with Bulletin No. 214's data.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**
She was his granddaughter — daughter of his son Duncan. This is stated directly by Duncan himself (her own father), and independently by Roy Brant, Hazel Brant, and Cleve Oram. Two sources instead call her Ansel's "great-niece" (the Ashlin Chronicle, and the main body of Orin Falke's 1959 county report) — but Falke's own report contains an appended note, written after he verified the point, that retracts this exact error: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." A source correcting its own error after checking outranks everything else here, and it also explains the Chronicle's identical mistake as a real, recurring, self-propagating slip rather than a competing genealogy. Settled: granddaughter.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**
Second cousins. Duncan Keddie (Ivy's father) lays out the chain explicitly: Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie (Duncan's mother) were siblings; their children, Roy Brant and Duncan, are therefore first cousins; Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy, as children of first cousins, are second cousins — Duncan explicitly rules out both "cousins once removed" and "aunt and niece" as loose popular shorthand. Three other sources agree. Cleve Oram alone calls it "first cousin once removed," a direct contradiction, but he is neither family nor working from a stated genealogy, so I weight Duncan's firsthand, explicit derivation higher. What the sources leave open: whether Emmet and Orra were full or half siblings is itself disputed (majority of firsthand testimony says full; one secondhand account says half). If they were half-siblings, Roy and Duncan would be half first cousins and Hazel/Ivy half second cousins — a distinction no source addresses.

**B3. Selby Vose's father, and why it matters.**
Gideon Vose, the Association's founder and first president. Established by Duncan Keddie, who directly names and rebuts a rival "nephew" claim from an earlier pamphlet, and independently confirmed by Bertram Kyle, the Association's own clerk: "the son, whatever is said at Ashlin... it is nonsense." It matters because Selby held the manager's post for 25 years (1913–1938 by the best-attested dating), the entire span covering the 1919 water-theory circular he personally signed, the 1923 glass condemnation, and the 1925 arbitration and dismissal of Keddie. Being the founder's son gave him an inherited standing that an outsider manager likely wouldn't have had, and the clerk who watched him for a quarter-century describes him as reading only bottom-line totals and signing without checking the figures above them — a plausible part of why a discrepancy recorded in the Association's own books every single year went unexamined at the management level for decades.

**B4. The Vose family and the Keddie family — any relationship?**
None, by the best evidence. Duncan Keddie states directly that "there was no connection whatever between us and the Voses," and identifies the likely source of confusion: a separate, unrelated Vose family in Ashlin, mistaken for Gideon's line. He is rebutting a specific rumor — that his mother Orra and Gideon Vose's wife were cousins — heard from "two women in this town." Cleve Oram, speaking decades later in 1977, repeats essentially the same rumor as though it were simply true, but sources it only to "my mother had it from her mother," admitting "I could not draw you the line on paper." I weight Duncan far more heavily: he is family, addressing a specific claim about his own mother with a specific counter-explanation, while Oram's account is explicit secondhand hearsay two generations removed. Conclusion: no real blood relationship; the rumor is best explained as confusion between two unrelated families sharing a surname, and it evidently outlived Duncan's own rebuttal in local talk.

## Section C — Math

**C1. The station-wide error.**

*(a) Proportion, and how it comes from the glassware.*
The pipettes were graduated to deliver a standard charge of 17.6 c.c. of milk into the test bottle. In 1958 Ivy Keddie measured the nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette and found they actually delivered an average of 16.27 c.c. Since the fat reading is scaled against the bottle's neck, which is marked for a full 17.6 c.c. charge, a short charge reads low in the same proportion it is short:
(17.6 − 16.27) / 17.6 = 1.33 / 17.6 ≈ 0.0756.
**So the tests ran about 7.6% below the true reading (equivalently, a recorded test was only about 92.4% of the true one) — derived purely from the pipette shortfall, independent of any test-record comparison.**

*(b) True average test, and the point-shortfall.*
The station's own long-run recorded average was 3.61 (Falke's 1959 report). The true average is best put at **3.80**, converging from three separate, independent measurements: (i) Falke's stated deficiency of "nineteen hundredths of a point" (0.19), constant in every month, added to 3.61 gives 3.80; (ii) during a nine-week stretch in 1911 when the station's measure broke and a substitute was used, the average rose to 3.80 and fell back to 3.61 once the original glass returned; (iii) Jerome Cudd's own 1919 test-book entries for four Hessel Bottom farmers hauling to a different creamery averaged 3.80 (3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82). Three independent routes landing on the same number is strong convergence.
**True average ≈ 3.80; recorded tests were short by 0.19 percentage points, constant year-round.**
One loose end I can't close: Falke separately reports the station's *actual* post-repair average from 1924 onward as 3.85, not 3.80 — a small (0.05) residual gap between the "corrected" figure and what was actually measured after the fix. The sources don't explain it (natural improvement over decades vs. rounding vs. some other factor); I flag it rather than force a match.
Note also that (a)'s proportional shortfall (~7.6%) and (b)'s point-shortfall (0.19/3.80 ≈ 5.0% relative) don't line up exactly either. These are two different, independently-sourced ways of estimating "how wrong," and the sources never reconcile them — I report both rather than pick one.

*(c) Pounds of butterfat patrons were not credited with, over the full affected years.*
The defective glass was in service from 1 May 1897 to its condemnation on 12 June 1923 — about 26 delivery seasons.
Two total-milk figures exist for "the long run of years" of the dispute: ~44,000,000 lbs (Rundle's pamphlet, exactly echoed by the Ashlin Chronicle — but the Chronicle admits it was assembled in a day and a half from a press release and phone calls, so I treat this as **one** source, not two independent ones) and Bertram Kyle's separate, rounder recollection of "forty million pounds" (a genuinely independent, if looser, estimate). These broadly agree.
Using 44,000,000 lbs × 0.0019 (the 0.19-point deficiency) ≈ **83,600 lbs of butterfat** the patrons should have been credited with and were not, over the affected span.
Caveat: the sources frame the 44M/40M figures as covering "the long run of years of complaint" (complaints began 1902), not explicitly the full 1897–1923 defect period — a somewhat different, probably shorter, span than the one the question asks about. A separate, directly-quoted contemporary figure exists — Frick reports the patrons' own 1925 reckoning as **67,000 lbs** — but that estimate was made before the precise, constant 0.19-point deficiency was known (established only by the 1958 bulletin), so it likely used cruder assumptions and/or a shorter window.
**Best-supported answer: roughly 80,000–84,000 lbs over the full 26-year affected period, derived as above; the patrons' own contemporaneous claim was lower (67,000 lbs), for reasons the sources don't fully explain. I would not present either number as exact.**

*(d) Money value, and the fraction actually received.*
Rundle's figures pair 44,000,000 lbs of milk with a stated loss of **$19,600** — I take this as the primary quoted money figure, since it's a source's own paired calculation rather than mine. As a rough independent check: 83,600 lbs (from (c)) × $0.25/lb (Kyle's stated long-run average butterfat price) ≈ $20,900 — within about 7% of Rundle's figure, reasonable convergence given everything above is approximate.
The 1925 arbitration award was **$3,800** (the best-attested figure across independent sources, against a $3,500 minority variant).
Fraction actually received: $3,800 / $19,600 ≈ **19%** — roughly one dollar in five of what the patrons' own contemporary reckoning said they were owed.

**C2. Emmet Brant (largest patron), 1910.**
His weigh-book leaf for 1910 records 200,000 lbs of milk delivered, at the station's recorded test of 3.42%.
*(a) Credited:* 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lbs of butterfat.**
*(b) Owed and not credited:* using the flat 0.19-point deficiency (true test 3.42 + 0.19 = 3.61%): 200,000 × 0.0019 = **380 lbs of butterfat.**
*(c) Value of the shortfall in 1910:* 380 lbs × $0.25/lb (Kyle's stated long-run average price) = **$95.00.**
Caveat: the 25-cent figure is explicitly a whole-period average ("fat years and lean"), not a price specific to 1910 — no source gives a year-specific price, so this is the best available estimate, not a documented 1910 figure.
