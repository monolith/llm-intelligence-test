# Answers — Part 2 of 3
## Section A — Reconstruction: Stories 5–8 of 8

### 5. Two Families on One Meadow

The Brants of Hessel Bottom (Emmet; his son Roy; his granddaughter Hazel) and the Keddies (Ansel; his wife Orra, Emmet's sister; their son Duncan; Duncan's daughter Ivy) are bound together by one marriage and split apart by one letter.

Emmet Brant farmed the largest of six holdings on the wet Hessel Bottom meadow and was the station's biggest patron throughout its life. He kept a private weigh book from 1 January 1897, entering his own scale weight beside the station's every delivery day; the two agreed to within a pound for thirty years, which is the clearest available proof that the discrepancy was never in the weighing. His sister Orra married the station's tester, Ansel Keddie, in 1889. When the manager's 1919 "water in the cans" circular went out, Emmet accused Ansel of having carried a paper himself that he should have refused to carry; the two men, brothers-in-law of thirty-six years, did not speak again until Emmet's death in 1929.

Their children, Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie, were first cousins (children of a brother and sister); their children in turn, Hazel Brant and Ivy Keddie, are second cousins — a relation both women state identically, and one Duncan Keddie takes care to spell out precisely because he has "twice seen it printed otherwise." Roy Brant explicitly and pointedly denies a rumor that Orra was only Emmet's half-sister "by a stepmother"; his own daughter Hazel repeats that exact rumor regardless — an unresolved crack within the family's own testimony (see Section E). Duncan's 1968 family notes are the fullest genealogical record in the whole set, and also debunk an unrelated valley rumor that the Keddies and the Association's founding Vose family were related by blood.

**Quantities:** Brant weigh book begun 1 January 1897 (Duncan, curator, Frick's exhibit list agree; Roy says 1898; Hazel says 1900); marriage 1889; Orra's birth year given as both 1866 and 1868 across sources.
**Documents:** r09 (Roy Brant), r17 (Hazel Brant), r18 (Duncan Keddie).

### 6. Living Through the Trouble

Vira Toland, a Hessel Bottom patron's daughter, Cleve Oram, the cream hauler's son, and Orra Keddie, the tester's own wife, supply the lived texture behind the documents.

Toland (taped 1979) recalls the 1919 circular arriving at her family's table, her father reading it with his hat still on and filing it in a drawer for the rest of his life, and the valley's settled belief — which she herself repeats as fact — that Keddie was simply caught skimming, the tests correcting themselves the moment he was found out. She also passes along two Vose-family claims (that Selby was Gideon's nephew, and that Orra and Mrs. Vose were cousins) that Duncan Keddie's own family notes elsewhere flatly deny. Oram (taped 1977), whose father drove the cream wagon down from the station for twenty years, recalls the crate sitting physically in the loft directly above two of the three 1925 hearing sessions, and his father's lifelong, ultimately mistaken conviction that cream volume was being shorted at the station end.

Orra Keddie's own diary (kept 1897–1930, published 1964) is the most intimate and contemporaneous record in the set. She notes the station's new glass arriving from Larrow Green on 1 May 1897; her husband's silence through the 1902 complaints and the 1908 committee visit; the 1919 circular, which she says he delivered by hand to eleven families (not the "nine" nearly every other source gives); her brother Emmet's angry confrontation in the yard and the thirty-six-year friendship it ended; and Ansel's stoic reaction to his 30 April 1925 dismissal — walking up the road at his ordinary hour, hanging his apron behind the shed door, and asking what there was for supper. A leaf found loose in the back of her third notebook, written the morning he died in February 1934, records that not one mourner at his funeral spoke the old accusation aloud — though none retracted it either.

**Quantities:** circular delivered 1919 to 9 or 11 families (sources conflict); dismissal 30 April 1925; Ansel Keddie's death, February 1934.
**Documents:** r01 (Toland), r21 (Oram), r24 (Orra Keddie's diary).

### 7. The Wet Hay Theory

Nyle Grigg (a Larrow Green farmer with no stake in the outcome, who chaired a 1908 investigating committee), Dr. Roland Kepp (a veterinarian who elaborated the theory scientifically in 1926), and Rosalie Cudd (a cheesemaker across Redlow Ridge who unknowingly refuted it) trace this theory's rise and fall.

In 1908 Grigg's committee sampled the Hessel Bottom herds through January, February, and March only, and concluded — finding "no dishonesty of any kind" — that the bottom's wet, sour hay was thinning the cream and "the remedy lies with the land." When the complaint resurfaced in 1919, Grigg reprinted the report with a covering letter that misremembers his own committee's work as having sampled "the whole of 1908... every month of that year," and cites the State Agricultural College's Bulletin No. 214 as finding the hay depressed the test by "two tenths" of a point — both claims contradicted by the very documents Grigg is introducing (the reprinted report says three winter months only; the bulletin's real finding, quoted directly elsewhere, was "not more than five hundredths" in winter and nothing measurable in summer). Dr. Kepp's 1926 paper independently repeats the identical "two tenths" misquotation and dismisses the 1911 measuring-glass anomaly as too short an interval — six weeks, by his count — to mean anything.

The theory was never tested against an outside standard until Rosalie Cudd, replying to a 1958 letter from Ivy Keddie, produced her late father's untouched thirty-year test record for the same Hessel Bottom herds' summer milk, delivered instead to the Alder Corners cheese factory: a steady 3.78–3.82 across sixteen seasons, wet years and dry alike — flatly incompatible with the idea that the ground itself could be depressing the fat.

**Quantities:** 1908 sampling window, 3 months only; Bulletin 214's actual finding, ≤0.05 point in winter, none in summer; Alder Corners average ≈3.80 across 16 seasons, 1915–1930.
**Documents:** r15 (Grigg), r16 (Kepp), r10 (Cudd).

### 8. The Glass — Made, Condemned, Kept

Junia Ammon (historian of the Tarnet supply house), Merle Strawn (the state dairy inspector), and Halbert Nym (the 1972 curator) trace the physical object at the center of the whole affair.

Ammon & Sons sold the Association a complete testing outfit for the new Ostrey Hollow station on 14 April 1897 — six dozen milk-test pipettes blown to deliver 17.6 cc, bottles, an acid measure, and a hand tester, totaling $77.93 — put into use 1 May 1897. Ammon's own 1949 company history cannot say from surviving records exactly what went wrong with that lot (the works books burned in 1931), but offers a plausible, non-fraudulent explanation: a reground mold producing glass that was consistently short and therefore invisible to ordinary spot-checking, since a check against a self-consistent lot simply confirms the lot's own (wrong) standard. Strawn, the circuit's inspector from 1907, visited the station roughly annually, always found the scales true, and never once found added water — including on a visit made specifically in response to the 1919 circular — but had no outside calibration standard for pipettes until the state began issuing burettes to field men in 1921. On 12 June 1923 he finally ran the station's glass against his burette, found it would not deliver its mark, and condemned the whole crate on the spot; new glass was in use within the month.

The condemned crate then sat, forgotten, in the station's own loft for thirty-five years until Ivy Keddie found it in 1958. Curator Halbert Nym's 1972 accession notes catalogue the crate, the invoice, the inspector's notebook, and related papers, and — without seeming to notice it — appear to swap the custodial attributions of two items: the station test book (clearly Keddie's own, by its contents) is labeled "kept by E. Brant," while the household farm weigh book (clearly the Brants', by its contents and provenance) is labeled "kept by A. Keddie."

**Quantities:** invoice 14 April 1897, $77.93, six dozen pipettes; condemned 12 June 1923; new glass in use by July 1923.
**Documents:** r06 (Strawn), r07 (Nym), r20 (Ammon).

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**
Granddaughter. Derivation: Ivy is the daughter of Duncan Keddie (r04, r18), and Duncan is explicitly Ansel's son (r03's byline, r18: "My father was Ansel Keddie... I am Duncan... their son"; and r18 directly: "My daughter is Ivy... which makes her my father's granddaughter"). Two sources point elsewhere and are wrong: the *Ashlin Chronicle* (r19) and county agent Falke's report (r22) both initially call her Ansel's "great-niece." Falke's own report catches and corrects this in its closing note ("she is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine"), which is decisive: a document that corrects itself against direct family testimony outranks the uncorrected error in the newspaper. Granddaughter is the resolved relationship.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**
Second cousins. Ivy's father, Duncan Keddie, and Hazel's father, Roy Brant, are first cousins — children of siblings Orra Keddie and Emmet Brant (stated by Duncan, r18; by Roy, r09; and by Hazel herself, r17). Children of first cousins are second cousins, which all three family-insider sources state identically, and which Duncan explicitly defends against a specific rival claim: "Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation." Two outside oral sources — Vira Toland (r01) and Cleve Oram (r21) — instead call the pair "first cousin once removed," which is simply wrong by the family's own consistent, converging account. Left open: the sources do not agree on whether Orra and Emmet (the linking generation) were full or half siblings (see B4-adjacent discussion in Section E) — this doesn't change the cousin arithmetic, but it is a genuine unresolved detail about how that generation's link was formed.

**B3. Selby Vose's father, and why it matters.**
Gideon Vose — the Association's founder, first president (1889–1911), and author of its by-laws, including Article VII. This is confirmed by three independent, well-placed sources: secretary Effie Loomis (r02, "son of the late president"), Duncan Keddie (r18, explicitly correcting "as I have heard it given, his nephew"), and clerk Bertram Kyle (r23, calling the nephew story "nonsense"). Two oral/folk sources — Vira Toland (r01) and Dell Rundle's pamphlet (r12) — say "nephew" instead; both are outvoted by more authoritative, direct testimony. It matters because Selby Vose, as manager from 1913, administered and refused to revisit the very rule his own father had written — that a station's own test is final and unappealable — and it was Selby who signed the 1919 accusatory circular and who saw and signed the incriminating "station gain" ledger line every year, including after bookkeeper Teague told him about it directly in 1916. The father-son relationship sharpens the sense that responsibility for how rigidly the rule was enforced sat inside one family across two generations.

**B4. Any relationship between the Vose and Keddie families.**
None established. Vira Toland (r01) asserts as common valley knowledge that Orra Keddie and "old Mrs. Vose" were first cousins; Cleve Oram (r21) repeats a vaguer version ("cousins of some sort... I could not draw you the line on paper"). Both are secondhand oral claims. Duncan Keddie — Orra's own son, and therefore the best-placed witness to his own mother's family connections — flatly denies it: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses... my mother would have told me... and she never once put a Vose in it." Given that Toland's account also independently gets the separate Selby Vose "nephew" question wrong, I treat this as a pattern of unreliable valley gossip about the Vose family specifically, and resolve that no Vose-Keddie relationship is established by the sources.
