### 1. The Association and the Rule That Bound the Valley

In 1889, farmers of the Larrow Valley met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, tired of taking whatever price a merchant chose to allow their butter. Gideon Vose was elected first president (serving until 1911) and personally drafted the by-laws. The main creamery was built at Larrow Green in 1891. Article VII — adopted in 1892, per secretary Effie Loomis's direct quotation from the record, the most authoritative date available — established that a patron is paid by the butterfat found in his milk by test, not by weight, and, critically, that "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern," with no appeal to any other test anywhere. Loomis, who kept the minute book from 1901 to 1940, is candid that the Association's own record has gaps: the 1889–1892 minute book was lost in an office move in 1938, so nothing about the founding years rests on anything but recollection and the printed by-laws pamphlet, which itself "carries no year on its face."

Later writers tried to retell this history and stumbled on the same gap. Dell Rundle's 1939 jubilee pamphlet gives Article VII the year 1891, apparently conflating it with the year the creamery/churn was built. Nyle Grigg, in an unrelated 1919 letter, dates "payment by test" to 1893, explicitly hedged as "how I have always understood it." County agent Orin Falke's 1959 report, working from the same undated pamphlet, declines to state a year at all rather than guess.

What matters for everything that follows: Article VII's "station governs" clause is the structural reason one broken instrument at one remote station could distort a generation of pay with no internal mechanism to catch it against the main creamery's own figures.

**Quantities:** founding 1889; creamery built 1891; Article VII adopted 1892 (best-supported); minute book lost 1938.
**Documents:** r02 (Loomis), r12 (Rundle), r22 (Falke); the by-laws text itself is transcribed in r07 (Item 1) and referenced in r23.

### 2. The Arbitration at Ashlin

In January 1925 the Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned the Association, saying their milk had tested low for a generation and asking to be made whole. A three-man board chaired by attorney Alonzo Frick sat three times: 17 March 1925 at the Association's Ashlin office, and two further sessions held at the station itself, in its own weigh room, so the board could see the plant firsthand. Three explanations were argued: added water (urged by manager Selby Vose, who had circulated a warning to the patrons in 1919), cream theft by the tester (implied in bookkeeper Wilbur Teague's testimony, put obliquely rather than as a direct accusation), and wet Hessel Bottom hay thinning the cream (revived from a 1908 committee report). Tester Ansel Keddie gave a nine-sentence statement on 17 March: he denied ever taking a pound of cream and said he could not account for the figures.

Four days before the board reported, on 30 April 1925, the Association dismissed Keddie outright — before the board had reached any conclusion. On 4 May 1925 the board found "no cause established" against anyone, yet awarded the patrons $3,800, charged to the station account, "without admission." Frick later recalled that the crate of condemned pipettes — sitting the whole time in the station's own loft, directly over two of the three sessions — was never introduced as evidence; the exhibit list held only four items: the station test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book, and the state inspector's 1923 notebook. Vose's own 1926 annual address defended the award as generous "peace money" for an unproved claim, and insisted — contrary to the state inspector's own repeated findings — that watering was the true cause and had stopped once the Association began "looking."

**Quantities:** petition January 1925; hearing 17 March–4 May 1925; dismissal 30 April 1925; award $3,800.
**Documents:** r03 (Keddie's statement and letter), r08 (Vose's address), r13 (Frick's recollections); the hearing's mechanics are corroborated in r02, r07, r21, r23, r24.

### 3. The Ledger That Told On Itself

Wilbur Teague (Association bookkeeper, 1905–1945), Hobart Sill (Larrow Green buttermaker, 1893–1934), and Bertram Kyle (office clerk) each independently held a piece of the exculpatory evidence for decades without recognizing it.

From 1905 Teague kept a "station gain" line in the Association's own books: every year, the butterfat that actually arrived at Larrow Green in the Ostrey Hollow cream exceeded what the station itself had credited to its patrons. The 1898 entry, typical of every year after: 57,760 lb credited, 60,800 lb received, a gain of 3,040 lb — "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" — simply carried to the Association's general account as found money. Sill, who reweighed incoming cream himself at the creamery end for decades, independently confirms the same pattern — "it ran heavy... always more, never less" — though Sill, unlike Teague, took this as proof the tester was stealing rather than as evidence against that theory. Teague states he raised the anomaly directly with manager Selby Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone, and did. Clerk Bertram Kyle, who copied these same entries onto the annual statements for a quarter-century, recalls that Vose "read the totals and never read the lines above them" — signing without engaging the meaning of what he signed — while independently and flatly rejecting a persistent rumor that Vose was Gideon Vose's nephew rather than his son.

None of the three men connected the surplus to a faulty pipette before 1958. Each treated "station gain" as a self-explaining bookkeeping fact rather than a clue — even though, mechanically, cream cannot run heavy at the delivery end if the tester upstream is skimming it off.

**Quantities:** sample entry, 1898: 57,760 lb credited / 60,800 lb received / 3,040 lb gain (5.25%); pattern recorded annually for 25+ years.
**Documents:** r05 (Teague), r14 (Sill), r23 (Kyle).

### 4. The Crate in the Loft

In February 1958 Ivy Keddie, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland and Ansel Keddie's granddaughter, climbed into the loft above the old station's intake room and found, under a tarpaulin, the crate of milk-test pipettes a state inspector had condemned in 1923 and that had simply been stored there since. Of six dozen pipettes bought new in 1897, nine survived intact. On 9 March 1958 she measured them against a calibrated burette and found a mean delivery of 16.72 cc against a stem blown for 17.6 cc — a shortfall of 0.88 cc, or 5% low. Because a butterfat test reads a fixed milk charge as a percentage against a bottle neck ruled for that exact charge, a charge 5% short yields a reading proportionally 5% low, in every test, indefinitely — fully explaining the station's decades of low readings without any wrongdoing by patrons, tester, or Association.

She and Professor Leland Vaught co-authored Bulletin No. 471 (State Agricultural College, October 1958), which formally cleared Ansel Keddie: "no milk was watered... and no cream was stolen." The *Ashlin Chronicle* covered the story on 3 October 1958, but carelessly: it called Ivy Ansel's "great-niece" (she was his granddaughter), repeated an internal error from the bulletin's own method section — a "16.27 cc" figure that contradicts the bulletin's own stated conclusion — miscounted the invoice total, and speculated without any supporting evidence that the glass supplier had knowingly sold a bad lot.

**Quantities:** 6 dozen pipettes bought 1897, invoice $77.93; 9 recovered 1958; mean delivery 16.72 cc vs. 17.6 cc mark; shortfall 0.88 cc (5%).
**Documents:** r04 (Ivy Keddie's talk), r11 (Vaught/Bulletin 471), r19 (*Ashlin Chronicle*).

### 5. Two Families on One Meadow

The Brants of Hessel Bottom (Emmet; his son Roy; his granddaughter Hazel) and the Keddies (Ansel; his wife Orra, Emmet's sister; their son Duncan; Duncan's daughter Ivy) are bound together by one marriage and split apart by one letter.

Emmet Brant farmed the largest of six holdings on the wet Hessel Bottom meadow and was the station's biggest patron throughout its life. He kept a private weigh book from 1 January 1897, entering his own scale weight beside the station's every delivery day; the two agreed to within a pound for thirty years, which is the clearest available proof that the discrepancy was never in the weighing. His sister Orra married the station's tester, Ansel Keddie, in 1889. When the manager's 1919 "water in the cans" circular went out, Emmet accused Ansel of having carried a paper himself that he should have refused to carry; the two men, brothers-in-law of thirty-six years, did not speak again until Emmet's death in 1929.

Their children, Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie, were first cousins (children of a brother and sister); their children in turn, Hazel Brant and Ivy Keddie, are second cousins — a relation both women state identically, and one Duncan Keddie takes care to spell out precisely because he has "twice seen it printed otherwise." Roy Brant explicitly and pointedly denies a rumor that Orra was only Emmet's half-sister "by a stepmother"; his own daughter Hazel repeats that exact rumor regardless — an unresolved crack within the family's own testimony (see Section E). Duncan's 1968 family notes are the fullest genealogical record in the whole set, and also debunk an unrelated valley rumor that the Keddies and the Association's founding Vose family were related by blood.

**Quantities:** Brant weigh book begun 1 January 1897 (Duncan, curator, Frick's exhibit list agree; Roy says 1898; Hazel says 1900); marriage 1889; Orra's birth year given as both 1866 and 1868 across sources.
**Documents:** r09 (Roy Brant), r17 (Hazel Brant), r18 (Duncan Keddie).

### 6. Living Through the Trouble

Vira Toland, a Hessel Bottom patron's daughter, Cleve Oram, the cream hauler's son, and Orra Keddie, the tester's own wife, supply the lived texture behind the documents.

Toland (taped 1979) recalls the 1919 circular arriving at her family's table, her father reading it with his hat still on and filing it in a drawer for the rest of his life, and the valley's settled belief — which she herself repeats as fact — that Keddie was simply caught skimming, the tests correcting themselves the moment he was found out. She also passes along two Vose-family claims (that Selby was Gideon's nephew, and that Orra and Mrs. Vose were cousins) that Duncan Keddie's own family notes elsewhere flatly deny. Oram (taped 1977), whose father drove the cream wagon down from the station for twenty years, recalls the crate sitting physically in the loft directly above two of the three 1925 hearing sessions, and his father's lifelong, ultimately mistaken conviction that cream volume was being shorted at the station end.

Orra Keddie's own diary (kept 1897–1930, published 1964) is the most intimate and contemporaneous record in the set. She notes the station's new glass arriving from Larrow Green on 1 May 1897; her husband's silence through the 1902 complaints and the 1908 committee visit; the 1919 circular, which she says he delivered by hand to eleven families (not the "nine" nearly every other source gives); her brother Emmet's angry confrontation in the yard and the thirty-six-year friendship it ended; and Ansel's stoic reaction to his 30 April 1925 dismissal — walking up the road at his ordinary hour, hanging his apron behind the shed door, and asking what there was for supper. A leaf found loose in the back of her third notebook, written the morning he died in February 1934, records that not one mourner at his funeral spoke the old accusation aloud — though none retracted it either.

**Quantities:** circular delivered 1919 to 9 or 11 families (sources conflict); dismissal 30 April 1925; Ansel Keddie's death, February 1934.
**Documents:** r01 (Toland), r21 (Oram), r24 (Orra Keddie's diary).

### 7. The Wet Hay Theory

Nyle Grigg (a Larrow Green farmer with no stake in the outcome, who chaired a 1908 investigating committee), Dr. Roland Kepp (a veterinarian who elaborated the theory scientifically in 1926), and Rosalie Cudd (a cheesemaker across Redlow Ridge who unknowingly refuted it) trace this theory's rise and fall.

In 1908 Grigg's committee sampled the Hessel Bottom herds through January, February, and March only, and concluded — finding "no dishonesty of any kind" — that the bottom's wet, sour hay was thinning the cream and "the remedy lies with the land." When the complaint resurfaced in 1919, Grigg reprinted the report with a covering letter that misremembers his own committee's work as having sampled "the whole of 1908... every month of that year," and cites the State Agricultural College's Bulletin No. 214 as finding the hay depressed the test by "two tenths" of a point — both claims contradicted by the very documents Grigg is introducing (the reprinted report says three winter months only; the bulletin's real finding, quoted directly elsewhere, was "not more than five hundredths" in winter and nothing measurable in summer). Dr. Kepp's 1926 paper independently repeats the identical "two tenths" misquotation and dismisses the 1911 measuring-glass anomaly as too short an interval — six weeks, by his count — to mean anything.

The theory was never tested against an outside standard until Rosalie Cudd, replying to a 1958 letter from Ivy Keddie, produced her late father's untouched thirty-year test record for the same Hessel Bottom herds' summer milk, delivered instead to the Alder Corners cheese factory: a steady 3.78–3.82 across sixteen seasons, wet years and dry alike — flatly incompatible with the idea that the ground itself could be depressing the fat.

**Quantities:** 1908 sampling window, 3 months only; Bulletin 214's actual finding, ≤0.05 point in winter, none in summer; Alder Corners average ≈3.80 across 16 seasons, 1915–1930.
**Documents:** r15 (Grigg), r16 (Kepp), r10 (Cudd).

### 8. The Glass — Made, Condemned, Kept

Junia Ammon (historian of the Tarnet supply house), Merle Strawn (the state dairy inspector), and Halbert Nym (the 1972 curator) trace the physical object at the center of the whole affair.

Ammon & Sons sold the Association a complete testing outfit for the new Ostrey Hollow station on 14 April 1897 — six dozen milk-test pipettes blown to deliver 17.6 cc, bottles, an acid measure, and a hand tester, totaling $77.93 — put into use 1 May 1897. Ammon's own 1949 company history cannot say from surviving records exactly what went wrong with that lot (the works books burned in 1931), but offers a plausible, non-fraudulent explanation: a reground mold producing glass that was consistently short and therefore invisible to ordinary spot-checking, since a check against a self-consistent lot simply confirms the lot's own (wrong) standard. Strawn, the circuit's inspector from 1907, visited the station roughly annually, always found the scales true, and never once found added water — including on a visit made specifically in response to the 1919 circular — but had no outside calibration standard for pipettes until the state began issuing burettes to field men in 1921. On 12 June 1923 he finally ran the station's glass against his burette, found it would not deliver its mark, and condemned the whole crate on the spot; new glass was in use within the month.

The condemned crate then sat, forgotten, in the station's own loft for thirty-five years until Ivy Keddie found it in 1958. Curator Halbert Nym's 1972 accession notes catalogue the crate, the invoice, the inspector's notebook, and related papers, and — without seeming to notice it — appear to swap the custodial attributions of two items: the station test book (clearly Keddie's own, by its contents) is labeled "kept by E. Brant," while the household farm weigh book (clearly the Brants', by its contents and provenance) is labeled "kept by A. Keddie."

**Quantities:** invoice 14 April 1897, $77.93, six dozen pipettes; condemned 12 June 1923; new glass in use by July 1923.
**Documents:** r06 (Strawn), r07 (Nym), r20 (Ammon).

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**
Granddaughter. Derivation: Ivy is the daughter of Duncan Keddie (r04, r18), and Duncan is explicitly Ansel's son (r03's byline, r18: "My father was Ansel Keddie... I am Duncan... their son"; and r18 directly: "My daughter is Ivy... which makes her my father's granddaughter"). Two sources point elsewhere and are wrong: the *Ashlin Chronicle* (r19) and county agent Falke's report (r22) both initially call her Ansel's "great-niece." Falke's own report catches and corrects this in its closing note ("she is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine"), which is decisive: a document that corrects itself against direct family testimony outranks the uncorrected error in the newspaper. Granddaughter is the resolved relationship.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**
Second cousins. Ivy's father, Duncan Keddie, and Hazel's father, Roy Brant, are first cousins — children of siblings Orra Keddie and Emmet Brant (stated by Duncan, r18; by Roy, r09; and by Hazel herself, r17). Children of first cousins are second cousins, which all three family-insider sources state identically, and which Duncan explicitly defends against a specific rival claim: "Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation." Two outside oral sources — Vira Toland (r01) and Cleve Oram (r21) — instead call the pair "first cousin once removed," which is simply wrong by the family's own consistent, converging account. Left open: the sources do not agree on whether Orra and Emmet (the linking generation) were full or half siblings (see B4-adjacent discussion in Section E) — this doesn't change the cousin arithmetic, but it is a genuine unresolved detail about how that generation's link was formed.

**B3. Selby Vose's father, and why it matters.**
Gideon Vose — the Association's founder, first president (1889–1911), and author of its by-laws, including Article VII. This is confirmed by three independent, well-placed sources: secretary Effie Loomis (r02, "son of the late president"), Duncan Keddie (r18, explicitly correcting "as I have heard it given, his nephew"), and clerk Bertram Kyle (r23, calling the nephew story "nonsense"). Two oral/folk sources — Vira Toland (r01) and Dell Rundle's pamphlet (r12) — say "nephew" instead; both are outvoted by more authoritative, direct testimony. It matters because Selby Vose, as manager from 1913, administered and refused to revisit the very rule his own father had written — that a station's own test is final and unappealable — and it was Selby who signed the 1919 accusatory circular and who saw and signed the incriminating "station gain" ledger line every year, including after bookkeeper Teague told him about it directly in 1916. The father-son relationship sharpens the sense that responsibility for how rigidly the rule was enforced sat inside one family across two generations.

**B4. Any relationship between the Vose and Keddie families.**
None established. Vira Toland (r01) asserts as common valley knowledge that Orra Keddie and "old Mrs. Vose" were first cousins; Cleve Oram (r21) repeats a vaguer version ("cousins of some sort... I could not draw you the line on paper"). Both are secondhand oral claims. Duncan Keddie — Orra's own son, and therefore the best-placed witness to his own mother's family connections — flatly denies it: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses... my mother would have told me... and she never once put a Vose in it." Given that Toland's account also independently gets the separate Selby Vose "nephew" question wrong, I treat this as a pattern of unreliable valley gossip about the Vose family specifically, and resolve that no Vose-Keddie relationship is established by the sources.

## Section C — Math (show working)

**C1.**
(a) The pipettes delivered 16.72 cc against a stem blown for 17.6 cc (Ivy Keddie's measurement, corroborated by the curator's transcription and by the bulletin's own final conclusion). 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 cc short; 0.88 ÷ 17.6 = 0.05. The glass delivered 95% of its marked volume — short by 1 part in 20 (5%). Because a butterfat test reads the milk charge directly as a percentage against a bottle neck ruled for the *full* charge, every reading came out at 95% of true — proportionally low by that same 5%, not by a fixed number of points. Cross-check: recorded average ÷ true average = 3.61 ÷ 3.80 = 0.95 — the same ratio, independently. (One source, Bulletin 471's own body text, gives a conflicting 16.27 cc / 0.9244 — this is an internal error in that document, addressed in Section E, and rejected in favor of the figure that is cross-confirmed by the invoice, the curator's transcription, and the bulletin's own conclusion.)

(b) True average = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. Shortfall = 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 points**.

(c) Over the 25 full delivery years (1898–1922), the station received 40,000,000 lb of milk.
Credited fat = 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb.
True fat = 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb.
Difference = **76,000 lb of butterfat** not credited.

(d) Value: 76,000 lb × $0.25/lb (average price over the period) = **$19,000**.
Patrons actually received: $3,800 (the arbitration award).
$3,800 ÷ $19,000 = **exactly one-fifth (20%)** of what was owed.

**C2.** Largest patron (Emmet Brant), year 1910: 200,000 lb of milk delivered, recorded test 3.42%, butterfat price that year $0.24/lb.
(a) Credited fat = 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb**.
(b) True test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60%. True fat = 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb. Owed and not credited = 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb**.
(c) Value = 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40**.

**C3.** Over the same 25 full delivery years, 40,000,000 lb of milk:
(a) Credited to patrons on the station's test: **1,444,000 lb**.
(b) Actually received at Larrow Green in the station's cream: **1,520,000 lb**.
(c) Difference = 76,000 lb. As a percentage, this depends on the denominator: **5.0% exactly of the fat actually delivered** (76,000 ÷ 1,520,000), or **≈5.25% of the fat already credited** (76,000 ÷ 1,444,000) — this second figure is the ledger's own recurring phrase, "five and a quarter per cent, as usual." Both are correct statements about the identical 76,000 lb; they differ only in what they are a percentage *of*, and the sources explicitly warn against treating them as interchangeable.

**C4.**
(a) Invoice total: **$77.93** (14 April 1897, Ammon & Sons). Line items sum correctly: 25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93, confirming this figure arithmetically. Two narrators (the *Ashlin Chronicle*, and — oddly — Ammon's own company historian in prose, though her own quoted invoice says 77.93) give "$77.39" instead; rejected as a digit-transposition error (see Section E).
(b) **Six dozen (72) pipettes**. Each was blown to deliver 17.6 cc but delivered a measured mean of 16.72 cc — short by **0.88 cc** per pipette.

**C5.**
(a) The faulty glass was physically in use from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 — **just over 26 years** (26 years and about six weeks). The money/butterfat calculation, however, runs over only **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922**. These differ because 1897 and 1923 were both partial years at the station (glass installed mid-1897, condemned mid-1923), and the loss calculation is built only from clean, complete annual totals — the two partial years at either end are excluded from that specific sum, though the glass was defective throughout the whole 26-year span.
(b) **Cannot be determined from the sources.** The curator explicitly states the station's first (partial) season's intake "is not in the ledgers and is nowhere else." Two informal, non-documentary personal recollections disagree: buttermaker Hobart Sill recalled "about nine hundred thousand pounds"; clerk Bertram Kyle recalled "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds." Neither is sourced to any record, and nothing in the sources lets us adjudicate between them.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The station's own pipette/measure broke on 8 May 1911; a borrowed measure was used until the station's own was restored on 10 July 1911 — **nine weeks**. (Two sources, Teague and Kepp, say "six weeks" instead; rejected in favor of the precise, dated, contemporaneous entries in Keddie's own book, corroborated by Ivy Keddie, Vaught, Roy Brant, and Orra Keddie's diary, all of which independently anchor the break to 8 May.) The variable that changed was the measuring instrument. Everything else was held constant: the same patrons, herds, pasture, season, acid, bottles, and tester, working the same routine. The result: the station's average rose from 3.61 to 3.80 for the full nine weeks and fell back to 3.61 the day the station's own measure returned. This is a natural experiment, and it establishes something a calculation from the 1958 measurement alone cannot: that the deficiency tracked the *instrument*, not the herds, the land, the season, or the tester's conduct — because none of those changed, and the figures did. It rules out, by direct demonstration rather than inference, every candidate cause except the glass.

What it does **not** establish: it doesn't by itself prove *why* the station's regular glass was short (manufacturing defect vs. any other cause), it doesn't independently confirm the borrowed measure's own accuracy (we only know it read closer to true), and it doesn't fix the exact size of the underlying shortfall — that required the actual 1958 physical measurement of the condemned pipettes against a calibrated burette. The two lines of evidence are complementary, not redundant.

**D2.** Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream:
1. **The ledger's own arithmetic.** Every year, the fat that actually arrived at Larrow Green in the station's cream *exceeded* what the station had credited to patrons (Teague's "station gain" line, ~5.25% every year). Theft at the station would reduce what arrived downstream, not increase it — the pattern is the opposite of what the accusation requires.
2. **Independent confirmation at the receiving end.** Buttermaker Hobart Sill, who personally reweighed incoming cream at Larrow Green for decades, confirms the same "always more, never less" pattern from his own scale, entirely independent of Teague's books.
3. **The 1911 natural experiment.** When a different measuring instrument was substituted for nine weeks — with no change whatsoever in the tester's supervision or opportunity — the readings rose. If theft explained the shortfall, changing the glass should not have changed the outcome.
4. (A fourth, for completeness) The state inspector's decades of unannounced visits, testing for added water with a lactometer and watching Keddie's technique directly, never found anything amiss.

These are independent because they come from three different people, using three different methods, at three different points in time (Teague's books, Sill's own scale, and the 1911 accident), none of which relies on any of the others. Reasons 1, 2, and 3 would **all still hold even if the glassware had never been measured in 1958** — none of them depends on Ivy Keddie's later physical test; they were sitting in the record decades before she opened the crate.

Did the bookkeeper (Teague) ever question the ledger entries that refute the accusation? Only once, and only privately: he says he raised the discrepancy with manager Selby Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone — and he did, for the rest of his career. He did not raise it publicly, including at the 1925 hearing, where his own testimony was instead used (by implication, per Frick) to suggest the tester might have been skimming — meaning Teague held, in his own books, the very evidence that undercut the theory his testimony helped support, and never connected the two on the record.

**D3.** Yes — the sources establish the manager knew, not merely that he should have known. Teague states directly that he "put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone" — an explicit conversation, not an inference. Separately, Teague states Vose "signed the sheet every year... a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him," and clerk Bertram Kyle corroborates that Vose personally reviewed and signed each annual statement, even if (per Kyle) he habitually read only the bottom total and not the lines above it. Taken together — an explicit warning given and dismissed in 1916, plus an annual signature on the document carrying the anomaly for the next seven years — the sources support that Vose had direct knowledge of the low tests well before the 1923 condemnation, and chose not to investigate.

## Section E — Contradictions

**Method note.** Where a quoted primary document (an invoice, a diary entry, a ledger line, a contemporaneous field notebook) conflicts with a later recollection, I generally favor the document. Where independent arithmetic checks agree with one figure and not another, I favor the figure the arithmetic confirms. Where family members disagree about their own family, I favor the account given by the person with the most direct, least-removed knowledge, and note when it's genuinely a toss-up. Two narrators repeating one figure sourced from the same origin count as one source, not two, per the instructions — I've tried to flag where that applies.

**A. Two narrators independently carrying the same wrong value:**
1. "Eleven" pipettes survived (correct: nine) — Rundle's 1959 pamphlet footnote (r12) and the *Ashlin Chronicle* (r19); likely the same origin (press coverage of the same college release), so weak as independent corroboration despite two names.
2. Ivy Keddie as Ansel's "great-niece" (correct: granddaughter) — the *Chronicle* (r19) and Falke's report (r22, page two) — resolved by Falke's own appended self-correction plus direct family testimony (r04, r18).
3. Glass condemned "the twelfth of July, 1923" (correct: 12 June 1923, per the inspector's own notebook) — Vose's address (r08) and Rundle's pamphlet (r12); resolved in favor of Strawn's contemporaneous field entry, quoted directly in r06 and r07.
4. "Five dozen" pipettes purchased (correct: six dozen, per the invoice) — Vaught's Bulletin 471 body text (r11) and Cleve Oram's recollection (r21).
5. Selby Vose as Gideon's "nephew" (correct: son) — Vira Toland (r01) and Rundle's pamphlet (r12); resolved by Loomis, Duncan Keddie, and Kyle (three independent, better-placed sources) all saying "son."
6. Orra Keddie and "Mrs. Vose" as cousins (unsubstantiated) — Toland (r01) and Oram (r21); explicitly denied by Duncan Keddie (r18), the only source with direct family knowledge.
7. Ivy Keddie as Hazel Brant's "first cousin once removed" (correct: second cousins) — Toland (r01) and Oram (r21); contradicted by three family-insider sources (r09, r17, r18).
8. Bulletin 214 misquoted as finding the test depressed by "two tenths" (correct: "not more than five hundredths," winter only) — Grigg's 1919 letter (r15) and Dr. Kepp's paper (r16).
9. Own testing glass entering use "in 1898" (correct: 1 May 1897) — Vaught's Bulletin 471 body text (r11) and Bertram Kyle's memoir (r23).
10. The 1911 interval given as "six weeks" (correct: nine weeks, 8 May–10 July) — Teague (r05) and Kepp (r16).
11. Mean pipette delivery given as "16.27 cc" with ratio 0.9244 (correct: 16.72 cc, ratio 0.95) — Vaught's own bulletin body (r11, contradicting its own conclusion) and the *Chronicle* (r19), apparently drawn from that same flawed passage.
12. Award given as "thirty-five hundred dollars" (correct: $3,800, per the minute book) — Vira Toland (r01) and Cleve Oram (r21) — both oral/folk memory against the quoted award text.

**B. A narrator contradicting itself on the page:**
1. Vaught's Bulletin 471 (r11): its own method section states five dozen pipettes, 16.27 cc, ratio 0.9244, true average 3.905, shortfall 0.295, and "118,000 lb" of fat — while its own concluding paragraph (quoted identically elsewhere) gives 76,000 lb and dates the glass "from the first of May, 1897." 2. Junia Ammon (r20): quotes the invoice verbatim as "Total — 77.93," then restates it in her own prose as "seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents."
3. Dell Rundle's pamphlet (r12): the main text asserts cream was stolen "by the back door," then a "Note Added While in Press" retracts it after reading the ledger: "I must go back on Chapter IV... I cannot make my paragraph stand."
4. Orin Falke's report (r22): calls Ivy Keddie "great-niece" on page two, then appends a note admitting "the error on page two is mine."
5. Nyle Grigg's letter (r15): claims his committee sampled "the whole of 1908... every month," directly above the reprinted 1908 report itself, which states the sampling covered only "January, February and March."
6. Halbert Nym's curator notes (r07): Item 3 (by its contents, clearly Keddie's own test book) is labeled "kept by E. Brant," while Item 4 (by its contents and provenance, clearly the Brant household's farm weigh book) is labeled "kept by A. Keddie" — the attributions appear swapped.

**C. Other unresolved contradictions, not cleanly fitting either category above:**
- Recorded station average given as 3.61 (Keddie's own book, curator, Ivy, Falke) vs. 3.16 (Teague, Frick) — possibly a digit transposition; resolved toward 3.61, a direct quotation from Keddie's own contemporaneous ledger.

Flagged as unreconciled rather than confidently resolved either way.

- Source of the 1911 borrowed measure: "from Larrow Green" (implied by Ivy/Vaught) vs. "the state inspector's own" (Roy Brant) — Strawn's own 1911 log entry mentions nothing about lending equipment; **cannot be determined from the sources**.
- Orra Keddie's birth year: 1866 (diary editor's preface, r24) vs. 1868 (Duncan, r18) — minor and unresolved.
- Emmet and Orra as full siblings (Roy Brant, explicit; Duncan; Orra's own diary, "my brother") vs. half-siblings "by a stepmother" (Hazel Brant) — resolved toward full siblings on the strength of three sources, including Orra's own son and Orra's own diary, against one more-removed source who is directly contradicted by her own father.
- Brant weigh book start date: 1 January 1897 (curator, Frick's exhibit list, Duncan) vs. 1898 (Roy Brant himself) vs. 1900 (Hazel Brant) — resolved toward 1897 by documentary/majority weight; notably, both direct descendants misremember it differently.

- Post-1923 average test for 1924: 3.80 (Strawn's field log) vs. 3.90 (Vose) vs. 3.85 (Falke, and separately Sill's independent estimate) — not fully resolvable to one figure; Strawn's is treated as most reliable, being contemporaneous and professionally disinterested, with Vose's figure suspect given the arithmetic inconsistency noted above.

## Section F — Theory

**The causal theory.** The Ostrey Hollow station's testing pipettes, bought new from Ammon & Sons in 1897, were manufactured short — delivering about 16.72 cc of milk into the test bottle when blown and marked for 17.6 cc, roughly 95% of the intended charge, most plausibly (per the supplier's own account) because a reground mold produced a lot that was consistently wrong and therefore self-consistent, invisible to any ordinary spot-check. Because a butterfat test reads the resulting column of fat directly as a percentage against a bottle neck ruled for the *full* 17.6 cc charge, every test made with that glass came out at a fixed 95% of the truth — a *proportional*, not additive, error, which is why it hit richer milk hardest and why the ratio between any two herds' tests still looked perfectly orderly. **Size:** fixed by the specific manufacturing shortfall of that one lot of glass (16.72 vs. 17.6 cc) — a physical fact about the defect, not about cows, land, or people. **Why it began when it did:** it began the moment that specific lot went into service, 1 May 1897 (before which the station used properly calibrated borrowed glass from Larrow Green and tested correctly). **Why it ended when it did:** it ended the moment an outside calibration standard — a burette, which only reached the state's field inspectors starting in 1921 — was finally applied to that glass, on 12 June 1923, leading to condemnation and replacement within the month. **Why nothing at the station or on the farms had to change:** because the defect lived entirely in an instrument that is, in ordinary dairy practice, itself the standard — nothing else in the plant (scales, acid, technique) could reveal it, and nothing about the herds needed to change for the readings to jump the moment the instrument did.

**The three competing explanations, and what defeats each:**
1. **Watering**, advanced by manager Selby Vose (1919 circular, 1925 hearing, 1926 address). Defeated by the state inspector's own decades of lactometer tests — including one made specifically in response to the 1919 circular — which never once found added water; and by the fact that the gap was flat year-round rather than concentrated in dry spells when watering would be most tempting.
2. **Cream theft by the tester**, advanced by Association officers at the 1925 hearing (implied in bookkeeper Teague's testimony) and taken as valley "settled opinion." Defeated by the ledger's own "station gain" line (cream arrived *heavier* in fat than credited — the opposite of theft), independently confirmed by buttermaker Sill's own reweighing, and by the 1911 natural experiment, where changing only the measuring instrument changed the result with no change in the tester's opportunity.
3. **Wet Hessel Bottom hay**, advanced by the 1908 Grigg committee and elaborated by Dr. Kepp (1926). Defeated by Bulletin 214's actual feeding-trial result (at most a 0.05-point depression, winter only, versus the observed flat 0.19-point year-round gap), by Alder Corners' independent cross-ridge test data showing the same herds' milk testing normally, and again by the 1911 natural experiment, where the same herds and hay produced normal readings for nine weeks under a different instrument.

**Subsidiary questions.** Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware? **No.** Loomis states plainly that the crate "was never mentioned in the hearing at all," and Frick's own exhibit list names only four items — the test book, the receiving book, Brant's weigh book, and the inspector's 1923 notebook — never the physical pipettes themselves, even though that notebook (which recorded the condemnation) was in the room. What did the tester himself believe the cause was? The sources give two different private answers that are not clearly reconciled: per Frick, Keddie told him privately in the hearing corridor that he "had always thought the patrons were at fault" (i.e., suspected the patrons of watering, though he would not say so publicly); per Duncan Keddie's family account, Keddie came home in 1911 already suspecting "something in the glass," but would not commit an unproven suspicion to writing, entering only a question mark in his book. Both may be true at different moments, or his suspicion may genuinely have been divided — the sources do not let this be resolved to a single, confident answer.

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

For 26 years, patrons at a remote skimming station in Ostrey Hollow were paid less for their milk than everyone else in the valley, and almost everyone blamed a person: the patrons for watering their milk, the tester for stealing cream, or the wet meadow for thinning it. Each theory did real damage — a family estrangement, an accusatory circular, a costly 1925 hearing, and a tester dismissed under a suspicion he carried to his grave. The true cause was mechanical: the station's 1897 glassware delivered 5% less milk than its markings claimed, so every test read proportionally low, richest milk worst of all — invisible from inside the plant because the instrument was itself the standard nothing else could check it against. Only in 1958, when the tester's granddaughter found the condemned glass in a loft and measured it against a calibrated burette, was he cleared. It took eight overlapping records because no single witness ever held both the ledger and the crate.
