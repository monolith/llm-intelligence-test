# Batch 1 Answers — Larrow Valley / Ostrey Hollow (attempt 5)

Method note: quoted primary document over paraphrase or admitted "impression"; contemporaneous
diary or letter over later recollection; named, dated self-correction over the error it corrects;
convergence of *independent* accounts over a lone outlier — repeats traced to one origin count as
one source, never several; arithmetic used to resolve a conflict where the instructions allow it.
Two corrections from earlier passes at this material are worth flagging up front, since they change
answers below: (1) the "1922" date belongs to the dispute over when the glassware was later
*condemned*, not to when it first went into service — it does not belong in Story 2's list of
competing dates, and I've kept it out. (2) For C1(a) and C1(d) I've gone back to the raw
best-attested figures (test averages, milk total, price, award) and carried the arithmetic straight
through, rather than substituting a close but separately-sourced round figure partway through the
chain — this changes both answers, and I explain why in each place.

## Section A — Reconstruction (first three of eight stories, in chronological order)

### 1. The Founding of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery (1889–1891)

In 1889, farmers across Larrow Valley, tired of hauling their butter all the way to Ashlin, met at
the Larrow Green schoolhouse and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. They
elected Gideon Vose as first president — "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the
law and thought better of it" — and he wrote the Association's by-laws himself, at his own kitchen
table. This comes from Dell Rundle's jubilee pamphlet, "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years"
(1939, reprinted 1959), the main narrative source for the founding.

In 1891 the Association built its creamery at Larrow Green, on the creek below the mill, set up its
churn, and began paying the valley in cash every month for the first time. That same year, per
Rundle — who quotes the text directly — the Association adopted Article VII of its by-laws: "Every
patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat
contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of
the station where he delivers shall govern." Rundle calls this "the sentence on which the whole
trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned" — it made one station's own test equipment, with
no cross-check against any other, the entire basis of every patron's pay.

A single later, weaker source — an 1919 letter from committeeman Nyle Grigg — gives the adoption
year as 1893 instead, but only "as I have always understood it," an admitted secondhand impression,
not a document. I favor Rundle's 1891, from the quoted text, over Grigg's impression, though not by
a wide margin. A 1959 county report by Orin Falke adds an independent reason this may never be fully
settled either way: the Association's early minute book was lost in an office move, and the by-laws
as preserved carry no date of adoption on their face.

Documents: Rundle's 1939/1959 pamphlet (founding narrative, quotes Article VII in full); Grigg's
1919 letter (competing 1893 date); Falke's 1959 report (confirms the minute book's loss).

### 2. Ostrey Hollow Opens, and the Purchase Behind Twenty-Six Years of Trouble (1896–1897)

The Association's second station, built over the winter of 1895–96 seven miles up the valley from
Larrow Green — a distance three independent sources (Frick, Sill, and Cleve Oram) all confirm —
opened on 4 May 1896. That date, the most precise given, comes from Orin Falke's 1959 county report
and is independently matched by buttermaker Hobart Sill's 1934 retirement remarks ("May 1896"). The
station served the Hessel Bottom farms at the head of the valley: milk was weighed, tested, and
separated on the spot; cream was hauled down to the Green; skim went home the same morning. Ansel
Keddie was appointed tester and separator man from the day it opened, a post he held without a break
for twenty-nine seasons, until 1925.

The station opened with no testing glassware of its own. Sill lent it his own outfit from Larrow
Green for its first, partial season (May 1896 to year's end), which took in somewhere between about
900,000 pounds (Sill's figure) and 1,100,000 pounds (clerk Bertram Kyle's later oral-tradition
estimate) of milk — neither figure documentary.

The station's own equipment arrived the following spring. An invoice from the supplying firm, Ammon
& Sons, dated 14 April 1897, itemizes six dozen milk-test pipettes (17.6 c.c. each, $25.20), twelve
dozen 8%-neck test bottles ($31.68), one acid measure ($0.90), one hand tester ($18.00), and freight
($2.15); the line items sum to $77.93, though the firm's own 1949 company history states the price as
$77.39 — an unexplained $0.54 discrepancy inside that single source. That history records the outfit
going into use "on the first of May, 1897," the best-supported of three competing claims for this
date across the retellings (1897, 1898, 1899), corroborated independently by Sill, by Ammon & Sons'
own invoice and prose, by the Ashlin Chronicle, and — most decisively — by tester's wife Orra
Keddie's contemporaneous diary, which records the same date. (Kyle's 1953 account gives an outlier
1898 date, from correspondence he says he handled decades later; Hazel Brant's 1981 tape gives a
further outlier, "ninety-nine," which she herself disclaims as unreliable family lore.)

This ordinary reorder of pipettes and bottles is where the defect that would go undetected for
twenty-six years began.

Documents: Falke's 1959 report; Sill's 1934 remarks; Ammon & Sons' 1949 company history and invoice;
the Ashlin Chronicle; Kyle's 1953 account; Hazel Brant's 1981 tape; Orra Keddie's diary.

### 3. The Hollow Complains, and the Committee Blames the Hay (1902–1908)

From 1902 — confirmed independently by four sources, including Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary
entry "March 1902" — Hessel Bottom farmers delivering to Ostrey Hollow began complaining that their
milk tested lower there than the same herds' milk tested at Larrow Green. Management did not believe
them.

One of these patrons, Emmet Brant, began keeping a private weigh-book of his own deliveries. His
granddaughter Hazel Brant (1981) says he started it 1 January 1900; his nephew Duncan Keddie (1968)
says 1 January 1897. That three-year gap is never resolved, and neither witness is obviously more
reliable than the other.

In January 1908 the Association appointed a committee, chaired by Nyle Grigg, to investigate. Grigg's
own report — preserved in full by quotation in his 1919 covering letter — describes the committee
visiting each of Hessel Bottom's six farms three or four times over the winter, drawing and sealing
its own milk samples rather than relying on the station's, watching the milking, hauling, and testing
firsthand, and checking both the station's and the patrons' scales against a known weight. It found
nothing to fault in the testing itself. Instead it pointed to the hay: Hessel Bottom's meadows lie
along the creek below the station, flooded in part every spring, thick with sedge and coarse sour
grass, cut late and fed heavily all winter — leaving the herds thin-flanked at winter's end and slow
to recover in spring. Conclusion: "a cow cannot put into her pail what her keep has never put into
her." Explicit finding: "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter." The Association accepted the
report, and the question was considered closed.

It stayed closed for eleven years, until 1919 — a later story. This good-faith verdict would
eventually be shown mistaken by direct measurement, but not until 1959.

Documents: Grigg's 1908 report, preserved by quotation in his own 1919 letter; Hazel Brant's 1981
tape; Duncan Keddie's 1968 letter; Orra Keddie's diary, which independently confirms both the 1902
complaint date and the January 1908 visit to her own family, the Brants.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1.** Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie: **granddaughter.** Orin Falke's 1959 county
report first calls Ivy Keddie — the graduate student who, with Professor Leland Vaught, co-authored
the 1958 college bulletin — the tester's "great-niece," then appends a note after the report had
gone to type: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the
great-niece; the error on page two is mine." A named author explicitly correcting and verifying his
own error, in the same document, dated 1959, is about as solid as this material gets, and nothing
else points elsewhere.

**B2.** Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant: **most likely second cousins — but the one
identification the whole derivation leans on is never stated outright, so I hold this at "probable,"
not "proven."** What's stated directly: Hazel Brant (1981) is Emmet Brant's granddaughter through his
son Roy Brant. Duncan Keddie (1968) is Emmet Brant's sister's son and named as Roy Brant's first
cousin — meaning some Brant woman, Emmet's sister, married a Keddie man and had Duncan. Separately,
the account of the 1908 committee's rounds notes that among the Hessel Bottom farms visited was
"Orra Keddie's own family, the Brants" — meaning Ansel Keddie's wife Orra was herself born a Brant
from that same neighborhood. Those three facts invite one obvious identification: that Orra is the
very sister who married a Keddie, making Duncan a son of Ansel and Orra. If that holds, every child
of Ansel and Orra — including whichever one is Ivy's parent — is a first cousin of Roy Brant, which
makes every grandchild of Ansel and Orra a second cousin of every grandchild of Roy Brant. Since Ivy
is Ansel's granddaughter (B1) and Hazel is Roy's daughter, they come out as second cousins regardless
of which specific child of Ansel and Orra is Ivy's parent — that detail is exactly the kind of
intervening-generation fact the sources leave open, and it turns out not to matter to the degree of
kinship. What does matter, and isn't stated anywhere I've read, is the founding identification itself
— that Orra is specifically Emmet's sister, rather than some other Brant from a neighborhood that
held "six farms' worth" of them. No source says this in so many words.

**B3.** Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**, the Association's founding president — not, as one
source has it, his uncle. Three independent, named sources say this directly and explicitly reject
the "nephew" story as a known circulating error: Selby Vose's own words ("My father, who was your
first president..."), Duncan Keddie's explicit 1968 correction, and clerk Bertram Kyle's 1953 account
("the son, whatever is said at Ashlin... I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it
is nonsense"). Only Dell Rundle's secondhand pamphlet says "nephew." This matters to Ostrey Hollow
because Selby Vose became manager in 1911 and held the post about twenty-five years — spanning the
Hollow's ongoing complaints, the 1919 circular accusing patrons of watering their milk (which he
alone signed), and the 1925 arbitration that ended in Ansel Keddie's dismissal. Being the founder's
actual son, not a more distant relation, fits Kyle's picture of an office where Vose's authority went
essentially unquestioned — Kyle says Vose "read the totals and never read the lines above them"
before signing — the kind of inherited, comfortable authority under which a real and growing
shortfall could go unexamined by management for decades.

**B4.** **None that the sources establish by blood or marriage — only an institutional one.** Gideon
Vose founded the Association and wrote the by-law making a station's own test the basis of every
patron's pay; his son Selby Vose managed the Association from 1911 and was Ansel Keddie's ultimate
superior, signing the 1919 circular that accused Keddie's own Hessel Bottom patrons of watering their
milk, and presiding as manager through the 1925 arbitration that ended in Keddie's dismissal. That is
an employer-and-employee relationship, not a family one. Whether any personal or marriage tie existed
between the two families beyond that cannot be determined from the sources — none is claimed
anywhere, but silence isn't proof of absence.

## Section C — Math (C1 and C2)

**C1(a).** **About 5%, exactly 5.00% by the cleanest derivation available.** The 1958 measurement of
the nine surviving pipettes found they "delivered materially less than the capacity blown upon their
stems, and in the same proportion, one to another" — a constant, not random, under-fill across the
whole set. In the test these pipettes were built for, a fixed volume (17.6 c.c., per the 1897
invoice) is charged into a bottle whose neck is scaled directly in percent butterfat; a pipette that
always delivers less than 17.6 c.c. by a constant fraction produces a fat reading low by that exact
same fraction every time, regardless of the true test that year. The sources don't give a raw
calibration number for the pipette itself, so the proportion has to be read back out of its effect on
the test record: recorded average 3.61, true average 3.80 (see (b)), deficiency 0.19 point. Dividing
the shortfall by the truth — the natural way to express "how far off was the recorded figure" — gives
0.19 ÷ 3.80 = 0.05, exactly 5%. That's close to, but not identical with, an entirely independent
figure: the bookkeeper's own recurring annual entry comparing butterfat credited to patrons against
butterfat actually received in cream at Larrow Green, "five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried
to the general account" (clerk Bertram Kyle, quoting bookkeeper Wilbur Teague's standing note). I've
used the clean 5.00% from the test-average gap as the answer, since it's derived directly from the
glassware's documented effect on the test record; the bookkeeping 5.25% and the 5.26% you'd get from
dividing by the recorded figure instead (0.19 ÷ 3.61) both corroborate that the shortfall really was
"about 5%," but neither lands on exactly 5.00%, and nothing in the sources explains that residual
quarter-point.

**C1(b).** True average: **3.80**. Shortfall: **0.19 point** below true, constant in every month of
the year, for the whole affected period (recorded average 3.61 + deficiency 0.19 = 3.80) — stated
directly in the 1958 bulletin as reported by Falke, not something I computed. One minor wrinkle:
Larrow Green's own average over the same years is separately given as 3.78, not 3.80. I don't read
this as an inconsistency needing resolution — Larrow Green and Ostrey Hollow are different stations
with different herds, and 3.78 is offered only as "an ordinary figure for this county," not as a
claim that the two stations should match exactly.

**C1(c).** **76,000 pounds.** Working: 40,000,000 lbs of milk received at Ostrey Hollow over the
affected years is the best-attested total — clerk Bertram Kyle personally compiled this exact figure
for the 1925 arbitration and states it implies 1,600,000 lbs a year, "year in and year out," i.e., a
25-year window (40,000,000 ÷ 1,600,000 = 25). Applying the 0.19-point deficiency directly to the milk
volume: 40,000,000 × (0.19 ÷ 100) = **76,000 lbs** of butterfat that should have been credited but
wasn't. (The calendar span most often given for the defect, 1897–1923, is 26 years, not 25 — a
one-year tension the sources never reconcile, though it doesn't change this multiplication, which
uses Kyle's own stated rate and total.) This differs from the patrons' own 1925 arbitration estimate
of 67,000 lbs, their contemporaneous guess made three decades before anyone measured the glassware —
two figures, two methods, two dates, not meant to be the same.

**C1(d).** **Worth: $19,000. Patrons received 20% of it.** Working, continuing the same chain: 76,000
lbs (part c) × 25 cents a pound — Kyle's own figure for the average butterfat price "over that whole
stretch... taking the fat years with the lean" — comes to $19,000. The 1925 arbitration award was
$3,800 "without admission," confirmed by five independent sources (Frick, Duncan Keddie, the Ashlin
Chronicle, Kyle, and Falke). $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = exactly **20%**, one-fifth of what they were shorted.

I want to flag the choice behind this. There's a second, independently-attested total-loss figure,
$19,600, given both by the patrons' 1925 petition and by a separate document, "Bulletin 471," which
the sources say computed the same number independently. $19,600 is better-attested in the narrow
sense that two sources state it outright, against zero sources stating $19,000 — $19,000 only exists
as the product of my own multiplication. I've still gone with $19,000 here, for two reasons: the
question asks what the pounds computed in part (c) were worth, which is a chain calculation, not a
lookup; and carrying that chain through to the award gives an exact 20% received, a clean result that
$19,600 does not produce (3,800 ÷ 19,600 ≈ 19.4%). That cleanness is circumstantial, not proof — but
it's the kind of internal-consistency signal the instructions for this test explicitly allow using to
resolve a conflict, and I'm using it here. The likeliest explanation for the roughly $600 (3%) gap
either way: 25 cents was only Kyle's own rough multi-year average, not exact year-by-year pricing,
while the petition/Bulletin 471 figure may reflect actual yearly prices — or may simply restate the
same 1925-era claim rather than being a truly independent calculation.

**C2.** **Cannot be determined from the sources.** Nothing in the material identifies who Ostrey
Hollow's largest patron was, or gives any individual patron's credited-butterfat figure for 1910
specifically. All three sub-parts depend on that patron's identity and volume, and the sources give
only aggregate, station-wide figures — the 40,000,000 lb / 25-year total, the 3.61/3.80 recorded/true
test averages, and the roughly 25-cents-a-pound average price — plus one individual patron's private
record, Emmet Brant's weigh-book, entered as arbitration evidence but never quoted for any single
year, including 1910, and never compared against other patrons' volumes to establish who was largest.
A plausible-sounding number could be invented here, but the instructions score a confident guess
worse than declining, and nothing in what I've read supports one.
