# Batch 1 Answers — Larrow Valley / Ostrey Hollow (attempt 6)

Method: a quoted primary document outranks a paraphrase or an admitted "impression"; a
contemporaneous diary or letter outranks later recollection; a named, dated self-correction
outranks the error it corrects; convergence of several *independent* accounts outranks a lone
outlier (repeats traced to one origin count as one source, never several); and where a directly
quoted, corroborated document figure conflicts with arithmetic I've chained together myself from
other figures, I favor the document. Where a conclusion depends on connecting facts the sources
never state together, I call it an inference and say exactly what's missing, rather than presenting
it as settled.

## Section A — Reconstruction (first three of eight stories, in chronological order)

The material's events fall into eight fairly distinct episodes by date: founding; the Ostrey
Hollow station's opening and equipment purchase; the 1902–08 complaints and hay-committee verdict;
the 1911 borrowed-glass episode; the 1919 watering circular; the 1923 condemnation; the 1925
arbitration; and the 1958–59 discovery and report. That split matches the batch structure exactly
(3 here, 3 more in Batch 2, the last 2 in Batch 3), so I've taken the first three below as one
discrete episode each.

### 1. Founding of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery (1889–1891)

In 1889 farmers throughout Larrow Valley, tired of hauling their butter all the way to Ashlin, met
at the Larrow Green schoolhouse and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association.
They elected Gideon Vose as first president — described as "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had
once thought of the law and thought better of it" — who wrote the Association's by-laws himself, at
his own kitchen table. This account comes from Dell Rundle's jubilee pamphlet, "The Larrow Valley
Creamery: Fifty Years" (1939, reprinted 1959), the fullest narrative source for the founding.

In 1891 the Association built its creamery at Larrow Green, on the creek below the mill, set up its
churn, and began paying the valley in cash every month for the first time. That same year, Rundle
quotes the Association's Article VII directly: "Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers
according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not
according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."
Rundle calls this "the sentence on which the whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward
turned" — because it made each station's own test equipment, with no built-in cross-check, the sole
basis of every patron's pay.

One weaker, later source complicates the date: an 1919 letter from committeeman Nyle Grigg gives the
adoption year as 1893 instead, but only "as I have always understood it" — an admitted secondhand
impression, not a document. I favor Rundle's 1891, from the quoted text, over Grigg's impression,
though not by a wide margin. A 1959 county report by Orin Falke adds an independent reason this may
never be settled either way: the Association's early minute book was lost in an office move, and the
by-laws as preserved carry no date of adoption on their face.

Documents: Rundle's 1939/1959 pamphlet; Grigg's 1919 letter; Falke's 1959 report.

### 2. Ostrey Hollow Opens, and the Purchase Behind Twenty-Six Years of Trouble (1896–1897)

The Association's second station was built over the winter of 1895–96, seven miles up the valley
from Larrow Green — a distance three independent narrators confirm — and opened on 4 May 1896, the
most precise date given, from Orin Falke's 1959 county report, independently matched by buttermaker
Hobart Sill's 1934 retirement remarks ("May 1896"). It served the Hessel Bottom farms at the head of
the valley: milk was weighed, tested, and separated on the spot; cream was hauled down to Larrow
Green; skim went home the same morning. Ansel Keddie was appointed tester and separator man from the
day the station opened, a post he held without a break for twenty-nine seasons, until 1925.

The station opened with no testing glassware of its own. Sill lent it his own outfit from Larrow
Green for its first, partial season, which took in somewhere between about 900,000 pounds (Sill's
estimate) and 1,100,000 pounds (clerk Bertram Kyle's later oral-tradition figure) of milk — neither
figure documentary.

The station's own equipment arrived the following spring. An invoice from the supplying firm, Ammon
& Sons, dated 14 April 1897, itemizes six dozen milk-test pipettes (17.6 cc each), twelve dozen test
bottles, an acid measure, and a hand tester; its line items sum to $77.93, though the firm's own 1949
company history states the price as $77.39 — an unexplained $0.54 discrepancy inside that one
source. That history records the outfit going into use "on the first of May, 1897," the
best-supported of several competing dates across the retellings (1897, 1898, 1899), corroborated by
Sill, by Ammon & Sons' own invoice and prose, by the Ashlin Chronicle, and — most decisively — by
tester's wife Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary, which records the identical date.

This ordinary reorder of pipettes and bottles is where the defect that would go undetected for
twenty-six years began.

Documents: Falke's 1959 report; Sill's 1934 remarks; Ammon & Sons' 1949 company history and invoice;
the Ashlin Chronicle; Bertram Kyle's 1953 account; Orra Keddie's diary.

### 3. The Hollow Complains, and the Committee Blames the Hay (1902–1908)

From 1902 — a date confirmed independently by four sources, including tester's wife Orra Keddie's
contemporaneous diary entry "March 1902" — Hessel Bottom farmers delivering to Ostrey Hollow began
complaining that their milk tested lower there than the same herds' milk tested at Larrow Green.
Management did not believe them.

One of these patrons, Emmet Brant, began keeping a private weigh-book of his own deliveries. His
granddaughter Hazel Brant (1981) says he started it 1 January 1900; his nephew Duncan Keddie (1968)
says 1 January 1897. That three-year gap is never resolved by the sources, and neither witness is
obviously more reliable than the other.

In January 1908 the Association appointed a committee, chaired by Nyle Grigg, to investigate.
Grigg's own report — preserved by quotation in his 1919 covering letter — describes the committee
visiting each of Hessel Bottom's six farms three or four times over the winter, drawing and sealing
its own milk samples rather than relying on the station's, watching the milking, hauling, and
testing firsthand, and checking both the station's and the patrons' scales against a known weight.
It found nothing to fault in the testing itself. Instead it pointed to the hay: Hessel Bottom's
meadows lie along the creek below the station, flooded in part every spring, thick with sedge and
coarse sour grass, cut late and fed heavily all winter — leaving the herds thin-flanked at winter's
end and slow to recover in spring. Its conclusion: "a cow cannot put into her pail what her keep has
never put into her." It stated explicitly that it found "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter."
The Association accepted the report, and the matter was considered closed.

It stayed closed for eleven years, until 1919 — a later story. This good-faith verdict would
eventually be shown mistaken by direct measurement, but not until 1959.

Documents: Grigg's 1908 report, quoted in his own 1919 letter; Hazel Brant's 1981 tape; Duncan
Keddie's 1968 letter; Orra Keddie's diary.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1.** Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie: **granddaughter.** Orin Falke's 1959 county report
first calls Ivy Keddie — the graduate student who, with Professor Leland Vaught, co-authored the
1958 college bulletin — the tester's "great-niece," then appends a note after the report had gone to
type: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the
great-niece; the error on page two is mine." A named author explicitly correcting and verifying his
own error, in the same document, dated 1959, is as solid as this material gets, and nothing else
points elsewhere.

**B2.** Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant: **most likely second cousins, but the single fact
the whole derivation stands on is never actually stated — only invited.** What's stated directly:
Hazel Brant (1981) is Emmet Brant's granddaughter through his son Roy Brant. Duncan Keddie (1968) is
named as Emmet Brant's sister's son and, separately, as Roy Brant's first cousin — which is
consistent, since a man and his sister's son are related exactly at that remove. Separately again,
the account of the 1908 committee's rounds notes it visited, among the Hessel Bottom farms, "Orra
Keddie's own family, the Brants" — meaning Ansel Keddie's wife Orra was herself born a Brant from
that same neighborhood.

If Orra is specifically the sister who married a Keddie and had Duncan, then Duncan is Ansel and
Orra's son, and every child of Ansel and Orra — including whichever one is Ivy's parent — is a
sibling of Duncan's and therefore also a first cousin of Roy Brant. That makes every grandchild of
Ansel and Orra a second cousin of every grandchild of Roy Brant, and since Ivy is Ansel's
granddaughter (B1) and Hazel is Roy's daughter, they come out as second cousins — regardless of
which specific child of Ansel and Orra is Ivy's parent. That detail (which child, born when) is a
genuine intervening-generation gap the sources leave open, but it doesn't change the derived degree
of kinship if the identification holds.

What does matter, and is nowhere stated outright, is that founding identification itself: that Orra
is precisely Emmet's sister, and not some more distant Brant from a neighborhood the sources
describe as holding "six farms' worth" of them. Nothing in the material says this in so many words —
it is the most natural explanation for why the Keddie and Brant lines of Hessel Bottom keep turning
up entangled, but it is my inference from convergent detail, not a documented fact, and I want that
distinguished clearly from the (genuinely open, but immaterial) question of which child carries the
line from Ansel and Orra down to Ivy.

**B3.** Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**, the Association's founding president — not, as one
source has it, his uncle. Three independent, named sources state this directly and explicitly reject
the "nephew" story as a known circulating error: Selby Vose's own words ("My father, who was your
first president..."), Duncan Keddie's explicit 1968 correction, and clerk Bertram Kyle's 1953 account
("the son, whatever is said at Ashlin... I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it
is nonsense"). Only Dell Rundle's secondhand pamphlet says "nephew." This matters to Ostrey Hollow
because Selby Vose became the Association's manager in 1911 and held the post about twenty-five
years — spanning the Hollow's ongoing complaints, the 1919 circular accusing patrons of watering
their milk (which he alone signed), and the 1925 arbitration that ended in Ansel Keddie's dismissal.
Being the founder's actual son, not a more distant relation, fits Kyle's picture of an office where
Vose's authority went essentially unquestioned — Kyle says Vose "read the totals and never read the
lines above them" before signing — exactly the kind of inherited, comfortable authority under which a
real and growing shortfall could go unexamined by management for decades.

**B4.** **None that the sources establish by blood or marriage — only an institutional one.** Gideon
Vose founded the Association and wrote the by-law making a station's own test the basis of every
patron's pay; his son Selby Vose managed the Association from 1911 and was Ansel Keddie's ultimate
superior, signing the 1919 circular that accused Keddie's own Hessel Bottom patrons of watering their
milk, and presiding as manager through the 1925 arbitration that ended in Keddie's dismissal. That is
an employer-and-employee relationship, running through Gideon Vose's own Article VII, not a family
one. Whether any personal or marriage tie existed between the two families beyond that cannot be
determined from the sources — none is claimed anywhere, but silence isn't proof of absence.

## Section C — Math (C1 and C2)

**C1(a). About 5% — precisely 5.00%, derived from the test-average gap, which is the figure that
follows directly from the glassware's documented behavior.**

The 1958 measurement of the nine surviving pipettes found they "delivered materially less than the
capacity blown upon their stems, and in the same proportion, one to another" — a constant, not
random, shortfall across the whole set. In this test a fixed nominal volume of milk is drawn into a
bottle whose neck is graduated directly in percent butterfat, on the assumption that the full nominal
volume was actually used. If the pipette really delivers only a fixed fraction (1 − x) of that
volume, the milk sample tested is smaller by exactly that fraction, so the fat that separates out —
and with it the reading on the neck — comes out smaller by the same fraction: recorded reading = true
reading × (1 − x).

The sources give no raw calibration number for the pipette itself (no "delivered so many cc out of
17.6"), so x has to be read back out of its effect on the station's own test record: recorded average
3.61, true average 3.80 (part b). x = 1 − (3.61 ÷ 3.80) = 1 − 0.95 = **0.05** — the pipette, and every
reading it produced, ran at 5.00% under the true figure.

This lands close to, but is not identical with, a second, independently derived figure: the
bookkeeper's own recurring annual entry comparing butterfat credited to patrons against butterfat
actually received in cream at Larrow Green — "five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the
general account" (clerk Bertram Kyle, quoting bookkeeper Wilbur Teague's standing note). That 5¼% is
a different ratio, measured a different way: (true − recorded) ÷ recorded = 0.19 ÷ 3.61 ≈ 5.26%,
using the low recorded figure as the base rather than the true one. The two ratios are structurally
different — one divides by the true value, the other by the recorded value — so a small gap between
5.00% and 5.25% is expected, not an unexplained discrepancy. That two completely independent kinds of
evidence — a laboratory test-average and a bookkeeping ledger — both land within a quarter of a point
of "5%" is itself strong corroboration that the glassware's true shortfall really was in that
neighborhood.

**C1(b).** True average: **3.80**. Shortfall: **0.19 point** below true, constant in every month of
the year, for the whole affected period (recorded average 3.61 + deficiency 0.19 = 3.80) — stated
directly in the 1958 bulletin as reported by Falke, not something I computed. Flag: Larrow Green's
own average over the same years is separately given as 3.78, not 3.80 — a 0.02-point gap the sources
don't explain, but Larrow Green and Ostrey Hollow are different stations with different herds, and
3.78 is offered only as "an ordinary figure for this county," not as a claim the two should match
exactly.

**C1(c). 76,000 pounds.** Working: 40,000,000 lbs of milk received at Ostrey Hollow over the
affected years is the best-attested total — clerk Bertram Kyle personally compiled this exact figure
for the 1925 arbitration and states it implies 1,600,000 lbs a year, "year in and year out" (a
25-year window; the calendar span most often given for the defect, 1897–1923, is actually 26 years, a
one-year tension the sources never reconcile, though it doesn't change this multiplication, which
uses Kyle's own stated total and rate directly). Applying the 0.19-point deficiency to that volume:
40,000,000 × (0.19 ÷ 100) = **76,000 lbs** of butterfat that should have been credited but wasn't.
This is distinct from the patrons' own 1925 arbitration estimate of 67,000 lbs, a contemporaneous
guess made three decades before anyone actually measured the glassware (that didn't happen until
1958) — two figures, two methods, two dates, not meant to be the same number.

**C1(d). Worth: $19,600. Patrons actually received $3,800 — about 19.4% of it (roughly one-fifth).**

Working: the 76,000 lb shortfall from part (c), multiplied by clerk Bertram Kyle's own figure for the
average butterfat price over the affected stretch — "twenty-five cents the pound, taking the fat
years with the lean" — gives 76,000 × $0.25 = $19,000 by straight arithmetic.

But the sources also give a directly quoted total for what the shortfall was worth, and it disagrees
with that arithmetic: the patrons' 1925 petition set their loss at "$19,600, to the dollar," and,
separately, Bulletin 471's own 1958 computation is independently reported to arrive at the identical
$19,600. Where a quoted, independently corroborated document figure conflicts with a figure I've
derived myself by chaining two other numbers together, I've followed the resolution order these
instructions describe and preferred the document: **$19,600**. This is reinforced by the sources
themselves treating $19,000 as suspect — one narrator's "$19,000" is explicitly flagged elsewhere as
"a weaker, contradicted outlier (likely rounding/transcription)" of $19,600 — which is exactly the
number my own multiplication also produces, whether by coincidence or not. I can't fully explain the
roughly $600 (3%) gap between the two methods; the likeliest explanations are that Kyle's 25 cents
was only his own rough multi-year average rather than true year-by-year pricing, or that the affected
window was actually 26 years rather than the 25 Kyle's own math implies — either of which would nudge
a from-scratch calculation away from the document-derived total.

The 1925 arbitration award was $3,800, "without admission," confirmed by five independent sources.
$3,800 ÷ $19,600 ≈ **19.4%**.

**C2. Cannot be determined from the sources.** Nothing in the material identifies who Ostrey Hollow's
largest patron was, or gives any individual patron's credited-butterfat figure for 1910 specifically.
All three sub-parts depend on that patron's identity and volume, and the sources give only aggregate,
station-wide figures — the 40,000,000 lb / 25-year total, the 3.61/3.80 recorded/true test averages,
and the roughly 25-cents-a-pound average price — plus one individual patron's private record, Emmet
Brant's weigh-book, entered as arbitration evidence but never quoted for any single year, including
1910, and never compared against other patrons' volumes to establish who was largest. A
plausible-sounding number could be invented here, but the instructions score a confident guess worse
than declining, and nothing in what I've read supports one.
