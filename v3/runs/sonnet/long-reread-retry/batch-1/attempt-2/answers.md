# Batch 1 Answers — Larrow Valley / Ostrey Hollow (attempt 2)

Method note: where sources conflict I favored, in this rough order: a quoted primary document over
a paraphrase or "impression"; a contemporaneous diary or letter over later recollection; a named,
dated self-correction over the error it corrects; and convergence of multiple *independent*
accounts over a lone outlier or over several copies of the same account. I did not count repeats
from a shared origin as separate votes. Where a conclusion rests on connecting two facts the
sources never state together, I say so and call it an inference, not a finding. This is a second
attempt after an earlier answer sheet was judged wrong; I re-checked every date, count, and
computation against the notes rather than carrying the first attempt's wording forward, and I
found and fixed at least one real error in it (a source-count for the 1897 glassware date that had
wrongly folded in a date belonging to a different event — see Story 1).

## Section A — Reconstruction (first three of eight stories, in chronological order)

### 1. The Founding and the Fateful Purchase (1889–1897)

In 1889 the farmers of Larrow Valley, weary of hauling their butter to Ashlin, met at the Larrow
Green schoolhouse and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. They elected
Gideon Vose — "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the law and thought better of
it" — as first president; he wrote the by-laws himself at his kitchen table. The main source for
the founding is Dell Rundle's 1939/1959 jubilee pamphlet, "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty
Years."

In 1891 the Association built a creamery at Larrow Green and began paying the valley in cash
monthly. That same year, per Rundle (who quotes the text directly), the Association adopted
Article VII: patrons would be paid by the butterfat test of the station where they delivered, not
by the weight of milk — later called "the hinge of the whole matter." A single weaker, secondhand
source (Nyle Grigg's 1919 letter, self-flagged as an "impression") gives 1893 instead; Falke's 1959
report adds that the exact adoption date can never be confirmed, because the Association's early
minute book was lost in an office move. I favor 1891 — the quoted document over an admitted
impression — but not by an overwhelming margin.

On 4 May 1896 (Falke's report, the most precise date, matched by "May 1896" in buttermaker Hobart
Sill's 1934 retirement remarks), the Association opened a second station at Ostrey Hollow, seven
miles up the valley, to serve the Hessel Bottom farms. Ansel Keddie was appointed tester, a post he
held for 29 unbroken seasons, to 1925. The station opened with no glassware of its own; Sill lent
his own testing outfit for its first season.

The station's own equipment arrived 1 May 1897 — the best-supported of three competing dates
(1897, 1898, 1899) — corroborated by four independent sources: Sill's retirement remarks, an April
1897 invoice and company history from supplier Ammon & Sons, the Ashlin Chronicle, and tester's
wife Orra Keddie's diary. (An earlier pass at this material claimed a fourth competing date of
1922 for this same event; that is wrong — 1922 belongs to a dispute about when the glass was later
*condemned*, not to when it first went into service, and I've dropped it.) That invoice — six
dozen milk-test pipettes, twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, a hand tester — totals $77.93
by its own line items but $77.39 in its prose summary, an unresolved discrepancy within that single
document. This unremarkable purchase would go undetected as defective for 26 years.

### 2. The Hollow's Complaint and the Wet-Hay Verdict (1902–1908)

From 1902, Hessel Bottom farmers delivering to Ostrey Hollow began complaining that their milk
tested lower there than the same herds tested at Larrow Green. Management didn't believe them.
This date is corroborated by four sources: Rundle's pamphlet, two independent retellings, and —
decisively — Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary, which records the complaints starting "March
1902."

One patron, Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom, began keeping a private weigh-book of his own
deliveries — starting 1 January 1900 by his granddaughter Hazel Brant's account (1981), or 1
January 1897 by his nephew Duncan Keddie's account (1968). This three-year gap is not resolved by
the sources; neither witness is obviously more authoritative than the other.

In January 1908 the Association appointed a committee, chaired by Nyle Grigg, to investigate.
Grigg's own report, reprinted in full in a later 1919 letter, describes the committee visiting each
of Hessel Bottom's six farms three or four times over the winter, drawing and sealing its own milk
samples, watching the milking, hauling, and testing firsthand, and checking both the station's and
the patrons' scales against a known weight. It found nothing wrong with the testing process itself.
Instead it pointed to Hessel Bottom's hay: cut late off wet, sedge-choked meadow flooded part of
every spring, and fed heavily all winter, leaving the herds thin and slow to condition — "a cow
cannot put into her pail what her keep has never put into her." The committee's explicit finding:
"no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter." The report was accepted, and the Association
considered the matter closed.

It stayed closed for eleven years. The documents bearing on this story are Rundle's pamphlet
(general narrative), Nyle Grigg's original 1908 committee report (preserved by quotation in his own
1919 letter), Hazel Brant's 1981 tape and Duncan Keddie's 1968 letter (on the weigh-book), and Orra
Keddie's diary (contemporaneous corroboration of the 1902 start date and, from the receiving end,
of the committee's 1908 visit to her own family, the Brants).

### 3. Nine Weeks with Borrowed Glass (1911)

On 8 May 1911 the Ostrey Hollow station's own measuring glass broke. For nine weeks, until 10 July,
tester Ansel Keddie used a measure borrowed from elsewhere to test milk from the same Hessel Bottom
herds he tested every day. Through those nine weeks, the station's recorded butterfat tests rose
markedly above their usual level. On the day the station went back to its own glass, the readings
fell straight back down. Keddie noted the pattern in his own test book at the time and set a
question mark against it — but nothing came of it then.

This episode is independently confirmed by two very differently sourced accounts: Orin Falke's
1959 report to the county board, drawing on the 1958 college bulletin prepared by Professor Leland
Vaught and Miss Ivy Keddie; and the contemporaneous diary of Orra Keddie, the tester's own wife,
which records the same nine-week window as it happened.

Its significance wasn't understood at the time. It would only become clear decades later, when
Falke's 1959 report treated the 1911 episode as the single most decisive piece of evidence in the
whole Ostrey Hollow question: same tester, same herds, same season and supervision — only the
measuring instrument changed, and the readings changed with it. That result ruled out both of the
theories argued over the years — water added to the milk, and the tester manipulating figures — and
pointed squarely at the glassware itself. But in 1911 it was only a curiosity, set aside for nearly
half a century until the defective glass was found again.

The documents bearing on this story are Falke's 1959 report (quoting the bulletin's own account of
the episode) and Orra Keddie's diary, whose entry for the period independently confirms both the
exact nine-week span and the tester's own contemporaneous doubt.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1.** Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie: **granddaughter.** Orin Falke's 1959 report first
calls Ivy Keddie (the graduate student who, with Professor Leland Vaught, co-authored the 1958
college bulletin) the tester's "great-niece," but Falke appends a note after the report had gone to
type: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the
great-niece; the error on page two is mine." A named author explicitly correcting and verifying his
own error, in the same document, dated 1959, is about as strong as this material gets — nothing
points elsewhere.

**B2.** **Second cousins** — the best-supported derivation, though one link in the chain is
inference rather than a single direct quote. Chain: Hazel Brant (1981) says Emmet Brant was her
grandfather, and is described as "Roy's daughter" — so Hazel is Emmet Brant's granddaughter through
his son Roy. Separately, Duncan Keddie (1968) is described as "Emmet's sister's son" and as "Roy
Brant's first cousin" — so Emmet had a sister who married a Keddie, and their son was Duncan.
Independently, the notes on the 1908 committee's rounds state that the committee visited "Orra
Keddie's own family, the Brants" — meaning Orra, tester Ansel Keddie's wife, was herself born a
Brant from Hessel Bottom. The simplest reading that satisfies all three facts at once is that Orra
*is* the sister who married into the Keddie family — making Duncan Keddie the son of Ansel and
Orra, and a first cousin to Roy Brant. If so, Ivy Keddie (Ansel's granddaughter, per B1) and Hazel
Brant (Emmet's granddaughter) are grandchildren of a sibling pair: second cousins. Left open by the
sources: no document states outright that Orra is specifically Emmet's sister rather than some
other Brant relation (Hessel Bottom held six farms' worth of Brants), and none names which of Ansel
and Orra's children is Ivy's own parent. Neither gap changes the second-cousin degree — it holds
for any child of that couple — but the answer does rest on connecting facts the sources never state
together.

**B3.** Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**, the Association's founding president. Three
independent, named sources state this directly and explicitly reject a competing "nephew" story as
a known error in circulation: Selby Vose's own words ("My father, who was your first
president..."), Duncan Keddie's explicit 1968 correction, and clerk Bertram Kyle's 1953 account
("the son, whatever is said at Ashlin... I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and
it is nonsense"). Only Dell Rundle's secondhand pamphlet says "nephew." Three direct or corrective
sources against one secondhand one settles this for "son." It matters to Ostrey Hollow because
Selby Vose became the Association's manager in 1911 and held the post about 25 years — spanning the
Hollow's ongoing complaints, the 1919 circular accusing patrons of watering their milk (which he
signed alone), and the 1925 arbitration that ended in tester Ansel Keddie's dismissal. Being the
founder's actual son, not a more distant relation, fits clerk Kyle's picture of an office where
Vose's authority went essentially unquestioned — Kyle says Vose "read the totals and never read the
lines above them" — the kind of comfortable, inherited authority under which a real and growing
shortfall could go unexamined by management for decades.

**B4.** **None that the sources establish by blood or marriage — only an institutional one.** Gideon
Vose founded the Association and wrote the by-law making a station's own test the basis of payment;
his son Selby Vose managed the Association from 1911 and was tester Ansel Keddie's ultimate
superior, signing the 1919 circular that accused Keddie's own Hessel Bottom patrons of watering
their milk, and presiding as manager over the 1925 arbitration that ended in Keddie's dismissal.
That is a relationship of employer and employee, not of family. Whether any personal or marriage
tie existed between the Vose and Keddie families beyond that cannot be determined from the
sources — none is claimed anywhere, but the absence of a claim is not proof of its absence.

## Section C — Math (C1 and C2)

**C1(a).** About **5.3%**, closely matching an independently recorded bookkeeping figure of **5¼%
(5.25%)**. Derivation from the glassware: the 1958 measurement of the nine surviving pipettes found
they "delivered materially less than the capacity blown upon their stems, and in the same
proportion, one to another" — a fixed, constant under-fill. The fat test (the acid measure and
hand-tester bought alongside the pipettes point to a Babcock-style test) reads the fat column
against a scale calibrated for the pipette's full nominal charge, so a pipette that under-delivers
by a constant fraction produces a reading low by that same constant fraction every time — which is
exactly why one fixed percentage covers the entire 26-year span regardless of the true test in any
given year. The sources give no raw calibration number for the physical shortfall itself, so the
proportion has to be read back out of its effect: recorded average 3.61, true average 3.80 (see
C1(b)), deficiency 0.19 point; 0.19 ÷ 3.61 ≈ 5.3%. That this lands within a hair of the
bookkeeper's own independent, recurring entry — "five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to
the general account" — arrived at by comparing actual received cream against credited totals, not
by measuring any pipette, is the strongest cross-check available.

**C1(b).** True average: **3.80**. Shortfall: **0.19 point** below true, every month of the year,
for the entire affected period (recorded average 3.61 + deficiency 0.19 = 3.80), per the 1958
college bulletin as reported by Falke. One flag: Larrow Green's own average over the same years is
separately given as 3.78, not 3.80 — a 0.02-point gap nothing in the sources explains. I used the
bulletin's directly-measured 0.19-point deficiency, since it comes from measuring the actual defect
rather than from comparing two different stations' different herds.

**C1(c).** **76,000 pounds.** Working: 40,000,000 lbs of milk received at Ostrey Hollow over the
affected years (the best-attested total — clerk Bertram Kyle compiled this figure himself for the
1925 arbitration, and it implies a roughly 25-year window at 1,600,000 lbs/year; note that
1897–1923 is actually 26 years, a one-year tension the sources don't reconcile but which doesn't
affect this computation) × the 0.19-point deficiency = 40,000,000 × 0.0019 = **76,000 lbs** of
butterfat patrons should have been credited but weren't. This is distinct from the patrons' own
1925 estimate of 67,000 lbs, which was their contemporaneous guess at arbitration time, made
decades before anyone actually measured the glassware (that didn't happen until 1958) — two
figures, two methods, two dates, not the same number and not meant to be.

**C1(d).** Worth **$19,600** — the figure given both by the patrons' 1925 petition and,
independently, by Bulletin 471's own 1958 computation (an odd but well-attested convergence, since
the petition predates any actual measurement of the defect by over 30 years). Patrons actually
received **$3,800**, the 1925 arbitration award, "without admission" of wrongdoing — confirmed by
five independent sources. $3,800 ÷ $19,600 ≈ **19.4%**, roughly a fifth of what they were shorted.
One flag: 76,000 lbs (from C1(c)) at clerk Kyle's flat 25-cents-a-pound average price gives
$19,000, not $19,600 — a $600 gap the sources don't explain, most likely because 25 cents was only
Kyle's rough multi-year average ("taking the fat years with the lean"), not the bulletin's
presumably more exact year-by-year pricing, which isn't given in the sources available to me.

**C2.** **Cannot be determined from the sources.** Nothing in the material identifies who Ostrey
Hollow's largest patron was, or gives any individual patron's credited-butterfat figure for 1910
specifically. The sources establish aggregate, station-wide figures only — 40,000,000 lbs of milk
and a 3.61/3.80 recorded/true test average over the full affected period, and a roughly
25-cents-a-pound average butterfat price over that same stretch — and describe exactly one
individual patron's private record, Emmet Brant's weigh-book, entered as arbitration evidence,
without ever quoting a figure from it for any single year, including 1910. There is no way to
identify the largest patron, let alone compute his 1910 numbers, without inventing data the sources
don't supply, which the instructions for this test score worse than simply declining.
