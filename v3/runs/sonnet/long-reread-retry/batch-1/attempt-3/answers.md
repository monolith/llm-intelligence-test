# Batch 1 Answers — Larrow Valley / Ostrey Hollow (attempt 3)

Method: where sources conflict I preferred, in this order — a quoted primary document over a
paraphrase or admitted "impression"; a contemporaneous diary or letter over later recollection; a
named, dated self-correction over the error it corrects; and convergence of several *independent*
accounts over a lone outlier. Repeats that trace back to one origin count as one source, not
several. Where a conclusion depends on connecting facts the sources never state together, I call
it an inference and say what's still open. This pass re-derives everything from the notes rather
than assuming the prior two answer sheets were right anywhere in particular; where I've changed
the approach from those sheets (mainly the story boundaries in Section A), I say why.

## Section A — Reconstruction (first three of eight stories, in chronological order)

A note on boundaries: the material names eight underlying stories. Laid end to end, the events in
the notes fall into eight fairly clean episodes by date — founding (1889–91), the Ostrey Hollow
station and its equipment purchase (1896–97), the wet-hay committee (1902–08), the borrowed-glass
incident (1911), the watering-milk circular (1919), the glassware's condemnation (1923), the
arbitration (1925), and the 1958–59 discovery and report. I've treated the first three of those as
this section's three stories, rather than folding the founding and the station-opening together,
so that each of the "first three" is one discrete event rather than two run together.

### 1. The Founding of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery (1889–1891)

In 1889, farmers throughout Larrow Valley, weary of hauling their butter all the way to Ashlin, met
at the Larrow Green schoolhouse and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association.
They elected Gideon Vose as first president — "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought
of the law and thought better of it" — and he wrote the Association's by-laws himself, at his own
kitchen table. This comes from Dell Rundle's 1939/1959 jubilee pamphlet, "The Larrow Valley
Creamery: Fifty Years," the main narrative source for the founding.

In 1891 the Association built its creamery at Larrow Green, on the creek below the mill, and began
paying the valley in cash every month for the first time. That same year, per Rundle — who quotes
the text directly — the Association adopted Article VII of its by-laws: every patron would be paid
by the butterfat test of the milk he delivered, not by its weight, and "the test of the station
where he delivers shall govern." This sentence is later called "the hinge of the whole matter": it
is what made one station's test equipment, rather than any cross-check, the actual basis of every
patron's pay.

A single later source — a 1919 letter from committeeman Nyle Grigg — gives the adoption year as
1893 instead, but only "as I have always understood it," an admitted impression, not a document. I
favor Rundle's 1891, from the quoted by-law text, over Grigg's secondhand 1893 — though not by a
wide margin. A 1959 county report (Orin Falke) adds an independent reason the question may never
be settled: the Association's own early minute book was lost in an office move, and by-laws
themselves carry no adoption date on their face.

Documents: Rundle's pamphlet (founding narrative, quoted Article VII); Grigg's 1919 letter
(competing date); Falke's 1959 report (confirms the minute book's loss).

*(~290 words)*

### 2. Ostrey Hollow Opens, and the Purchase That Would Cause Twenty-Six Years of Trouble (1896–1897)

The Association's second station, built over the winter of 1895–96 seven miles up the valley from
Larrow Green, opened on 4 May 1896 — the most precise date given, from Orin Falke's 1959 county
report, independently matched by buttermaker Hobart Sill's 1934 retirement remarks. It served the
Hessel Bottom farms at the head of the valley: milk was weighed, tested, and separated there on the
spot, cream hauled down to Larrow Green, skim sent home the same morning. Ansel Keddie was
appointed tester and separator man from the day it opened, a post he held without a break for
twenty-nine seasons, to 1925.

The station opened with no testing glassware of its own. Sill lent it his own outfit from Larrow
Green for its first, partial season, which took in somewhere between roughly 900,000 and 1,100,000
pounds of milk — two different oral estimates, neither documentary.

The station's own equipment did not arrive until the following spring. An invoice from the
supplying firm, Ammon & Sons, dated 14 April 1897, itemizes six dozen milk-test pipettes, twelve
dozen test bottles, an acid measure, and a hand tester; its line items total $77.93, though the
firm's own later company history states the price as $77.39 — an unexplained $0.54 discrepancy
inside that one source. Ammon's history records the Association "put that outfit into use at its
Ostrey Hollow station on the first of May, 1897." That date is the best-supported of four competing
claims found across different retellings (1897, 1898, 1899, and 1922), corroborated independently
by Sill, by Ammon & Sons' own invoice and prose, and — most decisively — by tester's wife Orra
Keddie's contemporaneous diary, which records the same date.

This unremarkable reorder of pipettes and bottles is where the defect that would go undetected for
twenty-six years actually began.

Documents: Falke's 1959 report; Hobart Sill's 1934 remarks; Ammon & Sons' 1949 company history and
invoice; Bertram Kyle's 1953 account (a contradicted 1898 date); Orra Keddie's diary.

*(~305 words)*

### 3. The Hollow Complains, and the Committee Blames the Hay (1902–1908)

From 1902 — a date independently confirmed by four sources, including Orra Keddie's contemporaneous
diary entry "March 1902" — Hessel Bottom farmers delivering to Ostrey Hollow began complaining that
their milk tested lower there than the same herds' milk tested down at Larrow Green. Management did
not believe them.

One of these patrons, Emmet Brant, began keeping a private weigh-book of his own deliveries. Two of
his relatives disagree on when: granddaughter Hazel Brant (1981) says 1 January 1900; nephew Duncan
Keddie (1968) says 1 January 1897. That three-year gap is never resolved by the sources, and
neither witness is obviously more reliable than the other.

In January 1908 the Association appointed a committee, chaired by Nyle Grigg, to investigate. Over
that winter the committee visited each of Hessel Bottom's six farms three or four times, drew and
sealed their own milk samples rather than relying on the station's, watched the milking, hauling,
and testing firsthand, and checked both the station's and the patrons' scales against a known
weight. They found nothing to fault in how the testing itself was done. Instead they pointed to the
hay: Hessel Bottom's meadows lie along the creek below the station, flooded part of every spring,
thick with sedge and coarse sour grass, cut late and fed heavy all winter — leaving the herds
thin-flanked and slow to recover in spring. Conclusion: "a cow cannot put into her pail what her
keep has never put into her." The committee stated explicitly it found "no dishonesty of any kind,
in any quarter." The Association accepted the report and considered the matter closed.

It stayed closed for eleven years, until 1919. This good-faith verdict would eventually be shown
mistaken — but not until 1959, a later story.

Documents: Grigg's original 1908 report, preserved by his own 1919 covering letter; Hazel Brant's
1981 tape; Duncan Keddie's 1968 letter; Orra Keddie's diary (confirming both the 1902 date and the
1908 visit to her own family, the Brants).

*(~300 words)*

## Section B — Relationships

**B1.** Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie: **granddaughter.** Orin Falke's 1959 report for
the county board first calls Ivy Keddie (the graduate student who, with Professor Leland Vaught,
co-authored the 1958 college bulletin) the tester's "great-niece," then appends a note after the
report had gone to type: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter
and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." A named author explicitly correcting and
verifying his own error, in the same document, dated 1959, is as solid as this material gets, and
nothing else points elsewhere.

**B2.** Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant: **most likely second cousins — but this rests on
an inference the sources don't quite complete.** What's stated directly: Hazel Brant (1981) is
Emmet Brant's granddaughter through his son Roy Brant. Duncan Keddie (1968) is Emmet Brant's
sister's son and Roy Brant's first cousin — meaning some Brant woman, Emmet's sister, married a
Keddie man and had Duncan. Separately, the 1908 committee's report is described as having visited,
among the Hessel Bottom farms, "Orra Keddie's own family, the Brants" — meaning Ansel Keddie's wife
Orra was herself born a Brant from that neighborhood. If Orra *is* the sister who married a Keddie,
then Duncan Keddie is Ansel and Orra's son and Roy Brant's first cousin, and Ivy Keddie (Ansel's
granddaughter, per B1) and Hazel Brant (Emmet's granddaughter) would be grandchildren of a sibling
pair — second cousins. No source states outright that Orra is specifically Emmet's sister (Hessel
Bottom held six farms' worth of Brants, so she could be a more distant relation), and none names
which of Ansel and Orra's children is Ivy's parent. Both gaps are silent on the *degree* of
kinship — if the identification holds at all, second cousins follows regardless of which child is
Ivy's parent — but the identification itself is my inference from convergent detail, not a stated
fact, and the sources leave it open.

**B3.** Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**, the Association's founding president. Three
independent, named sources state this directly and explicitly reject a competing "nephew" story as
a known error in circulation: Selby Vose's own words ("My father, who was your first
president..."), Duncan Keddie's explicit 1968 correction, and clerk Bertram Kyle's 1953 account
("the son, whatever is said at Ashlin... I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and
it is nonsense"). Only Dell Rundle's secondhand pamphlet says "nephew." This matters to Ostrey
Hollow because Selby Vose became the Association's manager in 1911 and held the post about 25
years — spanning the Hollow's ongoing complaints, the 1919 circular accusing patrons of watering
their milk (which he alone signed), and the 1925 arbitration that ended in tester Ansel Keddie's
dismissal. Being the founder's actual son, not a more distant relation, fits clerk Kyle's picture of
an office where Vose's authority went essentially unquestioned — Kyle says Vose "read the totals
and never read the lines above them" before signing — the kind of inherited, comfortable authority
under which a real and growing shortfall could go unexamined by management for decades.

**B4.** **None that the sources establish by blood or marriage — only an institutional one.** Gideon
Vose founded the Association and wrote the by-law making a station's own test the basis of payment;
his son Selby Vose managed the Association from 1911 and was tester Ansel Keddie's ultimate
superior, signing the 1919 circular that accused Keddie's own Hessel Bottom patrons of watering
their milk, and presiding as manager through the 1925 arbitration that ended in Keddie's dismissal.
That is an employer-and-employee relationship, running through Gideon Vose's own Article VII, not a
family one. Whether any personal or marriage tie existed between the Vose and Keddie families
beyond that cannot be determined from the sources — none is claimed anywhere, but the absence of a
claim in this material isn't proof of its absence in fact.

## Section C — Math (C1 and C2)

**C1(a).** **About 5% short — most precisely 5¼% (5.25%).** From the glassware: the 1958
measurement of the nine surviving pipettes found they "delivered materially less than the capacity
blown upon their stems, and in the same proportion, one to another" — a fixed, constant under-fill,
not a random one. A pipette that always under-delivers by the same fraction produces a butterfat
reading low by that same fraction every time, which is exactly why one flat percentage covers the
whole 26-year span regardless of the true test in any given year. The sources give no raw
calibration number for the pipette itself, so the proportion has to be read back out of its
effect: recorded average 3.61, deficiency 0.19 point (see C1(b)); 0.19 ÷ 3.61 ≈ 5.3%. That result
sits within a whisker of an entirely independent, directly quoted figure: the bookkeeper's own
recurring annual entry comparing butterfat credited to patrons against butterfat actually received
in cream at Larrow Green, "five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account."
Two figures arrived at from completely different data — one from the test-average gap, one from
comparing physical pounds of cream received — converging on essentially the same ~5% is the
strongest evidence available for this number.

**C1(b).** True average: **3.80**. Shortfall: **0.19 point** below true, constant in every month of
the year, for the whole affected period (recorded average 3.61 + deficiency 0.19 = 3.80), per the
1958 college bulletin as reported by Falke. Flag: Larrow Green's own average over the same years is
separately given as 3.78, not 3.80 — a 0.02-point gap the sources don't explain. I used the
bulletin's directly measured 0.19-point deficiency rather than substituting Larrow Green's figure,
since 3.80 comes from measuring the actual defect at Ostrey Hollow, while 3.78 is simply what a
different station, with different herds, happened to average — the two numbers were never claimed
to be identical.

**C1(c).** **76,000 pounds.** Working: 40,000,000 lbs of milk received at Ostrey Hollow over the
affected years is the best-attested total (clerk Bertram Kyle compiled this figure himself for the
1925 arbitration, and it implies a roughly 25-year window at 1,600,000 lbs/year — worth noting that
1897 to 1923 is actually 26 years, a one-year tension the sources never reconcile, though it doesn't
change this multiplication). 40,000,000 × (0.19 ÷ 100) = **76,000 lbs** of butterfat patrons should
have been credited but weren't. This is distinct from the patrons' own 1925 arbitration estimate of
67,000 lbs, which was a contemporaneous guess made more than three decades before anyone actually
measured the glassware (that didn't happen until 1958) — two figures, two methods, two dates, not
meant to be the same number.

**C1(d).** Worth: **$19,600** — the figure given both by the patrons' 1925 petition and,
independently, by Bulletin 471's own 1958 computation (a striking convergence, since the petition
predates any actual measurement of the pipette defect by over 30 years; a weaker "$19,000" figure
appearing in only one other source looks like a rounding or transcription slip against this).
Patrons actually received **$3,800** — the 1925 arbitration award, "without admission" of
wrongdoing, confirmed by five independent sources. $3,800 ÷ $19,600 ≈ **19.4%**, roughly one-fifth
of what they were shorted. Flag: 76,000 lbs (C1(c)) at clerk Kyle's flat 25-cents-a-pound average
price gives $19,000, not $19,600 — a $600 gap the sources don't explain, most likely because Kyle's
25 cents was only his own rough multi-year average ("taking the fat years with the lean"), while
the bulletin's $19,600 presumably reflects year-by-year pricing that isn't given anywhere in the
sources available to me.

**C2. Cannot be determined from the sources.** Nothing in the material identifies who Ostrey
Hollow's largest patron was, or gives any individual patron's credited-butterfat figure for 1910
specifically. (a) pounds credited, (b) pounds owed and not credited, and (c) the money value of the
difference all depend on knowing that patron's identity and volume, and the sources give
aggregate, station-wide figures only — 40,000,000 lbs and a 3.61/3.80 recorded/true test average
over the full affected period, plus a roughly 25-cents-a-pound average butterfat price over that
same stretch. They describe exactly one individual patron's private record — Emmet Brant's
weigh-book, entered as arbitration evidence — without ever quoting a figure from it for any single
year, including 1910, and without ever comparing Brant's or anyone else's volume against other
patrons' to establish who was largest. Answering (a)–(c) would mean inventing data the sources
don't supply, which the instructions for this test score worse than declining.
