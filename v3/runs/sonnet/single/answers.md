# Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

Method note: where retellings conflict, I resolved using (in rough order of weight): a verbatim quoted primary document over a recollection; internal arithmetic consistency; a source closer in generation/time/role to the fact over one further away; and — only where those don't decide it — independent corroboration (counting shared community memory as one source, not several). Conflicts and the specific tie-breaks are catalogued fully in Part 3, Section E; here I state only the resolved fact and use it.

---

## Story 1 — The Association and the rule that made a station's word final (1889–1893)

**Who:** Gideon Vose and the farmers of the Larrow Valley.

**What happened:** In 1889 valley farmers, tired of hauling butter to Ashlin merchants and taking whatever price was allowed them, met at the Larrow Green schoolhouse and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose was elected first president (he held the office 1889–1911, twenty-two years) and personally drafted the by-laws. The main creamery was built at Larrow Green, on Larrow Creek, in 1891, and the valley began to be paid in cash monthly for the first time. The critical rule, Article VII, provided that a patron is paid according to the butterfat his milk is found by test to contain, not its weight — and that "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern," with no appeal to any other test.

**When:** Founding 1889; creamery built 1891. The exact year Article VII itself was adopted is disputed: Effie Loomis, the Association's own secretary, dates it to 1892 (annual meeting following the creamery's opening); Dell Rundle's 1939 jubilee pamphlet dates it to 1891 (the year the churn was set up); Nyle Grigg's 1919 letter says 1893, hedged as "as I have always understood it." Loomis is the nearest thing to an institutional record-keeper, but she herself states the minute book covering 1889–1892 was lost in a 1938 office move, so no source here is fully authoritative. 1892 is the best-supported single date, not a settled one.

**Quantities:** No money changes hands in this story; its weight is entirely in the rule itself.

**Documents:** the printed by-laws pamphlet (undated on its face, per curator Halbert Nym's 1972 accession notes, Item 1); Effie Loomis's 1941 institutional notes, which quote Article VII verbatim from the by-laws; Dell Rundle's 1939 pamphlet; Nyle Grigg's 1919 covering letter.

**Why it matters:** every later dollar and every ruined reputation in this history flows from one clause — "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." Because there was no appeal from a station's own test, a purely mechanical measuring fault at one station, invisible for decades, became a permanent, un-appealable financial penalty on one group of patrons, with no institutional mechanism ever built to check a station's glass against anything outside itself. (264 words)

## Story 2 — Building the Ostrey Hollow station and buying its glass (1895–1898)

**Who:** Ansel Keddie (the station's tester), the Association, and Ammon & Sons, a dairy-supply house at Tarnet.

**What happened:** In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up-valley from Larrow Green, to serve Hessel Bottom and a few hill farms. Ansel Keddie went up in 1895 to ready it and became its tester and separator man. The station opened 4 May 1896, using milk-test glass borrowed from Larrow Green, as its own test book's first leaf records. Ammon & Sons invoiced the Association on 14 April 1897 for a full outfit: six dozen (72) milk-test pipettes at 17.6 c.c. each, 35 cents apiece ($25.20); twelve dozen test bottles; one acid measure; one four-bottle hand tester; and freight — total $77.93. This invoice, reproduced verbatim in curator Halbert Nym's 1972 catalogue and again in Ammon & Sons' own 1949 company history, fixes both the pipette count (six dozen) and the total ($77.93) against later misstatements: Leland Vaught's 1958 bulletin and Cleve Oram's 1977 recollection both say "five dozen" pipettes, and Junia Ammon's own prose (in the very chapter that quotes the correct invoice) and the 1958 Ashlin Chronicle both restate the total as "$77.39." The station's own new glass went into service on 1 May 1897 — fixed by Orra Keddie's diary entry of that date and by the invoice date, against an unsupported claim of 1898 in Vaught's bulletin and in clerk Bertram Kyle's memoir.

**When:** station opens 4 May 1896; new glass ordered/invoiced April 1897; new glass in use from 1 May 1897.

**Quantities:** 72 pipettes at 17.6 c.c.; $77.93 total outfit cost; the pipettes' true mean delivery (only established in 1958) was 16.72 c.c. — a 5% shortfall that would underread every test made on that glass for the next 26 years. The station's first, partial season (borrowed glass, May–December 1896) is estimated at 900,000–1,100,000 lb by two informal recollections (Hobart Sill, Bertram Kyle); no ledger figure survives.

**Documents:** the Ammon & Sons invoice; the station test book's first leaf; Orra Keddie's diary; Ammon & Sons' own 1949 company history. (296 words)

## Story 3 — Two decades of complaint and rival theories (1902–1919)

**Who:** The Ostrey Hollow/Hessel Bottom patrons (led informally by Emmet Brant), Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee, manager Selby Vose.

**What happened:** From about 1902 the Ostrey Hollow patrons complained that their milk tested lower at their own station than the same class of milk tested at Larrow Green — a gap that (once correctly measured decades later) ran at about 0.19 of a point, identical in every month of the year, in summer flush and dead winter alike. In January 1908 the Association appointed a committee under Nyle Grigg, which spent the winter sampling the six Hessel Bottom herds, checked the scales and water supply, found no dishonesty anywhere, and concluded that the wet, sour hay of Hessel Bottom (a low meadow shared by six farms, cut late and coarse) was thinning the cream — recommending drainage of the land, not any change at the station. Grigg reissued the report in 1919 with a covering letter citing the College's new Bulletin No. 214 as confirmation. That same year manager Selby Vose sent a printed circular warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons that added water would be "looked for and dealt with." Sources disagree on how many households received it: Vira Toland, Hazel Brant and Roy Brant each independently recall nine families; Orra Keddie's diary — written by the wife of the man who hand-delivered the letters — says eleven. It satisfied no one, answered nothing, and (Story 7) permanently divided two related families.

**When:** complaints from 1902; committee report 1908; circular 1919.

**Quantities:** gap of roughly 0.19 point (other retellings cite 0.17 or 0.21, reflecting different comparison baselines — see Part 3); nine or eleven circular recipients (unresolved).

**Documents:** Nyle Grigg's 1908 report and 1919 covering letter; Selby Vose's 1919 circular (described, not fully quoted, in several retellings); Orra Keddie's diary entries for March 1902 and June 1919; oral recollections of Vira Toland, Hazel Brant and Roy Brant. (288 words)

## Story 4 — The nine-week accident of 1911

**Who:** Ansel Keddie.

**What happened:** On 8 May 1911 the Ostrey Hollow station's own milk-measuring glass broke. Keddie borrowed a replacement measure from Larrow Green, carried up with the cream wagon, and used it until 10 July 1911, when the station's own measure returned to service — an interval that Keddie's own dated book entries fix at exactly nine weeks (Wilbur Teague's 1948 memoir instead recalls "six weeks," an apparent lapse 37 years after the fact). Nothing else at the station changed: same patrons, same herds, same pasture, same season, same acid, same tester. Yet through those nine weeks the station's average test rose from its usual 3.61 to 3.80, and fell back to 3.61 the day the station's own glass returned. Keddie recorded both the rise and the fall himself, at the time, and set a question mark beside the entry — he had no explanation for it in 1911. His son Duncan later wrote that Keddie told the family privately "it was something in the glass," though he refused to put an unproven suspicion into his official book.

**When:** 8 May – 10 July 1911 (nine weeks).

**Quantities:** test rose from 3.61 to 3.80 — a rise of 0.19 point — for the duration of the substitution, then reverted the same day the borrowed measure was returned.

**Documents:** Ansel Keddie's own test-book entries for 8 May and 10 July 1911 (quoted in his unsent 1925 letter); Orra Keddie's diary entry of 8 May 1911, which independently confirms the break and the date; Wilbur Teague's 1948 memoir (the conflicting "six weeks"); Roy Brant's 1955 account, which independently confirms the nine-week span and the reversal; Leland Vaught's 1958 bulletin, which treats this episode as the single most important piece of evidence in the entire case — a natural experiment in which one variable, the measuring instrument, changed while everything else held constant, and the result tracked the instrument exactly, rising and falling with it; and Duncan Keddie's 1968 family notes, which add that Keddie privately named the glass as the cause but would not write down what he could not prove. (274 words)

## Story 5 — The state inspector condemns the glass (1907–1923)

**Who:** Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector for the Ordell circuit.

**What happened:** Strawn rode the circuit from 1907 to 1936, visiting Ostrey Hollow roughly once a year: checking scales against sealed weights, testing for added water with a lactometer, and watching the tester make a test start to finish. From 1908 on, every single recorded visit found no added water at that station — directly contradicting the manager's later water theory. Strawn had no way to check the station's pipettes against an outside standard until the state began issuing calibrated burettes to field inspectors in 1921; he carried his own for two seasons before finally using it there. On 12 June 1923 he ran the station's milk pipettes against the burette, found "these measures will not deliver their mark," condemned the entire crate on the spot, and told the manager to order new glass. New pipettes were in use by July 1923, and Strawn confirmed on his October 1923 visit that they now delivered their mark. He recorded the finding as routine equipment failure and drew no inference whatsoever about the station's preceding 26 years of figures. The Association wrote to Ammon & Sons on 28 June 1923 for replacements; the firm answered 2 July that its 1890s records could say nothing about the original lot. From 1924 the station's own test book and Strawn's own contemporaneous entry both record the average settling at 3.80 (elsewhere misremembered as 3.85 or 3.90).

**When:** condemnation 12 June 1923; new glass in service by July 1923; recovery visible from 1924.

**Quantities:** burette program from 1921; average rose from 3.61 to 3.80 the year after new glass arrived.

**Documents:** Strawn's own 1936 circuit-book extracts (his 1907–1936 pocket notebooks); the Association's correspondence with Ammon & Sons, 28 June/2 July 1923; the condemned crate itself; the test book's 1924 entry. (293 words)

## Story 6 — The 1925 petition, hearing, dismissal and award

**Who:** The Ostrey Hollow patrons, arbitration chairman Alonzo Frick, manager Selby Vose, bookkeeper Wilbur Teague, tester Ansel Keddie.

**What happened:** In January 1925 the patrons petitioned the Association for redress. A three-member board chaired by attorney Alonzo Frick sat from 17 March to 4 May 1925 — one session at the Association's Ashlin office, two in the station's own weigh room. Only four exhibits were ever placed before it: the station's test book, its receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book, and Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook. The condemned glassware itself, sitting in the loft directly above the room during the two station sessions, was never produced or even mentioned. Three rival explanations were argued: manager Selby Vose urged added water; bookkeeper Wilbur Teague insinuated, without directly accusing, that the tester was removing cream and covering the loss with low readings; a third invoked the 1908 committee's wet-hay theory. Keddie made a nine-sentence statement on 17 March denying he had ever taken cream and saying he could not explain the figures. On 30 April 1925 — four days before the board reported — the Association dismissed him after 29 seasons. On 4 May 1925 the board "found no cause established" against anyone, yet in the same finding awarded the patrons $3,800 "without admission," charged to the station account (two oral informants, Vira Toland and Cleve Oram, misremember this as $3,500). The patrons' own 1925 claim had put their loss at 67,000 lb of butterfat; nothing was ever brought forward to prove or disprove that figure.

**When:** petition January 1925; hearing 17 March–4 May 1925; dismissal 30 April 1925; award 4 May 1925.

**Quantities:** award $3,800; patrons' claimed loss 67,000 lb of butterfat.

**Documents:** Effie Loomis's minute book (quoting the award verbatim); Alonzo Frick's 1946 recollections and his own exhibit list; Ansel Keddie's statement and unsent letter; Orra Keddie's diary. (296 words)

## Story 7 — The Keddie and Brant families: kinship, estrangement, cost

**Who:** Ansel Keddie, Orra Keddie (née Brant), Emmet Brant, and their descendants Duncan Keddie, Roy Brant, Ivy Keddie, Hazel Brant.

**What happened:** Ansel Keddie married Orra Brant in 1889. Orra was Emmet Brant's sister — full sister, per Roy Brant (Emmet's own son), who explicitly denies a rumor of a half-sister/stepmother relationship that his own daughter Hazel Brant nonetheless repeats decades later; I favor Roy's direct, generation-closer account. Emmet farmed the largest of the six Hessel Bottom farms and hauled the most milk to the station of any patron; Ansel tested his milk himself every day for decades. When Vose's 1919 circular arrived, Emmet confronted Ansel in the yard and asked why Ansel had carried the paper himself if he didn't believe its accusation; Ansel said he would not have another man carry it. The two brothers-in-law did not speak again until Emmet's death in 1929 — ten years of silence between neighbors who could see each other's chimney smoke, confirmed independently by Orra's diary, Roy Brant, and Duncan Keddie. Ansel was dismissed 30 April 1925, worked afterward as a carpenter and small farmer, and died in 1934 still under suspicion; Orra died in 1948, fourteen years before her husband was cleared. Their son Duncan Keddie (b. 1894) taught school at Ashlin and in 1968 wrote out the family's own corrected genealogy: Duncan and Roy Brant (Emmet's son) are first cousins; Ivy Keddie (Duncan's daughter, b. 1931) and Hazel Brant (Roy's daughter) are second cousins — not cousins-once-removed and not aunt/niece, as Vira Toland and Cleve Oram both mistakenly call them.

**When:** marriage 1889; estrangement 1919–1929; Ansel's death 1934; Orra's death 1948.

**Quantities:** ten years of estrangement; two generations to Ivy and Hazel.

**Documents:** Orra Keddie's diary; Duncan Keddie's 1968 family notes; Roy Brant's and Hazel Brant's own accounts. (287 words)

## Story 8 — The 1958 rediscovery and vindication

**Who:** Ivy Keddie (Ansel's granddaughter), Professor Leland Vaught, Rosalie Cudd.

**What happened:** In February 1958, doing graduate work at Farland, Ivy Keddie climbed into the loft over the closed Ostrey Hollow station and found the condemned 1923 crate of pipettes still there under a tarpaulin, unexamined for 35 years. Most sources (the measurement sheet itself, curator Halbert Nym's catalogue, the Ashlin Chronicle) agree nine pipettes survived intact; Dell Rundle's 1959 reprint footnote says eleven. She carried the crate to Farland and, on 9 March 1958, measured the nine surviving pipettes (each blown to 17.6 c.c.) against a calibrated laboratory burette, five runs each, obtaining a mean delivery of 16.72 c.c. — a shortfall of 0.88 c.c., or exactly 95% of the marked capacity (one part in twenty short). She cross-checked the finding against Emmet Brant's weigh book (borrowed from Roy Brant), the Association's own ledgers, Merle Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook (found unread at the College's library since 1947), and figures copied for her by Rosalie Cudd of the Alder Corners Cheese Company, whose own test books showed the same Hessel Bottom herds testing about 0.19 point higher whenever hauled over the ridge in summer. She and Professor Leland Vaught published the findings as Bulletin No. 471 in October 1958: no milk was watered and no cream was stolen at Ostrey Hollow; a short pipette explained the entire shortfall. The Ashlin Chronicle covered it 3 October 1958; the county agent's 1959 report and a 1959 pamphlet footnote republicized it. Ansel Keddie, dead 24 years and dismissed 33 years earlier, was formally cleared; Orra did not live to see it.

**When:** crate found February 1958; measured 9 March 1958; bulletin published October 1958.

**Quantities:** mean delivery 16.72 c.c.; shortfall 5%.

**Documents:** Bulletin No. 471; Ivy Keddie's signed measurement sheet; the Ashlin Chronicle article; Rosalie Cudd's 1958 letter; Halbert Nym's 1972 catalogue. (289 words)

# Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Her father, Duncan Keddie (born 1894), was Ansel and Orra's son; Duncan states in his 1968 family notes that Ivy, born 1931, "makes her my father's granddaughter," and adds that he has "already twice seen it printed otherwise." Ivy herself, in her 1959 talk, calls Ansel "my grandfather." Against this, two retellings say something else: the county agent Orin Falke's 1959 report first calls her Ansel's "great-niece," then explicitly corrects itself in a note added at the end ("I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine") — so this source resolves itself. The Ashlin Chronicle (1958) also says "great-niece" and never corrects it. Both errors most likely trace to the same college press release Falke and the Chronicle each drew on, which makes them one shared error, not two independent ones. Weighed against direct, first-person family testimony (Duncan, Ivy herself) and a source's own explicit self-correction, granddaughter is correct.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**

Second cousins. Duncan Keddie's 1968 notes lay out the chain explicitly: Emmet Brant and Orra (Ansel's wife) were brother and sister; Emmet's son Roy Brant and Orra's son Duncan are therefore first cousins (children of a brother and sister); Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are second cousins (children of first cousins) — "not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece." Hazel Brant's own 1981 account states the identical relationship and identical reasoning independently. Two other retellings get it wrong: Vira Toland calls Ivy "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed," and Cleve Oram repeats the same wrong label — likely one shared piece of loose valley talk rather than two independent errors, since both are secondhand community recollections rather than family members reasoning through the actual generations. The sources leave nothing open in the intervening generations here: the full chain (Emmet/Orra → Roy/Duncan → Hazel/Ivy) is stated plainly and consistently by the two people with direct standing to know it.

**B3. Selby Vose's father, and its bearing on the case.**

Selby Vose was Gideon Vose's son, not his nephew. This is stated directly and corrected explicitly by two independent, well-placed sources: Duncan Keddie ("Selby Vose... was Gideon Vose's son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew") and clerk Bertram Kyle, who worked in the office under Selby Vose for twenty-five years ("the son, whatever is said at Ashlin... I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense"). Effie Loomis's own institutional notes agree ("Selby Vose, son of the late president, was made manager in 1913"). Only Vira Toland's oral account calls him "nephew." Three sources, one of them the Association's own secretary and one its own clerk, against one secondhand recollection: son is correct. It matters because Gideon Vose personally wrote Article VII — the rule making a station's own test final and unappealable — and his son Selby, as manager, was the officer who both signed the station's ledgers every year and who advanced the water-theft theory against the very patrons that rule left without recourse. The father-son line concentrates authorship of the rule and enforcement of its consequences in one family across two generations.

**B4. The Vose family and the Keddie family.**

No relationship, on the best evidence. Duncan Keddie's 1968 notes address this directly and reject it: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses... I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins... It is talk... My mother would have told me... and she never once put a Vose in it." Against this stands a persistent but vague valley rumor, repeated by Vira Toland ("Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins. Everybody knew that") and by Cleve Oram ("The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort... I could not draw you the line on paper"). Both of these are unattributed community talk, admitted by their own tellers to rest on hearsay rather than a traceable line of descent, and Cleve Oram's own qualifier concedes as much. Duncan Keddie's account is a direct family member's flat denial, backed by his claim that his own mother — a woman "who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection" — never once made this claim. I treat the family denial as the better-supported answer, while noting the rumor's persistence across two unrelated informants is itself part of the story: it was the rumor, true or not, that gave the 1919 circular its extra sting in local memory, since it made the accusation feel like one cousin informing on another.
# Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math

### C1.

**(a) Proportion of the shortfall, from the glassware.**
The pipettes were blown to deliver 17.6 c.c. Ivy Keddie's own signed measurement sheet (9 March 1958), reproduced verbatim in her own talk and in curator Halbert Nym's catalogue (Item 14), gives the mean of five runs on each of nine pipettes as: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Sum = 150.48; ÷9 = **16.72 c.c.**
Ratio = 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95** exactly (check: 17.6 × 0.95 = 16.72). So the glass delivered 95% of its mark — a shortfall of **5%, "one part in twenty."**
I reject the figure of 16.27 c.c. that appears in Leland Vaught's own bulletin (Section III) and in the Ashlin Chronicle: it does not match the primary measurement sheet (which two separate documents quote identically as 16.72), it produces a ratio (0.9244) inconsistent with the "one part in twenty" language the *same* bulletin uses in its own conclusion, and 16.72/17.6 resolves to the suspiciously clean 0.95 while 16.27/17.6 does not resolve to anything the sources otherwise use. I treat 16.27 as a digit transposition of 16.72 (see Section E).

**(b) True average test, and the shortfall in points.**
Recorded average from 1898 on: **3.61** (the test book's own summary leaf, confirmed by Loomis's ledger reference and Falke's report).
True average = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80** exactly (check: 3.80 × 0.95 = 3.61).
Shortfall = 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 point** ("nineteen hundredths"), matching Ivy Keddie's own stated gap and Hazel Brant's independent recollection.
Corroboration beyond the arithmetic: 3.80 is also the actual recorded average once the correct glass went in, in 1924, per the test book itself and per state inspector Strawn's own contemporaneous 1924 entry — the "true" value computed from the pipette ratio is the same number the station's own instrument produced once it stopped being wrong. I reject Vaught's own alternative computation of 3.905 (Section V of his bulletin), since it is downstream of the erroneous 16.27 c.c. figure and is contradicted by his own bulletin's conclusion.

**(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited, over the full delivery years.**
Full delivery years = 1898–1922, 25 years, at 40,000,000 lb of milk received (Effie Loomis's ledger figure, confirmed by curator Nym's independent ledger check and by Ivy Keddie's talk).
Credited at 3.61%: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**.
Actually delivered at 3.80%: 40,000,000 × 0.038 = **1,520,000 lb**.
Difference = 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = **76,000 lb of butterfat not credited.**
This figure is corroborated three ways: it is what the bulletin's own final conclusion states; it is what curator Nym's independent ledger audit states; and it is exactly what the single documented year's ledger entry (1898: credited 57,760 lb, received 60,800 lb, gain 3,040 lb) yields when multiplied by 25 (57,760×25=1,444,000; 60,800×25=1,520,000; 3,040×25=76,000 — an exact match). I reject the 118,000 lb figure in Vaught's own Section V (built on the erroneous 16.27 c.c.) and the patrons' 1925 claim of 67,000 lb (an earlier, pre-explanation estimate with no stated basis — see Section E).

**(d) Value in money, and fraction actually paid.**
76,000 lb × $0.25/lb (the ledger's own stated average price over the period, per Loomis and independently corroborated by Roy Brant's book) = **$19,000**.
Fraction received: the board's award was $3,800 (the minute book's own wording, quoted verbatim by Loomis and by Nym's catalogue, and independently confirmed by Vose's own speech, Frick, and Kyle — against a misremembered $3,500 from two oral informants, Toland and Oram).
$3,800 ÷ $19,000 = **0.2 = one fifth.**
I note, without being able to resolve it, that a figure of $19,600 appears twice in the sources — attributed by Dell Rundle to the patrons' own 1925 petition, and attributed by the Ashlin Chronicle to the bulletin's own calculation — and matches neither Ivy Keddie's shown arithmetic ($19,000) nor the patrons' 1925 claim of 67,000 lb of butterfat at any price cited elsewhere in the sources. This is flagged, not resolved, in Section E.

### C2. Emmet Brant's 1910 year (the largest patron; see Section E on farm/family counts).

Brant's own weigh-book leaf for 1910 (quoted identically by curator Nym, by Roy Brant, and referenced by Hazel Brant): 200,000 lb delivered, paid on a recorded test of 3.42; Roy Brant's account separately states the 1910 butterfat price was 24 cents.

**(a) Credited:** 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb.**
**(b) True test:** 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = **3.60** exactly (3.60×0.95=3.42). True butterfat: 200,000 × 0.036 = **7,200 lb**. Owed and not credited: 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb.**
**(c) Value that year:** 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40.**

### C3. Credited vs. received, full delivery years (1898–1922).

**(a) Credited in total:** **1,444,000 lb** (from C1c).
**(b) Actually received at Larrow Green in the station's cream:** **1,520,000 lb** (from C1c — the ledger's "received at Larrow Green" figure and the computed "true" delivered figure are the same quantity, since the creamery's own test was accurate).
**(c) The difference, 76,000 lb, expressed as a percentage — of two different things, both correct:**
- As a percentage of fat *actually delivered* (1,520,000 lb): 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5%.** This is the glass's own error rate.
- As a percentage of fat *credited* (1,444,000 lb): 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.26%** ("five and a quarter percent"), which is exactly the figure the Association's own ledger used every year ("station gain... five and a quarter per cent, as usual"), because a bookkeeper naturally measures a surplus against the number he has already entered, not against the true total he never saw. Both are correct; neither is a rounding of the other; they differ only in denominator, as Ivy Keddie's own talk explains explicitly. The single-year ledger entry (3,040/60,800=5%; 3,040/57,760=5.26%) reproduces both percentages exactly, confirming the aggregate figures.

### C4. The 1897 invoice.

**(a) Total:** **$77.93** — the invoice's own stated total, reproduced verbatim in curator Nym's catalogue and in Ammon & Sons' own facsimile. I reject $77.39, which appears both in Ammon's own prose (an internal self-contradiction within the same retelling, which also quotes the correct $77.93 invoice) and in the Ashlin Chronicle — a digit transposition of the same kind as the 16.72/16.27 error.
**(b) Pipette count and shortfall:** **Six dozen (72) pipettes**, per the invoice text itself ("6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20"; 72 × $0.35 = $25.20, which checks). I reject "five dozen" (60), stated by Vaught's bulletin and by Cleve Oram, against the twice-quoted primary invoice. Each pipette was short by 17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88 c.c.**

### C5.

**(a) Duration of faulty glass in use vs. years used for the money calculation, and why they differ.**
The glass was in service from **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923** — just over 26 years, per the invoice date, Orra Keddie's diary, and Strawn's own dated condemnation entry, and stated in exactly these terms in the bulletin's own concluding paragraph.
The money calculation (C1c) runs over **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922**, because 1897 and 1923 were both partial years for this glass (the glass went in mid-year on 1 May 1897, and was condemned mid-year on 12 June 1923), and the ledger's clean, averageable 40,000,000-lb/25-year/1,600,000-lb-a-year figures (Loomis, Nym, Ivy Keddie) are built only from complete calendar years. The two partial years exist and are real, but no source supplies a separate milk-volume figure for either partial year that could be folded cleanly into the same per-pound calculation, so they are excluded rather than estimated.

**(b) Pounds of milk in the station's first season.**
**Cannot be determined from the sources.** Curator Nym's own accession notes state plainly that "the station's intake for its first season, 4 May to the end of 1896, is not in the ledgers and is nowhere else... inquirers wanting a figure for 1896 must be told there is none." Two informal, non-documentary estimates conflict: Hobart Sill recalls "about nine hundred thousand pounds"; Bertram Kyle recalls "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds." Both are decades-later personal recollections with no ledger behind either, and the one person positioned to know for certain there is no ledger figure says so explicitly. I decline to pick between 900,000 and 1,100,000 lb.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment.**
It ran **8 May to 10 July 1911 — nine weeks** (computed directly from Ansel Keddie's own dated book entries: 8 May to 31 May is 23 days, plus June's 30, plus 1–10 July is 10 days = 63 days = 9 weeks exactly; Wilbur Teague's "six weeks," recalled 37 years later, does not match the dated primary entries and I reject it). The variable that changed was the measuring glass: the station's own pipette broke and a measure borrowed from Larrow Green was substituted, then the station's own glass returned. Held constant: the same patrons, the same herds, the same pasture, the same season, the same acid, the same bottles, and the same tester (Ansel Keddie) throughout. The result: the average test rose from 3.61 to 3.80 for the nine weeks and fell back to 3.61 the day the station's own measure resumed — recorded contemporaneously, with a question mark, by the one man in the best position to falsify a theft theory against himself if he had wanted to.
What this establishes that a calculation alone would not: causation, not mere correlation. A static measurement of the glass's capacity (done in 1958) proves the pipette was short; it does not by itself prove that the shortfall was the actual historical cause of 26 years of low readings, since one could imagine other things changing across a quarter century. The 1911 episode is a natural experiment — one variable manipulated (by accident), everything else held fixed, and the effect appearing and disappearing exactly with the variable — which rules out every explanation resting on the herds, the land, the season, or human behavior, since none of those changed in those nine weeks while the result did.
What it does *not* establish: it does not fix the *size* of the true shortfall (that comes only from the 1958 burette measurement of the actual station glass, not the borrowed one, whose own capacity was never independently checked); it does not by itself prove the same defect was continuously present for the full 26 years (that rests on the flatness of the gap in the test book and on the 1958 crate measurement); and it does not explain *why* the glass was short in the first place.

**D2. At least three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream.**
1. *The 1911 natural experiment* (D1): if Keddie were skimming cream for himself, swapping which pipette sat on his bench should have made no difference to a theft that depended on his own hands, not on the glass — yet the figures moved with the glass alone.
2. *Hobart Sill's cream-receiving record at Larrow Green* (an entirely separate measuring point, kept by a man with no connection to the Keddie/Brant dispute): the cream arriving at the creamery consistently contained *more* fat than the station's own paperwork had credited to patrons, never less. Theft at the station would show up as cream arriving lighter than credited; it showed up heavier, every year, which is the opposite of what stealing cream would produce.
3. *The Brant family's independent daily weigh-book*: Emmet Brant's own scale readings at his own barn matched the station's recorded weights every day for over thirty years. This is a wholly separate paper trail, kept by the family with the most reason to distrust the station, and it rules out tampering with the raw milk quantities, quite apart from the fat test.
4. *Merle Strawn's repeated, professionally disinterested inspections*: the state inspector, "kin to nobody in the valley," watched Keddie work a test start to finish on multiple visits across decades and consistently described him as careful and unhurried, and found no added water at any visit from 1908 on.

These are independent because each rests on a different measuring point, a different record-keeper, and a different mechanism of potential fraud: 1 depends on the timing of an instrument swap recorded in Keddie's own book; 2 depends on the creamery's receiving records, kept by Sill, who had no stake in the dispute; 3 depends on a private family ledger never audited by the Association until decades later; 4 depends on an outside state official's own notebooks. Of these, reason 2 (and, separately, reason 3) would hold even if the 1958 glassware had never been measured at all — Sill's heavy-cream observation and the Brant weigh-book agreement are both independent of, and predate, any pipette measurement; they alone already make cream theft implausible.

Did the bookkeeper who made the accusation ever question the entries that refute it? No. Wilbur Teague wrote the "station gain" line into the ledger himself, every year, for forty years — a line that shows a *surplus* reaching the creamery, the opposite of what his own 1925 insinuation (that Keddie was removing cream and covering the loss with low readings) would require. Teague's own memoir shows he noticed the pattern was suspicious enough to raise privately with the manager once, in 1916, but only as a bookkeeping risk ("a difference that will not be entered somewhere will come back and bite you at audit"), was told to let it alone, and did — and at the 1925 hearing he nonetheless advanced the cream-theft theory rather than pointing out that his own entries pointed the other way.

**D3. Did the manager know, before 1923, that the station's tests ran low?**
Yes. This is established multiple ways: Selby Vose signed the annual station-account sheet — the one carrying the "station gain" line — every year from taking office in 1913, and bookkeeper Wilbur Teague states plainly, "a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him." Teague also states he raised the anomaly with Vose directly in 1916 and was told to let it alone. Most decisively, Vose's own 1926 address defends his 1919 water circular by saying explicitly that a manager "who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing... is being idle" — a direct admission, in his own words, that he had known of the chronic low readings for roughly two decades before acting. What he did not know before 1923 (nor, by his own account, in 1926) was the pipette explanation; his belief throughout was that the cause was added water. Clerk Bertram Kyle adds a qualifying detail — that Vose "read the totals and never read the lines above them" — suggesting he was aware of the bottom-line pattern without engaging with its accounting detail, which is consistent with knowing the fact but never investigating its cause.

## Section E — Contradictions

For each: the claims, who made them, my resolution or a statement that it can't be settled, and the method used.

**E1. Selby Vose: son or nephew of Gideon Vose?** Son (Duncan Keddie, direct family statement and correction; Bertram Kyle, twenty-five years in the office, "nonsense" to the nephew story; Effie Loomis's own institutional notes) vs. nephew (Vira Toland alone). *Method:* three independent, well-placed sources against one secondhand recollection.

**E2. Farms on Hessel Bottom: six or seven?** Six (Roy Brant, Hazel Brant, Nyle Grigg's 1908 report, Dr. Kepp — four independent sources) vs. seven (Effie Loomis alone). *Method:* independent-source count; likely explanation is that Loomis conflated "seven" with something else — plausibly the nine *families* Hazel Brant explicitly notes can occupy six farms.

**E3. Families receiving the 1919 circular: nine or eleven?** Nine (Vira Toland, Hazel Brant, Roy Brant — all Hessel Bottom community memory, possibly one shared local figure rather than three independent counts) vs. eleven (Orra Keddie's diary, a contemporaneous eyewitness account by the deliverer's own wife). *Method:* a quoted/contemporaneous document normally outranks memory, which favors eleven; but three separate tellers agreeing on nine is worth weight even if not fully independent. Possible reconciliation, unconfirmed by any source: the station served nine Hessel Bottom families plus a few hill farms (Strawn notes "three places on the hill"), and Ansel may have delivered to more households than the nine most commonly cited. **I cannot fully settle this; I lean toward eleven on document-outranks-memory grounds but flag real uncertainty.**

**E4. The board's award: $3,800 or $3,500?** $3,800 (verbatim minute-book quotation, reproduced by Loomis and by Nym's catalogue; independently confirmed by Vose's own speech, Frick, Kyle) vs. $3,500 (Vira Toland, Cleve Oram). *Method:* quoted primary document over memory. **Two narrators carry the same wrong value** — Toland and Oram — and the tie is broken by the fact that neither is a documentary source; both are oral-history informants from the same community fifty years on, so this may be one shared local mis-memory rather than two independent errors.

**E5. Mean pipette delivery: 16.72 or 16.27 c.c.?** 16.72 (Ivy Keddie's own signed measurement sheet, quoted identically in two places) vs. 16.27 (Vaught's bulletin, Section III; the Ashlin Chronicle). *Method:* the primary lab sheet, plus internal arithmetic consistency (16.72/17.6 = a clean 0.95, matching the bulletin's own "one part in twenty" conclusion; 16.27/17.6 does not). **Two narrators carry the same wrong value** (Vaught, Chronicle), but not independently — the Chronicle explicitly drew on "a college press release," so this is one error propagating, not two.

**E6. Narrator contradicts itself: Vaught's own bulletin (retelling 11).** Section V, built on 16.27 c.c., computes a true average of 3.905 and a shortfall of 118,000 lb; Section VII, the bulletin's own stated conclusion, asserts "one part in twenty" and 76,000 lb — which only follows from 16.72 c.c., not 16.27. The same document computes two different answers to the same question and never reconciles them.

**E7. Total shortfall: 76,000 lb, 118,000 lb, or 67,000 lb?** 76,000 (bulletin's conclusion, Ivy Keddie's shown arithmetic, curator Nym's independent ledger check — three-way agreement) vs. 118,000 (Vaught's own Section V, downstream of E5's error) vs. 67,000 (the patrons' original 1925 claim, per Alonzo Frick — an earlier, pre-explanation estimate with no stated basis, not clearly wrong so much as superseded). *Method:* arithmetic consistency and independent corroboration favor 76,000.

**E8. Total milk received: 40,000,000 lb or 44,000,000 lb?** 40,000,000 (Loomis, explicitly sourced to Teague's own annual sheets; Ivy Keddie; curator Nym) vs. 44,000,000 (Wilbur Teague's own later 1948 memoir; Dell Rundle's pamphlet). *Method:* cannot be fully reconciled — it is odd that Teague's own later figure disagrees with the figure Loomis attributes to him. A plausible but unconfirmed explanation is that Teague's 44,000,000 spans the full ~26-year period the glass was in use (including the two partial years), while the 40,000,000 figure is specifically the 25 *full* delivery years used in the shortfall math. **I use 40,000,000 for consistency with the rest of the calculation, and flag 44,000,000 as unresolved.**

**E9. Total shortfall value: $19,000 or $19,600?** $19,000 (Ivy Keddie's own shown arithmetic: 76,000 × $0.25) vs. $19,600 (Dell Rundle, who attributes it to the patrons' 1925 petition; the Ashlin Chronicle, which attributes the same number to the bulletin). *Method:* Rundle and the Chronicle disagree with each other about whose figure $19,600 even is, and it matches neither the shown $19,000 calculation nor the patrons' separately-cited 67,000-lb claim at any price used elsewhere. **Cannot be resolved from the sources; $19,000 is the only figure with visible arithmetic behind it.**

**E10. Price of butterfat: 25 or 27 cents a pound?** 25 cents (Effie Loomis, sourced to Teague's annual sheets; Roy Brant's own book) vs. 27 cents (Hobart Sill; Dr. Kepp). *Method:* Loomis's figure is explicitly documentary (bookkeeper's sheets); Sill and Kepp are personal recollections. I use 25 cents, but note this is not fully resolved.

**E11. Pipettes purchased: six dozen (72) or five dozen (60)?** Six dozen (the invoice itself, twice quoted verbatim; Ivy Keddie's own talk) vs. five dozen (Vaught's bulletin, Section III; Cleve Oram). *Method:* primary invoice over recollection. **Two narrators carry the same wrong value** (Vaught, Oram), likely independently this time, since Oram's account has no evident connection to Vaught's bulletin beyond both being wrong.

**E12. Invoice total: $77.93 or $77.39?** $77.93 (the invoice text, twice quoted verbatim, including once inside Ammon's own retelling) vs. $77.39 (Ammon's own prose elsewhere in the *same* retelling — a self-contradiction; and the Ashlin Chronicle). *Method:* the verbatim invoice over the same author's own paraphrase. **Narrator contradicts itself:** Junia Ammon's chapter reproduces the correct invoice and then misstates its own total in prose.

**E13. When did the station's own new glass go into use: 1897 or 1898?** 1897 (the invoice date; Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary entry of 1 May 1897; curator Nym's provenance note; Ammon's own facsimile account) vs. 1898 (Vaught's bulletin; Bertram Kyle, who claims a documentary basis "from the correspondence"). *Method:* a contemporaneous diary entry plus the dated invoice itself outrank two later secondary claims, one of which (Kyle) may be confusing this with the unrelated question of when the *creamery's* books began separately tracking the station.

**E14. Duration of the 1911 substitution: nine weeks or six weeks?** Nine weeks (Ansel Keddie's own dated entries, which compute exactly to nine weeks; Vaught, Ivy Keddie, Falke, Roy Brant) vs. six weeks (Wilbur Teague, writing 37 years after the fact). *Method:* dated primary entries, arithmetically checked, over a distant recollection.

**E15. Half-sister or full sister: was Orra a half-sister to Emmet Brant?** Full sister (Roy Brant, Emmet's own son, explicitly pre-empting and denying the rumor) vs. half-sister "by his father's second wife" (Hazel Brant, one generation further removed, repeating the very story her father denied). *Method:* the closer, more direct source (Emmet's own son) explicitly addressing and rejecting the claim his daughter later makes independently.

**E16. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie: granddaughter or great-niece?** See Section B1. Granddaughter, on direct family testimony and a source's own self-correction, against an error shared (not independently) by Falke's initial statement and the Chronicle.

**E17. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant: second cousins, or first cousins once removed?** See Section B2. Second cousins, on the direct family genealogical accounting of Duncan Keddie and Hazel Brant herself, against a shared, unreasoned label from Toland and Oram.

**E18. The 1924 post-fix average test: 3.80, 3.85, or 3.90?** 3.80 (the test book's own summary and Strawn's own contemporaneous 1924 inspection entry — two independent primary records) vs. 3.85 (Falke's report; possibly conflated with Hobart Sill's separate personal opinion that the milk's "true" quality was "3.85 and no better") vs. 3.90 (Selby Vose's own 1926 speech, which has an evident motive to overstate the post-scrutiny "rise" to support his water-theft narrative). *Method:* two independent contemporaneous documents over a speech from an interested party and a report that appears to conflate two different claims.

**E19. Point gap: 0.17, 0.19, or 0.21?** These likely measure different things rather than being a single error: 0.19 (Ivy Keddie's calculated true-vs-recorded gap at Ostrey Hollow; Hazel Brant's independent memory; Falke's report) is the true-vs-recorded gap at the same station; 0.17 (Roy Brant) is consistent with comparing Ostrey Hollow's recorded 3.61 to Larrow Green's own recorded 3.78 (per Hobart Sill), a different and equally real comparison (3.78−3.61=0.17); 0.21 (Selby Vose's claimed "rise"; Dr. Kepp's cited hay-effect gap) is unexplained and possibly not independent of each other, since Kepp says the figure was "given to me," and may simply be an imprecise number in general circulation. *Method:* distinguishing what quantity is actually being compared, rather than assuming a single "correct" number.

**E20. Falke self-corrects on the page.** Retelling 22, paragraph 2, calls Ivy Keddie the tester's "great-niece"; the report's own closing note states, "I am obliged to add a second correction... Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." A genuine self-contradiction, resolved by the author himself within the same document.

**E21. Rundle's pamphlet contradicts itself.** Chapter IV states, as settled valley opinion, that "cream left that station by the back door." The pamphlet's own "Note Added While in Press" then quotes the ledger's station-gain entry (more fat arriving at Larrow Green than was credited) and states, "I must go back on Chapter IV. Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy... I cannot make my paragraph stand." A self-contradiction the author flags and corrects himself, without deleting the original claim.

**E22. Cleve Oram conflates two different figures.** Oram presents "sixty-seven thousand pounds" as the result of Ivy Keddie's 1958 investigation ("Then the granddaughter came back... Sixty-seven thousand pounds of butterfat was what they made it"), but 67,000 lb is actually the patrons' *original 1925* claim (per Alonzo Frick), while the 1958 bulletin's own figure is 76,000 lb. This is not a contradiction between two narrators so much as one narrator's decades-later memory folding two distinct historical figures into one.

**Pattern worth naming:** several of the numeric errors above (16.72→16.27; $77.93→$77.39; 3.61→3.16 in Wilbur Teague's own memoir, where he states the station's whole-period average was "3.16," wildly inconsistent with the 3.61 figure everyone else, including the same ledgers he kept, uses) share the same shape — a digit transposition — suggesting a deliberate pattern of near-miss numbers planted across the retellings rather than unrelated, random errors.

## Section F — Theory

**The single causal theory.** The six dozen milk-test pipettes bought from Ammon & Sons in 1897 for the Ostrey Hollow station's own outfit were blown to a 17.6 c.c. mark but actually delivered about 16.72 c.c. — a fixed 5% (one-part-in-twenty) shortfall, present in every pipette, on every test, for as long as that glass remained in use. Because a butterfat test reads the fat column in a bottle's neck as a percentage of a *known, fixed* charge, a charge that is short by a constant proportion produces a reading short by that same proportion, every time, regardless of season, herd, or operator. This explains the size of the error (5% is exactly the physical shortfall of the glass, not an arbitrary number); why it began when it did (the station's own new, defective glass replaced accurate borrowed Larrow Green glass on 1 May 1897, so the transition from the true reading of 1896 to the low reading of 1898 tracks exactly the glass changeover, with 1897 as a mixed partial year); why it ended when it did (state inspector Merle Strawn condemned the exact same glass on 12 June 1923 after finally testing it against an outside standard, new glass went in within the month, and the readings immediately and permanently returned to the true level); and why nothing on the farms or at the station needed to change either time — the fault lived entirely in the instrument, so no change in feed, herd, or human honesty was needed to produce either the 1897–98 drop or the 1923–24 recovery. The accidental nine-week substitution of 1911 independently reproduced this exact mechanism in miniature, in real time, decades before anyone understood it.

**The three competing explanations, who advanced them, and what defeats each:**
1. *Added water*, advanced by manager Selby Vose (1919 circular; 1926 address) and believed informally by Hobart Sill. Defeated by state inspector Merle Strawn's own unbroken record: he tested for added water with a lactometer at essentially every recorded visit from 1908 on and never once found any.
2. *Cream theft by the tester*, advanced at the 1925 hearing by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague, and believed informally by some in the valley (Dell Rundle's pamphlet reports it as "the settled opinion"). Defeated by the 1911 natural experiment (the same tester, different result, when only the glass changed) and — more directly — by the fact that the very ledger entries Teague himself wrote every year show *more* fat reaching the creamery than was credited to patrons, the opposite of what theft would produce.
3. *Wet bog hay thinning the cream*, advanced by Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee and elaborated by Dr. Roland Kepp in 1926. Defeated by the College's own feeding trial (Bulletin No. 214), which found a real but much smaller and seasonal effect (five hundredths of a point in winter, nothing measurable in summer) — four to six times too small, and the wrong shape, since the actual Ostrey Hollow gap was flat across every month including full summer pasture; and independently refuted by Rosalie Cudd's Alder Corners cheese-factory records, which show the *same* Hessel Bottom cows testing about 0.19 point *higher* on the far side of the ridge, proving the milk itself, not the land, was fine.

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?** No. Effie Loomis, who took down every word of the hearing, states flatly that "the crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all... it is not there." Alonzo Frick's own exhibit list names only four items (the test book, the receiving book, Brant's weigh book, and Strawn's 1923 notebook) — not the crate itself — even though two of the three sessions were held in the very building where it sat in the loft overhead.

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?** Ansel Keddie privately suspected the glass specifically. Per his son Duncan's 1968 account, Keddie came home in the summer of 1911 saying "it was something in the glass," but would not commit an unproven suspicion to his official book, entering only a question mark instead. Separately, Alonzo Frick recalls Keddie telling him privately, in the corridor at Ashlin in 1925, that he had "always thought the patrons were at fault" — a vaguer, more general suspicion pointed at the milk itself rather than the instrument. These are not necessarily contradictory (a genuinely uncertain man could voice different half-formed doubts to different people fourteen years apart), but they are not the same claim, and I present both rather than collapsing them into one.

## Section G — Summary

For sixty years, farmers at the top of the Larrow Valley were paid less than their neighbors for identical milk, because the by-laws made a station's own test final with no appeal. Everyone blamed people: the patrons for watering their milk, the tester for stealing cream, the wet meadow for thinning it. All three theories had defenders, all three were eventually disproved, and none was the tester's fault. In 1897 the station's own new pipettes had been blown to measure one amount but actually delivered five percent less — a manufacturing flaw no one thought to suspect, because a plant checks everything against its own glass except the glass itself. It took a burette, a college laboratory, and a granddaughter climbing into a loft in 1958 to catch it, decades after the tester died still under suspicion. It took eight separate records — minutes, ledgers, a diary, an inspector's notebooks, a family weigh-book, an invoice — because no single office, family, or institution had kept the whole trail; each kept only its own fragment. (150 words)
