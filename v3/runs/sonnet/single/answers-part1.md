# Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

Method note: where retellings conflict, I resolved using (in rough order of weight): a verbatim quoted primary document over a recollection; internal arithmetic consistency; a source closer in generation/time/role to the fact over one further away; and — only where those don't decide it — independent corroboration (counting shared community memory as one source, not several). Conflicts and the specific tie-breaks are catalogued fully in Part 3, Section E; here I state only the resolved fact and use it.

---

## Story 1 — The Association and the rule that made a station's word final (1889–1893)

**Who:** Gideon Vose and the farmers of the Larrow Valley.

**What happened:** In 1889 valley farmers, tired of hauling butter to Ashlin merchants and taking whatever price was allowed them, met at the Larrow Green schoolhouse and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose was elected first president (he held the office 1889–1911, twenty-two years) and personally drafted the by-laws. The main creamery was built at Larrow Green, on Larrow Creek, in 1891, and the valley began to be paid in cash monthly for the first time. The critical rule, Article VII, provided that a patron is paid according to the butterfat his milk is found by test to contain, not its weight — and that "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern," with no appeal to any other test.

**When:** Founding 1889; creamery built 1891. The exact year Article VII itself was adopted is disputed: Effie Loomis, the Association's own secretary, dates it to 1892 (annual meeting following the creamery's opening); Dell Rundle's 1939 jubilee pamphlet dates it to 1891 (the year the churn was set up); Nyle Grigg's 1919 letter says 1893, hedged as "as I have always understood it." Loomis is the nearest thing to an institutional record-keeper, but she herself states the minute book covering 1889–1892 was lost in a 1938 office move, so no source here is fully authoritative. 1892 is the best-supported single date, not a settled one.

**Quantities:** No money changes hands in this story; its weight is entirely in the rule itself.

**Documents:** the printed by-laws pamphlet (undated on its face, per curator Halbert Nym's 1972 accession notes, Item 1); Effie Loomis's 1941 institutional notes, which quote Article VII verbatim from the by-laws; Dell Rundle's 1939 pamphlet; Nyle Grigg's 1919 covering letter.

**Why it matters:** every later dollar and every ruined reputation in this history flows from one clause — "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." Because there was no appeal from a station's own test, a purely mechanical measuring fault at one station, invisible for decades, became a permanent, un-appealable financial penalty on one group of patrons, with no institutional mechanism ever built to check a station's glass against anything outside itself. (264 words)

## Story 2 — Building the Ostrey Hollow station and buying its glass (1895–1898)

**Who:** Ansel Keddie (the station's tester), the Association, and Ammon & Sons, a dairy-supply house at Tarnet.

**What happened:** In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up-valley from Larrow Green, to serve Hessel Bottom and a few hill farms. Ansel Keddie went up in 1895 to ready it and became its tester and separator man. The station opened 4 May 1896, using milk-test glass borrowed from Larrow Green, as its own test book's first leaf records. Ammon & Sons invoiced the Association on 14 April 1897 for a full outfit: six dozen (72) milk-test pipettes at 17.6 c.c. each, 35 cents apiece ($25.20); twelve dozen test bottles; one acid measure; one four-bottle hand tester; and freight — total $77.93. This invoice, reproduced verbatim in curator Halbert Nym's 1972 catalogue and again in Ammon & Sons' own 1949 company history, fixes both the pipette count (six dozen) and the total ($77.93) against later misstatements: Leland Vaught's 1958 bulletin and Cleve Oram's 1977 recollection both say "five dozen" pipettes, and Junia Ammon's own prose (in the very chapter that quotes the correct invoice) and the 1958 Ashlin Chronicle both restate the total as "$77.39." The station's own new glass went into service on 1 May 1897 — fixed by Orra Keddie's diary entry of that date and by the invoice date, against an unsupported claim of 1898 in Vaught's bulletin and in clerk Bertram Kyle's memoir.

**When:** station opens 4 May 1896; new glass ordered/invoiced April 1897; new glass in use from 1 May 1897.

**Quantities:** 72 pipettes at 17.6 c.c.; $77.93 total outfit cost; the pipettes' true mean delivery (only established in 1958) was 16.72 c.c. — a 5% shortfall that would underread every test made on that glass for the next 26 years. The station's first, partial season (borrowed glass, May–December 1896) is estimated at 900,000–1,100,000 lb by two informal recollections (Hobart Sill, Bertram Kyle); no ledger figure survives.

**Documents:** the Ammon & Sons invoice; the station test book's first leaf; Orra Keddie's diary; Ammon & Sons' own 1949 company history. (296 words)

## Story 3 — Two decades of complaint and rival theories (1902–1919)

**Who:** The Ostrey Hollow/Hessel Bottom patrons (led informally by Emmet Brant), Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee, manager Selby Vose.

**What happened:** From about 1902 the Ostrey Hollow patrons complained that their milk tested lower at their own station than the same class of milk tested at Larrow Green — a gap that (once correctly measured decades later) ran at about 0.19 of a point, identical in every month of the year, in summer flush and dead winter alike. In January 1908 the Association appointed a committee under Nyle Grigg, which spent the winter sampling the six Hessel Bottom herds, checked the scales and water supply, found no dishonesty anywhere, and concluded that the wet, sour hay of Hessel Bottom (a low meadow shared by six farms, cut late and coarse) was thinning the cream — recommending drainage of the land, not any change at the station. Grigg reissued the report in 1919 with a covering letter citing the College's new Bulletin No. 214 as confirmation. That same year manager Selby Vose sent a printed circular warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons that added water would be "looked for and dealt with." Sources disagree on how many households received it: Vira Toland, Hazel Brant and Roy Brant each independently recall nine families; Orra Keddie's diary — written by the wife of the man who hand-delivered the letters — says eleven. It satisfied no one, answered nothing, and (Story 7) permanently divided two related families.

**When:** complaints from 1902; committee report 1908; circular 1919.

**Quantities:** gap of roughly 0.19 point (other retellings cite 0.17 or 0.21, reflecting different comparison baselines — see Part 3); nine or eleven circular recipients (unresolved).

**Documents:** Nyle Grigg's 1908 report and 1919 covering letter; Selby Vose's 1919 circular (described, not fully quoted, in several retellings); Orra Keddie's diary entries for March 1902 and June 1919; oral recollections of Vira Toland, Hazel Brant and Roy Brant. (288 words)

## Story 4 — The nine-week accident of 1911

**Who:** Ansel Keddie.

**What happened:** On 8 May 1911 the Ostrey Hollow station's own milk-measuring glass broke. Keddie borrowed a replacement measure from Larrow Green, carried up with the cream wagon, and used it until 10 July 1911, when the station's own measure returned to service — an interval that Keddie's own dated book entries fix at exactly nine weeks (Wilbur Teague's 1948 memoir instead recalls "six weeks," an apparent lapse 37 years after the fact). Nothing else at the station changed: same patrons, same herds, same pasture, same season, same acid, same tester. Yet through those nine weeks the station's average test rose from its usual 3.61 to 3.80, and fell back to 3.61 the day the station's own glass returned. Keddie recorded both the rise and the fall himself, at the time, and set a question mark beside the entry — he had no explanation for it in 1911. His son Duncan later wrote that Keddie told the family privately "it was something in the glass," though he refused to put an unproven suspicion into his official book.

**When:** 8 May – 10 July 1911 (nine weeks).

**Quantities:** test rose from 3.61 to 3.80 — a rise of 0.19 point — for the duration of the substitution, then reverted the same day the borrowed measure was returned.

**Documents:** Ansel Keddie's own test-book entries for 8 May and 10 July 1911 (quoted in his unsent 1925 letter); Orra Keddie's diary entry of 8 May 1911, which independently confirms the break and the date; Wilbur Teague's 1948 memoir (the conflicting "six weeks"); Roy Brant's 1955 account, which independently confirms the nine-week span and the reversal; Leland Vaught's 1958 bulletin, which treats this episode as the single most important piece of evidence in the entire case — a natural experiment in which one variable, the measuring instrument, changed while everything else held constant, and the result tracked the instrument exactly, rising and falling with it; and Duncan Keddie's 1968 family notes, which add that Keddie privately named the glass as the cause but would not write down what he could not prove. (274 words)
