# Answers — Part 2: Section A, Stories 5–8, and Section B

## Story 5 — The state inspector condemns the glass (1907–1923)

**Who:** Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector for the Ordell circuit.

**What happened:** Strawn rode the circuit from 1907 to 1936, visiting Ostrey Hollow roughly once a year: checking scales against sealed weights, testing for added water with a lactometer, and watching the tester make a test start to finish. From 1908 on, every single recorded visit found no added water at that station — directly contradicting the manager's later water theory. Strawn had no way to check the station's pipettes against an outside standard until the state began issuing calibrated burettes to field inspectors in 1921; he carried his own for two seasons before finally using it there. On 12 June 1923 he ran the station's milk pipettes against the burette, found "these measures will not deliver their mark," condemned the entire crate on the spot, and told the manager to order new glass. New pipettes were in use by July 1923, and Strawn confirmed on his October 1923 visit that they now delivered their mark. He recorded the finding as routine equipment failure and drew no inference whatsoever about the station's preceding 26 years of figures. The Association wrote to Ammon & Sons on 28 June 1923 for replacements; the firm answered 2 July that its 1890s records could say nothing about the original lot. From 1924 the station's own test book and Strawn's own contemporaneous entry both record the average settling at 3.80 (elsewhere misremembered as 3.85 or 3.90).

**When:** condemnation 12 June 1923; new glass in service by July 1923; recovery visible from 1924.

**Quantities:** burette program from 1921; average rose from 3.61 to 3.80 the year after new glass arrived.

**Documents:** Strawn's own 1936 circuit-book extracts (his 1907–1936 pocket notebooks); the Association's correspondence with Ammon & Sons, 28 June/2 July 1923; the condemned crate itself; the test book's 1924 entry. (293 words)

## Story 6 — The 1925 petition, hearing, dismissal and award

**Who:** The Ostrey Hollow patrons, arbitration chairman Alonzo Frick, manager Selby Vose, bookkeeper Wilbur Teague, tester Ansel Keddie.

**What happened:** In January 1925 the patrons petitioned the Association for redress. A three-member board chaired by attorney Alonzo Frick sat from 17 March to 4 May 1925 — one session at the Association's Ashlin office, two in the station's own weigh room. Only four exhibits were ever placed before it: the station's test book, its receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book, and Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook. The condemned glassware itself, sitting in the loft directly above the room during the two station sessions, was never produced or even mentioned. Three rival explanations were argued: manager Selby Vose urged added water; bookkeeper Wilbur Teague insinuated, without directly accusing, that the tester was removing cream and covering the loss with low readings; a third invoked the 1908 committee's wet-hay theory. Keddie made a nine-sentence statement on 17 March denying he had ever taken cream and saying he could not explain the figures. On 30 April 1925 — four days before the board reported — the Association dismissed him after 29 seasons. On 4 May 1925 the board "found no cause established" against anyone, yet in the same finding awarded the patrons $3,800 "without admission," charged to the station account (two oral informants, Vira Toland and Cleve Oram, misremember this as $3,500). The patrons' own 1925 claim had put their loss at 67,000 lb of butterfat; nothing was ever brought forward to prove or disprove that figure.

**When:** petition January 1925; hearing 17 March–4 May 1925; dismissal 30 April 1925; award 4 May 1925.

**Quantities:** award $3,800; patrons' claimed loss 67,000 lb of butterfat.

**Documents:** Effie Loomis's minute book (quoting the award verbatim); Alonzo Frick's 1946 recollections and his own exhibit list; Ansel Keddie's statement and unsent letter; Orra Keddie's diary. (296 words)

## Story 7 — The Keddie and Brant families: kinship, estrangement, cost

**Who:** Ansel Keddie, Orra Keddie (née Brant), Emmet Brant, and their descendants Duncan Keddie, Roy Brant, Ivy Keddie, Hazel Brant.

**What happened:** Ansel Keddie married Orra Brant in 1889. Orra was Emmet Brant's sister — full sister, per Roy Brant (Emmet's own son), who explicitly denies a rumor of a half-sister/stepmother relationship that his own daughter Hazel Brant nonetheless repeats decades later; I favor Roy's direct, generation-closer account. Emmet farmed the largest of the six Hessel Bottom farms and hauled the most milk to the station of any patron; Ansel tested his milk himself every day for decades. When Vose's 1919 circular arrived, Emmet confronted Ansel in the yard and asked why Ansel had carried the paper himself if he didn't believe its accusation; Ansel said he would not have another man carry it. The two brothers-in-law did not speak again until Emmet's death in 1929 — ten years of silence between neighbors who could see each other's chimney smoke, confirmed independently by Orra's diary, Roy Brant, and Duncan Keddie. Ansel was dismissed 30 April 1925, worked afterward as a carpenter and small farmer, and died in 1934 still under suspicion; Orra died in 1948, fourteen years before her husband was cleared. Their son Duncan Keddie (b. 1894) taught school at Ashlin and in 1968 wrote out the family's own corrected genealogy: Duncan and Roy Brant (Emmet's son) are first cousins; Ivy Keddie (Duncan's daughter, b. 1931) and Hazel Brant (Roy's daughter) are second cousins — not cousins-once-removed and not aunt/niece, as Vira Toland and Cleve Oram both mistakenly call them.

**When:** marriage 1889; estrangement 1919–1929; Ansel's death 1934; Orra's death 1948.

**Quantities:** ten years of estrangement; two generations to Ivy and Hazel.

**Documents:** Orra Keddie's diary; Duncan Keddie's 1968 family notes; Roy Brant's and Hazel Brant's own accounts. (287 words)

## Story 8 — The 1958 rediscovery and vindication

**Who:** Ivy Keddie (Ansel's granddaughter), Professor Leland Vaught, Rosalie Cudd.

**What happened:** In February 1958, doing graduate work at Farland, Ivy Keddie climbed into the loft over the closed Ostrey Hollow station and found the condemned 1923 crate of pipettes still there under a tarpaulin, unexamined for 35 years. Most sources (the measurement sheet itself, curator Halbert Nym's catalogue, the Ashlin Chronicle) agree nine pipettes survived intact; Dell Rundle's 1959 reprint footnote says eleven. She carried the crate to Farland and, on 9 March 1958, measured the nine surviving pipettes (each blown to 17.6 c.c.) against a calibrated laboratory burette, five runs each, obtaining a mean delivery of 16.72 c.c. — a shortfall of 0.88 c.c., or exactly 95% of the marked capacity (one part in twenty short). She cross-checked the finding against Emmet Brant's weigh book (borrowed from Roy Brant), the Association's own ledgers, Merle Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook (found unread at the College's library since 1947), and figures copied for her by Rosalie Cudd of the Alder Corners Cheese Company, whose own test books showed the same Hessel Bottom herds testing about 0.19 point higher whenever hauled over the ridge in summer. She and Professor Leland Vaught published the findings as Bulletin No. 471 in October 1958: no milk was watered and no cream was stolen at Ostrey Hollow; a short pipette explained the entire shortfall. The Ashlin Chronicle covered it 3 October 1958; the county agent's 1959 report and a 1959 pamphlet footnote republicized it. Ansel Keddie, dead 24 years and dismissed 33 years earlier, was formally cleared; Orra did not live to see it.

**When:** crate found February 1958; measured 9 March 1958; bulletin published October 1958.

**Quantities:** mean delivery 16.72 c.c.; shortfall 5%.

**Documents:** Bulletin No. 471; Ivy Keddie's signed measurement sheet; the Ashlin Chronicle article; Rosalie Cudd's 1958 letter; Halbert Nym's 1972 catalogue. (289 words)

# Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Her father, Duncan Keddie (born 1894), was Ansel and Orra's son; Duncan states in his 1968 family notes that Ivy, born 1931, "makes her my father's granddaughter," and adds that he has "already twice seen it printed otherwise." Ivy herself, in her 1959 talk, calls Ansel "my grandfather." Against this, two retellings say something else: the county agent Orin Falke's 1959 report first calls her Ansel's "great-niece," then explicitly corrects itself in a note added at the end ("I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine") — so this source resolves itself. The Ashlin Chronicle (1958) also says "great-niece" and never corrects it. Both errors most likely trace to the same college press release Falke and the Chronicle each drew on, which makes them one shared error, not two independent ones. Weighed against direct, first-person family testimony (Duncan, Ivy herself) and a source's own explicit self-correction, granddaughter is correct.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**

Second cousins. Duncan Keddie's 1968 notes lay out the chain explicitly: Emmet Brant and Orra (Ansel's wife) were brother and sister; Emmet's son Roy Brant and Orra's son Duncan are therefore first cousins (children of a brother and sister); Roy's daughter Hazel and Duncan's daughter Ivy are second cousins (children of first cousins) — "not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different generation, and not aunt and niece." Hazel Brant's own 1981 account states the identical relationship and identical reasoning independently. Two other retellings get it wrong: Vira Toland calls Ivy "Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed," and Cleve Oram repeats the same wrong label — likely one shared piece of loose valley talk rather than two independent errors, since both are secondhand community recollections rather than family members reasoning through the actual generations. The sources leave nothing open in the intervening generations here: the full chain (Emmet/Orra → Roy/Duncan → Hazel/Ivy) is stated plainly and consistently by the two people with direct standing to know it.

**B3. Selby Vose's father, and its bearing on the case.**

Selby Vose was Gideon Vose's son, not his nephew. This is stated directly and corrected explicitly by two independent, well-placed sources: Duncan Keddie ("Selby Vose... was Gideon Vose's son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew") and clerk Bertram Kyle, who worked in the office under Selby Vose for twenty-five years ("the son, whatever is said at Ashlin... I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense"). Effie Loomis's own institutional notes agree ("Selby Vose, son of the late president, was made manager in 1913"). Only Vira Toland's oral account calls him "nephew." Three sources, one of them the Association's own secretary and one its own clerk, against one secondhand recollection: son is correct. It matters because Gideon Vose personally wrote Article VII — the rule making a station's own test final and unappealable — and his son Selby, as manager, was the officer who both signed the station's ledgers every year and who advanced the water-theft theory against the very patrons that rule left without recourse. The father-son line concentrates authorship of the rule and enforcement of its consequences in one family across two generations.

**B4. The Vose family and the Keddie family.**

No relationship, on the best evidence. Duncan Keddie's 1968 notes address this directly and reject it: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses... I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins... It is talk... My mother would have told me... and she never once put a Vose in it." Against this stands a persistent but vague valley rumor, repeated by Vira Toland ("Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins. Everybody knew that") and by Cleve Oram ("The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort... I could not draw you the line on paper"). Both of these are unattributed community talk, admitted by their own tellers to rest on hearsay rather than a traceable line of descent, and Cleve Oram's own qualifier concedes as much. Duncan Keddie's account is a direct family member's flat denial, backed by his claim that his own mother — a woman "who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection" — never once made this claim. I treat the family denial as the better-supported answer, while noting the rumor's persistence across two unrelated informants is itself part of the story: it was the rumor, true or not, that gave the 1919 circular its extra sting in local memory, since it made the accusation feel like one cousin informing on another.
