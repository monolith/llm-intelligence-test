## Section A — Reconstruction (first three of eight stories, in chronological order)

### 1. The Founding, the Station, and the Fatal Purchase (1889–1897)

In 1889, valley farmers weary of hauling their butter all the way to Ashlin met at the Larrow Green
schoolhouse and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. They elected Gideon
Vose — "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the law and thought better of it" —
as first president; he wrote the Association's by-laws himself, at his own kitchen table. This
comes from Dell Rundle's 1939/1959 jubilee pamphlet, "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years," the
main secondary history of the founding.

In 1891 the Association built its creamery at Larrow Green, on the creek below the mill, and began
paying the valley in cash monthly for the first time. Rundle, quoting the text directly, dates
Article VII of the by-laws to that same year: every patron would be paid according to the butterfat
test of the station where he delivered, not the weight of his milk — later called "the hinge of the
whole matter." A single secondhand 1919 letter suggests 1893 instead, but only as a vague
recollected impression ("as I have always understood it"), so the 1891 dating, resting on a quoted
document, is preferred.

On 4 May 1896 — the most precise date, from county agent Orin Falke's 1959 report, loosely matched
by buttermaker Hobart Sill's own 1934 recollection of "May 1896" — the Association opened a second
station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley (three independent narrators agree on "seven"),
serving the Hessel Bottom farms. Ansel Keddie was appointed tester and separator man there, a post
he held for 29 unbroken seasons, to 1925. The station opened with no testing glass of its own; Sill
lent his own outfit from Larrow Green for its first season.

The station's own glassware arrived on 1 May 1897 — the best-supported of four rival dates (1897,
1898, 1899, 1922), corroborated by a supplier's invoice, that supplier's own company history, and a
tester's-wife's diary. The invoice, dated 14 April 1897, itemizes six dozen milk-test pipettes,
twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, and a hand tester; the line items total $77.93, though
the supplier's own prose gives $77.39 — an unresolved discrepancy inside that one source. This
unremarkable purchase turned out to be the origin of a defect that went undetected for the next 26
years.

### 2. Wet Hay and the Committee of 1908 (1902–1908)

From 1902 — independently confirmed by four sources, including a contemporaneous diary entry —
Hessel Bottom farmers delivering to Ostrey Hollow began complaining that their milk tested lower
there than the same herds tested at Larrow Green. Management did not believe them.

One such patron, Emmet Brant, kept his own private weigh-book, started either 1 January 1900 (per
his granddaughter Hazel Brant, speaking in 1981) or 1 January 1897 (per his nephew Duncan Keddie,
writing in 1968); the three-year gap between these two family members' accounts is not resolved by
the sources.

In January 1908 the Association appointed a committee, chaired by Nyle Grigg, to investigate. Over
that winter the committee visited each of Hessel Bottom's six farms three or four times, drew and
sealed their own milk samples, watched milking, hauling, and testing firsthand, and checked both
the station's and the patrons' scales against a known weight. They found nothing wrong with the
testing itself. Instead they blamed Hessel Bottom's hay — cut late off wet, sedge-choked meadowland
along the creek and fed all winter — reasoning that "a cow cannot put into her pail what her keep
has never put into her." Their explicit finding: "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter." The
report was accepted and the matter considered closed for eleven years, until 1919.

This good-faith wet-hay verdict would eventually be overturned — but not until 1959, when the
county agricultural agent tracked down the actual text of the scientific bulletin the committee's
1919 revival had leaned on, and found it supported nowhere near the claimed effect. That reversal
belongs to a later story; what matters here is that in 1908 the shortfall was investigated
honestly, thoroughly, and — as it later turned out — wrongly explained.

### 3. Nine Weeks with Borrowed Glass (1911)

On 8 May 1911, the Ostrey Hollow station's own measuring glass broke. For nine weeks, until 10 July
1911, tester Ansel Keddie used a measure borrowed from elsewhere to test milk from the same Hessel
Bottom herds he always tested.

Through those nine weeks, the station's recorded butterfat tests rose markedly above their usual
level. The day the station went back to its own glass, the readings fell straight back down. Keddie
noticed the pattern at the time, wrote it into his test book, and set a question mark against it —
but nothing came of it then.

This episode is confirmed independently by two very differently sourced accounts: Orin Falke's 1959
report for the county board, drawing on the 1958 college bulletin prepared by Professor Leland
Vaught and graduate student Ivy Keddie, and the contemporaneous diary of Orra Keddie, the tester's
own wife, who records the same nine-week window as it happened.

Its significance would only become clear decades later. Falke's 1959 report treats 1911 as the
single most decisive piece of evidence in the whole matter: same tester, same herds, same season
and supervision — only the instrument changed, and the readings changed with it. That rules out
both rival theories argued over the years — water added to the milk, and the tester manipulating
figures — and points squarely at the glassware itself. But at the time it was just a curiosity,
forgotten for 47 more years until Ivy Keddie found the condemned glass, still crated, in the
station loft in February 1958.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie: granddaughter — reworked from round 0.**

Round 0 gave the same word but derived it from only one thread; here are two, which is likely what
was missing. (1) Direct: Orin Falke's 1959 county report first calls Ivy Keddie "the great-niece of
the tester" (Ansel Keddie, who ran Ostrey Hollow for 29 seasons), then appends a note after the
report had gone to type: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter
and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine" — a named, dated self-correction, which
outranks the error it replaces. (2) Circumstantial but independently grounded: Ansel's wife, Orra
Keddie, writes in her own contemporaneous diary (January 1925) that Emmet Brant is "my brother" —
so Orra was born a Brant. Duncan Keddie (1968) independently identifies himself as "Emmet Brant's
sister's son," i.e., Orra's son — which makes him Ansel Keddie's son, the only child of Ansel's the
sources ever name. That gives a real, documented Keddie son through whom a granddaughter of Ansel's
could exist, exactly as Falke's correction describes Ivy. The sources never state outright that Ivy
is specifically Duncan's daughter, so this second line is corroborative rather than conclusive — but
it independently supports "granddaughter" over "great-niece," rather than resting on Falke's
say-so alone. I'm holding this with more caution than round 0's "about as strong as this material
gets," since that exact conclusion was checked and marked wrong; this is the best answer the notes
support, not a certainty.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant: most likely second cousins — reworked from round
0's overhedged "probably some cousin, maybe second."**

The chain, now mostly documented rather than speculative: Orra Keddie (Ansel's wife) is confirmed,
in her own diary, to be Emmet Brant's sister. Duncan Keddie, "Emmet Brant's sister's son," is
therefore Orra's son and (per B1) Ansel's son — making him the first cousin of Roy Brant, Emmet's
son. Hazel Brant is Roy's daughter. If Ivy Keddie (Ansel's granddaughter, per B1) is Duncan's
daughter, she and Hazel are children of first cousins — which makes them second cousins.

What's confirmed: the sibling link (Orra/Emmet) and the first-cousin link (Duncan/Roy). What's not:
no source states outright that Ivy is Duncan's specific daughter rather than a child of some other,
unmentioned child of Ansel's — Duncan is simply the only child of Ansel's the sources name, so he's
the best candidate by elimination, not by direct statement. **Second cousin is the best-supported
answer, resting on one confirmed generation-jump (the sibling tie) and one reasonable but unstated
assumption (Ivy = Duncan's daughter)** — genuinely stronger ground than round 0 had, but short of
proof.

**B3. Selby Vose's father: Gideon Vose — unchanged, confirmed correct on review.**

Three independent, direct or corrective sources say "son" and explicitly reject a circulating
"nephew" story: Selby Vose's own words ("My father, who was your first president..."), Duncan
Keddie's explicit 1968 correction, and clerk Bertram Kyle's 1953 account ("the son, whatever is
said at Ashlin... I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense"). Only
Dell Rundle, writing secondhand, says nephew. This matters to Ostrey Hollow because Selby Vose
became the Association's manager in 1911 and held the post about 25 years — through the Hollow's
complaints, the 1919 circular he personally signed accusing Hollow patrons of watering milk, and the
1925 arbitration. Being the founder's actual son, not a more distant relation, plausibly explains
both his unquestioned standing in the office and Kyle's observation that he "never read the lines
above" the bottom figure before signing — the kind of inherited, unchallenged authority under which
a multi-decade shortfall went unnoticed.

**B4. Relationship between the Vose and Keddie families: none the sources establish by blood or
marriage — an institutional link only. Re-examined, unchanged.**

Gideon Vose founded the Association and wrote Article VII, which made a station's own test the
basis of payment; his son Selby Vose managed the Association from 1911 and was Ansel Keddie's
ultimate superior — he signed the 1919 circular accusing Keddie's own patrons, and it was under his
management that Keddie was dismissed in 1925. Everything the notes newly established about the
Keddie family (the Orra–Emmet Brant sibling tie, Duncan Keddie's parentage) stays entirely inside
the Keddie/Brant side and touches no Vose. The two families connect only through the
employer/tester relationship at one station; whether any personal tie existed beyond that cannot be
determined from the sources.

## Section C — Math (C1 and C2)

**C1(a). Proportion: about 5%, most precisely 5¼% (5.25%) — unchanged, arithmetic re-verified.**

Two independent routes converge. Bookkeeping: clerk Bertram Kyle reports the Ostrey Hollow account
carried, every year, the gap between butterfat credited to patrons on the station test and
butterfat actually received in cream at Larrow Green, entered as "station gain" with the same
annual note: "five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account." Glassware
math: the bulletin's own figures give a recorded average of 3.61 against a deficiency of 0.19
point, so 0.19 ÷ 3.61 = 0.0526 → **5.26%**, matching Kyle's bookkeeping figure to within a
hundredth of a point from a completely different angle. Why one flat percentage covers 26 years:
the 1958 measurement found the surviving pipettes "delivered materially less than the capacity
blown upon their stems, and in the same proportion, one to another" — a pipette short-charged by a
fixed amount reads a fixed fraction low no matter what it's measuring.

**C1(b). True average = 3.80; recorded tests were short by 0.19 point, every month, every year of
the affected period — unchanged.**

Stated directly in the bulletin as reported by Falke: recorded average 3.61, deficiency 0.19 point
→ true average 3.61 + 0.19 = **3.80**. Flag, unresolved by the sources: Larrow Green's own average
over the same years is given separately as 3.78, not 3.80 — a 0.02-point gap nothing explains. I
used the bulletin's directly-measured deficiency (0.19) rather than backing into a number from the
Larrow Green comparison, since it comes from measuring the actual defect, not from comparing two
different stations' different herds.

**C1(c). 76,000 pounds — unchanged, recomputed.**

40,000,000 lbs of milk over the affected years (Kyle, the clerk who personally compiled this figure
for the 1925 arbitration; also gives 40,000,000 ÷ 1,600,000 lbs/year = 25 years) × 0.19 ÷ 100 =
**76,000 lbs** of butterfat that should have been credited but wasn't. This differs from the
patrons' own 1925 estimate of 67,000 lbs, which was a contemporaneous reckoning made before anyone
had actually measured the glassware (that didn't happen until 1958) — two figures, two methods, two
dates, not a contradiction to resolve away.

**C1(d). Worth $19,600; patrons actually received $3,800, about 19.4% (roughly one-fifth) of it —
unchanged, recomputed.**

$19,600 is the best-attested total: it's both the patrons' 1925 petition figure "to the dollar" and
independently the Bulletin's own computed figure (a weaker "$19,000" appears in only one
contradicted source). $3,800 is the 1925 arbitration award, confirmed by five independent accounts,
paid "without admission." $3,800 ÷ $19,600 = 0.1939 → **≈19.4%**. Cross-check: 76,000 lbs (C1c) ×
Kyle's flat 25¢/lb average price = $19,000, not $19,600 — a $600 gap most plausibly because 25¢ is
a rough multi-year average ("taking the fat years with the lean"), not the bulletin's presumed
year-by-year pricing, which the sources don't give.

**C2. Cannot be determined from the notes available to me — re-checked, (a) confirmed still
unanswerable from this evidence, with the earlier "wrong" mark noted.**

The reviewer flagged C2(a) as wrong, so I went back through the full notes a second time hunting
specifically for the string "1910," for any named "largest patron," or for any patron-level
credited-butterfat figure for any year. None of these appear anywhere in the material I have. The
only individual patron ever singled out with anything like a personal record is Emmet Brant, whose
private weigh-book is mentioned as evidence entered at the 1925 arbitration — but it's never quoted

station. The sources establish only station-wide aggregates (40,000,000 lbs over 25 years, recorded
average 3.61 vs. true 3.80, ~25¢/lb average price) — nothing at the level of a single patron in a
single year.

Given that, I can't produce (a), (b), or (c): without a named largest patron and his actual 1910
volume, any number here would be arithmetic performed on a made-up premise, which the instructions
for this test explicitly score worse than declining. My best guess at what happened: the real
underlying story material almost certainly does name this patron and his 1910 figures somewhere,
but that detail didn't survive into the ingest notes I'm restricted to using for this task — that's
a gap in the evidence trail available to me, not a claim that the question is unanswerable in
principle.
