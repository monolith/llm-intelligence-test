# Batch 1 Answers — Larrow Valley / Ostrey Hollow

Method note: where sources conflict I favored (in rough order) a quoted primary document over a
paraphrase, a contemporaneous diary over later recollection, a named/dated self-correction over
the original error, and convergence of multiple *independent* accounts over a lone outlier — never
raw nose-count, since repeats from the same origin are one source. Inferences are flagged as such.

---

## Section A — Reconstruction (first three of eight stories, in chronological order)

### 1. The Founding, the Station, and the Fatal Purchase (1889–1897)

In 1889, valley farmers, weary of hauling their butter all the way to Ashlin, met at the Larrow
Green schoolhouse and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. They elected
Gideon Vose — "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the law and thought better of
it" — as first president; he wrote the Association's by-laws himself, at his own kitchen table.
This comes from Dell Rundle's 1939/1959 jubilee pamphlet, "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty
Years," the main secondary history of the founding.

In 1891 the Association built its creamery at Larrow Green, on the creek below the mill, and paid
the valley in cash monthly for the first time. That same year (per Rundle, who quotes the text
directly — preferred over a rival 1893 date given only as a vague secondhand "impression" in a
1919 letter) the Association adopted Article VII: patrons would be paid by the butterfat test of
the station where they delivered, not by the weight of milk. That sentence would later be called
"the hinge of the whole matter."

On 4 May 1896 (the most precise date, from Orin Falke's 1959 county report, independently matched
by "May 1896" in buttermaker Hobart Sill's 1934 retirement remarks), the Association opened a
second station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley (three independent sources agree on
"seven"), serving the Hessel Bottom farms. Ansel Keddie was appointed tester and separator man
there — a post he held for 29 unbroken seasons, to 1925. The station opened with no glassware of
its own; Sill lent his own testing equipment from Larrow Green for the first season.

The station's own outfit arrived 1 May 1897 — the best-supported of four competing dates
(1897/1898/1899/1922), corroborated by a supplier's invoice, a company history, and a tester's
wife's diary. Ammon & Sons' invoice, dated 14 April 1897, itemizes 6 dozen milk-test pipettes, 12
dozen test bottles, an acid measure, and a hand tester, totaling either $77.93 (summing the line
items) or $77.39 (the supplier's own prose figure — an unresolved discrepancy within that one
source). This unremarkable purchase is the origin of a defect that went undetected for 26 years.

### 2. Wet Hay and the Committee of 1908 (1902–1908)

From 1902 (independently confirmed by four sources, including a contemporaneous 1902 diary entry),
Hessel Bottom farmers delivering to Ostrey Hollow began complaining that their milk tested lower
there than the same herds' milk tested at Larrow Green. Management did not believe them.

One such patron, Emmet Brant, began keeping his own private weigh-book — starting either 1 January
1900 (per his granddaughter Hazel Brant, speaking in 1981) or 1 January 1897 (per his nephew Duncan
Keddie, writing in 1968); the three-year gap between these two family members is not resolved by
the sources.

In January 1908 the Association appointed a committee chaired by Nyle Grigg to investigate. Over
that winter the committee visited each of Hessel Bottom's six farms three or four times, drew and
sealed their own milk samples, watched milking, hauling, and testing firsthand, and checked both
the station's and the patrons' scales against a known weight. They found nothing wrong with the
testing process. Instead they blamed the shortfall on Hessel Bottom's hay — cut late off wet,
sedge-choked meadowland along the creek and fed all winter — reasoning "a cow cannot put into her
pail what her keep has never put into her." Explicit finding: "no dishonesty of any kind, in any
quarter." The report was accepted and the matter considered closed for eleven years, until 1919.

This wet-hay verdict, reached in good faith, would eventually be overturned — but not until 1959,
when the county agricultural agent obtained the actual text of the scientific bulletin the
committee had come to rely on and found it didn't support anywhere near the claimed effect. That
reversal belongs to a later story.

### 3. Nine Weeks with Borrowed Glass (1911)

On 8 May 1911, the Ostrey Hollow station's own measuring glass broke. For nine weeks, until 10 July
1911, tester Ansel Keddie used a measure borrowed from elsewhere to test milk from the same Hessel
Bottom herds he always tested.

Through those nine weeks, the station's recorded butterfat tests rose markedly above their usual
level. The day the station returned to its own glass, the readings fell straight back down.
Keddie noticed the pattern at the time, wrote it into his test book, and set a question mark
against it — but nothing came of it then.

This episode is independently confirmed by two very differently sourced accounts: Orin Falke's
1959 report for the county board, drawing on the 1958 college bulletin, and the contemporaneous
diary of Orra Keddie, the tester's own wife, who records the same nine-week window as it happened.

Its significance would only become clear decades later. Falke's 1959 report treats 1911 as the
single most decisive piece of evidence in the whole matter: same tester, same herds, same season
and supervision — only the instrument changed, and the readings changed with it. That rules out
both rival theories argued over the years (water added to the milk, and the tester manipulating
figures) and points squarely at the glassware. But at the time it was just a curiosity, forgotten
for 47 more years until Ivy Keddie found the condemned glass crated in the station loft in
February 1958.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1.** Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie: **granddaughter.** This is well established.
Orin Falke's 1959 report for the county board first calls Ivy Keddie (the graduate student who,
with Professor Leland Vaught, co-authored the 1958 college bulletin) the tester's "great-niece,"
but Falke appends a note after the report had gone to type: "I am informed, and have verified,
that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." A
named author explicitly correcting and verifying his own error, in the same document, dated 1959,
is about as strong as this material gets.

**B2.** Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant: **probably some degree of cousin, most likely a
second cousin — but the exact link is not fully determinable.** What's solid: Hazel Brant is Emmet
Brant's granddaughter through his son Roy Brant. Duncan Keddie (writing 1968) is Emmet Brant's
sister's son and states he is Roy Brant's first cousin — meaning Emmet's sister married a Keddie
man. Separately, the notes on the 1908 committee's rounds describe the tester's wife, Orra Keddie,
as coming from "her own family, the Brants." *If* Orra is that same sister of Emmet's, Duncan
Keddie would be Ansel and Orra's son, and Ivy Keddie (Ansel's granddaughter, per B1) could descend
through that line — making her Hazel Brant's second cousin. But no source states outright that
Orra is specifically Emmet's sister (Hessel Bottom held six farms' worth of Brants, so she could be
from another branch), and no source names which of Ansel's children is Ivy's parent. The
intervening generations — exactly how Ivy descends from Ansel, and exactly how Orra relates to
Emmet — are left open by the sources; this is my inference from what's consistent, not a
documented fact.

**B3.** Selby Vose's father: **Gideon Vose**, the Association's founding president — not, as one
source claims, his uncle. Three independent, direct or corrective sources say "son" and explicitly
reject the nephew story as a known error in circulation: Selby Vose's own words ("My father, who
was your first president..."), Duncan Keddie's explicit 1968 correction, and Bertram Kyle's 1953
account ("the son, whatever is said at Ashlin... I have heard the nephew story twice in the last
year and it is nonsense"). Only Dell Rundle, writing secondhand, says nephew. This matters to
Ostrey Hollow because Selby Vose became the Association's manager in 1911 and held the office about
25 years — through the Hollow's complaints, the 1919 accusation circular he personally signed, and
the 1925 arbitration. Being the founder's actual son, not a more distant relation, plausibly
explains both his unquestioned authority in the office and clerk Kyle's observation that he "never
read the lines above" the bottom figure before signing — the kind of inherited, comfortable
authority under which a 5%-plus shortfall went unnoticed for decades.

**B4.** Relationship between the Vose and Keddie families: **none that the sources establish by
blood or marriage — only an institutional one.** Gideon Vose founded the Association and wrote the
by-law (Article VII) making a station's own test the basis of payment; his son Selby Vose managed
the Association from 1911 and was Ansel Keddie's ultimate superior — he signed the 1919 circular
accusing Keddie's own patrons of watering milk, and it was under his management that Keddie was
dismissed in 1925. The two families are linked only through the employer-tester relationship at
one station. Whether any personal or family tie existed beyond that cannot be determined from the
sources.

## Section C — Math (C1 and C2)

**C1(a).** Proportion: **about 5%, most precisely 5¼% (5.25%).** The most direct evidence is
bookkeeping, not the glassware itself: clerk Bertram Kyle reports that every year the bookkeeper
entered the gap between butterfat credited to patrons on the Ostrey Hollow test and butterfat
actually received in cream at Larrow Green as "station gain," followed by the same note every
year: "five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account." Independently, the
1958 college bulletin's own figures — a 0.19-point deficiency against a recorded average of
3.61 — give 0.19 ÷ 3.61 ≈ 5.3%, closely matching Kyle's bookkeeping figure from a completely
different angle. From the glassware: the 1958 measurement found the surviving pipettes "delivered
materially less than the capacity blown upon their stems, and in the same proportion, one to
another" — a short-charged pipette read as full reads short by a constant fraction, which is
exactly why one fixed percentage covers the whole 26 years of error.

**C1(b).** True average = **3.80**; recorded tests were short by **0.19 point**, every month of
the year, every year of the affected period (recorded average 3.61 + 0.19 deficiency = 3.80),
per the 1958 college bulletin as reported by Falke. Flag: Larrow Green's own average over the same
years is separately given as 3.78, not 3.80 — a 0.02-point gap the sources never explain. I used
the bulletin's directly-measured 0.19-point deficiency as the more authoritative number, since it
comes from measuring the actual defect rather than comparing two different stations' herds.

**C1(c).** **76,000 pounds.** Working: total milk intake at Ostrey Hollow over the affected years
is the best-attested 40,000,000 lbs (Kyle, the clerk who personally compiled this figure for the
1925 arbitration; also gives the window as 40,000,000 ÷ 1,600,000 lbs/year = 25 years). Apply the
0.19-point deficiency: 40,000,000 × (0.19 ÷ 100) = **76,000 lbs** of butterfat that should have
been credited but wasn't. This differs from the patrons' own 1925 estimate of 67,000 lbs, which was
their contemporaneous reckoning at arbitration time, made before anyone had actually measured the
glassware's defect (that didn't happen until 1958) — two figures, two methods, two dates.

**C1(d).** Worth: **$19,600** — Bulletin 471's own computed figure, which independently matches
the patrons' 1925 petition amount exactly (a competing "$19,000" figure appears in only one weaker
source and looks like rounding/transcription error against this). Patrons actually received
**$3,800** — the 1925 arbitration award, confirmed by five independent sources, "without admission"
of wrongdoing. $3,800 ÷ $19,600 ≈ **19.4%**, roughly one-fifth of what they were shorted. Flag:
using the 76,000-lb figure from C1(c) at Kyle's flat 25-cent average price gives 76,000 × $0.25 =
$19,000, not $19,600 — a $600 gap the sources don't explain, most likely because 25 cents is only a
rough multi-year average (Kyle himself says "taking the fat years with the lean") rather than the
bulletin's presumed year-by-year pricing, which isn't given in the sources I have.

**C2.** **Cannot be determined from the sources retained.** Nothing in the material passed down to
me identifies who Ostrey Hollow's largest patron was, or gives that patron's individual 1910
credited-butterfat figure. The sources establish only aggregate figures for the whole 25-year,
40,000,000-lb window (recorded average 3.61 vs. true 3.80, ~25-cent average butterfat price) and
one individual patron's record — Emmet Brant's private weigh-book — mentioned as arbitration
evidence but never quoted for any single year, including 1910. Without a named largest patron and
his specific 1910 volume, (a), (b), and (c) can't be computed; any number offered here would be a
guess dressed as arithmetic, which the instructions for this test specifically score worse than
declining.
