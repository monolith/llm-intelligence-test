The material is 24 retellings (r01–r24) of eight original underlying stories centered on the

### MAJOR SOURCE — Retelling 12, Dell Rundle, "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years" (jubilee
### pamphlet, Larrow Green, 1939, reprinted 1959)

- **1889 — founding:** farmers met at Larrow Green schoolhouse, organized the Larrow Valley
  Cooperative Creamery Association, tired of hauling butter to Ashlin. Anecdote: meeting ran late,
  a horse got loose.
- **Gideon Vose elected first president** — "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of
  the law and thought better of it." Wrote the by-laws himself at his own kitchen table.
- **SELBY VOSE'S RELATION TO GIDEON VOSE — RESOLVED in favor of "son."** Rundle (r12, secondhand)
  calls Selby Vose Gideon's "nephew," quoting Selby's own habitual phrase ("his uncle"). This
  contradicts r08 (Selby Vose's own first-person words: "My father, who was your first
  president..."), r18 (Duncan Keddie, 1968, explicit named correction: "Selby Vose... was Gideon
  Vose's son... not, as I have heard it given, his nephew"), and r23 (Bertram Kyle, clerk, 1953):
  "He was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose... — the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the
  nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense." Three independent, named,
  direct-or-corrective sources say "son" and explicitly reject "nephew" as a known circulating
  error; only Rundle (secondhand) says "nephew." **"Son" is very firmly the better-supported
  answer for B3.**
- **1891 — creamery built** at Larrow Green, on the creek below the mill; churn set up; valley paid
  in cash monthly for the first time.
- **Article VII, quoted in full:** *"Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according
  to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to
  the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."* Dated
  **1891** by Rundle (same year as the churn), called "the sentence on which the whole trouble at
  the head of the valley afterward turned." **CONFLICT (r15, Nyle Grigg's 1919 letter):** "Payment
  by test came in with the 1893 meeting, as I have always understood it" — thirdhand, explicitly
  flagged uncertain by Grigg himself. 1891 (quoting the article text) preferred over 1893
  (secondhand impression), not by an overwhelming margin.
- **Ostrey Hollow station, opened 1896:** seven miles up from the Green (r13, r14, r21/Cleve Oram
  all independently confirm "seven miles"; road "climbs the whole seven," bad pitch about two miles
  below the hamlet). Pull-down three hours in mud, impossible in March. Milk weighed, tested,
  separated on the spot; cream hauled to the Green; skim went home same morning. Building: plain,
  boarded/battened, intake room at road end, loft above (never cleared out since built); creek
  behind it has twice come over the sill in wet April.
  - r14 (Hobart Sill, buttermaker, 1934 retirement remarks): station opened **May 1896**. At
    opening it had no glassware of its own; Sill made up a temporary testing outfit from his own
    glass at Larrow Green; station used it through its first season. **Station's own equipment
    arrived "the spring after" (spring 1897)**, Sill's glass returned (one piece chipped).
  - **r20 (Junia Ammon, "Sixty Years of Ammon & Sons," 1949, ch. IX)** — supplying firm's own
    history, reproduces an invoice dated **14 April 1897** for the Ostrey Hollow outfit: 6 doz.
    milk test pipettes at 17.6 c.c. ($25.20), 12 doz. test bottles 8% neck ($31.68), 1 acid measure
    ($0.90), 1 hand tester 4-bottle ($18.00), freight ($2.15). Line items sum to **$77.93**, but
    the prose text states **$77.39** (matching r19's figure) — an unresolved internal arithmetic
    inconsistency in r20 itself (likely digit transposition). Ammon's history states the
    Association "put that outfit into use at its Ostrey Hollow station on the first of May, 1897."
    **NOTABLE INTERNAL CONTRADICTION IN r20:** elsewhere in the same chapter Ammon claims "The
    house sold nothing into Ordell County before 1900," yet reproduces this 1897 invoice into that
    same county — unresolved, unflagged inconsistency.
  - **1923 condemnation per r20's own letter-book:** state inspector tested pipettes with a
    burette in "the summer of 1923," condemned the lot. Association's order to Ammon & Sons dated
    **28 June 1923**; Ammon's reply dated **2 July 1923**, quoted in full: *"Your order of the 28th
    ultimo for six dozen milk pipettes and six dozen test bottles is entered and will go forward
    this month. We regret the trouble. Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you
    anything about the lot you name."* Ammon's own speculation (labeled as such): "the mold had
    been reground and the lot ran short... consistently wrong, which is the one fault a check of
    that kind cannot find."
  - **r17 (Hazel Brant, 1981 tape)** gives a third date for the glass entering service: "ninety-
    nine" (1899) — contradicted, secondhand family lore, 80+ years after the fact; Hazel disclaims

  - **r23 (Bertram Kyle, 1953)** gives a FOURTH date: station's own outfit "went into use on the

  - Committee "appointed in January last" (January 1908).

  - Explicit finding: **"no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter."**

  - **1919 covering letter:** reopened "by men who were boys when we made it." Grigg cites State

### r22 — Orin Falke, county agricultural agent, "The Ostrey Hollow question: a report for the
### county board," 1959 (Ordell County)

Prepared for the board of supervisors, numbered paragraphs, with a note appended after the report
"had gone to type," on receipt of Bulletin No. 214 itself from the State Agricultural College
library.

- References the actual 1958 college bulletin, prepared jointly by **Professor Leland Vaught**
  (dairy husbandry dept., Farland) and **Miss Ivy Keddie**, graduate student — first called (in the
  body of the report) "the great-niece of the tester," but **CORRECTED in Falke's own appended
  note: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the
  great-niece; the error on page two is mine."** This is a self-corrected, verified, named, dated
  (1959) statement — the strongest direct evidence for **B1: Ivy Keddie is Ansel Keddie's
  granddaughter.**
- Association organized 1889 at Larrow Green; creamery built there 1891; Article VII paraphrased
  (paid by test, station's own test governs — "the hinge of the whole matter").
  **By-laws carry no date of adoption on their face** ("adopted at the annual meeting" only); the
  Association's early minute book was **lost in an office removal** — Falke explicitly states his
  office cannot determine the adoption year and does not attempt to. (Relevant to the Article
  VII/1891-vs-1893 date conflict noted above — an independent reason the exact adoption date may
  never be recoverable from documents.)
- Ostrey Hollow station built winter 1895–96, **opened 4 May 1896** (most precise date yet; matches
  "May 1896" from r14). Seven miles up the valley, serving Hessel Bottom principally.
  **The tester and separator man from the opening was Ansel Keddie, who held the post for
  twenty-nine seasons.** (1896 + 29 = 1925 — matches his dismissal date exactly; internally
  consistent with r24's diary, which independently states the same "twenty-nine seasons.")
- **Station test averages, from the bulletin:** Ostrey Hollow's average over the long period of
  complaint = **3.61**; Larrow Green's own average over the same years = **3.78** ("an ordinary
  figure for this county"). **Deficiency = 0.19 point, constant in every month of the year** (same
  in June and February alike). First complaint of record: **1902**; renewed every decade after.
  [Note for Section C: 3.61 + 0.19 = 3.80, close to but not identical to Larrow Green's 3.78 — a
  minor internal wrinkle worth flagging if used for C1(b).]
- **The 1911 borrowed-glass natural experiment (independently and precisely dated by r24's diary
  too):** "On the 8th of May the [1911] station's own measuring glass was broken, and a measure
  borrowed from elsewhere was used in its place for nine weeks, until the 10th of July. Through
  those nine weeks the station's tests stood markedly higher. On the day the station resumed its
  own glass they fell back to their former level. The tester noted the fact in his book and set a
  question mark against it." This is treated by the bulletin as decisive evidence against both
  alternative theories (water adulteration and tester dishonesty), since the same tester, same
  herds, same season — only the measuring instrument changed — and the readings changed with it.
- **Three explanations argued before the 1925 arbitration board, and Falke's own assessment of
  each:**
  1. **Water added to milk** — the Association manager's own view (per his 1919 circular). **Not
     sustained**: "The state dairy inspector for this circuit tested with the lactometer at every
     visit from 1908 forward and never once found added water at that station."
  2. **Tester stealing cream / falsifying low tests** — urged at the hearing by "an officer of the
     Association." **Not sustained**: disposed of by the 1911 borrowed-glass episode (same tester,
     no different supervision, yet figures rose when using different glass).
  3. **Wet hay/pasture of Hessel Bottom thinning the cream** — 1908 committee report, PLUS "a
     veterinarian of standing at Ashlin wrote to the same purpose in 1926." Falke's 1959 report
     initially treats this as "a real if partial factor" — BUT in his appended note (after
     obtaining the actual Bulletin 214 text), he explicitly **withdraws this concession**: the real
     bulletin found bog hay depressed the test by only **0.05 point (five hundredths) in winter,
     and nothing measurable in summer** — "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not
     carry nineteen hundredths in July. I withdraw the concession made on page four." This
     directly contradicts Grigg's 1919 letter (r15), which claimed Bulletin 214 found a 0.2-point
     depression — Grigg's figure is shown, by the actual bulletin text, to be wrong (whether
     through misremembering, exaggeration, or a different reading). **The wet-hay theory is
     therefore authoritatively refuted by the primary source itself**, notwithstanding the sincere,
     good-faith 1908 committee finding and the 1926 veterinarian's letter (neither of which had, or
     used, the actual bulletin figures).
- **1923 events precisely dated:** 12 June 1923, state inspector on ordinary circuit visit ran the
  pipettes against a calibrated burette, found they would not deliver their mark, condemned the
  whole lot on the spot, directed the manager to order new. **New glass in use from "the following
  month"** (i.e., July 1923). **From 1924 the station's average test stood at 3.85 and remained
  there** — a precise recovery figure. "No person connected the one thing with the other at the
  time, and the inspector himself drew no inference whatever as to the years behind it."
- **February 1958:** Miss Keddie found the condemned glass, still crated, in the loft over the
  intake room, where it had lain since condemnation. **9 March 1958:** she measured the nine
  surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette at Farland — "delivered materially less than the
  capacity blown upon their stems, and in the same proportion, one to another." Bulletin's
  conclusion: a short charge into a bottle neck ruled for the full charge yields a reading short in
  the same proportion; this and nothing else explains it; no milk was watered, no cream was stolen.
- Recommendations: file report with county's dairy papers; leave surviving records with the
  Historical Rooms at Ashlin; no action lies for the county, all parties dead, Association's
  account long closed.

### r23 — Bertram Kyle, clerk (ret.), "What I saw of the office," Ordell County Historical Society,
### 1953

Written at 68 by the man who sat at the outer desk in the Cooperative's Larrow Green office for 25
years, "outside the manager's door."

- **Every year, the Ostrey Hollow station account carried three figures and a sentence:** pounds
  of butterfat credited to patrons on the station test; pounds of butterfat received at Larrow
  Green in the station cream; the difference, entered as "station gain," followed always by the
  bookkeeper's own words: **"five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general
  account."** Kyle: "It never once crossed my mind to wonder at it... A figure with a name on it has
  been explained, and you rule under it and go on to the next." **This "5¼%" is a directly quoted,
  named, recurring bookkeeping figure for the proportional shortfall at Ostrey Hollow — closely
  matching the ~5% implied independently by r22's deficiency figures (0.19/3.80 ≈ 5%) — two
  independently derived cross-checks converging near 5%.** Likely candidate answer for C1(a).
- Station's testing glass: borrowed for "its first season or two," own outfit "went into use on the
  first of May, 1898" (per Kyle, from correspondence he handled later) — a contradicted, outlier
  date versus the well-established 1897 (see above; corroborated by r24's diary too).
- First season's intake (oral tradition, not documentary): "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds."
- **"Forty million pounds of milk received at that station, which is sixteen hundred thousand
  pounds a year, year in and year out" — Kyle personally compiled this figure for the 1925
  arbitration** ("the figure was got out for the arbitration and I got it out"). Implies a **25-year
  affected window** (40,000,000 / 1,600,000 = 25).
- **"butterfat over that whole stretch averaged twenty-five cents the pound, taking the fat years
  with the lean, which was another thing I was set to find and did find."** — 25 cents/lb average
  price over the same 40,000,000 lb / 25-year window. Key data point for Section C money
  calculations.
- Selby Vose became manager in **1911**, "the son of Mr. Gideon Vose... the son, whatever is said
  at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense" (third
  independent, explicit rejection of "nephew" — see B3 above). Vose "sat behind that door for a
  quarter of a century" (~1911–1936). Kyle's characterization: courteous to every caller, but "he
  read the totals and he never read the lines above them... take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on
  the bottom figure, and sign" — explains organizationally how the shortfall went unnoticed for
  decades at the management level.
- **Wilbur Teague**, the bookkeeper, wrote the station lines "every year of his service... a dry
  man, very correct... never asked anybody anything in forty years." (Teague is the one who wrote
  the "5¼% as usual" line.)
- 1919 circular: Kyle personally typed it, confirms it warned Ostrey Hollow patrons about water,
  signed by Vose alone.
- **1922** (per Kyle) "the state man came into the office" — **inspector named "Strawn"** ("a heavy
  quiet man who wrote everything in a little book with a rubber band round it"), who had run the
  pipettes against his burette and condemned the whole lot; reorder went that week to "the supply
  house at Tarnet"; new glass came "within the month." This is a fourth, contradicted date for the
  condemnation (see 1923 discussion above) but is the source of the named inspector "Strawn" and
  the named supply-house town "Tarnet."
- **Arbitration:** patrons petitioned; board sat partly at Ashlin, partly at the station. Four
  documents entered, per Kyle (who compiled the list and "checked them off twice"): (1) the
  station's own test book, **"in Keddie's hand"** (confirms Ansel Keddie as the tester whose book
  was used); (2) the receiving book; (3) **Emmet Brant's private weigh book**, lent for the purpose,
  wanted back; (4) the state inspector's notebook for the year he condemned the glass. Kyle's own
  observation: nobody at the hearing appears to have compared item (2) against item (4) — i.e., no
  one connected the condemned-glass evidence to the historical shortfall at the time (matches r22's
