## THE CORE STORY: Ostrey Hollow testing scandal (Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery
## Association) — one of the 8 underlying stories, now the most heavily documented

### Institutional background (r02, Effie Loomis; r05, Wilbur Teague — both quote/paraphrase
official records)
- Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association organized 1889 at Larrow Green by valley
  farmers. Gideon Vose elected first president 1889, served until 1911 (22 yrs, longest-serving),
  died 1917. He personally drafted the by-laws (not a lawyer).
- Main creamery built at Larrow Green 1891, on Larrow Creek (same building still standing per r02).
- Article VII of by-laws, adopted 1892 (quoted verbatim, r02): "Every patron shall be paid for
  the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it,
  ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station
  where he delivers shall govern." Loomis calls the last clause "the hinge of the whole Ostrey
  Hollow business" — the station's own test is final, no appeal to another test/average.
- Officers named: Effie Loomis, secretary 1901–1940 (author of r02, written 1941, "Notes toward
  a history of the Association"). Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945 (author of r05, unpublished
  memoir "The Books of the Association," Ch. 9, written winter 1948, 3 yrs after retiring).
  Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green (churn book, cream receiving book). Selby Vose made
  manager 1913, served until 1938.
- Ostrey Hollow skimming station: 7 miles up-valley from Larrow Green, at the head of the Hollow.
  Took in milk from farms up there, separated it, sent cream down to Larrow Green, gave skim back
  to patrons. Best milk came off Hessel Bottom (wet meadow below the hamlet); r02 says "seven
  farms had rights on Hessel Bottom" and cut hay/pastured there; largest of these seven = Emmet
  Brant, "the station's largest patron in every year of which we have a record." r01 (Vira Toland)
  says "nine families" hauled milk to the station generally — not necessarily contradictory (9
  total patrons of the whole station vs. 7 farms specifically holding Hessel-Bottom grazing
  rights), but the two numbers never get reconciled explicitly anywhere read so far.
- Ansel Keddie ran/tested at the station: per r01 he went up in 1895 to ready the building, opened
  1896. Per Ansel's own book (quoted identically in r03 and r04): "Ostrey Hollow station. Begun
  this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie."
  (NOTE internal inconsistency: earlier in the SAME r03 letter Ansel says "The glass I began on
  in ninety-six was borrowed off the Ashlin creamery" — i.e. two different stated sources,
  Larrow Green vs. Ashlin creamery, for the 1896 borrowed glass, within one document.)
  Tested 29 seasons total, 1896 through 1924 inclusive (did not test in 1925). Dismissed
  ("put out") 30 April 1925. Died 1934 (r04).

### NEW (this segment): r06 — Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector (retired), "Extracts from
### twenty-eight circuit books," with a covering note, 1936 — found embedded in r06-long.md
### around line 3514 onward, surrounded by heavy distractor material. This is a major
### independent primary source that CORROBORATES the Ostrey Hollow exoneration story in detail.
- Strawn took the "Ordell circuit" in 1907 (~50 plants across 4 counties: creameries, cheese
  factories, skimming stations), laid it down (retired from it) in 1936 — a 29-year span. He
  kept one oilcloth-covered pocket book per year (28 books total handed to the state dairy
  office with this covering-note copy).
- His fixed 4-step routine at every plant, every visit, always in the same order: (1) check
  scales (intake scale and cream scale) against his sealed weights; (2) run the lactometer on the
  day's milk to test for added water; (3) check boiler, water supply, cold room; (4) stand at the
  tester's elbow and watch him run a test start to finish without letting him hurry.
- Explicit epistemic stance, stated twice: "I put down what I found. I did not put down what I
  thought about it... I never had a theory about a plant and I do not recommend having one."
- Year-by-year Ostrey Hollow entries (all quoted/paraphrased from his own field books):
  - **1907** (first round of the new circuit): Ostrey Hollow station "opened before my time"
    (consistent with the 1896 opening date given elsewhere). Scales true. "Their tester keeps his
    book in his own hand, and his hand is better than most... he works slow and clean and reads
    his own figure without being coaxed to it." (This is Ansel Keddie, unnamed but clearly him —
    an independent, professional, complimentary assessment of his integrity/competence as a
    tester, 11 years into his tenure.)
  - 1909: scales true, boiler in order, one man cautioned about a rusted seam.
  - 1911, June: scales true. (A routine visit; does NOT mention the 9-week broken/borrowed-pipette
    episode of May–July 1911 described in r03/r04 — silence, not a contradiction, since his visit
    may simply have fallen outside or briefly within that window without prompting comment.)
  - 1912: scales true, no added water. Cream shipped down-valley same day as separated.
  - 1913: at Larrow Green (not Ostrey Hollow this entry) — "New manager at the Association this
    year and he came out with me to the platform, which the last one never did." This is Selby
    Vose, who other sources confirm became manager in 1913.
  - 1915: scales true; wash-room floor holding water at the drain, ordered graded.
  - 1916: scales true, no added water. "The tester complains that his patrons blame him for their
    figures. I told him that is the trade and he said he knew it." (Same year, per notes-1, that
    Teague says he separately raised the test discrepancy with manager Selby Vose and was told to
    "let it alone" — two different 1916 threads: the tester being blamed by patrons, and the
    bookkeeper flagging the gap to management. Not explicitly connected in either source, but
    both dated 1916 and both about awareness of a testing problem well before the 1919 circular
    or 1925 hearing.)
  - 1917: short-handed year at plants generally; "two testers doing the work of three men, which
    is where mistakes live" (general circuit remark, not Ostrey-Hollow-specific).
  - **1919**: Ostrey Hollow. "The manager of the Association has sent a circular round to the
    patrons of this station about water in the cans. I have tested for water at this station on
    every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so." — This is an
    independent, contemporaneous, official (state inspector) statement that directly refutes the
    "water in the cans" dilution accusation described in r01/r03 as the 1919 circular signed by
    manager Selby Vose. Strengthens the case (per notes-1's 1958 exoneration) that the accusation
    was baseless from the start.
  - 1920: twice (spring and fall), scales true both times, no added water both times.
  - **1921**: "The state has begun issuing burettes to the field men this year, graduated, in a
    wooden case, for checking the delivery of measures and pipettes at the plants." Strawn's own
    burette arrived that spring with printed directions. He notes it is "a slow business" and
    most plants had never had their glass checked this way.
  - 1922: Ostrey Hollow — scales true, no added water, boiler in order, but "I did not run their
    glass against the burette this year, the day being short and the road home being what it
    was, and I have put that down because it is what happened." (An honest admission of a missed
    check — meaning the defective pipettes went unchecked one more year.)
  - **1923, June 12** (quoted as a direct extract from his field book): "Ostrey Hollow. Scales
    true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk pipettes against the
    burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of
    them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost
    them." — This is Strawn's own first-person, contemporaneous account of the exact pipette
    condemnation event that other sources (r02) describe only as "the state's inspector... in
    June 1923." Direct primary-source corroboration, with the exact date (12 June 1923).
  - 1923, October: "New glass in use since July. Ran the new pipettes against the burette. They
    deliver their mark. Scales true."
  - 1924: "Scales true, no added water, tests improved. Their book gives the year at 3.80." —
    Matches the "true" long-run average of 3.80 computed elsewhere from the pipette-defect math
    (16.72/17.6 = 0.95; 3.61/0.95 = 3.80).
  - 1925, fall: "Scales true, no added water, tests improved. New man at the tester's bench this
    year. He is quick, and quick is not the thing I look for." (The "new man" is Ansel Keddie's
    replacement after his 30 April 1925 dismissal.)
  - 1926: "Word from the state office that the burette program is discontinued from this year...
    Mine is in its case in my grip and has been used twice since." (State-level program wind-down,
    unrelated to any Ostrey Hollow specifics thereafter.)
  - 1928: Ostrey Hollow — "The loft over the intake room is full of odds and ends and I told them
    a loft over a milk room is a place for nothing but air, and was told in return that it had
    been full since before the war." — Plausibly the very loft where the condemned-pipette crate
    (stenciled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET," per notes-1's account of Ivy Keddie's 1958
    discovery) sat undisturbed for decades until Ivy found it in February 1958.
  - 1931: Larrow Green and Ostrey Hollow same week, both in order; a bad ice year across the
    circuit.
  - 1934: Ostrey Hollow — scales true, no added water; a separator bearing ran hot and was shut
    down two days, since repaired; "the tester's book is kept up, though not in the hand it used
    to be kept in" (i.e., no longer Keddie's hand — consistent with his 1934 death, per r04).
  - 1936, March (Strawn's last round before retiring): Ostrey Hollow, Larrow Green, and two
    factories over the county line, all in order, "nothing to note anywhere and nothing left to
    say about it."
  - Closing note: 28 field books (1907–1936 inclusive minus any skipped years) surrendered to the
    state dairy office along with this typed covering copy. Strawn refuses to offer any general
    "estimation" of a plant's standing, only the literal book: "I keep a book, and the book is
    open to anybody the state sends for it."

### Significance of r06 for the test questions
- r06 is an independent, official, contemporaneous documentary source (a state inspector's own
  field notes, written up in 1936 — 22 years before Ivy Keddie's 1958 talk in r04) that
  corroborates multiple load-bearing facts from the exoneration story: (1) no water was ever
  found in the cans at any inspection, including in 1919, the very year of the "water in the
  cans" circular; (2) the pipettes were condemned specifically on 12 June 1923 by Strawn himself,
  matching the "June 1923" date given secondhand in r02; (3) new glass went into use from July
  1923 and delivered its correct mark when checked in October 1923; (4) the recorded station
  average jumped to 3.80 in 1924, matching the "true" value computed from the pipette-defect
  ratio (16.72/17.6 = 0.95; 3.61/0.95 = 3.80) elsewhere in the source material.
- This means the 1958 exoneration (Bulletin No. 471, Professor Vaught, quoted by Ivy Keddie in
  r04) is now corroborated by an entirely separate, independent, contemporaneous witness (Strawn)
  whose notes were compiled in 1936, well before Ivy's 1958 research — not merely a single late
  retrospective account. This further undercuts r01 (Vira Toland)'s and r05 (Wilbur Teague)'s
  suspicion that Keddie was personally stealing cream: an inspector who watched Keddie test
  "through two tests" in 1907 and repeatedly found "scales true, no added water" over decades
  never once flagged theft or dilution — only the glass defect, discovered by the state's own
  burette program in 1923.

### The accusation and its escalation (chronology, cross-checked across r01/r02/r03/r05, and now
### r06)
- 1902: first complaints down at Larrow Green that Ostrey Hollow milk "did not test with the rest"
  (r03).
- 1908: a committee sampled herds through the winter, blamed the wet hay of the Bottom for
  "thinning the cream" — i.e., blamed the land, not the equipment or the tester (r03). This
  theory is later refuted (see 1958 findings below, and the State Agricultural College Bulletin
  No. 214 rebuttal).
- 1919: Association sent a printed circular, signed by manager Selby Vose, to nine Ostrey Hollow
  families, saying there was "water in the cans" (dilution accusation) (r01, r03 — both use the
  same phrase "water in the cans," so likely one real circular, remembered/quoted by two people).
  Devastating locally: many families kept the letter for decades (one pinned inside a trunk lid).
  Ansel Keddie's wife Orra was Emmet Brant's sister; Emmet was the largest patron and hardest-hit
  by the accusation; after the circular, Ansel/Orra and Emmet Brant never spoke again for the
  rest of their lives (confirmed independently by r01 AND by Ansel Keddie's own unsent letter in
  r03: "He and I have not spoken since the circular. That is the plainest fact in this letter.").
  Independently, r06 (Strawn) confirms he tested for water at every visit including 1919 and
  never found any, and told the manager so at the time.
- January 1925: Ostrey Hollow patrons formally petitioned the Association, saying their milk "had
  never tested as their neighbors' milk tested... for a very long time," asking to be made whole
  (r02).
- Board of arbitration sat at Ashlin under Alonzo Frick, attorney, 17 March – 4 May 1925 (dates
  agree across r02, r03, r05). Ansel Keddie gave a 9-sentence statement on 17 March 1925 (quoted
  verbatim in r03), denying ever taking a pound of cream, describing his testing method precisely
  (scale weighing, sampling into numbered jars over 10 days, warming/mixing, Babcock-style acid
  test, hand tester, reading fat column with dividers). Wilbur Teague also testified (r05),
  offering an "opportunity" theory (one man alone in the building for 29 seasons "can take a
  little and write down a little less") but explicitly NOT claiming to know what happened:
  "I do not say what any particular person did on any particular day... I was not there."
- VERDICT (quoted verbatim, identically in substance in r02 and confirmed by r05): "The board
  having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of
  the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum
  of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station
  account." NO fault was formally established against anyone.
- Ansel Keddie dismissed ("put out") 30 April 1925 anyway (r03), despite "no cause established."
- r02 also notes: a crate of milk-test measures/pipettes at the station was condemned by the
  state's inspector in June 1923, new ones ordered — and this was NEVER mentioned at the 1925
  hearing at all ("Not a word of it was spoken in any of the three sessions"). This omission is
  the pivot for the 1958 exoneration (below). r06 independently confirms and dates this exact
  event: 12 June 1923, condemned by Merle Strawn personally.

  quoted primary/official documents (secretary's minutes, the tester's own contemporaneous
  testimony, the bookkeeper's ledger) outrank a personal oral memory recorded 54+ years after the
  events and get the amount ($3,800, not $3,500) and date (1925, not 1919–20) right;
  majority-and-independent agreement (2 documentary sources with different institutional roles +
  the man's own sworn testimony) also favors $3,800/1925. r01's causal claim (tests improved
  right after Keddie was caught/dismissed in ~1919-20) is also contradicted by Ansel's own test
  book (see below): the recorded average stayed ~3.61% from 1898 clear through at least 1922 with
  no permanent rise until equipment was replaced in 1923, and r06 independently confirms the
  improvement shows up specifically in the 1924 book value of 3.80, i.e. after the June 1923
  glass replacement, not after any 1919-20 dismissal (Keddie in fact was NOT dismissed until
  1925).

### THE 1958 EXONERATION (r04, Ivy Keddie, talk to Ordell County Historical Society, 1959) — the
### single most important document for Section C math, now independently corroborated by r06
- Speaker: Ivy Keddie, explicitly identified as Ansel Keddie's GRANDDAUGHTER — "Ansel Keddie...
  was my grandfather. My father, Duncan Keddie, who taught school at Ashlin, was his son." This
  directly answers B1: Ivy is Ansel's granddaughter, via her father Duncan Keddie (Ansel's son).
- Five sources used: (1) station test book (Ansel's own, at the Historical Society's Rooms since
  "this year" [1958]); (2) Brant weigh book, kept at Hessel Bottom from 1897, lent by Roy Brant
  (Emmet Brant's son, kept up himself since his father died); (3) Alder Corners Cheese Company
  test figures, over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County, copied out by Rosalie Cudd (runs the factory,
  Jerome Cudd's niece — book itself never left Vessey County, Ivy never saw the original);
  (4) Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks (state dairy inspector for the county from 1908 [note: r06
  itself states Strawn started the circuit in 1907, a one-year discrepancy with r04's "1908" —
  minor, unresolved, likely rounding or which year is counted as "first"]), at Ivy's college
  library at Farland since 1947; (5) the Association's own annual ledgers.
- February 1958: Ivy found, in the loft of the old (closed) station building, a wooden crate
  stenciled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET," carried up there in June 1923 and untouched since.
  Inside: the station's condemned 1897 milk-test pipettes, packed in straw. Six dozen (72) bought
  new in 1897 on an invoice totaling $77.93 for the WHOLE outfit (pipettes, bottles, acid measure,
  hand tester, freight together). Nine pipettes survived unbroken.
- 9 March 1958: Ivy measured the nine pipettes at Farland against a calibrated burette. Her sheet
  (quoted verbatim): "Nine pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered
  against burette, mean of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74,
  16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c. Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark."
- MECHANISM: a Babcock-style butterfat test reads % against a charge measured to a specific mark;
  a short charge gives a proportionally short reading. 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly — EVERY test
  made with that glass read exactly 95% of the true value (a multiplicative/proportional error,
  constant regardless of season or actual richness).
- Cross-check against Ansel's own 1922 summary leaf (quoted IDENTICALLY in both r03 and r04):
  "Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61
  or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted
  in their place, when it stood at 3.80." Math: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80 exactly. 1896 used borrowed
  (non-defective) glass → true 3.80. 1898 onward used the station's own (defective) 1897 pipettes
  → false 3.61. The 9-week 1911 episode: station's own pipette broke 8 May 1911, a borrowed
  measure (from Larrow Green, "came up... with the cream wagon") was used until 10 July 1911 —
  during those 9 weeks the average read TRUE 3.80 again, then reverted to false 3.61 the day the
  defective "own" pipette came back into use. (Ansel's own entries, quoted in r03: "May 8. Pipette
  broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." / "July 10. Our own measure in use again from
  today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." — his own question mark, unanswered for 34 yrs.
  NOTE: r06/Strawn's June 1911 visit entry says only "scales true... watched a test through.
  Nothing here to note" — silent on the broken/borrowed-pipette episode, but Strawn's visit was
  early June, i.e. plausibly within the window when the borrowed [non-defective] measure was in
  use, consistent with, not contradicting, the true-reading period.)
- FINAL CONCLUSION — Bulletin No. 471, issued October 1958, wording of "Professor Vaught,"
  quoted verbatim in full by Ivy: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen
  there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low
  by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference
  — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers,
  year by year, as station gain." Defect window: 1 May 1897 – 12 June 1923 (~26 years). NOTE: the
  bulletin's condemnation date (12 June 1923) is now independently and exactly matched by r06's
  own field-book extract, "1923, June 12... Condemned the lot."
- Rebuttal of the 1908 "wet hay" theory: State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 214 (1911, quoted
  verbatim): "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more
  than five hundredths of one per cent (0.05%) in winter, and by nothing... in summer." Actual gap
  was 0.19 points, constant in every month/season — "four times too small and... the wrong shape."
  Also, Alder Corners Cheese Co. tested milk from 4 Hessel Bottom farmers in summer flush; over 14
  overlapping seasons, that SAME milk ran ~0.19 points HIGHER at Alder Corners than at home —
  confirms the defect was in the Ostrey Hollow glass, not the land.
- Both r01 (Vira Toland) and r05 (Wilbur Teague) — TWO independent narrators — believed/implied
  Keddie was personally stealing cream. This "majority" belief is REFUTED by the later quoted
  forensic/scientific document (Bulletin 471) with physical evidence (the actual pipettes,
  physically measured), and now further corroborated by r06's decades of "scales true, no added
  water" inspector findings and his direct 1907 assessment of Keddie's honesty/competence as a
  tester. Per the test's own instructions ("count who is independent... two narrators repeating
  the same figure... are one source" / "quoted document outranking a memory"), Bulletin 471 +
  Ansel's own contemporaneous book + the Alder Corners cross-check + Strawn's independent
  inspector notebooks together heavily outweigh the shared community suspicion. Also notable:
  Teague's ledger shows the "station gain" was formally credited to the ASSOCIATION's own general
  account (not hidden), and Teague says he raised the discrepancy with manager Selby Vose in 1916
  and was told to "let it alone" — i.e., management knew of the gap for at least 9 years and did
  nothing; the money went to the institution, not into Keddie's pocket, which is more consistent
  with a bookkeeping/equipment artifact than personal theft.
- Timeline coda given by Ivy: glass condemned 1923, cause found 1958 = 35 years. Ansel dismissed
  1925, cleared 1958 = 33 years. Ansel died 1934, had been dead 24 years when the exonerating
  bulletin was read in 1958.
