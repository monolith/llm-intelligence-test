## NEW THIS SEGMENT — MAJOR SOURCE: Retelling 12, Dell Rundle, schoolteacher, "The Larrow Valley
## Creamery: Fifty Years," a jubilee pamphlet printed at Larrow Green for the Association's 50th
## year, 1939 (reprinted 1959 with one footnote and closing note unaltered)

Compiled by the valley schoolteacher "in pieces, at funerals and in the back of the store," over
two winters of asking questions. This is a valley-official/institutional-memory account, not a
family memoir — useful as a structural spine for Section A but secondhand on many points where
family sources (r09, r10) are firsthand.

- **1889 — founding:** farmers met at the Larrow Green schoolhouse in cold weather and organized
  the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking
  whatever the merchant allowed. Anecdote: the meeting ran very late and somebody's horse got loose
  in the middle of it.
- **Gideon Vose elected first president.** Described as "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once
  thought of the law and thought better of it." He wrote the by-laws himself, at his own kitchen
  table, in a hand the Association's clerks were still complaining about thirty years later.
- **MAJOR NEW CONFLICT bearing directly on Section B3 — Selby Vose's relation to Gideon Vose:**
  Rundle writes: **"His nephew Selby Vose, afterward manager of the Association, used to say that
  his uncle wrote by-laws the way other men build stone wall — slowly, and to last."** This
  explicitly calls Selby Vose Gideon Vose's **nephew**, and attributes to Selby himself the words
  "his uncle." This directly contradicts r08, where Selby Vose is quoted in his own first-person
  words calling Gideon Vose "my father": *"My father, who was your first president and who wrote
  the by-law..."* (per notes-6's B3 resolution, which had treated this as settled). We now have two
  sources each purporting to give Selby Vose's own words about his relation to Gideon, and they
  flatly disagree (father vs. uncle). Resolution is NOT obvious: r08 is presented as Selby's own
  direct first-person quote (which would ordinarily be the strongest possible evidence for one's own
  parentage), but r12 is a documented, care-taken historical pamphlet compiled by a named
  schoolteacher who elsewhere in the same document is shown to be careful and self-correcting (see
  the theft-theory retraction below), and who explicitly attributes the "uncle" language to
  something Selby "used to say" repeatedly (a habitual, quotable turn of phrase, not a one-off).
  There is no clean way to reconcile "father" and "uncle" as literally true simultaneously (unless
  Selby was raised by his uncle after his father's death and used "father" loosely — a plausible but
  wholly speculative reconciliation not supported by any source). **This should be flagged to the
  next reader as a genuine, unresolved contradiction on a load-bearing fact for B3, not silently
  resolved either way.**
- **1891 — creamery built** at Larrow Green, on the creek below the mill; the churn was set up; the
  valley began to be paid in cash once a month for the first time ("There are men still living who
  can tell you what the first check did in their mother's kitchen").
- **Article VII, quoted in full for the first time:** *"Every patron shall be paid for the milk he
  delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and
  not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall
  govern."* Dated by Rundle to **1891**, the same year the churn was set up. Some men left the
  Association over it at the time; Rundle calls it "a just rule" in principle, but stresses the last
  clause — "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern," not the test of the creamery,
  not any test but the one made at the patron's own door — as "the sentence on which the whole
  trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned." This is the mechanism that let the defective
  Ostrey Hollow station's own readings stand unchallenged for 26 years (matches and sharpens the
  B3 answer already given in notes-6).
- **Ostrey Hollow station, opened 1896:** the Hollow is seven miles up from the Green; the pull down
  was three hours in mud and impossible at all in March. The Association built a skimming station
  there: milk was weighed, tested, and separated on the spot; cream went down to the Green by wagon;
  skim went home to the calves and pigs the same morning. Building description: plain, boarded and
  battened, intake room at the road end, a loft above (Rundle believes never cleared out since
  built); the creek behind it, which in a wet April comes up to the sill and has twice come over it.
  (This 1896 station-opening date is new and slots cleanly before the previously-established 14
  April 1897 invoice / 1 May 1897 glass-into-service date — i.e., the station opened in 1896, and a
  year later its own dedicated glassware/pipette outfit went into service.)
- **From 1902:** Hollow men complain their milk tests lower at Ostrey Hollow than the same class of
  milk at the Green — not believed. (Matches r09's 1902 date and Emmet Brant as one of the first
  complainers.)
- **1908:** Association committee under **Nyle Grigg** sampled Hessel Bottom herds through the
  winter, reported that wet hay thinned the cream and the remedy lay with the land. The Hollow did
  not accept it. (Matches r09.)
- **1919:** manager circulated a warning to Hollow patrons against adding water to their milk;
  after that the two ends of the valley "did not speak civilly to one another for six years"
  (1919-1925, consistent with the 1925 arbitration). Rundle, as the valley schoolteacher, notes she
  had the children of both ends in one schoolroom through those years and says the feud "came down
  to them entire, without a word of it being explained, the way a debt does."
- **The valley's folk theory, stated and then retracted (see below):** "It is the settled opinion of
  the valley that cream left that station by the back door, and the figures went right when the man
  who kept it did not." Rundle says she set this down "as I have heard it set down a hundred times,
  by men who were there and men who were not," and had never heard it contradicted at the store —
  until her own postscript below.
- **Glassware condemned — DATE CONFLICT:** Rundle gives **"the twelfth of July, 1923"** for when the
  state inspector condemned the station's glassware. This conflicts with r07 Item 15 and r11 Section
  VII (Bulletin 471's own conclusion, quoted verbatim and identically in both), which both give
  **"the twelfth of June, 1923."** Since r07 and r11 agree with each other word-for-word (both
  quoting the same primary bulletin text), and Rundle's pamphlet is a secondhand valley-memory
  compilation, "June" is preferred as better-attested; "July" is treated as Rundle's error (or a
  reprint slip).
- **January 1925:** the Hollow patrons petitioned the Association.
- **NEW FIGURE — total milk intake, CONFLICTS with established 40,000,000 lb:** "The station took
  in, over the years complained of, **forty-four million pounds** of milk." This is the patrons'
  own 1925 petition figure as reported by Rundle, not necessarily covering the identical 25-year
  window (1898-1922) used in the pipette-defect 76,000 lb calculation elsewhere — "years complained

- **NEW FIGURE — patrons' claimed loss, CONFLICTS (closely) with established $19,000:** "The

  versus $3,800/$19,000 = 20% (1/5) using the other figure. Flag both for the final answer.
- **NEW NAME — arbitrator:** the 1925 board of arbitration sat at Ashlin under **Alonzo Frick,
  attorney, of that town.** "It heard everybody. It found nothing established, it made an award to
  the patrons without admitting anything, and it sent this valley home to live with itself." The

- **Section V, "Over the Ridge":** Alder Corners Cheese Company "opened" in **1914**, per Rundle —
  matching r09's 1914 date, and conflicting with r10's own family/firsthand account (Jerome Cudd's

- Also notes: "It is Jerome Cudd's daughter, Rosalie Cudd, who makes the cheese at Alder Corners
  now" — stated in the ORIGINAL 1939 text, i.e., Rosalie was already understood locally to be doing
  the Alder Corners cheesemaking as of 1939, two years before her father's death in 1941 per r10's

- **FOOTNOTE added to the 1959 reprint (marked "[^1] Added to the 1959 reprint"):** "The Hollow
  matter has since been taken up again at the college at Farland by Miss Ivy Keddie, who is second
  cousin to Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom. **Eleven pipettes** out of the station's old condemned
  crate were found still to be in existence. I am too old to rewrite a pamphlet and I refer the
  reader to the college. — D.R." This independently corroborates the Ivy Keddie / Hazel Brant
  second-cousin relationship (matches r09, reinforcing B2). **NEW ODDITY/possible conflict:**
  "eleven pipettes... found still to be in existence" versus the established "nine surviving

  r11 Section III's reference to "the nine surviving pipettes"). Not necessarily a hard

- **"NOTE ADDED WHILE IN PRESS" — a major independent corroboration AND a documented 1939
  self-correction:** While the pamphlet was being typeset, Rundle was allowed to see the
  Association's old ledgers and found this recurring annual entry, in the bookkeeper's hand, quoted
  verbatim:
  > *"Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received
  > at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per
  > cent, as usual. Carried to the general account."*
  Arithmetic check: 60,800 − 57,760 = 3,040 lb gain; 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.264% ≈ "five and a quarter
  per cent" — internally consistent, and this is now a SEVENTH independent line of evidence
  (alongside the pipette burette math, the 1896 season, the 1911 nine-week episode, Strawn's 1924
  note, Larrow Green's own comparable tests, and r10's Alder Corners cheese-book data) converging on
  the ~5%/one-part-in-twenty "station gain" figure — this one taken directly from a single year's
  ledger line, independent of the 1958 pipette-defect derivation entirely.
  **Rundle then explicitly retracts her own Chapter IV theft narrative, using this arithmetic**:
  *"I must go back on Chapter IV. Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It
  reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand. I leave it as written
  and correct it here."* This is significant: a **contemporary, 1939, first-hand logical disproof**
  of the theft/water-dilution theory, arrived at independently and nineteen years BEFORE the 1958
  pipette-defect explanation was ever identified — the valley's own historian had already shown by

  own bookkeeping, well before anyone knew the true mechanism (short pipettes) or the true magnitude
  (76,000 lb / 0.19 points / 3.80 true average).
- **Closing Chapter VI ("Fifty Years")** — valedictory color, no new hard facts: fifty Junes of cream

## r10, Rosalie Cudd, cheesemaker, Alder Corners Cheese Company (Vessey County), letter to Ivy

An independent, disinterested, third-county source with zero stake in the Ostrey Hollow dispute.

- **Alder Corners factory:** built **1913** by Rosalie's father **Jerome Cudd**, on a flat below the
  church, chosen for spring water and a hard road east. He made cheese there until he **died in
  March 1941**, "between the setting and the cutting of a vat." Rosalie took over that same spring
  (1941), had run it **17 years** by 1958 (consistent). She was 12 when the first vat was set
  (implying birth ≈1901), though she also says she was "drying hoops before I was tall enough to
  see into one," suggesting she grew up around the plant from its start.
  **CONFLICT (now widened): r09 (Roy Brant) and r12 (Dell Rundle's pamphlet) both say the factory
  "opened in 1914"; r10 (the founder's own daughter, direct/firsthand) says 1913.** r10 is preferred

  uncertain independence — see above) should be carried forward as a live discrepancy, not erased.
- **Redlow Ridge:** four miles of switchback, cut originally for a lumber road, shuts in November,

  independent of price or preference.
- **The four Hessel Bottom patrons who hauled to Alder Corners, 1915-1930 (16 seasons), matching

  last of those summers was 1930." Reasons (matches r09): cheese paid better in flush month, the

- **Testing method:** Jerome Cudd tested independently, on his own bench, with his own glassware,

  ~3.80 in 1919 specifically.

  |---|---|---|---|
  | 1915 | 3.79 | 1923 | 3.81 |
  | 1916 | 3.81 | 1924 | 3.80 |
  | 1917 | 3.78 | 1925 | 3.79 |

## r11, Leland Vaught, professor of dairy husbandry, State Agricultural College at Farland —
## Introduction and Method sections of Bulletin No. 471, October 1958

- **Authorship note:** written under Vaught's byline, at the college where Ivy Keddie was a
  student/researcher in 1958. Open bibliographic question — whether Bulletin 471 is Vaught's own
  work reporting on Ivy Keddie's underlying research, jointly-credited, or Vaught writing up
  findings Keddie supplied. Not resolved by any source read so far.
- **Mechanism of the defect, explained in full:** the fat test is read directly off a graduated
  bottle-neck calibrated for one specific fixed milk charge; if the pipette delivers less than its
  rated mark, the reading comes out low **in the exact proportion of the shortfall** — multiplicative,
  not additive. Three reasons it went undetected 26 years: (1) proportional error keeps patrons'
  relative rankings internally consistent; (2) perfectly precise/repeatable (same instrument, same
  shortfall, every time, for a quarter century); (3) the pipette **is** the trade measuring standard
  and cannot be checked except against an outside burette — exactly what Ivy Keddie brought in, 1958.
- **Material examined:** matches r07 Items 5/14 — the nine surviving pipettes from the condemned
  Ostrey Hollow crate, blown to 17.6 c.c., delivered against a calibrated burette, five runs each.
  (Now set against r12's 1959 footnote figure of "eleven pipettes... found still to be in
  existence" — see new-segment conflict above.)
- **THE CENTRAL CONFLICT — pipette measurement and downstream figures (r11 self-contradicts):**
  - r11 Section III: "the mean delivery of the nine is 16.27 c.c." — versus r07 Item 14 (Ivy
    Keddie's own signed 9 March 1958 measurement sheet): nine individual readings (16.70, 16.71,

  - This conflicts with the well-established figures: 16.72 c.c. mean, 0.95 ratio (95%, "one part in
