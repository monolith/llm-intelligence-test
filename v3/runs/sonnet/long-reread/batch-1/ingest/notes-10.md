## THE EIGHT UNDERLYING STORIES — reconstruction context (Larrow Valley / Ostrey Hollow, Ordell County)

The material is 24 retellings (r01–r24) of eight original underlying stories centered on the
Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (founded 1889) and the defective Ostrey Hollow
skimming station. Many retellings are buried as the single real document inside thousands of
lines of generated noise (fake API specs, day-book ledgers, gossip transcripts among invented
characters).

## MAJOR SOURCE — Retelling 12, Dell Rundle, "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years" (jubilee
## pamphlet, Larrow Green, 1939, reprinted 1959 with one added footnote and one added note)

Compiled by the valley schoolteacher "in pieces, at funerals and in the back of the store," over
two winters of asking questions — an institutional/valley-official memory source, useful as a
structural spine for Section A but secondhand on points where family sources (r09, r10) are
firsthand.

- **1889 — founding:** farmers met at the Larrow Green schoolhouse in cold weather and organized
  the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking
  whatever the merchant allowed. Anecdote: the meeting ran very late and somebody's horse got loose.
- **Gideon Vose elected first president** — "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of
  the law and thought better of it." Wrote the by-laws himself at his own kitchen table.
- **MAJOR UNRESOLVED CONTRADICTION (Section B3 — Selby Vose's relation to Gideon Vose):** Rundle
  writes: *"His nephew Selby Vose, afterward manager of the Association, used to say that his uncle
  wrote by-laws the way other men build stone wall — slowly, and to last."* This calls Selby Vose
  Gideon Vose's **nephew**, quoting Selby's own habitual words ("his uncle"). This directly
  contradicts r08, where Selby Vose is quoted in his own first-person words calling Gideon Vose "my
  father": *"My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law..."* Two sources each
  purport to give Selby's own words about his relation to Gideon, and they flatly disagree
  (father vs. uncle). r08 is a direct first-person quote (ordinarily strongest evidence for one's
  own parentage); r12 is a careful, self-correcting, named-author historical pamphlet, attributing
  "uncle" to something Selby "used to say" repeatedly (habitual, not a one-off slip). No clean
  reconciliation exists (a "raised by uncle after father's death, called him father loosely" theory
  is pure speculation, unsupported by any source). **This must be flagged as a genuine, unresolved
  contradiction on a load-bearing fact for B3 — do not silently resolve either way.** (r13, read
  this segment, confirms Selby Vose was "the manager" at the time of the 1925 arbitration but does
  not address his parentage either way.)
- **1891 — creamery built** at Larrow Green, on the creek below the mill; churn set up; valley paid
  in cash monthly for the first time.
- **Article VII, quoted in full:** *"Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according
  to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to
  the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."* Dated to
  **1891**, same year as the churn. Rundle calls it "a just rule" in principle but stresses the last
  clause — "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" (not the creamery's test) — as
  "the sentence on which the whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned." This is the
  mechanism that let the defective Ostrey Hollow station's own readings stand unchallenged for
  26 years. (r13's Frick recollections restate this same rule in his own words as "the complaint,"
  confirming it independently: patrons paid on butterfat by test, and the test of the delivery
  station governs.)
- **Ostrey Hollow station, opened 1896:** seven miles up from the Green (r13 and r14 both
  independently confirm "seven miles"); pull down was three hours in mud, impossible in March.
  Milk weighed, tested, separated on the spot; cream went down to the Green by wagon; skim went
  home to calves/pigs same morning. Building: plain, boarded/battened, intake room at road end,
  loft above (never cleared out since built); creek behind it has twice come over the sill in wet
  April.
  - **NEW (r14, Hobart Sill, buttermaker, 1934 retirement remarks):** the station opened
    specifically in **May 1896** ("that was ninety-six, in May, and there was a crowd went up for
    it"). At opening, the station **had no glassware/testing outfit of its own** — its own
    equipment had been ordered but had not arrived, and there was talk of holding off the whole
    season. Sill made up a temporary testing outfit **out of his own glass** at Larrow Green and
    sent it up the valley by wagon so the station could open on schedule. The station used Sill's
    borrowed glass through its first season. **The station's own equipment arrived "the spring
    after"** (i.e., spring 1897), at which point Sill's borrowed glass was returned to him (one
    piece came back chipped). This independently corroborates and sharpens the previously
    established "14 April 1897 invoice / 1 May 1897 glass-into-service date": the station's own
    (ultimately defective) glassware went into service in spring 1897, NOT at the May 1896
    opening — meaning the defect-affected testing period should be dated from spring 1897, not
    from the station's opening eleven months earlier.
  - **NEW (r14):** the station's first season (partial: May 1896 to the end of that year) took in
    "about nine hundred thousand pounds" of milk — a new figure, first-season intake only, not
    previously seen.
- **From 1902:** Hollow men complain milk tests lower at Ostrey Hollow than same-class milk at the
  Green — not believed (matches r09's 1902 date and Emmet Brant as an early complainer). **r14
  independently corroborates 1902** as the date Sill first remembers hearing complaints: "a man in
  here shouting that his milk tested under his brother-in-law's down the creek."
- **1908:** committee under **Nyle Grigg** sampled Hessel Bottom herds through the winter, reported
  wet hay thinned the cream and the remedy lay with the land; the Hollow did not accept it (matches
  r09). **r13 (Frick) independently confirms this "hay" explanation was put before the 1925
  arbitration board**, with the 1908 committee's report read into the record and "the bog meadow at
  Hessel Bottom... before us for the better part of an afternoon."
- **1919:** manager circulated a warning to Hollow patrons against adding water to their milk; after
  that the two ends of the valley "did not speak civilly to one another for six years" (1919–1925,
  consistent with the 1925 arbitration). Rundle taught children of both ends through those years;
  says the feud "came down to them entire, without a word of it being explained, the way a debt
  does." **r14 independently confirms the 1919 circular**: "that would be nineteen nineteen — a
  circular, warning the Ostrey Hollow patrons about water in the milk." Sill approved of the
  manager's action ("I thought well of him for it... the only plain thing that office ever did").
- **Valley folk theory, stated then retracted:** "It is the settled opinion of the valley that
  cream left that station by the back door, and the figures went right when the man who kept it did
  not" (i.e., theft/dilution theory) — set down "as I have heard it set down a hundred times."
- **Glassware condemned — DATE CONFLICT:** Rundle gives **"the twelfth of July, 1923."** This
  conflicts with r07 Item 15 and r11 Section VII (Bulletin 471, quoted verbatim identically in
  both), which give **"the twelfth of June, 1923."** Since r07 and r11 agree word-for-word (quoting
  the same primary bulletin), "June" is preferred as better-attested; "July" is treated as Rundle's
  error or a reprint slip.
- **January 1925:** Hollow patrons petitioned the Association. **r13 (Frick) independently confirms
  January 1925 as the petition date**, and adds that "the by-laws provided for arbitration, and the
  officers... did not resist it."
- **NEW FIGURE — total milk intake, CONFLICTS with established 40,000,000 lb:** patrons' 1925
  petition claimed the station took in, "over the years complained of," **forty-four million
  pounds** of milk. Not necessarily the same 25-year window (1898–1922) used in the pipette-defect
  76,000 lb calculation — "years complained of" could span differently. Flagged as a genuine
  discrepancy, not substituted for the multiply-corroborated 40,000,000 figure, but carried forward.
- **NEW FIGURE — patrons' claimed loss, CONFLICTS (closely) with established $19,000:** patrons'
  1925 petition set their loss at **$19,600 "to the dollar."** Distinct from the $19,000 figure
  computed retrospectively in Bulletin 471 (1958, per r07 Item 6). Both legitimately sourced but not
  identical. Bears on Section C1(d) "fraction actually received": using $19,600, $3,800/$19,600 =
  19.4%; using $19,000, $3,800/$19,000 = 20% (1/5). Flag both.
- **NEW FIGURE — third loss claim, in butterfat pounds (r13, contemporary 1925 arbitration
  record):** Alonzo Frick's own arbitration-exhibit article states the patrons' "own reckoning of
  what they had lost was **sixty-seven thousand pounds of butter fat** over the years complained
  of, and nothing was ever brought forward to disturb that figure." This is a THIRD figure for the
  patrons' claimed shortfall, in yet another unit (pounds of butterfat, not milk pounds or dollars),
  and it CONFLICTS with the previously-established 76,000 lb butterfat shortfall figure (apparently
  derived from the 1958 pipette-defect/burette-test calculation in Bulletin 471). Both are
  independently and directly sourced (67,000 lb is the patrons' own 1925 claim as recorded
  verbatim by the arbitration chairman himself; 76,000 lb is presumably the later scientifically
  reconstructed figure from the pipette shortfall math). They are not the same calculation and
  need not agree — the 1925 patrons' figure was their own contemporary estimate, unverified even by
  Frick's own account ("nothing was ever brought forward to disturb that figure" — meaning also
  nothing proved it). For Section C1(c) ("how many pounds of butterfat were the patrons not
  credited with"), this is a live discrepancy: 76,000 lb (retrospective/pipette-based) vs. 67,000
  lb (patrons' own 1925 contemporary claim). Show both, explain the difference in method and date.
- **NEW NAME — arbitrator:** 1925 board of arbitration sat at Ashlin under **Alonzo Frick,
  attorney, of that town.** "It heard everybody. It found nothing established, it made an award to
  the patrons without admitting anything, and it sent this valley home to live with itself." Award
  amount not given here (established elsewhere as $3,800, per r07 Item 7). **r13, Frick's own
  first-person account, CONFIRMS the $3,800 award figure directly and with full context** (see
  below).
- **Section V, "Over the Ridge":** Alder Corners Cheese Company "opened" in **1914** per Rundle —
  matching r09's 1914 date, conflicting with r10's firsthand family account (Jerome Cudd's daughter
  Rosalie, writing from direct knowledge) that the factory was built in **1913**. Two
  valley-side/secondhand sources (r09, r12) agree on 1914 against one firsthand family source (r10)
  saying 1913 — but r09 and r12 may share the same oral tradition rather than being independently
  verified, so per the batch's independence-over-headcount rule this does NOT automatically overturn
  r10's firsthand 1913 date. Carry the discrepancy forward explicitly.
- Also: "It is Jerome Cudd's daughter, Rosalie Cudd, who makes the cheese at Alder Corners now" —
  stated in the ORIGINAL 1939 text, i.e., Rosalie was already locally understood to be doing the
  cheesemaking as of 1939, two years before her father's death in 1941 (per r10, which says she took
  over that spring after Jerome died "between the setting and the cutting of a vat," and had run it
  17 years by 1958). Not a hard contradiction — r10 also says she was "drying hoops before I was
  tall enough to see into one" (working there since childhood) — but flagged as a minor oddity in
  loose usage of "makes the cheese" for someone helping years before formally taking over.
- **FOOTNOTE added to the 1959 reprint:** "The Hollow matter has since been taken up again at the
  college at Farland by Miss Ivy Keddie, who is second cousin to Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom.
  **Eleven pipettes** out of the station's old condemned crate were found still to be in existence.
  I am too old to rewrite a pamphlet and I refer the reader to the college. — D.R." Independently
  corroborates the Ivy Keddie / Hazel Brant second-cousin relationship (matches r09, reinforcing
  B2). **ODDITY/possible conflict:** "eleven pipettes... found still to be in existence" versus the
  established "nine surviving pipettes" that were burette-tested (r07 Item 5/14's nine individual
  readings summing to 150.48; r11 Section III's "the nine surviving pipettes"). Not necessarily a
  hard contradiction (eleven could have been found/recovered, of which only nine were intact enough
  to run against the calibrated burette) — but flag as a discrepancy in the physical evidence
  central to the whole case, not silently folded into "nine."
- **"NOTE ADDED WHILE IN PRESS" — independent corroboration AND a documented 1939
  self-correction:** While typesetting, Rundle saw the Association's old ledgers and found this
  recurring annual entry, in the bookkeeper's hand, quoted verbatim:
  > *"Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received
  > at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per
  > cent, as usual. Carried to the general account."*
  Arithmetic check: 60,800 − 57,760 = 3,040 lb gain; 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.264% ≈ "five and a quarter
  per cent" — internally consistent. This is a SEVENTH independent line of evidence (alongside the
  pipette burette math, the 1896 season, the 1911 nine-week episode, Strawn's 1924 note, Larrow
  Green's own comparable tests, and r10's Alder Corners cheese-book data) converging on the
  ~5%/one-part-in-twenty "station gain" figure — taken directly from a single year's ledger line,
  independent of the 1958 pipette-defect derivation entirely.
  **Rundle then explicitly retracts her own Chapter IV theft narrative, using this arithmetic**:
  *"I must go back on Chapter IV. Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It
  reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand. I leave it as written
  and correct it here."* Significant: a **contemporary, 1939, firsthand logical disproof** of the
  theft/water-dilution theory, arrived at independently and 19 years BEFORE the 1958 pipette-defect
  explanation was identified — the valley's own historian had already shown by simple arithmetic
  that the "cream stolen" story could not be true, using only the Association's own bookkeeping,
  before anyone knew the true mechanism (short pipettes) or true magnitude (76,000 lb / 0.19 points
  / 3.80 true average).
  **NEW (r14) — an EIGHTH independent line of evidence for the "cream ran heavy" fact, and a named
  eyewitness contradicting Rundle's own retraction's implication:** Hobart Sill, the Larrow Green
  buttermaker for 41 years (1893–1934), testifies from his own daily first-hand handling and his
  own signed receiving-book entries ("you will find my hand on every page of it from ninety-three")
  that the Ostrey Hollow cream arriving at Larrow Green **always ran heavier in butterfat than the
  station's own paperwork said had been bought — "every year... Always more. Never less."** He
  double-weighed every delivery himself in the cold room as a check on the platform man. This
  independently confirms, from a second primary eyewitness source using his own independent
  records, the same "heavy on arrival" pattern that Rundle found in a single ledger year — and it
  is this very fact that makes the theft/dilution theory arithmetically impossible (stolen cream
  would arrive light, not heavy). **However, Sill himself did NOT draw Rundle's conclusion** —
  despite personally witnessing the "heavy" pattern for decades, Sill still believed in 1934 that
  the Hollow's low RECORDED test figures were due to patrons watering their milk: "if the figures
  they were paid on came out under [3.85 true value], then somebody up that hill road was helping
  the cans along, and I said so at the time and I say it now." This is a direct, named,
  self-contradictory-in-hindsight juxtaposition: the same fact pattern (heavy cream on arrival) that
  later disproves the water theory was known to Sill his whole career, yet he continued to hold the
  water theory to his retirement in 1934 — while Rundle, working from the same underlying fact
  pattern (a ledger showing the same "heavy" phenomenon), reached the opposite, correct conclusion
  in 1939. Both are firsthand, named, on-the-record sources; only Rundle drew the logically
  consistent inference. Retain this as a notable irony/contradiction in the "Theory" material.
- **Closing Chapter VI ("Fifty Years")** — valedictory color, no new hard facts: fifty Junes of
  cream coming down the road, fifty Januaries of it going down over ice with roughshod horses, a
  monthly check in kitchens that had known none, a creamery "built by men with nothing but each
  other and a piece of paper they had written themselves."

## NEW MAJOR SOURCE (this segment) — Retelling 13, Alonzo Frick, "Recollections of the Ordell
## Arbitration," article for the yearbook of the Ordell County Bar Association, 1946

Written by the attorney who chaired the 1925 board of arbitration, at age 74 (born c. 1872),
looking back 21 years. Includes the reprinted exhibit list from his own file. This is a primary,
named, official/legal source — very high authority for the specific figures and dates it states
directly, since Frick is recalling his own official function and quoting from his own retained
file, not secondhand oral tradition.

- **The rule restated independently:** "The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association pays a
  patron for the butter fat in his milk and not for the weight of it. The fat is found by test, and
  the test of the station where the man delivers is the test that governs him." (Matches Article
  VII / Rundle's account exactly, independently phrased.)
- **Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green.** Patrons "had been paid, year upon
  year, on an average test of **3.16**," while the rest of the valley tested better, and "the
  difference holds steady for a quarter of a century" (~25 years) — this is the RECORDED average
  test at the station, per Frick's own recollection of the case as presented to the board.
- **January 1925:** Hollow patrons petitioned the Association (matches Rundle). By-laws provided
  for arbitration; officers did not resist it. A three-person board was constituted; Frick was
  named chairman, "I have always supposed because I was the one of the three who kept no cow."
- **Sessions:** three sittings. First at the Association's office at **Ashlin**, **17 March 1925**.
  Second and third at **Ostrey Hollow itself**, in the station's own weigh room (Frick's own
  initiative, so the board could see the plant rather than have it described). The board watched
  the scales tried and the sampling/testing done; Frick (a lawyer, not a chemist) could not judge
  the chemistry itself but saw it "done the same way twice" by men "not hiding their hands."
- **Three rival explanations put to the board** (Frick stresses that all three were raised, by
  named or titled parties, and that none was proved, and that nobody in the room seemed troubled
  that the three did not sit together):
  1. **Water** — put by the manager, **Selby Vose**. He had circulated a warning to Hollow patrons
     "some years before" (matches the 1919 circular in Rundle/Sill) and stood by the water/dilution
     theory before the board, "without heat and without proof."
  2. **Tester theft** — put (by implication, not direct accusation) by the bookkeeper, **Wilbur
     Teague** (NEW NAME), who described how the station's account would look if a man were helping
     himself to cream and covering it with low tests, while stating he made no charge against
     anybody. Frick notes no cross-examination reaches a witness of that kind.
  3. **Bad hay / wet ground** — the 1908 committee report was read into the record; the Hessel
     Bottom bog meadow, wet ground, and coarse hay were discussed "for the better part of an
     afternoon." Frick calls it "an old opinion in that valley" with "the advantage of blaming
     nobody in the room." (Matches Rundle's Nyle Grigg 1908 committee.)
- **Ansel Keddie's testimony (17 March 1925):** Keddie had tested at Ostrey Hollow since it opened
  in 1896 and kept the test book in his own hand every season since. He testified he had tested "as
  he was taught and as the state man had seen him do it, **thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine
  seasons**," could not explain the figures, and had never taken a pound of cream from the station.
  Frick states plainly: "I believed him then and I believe him now." Frick observes that an honest
  denial "did him no good at all" because a man who cannot explain a figure is presumed by the room
  to be its author.
- **Keddie's private admission to Frick (not in the official minutes):** in the corridor at Ashlin,
  Keddie told Frick privately that he had "always thought the patrons were at fault" (i.e., he
  privately favored the water/dilution theory against his own neighbors) but would not say so in
  the hearing room. Frick specifically distinguishes: it is true Keddie "went to his grave accusing
  nobody" (publicly), but false that "he suspected nobody" (privately he did suspect the patrons).
  This is a first-person-reported private opinion from the tester himself, aligning him privately
  with Selby Vose's and Hobart Sill's water theory, even though publicly and in the record he took
  no position on cause.
- **30 April 1925** (four days before the award): the Association dismissed Ansel Keddie from his
  post. Frick stresses there was NO finding against him from the board, then or ever — "he was
  simply let go while the board was still sitting" — and Frick says he has "never been able to
  think well of the Association for it."
- **4 May 1925 — the award:** the board found "no cause established" and awarded the Ostrey Hollow
  patrons **$3,800**, without admission, charged to the station account. This CONFIRMS, from the
  arbitration chairman's own first-person account, the $3,800 figure previously known only from the
  1958 Bulletin 471 (r07 Item 7) — now doubly and independently sourced.
- **Patrons' own reckoning of loss (as presented to the board, unverified):** "sixty-seven thousand
  pounds of butter fat over the years complained of, and nothing was ever brought forward to
  disturb that figure." (See conflict with the 76,000 lb figure noted above, under Rundle's
  section — flagged there for Section C1(c).)
- **Exhibit list, reprinted verbatim from Frick's own file:**
  > EXHIBITS BEFORE THE BOARD OF ARBITRATION, OSTREY HOLLOW MATTER, 1925
  > 1. The test book of the Ostrey Hollow station, **4 May 1896 to 31 December 1924**, in the hand
  >    of Ansel Keddie.
  > 2. The receiving book of the said station.
  > 3. The private weigh book of **Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom**, begun **1 January 1897**.
  > 4. The circuit notebook of the state dairy inspector for the year **1923**.
  This gives an EXACT, primary-sourced date range for the station's official test-book record:
  4 May 1896 (very shortly after the May 1896 opening per r14) through 31 December 1924 — nearly
  29 full years, matching Keddie's "twenty-nine seasons." It also independently confirms Emmet
  Brant (Hessel Bottom) as a real, named, record-keeping complainant from as early as 1 January
  1897, consistent with him being an early complainer (matching prior notes crediting him from
  1902 in Rundle/r09 — his own private weigh book predates that by 5 years, so he may have been
  tracking the numbers privately well before going public with complaints).
- **Frick's closing reflection:** "A board of arbitration can only decide between the accounts that
  are put to it... not one of them was proved... whether the answer was in that room and simply was
  not in any of the three stories, and whether a lawyer who understood chemistry might have found
  it." This is a first-hand acknowledgment, from the presiding arbitrator himself in 1946, that the
  1925 board never identified the true cause (the defective pipettes were not discovered until
  1958, per other retellings) — none of the three theories offered to the board (water, theft, hay)
  was the correct one.

## NEW MAJOR SOURCE (this segment) — Retelling 14, Hobart Sill, buttermaker (ret.), remarks
## recorded at the Larrow Green creamery on the day he left it, 1934

Spoken remarks by the man who made the Association's butter for 41 years (1893–1934), taken down
verbatim as spoken; he is 64 years old at the time (so born c. 1870). Firsthand eyewitness of
everything that came down the wagon from Ostrey Hollow into his own churning room, but explicitly
states he "never went up there but twice in my life" — his evidence is about the cream as received
at Larrow Green, not about the station itself.

- **1893** — Sill came to Larrow Green; at that time there was "no floor in this room but plank,"
  and butter was cooled with creek ice from the pond.
- **The wagon:** cream came down from Ostrey Hollow every day but Sunday, seven miles of hill road;
  in July it arrived warm no matter how early they started. Weighed and sampled at the Larrow Green
  platform, then into the cold room, where Sill re-weighed every can himself as a check on the
  platform man ("I have never in my life taken another man's figure for what was going into my
  churn"). His own signed receiving book runs from 1893 forward.
- **KEY FINDING — cream consistently arrived "heavy":** "That cream ran heavy. Every year it ran
  heavy. There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there... Always
  more. Never less." Documented in his own receiving book "on page after page after page." (See
  cross-reference above, under Rundle's ledger-arithmetic retraction — this is independent
  corroboration of the same phenomenon from a second, different primary eyewitness and a different
  record set, spanning far more years than Rundle's single ledger line.)
- **Larrow Green's own patrons' milk (his own creek) tested 3.78** on average, "taking one year
  with another right through those years" — ordinary milk, ordinary cows, nothing unusual.
- **NEW FIGURE — Sill's own estimate of the Hollow's TRUE milk quality: 3.85, "and no better."**
  Sill states this plainly and repeatedly ("A 3.85 and no better, and I will say that to any man in
  this valley"). Notably, this is HIGHER than Larrow Green's own 3.78 — meaning Sill believed the
  Hollow's actual milk, properly measured, was slightly better than the Green's own, not worse.
  This conflicts with/needs reconciling against the RECORDED average test of 3.16 at the station
  (per r13/Frick) — a gap of about 0.69 points between Sill's estimate of true quality and the
  recorded figure Frick reports, larger than any previously-established "0.19 points short" gap
  noted from the pipette-defect calculation elsewhere. Sill's 3.85 is an informal buttermaker's
  estimate, not a measured figure — flag it as testimonial opinion evidence, not hard data, but
  record it as a distinct candidate figure for "true average" bearing on Section C1(b).
- **Sill's own theory of cause:** despite witnessing the "heavy" pattern his entire career, Sill
  believed the LOW recorded test figures reflected patrons watering their milk ("if the figures
  they were paid on came out under [3.85], then somebody up that hill road was helping the cans
  along, and I said so at the time and I say it now"). He describes one summer "along in the
  twenties" when "those figures came up and they stayed up, and nothing on this earth changed but
  the season" — implying some correction or improvement occurred, cause unstated/unclear ("Something
  got stopped. Something got said to somebody. I never knew what and I never asked"). This is
  vague and undated beyond "along in the twenties" — flag as an ambiguous, unconfirmed event,
  possibly related to the 1925 arbitration's aftermath but not explicitly tied to it.
- **1902** — "the first I remember of it": a man shouting in the creamery that his milk tested
  under his brother-in-law's down the creek on the same feed and water. (Matches Rundle's "from
  1902" and r09's Emmet Brant date — third independent corroboration of 1902 as the starting date
  of complaints.)
- **1919** — the manager (unnamed here, but = Selby Vose per other sources) "put out a paper" — a
  circular warning Ostrey Hollow patrons about water in the milk. Sill approved: "I thought well of
  him for it. It was the only plain thing that office ever did." Confirms it "changed nothing"
  besides making the Hollow resent the office further.
- **NEW FIGURE — butterfat price: 27 cents per pound**, "through those years, take one year with
  another." This is a new, directly load-bearing figure for the money calculations in Section C1(d)
  and C2(c) — not previously seen in earlier segments' notes. Flag prominently: if other sources
  give a different price-per-pound for a specific year (e.g., 1910 for C2), that more specific
  figure should be preferred for that year; 27 cents is presented as a rough multi-year average
  ("take one year with another"), not a single-year spot price.
- **Station opening, May 1896:** "there was a crowd went up for it." The station had **no testing
  equipment of its own yet** ("They had no outfit of their own. Not a piece. It was ordered and it
