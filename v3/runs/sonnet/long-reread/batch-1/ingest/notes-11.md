## THE EIGHT UNDERLYING STORIES — reconstruction context (Larrow Valley / Ostrey Hollow, Ordell County)

The material is 24 retellings (r01–r24) of eight original underlying stories centered on the
Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (founded 1889) and the defective Ostrey Hollow
skimming station. Many retellings are buried as the single real document inside thousands of
lines of generated noise (fake API specs, day-book ledgers, gossip transcripts among invented
characters, "gibberish" pseudo-prose).

## MAJOR SOURCE — Retelling 12, Dell Rundle, "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years" (jubilee
## pamphlet, Larrow Green, 1939, reprinted 1959 with one added footnote and one added note)

Compiled by the valley schoolteacher "in pieces, at funerals and in the back of the store," over
two winters of asking questions — an institutional/valley-official memory source, useful as a
structural spine for Section A but secondhand on points where family sources (r09, r10, r17) are
firsthand.

- **1889 — founding:** farmers met at the Larrow Green schoolhouse in cold weather and organized
  the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking
  whatever the merchant allowed. Anecdote: the meeting ran very late and somebody's horse got loose.
- **Gideon Vose elected first president** — "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of
  the law and thought better of it." Wrote the by-laws himself at his own kitchen table.
- **MAJOR UNRESOLVED CONTRADICTION (Section B3 — Selby Vose's relation to Gideon Vose):** Rundle
  writes: *"His nephew Selby Vose, afterward manager of the Association, used to say that his uncle
  wrote by-laws the way other men build stone wall — slowly, and to last."* This calls Selby Vose
  Gideon Vose's **nephew**, quoting Selby's own habitual words ("his uncle"). This directly
  contradicts r08, where Selby Vose is quoted in his own first-person words calling Gideon Vose "my
  father": *"My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law..."* Two sources each
  purport to give Selby's own words about his relation to Gideon, and they flatly disagree
  (father vs. uncle). r08 is a direct first-person quote (ordinarily strongest evidence for one's
  own parentage); r12 is a careful, self-correcting, named-author historical pamphlet, attributing
  "uncle" to something Selby "used to say" repeatedly (habitual, not a one-off slip). No clean
  reconciliation exists (a "raised by uncle after father's death, called him father loosely" theory
  is pure speculation, unsupported by any source). **This must be flagged as a genuine, unresolved
  contradiction on a load-bearing fact for B3 — do not silently resolve either way.** r13 (Frick)
  confirms Selby Vose was "the manager" at the time of the 1925 arbitration but does not address
  his parentage either way. This segment's new sources (r15, r16, r17) do not mention Selby Vose or
  the Vose family at all — no new evidence either way.
- **1891 — creamery built** at Larrow Green, on the creek below the mill; churn set up; valley paid
  in cash monthly for the first time.
- **Article VII, quoted in full:** *"Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according
  to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to
  the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."* Dated to
  **1891**, same year as the churn. Rundle calls it "a just rule" in principle but stresses the last
  clause — "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" (not the creamery's test) — as
  "the sentence on which the whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned." This is the
  mechanism that let the defective Ostrey Hollow station's own readings stand unchallenged for
  26 years. r13's Frick recollections restate this same rule in his own words as "the complaint."
  **NEW CONFLICT (r15, Nyle Grigg's 1919 letter):** Grigg states "Payment by test came in with the
  1893 meeting, as I have always understood it" — a THIRD-hand but explicit date of **1893** for
  the adoption of payment-by-test, conflicting with Rundle's dated **1891** Article VII. Grigg
  qualifies it himself ("as I have always understood it"), suggesting even he treats it as
  secondhand/uncertain. Flag as an open discrepancy: 1891 (Rundle, quoting the article text itself)
  vs. 1893 (Grigg, secondhand impression). Rundle's is closer to a primary documentary citation and
  is preferred, but not by an overwhelming margin.
- **Ostrey Hollow station, opened 1896:** seven miles up from the Green (r13 and r14 both
  independently confirm "seven miles"); pull down was three hours in mud, impossible in March.
  Milk weighed, tested, separated on the spot; cream went down to the Green by wagon; skim went
  home to calves/pigs same morning. Building: plain, boarded/battened, intake room at road end,
  loft above (never cleared out since built); creek behind it has twice come over the sill in wet
  April.
  - r14 (Hobart Sill, buttermaker, 1934 retirement remarks): station opened specifically in **May
    1896** ("that was ninety-six, in May"). At opening it had no glassware/testing outfit of its
    own; equipment ordered but not arrived; Sill made up a temporary testing outfit out of his own
    glass at Larrow Green and sent it up the valley so the station could open on schedule. Station
    used Sill's borrowed glass through its first season. **Station's own equipment arrived "the
    spring after"** (spring 1897), when Sill's glass was returned (one piece chipped).
  - **NEW CONFLICT (r17, Hazel Brant's 1981 tape) — a THIRD date for the station's own glassware
    going into service:** Hazel Brant says of the (later-condemned) glassware: *"The crate came out
    from Tarnet in ninety-nine"* — i.e., **1899**, not spring 1897 as per r14/Sill. This is a
    genuine, unresolved conflict between two named, on-the-record witnesses (Sill, an active
    buttermaker with 41 years of daily involvement and signed receiving-book entries, vs. Hazel
    Brant, recalling family lore from her own — later — vantage as a family/local historian). Sill's
    account is more contemporaneous and professionally grounded; Hazel's account is secondhand
    (from her father/grandfather) and given 80+ years after the fact on tape where she explicitly
    disclaims her own reliability on years ("Years I have not"). Spring 1897 (r14) is preferred as
    the better-grounded figure, but 1899 (r17) should be carried forward as an open discrepancy,
    not silently discarded — it may reflect a *second* order of glassware (a replacement/upgrade)
    rather than the original.
  - r14: station's first season (partial: May 1896–end of year) took in "about nine hundred
    thousand pounds" of milk.
- **From 1902:** Hollow men complain milk tests lower at Ostrey Hollow than same-class milk at the
  Green — not believed (matches r09's 1902 date and Emmet Brant as an early complainer). r14
  independently corroborates 1902 as the date Sill first remembers hearing complaints.
  **NEW: r17 (Hazel Brant) identifies "Emmet Brant" as her own grandfather** — a Hessel Bottom
  farmer, patron of the Ostrey Hollow station, who began the family weigh-book on 1 January 1900.
  This strongly implies (though the text does not use the exact phrase "early complainer") that the
  same Emmet Brant who complained from 1902 (per r09/Rundle/r14) is Hazel's grandfather and the
  source of her family's weigh-book data. See "Hazel Brant's tape" section below for the important
  math-relevant figures this yields.
- **1908:** committee under **Nyle Grigg** sampled Hessel Bottom herds through the winter (matches
  r09). r13 (Frick) independently confirms this "hay" explanation was put before the 1925
  arbitration board. **NEW (r15) — the committee's report reprinted in full, primary text:**
  - Committee "appointed in January last" (i.e., January 1908), reporting to "the President and
    Directors of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association."
  - Complaint: Ostrey Hollow milk tests below the rest of the valley; patrons paid less for same
    labor/cows as neighbors.
  - **Hessel Bottom described precisely: "six farms cut hay and turn out stock"** — a wet meadow
    along the creek under Ostrey Hollow, springy year-round, under water in part every spring;
    herbage is sedge, rush, coarse sour grasses; hay cut late and heavy, fed as the main winter
    dependence (November–April) on those farms.
  - Committee visited each of the six farms, some three or four times; drew and sealed their own
    samples; watched herds milked, cans filled and hauled, tests made; tried station scales against
    a known weight and patrons' scales against the station's; found nothing to remark on in the
    testing itself; inquired into water supply at each barn.
  - Observed herd condition: thin in the flank at winter's end, slow to condition in spring, rougher
    coats. Conclusion: **"a cow cannot put into her pail what her keep has never put into her"** —
    wet land gives quantity (milk volume) but withholds quality (fat).
  - Explicit finding: **"no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter."** Milk whole, scales honest.
    Opinion: wet hay of Hessel Bottom thins the cream; remedy lies with the land, not the station.
  - Signed: "NYLE GRIGG, chairman, and the committee" (other members unnamed in this text).
  - Report "received and accepted," question "closed... for eleven years" — i.e., 1908 to 1919.
  - **1919 covering letter (same document, r15):** Grigg reprints his own 1908 report because "the
    Ostrey Hollow question having been reopened this season by men who were boys when we made it."
    Grigg states he had no personal interest (not a patron of that station; hauls to Larrow Green
    himself; had bought hay from two Hessel Bottom men). Adds a new corroborating fact: **the State
    Agricultural College at Farland has since published its Bulletin No. 214**, a controlled feeding
    trial on bog hay, finding the bog hay **depressed the butter fat test by two tenths of a point
    (0.2) in winter feeding** — published "three years after" the 1908 report (i.e., roughly 1911).
    Grigg treats this as independent, disinterested, scientific vindication of the committee's 1908
    finding. Remedy prescribed: drain, sweeten and lime the bottom, keep stock off it in wet spring,
    feed the coarse hay mixed rather than alone.
- **1919:** manager circulated a warning to Hollow patrons against adding water to their milk; after
  that the two ends of the valley "did not speak civilly to one another for six years" (1919–1925,
  consistent with the 1925 arbitration). r14 independently confirms the 1919 circular. **NEW (r17,
  Hazel Brant) — the "water letter" described from the receiving end, with a NEW precise figure:**
  the manager sent the letter to **nine families** on Hessel Bottom specifically (six farms, but "a
  farm can hold more than one family and generally did," hence nine families on six farms). Not one
  family replied — "answered by none of them, which in that valley is a shout" (collective silent
  protest). Hazel's mother read her copy twice and burned it in the stove; Hazel (a small child at
  the time) remembers the stove itself, not the letter's content.
- **Glassware condemned — THREE-WAY DATE CONFLICT, now sharpened:** Rundle gives **"the twelfth of
  July, 1923."** r07 Item 15 and r11 Section VII (Bulletin 471, quoted verbatim identically in
  both) give **"the twelfth of June, 1923."** Since r07 and r11 agree word-for-word (quoting the
  same primary bulletin), "June 1923" is preferred as better-attested; "July 1923" is treated as
  Rundle's error or a reprint slip. **NEW (r17, Hazel Brant) — a THIRD, materially different date:**
  Hazel says plainly, "The state man condemned that glass in **twenty-one**" — i.e., **1921**, not
  1923 at all. This is a two-year discrepancy, not a same-year day/month slip like the June/July
  split. Hazel's account: an unnamed state inspector ("the state man") went at the glass "with some
  measuring instrument he carried in his coat," found it would not deliver what it was marked for,
  and told the Association to order new glass ("They ordered new. Nobody said one word about all
  the tests that were already in the book" — i.e., no retroactive correction was made at the time).
  Given Bulletin 471 is a documentary primary source quoted identically by two independent
  retellings, **1923 (June) remains the better-attested date**, but 1921 (Hazel, secondhand family
  memory, imprecise on years by her own admission) must be carried forward as a live, unresolved
  discrepancy, not silently dropped.
- **January 1925:** Hollow patrons petitioned the Association. r13 (Frick) independently confirms
  January 1925 as the petition date, and adds that "the by-laws provided for arbitration, and the
  officers... did not resist it."
- **Total milk intake, CONFLICTS with established 40,000,000 lb:** patrons' 1925 petition claimed
  the station took in, "over the years complained of," **forty-four million pounds** of milk. Not
  necessarily the same 25-year window (1898–1922) used in the pipette-defect 76,000 lb calculation.
  Flagged as a genuine discrepancy, carried forward, not substituted for the multiply-corroborated
  40,000,000 figure.
- **Patrons' claimed loss, CONFLICTS (closely) with established $19,000:** patrons' 1925 petition
  set their loss at **$19,600 "to the dollar."** Distinct from the $19,000 figure computed
  retrospectively in Bulletin 471 (1958, per r07 Item 6). Bears on Section C1(d) "fraction actually
  received": using $19,600, $3,800/$19,600 = 19.4%; using $19,000, $3,800/$19,000 = 20% (1/5). Flag
  both.
- **Third loss claim, in butterfat pounds (r13, contemporary 1925 arbitration record):** Alonzo
  Frick's own arbitration-exhibit article states the patrons' "own reckoning of what they had lost
  was **sixty-seven thousand pounds of butter fat** over the years complained of, and nothing was
  ever brought forward to disturb that figure." A THIRD figure for the patrons' claimed shortfall
  (67,000 lb), conflicting with the previously-established 76,000 lb butterfat shortfall figure
  (apparently derived from the 1958 pipette-defect/burette-test calculation in Bulletin 471). Both
  independently and directly sourced; not the same calculation and need not agree. For Section
  C1(c) this is a live discrepancy: 76,000 lb (retrospective/pipette-based) vs. 67,000 lb (patrons'
  own 1925 contemporary claim). Show both, explain the difference in method and date.
- **Arbitrator:** 1925 board of arbitration sat at Ashlin under **Alonzo Frick, attorney, of that
  town.** "It heard everybody. It found nothing established, it made an award to the patrons
  without admitting anything, and it sent this valley home to live with itself." Award amount
  established elsewhere as $3,800 (per r07 Item 7); r13, Frick's own first-person account, confirms
  the $3,800 award directly and with full context (see below). **NEW CONFLICT (r17, Hazel Brant) —
  the hearing's date and an important NEW outcome fact:** Hazel dates "the hearing" to **1926**, not
  1925. This conflicts with Frick's own precise, firsthand chairman account (three sittings,
  starting 17 March 1925, at his own retained file's dating) — Frick's account should be preferred
  as a quoted/documented primary source outranking a secondhand family memory recorded 55+ years
  later by someone who explicitly disclaims her own reliability on years. Still, flag the
  discrepancy. **NEW FACT (r17): the arbitration's practical outcome for Ansel Keddie.** Hazel
  reports her father Roy came back from the hearing and said the board "had heard everybody and
  found nothing" (nearly verbatim matching Frick's own words as recorded by Rundle: "It heard
  everybody. It found nothing established") — AND that "they had let Ansel go, which was the
  finding whatever they wrote in the book." I.e., **Ansel Keddie, the tester at Ostrey Hollow, was
  dismissed from his post as a practical consequence of the arbitration, even though the board's
  official written finding admitted no wrongdoing.** This is a new, significant, previously
  uncaptured fact about the human consequences of the case. Also: Roy Brant (Hazel's father) "never
  went to another meeting of that Association as long as he lived, and he was a man who liked
  meetings" — a detail on lasting valley alienation.
- **Section V, "Over the Ridge":** Alder Corners Cheese Company "opened" in **1914** per Rundle —

  Rosalie) that the factory was built in **1913**. **NEW corroboration this segment (r17, Hazel

  Bottom] men hauled over the ridge in the summer flush, to the cheese factory on the far side, and

- Also: "It is Jerome Cudd's daughter, Rosalie Cudd, who makes the cheese at Alder Corners now" —

- **"NOTE ADDED WHILE IN PRESS"** — Rundle's ledger-based retraction of her own theft/dilution

## MAJOR SOURCE — Retelling 13, Alonzo Frick, "Recollections of the Ordell Arbitration," article
## for the yearbook of the Ordell County Bar Association, 1946

Written by the attorney who chaired the 1925 board of arbitration, at age 74 (born c. 1872). A
primary, named, official/legal source — very high authority for the figures and dates it states
directly.

- **The rule restated independently:** matches Article VII / Rundle's account exactly.
- **Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green.** Patrons "had been paid, year upon
  year, on an average test of **3.16**," while the rest of the valley tested better, "the difference
  holds steady for a quarter of a century" (~25 years) — the RECORDED average test at the station.
  (See "Hazel Brant's tape" below for a specific patron-level test figure — 3.42 for Emmet Brant's
  farm in 1910 — which is higher than this 3.16 station-wide 25-year average; not contradictory,
  since one is a single farm in a single (apparently good) year and the other is a station-wide
  multi-decade average.)
- **January 1925:** Hollow patrons petitioned the Association (matches Rundle). Board of
  arbitration: three-person, Frick named chairman "I have always supposed because I was the one of
  the three who kept no cow."
- **Sessions:** three sittings. First at the Association's office at **Ashlin**, **17 March 1925**.
  Second and third at **Ostrey Hollow itself**, in the station's own weigh room. Frick (a lawyer,
  not a chemist) could not judge the chemistry itself but saw it "done the same way twice" by men
  "not hiding their hands."

## NEW MAJOR SOURCE (this segment) — Retelling 16, Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S. (veterinary surgeon) of
## Ashlin, "The Bog Meadows and the Milk," paper read before the Ordell County Farm Club, 1926,
## with floor discussion appended by the club secretary

A professional, scientifically-argued case FOR the wet-hay/soil-chemistry theory (as opposed to
the mechanical-defect/pipette theory), delivered by a disinterested expert (a vet, not a farmer or
Association official) with 30 years of practice in the county.

- **General claim:** wet, undrained bottomland is acid, poor in lime and phosphorus; sweet grasses
  are crowded out by sedge/rush/tussock grass, which is bulky and watery but poor in minerals and is
  cut late (further reducing nutritional quality).
- **Physiological argument:** a mineral-short cow "robs her own frame" to make milk, producing
  normal-to-generous volume but reduced butterfat, since fat is "the first economy she practices
  when she is pressed." Wet-bottom herds: thin in flank, slow to condition, rough-coated, prone to
  digestive trouble in fall, later breeding, worse feet — but NOT necessarily less milk in volume.
- **Kepp's own 30-year field observation:** wet bottoms across the whole county test poorer in fat
  "by a fifth of a point" (0.20), consistently, "farm for farm, wherever you look for it," and
  "does not depend on the breed, on the man, or on the barn" — only on the ground.
- **Hessel Bottom, described again, matching Grigg/Rundle:** "under Ostrey Hollow," six farms, "the
  completest example of wet meadow in this county," springs run in it year-round, unusable until
  well into May, sedge hay is "the winter dependence and very nearly the whole of it" on those
  farms.
- **NEW FIGURE — the test gap, per Kepp: "twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21"** between Ostrey
  Hollow station milk and the rest of the valley — Kepp says this is "as it has been given to me and
  as I have satisfied myself." This is a FOURTH figure for the magnitude of the gap, alongside:
  0.19 (multiply corroborated, including r17/Hazel Brant this segment, see below), the pipette-math
  derivation, and Kepp's own "fifth of a point" (0.20) field-average claim. Kepp states these three
  figures (Bulletin's 0.2, his own field 0.2, and the station's 0.21) "agree so nearly that I do not
  know what more a reasonable man requires" — but note that 0.19 (the figure independently given by
  Hazel Brant, a firsthand family/patron source, and multiply corroborated elsewhere) is not
  identical to 0.21 either, though close. Flag all of 0.19, 0.20, 0.21 as close-but-not-identical
  figures for the same underlying gap, from different sources and methods; do not silently round
  them together.
- **Duration of the complaint, per Kepp: "something like a quarter of a century" (~25 years)** —
  matches Frick's "quarter of a century" figure independently.
- **Cites Bulletin No. 214 again**, same "two tenths" (0.2) finding as Grigg's 1919 letter — see
  note above on this being a genuine point of independent convergence between r15 and r16.
- **CRITICAL NEW FACT — an "objection" raised from the floor, describing the 1911 episode, WITH A
  DURATION CONFLICT:** An unnamed gentleman (via Kepp, recounting the objection to rebut it) states:
  *"in the summer of 1911 the testing at that station was broken off for **six weeks**, their own
  apparatus having failed them and a borrowed one being used, and that the figures stood higher
  during those six weeks than before or after."* This closely parallels the "1911 nine-week episode"
  referenced as one of several convergent lines of evidence for the pipette-defect theory in earlier
  notes (notes-9/notes-10 reference "the 1911 nine-week episode" as one of several independent lines
  of evidence for the ~5% station-gain figure). **This is a genuine, new duration conflict: six
  weeks (r16, Kepp, 1926, secondhand via an unnamed objector) vs. nine weeks (established from an
  earlier, not-yet-fully-identified-in-this-segment source).** Both cannot be correct as literally
  stated; flag explicitly. Substantively, however, both versions describe the SAME underlying event
  (summer 1911, station's own apparatus failed, borrowed apparatus substituted, recorded test rose
  during the substitution) — which is strong, structurally important evidence FOR the
  mechanical-defect theory (a borrowed, presumably-accurate testing setup immediately produced
  HIGHER readings, meaning the station's own normal apparatus was under-reading). **Kepp dismisses
  this evidence** as coincidental with "the flush of the season" (June–July, when all herds test
  best and cattle are off wet ground) rather than engaging with the logical force of the comparison.
  This 1911 episode — regardless of whether six or nine weeks — is one of the single most important
  pieces of evidence in the entire record for adjudicating between the two rival theories (wet hay
  vs. defective glassware), and belongs prominently in any Theory discussion (batch 3), but is
  recorded here in full since this batch's notes must carry it forward.
- **Monetary figure — NEW:** "Fat has been worth twenty-seven cents the pound through these years"
  (Kepp, speaking in 1926, referring to recent years generally) — a butterfat price data point, of
  uncertain applicability to the 1910 or other specific years referenced in the Math section; flag
  for possible relevance but do not assume it applies to 1910 specifically.
- **Floor discussion (appended by club secretary):**
  - A member asked whether the difference might be in the cows/poorer farmers rather than the land.
    Kepp rejected this: some of the best-bred cattle in the county are on wet land; some indifferent
    cattle on hills test well; a man may improve his herd for twenty years and never raise his fat
    while his ground is sour.
  - A member asked whether the trouble might lie in the station itself, "as had been said in the
    valley for years" — i.e., the floor explicitly raises the rival (mechanical-defect) theory by
    name. Kepp declined to engage: he has "no interest in that quarrel and no competence in it,"
    has "never in his life read a test and would not know how," and prefers the converging
    soil/herbage/stock-condition/College evidence over "looking for a second cause in a building."
  - Chairman thanked Kepp; paper ordered printed for members.
