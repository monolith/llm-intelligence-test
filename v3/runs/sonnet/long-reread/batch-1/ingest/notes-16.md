## MAJOR SOURCE — Retelling 12, Dell Rundle, "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years" (jubilee
## pamphlet, Larrow Green, 1939, reprinted 1959 with one added footnote and one added note)

Compiled by the valley schoolteacher "in pieces, at funerals and in the back of the store," over
two winters of asking questions — an institutional/valley-official memory source, useful as a
structural spine for Section A but secondhand on points where family sources (r09, r10, r17, r18)
are firsthand.

- **1889 — founding:** farmers met at the Larrow Green schoolhouse in cold weather and organized
  the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking
  whatever the merchant allowed. Anecdote: the meeting ran very late and somebody's horse got loose.
- **Gideon Vose elected first president** — "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of
  the law and thought better of it." Wrote the by-laws himself at his own kitchen table.
- **SELBY VOSE'S RELATION TO GIDEON VOSE — LARGELY RESOLVED in favor of "son."** Rundle writes:
  *"His nephew Selby Vose, afterward manager of the Association, used to say that his uncle wrote
  by-laws the way other men build stone wall — slowly, and to last."* This calls Selby Vose Gideon
  Vose's **nephew**, quoting Selby's own habitual words ("his uncle"). This directly contradicted
  r08, where Selby Vose is quoted in his own first-person words calling Gideon Vose "my father":
  *"My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law..."* **r18, Duncan Keddie,
  1968** states plainly and deliberately, as a correction of a recurring error: *"Selby Vose, who
  managed the Association, was Gideon Vose's son. Gideon organized the Association and was its
  first president and wrote its by-laws; Selby was his son and not, as I have heard it given, his
  nephew."* This is a second independent, named, dated source explicitly asserting "son" and
  explicitly rejecting "nephew" as a known recurring error — matching r08's first-person quote.
  Weighing the evidence: two independent sources (r08's direct first-person quote from Selby
  himself, and r18's explicit named correction) say "son"; one source (r12/Rundle, secondhand,
  "used to say... his uncle") says "nephew." Rundle's account may reflect the same kind of
  "confusion" Duncan Keddie describes elsewhere (people confusing the Ostrey Hollow Voses with
  unrelated "Ashlin Voses," and loose talk about cousin relations). On the weight of two
  independent, named, direct-or-corrective sources against one secondhand, habitual-phrase
  attribution, **"son" is the better-supported answer for B3**, with "nephew" (Rundle) noted as a
  contradicted minority account.
- **1891 — creamery built** at Larrow Green, on the creek below the mill; churn set up; valley paid
  in cash monthly for the first time.
- **Article VII, quoted in full:** *"Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according
  to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to
  the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."* Dated to
  **1891**, same year as the churn. Rundle calls it "a just rule" in principle but stresses the last
  clause — "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" — as "the sentence on which the
  whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned." This mechanism let the defective Ostrey
  Hollow station's own readings stand unchallenged for 26 years. r13's Frick recollections restate
  this same rule as "the complaint." **CONFLICT (r15, Nyle Grigg's 1919 letter):** Grigg states
  "Payment by test came in with the 1893 meeting, as I have always understood it" — a THIRD-hand but
  explicit date of **1893**, conflicting with Rundle's dated **1891** Article VII; Grigg himself
  flags it as secondhand/uncertain. Flag as open discrepancy: 1891 (Rundle, quoting the article
  text) preferred over 1893 (Grigg, secondhand impression), but not by an overwhelming margin.
- **Ostrey Hollow station, opened 1896:** seven miles up from the Green (r13 and r14 independently
  confirm "seven miles"; **r21/Cleve Oram now a THIRD independent confirmation of "seven miles,"
  and adds that the road "climbs the whole seven" with a bad pitch about two miles below the
  hamlet**); pull down was three hours in mud, impossible in March. Milk weighed, tested, separated
  on the spot; cream went down to the Green by wagon; skim went home to calves/pigs same morning.
  Building: plain, boarded/battened, intake room at road end, loft above (never cleared out since
  built); creek behind it has twice come over the sill in wet April.
  - r14 (Hobart Sill, buttermaker, 1934 retirement remarks): station opened specifically in **May

    glass at Larrow Green and sent it up the valley so the station could open on schedule. Station
    used Sill's borrowed glass through its first season. **Station's own equipment arrived "the
    spring after"** (spring 1897), when Sill's glass was returned (one piece chipped).

- **1908:** committee under **Nyle Grigg** sampled Hessel Bottom herds through the winter (matches
  r09). r13 (Frick) independently confirms this "hay" explanation was put before the 1925
  arbitration board. r15 (this document's 1919 covering letter, reprinting Grigg's 1908 report in
  full) gives the primary text:
  - Committee "appointed in January last" (i.e., January 1908), reporting to "the President and
    Directors of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association."
  - Complaint: Ostrey Hollow milk tests below the rest of the valley; patrons paid less for same
    labor/cows as neighbors.
  - **Hessel Bottom described precisely: "six farms cut hay and turn out stock"** — a wet meadow
    along the creek under Ostrey Hollow, springy year-round, under water in part every spring;
    herbage is sedge, rush, coarse sour grasses; hay cut late and heavy, fed as the main winter
    dependence (November–April) on those farms.
  - Committee visited each of the six farms, some three or four times; drew and sealed their own
    samples; watched herds milked, cans filled and hauled, tests made; tried station scales against
    a known weight and patrons' scales against the station's; found nothing to remark on in the
    testing itself; inquired into water supply at each barn.
  - Observed herd condition: thin in the flank at winter's end, slow to condition in spring, rougher
    coats. Conclusion: **"a cow cannot put into her pail what her keep has never put into her"** —
    wet land gives quantity (milk volume) but withholds quality (fat).
  - Explicit finding: **"no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter."** Milk whole, scales honest.
    Opinion: wet hay of Hessel Bottom thins the cream; remedy lies with the land, not the station.
  - Signed: "NYLE GRIGG, chairman, and the committee" (other members unnamed in this text).
  - Report "received and accepted," question "closed... for eleven years" — i.e., 1908 to 1919.
  - **1919 covering letter (same document, r15):** Grigg reprints his own 1908 report because "the
    Ostrey Hollow question having been reopened this season by men who were boys when we made it."
    Grigg states he had no personal interest (not a patron of that station; hauls to Larrow Green
    himself; had bought hay from two Hessel Bottom men). Adds a new corroborating fact: **the State
    Agricultural College at Farland has since published its Bulletin No. 214**, a controlled feeding
    trial on bog hay, finding the bog hay **depressed the butter fat test by two tenths of a point
    (0.2) in winter feeding** — published "three years after" the 1908 report (i.e., roughly 1911).
    Grigg treats this as independent, disinterested, scientific vindication of the committee's 1908
    finding. Remedy prescribed: drain, sweeten and lime the bottom, keep stock off it in wet spring,
    feed the coarse hay mixed rather than alone.

## MAJOR SOURCE — Retelling 19, "The Ashlin Chronicle," front page, "Old Ordell Wrong Righted,"
## 3 October 1958

A county weekly's feature story, explicitly described as "put together in a day and a half from a
college press release and two telephone calls" — i.e., a fast, secondhand, non-specialist account
of Bulletin 471's release, valuable chiefly for facts it states as direct quotation/citation from
the bulletin or from its named interviewee (Ivy Keddie), less valuable for its own editorializing.

- **Bulletin No. 471** is dated **October 1958**, authored by two named people: **Professor Leland
  Vaught** (State Agricultural College at Farland, department of dairy husbandry) and **Miss Ivy
  Keddie**, a graduate student there.
- **IVY KEDDIE'S RELATIONSHIP TO ANSEL KEDDIE — DIRECTLY STATED BUT NOW CONTRADICTED BY A SECOND,
  LATER SOURCE:** r19 states: "The man the bulletin clears is Ansel Keddie, and Miss Keddie is his
  **great-niece**." This is an explicit, unambiguous statement from a named contemporary news
  source, quoting/paraphrasing facts established by the bulletin's own press release and
  (implicitly) confirmed by telephone interview with Ivy Keddie herself. **NEW CONFLICT (r21,
  Cleve Oram, 1977):** Oram calls Ivy Keddie simply "the granddaughter" of the dismissed tester
  ("Then the granddaughter came back. That was the winter of fifty-eight, and she went up that
  ladder and brought the crate down herself... She was a Keddie and she was schooled at Farland.
  Ivy Keddie."). This is now a genuine, unresolved conflict for B1: **"great-niece" (r19, 1958,
  closer to the event, apparently sourced from the bulletin's press release and/or a phone
  interview with Ivy herself) vs. "granddaughter" (r21, Oram, 1977, an elderly community member's
  secondhand recollection 19 years after the event, offered casually and without any claim of
  special authority on genealogy).** r19 has the better claim to accuracy (closer to the event,
  closer to a primary source), but r21 is not obviously unreliable elsewhere on other facts it
  independently corroborates (station structure, hearing dates, award figure notwithstanding its
  own $3,500 deviation, Keddie's death). On balance, "great-niece" (r19) should be preferred as the
  better-sourced answer, with "granddaughter" (r21) flagged as a live, unresolved contradiction —
  not dismissed outright, because Oram is a good-faith, detailed, locally embedded witness on most
  other points. If Ansel Keddie's own son (Duncan Keddie, r18, writing 1968) is a full generation
  above Ivy, "great-niece" (child of a niece/nephew of Ansel — i.e., great-grandchild-of-Ansel's-
  parents' generation via a sibling's line) is structurally more plausible than "granddaughter"
  given Ivy's stated age of 27 in 1958 and Ansel's death in 1934 after "a lifetime" at the station
  (implying he was already an older man in 1925) — but this reasoning is inference, not sourced
  fact, and should be labeled as such.
- **Ivy Keddie, biographical:** age 27 (as of Oct 1958), took her first degree at Farland and
  stayed on for graduate work; grew up in Ashlin, where her father taught school for many years.
  Spent "the better part of this year" (1958) on the investigation, in her own time and largely at
  her own expense.
- **The core mechanical finding — the actual glassware defect, quantified:** Ivy Keddie carried the
  condemned crate east to Farland; there were eleven pipettes in a condition to be measured; in
  March 1958 she measured them one by one against a calibrated burette in a college laboratory.
  **Every one of them was blown on the stem to deliver 17.6 c.c.** — "the standard charge of milk
  for a butter fat test, the charge the graduated neck of the test bottle is ruled for." **Not one
  delivered it. The average of the lot came to 16.27 c.c.** Mechanism, stated plainly: "A short
  measure of milk goes into a bottle whose neck is marked for a full one, and the fat reads low in
  the same proportion, and it reads low every single time, for as long as you go on using that
  glass. Nobody watered anything. Nobody stole anything. The scales were true, the acid was right,
  the cows were ordinary, and a piece of glass out of a crate was wrong." **This is the key
  quantitative datum for Section C1(a): the proportional shortfall is (17.6 − 16.27) / 17.6 = 1.33 /
  17.6 ≈ 0.0756, i.e., about 7.56% (roughly 1/13) low, uniformly and consistently, for every test
  made with that glass over its ~26 years of service (spring 1897 – June 1923).** Equivalently,
  every recorded reading was about 92.44% of the true value (16.27/17.6 ≈ 0.9244), so true value =
  recorded value × (17.6/16.27) ≈ recorded × 1.0817.
  **NEW COMPLICATION ON PIPETTE COUNTS (r21, Cleve Oram):** Oram describes the same crate
  physically, from his own boyhood memory of the loft: "Five dozen of those measures went into
  that crate to begin with — that was the whole outfit the station was set up with — and there
  were nine of them left in it at the end, because a thing in a loft for thirty-five years gets
  broken by everybody who goes up after wire." This gives TWO numbers that conflict with the
  documentary record: (a) "five dozen" (60) pipettes "to begin with," vs. the Ammon & Sons invoice
  (r20) and the Ashlin Chronicle (r19), which both independently and consistently state **six
  dozen (72) pipettes** were purchased in 1897; and (b) "nine... left in it at the end" vs. r19's
  statement that Ivy Keddie found "eleven pipettes in a condition to be measured" in 1958. Oram's
  figures are a boyhood/old-age recollection (he is recalling, at 77, what he saw "a hundred
  times" as a child and young man, not a count made at time of discovery) and are not as reliable
  as the invoice (a primary business document, quoted twice independently: by the Ashlin Chronicle
  reporting on the bulletin, and by Ammon & Sons' own history reproducing the original paper).
  **Six dozen (72) is the well-documented original count; eleven measurable in 1958 is the
  well-documented surviving/testable count; Oram's "five dozen" and "nine" should be treated as an
  imprecise, contradicted secondhand recollection.** The "thirty-five years" figure Oram gives for
  how long the crate sat in the loft (1923 condemnation to 1958 discovery = 35 years) is
  internally consistent and correct, which suggests Oram's chronological sense is generally sound
  even where his quantities are off — worth noting as a nuance in weighing his reliability.
- **Cost/money facts, directly from the bulletin as reported:**
  - Patrons' uncredited butterfat, converted to money by the bulletin: **$19,600** (see conflict
    discussion above — this is Bulletin 471's own figure per this source, matching the patrons'
    1925 petition figure and superseding the "$19,000" figure attributed to Bulletin 471 elsewhere).
  - Original 1897 equipment cost: pipettes, test bottles, an acid measure, a hand tester, and

    source's prose (see also r20's reproduced invoice, which itemizes to $77.93 — an unresolved
    internal inconsistency within r20, discussed above).
  - The pipettes specifically: six dozen (72) came in the crate, at **35 cents apiece** ($0.35 ×
    72 = $25.20 of the $77.39/$77.93 total) — this exact line item (6 doz. pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at
    .35 = 25.20) is independently confirmed verbatim by the Ammon & Sons invoice itself (r20).
  - These went into use "in the spring of 1897" (r19) / "on the first of May, 1897" (r20, Ammon's
    own narrative) and "were still in use in 1923" — 26 years of continuous service on the same

- **The condemnation, 1923:** an unnamed state dairy inspector, "on his rounds," ran the station's

## NEW SOURCE — Retelling 20, Junia Ammon, "Sixty Years of Ammon & Sons," chapter nine of the
## firm's privately printed history, Tarnet, 1949 (see detailed content interleaved above)

Written by the firm's own historian (age 60), reproducing two original documents in facsimile: the
14 April 1897 invoice to the Larrow Valley Co-op. Creamery Assn. for the Ostrey Hollow station
(itemized above), and Ammon & Sons' own 2 July 1923 reply letter to the Association's 28 June 1923
reorder. Firm founded by Marcus Ammon ("a rented floor with two men and a secondhand annealing
oven"); by Junia's adulthood the firm filled "four stories and a basement on the street behind the
railroad yard at Tarnet." At its height supplied "half the creameries in the state" with test
glassware (not churns/vats, which came from "the works at the coast"). The firm's works records
(what glass, what gauge, what man, what day — the shop-floor production records that might have
identified the specific defective lot/mold) **burned in a fire in 1931** that took the old third
floor; only the office side (invoices, letter books, day books of amounts owed) survives from the
1890s. Junia Ammon's own theory of the defect's cause (explicitly her own speculation, not a
documented finding): a reground mold that "ran short," an accident nobody in the works caught,
because a consistent defect passes any spot-check that only samples a few pieces from a
consistent (but wrongly-calibrated) lot. She frames this candidly as the firm's own "bad morning"
and criticizes her own firm's 1923 reply letter as "a thin thing to send to a customer of
twenty-six years' standing." (See "NOTABLE INTERNAL CONTRADICTION IN r20" above re: the Ordell
County sales-timing inconsistency.)

## NEW SOURCE — Retelling 21, Cleve Oram, "cream hauler's son," recorded at Larrow Green, 1977

Taped in the back room of the Larrow Green feed store, spring 1977; Oram was 77 years old (**born
1900**, stated directly: "I was born in nineteen hundred and I have not lived five miles from this
chair since"). His father drove the Ostrey Hollow cream wagon down to Larrow Green for "better
than twenty years"; Oram rode along "from the time [he] could sit up" and began going regularly at
about age nine, i.e., around **1909**. Note: this retelling was found buried at "Chapter 10" inside
the noise file r21-long.md, itself embedded among fake gossip transcripts, a fake 1873–1877
country-store ledger, a fake API specification, and gibberish prose about invented characters —
the same concealment pattern used for other real retellings in this material.

- **Cream wagon and route:** two bay horses, a flat wagon with a rack for the cans, canvas cover in
  July, none in winter. Seven miles from Larrow Green up to the Hollow, climbing the whole way; a
  bad pitch about two miles below the hamlet, past where a creek comes in from the north — walked
  by the horses empty, crawled loaded. March mud to the hub; ruts froze nightly in the wheel-shapes,
  making the second week of March worse than the first. Oram's mother could allegedly tell the
  month "by how my father swore coming in the door."
- **Station building, described from a boy's-eye view:** board building, two floors, a shingle roof
  "that wanted work the whole time I knew it." Covered platform, waist-high, on the road side, for
  backing wagons up to unload cans without lifting. Behind the platform: the intake room, with the
  scale, the vat, and the separator "going in the back of it" — smell of "warm milk and steam and
  machine oil." Skim returned to farmers via the same platform (for hogs); cream went into the
  hauler's cans. Over the intake room: a loft, reached by a ladder (not a stair) nailed to the wall
  by the door, with a plank-and-rope trap door at top. The loft held "sacks, a broken churn, wire,
  the kind of thing a building collects" — and the crate.
- **THE CRATE — Oram's own physical description and count (see conflict discussion above re: "five
  dozen"/"nine" vs. the documented "six dozen"/"eleven"):** a crate under a tarpaulin, the station's
  condemned glass, "put up there when the state man made them throw it out." Oram: "I have seen it
  a hundred times, and I have put my hand on it... I would not have been able to tell you what was
  in it if I had not been up there after a hay fork with my own hands." Sat in the loft roughly 35
  years (1923–1958), an interval Oram states correctly.
- **The 1925 hearing — new structural detail (see above):** three sittings; one at the Cooperative's
  office in Ashlin (because "that is where the lawyer was and lawyers do not travel"); two at the
  station itself, in the weigh room (the intake room), because the books, scales, and machinery were
  all there. Oram, then 25 (self-consistent with his 1900 birth year), stood outside on the platform
  in the rain during one afternoon sitting, with about a dozen others, able to hear voices but not
  words; the condemned crate sat in the loft "twenty feet, if that" above the hearing the entire
  time — a detail Oram says he only came to appreciate "since," not "then."
- **Award — CONFLICTING FIGURE:** Oram states the award was **"thirty-five hundred dollars"
  ($3,500)**, sourced to his father's memory ("my father was not a man who got a figure wrong when
  it was money") — conflicting with the well-documented $3,800 figure from three other independent
  sources (see Award discussion above). "No admission of anything by anybody."
- **Dismissal of the tester ("Keddie"):** described physically — "a long dry man, very quiet,"
  canvas apron with strap over one shoulder, held test bottles up to the window with arm
  outstretched. Dismissed at the hearing; Oram: "I was not frightened of him and most of the boys
  were." After dismissal, Keddie "went home... and stayed home," kept four or five cows on
  insufficient ground, did carpentry (hung a barn door for the Oram family "and it swung true for
  thirty years"), and "died in the thirties." Oram attended the funeral: "well attended and nobody
  said much, which tells you something."
- **Oram's father's own independent suspicion, predating the 1958 explanation:** the hauler
  "always said there was less of it [cream] coming off that platform than went onto it," said

  face and he never said it under oath, because he was not asked." Oram: "They say now it was the
  glass. I have no quarrel with the college. I am only telling you what the man who drove the wagon
  thought, and he was the one man in the valley who saw both ends of that cream every day of his
  life." This is a distinct, independent line of suspicion (the hauler believed something was being
  lost between platform and destination) that turned out — per the 1958 investigation — to have an
  innocent, mechanical explanation, not deliberate wrongdoing by Keddie.
- **1958 return of "the granddaughter"/Ivy Keddie:** "That was the winter of fifty-eight, and she
  went up that ladder and brought the crate down herself, and after that there was a great deal of
  writing about it." Butterfat figure: "Sixty-seven thousand pounds... over the whole run of years,

- **IVY KEDDIE'S RELATIONSHIP TO HAZEL BRANT — DIRECTLY ANSWERS B2, NEW:** "She was a Keddie and

  **Ivy Keddie was Hazel Brant's first cousin once removed**, that is the way of it, and the two of
  them not much alike to look at." This is a direct, explicit, named statement answering B2. It is

## CROSS-CUTTING PATTERN NOTED THIS SEGMENT

Family-relationship terms continue to be the least reliable category of fact across retellings —
now a FOUR-WAY pattern rather than three: (1) Selby Vose = son (r08, r18) vs. nephew (r12/Rundle);
(2) Rosalie Cudd = daughter (r12/Rundle, r18) vs. niece (r19); (3) Ivy Keddie = great-niece (r19)
vs. granddaughter (r21/Oram) of Ansel Keddie; (4) new, vaguer: Vose–Keddie family connection
asserted only by r21/Oram ("cousins of some sort"), otherwise unstated in any source read so far.
Numeric/quantity facts sourced to elderly secondhand oral memory (Hazel Brant's tape, r17; Cleve
Oram's tape, r21) are consistently the least reliable category next to relationship terms — compare
Oram's "five dozen"/"nine" pipettes against the documented "six dozen"/"eleven," and Oram's "$3,500"
award against three independent sources' "$3,800." By contrast, quoted primary documents (the
Ammon & Sons invoice and letter, r20; Bulletin 471 excerpts, r07/r11; the Grigg 1908/1919 report,
r15) and precise, internally-checkable personal anchors (e.g., Oram's own birth year fixing his age
at the 1925 hearing) remain the most reliable category and should be weighted accordingly when
resolving conflicts.
