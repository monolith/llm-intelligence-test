## THE EIGHT UNDERLYING STORIES — reconstruction context (Larrow Valley / Ostrey Hollow, Ordell
## County)

The material is 24 retellings (r01–r24) of eight original underlying stories centered on the
Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (founded 1889) and the defective Ostrey Hollow
skimming station. Many retellings are buried as the single real document inside thousands of
lines of generated noise (fake API specs, day-book ledgers, gossip transcripts among invented
characters, "gibberish" pseudo-prose).

## MAJOR SOURCE — Retelling 12, Dell Rundle, "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years" (jubilee
## pamphlet, Larrow Green, 1939, reprinted 1959 with one added footnote and one added note)

Compiled by the valley schoolteacher "in pieces, at funerals and in the back of the store," over
two winters of asking questions — an institutional/valley-official memory source, useful as a
structural spine for Section A but secondhand on points where family sources (r09, r10, r17, r18)
are firsthand.

- **1889 — founding:** farmers met at the Larrow Green schoolhouse in cold weather and organized
  the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking
  whatever the merchant allowed. Anecdote: the meeting ran very late and somebody's horse got loose.
- **Gideon Vose elected first president** — "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of
  the law and thought better of it." Wrote the by-laws himself at his own kitchen table.
- **SELBY VOSE'S RELATION TO GIDEON VOSE — NOW LARGELY RESOLVED in favor of "son."** Rundle
  writes: *"His nephew Selby Vose, afterward manager of the Association, used to say that his uncle
  wrote by-laws the way other men build stone wall — slowly, and to last."* This calls Selby Vose
  Gideon Vose's **nephew**, quoting Selby's own habitual words ("his uncle"). This directly
  contradicted r08, where Selby Vose is quoted in his own first-person words calling Gideon Vose "my
  father": *"My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law..."*
  **NEW (r18, Duncan Keddie, 1968):** Duncan states plainly and deliberately, as a correction of a
  recurring error: *"Selby Vose, who managed the Association, was Gideon Vose's son. Gideon
  organized the Association and was its first president and wrote its by-laws; Selby was his son
  and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew."* This is a second independent, named, dated
  source explicitly asserting "son" and explicitly rejecting "nephew" as a known recurring error —
  matching r08's first-person quote. **Weighing the evidence: two independent sources (r08's direct
  first-person quote from Selby himself, and r18's explicit named correction) say "son"; one source
  (r12/Rundle, secondhand, "used to say... his uncle") says "nephew."** Rundle's account may
  reflect the same kind of "confusion" Duncan Keddie describes elsewhere (people confusing the
  Ostrey Hollow Voses with unrelated "Ashlin Voses," and loose talk about cousin relations — see
  below), i.e., a documented pattern of the valley getting Vose family relations wrong. On the
  weight of two independent, named, direct-or-corrective sources against one secondhand,
  habitual-phrase attribution, **"son" should now be treated as the better-supported answer for
  B3, with "nephew" (Rundle) noted as a contradicted minority account, not a live 50/50 split.**
- **1891 — creamery built** at Larrow Green, on the creek below the mill; churn set up; valley paid
  in cash monthly for the first time.
- **Article VII, quoted in full:** *"Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according
  to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to
  the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."* Dated to
  **1891**, same year as the churn. Rundle calls it "a just rule" in principle but stresses the last
  clause — "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" (not the creamery's test) — as
  "the sentence on which the whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned." This is the
  mechanism that let the defective Ostrey Hollow station's own readings stand unchallenged for
  26 years. r13's Frick recollections restate this same rule in his own words as "the complaint."
  **CONFLICT (r15, Nyle Grigg's 1919 letter):** Grigg states "Payment by test came in with the
  1893 meeting, as I have always understood it" — a THIRD-hand but explicit date of **1893** for
  the adoption of payment-by-test, conflicting with Rundle's dated **1891** Article VII. Grigg
  qualifies it himself ("as I have always understood it"), suggesting even he treats it as
  secondhand/uncertain. Flag as an open discrepancy: 1891 (Rundle, quoting the article text itself)
  vs. 1893 (Grigg, secondhand impression). Rundle's is closer to a primary documentary citation and
  is preferred, but not by an overwhelming margin.
- **Ostrey Hollow station, opened 1896:** seven miles up from the Green (r13 and r14 both
  independently confirm "seven miles"); pull down was three hours in mud, impossible in March.
  Milk weighed, tested, separated on the spot; cream went down to the Green by wagon; skim went
  home to calves/pigs same morning. Building: plain, boarded/battened, intake room at road end,
  loft above (never cleared out since built); creek behind it has twice come over the sill in wet
  April.
  - r14 (Hobart Sill, buttermaker, 1934 retirement remarks): station opened specifically in **May
    1896** ("that was ninety-six, in May"). At opening it had no glassware/testing outfit of its
    own; equipment ordered but not arrived; Sill made up a temporary testing outfit out of his own
    glass at Larrow Green and sent it up the valley so the station could open on schedule. Station
    used Sill's borrowed glass through its first season. **Station's own equipment arrived "the
    spring after"** (spring 1897), when Sill's glass was returned (one piece chipped).
  - **CONFLICT (r17, Hazel Brant's 1981 tape) — a THIRD date for the station's own glassware
    going into service:** Hazel Brant says of the (later-condemned) glassware: *"The crate came out
    from Tarnet in ninety-nine"* — i.e., **1899**, not spring 1897 as per r14/Sill. This is a
    genuine, unresolved conflict between two named, on-the-record witnesses (Sill, an active
    buttermaker with 41 years of daily involvement and signed receiving-book entries, vs. Hazel
    Brant, recalling family lore from her own — later — vantage as a family/local historian). Sill's
    account is more contemporaneous and professionally grounded; Hazel's account is secondhand
    (from her father/grandfather) and given 80+ years after the fact on tape where she explicitly
    disclaims her own reliability on years ("Years I have not"). Spring 1897 (r14) is preferred as
    the better-grounded figure, but 1899 (r17) should be carried forward as an open discrepancy,
    not silently discarded — it may reflect a *second* order of glassware (a replacement/upgrade)
    rather than the original.
  - r14: station's first season (partial: May 1896–end of year) took in "about nine hundred
    thousand pounds" of milk.
- **From 1902:** Hollow men complain milk tests lower at Ostrey Hollow than same-class milk at the
  Green — not believed (matches r09's 1902 date and Emmet Brant as an early complainer). r14
  independently corroborates 1902 as the date Sill first remembers hearing complaints.
  r17 (Hazel Brant) identifies "Emmet Brant" as her own grandfather — a Hessel Bottom farmer,
  patron of the Ostrey Hollow station, who began the family weigh-book on 1 January 1900 (per
  Hazel's tape).
  **NEW CONFLICT ON WEIGH-BOOK START DATE (r18, Duncan Keddie):** Duncan Keddie states in Section V
  of his memoir, precisely: *"The Brant weigh book. Emmet Brant began it on the first of January,
  1897, and kept it until his death in 1929..."* — i.e., **1897**, not 1 January 1900 as reported
  via Hazel Brant's tape (r17, per notes-11). This is a clean three-year discrepancy on a specific,
  dateable fact (the founding date of a document both sources independently discuss in detail).
  Both sources are members of the same extended family with direct access to the book's history:
  Duncan Keddie is Emmet's sister's son (Roy Brant's first cousin) writing in 1968 with the express
  purpose of getting family facts "right on paper," citing exact hand-off dates for five different
  documents; Hazel Brant is Emmet's granddaughter (Roy's daughter) speaking in 1981 from family
  memory. Neither source is obviously more authoritative — Duncan's account is more systematic and
  document-focused (he treats this as historical record-keeping), Hazel's is oral family history.
  **Flag as a live, unresolved discrepancy: 1897 (Duncan Keddie, r18) vs. 1900 (Hazel Brant, r17).**
- **1908:** committee under **Nyle Grigg** sampled Hessel Bottom herds through the winter (matches
  r09). r13 (Frick) independently confirms this "hay" explanation was put before the 1925
  arbitration board. r15 (this document's 1919 covering letter, reprinting Grigg's 1908 report in
  full) gives the primary text:
  - Committee "appointed in January last" (i.e., January 1908), reporting to "the President and
    Directors of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association."
  - Complaint: Ostrey Hollow milk tests below the rest of the valley; patrons paid less for same
    labor/cows as neighbors.
  - **Hessel Bottom described precisely: "six farms cut hay and turn out stock"** — a wet meadow
    along the creek under Ostrey Hollow, springy year-round, under water in part every spring;
    herbage is sedge, rush, coarse sour grasses; hay cut late and heavy, fed as the main winter
    dependence (November–April) on those farms.
  - Committee visited each of the six farms, some three or four times; drew and sealed their own
    samples; watched herds milked, cans filled and hauled, tests made; tried station scales against
    a known weight and patrons' scales against the station's; found nothing to remark on in the
    testing itself; inquired into water supply at each barn.
  - Observed herd condition: thin in the flank at winter's end, slow to condition in spring, rougher
    coats. Conclusion: **"a cow cannot put into her pail what her keep has never put into her"** —
    wet land gives quantity (milk volume) but withholds quality (fat).
  - Explicit finding: **"no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter."** Milk whole, scales honest.
    Opinion: wet hay of Hessel Bottom thins the cream; remedy lies with the land, not the station.
  - Signed: "NYLE GRIGG, chairman, and the committee" (other members unnamed in this text).
  - Report "received and accepted," question "closed... for eleven years" — i.e., 1908 to 1919.
  - **1919 covering letter (same document, r15):** Grigg reprints his own 1908 report because "the
    Ostrey Hollow question having been reopened this season by men who were boys when we made it."
    Grigg states he had no personal interest (not a patron of that station; hauls to Larrow Green
    himself; had bought hay from two Hessel Bottom men). Adds a new corroborating fact: **the State
    Agricultural College at Farland has since published its Bulletin No. 214**, a controlled feeding
    trial on bog hay, finding the bog hay **depressed the butter fat test by two tenths of a point
    (0.2) in winter feeding** — published "three years after" the 1908 report (i.e., roughly 1911).
    Grigg treats this as independent, disinterested, scientific vindication of the committee's 1908
    finding. Remedy prescribed: drain, sweeten and lime the bottom, keep stock off it in wet spring,
    feed the coarse hay mixed rather than alone.
- **1919:** manager circulated a warning to Hollow patrons against adding water to their milk; after
  that the two ends of the valley "did not speak civilly to one another for six years" (1919–1925,
  consistent with the 1925 arbitration). r14 independently confirms the 1919 circular. r17 (Hazel
  Brant) describes the "water letter" from the receiving end, with a precise figure: the manager
  sent the letter to **nine families** on Hessel Bottom specifically (six farms, but "a farm can
  hold more than one family and generally did," hence nine families on six farms). Not one family
  replied — "answered by none of them, which in that valley is a shout" (collective silent
  protest). Hazel's mother read her copy twice and burned it in the stove; Hazel (a small child at
  the time) remembers the stove itself, not the letter's content.
- **Glassware condemned — THREE-WAY DATE CONFLICT:** Rundle gives **"the twelfth of July, 1923."**
  r07 Item 15 and r11 Section VII (Bulletin 471, quoted verbatim identically in both) give
  **"the twelfth of June, 1923."** Since r07 and r11 agree word-for-word (quoting the same primary
  bulletin), "June 1923" is preferred as better-attested; "July 1923" is treated as Rundle's error
  or a reprint slip. r17 (Hazel Brant) gives a THIRD, materially different date: Hazel says plainly,
  "The state man condemned that glass in **twenty-one**" — i.e., **1921**, not 1923 at all — an
  unnamed state inspector ("the state man") went at the glass "with some measuring instrument he
  carried in his coat," found it would not deliver what it was marked for, and told the Association
  to order new glass ("They ordered new. Nobody said one word about all the tests that were already
  in the book" — i.e., no retroactive correction was made at the time).
  **NEW CORROBORATING DETAIL (r18, Duncan Keddie) — the crate's subsequent fate, dated precisely:**
  Duncan states the station's condemned glassware ("the crate") "went up into the loft over the
  intake room in **1923**" — consistent with the June/July 1923 dating (r07/r11/Rundle), not the
  1921 date Hazel gave — "and stayed there until Ivy found it in **February of 1958**." This is an
  independent data point (from Ansel Keddie's own son, who would have direct family knowledge of
  when his father's station equipment was retired) supporting **1923** over Hazel's 1921. Given
  Bulletin 471 is a documentary primary source quoted identically by two independent retellings,
  plus this new corroboration, **1923 (June) is now the best-attested date**, with 1921 (Hazel,
  secondhand family memory, imprecise on years by her own admission) carried forward as a weaker,
  contradicted minority account.
- **January 1925:** Hollow patrons petitioned the Association. r13 (Frick) independently confirms
  January 1925 as the petition date, and adds that "the by-laws provided for arbitration, and the
  officers... did not resist it."
- **Total milk intake, CONFLICTS with established 40,000,000 lb:** patrons' 1925 petition claimed
  the station took in, "over the years complained of," **forty-four million pounds** of milk. Not
  necessarily the same 25-year window (1898–1922) used in the pipette-defect 76,000 lb calculation.
  Flagged as a genuine discrepancy, carried forward, not substituted for the multiply-corroborated
  40,000,000 figure.
- **Patrons' claimed loss, CONFLICTS (closely) with established $19,000:** patrons' 1925 petition
  set their loss at **$19,600 "to the dollar."** Distinct from the $19,000 figure computed
  retrospectively in Bulletin 471 (1958, per r07 Item 6). Bears on Section C1(d) "fraction actually
  received": using $19,600, $3,800/$19,600 = 19.4%; using $19,000, $3,800/$19,000 = 20% (1/5). Flag
  both.
- **Third loss claim, in butterfat pounds (r13, contemporary 1925 arbitration record):** Alonzo
  Frick's own arbitration-exhibit article states the patrons' "own reckoning of what they had lost
  was **sixty-seven thousand pounds of butter fat** over the years complained of, and nothing was
  ever brought forward to disturb that figure." A THIRD figure for the patrons' claimed shortfall
  (67,000 lb), conflicting with the previously-established 76,000 lb butterfat shortfall figure
  (apparently derived from the 1958 pipette-defect/burette-test calculation in Bulletin 471). Both
  independently and directly sourced; not the same calculation and need not agree. For Section
  C1(c) this is a live discrepancy: 76,000 lb (retrospective/pipette-based) vs. 67,000 lb (patrons'
  own 1925 contemporary claim). Show both, explain the difference in method and date.
- **Arbitrator:** 1925 board of arbitration sat at Ashlin under **Alonzo Frick, attorney, of that
  town.** "It heard everybody. It found nothing established, it made an award to the patrons
  without admitting anything, and it sent this valley home to live with itself." Award amount
  established elsewhere as $3,800 (per r07 Item 7); r13, Frick's own first-person account, confirms
  the $3,800 award directly and with full context (see below).
  **Hearing date — CONFLICT (r17, Hazel Brant):** Hazel dates "the hearing" to **1926**, not 1925.
  This conflicts with Frick's own precise, firsthand chairman account (three sittings, starting
  17 March 1925). Frick's account should be preferred as a quoted/documented primary source
  outranking a secondhand family memory recorded 55+ years later by someone who explicitly
  disclaims her own reliability on years. **NEW CORROBORATION FOR 1925 (r18, Duncan Keddie):**
  Duncan states his father "was dismissed on the thirtieth of April, 1925, four days before the
  board handed down what it called its finding" — i.e., the board's finding was handed down on or
  about **4 May 1925**. This is a precise, independent date from Ansel's own son, strongly
  corroborating Frick's 1925 dating (board sat starting 17 March 1925, concluded finding by early
  May 1925) and further undermining Hazel Brant's 1926 date. **1925 should now be treated as firmly
  established; 1926 (Hazel) is a contradicted, weakly-sourced minority account.**
  **Practical outcome for Ansel Keddie — now precisely dated (r17 + r18 together):** Hazel Brant
  (r17) reports her father Roy came back from the hearing and said the board "had heard everybody
  and found nothing" (nearly verbatim matching Frick's own words as recorded by Rundle) — AND that
  "they had let Ansel go, which was the finding whatever they wrote in the book." **r18 now gives
  the exact date: Ansel Keddie was dismissed 30 April 1925, four days before the official written
  finding (which admitted no wrongdoing) was announced.** I.e., the Association fired its tester
  in anticipation of the board's report, while the report itself, when it came, found nothing
  established — an important and now precisely dated irony/injustice for the "Theory" material
  (batch 3). Also: Roy Brant (Hazel's father) "never went to another meeting of that Association as
  long as he lived, and he was a man who liked meetings" — a detail on lasting valley alienation.
- **Section V, "Over the Ridge":** Alder Corners Cheese Company "opened" in **1914** per Rundle —
  matching r09's 1914 date, conflicting with r10's firsthand family account (Jerome Cudd's daughter
  Rosalie) that the factory was built in **1913**. r17 (Hazel Brant) confirms the cheese factory
  arrangement independently — "Four of those six [Hessel Bottom] men hauled over the ridge in the
  summer flush, to the cheese factory on the far side, and brought their milk back down to our own
  station the rest of the year" — but gives no date, so does not resolve the 1913 vs. 1914
  conflict.
  Also: "It is Jerome Cudd's daughter, Rosalie Cudd, who makes the cheese at Alder Corners now" —
  stated in the ORIGINAL 1939 text. **r18 (Duncan Keddie) independently confirms Rosalie Cudd's
  parentage precisely**, in his genealogy-correction section: "Rosalie Cudd of Vessey County, who
  ran the cheese company over the ridge after her father, was Jerome Cudd's daughter" — and in
  Section V (books) states she "took the factory on" in **1941** (when her father's test book
  passed to her) — a new, precisely dated fact for when Rosalie assumed control (distinct from the
  1913/1914 founding-date conflict, which concerns Jerome's original founding, not Rosalie's later
  succession).
- **FOOTNOTE added to the 1959 reprint:** "The Hollow matter has since been taken up again at the
  college at Farland by Miss Ivy Keddie, who is second cousin to Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom.
  **Eleven pipettes** out of the station's old condemned crate were found still to be in existence.
  I am too old to rewrite a pamphlet and I refer the reader to the college. — D.R." Independently
  corroborates the Ivy Keddie / Hazel Brant second-cousin relationship — **r18 (Duncan Keddie) now
  gives the full, precise, first-person genealogical derivation of exactly why they are second
  cousins** (see "Duncan Keddie's memoir" section below — this is now the single best, most
  authoritative source for Section B, being a direct, dated, deliberate genealogical correction by
  a family member one generation closer to the root of the tree than Hazel's own account).
  **ODDITY/possible conflict, unchanged:** "eleven pipettes... found still to be in existence"
  versus the established "nine surviving pipettes" that were burette-tested. Flag as a discrepancy
  in the physical evidence. **r18 now gives a precise date for this discovery: February 1958**
  (see below) — Ivy found the crate in the station loft in February 1958, sent it to Farland to be
  measured, and it came back in 1959 to be deposited (with the test book) at the Ordell County
  Historical Rooms.
- **"NOTE ADDED WHILE IN PRESS"** — Rundle's ledger-based retraction of her own theft/dilution
  theory (Chapter IV), using the recurring annual ledger line: *"Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited
  to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream,
  60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual."* Arithmetic check:
  60,800 − 57,760 = 3,040 lb; 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.264% ≈ "five and a quarter per cent" — internally
  consistent. A contemporary, 1939, firsthand logical disproof of the theft/water-dilution theory,
  19 years before the 1958 pipette-defect explanation was identified.
  r14 (Sill) independently corroborates the same "cream ran heavy" pattern from his own 41 years of
  daily receiving-book entries — but Sill himself never drew Rundle's conclusion and held the
  water-theft theory to his 1934 retirement despite personally witnessing the very fact that
  disproves it. Retained as a notable irony/contradiction for the "Theory" material (batch 3).
- **Closing Chapter VI ("Fifty Years")** — valedictory color, no new hard facts.

## MAJOR SOURCE — Retelling 13, Alonzo Frick, "Recollections of the Ordell Arbitration," article
## for the yearbook of the Ordell County Bar Association, 1946

Written by the attorney who chaired the 1925 board of arbitration, at age 74 (born c. 1872). A
primary, named, official/legal source — very high authority for the figures and dates it states
directly.

- **The rule restated independently:** matches Article VII / Rundle's account exactly.
- **Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green.** Patrons "had been paid, year upon
  year, on an average test of **3.16**," while the rest of the valley tested better, "the difference
  holds steady for a quarter of a century" (~25 years) — the RECORDED average test at the station.
  (See "Hazel Brant's tape" section for a specific patron-level test figure — 3.42 for Emmet
  Brant's farm in 1910 — which is higher than this 3.16 station-wide 25-year average; not
  contradictory, since one is a single farm in a single (apparently good) year and the other is a
  station-wide multi-decade average.)
- **January 1925:** Hollow patrons petitioned the Association (matches Rundle). Board of
  arbitration: three-person, Frick named chairman "I have always supposed because I was the one of
  the three who kept no cow."
- **Sessions:** three sittings. First at the Association's office at **Ashlin**, **17 March 1925**.
  Second and third at **Ostrey Hollow itself**, in the station's own weigh room (r18/Duncan Keddie
  independently notes that two of these three sessions took place literally underneath the loft
  where the condemned, still-uninspected glassware sat hidden for 35 years — "two of the three
  sessions of that arbitration sat in the room underneath it"). Frick (a lawyer, not a chemist)
  could not judge the chemistry itself but saw it "done the same way twice" by men "not hiding
  their hands."

## MAJOR SOURCE (prior segment) — Retelling 15, Nyle Grigg, covering letter of 1919 to the

and are integrated there for continuity). Key standalone facts:

  report, not just a description of it, and should be treated with high evidentiary weight for
  anything it states directly (committee composition, methodology, and conclusion).

  butterfat test depressed by "two tenths" (0.2) in winter feeding, published roughly 1911 ("three

  two separate, independent authors both invoke the same Bulletin No. 214 and both report the same
  "two tenths" (0.2) figure, which is a point of genuine convergence (not just repetition of one
  source, since Grigg and Kepp are writing 7 years apart for different audiences and neither cites
  the other).

## NEW MAJOR SOURCE (this segment) — Retelling 18, Duncan Keddie, "Notes for the family," written
## at Ashlin, 1968 (with an addition of 1970)

A retired schoolteacher, seventy-four years old (matches "born in 1894"), writes for his daughter
and her children "who everyone was and where every book went," because his own daughter (Ivy) "has
spent ten years putting the case right in print and nobody has ever put the family right on paper
at all." Explicitly corrective in purpose: "I have not tried to make anybody in these pages better
than he was, and I have not needed to." This is the single most direct, deliberate source on
family relationships found across all segments so far — written specifically to settle genealogy
disputes. Found as a real document embedded inside r18-long.md (titled in the file as "Chapter 24
— Retelling 18 — Duncan Keddie...") after ~5,700 lines of unrelated fake-API-spec/ledger/gossip/
gibberish noise, and followed by more of the same noise until the file's end at line 7478 ("End of
document.").

**Section I — The people:**
- **Ansel Keddie** (Duncan's father): born **1861**, died **1934**. Tester and separator man at the
  Ostrey Hollow skimming station of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association **from the
  year it opened** (i.e., 1896). "A careful man in small things to a degree that other people found
  trying" — this carefulness "was the making of the record he left and, I have sometimes thought,
  the unmaking of him."
- **Orra Brant** (Duncan's mother): born **1868** (but see arithmetic note below). Married Ansel in
  **1889**. She was **Emmet Brant's sister, the younger of the two**. Emmet farmed the largest
  place on Hessel Bottom and hauled the most milk into the station of any man on the creek. "That
  fact sat in the middle of our family for a generation and hardly anyone outside it ever noticed."
  **When the 1925 allegations said Ansel had been shorting the patrons, the patron he was said to
  have shorted worst was his own wife's brother** — Emmet Brant, who was at the Keddie table every
  Sunday and had been for thirty-six years. (A vivid detail on the personal cost/irony of the
  accusation.)
- **Duncan Keddie**: born **1894**, son of Ansel and Orra. Taught school at Ashlin "the better part
  of my life, arithmetic among other things." Writing this memoir in 1968.
- **Ivy** (Duncan's daughter): born **1931** — explicitly **Ansel Keddie's granddaughter**. Duncan
  states this "plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise and once heard it
  said aloud in this town by a man who ought to have known" — i.e., there is a documented pattern of
  Ivy's relationship to Ansel being misreported/misspoken in the valley, which Duncan is
  deliberately correcting. **This directly and authoritatively answers Section B1: Ivy Keddie is
  Ansel Keddie's granddaughter** (daughter of Ansel's son Duncan), not a niece or any other
  relation, despite apparently having been called something else at least twice in print and once
  aloud.

**Section II — "What the connections are, set out so that they can be copied":**
- Emmet Brant and Orra (Duncan's mother) were brother and sister.
- Emmet's son was **Roy Brant**, who farmed the Hessel Bottom place after him and kept his father's
  weigh book.
- **Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie are therefore first cousins** — "the children of a brother and a
  sister, which is the whole definition and there is nothing more to it."
- Roy's daughter is **Hazel Brant**. Duncan's daughter is **Ivy**.
- **Hazel and Ivy are second cousins: the children of first cousins.** Duncan is explicit and
  emphatic: "Not cousins once removed, which is a different relation and belongs to a different
  generation, and not aunt and niece, which people say because it is shorter." **This directly and
  authoritatively answers Section B2**, matches the Rundle 1959 footnote exactly, and gives the
  full generational chain: Orra Brant Keddie & Emmet Brant (siblings) → Duncan Keddie & Roy Brant
  (first cousins) → Ivy Keddie & Hazel Brant (second cousins). Duncan explicitly flags that
  "aunt and niece" is a commonly-heard but incorrect shorthand people use for this relationship —
  useful in case any other retelling made that error.
- **Selby Vose "was Gideon Vose's son... and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew."** See B3
  discussion above under the Rundle entry — this is the key new evidence resolving that
  contradiction in favor of "son."
- **Rosalie Cudd** of Vessey County, who ran the cheese company over the ridge after her father,
  "was Jerome Cudd's daughter" (confirms the established fact independently).
- **Merle Strawn**, the state's dairy inspector on this circuit, "was kin to nobody in the valley
  whatever — not to us, not to the Brants, not to the Voses, not to anybody on the creek." Duncan
  stresses this because "his notebooks turned out to matter, and it matters that the man who kept
  them had no interest in any of us" — i.e., Strawn's circuit notebooks (which included the 1923
  volume that Ivy found at Farland in 1958) are framed as disinterested, independent evidence,
  precisely because he had no family or economic stake in the valley's factions.

**Section III — "A thing that is said and is not so" (directly answers Section B4, Vose/Keddie-
Brant relationship):**
- **"There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses."** Duncan states people confused
  the Ostrey Hollow-area Voses (Gideon's family) with "the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to
  Mr. Gideon either" — i.e., there were two unrelated Vose families in the area, adding to the
  general confusion.
- He acknowledges a persistent rumor: "I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother
  and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her sit
  down." He explicitly labels this "talk," says "it has been talk since before I could walk," and
  states his mother (Orra) — who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection" (a
  meticulous family genealogist) — "never once put a Vose in it" when reciting who was related to
  whom.
  **Conclusion for B4: no verified family relationship between the Vose family and the
  Keddie/Brant family is established by any source.** A persistent but explicitly unverified and
  explicitly disclaimed rumor exists (via two unnamed townswomen) that Orra Keddie and Gideon
  Vose's wife were cousins (possibly first cousins) — but the person best positioned to know
  (Orra's own son, drawing on his mother's own meticulous genealogical habits) denies it. This
  should be reported as: the sources establish no confirmed Vose–Keddie/Brant kinship; an
  unverified rumor of a marital-side (Gideon's wife, not Gideon himself) cousin relationship to
  Orra Keddie exists but is explicitly and specifically rebutted by a family member.

**Section IV — "My father" (further detail on Ansel Keddie, highly relevant to the "Theory"
material for batch 3, and to dating the arbitration precisely):**
- **Ansel Keddie was dismissed on 30 April 1925, four days before the board handed down its
  finding** — "so that the finding found nothing and the dismissal said everything." He lived nine
  years after it and died in 1934 "with the imputation on him, in this county, among men he had
  weighed milk for since before their fathers died." (1925 + 9 = 1934, internally consistent.)
- **CRITICAL NEW FACT — the defect was privately suspected as early as 1911, fourteen years before
  the arbitration:** "He came home that summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, and he
  never dared put it in the book." Duncan (then 17 — 1894+17=1911, consistent) remembers the kitchen
  scene and that his father said it twice (at supper and again by the door), "which was not his
  way." What Ansel wrote in the station's test book instead was a question mark. "He was a man who
  would not put down what he could not prove... He had, after all, nothing to hold in his hand but a
  suspicion about a piece of glass, and against it stood the outfit he had been taught to trust and
  the practice of every station in the county. So he kept on. He kept on for another fourteen
  years, and wrote down every figure the glass gave him, and the figures were what convicted him."
  (1911 + 14 = 1925, consistent.) **This means Ansel Keddie himself privately suspected the
  glassware defect a full decade before the state inspector's condemnation (1921 per Hazel Brant,
  or 1923 per the better-attested Bulletin 471/Rundle/Duncan-corroborated date) and roughly 14
  years before the arbitration and his own dismissal — but kept recording the (wrong) figures
  because he could not prove the defect and had no authority to deviate from the official
  equipment.** This is a major, previously-uncaptured fact bearing on any "Theory"/culpability
  discussion, and on the human tragedy of the case.
- Orra (mother) outlived Ansel by fourteen years and "never spoke of the business except once a
  year, in April" (presumably around the anniversary of his dismissal/the arbitration). "She was
  eighty-two when she died in 1948." **Arithmetic note/internal inconsistency:** if born in 1868 (as
  Duncan states in Section I) and died in 1948, she would have been 80, not 82 — a two-year
  discrepancy within Duncan Keddie's own account. Minor, but worth flagging as an internal
  inconsistency (either the birth year or the age-at-death is off by two years). She kept every
  letter and left them to Duncan; "there is nothing in them that anybody would want, only the
  weather and the price of things and her brother's [Emmet's] health."

**Section V — "The books, and where they went" (provenance chain for the key documentary evidence;
also gives a precise chronology for Ivy Keddie's 1958 research, which the Rundle 1959 footnote
references):**
- **The station test book:** kept by Ansel Keddie in his own hand from 1896 until he left (i.e.,
  until his April 1925 dismissal). Impounded by the board of arbitration in March 1925. After the
  hearing, went to the Cooperative office at Ashlin, where it lay from 1925 to 1959. In 1959 it went
  to the Ordell County Historical Rooms (in Ashlin), where it remains ("is now").
- **The Brant weigh book:** Emmet Brant began it 1 January **1897** (see CONFLICT note above vs.
  Hazel Brant's 1900 date) and kept it until his death in 1929, when it passed to his son Roy. Roy
  lent it to Ivy in 1958 ("lent, and I want the word" — Duncan stresses it was returned, not given)
  and had it back; at Roy's death in 1962 it passed to his daughter Hazel; Hazel gave it to the
  Historical Rooms in 1963.
- **Strawn's circuit notebooks:** one volume per year Merle Strawn rode the Ordell circuit. Went to
  the state dairy office when he retired in 1936; in 1947 transferred to the library of the State
  Agricultural College at Farland — "which is where Ivy found the volume for 1923 eleven years
  later" (1947+11=1958, consistent). "Nobody had asked for them in the interval."
- **The Alder Corners test book:** kept by Jerome Cudd from the founding of his company; passed to
  his daughter Rosalie in 1941 when she took the factory on; in 1958 she had the figures copied out
  and sent to Ivy at the college. The book itself has never left Vessey County.
- **The crate (the station's condemned glassware):** went up into the loft over the intake room in
  **1923** (see corroboration of the 1923 condemnation date, above) and stayed there until **Ivy
  found it in February 1958**. It went east to Farland to be measured and came back; in 1959 it was
  deposited with the Historical Rooms along with the test book. Duncan's own gloss: "Twenty feet of
  ladder, thirty-five years, and two of the three sessions of that arbitration sat in the room
  underneath it" — i.e., the incriminating glassware sat undiscovered in the loft directly above
  the very room where two of the 1925 arbitration's three sessions were held.
- **Added, 1970 (postscript):** "Mrs. Cudd's [Rosalie's] test book has this year gone to the Vessey
  County Historical Society, and the five books are now in three buildings and are none of them
  ours." (I.e., by 1970, none of the five key documents — station test book, Brant weigh book,
  Strawn's notebooks, Alder Corners test book, and by implication the crate — remain in family
  hands; they are dispersed across at least three institutions: Ordell County Historical Rooms,
  State Agricultural College at Farland, and Vessey County Historical Society.)
- **Chronology this establishes for Ivy Keddie's research (relevant to dating the Rundle 1959
  footnote and the eventual "eleven pipettes" / Bulletin 471 discoveries):** February 1958 — Ivy
  finds the condemned crate in the Ostrey Hollow station loft. 1958 (same year) — Ivy finds
  Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook at the Farland college library; Rosalie Cudd sends Ivy copied
  Alder Corners figures. 1959 — the crate returns from measurement at Farland and is deposited,
  together with the station test book, at the Ordell County Historical Rooms; Rundle's pamphlet is
  reprinted the same year with the footnote referencing Ivy's work and the eleven pipettes.
