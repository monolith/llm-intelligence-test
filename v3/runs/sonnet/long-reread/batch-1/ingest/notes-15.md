## THE EIGHT UNDERLYING STORIES — reconstruction context (Larrow Valley / Ostrey Hollow, Ordell
## County)

The material is 24 retellings (r01–r24) of eight original underlying stories centered on the
Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association (founded 1889) and the defective Ostrey Hollow
skimming station. Many retellings are buried as the single real document inside thousands of
lines of generated noise (fake API specs, day-book ledgers, gossip transcripts among invented
characters, "gibberish" pseudo-prose).

## MAJOR SOURCE — Retelling 12, Dell Rundle, "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years" (jubilee
## pamphlet, Larrow Green, 1939, reprinted 1959 with one added footnote and one added note)

Compiled by the valley schoolteacher "in pieces, at funerals and in the back of the store," over
two winters of asking questions — an institutional/valley-official memory source, useful as a
structural spine for Section A but secondhand on points where family sources (r09, r10, r17, r18)
are firsthand.

- **1889 — founding:** farmers met at the Larrow Green schoolhouse in cold weather and organized
  the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking
  whatever the merchant allowed. Anecdote: the meeting ran very late and somebody's horse got loose.
- **Gideon Vose elected first president** — "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of
  the law and thought better of it." Wrote the by-laws himself at his own kitchen table.
- **SELBY VOSE'S RELATION TO GIDEON VOSE — LARGELY RESOLVED in favor of "son."** Rundle writes:
  *"His nephew Selby Vose, afterward manager of the Association, used to say that his uncle wrote
  by-laws the way other men build stone wall — slowly, and to last."* This calls Selby Vose Gideon
  Vose's **nephew**, quoting Selby's own habitual words ("his uncle"). This directly contradicted
  r08, where Selby Vose is quoted in his own first-person words calling Gideon Vose "my father":
  *"My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law..."* **r18, Duncan Keddie,
  1968** states plainly and deliberately, as a correction of a recurring error: *"Selby Vose, who
  managed the Association, was Gideon Vose's son. Gideon organized the Association and was its
  first president and wrote its by-laws; Selby was his son and not, as I have heard it given, his
  nephew."* This is a second independent, named, dated source explicitly asserting "son" and
  explicitly rejecting "nephew" as a known recurring error — matching r08's first-person quote.
  Weighing the evidence: two independent sources (r08's direct first-person quote from Selby
  himself, and r18's explicit named correction) say "son"; one source (r12/Rundle, secondhand,
  "used to say... his uncle") says "nephew." Rundle's account may reflect the same kind of
  "confusion" Duncan Keddie describes elsewhere (people confusing the Ostrey Hollow Voses with
  unrelated "Ashlin Voses," and loose talk about cousin relations). On the weight of two
  independent, named, direct-or-corrective sources against one secondhand, habitual-phrase
  attribution, **"son" is the better-supported answer for B3**, with "nephew" (Rundle) noted as a
  contradicted minority account.
- **1891 — creamery built** at Larrow Green, on the creek below the mill; churn set up; valley paid
  in cash monthly for the first time.
- **Article VII, quoted in full:** *"Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according
  to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to
  the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."* Dated to
  **1891**, same year as the churn. Rundle calls it "a just rule" in principle but stresses the last
  clause — "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern" — as "the sentence on which the
  whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned." This mechanism let the defective Ostrey
  Hollow station's own readings stand unchallenged for 26 years. r13's Frick recollections restate
  this same rule as "the complaint." **CONFLICT (r15, Nyle Grigg's 1919 letter):** Grigg states
  "Payment by test came in with the 1893 meeting, as I have always understood it" — a THIRD-hand but
  explicit date of **1893**, conflicting with Rundle's dated **1891** Article VII; Grigg himself
  flags it as secondhand/uncertain. Flag as open discrepancy: 1891 (Rundle, quoting the article
  text) preferred over 1893 (Grigg, secondhand impression), but not by an overwhelming margin.
- **Ostrey Hollow station, opened 1896:** seven miles up from the Green (r13 and r14 independently
  confirm "seven miles"); pull down was three hours in mud, impossible in March. Milk weighed,
  tested, separated on the spot; cream went down to the Green by wagon; skim went home to
  calves/pigs same morning. Building: plain, boarded/battened, intake room at road end, loft above
  (never cleared out since built); creek behind it has twice come over the sill in wet April.
  - r14 (Hobart Sill, buttermaker, 1934 retirement remarks): station opened specifically in **May
    1896** ("that was ninety-six, in May"). At opening it had no glassware/testing outfit of its
    own; equipment ordered but not arrived; Sill made up a temporary testing outfit out of his own
    glass at Larrow Green and sent it up the valley so the station could open on schedule. Station
    used Sill's borrowed glass through its first season. **Station's own equipment arrived "the
    spring after"** (spring 1897), when Sill's glass was returned (one piece chipped).
  - **CONFLICT (r17, Hazel Brant's 1981 tape) — a THIRD date for the station's own glassware
    going into service:** Hazel Brant says of the (later-condemned) glassware: *"The crate came out
    from Tarnet in ninety-nine"* — i.e., **1899**, not spring 1897 as per r14/Sill. Sill's account
    is more contemporaneous and professionally grounded; Hazel's is secondhand family lore given
    80+ years after the fact, and she explicitly disclaims her own reliability on years ("Years I
    have not"). Spring 1897 (r14) is preferred, but 1899 (r17) carried forward as an open
    discrepancy — possibly a second order/replacement rather than the original.
  - **NEW CORROBORATION FOR 1897 (r19, "The Ashlin Chronicle," 3 Oct 1958):** the newspaper article
    on Bulletin 471 states directly: the original testing outfit (pipettes, test bottles, an acid
    measure, a hand tester, plus freight) was bought new from a dairy supply house — **Ammon &
    Sons, of Tarnet** — in **1897** for **$77.39**. "Six dozen pipettes came in that crate, at
    thirty-five cents apiece [$0.35 × 72 = $25.20], and they went into use at the station in the
    spring of 1897. They were still in use in 1923." This is a THIRD independent source (alongside
    Sill/r14) putting the station's own glassware into service in spring 1897, and it names the
    supplier (Ammon & Sons, Tarnet) and the total invoice cost. **1897 is now firmly the
    best-attested date; 1899 (Hazel Brant) is a weak, contradicted minority account.** The
    Association wrote back to Ammon & Sons in 1923 for replacement glass; the firm (still in
    business as of the 1958 article) answered that its 1890s books "would not tell anybody anything
    about the lot in question" — which the Chronicle's reporter treats (editorially, not as
    established fact) as suspicious, arguing a knowingly short lot was sold and the firm's 1923
    silence is "proof" — this is the newspaper's opinion/rhetoric, not a documented finding, and
    should be flagged as such if used.
  - r14: station's first season (partial: May 1896–end of year) took in "about nine hundred
    thousand pounds" of milk.
- **From 1902:** Hollow men complain milk tests lower at Ostrey Hollow than same-class milk at the
  Green — not believed (matches r09's 1902 date and Emmet Brant as an early complainer). r14
  independently corroborates 1902 as the date Sill first remembers hearing complaints.
  r17 (Hazel Brant) identifies "Emmet Brant" as her own grandfather — a Hessel Bottom farmer,
  patron of the Ostrey Hollow station, who began the family weigh-book on 1 January 1900 (per
  Hazel's tape). **CONFLICT ON WEIGH-BOOK START DATE (r18, Duncan Keddie):** Duncan Keddie states
  precisely: *"The Brant weigh book. Emmet Brant began it on the first of January, 1897, and kept
  it until his death in 1929..."* — i.e., **1897**, not 1 January 1900 as reported via Hazel
  Brant's tape (r17). Clean three-year discrepancy on a specific, dateable fact. Both sources are
  members of the same extended family: Duncan Keddie is Emmet's sister's son (Roy Brant's first
  cousin) writing in 1968 expressly to get family facts "right on paper," citing exact hand-off
  dates for five different documents; Hazel Brant is Emmet's granddaughter (Roy's daughter)
  speaking in 1981 from family memory. Neither source is obviously more authoritative. **Flag as a
  live, unresolved discrepancy: 1897 (Duncan Keddie, r18) vs. 1900 (Hazel Brant, r17).**
- **1908:** committee under **Nyle Grigg** sampled Hessel Bottom herds through the winter (matches
  r09). r13 (Frick) independently confirms this "hay" explanation was put before the 1925
  arbitration board. r15 (this document's 1919 covering letter, reprinting Grigg's 1908 report in
  full) gives the primary text:
  - Committee "appointed in January last" (i.e., January 1908), reporting to "the President and
    Directors of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association."
  - Complaint: Ostrey Hollow milk tests below the rest of the valley; patrons paid less for same
    labor/cows as neighbors.
  - **Hessel Bottom described precisely: "six farms cut hay and turn out stock"** — a wet meadow
    along the creek under Ostrey Hollow, springy year-round, under water in part every spring;
    herbage is sedge, rush, coarse sour grasses; hay cut late and heavy, fed as the main winter
    dependence (November–April) on those farms.
  - Committee visited each of the six farms, some three or four times; drew and sealed their own
    samples; watched herds milked, cans filled and hauled, tests made; tried station scales against
    a known weight and patrons' scales against the station's; found nothing to remark on in the
    testing itself; inquired into water supply at each barn.
  - Observed herd condition: thin in the flank at winter's end, slow to condition in spring, rougher
    coats. Conclusion: **"a cow cannot put into her pail what her keep has never put into her"** —
    wet land gives quantity (milk volume) but withholds quality (fat).
  - Explicit finding: **"no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter."** Milk whole, scales honest.
    Opinion: wet hay of Hessel Bottom thins the cream; remedy lies with the land, not the station.
  - Signed: "NYLE GRIGG, chairman, and the committee" (other members unnamed in this text).
  - Report "received and accepted," question "closed... for eleven years" — i.e., 1908 to 1919.
  - **1919 covering letter (same document, r15):** Grigg reprints his own 1908 report because "the
    Ostrey Hollow question having been reopened this season by men who were boys when we made it."
    Grigg states he had no personal interest (not a patron of that station; hauls to Larrow Green
    himself; had bought hay from two Hessel Bottom men). Adds a new corroborating fact: **the State
    Agricultural College at Farland has since published its Bulletin No. 214**, a controlled feeding
    trial on bog hay, finding the bog hay **depressed the butter fat test by two tenths of a point
    (0.2) in winter feeding** — published "three years after" the 1908 report (i.e., roughly 1911).
    Grigg treats this as independent, disinterested, scientific vindication of the committee's 1908
    finding. Remedy prescribed: drain, sweeten and lime the bottom, keep stock off it in wet spring,
    feed the coarse hay mixed rather than alone.
- **1919:** manager circulated a warning to Hollow patrons against adding water to their milk; after
  that the two ends of the valley "did not speak civilly to one another for six years" (1919–1925,
  consistent with the 1925 arbitration). r14 independently confirms the 1919 circular. r17 (Hazel
  Brant) describes the "water letter" from the receiving end, with a precise figure: the manager
  sent the letter to **nine families** on Hessel Bottom specifically (six farms, but "a farm can
  hold more than one family and generally did," hence nine families on six farms). Not one family
  replied — "answered by none of them, which in that valley is a shout." Hazel's mother read her
  copy twice and burned it in the stove; Hazel (a small child at the time) remembers the stove
  itself, not the letter's content. **r19 (Ashlin Chronicle, 1958) independently confirms**: "The
  manager of the Association had circulated a warning to the Ostrey Hollow patrons about added
  water as early as 1919."
- **Glassware condemned — THREE-WAY DATE CONFLICT, now further corroborated toward 1923:** Rundle
  gives **"the twelfth of July, 1923."** r07 Item 15 and r11 Section VII (Bulletin 471, quoted
  verbatim identically in both) give **"the twelfth of June, 1923."** "June 1923" preferred as
  better-attested (two independent retellings quote the same primary bulletin verbatim); "July
  1923" treated as Rundle's error or a reprint slip. r17 (Hazel Brant) gives a THIRD, materially
  different date: "The state man condemned that glass in **twenty-one**" — i.e., **1921** — an
  unnamed state inspector ("the state man") tested the glass "with some measuring instrument he
  carried in his coat," found it would not deliver what it was marked for, told the Association to
  order new glass ("They ordered new. Nobody said one word about all the tests that were already in
  the book"). **CORROBORATING DETAIL (r18, Duncan Keddie):** the station's condemned glassware
  ("the crate") "went up into the loft over the intake room in **1923**" — consistent with
  June/July 1923 — "and stayed there until Ivy found it in **February of 1958**." **NEW
  CORROBORATION (r19, Ashlin Chronicle):** confirms "Last February [1958] Miss Keddie went up a
  ladder into the loft over the old station's intake room and found a wooden crate that had been
  sitting there under the dust since 1923" — a FOURTH independent match on the discovery date
  (February 1958) and on 1923 as the condemnation year, from the newspaper's own account of the
  bulletin's press release. r19 also gives the mechanism: "the state's dairy inspector on his
  rounds — he ran their measures against a burette, found they would not deliver what was marked on
  them, and told the manager to order new ones. He wrote down that he had condemned them. He wrote
  down nothing about what they might have been doing for the twenty-six years before" — 26 years
  (1897–1923) matches the corroborated spring-1897 service date exactly. **1923 (June) is now very
  firmly established** (Bulletin 471 text quoted twice verbatim, Duncan Keddie's independent family
  account, and now the Ashlin Chronicle's independent retelling of the same bulletin, all agree);
  1921 (Hazel, secondhand, imprecise on years by her own admission) is a contradicted minority
  account.
- **January 1925:** Hollow patrons petitioned the Association. r13 (Frick) independently confirms
  January 1925 as the petition date, and adds that "the by-laws provided for arbitration, and the
  officers... did not resist it."
- **Total milk intake, CONFLICTS with established 40,000,000 lb:** patrons' 1925 petition claimed
  the station took in, "over the years complained of," **forty-four million pounds** of milk. Not
  necessarily the same 25-year window (1898–1922) used in the pipette-defect 76,000 lb calculation.
  Flagged as a genuine discrepancy, carried forward, not substituted for the multiply-corroborated

- **Patrons' claimed loss — NOW BETTER RESOLVED toward $19,600:** patrons' 1925 petition set their

  Ashlin Chronicle, reporting directly on Bulletin 471's contents):** "The bulletin works out what
  the Ostrey Hollow patrons were never paid for the butter fat they actually hauled to that
  station, and the figure is nineteen thousand six hundred dollars ($19,600)." This is now a

  patrons' 1925 petition figure exactly — leaving r07 Item 6's "$19,000" as the outlier (likely a
  transcription/rounding artifact in that retelling rather than a genuine second historical

  C1(d)**, with $19,000 (r07 Item 6 alone) flagged as a weaker, now-contradicted figure. Using

- **Third loss claim, in butterfat pounds (r13, contemporary 1925 arbitration record):** Alonzo
  Frick's own arbitration-exhibit article states the patrons' "own reckoning of what they had lost

## NEW MAJOR SOURCE — Retelling 19, "The Ashlin Chronicle," front page, "Old Ordell Wrong Righted,"
## 3 October 1958

A county weekly's feature story, explicitly described as "put together in a day and a half from a
college press release and two telephone calls" — i.e., a fast, secondhand, non-specialist account
of Bulletin 471's release, valuable chiefly for facts it states as direct quotation/citation from
the bulletin or from its named interviewee (Ivy Keddie), less valuable for its own editorializing.

- **Bulletin No. 471** is dated **October 1958**, authored by two named people: **Professor Leland
  Vaught** (State Agricultural College at Farland, department of dairy husbandry) and **Miss Ivy
  Keddie**, a graduate student there.
- **IVY KEDDIE'S RELATIONSHIP TO ANSEL KEDDIE — NOW DIRECTLY STATED, PRIMARY-SOURCE-ADJACENT:**
  "The man the bulletin clears is Ansel Keddie, and Miss Keddie is his **great-niece**." This is an
  explicit, unambiguous statement from a named contemporary news source, quoting/paraphrasing facts
  established by the bulletin's own press release and confirmed by telephone interview with Ivy
  Keddie herself. This directly answers B1: Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's great-niece. (Cross-check
  against Duncan Keddie, r18: Duncan is Ansel's son, per earlier notes on the 30 April 1925
  dismissal being "my father." If Ivy is Ansel's great-niece, she is likely the granddaughter of one
  of Ansel's siblings, not a descendant of Duncan — the exact intervening generation/line is not
  given in any source read so far and should be flagged as unresolved for B2 if it requires more
  than "great-niece.")
- **Ivy Keddie, biographical:** age 27 (as of Oct 1958), took her first degree at Farland and
  stayed on for graduate work; grew up in Ashlin, where her father taught school for many years.
  Spent "the better part of this year" (1958) on the investigation, in her own time and largely at
  her own expense.
- **The core mechanical finding — the actual glassware defect, quantified:** Ivy Keddie carried the
  condemned crate east to Farland; there were eleven pipettes in a condition to be measured; in
  March 1958 she measured them one by one against a calibrated burette in a college laboratory.
  **Every one of them was blown on the stem to deliver 17.6 c.c.** — "the standard charge of milk
  for a butter fat test, the charge the graduated neck of the test bottle is ruled for." **Not one
  delivered it. The average of the lot came to 16.27 c.c.** Mechanism, stated plainly: "A short
  measure of milk goes into a bottle whose neck is marked for a full one, and the fat reads low in
  the same proportion, and it reads low every single time, for as long as you go on using that
  glass. Nobody watered anything. Nobody stole anything. The scales were true, the acid was right,
  the cows were ordinary, and a piece of glass out of a crate was wrong." **This is the key
  quantitative datum for Section C1(a): the proportional shortfall is (17.6 − 16.27) / 17.6 = 1.33 /
  17.6 ≈ 0.0756, i.e., about 7.56% (roughly 1/13) low, uniformly and consistently, for every test
  made with that glass over its ~26 years of service (spring 1897 – June 1923).** Equivalently,
  every recorded reading was about 92.44% of the true value (16.27/17.6 ≈ 0.9244), so true value =
  recorded value × (17.6/16.27) ≈ recorded × 1.0817.
- **Cost/money facts, directly from the bulletin as reported:**
  - Patrons' uncredited butterfat, converted to money by the bulletin: **$19,600** (see conflict
    discussion above — this is Bulletin 471's own figure per this source, matching the patrons'
    1925 petition figure and superseding the "$19,000" figure attributed to Bulletin 471 elsewhere).
  - Original 1897 equipment cost: pipettes, test bottles, an acid measure, a hand tester, and
    freight — bought new from Ammon & Sons, dairy supply house, Tarnet — total **$77.39**.
  - The pipettes specifically: six dozen (72) came in the crate, at **35 cents apiece** ($0.35 ×
    72 = $25.20 of the $77.39 total).
  - These went into use "in the spring of 1897" and "were still in use in 1923" — 26 years of
    continuous service on the same glass.
- **The condemnation, 1923:** an unnamed state dairy inspector, "on his rounds," ran the station's
  measures against a burette, found they would not deliver what was marked, and told the manager to
  order new ones. He recorded the condemnation but "wrote down nothing about what they might have
  been doing for the twenty-six years before" — i.e., no retroactive correction was made or even
  investigated at the time.
- **Ammon & Sons (supplier) still in business in 1958;** when the Association wrote to them in 1923
  for replacement glass, "they answered by return, entered the order, regretted the trouble, and
  said that their books of the nineties would not tell anybody anything about the lot in question."
  The Chronicle's own editorial voice speculates the firm knowingly sold defective glass and that
  its 1923 silence is "proof" of this — but this is the newspaper's opinion/rhetorical flourish
  ("that answer will not satisfy this newspaper..."), not a documented or established fact, and
  should not be treated as settled.
- **Crate discovery:** "Last February" (February 1958) Ivy Keddie went up a ladder into the loft
  over the old station's intake room and found the wooden crate, sitting there "under the dust
  since 1923." This matches Duncan Keddie's (r18) independent account of the crate's fate exactly
  ("stayed there until Ivy found it in February of 1958").
- **Narrative/thematic color (useful for Section A/valley-dispute framing, not for math):**
  "Everybody had a theory. The theories were about the men, because the theories in this county
  always are. The Ostrey Hollow farmers thought they were being cheated and said so at every annual
  meeting for a quarter of a century. The officers of the Association thought the Ostrey Hollow
