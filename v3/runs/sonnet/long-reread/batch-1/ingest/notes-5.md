## NEW THIS SEGMENT — MAJOR SOURCE: r07, Halbert Nym, curator, accession notes for the Ordell
## County dairy collection, Ordell County Historical Rooms, 1972 (catalogue items 1–16)

This is a museum curator's working notes for a catalogue, transcribing primary documents/objects
exactly, with brief curatorial glosses. It is by far the most precise and complete source found
for the Section C math, and independently corroborates nearly every figure from earlier segments.

- **Item 1.** By-laws pamphlet, Article VII, transcribed entire — identical wording to what was
  already known (test of delivery station governs pay; not according to weight). No year printed
  on the pamphlet itself, only "adopted at the annual meeting."
- **Item 2.** Invoice, Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, Tarnet, dated **14 April 1897**. Sold to the
  Association for the Ostrey Hollow station: 6 dozen (72) milk test pipettes at 17.6 c.c., $0.35
  each = $25.20; 12 dozen test bottles (8% neck), $0.22 each = $31.68; 1 acid measure $0.90; 1 hand
  tester (4 bottle) $18.00; freight $2.15; **total $77.93**. This outfit went into use at the
  station from **1 May 1897** — Larrow Green had lent glass until then. This means the defective
  pipettes (the ones later condemned in 1923) were the station's OWN first set of glass, in
  continuous use for the entire 1 May 1897 – 12 June 1923 span (about 26 years), never replaced in
  between — consistent with a manufacturing/calibration defect present from day one, not ordinary
  wear.
- **Item 3.** Station test book. Curatorial heading says **"kept by E. Brant"** — but the book's
  first leaf is the same "Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until
  our own comes. A. Keddie" already known from r03/r04, and is described as "one hand throughout,
  twenty-nine seasons" (i.e., Keddie's known 1896–1924 tenure). This is an internal
  inconsistency: the heading attributes the book to E. Brant, but the content evidence (signature,
  one continuous hand, 29-season span matching Keddie's tenure) points to Ansel Keddie as the
  actual writer. Summary leaf (made up in 1922): **"Average test of all patrons, this station:
  1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year
  since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80."** This
  is a critical, precise figure: the recorded average was 3.61 from 1898 on, EXCEPT during the
  1896 season (before the defective glass arrived — used Larrow Green's borrowed glass) and during
  a nine-week span in 1911 (matches the previously-noted "9-week broken/borrowed-pipette episode"
  — apparently good glass was borrowed/substituted briefly then too), both of which read 3.80 —
  matching the independently-computed "true" value. Provenance: kept at station from 4 May 1896;
  impounded by board of arbitration March 1925; in Association's office at **Ashlin** 1925–1959;
  received at the Historical Rooms in 1959.
- **Item 4.** "Farm weigh book, **kept by A. Keddie**." Kept daily from 1 January 1897: weights
  delivered vs. test paid on. Leaf for 1910: **"Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred
  thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42."** Provenance:
  "Begun at Hessel Bottom on 1 January 1897 and kept there by one household throughout; to Roy
  Brant in 1929; lent by him to Miss Ivy Keddie in 1958 and returned; to Miss Hazel Brant in 1962;
  received here in 1963." **This item's authorship attribution is contradicted by r09 (see below):
  Roy Brant's own 1955 account identifies this exact leaf, verbatim, as his father Emmet Brant's
  book**, kept in Emmet's own hand, at the Brant farm at Hessel Bottom. So the curator's "kept by
  A. Keddie" label for Item 4 is very likely an error — and, combined with Item 3's "kept by E.
  Brant" (also apparently wrong, per above), this strongly suggests curator Halbert Nym swapped
  or confused the Keddie and Brant attributions between these two items, plausibly because the two
  families' papers were intermixed in the collection (the families are linked by Orra Keddie née
  Brant, Ansel's wife and Emmet's sister — established in earlier segments). Net effect: Item 3
  (station test book) = really Ansel Keddie's; Item 4 (farm weigh book) = really Emmet Brant's.
  Also note a **one-year date discrepancy**: Item 4's provenance says the book was begun "1
  January 1897," but r09 (Roy Brant, Emmet's son) says his father began it "from the first of
  January, 1898" — a direct one-year conflict between the curator's provenance note and the
  family's own account; r09, being first-hand family testimony about their own book, is the more
  reliable source for this specific fact.
- **Item 5.** Crate with nine milk test pipettes, stenciled with supplier's name (matches Ammon &
  Sons), much stained, two chipped at the tip. Condemned by the state inspector **12 June 1923**.
  Found **in the Association office at Ashlin**, February 1958, by Miss Ivy Keddie (of the State
  Agricultural College at Farland), taken to Farland, measured, given to the Rooms in 1959.
  Measured mean delivery of the nine: **16.72 c.c.** — NOTE: this places the crate's post-1923
  resting place definitively at the Ashlin office, NOT at the Ostrey Hollow station itself; this
  undercuts the earlier (notes-4) speculative link to Strawn's 1928 remark about a cluttered loft
  "over the intake room" at Ostrey Hollow — that loft was almost certainly NOT where this crate
  sat; the crate was at Ashlin, along with the other arbitration-hearing records (Item 3, Item 7).
- **Item 6.** Annual ledgers, station account. Typical yearly entry (illustrative, not necessarily
  1910): "Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat
  received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter
  per cent, as usual." (Check: 60,800−57,760=3,040; 3,040/57,760=5.26% ["credited" denominator];
  3,040/60,800=5.00% exactly ["received/delivered" denominator] — curatorial note explicitly warns
  these two percentages are not interchangeable, each right of its own denominator.) **Curatorial
  note gives the definitive aggregate figures: station intake for the years 1898–1922 (25 years)
  = 40,000,000 lb of milk; station "gain" accumulated across those years = 76,000 lb of butterfat,
  worth $19,000 at the average price paid for fat over the period.** (Verified arithmetic: 25-yr
  milk intake 40,000,000 lb × recorded avg test 3.61% = 1,444,000 lb credited fat; × true avg test
  3.80% = 1,520,000 lb true fat; difference = 76,000 lb — exact match. $19,000/76,000 lb =
  $0.25/lb, the stated multi-year average price.)
- **Item 7.** Minute book of the board of arbitration, Ashlin, 17 March–4 May 1925, secretary's
  hand. Award verbatim, confirming the already-known $3,800 figure, "without admission," "finds no
  cause established," charged to the station account.
- **Item 8.** Statement of A. Keddie to the board, 17 March 1925, one leaf, both sides, signed —
  described as "in the hand of Item 3" (i.e. the same hand as the station test book), which is
  consistent with Keddie himself (not E. Brant) having actually written Item 3 throughout, per the
  inconsistency flagged above.
- **Item 9.** Photostat of M. Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook, 12 June entry, verbatim — matches
  what was already known from r06 exactly (scales true, no added water "as always here," pipettes
  condemned, "glass is glass," told manager to order new). Provenance: original went to the state
  dairy office on Strawn's 1936 retirement (matches notes-4); to the library of the State
  Agricultural College at Farland in 1947; the 1923 volume found there and copied in 1958 (matches
  Ivy Keddie's known Farland affiliation and 1958 research activity). The Rooms hold only a
  photostat; the originals are at the state/college level, not in this county's collection.
- **Item 10.** Letter, Ammon & Sons to the Association, **2 July 1923**, acknowledging the
  Association's order of **28 June 1923** for replacement pipettes/bottles, promising shipment
  that month — exactly matches and pins down Strawn's October 1923 note that "new glass in use
  since July." So: condemned 12 June 1923 → order placed 28 June 1923 → shipped/received during
  July 1923 → confirmed delivering to the mark by Strawn's October 1923 visit.
- **Item 11.** 1908 investigating-committee report (wet-hay theory), verbatim, confirms it covered
  only January–March 1908 sampling — already known, now with exact quote and precise scope.
- **Item 12.** State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 214 (1911), summary paragraph verbatim: bog
  hay depressed the test "by not more than five hundredths of one per cent [0.05 points] in
  winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture" — the scientific rebuttal of the
  wet-hay theory, now with the precise magnitude (0.05 points max), far too small to explain the
  observed ~0.19-point/5% gap.
- **Item 13.** Correspondence file, Vessey County, holding 1919 test-book figures from the Alder
  Corners Cheese Company, copied in 1958 (presumably by/for Ivy Keddie, for comparison purposes).
  Content not transcribed here. That test book has never been in Ordell County; passed to founder's
  daughter in 1941, then Vessey County Historical Society in 1970.
- **Item 14.** Measurement sheet, **9 March 1958**, typed, signed by **Miss Ivy Keddie**: "Nine
  pipettes from the Ostrey Hollow crate, stems blown 17.6 c.c. Delivered against burette, mean of
  five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean 16.72 c.c.
  Short 0.88 c.c. That is 95 hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the bottle
  was ruled for." (Verified: sum of the nine readings = 150.48, /9 = 16.72 exactly; 17.6−16.72=
  0.88; 16.72/17.6=0.95 exactly.) **This is the definitive source for how the 5% figure is derived
  "from the glassware": pipettes rated/blown at 17.6 c.c. actually delivered only 16.72 c.c., i.e.
  95% of their stated mark — a systematic 5% (one part in twenty) shortfall in every sample drawn,
  which mechanically produces a butterfat reading 5% lower than the milk's true fat content.**
- **Item 15.** Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958), closing paragraph, verbatim and now more complete
  than previously captured: **"No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there.
  From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one
  part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference —
  seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers,
  year by year, as station gain."** This is the key interpretive link: what the ledgers (Item 6)
  recorded every year as ordinary/expected "station gain" (the margin between cream received at
  Larrow Green and fat credited to patrons) was NOT a normal processing margin at all — it WAS the
  pipette-defect shortfall, mislabeled/misunderstood as routine gain for 26 years. This also gives
  the exact defect window: **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923.**
- **Item 16.** Larrow Green creamery's own receiving/churn books: over the same years the station
  recorded 3.61, Larrow Green's own average ran "a little under two tenths of a point better" —
  i.e., roughly 3.61+~0.19 ≈ 3.80, independently corroborating the 3.80 "true" figure yet again
  (a fourth/fifth independent corroboration alongside Strawn's 1924 note, the 1896-season figure,
  the 1911 nine-week exception, and the pipette-ratio arithmetic).
- **Notes and queries:** the station's very first-season intake (4 May–end of 1896) is not
  recorded anywhere and is permanently unknown; only the 3.80 average test (no poundage) survives
  for 1896.

### RESOLVED MATH FOR SECTION C1 (now very solidly cross-corroborated — probably the best-settled
### figures in the whole source set)
- **(a) Proportion of error / how derived from the glassware:** Pipettes rated 17.6 c.c.; measured
  mean actual delivery 16.72 c.c. (Item 14, Ivy Keddie's 1958 burette test of the 9 surviving
  pipettes). Shortfall = 0.88 c.c. = 5% of the rated mark (16.72/17.6 = 0.95). So every fat-test
  reading taken with this glass came out at 95% of the milk's true fat content — a systematic 5%
  (one-twentieth) understatement, confirmed independently by Bulletin 471's "low by one part in
  twenty."
- **(b) True average / points short:** Recorded average (1898 on) = 3.61 (Item 3 summary leaf).
  True average = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. Recorded tests were short by 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19
  percentage points**. (3.80 is independently corroborated at least four other ways: the 1896
  season itself recorded 3.80 using good borrowed glass before the defective glass arrived; the
  nine-week span in 1911 recorded 3.80 when apparently correct glass was in use; Strawn's own 1924
  field note says "their book gives the year at 3.80" after the new glass went in; and Larrow
  Green's own comparable tests ran ~0.19 points higher than the station's 3.61 over the same
  years.)
- **(c) Pounds of butterfat patrons were not credited with, over the full delivery years
  affected:** Item 6's curatorial note gives this directly for "the years 1898 to 1922" (25 full
  delivery years, excluding the partial 1897 season and the partial 1923 season that ended
  mid-year at the June 12 condemnation): milk intake 40,000,000 lb; **76,000 lb of butterfat** not
  credited (verified by independent arithmetic: 40,000,000 × 3.80% − 40,000,000 × 3.61% =
  1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 lb exactly). This exact 76,000 lb figure is independently repeated
  in Bulletin 471 (Item 15) as well — two separate documents, one a ledger-derived curatorial
  calculation and one the 1958 official college bulletin, agree exactly.
- **(d) Dollar value and fraction patrons actually received:** $19,000 (Item 6, "worth $19,000 at
  the average price paid for fat over the period"; this implies an average price of $19,000 ÷
  76,000 lb = **$0.25/lb** over 1898–1922). Patrons actually received the 1925 arbitration award of
  **$3,800** (Item 7). Fraction actually received = $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = **1/5 (20%)** — a very clean
  fraction, almost certainly the intended answer.

### RESOLVED MATH FOR SECTION C2 (largest patron's year of 1910)
Using Emmet Brant's own farm ledger leaf (r09, corroborating r07 Item 4) and the pipette-defect
ratio established above (recorded = 0.95 × true):
- **(a) Pounds of butterfat credited:** 200,000 lb milk × 3.42% (recorded/paid test) = **6,840 lb**.
- **(b) Pounds owed and not credited:** True test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = **3.60%** exactly (a clean
  number, reinforcing that this is the intended calculation). True/owed butterfat = 200,000 ×
  3.60% = 7,200 lb. Shortfall = 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb**.
- **(c) Value of the difference in 1910 money:** Using the year-specific 1910 price of **$0.24/lb**
  (r09, Roy Brant, more precise than the multi-year $0.25 average since the question asks about
  1910 specifically): 360 lb × $0.24 = **$86.40**. (If the $0.25/lb multi-year average were used
  instead, the figure would be $90.00; $86.40 is preferred as the better-fitted, year-specific
  answer.)
- **Caveat/inference flagged:** this calculation assumes Emmet Brant was in fact the single
  largest Ostrey Hollow patron specifically in 1910 (not just "in every year of which we have a
  record" generally, per r02) — no source gives a year-by-year ranked list of patron volumes to
  confirm 1910 specifically, so this remains a reasonable but not airtight inference, clearly the
  best-supported one available from the sources read so far.

## FULL CARRIED-FORWARD CONTENT FROM NOTES-4 (all of it, verbatim in substance):

### THE CORE STORY: Ostrey Hollow testing scandal (Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery
### Association) — one of the 8 underlying stories, now the most heavily documented

### Institutional background (r02, Effie Loomis; r05, Wilbur Teague — both quote/paraphrase
official records)
- Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association organized 1889 at Larrow Green by valley
  farmers. Gideon Vose elected first president 1889, served until 1911 (22 yrs, longest-serving),
  died 1917. He personally drafted the by-laws (not a lawyer).
- Main creamery built at Larrow Green 1891, on Larrow Creek (same building still standing per r02).
- Article VII of by-laws, adopted 1892 (quoted verbatim, r02): "Every patron shall be paid for
  the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it,
  ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station
  where he delivers shall govern." Loomis calls the last clause "the hinge of the whole Ostrey
  Hollow business" — the station's own test is final, no appeal to another test/average.
- Officers named: Effie Loomis, secretary 1901–1940 (author of r02, written 1941, "Notes toward
  a history of the Association"). Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945 (author of r05, unpublished
  memoir "The Books of the Association," Ch. 9, written winter 1948, 3 yrs after retiring).
  Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green (churn book, cream receiving book). Selby Vose made
  manager 1913, served until 1938.
- Ostrey Hollow skimming station: 7 miles up-valley from Larrow Green, at the head of the Hollow.
  Took in milk from farms up there, separated it, sent cream down to Larrow Green, gave skim back
  to patrons. Best milk came off Hessel Bottom (wet meadow below the hamlet); r02 says "seven
  farms had rights on Hessel Bottom" and cut hay/pastured there; largest of these seven = Emmet
  Brant, "the station's largest patron in every year of which we have a record." r01 (Vira Toland)
  says "nine families" hauled milk to the station generally — not necessarily contradictory (9
  total patrons of the whole station vs. 7 farms specifically holding Hessel-Bottom grazing
  rights), but the two numbers never get reconciled explicitly anywhere read so far. **[Segment 5
  update: r09 (Roy Brant, a lifelong Hessel Bottom farmer) now gives a THIRD figure — six farms —
  for the Hessel-Bottom-rights count specifically, directly conflicting with r02's seven; see
  above.]**
- Ansel Keddie ran/tested at the station: per r01 he went up in 1895 to ready the building, opened
  1896. Per Ansel's own book (quoted identically in r03 and r04): "Ostrey Hollow station. Begun
  this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie."
  (NOTE internal inconsistency: earlier in the SAME r03 letter Ansel says "The glass I began on
  in ninety-six was borrowed off the Ashlin creamery" — i.e. two different stated sources,
  Larrow Green vs. Ashlin creamery, for the 1896 borrowed glass, within one document.)
  Tested 29 seasons total, 1896 through 1924 inclusive (did not test in 1925). Dismissed
  ("put out") 30 April 1925. Died 1934 (r04). **[Segment 5 update: r07 Item 2 confirms the
  station's OWN glass (the eventually-defective set) arrived and went into use 1 May 1897,
  invoiced 14 April 1897 from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet, total cost $77.93 for 72 pipettes + bottles +
  acid measure + hand tester + freight — so the borrowed-glass period ran from 4 May 1896 to 1 May
  1897, about a year, before the eventually-defective own glass came into service.]**

### r06 — Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector (retired), "Extracts from twenty-eight circuit
### books," with a covering note, 1936 — found embedded in r06-long.md around line 3514 onward,
### surrounded by heavy distractor material. This is a major independent primary source that
### CORROBORATES the Ostrey Hollow exoneration story in detail.
- Strawn took the "Ordell circuit" in 1907 (~50 plants across 4 counties: creameries, cheese
  factories, skimming stations), laid it down (retired from it) in 1936 — a 29-year span. He
  kept one oilcloth-covered pocket book per year (28 books total handed to the state dairy
  office with this covering-note copy).
- His fixed 4-step routine at every plant, every visit, always in the same order: (1) check
  scales (intake scale and cream scale) against his sealed weights; (2) run the lactometer on the
  day's milk to test for added water; (3) check boiler, water supply, cold room; (4) stand at the
  tester's elbow and watch him run a test start to finish without letting him hurry.
- Explicit epistemic stance, stated twice: "I put down what I found. I did not put down what I
  thought about it... I never had a theory about a plant and I do not recommend having one."
- Year-by-year Ostrey Hollow entries (all quoted/paraphrased from his own field books):
  - **1907** (first round of the new circuit): Ostrey Hollow station "opened before my time"
    (consistent with the 1896 opening date given elsewhere). Scales true. "Their tester keeps his
    book in his own hand, and his hand is better than most... he works slow and clean and reads
    his own figure without being coaxed to it." (This is Ansel Keddie, unnamed but clearly him —
    an independent, professional, complimentary assessment of his integrity/competence as a
    tester, 11 years into his tenure.)
  - 1909: scales true, boiler in order, one man cautioned about a rusted seam.
  - 1911, June: scales true. (A routine visit; does NOT mention the 9-week broken/borrowed-pipette
    episode of May–July 1911 described in r03/r04 — silence, not a contradiction, since his visit
    may simply have fallen outside or briefly within that window without prompting comment.)
    **[Segment 5: r07 Item 3's summary leaf confirms the nine-week 1911 episode read 3.80, matching
    the "true" figure — good glass was apparently substituted for those nine weeks.]**
  - 1912: scales true, no added water. Cream shipped down-valley same day as separated.
  - 1913: at Larrow Green (not Ostrey Hollow this entry) — "New manager at the Association this
    year and he came out with me to the platform, which the last one never did." This is Selby
    Vose, who other sources confirm became manager in 1913.
  - 1915: scales true; wash-room floor holding water at the drain, ordered graded.
  - 1916: scales true, no added water. "The tester complains that his patrons blame him for their
    figures. I told him that is the trade and he said he knew it." (Same year, per notes-1, that
    Teague says he separately raised the test discrepancy with manager Selby Vose and was told to
    "let it alone" — two different 1916 threads: the tester being blamed by patrons, and the
    bookkeeper flagging the gap to management. Not explicitly connected in either source, but
    both dated 1916 and both about awareness of a testing problem well before the 1919 circular
    or 1925 hearing.)
  - 1917: short-handed year at plants generally; "two testers doing the work of three men, which
    is where mistakes live" (general circuit remark, not Ostrey-Hollow-specific).
  - **1919**: Ostrey Hollow. "The manager of the Association has sent a circular round to the
    patrons of this station about water in the cans. I have tested for water at this station on
    every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so." — This is an
    independent, contemporaneous, official (state inspector) statement that directly refutes the
    "water in the cans" dilution accusation described in r01/r03 as the 1919 circular signed by
    manager Selby Vose. **[Segment 5: this ALSO now directly contradicts Vose's own 1926 public
    theory (r08) that dilution was real and stopped once scrutiny began — see r08 section above.]**
  - 1920: twice (spring and fall), scales true both times, no added water both times.
  - **1921**: "The state has begun issuing burettes to the field men this year, graduated, in a
    wooden case, for checking the delivery of measures and pipettes at the plants." Strawn's own
    burette arrived that spring with printed directions. He notes it is "a slow business" and
    most plants had never had their glass checked this way.
  - 1922: Ostrey Hollow — scales true, no added water, boiler in order, but "I did not run their
    glass against the burette this year, the day being short and the road home being what it
    was, and I have put that down because it is what happened." (An honest admission of a missed
    check — meaning the defective pipettes went unchecked one more year.)
  - **1923, June 12** (quoted as a direct extract from his field book): "Ostrey Hollow. Scales
    true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk pipettes against the
    burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of
    them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost
    them." — This is Strawn's own first-person, contemporaneous account of the exact pipette
    condemnation event that other sources (r02) describe only as "the state's inspector... in
    June 1923." Direct primary-source corroboration, with the exact date (12 June 1923).
    **[Segment 5: Vose's 1926 speech (r08) mis-states this as "the twelfth of July, 1923" — an
    apparent error/distortion by the self-interested party; see r08 section above. r07 Item 10
    confirms replacement order placed 28 June 1923, new glass shipped/received during July 1923.]**
  - 1923, October: "New glass in use since July. Ran the new pipettes against the burette. They
    deliver their mark. Scales true."
  - 1924: "Scales true, no added water, tests improved. Their book gives the year at 3.80." —
    Matches the "true" long-run average of 3.80 computed elsewhere from the pipette-defect math
    (16.72/17.6 = 0.95; 3.61/0.95 = 3.80). **[Segment 5: Vose's 1926 speech instead claims 1924
    "stood at 3.90" — conflicts with this contemporaneous, disinterested figure; 3.80 is
    preferred, see r08 discussion above.]**
  - 1925, fall: "Scales true, no added water, tests improved. New man at the tester's bench this
    year. He is quick, and quick is not the thing I look for." (The "new man" is Ansel Keddie's
    replacement after his 30 April 1925 dismissal.)
  - 1926: "Word from the state office that the burette program is discontinued from this year...
    Mine is in its case in my grip and has been used twice since." (State-level program wind-down,
    unrelated to any Ostrey Hollow specifics thereafter.)
  - 1928: Ostrey Hollow — "The loft over the intake room is full of odds and ends and I told them
    a loft over a milk room is a place for nothing but air, and was told in return that it had
    been full since before the war." — Previously speculated (notes-1 through notes-4) as

  - 1931: Larrow Green and Ostrey Hollow same week, both in order; a bad ice year across the
    circuit.
  - 1934: Ostrey Hollow — scales true, no added water; a separator bearing ran hot and was shut
    down two days, since repaired; "the tester's book is kept up, though not in the hand it used
    to be kept in" (i.e., no longer Keddie's hand — consistent with his 1934 death, per r04).
  - 1936, March (Strawn's last round before retiring): Ostrey Hollow, Larrow Green, and two
    factories over the county line, all in order, "nothing to note anywhere and nothing left to
    say about it."
  - Closing note: 28 field books (1907–1936 inclusive minus any skipped years) surrendered to the
    state dairy office along with this typed covering copy. Strawn refuses to offer any general
    "estimation" of a plant's standing, only the literal book: "I keep a book, and the book is
    open to anybody the state sends for it."

### Significance of r06 for the test questions
- r06 is an independent, official, contemporaneous documentary source (a state inspector's own
  field notes, written up in 1936 — 22 years before Ivy Keddie's 1958 talk in r04) that
  corroborates multiple load-bearing facts from the exoneration story: (1) no water was ever
  found in the cans at any inspection, including in 1919, the very year of the "water in the
  cans" circular; (2) the pipettes were condemned specifically on 12 June 1923 by Strawn himself,
  matching the "June 1923" date given secondhand in r02; (3) new glass went into use from July
  1923 and delivered its correct mark when checked in October 1923; (4) the recorded station
  average jumped to 3.80 in 1924, matching the "true" value computed from the pipette-defect
  ratio (16.72/17.6 = 0.95; 3.61/0.95 = 3.80) elsewhere in the source material.
- This means the 1958 exoneration (Bulletin No. 471, Professor Vaught, quoted by Ivy Keddie in
  r04) is now corroborated by an entirely separate, independent, contemporaneous witness (Strawn)
  whose notes were compiled in 1936, well before Ivy's 1958 research — not merely a single late
  retrospective account. This further undercuts r01 (Vira Toland)'s and r05 (Wilbur Teague)'s
  suspicion that Keddie was personally stealing cream: an inspector who watched Keddie test
  "through two tests" in 1907 and repeatedly found "scales true, no added water" over decades
  never once flagged theft or dilution — only the glass defect, discovered by the state's own
  burette program in 1923. **[Segment 5: r08 shows that this exoneration, though scientifically
  and documentarily solid, was NOT yet accepted by everyone even in 1926 — manager Selby Vose
  publicly still believed and asserted the dilution theory as late as 1926, 32 years before the
  1958 Bulletin. Bulletin 471 (r07 Item 15) explicitly and directly refutes this: "No milk was
  watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there."]**

### The accusation and its escalation (chronology, cross-checked across r01/r02/r03/r05, and now
### r06, r07, r08, r09)
- 1902: first complaints down at Larrow Green that Ostrey Hollow milk "did not test with the rest"
  (r03).
- 1908: a committee sampled herds through the winter, blamed the wet hay of the Bottom for
  "thinning the cream" — i.e., blamed the land, not the equipment or the tester (r03). This
  theory is later refuted (see 1958 findings below, and the State Agricultural College Bulletin
  No. 214 rebuttal). **[Segment 5: r07 Item 11 confirms sampling was Jan–Mar 1908 only, and Item
  12 gives Bulletin 214's exact rebuttal figure: bog hay depresses test by at most 0.05 points in
  winter, nothing measurable on summer pasture — far too small to explain the ~0.19-point/5% gap.]**
- 1919: Association sent a printed circular, signed by manager Selby Vose, to nine Ostrey Hollow
  families, saying there was "water in the cans" (dilution accusation) (r01, r03 — both use the
  same phrase "water in the cans," so likely one real circular, remembered/quoted by two people).
  Devastating locally: many families kept the letter for decades (one pinned inside a trunk lid).
  Ansel Keddie's wife Orra was Emmet Brant's sister; Emmet was the largest patron and hardest-hit
  by the accusation; after the circular, Ansel/Orra and Emmet Brant never spoke again for the
  rest of their lives (confirmed independently by r01 AND by Ansel Keddie's own unsent letter in
  r03: "He and I have not spoken since the circular. That is the plainest fact in this letter.").
  Independently, r06 (Strawn) confirms he tested for water at every visit including 1919 and
  never found any, and told the manager so at the time. **[Segment 5: Vose (r08) himself defends
  this circular in 1926, describing it as milder/less accusatory than r01/r03 portray it, and
  reaffirms his belief that dilution was real — directly conflicting with Strawn's contemporaneous
  1919 finding of no water and with the eventual 1958 exoneration.]**
- January 1925: Ostrey Hollow patrons formally petitioned the Association, saying their milk "had
  never tested as their neighbors' milk tested... for a very long time," asking to be made whole
  (r02).
- Board of arbitration sat at Ashlin under Alonzo Frick, attorney, 17 March – 4 May 1925 (dates
  agree across r02, r03, r05, and now r07 Item 7). Ansel Keddie gave a 9-sentence statement on 17
  March 1925 (quoted verbatim in r03; r07 Item 8 catalogues the original leaf but does not
  re-transcribe it), denying ever taking a pound of cream, describing his testing method precisely
  (scale weighing, sampling into numbered jars over 10 days, warming/mixing, Babcock-style acid
  test, hand tester, reading fat column with dividers). Wilbur Teague also testified (r05),
  offering an "opportunity" theory (one man alone in the building for 29 seasons "can take a
  little and write down a little less") but explicitly NOT claiming to know what happened:
  "I do not say what any particular person did on any particular day... I was not there."
- VERDICT (quoted verbatim, identically in substance in r02, r05, AND now r07 Item 7): "The board
  having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of
  the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum
  of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station
  account." NO fault was formally established against anyone. **[Segment 5: r08 (Vose himself)
  confirms this exact figure and language, and states he personally supported the payment.]**
- Ansel Keddie dismissed ("put out") 30 April 1925 anyway (r03), despite "no cause established."
- r02 also notes: a crate of milk-test measures/pipettes at the station was condemned by the
  state's inspector in June 1923, new ones ordered — and this was NEVER mentioned at the 1925
  hearing at all ("Not a word of it was spoken in any of the three sessions"). This omission is
  the pivot for the 1958 exoneration (below). r06 independently confirms and dates this exact
  event: 12 June 1923, condemned by Merle Strawn personally. **[Segment 5: r07 Items 9 and 10
  fully document this with photostat and supplier correspondence; the crate itself (Item 5) and
  the pipette measurements (Item 14) are the physical/forensic proof behind Bulletin 471.]**

### CONFLICT: r01 (Vira Toland, oral memory, tape 1979) vs. everyone else on amount/date/verdict
- r01 says: patrons were paid "thirty-five hundred dollars" ($3,500), the hearing happened "the

- This conflicts with FOUR other, more documentary sources: r02 (secretary's official minute-book

  "no cause established"), and now r07 Item 7 (the arbitration board's own minute book, verbatim,
  same $3,800/1925/"no cause established" language) and r08 (Vose's own 1926 address, confirming
  $3,800 and 1925 explicitly). RESOLUTION: quoted primary/official documents (secretary's minutes,
  the arbitration board's own minute book, the tester's own contemporaneous testimony, the
  bookkeeper's ledger, the manager's own contemporaneous public address) outrank a personal oral
  memory recorded 54+ years after the events, per the test's own stated resolution method (a
  quoted document outranks a memory).
