## Section A — Reconstruction

### 1. The Founding, the Station, and the Fatal Purchase (1889–1897)

In 1889, farmers in the Larrow Valley, tired of hauling their butter all the way to Ashlin, met
at the Larrow Green schoolhouse and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association.
They elected Gideon Vose — "a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the law and
thought better of it" — as first president; he wrote the Association's by-laws himself, at his
own kitchen table. (Source: Dell Rundle's 1939/1959 jubilee pamphlet, "The Larrow Valley
Creamery: Fifty Years," the main history of the founding.)

In 1891 the Association built its creamery at Larrow Green, below the mill, and began paying the
valley in cash monthly for the first time. That same year — per Rundle, who quotes the text
directly, preferred over a rival 1893 date that appears only as a vague secondhand "impression"
in a 1919 letter — the Association adopted Article VII: patrons would be paid according to the
butterfat test of the station where they delivered, not the weight of their milk. This became
"the sentence on which the whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned."

On 4 May 1896 (Orin Falke's 1959 county report, matched independently by buttermaker Hobart
Sill's "May 1896"), the Association opened a second station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the
valley, serving the Hessel Bottom farms. Ansel Keddie was appointed tester and separator man
there, a post he held for 29 unbroken seasons, to 1925. The station opened with no glassware of
its own; Sill lent his own testing equipment from Larrow Green for its first season.

The station's own outfit — six dozen milk-test pipettes, twelve dozen test bottles, an acid
measure, and a hand tester, billed by supplier Ammon & Sons at $77.93 by the line items (though
Ammon's own prose gives $77.39, an unresolved discrepancy within that one source) — went into use
1 May 1897, the best-corroborated of three competing dates for when the glass entered service (a
clerk's 1953 memoir says 1898, a granddaughter's 1981 tape says 1899, both later, secondhand, and
self-disclaimed as uncertain on years); 1897 is confirmed by the supplier's invoice, the
supplier's own company history, and, most decisively, the tester's wife's contemporaneous diary
entry for that exact date. This unremarkable purchase, defectively made though no one yet knew
it, is the origin of a shortfall that would go undetected for 26 years.

### 2. Wet Hay and the Committee of 1908 (1902–1908)

From 1902 (independently confirmed by four sources — Rundle's pamphlet, retellings r09 and r14,
and a contemporaneous March 1902 diary entry from the tester's wife, Orra Keddie), farmers on
Hessel Bottom delivering to Ostrey Hollow began complaining that their milk tested lower there
than the same herds tested at Larrow Green. Management did not believe them.

One such patron, Emmet Brant, began keeping a private weigh-book of his own — starting either 1
January 1900 (per his granddaughter Hazel Brant, speaking in 1981) or 1 January 1897 (per his
nephew Duncan Keddie, writing in 1968); the three-year gap between these two family accounts is
not resolved by the sources.

In January 1908 the Association appointed a committee, chaired by Nyle Grigg, to investigate.
Over that winter the committee visited each of Hessel Bottom's six farms three or four times,
drew and sealed their own milk samples, watched the milking, hauling, and testing firsthand, and
checked both the station's and the patrons' scales against a known weight. They found nothing
wrong with the testing itself. Instead they blamed the shortfall on the wet, sedge-choked hay
Hessel Bottom's herds were fed all winter, reasoning that "a cow cannot put into her pail what her
keep has never put into her," and explicitly reported "no dishonesty of any kind, in any
quarter." The report was accepted and the matter considered closed — for eleven years, until 1919.

Only later did anyone test this verdict against hard numbers. In a 1919 covering letter reopening
the question, committee chairman Grigg cited a state college bulletin as finding the hay
depressed the test by a full 0.2 point. That specific citation was directly refuted forty years
later: county agent Orin Falke's 1959 report, after obtaining the actual bulletin text, found it
showed only a 0.05-point winter effect and nothing in summer — far too small to explain the gap —
and Falke withdrew the concession he'd initially made on the theory's behalf. The 1908 committee's
good-faith verdict rested on a citation that didn't hold, though that would not be established
until decades later.

### 3. Nine Weeks with Borrowed Glass (1911)

On 8 May 1911 the Ostrey Hollow station's own measuring glass broke. For nine weeks, until 10
July, tester Ansel Keddie used a measure borrowed from elsewhere to test milk from the same
Hessel Bottom herds he always tested.

Through those nine weeks the station's recorded butterfat tests rose markedly above their usual
level. The day the station went back to its own glass, the readings fell straight back down.
Keddie noticed the pattern at the time, wrote it into his test book, and set a question mark
against it — but nothing came of it then.

The episode is independently confirmed from two very different angles: Orin Falke's 1959 report
for the county board, drawing on a 1958 college bulletin, and the contemporaneous diary of Orra
Keddie, the tester's own wife, who records the same nine-week window as it happened — a
scientific reconstruction and a private diary landing on the identical dates, decades apart.

Its significance would not become clear for decades. Falke's 1959 report treats 1911 as the
single most decisive piece of evidence in the whole matter: same tester, same herds, same season
and supervision — only the measuring instrument changed, and the readings changed with it. That
rules out both rival explanations debated for years afterward — water added to the milk, and the
tester manipulating his figures — and points squarely at the glassware itself. But at the time it
was only a curiosity, set aside and forgotten for 47 more years, until Ivy Keddie found the
station's long-condemned glass still crated in the loft above the intake room in February 1958.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1.** Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie: **granddaughter.** Orin Falke's 1959 county
report first calls her "the great-niece of the tester" on page two, then adds a note after the
report had gone to type: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter
and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." A named author's own dated,
verified, self-correction is the strongest kind of evidence this material offers — "granddaughter"
stands.

**B2.** Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant: **not fully determinable — at best a plausible
second cousin.** What the notes establish directly: Hazel Brant is Emmet Brant's granddaughter
through his son Roy Brant. Duncan Keddie (1968) says he is Emmet's sister's son, i.e. Roy Brant's
first cousin — so one of Emmet's sisters married a Keddie man. Separately, Orra Keddie (Ansel's
wife) is described as being from "her own family, the Brants." If Orra were that same sister of
Emmet's, and if Duncan Keddie were Ansel and Orra's son, then Ivy (Ansel's granddaughter, per B1)
could descend through that line and come out as Hazel's second cousin. But no source states that
Orra is specifically Emmet's sister (Hessel Bottom held six farms' worth of Brants, so she could
be from another branch), and no source names which of Ansel's children is Ivy's parent. The
intervening generations are left open by the notes; this is an inference from what's consistent,
not a documented fact.

**B4.** Relationship between the Vose family and the Keddie family: **no blood or marriage
relationship is established anywhere in the notes — only an institutional one, running through
two Voses across Ansel Keddie's whole career.** This material is unusually careful about
genealogy elsewhere — it debates and resolves whether Selby Vose was Gideon Vose's "son" or
"nephew" (B3), and separately corrects Ivy Keddie's exact generation from "great-niece" to
"granddaughter" (B1) — yet at no point does any of the 24 retellings assert or imply a blood or
marriage tie between anyone named Vose and anyone named Keddie. Given how much attention the
material pays to exactly this kind of family detail elsewhere, that silence is meaningful rather
than just a gap. What the notes do establish is institutional: Gideon Vose founded the Association
and wrote Article VII, the by-law whose consequence — payment fixed to a defective station's own
test — is what eventually cost Keddie's patrons money; his son Selby Vose became manager in 1911
and was Keddie's superior for the back half of Keddie's 29-season tenure, personally signed the
1919 circular warning (in effect, accusing) Hessel Bottom's patrons of watering their milk, and
was still manager when Keddie was dismissed on 30 April 1925, four days before the arbitration
board awarded those same patrons $3,800. That is the entire connection the sources support: an
employer/manager-to-tester relationship spanning two generations of Voses, not a family one.

## Section C — Math

**C1(a).** Proportion: **about 5%, most precisely the bookkeeper's own recurring 5¼% (5.25%).**
Working:
- Direct bookkeeping evidence: clerk Bertram Kyle reports that every single year, the gap between
  butterfat credited to Ostrey Hollow patrons on the station test and butterfat actually received
  in cream at Larrow Green was entered as "station gain," followed by the same annual note:
  "five and a quarter per cent, as usual, carried to the general account" — a named, quoted,
  recurring figure of 5.25%.
- Independent cross-check from the 1958 bulletin's own averages (recorded 3.61, true 3.80,
  deficiency 0.19 point): 0.19 ÷ 3.61 = 5.26%; 0.19 ÷ 3.80 = 5.00% exactly. Both land within a
  quarter point of Kyle's bookkeeping figure, from a completely different kind of evidence
  (a calibration measurement vs. an annual ledger note). (Minor wrinkle: Larrow Green's own
  average is separately given as 3.78, not the 3.80 that 3.61+0.19 implies — a small, unexplained
  gap the notes flag but don't resolve; it doesn't change the ~5% conclusion.)
- From the glassware itself: the 1958 Farland measurement found the surviving pipettes
  "delivered materially less than the capacity blown upon their stems, and in the same
  proportion, one to another." A pipette miscast to deliver a fixed fraction short of its marked
  volume makes every reading taken with it low by that same fixed fraction, regardless of the
  actual butterfat level — which is exactly why one flat percentage covers the entire 26-year
  span of the error rather than a varying correction.

**C1(d).** Worth: **$19,600** is the best-attested total value of the shortfall; patrons actually
received **$3,800**, which is **≈19.4% (roughly one-fifth)** of it.
Working:
- $19,600: the patrons' 1925 petition set the loss "to the dollar" at $19,600, and Bulletin 471's
  own computed figure for the same loss independently comes to the same $19,600 (per the Ashlin
  Chronicle's report on the bulletin) — two independent methods landing on one number. A
  competing "$19,000" appears in only one weaker, uncorroborated source and reads as a
  rounding/transcription slip.
- Cross-check by multiplication: 76,000 lbs (the pounds not credited) × Kyle's flat average price
  of 25 cents/lb = $19,000 — close to, but $600 short of, $19,600. That's explainable rather than
  contradictory: Kyle's 25 cents is explicitly an averaged price "taking the fat years with the
  lean," not year-by-year pricing, so multiplying it through won't exactly reproduce a figure
  computed (presumably) from real annual prices.
- Fraction received: $3,800 ÷ $19,600 = 0.1939 → **≈19.4%**. The $3,800 award itself is confirmed
  by five independent sources (Frick, Duncan Keddie, Ashlin Chronicle, Kyle, Falke), against one
  contradicted outlier ($3,500, secondhand family memory).
