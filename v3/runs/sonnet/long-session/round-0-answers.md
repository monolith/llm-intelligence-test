# Round 0 Answers — Batch 1 of 3

## Section A — Reconstruction (first three of eight stories, in chronological order)

### 1. The Station at Ostrey Hollow

In 1889 farmers of the Larrow Valley met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, building its main creamery there in 1891. Article 7 of its by-laws fixed the principle that mattered most later: patrons were paid by the butterfat their milk tested at, not by its weight, and the test made at the delivery station governed pay. In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station seven miles up-valley at Ostrey Hollow to serve the farms of Hessel Bottom. It opened 4 May 1896, at first borrowing its testing glass from Larrow Green. Ansel Keddie was tester and separator man from the day it opened, a post he held for twenty-nine seasons.

The station's own glassware — six dozen milk-test pipettes graduated at 17.6 cc, twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, and a four-bottle hand tester — was ordered from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet, invoiced 14 April 1897 at a total of $77.93, and put into use 1 May 1897. Two later retrospective accounts (a clerk's 1953 memoir and a state college bulletin) instead place this installation in 1898. I resolve this for 1897: the Ammon & Sons invoice is a contemporaneous business record, and Orra Keddie, the tester's wife, wrote in her diary that same day, "A. says the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green" — a same-day entry outranks two decades-later memories.

This is the origin point of everything that follows: a station built to serve a handful of wet-ground farms, run for a generation by one man, on glassware bought once in 1897 and not replaced until it was condemned twenty-six years later. Nothing about the founding itself was irregular — by-laws, site, and hiring were unremarkable cooperative business. The trouble was contained entirely in that one crate of glass.

**Documents:** Falke's 1959 report (organization date, Article 7, station opening, Keddie's tenure); Ammon & Sons' invoice and 1949 firm history; Orra Keddie's diary (1897 entry); Bertram Kyle's 1953 memoir (dissenting 1898 date).

### 2. The Low Test

From early in its life, the Ostrey Hollow station's recorded butterfat tests ran low. Over the long period patrons complained of, the station averaged 3.61 against Larrow Green's contemporaneous 3.78 — an arithmetic difference of 0.17 points — though both a 1959 county report and, independently, a patron's granddaughter state the deficiency as "nineteen hundredths of a point" (0.19). I flag rather than force a resolution here: two sources agree on 0.19, but it does not subtract cleanly from the 3.61/3.78 pair those same and other sources give, and I found no way to reconcile the two figures. The gap held constant month to month, flush season and lean alike. First complaint of record: 1902.

Two theories were tried and abandoned, one lingered. A 1908 Association committee inspected the wet, coarse-hay pasture of Hessel Bottom and blamed the feed. A later correction — a 1911 state college feeding trial, cited but not worked through until 1959 — showed wet hay could depress a test by at most 0.05 points in winter and nothing measurable in summer, far short of a flat year-round 0.17–0.19 point shortfall. The theory that patrons watered their milk could not survive the lactometer readings a state inspector took at every visit from 1908 on, which never once found added water.

The clearest evidence arrived by accident. On 8 May 1911 the station's own measuring glass broke, and a borrowed measure did service for nine weeks, until 10 July. Through that interval, same tester, same herds, same season, recorded tests rose noticeably — and fell back the day the station's own glass resumed. The tester noted it himself and set a question mark against it. In hindsight it was the best clue available, twelve years before anyone acted on it.

**Documents:** Falke's report (averages, 1908 committee, Bulletin 214 correction); Hazel Brant's taped account (independent 0.19 figure); Orra Keddie's diary (dated 1902, 1908, and 1911 entries).

### 3. The Water Letter and the Brant Rift

Selby Vose, son of the Association's founding president Gideon Vose, became manager in 1911. In 1919, with the low-test mystery still unexplained, Vose circulated a written warning to the Ostrey Hollow patrons accusing them of adding water to their milk, signed over his own name. He did not send it by an ordinary hand: Ansel Keddie, the tester the patrons dealt with daily, carried it door to door and stood by while each recipient read it.

Sources disagree on the count. Hazel Brant, a patron's granddaughter, recalled nine families and nine letters. Orra Keddie — the tester's own wife, writing that same season — put it at eleven. I lean toward Orra's figure as the more contemporaneous record, though neither can be checked against an actual patron list, so I treat this as unsettled rather than resolved.

Two days after the letters went round, Emmet Brant — Orra's own half-brother by her father's second wife, and a patron on Hessel Bottom — confronted Ansel Keddie in the yard over why he had carried an accusation against his own neighbors himself. Keddie's answer, as Orra recorded it: he would not have another man carry it. The two brothers-in-law did not speak again for ten years, until Emmet Brant's death in spring 1929, which Orra's diary marks with the line that neither would go up the road first, "and now it is settled the way those things get settled."

The watering theory did not hold up under the one test that could have caught it directly: the state inspector's lactometer, run at every visit from 1908 onward, never once found added water. The circular cost the Association nothing on paper. It cost one family a decade.

**Documents:** Orra Keddie's diary (1919, two-days-after, and 1929 entries); Hazel Brant's taped account (nine-families figure, the stove); Falke's report (lactometer refutation).

## Section B — Relationships

**B1.** Ivy Keddie is Ansel Keddie's **granddaughter**. Orin Falke's 1959 report first called her "great-niece of the tester" but appended a note: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." This matches Hazel Brant's independent account, in which Ansel and Orra Keddie's son Duncan is Ivy's father. Since Falke's own correction and Hazel Brant's separate genealogy agree, I treat granddaughter as settled and the "great-niece" line as a retracted error, not a live conflict.

**B2.** Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant are **second cousins**, per Hazel Brant's own account: Orra Keddie (Ansel's wife) was a half-sister to Hazel's grandfather Emmet Brant, by their father's second wife. That makes Orra's son Duncan and Emmet's son Roy (Hazel's father) first cousins, and their respective daughters, Ivy and Hazel, second cousins. This rests on a single source — Hazel's own telling — with no independent corroboration; the sources leave open the exact birth order and dates of the two marriages that produced Orra and Emmet.

**B3.** Selby Vose's father was **Gideon Vose**, who organized the Association and was its first president (Bertram Kyle's memoir explicitly denies a rival "nephew" story). This matters because the manager who accused the Ostrey Hollow patrons of watering their milk in 1919, and who ran the station through the years the low-test mystery was argued out and eventually arbitrated, was not a neutral hired administrator but the founder's own son — giving his 1919 accusation more institutional weight than a hired manager's would likely have carried, and making the 1925 finding against the Association a more pointed outcome for the founding family.

**B4.** No source establishes any family relationship between the **Vose** and **Keddie** families. The two intersect only institutionally — Selby Vose was Ansel Keddie's employer, accuser, and eventual dismisser. I answer this one "cannot be determined from the sources": the material is simply silent on any kinship link, which is some evidence there wasn't one, but silence isn't proof.

## Section C — Math

**C1.**

*(a) Proportion by which the tests differed from the truth, and how derived from the glassware:*
The sources describe the mechanism — pipettes that deliver less than the volume marked on the stem yield a fat-percentage reading short "in the same proportion" — but no source gives a numeric percentage for how far short the glass itself ran; the 1958 re-measurement is described only qualitatively ("delivered materially less... in the same proportion, one to another"). The only numeric deficiency figures available are point-differences in recorded averages, and these do not agree with each other:
- 3.78 (Larrow Green, contemporaneous) − 3.61 (Ostrey Hollow, recorded) = 0.17 points → 0.17/3.78 ≈ **4.5%** short.
- 3.85 (Ostrey Hollow, own average after the 1923 glass replacement) − 3.61 = 0.24 points → 0.24/3.85 ≈ **6.2%** short.
- The figure stated outright by two independent narrators, "nineteen hundredths" (0.19), matches neither subtraction exactly.
**I cannot pin down one proportion from the sources.** My working estimate is a shortfall in the rough range of 4.5–6%, with 0.19 points as the best-corroborated (two independent sources) but arithmetically unreconciled deficiency figure.

*(b) True average test, and points short:*
Using the 0.19-point figure on the recorded 3.61: true average ≈ **3.80**, tests short by **0.19 points**. The station's own actual post-repair average (3.85, from 1924 on) runs a little higher still, which may mean the true figure is closer to 3.85 and the shortfall closer to 0.24 — but nothing in the sources rules out other factors (herd or management changes) contributing to that later rise, so I present 3.80/0.19 as the primary estimate and flag 3.85/0.24 as a plausible upper bound.

*(c) Butterfat pounds patrons were not credited with, over the affected years:*
Total milk received at the station over "the long run of years" complained of: **40,000,000 lbs** (Kyle's memoir: 1,600,000 lbs/year × 25 years — internally consistent with his own installation date of 1898 to the 1923 condemnation).
- Using 0.19 points: 40,000,000 × 0.0019 = **76,000 lbs** butterfat not credited.
- Using 0.17 points: 40,000,000 × 0.0017 = **68,000 lbs**.
I carry the 0.19-based figure (76,000 lbs) forward as primary, per the corroboration reasoning above.

*(d) Money value, and fraction patrons actually received:*
At Kyle's stated 25 cents/lb average butterfat price over the period:
- 76,000 lbs × $0.25 = **$19,000** (primary estimate); 68,000 lbs × $0.25 = $17,000 (alternate).
The 1925 arbitration award was **$3,800**, without admission of cause (Falke).
- $3,800 / $19,000 ≈ **0.20** — patrons received roughly **one-fifth** of the estimated shortfall.
- (Alternate: $3,800 / $17,000 ≈ 0.22.)

**C2.** Largest patron of Hessel Bottom, per Hazel Brant, was her grandfather Emmet Brant. His own weigh book gives, for 1910: 200,000 lbs delivered, paid on a recorded test of 3.42.

*(a) Butterfat credited:* 200,000 lbs × 0.0342 = **6,840 lbs**.

*(b) Butterfat owed and not credited:* Using the 0.19-point deficiency (the same figure Hazel Brant herself states, making this the internally consistent choice for her own family's data): 200,000 × 0.0019 = **380 lbs** not credited.

*(c) Value that year:* 380 lbs × $0.25/lb = **$95.00**. Note: no 1910-specific butterfat price is given anywhere in the sources; this uses Kyle's multi-year average of 25 cents/lb as the only available proxy, so it is an approximation, not a period-accurate figure.
