# Blind solve — Larrow Valley / Ostrey Hollow synthesis test

Worked from `v3/test-input/retellings/r01–r24` and `questions.md` only. Citations are by
retelling number; quoted documents are named where a document settles a point.

## Section A — Reconstruction

**How I cut the eight, and why.** I looked for strands that have their own beginning, middle and
end, their own cast, and their own documents — not for topics. Four of the eight are *institutional*
(the Association's founding rule; the station as a working plant; the ledger line; the 1925
hearing), three are *causal* (the glass itself; the hay theory and the outside check that killed
it; the 1958 proof), and one is *domestic* (the 1919 circular and the two families it split). Two
strands I deliberately did **not** promote to stories of their own: the nine weeks of 1911, which
lives inside the station's own test book and belongs to Story 2 (it is used again in 8); and the
Alder Corners cheese factory, which exists in this history only as the outside control on the hay
theory and belongs to Story 4. Every one of the twenty-four retellings has its core material
housed in exactly one of the eight.

### 1. The rule that made the test the master (1889–1893)

**Who.** Gideon Vose, first president 1889–1911 (d. 1917); the farmers of the Larrow Valley; the
officers who came after — Effie Loomis, secretary 1901–1940; Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945;
Hobart Sill, buttermaker from 1893; Selby Vose, Gideon's **son**, manager 1913–1938.

**What happened.** In 1889 the farmers met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association,
being tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking what the merchant allowed. Vose was elected
first president and held it twenty-two years, longer than any man since. In 1891 the creamery was
built at Larrow Green on the creek. Before it there was no common test and no common price and
every man's arithmetic was his own; after it the valley was paid in cash once a month, which had
never happened to it.

Vose drafted the by-laws himself at his kitchen table, a farmer and not a lawyer. The seventh
article is the hinge of the other seven stories: *"Every patron shall be paid for the milk he
delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and
not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall
govern."* The rule is just — it pays a man for what he brings, not for the water naturally in it —
but nobody quotes the last clause. The station's test, and no appeal from it: not the creamery's,
not the valley average, not a test made over again anywhere. Under payment by weight, nothing that
follows would have cost any man a dollar.

**When.** Organized 1889; creamery 1891; Article VII adopted at an annual meeting whose **year
cannot be determined**. Rundle says 1891, Loomis 1892, and the committee's own 1908 report recites
"the meeting of 1893." The printed by-laws carry no date, only "adopted at the annual meeting," and
the 1889–1892 minute book was carried out in the 1938 office move and never arrived. Certain only
that it postdates the creamery.

**Quantities.** 22 years of Gideon Vose's presidency; one lost minute book covering four annual
meetings; one article, numbered seven.

**Documents.** By-laws pamphlet with Article VII entire (r07 item 1; r02 §3; r12 II); Loomis's
*Notes* (r02); Rundle's pamphlet (r12); Falke ¶3 (r22); the 1908 committee report (r15).

### 2. The station at the head of the valley, and the man in it (1895–1946)

**Who.** Ansel Keddie (b. 1861, d. February 1934), tester and separator man; the nine families
hauling to the station; Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector on the Ordell circuit 1907–1936; the
Orams, who drove the cream wagon.

**What happened.** The Association built the station seven miles up the valley in 1895–96. Keddie went up in 1895 to get it ready; it opened **4 May 1896**. Milk weighed in, sampled per patron, tested, separated;
cream down daily but Sunday, skim back to the patron.

Keddie stood there **twenty-nine seasons, 1896 through 1924**, alone from spring to fall, beginning at half past
five. Each patron's sample went into his own numbered jar, made up over ten days, warmed to blood
heat and poured vessel to vessel; the charge drawn to the mark on the stem, the acid down the side,
four bottles in the hand tester, the fat read in percent with dividers. **Thirty-one tests a week.** The book ran five columns — date, patron,
pounds, test, fat — errors struck through and initialed, never a figure over a figure. Strawn found
the scales true at every visit for thirty years, never found added water, and watched whole tests
through.

The summary leaf of 1922 is the spine of the history: *1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And
3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted
in their place, when it stood at 3.80.* Those nine weeks: the measure broke **8 May 1911**, a
borrowed one was used, the tests rose to 3.80 and held; **10 July** the station's own returned and
they fell the same day. Keddie set a question mark against it and let it stand fourteen years. In
1924, on the glass bought after June 1923, it averaged **3.80** again. He was dismissed in
1925; a new man took the bench; the station closed in 1946.

**Quantities.** 7 miles; 29 seasons; 31 tests a week; 63 days in 1911; averages 3.80 / 3.61 / 3.80;
about 1,600,000 lb of milk a year.

**Documents.** Station test book, 4 May 1896 – 31 December 1924 (r03; r04; r07 item 3; r13 exhibit
1); Keddie's statement and unsent letter (r03); Strawn's circuit books (r06; r07 item 9); Orra
Keddie's diary (r24); r21; r01.

### 3. The short lot (1897–1923)

**Who.** Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet, supplying half the creamery glass in the
state; Junia Ammon, its historian; Merle Strawn.

**What happened.** In April 1897 the Association bought the station a complete testing outfit. It
went into use **1 May 1897**, the day Orra Keddie wrote that "the station's own glass came up and
they are done borrowing of Larrow Green." The lot was short: every pipette delivered about **16.72 c.c.** where its stem was blown **17.6**
— 0.88 under the mark, exactly **0.95** of it, one part in twenty.

Junia Ammon's account of how, written in 1949, is the only mechanism offered and it fits: the mold
had been reground and the lot ran short; a boy checks the first, the tenth and the fiftieth of a lot and finds them consistent, "because they are
consistent — they are consistently wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind cannot find."
Whether the house knew **cannot be determined**: the works books recording what glass, what gauge,
what man, what day burned in 1931, leaving only the invoices and letter books.

The glass ran twenty-six years. Strawn condemned it on **12 June 1923**, on an ordinary circuit
visit, with a burette the state had begun issuing its field men only in 1921 and which he had not
run at that station in 1922, and wrote down that he had not. He condemned the whole crate and told the manager to
order new. The order went on 28 June; Ammon's letter of 2 July entered it and promised shipment
that month; the new glass was in use from July and delivered its mark when Strawn checked it in
October. The condemned crate went up the ladder into the loft and stayed thirty-five years.

**Quantities.** Invoice of 14 April 1897 — 6 doz. pipettes at .35 = 25.20; 12 doz. test bottles, 8
per cent neck, at .22 = 31.68; acid measure .90; 4-bottle hand tester 18.00; freight 2.15; **total
$77.93**. 72 pipettes; 9 survived unbroken, two chipped at the tip; each short 0.88 c.c.
Replacement order: six dozen pipettes and six dozen bottles.

**Documents.** The invoice, reproduced twice independently (r07 item 2; r20); the Ammon letter
book, 2 July 1923 (r20; r07 item 10); Strawn's entry of 12 June 1923 in photostat (r06; r07 item
9); Junia Ammon, chapter IX (r20).

### 4. The hay, and the factory over the ridge (1902–1959)

**Who.** Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green, chairman of the 1908 committee and no patron of the station;
Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S., of Ashlin; Jerome Cudd and his daughter Rosalie at Alder Corners, Vessey
County.

**What happened.** From **1902** the Hollow men said their milk tested under the same class of milk
at the Green. The gap was **nineteen hundredths of a point**, and — the fact nobody would look at
— as much in July as in February, wet years and dry.

In January 1908 the Association named a committee under Grigg, which sampled the Hessel Bottom
herds through **January, February and March 1908**, drew and sealed their own samples, visited each of the
**six** farms, tried the scales against the patrons', and watched the test made. They found no
dishonesty in any quarter, the milk whole and the scales honest, and reported
that "the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and the remedy lies with the land and not
with the station." It closed the question for eleven years. Grigg reprinted it in 1919 claiming the College had since confirmed him at "two tenths" — it had
not — and that they had sampled every month of 1908; his own report says three. Kepp revived it in 1926 — sour, limeless
ground and sedge hay — giving the gap as 0.21 and waving off the 1911 interval as "six weeks of
summer figures."

The theory was dead before Grigg revived it. **Bulletin No. 214 (1911):** *"Bog hay fed as the whole
of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in
winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."* A quarter the size, and the
wrong shape.

Over Redlow Ridge lay an unintended control. From **1915** four of the six Bottom men hauled to
Jerome Cudd's cheese factory in the flush, over a switchback shut November to April
— **sixteen seasons, to 1930**. The same herds off the same wet ground ran
**3.78 to 3.82** every summer, mean about 3.80, while running 3.61 at home.

**Documents.** The 1908 report (r15; r07 item 11); Grigg's 1919 letter (r15); Bulletin No. 214 (r07
item 12; r04; r11; r22); Kepp 1926 (r16); Rosalie Cudd's letter and its sixteen-season table (r10).

### 5. The station gain: the answer in plain sight (1898–1945)

**Who.** Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper; Hobart Sill, buttermaker; Bertram Kyle, clerk; Selby Vose,
manager; Dell Rundle, schoolteacher.

**What happened.** Teague came in as bookkeeper in 1905 and devised the station-gain line himself, not
(whatever the valley said) a dodge of the founders. On one side he entered the fat credited to
patrons on the station's test; on the other, the fat that actually arrived at Larrow Green in the
station's cream and was weighed into the vats. The second was always the larger; he carried the
difference to the general account. The 1898 entry, and one like it every year after:

> *Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at
> Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as
> usual. Carried to the general account.*

The whole case, written annually for twenty-five years. 57,760 lb is 1,600,000 lb
of milk at 3.61 per cent; 60,800 lb is the same milk at 3.80. The gain is 5.26 per cent of the
credited figure ("five and a quarter") and exactly 5.00 per cent of the fat delivered — the glass, in ink, on an open shelf.

Nobody read it. Sill weighed that cream in himself for forty-one years and found it heavy every
year — always more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought, never less — and made nothing of it. Kyle copied the line twenty-five years and never wondered: "A figure
with a name on it has been explained." Vose signed the annual sheet every spring from 1913, putting
his thumb on the bottom figure and signing. Teague, by his own uncorroborated account, put it to
Vose once in 1916 and was told to let it alone. The line was read out at the annual meeting to
fifty farmers. Only Rundle drew the inference, at the ledgers while his 1939 pamphlet was in type,
and retracted his theft chapter in a note: stolen cream would have reached the Green light; it
reached it heavy.

**Quantities.** 5¼ % of credited = 5 % of delivered; 3,040 lb in 1898; **76,000 lb** accumulated
over 1898–1922, worth **$19,000**.

**Documents.** The annual ledgers, station account (r05; r07 item 6; r23; r12); the Larrow Green
receiving and churn books (r07 item 16; r14).

### 6. The circular of 1919, and two houses in sight of each other

**Who.** Ansel Keddie and his wife Orra (née Brant, b. 1866, d. 1948; married 1889); Emmet Brant
(1854–1929), her brother, largest farmer on Hessel Bottom — 60-odd of its 160 acres, 38 cows — and
the station's largest patron in every year of record; Selby Vose, who signed the circular; Bertram
Kyle, who typed it.

**What happened.** In June 1919, over the manager's signature and no one else's, a printed circular
went to the station's patrons warning that added water would be looked for and dealt with. It named
nobody. It went to **nine families** — six farms, but a farm holds more than one family. Keddie
would not mail them nor leave them on the platform; he carried them round himself, stood at each door while the man read it, and came home at eleven at night.

Two days later Emmet Brant came up and would not come in. He stood in the yard and said he had
hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and Ansel knew it better than any man
living, and asked why he had carried the paper if he did not believe it. Ansel said he
carried it because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet went home and never entered that
house again. Two families who had eaten Sunday dinner together for thirty years, in sight of each
other's chimney smoke, did not speak for **ten years** — not at the store, not at a funeral. It
ended in the spring of 1929, when Emmet died.

The valley kept the paper — one family has it in a desk drawer yet. Vose defended it in 1926 and would write every word again. The charge was groundless and known to be: Strawn had tested that
station with the lactometer at every visit, never found added water, and told the manager so in
1919 to his face; and over the ridge Jerome Cudd, who could not have been deceived — watered milk
gives a curd that will not knit — made no complaint of the four Bottom men in sixteen summers.

**Documents.** Orra Keddie's diary, June 1919 and two days after (r24); Keddie's unsent letter
(r03); Kyle on typing it (r23); Strawn's 1919 entry (r06); Vose's 1926 address (r08); r01; r09; r17.

### 7. The hearing of 1925, and the dismissal

**Who.** The patrons; Alonzo Frick, attorney of Ashlin, chairman of a board of three; Selby Vose;
Wilbur Teague; Effie Loomis, who took every word; Ansel Keddie.

**What happened.** In **January 1925** the patrons petitioned: their milk had never tested as their
neighbors' tested, they had been paid accordingly for twenty-five years, and they asked to be made
whole. The board sat **three times** — the first at the Association's office at Ashlin on **17 March
1925**, the second and third at Frick's insistence in the station's own weigh room, so it might see
the plant instead of hearing it described. Four exhibits went before it and nothing else: the station test book; the
station's receiving book; Emmet Brant's private weigh book, lent and wanted back; and Strawn's
circuit notebook for 1923 — the year he condemned the glass. Nobody laid one beside another and
looked.

Three explanations were argued: water, put by Vose without heat and without proof; theft by the
tester, insinuated by Teague, who then observed that he made no charge against anybody; and the hay,
read in from the 1908 report. None was proved and none accepted.

Keddie's statement of 17 March ran nine sentences: he had kept the book in his own hand; he had
never written a figure over a figure; he had
tested as he was taught and as the state's man had watched him do it, thirty-one times a week for
twenty-nine seasons; he could not tell them why the figures were what they were; he had never taken
a pound of cream out of that station.

On **30 April 1925** the Association dismissed him, four days before the board reported, with no
finding against him then or ever. On **4 May** the board found *"no cause established"* and awarded
**$3,800**, without admission, charged to the station account. Loomis wrote both halves of that
sentence into the minute book. The award was one fifth of what was owed, split among nine families
over twenty-five years. The condemned glass lay in the loft twenty feet above the room where two of
the three sittings were held, and was never mentioned by patrons, officers or board.

**Documents.** Minute book of the board (r07 item 7; r02 §6); Frick's exhibit list (r13); Keddie's
statement (r03; r07 item 8); (r23; r24; r08).

### 8. The ladder (1958–1959)

**Who.** Ivy Keddie, b. 1931, Duncan's daughter and Ansel's **granddaughter**, graduate student in
dairy husbandry at the State Agricultural College at Farland; Professor Leland Vaught.

**What happened.** In February 1958 Ivy Keddie went up the Hollow to the closed station, up a ladder nailed to the
wall, and found under a tarpaulin a deal crate stenciled AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET, carried
up in June 1923 and untouched since. Nine condemned pipettes were unbroken in the
straw.

On **9 March 1958** at Farland she delivered each of the nine against a calibrated burette, five
runs apiece: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. **Mean 16.72 c.c. against
a blown mark of 17.6 — short 0.88 c.c., ninety-five hundredths of the mark the bottle's neck was
ruled for.** The rest is arithmetic: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80.

She assembled five sets of records, no two ever in one room before: the station test book; Emmet
Brant's weigh book, lent by Roy Brant; the Alder Corners figures, copied for her by Rosalie Cudd;
Strawn's circuit books, unasked-for at Farland since 1947; and the Association's ledgers, where the
missing fat stood credited under a name.

**Bulletin No. 471** appeared in October 1958: *"No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream
was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests
were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the
difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own
ledgers, year by year, as station gain."*

The *Ashlin Chronicle* ran it on 3 October with half a dozen
figures wrong and an unsupported charge against Ammon & Sons. In 1959 Ivy read "What the Crate
Held" to the Historical Society with the crate on the table; Falke reported to the supervisors,
withdrawing his concession to the hay; the test book and crate went to the Historical Rooms.

**Quantities.** 9 pipettes; 5 sets of records; 0.88 c.c.; 76,000 lb; $19,000. Condemned 1923, cause
found 1958 — thirty-five years; dismissed 1925 — thirty-three; dead twenty-four years; his widow
ten.

**Documents.** Measurement sheet of 9 March 1958, signed (r07 item 14; r04); Bulletin No. 471 (r07
item 15; r11); Ivy Keddie's 1959 talk (r04); r10; r19; r22; r07; r18.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** Ansel married Orra Brant in 1889; their son
Duncan was born in 1894 (the diary has him five years old in September 1899 and seventeen in
October 1911); Duncan's daughter Ivy was born in 1931. Ivy says it herself in 1959 with the books
in front of her — "Ansel Keddie… was my grandfather. My father, Duncan Keddie, who taught school at
Ashlin, was his son" — and Duncan sets it out in 1968 expressly because he has "already twice seen
it printed otherwise." Roy Brant independently calls her "Duncan's girl." What points elsewhere is
the *Ashlin Chronicle* of 3 October 1958 and paragraph 2 of Falke's county report, both of which
call her Ansel's great-niece. Falke corrects himself in his own closing note ("I am informed, and
have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two
is mine"), and the *Chronicle* was put together in a day and a half from a press release and two
telephone calls. A family member's own statement, twice given, beats a hurried newspaper; and one
of the two errors is retracted by the man who made it.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins.** Derivation: Orra Brant (Ansel's wife) and
Emmet Brant were sister and brother → their children, Duncan Keddie and Roy Brant, are first
cousins → their daughters, Ivy and Hazel, are second cousins. All three of the people in a position
to know state it in the same terms — Roy ("my daughter Hazel and his daughter Ivy are second
cousins"), Duncan ("the children of first cousins… not cousins once removed, which is a different
relation"), and Hazel herself — and Rundle's 1959 footnote agrees. Toland and Oram both say "first
cousin once removed," which is the village's shorthand for any cousinship it cannot count; two
narrators repeating the same loose formula do not outweigh three people reciting their own descent.
What the sources leave open about the intervening generations: (i) whether Orra and Emmet were full
siblings — Roy and Duncan say full and Roy pre-empts the point ("whatever you may hear from people
who like a story with a stepmother in it"), while Hazel says half-siblings by their father's second
wife; if Hazel were right the two would be half-second-cousins, which does not change the degree;
(ii) neither Duncan's wife nor Roy's is ever named, nor Emmet's; (iii) no source says whether
Duncan or Roy had other children, so we cannot say Ivy and Hazel were the only pair in that
generation; (iv) Hazel's birth year is never given, and Roy's only as "sixty-seven" in 1955.

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose**, first president of the Association 1889–1911, who
drafted the by-laws. Selby says it himself from the platform in 1926 ("My father, who was your
first president and who wrote the by-law under which every patron in this Association is paid on
the test of the station where he delivers"); Loomis's record calls him "son of the late president";
Kyle, who sat outside his door for twenty-five years, says "the son, whatever is said at Ashlin";
Duncan lists it among the things always got wrong. Toland and Rundle say nephew — two narrators
carrying the same village error, neither of them in the office. It matters to Ostrey Hollow twice
over. First, structurally: Gideon wrote Article VII, which made the station's own test the whole of
the bargain with no appeal, so the patrons were bound to whatever figure the short glass produced
and had, as Frick put it, "no remedy on earth except to quarrel with a figure." Second,
personally: the manager who pressed the watering theory, signed the station-gain sheets for
twenty-five years without reading the lines above the total, and defended the 1919 circular to the
end, was the author's son defending his father's rule — and he put that authorship before the
membership in the same address in which he blamed the patrons.

**B4. No relationship between the Voses and the Keddies is established, and the claim that there
was one is refuted as far as such a claim can be.** Toland says Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were
first cousins — "everybody knew that and nobody wrote it down anywhere" — and Oram says the two
families "were cousins of some sort… I could not draw you the line on paper." Both label their own
evidence as hearsay, and both are relaying village talk rather than independent knowledge. Against
them stands Duncan Keddie, Orra's son, who says flatly that there was no connection whatever, that
he has heard it from two women in Ashlin, that it is talk and has been talk since before he could
walk, and that his mother — a woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the
connection — never once put a Vose in it; he adds that the confusion arises from the Ashlin Voses,
who were no relation to Gideon either. No document in any of the twenty-four sources names a tie.
Method: a family member's direct denial with a stated reason for confusion, against two
self-declared rumors, one of which admits it cannot be drawn on paper. Strictly, a negative cannot
be proved from these sources; what can be said is that nothing supports the tie and the person best
placed to know denies it.

## Section C — Math

### C1

**(a) The tests were low by 5 per cent — one part in twenty — of the truth.**
From the glassware: the Babcock test lets the operator read percent straight off the bottle's neck
only because the charge of milk is a fixed known quantity; the maker rules that neck for 17.6 c.c.
and no other charge. The nine surviving pipettes were blown 17.6 c.c. and delivered a mean of
16.72 c.c. (individual means of five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73,
16.74, 16.71; sum 150.48 ÷ 9 = 16.72).

    16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly
    short by 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c.;  0.88 ÷ 17.6 = 0.05

A charge short by one part in twenty liberates a fat column short by one part in twenty, and the
neck, which knows nothing of the substitution, is read in the ordinary way. Every test read
**95 per cent of the truth**. Note the error is *proportional, not additive* — it removes a
twentieth of whatever was there, so a herd recorded at 4.56 lost 0.24 of a point and one recorded
at 3.42 lost 0.18.

**(b) True average 3.80; recorded 3.61; short by 0.19 of a point.**

    3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80
    3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19   (= 5% of 3.80)

3.80 is confirmed six independent ways: the 1896 season on Larrow Green's borrowed glass (3.80);
the nine weeks of 1911 (3.80); 1924 on the new glass (3.80); the ledger's "received at Larrow
Green" figure (60,800 lb ÷ 1,600,000 lb = 3.80 per cent); the Alder Corners table (3.78–3.82, mean
about 3.80, sixteen summers); and the glass arithmetic above.

**(c) 76,000 lb of butterfat, over the 25 full delivery years 1898–1922.**

    milk received, 1898–1922                 40,000,000 lb  (avg 1,600,000/yr)
    credited at 3.61%   40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb
    delivered at 3.80%  40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb
    not credited                            =    76,000 lb
    check: 40,000,000 × 0.0019 = 76,000 lb

**(d) $19,000, of which the patrons received one fifth — $3,800.**

    76,000 lb × $0.25/lb = $19,000
    $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20 = one fifth

The price is the ledger-derived average of 25 cents over the period (Loomis from Teague's annual
sheets; Kyle, who was set to find it for the arbitration; the ledger's own $19,000 gloss). Sill and
Kepp both say 27 cents; see E.

### C2 — Emmet Brant, 1910

Given: 200,000 lb of milk delivered "even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing," paid on a test of
3.42; butterfat that year 24 cents.

    (a) credited:  200,000 × 0.0342 = 6,840 lb
    (b) true test: 3.42 ÷ 0.95      = 3.60 exactly
        owed:      200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb
        not credited: 7,200 − 6,840 =   360 lb
        check: 200,000 × (0.0360 − 0.0342) = 200,000 × 0.0018 = 360 lb
    (c) 360 lb × $0.24 = $86.40

(At the 25-cent period average it would be $90.00; 24 cents is the figure Roy Brant gives for that
particular year, and the question asks what it was worth *that year*.)

### C3 — the same 25 years

    (a) credited to the station's patrons:   1,444,000 lb  (40,000,000 × 3.61%)
    (b) reaching Larrow Green in the cream:  1,520,000 lb  (40,000,000 × 3.80%;
        equivalently 25 × the ledger's 60,800 lb)
    (c) difference 76,000 lb, which is:
          76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = 5.00 %   of the fat actually delivered
          76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 5.263 %  of the fat credited  ("five and a quarter")

**Be exact about the denominator.** Both percentages are right and they are not interchangeable.
The ledger's "five and a quarter per cent, as usual" is reckoned on the *credited* figure, because
a bookkeeper reckons a surplus against the entry he has already made (3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.263 %).
**5 per cent is the one that is also the glass** (0.88 ÷ 17.6). Teague's prose and Vaught's §V both
attach "five and a quarter" to the delivered fat; the entry Teague himself quotes proves them
wrong.

Caveat worth stating: (b) is the fat *weighed and tested into the vats at Larrow Green*, and it
comes out exactly equal to 3.80 per cent of the intake — so these books carry no allowance for fat
lost in the skim or in handling. Whatever such loss was, it is invisible in these figures and I
have not invented one.

### C4

**(a) $77.93.** The invoice of 14 April 1897, reproduced independently twice, itemizes:

    6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35     25.20
    12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at .22    31.68

### C5

**(a) In use 26 years and about six weeks; the money runs over 25 full delivery years.**
- Elapsed service: **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923** = 26 years, 1 month, 11 days. Deduct the nine
  weeks of 1911 when a borrowed measure was on the bench and the faulty glass was actually in use
  about **25 years 11 months**.
- Money: **25 full delivery years, 1898 through 1922**.

They differ because the two end-years are part-years and the ledger series will not carry them.
The glass went in on 1 May 1897, so 1897 is a broken season — the station's own summary leaf says
"1897, part year" and gives no average for it — and it was condemned on 12 June 1923, so 1923 is
broken at the other end. The claim, the ledger extract got out for the arbitration (40,000,000 lb),
and the run of 3.61 all begin with 1898 and end with 1922. The 1896 season is outside the question
altogether: the station was then testing on Larrow Green's borrowed glass and averaged 3.80.

**(b) Cannot be determined from the sources.** The curator states it plainly: the station column of
the ledgers begins with 1897, the intake for 4 May to the end of 1896 "is not in the ledgers and is
nowhere else," and inquirers must be told there is none. Kyle confirms that the first season's milk
was carried on the creamery's own books and the sheets were never put with the station papers.
Two recollections survive and they do not agree: Sill says "about nine hundred thousand pounds" and
Kyle says "a good eleven hundred thousand" — a spread of over 20 per cent. Neither is documented;
Kyle expressly says "it is not in any book I ever ruled" and he was about eleven years old in 1896,
so his "what I understood at the time" is hearsay; Sill's is a recollection given thirty-eight
years after the fact. What the sources establish is only that the first season was a partial one
(4 May to year-end) and unusually large for a hill station starting from nothing. That is not
enough to state a figure.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The nine weeks of 1911.**
*When and how long:* the station's own pipette broke on the morning of **8 May 1911** and a
borrowed measure was used until **10 July 1911**, when the station's own came back — 63 days,
exactly nine weeks. Both endpoints are dated entries in the test book made on the day
("*May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure.*" / "*July 10. Our own measure in
use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.*"), and Orra Keddie's diary
independently records A. coming home cross about a broken measure on 8 May 1911.

*Variable:* one object — the measure that drew the charge. *Held constant:* the same patrons, the
same herds on the same ground, the same season running through it, the same feed, the same acid,
the same test bottles, the same hand tester, the same scales, the same operator working the same
way at the same hour. Strawn happened to visit that June, watched a test through, and wrote
"Nothing here to note" — outside confirmation that the method had not changed.

*Result:* the station average rose from 3.61 to 3.80, held there for the nine weeks, and fell back
to 3.61 **on the day** the station's own measure returned. An A–B–A reversal with a same-day
return to baseline.

*What it establishes that a calculation cannot:* the 1958 burette shows only that the glass *could*
produce a 5 per cent deficit. The 1911 interval shows that changing the glass *did* remove it, in
the field, forty-seven years earlier, recorded contemporaneously by a man who did not know what he
was recording and set a question mark against it — an experiment nobody designed, nobody could
have biased, and nobody read. It kills at a stroke every explanation resting on the herds, the
ground, the hay, the season, the men or anyone's honesty, because none of those was altered in
those nine weeks and the figures were.

*What it does not establish:* (i) not the size of the error over the whole period — nine weeks of
one summer cannot fix a twenty-five-year average, and the flatness of the 0.19 gap has to come
from the test book; (ii) not the *direction* on its own — the interval proves only that the two
measures differed, not that the station's was short rather than the borrowed one long; direction
comes from the burette in 1958, from the 1896 season on Larrow Green glass, and from 1924 onward;
(iii) not *which* property of the glass differed — nobody measured the borrowed pipette, and it
went back; (iv) it establishes nothing about the money, and nothing about whether anyone ever
behaved dishonestly at any other time. Where the borrowed measure came from cannot be determined:
Ivy says it came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon, Teague says the replacement came "from
town," and Roy Brant says the state man left his own that June — Roy's version fails on the dates,
since the substitution began 8 May and Strawn's own book puts him there in June with nothing of the
kind recorded, but no document names the lender.

**D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream.**

1. **The cream arrived heavy at the other end of the road.** Hobart Sill weighed the Hollow's
   cream into the cold room himself, over again after the platform man, for forty-one years: "There
   was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there. Always more. Never less"
   — on page after page of the receiving book in his own hand. Stolen cream arrives light.
2. **The Association's own ledger says the same in a different currency.** The station-gain line,
   every year from 1898, shows fat received at Larrow Green exceeding fat credited at the station
   by about 5¼ per cent. Rundle drew exactly this inference in 1939, on seeing the line by
   accident, and retracted his own theft chapter in a note added while the pamphlet was in type.
3. **The nine weeks of 1911.** The same man, the same room, no second man and no lock added, no
   supervision changed — and the figures rose the day the measure changed and fell the day it came
   back. No thief calibrates his thefts to which glass tube is on the bench.
4. (Spare, and independent again.) **The shape of the loss.** It was the same exact fraction of
   every patron's test, rich milk and poor, in every month of twenty-six years. Hand-work gives
   scatter; this is a constant of proportionality.

*Why these are independent.* Each rests on a different physical record kept by a different man for
a different purpose in a different building: Sill's receiving book at Larrow Green (an observation
of matter, made by a man with no stake in the office's arithmetic); Teague's ledger at Ashlin (a
documentary record written by the very man who made the accusation, and therefore not shapeable to
his theory after the fact); and Keddie's test book at the station (dated field entries made
fourteen years before anyone accused him of anything). They fail together only if three separate
books, kept by three men who were not speaking to each other about it, are all wrong in the same
direction.

*Which survives without the 1958 measurement:* reasons 1, 2 and 4 all predate the burette entirely
and never depend on it. **Reason 1 in particular** — Sill's heavy cream — was on the record from
1893 onward and was stated aloud in 1934, twenty-four years before anyone measured a pipette.

*Did the bookkeeper ever question the entries that refute his own accusation?* On the record, no.
He devised the line himself in 1905, wrote it for forty years with the words "as usual" in it, and
admits what evenness does: "A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture."
His only claimed challenge is a private word to Vose in **1916**, after which he was told to let it
alone and did — and that rests solely on his own 1948 memoir, written thirty-two years later in
self-defence, and is contradicted by Kyle, who sat outside the manager's door and says Teague
"never asked anybody anything in forty years." Whether the 1916 conversation happened **cannot be
determined**. What is certain is that in 1925, standing before a board that was weighing a charge
of theft he had just insinuated, he did not tell it that his own books showed the cream arriving
heavy every year.

**D3. Did Vose know, before the glass was condemned, that the station's tests ran low? Yes —
unambiguously, and he said so himself.**
In his 1926 address: "For a generation the milk brought to that station tested below the milk
brought to Larrow Green." He had built policy on it: he wrote and signed the 1919 circular
precisely because "a manager who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing
to the men who make them is not being kind to them; he is being idle at their expense." The
grievance had been raised at annual meetings since 1902 and reached his desk in the form of a
petition. And he signed the annual sheet carrying the station-gain line every spring from 1913 —
"a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him."

The harder question — whether he knew the ledger showed the tests to be *understated* rather than
merely *low* — **cannot be determined from the sources**, and the two halves of the evidence point
opposite ways. For: the sheet was in front of him twenty-five times, and Teague says he was told
about the account in words in 1916 and answered "let it alone." Against: Kyle, an eyewitness for
twenty-five years, describes his signing habit exactly — "He read the totals and he never read the
lines above them… take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign" — and his
1926 address argues the watering case from the 1924 rise while never mentioning the station gain,
which is an argument no man who understood that line could have made in public without being
demolished by his own bookkeeper. Signing is not reading. What the sources establish is that the
information reached him annually and that he did not act on it; what they cannot establish is
whether he took it in.

## Section F — Theory

**F1. What actually produced the shortfall.**

In April 1897 the Association bought its Ostrey Hollow station a complete testing outfit from
Ammon & Sons of Tarnet, including six dozen milk pipettes blown to the state standard charge of
17.6 c.c. The lot ran short — on Junia Ammon's reconstruction, a reground mold and a gauge knocked
off its nail, an accident no check on the blowing floor could catch, because a spot check looks for
inconsistency and this lot was perfectly consistent. Every pipette in it delivered about 16.72
c.c.: 0.88 c.c. under the mark, exactly 0.95 of it.

*Why an error at all, and why exactly that size.* The Babcock test lets an operator read percent
straight off the neck of the bottle for one reason: the charge is a fixed known quantity, and the
maker rules the neck for that quantity and no other. Put in a short charge and less fat is
liberated, a shorter column stands in the neck, and the neck — which knows nothing of the
substitution — is read in the ordinary way. The reading is not merely low; it is low **in the exact
proportion by which the charge was short**. 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95. So every test at Ostrey Hollow for
twenty-six years read precisely 95 per cent of the truth: a station truly averaging 3.80 recorded
3.61, and the 0.19-point "gap" was never a fixed quantity at all but a twentieth of whatever was
there — which is why the best milk lost most (4.56 lost 0.24; 3.42 lost 0.18), why every herd still
ranked correctly against every other herd at that station, and why the books looked like excellent
work. The error was invisible from inside the plant because the pipette is the one instrument that
cannot be checked against anything else: it *is* what the plant measures with. Scales have test
weights, acid has a titration, the neck can be checked with mercury, the crank can be counted. An
instrument that is itself the measure cannot be caught by measuring with it. It can only be caught
by a burette brought in from outside.

*Why it began when it did.* On 1 May 1897 the station stopped testing on the outfit Hobart Sill had
lent it from his own bench at Larrow Green and put the Ammon glass into service. The 1896 season,
on Sill's glass, averaged 3.80 — the truth. 1897 is a broken year. From 1898, the first full year
on the new glass, the average is 3.61 and never moves.

*Why it ended when it did.* On 12 June 1923 Merle Strawn ran the station's pipettes against a
calibrated burette — an instrument the state began issuing to its field men only in 1921, and one
he had not run at that station in 1922 because the day was short and he wrote down that he hadn't.
He condemned the whole crate on the spot. The order went to Tarnet on 28 June, Ammon shipped that
month, the new glass was in use from July and delivered its mark when Strawn checked it in October.
From 1924 the average is 3.80.

*Why nothing at the station or on the farms had to change for either.* Because nothing at the
station or on the farms was ever wrong. The scales were true at every inspection for thirty years;
the lactometer never once found added water; the sampling, the acid, the bottles, the tester and
his method were identical on both sides of both boundaries. The only thing that changed on 1 May
1897, and again in July 1923, was which piece of glass drew the charge. That is exactly why the
1924 correction looked like a miracle to the valley: no man had done anything differently, no cow
had changed, and no one could give a reason — because the reason had never been in the cows or the
cans or the men.

**The three competing explanations.**

1. **Water in the cans.** Advanced by **Selby Vose**, manager: the 1919 circular, his evidence to
   the board in 1925, and his 1926 annual address, where he made the 1924 rise "the measure of it"
   — water was going in and it stopped when the Association began to look. *Defeated by:* Strawn's
   lactometer, run at that station on every visit from 1907 and never once finding added water,
   which he told Vose to his face in 1919; by Rosalie Cudd, since watered milk cannot be hidden from
   a cheesemaker — the curd comes soft and will not knit, and the cheese tells him the next morning
   — and in sixteen summers her father never docked one of the four Hessel Bottom men a cent; by
   Sill's cream arriving *heavy* at Larrow Green every year, which is impossible if the milk was
   thinned before it was tested; by the mechanical constancy of the deficit, since water is added by
   men and men are not uniform to two decimals for twenty-six years; and decisively by the nine
   weeks of 1911, when the same nine families' milk tested 3.80 without a thing changing in the
   cans. Vose's own supporting numbers are wrong and mutually inconsistent (a rise of 0.21 to an
   average of 3.90, off a base of 3.61).
2. **The tester stealing cream and writing low tests to cover the loss.** Advanced by **Wilbur
   Teague**, bookkeeper, insinuated to the board in 1925 and repeated in his 1948 memoir ("a
   business arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less…").
   *Defeated by his own ledger:* the station's cream arrived at Larrow Green carrying about 5¼ per
   cent more fat than the station's books said had been bought, every year, in an entry he wrote
   himself — and Sill weighed it in and said so for forty years. Stolen cream arrives light; this
   arrived heavy. Rundle worked it out in 1939 and retracted his chapter. Defeated also by the nine
   weeks of 1911 (same man, same room, no supervision added, figures up and back with the glass);
   by the fact that the loss was an exact constant fraction of every patron's test, which no hand
   can produce; and by the milk weights, since Emmet Brant's own scale and the station's agreed
   pound for pound for thirty years.
3. **The wet hay of Hessel Bottom.** Advanced by **Nyle Grigg's committee** in 1908, revived by
   Grigg in 1919, carried furthest by **Dr. Roland Kepp** in 1926, and half-conceded by **Falke** in
   1959. *Defeated by the College's own Bulletin No. 214 (1911):* bog hay as the whole of the
   roughage depressed the test by not more than 0.05 in winter and by nothing measurable on summer
   pasture — a quarter the size of the observed 0.19, and the wrong shape, since the Ostrey Hollow
   gap stood at 0.19 in July exactly as in February. Both Grigg and Kepp cite that bulletin as
   finding "two tenths"; it does not, and Falke withdrew his concession the moment he read it.
   Defeated also by Alder Corners — the same four Hessel Bottom herds, off the same wet ground, in
   the same summers, ran 3.78 to 3.82 over the ridge for sixteen seasons while running 3.61 at
   home — and by 1911, when the ground did not change and the figures did.

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware? No.** Effie Loomis, who took down every
word of all three sessions, states it flatly: the crate "was never mentioned in the hearing at all…
Not a word of it was spoken in any of the three sessions, by the patrons, by the officers, or by
the board," and the award's phrase about "the books and appliances of the station" was a form of
words the board used, not a list of anything. Frick's own exhibit list is four books and no glass.
The appliances the board saw tried at the station were the ones then in service — the replacement
lot bought in 1923, which delivered its mark. The condemned crate was in the loft twenty feet above
the room where two of the three sittings were held, and remained there another thirty-three years.

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was? The glass.** Duncan Keddie, seventeen that
summer and in the house, says his father came home in the summer of 1911 saying it was something in
the glass, said it twice — at supper and again by the door, which was not his way — and never dared
put it in the book, having nothing to hold in his hand but a suspicion about a piece of glass
against the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county.
What went into the book was the question mark he let stand for fourteen years. That squares with
everything he wrote: the 1925 statement ("I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are") and
the unsent letter, which puts nothing in the question mark's place and charges nobody. Against it
stands Frick's recollection, twenty-one years after the event, of a corridor remark that Keddie
privately thought the patrons were at fault. I do not accept it: it cannot be reconciled with
Keddie's own writing of that same spring, which expressly declines to carry any charge against the
men who signed the circular, and Frick offers it to correct a legend ("that he suspected nobody,
which is not [true]") that the glass suspicion equally corrects. It remains the one point on which
two first-hand accounts genuinely collide and neither can be eliminated. Ivy's public statement in
1958 — "I would like it printed that he never knew" — is generous and, on her uncle's… on her
father's evidence ten years later, wrong.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** For twenty-six years the Larrow Valley Association's station at Ostrey Hollow paid its
patrons on tests exactly five per cent too low, because the pipettes bought from Ammon & Sons in
1897 delivered 16.72 c.c. where they were marked 17.6, and a short charge in a bottle ruled for a
full one reads short in the same proportion. Seventy-six thousand pounds of butterfat, worth
nineteen thousand dollars, went uncredited. It took eight records to see it, because each held a
piece and none held two: the by-law making the station's test final; the tester's book; the invoice
and the inspector's condemnation; the hay theory and the cheese factory that refuted it; the ledger
line that spelled the answer out annually under a name; a circular that split two families; a
hearing that paid a fifth and dismissed an innocent man; and a granddaughter with a burette.

## Solver's notes

**Confidence in the eight-story cut: moderate-to-high on seven, low on one.** Seven of the eight
have a hard spine — a dated document set, a distinct cast, and a beginning and end (founding rule;
station and tester; glass; hay and the ridge; ledger; circular and family; hearing; discovery).
The cut I am least sure of is where the **1911 nine weeks** belongs. It has as much claim to be a
ninth story as the hay theory has to be a fourth, and if the original eight included it as its own
record, my Story 2 and Story 8 have each absorbed part of it. My second-guess partition would
promote 1911 and demote Alder Corners into the hay story entirely — which is what I have already
done with Alder Corners, so the risk is one-sided. I am also not certain whether the original set
treated the Ammon & Sons glass house and the 1958 discovery as one strand or two; I split them
(Stories 3 and 8) on the grounds that they have no cast in common and thirty-five years between
them.

**"Cannot be determined from the sources" — eight items.**
1. The year Article VII was adopted (1891 / 1892 / 1893). The minute book covering the first four
   annual meetings was lost in the 1938 office move; the printed by-laws bear no date. Falke
   declined to state it in 1959 for the same reason.
2. The milk received at the station in its first season, 4 May to 31 December 1896. Not in the
   ledgers and nowhere else; two undocumented recollections, 900,000 lb and 1,100,000 lb.
3. What the patrons' 1925 petition actually claimed. Frick recalls 67,000 lb; Rundle records
   $19,600; they do not reconcile at any price per pound, and the petition is not in the sources.
4. Where the borrowed measure of 1911 came from. Three answers (Larrow Green by cream wagon; "from
   town"; the state man left his own), no document, and the third fails on the dates.
5. Whether Ammon & Sons knew the lot ran short. The works books that recorded gauge, glass, man and
   day burned in 1931. The *Chronicle*'s accusation of bad faith rests on nothing but the firm's
   inability to answer.
6. Whether Selby Vose understood that the station-gain line contradicted his own theory (as
   distinct from knowing the tests ran low, which he certainly did).
7. Whether Teague really raised the station account with Vose in 1916. His own memoir, thirty-two
   years later, against Kyle's eyewitness "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years."
8. Whether the Alder Corners factory dates from 1913 or 1914.

**Where an apparent agreement is a document contradicting itself, or contradicted by one.**
- Vaught's Bulletin 471 is quoted with confidence by the *Chronicle*, Falke and Oram, but its own
  §V arithmetic (16.27 c.c. → 118,000 lb) contradicts its own §VII conclusion (one part in twenty →
  76,000 lb), and Vaught says so on the page. The bulletin is right where it is quoted and wrong
  where it calculates.
- Nym's accession notes are the most authoritative-looking document in the set and carry three
  errors: swapped keepers for items 3 and 4, the crate's find-place, and a Larrow Green average
  three-quarters of a point above the station's.
- Junia Ammon reproduces an invoice and then misreads its total in her own prose, and denies
  selling into Ordell County before 1900 on the page facing an 1897 Ordell County invoice.
- Grigg's 1919 letter misreports the report printed beneath it.
- Loomis, who is scrupulous about separating minutes from memory, still gives "seven farms" on
  Hessel Bottom from memory where the 1908 report says six.

**Self-contradicting retellings:** r01, r03, r05, r08, r11, r12, r13, r14, r15, r17, r18, r19, r20,
r22, r23, and (arithmetically) r06. Full list with the specific contradictions is in E1(ii). The
most instructive are r11 and r20, where the narrator quotes a document and then misstates it in the
adjoining sentence — the general pattern of this whole corpus, and the reason "document over
memory" resolves so much of it.

**Ambiguous items I resolved but would not defend to the last ditch.**
- *Nine vs eleven families* for the 1919 circular. I took nine, on Keddie's own written statement
  and three recipient households, over Orra's contemporaneous diary. A contemporaneous document
  losing to later ones is uncomfortable; the tiebreak is that the diary's figure is hearsay from
  the man whose own letter says nine.
- *What Keddie believed.* I took Duncan's 1911 memory over Frick's 1925 corridor recollection,
  because it squares with Keddie's own writing. Frick is an honest and careful witness and I may be
  wrong.
- *Orra and Emmet full or half siblings.* Two against one, and it changes no relationship degree.
- *The Association's office at Ashlin, not Larrow Green.* Kyle's own header says Larrow Green; the
  hearing's first session, Teague's memoir and Frick all say Ashlin. Possibly the office moved.
- *Larrow Green's own average at 3.78.* This rests on Sill and Falke against Nym; I took it because
  it is the reading under which the whole history coheres, which is a weaker argument than a
  document.

**One structural observation about the corpus.** The narrators nearest the facts and least
interested in them — Strawn, who refused to have a theory; Sill, who weighed the cream and thought
nothing of it; Kyle, who copied the line and never wondered; Rosalie Cudd, whose father never
compared a figure with anybody — hold the decisive evidence, and every one of them says in so many
words that he made nothing of it at the time. The narrators with theories carry the errors. That
is the shape of the test as much as the shape of the history.
