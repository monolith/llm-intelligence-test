# Answers — Part 2: Section A (Stories 5–8) and Section B

## Story 5: The 1925 Arbitration

**Who:** the Ostrey Hollow patrons (petitioners), Alonzo Frick (arbitration chairman, an Ashlin attorney), the three-man board, Ansel Keddie (dismissed).

**What:** In January 1925 the Ostrey Hollow patrons formally petitioned the Association for redress. A board of arbitration was formed under the by-laws, chaired by Alonzo Frick — "because I was the one of the three who kept no cow." It sat three times: first at the Association's Ashlin office on 17 March 1925, then twice more at the station itself, in its own weigh room, so the board could see the physical plant rather than have it described secondhand. All three rival explanations (water, cream theft, wet hay) were argued before it; none was accepted. Only four documents were formally entered as exhibits: the station test book, its receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book, and the state inspector's 1923 circuit notebook. The crate of the station's own old, already-condemned glassware — sitting in the loft directly above two of the three sessions — was never examined, never listed, and never mentioned by anyone in the room. On 30 April 1925, four days before any finding, the Association dismissed Ansel Keddie outright, with no formal cause ever found against him. On 4 May 1925 the board announced it found "no cause established" but nonetheless awarded the patrons $3,800, "without admission," charged to the station account — an outcome Frick himself later called "an unsatisfactory thing."

**When:** petition January 1925; hearings 17 March–4 May 1925; dismissal 30 April 1925; award 4 May 1925.

**Quantities:** $3,800 award (two later oral-history narrators misremember it as $3,500); four exhibits; the patrons' own original claim, per their petition, was 67,000 lb of unpaid butterfat, valued at $19,600 — different from the 76,000 lb / $19,000 later calculated by Bulletin 471 (see Story 7).

**Documents:** the board's minute book; Alonzo Frick's 1946 recollections and exhibit list; Ansel Keddie's statement; Bertram Kyle's clerk account; Selby Vose's 1926 address defending the outcome.

## Story 6: Over the Ridge — The Alder Corners Cheese Factory

**Who:** Jerome Cudd (founder), his daughter Rosalie Cudd (successor), four of the six Hessel Bottom farmers.

**What:** Jerome Cudd built the Alder Corners Cheese Company in Vessey County, over Redlow Ridge, on a lot with spring water and a hard road east (dated 1913 by Rosalie Cudd's own letter, 1914 by Dell Rundle's pamphlet). From 1915, four of the six Hessel Bottom farmers began hauling their milk over the mountain to Alder Corners during the summer flush — roughly the first of June to mid-September, while the switchback road was passable — returning to their own Ostrey Hollow station the rest of the year. They did this for cheese's better summer price and a shorter haul while the road stood open; the arrangement continued for sixteen consecutive seasons, through 1930, and was resented locally as near-disloyalty. Crucially, Jerome Cudd tested this milk himself, on his own bench, with no knowledge of or interest in what the Ostrey Hollow station made of the same cows' milk the rest of the year. His own book, later copied out for Ivy Keddie in 1958, showed those four farms' milk testing between 3.78 and 3.82 every single summer for sixteen years — essentially identical to the "true" Ostrey Hollow average later established at 3.80, and flatly inconsistent with any claim that Hessel Bottom's wet ground produced inherently poor milk. This became, in effect, an unwitting decades-long control experiment against the wet-hay theory, running in plain sight the whole time no one thought to compare the two books.

**When:** factory founded 1913 (Cudd) / 1914 (Rundle); farmers hauling there 1915–1930 (16 seasons).

**Quantities:** 4 of 6 Hessel Bottom farms; 16 seasons; test range 3.78–3.82, averaging about 3.80.

**Documents:** Rosalie Cudd's 1958 letter to Ivy Keddie, with her father's summer-average table; brief corroborating mentions by Roy Brant, Hazel Brant, and Dell Rundle.

## Story 7: Ivy Keddie's Discovery and Bulletin 471

**Who:** Ivy Keddie (graduate student, Ansel Keddie's granddaughter), Professor Leland Vaught (dairy husbandry, Farland), Rosalie Cudd, and — through his old notebooks — the retired inspector Merle Strawn.

**What:** In February 1958 Ivy Keddie climbed into the loft over the old station's intake room and found, under a tarpaulin, the wooden crate of the station's original glassware, condemned and shelved since 1923. She assembled five sets of records never before examined together: the station test book, the Brant family's private weigh book, the Alder Corners cheese factory's figures, Merle Strawn's state-inspector circuit notebooks, and the Association's own ledgers. On 9 March 1958, at Farland, she measured the nine surviving intact pipettes against a calibrated burette: each was blown to deliver 17.6 c.c., but the nine averaged only about 16.72 c.c. in actual delivery — roughly 95% of the marked charge. Because a butterfat test reads the risen fat column against a scale calibrated for the full, correct charge, a short charge produces a proportionally low reading; this exactly explained the station's persistent 0.19-point gap between its recorded average of 3.61 and the true 3.80. Working with Professor Vaught, she co-authored Bulletin No. 471, published in October 1958, concluding that no milk had been watered and no cream had been stolen at Ostrey Hollow — the tests had simply been low "by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty." The finding was reported the same month by the Ashlin Chronicle, and Ansel Keddie's name was publicly cleared, 24 years after his death.

**When:** February–October 1958.

**Quantities:** 9 of the original 72 pipettes survived intact; mean delivery 16.72 c.c. against a 17.6 c.c. mark; 76,000 lb of unpaid butterfat over 25 full delivery years, worth about $19,000.

**Documents:** Ivy Keddie's 1959 talk "What the Crate Held"; her 9 March 1958 measurement sheet; Bulletin No. 471; Rosalie Cudd's letter; the Ashlin Chronicle's October 1958 report.

## Story 8: Family, Books, and the Long Reckoning

**Who:** four generations of the Keddie and Brant families — Ansel and Orra Keddie; their son Duncan Keddie and Orra's nephew Roy Brant (first cousins); Duncan's daughter Ivy Keddie and Roy's daughter Hazel Brant (second cousins); and curator Halbert Nym.

**What:** Ansel Keddie lived under the valley's unspoken suspicion for the nine years between his 1925 dismissal and his 1934 death, never vindicated in his lifetime; his widow Orra lived a further fourteen years under the same shadow, dying in 1948, ten years before the bulletin cleared her husband's name. The family's own relationships were repeatedly garbled by outsiders and quietly corrected by insiders: Ivy Keddie was wrongly called Ansel's "great-niece" (by the Ashlin Chronicle, and initially by county agent Orin Falke, who corrected himself in print) rather than his actual granddaughter; Ivy and Hazel were wrongly called "first cousins once removed" (by two separate oral-history narrators) rather than the correct "second cousins" — both Duncan Keddie and Hazel Brant herself spelled out the generational math and rejected the error; manager Selby Vose was widely but wrongly called Gideon Vose's nephew rather than his son; and a supposed Vose-Keddie family connection, repeated as valley lore, was flatly denied by Duncan Keddie, who noted his mother would certainly have told him of it had it existed. Meanwhile the physical and documentary record scattered across institutions and private kitchens for decades before finally converging: the station test book, the Brant weigh book, Strawn's inspector notebooks, and the condemned crate itself all reached the Ordell County Historical Rooms between 1959 and 1963, while the Alder Corners test book alone stayed in Vessey County. The reckoning closed only in the memories recorded from Hazel Brant and others decades later, who had grown up under a settled family shame lifted just before their own old age.

**When:** 1925–1934 (Keddie's remaining years); 1948 (Orra's death); 1958–1972 (records gathered and catalogued).

**Quantities:** five distinct primary record sets; four family generations; at least four separate custodial locations before consolidation.

**Documents:** Duncan Keddie's 1968 "Notes for the family"; curator Halbert Nym's 1972 accession notes; Hazel Brant's and Vira Toland's oral histories; Orra Keddie's diary and its 1934 closing entry.

# Section B — Relationships

## B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie

**Granddaughter.** Derivation: Duncan Keddie states directly that Ansel Keddie was his father ("My father was Ansel Keddie... I am Duncan... their son") and that Ivy is his own daughter, "which makes her my father's granddaughter." Ivy Keddie herself calls Ansel "my grandfather" in her own 1959 talk. This is a clean two-generation chain (Ansel → Duncan → Ivy) stated by both the middle link and the endpoint in their own words. Two sources point elsewhere: the Ashlin Chronicle (1958) calls her his "great-niece," and county agent Orin Falke's 1959 report initially repeats the same error — but Falke explicitly corrects himself in his own document's closing note: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." I resolve to granddaughter on the strength of direct first-person family testimony (Duncan and Ivy) plus the sources' own self-correction, over an uncorrected press error that shares its mistake with only its own initial, later-retracted source.

## B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant

**Second cousins.** Derivation: Orra Brant (Ansel's wife) and Emmet Brant are siblings; Duncan Keddie (Orra and Ansel's son) and Roy Brant (Emmet's son) are therefore first cousins — children of siblings; Ivy (Duncan's daughter) and Hazel (Roy's daughter) are children of first cousins, which makes them second cousins. Both Duncan Keddie and Hazel Brant state this explicitly and both explicitly reject the commonly heard alternative, "first cousin once removed" — a claim made independently by Vira Toland and Cleve Oram, both drawing on general valley oral tradition rather than direct family knowledge. What the sources leave open: whether Orra and Emmet were full or half siblings. Roy Brant insists they were full siblings, "same mother and same father, whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it" — explicitly pre-empting a rumor. His own daughter Hazel Brant asserts the opposite: that Orra "was a half sister to my grandfather Emmet, by his father's second wife." The sources do not let me settle this father-daughter disagreement; I note it as open, while observing it does not change the cousin count under the terminology all narrators use.

## B3. Selby Vose's father, and why it matters

**Gideon Vose** — not his nephew, as is sometimes claimed. Two well-positioned narrators explicitly correct the record: Duncan Keddie ("Selby Vose... was Gideon Vose's son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew") and Bertram Kyle, the Association's own clerk who sat immediately outside both men's office for twenty-five years ("the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense"). Against them, Dell Rundle's pamphlet and Vira Toland's oral history both call Selby the "nephew" — but both rest on secondhand valley lore, not personal knowledge, and share the identical error. Why it matters: Gideon Vose organized the Association, presided over it for 22 years, and personally wrote Article VII — the rule that made one station's unchecked, unappealable test the sole measure of a patron's pay. Selby, as Gideon's own son rather than a more distant relative, inherited both the manager's chair (from 1913) and a direct personal stake in his father's institutional legacy. That plausibly explains why his 1926 address favors an explanation (patron dishonesty) that leaves the Association's own by-law, equipment, and bookkeeping blameless, rather than seriously entertaining a mechanical failure inside a plant built under a rule his own father wrote.

## B4. The Vose family and the Keddie family

**No relationship, so far as the sources establish** — and the claim that one existed is explicitly denied from inside the family. Duncan Keddie states flatly: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses," attributes the persistent rumor to confusion with an unrelated Ashlin family also named Vose, and notes that his mother — a woman who, by his account, "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection" of everyone in the valley — never once mentioned a Vose tie. Against this stand two vaguer claims: Vira Toland's assertion that "Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins," and Cleve Oram's hedged "the Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort... I could not draw you the line on paper." I weight Duncan's explicit, reasoned, first-person denial — offered with a stated evidentiary basis — above two outside oral accounts that cannot specify the connection themselves and plausibly confuse two unrelated families. I treat the claimed Vose-Keddie kinship as valley folklore rather than established fact.
