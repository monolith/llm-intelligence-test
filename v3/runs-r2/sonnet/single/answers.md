# Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

Method note: The 24 retellings are treated as scattered testimony about one connected history at Ostrey Hollow / Larrow Valley. I have partitioned that history into eight stories by narrative phase (founding rule → building the station → the tester's long ordeal → the rival theories → the arbitration → the Alder Corners cross-check → the 1958 discovery → the family/document aftermath). Any fact used below is credited to the retelling(s) that state it; where sources conflict I give the resolved (corrected) value only, per the section's own instruction, with the conflict itself reserved for Section E.

---

## Story 1: The Founding of the Association and the Rule That Made a Glass Flaw Matter

**Who:** Gideon Vose and the farmers of the Larrow Valley; later his son Selby Vose.

**What:** In 1889 the valley's farmers met at Larrow Green (in the schoolhouse, per Rundle) and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, tired of hauling butter to Ashlin merchants for whatever price was allowed. Gideon Vose was elected first president and personally drafted the by-laws himself, in his own hand, at his own kitchen table. In 1891 the Association built its main creamery at Larrow Green on Larrow Creek, and began paying members in cash monthly for the first time. Among the by-laws was Article VII, the rule that governs everything that follows: a patron is paid according to the pounds of butterfat his milk is found by test to contain, not by weight — and critically, "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern," with no appeal to any other test, anywhere. This single clause meant one local, uninspected instrument could permanently fix a family's income with no cross-check.

**When:** organizing meeting 1889; creamery built 1891. The exact year Article VII was adopted is disputed and, on the evidence, cannot be pinned down: Dell Rundle's 1939 pamphlet dates it to 1891 ("the year the churn was set up"); Effie Loomis, the Association's own secretary, dates it to 1892 ("the annual meeting... the year after the creamery opened"). Neither can be checked against the original record — the minute book covering 1889–1892 was lost in an office move in 1938 (by Loomis's own admission), and the printed by-laws pamphlet itself carries no date. County agent Orin Falke's 1959 investigation explicitly concluded the adoption year could not be established.

**Quantities:** Gideon Vose presided 22 years (1889–1911, per Loomis).

**Documents:** the by-laws pamphlet itself (Article VII, transcribed in curator Halbert Nym's accession notes); Effie Loomis's 1941 "Notes toward a history"; Dell Rundle's 1939 jubilee pamphlet; Orin Falke's 1959 county report (which found the adoption date indeterminable).

## Story 2: Building the Ostrey Hollow Station and Buying the Fatal Glass

**Who:** the Association, tester Ansel Keddie, Larrow Green buttermaker Hobart Sill, and the glass supplier Ammon & Sons of Tarnet.

**What:** In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up-valley, to serve Hessel Bottom's six farms. It opened 4 May 1896 with Ansel Keddie as tester. Because the station's own equipment had not yet arrived, it opened on glass borrowed from Larrow Green; buttermaker Hobart Sill states he personally supplied it from his own bench so the season would not be lost. Ammon & Sons, a dairy-supply house at Tarnet, invoiced the Association on 14 April 1897 for a complete testing outfit for the station. That outfit — six dozen milk-test pipettes blown to deliver 17.6 c.c. each, twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, a four-bottle hand tester, and freight — went into use at the station on 1 May 1897, per the invoice date, Ammon's own company history, and Orra Keddie's diary entry of that exact date ("A. says the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green"). Unknown to anyone for the next 26 years, the pipettes carried a manufacturing defect: they did not deliver their marked 17.6 c.c., only about 16.7 c.c. — a shortfall that silently distorted every butterfat test made at that station until 1923.

**When:** station built winter 1895–96; opened 4 May 1896; own (defective) glass installed 1 May 1897. Two later sources (Prof. Vaught's 1958 bulletin and clerk Bertram Kyle) place installation a year later, in 1898 — an error I resolve against the contemporaneous diary and the dated invoice; it likely arises from confusing the installation date with 1898, the first full calendar year in which the new glass's effect appears in the yearly averages.

**Quantities:** outfit cost $77.93 total; 72 pipettes at $0.35 each ($25.20 of the total); the station's first (partial) season intake is not recorded in any surviving ledger — oral estimates range from about 900,000 lb (Sill) to about 1,100,000 lb (Kyle), neither documented.

**Documents:** the Ammon & Sons invoice; the test book's first leaf; Orra Keddie's diary; Hobart Sill's 1934 remarks; Ammon's own 1949 company history.

## Story 3: Twenty-Nine Seasons at the Bench — Keddie, the Low Tests, and the 1911 Anomaly

**Who:** Ansel Keddie, tester; Emmet Brant, largest patron on Hessel Bottom (and Ansel's brother-in-law); Orra Keddie, Ansel's wife and Emmet's sister.

**What:** Keddie tested the milk of every Hessel Bottom farm at Ostrey Hollow for 29 consecutive seasons (1896–1924), keeping a meticulous five-column book (date, patron, pounds, test, fat), with no erasures — only crossed-out corrections initialed at the time. From 1902 the patrons complained that their milk tested below the rest of the valley's. Over the station's full delivery years the recorded average sat at 3.61 (against Larrow Green's own 3.78), a gap of 0.19 points that held identically in every month of the year, flush season or dead winter alike — ruling out any seasonal cause. On 8 May 1911 the station's own measuring glass broke; a borrowed measure was used for nine weeks, until 10 July, during which the average jumped to 3.80 and then fell straight back to 3.61 the instant the station's own glass returned. Keddie recorded this faithfully but could not explain it, entering only a bare question mark that stood unanswered for 47 years. In 1919 manager Selby Vose circulated a warning about "water in the cans" to nine Hollow families; Keddie personally delivered every copy by hand rather than let it go by mail or sit on the platform — an act that nonetheless ended civil relations between his household and his own brother-in-law Emmet Brant for the rest of both men's lives.

**When:** 1896–1924 (Keddie's testing seasons); complaints from 1902; the anomalous interval 8 May–10 July 1911; the circular June 1919.

**Quantities:** 3.61 recorded average vs. 3.78 at Larrow Green; 0.19-point gap; 29 seasons; nine letters to nine families spread across six farms.

**Documents:** Keddie's test book and 1922 summary leaf; his 1925 statement to the board and his unsent letter; Orra Keddie's diary (1897–1930); Roy Brant's weigh book, especially its 1910 leaf.

## Story 4: Three Theories, Two Decades — Water, Cream, and Hay

**Who:** Nyle Grigg (1908 committee chairman), Selby Vose (manager), Wilbur Teague (bookkeeper), Dr. Roland Kepp (veterinarian), the State Agricultural College.

**What:** Three rival explanations for the persistent Ostrey Hollow gap circulated for over twenty years before anyone thought to question the measuring glass itself. First, that patrons were adding water to their milk — manager Selby Vose's view, formalized in a 1919 circular to nine families and defended at length in his 1926 annual address, where he credited the "improvement" of 1924 to increased scrutiny rather than any mechanical cause. Second, that the tester himself was skimming cream and writing artificially low tests to hide it — raised at the 1925 hearing by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague, even though the very ledger entries he kept ("station gain") showed the opposite: consistently more butterfat arriving at Larrow Green in the station's cream than had ever been credited to the patrons — a surplus, not a deficit. Third, that the wet, sour hay of Hessel Bottom naturally thinned the milk's fat — the Association's own 1908 investigating committee, chaired by Nyle Grigg, reached this after sampling the herds through a whole winter, and Grigg reaffirmed it in a 1919 covering letter when the complaint reopened; veterinarian Dr. Roland Kepp elaborated the same theory scientifically in a 1926 paper, attributing it to lime- and phosphate-poor bog soil. The College's own controlled feeding trial, Bulletin No. 214 (1911), tested this directly and found bog hay depressed the fat test by at most 0.05 points in winter and nothing measurable in summer — a quarter the necessary size and confined to one season, whereas the real gap was flat year-round. Grigg and Kepp both later misquoted this trial as finding "two tenths" (0.20), a fourfold exaggeration of its true, much smaller finding.

**When:** 1908 (committee); 1911 (Bulletin 214 trial); 1919 (circular; Grigg's letter); 1925 (Teague's hearing testimony); 1926 (Vose's address, Kepp's paper).

**Quantities:** 0.05-point true maximum effect (Bulletin 214) vs. the actual 0.19–0.21-point gap variously reported, and the falsely cited 0.20.

**Documents:** the 1908 committee report; Bulletin No. 214; Grigg's 1919 letter; Vose's 1926 address; Teague's 1948 memoir; Kepp's 1926 paper.

## Story 5: The 1925 Arbitration

**Who:** the Ostrey Hollow patrons (petitioners), Alonzo Frick (arbitration chairman, an Ashlin attorney), the three-man board, Ansel Keddie (dismissed).

**What:** In January 1925 the Ostrey Hollow patrons formally petitioned the Association for redress. A board of arbitration was formed under the by-laws, chaired by Alonzo Frick — "because I was the one of the three who kept no cow." It sat three times: first at the Association's Ashlin office on 17 March 1925, then twice more at the station itself, in its own weigh room, so the board could see the physical plant rather than have it described secondhand. All three rival explanations (water, cream theft, wet hay) were argued before it; none was accepted. Only four documents were formally entered as exhibits: the station test book, its receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book, and the state inspector's 1923 circuit notebook. The crate of the station's own old, already-condemned glassware — sitting in the loft directly above two of the three sessions — was never examined, never listed, and never mentioned by anyone in the room. On 30 April 1925, four days before any finding, the Association dismissed Ansel Keddie outright, with no formal cause ever found against him. On 4 May 1925 the board announced it found "no cause established" but nonetheless awarded the patrons $3,800, "without admission," charged to the station account — an outcome Frick himself later called "an unsatisfactory thing."

**When:** petition January 1925; hearings 17 March–4 May 1925; dismissal 30 April 1925; award 4 May 1925.

**Quantities:** $3,800 award (two later oral-history narrators misremember it as $3,500); four exhibits; the patrons' own original claim, per their petition, was 67,000 lb of unpaid butterfat, valued at $19,600 — different from the 76,000 lb / $19,000 later calculated by Bulletin 471 (see Story 7).

**Documents:** the board's minute book; Alonzo Frick's 1946 recollections and exhibit list; Ansel Keddie's statement; Bertram Kyle's clerk account; Selby Vose's 1926 address defending the outcome.

## Story 6: Over the Ridge — The Alder Corners Cheese Factory

**Who:** Jerome Cudd (founder), his daughter Rosalie Cudd (successor), four of the six Hessel Bottom farmers.

**What:** Jerome Cudd built the Alder Corners Cheese Company in Vessey County, over Redlow Ridge, on a lot with spring water and a hard road east (dated 1913 by Rosalie Cudd's own letter, 1914 by Dell Rundle's pamphlet). From 1915, four of the six Hessel Bottom farmers began hauling their milk over the mountain to Alder Corners during the summer flush — roughly the first of June to mid-September, while the switchback road was passable — returning to their own Ostrey Hollow station the rest of the year. They did this for cheese's better summer price and a shorter haul while the road stood open; the arrangement continued for sixteen consecutive seasons, through 1930, and was resented locally as near-disloyalty. Crucially, Jerome Cudd tested this milk himself, on his own bench, with no knowledge of or interest in what the Ostrey Hollow station made of the same cows' milk the rest of the year. His own book, later copied out for Ivy Keddie in 1958, showed those four farms' milk testing between 3.78 and 3.82 every single summer for sixteen years — essentially identical to the "true" Ostrey Hollow average later established at 3.80, and flatly inconsistent with any claim that Hessel Bottom's wet ground produced inherently poor milk. This became, in effect, an unwitting decades-long control experiment against the wet-hay theory, running in plain sight the whole time no one thought to compare the two books.

**When:** factory founded 1913 (Cudd) / 1914 (Rundle); farmers hauling there 1915–1930 (16 seasons).

**Quantities:** 4 of 6 Hessel Bottom farms; 16 seasons; test range 3.78–3.82, averaging about 3.80.

**Documents:** Rosalie Cudd's 1958 letter to Ivy Keddie, with her father's summer-average table; brief corroborating mentions by Roy Brant, Hazel Brant, and Dell Rundle.

## Story 7: Ivy Keddie's Discovery and Bulletin 471

**Who:** Ivy Keddie (graduate student, Ansel Keddie's granddaughter), Professor Leland Vaught (dairy husbandry, Farland), Rosalie Cudd, and — through his old notebooks — the retired inspector Merle Strawn.

**What:** In February 1958 Ivy Keddie climbed into the loft over the old station's intake room and found, under a tarpaulin, the wooden crate of the station's original glassware, condemned and shelved since 1923. She assembled five sets of records never before examined together: the station test book, the Brant family's private weigh book, the Alder Corners cheese factory's figures, Merle Strawn's state-inspector circuit notebooks, and the Association's own ledgers. On 9 March 1958, at Farland, she measured the nine surviving intact pipettes against a calibrated burette: each was blown to deliver 17.6 c.c., but the nine averaged only about 16.72 c.c. in actual delivery — roughly 95% of the marked charge. Because a butterfat test reads the risen fat column against a scale calibrated for the full, correct charge, a short charge produces a proportionally low reading; this exactly explained the station's persistent 0.19-point gap between its recorded average of 3.61 and the true 3.80. Working with Professor Vaught, she co-authored Bulletin No. 471, published in October 1958, concluding that no milk had been watered and no cream had been stolen at Ostrey Hollow — the tests had simply been low "by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty." The finding was reported the same month by the Ashlin Chronicle, and Ansel Keddie's name was publicly cleared, 24 years after his death.

**When:** February–October 1958.

**Quantities:** 9 of the original 72 pipettes survived intact; mean delivery 16.72 c.c. against a 17.6 c.c. mark; 76,000 lb of unpaid butterfat over 25 full delivery years, worth about $19,000.

**Documents:** Ivy Keddie's 1959 talk "What the Crate Held"; her 9 March 1958 measurement sheet; Bulletin No. 471; Rosalie Cudd's letter; the Ashlin Chronicle's October 1958 report.

## Story 8: Family, Books, and the Long Reckoning

**Who:** four generations of the Keddie and Brant families — Ansel and Orra Keddie; their son Duncan Keddie and Orra's nephew Roy Brant (first cousins); Duncan's daughter Ivy Keddie and Roy's daughter Hazel Brant (second cousins); and curator Halbert Nym.

**What:** Ansel Keddie lived under the valley's unspoken suspicion for the nine years between his 1925 dismissal and his 1934 death, never vindicated in his lifetime; his widow Orra lived a further fourteen years under the same shadow, dying in 1948, ten years before the bulletin cleared her husband's name. The family's own relationships were repeatedly garbled by outsiders and quietly corrected by insiders: Ivy Keddie was wrongly called Ansel's "great-niece" (by the Ashlin Chronicle, and initially by county agent Orin Falke, who corrected himself in print) rather than his actual granddaughter; Ivy and Hazel were wrongly called "first cousins once removed" (by two separate oral-history narrators) rather than the correct "second cousins" — both Duncan Keddie and Hazel Brant herself spelled out the generational math and rejected the error; manager Selby Vose was widely but wrongly called Gideon Vose's nephew rather than his son; and a supposed Vose-Keddie family connection, repeated as valley lore, was flatly denied by Duncan Keddie, who noted his mother would certainly have told him of it had it existed. Meanwhile the physical and documentary record scattered across institutions and private kitchens for decades before finally converging: the station test book, the Brant weigh book, Strawn's inspector notebooks, and the condemned crate itself all reached the Ordell County Historical Rooms between 1959 and 1963, while the Alder Corners test book alone stayed in Vessey County. The reckoning closed only in the memories recorded from Hazel Brant and others decades later, who had grown up under a settled family shame lifted just before their own old age.

**When:** 1925–1934 (Keddie's remaining years); 1948 (Orra's death); 1958–1972 (records gathered and catalogued).

**Quantities:** five distinct primary record sets; four family generations; at least four separate custodial locations before consolidation.

**Documents:** Duncan Keddie's 1968 "Notes for the family"; curator Halbert Nym's 1972 accession notes; Hazel Brant's and Vira Toland's oral histories; Orra Keddie's diary and its 1934 closing entry.

# Section B — Relationships

## B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie

**Granddaughter.** Derivation: Duncan Keddie states directly that Ansel Keddie was his father ("My father was Ansel Keddie... I am Duncan... their son") and that Ivy is his own daughter, "which makes her my father's granddaughter." Ivy Keddie herself calls Ansel "my grandfather" in her own 1959 talk. This is a clean two-generation chain (Ansel → Duncan → Ivy) stated by both the middle link and the endpoint in their own words. Two sources point elsewhere: the Ashlin Chronicle (1958) calls her his "great-niece," and county agent Orin Falke's 1959 report initially repeats the same error — but Falke explicitly corrects himself in his own document's closing note: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." I resolve to granddaughter on the strength of direct first-person family testimony (Duncan and Ivy) plus the sources' own self-correction, over an uncorrected press error that shares its mistake with only its own initial, later-retracted source.

## B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant

**Second cousins.** Derivation: Orra Brant (Ansel's wife) and Emmet Brant are siblings; Duncan Keddie (Orra and Ansel's son) and Roy Brant (Emmet's son) are therefore first cousins — children of siblings; Ivy (Duncan's daughter) and Hazel (Roy's daughter) are children of first cousins, which makes them second cousins. Both Duncan Keddie and Hazel Brant state this explicitly and both explicitly reject the commonly heard alternative, "first cousin once removed" — a claim made independently by Vira Toland and Cleve Oram, both drawing on general valley oral tradition rather than direct family knowledge. What the sources leave open: whether Orra and Emmet were full or half siblings. Roy Brant insists they were full siblings, "same mother and same father, whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it" — explicitly pre-empting a rumor. His own daughter Hazel Brant asserts the opposite: that Orra "was a half sister to my grandfather Emmet, by his father's second wife." The sources do not let me settle this father-daughter disagreement; I note it as open, while observing it does not change the cousin count under the terminology all narrators use.

## B3. Selby Vose's father, and why it matters

**Gideon Vose** — not his nephew, as is sometimes claimed. Two well-positioned narrators explicitly correct the record: Duncan Keddie ("Selby Vose... was Gideon Vose's son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew") and Bertram Kyle, the Association's own clerk who sat immediately outside both men's office for twenty-five years ("the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense"). Against them, Dell Rundle's pamphlet and Vira Toland's oral history both call Selby the "nephew" — but both rest on secondhand valley lore, not personal knowledge, and share the identical error. Why it matters: Gideon Vose organized the Association, presided over it for 22 years, and personally wrote Article VII — the rule that made one station's unchecked, unappealable test the sole measure of a patron's pay. Selby, as Gideon's own son rather than a more distant relative, inherited both the manager's chair (from 1913) and a direct personal stake in his father's institutional legacy. That plausibly explains why his 1926 address favors an explanation (patron dishonesty) that leaves the Association's own by-law, equipment, and bookkeeping blameless, rather than seriously entertaining a mechanical failure inside a plant built under a rule his own father wrote.

## B4. The Vose family and the Keddie family

**No relationship, so far as the sources establish** — and the claim that one existed is explicitly denied from inside the family. Duncan Keddie states flatly: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses," attributes the persistent rumor to confusion with an unrelated Ashlin family also named Vose, and notes that his mother — a woman who, by his account, "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection" of everyone in the valley — never once mentioned a Vose tie. Against this stand two vaguer claims: Vira Toland's assertion that "Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins," and Cleve Oram's hedged "the Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort... I could not draw you the line on paper." I weight Duncan's explicit, reasoned, first-person denial — offered with a stated evidentiary basis — above two outside oral accounts that cannot specify the connection themselves and plausibly confuse two unrelated families. I treat the claimed Vose-Keddie kinship as valley folklore rather than established fact.
# Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C — Math

### C1

**(a) Proportion the tests differed from truth.**
Ivy Keddie measured the nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette (9 March 1958), five runs each: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Sum = 150.48; mean = 150.48 ÷ 9 = 16.72–16.73 c.c., matching her stated "Mean 16.72 c.c." Each pipette was blown to deliver 17.6 c.c. Ratio = 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95**, i.e., the glass delivered 19/20 of its mark — "short by one part in twenty," the exact phrase used in Bulletin 471's own conclusion. (I reject the alternative figures — 16.27 c.c. and ratio 0.9244 — that appear in the same bulletin's own body text; see Section E1, item 4: this is a self-contradiction, and the bulletin's own conclusion, plus Ivy's raw measurement data, both support 16.72/0.95, not 16.27/0.9244.)

**(b) True average test and shortfall.**
Recorded average = 3.61 (Keddie's own test book, quoted verbatim in three retellings). True average = recorded ÷ ratio = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. Shortfall = 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 points** ("nineteen hundredths"), reported consistently across the great majority of sources.

**(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited.**
Milk received over the 25 full delivery years (1898–1922) = 40,000,000 lb.
Credited fat = 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb.
True fat delivered = 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb.
Difference = 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = **76,000 lb**.

**(d) Money value and fraction received.**
Average butterfat price over the period = $0.25/lb (Loomis and Kyle, two independent bookkeeping sources).
76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000**.
The board's 1925 award was $3,800. Fraction actually received = 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = 0.20 = **1/5 (one-fifth)** — matching Ivy Keddie's own statement, "That is a fifth of it."

### C2 — Emmet Brant, 1910 (the largest patron)

Data (Brant weigh book, 1910 leaf): 200,000 lb of milk delivered; paid on a recorded test of 3.42%.

**(a) Fat credited** = 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb**.

**(b) Fat owed and not credited.** True test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60%. True fat = 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb. Owed-not-credited = 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb**.

**(c) Money value that year.** Roy Brant's own account gives the 1910 butterfat price specifically as $0.24/lb. 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40**.

### C3 — Over the 25 full delivery years (1898–1922)

**(a) Total credited** = 1,444,000 lb (as in C1c).

**(b) Total actually reaching the creamery in station cream** = 1,520,000 lb (as in C1c).

**(c) Percentage difference, with denominator stated.**
Difference = 76,000 lb.
As a percentage of the **credited** fat (the ledger's own basis, since the bookkeeper naturally measured the surplus against the figure already entered): 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.26% (≈5¼%)** — matching the ledger's recurring line, "five and a quarter per cent, as usual."
As a percentage of the fat **actually delivered**: 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5.00% exactly** — matching "one part in twenty," the figure that is also the glass's own shortfall ratio.
Both percentages are correct; they are not interchangeable because they use different denominators — a distinction the sources themselves flag explicitly (Ivy Keddie's talk; curator Nym's curatorial note).

### C4 — The testing outfit invoice

**(a) Total** = **$77.93**. Verified by summing the invoice's own line items: $25.20 (pipettes) + $31.68 (bottles) + $0.90 (acid measure) + $18.00 (hand tester) + $2.15 (freight) = $77.93. I reject the figure "$77.39," which appears in two places — Ammon & Sons' own company history (in prose, contradicting the very invoice reprinted two paragraphs earlier in the same document) and the Ashlin Chronicle — as an error (see Section E1).

**(b) Pipettes bought and shortfall.** The invoice reads "6 doz. milk test pipettes... at .35 — 25.20." Six dozen = **72 pipettes**; verified because 72 × $0.35 = $25.20 exactly, matching the invoice line. (The rival claim of "five dozen"/60 pipettes, made by Vaught's bulletin and by Cleve Oram, would give 60 × $0.35 = $21.00, which does not match the invoice — so six dozen is correct.) Each pipette was blown for 17.6 c.c. but delivered a measured mean of about 16.72 c.c. — short by **0.88 c.c.** per pipette (17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88).

### C5

**(a) Duration of faulty glass vs. years in the money calculation.**
The faulty glass was physically in service from 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 — about **26 years and six weeks**. The money calculation (C1c, C3) runs over only **25 full delivery years, 1898–1922**. These differ because 1897 (glass installed partway through the year — and explicitly marked "part year" on the station's own 1922 summary leaf) and 1923 (glass condemned partway through the year, 12 June) are both partial years. They are excluded from the annual-average-based totals (40,000,000 lb, 76,000 lb, etc.) so that those totals rest only on complete, comparable calendar years; the true total shortfall, if the two partial years were included, would be somewhat larger than the reported figures.

**(b) Milk received in the station's first season.**
**Cannot be determined from the sources.** No ledger records the station's first (partial) season, 4 May–31 December 1896. Curator Halbert Nym states this explicitly: the 1896 intake "is not in the ledgers and is nowhere else; the station column begins with 1897, and inquirers wanting a figure for 1896 must be told there is none." Two oral estimates exist, from memory six decades later, and they disagree: buttermaker Hobart Sill recalled "about nine hundred thousand pounds"; clerk Bertram Kyle recalled "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds," while himself noting, "It is not in any book I ever ruled." Neither is documented, and this season used the borrowed (not the faulty) glass in any case, so it is outside the shortfall calculation regardless.

## Section D — Logic

### D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment

**When/duration:** The station's own measuring glass broke 8 May 1911; a borrowed measure was used until 10 July 1911 — **nine weeks**.

**Variable:** the measuring instrument alone (the borrowed pipette/measure substituted for the station's own). Sources disagree on where the borrowed measure came from — Ivy Keddie's talk and Vaught's bulletin say it "came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon," while Roy Brant's account says "the state man left his own measure" — a conflict the sources do not let me settle (see Section E1).

**Held constant:** the same patrons, the same herds, the same pasture, the same season conditions across the switch, the same acid, the same bottles, the same tester at the crank, the same supervision (none).

**Result:** the recorded average rose from 3.61 to 3.80 for the nine weeks, then fell straight back to 3.61 the day the station's own glass returned (10 July) — recorded by Keddie himself, who could not explain it and marked it only with a question mark.

**What it establishes that calculation alone would not:** this is a naturally occurring controlled substitution, isolating one variable (the physical measuring glass) while everything about the cows, the land, the season, and the human operator stayed fixed. Because the reading moved with the instrument alone, it directly refutes, by observation rather than inference, both the wet-hay theory (nothing about feed or land changed in nine weeks) and the cream-theft theory (the same tester, with the same access and the same lack of supervision, nonetheless produced correct readings the moment the instrument changed — his conduct was not the variable in play). A calculation from the 1958 measurements shows the glass *could* produce such an error; the 1911 interval shows, in real time, that swapping the glass *did*.

**What the interval does not establish:** it does not by itself identify the mechanism (why the borrowed measure gave a different, correct reading), does not quantify the shortfall precisely, and does not prove the station's own glass had always been defective by this exact amount — only that it differed from whatever was substituted. The quantitative, mechanistic confirmation came only from Ivy Keddie's direct 1958 burette measurement of the actual pipettes.

### D2. The tester was not stealing cream

Three independent reasons:

1. **The Association's own "station gain" ledger entries** (kept every year from 1898 by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague) show that consistently *more* butterfat arrived at Larrow Green in the station's cream than had ever been credited to the patrons at the station — a surplus, not a deficit. Theft would produce the opposite pattern. This is independently corroborated by buttermaker Hobart Sill, who personally received that cream at Larrow Green for 41 years and testified it "ran heavy... more fat in the cans than the papers said... Always more. Never less."

2. **The 1911 substitution (D1):** the same tester, with the same unsupervised access, produced correct (higher) readings the instant a different measuring glass was used — if his own conduct (theft covered by false low readings) were the cause, changing the glass should not have "cured" it.

3. **Merle Strawn's professional inspections:** the state's disinterested dairy inspector — explicitly noted by Duncan Keddie to have "no interest in any of us... kin to nobody in the valley whatever" — personally watched Keddie perform tests start to finish on repeated visits across decades and consistently found the work careful, unhurried, and the book properly kept, with "nothing to note."

These are independent because they rest on different evidence and different observers: end-of-line product accounting (Teague's ledger, corroborated by Sill's direct physical handling), a real-time controlled natural substitution (Keddie's own book and Orra's diary), and third-party professional field observation (Strawn) — none derives from or depends on the others.

**Which would still hold if the glass had never been measured in 1958:** reason 1, the ledger's "station gain" surplus. It was recorded contemporaneously from 1898 onward and is logically incompatible with theft on its face — a thief produces a deficit, not a surplus — regardless of whether anyone ever explained *why* the surplus existed.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question his own entries?** No. Wilbur Teague, in his own 1948 memoir, repeats the "opportunity for theft" argument (a single, unsupervised man handling the whole process) without ever acknowledging that his own "station gain" entries — which he wrote every year and later cited to the board — show a fat surplus, not a shortfall, which is the very fact that undercuts the accusation he made in 1925. Nothing in the sources indicates he ever reconciled this.

### D3. Did the manager know before the glassware was condemned?

**Yes, from at least 1916.** Wilbur Teague's memoir states directly: "Mr. Vose knew what that account was doing. He signed the sheet every year... I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone." This is a specific, first-person account of an actual conversation in which the low-testing pattern was raised with Selby Vose directly, and he chose to suppress further inquiry rather than investigate. This is complicated by clerk Bertram Kyle's observation that Vose habitually signed sheets by their bottom total without reading the detail above it ("He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign") — which might otherwise suggest passive, uncomprehending sign-off rather than active knowledge. But Teague's account of a specific 1916 conversation is more direct and more specific evidence than Kyle's general characterization of Vose's habits, and I weight it as decisive: Vose had actual notice of the discrepancy well before 1923, and made an institutional choice not to pursue its cause.

## Section F — Theory

**The single causal theory.** The pipettes the Association bought from Ammon & Sons in 1897 for the Ostrey Hollow station were blown to the same 17.6 c.c. standard used everywhere else in the trade, but a manufacturing fault — plausibly a reground mold that ran short without anyone on the glassblowing floor catching it, as Junia Ammon herself speculates — meant they actually delivered only about 16.7 c.c., roughly 95% of their marked charge. Because a butterfat test reads a risen column of fat against a bottle-neck scale calibrated for the full, correct charge, a short charge produces a reading low in the same proportion, every time, regardless of the milk itself. This explains **the size** of the error (a flat ~5%, i.e., 0.19 points on a true average of 3.80 — exactly what a 5%-short pipette produces, and confirmed directly by Ivy Keddie's 1958 burette measurement); **why that particular size** (it is a fixed physical property of the glass, not a variable human or biological factor); **why it began when it did** (precisely when the new pipettes replaced the correct borrowed glass, spring 1897); **why it ended when it did** (precisely when state inspector Merle Strawn, on a routine visit in June 1923, finally checked the pipettes against an outside standard — the burette — something nobody had thought to do because, as the bulletin observes, "the pipette is the standard" the plant measures everything else with, and cannot be caught by measuring with itself); and **why nothing on the farms or at the station needed to change for either** (the same milk read correctly whenever it happened to pass through different glass — during the nine-week 1911 substitution, and every summer for sixteen years for the four farms whose flush milk went instead to the Alder Corners cheese factory, where it consistently tested near 3.80 on entirely different equipment).

**The three competing explanations, who advanced each, and what defeats each:**

1. **Added water** (Selby Vose, formalized in his 1919 circular and defended in his 1926 address). *Defeated by:* the state inspector's own lactometer tests, made at every visit from at least 1908 onward, which never once found added water at the station.

2. **The tester stealing cream** (insinuated by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague at the 1925 hearing). *Defeated by:* Teague's own "station gain" ledger entries, which show a persistent surplus of fat arriving at Larrow Green — the opposite of what theft produces — corroborated by buttermaker Hobart Sill's direct testimony that the cream "ran heavy... always more, never less," and by the 1911 natural experiment, where the same tester's readings moved with the instrument, not with any change in his conduct or supervision.

3. **The wet hay of Hessel Bottom** (the Association's 1908 committee under Nyle Grigg; reaffirmed by Grigg in 1919; elaborated by Dr. Roland Kepp in 1926). *Defeated by:* the College's own controlled feeding trial (Bulletin 214, 1911), which found an effect a quarter the necessary size and confined to winter, whereas the real gap was flat in every month; and by the Alder Corners cheese-factory data, showing the same cows on the same wet ground tested normally (~3.80) when measured on different equipment across the ridge.

**Subsidiary question 1 — did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?** No. Effie Loomis states directly that "the crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all... Not a word of it was spoken in any of the three sessions." Alonzo Frick's own exhibit list names only four items (the test book, receiving book, Brant's weigh book, and Strawn's 1923 notebook) — not the crate itself. Clerk Bertram Kyle, who physically carried the exhibits, confirms nobody "laid the second beside the fourth and looked." This is despite the crate sitting in the loft directly above two of the three arbitration sessions, which were held at the station itself.

**Subsidiary question 2 — what did the tester himself believe the cause was?** Formally and consistently, he refused to name one: both in his statement to the board and in his unsent letter, Ansel Keddie states, "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are," and "I will not put anything in its place that I do not know." Privately, the sources show two different, seemingly inconsistent suspicions voiced at different times: his son Duncan reports that in 1911 Keddie came home saying "it was something in the glass" but would not commit an unproven belief to his own book; Alonzo Frick separately reports that in a private corridor conversation during the 1925 hearing, Keddie told him "he had always thought the patrons were at fault." The sources do not let me reconcile these into one settled private belief — only that, whatever he privately suspected at various points, he never once stated a cause under oath or in writing that he could not verify.

## Section G — Summary (149 words)

For 26 years the Ostrey Hollow skimming station tested its patrons' milk lower than every neighboring station, costing them roughly $19,000 in unpaid butterfat. The valley blamed people: patrons for watering milk, tester Ansel Keddie for stealing cream, or Hessel Bottom's wet hay for thinning it. A 1925 arbitration fined the Association $3,800 without finding fault, and quietly dismissed Keddie, who died in 1934 still under suspicion. The true cause was mechanical: pipettes bought in 1897 were defectively blown, delivering 5% less milk than marked, so every test read exactly 5% low — flat across every season and every herd, until a 1923 inspection condemned the glass. Nobody connected the two facts until 1958, when Keddie's own granddaughter measured the surviving pipettes and proved it. It took eight separate, partial records — ledgers, diaries, an inspector's notebook, a bulletin — because no single witness ever held the whole mechanism in view at once.
