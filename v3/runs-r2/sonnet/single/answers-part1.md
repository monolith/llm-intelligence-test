# Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

Method note: The 24 retellings are treated as scattered testimony about one connected history at Ostrey Hollow / Larrow Valley. I have partitioned that history into eight stories by narrative phase (founding rule → building the station → the tester's long ordeal → the rival theories → the arbitration → the Alder Corners cross-check → the 1958 discovery → the family/document aftermath). Any fact used below is credited to the retelling(s) that state it; where sources conflict I give the resolved (corrected) value only, per the section's own instruction, with the conflict itself reserved for Section E.

---

## Story 1: The Founding of the Association and the Rule That Made a Glass Flaw Matter

**Who:** Gideon Vose and the farmers of the Larrow Valley; later his son Selby Vose.

**What:** In 1889 the valley's farmers met at Larrow Green (in the schoolhouse, per Rundle) and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, tired of hauling butter to Ashlin merchants for whatever price was allowed. Gideon Vose was elected first president and personally drafted the by-laws himself, in his own hand, at his own kitchen table. In 1891 the Association built its main creamery at Larrow Green on Larrow Creek, and began paying members in cash monthly for the first time. Among the by-laws was Article VII, the rule that governs everything that follows: a patron is paid according to the pounds of butterfat his milk is found by test to contain, not by weight — and critically, "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern," with no appeal to any other test, anywhere. This single clause meant one local, uninspected instrument could permanently fix a family's income with no cross-check.

**When:** organizing meeting 1889; creamery built 1891. The exact year Article VII was adopted is disputed and, on the evidence, cannot be pinned down: Dell Rundle's 1939 pamphlet dates it to 1891 ("the year the churn was set up"); Effie Loomis, the Association's own secretary, dates it to 1892 ("the annual meeting... the year after the creamery opened"). Neither can be checked against the original record — the minute book covering 1889–1892 was lost in an office move in 1938 (by Loomis's own admission), and the printed by-laws pamphlet itself carries no date. County agent Orin Falke's 1959 investigation explicitly concluded the adoption year could not be established.

**Quantities:** Gideon Vose presided 22 years (1889–1911, per Loomis).

**Documents:** the by-laws pamphlet itself (Article VII, transcribed in curator Halbert Nym's accession notes); Effie Loomis's 1941 "Notes toward a history"; Dell Rundle's 1939 jubilee pamphlet; Orin Falke's 1959 county report (which found the adoption date indeterminable).

## Story 2: Building the Ostrey Hollow Station and Buying the Fatal Glass

**Who:** the Association, tester Ansel Keddie, Larrow Green buttermaker Hobart Sill, and the glass supplier Ammon & Sons of Tarnet.

**What:** In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up-valley, to serve Hessel Bottom's six farms. It opened 4 May 1896 with Ansel Keddie as tester. Because the station's own equipment had not yet arrived, it opened on glass borrowed from Larrow Green; buttermaker Hobart Sill states he personally supplied it from his own bench so the season would not be lost. Ammon & Sons, a dairy-supply house at Tarnet, invoiced the Association on 14 April 1897 for a complete testing outfit for the station. That outfit — six dozen milk-test pipettes blown to deliver 17.6 c.c. each, twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, a four-bottle hand tester, and freight — went into use at the station on 1 May 1897, per the invoice date, Ammon's own company history, and Orra Keddie's diary entry of that exact date ("A. says the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green"). Unknown to anyone for the next 26 years, the pipettes carried a manufacturing defect: they did not deliver their marked 17.6 c.c., only about 16.7 c.c. — a shortfall that silently distorted every butterfat test made at that station until 1923.

**When:** station built winter 1895–96; opened 4 May 1896; own (defective) glass installed 1 May 1897. Two later sources (Prof. Vaught's 1958 bulletin and clerk Bertram Kyle) place installation a year later, in 1898 — an error I resolve against the contemporaneous diary and the dated invoice; it likely arises from confusing the installation date with 1898, the first full calendar year in which the new glass's effect appears in the yearly averages.

**Quantities:** outfit cost $77.93 total; 72 pipettes at $0.35 each ($25.20 of the total); the station's first (partial) season intake is not recorded in any surviving ledger — oral estimates range from about 900,000 lb (Sill) to about 1,100,000 lb (Kyle), neither documented.

**Documents:** the Ammon & Sons invoice; the test book's first leaf; Orra Keddie's diary; Hobart Sill's 1934 remarks; Ammon's own 1949 company history.

## Story 3: Twenty-Nine Seasons at the Bench — Keddie, the Low Tests, and the 1911 Anomaly

**Who:** Ansel Keddie, tester; Emmet Brant, largest patron on Hessel Bottom (and Ansel's brother-in-law); Orra Keddie, Ansel's wife and Emmet's sister.

**What:** Keddie tested the milk of every Hessel Bottom farm at Ostrey Hollow for 29 consecutive seasons (1896–1924), keeping a meticulous five-column book (date, patron, pounds, test, fat), with no erasures — only crossed-out corrections initialed at the time. From 1902 the patrons complained that their milk tested below the rest of the valley's. Over the station's full delivery years the recorded average sat at 3.61 (against Larrow Green's own 3.78), a gap of 0.19 points that held identically in every month of the year, flush season or dead winter alike — ruling out any seasonal cause. On 8 May 1911 the station's own measuring glass broke; a borrowed measure was used for nine weeks, until 10 July, during which the average jumped to 3.80 and then fell straight back to 3.61 the instant the station's own glass returned. Keddie recorded this faithfully but could not explain it, entering only a bare question mark that stood unanswered for 47 years. In 1919 manager Selby Vose circulated a warning about "water in the cans" to nine Hollow families; Keddie personally delivered every copy by hand rather than let it go by mail or sit on the platform — an act that nonetheless ended civil relations between his household and his own brother-in-law Emmet Brant for the rest of both men's lives.

**When:** 1896–1924 (Keddie's testing seasons); complaints from 1902; the anomalous interval 8 May–10 July 1911; the circular June 1919.

**Quantities:** 3.61 recorded average vs. 3.78 at Larrow Green; 0.19-point gap; 29 seasons; nine letters to nine families spread across six farms.

**Documents:** Keddie's test book and 1922 summary leaf; his 1925 statement to the board and his unsent letter; Orra Keddie's diary (1897–1930); Roy Brant's weigh book, especially its 1910 leaf.

## Story 4: Three Theories, Two Decades — Water, Cream, and Hay

**Who:** Nyle Grigg (1908 committee chairman), Selby Vose (manager), Wilbur Teague (bookkeeper), Dr. Roland Kepp (veterinarian), the State Agricultural College.

**What:** Three rival explanations for the persistent Ostrey Hollow gap circulated for over twenty years before anyone thought to question the measuring glass itself. First, that patrons were adding water to their milk — manager Selby Vose's view, formalized in a 1919 circular to nine families and defended at length in his 1926 annual address, where he credited the "improvement" of 1924 to increased scrutiny rather than any mechanical cause. Second, that the tester himself was skimming cream and writing artificially low tests to hide it — raised at the 1925 hearing by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague, even though the very ledger entries he kept ("station gain") showed the opposite: consistently more butterfat arriving at Larrow Green in the station's cream than had ever been credited to the patrons — a surplus, not a deficit. Third, that the wet, sour hay of Hessel Bottom naturally thinned the milk's fat — the Association's own 1908 investigating committee, chaired by Nyle Grigg, reached this after sampling the herds through a whole winter, and Grigg reaffirmed it in a 1919 covering letter when the complaint reopened; veterinarian Dr. Roland Kepp elaborated the same theory scientifically in a 1926 paper, attributing it to lime- and phosphate-poor bog soil. The College's own controlled feeding trial, Bulletin No. 214 (1911), tested this directly and found bog hay depressed the fat test by at most 0.05 points in winter and nothing measurable in summer — a quarter the necessary size and confined to one season, whereas the real gap was flat year-round. Grigg and Kepp both later misquoted this trial as finding "two tenths" (0.20), a fourfold exaggeration of its true, much smaller finding.

**When:** 1908 (committee); 1911 (Bulletin 214 trial); 1919 (circular; Grigg's letter); 1925 (Teague's hearing testimony); 1926 (Vose's address, Kepp's paper).

**Quantities:** 0.05-point true maximum effect (Bulletin 214) vs. the actual 0.19–0.21-point gap variously reported, and the falsely cited 0.20.

**Documents:** the 1908 committee report; Bulletin No. 214; Grigg's 1919 letter; Vose's 1926 address; Teague's 1948 memoir; Kepp's 1926 paper.
