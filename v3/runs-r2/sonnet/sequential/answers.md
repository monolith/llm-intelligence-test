### Story 1 — The Founding and the Rule (1889–1896)

Who: Gideon Vose and the farmers of the Larrow Valley.

What happened: In 1889 the valley's farmers met at Larrow Green and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Gideon Vose was elected first president and held the post twenty-two years, to 1911. He personally drafted the by-laws, including Article VII: a patron is paid on the butterfat his milk is found by test to contain, not on its weight — and, the clause that mattered later, "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern," with no appeal to any other test. The main creamery was built at Larrow Green in 1891; Article VII was adopted at the annual meeting, generally dated 1892, though the county agent's later report notes the exact year can't be confirmed because the Association's 1889–1892 minute book was lost in a 1938 office move.

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station seven miles up-valley at Ostrey Hollow to serve Hessel Bottom's six farms, opening it 4 May 1896. Ansel Keddie was hired as tester and separator man from day one and stayed twenty-nine seasons. Because the Association's own testing outfit had not yet arrived, buttermaker Hobart Sill lent the station glassware from his own bench at Larrow Green for that first partial season; it came back later, one piece chipped.

Quantities that matter, and where they're uncertain: 1889 founding; 1891 creamery; ~1892 Article VII (date itself uncertain). The station's first-season intake cannot be pinned down — Sill recalled "about nine hundred thousand pounds," Kyle recalled "a good eleven hundred thousand," and the collection's curator states flatly that no ledger figure for 1896 survives anywhere. This is a case the sources leave open.

Bearing documents: Loomis (r02), Nym (r07, item 1), Rundle (r12), Falke (r22), Sill (r14), Kyle (r23).

### Story 2 — The Station's Flawed Glassware (1897–1923)

Who: Ammon & Sons of Tarnet, manufacturer; the Association, purchaser.

What happened: On 14 April 1897 the Association ordered a full testing outfit for Ostrey Hollow from Ammon & Sons: six dozen (72) milk-test pipettes blown to 17.6 cubic centimeters, twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure, a four-bottle hand tester, and freight, totaling $77.93. The outfit went into service 1 May 1897 — confirmed independently by the invoice date, by Orra Keddie's diary entry of that exact day ("the station's own glass came up"), and by the museum catalog — replacing the borrowed Larrow Green glass used through 1896.

Unknown to anyone for the next twenty-six years, the pipette mold had apparently been reground slightly short at the factory, and the whole batch delivered a consistently reduced charge — a defect invisible to ordinary spot-checking because it was perfectly uniform. When finally measured in 1958, the nine surviving pipettes averaged 16.72cc delivered against their 17.6cc mark: a flat 95% of the intended charge, every time, in every season, for every patron.

Because a butterfat test simply reads the height of a fat column against a bottle-neck ruled for one fixed charge, a 5%-short charge produced a reading exactly 5% low, proportionally and silently, for as long as that glass stayed in use. The state's own inspector checked the station's scales, boiler, and water supply nearly every year without incident; only from 1921 did the state issue calibrated burettes for checking glassware itself, and the inspector did not get to Ostrey Hollow's pipettes with his until 12 June 1923, when he found "these measures will not deliver their mark" and condemned the whole crate on the spot. New, correctly calibrated glass was ordered within days and in use by July.

Quantities: $77.93 total invoice; 72 pipettes bought; 16.72cc actual vs. 17.6cc marked; a 5% (one-part-in-twenty) shortfall; about twenty-six years in continuous service.

Bearing documents: Keddie's statement and letter (r03), Ivy Keddie's talk (r04), Strawn's circuit-book (r06), Nym's catalog (r07), Vaught's bulletin (r11), Sill (r14), Ammon & Sons' own history (r20), Orra Keddie's diary (r24).

### Story 3 — Two Decades of Wrong Explanations (1902–1926)

Who: Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee, manager Selby Vose, veterinarian Dr. Roland Kepp.

What happened: From 1902 the Ostrey Hollow patrons complained their milk tested lower at their own station than identical milk tested at Larrow Green. In January 1908 the Association appointed a committee under Nyle Grigg, a disinterested Larrow Green farmer, to look into it. The committee sampled Hessel Bottom's herds through January, February, and March of 1908 only (its own printed report says so explicitly), found the scales honest and the milk whole, and concluded the bottom's wet, coarse hay was thinning the cream — "the remedy lies with the land." That finding held for eleven years.

In 1919, with the complaint renewed, Grigg republished his 1908 report with a covering letter in which he misremembered his own committee's work as having sampled "the whole of 1908, every month" — directly contradicting the very report he was reprinting beneath his own signature — and cited the State College's new Bulletin No. 214 as vindication by "two tenths of a point," when the bulletin's actual finding was at most five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer, a fourfold-or-worse exaggeration.

That same year, manager Selby Vose pursued a different theory, circulating a warning letter to the Hollow's patrons (nine families by most accounts; Orra Keddie's own diary says eleven) accusing them of adding water to their milk. In 1926, veterinarian Dr. Roland Kepp elaborated the hay theory with soil-chemistry and mineral-deficiency reasoning, repeated Grigg's same "two tenths" misquotation of Bulletin 214, and specifically dismissed the 1911 measure-swap anomaly as too brief and too seasonal to matter — a dismissal resting on a mistaken description of that interval (it ran May into July, not purely "summer flush," and the underlying gap was shown to be identical in winter and summer alike).

Both theories were eventually defeated by evidence: the inspector's lactometer never once found added water at the station, and Bulletin 214's real figures were too small and the wrong shape to explain a flat, year-round 0.19-point gap.

Bearing documents: Loomis (r02), Keddie's letter (r03), Vose's address (r08), Roy Brant (r09), Rundle (r12), Grigg (r15), Kepp (r16), Falke (r22), Orra Keddie's diary (r24).

### Story 4 — The Nine Weeks of 1911 (8 May – 10 July 1911)

Who: Ansel Keddie and the station itself.

What happened: On 8 May 1911 the station's own measuring pipette broke, and Keddie made shift with a borrowed measure for nine weeks — same patrons, same herds, same pasture, same season, same acid, bottles, and hand, with only the pipette changed. Through those nine weeks the recorded average rose from its usual 3.61 to 3.80, and on 10 July, the day the station's own (defective) glass resumed, the figures fell straight back to 3.61 — the same day, with no gradual drift. Keddie recorded both entries in his own hand and set an unanswered question mark beside them that stood for forty-seven years. His son Duncan later recalled him saying at home, at the time, that he thought "it was something in the glass" — but he never wrote that suspicion into his own book, because (his son believed) he would not put down what he could not prove. The state inspector happened through in June 1911 and noted nothing unusual, missing the anomaly entirely.

Decades later this episode became the single most important piece of evidence in the whole case: a naturally occurring, self-running experiment with one variable changed and everything else held fixed, supplying something no 1958 laboratory measurement alone could — real-time proof, from 1911 itself, that the shortfall tracked the instrument and not the cows, the land, the season, or the tester's honesty.

Quantities: nine weeks; average moved 3.61 → 3.80 → 3.61.

Bearing documents: Keddie's letter (r03), Ivy Keddie's talk (r04), Teague's brief mention (r05), Strawn's log (r06, which missed it), Roy Brant (r09), Vaught's bulletin (r11), Kepp's paper (r16, which dismisses it), Duncan Keddie (r18), Falke (r22), Orra Keddie's diary (r24).

### Story 5 — The Ledger's Silent Confession (1898–1925)

Who: bookkeeper Wilbur Teague, buttermaker Hobart Sill, clerk Bertram Kyle, manager Selby Vose.

What happened: from at least 1898, and formally tracked from 1905 by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague, the Association's own annual ledgers carried a "station gain" line for Ostrey Hollow: every year, more butterfat physically arrived at the Larrow Green creamery in the station's cream than had ever been credited to the patrons on the station's own test — a gain running steadily at 5.25% of the credited figure (5% of what was actually delivered), so regular the entry's standing phrase became "as usual." Teague raised the discrepancy with manager Selby Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone; he did. Vose himself signed the annual sheet every spring for a quarter century — clerk Bertram Kyle recalled him reading only the bottom total and never the lines above it — so whether through willful blindness or genuine inattention, the manager had the evidence in his own hand every year and never acted on it. Independently, buttermaker Hobart Sill confirmed for decades that the Hollow's cream consistently "ran heavy" coming off the wagon: more fat than the paperwork said had been bought.

This evidence logically ruled out the theft theory later leveled against tester Ansel Keddie: stolen cream would have reached Larrow Green light, not heavy. Nobody made that connection at the time. It took schoolteacher Dell Rundle, compiling a nostalgic 1939 jubilee pamphlet and stumbling onto the same ledger entry "on another errand altogether," to work it out — forcing him to add a note while his pamphlet was already in press, retracting the cream-theft story he had just told his readers two chapters earlier.

Quantities: sample entry — 57,760 lb credited, 60,800 lb received, 3,040 lb gain, "five and a quarter percent, as usual"; flagged to Vose in 1916.

Bearing documents: Teague's memoir (r05), Nym's catalog (r07), Vose's address (r08), Rundle's pamphlet (r12), Sill (r14), Kyle (r23).

### Story 6 — The Petition, the Hearing, and the Dismissal (1925)

Who: the Ostrey Hollow patrons, attorney Alonzo Frick, Ansel Keddie.

What happened: in January 1925 the patrons formally petitioned the Association, and a three-man board of arbitration formed under attorney Alonzo Frick, who insisted two of the three sittings be held at the station itself so the board could see the actual plant. The board sat 17 March to 4 May 1925 and heard three rival theories: manager Selby Vose's claim of added water; bookkeeper Wilbur Teague's implication that the tester had stolen cream and written low readings to cover it; and the Association's own 1908 finding blaming wet hay. Only four exhibits were entered: the station's test book, its receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book, and — significantly, though nobody present seems to have connected it to anything — the state inspector's 1923 circuit notebook recording the pipette condemnation. Ansel Keddie gave a brief sworn statement denying wrongdoing, and privately told Frick afterward, in the corridor, that he had always suspected the patrons himself, though he would not say so publicly.

On 30 April 1925, four days before the board reported, the Association dismissed Keddie outright — with no finding against him ever reached by the board itself. On 4 May the board issued its award: it "found no cause established" against any person or the station, yet awarded the patrons $3,800 (against their own claimed loss of roughly 67,000 pounds of butterfat), charged to the station account, "without admission." Even the secretary who wrote the award down remarked it was not her place to ask how both halves of that sentence could be true at once. Keddie went home, took up carpentry, and lived under the valley's suspicion for the rest of his life.

Quantities: petition, January 1925; hearing, 17 March–4 May 1925; dismissal, 30 April 1925; award, $3,800.

Bearing documents: Vira Toland (r01), Loomis (r02), Keddie's statement (r03), Nym (r07), Vose (r08), Roy Brant (r09), Rundle (r12), Frick (r13), Hazel Brant (r17), Duncan Keddie (r18), Oram (r21), Falke (r22), Kyle (r23), Orra Keddie's diary (r24).

### Story 7 — A Family Divided (1889–1958)

Who: Ansel and Orra Keddie, Emmet and Roy Brant, Duncan Keddie, Hazel Brant, Ivy Keddie.

What happened: Ansel Keddie married Orra Brant in 1889; Orra was sister to Emmet Brant, the largest patron at Hessel Bottom and the man hit hardest by the low tests. The sources disagree on whether Orra and Emmet were full siblings (her son Roy Brant insists "same mother and same father") or half-siblings by a second wife (her granddaughter Hazel Brant claims) — a disagreement the record does not settle. For thirty years the two households shared Sunday dinners. That ended in June 1919, when Ansel — rather than let the Association's water-accusation circular go out by mail — carried the letters himself, door to door, to his own brother-in-law's house among others. Emmet confronted him in the yard days later, asking why Ansel had carried the paper if he didn't believe it; Ansel answered he'd rather carry it himself than let another man do it. They never spoke again. Emmet died in 1929, ten years into the silence, with neither man having gone up the road first — a rift Orra's own diary records with visible, restrained grief.

The next generation stayed on better terms: Duncan Keddie (Ansel and Orra's son) and Roy Brant (Emmet's son) remained first cousins in good standing, close enough that decades later Roy lent his father's weigh book to Duncan's daughter Ivy for her 1958 research, and Roy's own daughter Hazel later helped keep the record straight. Ivy and Hazel — repeatedly misdescribed elsewhere in print as niece, first cousin once removed, or otherwise — are, by both of their own explicit and matching accounts, second cousins: the children of the first cousins Duncan and Roy.

Quantities: married 1889; rift begins 1919; Emmet dies 1929, unreconciled; vindication reached 1958, twenty-nine years after Emmet's death.

Bearing documents: Vira Toland (r01), Roy Brant (r09), Hazel Brant (r17), Duncan Keddie (r18), Oram (r21), Orra Keddie's diary (r24).

### Story 8 — The Crate in the Loft (1958)

Who: Ivy Keddie, Professor Leland Vaught, Rosalie Cudd, and (posthumously) Merle Strawn's records.

What happened: in February 1958, Ivy Keddie — Ansel's granddaughter, then a twenty-seven-year-old graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland — climbed a ladder into the loft over the old station's intake room and found, under a tarpaulin, the crate of pipettes condemned in 1923 and untouched since. On 9 March 1958, in a college laboratory, she measured the nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette, five runs each: mean delivery 16.72cc against their blown mark of 17.6cc — a consistent 95%, or 5% short, matching almost exactly the historical gap between the station's recorded 3.61 average and a true average of 3.80.

She did not rely on the glass alone. She tracked down Merle Strawn's original 1923 circuit-book, sitting unread for eleven years in the college's own library; she wrote to Rosalie Cudd of the Alder Corners Cheese Company over the ridge, whose father had independently tested milk from four of the same Hessel Bottom farms every summer for sixteen years and recorded a steady ~3.80 the whole time, on equipment nobody at Ostrey Hollow had ever touched; and she drew on the Brant family's own decades-old weigh book to confirm the intake weights had never been in question.

Published jointly with Professor Leland Vaught as Bulletin No. 471 in October 1958, the finding was unambiguous: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there" — the entire twenty-six-year, 76,000-pound shortfall traced to one defective batch of glass. It cleared Ansel Keddie thirty-three years after his dismissal and twenty-four years after his death; his widow Orra had died ten years too soon to see it. The Ashlin Chronicle covered the story within days (introducing several of its own errors), and county agent Orin Falke independently reviewed and confirmed the finding for the county board in 1959, correcting his own initial mistakes along the way.

Quantities: found February 1958; measured 9 March 1958; nine surviving pipettes; Bulletin 471, October 1958.

Bearing documents: Ivy Keddie's talk (r04), Strawn (r06), Nym (r07), Rosalie Cudd (r10), Vaught (r11), Hazel Brant (r17), Duncan Keddie (r18), Ashlin Chronicle (r19), Ammon & Sons (r20), Falke (r22), Orra Keddie's diary editor's note (r24).

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**
Granddaughter. Derivation: Duncan Keddie, Ansel's own son, states directly that Ivy is his daughter and "makes her my father's granddaughter" — about as authoritative a chain as exists, a father tracing his own child's relation to his own father. Sources pointing elsewhere: the Ashlin Chronicle calls her his "great-niece," and county agent Falke's report initially made the identical error before explicitly self-correcting in its own postscript once verified. Duncan himself anticipated this exact mistake, noting he'd "already twice seen it printed otherwise" — which the Chronicle and Falke's first draft between them supply. Resolved firmly as granddaughter.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**
Second cousins. Duncan Keddie and Hazel Brant herself both independently and explicitly state "second cousins," and both give the identical generational logic: Duncan and Roy Brant (Ivy's and Hazel's respective fathers) were first cousins, so their children are second cousins. What the sources leave open is not the generational math itself (that is well pinned down) but whether the underlying sibling link one generation up — Orra Keddie and Emmet Brant — was a full or half sibling relationship (Roy says full, Hazel says half); if it was a half-sibling relationship, some genealogists would qualify Duncan/Roy as "half first cousins" and, by extension, Ivy/Hazel as "half second cousins," a distinction the sources never address and do not resolve. Contrary claims exist (Cleve Oram's "first cousin once removed") but are single, admittedly uncertain, and outweighed by the two people actually in the relationship agreeing.

**B3. Selby Vose's father, and why it matters.**
Gideon Vose — the Association's founder, first president, and author of Article VII. This is corroborated directly and explicitly by two independent, well-positioned witnesses: Duncan Keddie (a family-history-minded outsider correcting the record) and Bertram Kyle (the office clerk who worked under both Voses for decades and calls the rival "nephew" story "nonsense"). It matters because it means the same family wrote the rule and administered it: Gideon wrote the by-law making the station's own test final and unappealable, and his son Selby then ran that very station regime as manager for twenty-five years, signed the 1919 water circular, and defended the no-appeal rule in his own 1926 address — a continuity of family authority over the mechanism that ultimately produced the injustice, though (see B4) not the kind of family conspiracy the valley's rumor mill assumed.

**B4. Relationship between the Vose and Keddie families.**
None, on the best evidence available. Duncan Keddie flatly denies any connection — attributing the rumor to confusion with unrelated "Ashlin Voses" and ordinary valley gossip, and noting that his own mother, who tracked every family connection in the valley meticulously, never once mentioned a Vose. Against this stand two vaguer claims: Vira Toland's assertion that Orra Keddie and "old Mrs. Vose" were first cousins, and Cleve Oram's admittedly uncertain "cousins of some sort... I could not draw you the line on paper." Given Duncan's specificity and direct rebuttal, corroborated indirectly by Kyle's parallel debunking of the "nephew" claim, I resolve this as no real relationship — though I cannot call it proven beyond doubt, since no genealogical record independent of these personal recollections exists in the sources.

## Section C — Math (show working)

**C1.**
(a) The nine surviving pipettes, blown to a 17.6cc mark, delivered a measured mean of 16.72cc against a calibrated burette (Ivy Keddie's 1958 measurement sheet, reproduced identically in two documents). 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly. So the glass delivered 95% of its intended charge — the tests read a flat 5% low, "one part in twenty." (I reject Bulletin 471's own mid-text alternative figures — 16.27cc, ratio 0.9244 — as an internal error, since they contradict the bulletin's own reproduced closing paragraph and don't reconcile with any other figure in the record; see Section E.)

(b) Recorded average: 3.61 (Ansel Keddie's own test-book summary, quoted identically in three separate documents). True average = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80** exactly. Shortfall = 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 points** (nineteen hundredths). This is triangulated three independent ways: this arithmetic; the 1911 natural experiment, which produced exactly 3.80 on borrowed glass; and the Alder Corners Cheese Company's independent tests of the same herds, averaging ~3.80 across sixteen summers.

(c) Over the 25 full delivery years (1898–1922), the station received 40,000,000 lb of milk (corroborated in four documents). Credited fat at 3.61% = 1,444,000 lb. True fat at 3.80% = 1,520,000 lb. Difference = **76,000 lb** of butterfat never credited.

(d) At the period's average price of $0.25/lb: 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000**. The patrons actually received the 1925 award of $3,800. $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = **1/5** (20%) of what was owed.

**C2.** Largest patron = Emmet Brant. His 1910 figures (his own household weigh book, corroborated by his granddaughter Hazel Brant): 200,000 lb of milk delivered, paid on a test of 3.42.
(a) Credited butterfat = 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb**.
(b) True test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.6 exactly. True butterfat = 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb. Owed and not credited = 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb**.
(c) 1910 butterfat price, per Roy Brant's own account: $0.24/lb. 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40**.

**C3.**
(a) Credited total, 25 full years: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**.
(b) Actually received at Larrow Green: 40,000,000 × 0.038 = **1,520,000 lb**.
(c) Difference = 76,000 lb. As a percentage of fat actually delivered (1,520,000 lb): 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5.0%** exactly — "the one that is also the glass," in Ivy Keddie's phrase. As a percentage of fat credited (1,444,000 lb — the denominator the bookkeeper's ledger conventionally used): 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 ≈ **5.26%** (the ledger's own "five and a quarter percent, as usual"). Both are correct for their own denominator; conflating them is one of the record's recurring small confusions.

**C4.**
(a) Invoice total: **$77.93** (identical in two independently reproduced copies of the same document — the museum catalog and Ammon & Sons' own company history). Two secondary tellings — the Ashlin Chronicle, and Ammon & Sons' own prose describing its own correctly-quoted invoice — both mangle this to $77.39.
(b) **72 pipettes** (six dozen, per the invoice, corroborated by Ivy Keddie's talk). Each was short by 17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88cc**.

**C5.**
(a) The faulty glass was in continuous service about **26 years** — from 1 May 1897 (invoice date; Orra Keddie's diary entry of that exact day; the museum catalog) to 12 June 1923 (the inspector's own contemporaneous log entry, corroborated by the museum catalog and Falke's report). The money calculation runs over only **25 full delivery years** (1898–1922) because it excludes the two partial years at each end: 1897 (Keddie's own book marks it "part year," since the new glass arrived mid-season) and 1923 (the glass was condemned mid-year and replaced within weeks). Using only complete calendar years avoids estimating a fractional year's milk volume.
(b) First-season (1896) intake: **cannot be determined precisely**. Two conflicting oral estimates exist — about 900,000 lb (Hobart Sill) and about 1,100,000 lb (Bertram Kyle) — and the museum curator states explicitly that no ledger record survives for 1896 at all, so neither estimate can be checked against a primary document.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran 8 May–10 July 1911 — nine weeks. Variable: the pipette itself (the station's own broke; a measure borrowed from Larrow Green was used until it was replaced). Held constant: same patrons, herds, pasture, season progression, acid, bottles, and tester. Result: the recorded average jumped from 3.61 to 3.80 for the full nine weeks and fell back to 3.61 the same day the station's own glass resumed. Because it isolates one variable while holding every other candidate cause fixed, this interval establishes — as no later laboratory measurement alone could — that the cause tracked the physical instrument in real time, contemporaneously, independent of anything found in 1958. It does **not** establish why the borrowed measure delivered a truer reading (nobody calibrated it against a standard; its correctness is inferred only because it produced the value other evidence later confirmed), nor the mechanism of the station's own pipette's defect (that required the 1958 burette measurement), nor the exact shortfall in any year outside those nine weeks.

**D2.** At least three independent reasons rule out cream theft by the tester. First, Roy Brant's private household weigh book recorded his own scale reading beside the station's for every delivery across three decades, and the two agreed almost to the pound — nothing was subtracted at intake, independent of any chemistry. Second, the Association's own ledgers (kept by Wilbur Teague, corroborated by Hobart Sill's independent recollection) show the station's cream consistently arriving at Larrow Green heavier in fat than patrons were ever credited — the opposite of what theft would produce, since stolen cream arrives light. Third, the 1911 interval shows the same tester, with a different instrument, immediately producing higher, truer figures — inconsistent with an actively diverting tester, since swapping glass wouldn't fix that. These are independent because they rest on entirely different record-keeping traditions (an outside family's private ledger; the Association's own internal accounts; a live operational anomaly) kept by mutually distrustful parties. The weigh-book agreement and the 1911 interval would both still hold even if the 1958 glassware had never been measured. As for the bookkeeper: no, Teague never questioned his own entries against his own accusation. He flagged the "gain" to Vose once, in 1916, and was told to drop it; at the 1925 hearing he nonetheless implied the tester was stealing cream, without ever reconciling that claim against the direction of the very ledger he had kept for two decades. The clerk Bertram Kyle later observed that nobody in that office ever laid the receiving book beside the inspector's notebook and looked.

**D3.** Yes — on the specific evidence of bookkeeper Wilbur Teague, who says he personally laid the annual station sheet before manager Selby Vose every spring for signature, and states he explicitly raised the discrepancy with Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone. That is a direct, dated claim of actual knowledge, not mere availability of information. Clerk Bertram Kyle's account explains how this could go unnoticed in an ordinary year — he recalls Vose habitually reading only a sheet's bottom total and never the lines above — but Teague's account describes a specific occasion when the substance was raised in words, removing ambiguity about whether the information had reached him. So: yes, from at least 1916, seven years before the glassware was condemned.

## Section E — Contradictions

Selected, load-bearing cases (not exhaustive):

1. **Cause of the low tests**: three incompatible theories (water/Vose; theft/Teague; hay/Grigg-Kepp) plus the evidence-based cause (defective glass/Vaught-Keddie). Resolved for the glass theory by the 1911 experiment, the ledger's gain direction, and the 1958 measurement — full treatment in Section F.

2. **Award amount**: $3,800 (the minute book itself, quoted verbatim by Loomis and independently by Nym) vs. $3,500 (Vira Toland's oral memory; independently, Cleve Oram relaying his father's oral memory). **Two narrators share the same wrong value**: Toland and Oram. Tie broken by source type — a quoted document beats personal memory decades on.

3. **Surviving pipette count**: nine (Ivy Keddie's own signed measurement sheet, reproduced twice) vs. eleven (Rundle's pamphlet footnote and the Ashlin Chronicle). **Two narrators share the same wrong value**: Rundle and the Chronicle — likely one copying the other, both hasty 1958–59 write-ups. Resolved for the primary signed sheet.

4. **Pipettes purchased**: six dozen/72 (the invoice, reproduced identically twice) vs. five dozen/60 (Vaught's bulletin; independently, Oram). **Two narrators share the same wrong value**: Vaught and Oram. Resolved for the invoice, reproduced from two independent custodial chains.

5. **Pipette delivery/ratio**: 16.72cc/0.95 (Ivy's measurement sheet, quoted twice) vs. 16.27cc/0.9244 (Vaught's bulletin body). **Self-contradiction**: Bulletin 471's own body math conflicts with its own reproduced conclusion (implying 16.72/0.95, "one part in twenty," 76,000 lb); Vaught flags the tension without resolving it. Resolved for the primary sheet; likely a digit-transposition error (72 vs. 27).

6. **Recorded station average**: 3.61 (Keddie's own test book, quoted identically three times) vs. 3.16 (Teague's memoir, uncorroborated — itself a digit-scramble of the same three digits). Resolved for the thrice-quoted primary record.

7. **Total milk intake, affected years**: 40,000,000 lb (Loomis, sourced from Teague's own contemporaneous sheets; independently Ivy Keddie, Nym, Kyle) vs. 44,000,000 lb (Teague's own later 1948 memoir; Rundle's pamphlet). Unusually, Teague contradicts his own earlier-recorded figure across time. I weight the number closer to the source event (Loomis quoting Teague's actual sheets in 1941) over Teague's distant 1948 recollection, while noting genuine uncertainty whether Rundle's matching 44,000,000 is independent or drawn from the same drift.

8. **Condemnation date**: 12 June 1923 (Strawn's own contemporaneous log, reproduced by Nym, cited by Falke) vs. 12 July 1923 (Vose's 1926 address; Rundle's pamphlet). **Two narrators share the same wrong value**: Vose and Rundle. Resolved for the inspector's own field notebook; note Vose had a motive to blur this, arguing against the glass explanation.

9. **Invoice total**: $77.93 (reproduced identically twice) vs. $77.39. **Self-contradiction**: Ammon & Sons' own chapter misstates in prose the very invoice it just quoted correctly; the same wrong figure appears independently in the Ashlin Chronicle. Resolved for the twice-reproduced document.

10. **Selby Vose's parentage**: son of Gideon (Duncan Keddie's explicit correction; Bertram Kyle's independent corroboration) vs. nephew (Vira Toland; Rundle's pamphlet). **Two narrators share the same wrong value**: Toland and Rundle. Resolved for the two direct, positioned denials.

11. **Ivy Keddie's relation to Ansel**: granddaughter (Duncan Keddie, her own father) vs. great-niece (the Ashlin Chronicle; Falke's report before its own correction). **Self-contradiction**: Falke's report states "great-niece" on one page, then explicitly corrects itself to "granddaughter" in its own postscript. Resolved as granddaughter.

12. **Self-contradictions on the page, collected**: Bulletin 471 (#5); Rundle's pamphlet (Chapter IV asserts the cream-theft theory as valley consensus, then a note added while in press retracts it after Rundle reads the ledger himself); Grigg's 1919 letter (claims year-round 1908 sampling while reprinting his own report stating only January–March); Ammon & Sons' chapter (#9); Falke's report (both the great-niece/granddaughter correction and a second correction withdrawing his concession to the hay theory once he obtained Bulletin 214's real figures).

13. **Number of families receiving the 1919 circular**: nine (Vira Toland; Ansel Keddie's own letter; Hazel Brant — three accounts) vs. eleven (Orra Keddie's own diary, written contemporaneously by the wife of the man who hand-delivered the letters). **I do not think the sources settle this.** Orra's diary is the most primary and contemporaneous witness, yet it is the outlier against three other accounts; numerousness is not the same as independence or authority, so I decline to force a majority resolution here.

## Section F — Theory

**F1.** The shortfall was caused by a single manufacturing defect in the station's own testing glassware. The pipettes bought new in 1897 were blown to a nominal 17.6cc mark but (per Ammon & Sons' own later account, likely a reground mold) actually delivered only about 16.72cc — a consistent 95% of the intended charge, invisible to ordinary use because a test-bottle neck is read as a fixed percentage of whatever charge goes in, and this defect delivered the same wrong charge every time. This explains the exact size of the gap (a flat 5%, ~0.19 points on a ~3.80 true average, producing the observed 3.61) as a pure geometric consequence of the glass, not an approximation; it explains why the gap began in 1897 (the flawed glass replaced the correctly borrowed glass used in the station's higher-testing first season) and why it ended in June 1923 (the state inspector, newly equipped with a calibrated burette, finally checked the glass itself rather than the scales or lactometer, and condemned it); and it explains why nothing about the cows, the land, or the patrons' honesty ever had to change, because the whole mechanism lived in one piece of equipment the ordinary practice of the trade never checks against anything external — the pipette *is* the plant's own standard.

Three competing explanations were advanced, each defeated by specific evidence. (1) Added water — Selby Vose (1919 circular, 1926 address) — defeated by the inspector's own lactometer readings, taken at nearly every visit across the whole period, never once finding added water. (2) Cream theft by the tester — implied at the 1925 hearing by Wilbur Teague — defeated by the ledger's own "station gain" entries showing more fat arriving than was ever credited (the opposite of theft), by Roy Brant's weigh-book agreement on intake weight, and by the 1911 interval showing the same tester producing different results with different glass. (3) Wet hay thinning the milk — the 1908 committee, elaborated by Dr. Roland Kepp in 1926 — defeated by the college's own feeding trial (Bulletin 214), an effect four-to-six times too small and winter-only against a gap flat in every month, and by Alder Corners' sixteen years of independent tests on milk from the same wet ground, holding steady near the true 3.80.

Subsidiary questions: the 1925 board does not appear to have meaningfully examined the condemned glassware. The Association's own secretary, who recorded every word of the hearing, states flatly the crate was never mentioned in any session. The inspector's 1923 notebook was technically one of only four exhibits entered, but by the clerk's own account nobody in the room ever laid it beside the receiving-book figures and drew the connection — the physical evidence was present in attenuated form, but its significance went unrecognized. As for what the tester himself believed: genuinely mixed, not simply resolvable. Publicly, in his sworn statement, Ansel Keddie committed to no theory and insisted he could not explain the figures. Privately he seems to have held two different suspicions at different times: he told the arbitration chairman, Frick, in a corridor conversation that he had always privately suspected the patrons, though he wouldn't say so in the room; but his son recalled him saying at home, specifically during the 1911 anomaly, that he thought the trouble was "something in the glass" — a suspicion never committed to paper because he could not prove it.

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

For twenty-six years a cooperative creamery's remote skimming station paid farmers on butterfat tests running about 5% low — the simple consequence of glass pipettes, bought new in 1897, that silently delivered less milk than their mark claimed. Nobody suspected the glass. The valley blamed the patrons for watering milk, the manager blamed the tester for stealing cream, and an official committee blamed the wet meadow hay — each theory pushed by people with a stake in a different answer, and each eventually defeated by evidence that had existed all along: a nine-week natural experiment in 1911, the creamery's own ledgers, and independent tests from across the ridge. The tester was fired anyway in 1925 and died unexonerated in 1934. Only in 1958, when his granddaughter climbed into a forgotten loft, was the true cause measured and published — requiring letters, ledgers, diaries, and three laboratories' data to finally add up.
