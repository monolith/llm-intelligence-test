# Answers — Part 3 of 3
## Section C — Math (show working)

**C1.**
(a) The nine surviving pipettes, blown to a 17.6cc mark, delivered a measured mean of 16.72cc against a calibrated burette (Ivy Keddie's 1958 measurement sheet, reproduced identically in two documents). 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly. So the glass delivered 95% of its intended charge — the tests read a flat 5% low, "one part in twenty." (I reject Bulletin 471's own mid-text alternative figures — 16.27cc, ratio 0.9244 — as an internal error, since they contradict the bulletin's own reproduced closing paragraph and don't reconcile with any other figure in the record; see Section E.)

(b) Recorded average: 3.61 (Ansel Keddie's own test-book summary, quoted identically in three separate documents). True average = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80** exactly. Shortfall = 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 points** (nineteen hundredths). This is triangulated three independent ways: this arithmetic; the 1911 natural experiment, which produced exactly 3.80 on borrowed glass; and the Alder Corners Cheese Company's independent tests of the same herds, averaging ~3.80 across sixteen summers.

(c) Over the 25 full delivery years (1898–1922), the station received 40,000,000 lb of milk (corroborated in four documents). Credited fat at 3.61% = 1,444,000 lb. True fat at 3.80% = 1,520,000 lb. Difference = **76,000 lb** of butterfat never credited.

(d) At the period's average price of $0.25/lb: 76,000 × $0.25 = **$19,000**. The patrons actually received the 1925 award of $3,800. $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = **1/5** (20%) of what was owed.

**C2.** Largest patron = Emmet Brant. His 1910 figures (his own household weigh book, corroborated by his granddaughter Hazel Brant): 200,000 lb of milk delivered, paid on a test of 3.42.
(a) Credited butterfat = 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb**.
(b) True test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.6 exactly. True butterfat = 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb. Owed and not credited = 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb**.
(c) 1910 butterfat price, per Roy Brant's own account: $0.24/lb. 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40**.

**C3.**
(a) Credited total, 25 full years: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**.
(b) Actually received at Larrow Green: 40,000,000 × 0.038 = **1,520,000 lb**.
(c) Difference = 76,000 lb. As a percentage of fat actually delivered (1,520,000 lb): 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5.0%** exactly — "the one that is also the glass," in Ivy Keddie's phrase. As a percentage of fat credited (1,444,000 lb — the denominator the bookkeeper's ledger conventionally used): 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 ≈ **5.26%** (the ledger's own "five and a quarter percent, as usual"). Both are correct for their own denominator; conflating them is one of the record's recurring small confusions.

**C4.**
(a) Invoice total: **$77.93** (identical in two independently reproduced copies of the same document — the museum catalog and Ammon & Sons' own company history). Two secondary tellings — the Ashlin Chronicle, and Ammon & Sons' own prose describing its own correctly-quoted invoice — both mangle this to $77.39.
(b) **72 pipettes** (six dozen, per the invoice, corroborated by Ivy Keddie's talk). Each was short by 17.6 − 16.72 = **0.88cc**.

**C5.**
(a) The faulty glass was in continuous service about **26 years** — from 1 May 1897 (invoice date; Orra Keddie's diary entry of that exact day; the museum catalog) to 12 June 1923 (the inspector's own contemporaneous log entry, corroborated by the museum catalog and Falke's report). The money calculation runs over only **25 full delivery years** (1898–1922) because it excludes the two partial years at each end: 1897 (Keddie's own book marks it "part year," since the new glass arrived mid-season) and 1923 (the glass was condemned mid-year and replaced within weeks). Using only complete calendar years avoids estimating a fractional year's milk volume.
(b) First-season (1896) intake: **cannot be determined precisely**. Two conflicting oral estimates exist — about 900,000 lb (Hobart Sill) and about 1,100,000 lb (Bertram Kyle) — and the museum curator states explicitly that no ledger record survives for 1896 at all, so neither estimate can be checked against a primary document.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran 8 May–10 July 1911 — nine weeks. Variable: the pipette itself (the station's own broke; a measure borrowed from Larrow Green was used until it was replaced). Held constant: same patrons, herds, pasture, season progression, acid, bottles, and tester. Result: the recorded average jumped from 3.61 to 3.80 for the full nine weeks and fell back to 3.61 the same day the station's own glass resumed. Because it isolates one variable while holding every other candidate cause fixed, this interval establishes — as no later laboratory measurement alone could — that the cause tracked the physical instrument in real time, contemporaneously, independent of anything found in 1958. It does **not** establish why the borrowed measure delivered a truer reading (nobody calibrated it against a standard; its correctness is inferred only because it produced the value other evidence later confirmed), nor the mechanism of the station's own pipette's defect (that required the 1958 burette measurement), nor the exact shortfall in any year outside those nine weeks.

**D2.** At least three independent reasons rule out cream theft by the tester. First, Roy Brant's private household weigh book recorded his own scale reading beside the station's for every delivery across three decades, and the two agreed almost to the pound — nothing was subtracted at intake, independent of any chemistry. Second, the Association's own ledgers (kept by Wilbur Teague, corroborated by Hobart Sill's independent recollection) show the station's cream consistently arriving at Larrow Green heavier in fat than patrons were ever credited — the opposite of what theft would produce, since stolen cream arrives light. Third, the 1911 interval shows the same tester, with a different instrument, immediately producing higher, truer figures — inconsistent with an actively diverting tester, since swapping glass wouldn't fix that. These are independent because they rest on entirely different record-keeping traditions (an outside family's private ledger; the Association's own internal accounts; a live operational anomaly) kept by mutually distrustful parties. The weigh-book agreement and the 1911 interval would both still hold even if the 1958 glassware had never been measured. As for the bookkeeper: no, Teague never questioned his own entries against his own accusation. He flagged the "gain" to Vose once, in 1916, and was told to drop it; at the 1925 hearing he nonetheless implied the tester was stealing cream, without ever reconciling that claim against the direction of the very ledger he had kept for two decades. The clerk Bertram Kyle later observed that nobody in that office ever laid the receiving book beside the inspector's notebook and looked.

**D3.** Yes — on the specific evidence of bookkeeper Wilbur Teague, who says he personally laid the annual station sheet before manager Selby Vose every spring for signature, and states he explicitly raised the discrepancy with Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone. That is a direct, dated claim of actual knowledge, not mere availability of information. Clerk Bertram Kyle's account explains how this could go unnoticed in an ordinary year — he recalls Vose habitually reading only a sheet's bottom total and never the lines above — but Teague's account describes a specific occasion when the substance was raised in words, removing ambiguity about whether the information had reached him. So: yes, from at least 1916, seven years before the glassware was condemned.

## Section E — Contradictions

Selected, load-bearing cases (not exhaustive):

1. **Cause of the low tests**: three incompatible theories (water/Vose; theft/Teague; hay/Grigg-Kepp) plus the evidence-based cause (defective glass/Vaught-Keddie). Resolved for the glass theory by the 1911 experiment, the ledger's gain direction, and the 1958 measurement — full treatment in Section F.

2. **Award amount**: $3,800 (the minute book itself, quoted verbatim by Loomis and independently by Nym) vs. $3,500 (Vira Toland's oral memory; independently, Cleve Oram relaying his father's oral memory). **Two narrators share the same wrong value**: Toland and Oram. Tie broken by source type — a quoted document beats personal memory decades on.

3. **Surviving pipette count**: nine (Ivy Keddie's own signed measurement sheet, reproduced twice) vs. eleven (Rundle's pamphlet footnote and the Ashlin Chronicle). **Two narrators share the same wrong value**: Rundle and the Chronicle — likely one copying the other, both hasty 1958–59 write-ups. Resolved for the primary signed sheet.

4. **Pipettes purchased**: six dozen/72 (the invoice, reproduced identically twice) vs. five dozen/60 (Vaught's bulletin; independently, Oram). **Two narrators share the same wrong value**: Vaught and Oram. Resolved for the invoice, reproduced from two independent custodial chains.

5. **Pipette delivery/ratio**: 16.72cc/0.95 (Ivy's measurement sheet, quoted twice) vs. 16.27cc/0.9244 (Vaught's bulletin body). **Self-contradiction**: Bulletin 471's own body math conflicts with its own reproduced conclusion (implying 16.72/0.95, "one part in twenty," 76,000 lb); Vaught flags the tension without resolving it. Resolved for the primary sheet; likely a digit-transposition error (72 vs. 27).

6. **Recorded station average**: 3.61 (Keddie's own test book, quoted identically three times) vs. 3.16 (Teague's memoir, uncorroborated — itself a digit-scramble of the same three digits). Resolved for the thrice-quoted primary record.

7. **Total milk intake, affected years**: 40,000,000 lb (Loomis, sourced from Teague's own contemporaneous sheets; independently Ivy Keddie, Nym, Kyle) vs. 44,000,000 lb (Teague's own later 1948 memoir; Rundle's pamphlet). Unusually, Teague contradicts his own earlier-recorded figure across time. I weight the number closer to the source event (Loomis quoting Teague's actual sheets in 1941) over Teague's distant 1948 recollection, while noting genuine uncertainty whether Rundle's matching 44,000,000 is independent or drawn from the same drift.

8. **Condemnation date**: 12 June 1923 (Strawn's own contemporaneous log, reproduced by Nym, cited by Falke) vs. 12 July 1923 (Vose's 1926 address; Rundle's pamphlet). **Two narrators share the same wrong value**: Vose and Rundle. Resolved for the inspector's own field notebook; note Vose had a motive to blur this, arguing against the glass explanation.

9. **Invoice total**: $77.93 (reproduced identically twice) vs. $77.39. **Self-contradiction**: Ammon & Sons' own chapter misstates in prose the very invoice it just quoted correctly; the same wrong figure appears independently in the Ashlin Chronicle. Resolved for the twice-reproduced document.

10. **Selby Vose's parentage**: son of Gideon (Duncan Keddie's explicit correction; Bertram Kyle's independent corroboration) vs. nephew (Vira Toland; Rundle's pamphlet). **Two narrators share the same wrong value**: Toland and Rundle. Resolved for the two direct, positioned denials.

11. **Ivy Keddie's relation to Ansel**: granddaughter (Duncan Keddie, her own father) vs. great-niece (the Ashlin Chronicle; Falke's report before its own correction). **Self-contradiction**: Falke's report states "great-niece" on one page, then explicitly corrects itself to "granddaughter" in its own postscript. Resolved as granddaughter.

12. **Self-contradictions on the page, collected**: Bulletin 471 (#5); Rundle's pamphlet (Chapter IV asserts the cream-theft theory as valley consensus, then a note added while in press retracts it after Rundle reads the ledger himself); Grigg's 1919 letter (claims year-round 1908 sampling while reprinting his own report stating only January–March); Ammon & Sons' chapter (#9); Falke's report (both the great-niece/granddaughter correction and a second correction withdrawing his concession to the hay theory once he obtained Bulletin 214's real figures).

13. **Number of families receiving the 1919 circular**: nine (Vira Toland; Ansel Keddie's own letter; Hazel Brant — three accounts) vs. eleven (Orra Keddie's own diary, written contemporaneously by the wife of the man who hand-delivered the letters). **I do not think the sources settle this.** Orra's diary is the most primary and contemporaneous witness, yet it is the outlier against three other accounts; numerousness is not the same as independence or authority, so I decline to force a majority resolution here.

## Section F — Theory

**F1.** The shortfall was caused by a single manufacturing defect in the station's own testing glassware. The pipettes bought new in 1897 were blown to a nominal 17.6cc mark but (per Ammon & Sons' own later account, likely a reground mold) actually delivered only about 16.72cc — a consistent 95% of the intended charge, invisible to ordinary use because a test-bottle neck is read as a fixed percentage of whatever charge goes in, and this defect delivered the same wrong charge every time. This explains the exact size of the gap (a flat 5%, ~0.19 points on a ~3.80 true average, producing the observed 3.61) as a pure geometric consequence of the glass, not an approximation; it explains why the gap began in 1897 (the flawed glass replaced the correctly borrowed glass used in the station's higher-testing first season) and why it ended in June 1923 (the state inspector, newly equipped with a calibrated burette, finally checked the glass itself rather than the scales or lactometer, and condemned it); and it explains why nothing about the cows, the land, or the patrons' honesty ever had to change, because the whole mechanism lived in one piece of equipment the ordinary practice of the trade never checks against anything external — the pipette *is* the plant's own standard.

Three competing explanations were advanced, each defeated by specific evidence. (1) Added water — Selby Vose (1919 circular, 1926 address) — defeated by the inspector's own lactometer readings, taken at nearly every visit across the whole period, never once finding added water. (2) Cream theft by the tester — implied at the 1925 hearing by Wilbur Teague — defeated by the ledger's own "station gain" entries showing more fat arriving than was ever credited (the opposite of theft), by Roy Brant's weigh-book agreement on intake weight, and by the 1911 interval showing the same tester producing different results with different glass. (3) Wet hay thinning the milk — the 1908 committee, elaborated by Dr. Roland Kepp in 1926 — defeated by the college's own feeding trial (Bulletin 214), an effect four-to-six times too small and winter-only against a gap flat in every month, and by Alder Corners' sixteen years of independent tests on milk from the same wet ground, holding steady near the true 3.80.

Subsidiary questions: the 1925 board does not appear to have meaningfully examined the condemned glassware. The Association's own secretary, who recorded every word of the hearing, states flatly the crate was never mentioned in any session. The inspector's 1923 notebook was technically one of only four exhibits entered, but by the clerk's own account nobody in the room ever laid it beside the receiving-book figures and drew the connection — the physical evidence was present in attenuated form, but its significance went unrecognized. As for what the tester himself believed: genuinely mixed, not simply resolvable. Publicly, in his sworn statement, Ansel Keddie committed to no theory and insisted he could not explain the figures. Privately he seems to have held two different suspicions at different times: he told the arbitration chairman, Frick, in a corridor conversation that he had always privately suspected the patrons, though he wouldn't say so in the room; but his son recalled him saying at home, specifically during the 1911 anomaly, that he thought the trouble was "something in the glass" — a suspicion never committed to paper because he could not prove it.

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

For twenty-six years a cooperative creamery's remote skimming station paid farmers on butterfat tests running about 5% low — the simple consequence of glass pipettes, bought new in 1897, that silently delivered less milk than their mark claimed. Nobody suspected the glass. The valley blamed the patrons for watering milk, the manager blamed the tester for stealing cream, and an official committee blamed the wet meadow hay — each theory pushed by people with a stake in a different answer, and each eventually defeated by evidence that had existed all along: a nine-week natural experiment in 1911, the creamery's own ledgers, and independent tests from across the ridge. The tester was fired anyway in 1925 and died unexonerated in 1934. Only in 1958, when his granddaughter climbed into a forgotten loft, was the true cause measured and published — requiring letters, ledgers, diaries, and three laboratories' data to finally add up.
