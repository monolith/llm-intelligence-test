# Answers — Part 1 of 3 (Section A, Stories 1–4)

*Partition note: the 24 retellings all orbit one continuous 70-year episode at the Ostrey Hollow
skimming station. I have split it into eight chronological chapters, each with its own cast,
documents, and quantities, rather than eight separate topics — that is the natural seam the
sources themselves fall into (each retelling touches several chapters; almost none stays inside
one).*

## Story 1 — The rule that made it matter: founding the Association and Article VII (1889–1893)

Farmers of the Larrow Valley organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association in 1889,
meeting at the Larrow Green schoolhouse in cold weather (Rundle's pamphlet records a horse getting
loose mid-meeting). They built the main creamery at Larrow Green in 1891. Gideon Vose, elected the
Association's first president in 1889, personally drafted its by-laws, including Article VII: every
patron would be paid according to the pounds of butterfat a *test* found in his milk, not according
to the milk's weight. Vose held the presidency until 1911 and died in 1917.

Article VII's adoption date is one of the few points in the whole record that cannot be cleanly
settled: three sources give three different years — 1891 (Rundle's pamphlet, tying it to the churn's
installation), 1892 (the multi-source baseline established across several earlier retellings), and
1893 (Nyle Grigg's own 1919 letter, "as I have always understood it"). None is a contemporaneous
document; none is corroborated by a second independent source of its own. I treat 1892 as the working
figure only because it has the broadest (if unverified) support, and flag the date itself as
genuinely undetermined.

This story matters less for its own events than for what it made possible. County agent Orin
Falke's 1959 report states the point plainly: "Under payment by weight nothing that follows in this
report would have cost any man a dollar." Article VII is the hinge — it is the reason a piece of
mismeasuring glass at a single station could translate into thirty years of real financial loss and
a man's dismissal, rather than being a curiosity nobody's money depended on.

**Documents:** Article VII's text (by-laws); Dell Rundle's 1939/1959 jubilee pamphlet.
**Quantities:** none of consequence beyond the disputed adoption year.

## Story 2 — Opening the station and buying the bad glass (1895–1898)

The Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up-valley, over the winter of
1895–96, to serve Hessel Bottom's six farms. It opened 4 May 1896. For its first season it borrowed
its testing glass from the Larrow Green creamery — buttermaker Hobart Sill personally supplied an
outfit from his own bench ("I went to my own bench in this room and I made up an outfit for them out
of my own glass"). Ansel Keddie was hired as tester and separator man from the opening day.

The Association's permanent outfit was ordered from Ammon & Sons, dairy supply house at Tarnet,
invoiced 14 April 1897. The invoice survives and has been reproduced in facsimile by the firm's own
1949 house history: six dozen (72) milk-test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. at $0.35 each ($25.20), twelve
dozen test bottles ($31.68), one acid measure ($0.90), one four-bottle hand tester ($18.00), and
freight ($2.15) — total **$77.93**. (A 1958 newspaper account garbles this to "$77.39"; the actual
invoice, quoted verbatim by the manufacturer's own historian, is authoritative here.) The new outfit
went into use 1 May 1897 — a date fixed independently by Orra Keddie's own diary entry of that day
("A. says the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green") and by
Bulletin 471's own concluding paragraph.

One lot in that shipment — the pipettes — was blown consistently short of its own mark, almost
certainly (per the manufacturer's own later theory) because a regrooved mold ran a hair narrow and
no check of the period could have caught a *consistent* error. Because a butterfat reading is taken
directly off the pipette's own charge, with no independent standard to compare against inside the
plant, this defect was invisible from the day it began.

**Documents:** the Ammon & Sons invoice (facsimile); Orra Keddie's diary, 1 May 1897 entry.
**Quantities:** 72 pipettes at 17.6 c.c. mark; $77.93 total invoice.

## Story 3 — Thirty years of low tests and the theories advanced for them (1902–1926)

From 1902, Ostrey Hollow's patrons began complaining publicly that their milk tested lower than
milk anywhere else in the county — a complaint repeated in every following decade. The true gap,
once quantified, was a steady **0.19 points**, present in every month of the year alike, winter and
flush season both (Hazel Brant: "not a little in the winter and none in the summer, the way a feed
thing goes... flat as a floor"). The station's long-run recorded average stood at 3.61%; the true
figure, established independently by Larrow Green's own average and by a wholly separate cheese
factory's records (Story 5), was about 3.80%.

Three theories circulated for over two decades, argued by different parties at different times:
wet Hessel Bottom pasture thinning the cream (Nyle Grigg's 1908 investigating committee, later
elaborated with a mineral-deficiency mechanism by veterinarian Dr. Roland Kepp in a 1926 paper);
tester theft (raised obliquely at the 1925 hearing by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague); and patrons
watering their milk (the Association's own official position from 1919). None of the three was ever
substantiated at the time, and each is independently contradicted by other evidence in the record
(detailed in Section F). Notably, the wet-hay theory's two chief advocates, Grigg and Kepp, both
independently misquoted the college's own relevant feeding-trial bulletin (No. 214) as finding a
0.20-point effect, when its actual verbatim text — quoted three times elsewhere in the record —
says "not more than five hundredths of one per cent (0.05) in winter, and nothing... in summer."

**Documents:** the 1908 committee report (full text, reprinted 1919 by Grigg); Bulletin No. 214;
Kepp's 1926 paper.
**Quantities:** 0.19-point gap; 3.61% recorded vs. ~3.80% true.

## Story 4 — The 1919 water circular and the valley's split reaction

In 1919, manager Selby Vose circulated a written warning to the Hessel Bottom patrons accusing them
of adding water to their milk. Clerk Bertram Kyle typed it; Ansel Keddie, rather than mail the
letters or leave them on the platform, delivered every one himself and stood at each door while the
man read it, coming home near midnight "with his hat still on" (per his wife Orra's diary). Reaction
split sharply along allegiance: Emmet Brant — Orra's own brother, the largest patron on the Bottom —
never spoke to the Keddies again, not over the accusation itself but because he felt Ansel should
have refused to carry it; Hobart Sill at Larrow Green, by contrast, approved of it outright ("I
thought well of him for it. It was the only plain thing that office ever did"). No patron ever
replied to the circular. Most sources put the number of families warned at nine (matching "nine
farms" language used elsewhere and Hazel Brant's explicit count of nine families across six farms);
Orra's own diary, however, says Ansel delivered it "to eleven families" — a genuine, unresolved
conflict between the closest contemporary witness and the numerical convention used everywhere else.

**Documents:** the circular (described, not directly quoted, by multiple witnesses); Orra Keddie's
diary.
**Quantities:** nine families (Hazel Brant, baseline) or eleven (Orra's diary) — undetermined.
# Answers — Part 2 of 3 (Section A, Stories 5–8; Section B)

## Story 5 — The control group across the ridge: Alder Corners Cheese Company (1913/1914–1958)

A cheese factory was founded across Redlow Ridge in neighboring Vessey County by Jerome Cudd — 1913
per his own daughter's later letter, or 1914 per two other independent accounts (Roy Brant's family
history and Dell Rundle's pamphlet); the primary source (the founder's own daughter) is outnumbered
two-to-one and this is not cleanly resolvable. Starting in 1915, four of Hessel Bottom's six farms
(not Roy Brant's) hauled their milk over the ridge to Alder Corners each summer flush — roughly
1 June to mid-September — returning to Ostrey Hollow the rest of the year, for sixteen consecutive
seasons through 1930. Jerome Cudd, and after his death in March 1941 his daughter Rosalie, kept
their own test book on their own glassware in a room off the intake, and — by Rosalie's own express
statement — never once compared their figures with Ostrey Hollow's ("it never entered his head").

Those records, entirely independent of the Association's own bookkeeping, show the same four men's
milk testing normally throughout: individual 1919 figures of 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82, and a
sixteen-season (1915–1930) average of about 3.80. Rosalie Cudd supplied this full table, at Ivy
Keddie's request, in a 1958 letter — the single clearest piece of evidence in the whole record that
the milk itself was never at fault, since the same cows produced normal test results the moment
they were measured on different glass.

**Documents:** Jerome Cudd's 1919 test-book entry ("Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over
the ridge"); Rosalie Cudd's 1958 letter with the full sixteen-season table.
**Quantities:** 16 seasons, 1915–1930, averaging ~3.80%.

## Story 6 — The condemnation of the glassware (1923)

Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector for the Ordell circuit since 1907, tested the station's pipettes
against his own calibrated burette on a routine visit and found they would not deliver their marked
capacity. He condemned the entire lot on the spot and directed the Association to order new glass.
The date is fixed by Strawn's own circuit book (independently confirmed by a curator's photostat of
that book) as **12 June 1923** — a primary-document date that outranks two later secondary claims of
"12 July" (Selby Vose's 1926 address and Rundle's pamphlet) and a third, still later claim of "1922"
(clerk Bertram Kyle's 1953 memoir), none of which agree even with each other.

New glass was ordered and in use within the month. From the following year the station's recorded
average rose to roughly 3.80–3.85 and stayed there. Nobody at the time — not even Strawn — drew any
connection between this finding and the twenty-six years of complaints already on record; the
condemned crate was simply carried up to the loft over the intake room and left there.

**Documents:** Strawn's circuit notebook.
**Quantities:** condemnation date 12 June 1923; post-fix average ~3.80–3.85%.

## Story 7 — The 1925 arbitration and Ansel Keddie's dismissal

The Hessel Bottom patrons petitioned the Association in January 1925. A three-man board of
arbitration, chaired by Ashlin attorney Alonzo Frick (chosen, by his own account, "because I was the
one of the three who kept no cow"), sat three times: once at the Association's Ashlin office
(17 March 1925), and twice at the station itself, in its own weigh room, at Frick's own insistence
"so the board could see the plant firsthand." The board heard all three theories from Story 3
argued in turn — water (put by Vose "without heat and without proof"), theft (put obliquely by
Teague), and wet hay (via the 1908 report read into the record) — and reached no formal finding of
cause, "without admission." Ansel Keddie, tester for twenty-nine seasons, was dismissed 30 April
1925, four days before the board's award, dated 4 May 1925, of **$3,800** to the patrons — a small
fraction of what they had claimed (variously reckoned at $19,600 or 67,000 lb of butterfat, two
different contemporary figures for the same claim).

The condemned pipette crate sat in the loft directly above the weigh room for both of the on-site
sessions and was never brought down or so much as mentioned; Frick's own reprinted exhibit list
names only four items (the test book, the receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book, and
Strawn's 1923 notebook). Keddie never advanced a public theory of his own — he had written only a
question mark in his book after the unexplained 1911 anomaly (Story 3's counterpart event) — and
spent his remaining nine years doing carpentry, dying in 1934 still under the valley's unspoken
suspicion, "a man [who] cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name," in his own words as recorded
by his widow.

**Documents:** Frick's 1946 recollection and exhibit list; Orra Keddie's diary entries for
March–April 1925.
**Quantities:** $3,800 award; dismissal 30 April 1925; award 4 May 1925.

## Story 8 — The 1958 discovery and Bulletin No. 471

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie — Ansel's granddaughter, then a graduate student at the State
Agricultural College at Farland — went up the ladder into the loft over the old intake room while
researching the station's history and found the 1923 crate still there, undisturbed, under a
tarpaulin. She carried it to Farland and, on 9 March 1958, measured the nine surviving pipettes
against a calibrated burette: each delivered about **16.72 c.c.** against its **17.6 c.c.** mark —
a consistent 5% ("one part in twenty") shortfall, exactly matching the historical test gap. She also
borrowed Roy Brant's family ledger (returned the same year) and obtained Rosalie Cudd's independent
Alder Corners figures as corroboration. Her findings, co-written with Professor Leland Vaught, were
published as **State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 471** in October 1958, formally clearing
Ansel Keddie of both theft and any complicity in watering — twenty-four years after his death. She
gave a public talk on the research, "What the Crate Held," to the Ordell County Historical Society
in 1959.

**Documents:** Bulletin No. 471; Ivy Keddie's own measurement sheet.
**Quantities:** 9 surviving pipettes (of the original 72); 16.72 c.c. measured mean vs. 17.6 c.c.
mark; 76,000 lb / $19,000 computed shortfall over 25 affected delivery years (worked in Section C).

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**
Granddaughter. Ivy is the daughter of Duncan Keddie, and Duncan is Ansel's son — a chain stated
explicitly and deliberately by Duncan himself in his 1968 family notes ("My daughter is Ivy... which
makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it
printed otherwise"). Two sources call her Ansel's "great-niece" instead: a 1958 newspaper feature,
and county agent Orin Falke's own 1959 report — but Falke's report is the more instructive case,
because Falke *catches and corrects his own error* in a postscript: "I am informed, and have
verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is
mine." A direct family statement plus a self-correcting official document together settle this
decisively; the newspaper's "great-niece" is simply wrong.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**
Second cousins. Ansel married Orra Brant; Orra's brother was Emmet Brant. Duncan (Ansel and Orra's
son) and Roy Brant (Emmet's son) were therefore first cousins; Ivy (Duncan's daughter) and Hazel
(Roy's daughter) are the children of first cousins, i.e. second cousins — worked out explicitly by
Duncan himself, and matching Roy Brant's own account and a 1959 pamphlet footnote. One source, Cleve
Oram, calls them "first cousin once removed" instead, but hedges it himself ("that is the way of it,"
offered without being able to "draw you the line on paper") — an outsider's guess against three
family-sourced, explicitly reasoned accounts. What the sources leave genuinely open: whether Orra and
Emmet were full siblings (the near-universal account, stated most emphatically by Roy Brant himself:
"same mother and father, whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in
it") or half-siblings by a second wife (Hazel Brant's own account, decades later) — strikingly, the
one person who explicitly pre-rebuts the "stepmother" story is Roy, Hazel's own father. I resolve
this in favor of full siblings, since it is Emmet's own son's first-hand, deliberate claim, made
specifically to preempt the very rumor his own daughter would later repeat; if Hazel's version were
instead correct, the cousin arithmetic and label would not change (the sources don't use "half"
terminology for cousin degree either way), so nothing in Section B2's answer depends on resolving it.

**B3. Selby Vose's father, and why it matters.**
Gideon Vose — founding president of the Association (1889–1911) and author of its by-laws,
including Article VII. This is now settled at 5-to-2 among sources, and decisively so: two
first-person, insider accounts explicitly and independently reject "nephew" as a documented error —
Duncan Keddie's family notes ("not, as I have heard it given, his nephew") and Bertram Kyle's own
office memoir ("the son, whatever is said at Ashlin... I have heard the nephew story twice in the
last year and it is nonsense"). It matters because it means the man who managed the station for a
quarter-century, signed the annual ledgers carrying the unexamined "station gain" line, and issued
the 1919 water circular was the son of, and inherited his position from, the very man whose Article
VII made a mismeasured pipette costly in the first place — a father-to-son continuity of authority
over the whole affair, not a more distant, arm's-length family connection.

**B4. Relationship between the Vose and Keddie families.**
None, on the best evidence available. Duncan Keddie addresses this directly and at length: "There
was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses...
It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk." He specifically notes his mother — a
woman, by his account, who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection" — never
once included a Vose in her reckoning of family ties. Against this stands only Cleve Oram's vague,
self-admittedly uncertain claim that "the Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort... though I
could not draw you the line on paper." A detailed, first-person denial from within the family, citing
a specific named source of the confusion, outweighs an outsider's hedge that supplies no names, no
generation, and no mechanism. I conclude the families were not related, and the belief that they were
is valley gossip conflating two unrelated Vose households.
# Answers — Part 3 of 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math

**C1.**

(a) The pipettes were marked (blown) to deliver 17.6 c.c. but Ivy Keddie's own 1958 measurement of
the nine survivors found a mean actual delivery of 16.72 c.c. Proportion delivered = 16.72 ÷ 17.6 =
**0.95, i.e. 95% of the mark — a 5% shortfall, "one part in twenty,"** matching Bulletin 471's own
concluding-paragraph language exactly. (I use the 16.72 c.c. / 76,000 lb figure, not Vaught's own
Section III/V figure of 16.27 c.c. / 118,000 lb, for reasons given under C1(c) and Section E.)

(b) True average test = 3.80% (established independently three ways: Larrow Green's own long-run
average of ~3.78–3.80%; the sixteen-season Alder Corners comparison for the same men, averaging
3.80%; and the arithmetic check below). Recorded average = 3.61%. Shortfall = 3.80 − 3.61 =
**0.19 points**, matching the "flat as a floor" gap multiple narrators independently describe.
Consistency check: recorded ÷ true = 3.61 ÷ 3.80 = 0.95 — exactly the same 95% ratio as (a),
which is strong internal confirmation that 3.80/3.61 and 16.72/17.6 describe the same underlying
mechanism.

(c) Using the established 40,000,000 lb total milk over 25 full delivery years (see reasoning in
Section E for why I prefer 40,000,000 lb over the competing 44,000,000 lb figure) and the 0.19-point
gap: 40,000,000 × 0.0019 = **76,000 lb of butterfat** never credited. This is also the figure stated
verbatim in Bulletin 471's own concluding paragraph, and is internally consistent with the
"station-gain" ledger's own arithmetic (see C3).

(d) At the established ~25-cents-per-pound average price for the affected years (Roy Brant's family
ledger and clerk Bertram Kyle's own book-derived figure both converge here, against a competing
27-cent estimate from two elderly men's personal recollections — see Section E): 76,000 × $0.25 =
**$19,000**. The patrons actually received **$3,800** — exactly **one-fifth (20%)** of that figure,
matching Ivy Keddie's own comment that the award was "a fifth of" the true loss.

**C2.** Largest patron = Emmet Brant, largest herd and most hay ground on Hessel Bottom. His own
1910 ledger leaf (quoted identically in three independent retellings) reads: "two hundred thousand
pounds even, delivered to the station... paid on a test of 3.42."

(a) Credited: 200,000 lb × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb of butterfat**.

(b) The shortfall mechanism is proportional, not additive — Professor Vaught's own bulletin states
this explicitly ("rich milk loses more absolute fat than poor milk, but herds still rank correctly
against each other"), so the correction is recorded ÷ 0.95, not recorded + 0.19. True test =
3.42 ÷ 0.95 = **3.60%**. True butterfat = 200,000 × 0.036 = 7,200 lb. Owed and not credited =
7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb of butterfat**.

(c) At the 1910-specific price of **24 cents/lb** (Emmet Brant's own ledger figure for that exact
year, more precise than any multi-year average): 360 × $0.24 = **$86.40**.

**C3.** Over the full 25 affected delivery years, at 40,000,000 lb total milk:

(a) Credited to patrons at the recorded 3.61% average: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**.

(b) Actually reaching Larrow Green in the station's cream, at the true 3.80% average:
40,000,000 × 0.038 = **1,520,000 lb**.

(c) Difference = 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 lb (matching C1(c) exactly — a good consistency
check). Expressed as a percentage, the answer depends on what you take the percentage *of*:
- As a fraction of the **true (delivered) total**: 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = **5.0% exactly** — this is
  the basis for Bulletin 471's own phrase "short by one part in twenty."
- As a fraction of the **credited (recorded) total**: 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = **5.26%** — this is the
  basis the Association's own bookkeeper actually used: Rundle's quoted ledger entry describes a
  single year's "station gain" of 3,040 lb on 57,760 lb credited as "five and a quarter per cent,"
  and 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26%, matching that ledger language almost exactly.
Both figures are correct; they are simply computed on different bases, and both appear, unlabeled,
in different sources — a likely source of some of the record's apparent numeric conflicts.

**C4.**
(a) Invoice total: **$77.93** (Ammon & Sons, 14 April 1897, reproduced in facsimile by the firm's
own 1949 history — the primary document, which I prefer over a 1958 newspaper's paraphrase of
"$77.39").
(b) The invoice bought **six dozen (72) pipettes** at $0.35 each (72 × $0.35 = $25.20, matching the
invoice line exactly — this arithmetic check is itself strong evidence against the competing "five
dozen (60)" figure found in Vaught's own bulletin introduction). Each was short by 17.6 − 16.72 =
**0.88 c.c.**

**C5.**
(a) The faulty glassware was in service from 1 May 1897 (fixed independently by the invoice/order
date and by Orra Keddie's own diary entry of that day) to 12 June 1923 (Strawn's circuit book) —
**just over 26 years** (26 years and about six weeks). The money calculation in C1–C3, however, runs
over **25 full delivery years**, because it counts only whole seasons and excludes the partial
fractions at each end (the station's opening months in 1896–97 before the new glass arrived, and the
partial 1923 season after it was replaced); Bertram Kyle's own clerk-computed figure of
1,600,000 lb/year × 25 years = 40,000,000 lb confirms 25 is the year-count actually used for the
official reckoning.

(b) This cannot be settled precisely. Buttermaker Hobart Sill, an eyewitness, gives the station's
first (partial) 1896 season as "about nine hundred thousand pounds." Clerk Bertram Kyle instead gives
"a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" for what he calls "that first year," before any separate
station account existed. The two may describe slightly different spans — Sill's explicit partial
calendar season (May–December 1896) versus Kyle's looser "first year" (possibly running to when the
permanent glass arrived the following spring, per Kyle's own account of borrowed glass "for its first
season or two") — but neither source dates its own period precisely enough to reconcile the two
figures with confidence, and neither is a contemporaneous document. I report both rather than pick
one.

## Section D — Logic

**D1.** The interval ran from **8 May to 10 July 1911 — nine weeks** (Ansel Keddie's own
contemporaneous log; independently confirmed by Roy Brant's family account and by county agent Orin
Falke's 1959 report, which gives the same start and end dates). A competing "six weeks" figure
appears in Wilbur Teague's 1948 memoir and Dr. Kepp's 1926 paper — but Kepp's own paper records that
this same point was raised as "an objection... from the floor" by an unnamed gentleman he does not
name, "possibly Teague"; if so, "six weeks" is really one source echoed once, not two independent
ones, and nine weeks (three independent sources, one of them contemporaneous) is the better-supported
figure.

Treating the interval as an experiment: during it, the station's own (defective) measure was broken
and a borrowed working one was substituted. The variable was the measuring instrument alone. Held
constant: the same tester, the same herds, the same pasture, and (within the nine weeks) essentially
the same season. The result: tests rose markedly to about the true level, then fell back to the
former level the instant the station's own glass resumed. This establishes something a static
calculation cannot: that the deficiency travelled with the *instrument*, not with the men, the cows,
or the season — ruling out theft (same tester, no different oversight, yet the numbers moved) and
feed/pasture theories (same herds and grass, yet the numbers moved) in one stroke. What it does
**not** establish: the precise size or mechanism of the instrument's fault, that it was specifically
a manufacturing defect in the pipettes rather than some other property of the borrowed glass, or that
the effect was constant over the full 26 years — those required the 1958 direct measurement.

**D2.** At least four independent reasons, drawn from different narrators and different kinds of
evidence:
1. The 1908 investigating committee's own fieldwork — they drew and sealed their own milk samples and
   tried the scales themselves, an investigation independent of any later equipment finding, and
   reported "no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter."
2. The 1911 substitution experiment (D1) — independent of any theory, a natural experiment recorded
   in Keddie's own contemporaneous log.
3. The station's own annual "station gain" ledger entries, kept by the bookkeeper every single year
   without exception, which show *more* cream reaching Larrow Green than the station's own books had
   credited to patrons — the opposite of what theft would produce, and a fact independent of the
   pipette measurement, sitting in plain sight in the Association's own books for decades.
4. Rosalie Cudd's independently-kept Alder Corners test records for the same four Hessel Bottom men,
   at an entirely separate factory on separate glassware, showing normal tests throughout — evidence
   independent of both the equipment and the internal ledger.
These are independent because they rest on different methods, different custodians, and (mostly)
different eras: valley fieldwork in 1908, an equipment substitution recorded in 1911, routine
bookkeeping kept continuously from the 1890s onward, and a rival factory's own separate books from
1915–1930. All four would still hold even if the 1958 measurement had never happened — none of them
depends on knowing the pipettes' exact shortfall.

Did the bookkeeper (Wilbur Teague) ever question the ledger entries that refute his own
theft-suspicion? Partially. He is on record as having raised the station-gain discrepancy directly
with manager Selby Vose once, in 1916, and was told to "let it alone." Yet at the 1925 hearing he
himself is recorded as putting the theft theory to the board obliquely — apparently without ever
connecting his own 1916 discovery of a *surplus* (which argues against theft) to the theory he later
advanced. Clerk Bertram Kyle's blanket characterization that Teague "never asked anybody anything in
forty years" sits a little oddly against this one documented exception, but a single private
exchange with the manager a decade before Kyle's own closer acquaintance with the office is not
necessarily something Kyle would have known about.

**D3.** Yes, on the best evidence, manager Selby Vose knew the tests ran low well before the 1923
condemnation. Wilbur Teague raised the station-gain discrepancy with him directly in 1916 and was
explicitly told to drop it — direct, unambiguous knowledge, seven years before the glassware was
condemned. Separately, the "station gain" line appeared on every annual station-account sheet that
passed under his signature for years; clerk Bertram Kyle's account suggests Vose's habitual practice
was to "read the totals and never read the lines above them... take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb
on the bottom figure, and sign" — which somewhat complicates (but does not overturn) the case, since
it suggests his day-to-day engagement with the substance of those particular lines may have been
perfunctory in most years. On balance: yes, he knew, at the latest from 1916, because he was told to
his face and chose institutional silence over investigation.

## Section E — Contradictions

*Carried forward (previously catalogued, not re-derived here): the Selby Vose son/nephew split; the
$3,500/$3,800 award figures; the 12 June/12 July 1923 condemnation-date split; the 44 vs. 40 million
lb / 3.16 vs. 3.61 average split; the curator's transposed exhibit labels; the test-book transfer
date; the "nine weeks"/"six weeks" split; the tone of the 1919 circular; the crate/loft detail; the

1891/1892/1893 date; Emmet Brant's ledger start date; the Alder Corners founding date; Rosalie Cudd's
niece/daughter correction; the pipette count (72 vs. 60) and survivor count (9 vs. 11); the 16.72 vs.
16.27 c.c. pipette-delivery figures and their downstream shortfall totals; the 1897/1898 glass-in-use
date within Bulletin 471 itself; the test-gap figure (0.19/0.17/0.21); the Bulletin 214 misquote;
Keddie's private belief vs. public silence; the 1919 circular's split reception; Rundle's
self-retraction; Grigg's self-contradicting sampling claim. I resolve the ones with new evidence from
r17–r24 below rather than repeat the earlier reasoning.*

**New contradictions found in r17–r24:**

1. **Ansel Keddie's start year at the station.** Hazel Brant (r17) says "ninety-five"; the station's
   own opening date (established via the county agent's report, the test book itself, and multiple
   earlier sources) is 4 May **1896**. Hazel was not yet born and is repeating secondhand family
   lore; the documentary date stands.

2. **Emmet Brant's private ledger start date — now a four-way spread.** 1 January 1897 (Frick's 1925
   exhibit list, and independently Duncan Keddie's 1968 family notes — two independent sources
   agreeing); 1 January 1898 (Roy Brant's own 1955 account of his father's book); "nineteen hundred"
   (Hazel Brant, r17, third-generation retelling). The two independent 1897 sources — a contemporary
   legal exhibit list and a careful, correction-minded family record — outweigh Roy's own memory and
   Hazel's later drift; I take this as a case of progressive generational date-creep (1897 → 1898 →
   1900 as the story passed from document to son to granddaughter).

3. **Orra Brant's relationship to Emmet — full or half sibling.** Roy Brant (r09, Emmet's own son)
   states explicitly they were full siblings, "same mother and father," and pre-emptively denies "any
   story with a stepmother in it." His own daughter Hazel (r17) tells exactly that denied story: Orra
   as a half-sister "by his father's second wife." I resolve in favor of full siblings — Roy's account
   is first-hand and was framed specifically to rule out this exact claim — treating Hazel's version
   as the very piece of drifted gossip her father warned against.

4. **Orra Keddie's birth year — a self-contradiction within a single document.** Duncan Keddie's own
   1968 notes (r18) state his mother was "born in eighteen sixty-eight" but also that she "was
   eighty-two when she died in 1948" — arithmetically requiring an 1866 birth year. The editor's
   preface to Orra's own diary (r24) independently gives 1866. Two independent lines (the diary
   editor's figure, and Duncan's own age-at-death arithmetic) both point to 1866; "1868" is simply an
   error Duncan made elsewhere in the same document.

5. **Number of families warned by the 1919 circular.** Nine (Hazel Brant's explicit count, "nine
   families, six farms," matching the baseline elsewhere) vs. eleven (Orra Keddie's own diary, the
   closest possible witness, being married to the man who delivered them). This is a genuine,
   unresolved conflict between a careful family tally and the single closest contemporaneous witness;
   I do not think the sources settle it.

6. **Number of pipettes purchased — resolved.** Six dozen (72), confirmed by the actual 1897 invoice
   reproduced in facsimile (with an exact arithmetic check: 72 × $0.35 = $25.20) against Vaught's

   dozen," but he explicitly signals his account is coloured by modern retellings ("That crate is the
   whole thing, they tell me now") — I treat his figure as an echo of the (erroneous) published
   bulletin, not an independent memory, so this is really one error repeated, not two.

7. **Number of pipettes surviving in the crate — now two against two, but not equally weighted.**

   article (it also misidentifies Ivy as Ansel's "great-niece" and Rosalie Cudd as "niece"), and may

   nine, from the bulletin's own direct measurement report.

8. **Ammon & Sons invoice total — resolved.** $77.93, from the actual invoice reproduced by the

   transposition). The primary document wins outright.

9. **Condemnation date — a third wrong variant appears.** Bertram Kyle (r23) states the state
   inspector came "in twenty-two" (1922), a third date alongside the established "12 July 1923"
   secondary error. Neither agrees with the other, which further weakens confidence in any
   memory-based account against Strawn's own circuit book (12 June 1923).

10. **Award date/year — a fourth account drifts.** Kyle (r23) says the award "was in twenty-four"

11. **Selby Vose's start as manager.** Established elsewhere as 1913; Kyle (r23) says 1911. I cannot

12. **First-season milk volume — likely different periods, not cleanly comparable.** Sill's "about

13. **Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant — resolved** (see B2): second cousins, against Cleve

14. **Vose–Keddie family connection — resolved** (see B4): none, against Cleve Oram's vague claim of

15. **What Keddie privately believed — two mutually exclusive accounts.** Alonzo Frick's 1946

    *patrons* were at fault (the water theory) but stayed silent out of loyalty. Duncan Keddie's own

## Section F — Theory

**F1.** The shortfall was produced by a single, purely physical defect: the entire lot of milk-test
pipettes bought new for the station in 1897 was blown to a nominal mark of 17.6 c.c. but actually
delivered only about 16.72 c.c. — a consistent 5% ("one part in twenty") shortfall, almost certainly
from an unnoticed mold error at the glass works. Because a butterfat test reads the fat percentage
directly off the graduated neck of a bottle charged by that exact pipette, and because — as
Professor Vaught's own bulletin puts it — "the pipette is the standard," with no independent check
possible from inside the plant, every single test made with that glass came out proportionally low,
every time, for as long as the glass was used.

This explains all the required features. **Size:** a fixed 5% instrument error, translating to the
observed ~0.19-point gap given the valley's normal ~3.80% milk. **Why that size:** it is exactly the
shortfall measured directly off the surviving pipettes in 1958 (16.72 vs. 17.6 c.c.), with no other
free parameter. **Why it began when it did:** the very day this specific lot went into service,
1 May 1897 — fixed independently by the invoice, by the bulletin's own concluding paragraph, and by
Orra Keddie's diary entry of that date. **Why it ended when it did:** 12 June 1923, when state
inspector Merle Strawn, on an ordinary circuit visit, tested the pipettes against his own calibrated
burette, found the defect, and had the lot replaced — after which the recorded test immediately rose
and stayed at the true level. **Why nothing at the station or on the farms had to change:** the
defect lived entirely inside the measuring instrument, not in the milk, the cows, the men, the
scales, or the acid; the 1911 nine-week natural experiment (Section D1) had already shown this
directly — identical herds, identical tester, identical season, and only the glass changed, yet the
readings moved with the glass alone.

**The three competing theories, their advocates, and what defeats each:**
1. *Tester theft* (Ansel Keddie skimming cream and under-reporting) — advanced obliquely at the 1925
   hearing by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague. Defeated by the 1911 substitution experiment (same tester, no
   change in oversight, yet the figures moved with the equipment); by the station's own annual
   "station gain" ledger entries, which every single year show *more* cream reaching Larrow Green
   than the station's books had credited — the opposite of what theft produces; and by Alonzo Frick's
   own eyewitness judgment at the hearing that the testers were "not hiding their hands."
2. *Patrons watering their milk* — the Association's official 1919 position, per manager Selby Vose's
   circular. Defeated by state inspector Merle Strawn's own lactometer tests at every visit from 1908
   onward, which never once found added water; and by Rosalie Cudd's independent Alder Corners
   records for the same four Hessel Bottom men, showing entirely normal tests (~3.80) at a separate
   factory on separate glassware during the very years the water theory was current.
3. *Wet Hessel Bottom hay/pasture* — the 1908 committee (Nyle Grigg, chairman) and later Dr. Roland
   Kepp's 1926 paper. Defeated by the 1911 experiment again (identical herds and pasture, yet the
   reading changed with the glass); by the gap's flat, non-seasonal character (a genuine feed effect
   should vary with season, and multiple narrators describe the 0.19-point gap as constant year-round);
   and by the college's own relevant feeding trial, Bulletin No. 214, whose actual finding (at most
   0.05 points in winter, none in summer) is four times too small to explain the observed gap — a
   finding both Grigg and Kepp separately misquoted upward to support their own theory.

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware?** No. Frick's own detailed exhibit list
names only four items, with no mention of any crate of pipettes, and multiple witnesses (Cleve Oram,
Duncan Keddie) independently note the crate sat in the loft directly above the room where two of the
three sessions were held, unexamined and undiscussed throughout.

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?** This is one of the record's real, unresolved
contradictions (see Section E, item 15): Alonzo Frick reports Keddie privately confided he believed
the *patrons* were at fault, while Keddie's own son Duncan reports him privately suspecting
*something in the glass* as early as 1911. Publicly, in both accounts, Keddie said nothing and
advanced no theory of his own.

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

Between 1897 and 1923, the Ostrey Hollow skimming station used a lot of milk-test pipettes
manufactured slightly short — delivering about 16.72 cc against a 17.6 cc mark, a fixed 5%
shortfall. Because a butterfat test is read directly off the pipette's own charge, this produced a
consistent, undetectable low reading for 26 years, denying the Hollow's patrons roughly 76,000 lb of
butterfat they had actually delivered. Contemporaries blamed the patrons (watering), the tester
(theft), or wet pasture instead, and a 1925 arbitration dismissed tester Ansel Keddie without ever
naming a cause. The truth surfaced only in 1958, when his granddaughter found the discarded,
condemned pipettes in the station's own loft and measured them against a calibrated burette. It took
eight separate records — ledgers, letters, a hearing chairman's memoir, an inspector's notebook, a
diary, an invoice, and a scientific bulletin — because no single witness ever held both the equipment
and the accounts at once.
