# Retention notes — retellings 1 through 16 (Ostrey Hollow / Ansel Keddie story cycle)

All sixteen retellings concern the same central historical episode: the Ostrey Hollow skimming
station of the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association, tester Ansel Keddie, a decades-long
shortfall in recorded butterfat tests, the 1925 board of arbitration, Keddie's dismissal, and the
1958/1959 scientific vindication showing the cause was miscalibrated equipment, not theft or fraud.
This file carries forward everything from notes-after-r08 (retellings 1–8) unchanged/expanded, and
adds everything learned from retellings 9–16.

## Cast of people (with relationships) — carried forward, updated

- **Ansel Keddie** — tester at Ostrey Hollow skimming station from its opening (4 May 1896) until
  dismissed 30 April 1925. Kept the station test book in his own hand for 29 seasons (1896–1924).
  Husband of Orra Keddie. Married into the Brant family (Orra is Emmet Brant's sister — per r09,
  full brother and sister, same mother and father, "whatever you may hear from people who like a
  story with a stepmother in it"). Died in the 1930s (r01 says "died in the thirties"; r04 gives
  exact year 1934). Father of Duncan Keddie; grandfather of Ivy Keddie.
  **NEW (r13, Frick's 1946 recollection):** In the corridor at Ashlin during the 1925 hearing,
  Keddie privately told Frick (the arbitration chairman) that he had always thought the *patrons*
  were at fault (i.e., privately believed the water/watering-milk theory) but would not say so in
  the hearing room, out of thirty years' loyalty to his neighbors. Frick: "it has been said since
  that he went to his grave accusing nobody, which is true, and that he suspected nobody, which is
  not." This contradicts the general impression (elsewhere) that Keddie held no theory of his own.
- **Orra Keddie** — Ansel's wife, Emmet Brant's full sister (r09, explicit), first cousin of "old
  Mrs. Vose." After the 1919 circular, she and her brother Emmet never spoke again; she'd walk to
  Larrow Green rather than pass his lane. Outlived Ansel "a good while."
- **Emmet Brant** — largest patron on Hessel Bottom (most cows, most hay ground, hauled the most
  milk); per r09, born **1854**, died **1929**; kept a private milk-weight ledger from **1 January
  1898** (his son Roy Brant's account, r09) — but Frick's 1925 exhibit list (r13) dates the same
  book as "begun **1 January 1897**" — a one-year discrepancy between Roy Brant's own account and
  the official arbitration exhibit list. Orra's brother; stopped speaking to the Keddies after the
  1919 circular, not over the watering accusation itself but because Ansel (who kept the test book)
  "ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not" (r09).
- **Roy Brant** — Emmet Brant's son, farmer at Hessel Bottom, born **1888** (67 years old in 1955);
  narrator of r09 (an account for the county farm bureau's local-history collection, written in
  longhand, winter 1955, with a postscript added 1958). Sixty-odd acres of Hessel Bottom's 160,
  thirty-eight cows in a good year — the largest of the six farms on the Bottom. Continued his
  father's milk ledger after 1929 "in the same book until it was filled and then in another."
  First cousin of Duncan Keddie; his daughter Hazel is Duncan's daughter Ivy's second cousin.
  Did NOT haul over Redlow Ridge to the cheese factory (unlike four other Bottom farms) — regrets
  this decision as costing money, made partly out of pride ("the biggest herd is the one that
  cannot afford to be talked about"). Inherited the farm weigh book 1929; lent it to Ivy Keddie in
  1958 (his own postscript, dated 1958, confirms this loan directly — "Duncan's girl Ivy came out
  to the place this year and asked whether she might borrow the book... I let her have it... It
  comes back to me when she is through, and after me it is Hazel's"); book later passed to Hazel
  Brant in 1962 (per notes-after-r08).
- **Hazel Brant** — Roy Brant's daughter; received the Brant farm weigh book in 1962; second cousin
  of Ivy Keddie; the 1958 researcher who visited Vira Toland in 1958 (r01) was "Hazel Brant's first
  cousin once removed."
- **Duncan Keddie** — Ansel's son, Ivy's father, taught school at Ashlin; Roy Brant's first cousin.
- **Ivy Keddie** — Ansel Keddie's granddaughter (Duncan's daughter). Gave a talk "What the Crate
  Held" to the Ordell County Historical Society at Ashlin in 1959 (r04), based on 1958 research.
  Found the crate of condemned pipettes February 1958. Measured the surviving pipettes against a
  calibrated burette at Farland, 9 March 1958. Her findings became State Agricultural College
  Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958), co-written with Professor Leland Vaught ("Professor Vaught's
  wording and mine together"). **NEW (r09):** borrowed her uncle-once-removed Roy Brant's farm
  ledger in 1958 for her research, returned it the same year. **NEW (r10):** also corresponded in
  1958 with Rosalie Cudd of Alder Corners Cheese Company (Vessey County), who sent her independent
  1915–1930 test figures for the four Hessel Bottom men who hauled over Redlow Ridge. **NEW (r12
  footnote, 1959 reprint):** described there as "second cousin to Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom"
  (matches r09's own reckoning) and credited with finding "eleven pipettes" surviving in the
  crate — CONFLICTS with r07 Item 5 and r11's own Section III, which both say **nine** pipettes
  survived.
- **Gideon Vose** — founding president of the Association (elected 1889, held office until 1911 —
  22 years). Personally drafted the by-laws, including Article VII (the fat-test payment rule).
  Died 1917. Kept a Jersey herd at the "home place." **Date of Article VII's adoption now has
  THREE conflicting versions across sources:** 1891 (r12, Dell Rundle's pamphlet: "Article VII
  dates from 1891, the year the churn was set up"); 1892 (notes-after-r08 baseline, from r02/r07);
  1893 (r15, Nyle Grigg's 1919 letter: "Payment by test came in with the 1893 meeting, as I have
  always understood it").
- **Selby Vose** — manager of the Association 1913–1938. **Relationship to Gideon Vose now split
  evenly:** r01 (Vira Toland) and **r12 (Dell Rundle's 1939 pamphlet: "His nephew Selby Vose,
  afterward manager of the Association")** both call him Gideon's **nephew**; r02, r05, and r08
  all call him Gideon's **son**. This is now a real, roughly-balanced conflict (2 sources nephew,
  3 sources son), not simply an error in one oral account. Signed the 1919 "water in the cans"
  circular; defended it and the 1925 settlement in his 1926 annual address (r08); his own family
  Jersey herd tested 4.56 in 1910.
- **Vira Toland** — narrator of r01, born 1912, interviewed 1979.
- **Effie Loomis** — Association secretary 1901–1940; wrote "Notes toward a history" 1941 (r02).
- **Wilbur Teague** — Association bookkeeper 1905–1945; wrote unpublished memoir "The Books of the
  Association" (Ch. 9, winter 1948, r05). Gave the outlier figures of 44,000,000 lb total milk and
  3.16 average test (r05) — **now corroborated by two additional, independent sources**: Dell
  Rundle's 1939/1959 pamphlet (r12) also states "The station took in, over the years complained
  of, forty-four million pounds of milk" (44,000,000 lb, matching Teague exactly), and Alonzo
  Frick's 1946 recollection (r13) independently states "an average test of 3.16" for the whole
  period (matching Teague's 3.16 exactly). This substantially strengthens the case that 3.16 /
  44,000,000 lb were real, circulating official-ish figures in the valley — not simply an old
  man's memory slip in 1948 — even though they still conflict with the 40,000,000 lb / 3.61–3.80
  figures given by Effie Loomis, Ivy Keddie, and the curator's notes. **NEW (r13):** Teague, at
  the 1925 hearing, put the theft-suspicion theory to the board "in the way an accountant puts a
  thing he wants said without saying it," per Frick.
- **Hobart Sill** — buttermaker at Larrow Green, kept the churn book and cream receiving book.
  **NEW, major (r14):** full retelling is his own 1934 retirement remarks. Came to Larrow Green
  in **1893** (41 years' service, retiring 1934, age 64). Personally supplied the original
  glassware used to open the Ostrey Hollow station in May 1896 ("I went to my own bench in this
  room and I made up an outfit for them out of my own glass... it went up the valley on the wagon
  and they opened on it. Their own came the spring after and they sent mine back to me, and one
  piece of it was chipped"). States Hollow cream "ran heavy" against the station's own paperwork
  every year without exception ("more fat in the cans than the papers said... Always more. Never
  less") — an eyewitness description of the same phenomenon documented in the "station gain"
  ledger entries. Personally believes the water/dishonesty theory is correct and says so bluntly:
  "You cannot tell me nine families all got honest in one summer... There was water in those
  cans and I said so at the time." Approved of Selby Vose's 1919 circular ("I thought well of him
  for it. It was the only plain thing that office ever did"). Gives his own "true" test estimates:
  Larrow Green's own patrons at **3.78**, and the Hollow's "real" test as **3.85** ("no better") —
  both figures diverge from the ~3.80 "true" consensus value established elsewhere. Gives
  butterfat price as **27 cents/lb** "through those years" (vs. 24–25 cents elsewhere). States the
  station's first (partial) season, May–Dec 1896, took in about **900,000 lb** of milk. Recalls
  the first Hollow complaint in **1902** ("a man in here shouting that his milk tested under his
  brother-in-law's down the creek").
- **Merle Strawn** — state dairy inspector, Ordell circuit 1907–1936. Condemned the Ostrey Hollow
  pipettes 12 June 1923 per his own circuit book (r06/r07 Item 9) — see condemnation-date conflict
  below.
- **Rosalie Cudd** — runs the Alder Corners Cheese Company (Vessey County), across Redlow Ridge.
  **CORRECTION/CONFLICT (r10, her own 1958 letter to Ivy Keddie):** she is explicitly **Jerome
  Cudd's daughter** ("My father, Jerome Cudd, built this factory in nineteen thirteen..."), NOT
  his niece as notes-after-r08 had inferred from the curator's catalog language. She was 12 years
  old when the factory's first vat was set (implying she was born around 1901, if the factory
  opened 1913 per her own account — or around 1902 if it opened 1914 per r09/r12, see conflict
  below). Took over the factory in spring 1941 when Jerome Cudd died (between the setting and
  cutting of a vat); has run it 17 years as of her 1958 letter (consistent with 1941→1958). Copied
  out, at Ivy's request, the factory's own independently-kept 1919-season test figures (and later,
  per her letter, the full 1915–1930 run of 16 seasons) for the four Hessel Bottom men who hauled
  there in summer — her father's own book, tested on his own glassware, never compared to the
  Ostrey Hollow station's figures ("it never entered his head... if you had suggested it to my
  father he would have laughed at you"). States plainly that watered milk cannot be hidden from a
  cheesemaker ("Cheese is a witness... it tells him the next morning, not next year") and that in
  16 summers her father never once suspected or docked those four men.
- **Jerome Cudd** — Rosalie's father, built/founded the Alder Corners Cheese Company. **Founding
  date now conflicting: 1913 (r10, his own daughter's letter, presumably most authoritative) vs.
  1914 (r09, Roy Brant's account: "The factory over there had opened in 1914"; and r12, Rundle's
  pamphlet: "in 1914 the Alder Corners Cheese Company was opened over Redlow Ridge... by Jerome
  Cudd").** Died March 1941, "between the setting and the cutting of a vat." Kept his own test
  book on his own bench, in a little room off the intake, never comparing figures with Ostrey
  Hollow. Believed the "wet hay" gossip about Hessel Bottom was "nonsense" and wrote so in his own
  book ("Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge" — his 1919 season-end entry,
  quoted in full below).
- **Halbert Nym** — curator, Ordell County Historical Rooms, wrote 1972 accession notes (r07).
- **Alonzo Frick** — attorney of Ashlin, chairman of the 1925 board of arbitration.
  **NEW, major (r13):** full retelling is his own 1946 recollection, written at age 74 for the
  Ordell County Bar Association yearbook (40 years at the bar, handled "two homicides"). Chosen
  as chairman "because I was the one of the three who kept no cow" (board of three; other two
  members unnamed). Held three sessions: first at the Association's Ashlin office, 17 March 1925;
  second and third at Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's own weigh room, at his own insistence,
  so the board could see the plant firsthand. Personally watched the scales tried and the test
  made twice, and though he "did not understand the test then and do not understand it now" (being
  a lawyer, not a chemist), judged the testers were "not hiding their hands." Recorded the board
  heard three explanations at the hearing: (1) water, put by Selby Vose "without heat and without
  proof"; (2) tester theft, put obliquely by Wilbur Teague; (3) wet hay, via the 1908 committee
  report read into the record. States the patrons' own reckoning of their loss was **sixty-seven
  thousand pounds of butter fat** — yet another distinct figure alongside 76,000 lb (computed true
  shortfall), $19,000 (its dollar value), and $19,600 (Rundle's pamphlet figure, r12). Confirms
  award date **4 May 1925** and dismissal date **30 April 1925** ("four days before we made our
  award"). Reprints his own exhibit list from the hearing (see Documents section below) — only
  four items, no mention of any crate of pipettes, consistent with Effie Loomis's earlier-noted
  observation that the crate was never discussed at the hearing.
- **Dell Rundle** — schoolteacher at Larrow Green; **NEW, r12.** Wrote the 1939 jubilee pamphlet
  "The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years," reissued 1959 with one footnote and an unaltered
  closing/retraction note added while the original was in press. Compiled the pamphlet from "the
  recollections of old members and two winters of asking questions at the store." Personally
  witnessed the ledger's "station gain" entry while the 1939 pamphlet was being typeset and used
  it to retract, in print, his own Chapter IV theft narrative ("Cream stolen at the Hollow would
  have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my
  paragraph stand. I leave it as written and correct it here.") — an early (1939), independent,
  self-correcting instance of noticing the very physical evidence (cream arriving "heavy") that
  Bulletin 471 would formally explain nineteen years later, though Rundle drew no mechanical
  explanation of his own, merely retracted the theft claim.
- **Nyle Grigg** — farmer of Larrow Green, not a patron of Ostrey Hollow station (hauls to Larrow
  Green himself); chairman of the 1908 investigating committee. **NEW, major (r15):** full
  retelling is his own 1919 covering letter reprinting his 1908 report (with the full report text
  included) after the Hollow complaint was "reopened this season." States he had "no interest
  whatever in the answer" and was "abused... by men on both sides of it," offered as evidence of
  impartiality. His letter states the committee "sampled those herds through the whole of 1908...
  every month of that year" — **this directly contradicts the report's own text, reprinted in the
  same document, which states sampling was done "through January, February and March"** (winter
  only) — an internal self-contradiction within r15, and consistent with the curator's earlier-
  noted observation (notes-after-r08) that sampling was winter-only. Grigg's letter also claims
  Bulletin No. 214 "found the bog hay to depress the butter fat test by two tenths in the winter
  feeding" — **this contradicts the bulletin's own verbatim wording** (quoted identically in r04,
  r07 Item 12, and r11 Section VI: "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter") — a
  fourfold overstatement (0.20 claimed vs. 0.05 actual) that happens to conveniently support
  Grigg's own wet-hay theory.
- **Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S.** — veterinary surgeon of Ashlin, 30 years' practice. **NEW, major
  (r16).** Full retelling is his 1926 paper "The bog meadows and the milk," read before the
  Ordell County Farm Club, plus an appended floor discussion. Advances a more sophisticated
  version of the wet-hay theory: acid, lime-and-phosphate-poor bog soil produces sedge/rush
  herbage lacking minerals, forcing a fat-depleted cow to "rob her own frame" — general effect he
  says he has observed countywide as "a fifth of a point" (0.20). Gives the *Hessel Bottom-
  specific* historical gap as "twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21" — **matching Selby Vose's
  own disputed "rise of twenty-one hundredths" figure from his 1926 address (r08)** (delivered
  the same year), rather than the ~0.19 figure otherwise established as the true gap. **Also
  misquotes Bulletin No. 214 exactly as Grigg did**, saying it found the test depressed "by two
  tenths in the winter feeding" (0.20) — contradicting the bulletin's real wording (0.05) — the
  SECOND independent source to make this identical misquotation, both misquotes conveniently
  supporting the wet-hay theory. In the discussion, addresses "an objection" about the 1911
  anomaly, describing it as lasting **"six weeks"** (not "nine weeks" — matches Wilbur Teague's r05
  figure, against Keddie's own log/summary leaf and most other sources, which say nine weeks) and
  dismisses it as meaningless because it fell in flush season. Gives butterfat price as **"twenty-
  seven cents the pound through these years"** — matching Hobart Sill's r14 figure exactly, against
  24–25 cents from documentary sources. In discussion, when asked whether the trouble might be in
  the station itself, Kepp disclaims all competence to judge ("he had never in his life read a
  test and would not know how"), illustrating how the true (mechanical) cause went uninvestigated
  even by the era's relevant experts.
- **Leland Vaught** — professor of dairy husbandry, State Agricultural College at Farland.
  **NEW, major (r11).** Wrote the introduction and method sections of Bulletin No. 471 (October
  1958) — the scientific document that (jointly with Ivy Keddie) exonerated Keddie. Explains the
  test mechanism (pipette charge → acid digestion → spun in tester → fat column read directly off
  the graduated neck "in percent," with no independent check possible on the pipette itself, since
  "the pipette is the standard"). Sets out three properties of a short-pipette error: it is
  proportional (not additive — rich milk loses more absolute fat than poor milk, but herds still
  rank correctly against each other); it is precise/consistent, not scattered; and it is invisible
  from inside the plant (can only be caught by an external burette). **Contains major internal
  numeric conflicts (see Conflicts section) between his own Sections III/V and the bulletin's
  familiar concluding paragraph (Section VII), which repeats the standard 76,000 lb / "one part
  in twenty" / 1897 figures found elsewhere.**

## Timeline of events — carried forward, with new/updated entries

(All entries from notes-after-r08 stand; new information from r09–r16 is added/flagged below in

- **1854** — Emmet Brant born (r09).
- **1889** — Association organized at Larrow Green; per r12, farmers "met in the schoolhouse, in
  cold weather," and "somebody's horse got loose in the middle of it."
- **1891** — Creamery built at Larrow Green, churn set up (r02 baseline; r12 additionally dates
  Article VII's adoption to this same year — conflict, see above).
- **1892** OR **1891** OR **1893** — Article VII adopted (three-way date conflict, see Gideon Vose
  entry above).
- **1893** — Hobart Sill comes to Larrow Green creamery as buttermaker (r14); "no floor in this
  room but plank," cooled with creek ice from the pond.
- **1897 or 1898** — Emmet Brant's private weigh-book begun: **1 January 1898** per his son Roy
  Brant's own 1955 account (r09), but **1 January 1897** per Alonzo Frick's 1925 arbitration
  exhibit list (r13) — one-year discrepancy.
- **1901** — Rosalie Cudd born, approximately (implied: she was "a girl of twelve when the first
  vat was set" — see factory founding-date conflict, 1913 vs. 1914, below).
- **1902** — First public complaints from Ostrey Hollow that their milk tested low, corroborated
  independently now by r09 (Roy Brant: "The first men to say so out loud said it in 1902... one of
  them was my father"), r14 (Hobart Sill: "Nineteen two is the first I remember of it"), and r15
  (Grigg's letter: "From about 1902 there had been grumbling").
- **1908** — Investigating committee (chaired by **Nyle Grigg**, named explicitly in r09, r12, and
  as author of r15) samples Hessel Bottom herds "through January, February and March" per the
  report's own text (r15) — but Grigg's own 1919 covering letter claims sampling ran "through the
  whole of 1908... every month" — an internal contradiction within r15 itself. Full text of the
  1908 report now available (r15) — see Documents section.
- **1910** — Emmet Brant's ledger records "two hundred thousand pounds even" delivered, paid on a
  test of 3.42 (quoted before, r07 Item 4; now also quoted in full by Roy Brant himself, r09, who
  adds that his father "was pleased about the evenness of it; he wrote 'even' and underlined it").
  Butterfat price that year was **24 cents** per Roy Brant (r09); "over the whole stretch of years
  the complaint covers, one year with another, it came to about twenty-five cents" (matches
  earlier 25-cent figure) — but Hobart Sill (r14) and Dr. Kepp (r16) both instead say fat was worth
  **27 cents/lb "through those years"** — a persistent three-way price conflict (24, 25, 27 cents).
- **1911 (summer)** — the "nine weeks" anomaly (8 May – 10 July per Keddie's own log) during which
  a borrowed measure was used and tests rose to the true ~3.80 value. **Duration now disputed by
  THREE separate tellings of "six weeks" instead of nine**: Wilbur Teague's memoir (r05, previously
  noted) and now also Dr. Kepp's 1926 paper (r16: "in the summer of 1911 the testing at that
  station was broken off for six weeks... Six weeks of June and July"). Roy Brant's own account
  (r09) calls it simply "nine weeks in the summer of 1911," agreeing with Keddie's own log. Kepp's
  paper also records that this same anomaly was raised as "an objection" from the floor at his
  1926 Farm Club talk by an unnamed "gentleman" (possibly Teague), which Kepp dismissed as
  meaningless because it fell in flush season.
- **1913** OR **1914** — Alder Corners Cheese Company founded by Jerome Cudd across Redlow Ridge
  in Vessey County. **1913** per Rosalie Cudd's own letter (r10: "My father, Jerome Cudd, built
  this factory in nineteen thirteen"). **1914** per both Roy Brant (r09: "The factory over there
  had opened in 1914") and Dell Rundle's pamphlet (r12: "in 1914 the Alder Corners Cheese Company
  was opened"). Two sources for 1914 vs. one (the founder's own daughter) for 1913.
- **1915–1930** — Four Hessel Bottom farms haul milk over Redlow Ridge to Alder Corners each
  summer flush (roughly 1 June to mid-September), returning to Ostrey Hollow the rest of the year;
  did this for **sixteen seasons**, the last being **1930** (r10, Rosalie Cudd's letter, which
  gives the full year-by-year table — see Quantities section). Consistent with r09's account
  (Roy Brant: "From 1915, four of the Bottom men hauled their milk over Redlow Ridge... for sixteen
  seasons"). Roy Brant's own farm did not participate.
- **1916** — Wilbur Teague raises the station-gain discrepancy with Selby Vose (established
  earlier); told to let it alone.
- **1919** — Selby Vose's "water in the cans" circular sent to the nine Ostrey Hollow patron
  families. **New reactions recorded:** Roy Brant's father Emmet Brant "was one who never forgave
  it" and stopped speaking to the Keddies (not over the accusation itself, but because Ansel
  "ought to have stood up... and did not," r09). By contrast, Hobart Sill (r14) approved of the
  circular: "I thought well of him for it. It was the only plain thing that office ever did." This
  is a genuine split in contemporary valley opinion about the circular, not just a difference in
  narrative framing. Also in 1919: Rosalie Cudd's father Jerome Cudd records the four Hessel
  Bottom men's summer test figures at Alder Corners (3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82) with the marginal
  comment "Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge" (r10) — and this same year

  Hollow question having been reopened this season."

## Theories advanced for the shortfall — now with corroborating/dissenting voices identified

The four theories catalogued in notes-after-r08 stand; new retellings show who personally believed
which theory, deepening the picture of contemporaneous belief vs. the eventual scientific finding:

1. **Cream theft by Keddie personally** — folk explanation (Vira Toland, r01); implicitly supported
   by Wilbur Teague's testimony (r05, r13 confirms Teague put this theory to the board obliquely).
2. **Wet hay / bad pasture** — the 1908 committee (Grigg, chairman); reasserted and elaborated by
   Grigg himself in his 1919 covering letter (r15) and by Dr. Roland Kepp in his more sophisticated
   1926 mineral-deficiency paper (r16). **Both Grigg (r15) and Kepp (r16) independently misquote
   Bulletin No. 214 as finding a 0.20-point effect (actual bulletin text: 0.05 points) — a
   convenient, matching overstatement by the theory's two chief advocates**, contradicting Ivy
   Keddie's 1958 finding that the true feeding-trial effect was "four times too small" to explain
   the gap.
3. **Patrons adding water to the milk** — the Association's official 1919 theory (Selby Vose).
   **New corroborators identified:** Hobart Sill (r14, the Larrow Green buttermaker, believes this
   firmly and publicly) and — privately, per Frick's disclosure (r13) — **Ansel Keddie himself**,
   who never said so publicly but told Frick in confidence that he thought the patrons were at
   fault. Directly contradicted by Merle Strawn (never found added water in any inspection) and by
   Rosalie Cudd's independent Alder Corners test figures (r10), which show the same four Hessel
   Bottom men's milk testing normally (~3.80) at an entirely separate, unrelated cheese factory
   during the very years the water theory was current.
4. **Miscalibrated pipettes (the proven true cause)** — Ivy Keddie's 1958 investigation and
   Bulletin No. 471. **New nuance (r11):** the professor's own introductory sections of the
   bulletin contain internally inconsistent figures (see above) that do not fully agree with the
   bulletin's own famous concluding paragraph, muddying an otherwise clean "solved" narrative.
