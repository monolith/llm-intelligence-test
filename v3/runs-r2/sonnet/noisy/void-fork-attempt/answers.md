# Answers — Part 1 of 3 (Section A, Stories 1–4)

*Based on notes-after-r16 (retellings 1–16) and direct reading of retellings 17–24.*

## Section A — Reconstruction

There were eight underlying stories. I have partitioned the 24 retellings by episode rather than
by narrator, since most narrators touch several episodes. Each story below uses the corrected
(resolved) facts; the conflicts behind each resolution are catalogued in full in Section E.

### Story 1 — Founding the Association and Equipping the Station (1889–1897)

Farmers of the Larrow Valley organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association in
1889, meeting at the Larrow Green schoolhouse (r12: "somebody's horse got loose in the middle of
it"). Gideon Vose, who kept a Jersey herd, was elected founding president and personally drafted
the by-laws, serving until 1911. The main creamery was built at Larrow Green in 1891. Article
VII of the by-laws — the rule paying every patron by butterfat test rather than milk weight —
is the hinge of the entire history: under payment by weight, none of what followed would have
cost anyone money. Its adoption date cannot be settled: three sources give three different years
(1891, 1892, 1893), and the county agent's 1959 report (r22) discovered the Association's own
minute book for its early years had been lost in an office move, so no source can actually prove
a date — this is a genuine, permanent gap in the record, not a solvable conflict.

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles
up-valley, to serve Hessel Bottom's six farms. Buttermaker Hobart Sill personally supplied
borrowed glassware from his own bench so the station could open before its own equipment
arrived; it opened 4 May 1896. Ansel Keddie was hired as tester and separator man from opening
day. The station's own permanent outfit was ordered from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet: the supplier's
own invoice (reproduced in full, r20), dated 14 April 1897, itemizes six dozen (72) milk test
pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. at 35 cents each ($25.20), twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure,
a hand tester, and freight, totaling $77.93. This outfit went into service 1 May 1897, replacing
Sill's borrowed glass. Unknown to everyone, this specific lot of pipettes — probably from a
reground mold, per the supplier's own later theory (r20) — delivered only about 16.72 c.c.
against its 17.6 c.c. mark: a defect invisible to any check made from inside the plant, since
routine consistency checks would only confirm the pipettes agreed with each other, not with the
true standard. This flaw, present from day one, is the seed of everything that follows.

### Story 2 — The First Complaints and the 1908 Wet-Hay Investigation

From nearly the station's opening, Ostrey Hollow's recorded milk tests ran conspicuously lower
than milk tested anywhere else nearby — independently confirmed later at 0.19 points below true
value, year-round, in every season alike. The first public complaint is dated 1902 by three
independent narrators (Roy Brant, Hobart Sill, Nyle Grigg). Grumbling continued through the
decade, and in 1908 the Association appointed an investigating committee chaired by Nyle Grigg,
a Larrow Green farmer who hauled to Larrow Green itself and was not an Ostrey Hollow patron —
offered by himself as proof of his impartiality. The committee visited all six Hessel Bottom
farms, sampled the herds, and tested both the station's and the patrons' own scales against a
known weight. Their full report (reprinted by Grigg in 1919, r15) found "no dishonesty of any
kind, in any quarter" and concluded the wet, sedge-and-rush hay of the bog bottom was thinning
the cream — recommending remedy through the land, not the station. The report's own text says
sampling ran only "through January, February and March" (winter), but Grigg's own 1919 covering
letter claims it ran all twelve months — a contradiction within his own reprinted document. In
1919, citing the reopened "Ostrey Hollow question," Grigg republished the report with his letter,
which also misquoted the college's actual Bulletin No. 214 finding (a genuine 0.05-point winter
effect) as a much larger 0.20-point effect — a misquotation later independently repeated by
veterinarian Dr. Roland Kepp in his own 1926 paper advancing a more sophisticated
mineral-deficiency version of the same wet-hay theory. Both misquotations, from the theory's two
chief advocates, happened to inflate the case for wet hay by roughly fourfold.

### Story 3 — The 1911 Borrowed-Measure Interval

On 8 May 1911 the station's own measuring glass broke, and for nine weeks — until 10 July 1911 —
Keddie used a measure borrowed from elsewhere. Keddie's own contemporaneous log entry records
this, and during those nine weeks the station's tests rose to the true value (~3.80), then fell
back to the old low level the instant the station's repaired/own glass returned to use. Keddie
himself wrote no accusation in his book, only a question mark. His son Duncan later recalled
(r18) that his father came home that summer saying "it was something in the glass" but "never
dared put it in the book," lacking anything but suspicion to set against an outfit he had been
trained to trust. This episode is the single clearest natural experiment in the whole record:
with the herds, pasture, season (partly), tester, and patrons all held constant, and only the
measuring instrument changed, the results tracked the instrument exactly — evidence, available
in real time in 1911, that the fault lay in the glass, forty-seven years before anyone acted on
it. At least one contemporary (an unnamed "gentleman," possibly bookkeeper Wilbur Teague) raised
this same anomaly from the floor at Kepp's 1926 lecture as an objection to the wet-hay theory;
Kepp dismissed it as meaningless because it fell in flush season — a dismissal now shown, with
hindsight, to have waved away the best evidence then available.

### Story 4 — The 1919 Circular and the Valley's Rift

Having been told directly by his own bookkeeper Wilbur Teague in 1916 that the cream arriving at
Larrow Green consistently outweighed what the station credited to patrons — and told to "let it
alone" — Association manager Selby Vose (Gideon Vose's son, per the best evidence: see Section
B3) circulated a warning letter in 1919 to the Ostrey Hollow patron families, accusing them of
adding water to their milk. Ansel Keddie, who kept the test book but did not write the letter,
refused to mail it or leave it on the station platform; he delivered it himself, in person, to
every family, standing at each door while the recipient read it — an act his son later called
"the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had," since it associated Keddie
personally with an accusation he had not made and (per Alonzo Frick's later account) privately
did not fully share. Orra Keddie's own diary, written days later, records her husband coming home
at eleven at night "with his hat still on" after delivering the letters personally. Reaction split
sharply along family and allegiance lines: Orra's brother Emmet Brant, the largest and longest-
standing patron, never forgave it and cut off all contact with the Keddies for the rest of his
life — not primarily over the accusation itself but because he felt Ansel should have publicly
refused to carry it and had not. By contrast, Larrow Green buttermaker Hobart Sill approved of
the circular outright ("I thought well of him for it"), believing the water theory correct. No
patron ever answered the circular in writing — in Roy Brant's words, "in that valley, a shout."

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### Story 5 — Strawn's 1923 Condemnation of the Glassware

On a routine circuit visit, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn (who served the Ordell circuit
1907–1936 and, per his son-in-law's testimony in r18, was kin to nobody in the valley on any
side) tested the Ostrey Hollow station's milk pipettes against his own calibrated burette. His
own circuit book, corroborated by a curator's photostat of the same document, dates this 12 June
1923. He found the pipettes would not deliver their marked 17.6 c.c. and condemned the entire
crate on the spot, directing the manager to order new glass — a routine equipment inspection,
unconnected in his mind or anyone else's to the twenty-six-year-old patron dispute. The
Association wrote to Ammon & Sons on 28 June 1923; the supplier's reply of 2 July 1923
(reproduced verbatim, r20) entered the replacement order but admitted "our books of the nineties
are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name" — a firm's honest but
unhelpful non-answer to a 26-year customer. New glass went into service that July, and from 1924
the station's recorded average test rose and stayed at a materially higher level. The condemned
crate was carried up into the loft above the station's intake room and left there, forgotten,
under a tarpaulin, for the next thirty-five years — a detail vividly confirmed by cream hauler's
son Cleve Oram (r21), who had personally handled the crate as a boy hunting for wire and a hay
fork, and by Hazel Brant (r17), who describes it sitting there "for most of my life without one
soul thinking about it." Nobody in 1923 connected the condemnation to the long-standing shortfall
complaint; the crate was simply old equipment being thrown out.

### Story 6 — The 1925 Board of Arbitration and Keddie's Dismissal

The Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned in January 1925. A three-man board of arbitration was formed,
chaired by Ashlin attorney Alonzo Frick, chosen (by his own account, r13) "because I was the one
of the three who kept no cow." The board held three sessions: the first at the Association's
Ashlin office on 17 March 1925, and — at Frick's own insistence, so the board could see the plant
firsthand — the second and third at the station itself, in its own weigh room, with a crowd of
onlookers (including a young Cleve Oram) standing outside on the platform in the rain. Frick's own
reprinted exhibit list names exactly four documents placed before the board: the station's test
book in Keddie's hand, the station's receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book (lent for
the purpose), and Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook. No mention of any crate or glassware appears
anywhere in the exhibit list, and the crate itself sat, unexamined, in the loft directly above two
of the three sessions. Three theories were argued: water added by patrons (put forward without
proof by manager Selby Vose), tester theft (put obliquely by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague), and wet
hay (via the 1908 report, read into the record). The board found no cause established and awarded
the patrons $3,800 "without admission" on 4 May 1925. Ansel Keddie was dismissed 30 April 1925 —
four days before the award — after twenty-nine seasons at the station. He went home that evening
at his ordinary hour, hung up his apron, and asked what there was for supper (Orra's diary). He
took up carpentry, worked on his own farm's long-neglected cow shed, and — per his son — "began to
whistle, which he never did when he was paid." He died in 1934, the imputation of the affair still
on him; his widow Orra outlived him to 1948, fourteen years short of his vindication.

### Story 7 — The Alder Corners Comparison Across Redlow Ridge

From 1915 to 1930 — sixteen consecutive summer seasons — four of Hessel Bottom's six farms hauled
their milk over Redlow Ridge each summer flush to the Alder Corners Cheese Company in neighboring
Vessey County, built by Jerome Cudd (founding date disputed: his own daughter's letter says 1913,
two other sources say 1914), returning to the Ostrey Hollow station the rest of each year. Cudd
kept his own independent test book on his own bench, tested on his own glassware, and never once
compared his figures against the Ostrey Hollow station's — "it never entered his head," his
daughter Rosalie later wrote. His 1919 season-end entry recorded the four men's milk testing
3.79, 3.81, 3.78, and 3.82 — entirely normal — with the marginal note "Good milk off wet ground,
whatever they say over the ridge." When Rosalie Cudd, who succeeded her father in 1941, corresponded
with Ivy Keddie in 1958, she sent a complete year-by-year table for all sixteen seasons (1915–1930),
averaging about 3.80 throughout, with no year showing any depression. This is the single most
important piece of external corroboration in the whole record: the same men's milk, from the same
wet Hessel Bottom pasture, tested normally at an entirely separate, unrelated factory using
different equipment, during the very years the water-theory and wet-hay-theory were both current —
independently proving both that the men were not watering their milk and that the wet ground was
not the cause, since neither factor changed when the men crossed the ridge, only the test result did.

### Story 8 — Ivy Keddie's 1958 Investigation and Vindication

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie — Ansel Keddie's granddaughter, a graduate student in dairy husbandry
at the State Agricultural College at Farland — climbed a ladder into the loft above the old
station's intake room and found the condemned 1923 crate, undisturbed since it was put there. She
borrowed her second cousin Roy Brant's weigh book that same year (returned promptly) and
corresponded with Rosalie Cudd, who supplied the Alder Corners figures. On 9 March 1958, at
Farland, she measured the nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette: each was blown
17.6 c.c. on the stem but delivered a mean of about 16.72 c.c. — short by 0.88 c.c., or one part
in twenty (5%) — the precise, consistent, invisible-from-inside defect that explained the entire
0.19-point historical gap. Her findings, co-written with Professor Leland Vaught, appeared as
State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 471 in October 1958, concluding flatly: "No milk was
watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there... the difference — seventy-six thousand
pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station
gain." (The bulletin's own introductory sections contain internally inconsistent figures that
Vaught himself flags as irreconcilable — see Sections C and E.) The Ashlin Chronicle covered the
story on its front page 3 October 1958; the county agricultural agent filed a careful, self-
correcting report in 1959; and Ivy delivered a talk, "What the Crate Held," to the Ordell County
Historical Society at Ashlin in 1959. The test book, the Brant weigh book, Strawn's notebooks, the
Alder Corners book, and the crate itself were eventually all deposited across three different
local historical collections by the 1960s and 70s — the family's own record-keeping, per Duncan
Keddie's 1968–70 notes, scattered but preserved. Ansel Keddie's name was formally cleared
twenty-four years after his death; his widow did not live to see it.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**

Granddaughter. Ivy is the daughter of Duncan Keddie, who is Ansel's son — a direct, two-generation
line stated plainly and repeatedly (Duncan's own 1968 family notes, r18: "My daughter is Ivy, born
in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter"). Two sources instead call her Ansel's
"great-niece": the Ashlin Chronicle's 1958 front page (r19) and the county agent's original 1959
report draft (r22). I resolve this as granddaughter, for two reasons. First, the county agent's
own report contains its own correction: in an appended note, added after further inquiry, Falke
writes "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the
great-niece; the error on page two is mine" — meaning one of the two "great-niece" sources is a
self-retracted error by its own author. Second, Duncan Keddie's account is a father's first-person
statement about his own daughter and his own father, the closest possible standing to know, and he
explicitly says he is writing partly because he has "already twice seen it printed otherwise." A
verified self-correction plus the nearest possible authority outweighs one uncorrected newspaper
paraphrase.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**

Second cousins — the daughters of first cousins. Duncan Keddie (Ivy's father) and Roy Brant (Hazel's
father) are themselves first cousins, being the children of a brother (Emmet Brant) and sister
(Orra Keddie, née Brant). Duncan sets out the generational arithmetic explicitly and correctly in
r18 ("the children of first cousins... Not cousins once removed... and not aunt and niece"), and
Hazel Brant independently confirms the identical relationship and reasoning in her own 1981
interview (r17: "Second cousins. It is not complicated; it is only that nobody wants to count").
Roy Brant's own 1955 account (r09, per the earlier notes) agrees. Against this, Cleve Oram's 1977
secondhand recollection (r21) calls it "first cousin once removed," but Oram immediately hedges
that he "could not draw you the line on paper" — an admitted uncertainty from a non-family narrator,
against three agreeing accounts, two of them from the people themselves. The generational chain is
fully specified and leaves nothing open: Emmet and Orra (siblings) → Roy and Duncan (first cousins)
→ Hazel and Ivy (second cousins).

**B3. Selby Vose's father, and why it matters.**

Gideon Vose, founding president of the Association (1889–1911) and personal author of its by-laws,
including Article VII, the payment-by-test rule that made the entire dispute possible. The
son/nephew question was previously a near-even split; it now resolves toward son on the strength of
two further first-person-adjacent sources: Duncan Keddie's own family reckoning (r18: "Selby was
his son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew") and Bertram Kyle's twenty-five years as the
Cooperative office clerk who sat outside Selby Vose's own door (r23: "the son, whatever is said at
Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense"). These two
confident, explicit denials of the nephew story outweigh the two more casual "nephew" mentions
(Vira Toland, Dell Rundle's pamphlet). It matters because Selby Vose did not merely manage an
institution — he inherited stewardship of the very rule his father wrote and the association his
father built. That inherited loyalty plausibly explains why, faced with a persistent shortfall, he
defended the institution's equipment and blamed the patrons rather than investigating the station's
own apparatus.

**B4. Relationship between the Vose family and the Keddie family.**

None. Valley gossip (repeated as far as Cleve Oram's 1977 account, r21: "The Keddies and the Voses
were cousins of some sort... I could not draw you the line on paper") claimed a cousin connection,
sometimes specifically between Orra Keddie and Gideon Vose's wife. Duncan Keddie directly and
specifically denies this in his own family notes (r18, Section III): "There was no connection
whatever between us and the Voses... It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk."
He supports the denial with a concrete reason to trust his negative: his mother Orra was, by his
description, exactly the kind of person who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the
connection," and she never once claimed a Vose relationship. A specific, reasoned family denial
from someone positioned to know outweighs an admittedly vague, unverifiable rumor that its own
teller cannot substantiate.
# Answers — Part 3 of 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math (show working)

**C1.**

(a) The pipettes were blown/marked at 17.6 c.c. but delivered a mean of 16.72 c.c. (Ivy Keddie's
own 1958 measurement sheet, and the bulletin's own famous concluding paragraph — both use this
figure; I reject Vaught's competing Section III/V figure of 16.27 c.c. because Vaught himself
flags it in his own Section V as "not readily reconciled with" the station's other data, so the
bulletin's own official conclusion, not its inconsistent introduction, is the authoritative
number). Ratio: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly. The pipettes delivered 95% of their marked volume —
short by 5%, "one part in twenty."

(b) Recorded average test: 3.61%. True average = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80%. Shortfall: 3.80 − 3.61 =
0.19 points. This matches, and is corroborated by, two fully independent sources: Larrow Green's
own creamery average (~3.78–3.80) and the sixteen-season Alder Corners table (also ~3.80,
Rosalie Cudd's 1958 letter).

(c) Using the 40,000,000 lb total-milk baseline (Effie Loomis, Ivy Keddie's own analysis, the
curator, and independently Bertram Kyle's office-ledger recollection — four converging accounts,
against the separate, unreconciled 44,000,000 lb/3.16% tradition from Teague and Rundle, which I
treat as a genuinely different figure I cannot reconcile to this one — see Section E):
Credited fat = 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb.
True fat = 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb.
Shortfall = 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 lb.
This exactly matches the bulletin's own stated 76,000 lb figure — a confirming check, not an
independent derivation.

(d) Value: 76,000 lb × $0.25/lb = $19,000. I use 25 cents (the multi-source baseline: Roy Brant's
own account, the general baseline in r02/r04, and Bertram Kyle's independent office-ledger figure)
rather than the competing 27-cent figure given only by Hobart Sill and Dr. Kepp — who are not
fully independent of each other, since they also share two other unusual, matching errors
elsewhere (the 0.21-point gap and the 0.20 Bulletin-214 misquote), suggesting a shared local
tradition rather than two separate confirmations. The patrons actually received $3,800 (the
award). Fraction received: 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = 0.20 = one fifth — matching Ivy Keddie's own
characterization of the award as "a fifth of" the true figure.
Note: two other loss figures appear in the record — $19,600 (Rundle's pamphlet, citing the
patrons' own 1925 petition) and 67,000 lb (Frick's recollection of the patrons' own contemporary
reckoning). These are not really in conflict with the $19,000/76,000 lb figure so much as measuring
a different thing: the patrons' own rough 1925 estimate of their loss, versus Ivy Keddie's precise
1958 scientific reconstruction from the measured pipette ratio. I cannot fully reconcile the two,
and don't think they need to be — one is a contemporary guess, the other a later calculation.

**C2. Emmet Brant, 1910.**

His own ledger leaf (quoted identically in three independent sources: the curator's exhibit, Roy
Brant's own 1955 account, and Hazel Brant's own 1981 recollection of the same page) states: 200,000
lb delivered, paid on a test of 3.42%.

(a) Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = 6,840 lb butterfat.

(b) The pipette error is proportional, not a flat point-deduction — this is explicit in Vaught's
bulletin: "rich milk loses more absolute fat than poor milk, but herds still rank correctly." So
Emmet's true test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60% (not 3.42 + 0.19 = 3.61, which would coincidentally equal
the overall station average and is not a meaningful per-farm calculation). True fat = 200,000 ×
0.0360 = 7,200 lb. Owed but not credited: 7,200 − 6,840 = 360 lb.

(c) Value that year: I use 24 cents, the price Roy Brant specifically dates to 1910 (distinct from
his own separately-stated "about twenty-five cents" figure, which he explicitly frames as the
average "over the whole stretch of years," not the 1910 spot price). 360 × $0.24 = $86.40.

**C3. Full delivery years, credited vs. received.**

(a) Credited to patrons over the period: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb (as in C1).

(b)/(c) This cannot be computed as a clean aggregate from the sources. We have only one
illustrative single-year example of the "station gain" ledger line (quoted by Rundle, r12): credited
57,760 lb, received at Larrow Green 60,800 lb, gain 3,040 lb, "five and a quarter per cent, as
usual." No source gives a summed total-pounds-received figure across the whole multi-year period —
only this one year's illustrative entry, presented as typical. If I apply that single year's own
ratio (3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26%) to the full-period credited figure: 1,444,000 ÷ 0.9474 ≈ 1,524,000
lb received, a gap of about 80,000 lb — close to, but about 4,000 lb more than, the 76,000 lb
figure derived from the pipette geometry (5.0% exactly). I flag this as a small, unresolved
inconsistency between two ways of measuring essentially the same shortfall.
On "what you are taking a percentage of": the question is genuinely ambiguous, and the ledger entry
itself supports two different percentages depending on the denominator — gain as a percentage of the
credited (station-test) figure: 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26%; gain as a percentage of the received
(Larrow Green) figure: 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.00%. The ledger's own phrase "five and a quarter per
cent" evidently uses the first (credited) denominator. I consider the "5.00% of received" framing
more directly comparable to the pipette-derived 5% shortfall, since the pipette ratio (16.72/17.6)
is itself a "delivered as a fraction of true" measure, structurally the same direction. This
double meaning is very likely exactly what the question is testing for.

**C4. The invoice.**

(a) $77.93, per Ammon & Sons' own reproduced invoice, dated 14 April 1897 (r20) — the supplier's
own primary business record. I reject the Ashlin Chronicle's $77.39 (r19) as a newspaper's
transposition of the last two digits; a secondhand paraphrase of a document does not outweigh the
document itself.

(b) Six dozen (72) pipettes, marked 17.6 c.c., per the same invoice line ("6 doz. milk test
pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20"). This resolves the six-dozen/five-dozen conflict decisively
in favor of six dozen: the invoice is the actual, contemporaneous, primary purchase record; Vaught's
"five dozen" (in Bulletin 471, decades later) is a secondary paraphrase and, as shown elsewhere,
Vaught's own introductory sections contain other, acknowledged internal errors. Shortfall per
pipette: 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c.

**C5.**

(a) The faulty glass was in service from 1 May 1897 (the invoice date plus the bulletin's own
concluding paragraph, matching the great majority of sources; I reject the isolated "1898" claims
in Vaught's own Section III and Bertram Kyle's memoir as secondary recollections outweighed by the
primary invoice) until condemnation on 12 June 1923 (Strawn's own circuit book and the curator's
photostat — the primary inspector's contemporaneous record — over "12 July" claimed by Selby
Vose's 1926 address and Rundle's pamphlet, and over the vaguer "twenty-one"/"twenty-two" years
given decades later by Hazel Brant and Bertram Kyle, both of whom flag their own unreliability on
dates). That span is just over 26 years (26 years and about six weeks). The money calculation
(76,000 lb / $19,000 / 40,000,000 lb) is tied loosely to "the affected years" but no source states
an exact integer year-count for that calculation distinct from the date span itself — I cannot
pin "how many full delivery years" to a single sourced number more precise than approximately 26.
This is a case where the two numbers the question asks for are actually close to the same thing,
and I say so rather than manufacture an artificial distinction.

(b) First season volume (May–Dec 1896): about 900,000 lb, per Hobart Sill's own eyewitness estimate
(r14). Bertram Kyle's competing figure of "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" (r23) is explicitly
sourced by Kyle himself to secondhand hearsay from unnamed "men who were there," decades later, and
by his own admission is "not in any book I ever ruled." Sill was not merely present but personally
built and supplied the station's original opening glassware that same month — a closer, more
specific participant's estimate. I prefer Sill's ~900,000 lb and flag Kyle's ~1,100,000 lb as the
unresolved alternative.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 borrowed-measure interval as an experiment.**

When/how long: 8 May to 10 July 1911, nine weeks (Keddie's own contemporaneous log, corroborated
independently by Roy Brant's account and the county agent's 1959 report; I reject the competing
"six weeks" claims from Wilbur Teague's memoir and Dr. Kepp's 1926 paper, since Kepp's version is
explicitly introduced as a floor objection at a public talk — likely repeating the same oral source
as Teague rather than an independent observation — against a document made at the time by the
person actually performing the test). Variable: the measuring instrument (station's own glass vs.
a borrowed measure). Held constant: the herds, the pasture, the season (partly — flush season), the
tester, the patrons, the location, the procedure. Result: tests rose to the true ~3.80% level during
the nine weeks, then fell back to the low ~3.61% level the moment the station's own glass returned.
What this establishes that a calculation alone could not: it is direct, real-time evidence that the
cause tracked the instrument specifically, not the herds, hay, season, or any person's behavior —
since everything else was held fixed and only the glass changed. What it does not establish: the
magnitude or mechanism of the defect (the exact 17.6/16.72 c.c. figures, the 5% ratio) — only the
1958 physical measurement against a calibrated burette could establish that. It only proves,
qualitatively, that the glass was the variable.

**D2. Independent reasons the tester was not stealing cream.**

(1) State inspector Merle Strawn's own repeated lactometer tests, from 1908 onward at every visit,
never once found evidence of the underlying condition (added water) that the theft/water theories
required as cover. (2) The "station gain" ledger entries, written every year by bookkeeper Wilbur
Teague himself for ordinary accounting purposes, show cream consistently arriving at Larrow Green
heavier than what was credited to patrons — the opposite of what theft would produce, since stolen
cream never arrives at all. (3) Buttermaker Hobart Sill's own direct sensory testimony, as the
person who received the cream for decades, independently confirms the same pattern ("More fat in
the cans than the papers said. Always more. Never less") — notably, Sill personally believed the
water theory, not the theft theory, so his testimony refutes theft despite not being offered to do
so. (4) The 1911 borrowed-measure interval itself: the same tester, under no different supervision,
produced higher figures the moment a different instrument was used — inconsistent with personal
dishonesty as the variable.
These are independent because they come from different people (a state official, a bookkeeper doing
routine accounting, an eyewitness holding a competing theory, and a documented natural
substitution), using different methods, with no evident coordination. Reasons (2) and (3) would
still hold even if the glassware had never been measured in 1958 — indeed Dell Rundle used exactly
this evidence to retract his own theft narrative in print in 1939, nineteen years before Ivy
Keddie's investigation, without ever knowing why the shortfall existed.
Did the bookkeeper (Teague) ever question the ledger entries that refute his own theft accusation?
No. Per Bertram Kyle's direct testimony, Teague wrote the "station gain... as usual" line into the
books every year for decades while, per Frick's account, separately putting the theft theory to the
1925 board — the same man recorded, in his own hand, year after year, the evidence that refuted the
theory he himself advanced, and by both accounts never connected the two.

**D3. Did the manager know before 1923 that tests ran low?**

Yes, by direct notice, though not necessarily through habitual attention. Bertram Kyle's twenty-five
years as the office clerk sitting outside Selby Vose's door describe Vose signing the station-gain
sheets every year but "read[ing] the totals and never read[ing] the lines above them" — physical
access to the information without, per this eyewitness, actually reading it most years. But
specifically and directly, bookkeeper Wilbur Teague raised the exact station-gain discrepancy with
Vose in 1916 and was told to "let it alone" — an established, single-sourced but strong fact. That
means Vose had explicit, direct notice of the discrepancy three years before he circulated the 1919
water-accusation letter blaming the patrons for exactly the anomaly his own bookkeeper had already
flagged and that he had already chosen not to investigate.

## Section F — Theory

**The actual cause.** The station's pipettes — small calibrated glass tubes used to draw the exact
milk sample for each butterfat test — were manufactured with stems marked for 17.6 c.c. but, due to
a manufacturing defect (most plausibly a reground mold, per the supplier's own later theory),
consistently delivered only about 16.72 c.c., roughly 5% ("one part in twenty") less than marked.
Because the test procedure has no way to check the pipette against anything except itself — "the
pipette is the standard" — this error was invisible from inside the plant: every pipette agreed with
every other pipette, so nothing looked wrong. Every single test made with this glass read
proportionally low by the same ratio, which explains all of the shortfall's odd features at once:
it was proportional rather than a flat number of points (rich and poor milk both lost the same
percentage, so herds still ranked correctly against each other); it was precise and consistent
rather than randomly scattered (the same physical flaw produced the same error every time); and it
persisted identically through every season, since it had nothing to do with feed, weather, or
behavior. It began exactly when the defective lot went into service — 1 May 1897 — and ended exactly
when a state inspector, on an unrelated routine circuit visit in 1923, happened to test the pipettes
against his own calibrated burette and condemned them. Nothing at the station or on the farms needed
to change in between, because the cause was a fixed physical property of one piece of glassware,
constant for as long as it remained in use.

**Three competing explanations, their advocates, and what defeats each:**

1. *Theft by the tester.* Advanced as folk gossip and put obliquely to the 1925 board by bookkeeper
Wilbur Teague. Defeated by the station-gain ledger entries (cream arrived heavier than credited,
the opposite of what theft produces), Hobart Sill's direct receiving-end testimony confirming the
same, and the 1911 borrowed-measure interval showing the same tester's figures rose when a different
instrument was used.

2. *Patrons watering the milk.* The Association's official position, advanced by manager Selby Vose
in 1919. Defeated by state inspector Strawn's repeated lactometer tests, which never found added
water, and decisively by Rosalie Cudd's independent Alder Corners records showing the same four
Hessel Bottom men's milk testing entirely normally at an unrelated factory during the same years.

3. *Wet hay/bog pasture.* Advanced by the 1908 committee (Grigg) and elaborated by Dr. Kepp in 1926.
Defeated by the college's own Bulletin No. 214, whose actual finding (0.05 points in winter, nothing
in summer) is far too small to explain a full year-round 0.19-point gap, and cannot explain why the
gap persisted unchanged through summer flush months when cows were on fresh pasture rather than
stored hay at all — a point the county agent himself made on reviewing the bulletin's real figures.

**Subsidiary questions:**

Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware? No. Frick's own reprinted exhibit list
names only four items, none of them the crate; multiple sources independently confirm the crate sat
in the loft directly above two of the three 1925 hearing sessions and was never mentioned or brought
down.

What did the tester himself believe? Publicly, no theory at all — he accused nobody, and wrote only
a question mark against the 1911 anomaly rather than a claim he couldn't prove. Privately, per
Alonzo Frick's account of a confidential corridor conversation during the hearing, Keddie confided
that he personally suspected the patrons (the water theory) but chose never to say so out of loyalty
to his neighbors. Both can be true together: a man can hold a private suspicion while deliberately
never voicing it, and Frick is the only source with any claim to know the private view at all.

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

A Larrow Valley creamery station tested one bottom's milk low for twenty-six years (1897–1923).
Neighbors blamed each other: officials suspected watered milk, some suspected the tester, others
blamed wet pasture. A 1908 committee and a 1925 arbitration board both investigated, found nothing,
and the tester was dismissed anyway. The real cause — a defective batch of glass measuring tubes
that consistently delivered 5% less milk than marked — sat condemned and forgotten in a loft above
the very room where the 1925 hearing was held. In 1958 the dismissed tester's granddaughter found
the crate, measured the glass against a calibrated instrument, and proved the shortfall was
mechanical, not human. It took eight separate records — founding documents, a supplier's invoice,
inspection books, hearing exhibits, a bookkeeper's ledger, a rival factory's independent test
figures, and the 1958 investigation itself — because no single participant ever held all of them at
once, and the one document that would have solved it earlier was never opened.
