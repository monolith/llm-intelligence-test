# Answers — Part 1 of 3 (Section A, Stories 1–4)

*Based on notes-after-r16 (retellings 1–16) and direct reading of retellings 17–24.*

## Section A — Reconstruction

There were eight underlying stories. I have partitioned the 24 retellings by episode rather than
by narrator, since most narrators touch several episodes. Each story below uses the corrected
(resolved) facts; the conflicts behind each resolution are catalogued in full in Section E.

### Story 1 — Founding the Association and Equipping the Station (1889–1897)

Farmers of the Larrow Valley organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association in
1889, meeting at the Larrow Green schoolhouse (r12: "somebody's horse got loose in the middle of
it"). Gideon Vose, who kept a Jersey herd, was elected founding president and personally drafted
the by-laws, serving until 1911. The main creamery was built at Larrow Green in 1891. Article
VII of the by-laws — the rule paying every patron by butterfat test rather than milk weight —
is the hinge of the entire history: under payment by weight, none of what followed would have
cost anyone money. Its adoption date cannot be settled: three sources give three different years
(1891, 1892, 1893), and the county agent's 1959 report (r22) discovered the Association's own
minute book for its early years had been lost in an office move, so no source can actually prove
a date — this is a genuine, permanent gap in the record, not a solvable conflict.

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles
up-valley, to serve Hessel Bottom's six farms. Buttermaker Hobart Sill personally supplied
borrowed glassware from his own bench so the station could open before its own equipment
arrived; it opened 4 May 1896. Ansel Keddie was hired as tester and separator man from opening
day. The station's own permanent outfit was ordered from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet: the supplier's
own invoice (reproduced in full, r20), dated 14 April 1897, itemizes six dozen (72) milk test
pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. at 35 cents each ($25.20), twelve dozen test bottles, an acid measure,
a hand tester, and freight, totaling $77.93. This outfit went into service 1 May 1897, replacing
Sill's borrowed glass. Unknown to everyone, this specific lot of pipettes — probably from a
reground mold, per the supplier's own later theory (r20) — delivered only about 16.72 c.c.
against its 17.6 c.c. mark: a defect invisible to any check made from inside the plant, since
routine consistency checks would only confirm the pipettes agreed with each other, not with the
true standard. This flaw, present from day one, is the seed of everything that follows.

### Story 2 — The First Complaints and the 1908 Wet-Hay Investigation

From nearly the station's opening, Ostrey Hollow's recorded milk tests ran conspicuously lower
than milk tested anywhere else nearby — independently confirmed later at 0.19 points below true
value, year-round, in every season alike. The first public complaint is dated 1902 by three
independent narrators (Roy Brant, Hobart Sill, Nyle Grigg). Grumbling continued through the
decade, and in 1908 the Association appointed an investigating committee chaired by Nyle Grigg,
a Larrow Green farmer who hauled to Larrow Green itself and was not an Ostrey Hollow patron —
offered by himself as proof of his impartiality. The committee visited all six Hessel Bottom
farms, sampled the herds, and tested both the station's and the patrons' own scales against a
known weight. Their full report (reprinted by Grigg in 1919, r15) found "no dishonesty of any
kind, in any quarter" and concluded the wet, sedge-and-rush hay of the bog bottom was thinning
the cream — recommending remedy through the land, not the station. The report's own text says
sampling ran only "through January, February and March" (winter), but Grigg's own 1919 covering
letter claims it ran all twelve months — a contradiction within his own reprinted document. In
1919, citing the reopened "Ostrey Hollow question," Grigg republished the report with his letter,
which also misquoted the college's actual Bulletin No. 214 finding (a genuine 0.05-point winter
effect) as a much larger 0.20-point effect — a misquotation later independently repeated by
veterinarian Dr. Roland Kepp in his own 1926 paper advancing a more sophisticated
mineral-deficiency version of the same wet-hay theory. Both misquotations, from the theory's two
chief advocates, happened to inflate the case for wet hay by roughly fourfold.

### Story 3 — The 1911 Borrowed-Measure Interval

On 8 May 1911 the station's own measuring glass broke, and for nine weeks — until 10 July 1911 —
Keddie used a measure borrowed from elsewhere. Keddie's own contemporaneous log entry records
this, and during those nine weeks the station's tests rose to the true value (~3.80), then fell
back to the old low level the instant the station's repaired/own glass returned to use. Keddie
himself wrote no accusation in his book, only a question mark. His son Duncan later recalled
(r18) that his father came home that summer saying "it was something in the glass" but "never
dared put it in the book," lacking anything but suspicion to set against an outfit he had been
trained to trust. This episode is the single clearest natural experiment in the whole record:
with the herds, pasture, season (partly), tester, and patrons all held constant, and only the
measuring instrument changed, the results tracked the instrument exactly — evidence, available
in real time in 1911, that the fault lay in the glass, forty-seven years before anyone acted on
it. At least one contemporary (an unnamed "gentleman," possibly bookkeeper Wilbur Teague) raised
this same anomaly from the floor at Kepp's 1926 lecture as an objection to the wet-hay theory;
Kepp dismissed it as meaningless because it fell in flush season — a dismissal now shown, with
hindsight, to have waved away the best evidence then available.

### Story 4 — The 1919 Circular and the Valley's Rift

Having been told directly by his own bookkeeper Wilbur Teague in 1916 that the cream arriving at
Larrow Green consistently outweighed what the station credited to patrons — and told to "let it
alone" — Association manager Selby Vose (Gideon Vose's son, per the best evidence: see Section
B3) circulated a warning letter in 1919 to the Ostrey Hollow patron families, accusing them of
adding water to their milk. Ansel Keddie, who kept the test book but did not write the letter,
refused to mail it or leave it on the station platform; he delivered it himself, in person, to
every family, standing at each door while the recipient read it — an act his son later called
"the most expensive virtue anybody in that valley ever had," since it associated Keddie
personally with an accusation he had not made and (per Alonzo Frick's later account) privately
did not fully share. Orra Keddie's own diary, written days later, records her husband coming home
at eleven at night "with his hat still on" after delivering the letters personally. Reaction split
sharply along family and allegiance lines: Orra's brother Emmet Brant, the largest and longest-
standing patron, never forgave it and cut off all contact with the Keddies for the rest of his
life — not primarily over the accusation itself but because he felt Ansel should have publicly
refused to carry it and had not. By contrast, Larrow Green buttermaker Hobart Sill approved of
the circular outright ("I thought well of him for it"), believing the water theory correct. No
patron ever answered the circular in writing — in Roy Brant's words, "in that valley, a shout."
