# Answers — Part 2 of 3 (Section A, Stories 5–8; Section B)

## Section A — Reconstruction (continued)

### Story 5 — Strawn's 1923 Condemnation of the Glassware

On a routine circuit visit, state dairy inspector Merle Strawn (who served the Ordell circuit
1907–1936 and, per his son-in-law's testimony in r18, was kin to nobody in the valley on any
side) tested the Ostrey Hollow station's milk pipettes against his own calibrated burette. His
own circuit book, corroborated by a curator's photostat of the same document, dates this 12 June
1923. He found the pipettes would not deliver their marked 17.6 c.c. and condemned the entire
crate on the spot, directing the manager to order new glass — a routine equipment inspection,
unconnected in his mind or anyone else's to the twenty-six-year-old patron dispute. The
Association wrote to Ammon & Sons on 28 June 1923; the supplier's reply of 2 July 1923
(reproduced verbatim, r20) entered the replacement order but admitted "our books of the nineties
are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name" — a firm's honest but
unhelpful non-answer to a 26-year customer. New glass went into service that July, and from 1924
the station's recorded average test rose and stayed at a materially higher level. The condemned
crate was carried up into the loft above the station's intake room and left there, forgotten,
under a tarpaulin, for the next thirty-five years — a detail vividly confirmed by cream hauler's
son Cleve Oram (r21), who had personally handled the crate as a boy hunting for wire and a hay
fork, and by Hazel Brant (r17), who describes it sitting there "for most of my life without one
soul thinking about it." Nobody in 1923 connected the condemnation to the long-standing shortfall
complaint; the crate was simply old equipment being thrown out.

### Story 6 — The 1925 Board of Arbitration and Keddie's Dismissal

The Ostrey Hollow patrons petitioned in January 1925. A three-man board of arbitration was formed,
chaired by Ashlin attorney Alonzo Frick, chosen (by his own account, r13) "because I was the one
of the three who kept no cow." The board held three sessions: the first at the Association's
Ashlin office on 17 March 1925, and — at Frick's own insistence, so the board could see the plant
firsthand — the second and third at the station itself, in its own weigh room, with a crowd of
onlookers (including a young Cleve Oram) standing outside on the platform in the rain. Frick's own
reprinted exhibit list names exactly four documents placed before the board: the station's test
book in Keddie's hand, the station's receiving book, Emmet Brant's private weigh book (lent for
the purpose), and Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook. No mention of any crate or glassware appears
anywhere in the exhibit list, and the crate itself sat, unexamined, in the loft directly above two
of the three sessions. Three theories were argued: water added by patrons (put forward without
proof by manager Selby Vose), tester theft (put obliquely by bookkeeper Wilbur Teague), and wet
hay (via the 1908 report, read into the record). The board found no cause established and awarded
the patrons $3,800 "without admission" on 4 May 1925. Ansel Keddie was dismissed 30 April 1925 —
four days before the award — after twenty-nine seasons at the station. He went home that evening
at his ordinary hour, hung up his apron, and asked what there was for supper (Orra's diary). He
took up carpentry, worked on his own farm's long-neglected cow shed, and — per his son — "began to
whistle, which he never did when he was paid." He died in 1934, the imputation of the affair still
on him; his widow Orra outlived him to 1948, fourteen years short of his vindication.

### Story 7 — The Alder Corners Comparison Across Redlow Ridge

From 1915 to 1930 — sixteen consecutive summer seasons — four of Hessel Bottom's six farms hauled
their milk over Redlow Ridge each summer flush to the Alder Corners Cheese Company in neighboring
Vessey County, built by Jerome Cudd (founding date disputed: his own daughter's letter says 1913,
two other sources say 1914), returning to the Ostrey Hollow station the rest of each year. Cudd
kept his own independent test book on his own bench, tested on his own glassware, and never once
compared his figures against the Ostrey Hollow station's — "it never entered his head," his
daughter Rosalie later wrote. His 1919 season-end entry recorded the four men's milk testing
3.79, 3.81, 3.78, and 3.82 — entirely normal — with the marginal note "Good milk off wet ground,
whatever they say over the ridge." When Rosalie Cudd, who succeeded her father in 1941, corresponded
with Ivy Keddie in 1958, she sent a complete year-by-year table for all sixteen seasons (1915–1930),
averaging about 3.80 throughout, with no year showing any depression. This is the single most
important piece of external corroboration in the whole record: the same men's milk, from the same
wet Hessel Bottom pasture, tested normally at an entirely separate, unrelated factory using
different equipment, during the very years the water-theory and wet-hay-theory were both current —
independently proving both that the men were not watering their milk and that the wet ground was
not the cause, since neither factor changed when the men crossed the ridge, only the test result did.

### Story 8 — Ivy Keddie's 1958 Investigation and Vindication

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie — Ansel Keddie's granddaughter, a graduate student in dairy husbandry
at the State Agricultural College at Farland — climbed a ladder into the loft above the old
station's intake room and found the condemned 1923 crate, undisturbed since it was put there. She
borrowed her second cousin Roy Brant's weigh book that same year (returned promptly) and
corresponded with Rosalie Cudd, who supplied the Alder Corners figures. On 9 March 1958, at
Farland, she measured the nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette: each was blown
17.6 c.c. on the stem but delivered a mean of about 16.72 c.c. — short by 0.88 c.c., or one part
in twenty (5%) — the precise, consistent, invisible-from-inside defect that explained the entire
0.19-point historical gap. Her findings, co-written with Professor Leland Vaught, appeared as
State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 471 in October 1958, concluding flatly: "No milk was
watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there... the difference — seventy-six thousand
pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station
gain." (The bulletin's own introductory sections contain internally inconsistent figures that
Vaught himself flags as irreconcilable — see Sections C and E.) The Ashlin Chronicle covered the
story on its front page 3 October 1958; the county agricultural agent filed a careful, self-
correcting report in 1959; and Ivy delivered a talk, "What the Crate Held," to the Ordell County
Historical Society at Ashlin in 1959. The test book, the Brant weigh book, Strawn's notebooks, the
Alder Corners book, and the crate itself were eventually all deposited across three different
local historical collections by the 1960s and 70s — the family's own record-keeping, per Duncan
Keddie's 1968–70 notes, scattered but preserved. Ansel Keddie's name was formally cleared
twenty-four years after his death; his widow did not live to see it.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie.**

Granddaughter. Ivy is the daughter of Duncan Keddie, who is Ansel's son — a direct, two-generation
line stated plainly and repeatedly (Duncan's own 1968 family notes, r18: "My daughter is Ivy, born
in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter"). Two sources instead call her Ansel's
"great-niece": the Ashlin Chronicle's 1958 front page (r19) and the county agent's original 1959
report draft (r22). I resolve this as granddaughter, for two reasons. First, the county agent's
own report contains its own correction: in an appended note, added after further inquiry, Falke
writes "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the
great-niece; the error on page two is mine" — meaning one of the two "great-niece" sources is a
self-retracted error by its own author. Second, Duncan Keddie's account is a father's first-person
statement about his own daughter and his own father, the closest possible standing to know, and he
explicitly says he is writing partly because he has "already twice seen it printed otherwise." A
verified self-correction plus the nearest possible authority outweighs one uncorrected newspaper
paraphrase.

**B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant.**

Second cousins — the daughters of first cousins. Duncan Keddie (Ivy's father) and Roy Brant (Hazel's
father) are themselves first cousins, being the children of a brother (Emmet Brant) and sister
(Orra Keddie, née Brant). Duncan sets out the generational arithmetic explicitly and correctly in
r18 ("the children of first cousins... Not cousins once removed... and not aunt and niece"), and
Hazel Brant independently confirms the identical relationship and reasoning in her own 1981
interview (r17: "Second cousins. It is not complicated; it is only that nobody wants to count").
Roy Brant's own 1955 account (r09, per the earlier notes) agrees. Against this, Cleve Oram's 1977
secondhand recollection (r21) calls it "first cousin once removed," but Oram immediately hedges
that he "could not draw you the line on paper" — an admitted uncertainty from a non-family narrator,
against three agreeing accounts, two of them from the people themselves. The generational chain is
fully specified and leaves nothing open: Emmet and Orra (siblings) → Roy and Duncan (first cousins)
→ Hazel and Ivy (second cousins).

**B3. Selby Vose's father, and why it matters.**

Gideon Vose, founding president of the Association (1889–1911) and personal author of its by-laws,
including Article VII, the payment-by-test rule that made the entire dispute possible. The
son/nephew question was previously a near-even split; it now resolves toward son on the strength of
two further first-person-adjacent sources: Duncan Keddie's own family reckoning (r18: "Selby was
his son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew") and Bertram Kyle's twenty-five years as the
Cooperative office clerk who sat outside Selby Vose's own door (r23: "the son, whatever is said at
Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense"). These two
confident, explicit denials of the nephew story outweigh the two more casual "nephew" mentions
(Vira Toland, Dell Rundle's pamphlet). It matters because Selby Vose did not merely manage an
institution — he inherited stewardship of the very rule his father wrote and the association his
father built. That inherited loyalty plausibly explains why, faced with a persistent shortfall, he
defended the institution's equipment and blamed the patrons rather than investigating the station's
own apparatus.

**B4. Relationship between the Vose family and the Keddie family.**

None. Valley gossip (repeated as far as Cleve Oram's 1977 account, r21: "The Keddies and the Voses
were cousins of some sort... I could not draw you the line on paper") claimed a cousin connection,
sometimes specifically between Orra Keddie and Gideon Vose's wife. Duncan Keddie directly and
specifically denies this in his own family notes (r18, Section III): "There was no connection
whatever between us and the Voses... It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk."
He supports the denial with a concrete reason to trust his negative: his mother Orra was, by his
description, exactly the kind of person who "could not pass a headstone without giving you the
connection," and she never once claimed a Vose relationship. A specific, reasoned family denial
from someone positioned to know outweighs an admittedly vague, unverifiable rumor that its own
teller cannot substantiate.
