# Answers — Part 3 of 3 (Sections C–G)

## Section C — Math (show working)

**C1.**

(a) The pipettes were blown/marked at 17.6 c.c. but delivered a mean of 16.72 c.c. (Ivy Keddie's
own 1958 measurement sheet, and the bulletin's own famous concluding paragraph — both use this
figure; I reject Vaught's competing Section III/V figure of 16.27 c.c. because Vaught himself
flags it in his own Section V as "not readily reconciled with" the station's other data, so the
bulletin's own official conclusion, not its inconsistent introduction, is the authoritative
number). Ratio: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly. The pipettes delivered 95% of their marked volume —
short by 5%, "one part in twenty."

(b) Recorded average test: 3.61%. True average = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80%. Shortfall: 3.80 − 3.61 =
0.19 points. This matches, and is corroborated by, two fully independent sources: Larrow Green's
own creamery average (~3.78–3.80) and the sixteen-season Alder Corners table (also ~3.80,
Rosalie Cudd's 1958 letter).

(c) Using the 40,000,000 lb total-milk baseline (Effie Loomis, Ivy Keddie's own analysis, the
curator, and independently Bertram Kyle's office-ledger recollection — four converging accounts,
against the separate, unreconciled 44,000,000 lb/3.16% tradition from Teague and Rundle, which I
treat as a genuinely different figure I cannot reconcile to this one — see Section E):
Credited fat = 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb.
True fat = 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb.
Shortfall = 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 lb.
This exactly matches the bulletin's own stated 76,000 lb figure — a confirming check, not an
independent derivation.

(d) Value: 76,000 lb × $0.25/lb = $19,000. I use 25 cents (the multi-source baseline: Roy Brant's
own account, the general baseline in r02/r04, and Bertram Kyle's independent office-ledger figure)
rather than the competing 27-cent figure given only by Hobart Sill and Dr. Kepp — who are not
fully independent of each other, since they also share two other unusual, matching errors
elsewhere (the 0.21-point gap and the 0.20 Bulletin-214 misquote), suggesting a shared local
tradition rather than two separate confirmations. The patrons actually received $3,800 (the
award). Fraction received: 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = 0.20 = one fifth — matching Ivy Keddie's own
characterization of the award as "a fifth of" the true figure.
Note: two other loss figures appear in the record — $19,600 (Rundle's pamphlet, citing the
patrons' own 1925 petition) and 67,000 lb (Frick's recollection of the patrons' own contemporary
reckoning). These are not really in conflict with the $19,000/76,000 lb figure so much as measuring
a different thing: the patrons' own rough 1925 estimate of their loss, versus Ivy Keddie's precise
1958 scientific reconstruction from the measured pipette ratio. I cannot fully reconcile the two,
and don't think they need to be — one is a contemporary guess, the other a later calculation.

**C2. Emmet Brant, 1910.**

His own ledger leaf (quoted identically in three independent sources: the curator's exhibit, Roy
Brant's own 1955 account, and Hazel Brant's own 1981 recollection of the same page) states: 200,000
lb delivered, paid on a test of 3.42%.

(a) Credited: 200,000 × 0.0342 = 6,840 lb butterfat.

(b) The pipette error is proportional, not a flat point-deduction — this is explicit in Vaught's
bulletin: "rich milk loses more absolute fat than poor milk, but herds still rank correctly." So
Emmet's true test = 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60% (not 3.42 + 0.19 = 3.61, which would coincidentally equal
the overall station average and is not a meaningful per-farm calculation). True fat = 200,000 ×
0.0360 = 7,200 lb. Owed but not credited: 7,200 − 6,840 = 360 lb.

(c) Value that year: I use 24 cents, the price Roy Brant specifically dates to 1910 (distinct from
his own separately-stated "about twenty-five cents" figure, which he explicitly frames as the
average "over the whole stretch of years," not the 1910 spot price). 360 × $0.24 = $86.40.

**C3. Full delivery years, credited vs. received.**

(a) Credited to patrons over the period: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb (as in C1).

(b)/(c) This cannot be computed as a clean aggregate from the sources. We have only one
illustrative single-year example of the "station gain" ledger line (quoted by Rundle, r12): credited
57,760 lb, received at Larrow Green 60,800 lb, gain 3,040 lb, "five and a quarter per cent, as
usual." No source gives a summed total-pounds-received figure across the whole multi-year period —
only this one year's illustrative entry, presented as typical. If I apply that single year's own
ratio (3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26%) to the full-period credited figure: 1,444,000 ÷ 0.9474 ≈ 1,524,000
lb received, a gap of about 80,000 lb — close to, but about 4,000 lb more than, the 76,000 lb
figure derived from the pipette geometry (5.0% exactly). I flag this as a small, unresolved
inconsistency between two ways of measuring essentially the same shortfall.
On "what you are taking a percentage of": the question is genuinely ambiguous, and the ledger entry
itself supports two different percentages depending on the denominator — gain as a percentage of the
credited (station-test) figure: 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26%; gain as a percentage of the received
(Larrow Green) figure: 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.00%. The ledger's own phrase "five and a quarter per
cent" evidently uses the first (credited) denominator. I consider the "5.00% of received" framing
more directly comparable to the pipette-derived 5% shortfall, since the pipette ratio (16.72/17.6)
is itself a "delivered as a fraction of true" measure, structurally the same direction. This
double meaning is very likely exactly what the question is testing for.

**C4. The invoice.**

(a) $77.93, per Ammon & Sons' own reproduced invoice, dated 14 April 1897 (r20) — the supplier's
own primary business record. I reject the Ashlin Chronicle's $77.39 (r19) as a newspaper's
transposition of the last two digits; a secondhand paraphrase of a document does not outweigh the
document itself.

(b) Six dozen (72) pipettes, marked 17.6 c.c., per the same invoice line ("6 doz. milk test
pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20"). This resolves the six-dozen/five-dozen conflict decisively
in favor of six dozen: the invoice is the actual, contemporaneous, primary purchase record; Vaught's
"five dozen" (in Bulletin 471, decades later) is a secondary paraphrase and, as shown elsewhere,
Vaught's own introductory sections contain other, acknowledged internal errors. Shortfall per
pipette: 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c.

**C5.**

(a) The faulty glass was in service from 1 May 1897 (the invoice date plus the bulletin's own
concluding paragraph, matching the great majority of sources; I reject the isolated "1898" claims
in Vaught's own Section III and Bertram Kyle's memoir as secondary recollections outweighed by the
primary invoice) until condemnation on 12 June 1923 (Strawn's own circuit book and the curator's
photostat — the primary inspector's contemporaneous record — over "12 July" claimed by Selby
Vose's 1926 address and Rundle's pamphlet, and over the vaguer "twenty-one"/"twenty-two" years
given decades later by Hazel Brant and Bertram Kyle, both of whom flag their own unreliability on
dates). That span is just over 26 years (26 years and about six weeks). The money calculation
(76,000 lb / $19,000 / 40,000,000 lb) is tied loosely to "the affected years" but no source states
an exact integer year-count for that calculation distinct from the date span itself — I cannot
pin "how many full delivery years" to a single sourced number more precise than approximately 26.
This is a case where the two numbers the question asks for are actually close to the same thing,
and I say so rather than manufacture an artificial distinction.

(b) First season volume (May–Dec 1896): about 900,000 lb, per Hobart Sill's own eyewitness estimate
(r14). Bertram Kyle's competing figure of "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds" (r23) is explicitly
sourced by Kyle himself to secondhand hearsay from unnamed "men who were there," decades later, and
by his own admission is "not in any book I ever ruled." Sill was not merely present but personally
built and supplied the station's original opening glassware that same month — a closer, more
specific participant's estimate. I prefer Sill's ~900,000 lb and flag Kyle's ~1,100,000 lb as the
unresolved alternative.

## Section D — Logic

**D1. The 1911 borrowed-measure interval as an experiment.**

When/how long: 8 May to 10 July 1911, nine weeks (Keddie's own contemporaneous log, corroborated
independently by Roy Brant's account and the county agent's 1959 report; I reject the competing
"six weeks" claims from Wilbur Teague's memoir and Dr. Kepp's 1926 paper, since Kepp's version is
explicitly introduced as a floor objection at a public talk — likely repeating the same oral source
as Teague rather than an independent observation — against a document made at the time by the
person actually performing the test). Variable: the measuring instrument (station's own glass vs.
a borrowed measure). Held constant: the herds, the pasture, the season (partly — flush season), the
tester, the patrons, the location, the procedure. Result: tests rose to the true ~3.80% level during
the nine weeks, then fell back to the low ~3.61% level the moment the station's own glass returned.
What this establishes that a calculation alone could not: it is direct, real-time evidence that the
cause tracked the instrument specifically, not the herds, hay, season, or any person's behavior —
since everything else was held fixed and only the glass changed. What it does not establish: the
magnitude or mechanism of the defect (the exact 17.6/16.72 c.c. figures, the 5% ratio) — only the
1958 physical measurement against a calibrated burette could establish that. It only proves,
qualitatively, that the glass was the variable.

**D2. Independent reasons the tester was not stealing cream.**

(1) State inspector Merle Strawn's own repeated lactometer tests, from 1908 onward at every visit,
never once found evidence of the underlying condition (added water) that the theft/water theories
required as cover. (2) The "station gain" ledger entries, written every year by bookkeeper Wilbur
Teague himself for ordinary accounting purposes, show cream consistently arriving at Larrow Green
heavier than what was credited to patrons — the opposite of what theft would produce, since stolen
cream never arrives at all. (3) Buttermaker Hobart Sill's own direct sensory testimony, as the
person who received the cream for decades, independently confirms the same pattern ("More fat in
the cans than the papers said. Always more. Never less") — notably, Sill personally believed the
water theory, not the theft theory, so his testimony refutes theft despite not being offered to do
so. (4) The 1911 borrowed-measure interval itself: the same tester, under no different supervision,
produced higher figures the moment a different instrument was used — inconsistent with personal
dishonesty as the variable.
These are independent because they come from different people (a state official, a bookkeeper doing
routine accounting, an eyewitness holding a competing theory, and a documented natural
substitution), using different methods, with no evident coordination. Reasons (2) and (3) would
still hold even if the glassware had never been measured in 1958 — indeed Dell Rundle used exactly
this evidence to retract his own theft narrative in print in 1939, nineteen years before Ivy
Keddie's investigation, without ever knowing why the shortfall existed.
Did the bookkeeper (Teague) ever question the ledger entries that refute his own theft accusation?
No. Per Bertram Kyle's direct testimony, Teague wrote the "station gain... as usual" line into the
books every year for decades while, per Frick's account, separately putting the theft theory to the
1925 board — the same man recorded, in his own hand, year after year, the evidence that refuted the
theory he himself advanced, and by both accounts never connected the two.

**D3. Did the manager know before 1923 that tests ran low?**

Yes, by direct notice, though not necessarily through habitual attention. Bertram Kyle's twenty-five
years as the office clerk sitting outside Selby Vose's door describe Vose signing the station-gain
sheets every year but "read[ing] the totals and never read[ing] the lines above them" — physical
access to the information without, per this eyewitness, actually reading it most years. But
specifically and directly, bookkeeper Wilbur Teague raised the exact station-gain discrepancy with
Vose in 1916 and was told to "let it alone" — an established, single-sourced but strong fact. That
means Vose had explicit, direct notice of the discrepancy three years before he circulated the 1919
water-accusation letter blaming the patrons for exactly the anomaly his own bookkeeper had already
flagged and that he had already chosen not to investigate.

## Section F — Theory

**The actual cause.** The station's pipettes — small calibrated glass tubes used to draw the exact
milk sample for each butterfat test — were manufactured with stems marked for 17.6 c.c. but, due to
a manufacturing defect (most plausibly a reground mold, per the supplier's own later theory),
consistently delivered only about 16.72 c.c., roughly 5% ("one part in twenty") less than marked.
Because the test procedure has no way to check the pipette against anything except itself — "the
pipette is the standard" — this error was invisible from inside the plant: every pipette agreed with
every other pipette, so nothing looked wrong. Every single test made with this glass read
proportionally low by the same ratio, which explains all of the shortfall's odd features at once:
it was proportional rather than a flat number of points (rich and poor milk both lost the same
percentage, so herds still ranked correctly against each other); it was precise and consistent
rather than randomly scattered (the same physical flaw produced the same error every time); and it
persisted identically through every season, since it had nothing to do with feed, weather, or
behavior. It began exactly when the defective lot went into service — 1 May 1897 — and ended exactly
when a state inspector, on an unrelated routine circuit visit in 1923, happened to test the pipettes
against his own calibrated burette and condemned them. Nothing at the station or on the farms needed
to change in between, because the cause was a fixed physical property of one piece of glassware,
constant for as long as it remained in use.

**Three competing explanations, their advocates, and what defeats each:**

1. *Theft by the tester.* Advanced as folk gossip and put obliquely to the 1925 board by bookkeeper
Wilbur Teague. Defeated by the station-gain ledger entries (cream arrived heavier than credited,
the opposite of what theft produces), Hobart Sill's direct receiving-end testimony confirming the
same, and the 1911 borrowed-measure interval showing the same tester's figures rose when a different
instrument was used.

2. *Patrons watering the milk.* The Association's official position, advanced by manager Selby Vose
in 1919. Defeated by state inspector Strawn's repeated lactometer tests, which never found added
water, and decisively by Rosalie Cudd's independent Alder Corners records showing the same four
Hessel Bottom men's milk testing entirely normally at an unrelated factory during the same years.

3. *Wet hay/bog pasture.* Advanced by the 1908 committee (Grigg) and elaborated by Dr. Kepp in 1926.
Defeated by the college's own Bulletin No. 214, whose actual finding (0.05 points in winter, nothing
in summer) is far too small to explain a full year-round 0.19-point gap, and cannot explain why the
gap persisted unchanged through summer flush months when cows were on fresh pasture rather than
stored hay at all — a point the county agent himself made on reviewing the bulletin's real figures.

**Subsidiary questions:**

Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware? No. Frick's own reprinted exhibit list
names only four items, none of them the crate; multiple sources independently confirm the crate sat
in the loft directly above two of the three 1925 hearing sessions and was never mentioned or brought
down.

What did the tester himself believe? Publicly, no theory at all — he accused nobody, and wrote only
a question mark against the 1911 anomaly rather than a claim he couldn't prove. Privately, per
Alonzo Frick's account of a confidential corridor conversation during the hearing, Keddie confided
that he personally suspected the patrons (the water theory) but chose never to say so out of loyalty
to his neighbors. Both can be true together: a man can hold a private suspicion while deliberately
never voicing it, and Frick is the only source with any claim to know the private view at all.

## Section G — Summary (≤150 words)

A Larrow Valley creamery station tested one bottom's milk low for twenty-six years (1897–1923).
Neighbors blamed each other: officials suspected watered milk, some suspected the tester, others
blamed wet pasture. A 1908 committee and a 1925 arbitration board both investigated, found nothing,
and the tester was dismissed anyway. The real cause — a defective batch of glass measuring tubes
that consistently delivered 5% less milk than marked — sat condemned and forgotten in a loft above
the very room where the 1925 hearing was held. In 1958 the dismissed tester's granddaughter found
the crate, measured the glass against a calibrated instrument, and proved the shortfall was
mechanical, not human. It took eight separate records — founding documents, a supplier's invoice,
inspection books, hearing exhibits, a bookkeeper's ledger, a rival factory's independent test
figures, and the 1958 investigation itself — because no single participant ever held all of them at
once, and the one document that would have solved it earlier was never opened.
