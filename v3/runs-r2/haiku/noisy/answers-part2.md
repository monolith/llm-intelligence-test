# Section A: Reconstruction of Eight Stories (Stories 5-8)

## Story 5: The Water Theory and the 1919 Circular (1919–1925)

In 1919, Selby Vose, manager of the Association since 1913, became convinced that the low tests were caused not by equipment or ground or hay, but by deliberate watering of milk by Ostrey Hollow patrons. Vose had no proof. Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, had tested repeatedly with his lactometer and found no added water. Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee had inquired into water supplies and found nothing suspicious. But Vose believed it nonetheless, and in June 1919, he circulated a letter to patrons warning them about water detection: "Water going into cans at the head of that valley and stopped when the Association began to look" (per his later account). He sent nine letters to nine families on Hessel Bottom—"a warning... about adding water to the milk, in a civil form of words but with no doubt at all in it about what was meant" (clerk Kyle's recollection).

Ansel Keddie received one. Rather than post them or leave them on the platform, Ansel took the nine letters himself from house to house, stood at each door while the farmer read it, and explained his position: he did not believe the charge, but he would not have another man carry it. He came home at eleven that night with his hat still on, which was not his way. Orra, his wife, understood immediately what her husband had done and why, and she wrote it in her diary. Her brother Emmet came to the house two days later, stood in the yard without coming in, and confronted Ansel: "He hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and A. knew it better than any man living, and then he asked him why he had carried the paper himself if he did not believe it." Ansel's answer was that he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet left. He had not been in that house since 1919 and would not reconcile until after his death in 1929, when his son Roy met his sister at the gate and was kind.

The 1919 circular damaged the Brant family irreparably and fractured the fabric of trust at the station. Ansel's careful, meticulous reputation—which should have protected him—became a liability instead: a man so conscientious as to personally deliver a letter accusing his wife's family looked like a man who believed the accusation. He did not. But the damage was done.

At the 1925 arbitration hearing, Vose presented his water theory to the board as the sole explanation for the low tests. He argued that water was "the oldest trick in the trade" and that "the station cannot test what is not there"—a technically sound principle. He submitted no evidence. The state inspector's consistent findings of no added water were not examined. The theory lived and died on Vose's insistence and on the public acceptance of blame that the 1919 circular had already established in the farmers' minds. When no cause was established by the board, Vose's theory was not refuted; it was simply set aside, along with the board's award of money with no explanation of why it was owed.

**Key quantities:** 1919 circular to 9 families; sent by Ansel himself to all patrons; Emmet did not speak to Orra again for 10 years (until Spring 1929, when he died); reconciliation never occurred; circular preceded dismissal by 6 years but established pattern of accusation.

**Documents:** Orra Keddie's diary (June 1919 entries); Ansel Keddie's unsent letter (spring 1925, references to 1919 circular); Bertram Kyle's retelling (1953, on typing the circular); Frick's 1946 recollections (Vose's testimony); Selby Vose's 1926 address (on water theory).

## Story 6: The 1911 Interval—An Unplanned Experiment (May 8–July 10, 1911)

On May 8, 1911, Ansel Keddie's measure broke. It was not a dramatic incident—a piece of glassware fractured—but it was an accident that, unknown to anyone at the time, would constitute the cleanest possible proof that the glassware was at fault for the low tests. For nine weeks, until July 10, 1911, the station used a borrowed measure (source unspecified in the records, but possibly from Larrow Green). During that nine-week interval, the station's average test rose from 3.61 to 3.80. On July 10, when Ansel's own measure was repaired or replaced and returned to use, the tests fell back to 3.61 immediately.

The experimental conditions were as nearly perfect as any real-world trial could be: same station, same tester, same patrons, same herds, same season (May through July, a consistent period for both years being part of the flush season), same acid, same test bottles, same hand tester, same procedure, same operator (Ansel). The only variable that changed was the measuring pipette. Ansel noted the phenomenon in his test book: "May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." On July 10, he wrote: "Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." The question mark was Ansel's honest notation of mystery. He did not explain it, either to himself or to anyone else, because he could not.

Leland Vaught, professor of dairy husbandry at the State Agricultural College, later called this interval "worth more than arithmetic." It ran itself under conditions no designed trial could have bettered. It lay unread in Ansel's test book for 47 years, until Ivy Keddie examined the book in 1958 and recognized its significance. The 1911 interval proves: (1) that the tester did not steal cream (because the tests rose when using borrowed glass); (2) that nothing in the herds, feed, or management changed (because all other variables held constant); (3) that the cause was in the equipment itself; and (4) that the problem was not water, theft, or feed stress, because none of those would resolve overnight by changing a single piece of glass.

The interval does *not* prove the exact magnitude of the error or identify which equipment was faulty (it could, in theory, have been the test bottles or acid measure, though the evidence points to the pipette). But it narrows the field decisively and provides a contemporaneous, internal consistency check on Ansel's honesty: the man who recorded this anomaly and marked it with a question mark was not a man hiding theft.

**Key quantities:** May 8–July 10, 1911 (9 weeks); tests rose from 3.61 to 3.80 during interval; fell back to 3.61 when own measure returned; recorded contemporaneously in test book.

**Documents:** Test book entries (May 8, July 10, 1911); Ansel's unsent letter (spring 1925, references 1911 anomaly); Vaught Bulletin 471 (October 1958, analysis of the interval); Roy Brant retelling (1955, noting he had wondered 47 years what it meant).

## Story 7: The 1925 Arbitration and the Failure to Look (March 17–May 4, 1925)

In January 1925, the patrons of Ostrey Hollow filed a petition of complaint against the station and the Association, claiming they had been underpaid because of low tests. The Association's officers appointed a board of arbitration to hear the dispute. The board was chaired by Alonzo Frick, an attorney from Ashlin, described as "able and impartial." Frick sat on the board, heard testimony, and tried the case as a lawyer would—examining claims, cross-examining witnesses, weighing evidence. He believed Ansel Keddie's denial immediately and still believed it in 1946 when he wrote his recollections. But Frick was not a scientist and did not understand the test. He had no knowledge of glassware or burettes or systematic error.

The board sat three times: first at the Association's office in Ashlin on March 17, 1925, then twice at the Ostrey Hollow station itself in the weigh room, at Frick's request, so that the board could see the machinery and scales and books. Frick noted: "Every physical appliance before board; saw scales tried, sampling done, test made same way twice, hands not hidden." The board had before it four documents: (1) Ansel Keddie's test book, kept in his own hand from 1896 onward; (2) the receiving book (milk received vs. fat credited); (3) Emmet Brant's private weigh book, showing his own scale matched the station's scale over the whole period; (4) Merle Strawn's circuit notebook for 1923.

The condemned crate of glassware sat in the loft directly over the weigh room, no more than twenty feet above the board's heads. No member of the board examined it. No member of the board asked for it to be brought down. No member of the board, in Frick's later recollection, "laid the second beside the fourth and looked"—that is, examined Strawn's June 12, 1923 entry condemning the glass against the long history of low tests.

Three explanations were presented: (1) Vose's water theory (disproven by Strawn's consistent finding of no added water); (2) Teague's implication of cream theft by the tester (refuted by the 1911 interval, though no one connected the two); (3) the 1908 committee's wet hay report (partially supported by college trials, but incomplete). The board dismissed all three as "not established." Yet it awarded the patrons $3,800 "without admission," meaning payment for what? Without knowing what went wrong, the board split the difference between the patrons' claimed loss (67,000 pounds butterfat, worth approximately $16,750 at 25 cents per pound) and nothing. The award was about one-fifth of the claimed loss—a face-saving gesture.

On April 30, 1925, four days before the board's official award of May 4, Ansel Keddie was dismissed. The dismissal said everything; the finding said nothing. Frick, years later, reflected: "An honest man ruined by unexplained figure, paid neighbors money without telling them what for... Four books and nothing else. It is a short list for a matter of that size... Wonders if answer was in room but not in any three explanations." The answer was literally in the room, in the loft, condemned since June 12, 1923, and never examined.

**Key quantities:** Petition January 1925; hearing March 17–May 4, 1925 (3 sessions); patrons' claimed loss 67,000 lb butterfat (~$16,750 at $0.25/lb); board found no cause established; awarded $3,800 without admission; dismissal April 30 (4 days before award); condemned crate 20 feet overhead in loft, never examined.

**Documents:** Test book; receiving book; Brant weigh book; Strawn notebook (1923); Frick's 1946 recollections; board minute book (March 17–May 4, 1925, award leaf); Kyle's retelling (1953, on the four books); Falke's 1959 report (paragraph 10).

## Story 8: The 1958 Rediscovery and the Proof (February–October 1958)

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, climbed a ladder into the loft over the intake room at the Ostrey Hollow station and found a wooden crate under a tarpaulin, broken and covered in chaff. It was the condemned glassware, stored since June 12, 1923. The original shipment had contained six dozen pipettes (seventy-two total). Thirty-five years of attrition—rodents, dust, builders climbing for wire, children exploring—had broken most. Nine pipettes remained intact and legible.

Ivy carried the crate east to Farland to the State Agricultural College's dairy laboratory. On March 9, 1958, she measured the nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette, running five deliveries from each. The data recorded on her measurement sheet:

- Pipette 1: 16.70 cc
- Pipette 2: 16.71 cc  
- Pipette 3: 16.72 cc
- Pipette 4: 16.72 cc
- Pipette 5: 16.72 cc
- Pipette 6: 16.73 cc
- Pipette 7: 16.73 cc
- Pipette 8: 16.74 cc
- Pipette 9: 16.71 cc
- Mean: 16.72 cc

The pipettes were blown for 17.6 cc. They delivered 16.72 cc on average. They were short by 0.88 cc, which is 95 hundredths of the mark—a 5% systematic low-bias error. If the pipette delivers 5% less milk than specified, the fat column in the test bottle stands proportionally lower than it should. Every test made with these pipettes read 5% too low, and the error was the same for every test because the glassware was consistently wrong.

Ivy's work was collaborated with Professor Leland Vaught of the college's dairy husbandry department. In October 1958, the college issued Bulletin No. 471, authored jointly by Ivy Keddie and Leland Vaught. The bulletin concluded: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference—seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat—stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

The mathematics closed a circle: 76,000 pounds of butterfat, at an average price of $0.25 per pound over 25 years, was worth $19,000. The patrons had received $3,800 in 1925—approximately one-fifth of the actual loss. Ansel Keddie had died in February 1934, four years after dismissal and without ever learning that his measure was accurate. Orra Keddie died in 1948, fourteen years after her husband, also without knowing. The exoneration came too late to save either of them, but it vindicated their steadfast assertion of innocence and proved that Ansel had done his job correctly for twenty-six years.

Ivy Keddie's name was printed in newspapers and college bulletins. The Ashlin Chronicle featured her story. But it was the questions that mattered: the question mark Ansel placed in his test book in July 1911; the question that Roy Brant carried for 47 years ("noticed the 1911 anomaly personally: 9 weeks when station's measure broken, all Bottom farms' tests rose, then fell back when own measure returned; could make nothing of it for 47 years"); the question that Frick posed in 1946 about whether the answer was in the room but not in any of the three explanations; and ultimately, the question Orra wrote in Ansel's diary in 1934: "He never asked them to."

**Key quantities:** Crate found February 1958; 6 dozen original (72 pipettes), 9 survived; measured March 9, 1958; mean delivery 16.72 cc (short 0.88 cc = 5%); 76,000 lb underpaid fat; worth $19,000 @ $0.25/lb; patrons received $3,800 (20%); Ansel died 1934 (24 years before proof); Orra died 1948 (10 years before proof).

**Documents:** Condemned crate; Ivy Keddie's measurement sheet (March 9, 1958); Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958, Vaught & Ivy Keddie); Ashlin Chronicle front page (October 3, 1958); Hazel Brant retelling (1981, eyewitness to loft); Duncan Keddie's 1968 family notes (on crate provenance); Leland Vaught's introduction to Bulletin 471 (mathematics of error).

# Section B: Relationships (8 points)

## B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie

Ivy Keddie (born 1931) was the granddaughter of Ansel Keddie. Ansel married Orra Brant in 1889 and was tester at Ostrey Hollow from 1896 onward. Their son was Duncan Keddie (born 1894), who taught school at Ashlin. Duncan's daughter was Ivy. Thus Ivy was Ansel's granddaughter, and she was born 36 years after his dismissal began and 3 years after his death. She never knew him as a living man, but she grew up hearing the story of his dismissal and vindication (or lack thereof). She became, in the end, the agent of his exoneration through her 1958 work measuring the condemned glassware. The Duncan Keddie family notes (1968) confirm: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter" and note that it had "already twice been printed otherwise." Some sources incorrectly call her his great-niece (Falke's 1959 report calls her "great-niece," then adds a note withdrawing this error: "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece").

---

## B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant are second cousins. Hazel is the daughter of Roy Brant. Roy is the son of Emmet Brant. Emmet was the brother of Orra Keddie, Ansel's wife. Duncan Keddie and Roy Brant are first cousins (children of a brother and sister—Ansel & Orra, and Emmet & Roy's mother). Ivy Keddie is Duncan's daughter, and Hazel is Roy's daughter. Therefore, Ivy and Hazel are second cousins (children of first cousins). The sources are consistent on this. Hazel held the weigh book after her father Roy (who inherited it after Emmet's death in 1929) and gave it to the Historical Rooms in 1963. She was a direct witness to the loft and the crate, as her family farm sat on Hessel Bottom. The two women were not close in age (Hazel was born around 1900, Ivy in 1931) and represented different generations' understanding of the same scandal.

---

## B3. Selby Vose's father and his role in what happened

Selby Vose (manager 1913–1938) was the son of Gideon Vose, founder and first president of the Association (1889–1911). The sources are emphatic about this: Duncan Keddie's notes (1968) state that Selby was "the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president — the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense." Bertram Kyle (1953) repeats the correction: "Mr. Selby Vose became manager in nineteen eleven. He was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose... the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense." This matters because it establishes Selby's authority as institutional heir and because Gideon's 1892 drafting of Article VII (payment by test, station test governs) created the system that, ironically, enabled the injustice. Gideon died by 1917 and could not explain his intentions. Selby inherited both the institution and, implicitly, his father's certainty about the water theory and low tests. The Association's culture of assumed dishonesty on the patrons' part can be traced partly to Gideon's original framing (a system that removed temptation to water) and Selby's elaboration of it in the 1919 circular and 1925 testimony.

---

## B4. The relationship between the Vose and Keddie families

The sources explicitly refute any family connection between the Voses and the Keddies. Duncan Keddie (1968) states: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either. I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her sit down. It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me. She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it." Loomis's notes record that Article VII was "hinge of whole Ostrey Hollow business; last clause (station's test governs, no appeal) written 1892 by man (Gideon Vose) who died 1917 and could not be asked about it." Cleve Oram (1977) reports a rumor: "And I will say this while I am on families, because you will hear it anyway and you may as well hear it from somebody who is not repeating it out of malice. The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley. My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper." But this is clearly hearsay that Oram cannot substantiate, and it contradicts Duncan's direct assertion based on his mother Orra's exhaustive knowledge of family relations. The most reliable answer is: no documented connection exists, though local rumor (untraceable and unconfirmed) claimed one. Duncan's testimony as an insider is stronger than Oram's hearsay.
