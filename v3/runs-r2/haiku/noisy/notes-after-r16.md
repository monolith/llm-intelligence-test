# Notes after Retelling 16

## People and Relationships

**Vira Toland** — Born 1912 in Ostrey Hollow, never left except 2 years at Ashlin; records the scandal 1979 at age 67. Narrator of retelling 1.

**Ansel Keddie** — Station tester at Ostrey Hollow, began work 1895, kept test book 1896–1924 in own hand over 29 seasons. Wife Orra Keddie. Eventually caught taking cream, dismissed April 30 (year unspecified but after 1925 arbitration). Died in 1930s (Vira) or 1934 specifically (Ivy). Made unsent letter spring 1925. Gave statement to arbitration board March 17, 1925 (9 sentences). Grandfather of Ivy Keddie. Ansel tested "as he was taught and as the state man had seen him do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons" (per Frick). Maintained he never took a pound of cream. Told Frick privately in corridor that he suspected the patrons (farmers) were at fault but would not accuse neighbors. Dismissed April 30, 1925 (4 days before board's May 4 award, while board still sitting, no finding against him). Defended the test as done the same way twice, hands not hidden (per Frick's observation at station).

**Orra Keddie** — Ansel's wife. First cousin of old Mrs. Vose (Mrs. Gideon Vose). Sister of Emmet Brant. After 1919 circular accusing water in cans, stopped speaking to her brother Emmet; had no communication for years. Outlived Ansel by many years.

**Emmet Brant** — Largest producer on Hessel Bottom, brought more milk to station than any other patron every year. Brother of Orra Keddie. Had most cows, most hay ground. Paid highest for water-accusation in 1919. Never spoke to Orra after 1919 circular (actually conflict lasted from 1919, Ansel notes he and Emmet "have not spoken since the circular"). Son is Roy Brant (had weigh book, lent to Ivy Keddie 1958). Father of Roy. Born 1854, died 1929. Kept weigh book from January 1, 1898 through his death (described as ledger, plain, ruled by hand, two columns per day of delivery: weight on own scale, then station's figure).

**Roy Brant** — Son of Emmet Brant. Writing 1955 at age 67, still milking. Kept weigh book after his father died (after 1929), continued in same way; planned to pass to Hazel when done. First cousin to Duncan Keddie (Ansel and Orra's son). Lent weigh book to Ivy Keddie 1958 for college work. Narrator of retelling 9. Notes two columns agreed perfectly for milk weight despite family conflict and test disputes. Observed 17-hundredth year-round gap (not seasonal). Noticed the 1911 anomaly personally: 9 weeks when station's measure broken, all Bottom farms' tests rose, then fell back when own measure returned; "could make nothing of it" for 47 years. Never forgave Ansel for not standing up to the 1919 water circular. From 1915, four Bottom men hauled summer milk over Redlow Ridge to Alder Corners cheese factory (opened 1914) for better price and shorter haul in flush, returned to Ostrey Hollow rest of year (16 seasons, last summer 1930); Roy's biggest herd couldn't afford to be talked about so didn't go over.

**Rosalie Cudd** — Daughter of Jerome Cudd (founder of Alder Corners 1913, died March 1941 between setting and cutting vat). Now runs Alder Corners (17 years from 1941 on, as of 1958). Was 12 when first vat set, drying hoops before tall enough to see in. Letter writer 1958 to Ivy Keddie. Jerome tested milk on own bench, kept own book, never compared with Ostrey Hollow figures (never entered his head). Four Hessel Bottom men brought milk summers 1915–1930 (starting before daylight to have cans on platform by 7 AM because milk warm after 4-mile switchback haul). Their milk tested 3.78–3.82 consistently (averaging 3.80 per season). Milk was good milk, never gave hour's trouble in vat, never one complaint from Jerome despite 1919 circular. Rosalie notes watered milk would fail in cheese (weak curd, soft body, low yield)—never happened in 16 seasons with these four men. Jerome wrote in summer of 1919: "The four Hessel Bottom men, summer of 1919: 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge." Listened to 40 years of Ordell County explaining Hessel Bottom to itself; never once saw anybody go up and look.

**Selby Vose** — Became manager 1913, served until 1938 (Loomis notes 1901–1940 context). Son of Gideon Vose. Signed annual station sheets every year. Sent 1919 circular warning Ostrey Hollow patrons of water detection. Supported $3,800 payment in 1925. Gave annual address 1926 presenting his theory: water was added to cans, stopped when Association looked; attributes 1924 test rise to scrutiny (not equipment). Said "without naming a name." At arbitration, put forward water theory: milk came in thinned, little water in can "oldest trick in the trade," station cannot test what is not there. Put it "without heat and without proof" (per Frick). In 1926 address, matter-of-factly mentioned June 12, 1923 (actually said "twelfth of July, 1923" erroneously) order for new apparatus, said "apparatus wears"; treats it as routine maintenance. Said "the average for 1924 stood at 3.90" (per his address), though station test book says 3.80 (discrepancy).

**Gideon Vose** — Founder/first president of Association 1889, held office until 1911 (22 years). Drafted by-laws himself. Father of Selby Vose. Died by 1917 (per Loomis: "died in 1917 and could not be asked about it afterward" re: Article VII). Old Gideon Vose is Mrs. Gideon Vose's husband.

**Old Mrs. Vose** — Gideon Vose's wife. First cousin of Orra Keddie. Vira notes the family connection made the 1919 accusation particularly damaging ("if you follow me, it was somebody's cousin saying it").

**Effie Loomis** — Secretary 1901–1940, kept minute book in own hand. Writes "Notes toward a history" 1941 (one year after leaving desk). Records Association history and gaps in documentation. Notes crate never mentioned in 1925 hearing. Notes 1889–1892 minute book lost in 1938 office move.

**Wilbur Teague** — Bookkeeper 1905–1945 (40 years). Invented "station gain" line 1905 to record difference between credited and received fat. Carried the consistent 5.25% station gain to general account every year. Testified to board 1925: cream portable, one man for 29 seasons allows systematic underpayment. Confronted Vose 1916 about the account, was told to let alone. Remarks he was "Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience." At arbitration, put forward cream-theft implication in the way an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it—was asked about the station's account and answered at length about how such an account would look if a man were helping himself, then observed that he made no charge against anybody. Writing retrospective 1948.

**Hobart Sill** — Buttermaker at Larrow Green 1893–1934 (41 years), never sent out a tub he would not eat himself. Speaking remarks recorded 1934 on his last day. Received Ostrey Hollow cream from the platform; cream always ran heavy (more fat in cans than papers said was bought at station). Every year, every page. Weighed it himself, most years; took receiving book figures and weighed over again in cold room, never took another man's figure. Larrow Green patrons' milk ran 3.78 taking one year with another. Hollow milk "really 3.85 and no better," per his observation. Something changed in "one summer, along in the twenties"—figures came up and stayed up, nothing changed but season, "something got stopped, something got said to somebody." 1919 circular about water—thought well of it at time (only plain thing office did), but it changed nothing because "a paper never emptied a can." Never went up to Hollow but twice in life. Made good butter from their cream regardless. Provided his own glassware to open Ostrey Hollow station 1896 (no outfit of their own yet, ordered and not come); went to his own bench and made up outfit for them, sent it up valley; they sent it back chipped spring 1897, never said a word about that. First season 1896 (May to end of year) took in about 900,000 pounds. Fat through those years worth 27 cents per pound.

**Merle Strawn** — State dairy inspector, Ordell circuit 1907–1936. Visited Ostrey Hollow station repeatedly: found scales true, no water added (consistently), condemned glassware June 12, 1923. Ran milk pipettes against burette June 12, 1923: "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them." Verified new glass October 1923. Notes in circuit book confirm "tests improved" by 1924 with new glass. Retired 1936, notebooks went to state dairy office; found at Farland library 1947, photostatted 1958. Kept 28 pocket oilcloth-cover circuit books. Entry June 12, 1923: "Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water, as always here. Ran their milk pipettes against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them." October 1923: "Ostrey Hollow. New glass in use since July. Ran the new pipettes against the burette. They deliver their mark. Scales true." 1924: "Ostrey Hollow. Scales true, no added water, tests improved. Their book gives the year at 3.80." 1925 fall: "Ostrey Hollow, in the fall. Scales true, no added water, tests improved. New man at the tester's bench this year. He is quick, and quick is not the thing I look for." 1928: "Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. The station is quieter than it was. The loft over the intake room is full of odds and ends and I told them a loft over a milk room is a place for nothing but air, and was told in return that it had been full since before the war."

**Ivy Keddie** — Granddaughter of Ansel Keddie, daughter of Duncan Keddie (who taught school at Ashlin). Student/researcher at State Agricultural College at Farland. Found condemned crate in Ostrey Hollow station loft February 1958. Measured 9 surviving pipettes against burette March 9, 1958: all blown 17.6 cc, delivered mean 16.72 cc (short 0.88 cc = 95 hundredths of mark). Proved in 1958 (33 years after Ansel's dismissal, 24 years after his death) that glassware was the cause. Gave talk to Ordell County Historical Society 1959 presenting mathematics of error and exoneration. Collaborated with Professor Vaught on Bulletin 471 (October 1958). Second cousin to Hazel Brant. Borrowed Roy Brant's weigh book 1958 for college work (postscript in retelling 9, written 1958, confirms this).

**Duncan Keddie** — Son of Ansel Keddie, father of Ivy. Taught school at Ashlin. First cousin to Roy Brant.

**Roy Brant** — [see above; doubled entry corrected in consolidated notes]

**Hazel Brant** — Kept weigh book 1962. First cousin once removed of young researcher (Ivy) from college (1958). Daughter of Duncan Keddie (per retelling 9: "My daughter Hazel and his daughter Ivy are second cousins"). Second cousin to Ivy Keddie.

**Halbert Nym** — Curator, Ordell County Historical Rooms. Catalogues 16 items 1972, writes accession notes.

**Alonzo Frick** — Attorney at Ashlin, chairman of board of arbitration March 17–May 4, 1925. Described as able and impartial. Writing recollections 1946 at age 74. Says he tried titles and boundaries 40 years, two homicides, but Ostrey Hollow stayed with him. Not litigation, arbitration with no appeal, no reported decision. Sat three sessions: first at Ashlin office March 17, 1925; second and third at Ostrey Hollow station itself in weigh room (Frick's request, to see plant instead of hearing it described). Every physical appliance before board; saw scales tried, sampling done, test made same way twice, hands not hidden. Did not understand the test (is chemistry, he is lawyer). Believed Ansel Keddie's denial of theft immediately and still in 1946. Regrets board dismissed Keddie April 30 while board still sitting (no finding against him from board). Found no cause established, awarded $3,800 without admission. Notes patrons' own reckoning of loss was 67,000 pounds fat (per their petition), never disturbed by any evidence. Award without finding is unsatisfactory. Wonders if answer was in room but not in any three explanations put forward. Three exhibits before board (four sources say): test book, receiving book, Brant weigh book, 1923 circuit notebook (per Frick: test book, receiving book, Brant weigh book, 1923 circuit notebook). Noted: "Four books and nothing else. It is a short list for a matter of that size."

**Dell Rundle** — Schoolteacher at Larrow Green, jubilee pamphlet author 1939 ("The Larrow Valley Creamery: Fifty Years"), reprinted 1959. Taught school "a good many years." Listened to history in pieces at funerals and back of store. Wrote it as "what the valley remembers, tidied." Covered founding 1889 (Gideon Vose president), creamery 1891, Article VII, Ostrey Hollow station 1896, trouble from 1902, 1908 committee, 1919 circular, settled opinion was cream theft, 1923 condemnation and new glass, 1925 arbitration without admission, 1914 Alder Corners opened over ridge (Jerome Cudd). NOTE ADDED IN PRESS (1939): Rundle checked Association's old ledgers in office, found station account entries showing station gain 5.25% every year with heavy cream reaching Larrow Green (not light cream stolen). Must correct his chapter on theft narrative—"Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand. I leave it as written and correct it here." 1959 reprint added footnote (by Rundle, now too old to rewrite): "The Hollow matter has since been taken up again at the college at Farland by Miss Ivy Keddie, who is second cousin to Hazel Brant of Hessel Bottom. Eleven pipettes out of the station's old condemned crate were found still to be in existence. I am too old to rewrite a pamphlet and I refer the reader to the college." [Note: contradiction on number of pipettes—Rundle says 11, other sources (Ivy Keddie measurement, Vaught bulletin) say 9.]

**Nyle Grigg** — Farmer of standing, chairman of 1908 investigating committee. Covering letter 1919 reissuing the 1908 report. Sampled Hessel Bottom herds through whole of 1908 (every month, barn and pasture, night's milk and morning's, unannounced). Weighed, watched test made and made again. Found milk whole, scales honest, no dishonesty of any kind. Concluded wet hay thins cream, remedy lies with land not station. Not patron of Ostrey Hollow station (hauls to Larrow Green), so no interest in answer. Been abused by men on both sides. Cites Bulletin No. 214 from State Agricultural College (feeding trial on bog hay, weighed rations, no interest in outcome) as finding "two tenths" depression in winter feeding, confirming his committee's finding arrived at "by another road and by better men, three years after we set our names to it." [Discrepancy: Bulletin 214 actually stated "five hundredths" in winter, "nothing that could be measured" in summer—Grigg's "two tenths" is 4× higher.] Says remedy is drain, lime, feed coarse hay with mixed ration not by itself. 1908 report: sampled through Jan–Mar 1908 only (winter months). Grigg says Hessel Bottom is wet meadow, springy year-round, under water every spring, sedge/rush/coarse sour grasses, hay cut late, fed November to April as main dependence. Herds thin in flank at end of winter, slow to come to flesh in spring, coats rough later than hill herds, do well in flow but wet land generous of milk withholds quality. Observed general condition of stock "as men who have kept cattle all their lives are entitled to observe it."

**Dr. Roland Kepp** — Veterinary surgeon, 30 years practice. Paper read 1926 to Ordell County Farm Club. Asks first what cattle standing on when herd doing badly; finds in nine of ten cases answer is under feet. Goes about county 30 years keeping eyes open. Wet bottoms yield milk poorer in fat by "a fifth of a point" and every practical man knows it. Set hill herds against bottom herds, find difference farm for farm. Difference does not depend on breed, man, barn—depends on ground. Hessel Bottom "completest example of wet meadow in this county." Six farms cut hay and pasture. Lies along creek; springs come out at foot of slope, run in it year-round; part under water every spring, not fit to walk on until well into May. Hay is sedge, main winter dependence, almost whole of it. Gap figure given to him and satisfied himself: "twenty-one hundredths of a point — 0.21" between Ostrey Hollow and valley at large. Complaint perfectly just; never doubted it. Committee of practical men went over ground 1908, found herds sound, milk whole, scales honest, laid cause to hay. Matter before committees and boards since, not settled, will not be settled by shouting. Settled already in literature for anybody who will read. Bulletin 214 from College: "The College fed the bog hay under control and measured what it did, and it found the butter fat test depressed by two tenths in the winter feeding." [Same discrepancy: Bulletin actually says "five hundredths," not "two tenths."] "Two tenths from the College, laid alongside the fifth of a point I have found in the field and the twenty-one hundredths that station has been complaining of, and the three agree so nearly that I do not know what more a reasonable man requires." Addresses 1911 objection: dismisses as "six weeks in the flush of the season... June and July, when every herd in the county is at its best and the stock are off the wet ground and up on the aftermath... I am astonished that anybody should found an argument on six weeks of summer figures. I could produce you six weeks in any year that would prove anything you liked." [Discrepancy: other sources say 1911 measure broken 9 weeks, not 6.] Remedy: drain bottom, open old ditches and cut new, lime again and again, sow down when it will carry seeding, keep stock off in wet of spring (hoof does more harm in day than season will repair), never feed coarse hay alone—feed with mixed ration and mineral supplement per College. Fat 27 cents per pound through those years; a fifth of a point taken off every hundredweight year-round will pay for great deal of tile. In discussion, dismissed whether problem in cows (some best cattle on wet land test poorly; some poor cattle on hills test well; man may improve herd 20 years and never get fat up while ground is sour) or station (no competence to judge testing, never read a test, would not know how; but when cause found in soil, herbage, stock condition, College trial, and every wet bottom in county alike, not good sense to look for second cause in building).

**Leland Vaught** — Professor of dairy husbandry, State Agricultural College at Farland. Introduction and method to Bulletin 471, October 1958 (collaboration with Ivy Keddie). States question asked at intervals 50 years: why butterfat tests at Ostrey Hollow skimming station ran below Larrow Green creamery 7 miles down same valley. Answer: piece of glass. Nobody lacked intelligence to find it; what they lacked was occasion to suspect the pipette (one instrument in test that plant cannot check against anything else, instrument on which every figure in plant depends). Test consists of four operations: measured charge milk drawn into pipette, delivered into test bottle; acid added dissolves everything but fat; bottle spun in hand tester, fat rises into graduated neck; operator reads fat directly in percent. Operator weighs nothing; reads length of column of fat in narrow neck, calls it percentage—entitled to do so for one reason only: charge of milk is fixed and known quantity. Maker rules neck for that quantity and no other. Every mark means "this much fat, out of the standard charge." Standard charge in state and on glassware examined: 17.6 cubic centimeters. If pipette delivers less than mark, less milk goes in, less fat liberated, shorter column stands in neck (which knows nothing of substitution), reading is low in exact proportion by which charge short. Three consequences: (1) error is proportional not additive (removes fixed fraction not fixed points, rich milk loses more absolutely, ratio between any two tests on same short glass undisturbed, station shows perfectly orderly internally consistent figures, herds rank correctly against each other, wrong only against outside world); (2) error is precise (leaking pipe or slipping scale gives scattered error attracting attention, pipette delivering same amount every time has appearance of excellent work); (3) error invisible from inside plant (every other element can be verified against standard—scales with test weights, tester's speed counted, acid checked for strength, bottle's graduation with mercury—but pipette not checked against anything because in ordinary practice pipette is the standard, the measure itself cannot be caught by measuring with it, can only be caught by burette brought from outside, burettes reached state's field men very late in history). Of lot bought 1897, five dozen pipettes purchased, nine recovered from condemned crate unbroken and legible. Stems blown 17.6 cc, delivered against calibrated burette five runs each, mean delivery of nine: 16.27 cc. [Discrepancy: Ivy Keddie measurement sheet says mean 16.72 cc; Vaught says 16.27 cc.] Station's own glass went into use May 1, 1898 [Ivy retelling says "spring following" 1897; Ammon & Sons invoice April 14, 1897, in use May 1, 1897—so May 1, 1897, not 1898], remained until state inspector condemned lot June 1923. Before and after, tests made on other glass. Method: fill pipette to mark with water at room temperature, wipe tip, deliver in ordinary working manner (same interval drainage, same touch to wall of receiver) into burette of certified graduation. Five deliveries from each, average. Afternoon's work, no apparatus creamery doesn't own save burette. No reason why every station in state should not be checked once this way. 1911 interval "worth more than arithmetic": nine weeks station's own pipette broken, borrowed measure used, patrons same, herds same, pasture same, season same, acid same, bottles same, tester same, tester's habits same. One variable changed. Average test rose from 3.61 to 3.80, held nine weeks, returned to 3.61 day own measure came back (recorded contemporaneously in operator's hand, man who didn't know what he was recording, set question mark). Spent working life urging students to prefer experiment to calculation. Here one that ran itself under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, lay unread 47 years. Disposes of every explanation looking to herds, ground, men (none altered, figures were). Ratio of delivered to marked: 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244. Recorded station average affected years: 3.61; true average: 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.905, shortfall 0.295 point. Applied to 40,000,000 lb milk received 25 full delivery years: 118,000 lb butterfat delivered not credited. [Discrepancy: Ivy Keddie notes say 76,000 lb; Vaught says 118,000 lb.] Not readily reconciled with Association's own average 3.80 in year following glassware replacement. Ledgers carried annually credit for fat received at creamery excess of fat credited at station, heading "station gain," 5.25% of butterfat actually delivered. Bulletin 214 (1911 College feeding trial on bog hay): "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Five hundredths in winter, nothing in summer. Discrepancy under examination present every month year-round, six times largest effect trial could produce under conditions harsher than farmer would impose. Closing paragraph (Vaught and Ivy Keddie collaboration): "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

## Places

**Larrow Valley** — Geographic region containing the Association and stations.

**Larrow Green** — Main creamery built 1891 on Larrow Creek. Office at Ashlin. Larrow Green also the location of the Association's founding 1889. Did better test averages than Ostrey Hollow (by little under 2 tenths point per Nym retelling). Main creamery where Hobart Sill buttermaker 1893–1934.

**Ostrey Hollow** — Skimming station 7 miles up valley from Larrow Green. At very top of Hollow where road bends and goes to nothing but ridge (per Vira). Low whitewashed building, cold inside, smelled of milk steam hot iron. Engine audible before last turn. Opened before Merle Strawn's circuit began (1907). Opening 1896 (May), May 4 entry in test book. Station also called "skimming station." Contains loft over intake room where crate was stored. Loft "has never been cleared out since it was put up" (per Rundle); full "since before the war" (per Strawn 1928). Building "plain one, boarded and battened, with the intake room at the road end and a loft over it... and behind it the creek, which in a wet April comes up to the sill and has twice come over it."

**Hessel Bottom** — Wet meadow below hamlet in Ostrey Hollow. 7 farms had rights on it (though Grigg and others say 6). Largest was Emmet Brant's. Milk from this region tested lower on average. "Hundred and sixty acres of wet meadow lying under the hill at Ostrey Hollow" (per Roy Brant). Water stands on low end into May. Soft place by alders "has taken a horse to the hock in my time and my father's." Hay coarse, cuts late. "Never failed to grow something" (per Roy). Springs come out at foot of slope, run in it year-round. Part under water every spring, not fit to walk on until well into May (per Kepp). Ground springy year-round, sedge/rush/coarse sour grasses (Grigg's 1908 report). Lying along creek under Ostrey Hollow (Grigg). Lying along creek under Ostrey Hollow, Kepp says "completest example of wet meadow in this county." Hay sedge (Kepp), "coarse and it cuts late" (Roy). Herds thin in flank at end of winter, slow to flesh in spring, coats rough later than hill herds (Grigg). Generous of flow, milk quantity good but fat first to suffer when stressed (Kepp).

**Alder Corners Cheese Company** — Factory over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County. Test book kept there. Never left Vessey County. Test figures copied 1958 from 1919 season book (Rosalie Cudd, niece of Jerome Cudd, now runs factory—actually daughter, took over 1941 when Jerome died). Book moved to Vessey County Historical Society 1970. Tested milk from Hessel Bottom men in summer flush; found milk tested about 19 hundredths higher at Alder Corners than at Ostrey Hollow station. Four Hessel Bottom men hauled summers 1915–1930 (16 seasons, last summer 1930). Road over Redlow Ridge, 4 miles switchback. Opens April/May most years, shuts November. No winter hauling possible (drifts in cut). Opened 1914 (Jerome Cudd built). Factory on flat below church, had spring water on lot, hard road out east. Jerome tested on own bench in room off intake, own glassware bought new, washed evening, kept in rack he made, wrote own book, never compared with other station's figures (never entered his head).

**Ashlin** — County seat. Office of Association. Location of arbitration board March 17–May 4, 1925. Where Duncan Keddie taught school. Where Vira lived 2 years after marriage. Where Alonzo Frick practices as attorney.

**Ordell County** — County containing Larrow Valley, Ashlin, Ostrey Hollow.

**Vessey County** — Across ridge from Ostrey Hollow. Contains Alder Corners Cheese Company (test figures 1919 season). Test book never left Vessey County. Moved to Vessey County Historical Society 1970. Over Redlow Ridge from Ostrey Hollow/Hessel Bottom. Hardest reach in winter (ridge closes).

**Farland** — Location of State Agricultural College. Merle Strawn's circuit books housed at library since 1947. Ivy Keddie studied there. Bulletin 471 issued October 1958. Bulletin 214 issued 1911. School where Ivy was student/researcher.

## Organizations and Entities

**Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association** — Founded 1889 at Larrow Green. Three components: creamery at Larrow Green (built 1891), office at Ashlin, skimming station at Ostrey Hollow (7 miles up valley). Article VII adopted 1892: patrons paid by butterfat test at station where delivered (no appeal from station test). By-laws drafted by Gideon Vose. Officers in period 1901–1940: Gideon Vose (president 1889–1911), Selby Vose (manager 1913–1938), Effie Loomis (secretary 1901–1940), Wilbur Teague (bookkeeper 1905–1945), Hobart Sill (buttermaker). Payment by test came in with 1893 meeting (per Grigg—notes say "by-laws provided" per 1892 Article VII, slight discrepancy in dating).

**Board of Arbitration** — Constituted 1925. Sat Ashlin March 17, 1925 (first session), then Ostrey Hollow station itself in weigh room (second and third sessions, per Frick's request). Chairman Alonzo Frick. Heard patrons and officers, had books and appliances of station before it. Rendered award: "The board having heard the patrons and the officers, and having had before it the books and appliances of the station, finds no cause established. The board awards to the Ostrey Hollow patrons the sum of thirty-eight hundred dollars ($3,800.00), without admission, to be charged to the station account." Award date May 4, 1925 (last leaf of minute book).

**Ammon & Sons Dairy Supply, Tarnet** — Supplier. Invoice 14 April 1897 for initial equipment (6 doz pipettes 17.6 cc @ .35 = $25.20, 12 doz test bottles 8% neck @ .22 = $31.68, acid measure $.90, hand tester $18.00, freight $2.15, total $77.93). In use 1 May 1897 (per Sill; per Vaught says May 1, 1898 for "station's own glass" but Ammon invoice clearly 1897). Letter 2 July 1923 acknowledging order 28 June for replacement pipettes and bottles, promising shipment that month, declining to comment on earlier lot.

**State Agricultural College at Farland** — Issued Bulletin No. 214 (1911): "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." (Note: Grigg, Kepp, Frick incorrectly cite as "two tenths" or garble the exact statement; actual text says "five hundredths" in winter, "nothing that could be measured" in summer.) Issued Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958): "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

**Ordell County Historical Rooms** — At Ashlin. Received station test book 1959, Ammon & Sons crate 1959, Brant weigh book 1963. Catalogued 16 items by Nym 1972.

**Vessey County Historical Society** — Received Alder Corners Cheese Company test book 1970.

**Farland library** — Received Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks (28 volumes) 1947 from state dairy office; notebooks photostatted 1958.

## Documents Cited

### Test Book (Item 3 — Nym retelling; Item 1 in Frick's list)
First leaf: "Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie."
- Columns: date, patron, pounds, test, fat
- Kept one hand, 29 seasons (1896–1924)
- Never figure written over figure, errors crossed with initials

Summary leaf made up 1922 by Ansel Keddie:
"Average test of all patrons, this station: 1896, 3.80. 1897, part year. 1898, 3.61. And 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80."

Entries for 1911 anomaly:
- "May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure."
- "July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."

Provenance: station 4 May 1896; impounded by board of arbitration March 1925; Association office Ashlin 1925–1959; Rooms 1959.

### Emmet Brant Weigh Book (Item 4 — Nym retelling; Item 3 in Frick's list; Roy Brant retelling 9)
Small quarto, sheepskin, greasy corners. Daily from 1 January 1897 [1898 in Nym, 1897 in Roy]. Actually began 1 January 1898 per Roy Brant entry: "Year 1898... Paid on test of 3.42" (not 1910 entry as sometimes cited). Kept by Hessel Bottom household throughout. Entry for 1910 (Roy Brant quotes it verbatim): "Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42." Roy emphasizes two scales agreed perfectly throughout whole run of years, barring pound or two on days can slopped. Price that year 24 cents; over whole stretch years, average one year with another, about 25 cents. ## Critical Facts and Calculations

**Glass Defect (Two measurements):** 
- Six dozen pipettes purchased Ammon & Sons invoice 14 April 1897, blown for 17.6 c.c. delivery
- Vaught (Bulletin 471): Nine surviving pipettes measured against burette (five runs each), mean delivery 16.27 c.c., short 1.33 c.c. = 92.44% of mark
- Ivy Keddie (measurement sheet, March 9, 1958): Nine pipettes, mean of five runs each, 16.72 c.c., short 0.88 c.c. = 95% of mark
- [DISCREPANCY: Vaught and Ivy give different numbers; Ivy's data (16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71) average to 16.72, not 16.27. Vaught may have used different subset or method.]
- Every test made at station for 26+ years read at ~95% of truth (5% systematic low-bias error per Ivy; 7.56% per Vaught's 16.27 calculation).

**Math of Underpayment:**
- Recorded average test (Ostrey Hollow): 3.61%
- True average test (corrected 95%): 3.80% (3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.8)
- Gap: 19 hundredths of a percentage point
- This gap = 5% of true fat content (19 hundredths is 5% of 3.80)
- Also = 5.25% of recorded fat content (19 hundredths is 5.25% of 3.61)
- [Vaught's alternative: 16.27 cc delivery gives ratio 0.9244, true average 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.905, gap 0.295 point, but this differs from the 19-hundredth gap consistently cited.]

**Total Milk and Fat Over 25 Years (1897/1898–1922/1923):**
- Station intake: 40 million pounds (Loomis, Nym, Vaught); Teague says 44 million
- Recorded fat at 3.61%: 1,444,000 pounds
- True fat at 3.80%: 1,520,000 pounds
- Difference (unpaid): 76,000 pounds fat (Ivy Keddie, Vaught bulletin conclusion)
- Vaught calculation Section V: 118,000 lb (based on 16.27 cc mean, 0.9244 ratio, 40M lb milk)
- At average 25 cents/pound: $19,000 owed (based on 76,000 lb); Vaught's 118,000 would be $29,500
- Arbitration award: $3,800 (approximately one-fifth of $19,000, or 13% of $29,500)

**Test Readings:**
- Ostrey Hollow average 1896–1922: 3.61% (recorded)
- Ostrey Hollow 1911 May 8–July 10 (borrowed measure, 9 weeks): 3.80%
- Ostrey Hollow 1924 (new glass): 3.80% (per Strawn); 3.90% (per Vose's 1926 address—discrepancy)
- Larrow Green average over same period: "a little under two tenths of a point better" than 3.61 (so approximately 3.80–3.85%); Sill says 3.78
- Hessel Bottom at Alder Corners (summer, 16 seasons 1915–1930): 3.78–3.82 (averaging 3.80)
- Hessel Bottom milk tested "0.17 to 0.21 points" below Larrow Green (depending on source: Roy Brant 0.17, Kepp 0.21, others ~0.19)

**Glass Measurements:**
- Pipette specification: 17.6 c.c.
- Actual mean delivery (Ivy's sheet, 9 pipettes, 5 runs each): 16.72 c.c.

- Percentage (Vaught): 92.44% (16.27 / 17.6 = 0.9244)
- Systematic error: 5% low bias (Ivy) or 7.56% low bias (Vaught)

**Prices:**

- Roy Brant: 24 cents year 1910, average 25 cents overall
- Sill: 27 cents through those years
- Kepp: 27 cents through those years
- Nym notes: 25 cents average over 25 years

## Conflicting Narratives and Interpretations

**Cream Theft vs. Water Addition vs. Equipment Defect vs. Wet Hay vs. Feed Stress:**

1. **Vira Toland (1979):** Ansel Keddie "took the cream" and was caught at it; this is "why the numbers went right the summer after."

2. **Selby Vose (1926 address):** Water was being added to cans by Ostrey Hollow patrons; "Water going into cans at the head of that valley and stopped when the Association began to look." He defends his 1919 circular warning about water detection. "Little water in a can is not a felony in a man's own mind. It is a way of making a hard year come out even." At arbitration (per Frick): milk came in thinned, water "oldest trick in the trade," station cannot test what is not there.

3. **Ansel Keddie (1925 letter, 1925 statement):** Denies theft. "I have never taken a pound of cream out of that station and there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself." Offers no explanation for low readings except the unexplained 1911 anomaly marked "?". Privately told Frick he suspected the patrons (farmers) were at fault but would not accuse neighbors.

4. **Wilbur Teague (1925 testimony, 1948 retrospective):** Structural opportunity for theft: "cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons... a business which is arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken from it." At arbitration (per Frick): implied cream theft through his description of how such an account would look, but made no charge against anybody.

5. **Hobart Sill (1934):** Cream from Hollow always came in heavy (more fat than papers said). "Whatever was wrong at that station—and something was wrong at that station for a very long time—it was not the weighing." Notes something changed "one summer, along in the twenties"—figures came up and stayed up, "something got stopped." Thinks water was added (supported 1919 circular at the time).

6. **1908 Committee (Nyle Grigg, chairman):** Sampled herds Jan–Mar 1908 (winter months only). Found milk whole, scales honest, no dishonesty of any kind. Concluded wet hay of bottom thins cream, remedy is land not station.

7. **Nyle Grigg (1919 covering letter):** Stands by 1908 report. Cites Bulletin 214 (incorrectly) as confirming "two tenths" depression from bog hay (actual Bulletin says "five hundredths" in winter, "nothing" in summer).

8. **Dr. Roland Kepp (1926):** Wet meadows are acid, poor in lime/phosphates; cows rob their own frames when minerals low, fat is first economy; gap is 0.21 (21 hundredths). Discusses Bulletin 214 (also incorrectly citing "two tenths"). Dismisses 1911 anomaly as six weeks of summer flush (cows off ground, on aftermath)—says summer figures prove anything you want. [Note: May 8–July 10 is not "six weeks" (more like 9 weeks) and includes May when cows may not yet be on full pasture.]

9. **Merle Strawn (1923, 1924, 1928):** Scales true, no water added ("as always here"). June 12, 1923: condemned pipettes—"measures will not deliver their mark." October 1923: new glass verified, "deliver their mark." 1924: "tests improved." No mention of theft, water addition, or feed effects; focuses on equipment.

10. **Alonzo Frick (1925–1946):** Heard three explanations (water, theft, wet hay). Believed Ansel's denial. Found no cause established (award without admission). Regrets dismissing honest man by unexplained figure.

11. **Dell Rundle (1939, with 1939 NOTE IN PRESS):** Settled opinion was cream theft. But when he checked ledgers, found station gain (5.25%) was heavy cream arriving, not light cream stolen—"Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand."

12. **Rosalie Cudd (1958):** Four Hessel Bottom men's milk at Alder Corners tested 3.78–3.82 (averaging 3.80) for 16 summers. Milk was good milk, never gave trouble in vat, never complained. Watered milk would have failed in cheese (weak curd, low yield)—never happened. Dismisses the theories: "It came to me the way weather comes, in pieces and late... I never once heard anybody go up there and look."

13. **Roy Brant (1955):** Two scales (father's and station's) agreed perfectly for weight; gap was in test, not weighing. 17-hundredth gap was year-round (contradicts seasonal wet-hay theory). 1911 anomaly: 9 weeks borrowed measure, all Bottom farms' tests rose, then fell back—"could make nothing of it" for 47 years. Never forgave Ansel for not defending against 1919 circular.

14. **Ivy Keddie (1958 measurement, 1958 talk, 1958 Bulletin 471):** Definitive proof—defective glass. "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there... the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty." (Note: this is consistent with Ivy's own 16.72 cc mean, not Vaught's 16.27 cc.)

15. **Leland Vaught (1958 Bulletin 471):** Confirms defective glass. Emphasizes pipette is the one instrument plant cannot check against anything else, and it is the measure itself (cannot be caught by measuring with it, only by external burette). 1911 anomaly disposes of every explanation looking to herds, ground, men. Error is proportional (rich milk loses more absolutely, ratio between tests on short glass undisturbed—orderly figures, wrong only against outside world). Precise error (delivers same amount every time, not scattered). Invisible from inside (pipette is the standard, cannot measure the measure). [Calculation discrepancy: Vaught's 16.27 cc mean vs. Ivy's 16.72 cc; Vaught derives 118,000 lb underpaid, but Bulletin conclusion cites 76,000 lb.]

**The 1911 Anomaly — The Definitive Experiment:**
- May 8, 1911: station's pipette broke (per Ansel's entry)
- Borrowed measure used (from Larrow Green, properly calibrated at 17.6 cc)
- Tests rose from 3.61 to 3.80 immediately (all Bottom farms, all patrons)
- July 10, 1911: own measure returned, back in use
- Tests dropped to 3.61 same day
- Ansel noted: "Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."
- Explanation (proven 1958): borrowed measure was properly calibrated (17.6 cc true); station's own pipettes were 5% short; short measures compressed readings by 5%; when proper measures used, true readings appeared (3.80); when station's short measures back, readings dropped again to 3.61 (the 95% reading).

If theft was occurring: tests should remain low even with borrowed measure (because Ansel would still be stealing). They didn't—they rose to true level. This proves low readings were due to measurement error, not theft.

If water was being added: tests should remain low with borrowed measure (because water would still be in cans). They didn't—they rose. This proves water-addition theory (at least for that 9-week period) is false, or that patrons stopped adding water only during that period (unlikely).

If feed was primary cause: tests should remain lower than true average with borrowed measure (because feed didn't change). They rose to 3.80, the true average. This proves feed/wet hay alone cannot account for the gap, though it may contribute.

Vaught (1958): "I have spent my working life urging students to prefer an experiment to a calculation. Here is one that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven years. It disposes of every explanation that looks to the herds, to the ground or to the men, for the sufficient reason that none of those was altered in those nine weeks, and the figures were."

**Bulletin 214 Misinterpretation:**
- Actual text: "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."
- Grigg (1919): claims it found "two tenths" depression (4× actual)
- Kepp (1926): also claims "two tenths" depression
- Vaught (1958): correctly states "five hundredths" in winter, "nothing" in summer
- Discrepancy between actual 0.19 gap and Bulletin's max 0.05 in winter is 4:1. Kepp calls this "nearly agree so well" but actual factor is 4. Grigg's "two tenths" would be 2:1 and closer to actual, but still wrong.

**Arbitration Paradox:**
Board of Arbitration found "no cause established" yet awarded $3,800 without admission.
- Vose: possibly "generous settlement of an unproved claim" to keep peace
- Teague: notes both halves of Frick's sentence "true at once"; notes Vose signed sheets showing station gain every year
- Frick: unsatisfactory award; found no cause but paid neighbors without telling them what for
- The truth (discovered 33 years later): there WAS cause (defective glass), but it was not discovered because the crate was never examined in the hearing or brought as evidence

**The Crate Never Mentioned in Hearing:**
Loomis specifically notes (1941): "The second is the crate. The crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all. I took every word down and it is not there. I mean the crate of measures that the state's inspector condemned up at the station in June of 1923, which was set aside and new ones ordered in its place. Not a word of it was spoken in any of the three sessions, by the patrons, by the officers, or by the board, and the award's phrase about 'the books and appliances of the station' is a form of words the board used and not a list of anything."

Frick (1946) notes board had "the books and appliances of the station" before it, but the crate (the actual condemned appliances—the pipettes in their original container) was not examined or brought as evidence. It remained in the loft at Ostrey Hollow station and was not found again until February 1958 (35 years after condemnation, 33 years after hearing).

Yet Frick explicitly states four exhibits were before the board: test book, receiving book, Brant weigh book, 1923 circuit notebook. None of these was the crate itself or the pipettes (the actual defective implements).

**What Vose Knew:**
Teague notes Vose signed the station account sheets every year showing the 5.25% station gain. Teague confronted Vose about it in 1916 (9 years before arbitration) and was told to "let it alone." This suggests Vose either:
- Understood there was a problem but did not investigate
- Suspected theft but had no proof
- Observed the gain but took it as normal accounting difference
- Knew the equipment was defective but did not mention it

Vose's 1926 address treats the June 12, 1923 condemnation matter-of-factly: "the state man...condemned the station's testing apparatus on the twelfth of July, 1923 [date error: actually June 12], and told us to order new. We ordered new inside the month and had it in use before the flush was over. Apparatus wears." He does not claim the apparatus was working fine before; he treats it as routine maintenance. He attributes 1924 rise to water-detection effectiveness, not equipment replacement.

**Strawn's Documentation:**
Strawn's circuit notebooks explicitly document June 12, 1923 condemnation and October 1923 verification of new glass. These records existed continuously (state dairy office 1936, Farland library 1947) but were not consulted in the arbitration (or consulted but not used as evidence). Strawn did not attend the hearing.

## Oddities and Unresolved Questions

1. **Why was the crate not found during arbitration?** The crate was in the Ostrey Hollow station building (per Nym: "loft over what was the intake room"). The arbitration board visited the station and had "the books and appliances of the station" before it, yet the crate (the actual appliances—the pipettes—in their original container) was not examined. Loomis explicitly notes it was not mentioned in any of the three hearing sessions. This suggests either:
   - The crate was already in the loft and overlooked (Strawn 1928 notes loft full of "odds and ends")
   - The crate was stored elsewhere and not brought to the hearing
   - The board did not think to inspect the discarded glassware
   - The crate was intentionally not brought

2. **Why did Vaught calculate 118,000 lb but the Bulletin conclude 76,000 lb?** Vaught's Section V (Magnitude) states: "Applied to the 40,000,000 lb of milk received at the station in the twenty-five full delivery years, this yields 118,000 lb of butterfat delivered and not credited." His ratio 16.27 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244 gives (3.61 ÷ 0.9244) = 3.905, gap 0.295, times 40M = 118K lb. But the Bulletin's final paragraph cites "seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat." Either:
   - Vaught's Section V calculation is wrong (wrong ratio, wrong milk intake)
   - Ivy Keddie's measurement (16.72 cc mean) is the correct one, giving ~76,000 lb
   - Different time period (Vaught may have used different years or accounting basis)
   - Discrepancy never resolved in sources

3. **Why did Rundle's 1959 footnote say "eleven pipettes" when all other sources say nine?** Rundle writes: "Eleven pipettes out of the station's old condemned crate were found still to be in existence." Ivy's measurement sheet explicitly measures nine pipettes. Vaught says nine. No other source mentions eleven. Possible explanations:
   - Rundle misremembered or misheard (he was too old to rewrite pamphlet in 1959)
   - Some pipettes were whole (unbroken) vs. measured—perhaps 11 whole, 9 measured?
   - Typo or miscount
   - Unknown discrepancy

4. **Why did Grigg and Kepp claim Bulletin 214 found "two tenths" depression when it actually said "five hundredths"?** Both cite the College as confirming their findings. Both misstate the magnitude by 4:1 in Grigg's case (claiming 0.20 vs actual 0.05) and by 4:1 in Kepp's case (claiming 0.20 vs actual 0.05). Possible explanations:
   - Misreading or misremembering the Bulletin
   - Rounded approximation (0.05 is close to 0.02 if you're rounding generously)
   - Different interpretation of "two tenths" meaning something else (two-tenths of a percentage point would be 0.20, but Bulletin says it differently)
   - Deliberate distortion (unlikely)

5. **Why did Vaught say station's own glass went into use May 1, 1898, when the Ammon invoice is April 14, 1897, and Ansel's letter says "spring following 1897"?** 
   - Vaught: "The station's own glass went into use on the first of May, 1898"
   - Ammon invoice: April 14, 1897, for equipment "in use 1 May 1897"
   - Ansel's letter: glass "came up the valley in a crate the spring following" (1897, meaning spring after May 4, 1896 opening, so spring 1897)
   - Sill: "they were ready to start taking milk, and there was talk of holding off the whole season" (1896); "they sent mine back to me... the spring after" (1897)
   - Clear error in Vaught: should say May 1, 1897, not 1898

6. **Why did Vose (1926) say the state man's visit was "the twelfth of July, 1923" when Strawn's notebooks clearly show June 12, 1923?** 
   - Vose: "the state man...condemned the station's testing apparatus on the twelfth of July, 1923"
   - Strawn's entry: "Ostrey Hollow. [date entry shows] June 12, 1923"
   - Off by exactly one month—possible mis-memory by Vose, or misquote in Rundle's pamphlet

7. **Why did Kepp dismiss the 1911 anomaly as "six weeks" when it was actually nine weeks?**
   - Kepp: "six weeks in the flush of the season... June and July"
   - Ansel's entry: May 8 (broke), July 10 (returned)—9 weeks, spanning May-June-July
   - Kepp compresses the timeframe to make it sound like pure summer flush; actual span includes May (not peak flush for all cows)

8. **Why 3.80 in 1924 (per Strawn) but 3.90 in Vose's 1926 address?**
   - Strawn 1924: "Their book gives the year at 3.80"
   - Vose 1926: "the average for 1924 stood at 3.90"
   - Test book (per Ivy Keddie): 3.80
   - Sources disagree. Possible explanations:
     - Vose rounded or spoke from memory
     - Different set of patrons or different period (not calendar year)
     - The book was later amended
     - Vose's address is wrong

9. **Why didn't Merle Strawn investigate the 1911 anomaly during his visits?** Strawn began his circuit in 1907, so he visited Ostrey Hollow from 1909 onward. He visited in 1911 during the broken-measure period (May 8–July 10) but recorded nothing about it in his field notes (at least not in excerpts provided). Possible explanations:
   - Strawn visited outside the May 8–July 10 window (though "1911" entry doesn't specify date)
   - Strawn did not ask about the measure or note it
   - Strawn's burette was not available yet (burettes reached state's field men "very late in history" per Vaught)
   - Ansel did not show him the test book with the "?" entry

10. **The 1919 circular:** Selby Vose sent a circular to 9 Ostrey Hollow families warning about water in cans. The board of arbitration (1925) and Vose's own address (1926) both treat this as justified management action. Yet:
    - Merle Strawn's records state he tested for water every visit to Ostrey Hollow and never found any
    - The true cause was equipment, not farmer behavior
    - The circular created enduring family conflict (Ansel and Emmet Brant never spoke again)
    - Roy Brant says his father "never forgave it" and "did not forgive it"
    - Rosalie Cudd notes Jerome would have caught watered milk in the cheese (weak body, low yield)—never complained in 16 summers with those four men
    - Was Vose's motivation legitimate concern based on some knowledge (maybe Teague's station-gain entries), or pretext for something else?

11. **Why was Ansel Keddie dismissed on April 30, 1925 if the board found "no cause established" (award dated May 4)?** The award was dated May 4, but Ansel states he was "put out on the thirtieth of April." If the board met March 17–May 4, the dismissal pre-dates the final award by 4 days. Possible explanations:
    - Ansel's dismissal was predetermined or decided before the board's award
    - Or the board's award came down before May 4 but was not formally published until May 4
    - Or Ansel's letter uses "April 30" as a general date and the actual dismissal was slightly different
    - Association wanted him gone before the award was made public

12. **The weigh book attribution ambiguity:** Nym's Item 4 accession notes (in Nym's own retelling) state "Farm weigh book, kept by A. Keddie" but the text says "kept by E. Brant" (Emmet Brant's farm, household). Nym seems to have attributed it to Ansel in error in the title, then corrected in the provenance details. Roy Brant's retelling 9 confirms it was his father Emmet who kept the book from January 1 [1897 or 1898]. No contradition in end, but cataloging error by Nym.

13. **Why three different approaches to "wet hay" blame?** Three explanations offered at arbitration:
    - 1908 Committee: wet hay thins cream (remedy: land)
    - Bulletin 214 (College): bog hay depresses fat by five hundredths in winter, nothing in summer (but discrepancy in seasonal pattern—actual gap is year-round)
    - Kepp (1926): wet meadow soil acid/low mineral, cows rob frames, fat is first economy (similar to Grigg's hay explanation but adds mineral/soil mechanism, not just feedstuff)
    - Yet Alder Corners milk from same cows, same meadow, tested 3.80 (not 3.61) when tested at different location
    - This proves the gap is NOT in the milk (not caused by feed or land), but in the TEST (the equipment)

14. **Frick's implicit critique:** He says "Four books and nothing else. It is a short list for a matter of that size." He wonders whether the answer was in the room but not in any of the three stories. The crate with the condemned pipettes was in the building (loft). Had the board opened the crate and measured the pipettes against a burette (or even eyeballed them against the specification), the answer would have been found. The crate was not examined.

15. **Discrepancy on the gap magnitude:** Various sources cite different numbers:
    - Roy Brant: 17 hundredths (0.17)
    - Kepp: 21 hundredths (0.21)
    - Grigg/1908 committee: "complained for something like a quarter of a century," "gap" mentioned but no specific figure in committee report
    - Strawn: never gives a test difference figure
    - Vaught: 19 hundredths (0.19) based on 3.61 to 3.80 corrected
    - [0.17 to 0.21 is reasonable range; 0.19 is middle estimate; sources agree on ballpark]

- Ostrey Hollow average 1896–1922: 3.61% (recorded) or 3.80% (true, corrected for glass defect)

- Hessel Bottom at Alder Corners (summer 1915–1930, 16 seasons): 3.78–3.82 (averaging 3.80 per Cudd)
- Gap between Ostrey Hollow and Larrow Green/Alder Corners: 0.17–0.21 percentage points (17–21 hundredths)

**Fat:**
- Recorded fat over 25 years: 1,444,000 pounds (40M × 0.0361)
- True fat: 1,520,000 pounds (40M × 0.0380)
- Underpayment (Ivy Keddie, Vaught bulletin): 76,000 pounds
- Alternative calculation (Vaught Section V): 118,000 pounds [discrepancy not resolved]

- Pipette specification: 17.6 c.c.

## Summary of All 16 Retellings

**Retelling 1 (Vira Toland, 1979):** Settles on theft narrative; Ansel caught taking cream.

**Retelling 2–8:** [From previous notes, r1–r8] Establish core case: systematic underpayment via defective glass, Ansel's dismissal without proof, 1908 wet-hay committee, 1919 water circular, 1925 arbitration, 1911 anomaly, archival evidence.

**Retelling 9 (Roy Brant, 1955):** Weigh book agreement (scales matched perfectly), year-round gap, 1911 anomaly, family conflict over 1919 circular, borrowed book to Ivy 1958.

**Retelling 10 (Rosalie Cudd, 1958):** Alder Corners milk 3.78–3.82 (16 summers), proving milk was good, watering would have failed in cheese.

**Retelling 11 (Leland Vaught, 1958):** Bulletin 471 introduction/method; pipette is unchecked standard; defective glass short 5%, causing proportional error; 1911 anomaly disposes of alternative explanations; [discrepancy: 16.27 cc vs. Ivy's 16.72].

**Retelling 12 (Dell Rundle, 1939, reprinted 1959):** Jubilee pamphlet; NOTE IN PRESS corrects theft narrative (heavy cream at Green, not light stolen); 1959 footnote mentions Ivy's work [discrepancy: "eleven pipettes"].

**Retelling 13 (Alonzo Frick, 1946):** Arbitration chairman; heard three explanations (water, theft, wet hay); believed Ansel's denial; found no cause, awarded $3,800; regrets dismissing honest man; four exhibits (no crate).

**Retelling 14 (Hobart Sill, 1934):** Buttermaker 41 years; cream always came in heavy; something changed in 1920s; made good butter regardless; provided own glassware 1896.

**Retelling 15 (Nyle Grigg, 1919):** Committee chair reissues 1908 report; winter sampling only; cites Bulletin 214 (misquotes as "two tenths"); stands by wet-hay finding.

**Retelling 16 (Roland Kepp, 1926):** Vet surgeon paper; wet-soil mechanism (acid, low minerals, cows rob frames); gap 0.21; cites Bulletin 214 (misquotes "two tenths"); dismisses 1911 anomaly as summer flush; remedy: drain, lime, mineral supplement.

---

**Key Insight from All 16:** The case is closed by physical evidence (9 surviving pipettes, measured 1958), not by logical argument or testimony. Every theory offered before 1958 (theft, water, wet hay, bad management) was plausible given the information available—and every one was wrong. The 1911 anomaly was the key clue (self-running experiment), but lay unread for 47 years until Vaught published it in 1958 alongside Ivy's measurement.
