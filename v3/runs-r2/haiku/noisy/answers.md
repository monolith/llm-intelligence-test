# Section A: Reconstruction of Eight Stories (Stories 1-4)

## Story 1: The Larrow Valley Association and the System of Payment by Test (1889–1896)

In the winter of 1889, Gideon Vose organized a farmers' cooperative at Larrow Green to consolidate the milk supply from the Larrow Valley and surrounding areas. The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association formalized a radical principle: farmers would be paid not by the weight of their milk, but by the *butterfat content as measured at the Association's testing stations*. This principle, codified as Article VII of the Association's by-laws (adopted 1892, though the minute book recording its exact date was lost in a 1938 office move), became the hinge upon which the entire case would turn. Under Article VII, the test made at the station where milk was delivered governed the patron's pay, with no appeal allowed.

Gideon Vose, who died by 1917, drafted these by-laws himself. His son Selby Vose later became manager (1911–1938) and inherited both his father's institutional authority and, as events unfolded, his father's interpretive ambiguities. The Association built its main creamery at Larrow Green in 1891, processing cream into butter and operating a general account. Seven miles up the valley at the hamlet of Ostrey Hollow, in a low, whitewashed building beside a creek at the valley's head, the Association opened a skimming station in May 1896. Here milk from Hessel Bottom—the wet meadow farms clustered around Ostrey Hollow—would be weighed, sampled, tested for butterfat percentage, separated, with cream hauled back down to Larrow Green and skim returned to farmers. The tester and separator man from opening day was Ansel Keddie.

The system was theoretically elegant: it removed the farmer's temptation to water milk (payment was by fat, not bulk) and promised scientific, impartial assessment. Payment by test became routine at the 1893 annual meeting. For Hessel Bottom in particular, the system seemed necessary—their milk routinely tested lower than milk from the valley's hill farms. The system itself was never questioned. For nearly three decades, it worked, was administered honestly, and generated a detailed written record. The problem was not in the system's design but in the instrument that implemented it.

**Key quantities:** Association founded 1889 at Larrow Green; creamery built 1891; Ostrey Hollow station opened May 4, 1896; Article VII: payment by station test, no appeal; Ansel Keddie hired 1896, kept test book for 29 seasons in his own hand.

**Documents:** Article VII (by-laws); test book opening leaf (May 4, 1896); minute books (missing 1889–1892); Effie Loomis notes on history; Alonzo Frick's 1946 recollections.

## Story 2: Ansel Keddie—Twenty-Nine Seasons and the Question Mark (1896–1934)

Ansel Keddie, born 1861, came to the Ostrey Hollow station when it opened May 4, 1896, and held the post of tester and separator man for twenty-nine complete seasons. He was, by every account, a careful man—careful in small things to a degree others found trying. He kept the station's test book in his own hand, never writing over a figure, crossing errors with his initials. He tested milk thirty-one times a week (per his own statement). He maintained that neither patrons nor officers came to watch him work, though no man in the room questioned his denial at the 1925 hearing.

Ansel married Orra Brant in 1889, before coming to the station. Orra was the younger sister of Emmet Brant, who farmed the largest place on Hessel Bottom and hauled more milk to the station than any other patron. Thus Ansel, through marriage, tested the milk of his wife's brother for nearly thirty years—a fact that sat in the middle of the family for a generation and hardly anyone outside it ever noticed. Their son Duncan was born 1894 and eventually taught school at Ashlin. Duncan's daughter Ivy, born 1931, would become the agent of her grandfather's exoneration, though Ansel would not live to see it.

Ansel's signal act occurred in summer 1911, when the station's own measure broke (May 8). A borrowed measure was used for nine weeks until July 10, when the station's own glass returned to use. During those nine weeks, the station's tests stood markedly higher—rising from the usual 3.61 to 3.80. When his own measure returned, the tests fell back to 3.61. Ansel noted this fact in his test book and marked it with a question mark, which he never explained. It was the most expensive silence in the valley's history.

From 1902 forward, milk from Ostrey Hollow tested consistently lower than milk from anywhere else—about 0.19 points lower than the Larrow Green creamery's average. The shortfall was reported and debated at every annual meeting for twenty-three years. On April 30, 1925, four days before a board of arbitration rendered its decision, Ansel was dismissed. The board found no cause established and awarded the patrons $3,800 "without admission"—payment without explanation. Ansel came home that day at his ordinary hour, hung his apron behind the shed door, and asked what there was for supper. He lived nine years after dismissal, doing carpentry work at a quarter of his former pay. He died February 1934, at home, in the forenoon. Men who would not speak his name in 1925 came to his funeral and praised his skill with a door. He never asked them to.

**Key quantities:** Station 1896–1924 (29 seasons); average test 3.61 at Hollow vs. 3.78 at Larrow Green (0.19 shortfall); 1911 interval May 8–July 10 (9 weeks), tests rose to 3.80, fell back; dismissal April 30, 1925; died February 1934.

**Documents:** Test book (May 4, 1896–1924); Orra's diary (1897–1930, loose leaf 1934); Ansel's unsent letter (spring 1925); Ansel's statement to board (March 17, 1925); Frick's recollections; Duncan Keddie family notes (1968).

## Story 3: The Equipment from Ammon & Sons and the Glass That Would Not Deliver (1897–1923)

On April 14, 1897, the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association ordered its complete testing outfit from Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet. The invoice, still preserved, shows: six dozen milk test pipettes blown for 17.6 cubic centimeters at $0.35 each ($25.20); twelve dozen test bottles with 8-percent necks at $0.22 each ($31.68); one acid measure ($0.90); one four-bottle hand tester ($18.00); freight ($2.15). Total: $77.93. The outfit arrived at Ostrey Hollow in May 1897 and was put into use May 1, 1897, replacing borrowed glassware from the Larrow Green creamery's own bench.

The pipette—a simple glass tube with a bulb and a stem marked at the delivery point—was designed to deliver precisely 17.6 cubic centimeters of milk into a test bottle whose graduated neck was ruled for that exact quantity. The milk would be mixed with acid, spun in a hand tester at high speed, causing the fat to rise into the graduated neck where it could be read as a percentage. The whole of the method depended on the pipette delivering its marked quantity. If it delivered less milk, the fat would read proportionally low, without detection, because the neck of the bottle could not tell that the charge was short.

For twenty-six years, May 1, 1897, to June 12, 1923, Ansel Keddie tested milk using these pipettes. In June 1923, Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector for the Ordell circuit, made a routine visit to the station. He carried a calibrated burette—a precision measuring instrument that the station's own operators could never use to check their work, because burettes were not available to farm operators. Strawn ran the station's milk pipettes against his burette. He found that the lot would not deliver its mark. He condemned the entire crate on the spot ("These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them"). He directed the manager to order new glass immediately.

The Association ordered replacement pipettes and bottles on June 28, 1923. Ammon & Sons replied on July 2: "Your order of the 28th ultimo for six dozen milk pipettes and six dozen test bottles is entered and will go forward this month. We regret the trouble. Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name." By October 1923, new glassware was in use. In 1924, with new glass, the station's average test rose to 3.80 and remained there. At the time, no one drew an inference about the years before. The inspector himself drew no inference. The condemned crate was simply stored in the loft over the intake room.

**Key quantities:** Invoice April 14, 1897; 6 dozen pipettes @ 17.6 cc; equipment cost $77.93; put in use May 1, 1897; condemned June 12, 1923; new glass in use October/November 1923; crate sat in loft 35 years.

**Documents:** Ammon & Sons invoice (April 14, 1897); Ammon & Sons letter (July 2, 1923); Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks (June 12, 1923, October 1923 entries); Junia Ammon's 1949 chapter nine ("The Creamery Trade, and One Lot of Pipettes"); Orin Falke's 1959 report (paragraph 11).

## Story 4: Hessel Bottom, Wet Ground, and the Hay Theory (1908–1926)

Hessel Bottom is a wet meadow of about 160 acres lying beneath Ostrey Hollow, under the slope at the valley head. Six farms had rights to cut and pasture on it, though one narrator (Roy Brant) counts six while another (Nyle Grigg's 1908 report) says six definitively. The largest piece was Emmet Brant's. The ground lies along a creek that runs cold year-round from springs that emerge at the foot of the slope. Part of the bottom is under water every spring, impassable until well into May. The hay is sedge—coarse, cut late, a poor feed that must be supplemented. Herds grazing on wet land and fed that hay test lower in butterfat. Every practical farmer in the county knew this as a matter of observation: "Wet bottoms yield milk poorer in fat by 'a fifth of a point' and every practical man knows it" (Dr. Kepp, 1926). Hessel Bottom was "the completest example of wet meadow in this county."

In January 1908, on behalf of the Association, a committee of three men under Nyle Grigg, a farmer of standing from Larrow Green, visited Hessel Bottom to investigate the persistently low tests. Grigg himself was not a patron of Ostrey Hollow station (he hauls to Larrow Green, so he had no financial interest in the outcome). The committee sampled the herds of six Hessel Bottom farms through January, February, and March 1908—winter months only. They weighed and watched each farmer's milk being tested. They examined the station scales with known weights. They inquired into the water supply at each barn and satisfied themselves. They observed the general condition of the stock—"herds thin in the flank at the end of winter feeding and slow to come to their flesh in the spring. Their coats are rough later than the coats of hill herds." They concluded: "The milk is whole and the scales are honest. It is our opinion that the wet hay of that bottom is what thins the cream, and the remedy lies with the land and not with the station."

This finding was revisited in 1926 by Dr. Roland Kepp, a veterinary surgeon with thirty years' practice in the county. Kepp wrote a paper for the Ordell County Farm Club establishing that wet ground acids soils and depletes minerals, that wet hay carries bulk without nutrition, and that underfed cattle rob their own frames for fat (fat is the first economy when nutrition is short). He set hill herds against bottom herds farm for farm and found the gap: "twenty-one hundredths of a point—0.21" between Ostrey Hollow and the valley at large. He regarded the complaint as perfectly just. The problem, he argued, was drainage, liming, and better feed rations, not testing.

In the summer of 1915, four Hessel Bottom men—unable or unwilling to accept Ostrey Hollow's low test averages—began hauling milk over Redlow Ridge to the Alder Corners Cheese Company in Vessey County, traveling 4 miles by switchback road. The same milk that tested 3.61 at Ostrey Hollow tested 3.78–3.82 at Alder Corners, consistently, for sixteen seasons (1915–1930). Rosalie Cudd, who ran Alder Corners after her father Jerome's death in 1941, confirmed: "Milk was good milk, never gave hour's trouble in vat, never one complaint from Jerome despite 1919 circular. Rosalie notes watered milk would fail in cheese (weak curd, soft body, low yield)—never happened in 16 seasons with these four men."

The wet hay theory was plausible, partially supported by College feeding trials (though those trials found only 0.05 point depression, not 0.19), and advanced by honest men who walked the ground. But it was also incomplete: it could not explain why the same four farms' milk tested 0.19 points lower at Ostrey Hollow but 0.19 points higher at Alder Corners using the same milk on the same days. It could not explain the 1911 interval when Ansel's own measure broke and tests rose to 3.80. It could not explain why the problem resolved entirely on a single day in July 1923 when new glass was installed and no other change occurred.

**Key quantities:** 6 farms on wet meadow; 1908 sampling (winter only, Jan–Mar); gap 0.19 (later confirmed as 0.21 by Kepp); 4 farms hauled 1915–1930 (16 seasons); milk tested 3.61 at Hollow, 3.78–3.82 at Alder Corners; no difference in quality (no watered-milk defects); no other variables changed.

**Documents:** 1908 committee report (Nyle Grigg); Grigg's 1919 covering letter; Dr. Kepp's 1926 paper; Roy Brant's retelling (1955, recalling 47-year-old 1911 anomaly); Rosalie Cudd's 1958 letter; Alder Corners test book (records 1915–1930, copied 1958); State College Bulletin 214 (1911, bog hay feeding trial).

## Story 5: The Water Theory and the 1919 Circular (1919–1925)

In 1919, Selby Vose, manager of the Association since 1913, became convinced that the low tests were caused not by equipment or ground or hay, but by deliberate watering of milk by Ostrey Hollow patrons. Vose had no proof. Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, had tested repeatedly with his lactometer and found no added water. Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee had inquired into water supplies and found nothing suspicious. But Vose believed it nonetheless, and in June 1919, he circulated a letter to patrons warning them about water detection: "Water going into cans at the head of that valley and stopped when the Association began to look" (per his later account). He sent nine letters to nine families on Hessel Bottom—"a warning... about adding water to the milk, in a civil form of words but with no doubt at all in it about what was meant" (clerk Kyle's recollection).

Ansel Keddie received one. Rather than post them or leave them on the platform, Ansel took the nine letters himself from house to house, stood at each door while the farmer read it, and explained his position: he did not believe the charge, but he would not have another man carry it. He came home at eleven that night with his hat still on, which was not his way. Orra, his wife, understood immediately what her husband had done and why, and she wrote it in her diary. Her brother Emmet came to the house two days later, stood in the yard without coming in, and confronted Ansel: "He hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and A. knew it better than any man living, and then he asked him why he had carried the paper himself if he did not believe it." Ansel's answer was that he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet left. He had not been in that house since 1919 and would not reconcile until after his death in 1929, when his son Roy met his sister at the gate and was kind.

The 1919 circular damaged the Brant family irreparably and fractured the fabric of trust at the station. Ansel's careful, meticulous reputation—which should have protected him—became a liability instead: a man so conscientious as to personally deliver a letter accusing his wife's family looked like a man who believed the accusation. He did not. But the damage was done.

At the 1925 arbitration hearing, Vose presented his water theory to the board as the sole explanation for the low tests. He argued that water was "the oldest trick in the trade" and that "the station cannot test what is not there"—a technically sound principle. He submitted no evidence. The state inspector's consistent findings of no added water were not examined. The theory lived and died on Vose's insistence and on the public acceptance of blame that the 1919 circular had already established in the farmers' minds. When no cause was established by the board, Vose's theory was not refuted; it was simply set aside, along with the board's award of money with no explanation of why it was owed.

**Key quantities:** 1919 circular to 9 families; sent by Ansel himself to all patrons; Emmet did not speak to Orra again for 10 years (until Spring 1929, when he died); reconciliation never occurred; circular preceded dismissal by 6 years but established pattern of accusation.

**Documents:** Orra Keddie's diary (June 1919 entries); Ansel Keddie's unsent letter (spring 1925, references to 1919 circular); Bertram Kyle's retelling (1953, on typing the circular); Frick's 1946 recollections (Vose's testimony); Selby Vose's 1926 address (on water theory).

## Story 6: The 1911 Interval—An Unplanned Experiment (May 8–July 10, 1911)

On May 8, 1911, Ansel Keddie's measure broke. It was not a dramatic incident—a piece of glassware fractured—but it was an accident that, unknown to anyone at the time, would constitute the cleanest possible proof that the glassware was at fault for the low tests. For nine weeks, until July 10, 1911, the station used a borrowed measure (source unspecified in the records, but possibly from Larrow Green). During that nine-week interval, the station's average test rose from 3.61 to 3.80. On July 10, when Ansel's own measure was repaired or replaced and returned to use, the tests fell back to 3.61 immediately.

The experimental conditions were as nearly perfect as any real-world trial could be: same station, same tester, same patrons, same herds, same season (May through July, a consistent period for both years being part of the flush season), same acid, same test bottles, same hand tester, same procedure, same operator (Ansel). The only variable that changed was the measuring pipette. Ansel noted the phenomenon in his test book: "May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." On July 10, he wrote: "Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." The question mark was Ansel's honest notation of mystery. He did not explain it, either to himself or to anyone else, because he could not.

Leland Vaught, professor of dairy husbandry at the State Agricultural College, later called this interval "worth more than arithmetic." It ran itself under conditions no designed trial could have bettered. It lay unread in Ansel's test book for 47 years, until Ivy Keddie examined the book in 1958 and recognized its significance. The 1911 interval proves: (1) that the tester did not steal cream (because the tests rose when using borrowed glass); (2) that nothing in the herds, feed, or management changed (because all other variables held constant); (3) that the cause was in the equipment itself; and (4) that the problem was not water, theft, or feed stress, because none of those would resolve overnight by changing a single piece of glass.

The interval does *not* prove the exact magnitude of the error or identify which equipment was faulty (it could, in theory, have been the test bottles or acid measure, though the evidence points to the pipette). But it narrows the field decisively and provides a contemporaneous, internal consistency check on Ansel's honesty: the man who recorded this anomaly and marked it with a question mark was not a man hiding theft.

**Key quantities:** May 8–July 10, 1911 (9 weeks); tests rose from 3.61 to 3.80 during interval; fell back to 3.61 when own measure returned; recorded contemporaneously in test book.

**Documents:** Test book entries (May 8, July 10, 1911); Ansel's unsent letter (spring 1925, references 1911 anomaly); Vaught Bulletin 471 (October 1958, analysis of the interval); Roy Brant retelling (1955, noting he had wondered 47 years what it meant).

## Story 7: The 1925 Arbitration and the Failure to Look (March 17–May 4, 1925)

In January 1925, the patrons of Ostrey Hollow filed a petition of complaint against the station and the Association, claiming they had been underpaid because of low tests. The Association's officers appointed a board of arbitration to hear the dispute. The board was chaired by Alonzo Frick, an attorney from Ashlin, described as "able and impartial." Frick sat on the board, heard testimony, and tried the case as a lawyer would—examining claims, cross-examining witnesses, weighing evidence. He believed Ansel Keddie's denial immediately and still believed it in 1946 when he wrote his recollections. But Frick was not a scientist and did not understand the test. He had no knowledge of glassware or burettes or systematic error.

The board sat three times: first at the Association's office in Ashlin on March 17, 1925, then twice at the Ostrey Hollow station itself in the weigh room, at Frick's request, so that the board could see the machinery and scales and books. Frick noted: "Every physical appliance before board; saw scales tried, sampling done, test made same way twice, hands not hidden." The board had before it four documents: (1) Ansel Keddie's test book, kept in his own hand from 1896 onward; (2) the receiving book (milk received vs. fat credited); (3) Emmet Brant's private weigh book, showing his own scale matched the station's scale over the whole period; (4) Merle Strawn's circuit notebook for 1923.

The condemned crate of glassware sat in the loft directly over the weigh room, no more than twenty feet above the board's heads. No member of the board examined it. No member of the board asked for it to be brought down. No member of the board, in Frick's later recollection, "laid the second beside the fourth and looked"—that is, examined Strawn's June 12, 1923 entry condemning the glass against the long history of low tests.

Three explanations were presented: (1) Vose's water theory (disproven by Strawn's consistent finding of no added water); (2) Teague's implication of cream theft by the tester (refuted by the 1911 interval, though no one connected the two); (3) the 1908 committee's wet hay report (partially supported by college trials, but incomplete). The board dismissed all three as "not established." Yet it awarded the patrons $3,800 "without admission," meaning payment for what? Without knowing what went wrong, the board split the difference between the patrons' claimed loss (67,000 pounds butterfat, worth approximately $16,750 at 25 cents per pound) and nothing. The award was about one-fifth of the claimed loss—a face-saving gesture.

On April 30, 1925, four days before the board's official award of May 4, Ansel Keddie was dismissed. The dismissal said everything; the finding said nothing. Frick, years later, reflected: "An honest man ruined by unexplained figure, paid neighbors money without telling them what for... Four books and nothing else. It is a short list for a matter of that size... Wonders if answer was in room but not in any three explanations." The answer was literally in the room, in the loft, condemned since June 12, 1923, and never examined.

**Key quantities:** Petition January 1925; hearing March 17–May 4, 1925 (3 sessions); patrons' claimed loss 67,000 lb butterfat (~$16,750 at $0.25/lb); board found no cause established; awarded $3,800 without admission; dismissal April 30 (4 days before award); condemned crate 20 feet overhead in loft, never examined.

**Documents:** Test book; receiving book; Brant weigh book; Strawn notebook (1923); Frick's 1946 recollections; board minute book (March 17–May 4, 1925, award leaf); Kyle's retelling (1953, on the four books); Falke's 1959 report (paragraph 10).

## Story 8: The 1958 Rediscovery and the Proof (February–October 1958)

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland, climbed a ladder into the loft over the intake room at the Ostrey Hollow station and found a wooden crate under a tarpaulin, broken and covered in chaff. It was the condemned glassware, stored since June 12, 1923. The original shipment had contained six dozen pipettes (seventy-two total). Thirty-five years of attrition—rodents, dust, builders climbing for wire, children exploring—had broken most. Nine pipettes remained intact and legible.

Ivy carried the crate east to Farland to the State Agricultural College's dairy laboratory. On March 9, 1958, she measured the nine surviving pipettes against a calibrated burette, running five deliveries from each. The data recorded on her measurement sheet:

- Pipette 1: 16.70 cc
- Pipette 2: 16.71 cc  
- Pipette 3: 16.72 cc
- Pipette 4: 16.72 cc
- Pipette 5: 16.72 cc
- Pipette 6: 16.73 cc
- Pipette 7: 16.73 cc
- Pipette 8: 16.74 cc
- Pipette 9: 16.71 cc
- Mean: 16.72 cc

The pipettes were blown for 17.6 cc. They delivered 16.72 cc on average. They were short by 0.88 cc, which is 95 hundredths of the mark—a 5% systematic low-bias error. If the pipette delivers 5% less milk than specified, the fat column in the test bottle stands proportionally lower than it should. Every test made with these pipettes read 5% too low, and the error was the same for every test because the glassware was consistently wrong.

Ivy's work was collaborated with Professor Leland Vaught of the college's dairy husbandry department. In October 1958, the college issued Bulletin No. 471, authored jointly by Ivy Keddie and Leland Vaught. The bulletin concluded: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference—seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat—stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

The mathematics closed a circle: 76,000 pounds of butterfat, at an average price of $0.25 per pound over 25 years, was worth $19,000. The patrons had received $3,800 in 1925—approximately one-fifth of the actual loss. Ansel Keddie had died in February 1934, four years after dismissal and without ever learning that his measure was accurate. Orra Keddie died in 1948, fourteen years after her husband, also without knowing. The exoneration came too late to save either of them, but it vindicated their steadfast assertion of innocence and proved that Ansel had done his job correctly for twenty-six years.

Ivy Keddie's name was printed in newspapers and college bulletins. The Ashlin Chronicle featured her story. But it was the questions that mattered: the question mark Ansel placed in his test book in July 1911; the question that Roy Brant carried for 47 years ("noticed the 1911 anomaly personally: 9 weeks when station's measure broken, all Bottom farms' tests rose, then fell back when own measure returned; could make nothing of it for 47 years"); the question that Frick posed in 1946 about whether the answer was in the room but not in any of the three explanations; and ultimately, the question Orra wrote in Ansel's diary in 1934: "He never asked them to."

**Key quantities:** Crate found February 1958; 6 dozen original (72 pipettes), 9 survived; measured March 9, 1958; mean delivery 16.72 cc (short 0.88 cc = 5%); 76,000 lb underpaid fat; worth $19,000 @ $0.25/lb; patrons received $3,800 (20%); Ansel died 1934 (24 years before proof); Orra died 1948 (10 years before proof).

**Documents:** Condemned crate; Ivy Keddie's measurement sheet (March 9, 1958); Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958, Vaught & Ivy Keddie); Ashlin Chronicle front page (October 3, 1958); Hazel Brant retelling (1981, eyewitness to loft); Duncan Keddie's 1968 family notes (on crate provenance); Leland Vaught's introduction to Bulletin 471 (mathematics of error).

# Section B: Relationships (8 points)

## B1. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie

Ivy Keddie (born 1931) was the granddaughter of Ansel Keddie. Ansel married Orra Brant in 1889 and was tester at Ostrey Hollow from 1896 onward. Their son was Duncan Keddie (born 1894), who taught school at Ashlin. Duncan's daughter was Ivy. Thus Ivy was Ansel's granddaughter, and she was born 36 years after his dismissal began and 3 years after his death. She never knew him as a living man, but she grew up hearing the story of his dismissal and vindication (or lack thereof). She became, in the end, the agent of his exoneration through her 1958 work measuring the condemned glassware. The Duncan Keddie family notes (1968) confirm: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter" and note that it had "already twice been printed otherwise." Some sources incorrectly call her his great-niece (Falke's 1959 report calls her "great-niece," then adds a note withdrawing this error: "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece").

---

## B2. Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant are second cousins. Hazel is the daughter of Roy Brant. Roy is the son of Emmet Brant. Emmet was the brother of Orra Keddie, Ansel's wife. Duncan Keddie and Roy Brant are first cousins (children of a brother and sister—Ansel & Orra, and Emmet & Roy's mother). Ivy Keddie is Duncan's daughter, and Hazel is Roy's daughter. Therefore, Ivy and Hazel are second cousins (children of first cousins). The sources are consistent on this. Hazel held the weigh book after her father Roy (who inherited it after Emmet's death in 1929) and gave it to the Historical Rooms in 1963. She was a direct witness to the loft and the crate, as her family farm sat on Hessel Bottom. The two women were not close in age (Hazel was born around 1900, Ivy in 1931) and represented different generations' understanding of the same scandal.

---

## B3. Selby Vose's father and his role in what happened

Selby Vose (manager 1913–1938) was the son of Gideon Vose, founder and first president of the Association (1889–1911). The sources are emphatic about this: Duncan Keddie's notes (1968) state that Selby was "the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president — the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense." Bertram Kyle (1953) repeats the correction: "Mr. Selby Vose became manager in nineteen eleven. He was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose... the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense." This matters because it establishes Selby's authority as institutional heir and because Gideon's 1892 drafting of Article VII (payment by test, station test governs) created the system that, ironically, enabled the injustice. Gideon died by 1917 and could not explain his intentions. Selby inherited both the institution and, implicitly, his father's certainty about the water theory and low tests. The Association's culture of assumed dishonesty on the patrons' part can be traced partly to Gideon's original framing (a system that removed temptation to water) and Selby's elaboration of it in the 1919 circular and 1925 testimony.

---

## B4. The relationship between the Vose and Keddie families

The sources explicitly refute any family connection between the Voses and the Keddies. Duncan Keddie (1968) states: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either. I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her sit down. It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me. She told me a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it." Loomis's notes record that Article VII was "hinge of whole Ostrey Hollow business; last clause (station's test governs, no appeal) written 1892 by man (Gideon Vose) who died 1917 and could not be asked about it." Cleve Oram (1977) reports a rumor: "And I will say this while I am on families, because you will hear it anyway and you may as well hear it from somebody who is not repeating it out of malice. The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley. My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper." But this is clearly hearsay that Oram cannot substantiate, and it contradicts Duncan's direct assertion based on his mother Orra's exhaustive knowledge of family relations. The most reliable answer is: no documented connection exists, though local rumor (untraceable and unconfirmed) claimed one. Duncan's testimony as an insider is stronger than Oram's hearsay.
# Section C: Mathematics (14 points; show working)

## C1. The proportional error in the glassware

**(a) Proportion and derivation:**
The pipettes were blown for 17.6 cubic centimeters and delivered 16.72 cubic centimeters (mean of Ivy Keddie's March 9, 1958 measurement of nine pipettes, five runs each). 

Proportion: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 (95% of marked value)
Short by: 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 cc
Percent short: 0.88 ÷ 17.6 = 0.05 = 5%

**Derivation from glassware:** A milk test pipette works by delivering a *fixed* quantity of milk into a test bottle whose neck is graduated for that exact quantity. The neck has marks for fat percentage scaled to the standard charge. If the pipette delivers 5% less milk than marked, the charge is short by 5%, so the fat column stands proportionally lower. The reading system cannot detect this because the neck was graduated for the assumed charge, and the operator simply reads the length of the fat column, unaware that the charge was short. Thus the proportion of error in delivery (5%) becomes the proportion of error in the reading (5% low).

**(b) True average and point shortfall:**
Recorded average test at Ostrey Hollow: 3.61% (per Ansel's summary leaf, confirmed by Strawn 1924 entry, Loomis, Falke, Kyle, and independent sources)

If readings are 5% low, the true average = recorded average ÷ 0.95 = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.8053%

**True average: 3.80% (or 3.805%)**
Shortfall: 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 percentage points** (19 hundredths)

**(c) Pounds of butterfat underpaid:**
Station intake (full delivery years 1898–1922, 25 full years): 40,000,000 pounds milk (per Loomis "average 1.6 million/year," Teague "44 million" for entire period, Nym notes) 

Recorded butterfat credited: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb
True butterfat delivered: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb
**Underpaid: 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 pounds butterfat**

This matches Vaught's and Ivy's calculations in Bulletin 471.

**(d) Money value and fraction received:**
Average butterfat price over 25 years: $0.25 per pound (confirmed by Nym notes, Sill, Kepp; Roy Brant said 24 cents that year, average 25 overall)

Total owed: 76,000 lb × $0.25/lb = **$19,000**
Arbitration award (May 4, 1925): **$3,800**
**Fraction received: $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20 = 20% (one-fifth)**

# Section D: Logic (10 points)

## D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment

**When and duration:** May 8–July 10, 1911 (9 weeks)

**The variable changed:** The measuring pipette. Ansel's own measure broke on May 8 and was replaced with a borrowed measure from an unspecified source (possibly Larrow Green). On July 10, Ansel's own measure was repaired or replaced and returned to service.

**What was held constant:** Same station, same tester (Ansel Keddie), same patrons, same herds, same season (May–July flush, a consistent period), same acid, same test bottles, same hand tester, same procedure, same environmental conditions, same personnel.

**The result:** Station average test rose from 3.61% to 3.80% during the interval with borrowed glass, then fell back to 3.61% when Ansel's own glass returned. Ansel recorded: "May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." Then: "July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."

**What it establishes:** The interval proves that (1) the tester did not steal cream, because cream theft would show no change when equipment changed; (2) the problem was not in the herds, feed, or management, because none of these changed; (3) the problem originated in the station's own equipment, specifically in the measuring glass; (4) something in the borrowed glass was correct that Ansel's own glass was not. Leland Vaught called this "worth more than arithmetic" and noted it "ran itself under conditions no designed trial could have bettered."

**What it does NOT establish:** The interval does not identify which piece of equipment was faulty (though evidence strongly points to the pipettes). It does not measure the exact magnitude of the error in the original glass (that required Ivy's 1958 measurement with a burette). It does not establish that Ansel was right to mark it with a question mark—it could also be interpreted as evidence that he suspected his equipment but did not report it, though the careful record-keeping argues otherwise.

## D2. Three independent reasons the tester did not steal cream

**(1) The 1911 interval (1911; recorded contemporaneously):** When Ansel used borrowed glass for nine weeks, the tests rose to 3.80 and fell back when his glass returned. If Ansel were stealing cream, this interval should show no change (his opportunity to steal continued regardless of glassware). The fact that tests rose with different equipment proves the low readings were not from his actions. This reason survives whether or not the glass was ever measured in 1958.

**(2) Hobart Sill's receiving book (1896–1934; 41-year professional standard):** Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green for 41 years, received the Ostrey Hollow cream on the platform and weighed it himself most years in his receiving book. Sill states: "Hollow milk 'really 3.85 and no better,' per his observation." More importantly, he notes: "cream always ran heavy (more fat in cans than papers said was bought at station). Every year, every page." If Ansel were removing cream, less fat would reach Larrow Green, and Sill would have noted "light cream." Instead, he noted it ran heavy. This independent observation survives the 1958 glassware discovery.

**(3) Rosalie Cudd's account of four Hessel Bottom farmers hauling to Alder Corners (1915–1930; 16-year independent test):** Four farmers from Hessel Bottom hauled milk to Alder Corners Cheese Company in summer months. Their milk tested 3.78–3.82 at Alder Corners but 3.61 at Ostrey Hollow—a 0.19-point difference each season. Rosalie Cudd states: "Milk was good milk, never gave hour's trouble in vat, never one complaint from Jerome despite 1919 circular." She notes that watered milk would fail in cheese (weak curd, soft body, low yield), and this never happened. Stolen cream would mean the same milk was present in lower concentration at Alder Corners; instead, it tested higher. This accounts for the fat independently of any internal station mechanism and refutes theft.

**Independence:** Reason 1 depends only on the 1911 equipment change. Reason 2 depends on Sill's independent receiving records from a different station. Reason 3 depends on an external cheese factory's independent testing and product quality. None of these depend on each other, and Reason 1 requires no 1958 measurement to be valid.

**Did the bookkeeper question the entries?** Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper, created the "station gain" line in 1905 and carried it every year as "five and a quarter per cent, as usual." At the 1925 arbitration, Teague testified about the account but, according to Frick, framed it as an implication of cream theft without making a direct charge. The account itself was never questioned as to *why* it existed—Teague seems to have accepted it as routine operation of any station (cream separates differently at different temperatures and handling; some "loss" was expected). There is no record of Teague demanding explanation for why the station consistently gained 5.25% year after year for twenty years. Kyle notes of Teague: "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years. That is not a criticism. It was his notion of the position: the figures were the figures."

## D3. Did the manager know tests ran low?

**Answer: Yes, Selby Vose knew the tests ran low before the glassware was condemned. He believed he understood the cause (water) and acted on that belief, but he did not investigate the equipment.**

**Justification:** Selby Vose became manager in 1913 and served until 1938. He signed the annual station sheets every year, as confirmed by Loomis's notes. Annual ledger entries showing the 3.61% average and the consistent 5.25% "station gain" would have been presented to him. He wrote his 1926 address explaining the low tests as evidence of water being added to milk, stating "the average for 1924 stood at 3.90" (though the station test book says 3.80—a discrepancy). His 1919 water circular shows he believed low tests indicated dishonesty by patrons, not a problem with the station's equipment. He presented the water theory at the 1925 arbitration, implying he had been convinced for years that this was the cause.

However, Vose never examined the condemned glassware in the loft over his head during the 1925 hearing. He never requested measurement of the pipettes against a burette. He never asked Strawn (the inspector who condemned them) for details beyond "order new glass." He had no education in dairy chemistry and apparently never sought expert consultation beyond his own theory-driven interpretation. Bertram Kyle notes that Vose "read only bottom figures"—he "would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign." He knew the numbers were wrong; he did not know why; and he blamed the farmers rather than investigating his own equipment.

# Section F: Theory (6 points)

## F1. The causal theory and the three competing explanations

**The actual cause:** From May 1, 1897, to June 12, 1923, the Ostrey Hollow station's testing pipettes delivered 5% less milk than their marked capacity (16.72 cc instead of 17.6 cc). This systematic short delivery caused every test made with them to read proportionally low—not by a fixed points deduction, but by the same ratio. A herd truly testing 3.80% would read as 3.61%. The error was not in Ansel's procedure, not in the acid, not in the bottles or tester, not in the patrons' milk or management. It was in the one component of the test that could not be checked against any external standard: the pipette itself. The error persisted unchanged for 26 years because (1) the measurement standard (the pipette) *is* the criterion against which a plant checks everything else, creating a logical loop that cannot detect internal error; (2) the glassware looked fine and functioned smoothly, so no operator would suspect it; and (3) no dairy inspector visiting the station until 1923 thought to run the plant's own glassware against an external burette. When Merle Strawn did so on June 12, 1923, the defect was immediately visible and he condemned the lot. New glass was ordered, installed by October 1923, and from 1924 onward, tests averaged 3.80—matching what the milk actually contained.

**Why it began when it did:** The pipettes were manufactured by Ammon & Sons in 1897. Junia Ammon (1949) speculates that the mold was reground and the lot ran short—an accident in the glassblowing that went undetected by their own quality check (a blower checking the first, tenth, and fiftieth pipette from a batch might find them all consistently wrong, which appears as good consistency, not error). The lot was sold in April 1897 and put in use May 1, 1897.

**Why it ended when it did:** The glassware was condemned June 12, 1923 (Strawn's inspection), new glass was ordered June 28 and arrived by July (per Ammon's reply), and new glass was in use by October 1923. From 1924 forward, tests read 3.80—the true value.

**Why nothing had to change at the station or farms:** The station's procedure, the tester's competence, the acid, the bottles, the herds, the feed, the land management—all remained identical throughout. The only variable was the measuring pipette, and changing it changed everything.

### The three competing explanations and what defeats each:

**(1) Selby Vose's water theory (manager, 1919 circular and 1925 testimony):**
Water was being deliberately added to milk by patrons to increase volume and profit from higher weight sales before testing. Vose presented this at the 1925 hearing, arguing "water is the oldest trick in the trade" and "the station cannot test what is not there."

**What defeats it:** Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, tested with a lactometer (a hydrometer that detects added water) at every visit from 1908 forward and "never once found added water at that station." Strawn's entries are contemporaneous (not memory) and cover 28 years of circuit visits. His June 12, 1923 notation explicitly states: "Lactometer, no added water, as always here." The four Hessel Bottom farmers who hauled to Alder Corners during summer months (1915–1930) had their milk tested there and found no defect consistent with watering—watered milk would show weak curd and low yield in cheese, and Rosalie Cudd explicitly states this never happened. The milk tested higher at Alder Corners (3.78–3.82) than at Ostrey Hollow (3.61), opposite to what watering would predict.

**(2) Wilbur Teague's cream-theft implication (bookkeeper, 1925 hearing):**
At arbitration, Teague presented the station account showing consistent 5.25% "station gain" and testified about how such an account would look if a man were stealing cream. He made no direct accusation, but the implication was clear.

**What defeats it:** The 1911 interval proves Ansel did not steal cream. When Ansel's measure broke and he used borrowed glass for nine weeks, tests rose to 3.80 and fell back when his glass returned. If Ansel were stealing cream, his opportunity to do so would not change when equipment changed; the tests should have remained low and the "theft" would have continued. The fact that tests rose with different equipment proves the low tests were not from his actions. This evidence survives independently of any 1958 measurement and requires only that we trust Ansel's contemporaneous record-keeping (which was meticulous and never altered).

**(3) The 1908 committee's wet hay theory (Nyle Grigg's report, Kepp's 1926 paper):**
Hessel Bottom is a wet meadow with poor hay. Wet land yields lower-fat milk; Kepp demonstrated this in principle (wet soils are acid, forage is mineral-poor, underfed cattle rob fat stores). The 1908 committee sampled the herds and found them in sound condition but noted they were thin in flanks and rough-coated—consistent with feed stress. Bulletin 214 (1911) found that bog hay fed alone depressed butterfat by "not more than five hundredths" in winter.

**What defeats it:** The 0.19 gap is nearly four times the size of Bulletin 214's maximum finding (0.05). The gap is constant year-round, not seasonal—it appears in June and July as much as in January and February, opposite to a feed-related effect (which would be worst in winter when hay-feeding is heaviest). The four Hessel Bottom farmers' milk, when hauled to Alder Corners in summer (when animals are on pasture, not hay), still tested 0.19 points lower at Ostrey Hollow—suggesting the problem is not the hay or the land but something at Ostrey Hollow station. Most decisively, the same milk tested 3.78–3.82 at Alder Corners but 3.61 at Ostrey Hollow, a difference of 0.19 points, on the very same days—proving the low reading was not in the milk or the cows but in the measurement.

### Subsidiary questions:

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?** No. The crate sat in the loft directly above the weigh room where the board sat (within twenty feet). Strawn's June 12, 1923 entry, which explicitly mentioned running the pipettes against a burette and condemning them, was presented to the board (it was item 4 of the four books). But no member of the board examined the crate itself, no member asked for it to be brought down, and no member appears to have connected Strawn's note about June 1923 condemnation to the 26-year history of low tests. Frick later wrote: "I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked"—i.e., no one connected the receiving book to Strawn's notebook.

**What did the tester himself believe?** Ansel Keddie believed something was wrong with the glass. In summer 1911, when his measure broke and he used borrowed glass, the tests rose to 3.80. He recorded this and marked it with a question mark, refusing to explain it. In his unsent letter (spring 1925), he describes the entire testing procedure but gives no explanation for the low readings except the unexplained 1911 anomaly. He told Alonzo Frick privately (in the corridor, not on the record) that he "suspected the patrons (farmers) were at fault but would not accuse neighbors." But this seems to have been a politic deflection; his actions—carrying the 1919 water circular himself, refusing to have another man carry it—suggest he did not actually believe in the water theory. His real belief is most likely what the evidence points to: he knew something was wrong with his equipment, but he could not prove it, and his training as a scientist taught him not to write down what he could not prove. He lived with the question mark for 14 years and died with it.

# Section G: Summary (4 points)

## G1. Eight-story summary (≤150 words)

In 1889, Gideon Vose founded a farmers' cooperative creamery with a radically fair payment system: farmers paid by butterfat *test*, not milk weight. By 1896, the association's skimming station at Ostrey Hollow had been testing milk for one precise purpose. But in 1897, the station received defective pipettes from Ammon & Sons—each one delivering 5% less milk than marked. For 26 years, every test read proportionally low. Patrons claimed cheating; three competing theories emerged (water-doctoring, cream theft, poor hay). In 1911, a broken measure accidentally proved it was equipment, but the insight was missed. The 1925 arbitration awarded money without explanation. Ansel Keddie, the honest tester, was dismissed. In 1958, his granddaughter Ivy discovered the condemned crate and measured the pipettes. Science proved what arithmetic and integrity could not: no theft, no watering—just glass that would not deliver its mark. By then Ansel was 24 years dead. The eight accounts tell how truth hid in plain sight for a generation.
