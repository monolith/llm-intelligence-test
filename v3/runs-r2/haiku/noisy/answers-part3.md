# Section C: Mathematics (14 points; show working)

## C1. The proportional error in the glassware

**(a) Proportion and derivation:**
The pipettes were blown for 17.6 cubic centimeters and delivered 16.72 cubic centimeters (mean of Ivy Keddie's March 9, 1958 measurement of nine pipettes, five runs each). 

Proportion: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 (95% of marked value)
Short by: 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 cc
Percent short: 0.88 ÷ 17.6 = 0.05 = 5%

**Derivation from glassware:** A milk test pipette works by delivering a *fixed* quantity of milk into a test bottle whose neck is graduated for that exact quantity. The neck has marks for fat percentage scaled to the standard charge. If the pipette delivers 5% less milk than marked, the charge is short by 5%, so the fat column stands proportionally lower. The reading system cannot detect this because the neck was graduated for the assumed charge, and the operator simply reads the length of the fat column, unaware that the charge was short. Thus the proportion of error in delivery (5%) becomes the proportion of error in the reading (5% low).

**(b) True average and point shortfall:**
Recorded average test at Ostrey Hollow: 3.61% (per Ansel's summary leaf, confirmed by Strawn 1924 entry, Loomis, Falke, Kyle, and independent sources)

If readings are 5% low, the true average = recorded average ÷ 0.95 = 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.8053%

**True average: 3.80% (or 3.805%)**
Shortfall: 3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 percentage points** (19 hundredths)

**(c) Pounds of butterfat underpaid:**
Station intake (full delivery years 1898–1922, 25 full years): 40,000,000 pounds milk (per Loomis "average 1.6 million/year," Teague "44 million" for entire period, Nym notes) 

Recorded butterfat credited: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 lb
True butterfat delivered: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 lb
**Underpaid: 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 pounds butterfat**

This matches Vaught's and Ivy's calculations in Bulletin 471.

**(d) Money value and fraction received:**
Average butterfat price over 25 years: $0.25 per pound (confirmed by Nym notes, Sill, Kepp; Roy Brant said 24 cents that year, average 25 overall)

Total owed: 76,000 lb × $0.25/lb = **$19,000**
Arbitration award (May 4, 1925): **$3,800**
**Fraction received: $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20 = 20% (one-fifth)**

**(c) Dollar value that year:**

This represents Emmet's loss for that single year. Multiplied across his decades of hauling, the cumulative loss was substantial.

# Section D: Logic (10 points)

## D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment

**When and duration:** May 8–July 10, 1911 (9 weeks)

**The variable changed:** The measuring pipette. Ansel's own measure broke on May 8 and was replaced with a borrowed measure from an unspecified source (possibly Larrow Green). On July 10, Ansel's own measure was repaired or replaced and returned to service.

**What was held constant:** Same station, same tester (Ansel Keddie), same patrons, same herds, same season (May–July flush, a consistent period), same acid, same test bottles, same hand tester, same procedure, same environmental conditions, same personnel.

**The result:** Station average test rose from 3.61% to 3.80% during the interval with borrowed glass, then fell back to 3.61% when Ansel's own glass returned. Ansel recorded: "May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure." Then: "July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."

**What it establishes:** The interval proves that (1) the tester did not steal cream, because cream theft would show no change when equipment changed; (2) the problem was not in the herds, feed, or management, because none of these changed; (3) the problem originated in the station's own equipment, specifically in the measuring glass; (4) something in the borrowed glass was correct that Ansel's own glass was not. Leland Vaught called this "worth more than arithmetic" and noted it "ran itself under conditions no designed trial could have bettered."

**What it does NOT establish:** The interval does not identify which piece of equipment was faulty (though evidence strongly points to the pipettes). It does not measure the exact magnitude of the error in the original glass (that required Ivy's 1958 measurement with a burette). It does not establish that Ansel was right to mark it with a question mark—it could also be interpreted as evidence that he suspected his equipment but did not report it, though the careful record-keeping argues otherwise.

## D2. Three independent reasons the tester did not steal cream

**(1) The 1911 interval (1911; recorded contemporaneously):** When Ansel used borrowed glass for nine weeks, the tests rose to 3.80 and fell back when his glass returned. If Ansel were stealing cream, this interval should show no change (his opportunity to steal continued regardless of glassware). The fact that tests rose with different equipment proves the low readings were not from his actions. This reason survives whether or not the glass was ever measured in 1958.

**(2) Hobart Sill's receiving book (1896–1934; 41-year professional standard):** Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green for 41 years, received the Ostrey Hollow cream on the platform and weighed it himself most years in his receiving book. Sill states: "Hollow milk 'really 3.85 and no better,' per his observation." More importantly, he notes: "cream always ran heavy (more fat in cans than papers said was bought at station). Every year, every page." If Ansel were removing cream, less fat would reach Larrow Green, and Sill would have noted "light cream." Instead, he noted it ran heavy. This independent observation survives the 1958 glassware discovery.

**(3) Rosalie Cudd's account of four Hessel Bottom farmers hauling to Alder Corners (1915–1930; 16-year independent test):** Four farmers from Hessel Bottom hauled milk to Alder Corners Cheese Company in summer months. Their milk tested 3.78–3.82 at Alder Corners but 3.61 at Ostrey Hollow—a 0.19-point difference each season. Rosalie Cudd states: "Milk was good milk, never gave hour's trouble in vat, never one complaint from Jerome despite 1919 circular." She notes that watered milk would fail in cheese (weak curd, soft body, low yield), and this never happened. Stolen cream would mean the same milk was present in lower concentration at Alder Corners; instead, it tested higher. This accounts for the fat independently of any internal station mechanism and refutes theft.

**Independence:** Reason 1 depends only on the 1911 equipment change. Reason 2 depends on Sill's independent receiving records from a different station. Reason 3 depends on an external cheese factory's independent testing and product quality. None of these depend on each other, and Reason 1 requires no 1958 measurement to be valid.

**Did the bookkeeper question the entries?** Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper, created the "station gain" line in 1905 and carried it every year as "five and a quarter per cent, as usual." At the 1925 arbitration, Teague testified about the account but, according to Frick, framed it as an implication of cream theft without making a direct charge. The account itself was never questioned as to *why* it existed—Teague seems to have accepted it as routine operation of any station (cream separates differently at different temperatures and handling; some "loss" was expected). There is no record of Teague demanding explanation for why the station consistently gained 5.25% year after year for twenty years. Kyle notes of Teague: "Teague never asked anybody anything in forty years. That is not a criticism. It was his notion of the position: the figures were the figures."

## D3. Did the manager know tests ran low?

**Answer: Yes, Selby Vose knew the tests ran low before the glassware was condemned. He believed he understood the cause (water) and acted on that belief, but he did not investigate the equipment.**

**Justification:** Selby Vose became manager in 1913 and served until 1938. He signed the annual station sheets every year, as confirmed by Loomis's notes. Annual ledger entries showing the 3.61% average and the consistent 5.25% "station gain" would have been presented to him. He wrote his 1926 address explaining the low tests as evidence of water being added to milk, stating "the average for 1924 stood at 3.90" (though the station test book says 3.80—a discrepancy). His 1919 water circular shows he believed low tests indicated dishonesty by patrons, not a problem with the station's equipment. He presented the water theory at the 1925 arbitration, implying he had been convinced for years that this was the cause.

However, Vose never examined the condemned glassware in the loft over his head during the 1925 hearing. He never requested measurement of the pipettes against a burette. He never asked Strawn (the inspector who condemned them) for details beyond "order new glass." He had no education in dairy chemistry and apparently never sought expert consultation beyond his own theory-driven interpretation. Bertram Kyle notes that Vose "read only bottom figures"—he "would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign." He knew the numbers were wrong; he did not know why; and he blamed the farmers rather than investigating his own equipment.

# Section F: Theory (6 points)

## F1. The causal theory and the three competing explanations

**The actual cause:** From May 1, 1897, to June 12, 1923, the Ostrey Hollow station's testing pipettes delivered 5% less milk than their marked capacity (16.72 cc instead of 17.6 cc). This systematic short delivery caused every test made with them to read proportionally low—not by a fixed points deduction, but by the same ratio. A herd truly testing 3.80% would read as 3.61%. The error was not in Ansel's procedure, not in the acid, not in the bottles or tester, not in the patrons' milk or management. It was in the one component of the test that could not be checked against any external standard: the pipette itself. The error persisted unchanged for 26 years because (1) the measurement standard (the pipette) *is* the criterion against which a plant checks everything else, creating a logical loop that cannot detect internal error; (2) the glassware looked fine and functioned smoothly, so no operator would suspect it; and (3) no dairy inspector visiting the station until 1923 thought to run the plant's own glassware against an external burette. When Merle Strawn did so on June 12, 1923, the defect was immediately visible and he condemned the lot. New glass was ordered, installed by October 1923, and from 1924 onward, tests averaged 3.80—matching what the milk actually contained.

**Why it began when it did:** The pipettes were manufactured by Ammon & Sons in 1897. Junia Ammon (1949) speculates that the mold was reground and the lot ran short—an accident in the glassblowing that went undetected by their own quality check (a blower checking the first, tenth, and fiftieth pipette from a batch might find them all consistently wrong, which appears as good consistency, not error). The lot was sold in April 1897 and put in use May 1, 1897.

**Why it ended when it did:** The glassware was condemned June 12, 1923 (Strawn's inspection), new glass was ordered June 28 and arrived by July (per Ammon's reply), and new glass was in use by October 1923. From 1924 forward, tests read 3.80—the true value.

**Why nothing had to change at the station or farms:** The station's procedure, the tester's competence, the acid, the bottles, the herds, the feed, the land management—all remained identical throughout. The only variable was the measuring pipette, and changing it changed everything.

### The three competing explanations and what defeats each:

**(1) Selby Vose's water theory (manager, 1919 circular and 1925 testimony):**
Water was being deliberately added to milk by patrons to increase volume and profit from higher weight sales before testing. Vose presented this at the 1925 hearing, arguing "water is the oldest trick in the trade" and "the station cannot test what is not there."

**What defeats it:** Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, tested with a lactometer (a hydrometer that detects added water) at every visit from 1908 forward and "never once found added water at that station." Strawn's entries are contemporaneous (not memory) and cover 28 years of circuit visits. His June 12, 1923 notation explicitly states: "Lactometer, no added water, as always here." The four Hessel Bottom farmers who hauled to Alder Corners during summer months (1915–1930) had their milk tested there and found no defect consistent with watering—watered milk would show weak curd and low yield in cheese, and Rosalie Cudd explicitly states this never happened. The milk tested higher at Alder Corners (3.78–3.82) than at Ostrey Hollow (3.61), opposite to what watering would predict.

**(2) Wilbur Teague's cream-theft implication (bookkeeper, 1925 hearing):**
At arbitration, Teague presented the station account showing consistent 5.25% "station gain" and testified about how such an account would look if a man were stealing cream. He made no direct accusation, but the implication was clear.

**What defeats it:** The 1911 interval proves Ansel did not steal cream. When Ansel's measure broke and he used borrowed glass for nine weeks, tests rose to 3.80 and fell back when his glass returned. If Ansel were stealing cream, his opportunity to do so would not change when equipment changed; the tests should have remained low and the "theft" would have continued. The fact that tests rose with different equipment proves the low tests were not from his actions. This evidence survives independently of any 1958 measurement and requires only that we trust Ansel's contemporaneous record-keeping (which was meticulous and never altered).

**(3) The 1908 committee's wet hay theory (Nyle Grigg's report, Kepp's 1926 paper):**
Hessel Bottom is a wet meadow with poor hay. Wet land yields lower-fat milk; Kepp demonstrated this in principle (wet soils are acid, forage is mineral-poor, underfed cattle rob fat stores). The 1908 committee sampled the herds and found them in sound condition but noted they were thin in flanks and rough-coated—consistent with feed stress. Bulletin 214 (1911) found that bog hay fed alone depressed butterfat by "not more than five hundredths" in winter.

**What defeats it:** The 0.19 gap is nearly four times the size of Bulletin 214's maximum finding (0.05). The gap is constant year-round, not seasonal—it appears in June and July as much as in January and February, opposite to a feed-related effect (which would be worst in winter when hay-feeding is heaviest). The four Hessel Bottom farmers' milk, when hauled to Alder Corners in summer (when animals are on pasture, not hay), still tested 0.19 points lower at Ostrey Hollow—suggesting the problem is not the hay or the land but something at Ostrey Hollow station. Most decisively, the same milk tested 3.78–3.82 at Alder Corners but 3.61 at Ostrey Hollow, a difference of 0.19 points, on the very same days—proving the low reading was not in the milk or the cows but in the measurement.

### Subsidiary questions:

**Did the 1925 board examine the condemned glassware?** No. The crate sat in the loft directly above the weigh room where the board sat (within twenty feet). Strawn's June 12, 1923 entry, which explicitly mentioned running the pipettes against a burette and condemning them, was presented to the board (it was item 4 of the four books). But no member of the board examined the crate itself, no member asked for it to be brought down, and no member appears to have connected Strawn's note about June 1923 condemnation to the 26-year history of low tests. Frick later wrote: "I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked"—i.e., no one connected the receiving book to Strawn's notebook.

**What did the tester himself believe?** Ansel Keddie believed something was wrong with the glass. In summer 1911, when his measure broke and he used borrowed glass, the tests rose to 3.80. He recorded this and marked it with a question mark, refusing to explain it. In his unsent letter (spring 1925), he describes the entire testing procedure but gives no explanation for the low readings except the unexplained 1911 anomaly. He told Alonzo Frick privately (in the corridor, not on the record) that he "suspected the patrons (farmers) were at fault but would not accuse neighbors." But this seems to have been a politic deflection; his actions—carrying the 1919 water circular himself, refusing to have another man carry it—suggest he did not actually believe in the water theory. His real belief is most likely what the evidence points to: he knew something was wrong with his equipment, but he could not prove it, and his training as a scientist taught him not to write down what he could not prove. He lived with the question mark for 14 years and died with it.

# Section G: Summary (4 points)

## G1. Eight-story summary (≤150 words)

In 1889, Gideon Vose founded a farmers' cooperative creamery with a radically fair payment system: farmers paid by butterfat *test*, not milk weight. By 1896, the association's skimming station at Ostrey Hollow had been testing milk for one precise purpose. But in 1897, the station received defective pipettes from Ammon & Sons—each one delivering 5% less milk than marked. For 26 years, every test read proportionally low. Patrons claimed cheating; three competing theories emerged (water-doctoring, cream theft, poor hay). In 1911, a broken measure accidentally proved it was equipment, but the insight was missed. The 1925 arbitration awarded money without explanation. Ansel Keddie, the honest tester, was dismissed. In 1958, his granddaughter Ivy discovered the condemned crate and measured the pipettes. Science proved what arithmetic and integrity could not: no theft, no watering—just glass that would not deliver its mark. By then Ansel was 24 years dead. The eight accounts tell how truth hid in plain sight for a generation.
