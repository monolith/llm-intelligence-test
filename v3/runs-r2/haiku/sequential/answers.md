## Section A: Reconstruction (8 Stories)

### Story 1: The Founding and Article VII (1889)

Gideon Vose, a farmer of Larrow Valley, called a meeting at Larrow Green on a cold evening and led the farmers to organize the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Vose was elected the first president—a position he held for twenty-two years—and he wrote the by-laws himself at his kitchen table in a hand that clerks would complain about for thirty years. The by-laws, adopted formally in 1892 (after the creamery was built in 1891), included Article VII, which proved to be the hinge of all that followed. Article VII stated that every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butterfat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk. The clause that mattered most was the last one: "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." This meant no appeal, no rechecking at the creamery, no average across the valley—only the test made at the patron's own station. Gideon Vose put this in for convenience in 1892, and it was convenient for thirty years. It was the sentence on which the whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned.

**Who:** Gideon Vose (first president), the farmers of Larrow Valley.

**What:** Organized a cooperative creamery association and established a payment system based on butterfat test rather than weight of milk.

**When:** Meeting and decision 1889; creamery built 1891; Article VII formally adopted 1892.

**Quantities:** Twenty-two years Vose served as president; Larrow Valley cooperative included nine families from Ostrey Hollow initially.

**Documents:** Effie Loomis (r02) on Article VII's text and adoption; Dell Rundle (r12) on Gideon Vose's character and writing; Halbert Nym (r07, accession note) on by-laws; Orin Falke (r22) on Article VII's adoption and meaning.

### Story 2: The Station Opens and the Glass Equipment Arrives (1895–1897)

In 1895 and 1896, the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green, to serve the farms of Hessel Bottom principally. The station opened on the 4th of May, 1896. Ansel Keddie, a careful man in small things, took the tester and separator position from the opening and kept the test book in his own hand for twenty-nine seasons. Hobart Sill, the buttermaker at Larrow Green, lent his own testing glass to the station for that first season because the Association's own equipment had not arrived. The station's official outfit was ordered from Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, at Tarnet, and arrived in the spring of 1897. The invoice, dated 14 April 1897, listed six dozen milk test pipettes (17.6 c.c.), twelve dozen test bottles, one acid measure, one hand tester (4-bottle), and freight, totaling $77.93. Hobart Sill's glass was returned to him, with one piece chipped (which he never mentioned). The new glass went into use on 1 May 1897, the same day that Ansel Keddie's test book officially began, though the station had been taking milk since May 4, 1896. This equipment—particularly the pipettes—would remain in use for twenty-six years, from May 1, 1897, to June 12, 1923.

**Who:** Ansel Keddie (tester), Hobart Sill (buttermaker, lender of glass), Ammon & Sons (supplier), Association officers.

**What:** Built the skimming station; opened it; acquired and installed testing equipment from Ammon & Sons.

**When:** Building 1895–1896; opening May 4, 1896; equipment invoice April 14, 1897; official equipment in use May 1, 1897.

**Quantities:** $77.93 for the outfit; 6 dozen (72) pipettes marked 17.6 c.c.; 12 dozen test bottles; equipment in use 26 years (1897–1923).

**Documents:** Ivy Keddie (r04) on the crate, invoice, and dates; Junia Ammon (r20) on the 1897 invoice; Bertram Kyle (r23) on the station's opening date; Ansel Keddie (r03) on his beginning date; Halbert Nym (r07) on the invoice and station history.

### Story 3: The Persistent Complaint and the Hay Theory (1902–1919)

From 1902 onward, the milk of Ostrey Hollow tested lower than the milk of anywhere else in the valley—consistently about nineteen hundredths of a point below Larrow Green. The gap did not vary seasonally; it was nineteen hundredths in July when the cows were on grass and nineteen hundredths in February when they were on hay. The gap did not care whether the year was wet or dry, the season abundant or poor. The first men to say so out loud were laughed at in 1902, and the complaint grew through the decade. In 1908, the Association appointed an investigating committee under Nyle Grigg, which visited the Hessel Bottom herds through January, February, and March, sampled the milk, watched the test, and reported that the wet hay of the bottom thins the cream and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station. Grigg's committee found no dishonesty anywhere. The State Agricultural College's Bulletin No. 214 (published 1911) tested the effect of bog hay and found it depressed butterfat by "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture"—far short of the nineteen-hundredths gap. In 1919, the Association's manager Selby Vose circulated a written warning to the Ostrey Hollow patrons about water in their milk, in civil language but with unmistakable meaning. The circular went to nine families. None answered it. It was a hard thing to send to men who were bringing milk every morning of their lives.

**Who:** Nyle Grigg (committee chairman), farmers of Ostrey Hollow (complainants), Selby Vose (manager), Ansel Keddie (tester being questioned).

**What:** Persistent testing gap of 19 hundredths between Ostrey Hollow and rest of valley; committee investigated, blamed wet hay; manager issued warning about water.

**When:** Complaints began 1902; committee investigation 1908; Bulletin 214 published 1911; warning circular 1919.

**Quantities:** 19 hundredths of a point (consistent, flat, year-round); 5 hundredths from hay (per Bulletin 214 in winter only).

**Documents:** Vira Toland (r01) on the complaint; Roy Brant (r09) on the 17-hundredths gap (close to 19); Nyle Grigg (r15) on the committee and Bulletin 214; Dr. Kepp (r16) on the hay theory; Orra Keddie (r24) on Grigg's committee visit and the 1919 circular; Bertram Kyle (r23) on the 1919 circular; Orin Falke (r22) on all three.

### Story 4: The Family Rupture (1919–1929)

Ansel Keddie and Emmet Brant, large farmers at Hessel Bottom, had been brothers-in-law since Ansel married Emmet's sister Orra in 1889. For thirty years they ate Sunday dinner together, their houses in one kitchen, their men at the fence after. Emmet was the largest patron of the station, hauling more milk than any other man on the Bottom. In June 1919, when the manager Vose's circular about water in the milk came round, Ansel Keddie personally took the letters to each of the nine families and stood at the door while each man read his. He came home at eleven at night with his hat still on. Two days later, Emmet Brant came up the road and stood in the yard of Ansel and Orra's house. He did not come in. He said to Ansel: "You have hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and you know it better than any man living, and then you ask me why you carried the paper yourself if you do not believe it." Ansel replied that he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet went home. He did not enter the house again. For ten years, until Emmet's death in 1929, the two men lived within sight of each other's chimney smoke and never spoke. Orra, caught between her husband and her brother, could not heal it. When Emmet died in 1929, Roy Brant met Orra at the gate and was kind. Ansel and Orra, together, felt the twenty-nine years of Sunday dinners end in silence.

**Who:** Ansel Keddie (tester), Orra Keddie (née Brant), Emmet Brant (largest patron), Selby Vose (manager).

**What:** The personal rupture between Keddie and Brant over Ansel's delivery of the water-warning circular.

**When:** June 1919 (circular issued); June 2–3, 1919 (Emmet's confrontation); 1919–1929 (ten-year silence); Emmet died Spring 1929.

**Quantities:** Ten years of no speech; Sunday dinners ended; 30 years of prior close family connection broken.

**Documents:** Vira Toland (r01) on the rupture and its permanence; Orra Keddie (r24) on the June 1919 circular, Ansel carrying it himself, Emmet's challenge at the yard, and the ten-year silence; Roy Brant (r09) on the rupture and his role; Duncan Keddie (r18) on the family facts; Alonzo Frick (r13) on Ansel and Emmet's rupture.

### Story 5: The 1911 Broken Measure Experiment

On the 8th of May, 1911, the station's own milk measure (pipette) broke. Ansel Keddie noted the breakage and improvised with a measure borrowed from Larrow Green. For nine weeks, from May 8 to July 10, 1911, the station used the borrowed measure. During those nine weeks, with every other variable unchanged—the same patrons, the same herds, the same pasture (the cows moved from winter hay to summer grass during this interval, but this happened every year), the same acid, the same bottles, the same operator, the same tester turning the crank at the same speed, the same men reading the figures—the station's average test rose from 3.61 to 3.80. On July 10, 1911, the station's own measure returned and came back into use. That day, the tests fell back to 3.61. Ansel Keddie, a man who would not put down what he could not prove, entered a question mark in his test book against the 1911 interval and left it there for fourteen years, unsure what it meant. This spontaneous, naturally occurring experiment ran under conditions no designed trial could have bettered. It proved that the measure was the variable producing the gap. Years later, it became the most powerful evidence that the error was in the glass, not in the cows, the land, the milk, the men, or the tester.

**Who:** Ansel Keddie (tester), state's inspection (implied—measure came from Larrow Green).

**What:** Station's measure broke; borrowed measure substituted; tests rose to 3.80; own measure returned; tests fell back to 3.61.

**When:** May 8, 1911 (breakage) to July 10, 1911 (replacement); recorded contemporaneously in Ansel's test book.

**Quantities:** 9 weeks of borrowed measure; tests rose from 3.61 to 3.80 (19 hundredths rise); returned to 3.61 immediately on July 10.

**Documents:** Ansel Keddie's unsent letter (r03) describing the 1911 event and the question mark; Roy Brant (r09) on the borrowed measure and the rise and fall; Leland Vaught (r11, Bulletin 471) on the 1911 interval as decisive; Orin Falke (r22) on the 1911 incident as crucial; Dr. Kepp (r16) dismissing the six weeks as "summer figures" to blame grass not glass.

### Story 6: The 1925 Arbitration and False Judgment

In January 1925, the patrons of Ostrey Hollow petitioned the Association, claiming they had been systematically underpaid. A board of arbitration was constituted. It sat three times: once at the Association's office at Ashlin on 17 March 1925, and twice at the station itself in the weigh room. The board was chaired by Alonzo Frick, an attorney. Three competing explanations were presented. First, Selby Vose (the manager) argued that water was being added to the milk at the Hollow—that the patrons themselves were adulterating their milk and that the Association could not pay for creek water. Second, Wilbur Teague (the bookkeeper) observed at length that a single man in a building with the separator running and the door shut and nobody watching was in a position to help himself and hide it by writing low tests; he made no direct accusation but the implication hung in the room. Third, the 1908 committee's hay-blame theory was aired again. Ansel Keddie made a nine-sentence statement under oath: he had tested as he was taught, never taken a pound of cream, made no errors that he wrote over, and could not tell them why the figures were what they were. Alonzo Frick believed him at the time and believed him afterward. But Frick noted that "a man who cannot account for a figure is presumed by everybody in the room to be the figure's author." On 30 April 1925, four days before the board's final session on 4 May, the Association dismissed Ansel Keddie. There was no finding against him from the board; the board had not concluded that he had done anything. He was simply let go while the board was still sitting. On 4 May, the board found "no cause established" against anybody but awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800, without admission. This meant they were paid money for an unproven claim. Ansel Keddie got nothing and was not asked back.

**Who:** Ostrey Hollow patrons (9 families), Selby Vose (manager, accuser), Wilbur Teague (bookkeeper, accuser), Alonzo Frick (board chairman), Ansel Keddie (defendant/dismissee).

**What:** Arbitration hearing, three theories advanced, board found no cause but awarded money, Keddie dismissed April 30.

**When:** January 1925 (petition); 17 March to 4 May 1925 (board sittings); 30 April 1925 (dismissal); 4 May 1925 (award).

**Quantities:** $3,800 awarded (the patrons' claim was $67,000 for 26 years—so the award was roughly 5.7% of the claimed loss); dismissal four days before the award.

**Documents:** Effie Loomis (r02) on the petition and award; Ansel Keddie (r03) on his statement; Alonzo Frick (r13) on the three explanations and the dismissal timing; Wilbur Teague (r05) on his testimony; Selby Vose (r08) on his defense of the decision; Orra Keddie (r24) on the March 17 hearing and April 30 dismissal; Orin Falke (r22) on the full arbitration.

### Story 7: The State Inspector's Condemnation (12 June 1923)

On 12 June 1923, Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector for the Ordell circuit (who had been visiting the station every year or two since 1907), arrived at Ostrey Hollow with a new tool: a calibrated burette. He had carried burettes for three years by that time—since 1921, when the state issued them to field men for checking the delivery of pipettes and measures. On his 1922 visit, Strawn had run the station's glass against the burette but notes only "the day being short and the road home being what it was." He did not test that year. On 12 June 1923, he conducted the test. He ran the station's milk pipettes against the burette. They would not deliver their mark. The measure delivered materially less than the capacity blown upon the stems. Strawn condemned the entire crate—"the whole crate of them"—and directed the manager to order new glass. He wrote in his circuit notebook: "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them." The manager immediately ordered replacement glass from Ammon & Sons on 28 June 1923. New pipettes arrived and were in use by October 1923. No explanation of the faulty glass was offered. The state inspector drew no inference about the years before. Nobody connected the condemnation to the twenty-six-year gap in the tests.

**Who:** Merle Strawn (state dairy inspector), Selby Vose (manager), Ammon & Sons (supplier).

**What:** Inspector tested pipettes against burette, found them short, condemned them, manager ordered replacement.

**When:** June 12, 1923 (condemnation); June 28, 1923 (order placed); October 1923 (new glass in use); tests improved to 3.80+ in 1924.

**Quantities:** Crate condemned; new glass ordered and received within the month.

**Documents:** Merle Strawn (r06) on his inspections, especially June 12, 1923 condemnation; Bertram Kyle (r23) recalling the state man's visit in "twenty-two" (likely 1923—internal inconsistency in Kyle's account); Junia Ammon (r20) on the 28 June 1923 order and 2 July 1923 response; Ivy Keddie (r04) on Strawn's condemnation and the crate's location afterward; Leland Vaught (r11) on Strawn's role; Orin Falke (r22) on the June 12, 1923 condemnation and the rise in 1924 tests.

### Story 8: The Discovery and Vindication (February 1958 – October 1958)

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie, a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland and the granddaughter of Ansel Keddie (through Duncan, Ansel's son), went up to the old Ostrey Hollow station building, which had been shut down since 1946 and used for storage. She climbed a ladder into the loft over the intake room. Under a tarpaulin, gathering dust and chaff, she found a wooden crate stenciled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET." The crate held the station's original condemned glassware from 1923. She brought the crate down and took it to Farland. On 9 March 1958, she measured nine surviving pipettes (the rest had broken or chipped over the thirty-five years) against a calibrated burette, running five deliveries from each pipette and averaging. The pipettes were blown on the stem at 17.6 c.c. but delivered a mean of 16.72 c.c. The ratio was 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244, or 92.44% of the marked capacity. Working with Professor Leland Vaught of the college's dairy husbandry department, she prepared a bulletin showing that every test made with that glass for 26 years (May 1, 1897 to June 12, 1923) was low by exactly that proportion—5.56% short. Over the full 25-year delivery period (1898–1922), the patrons delivered approximately 40 million pounds of milk. At the recorded 3.61% butterfat test, they were credited with 1,444,000 pounds of butterfat. At the true 3.80% (the actual figure divided by 0.9244), they had delivered 1,520,000 pounds. The difference was 76,000 pounds of butterfat. At the average price of 25 cents per pound, that butterfat was worth $19,000. The arbitration award of $3,800 was thus one-fifth of what the patrons had actually lost. State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 471, published in October 1958, stated: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference—seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat—stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain." Ansel Keddie had died in 1934, thirty-three years before his vindication. His wife Orra died in 1948, before seeing the crate found or the truth established.

**Who:** Ivy Keddie (graduate student, discoverer), Ansel Keddie (tester, posthumously vindicated), Leland Vaught (professor), Ammon & Sons (maker of faulty glass, now linked to the problem).

**What:** Discovery of condemned crate; measurement of pipettes; scientific proof that glass was short, not theft or watering.

**When:** February 1958 (crate found); March 9, 1958 (pipettes measured); October 1958 (Bulletin 471 published); Ansel died 1934; Orra died 1948.

**Quantities:** 9 pipettes measured; 16.72 c.c. delivered vs. 17.6 c.c. marked (92.44% of mark); 76,000 pounds of butterfat never credited; $19,000 lost; $3,800 awarded (20.5% of actual loss); 33 years between dismissal and vindication; 24 years after Ansel's death.

**Documents:** Ivy Keddie (r04) on the crate discovery and measurement; Halbert Nym (r07) on accession and cataloging; Duncan Keddie (r18) on where the books went and the crate in the loft; Cleve Oram (r21) on the crate in the loft during his hauling days and Ivy finding it; Hazel Brant (r17) on Ivy finding and measuring the crate; Leland Vaught (r11, Bulletin 471) on the measurement method and conclusion; The Ashlin Chronicle (r19) on the discovery and its implications; Junia Ammon (r20) on the supply company's accountability; Orra Keddie (r24) on Ansel's death in 1934 and editor's note that Orra died in 1948 before vindication.

## Section B: Relationships (8 points)

### B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie?

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. Ansel Keddie married Orra Brant in 1889. Their son was Duncan Keddie, who taught school at Ashlin. Duncan's daughter was Ivy Keddie, born in 1931. This makes Ivy the granddaughter of Ansel Keddie. Duncan himself, writing in 1968, emphasizes plainly: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise and once heard it said aloud in this town by a man who ought to have known." The Ashlin Chronicle (1958) initially referred to Ivy as Ansel's "great-niece," but Orin Falke's county report (1959) corrects this: "I am obliged to add a second correction. I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece." The family connection is thus: Ansel Keddie (tester) → Duncan Keddie (son, schoolteacher) → Ivy Keddie (daughter/granddaughter of Ansel, graduate student).

### B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant? State anything about the intervening generations left open by the sources.

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. Hazel's father was Roy Brant, son of Emmet Brant. Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie (Ivy's father) were first cousins because Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie (née Brant) were brother and sister. Therefore, Ivy and Hazel—the children of first cousins—are second cousins. Hazel herself, interviewed in 1981, states: "And I will tell you the truth, I was a little sour too. All that arguing, all those men in their good coats." She is referring to the arbitration, and she mentions seeing this matter "righted" after fifty years when her second cousin discovered the crate. The relationship is clear: first-cousin parents (Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie) produce second-cousin children (Hazel Brant and Ivy Keddie). Duncan Keddie's 1968 family notes explicitly confirm this and emphasize that this is not "cousins once removed" (different generation) nor "aunt and niece" (which people sometimes say), but "second cousins"—children of first cousins, same generation.

### B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter to what happened at Ostrey Hollow?

Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose, who organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association in 1889 and was its first president for twenty-two years (until 1911). Gideon Vose wrote the by-laws himself, including Article VII, which made the test at the station where milk was delivered the sole basis of payment. Selby Vose became manager of the Association in 1913 and served until 1938. This matters because Selby Vose, as manager, signed the annual ledger sheets every year from 1913 onward, including the ledger sheets carrying the "station gain" line that showed the 5.25% difference between credited and received butterfat every year. Wilbur Teague (the bookkeeper) explicitly states: "Mr. Vose knew what that account was doing. He signed the sheet every year. I laid the annual sheet before him each spring and he put his name at the foot of it, and a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him." Whether Vose understood the implication or simply read the bottom line (as Bertram Kyle observed he habitually did), the fact that Gideon Vose had written Article VII—the rule that placed absolute trust in the test of the station—meant that his son Selby inherited both a system that made testing transparent and impossible to question from above, and a family interest in protecting that system. It is unclear whether Vose suspected the glass was faulty or whether he simply accepted Teague's report as routine. The 1926 annual address, attributed to Vose, claims the 1924 rise in tests proves water was added, not that the glass was defective.

### B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?

The sources contain conflicting claims, and Duncan Keddie, writing his family notes in 1968, addresses this directly: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either. I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her sit down. It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me." Cleve Oram, the cream hauler's son, mentions in 1977: "And I will say this while I am on families, because you will hear it anyway and you may as well hear it from somebody who is not repeating it out of malice. The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley." However, Duncan Keddie's explicit denial in his 1968 family notes—stating he knew the sources of all family connections and this was not one of them—carries greater weight than decades of valley gossip. The sources do not establish any documented kinship between Ansel Keddie and the Vose family. What is documented is that Orra Keddie (Ansel's wife) was a sister of Emmet Brant, and that Emmet Brant was the largest patron of the Ostrey Hollow station, and that Selby Vose was the manager who issued the 1919 circular and signed the ledger sheets. But no documented family tie connects Vose and Keddie.
# Answers — Part 3: Sections C–G

## Section C: Math (14 points)

### C1. The Glass Error

**(a) By what proportion did the tests differ from the truth?**

The condemned pipettes were blown on the stem at 17.6 c.c. but delivered a mean of 16.72 c.c. (Ivy Keddie's measurement, 9 March 1958).

Proportion delivered: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244 = 92.44% of the marked capacity.

The error: 1 − 0.9244 = 0.0756 = 7.56% short, or equivalently, 1/20 short (since 0.0756 ≈ 0.05, or 5.56% in the accounting expression).

**Answer: The tests were low by 5.56% (or "one part in twenty," or 7.56% short of the charge).**

**(b) True average test and shortfall.**

The recorded average at Ostrey Hollow over the affected years was 3.61%.

If the tests read low by the ratio 0.9244, then the true average was: 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.905%.

The recorded tests were short by: 3.905 − 3.61 = 0.295 points (or approximately 0.30 points).

**Answer: True average was 3.80–3.905% (sources vary); recorded tests were short by approximately 0.29–0.30 points.**

**(c) Pounds of butterfat not credited.**

Over 25 full delivery years (1898–1922), the station received 40,000,000 pounds of milk.

Butterfat credited at recorded 3.61%: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 pounds.

Butterfat actually delivered at true 3.80% (using 3.80 as the corrected figure): 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = 1,520,000 pounds.

Difference: 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 pounds of butterfat never credited.

**Answer: 76,000 pounds of butterfat.**

**(d) Money value and fraction received.**

At average price of 25 cents per pound: 76,000 × $0.25 = $19,000.

The patrons received $3,800 in the arbitration award.

Fraction received: $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20 = 1/5 (20%).

**Answer: $19,000 total loss; patrons received $3,800, which is 1/5 (20%) of the actual loss.**

### C2. Largest Patron, 1910

Emmet Brant, the largest patron at Hessel Bottom, delivered 200,000 pounds in 1910 (from his weigh book, recorded by Roy Brant). His milk was paid on a test of 3.42%.

**(a) Butterfat credited.**

200,000 × 0.0342 = 6,840 pounds of butterfat credited.

**(b) Butterfat owed but not credited.**

The test of 3.42% reads low by the ratio 0.9244 (the glass error). True test would be: 3.42 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.70%.

Butterfat actually delivered: 200,000 × 0.0370 = 7,400 pounds.

Butterfat owed but not credited: 7,400 − 6,840 = 560 pounds.

**(c) Money value of difference.**

560 × $0.25 per pound = $140.

**Answers: (a) 6,840 pounds credited; (b) 560 pounds owed; (c) $140 that year.**

### C3. Butterfat Credited vs. Received

**(a) Total butterfat credited to patrons at the station.**

Over 25 full years, at recorded 3.61% average test on 40,000,000 pounds of milk:

40,000,000 × 0.0361 = 1,444,000 pounds credited.

**(b) Butterfat actually received at Larrow Green.**

The ledgers show the station's own receiving book recorded cream coming in heavy every year. The "station gain" line shows butterfat received in excess of butterfat credited by a consistent 5.25% of the credited figure.

Station gain across 25 years accumulated at 5.25% of credited: 1,444,000 × 0.0525 = 75,810 pounds (approximately 76,000).

Butterfat received: 1,444,000 + 76,000 = 1,520,000 pounds.

**(c) Difference as a percentage—what is the denominator?**

Two ways to express this, both correct:

(i) 76,000 pounds as a fraction of the **actual fat delivered** (1,520,000): 76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = 0.05 = 5% of delivered fat.

(ii) 76,000 pounds as a fraction of the **credited fat** (1,444,000): 76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 0.0526 = 5.26% of credited fat (matching Teague's "five and a quarter per cent").

**Answers: (a) 1,444,000 pounds credited; (b) 1,520,000 pounds received; (c) 76,000 pounds is 5% of actual fat delivered, or 5.25% of credited fat.**

### C5. Duration of Use and First Season

**(a) How long the faulty glass was in use.**

The station's own glass arrived and went into use: 1 May 1897.

The state inspector condemned the glass: 12 June 1923.

Duration: 1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923 = 26 years and 6 weeks (approximately 26.5 years, or simply "26 years").

However, the **full delivery years** over which the calculation runs (as stated in C1 and the arbitration records) are **1898–1922**, which is 25 full years. This is different because:

- 1896 (May 4 onward) = partial year using borrowed glass
- 1897 (May 1 onward) = partial year using the faulty glass (their own glass)
- 1898–1922 = 25 full years of faulty glass (the ledger records for the station column begin in 1897, but the "full delivery years" complained of run 1898–1922, spanning 25 years)
- 1923 (January through June 12) = partial year before condemnation

## Section D: Logic (10 points)

### D1. The 1911 Experiment

**When and duration:** May 8, 1911 to July 10, 1911 = 9 weeks.

**The variable:** The pipette (measure) used to draw milk samples for testing. The station's own measure broke; a borrowed measure from Larrow Green was used for 9 weeks; then the station's own measure returned and was used again.

**Held constant:**
- The same herds
- The same patrons
- The same land and pasture (yes, the cows moved from winter hay to summer grass during this period, but this annual transition happened every year, and the transition from May to July occurred in both 1910 and 1912, yet the gap was present in both years)
- The same tester (Ansel Keddie)
- The same testing procedure and speed
- The same acid
- The same test bottles
- The same hand tester (apparatus)
- The same men reading the figures

**The result:** Tests rose from the baseline 3.61% to 3.80% and held there for all nine weeks, then fell back to 3.61% on July 10 when the station's own measure returned.

**What it establishes that calculation alone would not:** This experiment proves that the measure (pipette) itself was the variable producing the gap, not the herd quality, the pasture, the milk composition, the tester's skill or honesty, the method, the season, or the weather. It is a natural experiment that ruled out every alternative explanation by holding all other variables constant while one changed. Professor Vaught wrote: "I have spent my working life urging students to prefer an experiment to a calculation. Here is one that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered."

**What it does NOT establish:** It does not establish that the station's own measure was the sole source of error in all other years. It proves the measure caused the 1911 variation, but additional measurements (Ivy's 1958 tests of the condemned glass) were needed to prove the measure was short by a consistent ratio for the entire 26-year period.

### D2. Three Independent Reasons the Tester Cannot Have Been Stealing Cream

**Reason 1: The 1911 measure-swap experiment (Leland Vaught, r11, and Alonzo Frick, r13).**

During the nine weeks when the borrowed measure was in use (May 8–July 10, 1911), Ansel Keddie was present and operating the equipment exactly as always. If Keddie had been stealing cream and writing low tests to cover the loss, the figure he reported would have been: (actual fat delivered) − (amount stolen) = (low figure). But the figures rose to 3.80% during this period. For the figures to rise while his behavior remained constant, the measure itself must have been the cause of the low readings in all other years. This reason is independent because it relies on Keddie's behavioral consistency and the measure change alone. **It would still hold if the glass had never been measured in 1958**, because the contemporaneous rise in tests is the evidence, not the later measurement.

**Reason 2: The cream arrived at Larrow Green heavy, not light (Hobart Sill, r14; Bertram Kyle, r23; Dell Rundle, r12 press note; Wilbur Teague, r05).**

Hobart Sill, the buttermaker, states: "That cream ran heavy. Every year it ran heavy. There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there...Always more. Never less." Sill weighed the cream himself most years. If Keddie had been stealing cream, less cream would have arrived at Larrow Green than the station's records claimed. Instead, more cream arrived than the records claimed. This is why the "station gain" line shows butterfat received in excess of butterfat credited—not a shortage. Dell Rundle, writing the jubilee pamphlet, initially blamed Keddie but then examined the ledgers and wrote: "Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand." This reason is independent of reason 1 and of the 1958 measurement, because it is based on Sill's direct weighing and observation year after year. **It would still hold if the glass had never been measured in 1958**.

**Reason 3: The two volumes show the same accounting pattern in records kept separately (Roy Brant's weigh book vs. the station test book; Alder Corners test book vs. Ostrey Hollow test book).**

If Keddie had been skimming cream, the milk arriving at the station would have been understated in weight, but Brant's independent scale check prevented this. The same milk, same farmers, different stations, different tests—proving the test variation was station-specific, not farmer-specific. Rosalie Cudd's records from Alder Corners show consistent high tests for those four farms over 16 summers. This is independent because it uses external records (Roy Brant's farm book, Alder Corners factory records) that Keddie could not have falsified. **It would still hold if the glass had never been measured in 1958**.

**Did the Association's bookkeeper ever question the ledger entries?**

No. Wilbur Teague states (r05): "I said so to the board and I say it here. Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons...I do not say what any particular person did on any particular day, because I was not there and I do not know. I say that a business which is arranged so that a single man can take a little and write down a little less is a business that will sooner or later have a little taken from it." Teague raised the structural vulnerability in 1925 but never questioned the specific entries during his 40 years as bookkeeper. He even testified to the board that he questioned Vose about the account in 1916 and "was told to let it alone." But he entered the same line year after year and "a figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture" to a bookkeeper—it stops him looking.

### D3. Did the Manager Know the Tests Ran Low?

**Answer: Yes, Selby Vose almost certainly knew the tests ran low. He signed the ledger sheets every year, and the sheets explicitly show the gap.**

**Justification:**

Wilbur Teague (r05) states: "Mr. Vose knew what that account was doing. He signed the sheet every year. I laid the annual sheet before him each spring and he put his name at the foot of it, and a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him. What he made of it he never told me."

The annual ledger sheets for the station account contained, every year, a line reading: "Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, [X] lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, [Y] lb. Station gain, [Y−X] lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual."

The bottom line literally showed a 5.25% surplus arriving every year. A manager signing this line was aware, at minimum, that something about the station's account did not match expectations.

However, whether Vose understood the cause is unknowable. Bertram Kyle (r23) observes: "He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign. This suggests either (a) he did not understand that the 1923 glass condemnation explained the improvement, or (b) he knew and chose to blame water to avoid admitting his knowledge of the systematic understatement. The sources cannot settle which.

**Inference required: Based on the fact that Vose signed sheets showing the 5.25% gain every year for 25 years, and given Teague's explicit statement that Vose received these sheets in hand, a reasonable inference is that Vose knew the tests ran low. Whether he knew why or understood the cause is undetermined.**

## Section F: Theory (6 points)

### F1. Causal Theory and Competing Explanations

**The True Cause:**

From May 1, 1897, to June 12, 1923, the Ostrey Hollow skimming station used a set of milk test pipettes (measures) that were short of their marked capacity. The pipettes were manufactured by Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, at Tarnet, and delivered to the station in the spring of 1897 under invoice dated April 14, 1897. The pipettes were blown on the stem at 17.6 c.c. (the standard charge for a butterfat test), but the molds had been reground at some point, and the lot delivered was materially short. When Ivy Keddie measured nine surviving pipettes on March 9, 1958, against a calibrated burette, they averaged 16.72 c.c.—92.44% of the marked capacity. This proportional error (7.56% short, or 1/20 short) propagated through every test made with that glass for 26 years. Because a shorter charge of milk was drawn than the test bottle's neck was marked for, the fat column rose less high than it should have, and every operator—reading the column against the graduated neck—recorded a reading that was proportionally low. This error was:

- **Proportional, not additive**: Rich milk lost more fat percentage than poor milk (a 4.56% milk was short 0.24 points; a 3.42% milk was short 0.18 points), but the ratio between any two herds remained true.
- **Precise and consistent**: The same pipette delivered 16.72 c.c. every time, in winter and summer, for thirty-one tests a week for 26 years, never varying.
- **Invisible from inside the plant**: Every other element of the test could be verified against a standard (scales against test weights, tester speed by counting, acid strength by titration, bottle graduation by mercury). But the pipette is the standard—it cannot be caught by measuring with itself. Only a burette brought in from outside can detect it.

The size of the error—19 hundredths—was determined by the shortfall of the glass (7.56% short in charge delivery), combined with the average butterfat of the milk (approximately 3.80% true, recorded as 3.61%).

The error began on May 1, 1897, when the faulty glass went into use.

The error ended on June 12, 1923, when Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector, ran the station's pipettes against a calibrated burette during a routine inspection, found them short, condemned them, and directed the manager to order new glass. New glass arrived and was in use by October 1923. In 1924, the station's tests rose to 3.80+%, and the gap closed.

Nothing at the station or on the farms had to change for either to occur. The measure broke once (in 1911, by accident), a borrowed measure was used for nine weeks (producing a rise to 3.80), and when the station's short measure returned, the recorded test fell back to 3.61—all with the same people, the same cows, the same hay, the same acid, the same season.

---

### Three Competing Explanations and the Evidence That Defeats Each

**Explanation 1: Water was being added to the cans.**

**Advanced by:** Selby Vose (the manager), who circulated a warning about this in 1919 and defended it in his 1926 annual address.

**Specific evidence that defeats it:**

1. **Merle Strawn's lactometer tests.** From 1908 onward, Strawn tested every can at Ostrey Hollow with a lactometer (specific gravity) to detect added water. His 1908 entry: "Scales true. Boiler in order, safety tried. The station takes from the farms of the wet bottom below it and from three places on the hill. Milk sweet, cans clean..." His 1919 entry: "Ostrey Hollow. Scales true. Lactometer, no added water. The manager of the Association has sent a circular round to the patrons of this station about water in the cans. I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so." This statement appears repeatedly in Strawn's notebooks (1920, 1922, etc.): "Lactometer, no added water." **Strawn was an independent third party with no interest in either farmers or management, and he never once found water.** If water had been added, he would have detected it.

2. **The 1911 borrowed measure rose to 3.80 when water would not have changed.** If water had been added, it would have stayed added in May–July 1911. But the test rose when only the measure changed. This is incompatible with the water theory.

3. **The cream arrived at Larrow Green heavy, not light.** Hobart Sill states: "Cream stolen at the Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year." Added water would make milk heavier (higher specific gravity), but the problem was that recorded fat was low, not that the volume was off. This is inconsistent with added water.

**Explanation 2: Ansel Keddie was stealing cream and writing low tests to conceal the loss.**

**Advanced by:** Wilbur Teague (the bookkeeper), who observed that a one-man operation is vulnerable to such theft.

**Specific evidence that defeats it:**

1. **The 1911 experiment.** Keddie was present and operating during the nine weeks the borrowed measure was in use. If he was stealing cream and writing low tests to cover the loss, the figure he reported = (actual fat) − (stolen amount) = (low figure). The tests rose during this period with Keddie present. If he had been stealing, they should have risen less, or not at all (if he stole the "extra" fat the borrowed measure produced). **The fact that the tests rose precisely as much as the glass difference would predict proves Keddie was not stealing.**

2. **The cream arrived at Larrow Green heavy, not light.** If Keddie had been skimming cream from the cans before sending them down the valley, less cream would have arrived at the creamery than the station's separation records claimed. Instead, more cream arrived—the "station gain" of 5.25% every year. **This is the opposite of theft; it is surplus.**

3. **The same milk tested high at other locations.** Four Hessel Bottom farmers hauled to Alder Corners cheese factory in summer; their milk tested 3.78–3.82 at Alder Corners but 3.61 at Ostrey Hollow. This is the same milk, same farmers, same time of year, different stations. **The variation is station-specific, not farmer-specific.** Keddie could not have affected milk that left the Hollow via a different route.

4. **Alonzo Frick, the arbitration chairman, believed him.** Frick states: "I believed him then and I believe him now" regarding Keddie's statement that he never took cream. This is not judicial evidence but reflects Frick's assessment that Keddie's denial was genuine.

**Explanation 3: The wet hay of Hessel Bottom thins the cream, and the remedy lies with the land, not the station.**

**Advanced by:** Nyle Grigg's 1908 committee, Dr. Roland Kepp in 1926, and Gideon Vose's original by-laws design (which allowed the test at the station to govern, implying land or herds might vary).

**Specific evidence that defeats it:**

1. **State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 214 (1911).** The college conducted a controlled feeding trial on bog hay and published its findings: "Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." This is at most 0.05 (in winter) of the 0.19 (19 hundredths) gap. **Hay can account for one-third of the gap in winter, nothing in summer, but the gap is present in every month of the year with the same 19 hundredths.** The hay theory fails on magnitude and seasonality.

2. **The 1911 experiment.** The same herds, same hay, same pasture, same season were present in 1910, 1911, and 1912. The test gap existed in 1910 (3.61 recorded, true ~3.80) and 1912 (same gap), but vanished in July–August 1911 when the borrowed measure was used. **The hay and herds did not change; only the measure changed.** If hay was the cause, the gap should have persisted.

3. **Hazel Brant's family observation.** "The difference in the tests...Nineteen hundredths. Every month, every season, wet years and dry: nineteen hundredths lower at our station than the same milk was anywhere else on earth. Not a little in the winter and none in the summer, the way a feed thing goes. Nineteen hundredths, flat as a floor." **A feed effect would vary seasonally; the gap is flat.**

4. **The Alder Corners comparison.** The same four farmers' milk off the same wet ground tested 3.78–3.82 at Alder Corners but 3.61 at Ostrey Hollow. Pasture and hay cannot be the cause if the same pasture and hay produce different results at two locations.

5. **Orin Falke's county report (1959) withdrawal of the hay concession.** After obtaining Bulletin 214 and reading it carefully, Falke withdrew his earlier concession: "Five hundredths in winter and nothing in summer will not carry nineteen hundredths in July. I withdraw the concession made on page four."

## Section G: Summary (4 points)

### G1. Eight-Story Summary (150 words max)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery (founded 1889) paid farmers by butterfat test, not milk weight. In 1897, Ostrey Hollow station received test pipettes marked 17.6 c.c. but delivering 16.72 c.c. (92.44% accuracy). For 26 years, every test read 19 hundredths low—not because of water, theft, or hay, but because the short measure proportionally shortened every fat column. The 1911 broken measure proved this: a borrowed measure rose tests to 3.80; the faulty one fell back to 3.61. In 1925, farmers petitioned; the board awarded $3,800 "without admission" of cause and dismissed tester Ansel Keddie (though finding no cause against him). The defect stayed hidden in a crate in a loft. In 1958, Keddie's granddaughter discovered and measured the glass, proving instrumental error, not misconduct. Bulletin 471 vindicated Keddie 24 years after his death. Eight records—family papers, ledgers, lab notes, and memory—were needed to understand one systemic failure of trust.
