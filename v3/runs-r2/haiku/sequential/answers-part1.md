# Answers — Part 1: Section A, Stories 1–4

## Section A: Reconstruction (8 Stories)

### Story 1: The Founding and Article VII (1889)

Gideon Vose, a farmer of Larrow Valley, called a meeting at Larrow Green on a cold evening and led the farmers to organize the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. Vose was elected the first president—a position he held for twenty-two years—and he wrote the by-laws himself at his kitchen table in a hand that clerks would complain about for thirty years. The by-laws, adopted formally in 1892 (after the creamery was built in 1891), included Article VII, which proved to be the hinge of all that followed. Article VII stated that every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butterfat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk. The clause that mattered most was the last one: "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." This meant no appeal, no rechecking at the creamery, no average across the valley—only the test made at the patron's own station. Gideon Vose put this in for convenience in 1892, and it was convenient for thirty years. It was the sentence on which the whole trouble at the head of the valley afterward turned.

**Who:** Gideon Vose (first president), the farmers of Larrow Valley.

**What:** Organized a cooperative creamery association and established a payment system based on butterfat test rather than weight of milk.

**When:** Meeting and decision 1889; creamery built 1891; Article VII formally adopted 1892.

**Quantities:** Twenty-two years Vose served as president; Larrow Valley cooperative included nine families from Ostrey Hollow initially.

**Documents:** Effie Loomis (r02) on Article VII's text and adoption; Dell Rundle (r12) on Gideon Vose's character and writing; Halbert Nym (r07, accession note) on by-laws; Orin Falke (r22) on Article VII's adoption and meaning.

### Story 2: The Station Opens and the Glass Equipment Arrives (1895–1897)

In 1895 and 1896, the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley from Larrow Green, to serve the farms of Hessel Bottom principally. The station opened on the 4th of May, 1896. Ansel Keddie, a careful man in small things, took the tester and separator position from the opening and kept the test book in his own hand for twenty-nine seasons. Hobart Sill, the buttermaker at Larrow Green, lent his own testing glass to the station for that first season because the Association's own equipment had not arrived. The station's official outfit was ordered from Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, at Tarnet, and arrived in the spring of 1897. The invoice, dated 14 April 1897, listed six dozen milk test pipettes (17.6 c.c.), twelve dozen test bottles, one acid measure, one hand tester (4-bottle), and freight, totaling $77.93. Hobart Sill's glass was returned to him, with one piece chipped (which he never mentioned). The new glass went into use on 1 May 1897, the same day that Ansel Keddie's test book officially began, though the station had been taking milk since May 4, 1896. This equipment—particularly the pipettes—would remain in use for twenty-six years, from May 1, 1897, to June 12, 1923.

**Who:** Ansel Keddie (tester), Hobart Sill (buttermaker, lender of glass), Ammon & Sons (supplier), Association officers.

**What:** Built the skimming station; opened it; acquired and installed testing equipment from Ammon & Sons.

**When:** Building 1895–1896; opening May 4, 1896; equipment invoice April 14, 1897; official equipment in use May 1, 1897.

**Quantities:** $77.93 for the outfit; 6 dozen (72) pipettes marked 17.6 c.c.; 12 dozen test bottles; equipment in use 26 years (1897–1923).

**Documents:** Ivy Keddie (r04) on the crate, invoice, and dates; Junia Ammon (r20) on the 1897 invoice; Bertram Kyle (r23) on the station's opening date; Ansel Keddie (r03) on his beginning date; Halbert Nym (r07) on the invoice and station history.

### Story 3: The Persistent Complaint and the Hay Theory (1902–1919)

From 1902 onward, the milk of Ostrey Hollow tested lower than the milk of anywhere else in the valley—consistently about nineteen hundredths of a point below Larrow Green. The gap did not vary seasonally; it was nineteen hundredths in July when the cows were on grass and nineteen hundredths in February when they were on hay. The gap did not care whether the year was wet or dry, the season abundant or poor. The first men to say so out loud were laughed at in 1902, and the complaint grew through the decade. In 1908, the Association appointed an investigating committee under Nyle Grigg, which visited the Hessel Bottom herds through January, February, and March, sampled the milk, watched the test, and reported that the wet hay of the bottom thins the cream and that the remedy lies with the land and not with the station. Grigg's committee found no dishonesty anywhere. The State Agricultural College's Bulletin No. 214 (published 1911) tested the effect of bog hay and found it depressed butterfat by "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture"—far short of the nineteen-hundredths gap. In 1919, the Association's manager Selby Vose circulated a written warning to the Ostrey Hollow patrons about water in their milk, in civil language but with unmistakable meaning. The circular went to nine families. None answered it. It was a hard thing to send to men who were bringing milk every morning of their lives.

**Who:** Nyle Grigg (committee chairman), farmers of Ostrey Hollow (complainants), Selby Vose (manager), Ansel Keddie (tester being questioned).

**What:** Persistent testing gap of 19 hundredths between Ostrey Hollow and rest of valley; committee investigated, blamed wet hay; manager issued warning about water.

**When:** Complaints began 1902; committee investigation 1908; Bulletin 214 published 1911; warning circular 1919.

**Quantities:** 19 hundredths of a point (consistent, flat, year-round); 5 hundredths from hay (per Bulletin 214 in winter only).

**Documents:** Vira Toland (r01) on the complaint; Roy Brant (r09) on the 17-hundredths gap (close to 19); Nyle Grigg (r15) on the committee and Bulletin 214; Dr. Kepp (r16) on the hay theory; Orra Keddie (r24) on Grigg's committee visit and the 1919 circular; Bertram Kyle (r23) on the 1919 circular; Orin Falke (r22) on all three.

### Story 4: The Family Rupture (1919–1929)

Ansel Keddie and Emmet Brant, large farmers at Hessel Bottom, had been brothers-in-law since Ansel married Emmet's sister Orra in 1889. For thirty years they ate Sunday dinner together, their houses in one kitchen, their men at the fence after. Emmet was the largest patron of the station, hauling more milk than any other man on the Bottom. In June 1919, when the manager Vose's circular about water in the milk came round, Ansel Keddie personally took the letters to each of the nine families and stood at the door while each man read his. He came home at eleven at night with his hat still on. Two days later, Emmet Brant came up the road and stood in the yard of Ansel and Orra's house. He did not come in. He said to Ansel: "You have hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and you know it better than any man living, and then you ask me why you carried the paper yourself if you do not believe it." Ansel replied that he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet went home. He did not enter the house again. For ten years, until Emmet's death in 1929, the two men lived within sight of each other's chimney smoke and never spoke. Orra, caught between her husband and her brother, could not heal it. When Emmet died in 1929, Roy Brant met Orra at the gate and was kind. Ansel and Orra, together, felt the twenty-nine years of Sunday dinners end in silence.

**Who:** Ansel Keddie (tester), Orra Keddie (née Brant), Emmet Brant (largest patron), Selby Vose (manager).

**What:** The personal rupture between Keddie and Brant over Ansel's delivery of the water-warning circular.

**When:** June 1919 (circular issued); June 2–3, 1919 (Emmet's confrontation); 1919–1929 (ten-year silence); Emmet died Spring 1929.

**Quantities:** Ten years of no speech; Sunday dinners ended; 30 years of prior close family connection broken.

**Documents:** Vira Toland (r01) on the rupture and its permanence; Orra Keddie (r24) on the June 1919 circular, Ansel carrying it himself, Emmet's challenge at the yard, and the ten-year silence; Roy Brant (r09) on the rupture and his role; Duncan Keddie (r18) on the family facts; Alonzo Frick (r13) on Ansel and Emmet's rupture.
