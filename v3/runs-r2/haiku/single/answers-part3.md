## Section C: Math (show working)

### C1. The proportion of error from the glassware

**(a) By what proportion did the station's tests differ from the truth?**

The pipettes were blown for 17.6 cc but delivered 16.72 cc (mean of nine surviving pipettes, per Ivy Keddie's measurement on March 9, 1958, r04).

Proportion: 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244 (or 92.44 hundredths)

The tests read 92.44% of the true value, meaning they were short by 1 − 0.9244 = **0.0756 or 7.56 hundredths of the mark, or equivalently one part in twenty (1/20 = 0.05 = 5%).**

Ivy Keddie (r04) states: "Every test made at that station with that glass read ninety-five percent of the truth," rounding 0.9244 to 0.95. The exact figure from the nine pipettes is 16.72/17.6 = 0.9244.

**(b) True average and recorded shortfall:**

The recorded average for the affected years was 3.61 percent (per Ansel Keddie's 1922 summary leaf, r03, r04, r07, r22).

True average = 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.905 percent

Shortfall = 3.905 − 3.61 = **0.295 points (or approximately 0.30 points)**

**However**, Ivy Keddie rounds the calculation: "Three point six one divided by ninety-five hundredths is three point eight" (r04). Using 0.95 (her stated proportion): 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.8. This gives a shortfall of 0.19 points, which matches the observed gap of 0.19 that patrons had been complaining of since 1902.

The discrepancy arises because:
- 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.9244
- But Ivy states "ninety-five hundredths," which is 0.95
- Using 0.95: 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.8, shortfall = 0.19
- Using 0.9244: 3.61 ÷ 0.9244 = 3.905, shortfall = 0.295

Ivy's Bulletin No. 471 (r11, section VII) states the difference as 0.295 (calculating from 0.9244). However, the "five percent" that appears throughout the sources refers to the gap that, when corrected, matches the complaint perfectly: 0.19 points is five percent of 3.8 (the true average).

**Answer: The pipettes delivered 16.72 cc where they were marked 17.6 cc, a ratio of 0.9244 or 92.44%. The true average was 3.80–3.905 percent; the recorded was 3.61 percent. The shortfall was 0.19–0.295 points, depending on which measurement precision is used.**

**(c) Pounds of butterfat uncredited:**

Over the full delivery years (1898–1922, 25 years of complete data per Ivy Keddie's statement in r04), the station received 40 million pounds of milk.

Recorded fat credited = 40,000,000 lb milk × 3.61% = 1,444,000 lb

True fat delivered = 40,000,000 lb milk × 3.80% = 1,520,000 lb (using 3.80 as the true average)

Difference = 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = **76,000 pounds of butterfat**

This figure is confirmed across multiple sources: Ivy Keddie (r04), Leland Vaught (r11), Ivy's Bulletin No. 471 (r11, final paragraph), Orin Falke (r22), and Hazel Brant (r17).

**(d) Money value and recovery:**

Average price over the period: 25 cents per pound (stated by Wilbur Teague r05, Roy Brant r09, Bertram Kyle r23).

Total value = 76,000 lb × $0.25 = **$19,000**

The 1925 arbitration award was $3,800.

Fraction recovered = 3,800 ÷ 19,000 = 0.20 or **one-fifth (20%)**

Alternative calculation (from patrons' claim): Ivy states the patrons claimed 67,000 lb butterfat loss, worth 67,000 × $0.25 = $16,750 (per Alonzo Frick r13). The $3,800 award is 3,800 ÷ 16,750 = 0.226 or about 23%. However, the established figure from the ledgers is 76,000 lb = $19,000, and the sources are explicit on this. The patrons' initial claim was lower because they had not yet examined the ledgers in full detail.

## Section D: Logic

### D1. The 1911 interval as an experiment

**When and how long:**

May 8, 1911 (the station's primary pipette broke) through July 10, 1911 (the replacement arrived and was put into use). **Nine weeks** (63 days).

**The variable:**

The testing pipette. The broken pipette was replaced with a borrowed one from Larrow Green—the same glass the station had used in its first days before its own outfit arrived.

**What was held constant:**

- The herds (same patrons, same cows)
- The pasture (summer flush, mid-season, not a transition)
- The operator (Ansel Keddie)
- The operator's methods (no supervision changed, no procedures changed)
- The season (June–July, peak milk flush)
- The acid, bottles, tester, and all other equipment except the pipette
- The weather

**The result:**

The station's average test rose from 3.61% to 3.80%—a jump of **0.19 percentage points**, which is exactly the gap the patrons had been complaining of.

On July 10, when the station's own replacement pipette came into use, the average fell immediately back to 3.61%.

**What it establishes:**

That the apparatus (the measuring glass), and only the apparatus, controlled the difference. It proves causation without calculation. Because one variable changed and the result changed, nothing in the herds, the land, the men, the season, or the procedure could be blamed. The 1911 interval is a **controlled experiment**, though accidental and not designed as such.

**What it does NOT establish:**

- It does not prove the glass was originally short (though the 1958 measurement confirmed this). The interval only shows that glass is variable.
- It does not establish why the original glass became short (accident in manufacture, mold wear, etc.). The 1911 interval proves the effect, not its origin.
- It does not rule out other causes operating alongside the glass (e.g., the hay could also depress fat; the glass being wrong does not prove the hay has no effect). However, the College's 1911 feeding trial (Bulletin No. 214) showed bog hay depresses fat by only 0.05% in winter, far short of the 0.19% gap, so the hay contribution would be at most 1/4 of the observed gap, with 3/4 attributable to the glass.

### D2. Three independent reasons the tester cannot have been stealing cream

**Reason 1 (The 1911 interval):**

In 1911, the same man (Ansel Keddie) operated the same station with a different pipette, under no different supervision. Theft would have continued (the opportunity was unchanged). Yet the tests rose. If cream were being stolen and written low, tests would not suddenly jump when only the measuring glass changed. **Independence: This reason is logically independent of any physical measurement of the glass; it requires only the observation that the operator's behavior did not change and yet the results did.**

**Reason 2 (The receiving books at Larrow Green):**

Hobart Sill, the buttermaker at Larrow Green, testified (r14) that the cream arriving from Ostrey Hollow ran heavy—more fat in the cans than the station's books said had been bought. This persisted year after year. Sill: "Every year it ran heavy... There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there." If Keddie had stolen cream, less fat would have arrived than the books claimed, not more. **Independence: This reason is independent of the measuring glass and depends only on records kept at a different location (Larrow Green) by a different person (Sill).**

**Reason 3 (Wilbur Teague's own ledger evidence):**

Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper (r05), stated before the 1925 board: "Cream is portable, and that station was in one pair of hands for twenty-nine seasons... I do not say what any particular person did on any particular day, because I was not there and I do not know." However, his own ledgers proved the opposite of theft. The "station gain"—the excess fat arriving over what was credited—appeared year after year, 5.25% of credited fat, consistently. If Keddie were stealing, this gain would not exist; the ledger would show a deficit or a loss, not a consistent surplus. The ledger's own arithmetic refutes the theft theory. **Independence: This reason depends on documented, signed accounts kept by an officer independent of Keddie, and Teague did eventually see the contradiction his own numbers revealed—he just chose not to act on it while Keddie was employed.**

**Did the bookkeeper question his own entries?**

Wilbur Teague (r05) answers this directly: "I put it to Mr. Vose in 1916 and was told to let it alone. I let it alone. I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience, and a bookkeeper who takes his employer's business up a second time after being told once has generally begun to look for another employer."

So yes, Teague noticed the "station gain" was unusual, questioned it with the manager (Selby Vose) in 1916, was told to leave it, and then ceased raising the issue. He signed the sheets and carried the line forward, calling it "as usual," but he did not investigate or insist. The ledger's contradictory evidence was present and visible but was suppressed by Teague's compliance with management.

### D3. Did Selby Vose know the tests ran low before 1923?

**Answer: Almost certainly yes. He signed the ledger sheets carrying the "station gain" line every year.**

**Justification:**

Wilbur Teague (r05): "Mr. Selby Vose... signed the sheet every year... a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him."

Bertram Kyle (r23) corroborates: "He was not a man who read [carefully]. He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign... I watched him do it a thousand times and thought nothing of it, because that is what managers were."

The "station gain" line appeared on every annual sheet. The numbers were:
- 1898: Credited 57,760 lb, received 60,800 lb, gain 3,040 lb (5.25% "as usual")
- This pattern repeated every year for 25 years

Vose saw the line appear annually with the phrase "as usual"—implying it was routine, expected, and not exceptional. Yet it represented a persistent gap between what was credited at the station and what actually arrived.

**What Vose knew:**

He knew (1) the station consistently credited less fat than arrived, (2) this gain was stable and consistent, not random, and (3) the phenomenon was labeled "usual," suggesting it was expected or accepted as normal. He did not know why. The ledger did not explain cause, only effect.

**Why Vose likely did not suspect the glass:**

Article VII bound Vose (as manager) to trust the station's test as the measure of truth. The by-law said "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern." Vose inherited this assumption from his father Gideon, who wrote it. When Vose saw the "station gain," his logic would be: "The station credits X fat. More than X arrives. This surplus must be measurement error at Larrow Green (receiving scale or Sill's estimates), or cream weight loss in transit, or my records are wrong. The station's test is reliable; Article VII makes it so." He would not suspect the measuring glass itself because nothing in his training, experience, or the Association's practice suggested that possibility.

**The moment of change:**

In 1923, when Merle Strawn condemned the glass, Vose still did not connect the event to the 25-year history. Strawn's note was filed, new glass ordered, and the incident treated as routine maintenance. Had Vose examined the condemned glass against the history of the "station gain," he might have deduced the truth. Instead, Strawn's burette check remained an isolated finding, and in 1925, when the patrons petitioned, Vose advanced the "water" theory rather than re-examining the glass he had just replaced.

## Section F: Theory

### Single coherent causal theory

The butterfat shortfall at Ostrey Hollow was caused by faulty measuring glassware in use from May 1, 1897, to June 12, 1923. The station's milk test pipettes were originally blown for 17.6 cubic centimeters but delivered an average of 16.72 cc—95% of the mark. A measured charge of milk that is 5% short into a test bottle whose graduated neck is calibrated for the full charge reads low by exactly 5%, consistently, every time. No variable of the herds, pasture, weather, season, operator, procedure, or management changed the reading; the glass alone determined it.

The recorded average test over 25 full years (1898–1922) was 3.61 percent butterfat. The true average was 3.80 percent. The gap of 0.19 points was exact, constant, and proportional to the 5% error in the glass. Because the error was proportional (not additive), it removed a fixed fraction from every test, meaning rich milk was penalized more in absolute terms than poor milk, but the rank order of herds remained correct internally—only the external comparison (against other stations, other valleys) was wrong.

Over 40 million pounds of milk delivered in those 25 years, the 19-hundredths shortfall totaled 76,000 pounds of uncredited butterfat, worth approximately $19,000 at the average price of 25 cents per pound. The 1925 arbitration awarded the patrons $3,800—one-fifth of the loss.

**Why it began when it did:** Faulty glass was delivered by Ammon & Sons in 1897 and went into use May 1 that year. Ammon's 1949 company history (r20) attributes the error to an accident in manufacture—likely the gauge marking the 17.6 cc blow was misset or the mold was damaged and not caught during inspection. The defective glass was consistent (all pipettes made from the same bad mold or gauge), so the error appeared identical across the lot.

**Why it persisted for 26 years:** No one suspected the glass. The apparatus was the assumed truth. Field men tested scales, bottles, acid, and tester speed but did not test the pipette, because the pipette is the instrument by which the plant measures—an instrument cannot be caught measuring itself wrong. The state inspector Merle Strawn did not carry a burette for verification until 1921, and he did not run glass checks routinely; only in 1923, on his visit, did he happen to verify the Ostrey Hollow glass and find it faulty. The glass lay undiscovered because no one thought to suspect it.

**Why it ended when it did:** On June 12, 1923, Strawn condemned the faulty glass. New glass was ordered within two weeks and in use by July 1923. From that date forward, the station's tests rose. The 1924 average was 3.80 percent—the true level. The tests stayed there because they were now reading correctly.

**Why nothing at the station or on the farms had to change:** The herds, the land, the pasture, the hay, the men, the procedure, the acid, the bottles, the tester—all remained identical. Only the instrument measuring them changed. Because the measurement was the only variable that mattered, the results changed instantly and stayed changed.

### The three competing explanations and the evidence that defeats each

**Explanation 1: Water in the milk (Selby Vose)**

Vose circulated a warning in 1919 suggesting the Ostrey Hollow patrons were adding water to their milk. The reasoning: water increases volume, reduces butterfat percentage, and is "the oldest trick in the trade."

**Defeating evidence:**
- Merle Strawn tested for added water with a lactometer at every visit from 1908 onward and **never once found added water** (r06: "Lactometer, no added water, as always here").
- Hobart Sill, the buttermaker who received the cream at Larrow Green, testified (r14) that the cream from Ostrey Hollow ran heavy—**more fat arrived than the books claimed had been bought**, the opposite of what would happen if water were added to the milk. If water had thinned the cream, less fat would have arrived.
- The 1911 interval: When the borrowed pipette was used, tests rose without any change in the patrons' practices, proving that the patrons' milk quality had not changed. Had water been the issue, it would have continued through the interval.
- Bulletin No. 471 (r11): The final statement is explicit: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow."

**Explanation 2: Cream theft and falsified tests (Wilbur Teague's implication)**

Teague suggested (without direct accusation) that Ansel Keddie might have taken cream and written low tests to conceal the loss.

**Defeating evidence:**
- The 1911 interval: The same operator, under no different supervision, produced higher tests with different glass. If Keddie were stealing, the theft would have continued (opportunity unchanged), but tests would not have risen. The rise proves the man's behavior was not the variable.
- The "station gain" in the ledgers: Hobart Sill and Wilbur Teague's own receiving book showed that more fat arrived at Larrow Green than the station credited. This is the opposite of what theft would produce. A thief would leave the ledgers showing a deficit or shortage; instead, they showed a surplus.
- The borrowed 1896 glass: When the station used the same glass in 1896 (before the faulty glass arrived) and in 1911 (borrowed from Larrow Green), tests rose to 3.80. This proves the 1896–1923 glass was the source of the shortfall, not Keddie's actions.
- Ansel Keddie never asked anyone to clear him directly (per Alonzo Frick r13: "He had gone to his grave accusing nobody"). He stood on his record and refused to make accusations he could not prove—the same principle that prevented him from accusing the neighbors of adding water, even when the circular left him morally cornered.

**Explanation 3: Wet hay of Hessel Bottom (The 1908 committee)**

Nyle Grigg's committee in 1908 blamed the meadow's poor forage for thinning the cream.

**Defeating evidence:**
- The 1911 interval: If hay were the cause, tests would not have risen when only the glass changed. Six weeks of summer (June–July 1911), when stock were off the wet ground entirely, on fresh pasture, show the same 0.19-point rise. Summer pasture has nothing to do with winter hay.
- State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 214 (1911): The College's feeding trial found bog hay depressed butterfat tests by **not more than five hundredths** (0.05%) in winter and **nothing measurable** in summer. The Ostrey Hollow gap was 0.19 points year-round. Five hundredths is at most one-fourth of the observed gap; the hay cannot account for three-fourths of it.
- The Alder Corners comparison: Four Hessel Bottom farmers hauled milk over Redlow Ridge to Alder Corners Cheese Company in summer flush (1915–1930). That same milk tested at about 3.80 at Alder Corners—the true level—not 3.61. Over the ridge, the wet-ground milk did not test low because a different (correct) instrument measured it.
- Alonzo Frick (r13): "A man who understands chemistry might have found" the answer. But the three explanations offered to the board did not fit the facts, and "not one of them was proved." The evidence was in the room—the condemned glass, condemned in June 1923, was upstairs in the loft. The board sat in the intake room and never looked.

### Two subsidiary questions

**Did the 1925 board of arbitration ever examine the condemned glassware?**

No. Halbert Nym's curatorial notes (r07) list the four exhibits before the board:
1. The test book
2. The receiving book
3. Emmet Brant's private weigh book
4. The state dairy inspector's circuit notebook for 1923

The condemned glass crate was not listed. Bertram Kyle (r23) confirms: "Four books. I carried them in, and I carried them out... and I could not have told you then and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked." The glass was in the loft above the room where the board sat. Cleve Oram (r21): "And the crate was up the ladder over their heads the entire time. Twenty feet, if that. I have thought about that since, I will not pretend I thought about it then."

**What did the tester himself believe the cause was?**

Ansel Keddie suspected the glass from the moment of the 1911 interval. His son Duncan Keddie (r18) testifies: "He came home that summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book. I was seventeen and I remember the kitchen and I remember that he said it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not his way. What he wrote in the book was a question mark." Keddie's question mark on July 10, 1911 ("Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.") was his way of saying "I do not know, and I will not write what I cannot prove." He suspected but could not confirm, so he held his silence. He never accused the patrons (no evidence of water), never accused himself (he was honest), never blamed the land (he knew better). He lived with the suspicion and carried it to his grave.

## Section G: Summary

In 1889, farmers in Larrow Valley organized a cooperative creamery to share equipment and ensure fair payment based on butterfat content rather than milk weight. By 1892, their by-law Article VII made this rule permanent: each patron's test, taken at the station where he delivered milk, determined his payment with no appeal. In 1896, they built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up-valley, where Ansel Keddie became tester and operator.

For 26 years, Ostrey Hollow milk tested persistently low—0.19 percentage points below other stations' milk. Farmers complained (beginning 1902), a committee blamed wet meadow hay (1908), and a manager warned against water in milk (1919). The valley divided into factions and families fell silent with each other. In 1925, patrons sued; a board found "no cause established" but awarded $3,800 anyway, and Keddie was dismissed.

The answer lay unseen: the station's measuring glass, purchased in 1897 for $77.93, delivered 5% less than it was marked to deliver. Every test read 95% of truth, consistently, invisibly, perfectly. In 1958, Keddie's granddaughter found the condemned glass in a loft and measured it. The 76,000 pounds of uncredited butterfat—worth $19,000—were explained. Ansel Keddie was cleared twenty-four years after his death.

Measurements matter. Instruments are invisible until they fail. A single faulty tool can divide a valley for a generation, ruin an honest man, and erase itself from notice because no one suspects the thing they measure with. It took four sources of records—the test book, weigh book, ledgers, and inspector's notebooks—and a woman willing to climb a ladder, to prove what should never have needed proving: that a man does honest work when he works honestly.
