# Test Answers: Part 1 (Section A, Stories 1-4)

## Section A: Reconstruction of Eight Stories

### Story 1: The Founding and Framework (1889–1896)

The Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association was founded in 1889 by farmers meeting at Larrow Green who sought fair payment for their milk. Gideon Vose, the first president, drafted the by-laws himself. The Association built a main creamery at Larrow Green in 1891 on Larrow Creek. Article VII, adopted at the 1892 annual meeting (per Effie Loomis's notes, though Dell Rundle says 1891), established the operational principle: patrons would be paid according to butterfat test, not milk weight. Critically, "the test of the station where he delivers shall govern"—final, unchangeable, unappealed.

In 1895–1896, the Association built a skimming station at Ostrey Hollow, seven miles up the valley, to serve farms at the head including Hessel Bottom. The station opened May 4, 1896. Ansel Keddie was hired as tester and separator operator from the opening and served 29 seasons (through 1924).

The station's testing outfit—six dozen pipettes marked 17.6 cc, test bottles, acid measure, hand tester—was ordered from Ammon & Sons, Tarnet, on April 14, 1897 (invoice $77.93 for complete outfit including freight). The glass went into use May 1, 1897, after borrowing from Larrow Green in 1896.

Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom was the largest patron from opening day. He kept his own weigh book, recording all deliveries. Critically, Ansel Keddie married Orra Brant, Emmet's sister, in 1889, before the Association formed. Their son Duncan was born 1894. The family connection meant that any future accusation against Keddie would directly implicate Emmet's interests. Keddie established a meticulous test book, one hand, all corrections initialed and ruled through. The system and infrastructure were sound; neither the operator's care nor the establishment of routine oversight prevented what came next.

### Story 2: The Gap Emerges and First Investigation (1902–1908)

Around 1902, Ostrey Hollow patrons noticed their milk tested consistently lower than comparable milk tested at Larrow Green or other stations. The gap was approximately 0.17–0.21 percentage points of butterfat. Month after month, winter and summer, dry years and wet—the shortfall held steady. By Article VII's terms, they had no recourse. Complaints came to annual meetings and store conversations but were disbelieved or dismissed.

In January 1908, the Association appointed a committee of local farmers to investigate. Nyle Grigg, a man of standing who hauled to Larrow Green (and thus had no direct stake in Ostrey Hollow's result), chaired it. The committee visited Hessel Bottom farms through January–March 1908, sampled herds themselves, sealed samples, observed milking and testing, checked scales against known weights, examined water supplies and hay quality.

Their report, delivered in 1908, found: no dishonesty of any kind, the milk was whole, the scales honest. They observed that Hessel Bottom's stock appeared thin in winter and slow to condition in spring—consistent with poor-quality forage. They concluded "the wet hay of that bottom thins the cream, and the remedy lies with the land and not with the station." The report was accepted and the question was closed officially for eleven years.

The finding satisfied few Ostrey Hollow farmers because it offered no solution. However, it established two crucial facts: the 1908 committee did not blame Ansel Keddie or find fault with testing procedure, and blame fell upon the land itself. The wet meadow remained wet; the gap persisted. By 1919, skeptics had aged and their distrust had deepened.

### Story 3: Suspicions Intensify and the Manager's Circular (1919–1920)

By 1919, multiple competing theories circulated. Some still believed the hay thinned cream. Others whispered that water was added to milk—the "oldest trick in the trade." A third theory, pushed quietly by some in the Association's office, was that Ansel Keddie himself was taking cream and writing low tests to conceal the loss.

None of these theories was proven. But the persistent gap year after year, combined with the social divisions in a small valley, created suspicion and resentment.

In June 1919, Selby Vose, the Association's manager (Gideon Vose's son, not nephew), took decisive action. He circulated a printed circular to nine families at Ostrey Hollow warning them that "added water will be looked for and dealt with." The circular named no one, but its message was unmistakable: the Association suspected water.

This created an impossible moral position for Ansel Keddie. He was married to Orra, the sister of Emmet Brant—the largest patron, the one who would lose most if water accusations were true. Ansel would not allow another man to carry the accusation to his neighbors (which would dishonor them and him). So he carried the circulars himself, standing at each of eleven doors while men read them. When Emmet received his copy, he confronted Ansel: "If you believe in the patrons' honesty, why carry this warning?" Ansel's answer—"I carry it because I will not have another man carry it"—was a statement of personal honor that satisfied no one. From June 1919 onward, Emmet and Ansel did not speak. Orra's diary: "He has not been in this house since, and he is my brother." The rupture lasted until Emmet's death in 1929.

The circular changed nothing measurable. Tests continued at 3.61 percent. But it poisoned the valley's social fabric and set the stage for confrontation.

### Story 4: The Natural Experiment of 1911 and Its Meaning (May 8 – July 10, 1911)

In May 1911, the station's primary testing pipette broke. Ansel obtained a borrowed pipette from Larrow Green—the same glass used during the station's first months in 1896 before the new outfit arrived. For nine weeks, this borrowed measure was in daily use.

During those nine weeks, all tests at the station ran higher. The station's average rose from the usual 3.61 percent to 3.80 percent—exactly 19 hundredths. When the replacement pipette arrived July 10, Ansel switched back to the station's own glass, and tests immediately fell back to 3.61 percent.

Critically, only one variable changed. The herds were identical, the pasture the same (mid-summer, not seasonal shift), the acid unchanged, the bottles unchanged, the man and his habits unchanged, the season and weather unchanged. Ansel recorded this fact in the test book on July 10: "Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K." That question mark stood unanswered for 14 years because Ansel would not theorize beyond what he could prove.

This was a perfect natural experiment. It established with scientific certainty that the apparatus—not milk, land, cows, men, or season—controlled the gap. The 1911 interval proved in real time that a measuring error would produce exactly the pattern observed: consistent, proportional, invisible from within the plant. It took 47 years for anyone to recognize what it meant.
