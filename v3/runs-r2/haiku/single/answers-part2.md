# Test Answers: Part 2 (Section A, Stories 5–8, and Section B)

## Continuation of Section A: Stories 5–8

### Story 5: The Inspection and Condemnation (June 1923)

On June 12, 1923, Merle Strawn, the state dairy inspector for the Ordell circuit, visited the Ostrey Hollow station on his routine rounds. He had visited annually or biennially since 1907 and had never found added water. On this visit, carrying a new tool—a calibrated burette issued to field men starting in 1921—Strawn ran the station's milk pipettes against the burette to verify their delivery.

The glass would not deliver its mark. Strawn condemned the entire crate—all original pipettes from 1897—on the spot. He directed the manager to order new equipment immediately. His circuit book entry reads: "These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new. Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them."

On June 28, 1923, the Association wrote to Ammon & Sons ordering replacement pipettes and bottles. Ammon's reply, July 2, 1923, confirmed the order would go forward that month but stated: "Our books of the nineties are not such that we can tell you anything about the lot you name." New glass was in use by July; the station's own broken measure was replaced the same summer.

Crucially, no one at the time connected the condemnation to the 26-year history of low tests. The condemned glass was carried to the loft above the intake room, placed in a wooden crate, covered with a tarpaulin, and left there. No one asked questions. Strawn made no inference about past tests. The Association saw it as routine maintenance—apparatus wears, must be replaced. No mention was made in the office records or minutes. Wilbur Teague, the bookkeeper, entered it as a small replacement charge. No statement was filed. No inquiry opened.

The timing was purely accidental. Had the glass been condemned in 1910 or 1930, the history would have been different. But in 1923, it arrived without context or implication, and the faulty glass vanished into storage while the investigation that would eventually open lay thirty-five years in the future.

### Story 6: The 1925 Arbitration and Dismissal (January–May 1925)

In January 1925, the Ostrey Hollow patrons formally petitioned the Association. They had waited long enough. Their claim: milk delivered at their station had tested 0.19 points below true value for 25 years. Over that span, at an average price of 25 cents per pound of butterfat, they had been underpaid approximately $67,000 (per their own calculation, which Alonzo Frick recorded but never settled).

The Association invoked its by-law provision for arbitration. A board of three was constituted: Alonzo Frick, an attorney from Ashlin, as chairman; and two others. They were asked to determine whether the Ostrey Hollow patrons had been treated unfairly and, if so, what remedy applied.

The board sat three times: March 17, 1925, at Ashlin in the Association's office (initial hearing); then twice more at Ostrey Hollow itself, in the station's intake room, so that board members could see the plant and observe the test procedure. On the third trip, they saw Ansel Keddie execute a test from start to finish—samples prepared properly, milk measured, acid added, tester spun at correct speed, fat column read with precision. The procedure was meticulous; the operator appeared honest. Frick, though he did not understand the chemistry, could see it was done the same way twice and without concealment.

Three explanations were argued:
1. **Water in milk** (Selby Vose's theory): The Ostrey Hollow patrons had been adding water. The state inspector's 1919 circular testified to this theory. Selby Vose stood by it before the board with no proof.
2. **Cream theft** (Wilbur Teague's implication): Ansel Keddie had been taking cream and writing low tests to conceal it. Teague spoke at length about how such an account would look, then observed he made no charge against anybody. This allowed the suggestion to hang in the room without evidence.
3. **The hay theory** (1908 committee): The wet meadow's forage thinned cream; the remedy lay with land management, not the station.

Ansel Keddie testified on March 17. His statement was nine sentences. He said: tested as taught, tested as state man saw him do it (31 times per week, 29 seasons), heard what patrons and officers said, but neither had stood at his elbow on a working morning, asked to account for figures he read and wrote, never taken a pound of cream, "there is not a man in this room who thinks I have when he is by himself."

The statement was strong but did not convince. A man who cannot account for a figure is presumed guilty of creating it. No amount of assertion overrides that presumption in a room where money has been lost.

On April 30, 1925—four days before the board would hand down its award and while the hearing was still technically ongoing—the Association dismissed Ansel Keddie. His letter of dismissal gave no cause. He was 64 years old. He had tested milk at that station for 29 seasons, since before most of the valley's young men were born.

On May 4, 1925, the board issued its award. It found "no cause established" against anyone. Yet it awarded the Ostrey Hollow patrons $3,800 "without admission"—a lawyer's phrase meaning: "We are paying you and we are not saying why." The award was charged to the station account, which meant the working patrons of the valley collectively paid it, though the Ostrey Hollow men had borne the loss.

The $3,800 was one-twentieth of the $67,000 the patrons claimed they were owed—a fifth of it, no more. Ansel Keddie received nothing and was never asked back. He lived nine years after dismissal (to 1934) with the valley's suspicion on him, though he had been legally cleared of nothing because the board found no cause to clear him of.

### Story 7: Ansel Keddie's Later Life and Death (1925–1934)

After April 30, 1925, Ansel Keddie came home with his apron rolled under his arm, hung it behind the shed door, and asked what there was for supper. His wife Orra noted in her diary: "There was ham. He ate it."

He was dismissed at the height of his skill and reputation. No other trade followed him to a second career. He had been trained from youth as a tester; he had refined that work for 29 years; he had made the station's books models of precision. He was 64.

He took up carpentry as a means to survive. He was a good carpenter—Cleve Oram noted that a barn door Keddie hung for him swung true for thirty years. But carpentry paid a quarter of what testing had paid. He mended stairs, gates, doors throughout the valley, working for "what they would offer." He did repairs on neighbors' properties, including properties of men who had testified against him or accepted the circular without protest. He whistled while he worked—Orra noted this with some surprise, as he had not whistled when he was paid.

Orra outlived the rift with her brother Emmet by several years. Emmet died in 1929. Orra went to his house for the funeral and was received kindly by Roy (Emmet's son). But she and Ansel had lost ten years of family connection—years that could not be recovered. Orra's diary notes: "Roy met me at the gate and was kind. I sat an hour in that kitchen where I was a girl and came home. Ten years and neither of them would go up the road first, and now it is settled the way those things get settled."

Ansel died in February 1934, at home, "without much pain at the end of it." Orra's final diary entry: "Nine years they have had to say it and they had not said it and they did not say it then. He never asked them to. He said to me once and only once, and not lately, that a man cannot be cleared of a thing nobody will name."

He died with the imputation on him in the county, among the men whose milk he had weighed and tested. The valley knew he had been dismissed; it did not know why, and most did not care to ask. Twenty-four years later, his name would be cleared by a bulletin from the State Agricultural College, but he had been dead two decades by then.

### Story 8: The Discovery and Resolution (1958)

In February 1958, Ivy Keddie, Ansel's granddaughter and a graduate student in dairy husbandry at the State Agricultural College at Farland, went to the abandoned Ostrey Hollow station building to research the old case. In the loft above the intake room, reachable only by a ladder nailed to the wall, under a tarpaulin and covered with dust and chaff, she found a wooden crate stenciled "AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET."

Inside, packed in straw, were pipettes—the condemned glassware from 1923, sixty dozen originally, nine surviving unbroken. She carried the crate to Farland. On March 9, 1958, she measured the nine pipettes against a calibrated burette: all blown for 17.6 cc, all delivering an average of 16.72 cc—95 hundredths of the mark.

What followed was arithmetic. A short charge produces a short reading in exact proportion. The recorded average at Ostrey Hollow over 25 full delivery years (1898–1922) was 3.61 percent. Divided by 0.95 (the proportion factor), the true average was 3.80 percent. The gap was 0.19 points—exactly the 0.19 the patrons had complained of. Over 40 million pounds of milk delivered in those 25 years, the shortfall was 76,000 pounds of butterfat—worth approximately $19,000 at the average price of 25 cents per pound.

She published her findings jointly with Professor Leland Vaught in State Agricultural College Bulletin No. 471 (October 1958), titled "No Milk Was Watered at Ostrey Hollow." The bulletin's closing statement: "From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference—seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat—stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."

The 1911 interval received special emphasis. Nine weeks when a borrowed measure was used, everything rose to 3.80 and fell back immediately upon the return of the faulty glass—a natural experiment that required no theory, only observation and honesty. Vaught wrote: "I have spent my working life urging students to prefer an experiment to a calculation. Here is one that ran itself... and lay unread for forty-seven years."

Ansel Keddie's name was cleared twenty-four years after his death. No remedy could be applied—all parties were dead, the Association's account long closed. The 1925 award of $3,800 stood as the only payment made, covering one-fifth of the loss. Ivy Keddie, in an interview with the Ashlin Chronicle, said: "The glass was short, and Ansel Keddie was not. He tested the milk in front of the state's own man and he tested it the way he was taught, and the reason his figures would not come right was in a crate over his head the entire time. I would like it printed that he never knew. It would have been a kindness if somebody in this county had thought to climb a ladder."

The crate and surviving pipettes were deposited at the Ordell County Historical Rooms. The test book, receiving book, Emmet Brant's weigh book, and Merle Strawn's 1923 circuit notebook became core documents of the investigation and remain in the county's archive.

## Section B: Relationships (1 paragraph each, with reasoning)

### B1. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Ansel Keddie? Derive it, and resolve anything in the sources that points elsewhere.

Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter. The chain is: Ansel married Orra Brant (1889); their son was Duncan Keddie (born 1894); Duncan's daughter was Ivy (born 1931). Duncan taught school at Ashlin and lived until at least 1968 (when he wrote "Notes for the Family"). The Ashlin Chronicle (r19) initially called Ivy "his great-niece," but Orin Falke (r22, the county agent who prepared the report) corrected this in a note added after publication, stating "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." Duncan himself (r18) confirms: "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise." The evidence is consistent when sources are traced to direct family documents rather than newspaper reporting.

### B2. What was Ivy Keddie's relationship to Hazel Brant? Derive it, and state anything about the intervening generations that the sources leave open.

Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins. The chain is: Emmet Brant and Orra Brant (née Brant) were brother and sister; Emmet's son was Roy Brant; Roy's daughter was Hazel. Ansel Keddie married Orra; their son was Duncan; Duncan's daughter was Ivy. Therefore: Emmet and Orra were siblings (one generation); their children Roy and Duncan were cousins (second generation); their grandchildren Hazel and Ivy are second cousins (third generation). Hazel Brant herself (r17) confirms this: "Ivy is Duncan's girl, and I am Roy's, so Ivy Keddie and I are second cousins. People are forever telling me she is my niece or my cousin once removed or some such, and she is not. Second cousins." Duncan corroborates (r18): "Roy Brant and I are therefore first cousins... Roy's daughter is Hazel Brant. My daughter is Ivy. Hazel and Ivy are second cousins." The relationship is unambiguous between direct sources. What remains open: whether Orra and Emmet were full siblings or half-siblings. Hazel states: "Orra Keddie was my father's sister. They were full brother and sister, same mother and same father, whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it." The parenthetical suggests some uncertainty circulated in the valley, but Hazel's assertion (based on family knowledge) contradicts the stepmother rumor.

### B3. Who was Selby Vose's father, and why does that matter to what happened at Ostrey Hollow?

Selby Vose was the son of Gideon Vose, the Association's founder and first president (who held office 1889–1911, twenty-two years). Bertram Kyle (r23), the office clerk, states plainly: "Mr. Selby Vose became manager in nineteen eleven. He was the son of Mr. Gideon Vose who organized the Association and was its first president—the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense." Duncan Keddie (r18) confirms: "Selby Vose... was Gideon Vose's son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew." This matters because Gideon Vose wrote Article VII, the by-law making the station's test final and unappealed. Selby, as son of the founder and manager from 1913–1938, inherited both the system's authority and, arguably, his father's assumption that the station's test was beyond suspicion. When suspicion fell in 1919, Selby issued the water-warning circular—operating within the logic Article VII gave him: the test is final, so if the test is low, the milk must be wrong. He never questioned whether the test itself could be wrong, because Article VII bound him to accept it as the measure of truth. The founder's son administering the founder's flawed rule, unaware that the rule's foundation was faulty glass.

### B4. What was the relationship, if any, between the Vose family and the Keddie family?

The sources offer conflicting and uncertain testimony on this point. Duncan Keddie (r18) states explicitly: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either. I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her sit down. It is talk, and it has been talk since before I could walk. My mother would have told me." Duncan asserts that his mother Orra—who was known to be precise about family connections—never mentioned any Vose relation. However, Cleve Oram (r21), the cream hauler's son, offers a different account: "The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley. My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper." Oram's source was hearsay ("had it from her mother") and acknowledged as uncertain. The primary source (Duncan, writing family notes with direct knowledge) denies any connection; the secondary source (Oram, reporting valley gossip) asserts one but admits it was unverified. The most reliable answer: no proven family connection between the Voses and Keddies. The rumor circulated but was denied by the family member best positioned to know (Orra, through her son Duncan). The gossip likely arose from the coincidence that two prominent valley families were drawn into the same dispute, which small-town logic sometimes attributed to kinship.
