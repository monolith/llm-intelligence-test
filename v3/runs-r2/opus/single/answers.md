# Answer sheet — Part 1

## Section A — Reconstruction (stories 1–4)

### Story 1 — The Rule That Made a Figure into Money

**Who.** The farmers of the Larrow Valley, Ordell County; Gideon Vose, their first president; Effie Loomis, secretary 1901–1940, who kept and later mourned the record.

**What happened, and when.** In 1889 the farmers met at Larrow Green — in the schoolhouse, in cold weather, very late, with somebody's horse loose in the middle of it — and organized the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. They were tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking whatever the merchant allowed for it. Gideon Vose, a heavy, quiet, obstinate man who had once thought of the law and thought better of it, was elected president and held the office twenty-two years, to 1911; he died in 1917. In 1891 the creamery was built at Larrow Green on Larrow Creek — the same building still — and the valley began to be paid cash once a month, which had never happened to it before. Vose wrote the by-laws himself, at his own kitchen table, in a hand the clerks were still complaining about thirty years later.

Article VII is the hinge of everything that follows, and it is worth having entire: *"Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern."* Two halves, and the valley only ever quoted the first. The first half is just: a man is paid for what he actually brings and not for the water naturally in it. The second half — *the test of the station where he delivers shall govern* — makes the test made at a man's own delivery point final. Not the creamery's test, not the valley's average, no re-test anywhere, no appeal. Under payment by weight, nothing that happened at Ostrey Hollow afterward would have cost any man a dollar. Because of that clause, one instrument in one room became the whole of the bargain.

**The quantity that matters.** The year of adoption **cannot be determined from the sources**. Rundle says 1891, Loomis 1892, Grigg 1893; the printed pamphlet carries no year on its face, only "adopted at the annual meeting"; and the 1889–1892 minute book was carried out in the office move of 1938 and never arrived. Loomis, the closest thing to a documentary witness, states plainly that she came in only in 1901 and that exactly those papers are the lost ones. Falke's refusal to name a year is the correct position.

**Documents.** The printed by-laws, gray wrappers (curator's Item 1); Loomis's *Notes toward a history*, 1941; Rundle's jubilee pamphlet, 1939; Falke's county report, 1959. The lost minute book is itself a fact of the case.

### Story 2 — The Station at Ostrey Hollow and the Man Who Kept Its Book

**Who.** Ansel Keddie (b. 1861, d. February 1934), tester and separator man; the nine patron families at the head of the valley; Hobart Sill, buttermaker at Larrow Green from 1893.

**What happened, and when.** Ostrey Hollow lies seven miles up from Larrow Green and climbs the whole seven; the pull down was three hours in mud and could not be done at all in March. So the Association put a skimming station at the head of it: a boarded-and-battened building on the flat by the creek, a covered waist-high platform on the road side, the intake room with scale and vat, the separator behind, and — reached by a ladder nailed to the wall, not a stair — a loft over the intake room. It opened on **4 May 1896**. Milk was weighed in, sampled patron by patron, tested for butterfat, then separated; the cream went down to Larrow Green daily, and the skim went home to the hogs and calves the same morning.

The station's own testing outfit had not come. Rather than hold off the season, Hobart Sill made up an outfit from his own glass at his own bench at Larrow Green and sent it up the valley on the wagon. The test book's first leaf records it: *"Ostrey Hollow station. Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes. A. Keddie."* (Keddie's 1925 letter says the glass was borrowed off the Ashlin creamery; his own book, written at the time, outranks his memory, and Sill independently confirms it was his.)

His day began at half past five, the lamps lit and the scale swinging free before the first wagon. Every can weighed and the weight called and written before the milk moved; a sample to each patron's own numbered jar, made up over ten days, then warmed blood-warm and poured vessel to vessel until the risen cream was carried down through the whole of it; the charge drawn to the mark on the stem, tip wiped, acid poured down the side, four bottles in the hand tester, the crank at the speed he was taught and no faster, the fat column read against the rulings with dividers. The book ran five columns — date, patron, pounds, test, fat — errors crossed and initialed, never a figure written over a figure. **Thirty-one tests a week for twenty-nine seasons, 1896 through 1924**; he tested no pound in 1925. Merle Strawn, the state's man, watched him through whole tests visit after visit: "He works slow and clean and reads his own figure without being coaxed to it."

**The quantities that matter.** The 1922 summary leaf at the back: 3.80 in 1896; 1897 part year; **3.61 from 1898 and within three hundredths of it every year since** — save nine weeks of 1911, when it stood at 3.80. That leaf is the record of the whole injury and nobody read it as one for thirty-six years.

**Documents.** Station test book, 4 May 1896 – 31 December 1924 (Item 3); Keddie's unsent letter, spring 1925; Sill's remarks, 1934; Strawn's circuit books; Cleve Oram on the building and the ladder.

### Story 3 — A Crate of Short Pipettes from Tarnet

**Who.** Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet — Marcus Ammon's house, four stories and a basement behind the railroad yard, supplier of half the glass in the state — and Junia Ammon, its historian, who printed the firm's bad morning alongside its good ones.

**What happened, and when.** A milk test pipette must *deliver* a stated quantity of milk, not hold it: a harder measurement, because milk clings to the wall of the glass as it runs out. A man gathers his glass, blows the bulb, draws the stem, and works to a gauge hanging on a nail at his elbow; the mark goes on while the glass is warm; the piece anneals overnight and is gauged next morning and packed or thrown in the cullet barrel. Junia Ammon's reconstruction, offered because nobody else was going to offer one: a mold came back from the shop reground a hair different and looked exactly as it had looked before, and the lot ran short. No check on the floor could catch it, because a boy checks the first, the tenth and the fiftieth of a lot and finds them consistent — they *are* consistent; they are consistently wrong, which is the one fault that kind of check cannot find. The works books that would have shown the lot number burned with the old third floor in 1931.

On **14 April 1897** the firm billed the Association: *6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20; 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at .22 — 31.68; 1 acid measure — .90; 1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00; Freight — 2.15; Total — 77.93.* Seventy-two pipettes; **$77.93** for a station's whole arithmetic. The outfit went into use at Ostrey Hollow on **1 May 1897**, which Orra Keddie entered in her diary that same day: "A. says the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow Green. He is pleased about it."

**The mechanism, which is the whole story.** The neck of a test bottle is ruled to read percent for a charge of 17.6 c.c. and for no other charge whatever. Put in a short charge: less milk, less fat liberated, a shorter column standing in the neck — and the neck, which knows nothing of the substitution, is read off in the ordinary way. The reading is not merely low; it is low *in the exact proportion by which the charge was short*. Measured in 1958, the nine surviving pipettes delivered a mean of **16.72 c.c.** against **17.6** blown on the stem: short **0.88 c.c.**, ninety-five hundredths of the mark, one part in twenty.

**Why it survived twenty-six years.** Three properties. It was *proportional*, not additive, so the station's herds still ranked correctly against one another and the books looked orderly and internally consistent — only against the outside world were they wrong. It was *precise*: a slipping scale scatters and scatter attracts attention, but a pipette short by 0.88 c.c. is short by 0.88 c.c. every time, in January and July, for a careful operator and a careless one; it has the appearance of excellent work. And it was *invisible from inside the plant*: scales check against test weights, acid against strength, bottle graduations against mercury, but the pipette is not checked against anything, because in ordinary practice the pipette *is* the standard. An instrument that is itself the measure cannot be caught by measuring with it. It can be caught only by a burette brought in from outside — and the state began issuing burettes to its field men only in 1921.

**Documents.** The invoice of 14 April 1897 (Item 2, reproduced in facsimile by Ammon and transcribed by the curator); Ammon's letter book, 2 July 1923; the crate and its nine pipettes (Item 5); measurement sheet, 9 March 1958 (Item 14); Bulletin No. 471.

### Story 4 — Twenty-Three Years of Complaint and Three Wrong Answers

**Who.** The Hollow patrons, Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom foremost; Nyle Grigg's investigating committee; Selby Vose; Dr. Roland Kepp, veterinary surgeon of Ashlin; and, unconsulted over Redlow Ridge, Jerome Cudd and his daughter Rosalie.

**What happened, and when.** Hessel Bottom is a hundred and sixty acres of wet meadow under the hill, **six farms** with rights on it (Loomis's "seven" is the outlier against the 1908 report, Roy Brant, Hazel Brant and Kepp), Emmet Brant's the largest, thirty-eight cows in a good year. From **1902** the Hollow said out loud that its milk tested under the same class of milk at Larrow Green — 3.61 against the creamery's 3.78, seventeen hundredths on the pay slip. Emmet Brant was one of the first to say it and was laughed at. The fact nobody would take account of, then or since, was the *shape* of the gap: the same in July as in February, on grass and on dry hay, in wet years and dry. A feed effect comes and goes with the season. This one stood flat as a floor.

Three explanations held the field for a generation, and each of them blamed somebody in the valley.

**The hay.** In January 1908 the Association named a committee under Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green — chosen because he was not a patron of that station and had no cent in the answer. It sampled the Hessel Bottom herds **through January, February and March 1908 only**, drew and sealed its own samples, tried the station scales against a known weight and the patrons' scales against the station's, watched the test made, and found the milk whole, the scales honest, and *"no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter."* It laid the cause on the bottom's coarse sedge hay: *"the remedy lies with the land and not with the station."* A winter-only sample could not possibly have seen that the gap was seasonless. Grigg reprinted the report in 1919 with a covering letter claiming the committee had sampled "every month of that year," which his own reprinted report contradicts, and claiming the College's Bulletin No. 214 had found bog hay to depress the test "by two tenths in the winter feeding." Dr. Kepp repeated both errors in 1926 and added his own unmeasured field impression of "a fifth of a point." Bulletin No. 214 actually reads: *"Bog hay fed as the whole of the roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture."* Four times too small in winter, and infinitely too small in summer.

**Water**, which was the manager's; and **stolen cream**, which was the valley's. Both are taken up in Stories 5 and 6.

**The control nobody consulted.** Jerome Cudd built the Alder Corners Cheese Company over Redlow Ridge in Vessey County — 1913 by his daughter's first-hand account, 1914 as Ordell County tells it. The ridge is four miles of switchback, shut November to April, so no Ordell man ever hauled over it in winter. But **from 1915 four Hessel Bottom men hauled to Cudd in the summer flush, for sixteen seasons, ending 1930.** Cudd tested on his own bench with his own glass and never compared a figure with anybody, because it never entered his head to. His season averages for the four men taken together ran **3.78 to 3.82 — about 3.80 — every summer, wet years and dry**. His own note, summer 1919: *"The four Hessel Bottom men: 3.79, 3.81, 3.78, 3.82. Good milk off wet ground, whatever they say over the ridge."* In sixteen summers he never docked one of them a cent, and a cheesemaker cannot be deceived about water: the curd comes soft and will not knit, and it tells him the next morning.

**Documents.** Report of the investigating committee, 1908 (Item 11); Grigg's covering letter, 1919; Bulletin No. 214 (Item 12); Kepp's paper and its recorded discussion, 1926; Rosalie Cudd's letter of 1958 with its sixteen-season table; Roy and Hazel Brant on the six farms and the four men.

### Story 5 — The Circular of 1919 and the Ten Years' Silence

**Who.** Selby Vose, manager of the Association from 1913, son of Gideon Vose who wrote Article VII; Ansel and Orra Keddie; Emmet Brant; Bertram Kyle, the clerk who typed it.

**What happened, and when.** In **June 1919** the manager sent a printed circular over his own signature — and no one else's — to the patrons of the Ostrey Hollow station, warning them in temperate language that added water would be looked for and would be dealt with. It named nobody and accused nobody by name, which in a place that size is not the same as accusing nobody. Kyle typed it, remembered typing it, thought at the time it was a hard thing to send to men bringing milk in every morning of their lives, and typed it anyway. Vose defended it in his annual address of 1926 and said he would write every word of it again tomorrow: "A manager who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing to the men who make them is not being kind to them; he is being idle at their expense."

**The quantity.** **Nine families** received it — Vira Toland's father was one, Emmet Brant another, Hazel Brant's mother a third. Four independent recipients or deliverers give nine, including Keddie himself, who carried them. Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary says eleven; that is the one figure in this strand that cannot be reconciled, and I do not force it. Nine families on six farms, because a farm holds more than one family and generally did.

Ansel Keddie would not put the circulars in the mail nor leave them on the platform. He took them round himself, stood at each man's door while he read it, and came home at eleven at night with his hat still on. Two days later Emmet Brant came up and would not come into the house. He stood in the yard and said he had hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and that Ansel knew it better than any man living, and asked him why he had carried the paper himself if he did not believe it. Ansel said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet went home and never entered that house again.

**Why it cut so deep.** Emmet Brant was Orra Keddie's brother — full brother and sister, whatever the version with a stepmother in it says — and the station's largest patron in every year of record. So the man who tested the milk had married the sister of the man the circular cost the most; they had eaten Sunday dinner in one kitchen for thirty years. From 1919 the two did not speak: not at the store, not at a funeral. Orra would walk down to Larrow Green rather than pass her brother's lane. It ran **ten years**, until Emmet died in the spring of 1929, and Orra went down and sat an hour in the kitchen where she had been a girl and came home. Papers were kept forty years to prove what had been said of men: one woman pinned hers inside the lid of her trunk; Hazel Brant's mother read hers twice and put it in the stove.

Against all of it stands Strawn's field entry for 1919: *"I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found any, and I told him so."*

**Documents.** The circular itself (surviving only in kitchen drawers; none in the collection); Orra Keddie's diary, June 1919 and two days after; Strawn's circuit book, 1919; Kyle's account, 1953; Vose's annual address, 1926; Keddie's unsent letter.

### Story 6 — The Station Gain: The Answer in the Association's Own Ledgers

**Who.** Wilbur Teague, bookkeeper 1905–1945; Hobart Sill, buttermaker 1893–1934; Bertram Kyle, clerk at the outer desk for twenty-five years; Selby Vose, who signed.

**What happened, and when.** The station account had two sides, and Teague invented the second one himself when he came in 1905. On one side he entered the butterfat credited to the patrons on the station's own test — what the station's books said it had bought. On the other, the butterfat that actually came down the valley in the station's cream and was weighed and tested into the vats at Larrow Green — what the Association had in hand. The two were never the same, and **the second was always the larger.** He named the difference "station gain" and carried it to the general account of the Association, because a difference that will not be entered somewhere comes back and bites you at audit. The typical annual entry, in the same terms and the same hand for forty years:

> *Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual. Carried to the general account.*

That line is the whole case, written out annually in ink on an open shelf. 1,600,000 lb of milk at the recorded 3.61 gives 57,760 lb of fat; the same milk at the true 3.80 gives 60,800; the difference is 3,040 — five percent of the fat actually delivered, five and a quarter percent of the fat credited. It ran at that rate every year for a quarter of a century, and the evenness is precisely what buried it. Teague is honest about the mechanism: "A figure that jumps about will keep you awake. A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture." Kyle says the same in a clerk's words: it had a name, and a figure with a name on it has been explained, so you rule under it and go on. "It was not a secret. It was worse than a secret. It was in plain sight with a name on it."

Sill confirms the same fact independently and physically, from the receiving end and with no paper involved. He took the Hollow's cream off the platform, and then weighed it over again himself when it came into the cold room, for forty-one years, because he never in his life took another man's figure for what was going into his churn — and it ran heavy every year. "There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there. Always more. Never less." He said so in that room a hundred times and nobody thought it was worth anything, and nobody ever asked him.

**What the line disproves.** Stolen cream reaches the creamery *light*. This cream reached it heavy, every year, by a steady five and a quarter percent. Dell Rundle, who had printed the valley's settled opinion that cream left the station by the back door, was shown the old ledgers while his 1939 pamphlet was in type and retracted in a note added while in press: "It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot make my paragraph stand. I leave it as written and correct it here."

**And the men who wrote it never read it.** Teague put the account to Vose once, in 1916, was told to let it alone, and let it alone — "I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience." In 1925 he was the man who put the cream-theft account to the board of arbitration, and in 1948 he restated it in the same words, his own ledger line refuting him on the same page.

**Documents.** Annual ledgers, station account, from 1897 (Item 6); Teague's *The Books of the Association*, 1948; Sill's remarks, 1934; Kyle's account, 1953; Rundle's note added while in press, 1939.

### Story 7 — The Condemnation of 1923 and the Arbitration of 1925

**Who.** Merle Strawn, state dairy inspector on the Ordell circuit 1907–1936, kin to nobody in the valley whatever; Alonzo Frick, attorney of Ashlin, chairman of the board of three; Ansel Keddie.

**What happened, and when.** Strawn did four things at every plant in the same order, so as not to have to remember which he had done: scales against the sealed weights he carried, loaded light and loaded heavy; the lactometer on the day's milk for added water; the boiler, water supply and cold room; and then — the one most men grudged him — he stood at the tester's elbow and watched a test through from sample to reading and did not let him hurry it. He recorded what he weighed and never what he thought. At Ostrey Hollow, visit after visit from 1907: scales true; lactometer, no added water, *"as always here."*

The state began issuing burettes to field men in **1921**, in a wooden case with a printed sheet of directions; it was slow work and most plants had never had it done. Strawn did not run the Hollow's glass in **1922** — "the day being short and the road home being what it was, and I have put that down because it is what happened." On **12 June 1923** he did: *"Ran their milk pipettes against the burette. These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new."* The Association ordered on 28 June; Ammon & Sons entered the order on 2 July and shipped that month; new glass was in use from July. In October Strawn ran the new pipettes against the burette: they delivered their mark. In **1924 the station's book gave the year at 3.80** — a rise of nineteen hundredths, eighteen months before any petition or hearing. Strawn drew no inference whatever about the twenty-six years behind it, and the condemned crate went up the ladder into the loft.

In **January 1925** the patrons petitioned, asking to be made whole for twenty-five years. A board of three sat under Frick from **17 March to 4 May 1925**: the first session at the Association's office at Ashlin; the second and third, at Frick's own insistence, in the station's own weigh room, so the board could see the plant instead of hearing it described — with men standing outside on the platform in the rain. Three explanations were argued and not one was proved: water (Vose's), stolen cream (Teague's, put in the way an accountant puts a thing he wants said without saying it), and the hay (the 1908 report read into the record). **Four exhibits and nothing else:** the station test book; the station receiving book; Emmet Brant's private weigh book, lent and wanted back; and Strawn's circuit notebook for 1923 — the book that records the condemnation. Nobody laid the first beside the fourth.

Keddie made a nine-sentence statement on 17 March: he had tested as he was taught and as the state man had seen him do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons; he could not tell them why the figures were what they were; and he had never taken a pound of cream out of that station. On **30 April** the Association dismissed him — twenty-nine seasons and a letter; he came up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm, hung it behind the shed door, and asked what there was for supper. On **4 May** the board found no cause established and awarded the patrons **$3,800, without admission, charged to the station account**. The crate of condemned glass lay about twenty feet above two of the three sittings and was never once mentioned by anybody.

**Documents.** Strawn's circuit books, 1921–1925 (the 12 June 1923 entry survives as a photostat, Item 9); Ammon's letter of 2 July 1923 (Item 10); the arbitration minute book (Item 7); Keddie's statement (Item 8); Frick's exhibit list, 1946; Orra Keddie's diary, March–April 1925.

### Story 8 — The Ladder, the Burette and Bulletin No. 471

**Who.** Ivy Keddie, born 1931, Ansel's granddaughter, graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland; Professor Leland Vaught; Rosalie Cudd; Roy and Hazel Brant; Halbert Nym, curator at the Ordell County Historical Rooms.

**What happened, and when.** Five sets of records bore on the question and no two of them had ever been in one room: the station test book (in the Association's office 1925–1959); Emmet Brant's farm weigh book, **lent** by Roy Brant in 1958 — lent, and the word matters to that family; the Alder Corners figures, copied out for her by Rosalie Cudd, Jerome's daughter, who went into her father's books on a stranger's say-so; Merle Strawn's circuit notebooks, in Farland's own college library since 1947 and unasked-for for eleven years; and the Association's annual ledgers.

In **February 1958** Ivy went up the Hollow to the old station, climbed the ladder into the loft over the intake room, and found under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff a wooden crate stenciled AMMON & SONS DAIRY SUPPLY TARNET, carried up there in June 1923 and never touched since. Nine of the original seventy-two pipettes were unbroken. On **9 March 1958** she delivered each of the nine five times against a calibrated burette at Farland: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71 — **mean 16.72 c.c.** against 17.6 blown on the stem, short 0.88, ninety-five hundredths of the mark.

**The quantities.** 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80**. Over the twenty-five full delivery years 1898–1922 the station took in **40,000,000 lb** of milk: credited at 3.61 that is **1,444,000 lb** of fat; delivered at 3.80 it is **1,520,000 lb**; the difference is **76,000 lb**, worth **$19,000** at the twenty-five cents a pound the period averaged — against an award of $3,800, which is one fifth of it. And because the error was a proportion and not a fixed quantity, it fell hardest on the best milk: a herd recorded at 4.56 lost 0.24 of a point, one recorded at 3.42 lost 0.18. No man had any way of knowing his good cows cost him more than his poor ones.

**Bulletin No. 471**, October 1958, over Vaught's name and hers, closed: *"No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there... the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain."* The *Ashlin Chronicle* put it on the front page, got her relationship to Ansel wrong, and accused the glass house of deliberate fraud on no evidence at all. Falke's county report of 1959 conceded the hay as a partial factor and then withdrew the concession on actually reading Bulletin 214, and corrected her relationship in the same note.

The glass was condemned in 1923 and the cause found in 1958: thirty-five years. Keddie had been out of his place thirty-three years and dead twenty-four. Orra died in 1948 and did not see it. By 1970 the five books stood in three buildings and none of them belonged to the family.

**Documents.** Measurement sheet, 9 March 1958 (Item 14); Bulletin No. 471 (Item 15); Ivy's talk to the Historical Society, 1959; the *Chronicle*, 3 October 1958; Falke's report, 1959; Nym's accession notes, 1972; Duncan Keddie's family notes, 1968 and 1970.

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.**
The line: Ansel Keddie (b. 1861) married Orra Brant in 1889; their son Duncan was born in 1894 and taught school at Ashlin; Duncan's daughter is Ivy, born 1931 (the *Chronicle* has her at twenty-seven in 1958, which agrees). Both principals state it in the first person: Ivy opens her 1959 talk "Ansel Keddie... was my grandfather. My father, Duncan Keddie, who taught school at Ashlin, was his son"; Duncan writes "My daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed otherwise." Two sources point elsewhere and both say **great-niece**: the *Ashlin Chronicle* of 3 October 1958 and Orin Falke's county report of 1959. They are not two pieces of evidence. The *Chronicle* was assembled in a day and a half from a college press release and two telephone calls, and Falke, writing the year after, took the newspaper's version — and then found it out himself and corrected it in a note appended to his own report: "I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine." A retraction by the man who made the error, plus first-person statements from both the granddaughter and the intervening son, settles it. **Method: first-person family testimony plus an author's own published correction, over a derivative newspaper report.**

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins.**
Derivation, one generation at a time: Emmet Brant and Orra (Brant) Keddie were brother and sister. Their children were Roy Brant and Duncan Keddie, who are therefore **first cousins** — the children of a brother and a sister, which is the whole definition. Roy's daughter is Hazel; Duncan's daughter is Ivy; the children of first cousins are **second cousins**. Duncan and Hazel each derive it independently and each complains that the valley gets it wrong; Rundle's 1959 footnote agrees. Against them, Vira Toland and Cleve Oram both say "first cousin once removed" — the same village shorthand, and both Duncan and Hazel name that exact error as the one people make ("People are forever telling me she is my niece or my cousin once removed... It is not complicated; it is only that nobody wants to count").
What the intervening generations leave open: **whether Emmet and Orra were full or half siblings.** Roy Brant says full brother and sister, "same mother and same father, whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it." Hazel — Roy's own daughter, a generation further off, and self-declared unreliable on anything but quantities — says Orra was "a half sister to my grandfather Emmet, by his father's second wife." Duncan simply says brother and sister. I resolve toward full siblings, because Roy is the closer generation and is explicitly rebutting the story Hazel repeats, and because Duncan, the most careful genealogist in the bundle, says nothing of a second wife. But note it does not disturb the answer: children of half-siblings are half first cousins and their children half second cousins, still "second cousins" in ordinary speech. Also open: nothing in Orra's own hand survives from before her 1889 marriage — the diary's editor states the girlhood pages were not preserved — so her birth family has no documentary trace at all. Birth order is settled only loosely (Duncan says Orra was the younger; Emmet b. 1854 and Orra b. 1866 agree).

**B3. Selby Vose's father was Gideon Vose.**
Four sources, three of them close to the office: Effie Loomis ("Selby Vose, son of the late president, was made manager in 1913"); Bertram Kyle, who sat outside his door ("the son, whatever is said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense"); Duncan Keddie ("Selby was his son and not, as I have heard it given, his nephew"); and Selby himself, from the platform in 1926 — "My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law under which every patron in this Association is paid on the test of the station where he delivers." Vira Toland and Dell Rundle both say nephew; both are repeating the valley's talk, and Kyle and Duncan each identify that talk as the error it is. **Method: self-identification in a printed address, corroborated by two office insiders, over village report.**
Why it matters: Gideon wrote Article VII, the clause that made a man's own station's test final and unappealable. His son, as manager from 1913 to 1938, was the officer who administered that rule against the Ostrey Hollow patrons, who signed the annual sheet carrying the station-gain line every year, who was told about that account by Teague in 1916 and told him to let it alone, who sent the 1919 water circular, and who told the whole membership in 1926 that the cause was water in the cans. The rule that left a dissatisfied patron with no remedy but to quarrel with a figure, and the man whose job it was to refuse to reopen the figure, were father and son. That is the concrete reason the valley felt "the family always thought the valley belonged to them a little," and the structural reason the by-law was never going to be revisited from inside the office. It also explains Selby's rhetorical use of his father's home-place Jerseys, tested at 4.56 in 1910, to prove the valley's land was not to blame.

**B4. No relationship between the Voses and the Keddies is established, and the sources positively suggest there was none.**
Two narrators assert one. Vira Toland: "Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins. Everybody knew that" — and she uses it to give the 1919 circular its sting, "it was somebody's cousin saying it." Cleve Oram: "The Keddies and the Voses were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley. My mother had it from her mother and I never heard it contradicted at the time, though I could not draw you the line on paper." **These are not two sources; they are one.** Both are unattributed village talk of identical shape, Oram gives its route (mother from grandmother) and admits he cannot draw the line, and Toland offers it as the thing "everybody up here knew and nobody wrote down anywhere" — which is a description of a rumor.
Against them, Duncan Keddie denies it flatly and specifically, and gives the rumor's anatomy: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either. I have heard it from two women in this town that my mother and Mr. Gideon's wife were cousins, and one of them will tell you first cousins if you let her sit down." He adds the strongest kind of negative evidence available: his mother "could not pass a headstone without giving you the connection," told him a great deal about who was who in that valley, "and she never once put a Vose in it."
**Resolution: no kinship. Method: source-independence (the two affirmatives collapse into one line of gossip, whose local origin a third party names), plus a family member's direct access to the person alleged to be the cousin, plus the total absence of any document.** Strictly, a negative of this kind cannot be proved, so the honest form is: nothing in the sources supports a Vose–Keddie connection and the family denies it in detail. Worth setting beside Duncan's other genealogical note, made for the same reason: Merle Strawn, the state inspector whose notebooks turned out to decide the matter, "was kin to nobody in the valley whatever."

## Section C — Math

Values used throughout, and why:

- **Pipette mark: 17.6 c.c.** (invoice, the nine surviving stems, Bulletin 471 — undisputed).
- **Mean delivery: 16.72 c.c.** — from Ivy Keddie's measurement sheet of 9 March 1958, whose nine individual readings (16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71) sum to 150.48 and average exactly 16.72. Vaught's bulletin text and the *Chronicle* both print 16.27 — a transposition; see E1.
- **Recorded station average: 3.61** — the test book's 1922 summary leaf, transcribed identically in three retellings. Teague and Frick both say 3.16 — a transposition; see E1.
- **Milk received, 25 full delivery years 1898–1922: 40,000,000 lb** (1,600,000 lb/yr) — Association ledgers via Loomis, Kyle, Nym, Ivy, Vaught. Teague's and Rundle's 44,000,000 is refuted by Teague's own quoted entry; see E1.
- **Average price of butterfat over the period: $0.25/lb** — ledgers via Loomis and Kyle, corroborated independently by Roy Brant. Sill and Kepp say 27 cents; see E1.

---

### C1

**(a) Proportion.** The pipettes were blown and marked to deliver 17.6 c.c. and actually delivered a mean of 16.72 c.c.

  16.72 ÷ 17.6 = **0.95 exactly** (check: 17.6 × 0.95 = 16.72).
  Shortfall 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c.; 0.88 ÷ 17.6 = 0.05.

**Every test made on that glass read 95% of the truth — low by one part in twenty (5%).** How you get it from the glassware: the neck of the test bottle is ruled to read percent for a 17.6 c.c. charge and no other charge; a short charge liberates proportionally less fat and stands a proportionally shorter column in a neck that knows nothing of the substitution. The reading is therefore low in the exact proportion the charge was short. The error is a *ratio*, not a fixed number of points.

**(b) True average and shortfall.**

  3.61 ÷ 0.95 = **3.80** (check: 3.80 × 0.95 = 3.61).
  3.80 − 3.61 = **0.19 of a point — nineteen hundredths.**

Two independent confirmations that 3.80 is the real figure and not merely an arithmetic artifact: the station's own average stood at 3.80 during the nine weeks of 1911 on a borrowed measure, and again from 1924 on new glass (Strawn's field book: "Their book gives the year at 3.80"); and the same four Hessel Bottom herds tested 3.78–3.82, about 3.80, at Alder Corners over sixteen summers.

**(c) Butterfat not credited, over the 25 full delivery years 1898–1922.**

  Credited: 40,000,000 × 0.0361 = **1,444,000 lb**
  Actually delivered: 40,000,000 × 0.0380 = **1,520,000 lb**
  Not credited: 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = **76,000 lb**
  (Cross-check: 40,000,000 × 0.0019 = 76,000. ✔)

**(d) Money, and the fraction received.**

  76,000 lb × $0.25 = **$19,000**
  Award actually paid: $3,800
  3,800 ÷ 19,000 = 0.20 = **one fifth (20%)**

### C2 — Emmet Brant, the largest patron, year 1910

Source line (weigh book, quoted identically by Nym, Roy Brant and Hazel Brant): *"Year 1910. Delivered to the station, two hundred thousand pounds even, by my own scale and theirs agreeing. Paid on a test of 3.42."*

**(a)** 200,000 × 0.0342 = **6,840 lb of butterfat credited.**

**(b)** True test: 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = **3.60**.
  True fat: 200,000 × 0.0360 = 7,200 lb.
  Owed and not credited: 7,200 − 6,840 = **360 lb.**
  (Cross-checks: 6,840 ÷ 0.95 = 7,200 ✔; 360 = 5% of 7,200 ✔; the per-point shortfall 3.60 − 3.42 = 0.18, which is exactly Ivy Keddie's worked example "a herd recorded at 3.42 was short 0.18." ✔)

**(c)** Roy Brant gives the price for that specific year: "The price of butterfat that year was twenty-four cents."

  360 × $0.24 = **$86.40 for 1910.**

At the twenty-five-cent period average it would be $90.00; I use the year-specific figure because the question asks for that year, and flag that the 24-cent figure rests on a single source (Roy Brant, reading his father's book and its context).

### C3 — Over the same 25 full delivery years

**(a)** Credited to the patrons: **1,444,000 lb** (40,000,000 × 0.0361).

**(b)** Reached Larrow Green in the station's cream: **1,520,000 lb** (40,000,000 × 0.0380).

**(c)** Difference: 76,000 lb. **The percentage depends entirely on the denominator, and both figures in the sources are right of their own denominator and of no other:**

  76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = 0.0500 = **5.00% of the butterfat actually delivered**
  76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 0.052632 = **5.26% ≈ five and a quarter percent of the butterfat credited**

The Association's ledger line uses the second ("five and a quarter per cent, as usual") because a bookkeeper reckons a surplus against the figure he has already entered. Five percent is the one that is also the glass — one part in twenty, the same ratio as 16.72 to 17.6.

Independent check on a single year, from the ledger entry itself: 57,760 credited, 60,800 received, gain 3,040. 3,040 ÷ 60,800 = 5.00%; 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.263%. And 57,760 = 1,600,000 × 0.0361 ✔; 60,800 = 1,600,000 × 0.0380 ✔; 3,040 × 25 years = 76,000 ✔. The typical annual entry proves the 40,000,000 lb figure from the inside and refutes the 44,000,000 lb one.

### C4 — The invoice, 14 April 1897

**(a)** 25.20 + 31.68 = 56.88; + 0.90 = 57.78; + 18.00 = 75.78; + 2.15 = **$77.93**, exactly as the invoice totals it. (Ammon's own history and the *Chronicle* both render this in words as "seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents" — a transposition; Ammon prints the correct total in facsimile two paragraphs above her own error.)

**(b)** "6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20" = **six dozen = 72 pipettes.** (Check: 72 × $0.35 = $25.20 ✔. Five dozen would be $21.00, which is not what the invoice says.) Vaught's "five dozen" and Cleve Oram's "five dozen" are both defeated by that line's own arithmetic.

Each was **short by 0.88 c.c.** on the mean (17.6 − 16.72). Strictly, the 0.88 is the mean of the nine that survived to be measured; individually those nine were short by 0.86 to 0.90 c.c. (delivering 16.70–16.74). We cannot know the other 63 individually, but their consistency is the point — Junia Ammon's reground-mold explanation predicts exactly this: a lot uniformly wrong.

### C5

**(a) Two different spans, and they are different for a reason.**

- **In use:** from **1 May 1897** (invoice 14 April 1897; office file; Orra Keddie's diary entry of that day; Bulletin 471's conclusion) to **12 June 1923** (Strawn's condemnation) = **26 years and about six weeks** — "six and twenty years," as Ammon puts it.
- **Money calculation:** **25 full delivery years, 1898 through 1922 inclusive.**

Why they differ: 1897 and 1923 are both part-years for the glass — it went into service on 1 May 1897 (eight months of that season) and was condemned on 12 June 1923 (five and a half months of that season) — while the Association's station column is kept by whole calendar years, so only 1898–1922 gives twenty-five clean annual sheets summing to a round 40,000,000 lb at 1,600,000 lb a year. Separately, the station's very first season (4 May–31 December 1896) was worked on borrowed Larrow Green glass, was not affected at all, and was never entered in the station column in the first place. So the glass was faulty for a little over twenty-six years, but the loss can only be *computed* over twenty-five of them; the two part-years and the 1897 fraction represent real, uncomputed additional loss.

**(b) First-season intake: cannot be determined from the sources.**

The curator states it flatly: "The station's intake for its first season, 4 May to the end of 1896, is not in the ledgers and is nowhere else; the station column begins with 1897, and inquirers wanting a figure for 1896 must be told there is none." Kyle, who ruled the pages, explains why: before there was a station account, that milk was weighed at the Hollow and carried on the creamery's own books, and those sheets were never put with the station papers. The only figures offered are two irreconcilable recollections, sixty and fifty-seven years after the fact: Hobart Sill, "about nine hundred thousand pounds"; Bertram Kyle, "a good eleven hundred thousand pounds, which is what I understood at the time from men who were there" — and Kyle himself adds, "It is not in any book I ever ruled." Neither man handled the figure; there is no document to break the tie; and the two are 200,000 lb apart with no third witness. What the sources *do* establish is only that it was a partial season of roughly eight months and was regarded as a large take for a hill station starting from nothing. **That is not enough to state a number.**

## Section D — Logic

### D1 — The nine weeks of 1911 as an experiment

**When and how long.** The station's own milk pipette broke on the morning of **8 May 1911** and a borrowed measure was used until the station's own was back in service on **10 July 1911** — **nine weeks** (63 days exactly). Both endpoints are contemporaneous entries in the test book in the tester's hand: *"May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure."* and *"July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K."* Orra Keddie's diary independently fixes the first date: "May 8, 1911. A. home cross about a broken measure." Teague and Kepp both say six weeks; the dated entries settle it.

**The variable.** One object: the milk pipette. Ivy Keddie says the substitute came up from Larrow Green with the cream wagon. Roy Brant says the state inspector left his own measure "that June" — which cannot be right: the borrowing began on 8 May, before Strawn's June visit; Strawn's 1911 entry records only "Watched a test through. Nothing here to note"; and Strawn had no burette or spare glass programme until 1921. So the substitute was not the inspector's; that it came from Larrow Green rests on Ivy alone and I hold it lightly. **What matters is only that it was a different pipette.**

**Held constant.** The same patrons, the same herds, the same pasture and the same season (the interval spans late spring into midsummer, so the herds went through their ordinary seasonal change inside it), the same acid, the same test bottles, the same hand tester, the same building, the same supervision — and the same man at the crank, with the same habits, taught the same way. Nothing was known to be under test, so no one had a motive to behave differently.

**Result.** The station's average rose from 3.61 to 3.80 and held there for the nine weeks, and fell back to 3.61 **on the day** the station's own measure returned.

**What it establishes that a calculation would not.** The 1958 burette work proves that nine pipettes surviving in a crate were short in 1958. It does not, by itself, prove that the glass in daily use through those years was what moved the figures. The 1911 interval does: it is a controlled substitution performed *in situ*, at the time, on the working plant, under conditions no designed trial could have bettered — and recorded contemporaneously by a man who did not know he was recording an experiment and put a question mark against it, which removes any possibility of the record being shaped to a conclusion. It disposes at a stroke of every explanation lodged in the herds, the ground, the feed, the weather, the men, or anyone's honesty, for the sufficient reason that none of those changed in those nine weeks and the figures did.

**What it does not establish.** (i) It does not identify the fault — that the station's pipettes were *short*, and by how much, comes only from the burette in 1958; the interval shows only that the two measures differed. (ii) It says nothing about the accuracy of the borrowed measure; strictly, the 3.80 could have been the borrowed glass reading high, and it is the 1958 measurement plus the Alder Corners figures plus the post-1923 average that fix 3.80 as the truth. (iii) It does not prove the same defect ran for the whole twenty-six years — that comes from the flat, seasonless 0.19 gap in every year of the test book. (iv) It does not by itself exclude coincidence, though the same-day return on 10 July reduces that to a formality. (v) It says nothing about whether anyone was *also* watering cans or taking cream; those are excluded by other evidence entirely.

### D2 — Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream

**Reason 1 — the Association's own ledger.** Every year the station account showed the butterfat received at Larrow Green in the station's cream *exceeding* the butterfat credited to patrons on the station's test, by a steady five and a quarter percent: "Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as usual." Stolen cream reaches the creamery **light**. This cream reached it **heavy**, every year, for a quarter of a century. Dell Rundle, who had printed the theft theory as the settled opinion of the valley, retracted it in a note added while his pamphlet was in press on seeing exactly this line.

**Reason 2 — Hobart Sill's independent re-weighing.** Sill was not reading the office's paper; he was handling the cans. He took the Hollow's cream off the platform and then weighed it over again himself as it went into the cold room, for forty-one years, on the principle that he never took another man's figure for what was going into his churn — "There was more fat in the cans than the papers said had been bought up there. Always more. Never less." He also disposes of the hauler's story (Oram's father: "there was less of it coming off that platform than went onto it") by weighing at the far end of that same journey.

**Reason 3 — the nine weeks of 1911.** The same man made every test in that interval, in the same building, alone, under no different supervision, with no one watching him — and the figures **rose**. A thief does not steal less when nothing has changed but a piece of glass, and does not resume on the exact day his own measure comes back.

**Reason 4 — the state's disinterested observer.** Merle Strawn stood at Keddie's elbow and watched full tests through from sample to reading on visit after visit from 1907, without letting him hurry; found the scales true against sealed weights every time; and found no added water with the lactometer at any visit. Duncan Keddie notes, for exactly this reason, that Strawn "was kin to nobody in the valley whatever."

**Why these are independent of one another.** They come from four different physical positions, four different kinds of evidence, and four parties with different interests: (1) the Association's own bookkeeping, written by the Association's own man and against the Association's interest to notice; (2) a physical re-weighing at the receiving end by a craftsman with no stake in the office's paper and an explicit refusal to trust it; (3) a contemporaneous record made by the accused himself, six years before any accusation existed and fourteen before the hearing, which he could not have shaped to a defence he had not yet needed; (4) an outside state officer with no kin, no property and no opinion in the valley, whose method was fixed and who declined on principle to record what he thought. No one of them depends on any other being true.

**Which would still hold if the glassware had never been measured in 1958.** Reasons 1, 2 and 3 all stand entirely without the burette. Reason 1 in particular is decisive on its own: it existed in ink on an open shelf in Ashlin from 1898 and was read out at every annual meeting.

**Did the bookkeeper ever question the ledger entries that refute his own accusation? No.** Teague wrote those entries himself — he invented the station-gain line in 1905 and carried it in the same terms for forty years, and the words "as usual" got into it and stayed. He is candid about why he stopped looking: "A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture." He raised the station *account* with Selby Vose once, in 1916, was told to let it alone, and let it alone — "I was the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience" — and that is the whole of his questioning. In 1925 he was nevertheless the man who put the cream-theft account before the board, and in 1948 he restated it "in the same words, because I have not changed my mind." He never once laid his own gain line beside his own accusation, and his memoir prints them three pages apart without noticing.

### D3 — Did the manager know the tests ran low before the glass was condemned?

**Yes — that the tests ran low, certainly, and on his own testimony.** He says so himself from the platform in 1926: "For a generation the milk brought to that station tested below the milk brought to Larrow Green, and every year we were told it was the wet hay, or the tester, or the office, or the weather." That is not a discovery of 1923; it is a description of the twenty years before it. The 1919 circular is direct proof of the same knowledge, and he grounds it in exactly that: "A manager who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says nothing to the men who make them is not being kind to them; he is being idle at their expense." Teague also put the station account to him in 1916 and was told to let it alone — so the matter had been raised with him by his own bookkeeper at least once. He had taken office in 1913 and signed the annual sheet carrying the station-gain line every year thereafter; as Teague puts it, "a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in front of him."

**But separate two questions, because the sources separate them.** That he knew the tests ran low is settled beyond argument. That he ever understood what the *station-gain line* meant — that it proved fat was arriving at the creamery in excess of what the station had credited, and therefore that nothing was being taken and something was being under-measured — is **not established, and the evidence leans against it.** Kyle, who watched him do it from a desk a foot from his door: "He read the totals and he never read the lines above them. He was not a man who read. He would take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign. I watched him do it a thousand times." Teague, who laid the sheet before him: "What he made of it he never told me." So the fair answer is: he knew the figures ran low for twenty years and acted on that knowledge by accusing his patrons; the one document in his own office that would have redirected the accusation passed under his signature annually and, on the best evidence available, unread.

## Section E — Contradictions

### E1(i) — Cases where TWO narrators carry the same wrong value

In every case below the tie is broken the same way in principle — **a transcribed document, or arithmetic internal to a document, outranks any number of memories or derivative reports; and I count independent sources, not narrators.** Note the recurring pathology: nearly all of them are digit transpositions, which is itself evidence that they are copying errors travelling down a chain rather than independent observations.

1. **Pipette mean delivery 16.27 c.c.** — Vaught (r11, §III and §V) and the *Ashlin Chronicle* (r19). **Correct: 16.72.** Broken by: Ivy Keddie's measurement sheet (Item 14), which lists all nine readings (16.70–16.74) — they cannot average 16.27, they average 16.72; by 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80 matching the 1911 interval, the 1924 average and the Alder Corners table, where Vaught's 0.9244 gives 3.905, matching nothing; and by Vaught's own bulletin conclusion, reproduced entire at the foot of his own text, saying "one part in twenty" and "seventy-six thousand pounds." **They are not two sources:** the *Chronicle* states it was made "from a college press release and two telephone calls." One error, printed twice.
2. **Station recorded average 3.16** — Teague (r05) and Frick (r13). **Correct: 3.61.** Broken by the test book's summary leaf, transcribed identically in r03, r04 and r07; by 3.61 against Larrow Green's 3.78 giving exactly Roy Brant's seventeen hundredths; and by 3.16 ÷ 0.95 = 3.33, which corresponds to nothing anywhere. Not independent: Frick had the Association's figures at the hearing, and Teague was the officer who supplied them.

### E1(ii) — Narrators who contradict themselves on the page

- **Vaught (r11)** — the worst case, and instructive because it is the most authoritative-looking document in the bundle. His §III gives "five dozen" purchased and a mean delivery of 16.27; his §V computes 0.9244, a true average of 3.905, a shortfall of 0.295 and 118,000 lb; his §VI calls the hay effect "some six times" too small. His §VII then reproduces his own bulletin's conclusion — "low by one part in twenty… seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat." One part in twenty is 0.95, not 0.9244; 76,000 lb is not 118,000. He even flags the wound himself: "It should be said that this figure is not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in the year following the replacement of the glassware." It is not reconcilable because 16.27 is a transposition of 16.72. His §V also misstates the denominator, calling the 5¼% gain a percentage "of the butterfat actually delivered" when the ledger's own arithmetic makes 5¼% the figure against *credited* fat.
- **Teague (r05)** — quotes the ledger entry that proves 1,600,000 lb/yr at 3.61 and 3.80, then states the station's average test was 3.16 and its intake 44,000,000 lb, neither of which his own entry can produce.
- **Ammon (r20)** — reproduces the invoice totalling 77.93 and immediately restates it as "seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents."
- **Grigg (r15)** — his 1919 covering letter says the committee "sampled those herds through the whole of 1908 — every month of that year, in the barn and on the pasture"; the report he is reprinting on the same sheet says "We have sampled the herds of Hessel Bottom through January, February and March." The curator confirms: "The sampling was of January, February and March 1908 only, as the report states." The inflation matters enormously, because a winter-only sample could not have detected that the gap was seasonless — which is the fact that kills the hay theory.
- **Nym (r07)** — his Item 3 is headed "Station test book, kept by E. Brant" but its transcribed first leaf is signed A. Keddie; his Item 4 is headed "Farm weigh book, kept by A. Keddie" but its transcribed leaf is Emmet Brant's 1910 entry. **The two headings are swapped**, while the transcriptions are correct. Separately, Item 5 says the crate "was found in the Association office at Ashlin, in February 1958," where every other source — Ivy, Hazel, Oram, Duncan, Falke, the *Chronicle* — places the find in the loft over the station's intake room. Resolution: the loft; the curator's provenance line is a slip, and his own transcriptions are otherwise exact.
- **Keddie (r03)** — his letter says the 1896 glass "was borrowed off the Ashlin creamery," while the first leaf of his own test book, quoted in the same document, says "Glass borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes." Sill confirms it was his, from Larrow Green. The book, written on the day, outranks the letter written twenty-nine years later.
- **Vose (r08)** — says the tests "rose by twenty-one hundredths" *and* that "the average for 1924 stood at 3.90," which implies a prior average of 3.69, a figure that exists nowhere; the book gives 3.61 rising to 3.80, a rise of 0.19. He also attributes the rise to the scrutiny that began with the January 1925 petition while himself dating the new glass to 1923 — the rise is in the 1924 book, before the petition was filed.
- **Duncan Keddie (r18)** — gives his mother's birth as 1868 and also says she was eighty-two when she died in 1948, which requires 1866. The diary's editorial preface gives 1866, and Orra's own entry of August 1930 ("I am sixty-four") confirms it. 1866.
- **Rundle (r12)** — Chapter IV states the theft theory as the valley's settled opinion; the Note Added While In Press retracts it on the ledger evidence and leaves both standing. This is the one self-contradiction in the bundle that is a virtue rather than a fault, and he flags it himself.
- **Falke (r22)** — paragraph 9 declines to dismiss the wet hay and calls it "a real if partial factor"; the appended note withdraws the concession entirely on reading Bulletin 214's actual text. Also para 11 gives the post-1923 average as 3.85 against Strawn's and Keddie's contemporaneous 3.80, and para 2's "great-niece" is retracted in the same note.
- **Loomis (r02)** — says the board "sat at Ashlin," where Frick, Oram and Orra Keddie all record two of the three sittings at the station's weigh room. Resolvable: her minute book carries the formal caption of the proceeding, and the curator repeats the caption; it is not a claim about every session's venue.
- **Strawn (r06)** — took the circuit in 1907 and laid it down in 1936, thirty years, but describes "twenty-eight of them, one to the year." Minor and unresolvable; nothing turns on it.
- **Hazel Brant (r17)** — announces her own failure mode ("Quantities I have. Years I have not") and then demonstrates it perfectly. Every quantity she gives is right: nineteen hundredths, six farms, four men over the ridge, nine families, 200,000 lb even, a test of 3.42. Every date she gives is wrong: the glass condemned "in twenty-one" (1923 — 1921 is when the burettes were issued), the hearing "in twenty-six" (1925), the crate out from Tarnet "in ninety-nine" (1897), her grandfather beginning the weigh book "in nineteen hundred" (1897). She is a reliable witness read according to her own instructions.
- **Kyle (r23)** — the award "was in twenty-four" (1925); Vose became manager "in nineteen eleven" (1913, per Strawn's contemporaneous "New manager at the Association this year," Loomis's minutes and Vose's own count of thirteen years from 1913); the state man came in "in twenty-two" (1923 — and Strawn's book explicitly records *not* running the glass in 1922); the station's own glass in use "on the first of May, 1898" (1897).
- **Vira Toland (r01)** — telescopes six years: "the tests went up… And after that there was the hearing down at Ashlin. That was the winter and spring after." The tests went up with the new glass in 1923–24; the hearing was in 1925.
- **The *Chronicle* (r19)** — asserts "the committee that sat on the matter in 1908 sat again in 1925," conflating Grigg's investigating committee with Frick's board of arbitration, which shared no members and had different constitutions. It also accuses Ammon & Sons of knowingly selling a cheap short lot and calls the firm's 1923 silence "the proof of it" — an inference from nothing, contradicted by the firm's own later account of a reground mold and a lost works book, and by the fact that a house selling short glass deliberately would have no reason to keep doing business with the customer for twenty-six years.

### E1(iii) — Other single-source conflicts and how they resolve

- **Farms on Hessel Bottom: six or seven?** Loomis says seven; the 1908 report ("where six farms cut hay"; "visited each of the six farms"), Roy Brant, Hazel Brant, Kepp and the *Chronicle* say six. **Six** — a quoted contemporaneous document plus two men who farmed it.
- **Nine or eleven families got the 1919 circular?** Nine from Vira Toland (her father got one), Ansel Keddie (he carried them), Roy Brant (his father got one) and Hazel Brant (her mother burned hers) — four genuinely independent positions. Eleven from Orra Keddie's diary, written within days. **Unresolved, leaning nine.** Contemporaneity is normally the stronger tiebreaker, but here four independent recipient-side sources agree and one of them is the man who delivered them; Orra may be counting doors he stopped at rather than circulars addressed. I decline to force it.
- **Who lent the 1911 measure?** Ivy says Larrow Green, with the cream wagon; Roy Brant says the state man left his own. Roy is defeated by chronology (borrowing began 8 May; Strawn came in June) and by Strawn's silence and his lack of any such programme before 1921. **Not the inspector; Larrow Green probable but single-sourced.**
- **Alder Corners founded 1913 or 1914?** Rosalie Cudd, present as a girl of twelve at the first vat, says 1913; Rundle and Roy Brant, both on the Ordell side of the ridge, say 1914. **1913, weakly** — first-hand over hearsay, but I would not defend it hard, and nothing turns on it.
- **Emmet Brant's weigh book begun 1897, 1898 or 1900?** Frick's contemporaneous exhibit list and the curator's accession both say 1 January 1897, and Duncan agrees; Roy says 1898, Hazel says 1900. **1897.**
- **1924 station average: 3.80, 3.85 or 3.90?** Strawn's field book ("Their book gives the year at 3.80") and Keddie's letter ("In 1924 the average of this station was 3.80") against Falke's 3.85 and Vose's 3.90. **3.80** — and it matters, because 3.80 is exactly 3.61 ÷ 0.95 and exactly the Alder Corners figure.
- **Article VII adopted 1891, 1892 or 1893?** Rundle, Loomis, Grigg respectively; the pamphlet is undated and the minute book is lost. **Cannot be determined from the sources.**
- **First-season intake 900,000 or 1,100,000 lb?** Sill against Kyle, no document. **Cannot be determined.**
- **Emmet and Orra: full or half siblings?** Roy and Duncan against Hazel. **Full, leaning strongly**, but see B2.
- **The patrons' own 1925 claim.** Frick recalls it as 67,000 lb of butterfat; Rundle says the petition stated $19,600 to the dollar; Cleve Oram attributes 67,000 lb to the 1958 college work, which is a misattribution — the college figure is 76,000. Note 67,000/76,000 and 19,600/19,000 are the same class of transposition as the rest. **The patrons' 1925 arithmetic cannot be reconstructed from the sources**; the 1958 figures are 76,000 lb and $19,000.

## Section F — Theory

### F1 — What actually produced the shortfall

Some time before April 1897, on the blowing floor at Ammon & Sons in Tarnet, a mold for milk test pipettes came back from the shop reground a hair different and looked exactly as it had looked before. The lot blown on it was marked, as ordered, at 17.6 c.c., and delivered a mean of 16.72. Nobody in the works caught it, because the works check tests a lot for *consistency* — the first, the tenth, the fiftieth — and this lot was perfectly consistent. It was consistently wrong, which is the one fault that check cannot find. Six dozen of them were invoiced to the Larrow Valley Cooperative Creamery Association on 14 April 1897 for the Ostrey Hollow station, and went into use there on 1 May 1897.

A butterfat test reads percent directly off the length of a fat column in a graduated neck, and it is entitled to do so for exactly one reason: the charge of milk is a fixed known quantity, and the maker rules the neck for that quantity and no other. Deliver a short charge and less fat is liberated, a shorter column stands, and the neck — which knows nothing of the substitution — is read in the ordinary way. **The reading is low in the exact proportion the charge was short.**

**Why that size.** 16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95, exactly one part in twenty. So every figure the station produced for twenty-six years was 95% of the truth: a recorded 3.61 where the milk was 3.80, a recorded 3.42 where a herd was really 3.60, a recorded 4.56 where a herd was really 4.80. The size of the error is not a property of the milk, the land, the cows or the men; it is a property of a mold in another county. That is also why it was a *ratio* and not a fixed deduction: the station did not lose a set number of points, it lost a twentieth of whatever was there, so the error fell hardest on the best milk and no man could see that his good cows cost him more than his poor ones.

**Why it began when it did, and ended when it did, with nothing changing at either end.** It began on 1 May 1897, the day that outfit replaced the glass borrowed from Larrow Green — which is why the first season, 1896, tested 3.80 and every season from 1898 tested 3.61. It ended on 12 June 1923, when Merle Strawn, carrying a burette the state had begun issuing to field men in 1921, ran the station's pipettes against it, found they would not deliver their mark, condemned the whole crate and told the manager to order new; new glass was in use from July, and the 1924 book gives the year at 3.80. Nothing on the farms or at the station had to change at either end because nothing on the farms or at the station was ever wrong. The fault entered the building in a crate on a wagon in 1897 and left the working floor in the same crate in 1923 — up a ladder into the loft, where it sat for another thirty-five years.

**Why nobody found it.** Three properties, and it took all three. *Proportional*: the station's figures stayed internally orderly, its herds ranked correctly against one another, and only against the outside world were they wrong. *Precise*: no scatter, no drift, no bad days — a slipping scale attracts attention, a uniformly short pipette has the appearance of excellent work. *Unverifiable from inside*: scales check against test weights, acid against strength, bottle graduations against mercury, but the pipette is not checked against anything, because in the practice of the trade **the pipette is the standard**. An instrument that is itself the measure cannot be caught by measuring with it; it can only be caught by a burette brought in from outside, and burettes did not reach the state's field men until 1921. Meanwhile the missing fat was not missing at all: it arrived at Larrow Green in the cream, was weighed into the vats, and was entered every year in the Association's own ledger as "station gain, five and a quarter per cent, as usual" — and carried to the general account, so that the Ostrey Hollow patrons quietly subsidized every other member of the co-operative for twenty-five years.

### The three competing explanations, and what defeats each

**1. Added water in the patrons' cans.** Advanced by **Selby Vose**, manager — in the circular of 1919, before the board in 1925, and from the platform in 1926 — and seconded by **Hobart Sill**, the buttermaker ("You cannot tell me nine families all got honest in one summer"). Defeated by:
- Merle Strawn tested with the lactometer at that station on every visit from 1907 onward and **never once found added water**: "Lactometer, no added water, as always here" — and he told the manager so to his face in 1919, the year the circular went out.
- Rosalie Cudd's cheese-vat argument, which is physical and independent: watered milk cannot be concealed from a cheesemaker, because the curd comes soft and will not knit and the yield drops, and it tells him the next morning. In sixteen summers her father never complained of those four Hessel Bottom men, never docked one a cent, and said nothing of their milk except that he wished he had more of it in July.
- Shape: the gap stood at nineteen hundredths in February and nineteen hundredths in July, in wet years and dry, for twenty-six years, across nine or eleven households. No habit of watering distributes itself that evenly.
- Chronology: Vose's own proof is that "the tests came up when the looking began," dating the rise to the 1925 petition and hearing. The rise is in the **1924** book — it followed the new glass of July 1923 by months and preceded the petition by more than a year.

**2. The tester was taking cream and writing low tests to cover it.** Put to the board by **Wilbur Teague**, the bookkeeper, in the manner of a man who wants a thing said without saying it; believed by the hauler (Cleve Oram's father), by Vira Toland, and by the valley at large; printed as the settled opinion by **Dell Rundle** in 1939. Defeated by:
- The Association's own station-gain line: the cream arrived at Larrow Green **heavy** every year, by five and a quarter percent of the fat credited. Theft makes cream arrive light. Rundle himself retracted the chapter on seeing it.
- Hobart Sill's independent re-weighing of every can into the cold room for forty-one years: "Always more. Never less."
- The nine weeks of 1911: same man, same room, same absence of supervision, and the figures rose the moment the glass changed and fell back the day it returned.
- The character of the error itself: a proportional, invariant 5% is not what pilferage looks like; theft is opportunistic and scatters.

**3. The wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinned the cream.** The finding of the **1908 committee under Nyle Grigg**, reasserted by Grigg in 1919, elaborated by **Dr. Roland Kepp** in 1926, and conceded (then withdrawn) by **Orin Falke** in 1959. Defeated by:
- Bulletin No. 214, the College's own controlled feeding trial, whose actual text reads "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and… nothing that could be measured on summer pasture." Against a flat 0.19 in every month, that is four times too small in winter and infinitely too small in summer: wrong size **and** wrong shape. Grigg and Kepp both quote it as "two tenths," quadrupling it.
- The 1908 committee sampled only January, February and March, so it could not have discovered the one feature that refutes it — that the gap does not vary with the season.
- Alder Corners: the same four men's milk, off the same wet ground, in the same summers, tested 3.78–3.82 at a factory over the ridge for sixteen seasons.
- The nine weeks of 1911: the ground did not change and the figures did.
- Kepp's dismissal of that interval as "six weeks of June and July" when every herd is at its best misses the comparison entirely — the interval is compared not with other herds but with the same station's own figures immediately before and after.

### Two subsidiary questions

**Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware? No.** The award recites that the board had "before it the books and appliances of the station," but Effie Loomis, who took down every word of all three sessions, is explicit: "The crate was never mentioned in the hearing at all. I took every word down and it is not there… the award's phrase about 'the books and appliances of the station' is a form of words the board used and not a list of anything." What the board did examine were the station's *current* appliances — Frick had two of the three sittings moved into the weigh room and they watched the scales tried and a test made on the **new** glass installed in July 1923. The condemned crate lay under a tarpaulin in the loft roughly twenty feet above their heads throughout. Worse, Strawn's circuit notebook for 1923 — the book recording the condemnation — was Exhibit 4 before them, and the station's test book was Exhibit 1, and as Kyle puts it, no man in that room ever laid the one beside the other and looked.

**What did the tester himself believe?** Two different things at two different times, and they must be kept apart.
In 1911 he suspected the glass. Duncan Keddie, seventeen that year, remembers it precisely because it was out of character: "He came home that summer of 1911 saying it was something in the glass, and he never dared put it in the book… he said it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not his way." What he wrote in the book instead was a question mark, and he let it stand fourteen years: "I could not answer it then and I cannot answer it now, and I will not put anything in its place that I do not know." He had nothing in his hand but a suspicion about a piece of glass, and against it stood the outfit he had been taught to trust and the practice of every station in the county — so he kept on, and wrote down every figure the glass gave him, and those figures convicted him.
By 1925 he had no public theory at all: to the board he said flatly, twice, "I cannot tell you why the figures are what they are." Privately, in the corridor at Ashlin, he told Frick "that he had always thought the patrons were at fault and would not say so in the room" — not bitterly; he had lived among them thirty years and would not stand up and accuse his neighbors. So: a suspicion of the glass in 1911 that he never dared record; a private suspicion of the patrons by 1925 that he never voiced; and in public, honestly, nothing.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** For twenty-six years a skimming station at the head of an Ordell County valley paid its farmers on tests that read exactly ninety-five percent of the truth, because the pipettes it drew its milk with — bought new in 1897, blown for 17.6 c.c. — delivered 16.72. Under the Association's by-law the station's own test was final, so the error was money: 76,000 pounds of butterfat, $19,000, credited instead to the co-operative as annual "station gain." The valley blamed water, theft and wet hay; it ruined its tester in 1925 and paid the patrons $3,800 without admitting anything. Not until 1958 did his granddaughter climb a ladder to the loft and measure the condemned glass. It took eight separate records — a by-law, a test book, an invoice, a farm weigh book, a state inspector's field notes, a cheese factory's ledger over the ridge, the Association's own gain line, and one burette — because no one of them alone could say what the others held.
