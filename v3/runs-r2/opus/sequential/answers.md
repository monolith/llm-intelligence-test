# Answers — Part 1

## Section A — Reconstruction (stories 1–4)

---

### Story 1. The Association, and the Article That Made the Test the Master

In 1889 the farmers of the Larrow Valley, in Ordell County, met at Larrow Green — in the
schoolhouse, in cold weather, and it went on very late — and organized the Larrow Valley
Cooperative Creamery Association. They were tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking what
the merchant allowed them. Gideon Vose was elected first president and held the office
twenty-two years, until 1911; he died in 1917. In 1891 the creamery was built at Larrow Green
on Larrow Creek — the same building still standing fifty years later — and the valley began to
be paid in cash once a month, which had never happened to it before. Hobart Sill came in as
buttermaker in 1893 and stood at that churn forty-one years.

Gideon Vose wrote the by-laws himself, at his own kitchen table, not being a lawyer. The
seventh article governs everything that follows:

> *Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of
> pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of
> the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*

Before it, a hundred pounds of milk was a hundred pounds of milk whosever cow it came from.
After it, a man was paid for the fat found in his milk by a test made where he delivered. It is
a just rule and nobody in the valley ever seriously argued otherwise. But the last clause is the
one nobody quotes: *the test of the station where he delivers shall govern* — not the creamery's
test, not the valley's average, not a test made over again anywhere else, and no appeal from it.
That clause made a single figure, produced by a single instrument in a single room, the whole of
the bargain, and left a dissatisfied man no remedy on earth except to quarrel with it.

Article VII was adopted at the annual meeting of 1892, the year after the creamery opened. I take
that from Effie Loomis, secretary 1901–1940, writing from the minute book. Dell Rundle says 1891
and Nyle Grigg says 1893; the printed by-laws carry no year, only "adopted at the annual meeting,"
and the county agent in 1959 honestly declined to state one. Loomis had the minutes in front of
her; the others had the store and their memories. The 1889–1892 minute book was lost when the
office was moved in 1938 — carried out with the rest and never arriving — so the year cannot now
be proved from the record itself.

---

### Story 2. The Station, and the Crate from Tarnet

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station seven miles up the valley at
Ostrey Hollow, because the pull down to Larrow Green was three hours in mud and impossible in
March. Milk was weighed in at the intake, a sample drawn from each patron's cans, the sample
tested for butterfat, the milk separated, the cream hauled down to the creamery, and the skim
handed back the same morning for the hogs and calves. The station opened on 4 May 1896. Ansel
Keddie, born 1861, was its tester and separator man from the opening; he went up in 1895 to get
the building ready and served twenty-nine seasons, 1896 through 1924. He kept the test book in
his own hand in five columns — date, patron, pounds, test, fat — crossed his errors and initialled
the crossing, and never wrote a figure over a figure.

The station had no glassware of its own for its first season. Hobart Sill made up an outfit out of
his own bench glass at Larrow Green and sent it up the valley on the wagon so the station could
open on time. The test book's first leaf records it: *"Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass
borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes."* That first season, on borrowed and sound glass,
the station's average test was **3.80**.

On 14 April 1897 Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet, invoiced the Association for the station's
own outfit:

> 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20; 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at
> .22 — 31.68; 1 acid measure — .90; 1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00; Freight — 2.15. **Total —
> 77.93.**

The glass came up the valley in a crate and went into use on **1 May 1897**. Orra Keddie wrote
that day that her husband was pleased: they were done borrowing of Larrow Green. From 1898 the
station's average test was **3.61**, and it stayed at 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every
year afterwards.

Seventy-two pipettes had come in that crate, every one of them blown on the stem at 17.6 c.c.
Every one of them delivered about 16.72. The firm's own historian, writing in 1949, reconstructed
how: a mold reground, a gauge knocked off its nail, a lot running short, and a boy checking the
first, the tenth and the fiftieth and finding them consistent — consistently wrong, which is the
one fault a check of that kind cannot find. The works books that might have proved it burned in
1931.

### Story 3. Twenty Years of Grievance, and the Hay That Was Blamed

From 1902 the men of Ostrey Hollow said out loud that their milk tested under the milk of the rest
of the valley. Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom was among the first, and they were laughed at.
Hessel Bottom is a hundred and sixty acres of wet meadow under the hamlet; six farms had rights
on it, cut hay and pastured stock there, and Emmet Brant's was the largest — sixty-odd acres and
thirty-eight cows. The hay is sedge and rush, cut late, and it is the winter dependence of those
farms.

In January 1908 the Association appointed a committee under Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green, who was
not a patron of the station and had no interest in the answer. The committee visited each of the
six farms, drew and sealed its own samples, watched the tests made, tried the station scales
against a known weight and the patrons' scales against the station's, and reported: the milk is
whole, the scales are honest, no dishonesty of any kind in any quarter — and it is the wet hay of
that bottom that thins the cream, and the remedy lies with the land and not with the station. That
report closed the question for eleven years.

Two things defeat it, and both were available. First, the committee sampled only January, February
and March — Grigg's own report says so, though his 1919 covering letter claimed they sampled every
month of the year. A winter-only sample cannot detect an error that is the same size in July as in
February, and the shape of this error was the whole clue: it stood flat, wet years and dry, on
pasture and on hay. Second, the State Agricultural College at Farland ran a controlled feeding
trial on bog hay and published it in 1911 as Bulletin No. 214: *bog hay fed as the whole of the
roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter,
and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture.* Grigg in 1919 and Dr. Roland Kepp in his
1926 farm-club paper both cited that bulletin as finding **two tenths**, and built their case on
the inflated figure. It is four times too small and the wrong shape.

There was also an outside check nobody in Ordell County ever thought to make. From 1915, four of
the six Hessel Bottom men hauled over Redlow Ridge in the summer flush to Jerome Cudd's Alder
Corners Cheese Company in Vessey County, opened 1913, and back to their own station the rest of
the year — sixteen seasons, ending 1930. Cudd tested on his own bench with his own glass and never
compared a figure with anybody. His season averages for those four men, taken together, sat
between 3.78 and 3.82 every single year: about **3.80**. The same milk, off the same wet ground,
in the same summers.

### Story 4. The Nine Weeks of 1911

On the morning of 8 May 1911 the station's own measuring pipette broke. Orra Keddie's diary has
her husband home cross about it that evening, and rain all day. Keddie made shift with a borrowed
measure — most probably one sent up from Larrow Green, as in 1896; Roy Brant remembered it as the
state inspector's, but the inspector did not come through until June and the change dates from 8
May, so that memory is excluded by the calendar.

From that morning the station's tests came up and stood at 3.80. They held there for nine weeks.
On 10 July the station's own measure came back into use and the tests went down again the same
day. Keddie entered both facts as they happened:

> *May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure.*
>
> *July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.*

He let that question mark stand for fourteen years and would not put anything in its place that
he did not know. But he came home that summer saying it was something in the glass — his son
Duncan, then seventeen, remembered him saying it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was
not his way. Against a suspicion about a piece of glass stood the outfit he had been taught to
trust and the practice of every station in the county, and there was no instrument in the county
that could have settled it: the state did not begin issuing burettes to its field men until 1921.
So he kept on, and wrote down every figure the glass gave him for another fourteen years, and the
figures were what convicted him.

It was an experiment that ran itself, and nobody knew it was one. The patrons were the same, the
herds the same, the pasture, the season, the feed, the acid, the bottles, the hand tester, the
operator and his habits all the same. **One object changed**, and changed back, and the average
moved with it both times. Every farm on the Bottom saw its tests rise together and fall together.
Emmet Brant noticed the rise and noticed the fall and entered both in his weigh book and could
make nothing of them.

Two witnesses stood inside those nine weeks without seeing them. Merle Strawn, the state's dairy
inspector, visited Ostrey Hollow in June 1911, watched a test through and wrote: *Nothing here to
note.* And the College published Bulletin 214 that same year, disposing of the hay, while the
station itself was quietly disposing of it too. The interval lay unread for forty-seven years.

### Story 5. Station Gain: The Money in the Association's Own Books

The Association ran three things on paper: the creamery at Larrow Green, an office at Ashlin, and
the skimming station. Selby Vose was manager from 1913 to 1938; Effie Loomis was secretary
1901–1940 and kept the minutes; Wilbur Teague sat at the ledger 1905–1945; Bertram Kyle copied at
the outer desk and ruled the annual sheets.

The station account carried two figures that ought to have matched and never did. On one side went
the butterfat credited to the patrons on the station's own test — what the station's books said it
had bought. On the other went the butterfat actually weighed and tested into the vats at Larrow
Green out of the station's cream — what the Association had in hand. The second was always the
larger. The difference was entered as *station gain* and carried to the general account. The
entry for 1898, in the same form every year afterwards:

> *Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received
> at Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per
> cent, as usual. Carried to the general account.*

That is the whole shortfall, on the Association's own side of the account, in ink, on an open
shelf, read out at the annual meeting to fifty farmers with their hats on their knees, for a
quarter of a century. Two things kept it invisible. It was even — 5.25 per cent year after year,
until the words "as usual" got into the entry and stayed — and evenness stops a bookkeeper
looking; a figure that jumps about keeps you awake, and one that comes in the same every year is
furniture. And it had a name. As Kyle put it, a figure with a name on it has been explained, and
you rule under it and go on to the next. It was not a secret. It was worse than a secret: it was
in plain sight with a name on it.

Hobart Sill had the physical half of the same fact. He weighed the Hollow's cream himself when it
came into the cold room, over and above the platform man, from 1893 onward, and it always ran
heavy — more fat in the cans than the office sheet said had been bought up there. Always more,
never less. He said so in that room a hundred times and nobody thought it worth anything,
including him: cream is a thick thing that clings, and he put it down to that.

Teague put the account to Vose once, in 1916, and was told to let it alone, and let it alone. Vose
signed the annual sheet every spring — though Kyle watched him sign a thousand sheets by putting
his thumb on the bottom total without reading the lines above it.

### Story 6. The Circular of 1919, and the Two Kitchens

Ansel Keddie married Orra Brant in 1889. Orra was Emmet Brant's sister — the younger of the two.
So the man who weighed, sampled, tested and wrote the figure for every can on Hessel Bottom was
married to the sister of the patron who lost most by those figures, and had been at his table on
Sundays for thirty-six years. Their son Duncan, born 1894, went down to teach school at Ashlin at
seventeen.

In June 1919 a printed circular went up to the station over Selby Vose's signature, warning the
Ostrey Hollow patrons that added water would be looked for and dealt with. Kyle typed it and
thought at the time it was a hard thing to send to men who brought their milk in every morning of
their lives. It named nobody and accused nobody, and Vose defended every word of it to the annual
meeting in 1926 and said he would write it again tomorrow.

Ansel Keddie would not put the letters in the mail nor leave them on the platform. He carried them
round himself, stood at each door while the man read it, and came home at eleven at night with his
hat still on. Nine families received it — his own count, in 1925, and the count of every patron's
child who remembered it; his wife's diary says eleven, and I cannot reconcile that except by
noting that she did not carry them.

Two days later Emmet Brant came up and would not come into the house. He stood in the yard and
said he had hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and Ansel knew it better
than any man living, and asked why, if he did not believe it, he had carried the paper himself.
Ansel said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet went home. He never
entered that house again. The two men lived within sight of each other's chimney smoke and did not
speak for ten years, not at the store and not at a funeral, and Orra would walk the long way round
rather than pass her brother's lane. It ended in the spring of 1929 when Emmet died and Orra went
down and sat an hour in the kitchen where she had been a girl.

The letters themselves outlived the men. One went into a desk drawer and was still there when its
owner died; one woman kept hers pinned inside the lid of her trunk; one was put straight into the
stove. You may tell people a thing like that is only business, and they will keep the paper forty
years to prove what was said of them.

### Story 7. The Arbitration of 1925, and the Turning Out of Ansel Keddie

In January 1925 the patrons of Ostrey Hollow petitioned the Association: their milk had never
tested as their neighbours' tested, they had been paid accordingly for a very long time, and they
asked to be made whole. Emmet Brant's name was on it. The by-laws provided for arbitration and the
officers did not resist it. A board of three was constituted under Alonzo Frick, attorney, of
Ashlin — chairman, he supposed, because he was the one of the three who kept no cow.

There were three sittings between 17 March and 4 May 1925. The first was at the Association's
office at Ashlin; the second and third Frick asked to have in the station's own weigh room, so the
board could see the plant instead of hearing it described. They saw the scales tried and a test
made, on the new glass that had been in use since July 1923. Four books came before them, and
nothing else: the station test book in Keddie's hand; the station's receiving book; Emmet Brant's
private weigh book, lent and wanted back; and Merle Strawn's circuit notebook for 1923.

Three explanations were argued. Water, urged by Selby Vose without heat and without proof. Cream
taken by the tester, insinuated by Wilbur Teague in the manner of an accountant saying a thing
without saying it, and then declining to charge anybody. The wet hay, read into the record from
the 1908 report, which had the advantage of blaming nobody in the room. Each was urged by a man of
standing; each would have been believed by half the valley; not one was proved.

Keddie made a statement of nine sentences, standing, on 17 March: that he had kept the book in his
own hand since the station opened, that he had tested as he was taught and as the state's man had
watched him do it, thirty-one times a week for twenty-nine seasons, that neither the patrons nor
the officers had ever come up that road on a working morning and stood at his elbow, that he could
not tell them why the figures were what they were, and that he had never taken a pound of cream
out of that station. His denial was accurate and it did him no good, because a man who cannot
account for a figure is presumed to be its author.

On **30 April 1925** the Association dismissed him — twenty-nine seasons and a letter — while the
board was still sitting. He came up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his
arm, hung it behind the shed door, and asked what there was for supper. On **4 May** the board
found *no cause established* and awarded the patrons **$3,800, without admission**, charged to the
station account. An award without a finding, four days after a dismissal without one.

### Story 8. The Crate in the Loft, and the Clearing of a Dead Man

On 12 June 1923 Merle Strawn came up on an ordinary circuit visit carrying the burette the state
had begun issuing to field men in 1921. He ran the station's milk pipettes against it and wrote:
*These measures will not deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told
the manager to order new.* The Association ordered from Ammon & Sons on 28 June; the firm answered
on 2 July, entered the order, regretted the trouble, and said its books of the nineties would tell
nobody anything. New glass was in use from July. Strawn ran the new pipettes in October and they
delivered their mark. From 1924 the station's average stood at **3.80** and stayed there. Nobody
connected the one thing with the other; the condemned crate went up the ladder into the loft over
the intake room, and it was in that loft, twenty feet above their heads, while two of the three
arbitration sessions sat in the room beneath.

It stayed there thirty-five years. In February 1958 Ivy Keddie — Ansel's granddaughter, born 1931,
a graduate student at the State Agricultural College at Farland — climbed that ladder and found it
under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff. Nine pipettes were unbroken. On 9 March she measured them
at Farland against a calibrated burette, five runs each: mean **16.72 c.c.** against a mark of
17.6, short 0.88 — **ninety-five hundredths of the mark, and the mark is what the neck of the
bottle was ruled for.**

She brought five sets of records into one room, which had never happened: the station test book,
the Brant weigh book (lent by Roy Brant), the Alder Corners figures copied out for her by Rosalie
Cudd, Strawn's circuit notebooks (in her own college's library since 1947, unasked-for eleven
years), and the Association's annual ledgers. 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80. The recorded gap of nineteen
hundredths was not a fixed quantity but a twentieth of whatever was there — so it fell hardest on
the best milk.

Bulletin No. 471 was published in October 1958 over Professor Leland Vaught's name and hers:

> *No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen there. From the first of May,
> 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by one part in twenty because
> its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference — seventy-six thousand pounds
> of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year by year, as station gain.*

The glass was condemned in 1923 and the cause found in 1958: thirty-five years. Ansel Keddie was
dismissed in 1925 and had been dead twenty-four years. The test book and the crate went to the
Ordell County Historical Rooms in 1959; the weigh book followed in 1963. "It would have been a
kindness," Ivy Keddie told the county paper, "if somebody in this county had thought to climb a
ladder."

## Section B — Relationships

**B1. Ivy Keddie was Ansel Keddie's granddaughter.** The line is: Ansel (b. 1861) and Orra (b.
1866) married 1889; their son Duncan was born 1894 and taught school at Ashlin; Duncan's daughter
Ivy was born 1931 and was twenty-seven in 1958. Duncan states it flatly in his 1968 family notes
and says he writes it plainly because he has twice seen it printed otherwise and once heard it
said aloud in Ashlin. Ivy herself opens her 1959 talk by saying a granddaughter who reads her
grandfather's books ought to say so. Two sources point elsewhere: the *Ashlin Chronicle* of 3
October 1958 calls her his great-niece, and the county agent's 1959 report repeats it on page two.
These are not independent — both worked from the college press release — and the county agent
appended a correction: *"I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter
and not the great-niece; the error on page two is mine."* Family testimony plus a checked
retraction beats a weekly assembled in a day and a half.

**B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant were second cousins.** Derivation: Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie
were brother and sister. Emmet's son Roy and Orra's son Duncan are therefore first cousins — the
children of a brother and a sister, which is the whole definition. Roy's daughter Hazel and
Duncan's daughter Ivy are the children of first cousins, which is second cousins. All three
people in a position to know say so in the same words: Roy in 1955, Duncan in 1968, Hazel in 1981
("People are forever telling me she is my niece or my cousin once removed. She is not"). Vira
Toland and Cleve Oram both say "first cousin once removed"; that is village shorthand, from
outside both households, and it is the relation of a different generation. What the sources leave
open is the *intervening* generation on the Brant side: Roy insists Orra and Emmet were full
brother and sister, "same mother and same father, whatever you may hear from people who like a
story with a stepmother in it"; Hazel says Orra was a half sister by Emmet's father's second
wife; Duncan says only "brother and sister." Orra's diary survives from 1897 only — the pages
covering her family before her marriage were not preserved — so no document settles it. It does
not disturb the answer: half-siblings produce first cousins and second cousins exactly as full
siblings do.

**B3. Selby Vose was Gideon Vose's son**, not his nephew. Selby says so himself from the platform
in 1926 ("My father, who was your first president and who wrote the by-law under which every
patron in this Association is paid on the test of the station where he delivers"); Effie Loomis
writes "Selby Vose, son of the late president"; Bertram Kyle says "the son, whatever is said at
Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense"; Duncan
Keddie lists it among the things that are always got wrong. Vira Toland and Dell Rundle say
nephew, and both are reporting valley talk. It matters for three reasons. First, the manager who
sent the 1919 water circular, who urged the watering theory before the board in 1925, and who
defended both to the membership in 1926, was the son of the man who wrote Article VII — the clause
that made the station's own test unappealable and so made the whole loss possible. Second, that
made the by-law effectively unchallengeable in the valley: it was family property as much as
Association property, and the Voses, as Vira Toland put it, always thought the valley belonged to
them a little. Third, Selby signed the annual sheet carrying the station-gain line every year of
his management, and told Teague in 1916 to let it alone; the article he was protecting and the
account he would not look into were the same interest.

**B4. No relationship between the Voses and the Keddies is established, and the claim is probably
false.** Vira Toland says Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins — "everybody knew that"
— and makes it the sting of the 1919 letter: not only the Association saying it, but somebody's
cousin saying it. Cleve Oram says the Keddies and the Voses "were cousins of some sort," that he
had it from his mother's mother, and that he could not draw the line on paper. Those two are not
independent evidence; they are one piece of valley talk reported twice, and neither can trace it.
Against them, Duncan Keddie denies it from inside the household: people confuse them with the
Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Gideon either; he has heard it from two women in Ashlin, one
of whom will tell you first cousins if you let her sit down; and his mother, who could not pass a
headstone without giving you the connection, never once put a Vose in it. Orra's own diary, kept
thirty-three years, never mentions a Vose connection. Strictly, the negative cannot be proved —
her pre-1889 family pages do not survive and the Association's first minute book is lost — so the
honest answer is: not established, contradicted by the only first-hand witness, and best explained
as confusion with the unrelated Ashlin Voses.

# Answers — Part 3

## Section C — Math

### C1.

**(a) The tests were low by one part in twenty — the recorded figure was 0.95 of the truth.**

Nine pipettes survived in the condemned crate. Each was blown on the stem at **17.6 c.c.**, the
standard charge in that state, and the neck of a test bottle is ruled to read percent for that
charge and no other. Ivy Keddie delivered each against a calibrated burette, five runs apiece, on
9 March 1958: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74, 16.71. Mean **16.72 c.c.**,
short 0.88.

    16.72 ÷ 17.6 = 0.95 exactly

A short charge liberates a proportionally short column of fat, and the neck knows nothing of the
substitution. So every test made on that glass read **95%** of the truth. (I reject Vaught's
16.27 c.c. and his ratio 0.9244: the nine individual readings quoted on the signed sheet average
16.72, not 16.27, and 0.9244 yields results Vaught's own bulletin contradicts. See E1.)

**(b) True average 3.80; the recorded tests were short by 0.19 of a point.**

    recorded 3.61 ÷ 0.95 = 3.80
    3.80 − 3.61 = 0.19

Corroborated three ways independently of the glass: the 1896 season on borrowed Larrow Green glass
averaged 3.80; the nine weeks of 1911 on a borrowed measure stood at 3.80; the year 1924 on new
glass stood at 3.80. Note the crucial property — 0.19 is not a fixed deduction, it is **5% of
3.80**. The station lost a twentieth of whatever was there, so a herd recorded at 4.56 was short
0.24 and a herd recorded at 3.42 was short 0.18.

**(c) 76,000 pounds of butterfat.**

Over the twenty-five full delivery years 1898–1922 the station received 40,000,000 lb of milk
(1,600,000 lb a year):

    credited: 40,000,000 × 3.61% = 1,444,000 lb
    true:     40,000,000 × 3.80% = 1,520,000 lb
    not credited: 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000 lb

**(d) $19,000, of which the patrons received one fifth.**

    76,000 lb × $0.25 (average price of butterfat over the period) = $19,000
    award $3,800 ÷ $19,000 = 0.20 — one fifth

### C2. Emmet Brant, the station's largest patron, 1910.

Delivered 200,000 lb even, by his own scale and the station's agreeing; paid on a test of 3.42.
Butterfat that year was 24 cents.

    (a) credited: 200,000 × 3.42% = 6,840 lb
    (b) true test: 3.42 ÷ 0.95 = 3.60; true fat: 200,000 × 3.60% = 7,200 lb
        owed and not credited: 7,200 − 6,840 = 360 lb
    (c) 360 lb × $0.24 = $86.40

(At the period average of 25 cents it would be $90.00; I use 24 cents because Roy Brant gives that
as the price of that particular year.)

### C3.

    (a) credited to patrons on the station test: 1,444,000 lb
    (b) received at Larrow Green in the station's cream: 1,520,000 lb
    (c) difference 76,000 lb

Be exact about the base:

    76,000 ÷ 1,520,000 = 5.00%  — of the fat actually delivered
    76,000 ÷ 1,444,000 = 5.26%  — of the fat credited ("five and a quarter per cent")

Both are right of their own denominator and of no other. The ledger's standing phrase "five and a
quarter per cent, as usual" is a percentage **of the credited figure**, because a bookkeeper
naturally reckons a surplus against the figure he has already entered. Teague and Vaught both
describe that 5.25% as a percentage of the fat actually delivered; both are wrong, and the same
slip in two places. **Five per cent is the figure that is also the glass** — 1 − 0.95 — which is
why the two denominators matter. Check against the 1898 entry: 3,040 ÷ 57,760 = 5.26%; 3,040 ÷
60,800 = 5.00%.

### C4.

    (a) $77.93 — and the line items foot exactly:
        25.20 + 31.68 + 0.90 + 18.00 + 2.15 = 77.93
    (b) 6 doz. = 72 pipettes, at $0.35 each = $25.20.
        Each short 17.6 − 16.72 = 0.88 c.c.

(The invoice's own arithmetic settles two disputes: "$77.39" cannot be right because the columns
add to 77.93, and "five dozen" cannot be right because 60 × 0.35 = $21.00 would not foot.)

### C5.

**(a) The glass was in use 26 years and about six weeks; the money runs over 25 full delivery
years.**

- In use: **1 May 1897 to 12 June 1923** — 26 years, 1 month, 12 days.
- Money calculation: **1898 to 1922** — 25 full delivery years, 40,000,000 lb.

They are different numbers because the two end years are partial and unusable. 1897 is entered in
the test book as "part year" — the station ran from January on the sound borrowed glass and only
from 1 May on its own — and 1923 was cut in half by the condemnation on 12 June, with new glass
from July. The ledger's annual sheets and the test book's annual averages support whole years
only, so the claim was framed on the twenty-five clean ones. The loss over the two part-years is
real and simply was never computed. (1896, the first season, was entirely on sound Larrow Green
glass and carries no loss at all.)

**(b) Cannot be determined from the sources.** The curator states it plainly: the station's intake
for 4 May to the end of 1896 is not in the ledgers — the station column begins with 1897 — and is
nowhere else, and inquirers wanting a figure for 1896 must be told there is none. Two men offer
one from memory and they are far apart: Hobart Sill says "about nine hundred thousand pounds" and
Bertram Kyle says "a good eleven hundred thousand," each recalling at forty and fifty-five years'
distance, and Kyle explicitly adds "it is not in any book I ever ruled." They are independent of
each other, which is exactly why their disagreement cannot be resolved: there is no document to
break the tie. Declining.

## Section D — Logic

### D1. The nine weeks of 1911.

**When and how long:** 8 May to 10 July 1911 — nine weeks, dated in the test book on both ends by
the operator on the day. (Teague says six weeks and Kepp says six weeks; the two dated entries
say otherwise, and 8 May to 10 July is nine weeks and a day.)

**The variable:** one object — the station's own milk pipette, broken on 8 May, replaced by a
borrowed measure, restored on 10 July.

**Held constant:** the same patrons, the same herds, the same pasture and feed, the same season
running through it, the same acid, the same test bottles, the same hand tester, the same building,
the same operator, the same habits of work, the same supervision (none).

**Result:** the station's average rose from 3.61 to 3.80, held there nine weeks, and fell back to
3.61 **on the day** the station's own measure returned. Every farm on the Bottom rose and fell
together.

**What it establishes that a calculation cannot:** the 1958 measurement shows only that the glass
recovered from the crate *would* produce a 5% error. The 1911 interval shows that the station's
actual figures *did* move when the glass moved, in real time, and moved back on demand — an
intervention with a restoration, which is stronger than any correlation. And because nothing but
the glass changed, it kills every explanation located in the herds, the ground, the feed, the
season or the men at a stroke: none of those was altered and the figures were. It also has the
rare virtue of being recorded before anyone had a theory, by a man who did not know what he was
recording and set a question mark against it — and against his own interest, since the rise was
evidence that his own instrument was wrong.

**What it does not establish:** it does not say *what* was wrong with the station's pipettes or by
how much they were short — nothing was measured in 1911, and no burette existed in the county
until 1921. It does not prove the borrowed measure was itself accurate; it proves only that the
two differed. (That 3.80 is the true figure comes from elsewhere: the 1896 season on Larrow Green
glass, the sixteen Alder Corners summers, and 1924 on new glass.) It does not establish that the
fault ran unchanged for twenty-six years — that comes from the flat 3.61 in the test book and the
flat 5.25% in the ledger. And by itself it does not formally exclude a human act, since one could
imagine a man behaving differently for nine weeks; it takes the ledger's heavy cream to close
that door.

### D2. Why the tester cannot have been stealing cream.

**(i) The cream arrived heavy — every year, in the Association's own books.** The station-gain
line records more butterfat reaching Larrow Green in the station's cream than had been credited to
patrons at the station: 60,800 lb against 57,760 lb in 1898, and about 5.25% surplus every year
after. Stolen cream arrives *light*. Dell Rundle, shown those ledgers on another errand while his
pamphlet was in press, saw the point himself and retracted his chapter: "Cream stolen at the
Hollow would have reached the Green light. It reached the Green heavy, every year, and I cannot
make my paragraph stand."

**(ii) The nine weeks of 1911.** The same man, in the same building, under no different
supervision, with only a piece of glass changed — and the figures rose, and fell when it was
changed back. A thief does not steal less because he is using a borrowed pipette. And it was the
tester himself who wrote the rise into the book.

**(iii) The milk tested right on somebody else's bench.** For sixteen summers the same four Hessel
Bottom herds ran 3.78–3.82 at Alder Corners, over the ridge in another county, on Jerome Cudd's
own glass — a man who never compared a figure with anyone, had no interest in Ordell County, and
never docked one of those four men a cent. That tests the *milk*, not the station, and shows the
milk was never poor.

**(iv, spare) Hobart Sill's independent weighing.** He weighed the Hollow's cream into the churn
room himself, over the platform man's figure, for forty-one years, and it always ran heavy — a
physical observation seven miles from the station by a man who believed the opposite conclusion.

**Why these are independent:** (i) is documentary and lives at the receiving end and in the office
ledger; (ii) is a within-station time series recorded in 1911 by a man with no theory; (iii) comes
from a different county, a different operator, a different set of glassware and a witness who had
never heard the Ordell arithmetic; (iv) is a separate physical weighing by a hostile witness. None
of them depends on the 1958 measurement, and none is derived from any of the others.

**One that holds with no 1958 measurement at all:** (i). The ledger arithmetic refutes theft on its
own face and did so from 1898 onward. So does (ii). So does (iii).

**Did the bookkeeper ever question those entries?** No. Wilbur Teague wrote them himself for forty
years, wrote "as usual" into them, and admits what evenness does: "A figure that jumps about will
keep you awake. A figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture." He raised
the account with Vose once, in 1916, was told to let it alone, and never raised it again — "I was
the Association's bookkeeper and not its conscience." And at the 1925 hearing he advanced the
cream-theft insinuation while the entry in his own hand refuted it, and had still not reconciled
the two when he wrote his memoir in 1948, where he reprints the entry and repeats the suspicion on
the same page.

### D3. Did Selby Vose know, before 1923, that the station's tests ran low?

**Yes — that they ran low, certainly. That they were *wrong*, no; and that distinction is the
whole of his defence and the whole of his failure.**

He knew the tests ran low because (a) the complaint had been before the Association since 1902 and
was renewed in every decade — the 1908 committee was appointed on it and its report crossed his
predecessor's desk and then his; (b) he wrote and signed the 1919 circular, whose entire premise
is that Ostrey Hollow's figures had been going one way for twenty years, and he said so himself
from the platform in 1926: "A manager who sees his figures going one way for twenty years and says
nothing to the men who make them is not being kind to them"; (c) Teague put the station-gain
account to him in 1916 and was told to let it alone — which is knowledge of the specific ledger
line, not merely of the grievance; and (d) he signed the annual sheet carrying that line every
spring from 1913, and Teague's inference is that a man who signs a sheet has had the sheet in
front of him.

Point (d) is the weakest and should not be leaned on: Kyle, who sat outside the door and watched
him sign a thousand sheets, says Vose read totals and never read the lines above them — "he would
take a sheet, turn it, put his thumb on the bottom figure, and sign." So the signature proves the
paper was in front of him, not that he read it. But (b) and (c) do not depend on his reading
habits at all. The 1916 exchange is decisive: he was told, in terms, that the station account was
running an unexplained annual surplus, and his answer was to let it alone.

My inference, stated as inference: he did not know the cause, and he had a preferred explanation —
water — that put the fault on the patrons and cost the Association nothing, while any other
explanation would have cost it the surplus he was carrying to the general account. 1916 is the
point at which his ignorance stopped being innocent and became a choice not to inquire.

## Section E — Contradictions

Method used throughout, in order of precedence: **(1)** a document quoted verbatim beats a memory
of it; **(2)** arithmetic that closes beats arithmetic that does not; **(3)** a narrator's
internal consistency, especially where a figure convicts itself; **(4)** proximity — a participant
over a hearer, a contemporaneous entry over a recollection; **(5)** independence, not numbers —
two narrators repeating one rumour are one source.

### Cases where TWO NARRATORS CARRY THE SAME WRONG VALUE

1. **Station average "3.16"** — Wilbur Teague (r05) and Alonzo Frick (r13). Correct: **3.61**.
   *Tie-breaker:* the test book's own summary leaf, quoted verbatim by three separate holders, and
   arithmetic — 40,000,000 × 3.61% = 1,444,000, which with 3.80 yields exactly the 76,000 lb and
   the 5.25%/5% pair standing in the 1898 ledger entry. 3.16 reproduces nothing. It is also a
   digit transposition. *Not independent:* Frick took his figures from the hearing, where the
   Association's bookkeeper supplied them.

2. **Mean delivery "16.27 c.c."** — Vaught's Bulletin 471 body text (r11) and the *Ashlin
   Chronicle* (r19). Correct: **16.72**. *Tie-breaker:* the signed measurement sheet of 9 March
   1958 lists the nine individual runs (16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74,
   16.71) whose mean is 16.72 — no single reading is anywhere near 16.27. And 16.72/17.6 = 0.95
   exactly, reproducing 3.61→3.80 and 76,000 lb; 16.27/17.6 = 0.9244 gives 3.905 and 118,000 lb,
   which Vaught himself flags as "not readily reconciled with the station's own average of 3.80 in
   the year following," and which contradicts **his own concluding paragraph** ("low by one part
   in twenty… seventy-six thousand pounds"). *Not independent:* the paper worked from the college
   press release, i.e. from the same corrupted text.

3. **Condemnation dated "12 July 1923"** — Selby Vose (r08) and Dell Rundle (r12). Correct: **12
   June 1923**. *Tie-breaker:* Strawn's own dated field entry, photostatted; and a decisive
   chronology — the Association ordered replacements on 28 June and Ammon & Sons answered on 2
   July. A 12 July condemnation would fall after both the order and the reply.

4. **"Forty-four million pounds"** — Teague (r05) and Rundle (r12). Correct: **40,000,000**.

   which nobody reports. Rundle saw the ledgers once, on another errand.

5. **"Sixty-seven thousand pounds"** — Frick (r13) and Cleve Oram (r21). Correct: **76,000**.
   *Tie-breaker:* 1,520,000 − 1,444,000 = 76,000, and Bulletin 471's conclusion. Another
   transposition. *Not independent:* Oram is repeating "what they made it" after 1958.

6. **"$19,600"** — Rundle (r12) and the *Chronicle* (r19). Correct: **$19,000** (76,000 × $0.25).
   *Tie-breaker:* arithmetic. Rundle's is anachronistic besides — he attributes a 1958 figure to

8. **Bulletin 214 read as "two tenths"** — Nyle Grigg (r15) and Dr. Kepp (r16). Correct: **"not

   This is the most consequential of the shared errors: it is the entire evidential basis of the

9. **Award "$3,500"** — Vira Toland (r01) and Cleve Oram (r21). Correct: **$3,800**.
   *Tie-breaker:* the award text in the minute book, quoted verbatim by the secretary who recorded

10. **Selby Vose as Gideon's "nephew"** — Vira Toland (r01) and Rundle (r12). Correct: **son**,
    on Selby's own words from the platform, plus Loomis, Kyle and Duncan Keddie.

17. **Vaught (r11).** Section III gives five dozen pipettes, a mean of 16.27 and glass in use from

19. **Teague (r05).** He says the station-gain line "was my own device; I began it when I came in

    the curator confirms the station column runs from 1897. *Resolution:* he inherited the line

## Section F — Theory

### F1. What actually produced the shortfall.

The station's own milk pipettes, bought new from Ammon & Sons of Tarnet on 14 April 1897 and put
into service on 1 May 1897, were short. They were blown on the stem at 17.6 c.c. and delivered a
mean of 16.72 — **ninety-five hundredths of the mark, one part in twenty short**. In this test the
operator weighs nothing: he draws a measured charge into a pipette, delivers it into a bottle whose
graduated neck is ruled to read percent *for that charge and no other*, adds acid, spins, and reads
the column of fat directly as a percentage. Put in a short charge and less fat is liberated, a
shorter column stands in the neck, and the neck — which knows nothing of the substitution — is read
off in the ordinary way. The reading is low **in the exact proportion by which the charge was
short**.

*Why that size:* 5%, because the glass was 5% short, and the glass was 5% short because one lot
came off one reground mold and every piece in the crate carried the same error. Being proportional
rather than additive, it removed a twentieth of whatever was there — 0.19 of a point at a true
3.80, more from rich milk and less from poor, so that a man's best cows cost him the most and no
one could see it. Being a lot error rather than a wear or leak error, it was *precise*: the same
every time, in January and July, for twenty-six years, for a careful operator and a careless one.
Scatter attracts attention; this had the appearance of excellent work, and the station's herds
still ranked correctly against one another. Only against the outside world was it wrong.

*Why nobody caught it:* every other element can be checked against a standard — scales against
test weights, acid for strength, the tester's speed by counting, the bottle's graduation with
mercury. The pipette cannot, because in the practice of the trade **the pipette is the standard**.
It is what the plant measures with, and an instrument that is itself the measure cannot be caught
by measuring with it. It can be caught only by a burette brought in from outside, and the state
did not issue burettes to its field men until 1921.

*Why it began when it did:* on 1 May 1897 the station stopped borrowing Larrow Green's sound glass
and began using its own. The 1896 season, on the borrowed glass, averaged 3.80; from 1898 the
average was 3.61 and stayed there.

*Why it ended when it did:* on 12 June 1923 Merle Strawn ran those pipettes against the burette he
had carried two seasons, found they would not deliver their mark, condemned the whole crate, and
told the manager to order new. New glass was in use from July; he checked it in October and it
delivered; the 1924 average was 3.80.

*Why nothing had to change at either end:* because nothing at the station or on the farms was ever
wrong. Same cows, same wet meadow, same feed, same scales (true at every inspection), same
lactometer readings (no added water, ever), same acid, same bottles, same operator, same book. One
object changed — in 1897, for nine weeks in 1911, and again in 1923 — and the figures moved with
it every time and by the same amount.

### The three competing explanations, and what defeats each.

**1. Water in the patrons' cans.** Advanced by **Selby Vose**, manager: circulated as a printed
warning in 1919, urged before the board in 1925, and defended from the platform in 1926; endorsed
independently by **Hobart Sill** ("you cannot tell me nine families all got honest in one summer").
*Defeated by:* Strawn's lactometer, applied at every visit from 1907 onward, never once finding
added water at that station — recorded in his own field books at the time, and he told the manager
so in 1919. By Rosalie Cudd: watered milk cannot be hidden from a cheesemaker, because the curd
comes soft and will not knit, and in sixteen summers her father never docked one of those four men
a cent. By the shape of the thing: a gap identical in every month of every year for twenty-six
years is not nine families' intermittent dishonesty. By the 1911 nine weeks, when nothing was
asked of the patrons and the figures rose anyway. And, fatally, by Vose's own best argument turned
around: he says the tests rose "when the looking began," but the glass was condemned on 12 June
1923 and replaced in July — eighteen months **before** the petition of January 1925. The rise
preceded the scrutiny. He misdates the condemnation to 12 July 1923 and inflates the 1924 average
to 3.90; correcting either destroys his case.

**2. The tester taking cream and writing low tests to cover it.** Insinuated at the hearing by
**Wilbur Teague**, the bookkeeper, in the manner of a man who wants a thing said without saying it,
and then declining to charge anybody; held as settled opinion by the valley, by Vira Toland, by
Cleve Oram's father the cream hauler, and printed as fact by Dell Rundle. *Defeated by:* the
station-gain entry in Teague's own hand — more fat arrived at Larrow Green than had been credited
at the station, every year, 60,800 lb against 57,760 lb in 1898 and about 5.25% thereafter. Stolen
cream arrives light; this arrived heavy. Rundle saw the entry while his pamphlet was in press and
retracted his own chapter on the spot. Also defeated by Sill's independent re-weighing at the cold
room, by the 1911 interval (same man, figures rose), and by the fact that Emmet Brant's own barn
scale and the station's agreed to a pound or two for thirty years, so nothing was going astray at
the weighing either.

**3. The wet hay of Hessel Bottom thinning the cream.** Advanced by the **1908 committee under Nyle
Grigg**, reissued by Grigg himself in 1919, and elaborated into a soil-and-mineral theory by **Dr.
Roland Kepp** in 1926; conceded as "a real if partial factor" by the county agent in his first
draft of 1959. *Defeated by:* the College's own Bulletin No. 214 (1911) — bog hay as the whole of
the roughage depressed the test by **not more than five hundredths in winter and by nothing
measurable on summer pasture**. That is a quarter the size of the gap and the wrong shape entirely,
since the gap stood flat in July as in February. Grigg and Kepp both quote that bulletin as finding
"two tenths," a fourfold inflation, and the whole weight of their case rests on the misquotation.
Defeated further by the Alder Corners figures — the same milk, off the same wet ground, in the same
summers, running 3.78 to 3.82 for sixteen seasons on the other side of the ridge; by the 1911 nine
weeks, in which the hay did not change and the figures did; and by the 1908 committee's own design,
which sampled January to March only and therefore could not see the one feature that damns the
hypothesis. Falke, on obtaining the bulletin entire, withdrew his concession in a note at the foot
of his own report — the only narrator in the collection who corrects himself twice on the page.

### Subsidiary questions.

**(a) Did the 1925 board ever examine the condemned glassware? No.** The award's phrase "having had
before it the books and appliances of the station" was, as the secretary who wrote it down says
plainly, a form of words the board used and not a list of anything; the crate was never mentioned
in any of the three sessions, by the patrons, by the officers, or by the board. The exhibits were
four books. The board did examine the *appliances then in use* — the new glass, sound since July
1923 — and watched a test made with it, which if anything reassured them. Two ironies stand. The
condemned crate lay in the loft over the intake room, twenty feet up a ladder, and two of the three
sessions sat in the room directly beneath it. And Exhibit 4 was Strawn's circuit notebook for 1923,
which contains the entry *These measures will not deliver their mark* — so the answer was in that
room, in writing, and nobody, as Kyle put it, laid the second book beside the fourth and looked.

**(b) What did the tester himself believe?** Publicly, nothing: "I cannot tell you why the figures
are what they are," said to the board and repeated in his unsent letter, and a question mark set
against the July 1911 entry and left standing fourteen years — "I will not put anything in its
place that I do not know." Privately, he believed it was the glass, and had believed it since 1911:
his son Duncan remembered him coming home that summer saying it was something in the glass, and
saying it twice in one evening, which was not his way. He would not write down what he could not
prove, and there was no instrument in the county capable of proving it until 1921. Frick's
recollection that Keddie privately blamed the patrons is uncorroborated, twenty-one years after the
fact, and contradicted by everything Keddie wrote. He suspected the right thing, could not
demonstrate it, and was ruined by the figures he honestly recorded.

## Section G — Summary

**G1.** (147 words)

A valley cooperative paid its farmers for butterfat, and its by-law made the test at each man's own
station final, with no appeal. At the Ostrey Hollow skimming station the pipettes bought in 1897
were short by one part in twenty, so every test read 95% of the truth — 3.61 instead of 3.80 — for
twenty-six years. Nine families were suspected of watering; the tester was suspected of stealing
cream; the wet meadow was blamed. All three were false, and the missing 76,000 pounds of fat sat in
the Association's own ledgers under a name, "station gain." A state inspector condemned the glass
in 1923 without looking back; a board paid $3,800 in 1925 and dismissed the tester. In 1958 his
granddaughter climbed a ladder and measured the crate. Eight records were needed because no single
one held the ledger, the diary, the field book, the cheesemaker's figures and the glass together.
