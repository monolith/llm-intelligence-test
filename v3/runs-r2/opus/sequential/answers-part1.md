# Answers — Part 1

## Section A — Reconstruction (stories 1–4)

---

### Story 1. The Association, and the Article That Made the Test the Master

In 1889 the farmers of the Larrow Valley, in Ordell County, met at Larrow Green — in the
schoolhouse, in cold weather, and it went on very late — and organized the Larrow Valley
Cooperative Creamery Association. They were tired of hauling butter to Ashlin and taking what
the merchant allowed them. Gideon Vose was elected first president and held the office
twenty-two years, until 1911; he died in 1917. In 1891 the creamery was built at Larrow Green
on Larrow Creek — the same building still standing fifty years later — and the valley began to
be paid in cash once a month, which had never happened to it before. Hobart Sill came in as
buttermaker in 1893 and stood at that churn forty-one years.

Gideon Vose wrote the by-laws himself, at his own kitchen table, not being a lawyer. The
seventh article governs everything that follows:

> *Article VII. Every patron shall be paid for the milk he delivers according to the number of
> pounds of butter fat contained in it, ascertained by test, and not according to the weight of
> the milk; and the test of the station where he delivers shall govern.*

Before it, a hundred pounds of milk was a hundred pounds of milk whosever cow it came from.
After it, a man was paid for the fat found in his milk by a test made where he delivered. It is
a just rule and nobody in the valley ever seriously argued otherwise. But the last clause is the
one nobody quotes: *the test of the station where he delivers shall govern* — not the creamery's
test, not the valley's average, not a test made over again anywhere else, and no appeal from it.
That clause made a single figure, produced by a single instrument in a single room, the whole of
the bargain, and left a dissatisfied man no remedy on earth except to quarrel with it.

Article VII was adopted at the annual meeting of 1892, the year after the creamery opened. I take
that from Effie Loomis, secretary 1901–1940, writing from the minute book. Dell Rundle says 1891
and Nyle Grigg says 1893; the printed by-laws carry no year, only "adopted at the annual meeting,"
and the county agent in 1959 honestly declined to state one. Loomis had the minutes in front of
her; the others had the store and their memories. The 1889–1892 minute book was lost when the
office was moved in 1938 — carried out with the rest and never arriving — so the year cannot now
be proved from the record itself.

### Story 2. The Station, and the Crate from Tarnet

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association built a skimming station seven miles up the valley at
Ostrey Hollow, because the pull down to Larrow Green was three hours in mud and impossible in
March. Milk was weighed in at the intake, a sample drawn from each patron's cans, the sample
tested for butterfat, the milk separated, the cream hauled down to the creamery, and the skim
handed back the same morning for the hogs and calves. The station opened on 4 May 1896. Ansel
Keddie, born 1861, was its tester and separator man from the opening; he went up in 1895 to get
the building ready and served twenty-nine seasons, 1896 through 1924. He kept the test book in
his own hand in five columns — date, patron, pounds, test, fat — crossed his errors and initialled
the crossing, and never wrote a figure over a figure.

The station had no glassware of its own for its first season. Hobart Sill made up an outfit out of
his own bench glass at Larrow Green and sent it up the valley on the wagon so the station could
open on time. The test book's first leaf records it: *"Begun this 4th day of May, 1896. Glass
borrowed of Larrow Green until our own comes."* That first season, on borrowed and sound glass,
the station's average test was **3.80**.

On 14 April 1897 Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, of Tarnet, invoiced the Association for the station's
own outfit:

> 6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20; 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at
> .22 — 31.68; 1 acid measure — .90; 1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00; Freight — 2.15. **Total —
> 77.93.**

The glass came up the valley in a crate and went into use on **1 May 1897**. Orra Keddie wrote
that day that her husband was pleased: they were done borrowing of Larrow Green. From 1898 the
station's average test was **3.61**, and it stayed at 3.61 or within three hundredths of it every
year afterwards.

Seventy-two pipettes had come in that crate, every one of them blown on the stem at 17.6 c.c.
Every one of them delivered about 16.72. The firm's own historian, writing in 1949, reconstructed
how: a mold reground, a gauge knocked off its nail, a lot running short, and a boy checking the
first, the tenth and the fiftieth and finding them consistent — consistently wrong, which is the
one fault a check of that kind cannot find. The works books that might have proved it burned in
1931.

### Story 3. Twenty Years of Grievance, and the Hay That Was Blamed

From 1902 the men of Ostrey Hollow said out loud that their milk tested under the milk of the rest
of the valley. Emmet Brant of Hessel Bottom was among the first, and they were laughed at.
Hessel Bottom is a hundred and sixty acres of wet meadow under the hamlet; six farms had rights
on it, cut hay and pastured stock there, and Emmet Brant's was the largest — sixty-odd acres and
thirty-eight cows. The hay is sedge and rush, cut late, and it is the winter dependence of those
farms.

In January 1908 the Association appointed a committee under Nyle Grigg of Larrow Green, who was
not a patron of the station and had no interest in the answer. The committee visited each of the
six farms, drew and sealed its own samples, watched the tests made, tried the station scales
against a known weight and the patrons' scales against the station's, and reported: the milk is
whole, the scales are honest, no dishonesty of any kind in any quarter — and it is the wet hay of
that bottom that thins the cream, and the remedy lies with the land and not with the station. That
report closed the question for eleven years.

Two things defeat it, and both were available. First, the committee sampled only January, February
and March — Grigg's own report says so, though his 1919 covering letter claimed they sampled every
month of the year. A winter-only sample cannot detect an error that is the same size in July as in
February, and the shape of this error was the whole clue: it stood flat, wet years and dry, on
pasture and on hay. Second, the State Agricultural College at Farland ran a controlled feeding
trial on bog hay and published it in 1911 as Bulletin No. 214: *bog hay fed as the whole of the
roughage depressed the butter fat test by not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter,
and by nothing that could be measured on summer pasture.* Grigg in 1919 and Dr. Roland Kepp in his
1926 farm-club paper both cited that bulletin as finding **two tenths**, and built their case on
the inflated figure. It is four times too small and the wrong shape.

There was also an outside check nobody in Ordell County ever thought to make. From 1915, four of
the six Hessel Bottom men hauled over Redlow Ridge in the summer flush to Jerome Cudd's Alder
Corners Cheese Company in Vessey County, opened 1913, and back to their own station the rest of
the year — sixteen seasons, ending 1930. Cudd tested on his own bench with his own glass and never
compared a figure with anybody. His season averages for those four men, taken together, sat
between 3.78 and 3.82 every single year: about **3.80**. The same milk, off the same wet ground,
in the same summers.

### Story 4. The Nine Weeks of 1911

On the morning of 8 May 1911 the station's own measuring pipette broke. Orra Keddie's diary has
her husband home cross about it that evening, and rain all day. Keddie made shift with a borrowed
measure — most probably one sent up from Larrow Green, as in 1896; Roy Brant remembered it as the
state inspector's, but the inspector did not come through until June and the change dates from 8
May, so that memory is excluded by the calendar.

From that morning the station's tests came up and stood at 3.80. They held there for nine weeks.
On 10 July the station's own measure came back into use and the tests went down again the same
day. Keddie entered both facts as they happened:

> *May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure.*
>
> *July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.*

He let that question mark stand for fourteen years and would not put anything in its place that
he did not know. But he came home that summer saying it was something in the glass — his son
Duncan, then seventeen, remembered him saying it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was
not his way. Against a suspicion about a piece of glass stood the outfit he had been taught to
trust and the practice of every station in the county, and there was no instrument in the county
that could have settled it: the state did not begin issuing burettes to its field men until 1921.
So he kept on, and wrote down every figure the glass gave him for another fourteen years, and the
figures were what convicted him.

It was an experiment that ran itself, and nobody knew it was one. The patrons were the same, the
herds the same, the pasture, the season, the feed, the acid, the bottles, the hand tester, the
operator and his habits all the same. **One object changed**, and changed back, and the average
moved with it both times. Every farm on the Bottom saw its tests rise together and fall together.
Emmet Brant noticed the rise and noticed the fall and entered both in his weigh book and could
make nothing of them.

Two witnesses stood inside those nine weeks without seeing them. Merle Strawn, the state's dairy
inspector, visited Ostrey Hollow in June 1911, watched a test through and wrote: *Nothing here to
note.* And the College published Bulletin 214 that same year, disposing of the hay, while the
station itself was quietly disposing of it too. The interval lay unread for forty-seven years.
