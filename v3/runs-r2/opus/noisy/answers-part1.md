# Answer sheet — Part 1 of 3

**Section A — Reconstruction, stories 1–4**

Partition chosen: the twenty-four retellings resolve into eight strands, each with its own cast,
its own span of years, and its own documentary base. I have cut them where the documents cut
them, not where the calendar does: (1) the founding and the by-law; (2) the glass, from the
supplier's floor to the loft; (3) the tester's working life and household; (4) the complaint and
the two committees that answered it wrongly; (5) the nine weeks of 1911; (6) the 1919 circular
and the family it broke; (7) the burette, the board and the dismissal; (8) the ledger line that
told the truth every year, and the woman who finally read it.

---

## 1. The Hinge Clause — the Association, its by-law, and the station it built (1889–1896)

In 1889 the farmers of the Larrow Valley met in the schoolhouse at Larrow Green in cold weather.
The meeting ran very late and somebody's horse got loose in the middle of it. They were tired of
hauling butter to Ashlin and taking what the merchant allowed them, and they organized the Larrow
Valley Cooperative Creamery Association. **Gideon Vose** was elected first president and held the
office until 1911 — twenty-two years, longer than any other man. He was heavy, quiet and obstinate,
had once thought of the law and thought better of it, and wrote the by-laws himself at his own
kitchen table in a hand the Association's clerks were still complaining about thirty years later.

The creamery was built at Larrow Green on the creek below the mill in **1891**, and the valley
began to be paid in cash once a month. **Hobart Sill** came to the churn in 1893 and stayed
forty-one years.

The by-law that matters is **Article VII**: every patron is paid for the pounds of butterfat his
milk is found by test to contain, not for the weight of his cans — *and the test of the station
where he delivers shall govern*. Effie Loomis, Rundle and Frick all fasten on that last clause and
they are right to: no re-test elsewhere, no valley average, no appeal. It made a figure the whole
of the bargain and left a dissatisfied man with no remedy but to quarrel with it. Under payment by
weight nothing that followed would have cost any man a dollar.

**The year of adoption cannot be determined.** The printed copies carry no date, only "adopted at
the annual meeting"; the minute book of 1889–1892 was lost in an office removal in 1938. Effie
Loomis says 1892, Rundle 1891, Grigg 1893 — and Falke, the county agent, declined in 1959 to state
a year at all, which is the correct answer.

In the winter of 1895–96 the Association put up a **skimming station at Ostrey Hollow**, seven
miles up the valley, because the pull down was three hours in mud and could not be done at all in
March. Ansel Keddie went up in 1895 to get it ready. The station opened **4 May 1896** on glass
borrowed from Larrow Green — Sill's own, made up at his own bench and sent up on the wagon,
returned the spring after with one piece chipped. That first season it took in something like nine
hundred thousand pounds of milk and averaged **3.80**, which was the truth about that valley's milk
and the last true figure the station produced for twenty-six years.

*Documents:* the by-laws pamphlet (Nym Item 1); Effie Loomis's *Notes*; Rundle's jubilee pamphlet;
Sill's remarks of 1934; Kyle's clerkship; Falke's 1959 report; the test book's first leaf.

## 2. The Short Lot — Ammon & Sons of Tarnet, and a mold that had been reground (1897–1923)

Ammon & Sons, Dairy Supply, filled four stories behind the railroad yard at Tarnet and supplied
half the glass in the state: bottles, pipettes, acid measures, burettes. A milk test pipette must
*deliver* a stated quantity, not hold it, which is the harder measurement, because milk clings to
the wall as it runs out. The mark goes on the stem while the glass is warm; the piece anneals
overnight, is gauged in the morning, and is packed or thrown in the cullet barrel.

On **14 April 1897** the house billed the Larrow Valley Association for the Ostrey Hollow station:
6 doz. milk test pipettes, 17.6 c.c., at .35 — 25.20; 12 doz. test bottles, 8 per cent neck, at
.22 — 31.68; 1 acid measure — .90; 1 hand tester, 4 bottle — 18.00; freight — 2.15; **total 77.93**.
Seventy-two pipettes. They went into use at the station on **1 May 1897** — fixed by the invoice, by
Nym's accession note, by Bulletin 471's conclusion, and decisively by Orra Keddie's diary entry of
that very date: "A. says the station's own glass came up and they are done borrowing of Larrow
Green."

Every one of them was short. The nine measured in 1958 delivered a mean of **16.72 c.c.** against a
17.6 mark — 0.88 c.c. short, **95 percent, one part in twenty** — and they were short consistently,
within four hundredths of each other across the lot.

Junia Ammon, the firm's own historian, reconstructed it in 1949 and printed it against herself:
the mold had been reground and the lot ran short; a gauge gets knocked off its nail and hung back;
"a boy checks the first of a lot and the tenth and the fiftieth and finds them consistent, because
they are consistent — they are consistently wrong, which is the one fault a check of that kind
cannot find." The works books that would have settled it burned in the fire of 1931.

When the Association wrote on **28 June 1923** for replacement glass, the firm's reply of **2 July**
entered the order, promised shipment that month, regretted the trouble, and said its books of the
nineties would tell nothing. Junia Ammon calls that letter "a thin thing to send to a customer of
twenty-six years' standing" and says the letter, not the accident, is what she regrets.

Two errors attach here. The *Ashlin Chronicle* in 1958 called the firm's silence proof that "a
cheap short lot was put off on the Association knowingly" — an inference no source supports.
And r20 contradicts itself twice on its own pages: it says the house sold nothing into Ordell
County before 1900, facing an 1897 invoice to Ordell County, and reads its own total as
"seventy-seven dollars and thirty-nine cents."

*Documents:* the Ammon invoice (Nym Item 2, reproduced in facsimile in r20); the letter of 2 July
1923 (Nym Item 10, reproduced entire in r20); Ivy Keddie's measurement sheet; Orra Keddie's diary.

## 3. Twenty-Nine Seasons — Ansel Keddie at the bench, and the house above the station (1896–1934)

**Ansel Keddie**, born 1861, married **Orra Brant**, born 1866, in 1889; their son **Duncan** was
born in 1894. Ansel was tester and separator man at Ostrey Hollow from the opening until they put
him out — twenty-nine seasons, 1896 through 1924, thirty-one tests a week.

His day began at half past five. Wagons came up in the dark most of the year; the intake room was
open, the lamps lit and the scale swinging free before the first one reached the door. Every can
was weighed and the weight called and written before the milk went anywhere else. A sample from
each patron went into a jar numbered to him and nobody else; the composite was made up over ten
days. On testing morning the jar was warmed blood-warm right through and poured vessel to vessel
until the risen cream was carried down through the whole of it. Then the charge: milk drawn to the
mark on the stem, the tip wiped, the charge blown into the bottle carrying the patron's number.
Acid down the side, the bottle turned till the milk went dark, four bottles in the hand tester
weighted against each other, cover down, the crank turned at the speed he was taught and no
faster. The fat rose into the ruled neck and he read it off with dividers.

The book ran in five columns — date, patron, pounds, test, fat — from **4 May 1896 to 31 December
1924**, one hand throughout. Errors were crossed through with his letters against the crossing,
never written over: "a figure written over another figure is a figure no man can swear to
afterward."

Orra's diary gives the house. Ground frozen a spade down in April; the path to the station broken
himself at four o'clock at twenty below; strawberries thick along the station road in July.
March 1902, when the store talk began: "A. came home and would not eat. I said it is not your cows
and not your hands, and he said no, but it is my figures they will look at." He was up before four
every day of his life and never once said he was tired in her hearing and never once was late.

Roy Brant put the fact nobody could use: the man who kept the test book was married into the
family that had more to lose by that station than any other on the meadow. Cleve Oram remembered
him at the bench — a long dry man, canvas apron with the strap over one shoulder, a bottle held up
to the window with his arm straight out.

After 1925 he kept four or five cows on ground too small for them and did carpentry; he hung a
barn door for the Orams that swung true for thirty years. Orra, October 1927: "A carpenter at
sixty-six. He is better at it than he ever was at the other and it pays a quarter of what the
other did." He died at home in **February 1934**. More came to the burying than she looked for, and
men who had not spoken to him in nine years shook her hand and every one of them said what a hand
he was with a door, and not one said the other thing. She died in **1948**.

*Documents:* Keddie's unsent letter of 1925; the station test book; Orra Keddie's diary; Duncan
Keddie's family notes; Strawn's circuit books; Frick; Oram.

## 4. The Complaint and the Committees — the wrong answer, twice, in print (1902–1926)

The complaints began in **1902**, when it was first said down at Larrow Green that Ostrey Hollow
milk did not test with the rest. Emmet Brant was one of the first to say it out loud and was
laughed at. Sill remembered a man in the churn room shouting that his milk tested under his
brother-in-law's down the creek on the same feed and the same water.

In **January 1908** the board appointed an investigating committee under **Nyle Grigg** of Larrow
Green, who hauled to the creamery and was not a station patron. Orra Keddie watched them come:
"Four men up the road today in Grigg's sleigh… They went to my brother Emmet's first, being the
biggest, and he gave them their dinner… They wanted to see the barns and not the station."

Their method was thorough and exactly wrong. They visited each of the **six** farms on Hessel
Bottom, some three and four times; drew and sealed their own samples; watched the herds milked,
the cans filled, the hauling and the weighing in at the intake; stood by while the test was made;
tried the station scales against a known weight and the patrons' own scales against the station's;
and inquired into every barn's water supply. **They checked everything in that building except the
pipette** — and no burette existed on the circuit to check it with until 1921.

They reported in March. "We found no dishonesty of any kind, in any quarter, and we wish that
finding to be printed in the same type as the rest." And then: "the wet hay of that bottom is what
thins the cream, and the remedy lies with the land and not with the station." Their sampling
window, in their own words, was **January, February and March** — winter only, the one window in
which bog hay could have had any effect at all.

Eleven years later, the question reopened, Grigg reprinted the report with a covering letter that
contradicts the document printed beneath his own signature. He claimed they had sampled "through
the whole of 1908 — every month of that year, in the barn and on the pasture," and he inflated the
College's Bulletin No. 214 — "not more than five hundredths of one per cent in winter, and nothing
that could be measured on summer pasture" — into "two tenths, gentlemen," dropping the summer
clause. He then rested his case on the agreement between his finding and the College's.

In **1926** Dr. Roland Kepp, V.S., read a paper to the Ordell County Farm Club that inherited the
misquote whole. He laid "two tenths from the College" beside Vose's inflated 0.21 and his own
unmeasured "fifth of a point," and declared that three independent figures agreed — a false
consensus built out of one error repeated and two guesses. He dismissed the 1911 interval as "six
weeks in the flush of the season" without ever asking what the station's other summers read. And
he conceded the thing that should have stopped him: "Their complaint is perfectly just and I have
never doubted it" — before ruling out the station on the ground that he had never in his life read
a test and would not know how.

*Documents:* the 1908 report (Nym Item 11; reprinted entire in Grigg's 1919 letter); Bulletin
No. 214 (quoted entire by Falke in 1959); Orra Keddie's diary; Roy Brant; Kepp's paper; Rundle.
