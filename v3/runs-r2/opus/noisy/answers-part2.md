## 5. The Nine Weeks — an experiment that ran itself and lay unread for forty-seven years (1911)

Two entries in Ansel Keddie's own hand, fourteen months apart in the book and nine weeks apart on
the calendar, contain the answer to the whole affair.

> *May 8. Pipette broke this morning. Using a borrowed measure.*
> *July 10. Our own measure in use again from today. Tests are back where they were. ? — A.K.*

Orra's diary, the same day: "May 8, 1911. A. home cross about a broken measure. Rain all day, the
low piece under water again." **8 May to 10 July 1911 — nine weeks and three days.**

Through those nine weeks the station's average stood at **3.80**. On the day its own measure came
back it fell to **3.61** and stayed there. The 1922 summary leaf at the back of the book records
this without understanding it: "3.61 or within three hundredths of it every year since, save the
nine weeks of 1911 that are noted in their place, when it stood at 3.80."

**One variable changed.** The same patrons, the same herds, the same ground, the same pasture, the
same season, the same feed, the same acid, the same test bottles, the same hand tester, the same
scales, the same procedure at the same hour, and the same man at the crank. Only the measure was
different. Vaught in 1958: "an experiment that ran itself, under conditions no designed trial could
have bettered, and lay unread for forty-seven years. It disposes of every explanation that looks to
the herds, to the ground or to the men, for the sufficient reason that none of those was altered in
those nine weeks, and the figures were."

Roy Brant confirms it independently from his father's private book, which had no connection with
the station's arithmetic: "Through those nine weeks our tests came up, all of us, every farm on the
Bottom; and then the station's own came back into use and the figures went down again… My father
noticed the rise and he noticed the fall and he entered both, and he could make nothing of it, and
I could make nothing of it either, and there it sat for near on fifty years."

Duncan Keddie adds the piece nobody else has. His father came home that summer saying it was
something in the glass, and said it twice, at supper and again by the door, which was not his way —
Duncan was seventeen and remembers the kitchen. What he wrote in the book was a question mark, and
he let it stand fourteen years: "I could not answer it then and I cannot answer it now, and I will
not put anything in its place that I do not know." He had nothing to hold in his hand but a
suspicion against the whole settled practice of the trade, and he kept on, and wrote down every
figure the glass gave him, and those figures were what convicted him.

Two loose ends. **Where the substitute came from is not settled**: Ivy says it came up from Larrow
Green on the cream wagon, Teague says the station's own came up from town, and Roy Brant says the
state inspector left his own measure that June — which cannot be right, since the borrowed measure
was already in use from 8 May and Strawn's own June entry records no loan. Keddie says only "I made
shift with one borrowed." And **the interval's length was twice reported wrong**: Teague and Kepp
both call it six weeks, and Kepp built his entire dismissal of it on that number.

Strawn himself came through that June, watched a test through, and wrote "Nothing here to note."
He was standing over the only true figures the station made in twenty-six years and had no reason
in the world to remark on them.

*Documents:* the station test book's two 1911 entries and 1922 summary leaf; Orra Keddie's diary;
the Brant weigh book; Bulletin 471 §IV; Duncan Keddie; Strawn's circuit book for 1911.

## 6. The Circular — nine or eleven letters, and a brother in the yard (1919)

In June 1919 a printed letter went out over the manager **Selby Vose's** signature to the patrons
of Ostrey Hollow, saying that added water would be looked for and would be dealt with. Bertram
Kyle typed it, and remembers typing it, and thought at the time it was a hard thing to send to men
who were bringing their milk in every morning of their lives, and typed it anyway. It named nobody
and it accused everybody who received it.

**How many families received it cannot be settled.** Vira Toland, Roy Brant and Hazel Brant all say
nine. Orra Keddie's contemporaneous diary says eleven. The documentary rule would prefer the diary;
the count of independent memories prefers nine; and the two "nine" households are independent of
each other. I record both and decline.

Ansel Keddie would not mail them and would not leave them on the platform. He took them round
himself, stood at each door while the man read, and came home at eleven at night with his hat still
on. Two days later Emmet Brant came up, would not come into the house, stood in the yard, and said
he had hauled whole milk to that station since the day it opened and Ansel knew it better than any
man living — and then asked why he had carried the paper himself if he did not believe it. Ansel
said he carried it because he would not have another man carry it. Emmet went home and was never in
that house again: **ten years, brother and sister, until his death in 1929.** Orra: "and he is my
brother."

That is the true shape of the rupture, and it took three accounts to see it. Vira thought the
letter simply cost Emmet most. Roy Brant knew his father resented Keddie's *silence* — "the man who
kept the test book ought to have stood up in front of that circular and did not." Only Orra's diary
supplies the fact that makes both make sense: Keddie did not stay silent, he personally handed the
accusation to his wife's brother at his own door.

Vira Toland's father read his at the table with his hat still on, which he never did, and read it
twice, and put it in the desk drawer in the front room where it stayed until he died. It rained
that day. Some families kept theirs till they died; one woman had hers pinned inside the lid of her
trunk. Hazel Brant's mother read hers twice and put it in the stove.

**The circular's premise was false, and Vose had been told so by the state.** Strawn's 1919 entry:
"I have tested for water at this station on every visit I have made to it and I have never found
any, and I told him so." Vose defended every word of it in 1926 and said he would write it all
again tomorrow, without mentioning that. Sill was the only other man who approved of it. Grigg
reprinted the 1908 report that same season because the question had been "reopened by men who were
boys when we made it." Rundle says the two ends of the valley did not speak civilly for six years,
and that the quarrel came down into his schoolroom entire, "without a word of it being explained,
the way a debt does."

*Documents:* Orra Keddie's diary, June 1919; Vira Toland's tape; Roy Brant; Kyle; Strawn's 1919
entry; Vose's 1926 address; Grigg's covering letter; Rundle; Hazel Brant.

## 7. The Burette, the Board, and the Dismissal (1921–1925)

In **1921** the state began issuing graduated burettes to its field men, in wooden cases with a
printed sheet of directions — the first instrument on the circuit capable of checking a pipette.
Strawn's came in the spring. "It is a slow business… most plants have never had it done."

In **1922** he was at Ostrey Hollow: scales true, no added water, boiler in order — and he **did
not** run their glass against the burette, "the day being short and the road home being what it
was, and I have put that down because it is what happened." That omission cost a year.

On **12 June 1923** he did. "Ran their milk pipettes against the burette. These measures will not
deliver their mark. Condemned the lot, the whole crate of them, and told the manager to order new.
Glass is glass and I did not weigh what it has cost them." The crate went up the ladder into the
loft over the intake room. The order went to Tarnet on 28 June, the reply came 2 July, and new
glass was in use in July — Orra: "A. says they have new glass at the station and new pails, and the
roof is still bad, which is the Association all over." In October Strawn ran the new pipettes
against the burette and they delivered their mark. **1924, the first full year on true glass:
3.80.**

Nobody connected the one thing with the other. Kyle entered the order and filed the inspector's
word for it and says there was no more said of it in the office "than there would have been of a
broken window." Vose called it "a piece of housekeeping that appears in our own order book with a
date and a price against it." Rundle wrote that the glass was condemned "for reasons of his own
that had nothing to do with the quarrel."

In **January 1925** the patrons petitioned — "in the winter, when a farmer has time to be angry."
A **board of arbitration** sat from **17 March to 4 May 1925** under **Alonzo Frick**, attorney of
Ashlin, who supposed he was made chairman because he was the one of the three who kept no cow.
Three sessions: the first at Ashlin, the second and third in the station's own weigh room at
Frick's request, "so that the board might see the plant it was to pass upon instead of hearing it
described by men with an interest in the description." Cleve Oram stood on the platform in the rain
through one of those afternoons and could hear the voices and not the words. The condemned crate
was twenty feet up the ladder over their heads.

**Four exhibits and nothing else**: the station test book; the receiving book; Emmet Brant's private
weigh book; and the state inspector's circuit notebook for 1923 — the book with the condemnation
entry in it. Kyle made the list and carried the books in and out: "I could not have told you then
and cannot tell you now that any man in that room laid the second beside the fourth and looked."

Keddie gave nine sentences standing. Three explanations were argued — water by Vose, theft
insinuated obliquely by Teague, the hay read in from the 1908 report — and not one was proved.
On **30 April 1925** the Association dismissed him, four days before the award and while the board
was still sitting. Frick: "There was no finding against him from us and there never was one… I have
never been able to think well of the Association for it." Orra, that day: "Twenty-nine seasons and a
letter. He came up the road at his ordinary hour with his apron rolled under his arm and hung it
behind the shed door and asked what there was for supper. There was ham. He ate it."

On **4 May** the board found no cause established and awarded the patrons **$3,800**, without
admission, charged to the station account.

*Documents:* Strawn's circuit books 1921–1924; Frick's recollections and exhibit list; Effie
Loomis's minute-book transcription of the award; Kyle; Oram; Orra Keddie's diary; Falke's report.

## 8. Station Gain — the answer in the Association's own books, and the crate in the loft (1897–1970)

From 1897 the Ostrey Hollow station account carried, every year, three figures and a sentence.
The 1898 entry, quoted identically by Teague, Nym and Rundle and described from the copying desk by
Kyle:

> *Ostrey Hollow: butter fat credited to patrons on station test, 57,760 lb. Butter fat received at
> Larrow Green in station cream, 60,800 lb. Station gain, 3,040 lb, five and a quarter per cent, as
> usual. Carried to the general account.*

57,760 ÷ 60,800 = **0.95 exactly**. The Association's own ledgers stated the ratio of the fault
every year for a quarter of a century, in ink, on an open shelf, read out at the annual meeting to
fifty farmers with their hats on their knees. Kyle: "It was not a secret. It was worse than a
secret. It was in plain sight with a name on it… A figure with a name on it has been explained, and
you rule under it and go on to the next."

Teague, who wrote the line, called it furniture: "A figure that jumps about will keep you awake. A
figure that comes in at five and a quarter every year is furniture." He put the question to Vose
once, in 1916, was told to let it alone, and let it alone — and then in 1925 used the same account
to insinuate that the tester was helping himself.

Four men held independent disproofs and none of them put two of them together. **Sill** weighed the
Hollow cream twice himself daily and it was always heavier than the office sheet — "Always more.
Never less" — and made nothing of it: "nobody ever asked me to." **Roy Brant's** scale and the
station's never fell out in thirty years, which excluded the weighing. **Strawn's** lactometer found
no added water on any visit in twenty-eight years. **Jerome Cudd** at Alder Corners tested the same
four Hessel Bottom men's summer milk at 3.78–3.82 for sixteen seasons, 1915–1930, and never docked
one of them a cent — and never thought to compare, because, as his daughter Rosalie said, "he is not
keeping a check on another man's arithmetic seven miles off over a mountain."

**Ivy Keddie**, Ansel's granddaughter, born 1931, of the State Agricultural College at Farland,
assembled five record sets that had never been in one room: the station test book, the Brant weigh
book, the Alder Corners figures, Strawn's notebooks (at Farland since 1947, unasked for eleven
years) and the Association's ledgers. In **February 1958** she went up the ladder into the station
loft and found the crate under a tarpaulin and an inch of chaff. On **9 March 1958** she measured
nine pipettes against a calibrated burette: 16.70, 16.71, 16.72, 16.72, 16.72, 16.73, 16.73, 16.74,
16.71 — **mean 16.72 c.c., short 0.88, ninety-five hundredths of the mark.**

**Bulletin No. 471**, October 1958: "No milk was watered at Ostrey Hollow and no cream was stolen
there. From the first of May, 1897, to the twelfth of June, 1923, the station's tests were low by
one part in twenty because its pipettes were short by one part in twenty, and the difference —
seventy-six thousand pounds of butter fat — stands credited in the Association's own ledgers, year
by year, as station gain."

Thirty-five years after the condemnation, thirty-three after the dismissal, twenty-four after his
death. Ivy: "I have no remedy to propose. I have only the crate, and it is on the table, and you may
look into it on your way out."

Afterward the books dispersed: the test book and the crate to the Ordell County Historical Rooms in
1959; the Brant weigh book to Hazel Brant in 1962 and the Rooms in 1963; the Alder Corners book to
the Vessey County Historical Society in 1970. Duncan Keddie, 1970: "the five books are now in three
buildings and are none of them ours."

*Documents:* the annual ledgers (Nym Item 6); Kyle; Teague; Rundle's retraction; Sill's receiving
and churn books (Nym Item 16); Rosalie Cudd's table; Ivy's measurement sheet; Bulletin No. 471.

# Section B — Relationships

## B1. Ivy Keddie and Ansel Keddie

**Granddaughter.** The chain is stated by the man in the middle of it: Duncan Keddie, born 1894,
son of Ansel and Orra, writes in 1968 that "my daughter is Ivy, born in 1931, which makes her my
father's granddaughter, and I write that plainly because I have already twice seen it printed
otherwise." Ivy herself calls Ansel her grandfather throughout her 1959 talk, and her closing
arithmetic — dead twenty-four years in 1958 — is that of a granddaughter, not a collateral.

Two sources say **great-niece**: the *Ashlin Chronicle* of 3 October 1958 and Orin Falke's county
report of 1959. They are not independent — both were written off the college's press release
within months of each other — and Falke corrected himself in a note appended to his own report:
"I am informed, and have verified, that Miss Keddie is the granddaughter and not the great-niece;
the error on page two is mine." So the only surviving carrier of the error is the newspaper, and
it is contradicted by the subject, by her father, and by the one narrator who withdrew it.

## B2. Ivy Keddie and Hazel Brant

**Second cousins.** Derive it: Emmet Brant and Orra Keddie were brother and sister. Emmet's son was
Roy Brant; Orra's son was Duncan Keddie; Roy and Duncan are therefore **first cousins**, being the
children of a brother and a sister. Hazel is Roy's daughter and Ivy is Duncan's; the children of
first cousins are **second cousins**.

Both men in the intervening generation state this independently and in almost the same words —
Roy Brant in 1955 and Duncan Keddie in 1968 — and Hazel states it herself in 1981 ("People are
forever telling me she is my niece or my cousin once removed… Second cousins. It is not
complicated; it is only that nobody wants to count"). Rundle's 1959 footnote agrees. Two narrators
carry "first cousin once removed" — Vira Toland and Cleve Oram — and both are neighbors repeating
valley talk, not family; the relation is settled by derivation, not by counting heads.

**What the sources leave open in the intervening generations:** Hazel Brant says Orra was Emmet's
*half* sister, by their father's second wife. If true it would make Roy and Duncan half-first
cousins and Hazel and Ivy half-second cousins — the same degree, a different half. It is not true:
Roy Brant flatly denied it in 1955 ("They were full brother and sister, same mother and same
father, whatever you may hear from people who like a story with a stepmother in it"), Duncan calls
Orra simply Emmet's sister and the younger of the two, and Orra's own diary says "my brother
Emmet." Hazel — who opens by warning that she has quantities and not years — has absorbed the exact
rumor her own father tried to kill twenty-six years earlier. Also open: Orra's birth year, given as
1866 by the diary's editor and 1868 by Duncan, whose own statement that she was eighty-two when she
died in 1948 makes 1866 the right one.

## B3. Selby Vose's father

**Gideon Vose** — the Association's organizer, its first president for twenty-two years, and the man
who wrote Article VII at his own kitchen table.

Four sources say son and they include the two best placed: Vose's own words to the membership in
1926 ("My father, who was your first president"), Teague, Duncan Keddie, and Bertram Kyle, who sat
outside the manager's door for twenty-five years and says so with impatience — "the son, whatever is
said at Ashlin, and I have heard the nephew story twice in the last year and it is nonsense."
Vira Toland and Rundle say nephew; Rundle is transcribing valley talk by his own admission.

**Why it matters:** Article VII's last clause — *the test of the station where he delivers shall
govern* — is the whole reason a short pipette could cost anyone a dollar, and it was written by
Selby Vose's father, who died in 1917 and could not be asked about it afterward. The manager
defending the station's figures for twenty-five years was defending his father's rule as well as
his own management, and he defended it hardest in 1919 and 1926, by accusing the patrons. It also
disposes of the suggestion that the Voses' hold on the Association was collateral or accidental:
father and son ran it from 1889 to 1938, and in that half-century the one question neither of them
would have looked into was whether the by-law itself made the valley defenseless against a wrong
figure.

## B4. Vose and Keddie

**No relationship, so far as the sources establish — probably none at all.**

The claim comes from Vira Toland in 1979: Orra Keddie and old Mrs. Vose were first cousins, "and
everybody knew that." Cleve Oram repeats a vaguer form of it in 1977 — "The Keddies and the Voses
were cousins of some sort. It is a small valley. My mother had it from her mother." Both are
avowedly second-hand, and Oram cannot draw the line on paper.

Against them stands the son of the woman said to be the cousin. Duncan Keddie devotes a section to
it: "There was no connection whatever between us and the Voses. People confused them with the
Ashlin Voses, who were no relation to Mr. Gideon either… My mother would have told me. She told me
a great deal about who was who in that valley, being the sort of woman who could not pass a
headstone without giving you the connection, and she never once put a Vose in it." Orra's diary
mentions the manager only as the signature on a circular and never as kin.

I resolve it against the rumor: a denial from inside the household, naming the specific confusion
that generated it, outranks two neighbors reporting hearsay a generation removed. But it cannot be
positively disproved — the diary's editor notes that everything in Orra's hand from before her 1889
marriage was not preserved, so the record that would settle her own family connections does not
exist. **The claim is very probably false; it is not demonstrably false.**

If it were true it would matter, because Vira drew the inference that gives it its force: the 1919
circular would then have been not merely the Association speaking to nine or eleven families, but
somebody's cousin accusing them.
